Phytochemical Characterisation, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antibiofilm, anticancer properties and therapeutic potentials of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil
Authors: Divya Lad, Masidd Khalate, Shaeesta Khaleelahmed Bhavikatti, Shahabe Saquib Abullais, Ahmed A Albariqi, Mohammad Fareed, Venkata Suresh Venkataiah, Mohmed Isaqali Karobari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Eucalyptus globulus essential oil (EGEO) is widely recognized for its pharmacological relevance; however, comprehensive evaluation of its phytochemical composition and bioactivities remains limited. In this study, EGEO was extracted from fresh leaves via hydro-distillation and characterized using GC-MS, LCMS, FTIR, and UV-Visible spectroscopy. Qualitative screening confirmed the presence of key secondary metabolites, including triterpenoids, flavonoids, phenols, steroids. Quantitative analysis revealed high levels of total phenolics (65.12 ± 0.01 mg GAE/g) and flavonoids (57.75 ± 0.01 mg QE/g). The oil exhibited concentration-dependent antioxidant activity in the DPPH assay with an IC₅₀ value of 604.01 µl/mL, indicating potent radical-scavenging ability. Anti-inflammatory assessment through protein denaturation and human red blood cell (HRBC) membrane stabilization assays demonstrated moderate inhibitory and cytoprotective effects comparable to standard drugs. EGEO also showed strong antibacterial efficacy against Tannerella forsythia, Prevotella intermedia and Aggregatibacter with inhibition zones of 19–25 mm while showed notable antibiofilm activity against T. forsythia. Cytotoxic evaluation indicated selective action, showing minimal toxicity toward normal L929 fibroblasts and moderate inhibition of B16F10 melanoma cells. Overall, these findings suggest that Eucalyptus globulus essential oil is a rich source of bioactive phytoconstituents with potential therapeutic applications as a natural antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anticancer agent.Keywords: Eucalyptus globulus, Essential oil, Phytochemical profiling, Antioxidant, Anti-inflammatory, Antibacterial, Antibiofilm, AnticancerSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

The genus Eucalyptus (family: Myrtaceae) comprises nearly 900 species and subspecies, primarily native to Australia and Tasmania, but now widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide1. In India, Eucalyptus was first introduced in the late 18th century at Nandi Hills, Mysore, and has since become naturalized across several states, including Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Punjab, and Haryana. Among the species distributed in India, Eucalyptus globulus and Eucalyptus citriodora are the most prominent due to their significant essential oil yield and versatile therapeutic potential1,2.

Eucalyptus species are tall, evergreen trees characterized by rapid growth, adaptability to diverse soils, and high essential oil content, making them valuable for commercial cultivation2. The essential oils, commonly referred to as Eucalyptus essential oils (EEOs), are typically obtained from leaves via steam or hydro distillation1,3. These oils are complex mixtures dominated by monoterpenoids such as eucalyptol (1,8-cineole), eudesmol, limonene, α- and β-pinene, and terpineol, which confer distinct aromatic and pharmacological properties. These bioactive constituents are responsible for the characteristic aroma and multiple pharmacological effects of the oil3. The essential oil of E. globulus has been traditionally utilized for its potent antiseptic, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties, as well as its ability to alleviate respiratory ailments such as bronchitis, asthma, and sinusitis4.

Phytochemical investigations have shown that E. globulus essential oil exhibits a wide range of biological activities, including antibacterial, antioxidant, antiviral, and anticancer effects1–4. The high concentration of 1,8-cineole contributes significantly to its antimicrobial potency against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Listeria monocytogenes3,5. Compounds such as α-terpineol, para-isopropylphenol, and macrocarpals (H, I, and J) have demonstrated antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy against several oral pathogens3,5. Furthermore, the phenolic and terpenoid constituents in EGEO act as efficient radical scavengers, conferring strong antioxidant activity that helps mitigate oxidative stress and cellular damage1,6.

In addition to its antimicrobial potential, E. globulus has shown anti-inflammatory and anti-diabetic properties1,3. The principal constituent eucalyptol has been reported to inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokines and aid in the management of respiratory tract infections and other steroid-sensitive conditions3,7. Recent evidence also indicates that E. globulus essential oil promotes wound healing through its antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects, while some of its terpenoid compounds display selective cytotoxicity toward cancer cells by inducing apoptosis1–3. In addition, bioactive components like terpinen-4-ol, α-pinene, and γ-terpinene have been implicated in cytotoxic and pro-apoptotic effects against various cancer cell lines, highlighting its potential as a natural anticancer agent8–10.

Given its broad pharmacological potential, E. globulus essential oil has emerged as a promising natural bioactive agent for therapeutic and industrial applications. However, systematic evaluation of its phytochemical composition and biomedical properties remains essential for validating its efficacy and potential use in pharmaceutical formulations. Therefore, the present study aims to extract, characterize, and evaluate the phytochemical constituents of E. globulus essential oil and investigate its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibiofilm, antibacterial activity against selected oral pathogens, and anticancer potential. Understanding the chemical basis and bioactivity of this oil can provide valuable insights into its therapeutic applications and contribute to the development of novel plant-based formulations for human health and wellness.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval and collection of the plant material

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Infinite Biotech Institute of Research and Analytics, Sangli, Maharashtra, India, under protocol code IAEC/IBIR/2025/12. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). A bulk sample of fresh E. globulus leaves were collected from a nursery in Sangli, Maharashtra, India. The permission to collect E. globulus was obtained. Then, it was washed with three litres of distilled water to remove dirt from the leaf surface. The leaves of E. globulus were identified taxonomically for their authenticity by a senior botanist, Dr N J Wadkar, at Infinite Biotech Institute of Research, Sangli, India and were identified by the herbarium and a voucher (IBIR996502) specimen of the plant species was deposited for further reference11.The plant collection and research activities were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. The study complies with the principles outlined by the IUCN Policy Statement on Research Involving Species at Risk of Extinction12 and the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora13.

Extraction of the Eucalyptus globulus essential oil

The Clevenger apparatus was used to extract E. globulus essential oil by hydro distillation method. Hydro-distillation of leaves was performed in triplicate (n = 3) under identical conditions. The leaves were cut into small pieces. A 500 mL round bottom flask filled with 100 gm of E. globulus leaves with sufficient volume of distilled water and subjected to hydro distillation in a Clevenger apparatus for 4 h. at 55 °C. Lower temperature was employed to minimize thermal degradation and hydrolytic changes during prolonged exposure to high temperatures. The steam-volatile oil vapours were condensed with circulating water, and the essential oil was separated from the aqueous phase in the apparatus. The collected oil was stored in amber colour glass vial covered with aluminium foil to protect it from light at 2–8 °C11,14.

Phytochemical analysis

To determine phytoconstituents, preliminary qualitative tests were performed to detect active secondary metabolites.

Preliminary Qualitative screening of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil

The Eucalyptus globulus essential oil extracted was screened for active secondary metabolites such as terpenoids, cardiac glycosides, saponins, alkaloids, flavonoids, phenols, tannins by using reported methods as below15,16.

Test for Terpenoids

Salkowski test

When EGEO was treated with 0.5 mL of concentrated sulphuric acid, shaken and allowed to stand lower layer turns red indicating presence of triterpenoids.

Liebermann Burchard test

The EGEO was treated with 2 mL of acetic anhydride and mixed well. When 1–2 drops of concentrated sulphuric acid was added from the sides of the test tube, it shows a brown ring at the junction of the two layers and the upper layer turns green.

Test for Cardiac Glycosides

Keller test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with 1.5 mL of glacial acetic acid in 2 mL of 5% ferric chloride solution and concentrated sulphuric acid was added from the side of the test tube which shows the separation between two layers, lower layer shows reddish brown and upper layer turns bluish green in colour.

Raymond’s test

1 mL EGEO was treated with 0.5 mL of dinitrobenzene in hot methanolic alkali gives violet colour.

Legal’s test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with 1 mL pyridine followed by addition of 1 mL sodium nitroprusside which gives pink to red colour.

Test for Saponins

Foam test

1 mL of EGEO when mixed with water and shaken show the formation of foam which is stable at least for 15 min indicates positive foam test.

Raymond’s test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with dinitrobenzene in hot methanolic alkali gives violet colour.

Test for Alkaloids

Mayer’s test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with 1–2 drops of Mayer’s reagent (potassium mercuric iodide) gives, cream coloured precipitate.

Wagner’s test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with 1–2 drops of Wagner’s reagent (iodine in potassium iodide) along the side of the test tube gives, brown precipitate.

Hager’s test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with 1–2 mL of Hager’s reagent (saturated picric acid solution) gives yellow precipitate.

Test for Flavonoids

Ferric chloride test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with few drops of 5% ferric chloride solution shows intense green/bluish black colour.

Shinoda test

1 mL of EGEO was mixed with 5mL of alcohol followed by addition of few fragments of magnesium ribbon and few drops of concentrated hydrochloric acid show pink to magenta red colour.

Zinc-Hydrochloric acid-reduction test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with pinch of zinc dust followed by treatment with 4–5 drops of concentrated hydrochloric acid shows magenta red colour.

Alkaline reagent test

1 mL of EGEO when treated with 2 mL of 2% sodium hydroxide solution along with few drops of dil. HCl, the intense yellow colour becomes colourless on addition of diluted acid.

Lead acetate solution test

1 mL of EGEO was treated with few drops of lead acetate (10% w/v) gives, yellow precipitate.

Test for Phenols

Ferric Chloride Test

Aqueous EGEO was treated with 1 to 2 drops of Iron III chloride (FeClɜ) solution. A blue precipitate appears indicates a positive test.

Test for Steroids

Chloroform Test

1 mL of EGEO was mixed with 1 mL of chloroform in a test tube. An equal volume of concentrated sulphuric acid was carefully added along the side of the tube to form a separate layer. The development of a red coloration in the upper chloroform layer along with a yellow colour exhibiting green fluorescence in the sulphuric acid layer was considered indicative of the presence of steroidal compounds.

Salkowski’s test

The second portion of solution above was mixed with concentrated sulphuric acid carefully so that the acid formed a lower layer and the interface was observed for a reddish-brown color indicative of steroid ring.

Test for tannins

Ferric-chloride test

1 mL of EGEO upon treatment with few drops of 10% of ferric chloride solution gives dark colour.

Quantitative estimation of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil

Estimation of total phenolic content by Folin-Ciocalteu phenol reagent

The total phenolic contents in E. globulus essential oil (EGEO) were estimated by using Folin-Ciocalteu reagent with Gallic acid as standard. The EGEO in the concentration range of 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000 µl/mL was used in the analysis. The reaction mixture consists of 1 mL of EGEO and 9 mL of distilled water in a 25 mL volumetric flask as the reaction between phenolics and reagents occurs in aqueous medium. A volume of 1 mL Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was added to the prepared mixture and mixed thoroughly. After 5 min, 10 mL of 7% sodium carbonate solution was introduced, and the final volume was made up to 25 mL with distilled water. In parallel, standard gallic acid solutions (200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 µl/mL) were prepared following the same procedure. The reaction mixtures were incubated for 90 min at room temperature, after which the absorbance of both test and standard solutions was recorded at 550 nm against a reagent blank using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. The total phenolic content of the extract was calculated and expressed as milligrams gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per gram of extract17.

Estimation of total flavonoid content by Aluminium Chloride (AlClɜ) method

The total flavonoid content in E. globulus essential oil (EGEO) was determined using the aluminium chloride colorimetric assay. Due to hydrophobic nature of the essential oil, 10µL of EGEO was dissolved in methanol and the volume was adjusted to 1 mL to ensure complete solubilization. This methanolic solution was then transferred into a 10 mL of volumetric flask and mixed with 4 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, 0.3 mL of 5% sodium nitrite solution was added, followed by the addition of 0.3 mL of 10% aluminium chloride solution after 5 min. After another 5 min, 2 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide was introduced, and the final volume was made up to 10 mL with distilled water. Standard quercetin solutions (200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 µl/mL) were prepared using the same procedure. The absorbance of the EGEO and standard was recorded at 510 nm against a reagent blank using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. The flavonoid concentration was expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents (QE) per gram of extract17,18.

Characterization of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) profiling

The volatile constituents of E. globulus essential oil were analysed using an Agilent 5975 GC-MS system equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.e., 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium served as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL min⁻¹. The oven temperature was programmed from 50 °C (held for 5 min) to 250 °C at a rate of 3 °C min⁻¹ and maintained at 250 °C for 10 min. The injector temperature was set at 250 °C with a split ratio of 1:50, and the injection volume was 0.1 µL. Mass spectra were recorded in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, scanning from m/z 40–500. The ion source and quadrupole temperatures were maintained at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. Compound identification was performed by comparing the calculated retention indices (RI), obtained using n-alkanes (C₈-C₂₀) under the same conditions, with values reported in the literature and by matching mass spectra with the Wiley 7n library. Quantitative analysis was carried out using an external standard method with calibration curves generated from representative compounds19,20.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis

The chemical composition of the Eucalyptus globulus essential oil was analysed using Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS). The oil sample was diluted in HPLC-grade methanol (1:10, v/v) and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter before analysis. Chromatographic separation was carried out on a reverse-phase C18 column using a gradient mobile phase consisting of water containing 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B). The flow rate was maintained at 0.3 mL/min with an injection volume of 5 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using electrospray ionization in positive ion mode over an m/z range of 50–1000. The obtained total ion chromatogram (TIC) was used to detect the major constituents, which were tentatively identified based on their retention times and mass spectral data21.

UV-visible spectroscopy

UV-spectrophotometric analysis of E. globulus essential oil was recorded in the wavelength range of 300–800 nm was conducted using UV-VIS spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan) with double-distilled water as the blank. The optical properties were evaluated based on characteristic absorption resulting from excitation and subsequent de-excitation of surface plasmons22.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis

The FT-IR spectrum of E. globulus essential oil was recorded using an FTIR spectrophotometer (Bruker ALPHA II, USA) in the wavenumber range of 3500–1000 cm⁻¹ with a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. The analysis was performed using a liquid sample, prepared by placing a single drop of the colloidal suspension onto the instrument’s pedestal23.

Antibacterial activity of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil against selected pathogens by well diffusion method

Bacterial strains

The antibacterial activity of extracted E. globulus essential oil (EGEO) evaluated against Gram negative bacteria T. forsythia (ATCC 43037), Prevotella intermedia (ATCC 25611) and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (ATCC 29522). These bacterial strains were obtained from the NCIM CSIR NCL Pune and maintained on appropriate anaerobic culture media. Bacterial cultures were prepared by transferring a single colony into a fresh medium and grown overnight under anaerobic condition at 37 °C for 24 h. Turbidity of the culture was adjusted with sterile saline solution to match 0.5 Mc Farland standard.

Well diffusion method

The antibacterial activity of EGEO was evaluated using the agar well diffusion method against Gram negative periodontal pathogens. The inoculum culture was prepared using bacterial cultures, followed by standardization. Approximately 15 mL of molten nutrient agar (HiMedia) was poured into sterilized petri plate and allowed to solidify. Once set, 100 µL of the bacterial suspension was evenly spread over the agar surface until the medium absorbs the bacterial suspension completely using a sterile glass spreader. Wells of 6 mm diameter were created aseptically in the agar using a sterile cork borer. The test formulations were prepared in DMSO and loaded into the well. Metronidazole (1 mg/mL) served as the positive control, while sterile distilled water was used as the negative control. Strict anaerobic conditions were maintained to ensure proper growth of the obligate anaerobic periodontal pathogens. An oxygen-free environment was established using an anaerobic jar/incubator containing a gas mixture of approximately 85% nitrogen, 10% hydrogen, and 5% carbon dioxide. Culture media were pre-reduced with thioglycolate or cysteine to remove dissolved oxygen and handled carefully to minimize oxygen exposure. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C under controlled humidity, with the pH maintained between 7.2 and 7.6. Anaerobic indicators such as resazurin or methylene blue were used to confirm the absence of oxygen throughout the incubation period. Post-incubation, the antibacterial activity was determined by measuring the diameter of the zone of inhibition (ZI) surrounding each well, expressed in millimetres27,29.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC of E. globulus essential oil was evaluated using resazurin based microdilution assay against Gram-negative bacteria T. forsythia, P. intermedia, and A. actinomycetemcomitans. The resazurin was prepared by dissolving 270 mg of resazurin in 40 mL of sterile distilled water followed by vortexing to ensure complete dissolution of resazurin with solvent. The assay was performed in sterile 96 well plates. The EGEO was prepared in dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) of various concentrations ranging from 7.8, 15.6, 31.2, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, 1000 µg/mL. 100 µL EGEO was dispensed into the wells followed by addition of 50 µL of sterile nutrient broth. Subsequently, 10 µL of resazurin indicator and 10 µL of microbial suspension were added to each well. Streptomycin was used as reference antibiotic at same concentrations. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h under strict anaerobic conditions for T. forsythia and P. intermedia, and in 5% CO₂ atmosphere for A. actinomycetemcomitans. The wells which showed the colour change from blue to pink were considered positive for microbial growth whereas the wells retaining blue colour indicated inhibition of microbial growth. This represents the reduction of resazurin dye (blue) to resorufin (pink). Absorbance was measured at 600 nm using an ELISA microplate reader. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), defined as the lowest concentration of EGEO that prevents visible microbial growth27,31.

Cytotoxic evaluation of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil by MTT Assay over L929 cell line (Murine fibroblast cell line)

The cytotoxic potential of E. globulus essential oil was evaluated using the MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in complete culture medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ to allow cell attachment. Subsequently, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of EGEO (200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 µl/mL) in triplicate, while untreated cells served as the control. After 24 h. of incubation under identical conditions, the culture medium was carefully aspirated, and 20 µL of MTT reagent (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well. Plates were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C to allow the conversion of MTT to insoluble formazan crystals by metabolically active cells. The formation of purple formazan was visually confirmed under an inverted phase-contrast microscope. The supernatant was then removed, and 200 µL of DMSO was added to each well to solubilize the crystals. After a 10-min incubation at 37 °C (protected from light), the absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to the untreated control, reflecting the cytotoxic effect of EGEO at different concentrations27,32.

Evaluation of anticancer potential of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil by MTT assay over B16F10 (murine melanoma cell line)

B16F10 murine melanoma cells (derived from C57BL/6J mice) were cultured in complete growth medium and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded in 96-well tissue culture plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 100 µL of medium and allowed to adhere for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were treated with varying concentrations (200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 µl/mL) of the EGEO and the standard drug 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU). Control wells received 0.2% DMSO in PBS under identical conditions. All treatments were performed in triplicate. Following 24 h. of exposure, the culture medium was carefully removed, and 20 µL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well. The plates were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ to allow viable cells to reduce MTT to insoluble purple formazan crystals. Crystal formation was examined under an inverted phase-contrast microscope. The medium was then aspirated, and 200 µL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan. Plates were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C in the dark (wrapped with aluminium foil). Absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated relative to the untreated control to determine the cytotoxic effects of EGEO and 5-Fluorouracil on B16F10 cells33.

The total ion chromatogram revealed several peaks within the retention time range of approximately 13–38 min, indicating the presence of multiple volatile constituents. A prominent peak observed at ~ 13.4 min suggests the dominance of 1,8-Cineole, which is commonly reported as the major component of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil. Other notable constituents included longifolene (4.75%), p-cymene-2,5-diol (4.26%) and several fatty-acid derivatives (e.g., tetradecanoic acid 3.83%). LC-MS analysis of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis was performed to further characterize the chemical constituents present in Eucalyptus globulus essential oil (EGEO). The chromatographic profile exhibited several well-resolved peaks across the retention time range of approximately 4–17 min, indicating the presence of multiple bioactive compounds within the essential oil. Among these, relatively intense peaks were observed within the mid-retention time region (around 7–12 min), suggesting the predominance of certain constituents in the oil. Additional minor peaks appearing at higher retention times represent compounds present in comparatively lower abundance. The mass spectral data associated with these peaks revealed molecular ion fragments characteristic of terpenoid and oxygenated terpenoid derivatives, which are commonly reported constituents of Eucalyptus essential oils (Table 4). The detection of multiple compounds confirms that EGEO possesses a chemically diverse profile composed of volatile and semi-volatile phytoconstituents. Such terpenoid-rich composition is known to contribute significantly to the pharmacological properties of the oil, including its antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antibiofilm activities (Fig. 3).

Screening of biomedical properties of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil

Evaluation of antioxidant potential of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil

The antioxidant potential of EGEO was evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay at concentrations ranging from 200 to 1000 µl/mL. EGEO exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in radical scavenging activity, with inhibition ranging from 32.64% at 200 µl/mL to 60.10% at 1000 µl/mL, whereas the standard antioxidant ascorbic acid showed higher scavenging activity across the tested concentrations. The IC₅₀ value of EGEO was 604.01 µL/mL, indicating moderate antioxidant potential (Table 6). Statistical analysis using Two-way ANOVA indicated no statistically significant difference between the standard and EGEO treatments (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that EGEO contains bioactive constituents capable of donating electrons to neutralize free radicals. (Fig. 6 A).

Anti-inflammatory activity of the Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil by protein denaturation method

The anti-inflammatory activity of EGEO was evaluated using the protein denaturation assay at concentrations ranging from 200 to 1000 µl/mL and compared with the standard drug diclofenac sodium (Fig. 6B). The results showed a concentration-dependent increase in percentage inhibition, indicating the ability of EGEO to inhibit protein denaturation. At the highest tested concentration (1000 µl/mL), EGEO exhibited 58.44% inhibition, whereas the standard drug showed 85.06% inhibition. The IC₅₀ value of EGEO was 640.54 µl/mL, suggesting moderate anti-inflammatory potential. Statistical analysis using Two-way ANOVA indicated no statistically significant difference between the standard and EGEO treatments (p > 0.05). Although the inhibitory effect of EGEO was lower than that of the standard, the gradual increase in activity with concentration suggests the presence of bioactive constituents capable of stabilizing protein structures and contributing to anti-inflammatory effects.

Evaluation of anti-inflammatory potential of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil by membrane stabilization assay

The anti-inflammatory potential of EGEO was evaluated using the human red blood cell (HRBC) membrane stabilization assay. EGEO demonstrated a concentration-dependent protective effect against hypotonic solution-induced haemolysis of human erythrocytes, reaching a maximum membrane stabilization of 87.70% at the highest tested concentration (1000 µl/mL). In comparison, aspirin, used as a reference standard, exhibited higher membrane stabilization with a maximum protection of 93.46%. Although the standard drug showed greater activity, statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA revealed that the difference between EGEO and aspirin was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The IC₅₀ value of EGEO was calculated to be 815.13 µl/mL. Membrane stabilization represents the proportion of erythrocytes protected from lysis, where higher percentages indicate stronger anti-inflammatory potential (Fig. 6 C).

Heat induced haemolysis assay

The anti-inflammatory activity of EGEO was evaluated using the heat-induced haemolysis assay of human red blood cells. EGEO demonstrated a concentration-dependent protective effect against erythrocyte lysis. The percentage of haemolysis decreased from 34.39% at 200 µl/mL to 13.29% at 1000 µl/mL, corresponding to membrane stabilization values increasing from 65.61% to 86.71%. These findings indicate that EGEO effectively protects erythrocyte membranes from heat-induced damage, suggesting potential anti-inflammatory activity. Statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA indicated that the differences among concentrations were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Antibacterial activity of E. globulus essential oil against selected pathogens by well diffusion method

The antibacterial activity of EGEO was assessed against Gram negative bacteria T. forsythia, P. intermedia, and A. actinomycetemcomitans using the well diffusion method, with Metronidazole (1 mg/mL) as the reference. EGEO exhibited notable inhibition, producing zones of 25 mm, 23 mm, and 19 mm, respectively. In comparison, Metronidazole showed inhibition zones of 20 mm, 25 mm, and 16 mm. These findings indicate that EGEO possesses strong antibacterial potential (Fig. 7 A, B, C, Table 7).

Antibiofilm activity of Eucalyptus. globulus essential oil by crystal violet microtiter plate assay

The antibiofilm activity of EGEO was evaluated against T. forsythia using the crystal violet microtiter plate assay. This organism was selected for the antibiofilm study based on the antibacterial screening results, where it exhibited the highest susceptibility among the tested periodontal pathogens, including P. intermedia and A. actinomycetemcomitans. The findings revealed that both EGEO and the reference drug, Metronidazole, inhibited biofilm formation in a concentration-dependent manner. The standard drug demonstrated stronger antibiofilm activity, with inhibition increasing from 34.35% at 200 µL/mL to 74.06% at 1000 µL/mL. In comparison, EGEO exhibited moderate inhibition of biofilm formation, ranging from 29.57% to 46.09% across the tested concentrations. These results suggest that EGEO possesses the ability to suppress biofilm formation of T. forsythia, although its inhibitory effect was less pronounced than that of the standard drug (Fig. 6D). Statistical analysis using two-way ANOVA indicated that the difference between the treatments was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC of EGEO was determined using resazurin method against the tested periodontal pathogens. The wells retaining the blue colour indicated the inhibition of bacterial growth whereas pink colour change indicates the metabolically active bacteria. Across the three tested organisms-A. actinomycetemcomitans, P. intermedia and T. forsythia. EGEO demonstrated concentration-dependent antibacterial activity. The MIC values of EGEO were approximately 125 µl/mL for A. actinomycetemcomitans, 250 µl/mL for T. forsythia, and 125 µl/mL for P. intermedia. In comparison, streptomycin showed stronger antibacterial activity with MIC values around 31.2 µl/mL against all tested strains. These findings indicate moderate antibacterial efficacy of EGEO relative to the standard antibiotic (Fig. 8 A, B, C).

The cytotoxicity of EGEO was assessed on L929 fibroblast cells across concentrations of 200–1000 µl/mL. Discussion

The present study investigated the phytochemical composition and biological activities of essential oil obtained from Eucalyptus globulus. The essential oil yield obtained through hydro-distillation was 1.00 ± 0.05% (v/w), which falls within the range previously reported for E. globulus essential oil (EGEO). Previous studies have reported that the yield of EGEO generally ranges between 0.5 and 2.0% depending on geographical origin, climatic conditions, and extraction techniques1,2. Environmental factors such as plant maturity, harvesting season, and extraction method can significantly influence the quantity and composition of essential oils11,14.

Preliminary phytochemical screening confirmed the presence of several bioactive constituents, including phenols, flavonoids, steroids, and triterpenoids, indicating the chemical richness of the essential oil. These phytochemicals are widely recognized for their diverse pharmacological properties. In particular, phenolic compounds and flavonoids are well known for their antioxidant potential due to their ability to donate hydrogen atoms or electrons and neutralize free radicals15,17. In the present study, the essential oil exhibited appreciable levels of total phenolic content and total flavonoid content, which may contribute to its observed biological activities. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies where Eucalyptus extracts showed considerable phenolic and flavonoid content associated with antioxidant properties6,10.

The chemical characterization of the essential oil using Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry revealed the presence of several volatile terpenoid compounds. The chromatographic profile indicated that 1,8-cineole (eucalyptol) is the major component of the oil. This compound is widely recognized as the principal constituent of EGEO and is responsible for many of its biological activities20,31. Other monoterpenes commonly reported in Eucalyptus oil include α-pinene, β-pinene, and limonene, which contribute to antimicrobial and antioxidant properties5,21. The predominance of oxygenated monoterpenes observed in the present study is consistent with earlier reports describing the chemical composition of Eucalyptus essential oil32,33.

The antioxidant potential of EGEO was evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay, which demonstrated concentration-dependent free radical scavenging activity. Although the activity of EGEO was lower than that of the standard antioxidant, the essential oil still exhibited moderate antioxidant potential. The antioxidant activity may be attributed to the presence of phenolic compounds and terpenoid constituents capable of neutralizing free radicals and preventing oxidative damage24,25. Previous investigations have similarly reported that Eucalyptus essential oil possesses significant antioxidant properties due to its phytochemical constituents26. From an industrial perspective, essential oils with moderate antioxidant activity can still serve as natural additives or supportive antioxidant agents in pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and food preservation applications. Eucalyptus globulus essential oil is already widely used in oral care products, antiseptic formulations, and herbal preparations, where its combined antimicrobial and antioxidant properties enhance overall product efficacy.

Anti-inflammatory activity was assessed using protein denaturation inhibition and membrane stabilization assays. Protein denaturation is a widely recognized mechanism involved in inflammatory disorders, and compounds capable of preventing denaturation may act as anti-inflammatory agents. In the present study, EGEO demonstrated the ability to inhibit protein denaturation and stabilize erythrocyte membranes. The stabilization of erythrocyte membranes suggests that the essential oil may prevent the release of lysosomal enzymes that contribute to inflammatory processes27,28. The anti-inflammatory activity observed in this study may therefore be associated with the presence of terpenoid compounds that interact with membrane proteins and enhance membrane stability.

The antibacterial activity of EGEO was evaluated against major periodontal pathogens including P. intermedia, A. actinomycetemcomitans, and T. forsythia. Among the tested microorganisms, T. forsythia exhibited the highest susceptibility to the essential oil. Essential oils rich in monoterpenes are known to exert antibacterial effects by disrupting bacterial cell membranes, altering membrane permeability, and interfering with metabolic pathways26,34. Such mechanisms ultimately lead to leakage of cellular components and inhibition of bacterial growth.

Based on the antibacterial findings, T. forsythia was selected for further evaluation of antibiofilm activity. Biofilm formation is a critical factor in periodontal infections because microbial communities embedded in biofilms exhibit increased resistance to antimicrobial agents and host defense mechanisms. The crystal violet microtiter plate assay demonstrated that EGEO inhibited biofilm formation in a concentration-dependent manner. Although the inhibition was lower than that of the standard drug, the essential oil still demonstrated measurable antibiofilm activity. Previous studies have also reported that EGEO and its major component 1,8-cineole exhibit antibiofilm and antimicrobial properties against pathogenic microorganisms29,30.

Cytotoxicity assessment further revealed that EGEO exhibited low toxicity toward normal L929 fibroblast cells while exerting moderate inhibitory effects against B16F10 melanoma cells. This selective cytotoxicity is a desirable property in potential anticancer agents, as it indicates targeted action with minimal harm to healthy cells. Comparable observations were made by Azadmanesh et al., who reported that Eucalyptus oils and their nano-formulations induce apoptosis in cancer cells through oxidative stress modulation and mitochondrial pathway activation33. The moderate anticancer activity observed in this study can thus be attributed to the synergistic action of its terpenoid components, particularly 1,8-cineole and terpineol, which have been shown to interfere with tumor cell proliferation9.

However, while the in vitro results are promising, in vivo validation before confirming the biological potential like anti-cancer activity and molecular-level investigations remain essential to elucidate its pharmacodynamic pathways, optimal dosage, and long-term safety. Advanced studies focusing on nanoencapsulation, controlled release formulations, and synergistic interactions with conventional drugs could further enhance its therapeutic utility. A limitation of the present study is the lack of investigation into molecular mechanisms, such as membrane disruption or apoptosis-related pathways. Future studies will focus on these aspects to better elucidate the therapeutic potential of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil.

Overall, the results of this study demonstrate that the Eucalyptus globulus essential oil possesses a rich phytochemical composition and exhibits multiple biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and antibiofilm effects. These activities are primarily associated with the presence of monoterpenes such as 1,8-cineole and related terpenoid compounds, which have been widely reported to possess significant pharmacological potential30,34. Therefore, Eucalyptus globulus essential oil may serve as a promising natural source for the development of therapeutic agents, particularly for the management of oxidative stress, inflammatory conditions, and periodontal infections.

Conclusion

The present investigation provides a detailed physicochemical and biological evaluation of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil (EGEO), affirming its remarkable potential as a multifunctional natural bioactive agent. The systematic characterization through GC-MS, FTIR, and UV-Visible spectroscopy revealed the dominance of oxygenated monoterpenes such as 1,8-cineole, α-pinene, and terpineol, which are largely responsible for the oil’s diverse therapeutic properties. The qualitative and quantitative phytochemical analyses further confirmed the presence of phenolics, flavonoids, tannins, terpenoids, alkaloids, and saponins, with substantial total phenolic (65.12 ± 0.01 mg GAE/g) and flavonoid (57.75 ± 0.01 mg QE/g) contents, signifying strong antioxidant potential.

Biological evaluations demonstrated that EGEO exerts significant radical scavenging activity with an IC₅₀ value of 604.01 µl/mL, reflecting its capacity to neutralize reactive oxygen species and prevent oxidative stress-induced cellular damage. The oil exhibited moderate anti-inflammatory activity in both protein denaturation and HRBC membrane stabilization assays, indicating its ability to maintain protein integrity and protect cellular membranes under inflammatory conditions. Furthermore, EGEO showed notable antibacterial efficacy against oral pathogens, particularly Tannerella forsythia, Prevotella intermedia, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, alongside substantial antibiofilm inhibition, which enhances its relevance in managing biofilm-associated infections.

In cytotoxic assays, EGEO displayed selective biological activity; showing minimal toxicity toward normal fibroblast (L929) cells while moderately inhibiting melanoma (B16F10) cancer cells; suggesting its potential as a safe and naturally derived anticancer agent. These collective findings underscore the therapeutic promise of Eucalyptus globulus essential oil as a source of natural antioxidants and anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antibiofilm, and anticancer compounds.

In present findings confirm that, EGEO serves as a valuable phytopharmaceutical candidate with diverse pharmacological properties. Its integration into modern formulations could support the development of eco-friendly and biocompatible therapeutic products for managing oxidative stress, inflammation, microbial infections, and cancer-related disorders. Future studies focusing on purification of individual components, mechanistic investigations, in vivo validation, and formulation optimization are warranted to advance the translational potential of EGEO in pharmaceutical and biomedical applications.
