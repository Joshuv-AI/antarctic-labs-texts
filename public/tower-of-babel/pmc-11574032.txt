L-shaped association of plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol with atrial fibrillation recurrence after catheter ablation: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Huiming Zou, Qianghui Huang, Qianwei Huang, Bingchao Hu, Wenhao He, Zirong Xia, Zongcai Duan, Guoqing Li, Jianxin Hu, Jinzhu Hu, Biming Zhan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe association between plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence after catheter ablation remains unclear. We aimed to assess the relationship between preprocedural LDL-C and the AF recurrence in patients undergoing catheter ablation. The cohort study consecutively included AF patients who underwent de novo catheter ablation between April 2021 and January 2023 in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University in Jiangxi Province, China. Patients were divided into quartiles based on their baseline fasting LDL-C level (Q1-Q4). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence. Our analysis included the use of a generalized additive model and smooth curve fitting (penalized spline method), and two-piecewise Cox proportional hazards models, to address the nonlinearity between preprocedural LDL-C and AF recurrence. A total of 482 AF patients with de novo catheter ablation were enrolled, with a median follow-up period of 15.00 months, AF recurrence occurred in 96 (19.92%) patients. The relationship between preprocedural LDL-C and AF recurrence after ablation presented as an L-shape, and the inflection point for the curve was found at the LDL-C level of 3.20 mmol/L (Log likelihood ratio P = 0.031). The hazard ratios (HR) [(95% confidence intervals (CI)] for AF recurrence were 0.50 (0.33–0.74) and 2.11 (0.76–5.89) to the left and right of the inflection point, respectively. Lower LDL-C level is associated with increased AF recurrence risk after catheter ablation were consistent across all subgroups.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, Radiofrequency catheter ablation, Recurrence, Cohort studySubject terms: Medical research, Cardiovascular diseases

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation, also referred to as “AF”, is a common clinical arrhythmia. As the global population ages, the incidence and prevalence of AF are rising worldwide1,2. AF might severely affect a patient’s quality of life as it is associated with serious complications, such as stroke, heart failure, cognitive impairment and aggravates economic burden3. Current treatment of AF is in large part based on radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA), which enhance quality of life for symptomatic patients4. However, the high recurrence rate post-ablation remains a major concern, and its underlying risk factors are complicated5.

Abnormal plasma cholesterol level is associated with cardiovascular diseases, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) level above the normal range is recognized as a risk factor for coronary heart diseases6,7.Nevertheless, the relationship between cholesterol and atrial fibrillation appears to differ. Previous research has revealed a “cholesterol paradox” in relation to AF, this paradox suggests that cholesterol level, particularly LDL-C, have an inverse correlation with the risk of developing AF8–10.However, the association between plasma LDL-C and AF recurrence after catheter ablation remains unclear. To date, Only two single-center studies have found a similar inverse relationship between cholesterol level and AF recurrence11,12, but sufficient clinical evidences are still lacking, and there exist no prospective studies specifically examining the potential nonlinear association between preprocedural LDL-C and postoperative AF recurrence. Additionally, neither the ACC1nor ESC13 Atrial fibrillation guidelines include LDL-C management recommendations for AF patients, particularly during the perioperative period of RFCA. Thus, our study aims to investigate the relationship between plasma LDL-C and the risk of AF recurrence in patients underwent catheter ablation.

Methods

Study design and populations

This study included patients aged 18 to 80 years who underwent de novo catheter ablation in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University from April 2021 to January 2023. All participants were followed postoperatively until February 2024. The exclusion criteria were as follows: left atrial thrombosis, left atrial anteroposterior diameter > 55 mm, moderate to severe valvular heart disease, uncontrolled thyroid dysfunction, congenital heart disease, severe hepatic or renal impairment, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class III-IV, hematologic disorders, anticoagulation contraindications, expected malignancies with a prognosis of less than one year. Additional exclusions are outlined in Sample selection flow chart (Fig. 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the trial was registered at Clinical Trial (ChiCTR2100054521), All patients provided informed consent prior to the procedures.

Sample selection flowchart.

Data collection

Before catheter ablation, baseline characteristics were collected for all participants, this included sex, age, current smoking, current drinking, AF type, body mass index (BMI), Duration of AF, and history of hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), coronary artery disease (CAD), HF, and prior stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA). Pre-ablation medication usages were also recorded: covering angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers (CCB), and lipid lowering drugs (LLD). Persistent and long-standing persistent AF were collectively termed non-paroxysmal AF. Prior to ablation 1–5 days, all patients fasted for over eight hours, and peripheral blood samples were taken for laboratory analyses. These analyses provided data on white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, neutrophil (NEU) count, monocyte (MONO) count, free triiodothyronine (FT3) level, free thyroxine (FT4) level, uric acid (UA) level, serum creatinine (Scr) level, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) level, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level, total cholesterol (TC) level, triglycerides (TG) level, LDL-C level, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) level, and echocardiographic measures including left atrial diameter(LAD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). Relevant scoring data were also documented according to the CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores.

LDL-C level determination and classification

Baseline LDL-C level prior to the procedure were used for analysis. Fasting plasma LDL-C samples were collected the morning after admission and were measured using a homogeneous direct method. The LDL-C level was classified into quartiles as follows: Q1 < 1.87 mmol/L; Q2: 1.87–2.39 mmol/L; Q3: 2.39–2.88 mmol/L; and Q4 > 2.88 mmol/L.

Ablation procedure and post-procedural management

RFCA was performed using a three-dimensional mapping system (CARTO or Ensite system). Experienced clinicians performed pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), with additional ablations procedures left to the discretion of the operators, including linear isolations—roof line, posterior wall line, mitral valve isthmus, tricuspid valve isthmus, or substrate modification. If atrial tachyarrhythmia persisted on ablation completion, electrical conversion was performed to restore sinus rhythm. After ablation, patients without contraindications were routinely prescribed antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs) and oral anticoagulants for three months. Holter monitoring was performed at least once within the first three months after the procedure. The primary endpoint was AF recurrence, defined as an electrocardiographically documented episode of AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia lasting at least 30 s after a three-month blanking period following ablation. Follow-up assessments were conducted at 3, 6, and 12 months using 12-lead electrocardiograms or 24-h Holter monitoring. Additionally, whenever patients experienced symptoms related to AF, further electrocardiographic or 24-h Holter recordings were obtained to evaluate recurrence. Telephone interviews were conducted every six months to report any discomfort after one year.

Statistical analysis

Data with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared via one-way ANOVA. Data with skewed distribution were expressed as the median and inter-quartile range and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and compared with the chi-square test. Patients were divided into quartiles based on LDL-C level, we used either a chi-square test (categorical variables) or linear regression model (continuous variables) to calculate differences among different groups. To investigate the association between LDL-C and AF recurrence, our statistical analyses consisted of 3 main steps. Step 1: adjusted only univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis were used. Using the lowest quartile (Q1) as a reference group, the association between LDL-C level quartiles and AF recurrence risk was examined using the Cox proportional hazards model. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were then calculated. The Cox regression estimates were calculated using three models: Crude Model adjust for none. Model I adjusted for age and sex, Model II considered additional factors including AF type, duration of AF, BMI, current smoking, current drinking, HTN, HF, CAD, prior stroke/TIA, DM, CHA2DS2-VASc score, HAS-BLED score, TG level, HDL-C level, FPG level, pre-ablation medications(ACEI/ARB, beta-blockers, CCB and LLD), NEU count, MONO count, UA level, LAD, LVEDD, LVESD, and LVEF. To ensure the robustness of our data analysis, we conducted sensitivity analysis. We performed testing for linear trends by entering the median value of each category of LDL-C as a continuous variable in the models. Step 2: Generalized additive model (GAM) and Cox proportional hazards regression with cubic spline functions model and smooth curve fitting (penalized spline method) were conducted to address the nonlinearity of LDL-C and AF recurrence. If nonlinearity was detected, we first calculated the inflection point using a recursive algorithm, and then constructed a two-piecewise Cox proportional hazards model on both sides of the inflection point. The threshold level was determined by choosing the inflection point, which provided the maximum model likelihood, using the trial and error method, along with a log likelihood ratio test comparing the one-line Cox proportional hazards model with a two-piecewise Cox proportional hazards model to examine the statistical significance. The P-value for the log-likelihood ratio test was < 0.05, suggesting that the two-piecewise Cox model was a better fit for this association. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the threshold effects of LDL-C on risk of AF recurrence. Step 3: Several subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to explore potential relationships and interactions between LDL-C level and AF recurrence risk factors such as age (≤ 60 or > 60 years), sex, AF type, BMI (≤ 24 kg/m2 or > 24 kg/m2), use of LLD, hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, and heart failure.Analyses were conducted using the statistical software package R (version 4.3.1) or Empowerstats (version 4.2). P-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 561 patients with AF who underwent successful RFCA were screened for eligibility, and 482 patients with AF were included in the final analysis. Table 1 displays the baseline characteristics of selected patients across LDL-C quartiles. These patients with a mean age of 62.84 ± 10.07 years(the youngest of them was 21 years old and the oldest was 80 years old), of whom 225 (46.68%) were female and 257(53.32%) were male. From the lowest to the highest LDL-C quartile, PLT, TC, TG, and FPG levels gradually increased, while age, LAD, HF, and prior stroke/TIA levels gradually decreased. Detection of nonlinear relationships

To account for the nonlinearity in the relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence, we applied a Cox proportional hazards regression model using a GAM with smooth curve fitting via the penalized spline method (Fig. 2). An L-shaped relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence was identified, with a statistically significant threshold at an inflection point of 3.20 mmol/L. Consequently, as shown in in Table 4, we further explored the nonlinear relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence by applying a two-piecewise linear regression model in the multivariate analysis (P-value for the log-likelihood ratio = 0.031). When LDL-C was < 3.20 mmol/L, higher LDL-C level was significantly associated with a reduced risk of AF recurrence (HR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.33–0.74). However, when LDL-C exceeded 3.20 mmol/L, increased LDL-C was associated with a higher risk of AF recurrence, though this result did not reach statistical significance (HR, 2.11; 95% CI, 0.76–5.89).

Subgroup analysis for the association between LDL-C level and AF recurrence

Further subgroup analyses indicated that the relationship between LDL-C level and AF recurrence varied across different groups (Fig. 3). A significant negative correlation between LDL-C levels and AF recurrence was observed in subgroups stratified by age, BMI, use of LLD, and history of hypertension (P-value < 0.05). However, interaction tests revealed no statistically significant differences in the LDL-C and AF recurrence relationship across these strata. The results demonstrated that sex, age, BMI, AF type, use of lipid-lowering drugs, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and heart failure did not significantly modify this negative association (P-value for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

Our cohort study explored the association between plasma LDL-C level and the risk of atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence in patients who underwent catheter ablation. Notably, this study is the first to utilize curve fitting and segmented linear models to identify an L-shaped relationship between preoperative LDL-C level and AF recurrence risk. We observed that at lower LDL-C level, the risk of AF recurrence increased significantly, with a critical inflection point at 3.20 mmol/L, elevated risks of AF recurrence were observed in both lower and higher LDL-C categories compared to the 3.20 mmol/L level. However, the association for LDL-C level above 3.20 mmol/L did not achieve statistical significance, which could be limited by the relatively small sample size of cases.

A higher level of LDL-C is clearly associated with an increased risk of developing atherosclerotic heart disease14,15. Trimarco et al.16 used a clinic data of 725 patients showed that a 7-year stable control of LDL-C reduces the incidence of cardiovascular events by 40% in nondiabetic subjects. However, our study identifies an L-shaped relationship between preprocedural LDL-C and AF recurrence, indicating that some AF recurrence causes differ from atherosclerotic heart disease mechanisms. In 1997, a study conducted by Psaty et al.17 examined a group of 4,844 elderly individuals, they discovered that lower cholesterol level was associated with an increased risk of AF, providing initial support for the “cholesterol paradox” in AF. Recent research by Yao et al.18has also shown that a correlation between lower baseline LDL-C level and a higher risk of AF, another data included 392,783 participants suggested that low remnant cholesterol level was associated with an increased risk of incident AF19, which suggest that low cholesterol level may increase AF susceptibility. In addition, Qi20have found through research that LDL-C was an independent predictor of ischemic stroke, but another study showed that the noninclusion of dyslipidemia in ischemic stroke risk stratification of patients with AF21. These contradictory findings suggest that LDL-C is of great significance in the management of atrial fibrillation and in the development of prevention strategies. However, there was limited evidence on the relationship between cholesterol level and AF recurrence so far. Two observational studies found a negative correlation between LDL-C level and AF recurrence in female patients, while this correlation was not statistically significant in male patients11,12. However, three larger-scale population studies have shown that no sex-based differences were observed in the patients of AF recurrence after catheter ablation22–24. We speculate the discrepancies in these results may be attributed to differences in cohort study characteristics, sample sizes, and statistical methods. Although in our study there appeared to be a stronger statistical effect in women, we conducted interaction test study based on subgroup analysis with a larger sample size to directly compare sex influence in AF recurrence, and found sex have no statistical difference in this negative relationship, which further confirmed the theory that the risk of AF recurrence was similar in sexes. Importantly, previous studies explored only linear relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence and did not address potential nonlinearities. Our study is the first to explore the nonlinear relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence using a smooth curve fitting, and we found an L-shaped association with statistically significant inflection point even after adjusting for important confounders, addressing a significant evidence gap in patients undergoing catheter ablation. The outcomes may provide valuable preoperative advice for clinically managing AF recurrence risk.

Pleiotropic effects is a strength of lipid-lowering drugs (LLD) such as statins25.In addition to lowering cholesterol, these drugs improve the electromechanical function of atrial and myocardial tissues and exerts anti-inflammatory effects, suggesting potential benefits in preventing and treating AF26, a meta-analysis revealed that in AF patients, statin therapy was associated with a reduction in all-cause and cardiovascular mortality are reduced by 41% and 25%, respectively27. However, a double-blind randomized controlled trial by Suleiman et al.28 found no significant relationship between statins use and AF recurrence. Similarly, a meta-analysis of large-scale randomized controlled trials conducted by Rahimi et al.29 did not find a statistically significant correlation between LLD and the risk of AF recurrence. Our study supports these findings, as our sensitivity analysis reaffirmed that LDL-C level is a critical factor in AF recurrence, thus reinforcing the stability and reliability of our results.

Nevertheless, the exact mechanism linking LDL-C to AF recurrence remain unclear. Several possible explanations could account for the association between low LDL-C and increased risk of AF recurrence. Previous study has indicated that cholesterol plays a crucial role in maintaining the anti-inflammatory properties and structural integrity of cardiac membranes30. Cholesterol interacts with sphingomyelin on the cell membrane, regulating membrane potential31, and a balanced lipid composition in the cell membrane prevents arrhythmogenic potentials32. Research by Goonasekara33suggests cholesterol imbalances can alter cell membrane properties through interactions with membrane proteins, changes in membrane fluidity, or associations with lipid rafts, thereby increasing arrhythmia risks such as AF. Furthermore, lipoproteins exhibit anti-inflammatory effects34. Kim et al.35noted that in AF patients, compromised lipoprotein characteristics lead to increased inflammation and oxidative stress, risk factors for AF recurrence. LDL-C and HDL-C are two vital lipoproteins in plasma. Notably, a cohort study included 11,987 patients with hypertension, which demonstrate that HDL-C increases cardiovascular risk in people especially in male patients36. However, a comprehensive study of 88,785 patients found an inverse relationship between LDL-C level and incident AF over a seven-year period. However, no substantial association was found between AF and HDL-C37. These studies indicated that the complex interplay between HDL-C and AF risk is yet to be fully elucidated. The LDL-C/HDL-C ratio is increasingly recognized as a predictor of ischemic heart disease offering insights beyond LDL-C levels alone, because it influenced the occurrence and development of AF38, Guan39elaborated independent inverse correlation between the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio and the risk of AF in CKD patients, and advised to refrain from excessively aggressive reduction of LDL level in patients, because it could elevate the risk of AF. Thus, the imbalance level of LDL-C may increase the risk of AF recurrence risk by influencing membrane potential and promoting inflammation30,31,35. Additionally, previous studies has demonstrated a close relationship between metabolic syndrome and AF recurrence40. Tadic41 noted that the combination of multiple components of metabolic syndrome with excessively high cholesterol levels may induce left ventricular hypertrophy and atrial conduction abnormalities. This could explain why in this group of subjects, AF recurrence did not decrease with continuously increasing LDL-C level. However, further research is required to verify these mechanisms.

There are some limitations in our study. First, the main limitation of this study was its observational nature. Although we try our best to provide patients with the convenience of medical examinations, the follow-up bias could not be avoided. Second, we adopted the baseline plasma LDL-C for analysis, we did not further analyze the relationship between the long-term fluctuation of LDL-C and AF recurrence, which may limit the ability to assess the therapeutic necessity of LLD for AF patients. Third, regular ECG monitoring was performed every 3 or 6 months after the blank period, and AF-related symptoms prompted the initiation of unscheduled surveillance. Though we encouraged patients to contact the physician in the case of suspected recurrence symptoms, it is likelihood that paroxysmal or asymptomatic AF recurrences may go undetected. Finally, our study confinement to a single center. To address these limitations, highlighting the need for future multicenter studies with larger cohorts to explore the association between LDL-C and AF recurrence.

Conclusion

In summary, our study uncovered an L-shaped relationship between LDL-C and AF recurrence, for patients who underwent catheter ablation, maintaining preoperative plasma LDL-C level at 3.20 mmol/L may be considered beneficial in reducing the risk of AF recurrence. In our clinical work, LDL-C values above and below the threshold may warrant particularly vigilance. For patients with low LDL-C level, target level can be achieved through adjust dietary and avoid using LLD. Moreover, for patients above the optimal LDL-C level, appropriately using LLD may be helpful. Looking forward, further randomized controlled trials could be conducted to elucidate the actual effect of LDL-C on AF recurrence in patients receiving catheter ablation as well as the potential biological mechanisms.
