Comparison of effects of different vermicompost types on growth, nodule formation, and yield response of soybean
Authors: Melkamu Dugassa, Walelign Worku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While there is a general understanding of the benefits of vermicompost, more specific research is needed to compare the effects of various feedstock-derived vermicompost on soybean growth, nodule formation, and yield response. An experiment was conducted in a glasshouse at the College of Agriculture, Hawassa University in 2023 to evaluate the effect of different types of vermicompost on the growth, nodule formation, yield related parameters and yield of soybean. Twelve vermicompost types, along with a control, were arranged in a completely randomized block design with six replications. Three replications were used for destructive sampling, while the remaining replications were used for yield and related parameters. The soybean variety Nyala, released by the Hawassa Agricultural Research Center in 2014, was used for the study. Results indicated that regardless of the type of vermicompost used, its application significantly improved the growth and yield and related parameters of soybean compared to plants without vermicompost. Vermicompost from different sources increased the leaf area per plant by 7 to 62% compared to those without vermicompost. Among the vermicompost types used, the one prepared from common bean straw + coffee husk resulted in the highest aboveground biomass and grain yield, surpassing plants without vermicompost by 28% and 60%, respectively. Overall, the various vermicompost types used increased aboveground biomass and grain yield per plant in a range of 5% to 30% and 18% to 60%, respectively, compared to the control. On the other hand, root nodule number per plant, effective nodule per plant, fresh, and dry weight were significantly reduced under vermicompost application compared to the control. Based on the grain yield results, it is advisable to use vermicompost made from a mixture of common bean straw and coffee husk for optimal soybean growth and yield. Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max L.) is a vital crop in global agriculture, renowned for its high nutritional value and potential to enhance food security, particularly in challenging climatic conditions1,2. The crop is highly valued worldwide for its versatility as a cost-effective source of protein, healthy unsaturated fats, and carbohydrates for human consumption, livestock feed, and aquaculture feed3. Soybean is primarily cultivated for its high-quality proteins and oil4. It serves as a viable alternative to animal protein due to its essential amino acids essential for human nutrition5. Soybeans play a critical role in ensuring food and nutritional security for nations and offer a sustainable source of nutrition for a growing population1. Despite the nutritional quality and potential of the crop in food supply, food insecurity persists in developing countries due to population growth. Protein-energy malnutrition is a significant concern affecting over 170 million preschool children and lactating women in Asian and African countries6. Giving emphasis for the production of legume crops including soybean can help address food insecurity and protein-energy malnutrition in developing nations. The decline in soil fertility poses a major challenge for farmers, particularly those with limited incomes facing escalating costs of commercial fertilizers. Immediate interventions are necessary to enhance crop productivity.

Research on soybean management, including effective fertilization methods, is essential for optimizing production practices and enhancing yields7. The use of vermicompost is highly beneficial for improving the soil structure and nutrient availability8. The importance of vermicompost in improving soil structure and crop yield has been indicted in several studies9–11. It can improve soil structure, water retention, and nutrient availability, leading to healthier plants and increased yields. By studying the effects of vermicompost on soybean growth, farmers can make informed decisions on its use in their agricultural practices, potentially reducing their reliance on synthetic fertilizers and promoting sustainable farming methods8. It can be a sustainable alternative to chemical fertilizers, offering slow-release nutrients that plants can easily absorb12. Previous studies have assessed the quality of vermicompost made from different organic materials13–16. The choice of using vermicompost for crop growth shows promise, but its effectiveness can vary depending on the materials used. Some studies indicated that the different sources of vermicompost could have varying effects on plant growth and other parameters13,17,18. However, there is a lack of information on how vermicompost made from different organic materials affects soybean growth, nodule formation, and yield. To address this gap, this study aimed to determine the most effective type of vermicompost for enhancing soybean growth and yield. The research evaluated the impact of various types of vermicompost on soybean growth, nodule formation, and yield to identify the most beneficial type.

Materials and methods

Description of experimental setup

The pot experiment was conducted in a glasshouse at the College of Agriculture, Hawassa University, Ethiopia in 2023. The area is situated at a latitude of 7.05°, a longitude of 38.47°, and an altitude of 1688 m above sea level. The glasshouse has a transparent roof to allow sufficient light penetration for the experimental plants. The temperature ranged from 12 to 31 °C throughout the year, with occasional drops to 9 °C and peaks at 34 °C. The study site is close to Lake Hawassa, which can affect the relative humidity, typically ranging from 59 to 74%.

Experimental materials, treatments, design, and procedure

Experimental materials and treatments

The materials used for the experiment included the soybean variety Nyala, released in 2014 by the Hawassa Research Center, and vermicompost prepared from various organic materials. The vermicompost materials were prepared from common bean straw, maize stalk, coffee husk, alone or in combination of 50%, 33.33%, and 25% on a dry weight basis when two materials, three materials, and four materials were mixed respectively and were mixed with cow dung in a 2:1 ratio. The common bean straw used for the vermicompost was purchased from local farmers who grew the Hawassa Dume variety. The coffee husks were collected from Yirgalem Industry Park, and the maize stalks were purchased from nearby farmers who grew the BH 540 hybrid variety. The different materials combinations varied in their important physicochemical properties and nutrient composition as shown in Table 3. The twelve vermicompost types were prepared from different organic materials and cow dung using Eisenia fetida earthworms in the aforementioned proportion. There were thirteen treatments, including a control, and each were replicated six times and arranged in completely randomized block design. Three replications were used to gather data for the yield and related parameters, while the remaining three replications were used for collecting data on destructive samples. The three plants in each pot were used to collect data on both destructive and non-destructive samples. By taking the average of the plants from each of the three replications, the data for the treatment were recorded. Soybean development progresses through distinct vegetative and reproductive stages, each with specific growth characteristics and management requirements19. The growth stages of soybeans are divided into vegetative stages (V1-Vn) based on the number of fully developed trifoliate leaves, and reproductive stages (R1-R8) marked by flowering, pod development, seed development, and maturity20. The crop management, including weeding and watering, was carried out for the treatments uniformly following the crop requirements throughout the crop growth period. Experimental design and procedure

A randomized block design with six replications was used for the experiment. The soil for the potting media was obtained from the Agronomy field at the College of Agriculture, Hawassa University. The soil was air-dried and sieved through a 4 mm sieve to remove stones and debris. Pots were filled with soil, leaving 5 cm of space at the top for water application. Six seeds were planted in each pot, and seedlings were thinned to three plants per pot at the first trifoliate leaf stage. The pots used had a capacity of eight kg, with dimensions of 30 cm in height and 25 cm in upper diameter. Vermicompost was added as a fertilizer at a rate of 40 g per pot, mixed thoroughly with the soil based on the recommended application rate of 10 tons per hectare to grow soybean21. The mixed media was kept for two weeks before planting to allow for the stabilization of vermicompost for better germination. Watering was done manually as needed. When the crops reached maturity, which was indicated by leaf shedding and pod color changing from green to yellow and then to brown, harvesting was done manually. Plants in each pot were arranged separately to record the desired crop data.

Soil and vermicompost sampling and analysis

The soil samples used in the experiment were collected from the Agronomy field of the College of Agriculture, Hawassa University, from the top 0–20 cm layer. The samples were air-dried, crushed, and sieved through a 2 mm sieve to remove unwanted materials and prepare them for the analysis of selected physicochemical properties. Soil particle size distribution was analyzed using the hydrometer method as outlined by22. The soil’s textural class names were determined using the textural triangle of the USDA system, as defined by23. The bulk density (BD) of the core-sampled soil was calculated by dividing the mass of the oven-dry soil (g) by its volume (cm−3), as described in24. A constant value of 2.65 g/cm3 was used for the particle density. The pH and electrical conductivity measurements for the soil and vermicompost were performed in a 1:2.5 soil water extract according to the method described by26. The potassium dichromate wet oxidation method was used to determine the soil and vermicompost organic carbon content27. For total Kjeldahl nitrogen, the soil and vermicompost samples were digested and measured in 0.1 g samples using the micro-Kjeldahl titrimetric method28. Available phosphorus in the soil and vermicompost was determined colorimetrically using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer by extracting the sample with 0.5 M sodium bicarbonate adjusted to pH 8.5 following the Olsen extraction method29. The cation exchange capacity for the soil and vermicompost was determined using the ammonium acetate method as described in30.

Soil and vermicompost physicochemical properties

Soil properties

The recorded pH of the experimental soil was neutral31. The soil organic carbon and total nitrogen contents were low, as described by32 and33, respectively (Table 2). The soil CEC was intermediate while the soil AvP was in the low range as indicated in34. The experimental soil’s particle size distribution was 23% clay, 43% silt, and 34% sand. According to the USDA soil texture classification system, the soil’s textural class was loam, as explained by23. The soil’s particle density and total porosity were 2.36 g/cm3 and 43%, respectively, while its bulk density was 1.30 g/cm3. The soil’s bulk density was low and within an acceptable range, according to35. The experimental soil’s porosity was within an acceptable range, as shown by36. The experimental soil had low levels of total nitrogen, which is a key nutrient that primarily limits crops yield (EthioSIS, 2014). Data collection and analysis

Growth and yield data

Crop growth and yield data were collected at appropriate times and conditions for the parameters. Leaf area per plant was measured using a portable area meter LI-3000A (LI-COR, USA) at mid-flowering. Plant height was measured at maturity using a meter with the zero mark placed at the top of the soil level in the pot to the highest point of the plant, expressed in centimeters43. The number of branches per plant was determined by counting the branches produced on each plant. The number of pods per plant was calculated for three plants per pot, and the average number of pods per plant was recorded. The number of seeds per pod was determined by randomly selecting five pods from the bulk of pods harvested from each pot, and the average was recorded. Aboveground biomass per plant was determined after sun-drying the harvested biomass for 7 days. The grain moisture content was adjusted to 11% to ensure fair comparison between samples and accurately record yield data using the formula as indicated in44.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Adjusted}}\;{\mathrm{yield}} = \frac{{{\mathrm{Harvested}}\;{\mathrm{yield}}\;(100 - {\mathrm{harvest}}\;{\mathrm{moisture}})}}{{100 - {\mathrm{desired}}\;{\mathrm{moisture}}}}$$\end{document}

Nodule data

Nodules from plants in pots were harvested and prepared for analysis. Carefully dig around the plant, lift it gently, and extract as much of the root system as possible. The soybean roots were washed with tap water, and the nodules were separated, counted, and quantified per plant. The number of effective nodules per plant was determined by dissecting the nodules and counting separately them based on their color45. Effective nodules are usually pink or red inside, containing leghemoglobin for nitrogen fixation while white or green nodules indicate ineffective nitrogen fixation46. Nodules, including dissected ones, were weighed to determine their fresh weight. Subsequently, the nodules were oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h to determine their dry weight.

Data analysis

The data collected in this experiment were analyzed using ANOVA in SAS version 9.4. The normality of the data was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Treatment means were compared using the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) test (p < 0.05).

Leaf area per plant showed a strong positive correlation with plant height, aboveground biomass, and grain yield (r = 0.76), (r = 0.87 and 0.91, respectively). Plant height showed a strong positive correlation with grain yield (r = 0.81). The number of pods per plant, number of seeds per pod, and aboveground biomass per plant exhibited strong positive correlations with grain yield (r = 0.82, 0.83, and 0.89, respectively). This could be due to the strong contributions of these yield-related traits to the grain yield. The number of nodules per plant had negative correlation to available phosphorus and total nitrogen of the vermicompost (Table 7). Discussion

Effects on growth parameters

The application of vermicompost, regardless of type, significantly enhanced the growth parameters of soybean in this study. This improvement is attributed to the beneficial effects of vermicompost on plant growth, as it provides essential nutrients, beneficial microbes, and plant growth hormones, resulting in overall enhancements in plant growth47. The leaf area per plant was significantly influenced by the type and application of vermicompost. This increase in leaf area with vermicompost application may be due to the nutrients, hormones, enzymes, humic substances, and organic matter it provides to the soil48. Moreover, the significant improvement in growth parameters of soybean with vermicompost application could be related to its nutrient-supplying power and improving soil structure49. The dominance of CBS + CH VC can be attributed to its superior nutrient content, which enhances soil structure and fosters optimal conditions for plant growth. Previous studies have reported that vermicompost application increases the leaf area of cowpea (Vigna unguiculata)50.

The application of different types of vermicompost had varying effects on plant height. Vermicompost application generally increases plant height, along with other parameters such as leaf size and stem girth, due to its ability to improve soil fertility and nutrient availability51. The variations in treatments may be due to differences in plant growth hormones and nutrient levels. An increase in shoot height due to vermicompost application has been reported in a previous study52. In a similar pattern to the other growth parameters mentioned, the type of vermicompost significantly affected the number of branches per plant, which could be attributed to the nutrient supply power of the vermicompost. Consistent with the present results, differences in the number of branches per plant in lentils (Lens culinaris) due to vermicompost types is reported53.

The study showed that vermicompost application and type significantly affected the number of nodules per plant, effective nodules, and nodule fresh and dry weight, with noticeable differences among the vermicompost types. The decrease in nodule parameters may be linked to the increased availability of nitrogen, which could inhibit nodulation54. The varying effects of different types of vermicompost on nodule activity may be attributed to differences in their nitrogen content. The fresh and dry weight of nodules per plant significantly decreased with vermicompost application, possibly due to a reduction in the number of nodules per plant. Some other studies also reported a decrease in nodule number and effectiveness in different legumes with vermicompost application55,56. In line with the findings of the current study, a previous study reported a higher number of nodules per plant in the control group compared to vermicompost-treated plants in vegetable soybean57. Moreover, a prior study found that applying vermicompost to French beans (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) significantly reduced the fresh and dry weight of nodules by 44.9% and 44.5%, respectively58.

Effects on yield and related parameters

Vermicompost application significantly improved plant biomass, with considerable differences among the types of vermicompost used. The improved plant biomass may be due to the enhanced soil structure, increased water retention capacity, and stimulated beneficial microbial activity in the soil resulting from the application of vermicompost8,48. These combined effects contribute to overall plant growth and improved biomass production. The differences in biomass yield treated with different types of vermicompost are attributed to their varying contents of important plant nutrients. In line with the present findings, previous studies have reported differences in biomass yield due to the use of vermicompost from different sources in faba bean (Vicia faba L.) and fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum)59,60. Application of enset leaves vermicompost resulted in lower yield and related parameters compared to the other vermicompost types, which may be attributed to the lower nutrient contents of this vermicompost type.

Differences were observed in the seed weight of soybeans based on the application of different types of vermicompost. The variations may be attributed to the nutrients provided by the vermicompost. Previous studies have shown that soybean seed weight was maximized with vermicompost application compared to the control group61. In other studies, an increase in the seed weights of was observed in rapeseed (Brassica napus L.)62, due to vermicompost application. The application of different types of vermicompost has shown a significant impact on the grain yield of soybeans. This could be due to variations in the nutrient provision capacity of the vermicompost for promoting proper plant growth and, ultimately, yield. Vermicompost application has been reported to increase the yield of legume crops, such as chickpeas (Cicer arietinum L.)63 and faba bean (Vicia faba L.)59. Consistent with the findings of this study, previous research has also shown variations in lentil grain yield as a result of using vermicompost from different sources, possibly attributed to their varying nutrient compositions53.

In this study, the application of vermicompost from different sources reduced nodulation while increasing grain yield. This contradicts with what has been observed in some literature64. The reduced nodule formation may be due to the inhibitory effects of vermicompost on nodulation, while the increased grain yield could be attributed to the sufficient nutrients supplied to the plants by vermicompost65. When there is not enough nitrogen available, plants may develop nodules to meet their nitrogen requirements. However, when there is an adequate supply of nitrogen, plants may not actively fix atmospheric nitrogen and form nodules on their roots66.

The positive correlation between phosphorus and total nitrogen in vermicompost is due to the nutrient-rich materials used in the vermicomposting process. Vermicomposting, which involves earthworms decomposing organic matter, enhances the availability of nutrients like phosphorus and nitrogen for plants67. Vermicompost-available phosphorus showed a strong positive correlation with all yield and related parameters, which could be attributed to the sufficient nutrients supplied to the crop through vermicompost. A strong correlation was observed between leaf area and grain yield of the plant. This strong correlation is likely because a larger leaf area captures more sunlight for photosynthesis, resulting in increased carbohydrate production that can be converted into grain during the filling stage. In line with the present results, a strong correlation of leaf area to grain yield in soybean (G. max L.) is previously reported46. A strong positive correlation was observed between plant height and grain yield, which could be attributed to the increased space/length for bearing pods by the plant. There was also a strong positive correlation between the number of pods per plant and grain yield, which might be attributed to the higher contribution of pods to the yield, as yield is a product of many interrelated components, including the number of pods. In agreement with the present findings, a strong positive correlation between the number of pods per plant and grain yield in pea (Pisum sativum L.) is reported68. The number of pods per plant and 100-seed weight also showed a positive correlation with grain yield, likely due to their significant contribution to the yield. Consistent with the present results, another study reported a strong positive correlation between seed yield per plant, the number of pods per plant, and 100-seed weight in soybean (G. max L.) is reported69.

Phosphorus showed negative correlation to the number of nodules in this study. When phosphorus levels are high, it signals the plant to reduce the reliance on nitrogen fixation through nodules. Phosphorus plays a key role in ATP production, necessary for nitrogen fixation, and its abundance can downregulate genes involved in nodule development and nitrogen fixation70.The negative correlation of total nitrogen to the number of nodules could be attributed to the inhibitory effects of the nitrate in the nitrogen66,71. The negative correlation between yield and number of nodules is probably be due to increased yield and reduced nodule formation under vermicompost application55. The correlation analysis revealed that vermicompost application has a strong positive correlation with the growth and yield of soybeans. However, it negatively affects nodule formation, possibly because the nutrients in vermicompost support normal growth and yield without the need for additional investment in nitrogen fixation through nodules.

Conclusions

Twelve vermicompost types prepared from different organic materials along with control group were used to evaluate their effects on growth, nodule formation and yield of soybean under glasshouse conditions using a pot experiment. The crop management activities were carried out regularly and uniformly for all the treatments. The results of the study indicated that vermicompost application significantly improved the growth and yield performance of soybean, regardless of the vermicompost type. On the other hand, the application of vermicompost reduced nodule formation, possibly due to the availability of sufficient nutrients in the vermicompost. However, the growth and yield responses of the crop differed with the vermicompost type. Generally, the application of vermicompost prepared from common bean straw + coffee husk resulted in significantly higher growth and yield performance of the study crop. Therefore, vermicompost prepared from common bean straw + coffee husk could be the best choice to enhance soybean crop productivity. However, further verification of these vermicompost types in different field conditions and locations is recommended, as this study was limited to glasshouse conditions.
