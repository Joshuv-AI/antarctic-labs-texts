Overlooked deforestation from global mining activities in the 21st century
Authors: Xiaoxin Zhang, Bin Chen, Jiafu An, Chen Lin, Peng Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMining is a major driver of deforestation, yet the full scale of its contribution to global natural forest loss and associated carbon emissions remains obscured by the incomplete inventories of mining activities. Here, we present a comprehensive global mining inventory, including 236,028 mining areas with an overall accuracy of 87.37% to quantify direct deforestation and the associated forest carbon emissions from mining activities between 2001 and 2023. Our results reveal that mining-induced deforestation is two to three times higher than previous estimates from existing datasets, accounting for 19,765 km2 of deforestation with associated emissions of 0.75 Pg CO2 over this period. Notably, over half (50.29%) of this deforestation is linked to unrecorded mining activities. These findings highlight the substantially underestimated role of mining, particularly informal and unrecorded activities, in driving global deforestation and climate change, underscoring the urgent need for improved monitoring and regulation of mining impacts on the environment across regions.Subject terms: Sustainability, Environmental impact, Forestry

Introduction

Numerous industries globally rely on mining products1. The mining sector contributes 2-4% to global Gross Domestic Product (GDP), supports millions of people directly through mining-related activities, and accounts for approximately 4–7% of global greenhouse gas emissions1. Driven by rising demand for minerals used in consumer products, industrial infrastructure, and energy transition technologies, global mining extraction has expanded at an unprecedented rate in the 21st century1,2. This surge in mineral demand has led to various environmental impacts, including disturbances of forest ecosystems and protected areas3,4, biodiversity loss5–7, soil pollution8,9, and water scarcity8,10.

In the pursuit of economic incentives through mining operations, a considerable amount of mining activities have been reported in several African and low-income countries, along with the associated environmental issues11. Mining is now regarded as one of the major human-driven contributors to deforestation, second only to agriculture, infrastructure, and urban expansion12. The deforestation induced by mining activities not only disrupts ecosystem functioning but also reduces carbon sequestration capacities, posing considerable risks to global climate stability13,14. A pantropical assessment reported 3264 km2 of forest was directly lost due to industrial mining from 2000 to 20192. Plenty of case studies have reported the substantial impacts of mining activities in tropical rainforest, as seen in countries such as Brazil3, Colombia15, Congo16, and Indonesia17. In light of the negative impacts of mining activities on forest ecosystems (e.g., deforestation and degradation), local, regional, and global initiatives have invested considerable effort to address these issues7. However, challenges persist due to insufficient regulatory frameworks and weak enforcement capabilities for monitoring and mitigating these impacts10,18. The central issue of this problem is the lack of a relatively comprehensive global inventory of mining activities, which hampers effective monitoring and enforcement19. Currently, the extent and impact of mining activity are often estimated regionally or inferred through extrapolations. There is an urgent need for a comprehensive global inventory of mining activities to enable a complete and holistic assessment of their impacts on deforestation and associated environmental costs.

Mining activities often involve a mixed land use of bare ground, water, and various stages of vegetation regrowth20, complicating efforts to investigate and quantify their spatial distribution and extent19–21. Traditionally, inventories of mining activities have relied on records from mining companies, materials databases, or data collected by institutions such as mindat.org or the Standard & Poor’s (S&P) SNL metal and mining database22,23. Recently, several studies have attempted to locate and delineate mining areas independently through multi-scale satellite observations21,24,25. However, these studies primarily focus on reported regular mining activities and the areas surrounding those recorded industrial mining sites19,21,25, limiting their ability to offer an inventory of global mining activities—particularly for remotely located and small-scale operations19. One recent commentary highlights that nearly half of the world’s mining activities go unreported compared to recorded mining sites, but its full impact is not well quantified19. Unrecorded mining activities are often informal, less regulated, and frequently employ environmentally harmful extraction methods with minimal rehabilitation measures in place11,19,26. Moreover, these mining activities, typically conducted at the surface level, pose considerable risks to natural forests and ecosystems27,28. Therefore, a comprehensive global inventory of mining activities and their associated deforestation impacts is highly needed to enhance understanding of the socio-economic and environmental consequences.

To address the challenges outlined above, we present an up-to-date, comprehensive analysis of global mining activities and their contributions to deforestation from 2001 to 2023. Firstly, we compile a global inventory of mining activity by integrating multiple mining-related datasets, including satellite-based mining footprints, crowdsourcing mining polygons, and SNL commercial mining sites. We then distinguish between recorded and unrecorded mining activities worldwide and assess associated deforestation and carbon emission within mining areas by leveraging high-resolution forest cover change monitoring. Specifically, we aim to answer the three scientific questions: (1) What is geographic distribution of different mining activities globally? (2) To what extent do mining activities contribute to deforestation and associated carbon emissions? (3) Which types of mining activities have been the primary drivers of deforestation over the 21st century?

Results

A global inventory of mining activities

We combined four global available mining area datasets to enable a comprehensive inventory of mining activities, incorporating global mining footprints21,25, crowdsourcing mining polygons29, manual delineated artisanal mining areas8, and SNL commercial mining sites22 (Supplementary Tables S1,2). Our dataset includes 236,028 mining areas (each over 1000 m2) worldwide, extending over 145,900 km2 (Fig. 1, Table 1). The overall accuracy of these global mining areas through our independent validation is 87.37% (see Methods, Supplementary Tables S3,4). Our mining data primarily relies on published footprints of mining activities obtained through crowdsourcing and manual visual inspection methods8,20,21,29, including surface mining, placer mining, and land footprints directly related to underground mining operations (Supplementary Fig. S1). The map shows widely distributed mining activities across 209 countries (Fig. 1a,b). We defined mines not recorded in the SNL commercial mining database as unrecorded mines19, resulting in a total of 230,068 polygons, accounting for 93,596 km2 (Table 1). These unrecorded mines, which are often overlooked in the previous estimation, account for 64.15% of global mining areas (Table 1). Asia has the most mining activities, with 76,973 mining polygons spanning 57,043 km2, of which approximately 66.81% are classified as unrecorded. Europe shows high rates of unrecorded mining activities at 81.57% (Table 1), primarily contributed by small mines and quarries that are often unreported in the SNL mining database (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Table S5).

Our results show that a total of 7441 global protected areas have been or are currently affected by mining activities, based on the intersection with World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA)30 (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. S3). Specifically, according to International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) management categories, 135 protected areas with IUCN management class of Ia (strict nature reserves) and 68 protected areas with IUCN management class of Ib (wilderness areas) have been observed with mining activities (Methods), which was strictly prohibited mining activities and infrastructural access. Additionally, 497 protected areas with IUCN management class of II (national parks) and 348 protected areas with IUCN management class of III (natural monuments) are features overlap with mining areas; these are generally also forbidden with mining extractive activities. Furthermore, 1749 protected habitat or species management areas with IUCN management class of IV overlap with mining areas, and 1795 protected nature landscape and seascape areas with IUCN management class of V have observed mining activities. Noted, 2491 other protected areas have also observed mining activities; however, these areas are not reported or assigned management categories on WDPA31, particularly in low-income countries where environmental regulations for sustainable mining are probably weakest. This highlights the potential extensive impacts of mining operations on protected areas worldwide.

Deforestation from mining activities in the 21st century

We overlaid the global high-resolution forest loss and loss year layers12 with mining areas during 2001–2023 and compared deforestation rates between recorded and unrecorded mines. A total of 19,765 km2 of forested areas (the forest measured in this study is consistent with the definition from the original dataset that woody vegetation with >5 meters at 30-meter resolution12) were directly deforested by global mining activities during this period (Table 1, Supplementary Fig. S5), which is approximately two to three times higher than the previously reported statistics (Supplementary Table S6). The observed deforestation has resulted in a gross carbon emission of 0.75 Pg CO2 (Fig. 2a,b). Notably, 66.52% of the mining-related deforestation was attributed to global unrecorded mining activities. As for the continental-scale comparison, Asia experienced the highest deforestation in mining areas, accounting for 42.88% (7907 km2) of deforestation (Table 1), followed by South America (4735 km²), North America (3287 km²), Africa (1600 km²), Europe (1238 km²), and Australia (998 km²). However, South America was identified to account for the highest share (78.32%) in unrecorded mining driven deforestation (Table 1).

Throughout the 21st century (2001–2023), 175 countries worldwide experienced deforestation in mining areas (Fig. 2a). Tropical forests were seriously affected, with 10,824 km2 of mining-driven deforestation accounting for 0.56 Pg CO2 in carbon emissions, making them visible as hotspots (Fig. 2b). Cold and temperate regions also faced considerable deforestation, with 5162 km2 and 3470 km2 of mining-driven deforestation, respectively. Between 2001 and 2023, the top 10 countries with the largest mining-linked deforestation made up 78.99% of global deforestation, equating to 0.61 Pg CO2 in forest carbon emissions (Fig. 2c). Indonesia experienced the highest deforestation in mining areas, representing 21.72% (4292.33 km2) of total deforestation and 0.22 Pg CO2 in carbon emissions (Fig. 2c). Russia and Brazil followed, accounting for 10.81% and 10.58% of global mining-related deforestation, respectively, which correspond to 0.03 Pg CO2 and 0.12 Pg CO2. Our findings show that in the tropical biomes, unrecorded mining activities played a more prominent role in mining-related deforestation, as seen in countries such as Peru (99.95%), Myanmar (98.26%), and Venezuela (94.43%) (Fig. 2c).

The two-decade trajectory reveals an increasing trend in global mining-driven deforestation from 2001 to 2012 ( + 90.26 km2/year, p < 0.001), followed by a decline since 2013 (−50.73 km2/year, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2d). This temporal pattern corresponds closely with the trend in global exploration budgets observed throughout the 21st century (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Gold and coal mining dominated deforestation across the globe

We analyzed the relationship between mining-related deforestation and the primary commodities of mining operations which were provided in the SNL mining database (Methods). Gold (41.71%) and coal (26.33%) mining emerged as the leading contributors to global mining-related deforestation, accounting for 0.39 Pg CO2 and 0.18 Pg CO2 of carbon emission, respectively (Fig. 3a,b). The top six countries responsible for gold mining-related deforestation are Brazil (1507.42 km2), Russia (1043.21 km2), Indonesia (998.51 km2), Peru (914.72 km2), Ghana (735.24 km2), and Suriname (624.93 km2) (Fig. 3b). Our results also reveal that 73.21% of gold mining-related deforestation was due to unrecorded mining activities during 2001–2023, which facilitated the persistence of unrecorded mining activities on SNL databases (Fig. 3c). Specifically, unrecorded gold mining is widespread in Brazil, Peru, Myanmar, and Guyana, accounting for 86.22%, 99.87%, 99.33%, and 78.18%, respectively. Additionally, 48.54% of coal mining-related deforestation was due to unrecorded mining. Indonesia accounted for the largest deforestation caused by coal mining activities at 49.18% (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, from 2001 to 2012, deforestation caused by coal and other mining commodities exhibited an increasing trend, followed by a decline after 2012. In contrast, deforestation in gold mining areas continued steadily from 2001 to 2017 and witnessed a decrease since 2017. However, gold mining activities still accounted for an annual deforestation of 435 km² after 2018, which is two times higher than coal mining-related deforestation (150 km2) (Fig. 4a).

The global transition to renewable energy has gradually decreased the demand for coal mining activities, leading to a reduction in coal mining-related deforestation after 2012 (Fig. 4a). However, the development of renewable energy technologies and infrastructure has simultaneously increased the demand for metal materials32. We grouped this mining-related deforestation by the mining commodity types to categories the mining demand by renewable/non-renewable energy production33 with the energy transition (Supplementary Table. S7, see Methods). The result suggests that the growing emphasis on renewable energy could be an underlying driver of intensified deforestation. Our results indicate that between 2001 and 2012, 66.20% of deforestation in mining areas occurred in regions where minerals were extracted, which could potentially be used for renewable energy production (see Methods, Fig. 4b). After 2012, a higher percentage of deforestation (74.88%) was observed in mines targeting minerals required for renewable energy production, compared to those for non-renewable energy production (25.11%) (Fig. 4b). This finding highlights the possibility that the increasing demand for minerals used in renewable energy production and infrastructure may further exacerbate mining-related deforestation in the future.

Discussion

Mining activity has seen substantial growth due to rising customers’ demand and its role in supporting industrial development in the 21st century, resulting in extensive environmental impacts worldwide34,35. Systematic and continuous monitoring of global mining activities is essential to provide quantitative, robust evidence of changes over time. Consistent and temporally comparable assessments are especially crucial for characterizing spatially explicit trajectories of these mining-related environmental degradation. This study provides a more comprehensive inventory for mining areas than those used in previous studies2,32, encompassing 236,028 mining locations worldwide, including a substantial number of previously unrecorded mining operations sites not captured in SNL mining database. Building upon this enhanced inventory, we provide an up-to-date and spatially explicit assessment of these mining activities’ direct impact on forest loss, revealing that mining has led to a total loss of 19,765 km2 of deforestation, contributing to 0.75 Pg CO2 emissions from 2001 to 2023. While decades of case-based studies have highlighted the role of mining activities in driving deforestation and the conflicts they create between resource extraction and conservation efforts3,15,17. Previous studies have largely relied on the SNL mining database, focusing on industrial-scale mining activities2,3. As a result, they have underestimated the overall impacts of global mining activities, including unrecorded operations in previous studies using SNL mining database. Our findings indicate that mining-related deforestation is approximately twice as high as the estimates reported in recent studies2, largely due to the omission of these unrecorded mining activities. Our study addresses this gap by assembling comprehensive global mining areas and demonstrating the potential for a full-scale global assessment of mining-related deforestation.

Among various mining materials, gold and coal mines have dominated mining-related deforestation, consistent with the previous research2. Large-scale deforestation linked to unrecorded gold mining operations has been rampant in tropical countries, such as Brazil36,37, Peru26, Ghana11, and Suriname38. These mining activities, often characterized by artisanal small-scale practices with inadequate rehabilitation measures, also contribute to soil and water pollution32, posing threats to Indigenous health39. The recent surge in gold prices has further fueled illegal mining activities28, accelerating the losses of protected forests in Amazonian regions37. These informal mining operations, marked by low barriers to entry and less-skill requirements, is prevalent in rural communities in Africa and South Asia40. Nonetheless, an estimated 40–150 million people in the low- and middle-income countries rely directly or indirectly on artificial small-scale mining for their livelihoods16,18. Therefore, promoting forest-smart mining policies41 is critical in these low-income countries, enabling them to benefit from the increasing demand for minerals essential to transitioning to a decarbonized economy while minimizing the environmental impact of their extraction28.

Our findings indicate a declining trend in annual mining-related deforestation since 2011, potentially linked to a decrease in global mining investment driven by reduced demand for traditional energy materials like coal42. However, as the global energy system shifts toward renewable sources, the demand for metals is expected to continue rising. While renewable energy production can mitigate climate change and promote a low-carbon economy, unchecked growth in metal demand could severely degrade forests and pose other environmental challenges10,32. For instance, the mining of nickel—used in batteries for electric vehicles and energy storage systems—often involve large-scale changes to land use43, including deforestation in regions rich in biodiversity6. It becomes imperative to integrate environmental and social considerations into the development of mining policies. Moreover, the temporal changes in mining-related deforestation appear to be influenced by global exploration budgets of nonferrous minerals, potentially reflecting the demand dynamics driven by the international mineral market (Supplementary Fig. S5). Previous studies have shown that fluctuations in the prices of special minerals can trigger the expansion of mining activities44,45. Therefore, understanding the drivers of mining-related deforestation necessitates an integrated consideration of both global mineral market trends and the effects of regional and local mining management practices.

Our study is subject to several levels of uncertainty. First, although the mining datasets compiled in this study represent the most comprehensive global inventory of mining activities to date—integrating multiple sources such as manual delineated mining footprints and crowdsourcing mining polygons from OpenStreetMap (OSM) map—they predominately focus on polygons or land-use parcels broadly defined surrounding visibly identifiable mining areas. As a result, these datasets are limited in their ability to precisely capture the actual extent of mining operations. To improve individual-level mining recognition and delineation, future efforts should prioritize multi-scale, wall-to-wall detection and segmentation of mining areas using high-resolution satellite imagery. This could enable more accurate delineation of the mining extents and their associated land cover changes. Second, we defined “recorded mines” based on spatial overlays with data from the SNL mining database provided by S&P Global Market Intelligence, which reports mining sites as point locations. However, this database still suffers from its inherent biases and limitations due to reliance on public disclosures, potentially leading to uncertainties regarding the classification of unrecorded mining areas34,40. Incorporating more diverse datasets and integrated approaches will be critical for improving the identification of mining activities in different forms and quantifying their associated impacts. Furthermore, the primary dataset we used to assess deforestation is the high-resolution global forest cover change maps that delineate tree cover change at a spatial resolution of 30 m provided by Global Forest Change dataset(GFC)12. The accuracy and uncertainty inherent in this dataset are sensitive to the reliability of associated findings. To ensure robustness, we have performed extensive validations. On the one hand, we conducted an independent assessment of GFC-based deforestation in mining areas through visual interpretations using Landsat time series. Specifically, we randomly selected 2000 deforested pixels and 200 non-deforested pixels in mining areas across various regions using stratified random sampling methods (Supplementary Table S8). This represents a substantially larger sample size compared to the original global product assessment (v.1.0, n = 1500). We then visually interpreted these samples using Landsat time series to evaluate the accuracy of GFC-based deforestation data in mining areas. The overall accuracy across various regions exceeds 82% (Supplementary Table S9). On the other hand, we benchmarked the GFC data against the Tropical Moist Forest (TMF) dataset46 using a time-series cross-comparison. The deforestation trends from 2001 to 2023 are consistently aligned between the two datasets, with the TMF-based deforestation differing from GFC-based deforestation by an uncertainty of 12.43% in tropical regions, suggesting plausible accuracy (Methods, Supplementary Fig. S6). Given this satisfactory accuracy and consistent temporal trends, future work with high-resolution surveillance could still be invested such as incorporating satellites such as 10-meter Sentinel-1/2 and 3-meter PlanetScope to better capture small-scale mining activities across different regions. Lastly, this study focused solely on the areas of deforestation within mining zones, while the indirect impacts of mining activities, such as in-migration driven by the mining industry, expansion of human settlements, and construction of mining-related infrastructure, which can often surpass the direct impacts of mining activities3, were not included. Additionally, this study assessed deforestation in forests exceeding 5 meters in height due to two considerations: (1) vegetation below 5 meters often regenerates more rapidly, introducing uncertainties when estimating global impacts; and (2) some deforested areas transition into lower vegetation types, such as shrubs or grasslands, complicating time-series assessments with higher uncertainties. However, mining activities may impact natural vegetation below 5 meters. To address this, we utilized the 30-meter time-series land-cover dynamics monitoring product47 to quantify losses in shrubland and grassland from 2000 to 2022. Our analysis revealed a loss of 1253 km² of shrubland and 1589 km² of grassland, which were converted into bare land as a direct result of mining activities during this period (Supplementary Table S10). Taken together, some next-step initiatives need to consider the indirect and cumulative forest and other vegetation impacts, beyond the immediate deforestation observed at surface levels3.

Methods

To estimate the impact of global mining activities on natural forests, we first integrated a comprehensive inventory of global mining areas by combining four previously published mining areas databases. We categorized these mining areas into recorded and unrecorded mines based on integration with S&P SNL metals and mining database. We then analyzed the mining-driven deforestation and forest carbon emission in these mining areas from 2001 to 2023 using the Global Forest Change forest loss dataset and global forest carbon fluxes. The flowchart is shown in Supplementary Fig. S7.

A comprehensive inventory of global mining areas

We combined four mining databases from multiple sources, including global mining polygons in 2019 and Global mining footprint in 202221, artisanal mining areas in 20238 and OpenStreetMap-based mining land use in 2024 (details in Supplementary Tables S1,2). The specific criteria and definition for each dataset are as following:

(1) The global mining polygons25 delineated all underground and above-ground mining infrastructure visible in satellite images. This dataset, derived from the visual interpretation of Sentinel-2 images for 2019, contained 44,929 polygon features covering 101,583 km2. The polygons represent mining-related footprints that were delineated spatially around the actual mining activities, focusing on mining infrastructures and signatures indicative of active mining operations.

(2) Global Mining Footprint in 2022 provided by Tang et al.21, also was collected here, as another source for mining areas. The dataset was mapped from remote sensing analysis of high-resolution imagery, comprising 74,548 mining polygons, covering 65,585 km2. The boundaries of the mining footprints were manually delineated based on visual interpretation of the imagery, utilizing spectral, textural, and contextual cues to identify active extraction zones. The delineation focused primarily on features representing the operational core—namely, the areas of actual excavation and processing—rather than peripheral or ancillary land cover modifications.

(3) The artisanal mining areas8 were visually identified and outlined through a combination of literature and popular media review, mining company and national reporting on mining activity, social media investigations, and extensive manual satellite and aerial imager review. This dataset includes 660 mining polygons that are notable for artisanal mining activities in tropical regions, which primarily focus on river mineral mining, including selected global and demand mining8. The delineation of artisanal mining areas was based on visual interpretation and corroborating secondary sources, emphasizing the spatial boundary of active artisanal mining sites across tropical rivers.

(4) We also extracted all mining areas categorized as “quarry” in land use type from OpenStreetMap48,49 (collected by January 2024), resulting in 170,620 mining-related polygon features. The quarry is defined as an area of land used for surface extraction (open-pit mining) of mineral or organic resources like gravel, sand, coal, or other land29. The OSM data was contributed by volunteers and reflects widely quarrying operations, defined by open pits or surface mining activities.

We combined these four mining datasets spatially to create a comprehensive global footprint for mining activities (Supplementary Fig. S7, Supplementary Table S2). By combining these mining datasets, our study captures a broad spectrum of mining activities, from large-scale industrial to smallholder mining operations, with varied delineation scales ranging from detailed individual infrastructures to broader land use categories. These included all surface mining footprints and underground mining features recognizable from satellite images or community knowledge, such as open pits, quarries, water storage ponds, milling infrastructures, waste rock dumps, and tailings storage facilities which are directly associated with mining operations (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S1). Additionally, mining polygons smaller than 1000 m2 were filtered out due to the potential low accuracy associated with very small mining areas. Our global mining datasets comprise 236,028 mining polygons, covering a total land of 145,900 km2 (Supplementary Table S2).

Additionally, we performed an assessment to estimate the accuracy for the mining polygons using stratified random sampling50. We randomly sample ~5% of the total mining polygons across different mining size groups, resulting in randomly selected 11,878 mining polygons to assess the overall accuracy of the mining datasets, which also spanned different geographical regions (Supplementary Fig. S8). Specifically, we classify mining polygons into different categories based on the area of mining polygon and then randomly selected 5% mining polygons across different mining sizes (Supplementary Table S4). We then visually inspected manually sampled polygons using Google Earth satellite imagery to confirm the presence of mining activities in each mining polygon. The overall accuracy of the global mining areas was 87.37% from these validated mining polygons (Supplementary Table S4). In general, larger mining areas show higher overall accuracy; specifically, those mines greater than >5 km2 showed an accuracy >94% (Supplementary Table S4). Among the sampled mining polygons, 383 mining polygons were recorded by SNL mining dataset (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Definition of unrecorded and recorded mining activity

We categorized the 236,028 mining areas as recorded and unrecorded mines based on integration with SNL metals and mining database. The SNL metals and mining database provided by SNL collected the spatial location of 40,410 global mining sites, detailing mining status, owners, and primary commodities (updated until January 2024, Supplementary Fig. S9). The SNL mining sites include both active and inactive mining operations, reflecting various stages from grassroot exploration to closure, and provide insight into current investments and industrial mining activities19,25,33. However, scan reporting mining and informal mining activities such as artisanal and small-scale mining areas typically unrecorded in SNL mining database19,25. Similarly, the historic and abandoned mining sited areas typically missed25. Despite these limitations, the SNL database is one of the most comprehensive and up-to-data sources of mining data, and it is commonly referenced in previous studies documents mining sites, acting as a proxy for reported mining sites25. Mining areas that spatially interact with SNL mining sites were classified as recorded mines, whereas mining polygons that did not overlaid with the SNL mining database were categorized as unrecorded mines according to ref. 25. The unrecorded mines highlight the potential underestimation of the impact of these mining activities by the usage of SNL mining database in previous studies. These mines may be subjected to permitted local regulations but have not been captured in the SNL database, including many small mines and quarries. We further identified the primary products of the mining polygons by analyzing the spatial nearest-neighbor mining activities, as reported in the SNL mining database, based on the Euclidean distance (Supplementary Fig. S10). Most mining polygons (96%) have associated with their nearest SNL mining sites within the 1-km surrounding areas (Supplementary Fig. S11). Noted, the by-products of mining activities were not considered in this study due to the limitations in the attribute information provided by SNL mining database.

Additionally, we aggregated this mining by the type of primary commodity to determine the mining demand by renewable/non-renewable energy production, following the classification by Sonter et al.33 (Supplementary Table S6). The classification is based on the projected material demand under 2050 low-carbon energy scenarios51. This project primarily focused on two renewable-energy technologies: wind turbine manufacturing and solar photovoltaic installations, along with one energy-using technology: storage batteries for electric vehicles. Other technologies, such as carbon capture and storage installation, nuclear electricity generation installation, electric vehicle manufacturing, and lithium-Ion batteries, are also considered part of the renewable energy sector33. The mining properties that are not critical for renewable energy are regarded as material for non-renewable energy production.

Mining-related deforestation in the 21st century

To quantify forest cover changes associated with the expansion of mining areas from 2001 to 2023, we used a high-resolution map of global forest cover change that maps tree cover change at a spatial resolution of 30 m provided by Global Forest Change 2000–2023 (GFC, Version 1.11)12 (Supplementary Fig. S4). The dataset defines trees as all vegetation taller than 5 m in height, where forests are identified based on a tree canopy coverage threshold of at least 30%12. Forest loss and the year of forest loss indicated where and when forests were removed during the period from 2000 to 2023. The GFC dataset has been widely used as a proxy for deforestation with the user’s and producer’s accuracies >83%12,52,53. We estimate the loss areas of forest loss in the mining areas over the period from 2001 to 2023 to show the deforestation related to global mining activity12. The mining areas were overlaid with data from the Global Forests Change forest loss, regarded as mining-related deforestation.

To assess the accuracy of GFC forest loss in mining areas, we perform an independent assessment of deforestation in mining areas using stratified random sample methods. We randomly sample 2000 deforested pixels and 200 non-deforested pixels in our mining areas across six continents (Supplementary Fig. S12), which represents a much larger sample population than the original assessment of global product (v.1.0, n = 1500)12. As the mining areas are about 5% of the global forest cover, the 2200 samples adequately reflected the overall accuracy of mining-related deforestation. Specifically, we randomly selected 2000 deforested pixels in mining areas across six continents, based on the percentage of deforestation areas (Supplementary Table S8, Supplementary Fig. S12). We then aggregated the annual medoid values (the actual data in a dataset that is most centrally located)54 for Landsat time series (level-2 product with atmospheric correction, masked for cloud cover) and plotted the time series for the Normalized Burn Ratio (NBR), Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) from 2000–2023 using Google Earth Engines (Supplementary Fig. S13). We visually interpreted these pixels to validate whether deforestation occurred and when deforestation took place using Landsat imagery with true color visualization (RGB). The annual time series of NBR, NDVI and EVI for these samples assisted was plotted to support the visual validation of the sample pixels (Supplementary Fig. S13), which are widely used indicators for deforestation monitoring55. We subsequently calculated the error matrix, which includes overall accuracy (OA), user’s accuracy (UA) and producer’s accuracy (PA). From our assessment, we estimate the OA, UA and PA for GFC deforestation in mining areas. In general, the OAs are > 82%, UAs are > 98% and PAs are > 81% in each continent, underscoring the robustness of GFC forest loss in mining areas (Supplementary Table S9). The uncertainty of GFC loss year is 0.80, Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the loss year is 2.60 years (Supplementary Fig. S14), confirming that there is no notable bias in the observed deforestation changes in mining areas over time.

Additionally, we compared deforestation in mining areas across tropical regions using the Tropical Moist Forest (TMF,‘TMFv2023’ version) dataset from the European Commission Joint Research Center (JRC)46. The TMF dataset maps disturbances in tropical humid forest from 1990 to 2023 derived from the Landsat archive collection. The TMF dataset employed an algorithm that detects forest disruptions for each 30-meter pixel using available Landsat image, classifying the status of forest based on the duration these disruptions are visible46. The dataset identifies various types of forest disruptions, including both direct deforestation and deforestation after degradation. We compared the total deforestation reported by TMF dataset with that from GFC dataset in mining areas for the same period, from 2001 to 2023 (Supplementary Fig. S6). The result demonstrates that the deforestation in mining areas, as mapped by TMF (10,403.42 km²), closed aligns with GFC mapped deforestation (11,880.64 km²), showing an uncertainty of 12.43% in tropical regions (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Net carbon flux by mining-related deforestation

We calculated the forest carbon emissions assisted with mining-related deforestation by incorporating the Global Forest Carbon Fluxes dataset for 2001–2023 from Harris et al.56. This dataset was created by integrating ground measurements, the spaceborne Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) observations from the Geoscience Laser Altimeter System (GLAS), and variables derived from satellite products56. The net forest carbon flux from this dataset represents the exchange of carbon between the forest and the atmosphere between 2001 and 2023, which was used to calculate the balance between carbon emitted by deforestation in mining areas. The net forest carbon flux with the units of megagrams CO2 emissions/ha, at ~30 m spatial resolutions56. We aggregate the net forest carbon flux in deforested pixels to calculate the total gross CO2 emissions by mining-related deforestation for 2001–2023. We also compare the forest carbon emissions by recorded and unrecorded mining activities.

Mining activities in protected areas

Protected areas are some of the most critical areas for the conservation of natural landscapes, wildlife, and biodiversity and hence mining activities and deforestation in these areas pose a threat to the global protected areas. To explore the potential impacts of mining activities on protected areas, we calculated the number of protected areas overlay in mining areas based on World Database Protected Areas (WDPA)30. WDPA protected areas are carried out by the Union Nation in collaboration with governments, non-governmental organizations, academia and industry. This provides the most comprehensive database for global protected areas. According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), protected areas are classified into six distinct management categories: Ia: strict nature reserve; Ib: wilderness area; II: national park; III: natural monument or feature; IV: habitat/species management area; V: protected landscape/seascape; VI: protected area with sustainable use of natural resources (details in Supplementary Table. S11). These categories are not listed for some protected areas due to omissions in the WDPA dataset, but they does not reveal any information whether management objectives are in place or if the site is of importance30,57. We filtered protected areas in the WDPA datasets where mining activities were observed within their boundaries. Considering the issue of overlap protected areas in the WDPA datasets31, we dissolved polygons where the overlapping area exceeds 50% of each polygon’s area. For the merged polygon, we assigned the management class based on the highest IUCN management classes present in dissolved polygons, following this hierarchy: Ia > Ib > II > III > IV > V > VI > Not Applicable/Reported/Assigned. We reported the counts of protected areas across various IUCN management categories that are potentially threatened by mining activities.

Auxiliary datasets

We utilized the Large Scale International Boundary (LSLB) Polygons from United States Office of the Geographer at the country level to analyze mining areas and associated with deforestation. Global exploration budgets for nonferrous from S&P Global Commodity Insights were collected to indicate the annual budgets for mining activities for 2001–2023 (Supplementary Fig. S5).
