Pan-cancer analysis of the upstream regulator FDX1 in cuproptosis
Authors: Yan Xue, Qiong Tang, Wu Liu, Baoqing Zhao, Wanwan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global incidence and mortality of cancer continue to rise rapidly, and cancer remains one of the most severe challenges in the field of public health. Several studies have revealed significant differences in FDX1 expression between various tumor cells and normal tissues, suggesting that it may be involved in tumor initiation, progression, and the regulation of malignant phenotypes. FDX1 is an iron-sulfur protein located in the mitochondria that functions in intracellular electron transfer, shuttling electrons from NADPH to mitochondrial cytochrome P450 and participating in steroid, vitamin D, and bile acid metabolism. Research has demonstrated that FDX1 is a key regulator of cuproptosis. When intracellular free copper levels become excessively high, FDX1 reduces Cu²⁺ to the more toxic Cu⁺. Meanwhile, acting as an upstream regulator of lipoylation, FDX1 promotes the lipoylation of enzymes involved in the TCA cycle. These processes ultimately lead to protein aggregation, mitochondrial destabilization, and the induction of cuproptosis.It is therefore necessary to conduct a systematic pan-cancer analysis of FDX1. In our study, we examined the expression differences of FDX1 between various tumor types and normal tissues, as well as its associations with clinical parameters such as tumor stage, to evaluate its diagnostic and prognostic potential. Furthermore, we investigated the relationship between FDX1 and the tumor immune microenvironment, exploring its possibility as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy.Keywords: Cuproptosis, FDX1, Pan-cancer, Prognosis, Immune

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with rapidly increasing incidence and death rates, posing an extremely severe therapeutic challenge [1–2]. About one in five people will develop cancer during their lifetime, and roughly one in nine men and one in twelve women will die from the disease [3–4]. In terms of mortality, lung cancer, colorectal cancer, liver cancer, gastric cancer, and breast cancer account for the highest number of cancer-related deaths [4].In 177 out of 183 countries, cancer ranks among the top three causes of death for individuals aged 30 to 69 years. During this century, cancer is projected to surpass cardiovascular diseases to become the leading cause of premature death in most countries [1].

The characteristics of tumors involve complex biological processes, such as sustaining proliferative signaling, evading growth suppressors, resisting cell death, enabling replicative immortality, inducing angiogenesis, and activating invasion and metastasis [5]. Given both the high prevalence of cancer and the complexity of tumorigenesis, it is of great importance to comprehensively investigate the expression of relevant genes across pan-cancer and to evaluate their expression levels for clinical therapeutic decision-making and prognostic prediction.

FDX1 (ferredoxin 1) is a small iron–sulfur protein that catalyzes the conversion of substrates such as cholesterol to pregnenolone, aldosterone, and cortisol by reducing mitochondrial cytochrome P450 enzymes [6]. The expression of FDX1 is closely associated with various types of cancers. For example, downregulation of FDX1 activates mitophagy and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby promoting the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through the induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [7]. FDX1 expression also differs between colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues and normal counterparts, and FDX1 overexpression suppresses CRC growth and progression by attenuating epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [8]. In clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the expression of FDX1 is markedly lower in tumor samples compared with normal tissues [9]. Overall, FDX1 represents a promising prognostic biomarker across multiple cancers, and regulating its expression may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for tumor [10].

Meanwhile, FDX1 is also a key regulatory factor in cuproptosis. Cuproptosis is a newly defined form of programmed cell death (PCD), first described by Tsvetkov et al. in 2022 [11].FDX1 serves as an essential upstream regulator of this process: upon delivery of Cu²⁺ into mitochondria by the copper ionophore Elesclomol, FDX1 reduces Cu²⁺ to the more toxic Cu⁺, thereby triggering cell death [12–13]. In addition, FDX1 directly interacts with lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS) to enhance its enzymatic activity, promoting protein lipoylation and initiating the cuproptosis signaling pathway [14]. Although loss of FDX1 markedly increases cellular resistance to cuproptosis, protein lipoylation is essential for proper TCA cycle function; thus, FDX1 deficiency results in TCA cycle dysfunction, impaired mitochondrial respiration and oxidative phosphorylation, and significantly restricted cell growth, particularly under low-glucose conditions [14].

Pan-cancer analysis integrates multi-omics data across multiple tumor types to systematically compare the molecular features of different cancers, facilitating the identification of both shared mechanisms and tumor-specific differences. Since cuproptosis is difficult to quantify directly in large-scale multi-omics datasets, FDX1 is often used as a key surrogate indicator of its functional state. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically assess FDX1 through pan-cancer analysis, including its expression patterns, prognostic value, and relationship with tumor immunity across different cancers, in order to clarify its general role in tumor development and to reveal the activity and biological significance of cuproptosis in diverse tumor contexts.

Materials and methods

To ensure consistency in the selection strategy across different analysis modules, we applied a unified cancer inclusion criterion throughout the study. For all pan-cancer analyses, priority was given to cancer types that exhibited significant expression differences in the overall TCGA cohort (ANOVA or t-test, p < 0.05). Cancer types with missing clinical information or without significant expression differences for the target molecule were excluded.

To ensure comparability between different datasets, gene expression data from TCGA and GTEx were uniformly processed and normalized to TPM values, followed by log₂(TPM + 1) transformation. Batch effects between datasets were minimized using the ComBat algorithm in R. Additionally, whenever applicable, publicly processed datasets were preferentially used. For analyses directly visualized from public databases, all available TCGA samples were included, and any internal data filtering was performed strictly according to the default settings of these resources.

Differential expression of FDX1 between tumor and normal tissues

Gene expression data for TCGA tumors were obtained from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/), including only cancer types with sufficient normal samples in TCGA. For cancer types lacking normal controls, uniformly processed expression data from TCGA and GTEx were retrieved from the GEPIA2 platform (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#analysis). This platform processes raw RNA-seq data using the UCSC Toil RNA-seq workflow to generate standardized and quantified expression values. All expression data were transformed using log₂(TPM + 1). Differential expression between tumor and normal tissues was then analyzed.

Additionally, for cancer types showing significant upregulation or downregulation, differential protein expression analysis was performed using the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html). Log₂ spectral count ratio values from CPTAC were first normalized within each sample profile and then normalized across samples. Z-values represent the number of standard deviations from the median across samples for the corresponding cancer type.

We also obtained immunohistochemistry (IHC) images of FDX1 in normal and tumor tissues from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; https://www.proteinatlas.org/). Images were selected based on histological features such as staining intensity and distribution of positive cells, highlighting representative cancer expression patterns that clearly differ from corresponding normal tissues. In addition, we analyzed immunofluorescence images from HPA to examine the subcellular localization of FDX1 in human cell lines, including A431 (skin squamous cell carcinoma), U‑251MG (glioblastoma), and U2OS (osteosarcoma), focusing on the nucleus, microtubules, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER).

Association of FDX1 expression with tumor stage

As tumors progress from early to advanced stages, gene expression profiles of tumor cells may undergo significant alterations [15]. Standardized TCGA data were downloaded from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/). Samples with FDX1 expression values of 0 were excluded, and expression data were transformed using log₂(x + 1). Cancer types with fewer than three samples were removed, leaving 27 cancer types for analysis. Within each cancer type, FDX1 expression levels were compared across clinical stages (Stage I–IV). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test differences among multiple groups, followed by unpaired t-tests for pairwise comparisons.

Diagnostic value of FDX1

FDX1 expression data were downloaded and curated from the GDC Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Samples lacking clinical annotations were excluded. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using the pROC R package, and the results were visualized with ggplot2. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to quantify the diagnostic value of FDX1. Different AUC values indicate different levels of clinical diagnostic performance: low (AUC: 0.5–0.7), medium (AUC: 0.7–0.9), and high (AUC: >0.9). An AUC closer to 1 reflects stronger capability to distinguish between diseased and healthy samples.

Prognostic value of FDX1

Using expression data and corresponding clinical survival information downloaded from GDC Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed for overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI), and prognostic forest plots were generated [16]. For cancer types showing significant associations (P < 0.05), Kaplan–Meier survival curves were further plotted for detailed analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using the R packages survival, survminer, and ggplot2.

Differential methylation of FDX1

DNA methylation is a regulatory mechanism that influences gene transcription without altering the DNA sequence. Methylation occurring in gene promoter regions often directly suppresses gene expression and is considered one mechanism leading to low expression [17]. We compared FDX1 promoter methylation levels between normal and tumor tissues using the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html), which employs unpaired two-sample t-tests to assess the significance of differences between groups. Beta values represent DNA methylation levels [18]. CpG distribution diagrams were generated using SMART (http://www.bioinfo-zs.com/smartapp/), as CpG sites are the primary locations of DNA methylation. Finally, MethSurv (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/methsurv/) was used to analyze the relationship between FDX1 CpG methylation and patient prognosis.

Genetic alterations of FDX1 across pan-cancer

Genetic alterations of FDX1 across various cancer types in all TCGA tumors were obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). TCGA expression data were downloaded from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/), and level 4 Simple Nucleotide Variation (SNV) data processed by MuTect2 were obtained from the GDC Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) for mutation analysis. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) for each tumor sample was calculated using the tmb function in the R package maftools. Microsatellite instability (MSI) scores for each tumor were obtained from previous studies [19], and neoantigen (NEO) data were collected as reported [20]. MSI and NEO data were integrated with gene expression data at the sample level.

FDX1 in tumor immune microenvironment

The tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of cancer cells, stromal cells, fibroblasts, and innate and adaptive immune cells, plays a critical role in cancer progression and therapeutic response [21]. Interactions among cancer cells, stromal cells, and infiltrating immune cells render the TME a complex network [22]. Immune cell composition varies substantially across different cancer types, indicating that immunotherapy efficacy may not be uniform across all cancers or patients.

To systematically evaluate the immunoregulatory role of FDX1 in the tumor microenvironment, we conducted a comprehensive analysis from three aspects: immune cell infiltration, immune subtype distribution, and the correlation with immunoregulatory genes.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration is a key component of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and plays an important role in tumor immune regulation and progression. Immune infiltration analysis can evaluate the relationship between key genes and the tumor immune microenvironment [23].

Based on the ssGSEA algorithm provided in the R package GSVA [24], the immune infiltration levels of 24 immune cell types were calculated using cell-type-specific marker genes reported in the Immunity study [25]. Spearman correlation analysis between FDX1 expression and the immune infiltration matrix across pan-cancer datasets was performed using R, and the results were visualized as heatmaps with the ggplot2 package.

FDX1 expression across immune and molecular cancer subtypes

FDX1 expression data were retrieved from TISIDB (http://cis.hku.hk/TISIDB/index.php) and samples were grouped according to immune subtypes (C1-C6) as defined by Thorsson et al., 2018 [20]. Simultaneously, samples were classified according to molecular subtypes defined for each cancer type in TCGA studies. Within each cancer type, the Kruskal–Wallis test, a non-parametric method, was used to compare FDX1 expression across different immune or molecular subtypes. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

FDX1 correlation with immune regulators and checkpoints

Immunoregulatory genes are those involved in modulating immune responses. Immune checkpoints (ICPs) represent a subclass of these genes, primarily consisting of inhibitory receptor–ligand pairs that regulate the activity of T cells and other immune cells. They play a critical role in maintaining immune tolerance and preventing autoimmunity.

TCGA pan-cancer datasets were downloaded from the UCSC Xena database (https://xenabrowser.net/), and expression data for FDX1 and marker genes of 150 immunoregulatory pathway genes across five categories—chemokines (41), receptors (18), major histocompatibility complex [MHC] (21), immunoinhibitors (24), and immunostimulators (46)—were extracted for each sample. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between FDX1 expression and the marker genes of these five immunoregulatory pathways. Similarly, expression data for FDX1 and 60 immune checkpoint pathway genes, divided into inhibitory (24) and stimulatory (36) categories, were extracted for each sample. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between FDX1 expression and the marker genes of the two immune checkpoint pathways.

Gene enrichment analysis

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks centered on FDX1 were constructed using STRING (https://string-db.org/), and the top 50 genes with the strongest direct interactions with FDX1 were extracted. Using the GEPIA2, the top 100 genes co-expressed with FDX1 were identified separately in TCGA tumor and normal tissue datasets. The combined set of 150 genes from these two databases was subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses using the R packages org.Hs.eg.db and clusterProfiler, examining biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and KEGG pathway. The top five genes most strongly correlated with FDX1 expression in GEPIA2 were selected, and their Pearson correlation with FDX1 in TCGA samples was evaluated using the TIMER2.0 (https://compbio.cn/timer2/). Correlation results were visualized using scatter plots.

Association between FDX1 and functional states across different cancer types

CancerSEA (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CancerSEA/) is a database that integrates single-cell transcriptomic data for 14 cellular functional states across major cancers and computes correlations between genes and each functional state, including angiogenesis, apoptosis, cell cycle, differentiation, DNA damage and repair, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), hypoxia, inflammation, invasion, metastasis, proliferation, quiescence, and stemness. We retrieved the correlation between FDX1 and these functional states, and significant associations were defined using thresholds of correlation coefficient > 0.3 and P < 0.05.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis indicated that FDX1 exhibited low diagnostic accuracy in 13 cancer types: COAD, CESC, HNSC, LIHC, PRAD, READ, THYM, UCEC, OSCC, ESCC, ESCA, CESC, and PAAD; moderate diagnostic accuracy in 10 cancer types: BRCA, CHOL, KICH, LUAD, LUSC, SKCM, STAD, THCA, STAD, and ESAD; and high diagnostic accuracy in 5 cancer types: PCPG, KIRC, GBM, KIRP, and SARC (Fig. 4).

Prognostic value of FDX1

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that high FDX1 expression was associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in patients with ACC, HNSC, and LGG, whereas it correlated with better OS in KIRC patients (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 5). Regarding disease-specific survival (DSS), high FDX1 expression was significantly associated with worse outcomes in ACC and LGG patients, and better outcomes in KIRC and KIRP patients (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 6). For progression-free interval (PFI), high FDX1 expression correlated with poorer outcomes in ACC, HNSC, and LGG patients, and better outcomes in KIRC, LIHC, and THCA patients (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 7).

Genetic alterations of FDX1 across pan-cancer

Genetic alterations of FDX1 were detected in 24 cancer types, with deep deletion being the predominant form, particularly in TGCT, CESC, SKCM, and UVM, where the frequency of deep deletion reached approximately 2–3%. These findings suggest that in these tumors, FDX1 may be functionally downregulated through copy number loss, potentially contributing to tumor initiation or progression. Amplification occurred at frequencies above 1% in UCS, OV, and KICH, and was also the main type of alteration in KICH, LUSC, LAML, GBM, and LGG. Mutations were most frequent in UVM, whereas structural variants were rare events, observed only in a few cancers such as CESC and GBM at very low frequencies (Fig. 9A). The mutation counts of FDX1 across pan-cancer are shown in Fig. 9B. FDX1 expression was significantly positively correlated with tumor mutation burden (TMB) in nine cancers, including GBMLGG, LGG, ESCA, STES, SARC, STAD, UCEC, HNSC, and LUSC (Fig. 10A). Significant positive correlations between FDX1 expression and microsatellite instability (MSI) were observed in five cancers: STES, KIPAN, STAD, UCEC, and KIRC (Fig. 11B). FDX1 was also significantly positively correlated with neoantigen load (NEO) in three cancers: UCEC, UCS, and CHOL (Fig. 10C).

FDX1 in tumor immune microenvironment

Immune infiltration

The analysis of immune cell infiltration showed that in ACC and THCA, FDX1 is negatively correlated with multiple immune cell types; in BRCA, LGG, PCPG, TGCT, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LIHC, LUAD, PAAD, and SARC, FDX1 is positively correlated with multiple immune cells. In CHOL, the correlation between FDX1 and all immune cells is not significant (Fig. 11A). Cancer types showing significant correlations between FDX1 expression and immune cell infiltration (P < 0.05) with relatively high correlation coefficients were selected for further presentation. FDX1 shows a strong positive correlation with T helper cells and Tcm in UVM (Fig. 12B-C); with T helper cells and T cells in THYM (Fig. 11D-E); and with DC and macrophages in TGCT (Fig. 11F-G).

Correlation between FDX1 expression and tumor immune subtypes

FDX1 Expression Across Immune and Molecular Cancer Subtypes

FDX1 expression exhibited significant differences across immune and molecular subtypes in multiple cancers (P < 0.05). At the immune subtype level, FDX1 gradually increased across C1-C4 in LIHC and THCA; in ACC, KIRC, and KIRP, FDX1 expression was significantly higher in C4 and C5 compared with other subtypes. In PRAD and STAD, FDX1 expression gradually decreased across C1-C3. Significant differences in FDX1 expression among immune subtypes were also observed in BRCA, LGG, SARC, and UCEC (Fig. 12).

At the molecular subtype level, FDX1 also displayed notable differential expression patterns. In BRCA, Basal and Her2 subtypes showed higher FDX1 expression than the Luminal subtype. In ESCA, the CIN subtype had higher expression than MSI and HM-indel subtypes. In KIRP, C2a exhibited significantly higher FDX1 expression than C1/C2c-CIMP subtypes. In LGG, G-CIMP-low, Mesenchymal-like, and PA-like subtypes showed higher FDX1 expression than other subtypes. In LIHC, iCluster2 had the highest expression; in STAD, the CIN subtype was higher than other subtypes. In PCPG, the Cortical-admixture subtype displayed markedly higher FDX1 expression than the other three subtypes. Additionally, significant differences in FDX1 expression among molecular subtypes were observed in ACC, SKCM, UCEC, and other cancers (Fig. 13).

FDX1 correlation with immune regulators and checkpoints

FDX1 expression is significantly positively correlated with the majority of immunoregulatory genes in KICH, TGCT, PAAD, OV, UCEC, HNSC, LUAD, BLCA, and GBM. In PCPG, FDX1 expression is significantly positively correlated with most chemokine receptors, MHC molecules, immune inhibitors, and immune stimulators; in CHOL, it is significantly positively correlated with most chemokines, MHC molecules, and immune stimulators; and in LAML, it is significantly positively correlated with most immune stimulators. Conversely, in LUAD, SKCM, and MESO, FDX1 expression is negatively correlated with a small subset of immunoregulatory genes (Fig. 14A). Overall, FDX1 appears to function more as a key gene reflecting and participating in changes of the tumor immune landscape, rather than as a molecule restricted to a single pathway.

FDX1 expression levels are significantly positively correlated with most immune checkpoint genes in UVM, LGG, PCPG, DLBC, SARC, ESCA, UCS, UCEC, and PRAD, suggesting that FDX1 may be involved in immune cell activation or the regulation of immune signaling pathways. And FDX1 expression levels are negatively correlated with only a few immune checkpoint genes, such as TNFRSF18, in KIRC, READ, UCS, GBM, and BLCA (Fig. 14B).

2.8 Gene Enrichment Analysis

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network constructed by STRING showed that FDX1 interacts extensively with multiple proteins related to the mitochondrial respiratory chain (e.g., NDUFS3, SDHA) and steroid biosynthesis (e.g., CYP11A1, CYP17A1) (Fig. 15A). Co-expression analysis using TIMER2.0 indicated that CYP21A2, NR5A1, SCARB1, CYP17A1, and MC2R are significantly positively correlated with FDX1 expression (P < 0.001), exhibiting a clear co-expression pattern in TCGA samples (Fig. 15B-F).

Functional enrichment analysis of FDX1-related genes. A FDX1 co-expression network constructed using STRING. B-F Correlation between FDX1 and CYP21A2, SCARB1, NR5A1, MC2R, and CYP17A1 analyzed via GEPIA2 (P < 0.001)

GO functional enrichment analysis revealed that these related genes are significantly enriched in biological processes (BP) including small molecule metabolic processes, redox processes, steroid metabolic processes, organic hydroxy compound metabolism, and steroid biosynthesis (Fig. 16A). In terms of cellular components (CC), they are primarily localized to mitochondria, mitochondrial parts, mitochondrial membranes, mitochondrial envelopes, and inner mitochondrial membranes (Fig. 16B). For molecular functions (MF), they are enriched in oxidoreductase activity, cofactor binding, iron ion binding, heme binding, and tetrapyrrole binding (Fig. 16C).

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of FDX1-related genes. A BP (Biological Process) analysis. B CC (Cellular Component) analysis. C MF (Molecular Function) analysis. D KEGG pathway analysis

KEGG pathway analysis further indicated that these genes are mainly involved in metabolic pathways, Parkinson’s disease, thermogenesis, oxidative phosphorylation, retrograde endocannabinoid signaling, cortisol synthesis and secretion, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), Cushing syndrome, steroid biosynthesis, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (Fig. 16D). These results suggest that FDX1 may influence tumor initiation and progression by regulating mitochondrial metabolism and redox homeostasis.

Relationship between FDX1 and functional states across different cancer types

Positive correlations are mainly observed with stemness, dormancy, and inflammatory states, whereas negative correlations are primarily concentrated in DNA repair, cell cycle, and DNA damage states (Fig. 17A). Specifically, in RB, FDX1 is significantly positively correlated with differentiation and angiogenesis, and significantly negatively correlated with DNA repair, cell cycle, and DNA damage (Fig. 17B). In UM, FDX1 is negatively correlated with DNA repair, DNA damage, and apoptosis, with the strongest negative correlations observed for metastasis and dormancy (Fig. 17C). In AML, FDX1 shows significant positive correlations with stemness and proliferation (Fig. 16F). RCC and ALL also exhibit positive correlations between FDX1 and stemness (Fig. 17D-E).

Discussions

In this study, we observed that FDX1 was expressed at low levels in most tumors, but relatively highly in STAD and GBM. FDX1 is closely associated with mitochondrial metabolism, suggesting that these tumor cells may exhibit distinct mitochondrial metabolic states. Previous studies have shown that drug combinations targeting mitochondrial homeostasis defects can induce mitochondria-mediated cell death in STAD, highlighting a potential link between mitochondrial function and tumor cell survival or death pathways [27]. Inter-organelle interactions, such as mitochondria–ER contacts, also play crucial roles in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and regulating cell fate, and may intersect with tumor-related signaling pathways [28]. Given the central role of mitochondria in cuproptosis and immune regulation, the FDX1–mitochondrial metabolism–mitophagy axis may represent a key mechanistic pathway connecting metabolic stress, immune modulation, and tumor progression.

As a key regulator of cuproptosis, FDX1 participates in mitochondrial redox reactions and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, linking cellular metabolic states to cell fate. Its expression is positively correlated with stemness, dormancy, and inflammatory processes, but negatively correlated with proliferation-related processes such as DNA repair and cell cycle progression. Considering its central role in cuproptosis, FDX1 may modulate tumor cell fate through regulation of mitochondrial metabolism and redox homeostasis. The negative correlations with cell cycle and DNA repair further suggest that FDX1 could suppress tumor cell proliferation. These findings are based on correlative analyses, and the specific mechanisms require further experimental validation.

From an epigenetic perspective, FDX1 exhibits significantly increased promoter methylation in multiple tumor types, with different CpG sites showing distinct prognostic effects across cancers, indicating site-specific regulation of FDX1. This methylation heterogeneity may determine whether FDX1 acts as a tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressing factor in different cancers, providing a mechanistic explanation for its marked cancer type–specific effects. In contrast, copy number gains or mutations are observed in certain cancers, further highlighting its cancer type–specific characteristics.

At the immune level, our study found that FDX1 is broadly associated with immune cell infiltration, immune regulatory molecules, and immune checkpoint molecules, and shows coordinated changes with chemokines, MHC molecules, immune stimulatory factors, and immune inhibitory factors across multiple cancer types. This suggests that FDX1 is not limited to a single immune pathway but may function as a key node within the tumor immune regulatory network. Considering that mitochondrial metabolic states can influence antigen presentation and immune cell function [29–30], FDX1 may indirectly shape the tumor immune microenvironment through regulation of metabolic reprogramming, potentially affecting responses to immunotherapy. However, the specific mechanisms require further experimental validation.

The differential expression of FDX1 across immune and molecular subtypes suggests its potential involvement in the tumor immune microenvironment and metabolic reprogramming, as well as its association with tumor molecular features, supporting its role as a potential biomarker.

In summary, FDX1 is more likely to act as a key molecule linking metabolism and immunity rather than as a single-function gene. Its role depends on the molecular background, epigenetic status, and immune environment of specific tumors, making it a biologically significant and clinically promising candidate target in pan-cancer studies.

Limitations and future directions

This study systematically characterized FDX1 in a pan-cancer context, including its expression patterns, epigenetic regulation, genomic alterations, associations with the immune microenvironment, and clinical prognostic value. Our findings suggest that FDX1 may influence tumor immunity and cell fate through regulation of mitochondrial metabolism or cuproptosis. However, these analyses are purely theoretical, and experimental validation is lacking; the precise molecular mechanisms of FDX1 require extensive further investigation. In addition, this study is primarily based on bioinformatic analyses of public databases, with conclusions largely limited to correlative observations. For some cancer types, limited sample sizes may also affect the robustness of statistical analyses.

Future studies should further validate the specific regulatory mechanisms of FDX1 in metabolic reprogramming, immune modulation, and tumor cell behavior within the cuproptosis pathway using in vitro and in vivo experiments. Clinically, prognostic and therapeutic predictive models integrating FDX1 with other metabolic, epigenetic, and immune parameters should be developed to enhance its translational potential as a precision medicine biomarker. With the ongoing development of small-molecule drugs targeting the cuproptosis pathway and corresponding therapeutic strategies, FDX1 holds promise as a potential target and diagnostic marker in cancer therapy, providing both a theoretical rationale and practical basis for innovative metabolism–immunity combination treatment approaches.
