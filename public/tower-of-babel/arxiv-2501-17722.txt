Abstract




Abstract.

We present a general non-parametric statistical inference theory for integrals of quantiles without assuming any specific sampling design or dependence structure. Technical considerations are accompanied by examples and discussions, including those pertaining to the bias of empirical estimators. To illustrate how the general results can be adapted to specific situations, we derive – at a stroke and under minimal conditions – consistency and asymptotic normality of the empirical tail-value-at-risk, Lorenz and Gini curves at any probability level in the case of the simple random sampling, thus facilitating a comparison of our results with what is already known in the literature. Results, notes and references concerning dependent (i.e., time series) data are also offered. As a by-product, our general results provide new and unified proofs of large-sample properties of a number of classical statistical estimators, such as trimmed means, and give additional insights into the origins of, and the reasons for, various necessary and sufficient conditions.

Introduction

Although well understood, developing statistical inference for quantiles, denoted by
F^{-1}(p)
for various
p\in[0,1]
and known as values-at-risk in banking and insurance, is a challenging task. This is due to the fact that empirical quantiles are order statistics
(
Arnold et al., 2008
;
David and Nagaraja, 2003
, e.g.,)
, unlike empirical cumulative distribution functions (cdf’s) that are sums of (Bernoulli) random variables and can therefore be easily tackled using classical techniques of mathematical statistics and probability theory
(
Shorack, 2017
, e.g.,)
. Inevitably, therefore, in the case of quantiles, quite restrictive assumptions on the population cdf
F
arise. For example, consistency of the empirical quantile requires continuity of the population quantile function at the specified probability level, while asymptotic normality requires conditions on the underlying probability density function (pdf) at the specified quantile
(
Serfling, 1980
;
Shao, 2003
, e.g.)
.

In many applications that arise in economics, finance, and insurance, quantiles are often integrated. Visualized in Figure

\mathrm{LC}(p)
.

.

Since, generally speaking, integrals are linear functionals of their integrands, which are quantiles in our case, researchers often use the aforementioned asymptotic results for quantiles and then apply the continuous mapping theorem
(
Billingsley, 1999
, e.g.,)
to derive desired results for integrated quantiles
(
Csörgő et al., 1986
;
Bellini et al., 2022
;
Han et al., 2024
, e.g.,)
. This approach, however, involves the aforementioned assumptions on the population pdf, although we shall soon see that such assumptions are unnecessary for integrated quantilies; even the very existence of pdf is unnecessary.

Indeed, when developing an asymptotic theory for the Lorenz curve, whose definition is based on integrated quantiles as pointed out by
Gastwirth (1971)
,
Csörgő and Zitikis (1996)
observed that neither the original formulation of the problem nor the obtained large-sample asymptotic distribution requires the existence of pdf, let alone assumptions on it. Based on the observation, they set out to find a path that would lead to desired asymptotic results without involving pdf’s. They succeeded in achieving this goal by introducing a technical tool that they called the Vervaat process, named after the Dutch mathematician Wim Vervaat, whose pioneering results
(
Vervaat, 1972a
;
Vervaat, 1972b
)
on a combination of the uniform on
[0,1]
quantile and empirical processes served an inspiration. We refer to
Zitikis (1998)
for details and references on the topic.

Since the quantile function is not the ordinary inverse of

A long series of research articles by various authors followed
Csörgő and Zitikis (1996)
, exploring theoretical and empirical aspects of integrated quantiles
without

Wei and Zitikis (2023)
suggested an extension of the theory initiated by
Csörgő and Zitikis (1996)
. The current paper takes these developments even further by establishing asymptotic results for integrated quantiles without assuming any specific sampling design or dependence structure between the underlying random variables. In fact, the main results are formulated for generic cdf’s

An overview

We have organized the rest of the paper as follows. In Section
3

(2.1)

\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

of quantiles over the upper-most layer
(p,1)
of probabilities, where
p\in(0,1)
is a fixed boundary probability. We call integral (
2.1
) the upper-layer integral. It plays a particularly prominent role in insurance, where loss random variables are often non-negative, and thus the right-hand tails of their distributions become of particular concern. Risk measures such as the expected shortfall (ES) and its sister risk measure called the “upside” tail-value-at-risk (TVaR) arise
(
Denuit et al., 2005
, e.g.,)
, which in turn lead to considerations of more general integrals called distorted expectations
(e.g.,
Dhaene et al., 2012
, and references therein)
. We shall rigorously define and discuss TVaR later in this paper (see Example
7.1
in particular).

In Section
4
we shall offer several simulated experiments that will clarify some of the statistical properties of the empirical upper-layer integral.

Before we delve into the topics of the following sections, we present several notes that are useful for understanding and appreciating the results of this paper, and in particular the assumptions under which the results will be established.

Note 2.1
.

When
p=0
, integral (
2.1
) reduces to the mean
\mu

of the cdf
F
, given by equation (
1.5
). Statistical inference for
\mu
under various sampling designs can, of course, be developed without invoking the existence of the pdf of
F
. When
p=1
, integral (
2.1
) vanishes. Hence, by restricting ourselves to
p\in(0,1)
we are just excluding the two statistically trivial cases
p=0
and
p=1
.

Note 2.2
.

There is a deeper reason for separating the boundary case
p=0
from
p\in(0,1)
. First, when
p=0
, in the case of the simple random sampling (SRS), that is, when we work with
n
independent and identically distributed (iid) random variables, the empirical estimator of
\mu
is an asymptotically normal estimator under the only requirement that the second moment of the underlying random variable is finite. No assumption related to continuity of the cdf
F
at any point of the real line is needed. In the case
p\in(0,1)
, however,
for an empirical estimator of the upper-layer integral
\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
to be asymptotically normal, it is necessary to assume that the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the point
p
. The necessity of this condition is demonstrated by
Stigler (1973)
when the empirical estimator is a trimmed mean based on SRS. This assumption is also needed for our general results as they absorb the SRS-based trimmed mean as a special case. No such condition, however, is required for
consistency
of the empirical estimator of the upper-layer integral
\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
. We shall illustrate this phenomenon with a simulated example in Section
4
.

.

The aforementioned continuity of the quantile function
F^{-1}
at the point

permeates the entire paper. In our minds, however, we rarely visualize distributions in terms of quantiles – we often think of them in terms of cdf’s. For this reason it is beneficial to follow
Shao (2003)
and reformulate the continuity of

at the point
p\in(0,1)
in the form of the bounds

(2.2)

F\left(F^{-1}(p)-\varepsilon\right)<p<F\left(F^{-1}(p)+\varepsilon\right)

that need to be satisfied for every
\varepsilon>0
.

3
The upper-layer integral

In this section we develop statistical inference for the upper-layer integral
\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
at any fixed lower probability level

p\in(0,1).

Of course, we need to assume that
F
is from the class
\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
of those cdf’s for which the upper quantiles are integrable, that is, the integral
\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
is finite. Note that this condition is the same as to require that the positive part
X^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=X\vee 0
of the random variable
X\sim F
has a finite first moment
\mathbb{E}(X^{+})<\infty
.

The functional

\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}\ni F\mapsto\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\in\mathbb{R}

is not linear, which makes it challenging to derive statistical inference. It appears, however, that when developing statistical inference, the functional can be approximated by the linear one

(3.1)

\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}\ni G\mapsto\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}.

Indeed, we shall see from the following corollaries that the difference between the empirical upper-layer integral
\int_{p}^{1}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
and its population counterpart
\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
gets asymptotically close to the difference between
\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F_{n}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x
and
\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x
, where
F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are cdf’s approaching
F
when the parameter
n
grows indefinitely.

Note 3.1
.

If
F_{n}
is a deterministic cdf, that is,
F_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
, then the meaning of statements like
F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
and
\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F_{n}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}
is clear.
If, however,
F_{n}
is a random cdf, that is,
F_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
, where
\Omega
denotes the sample space, then
F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
means that, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, the deterministic cdf
F_{n}(\omega,\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is an element of
\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
. Likewise,
\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F_{n}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}
means
\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F_{n}(\omega,x)\big)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}
for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
. Furthermore, to enable ourselves to calculate, e.g., the expected values of integrals like
\int_{p}^{1}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
and
\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F_{n}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x
, we need joint measurability of
F_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
. We do not emphasize these subtle properties in the paper because they are automatically satisfied by empirical cdf’s arising from
n
random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
, and all random cdf’s in this paper are of such type. Note in this regard, for example, that
\int_{0}^{1}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\in\mathbb{R}
is equivalent to
(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n})/n\in\mathbb{R}
, and the latter statement is, of course, true almost surely. Nevertheless, it is prudent to keep a watchful eye on all these matters.

The aforementioned corollaries rely on the following fundamental theorem, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
F
and
G
be any two cdf’s from
\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
. Then

(3.2)

\int_{p}^{1}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x-\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G),

where the remainder term

(3.3)

\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)=\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-p\big)\mathrm{d}x

is non-negative, that is,
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)\geq 0
, and satisfies the bounds

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)

\displaystyle\leq\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq\big|G^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(p)\big|\penalty\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|G(x)-F(x)\big|.

Note 3.2
.

The functional
(F,G)\mapsto\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)\in[0,\infty)
is not symmetric. Note that its symmetrization
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)+\mathrm{Rem}(p;G,F)
is equal to the right-hand side of bound (
3.4
), that is,

(3.6)

\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)+\mathrm{Rem}(p;G,F)=\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x.

This equation implies bound (
3.4
) because the remainder term
\mathrm{Rem}(p;G,F)
is non-negative, just like
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)
is.

We are now ready to formulate and discuss several corollaries to Theorem
3.1
. Their formulations will involve approximating sequences
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots
of cdf’s, which may or may not be random, depending on the application. For example, we might be dealing with deterministic “contaminated” cdf’s
F_{n}
given by the equation

F_{n}(x)=(1-\delta_{n})F(x)+\delta_{n}C(x),

where
C
is a (deterministic) cdf and
(0,1)\ni\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
are contamination proportions. An obvious example of random cdf’s would be the empirical cdf

F_{n}(x)={1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\}

based on random variables
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, where
\mathds{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\}
is the indicator that takes value
1
when the statement
X_{i}\leq x
is correct and
0
otherwise. Nevertheless, since every deterministic case is a special (i.e., degenerate) case of the random one, irrespective of the nature of the approximating cdf’s
F_{n}
we can always treat them as random functions, that is, defined on
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
, where
\Omega
is the sample space, given the underlying probability space
\{\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P}\}
. Hence, for example, the requirement
F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
that we shall see in the following corollary will mean that, almost surely,
F_{n}
is an element of the class
\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
, that is, the integral
\int_{p}^{1}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
is finite almost surely, for every value of
p\in(0,1)
.

Corollary 3.1

(consistency)
.

Suppose that
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
, and let
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
be any sequence of cdf’s such that

(3.7)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{n}(x)-F(x)\big|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}0

when
n\to\infty
. Then

(3.8)

\int_{p}^{1}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(F(x)-F_{n}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

and, therefore, the consistency statement

(3.9)

\int_{p}^{1}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

holds if and only if

(3.10)

5
The lower-layer integral

In this section we develop statistical inference for the lower-layer integral
\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
for any fixed upper probability level

p\in(0,1).

Naturally, we assume that the cdf
F
is from the class
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{1}
of those cdf’s for which the integral
\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
is finite, which is the same as to require that the negative part
X^{-}=(-X)\vee 0
of the random variable
X\sim F
has a finite first moment
\mathbb{E}(X^{-})<\infty
.

Note 5.1
.

When progressing through this section, we shall see that the narrative closely follows that of Section
3

We continue our analysis of the lower-layer integral by noting that the functional

\mathcal{F}^{-}_{1}\ni F\mapsto\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\in\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{F}^{-}_{1}\ni G\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}.

6
The middle-layer integral

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the class of all cdf’s.
Fix any pair of probability levels
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
such that

0<p_{1}<p_{2}<1.

The middle-layer integral
\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
is finite for every
F\in\mathcal{F}
. The functional

\mathcal{F}\ni F\mapsto\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\in\mathbb{R}

is not linear, but when developing statistical inference it can be approximated by the linear one

(6.1)

\mathcal{F}\ni G\mapsto\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}.

Indeed, we shall see
from the following corollaries that the difference between the empirical middle-layer integral
\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
and its population counterpart
\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
gets asymptotically close to the difference between the integrals
\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x
and
\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x
, where
F_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are cdf’s approaching
F
when the parameter
n
grows indefinitely. The corollaries rely on the following theorem, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.2
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
F
and
G
be any two cdf’s. Then

(6.2)

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{Rem}(p_{2};F,G)-\mathrm{Rem}(p_{1};F,G),

where the non-negative remainder terms
\mathrm{Rem}(p_{1};F,G)
and
\mathrm{Rem}(p_{2};F,G)
are defined by equation (
3.3
) and satisfy bounds (
3.4
) and (
3.5
).

Note 6.1
.

It is tempting to collapse equations (
1.6
), (
3.2
), (
5.2
), and (
6.2
) into one by relaxing the restriction
0<p_{1}<p_{2}<1
to
0\leq p_{1}<p_{2}\leq 1
. This is indeed possible by augmenting the definition of the remainder term
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)
, which has so far been given only for
p\in(0,1)
, by setting
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)
to
0
when
p\in\{0,1\}
. If we agree with this augmentation, then it is also imperative to replace
F^{-1}(p_{1})
by
-\infty
when
p_{1}=0
in the integral

(6.3)

\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x

on the right-hand side of equation (
6.2
), and to also replace
F^{-1}(p_{2})
by
\infty
when
p_{2}=1
. Note that
F^{-1}(0)
may or may not be equal to
-\infty
, and
F^{-1}(1)
may or may not be equal to
\infty
. Hence, although the noted replacements may look artificial, they are absolutely necessary, as next Example
6.1
shows.
Consequently, the role of infinities

and
\infty
instead of

and

), (

), (

), and (

) in the toolbox, instead of having just one general equation with a number of caveats and adjustments – the four separate equations should help to avoid potential overlooks and pitfalls.

Example 6.1
.

Let
F\sim U_{[0,1]}
and
G\sim U_{[0,2]}
be two random variables with uniform on the intervals
[0,1]
and
[0,2]
distributions, respectively. That is,

when

and
G(x)=x/2
when

. Consequently,

and

when

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\big(G^{-1}(t)-F^{-1}(t)\big)\mathrm{d}t={1\over 2},

\displaystyle\int_{F^{-1}(0)}^{F^{-1}(1)}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x={1\over 4},

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x={1\over 2}.

Hence,

\int_{0}^{1}\big(G^{-1}(t)-F^{-1}(t)\big)\mathrm{d}t=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x,

which is what equation (

\int_{0}^{1}\big(G^{-1}(t)-F^{-1}(t)\big)\mathrm{d}t\neq\int_{F^{-1}(0)}^{F^{-1}(1)}\big(F(x)-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x.

This concludes Example
6.1
.

We are now ready to formulate and discuss several corollaries to Theorem

.

Corollary 6.1

(consistency)
.

Let
F
be any cdf, and let
F_{1},F_{2},\ldots
be any sequence of cdf’s satisfying condition (

). Then

(6.4)

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(F(x)-F_{n}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

(6.6)

\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F_{n}(x)\mathrm{d}x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x.

The corollary follows from Theorem

and bound (

) with

instead of

, because for

is finite and

). This establishes the equivalence of statements (
6.5
) and (

), and concludes the proof of Corollary

.
∎

is the variance of the random variable
Y_{\textsc{middle}}(p_{1},p_{2})
. The variance can be expressed by the formula (see Note
A.1
)

(6.14)

\sigma_{\textsc{middle}}^{2}(p_{1},p_{2})=\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(F(x\wedge y)-F(x)F(y)\big)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Computation 6.1

(SRS)
.

To obtain a convenient computational formula for the empirical middle-layer integral
\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n,\textsc{srs}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
, we can start with the equation

(6.15)

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n,\textsc{srs}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{0}^{p_{2}}F_{n,\textsc{srs}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{0}^{p_{1}}F_{n,\textsc{srs}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

and then apply, for example, computational formula (
5.17
) for the two empirical lower-layer integrals on the right-hand side of equation (
6.15
). (All empirical integrals are finite, and so manipulations like those in equation (
6.15
) do not pose any technical issues.) In particular, we obtain the following equations

\displaystyle\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n,\textsc{srs}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle={1\over n}\sum_{i=\lceil np_{1}\rceil+1}^{\lceil np_{2}\rceil}X_{i\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-\left({\lceil np_{2}\rceil\over n}-p_{2}\right)X_{\lceil np_{2}\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}+\left({\lceil np_{1}\rceil\over n}-p_{1}\right)X_{\lceil np_{1}\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}

\displaystyle={\lceil np_{2}\rceil-\lceil np_{1}\rceil\over n}\underbrace{{1\over\lceil np_{2}\rceil-\lceil np_{1}\rceil}\sum_{i=\lceil np_{1}\rceil+1}^{\lceil np_{2}\rceil}X_{i\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}}_{\textrm{trimmed mean}}

\displaystyle\qquad\underbrace{-\left({\lceil np_{2}\rceil\over n}-p_{2}\right)X_{\lceil np_{2}\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}+\left({\lceil np_{1}\rceil\over n}-p_{1}\right)X_{\lceil np_{1}\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}}_{\textrm{asymptotically negligible terms}}

with the right-hand side connecting the empirical middle-layer integral with the trimmed (on both sides) mean that was considered by
Stigler (1973)
and played a pivotal role in the development of robust statistics, including the introduction of the method of trimmed moments (MTM) by
Brazauskas et al. (2009)
and the method of Winsorized moments (MWM) by
Zhao et al. (2018)
.

Upside tail-value-at-risk

8
Illustrations: stationary time series

Often in applications, data arrive in the form of time series. Depending on the class of time series, and there are many of them, we may see different normalizing constants, different asymptotic distributions, and even in the case of normal asymptotic distributions, we may see different asymptotic variances. Therefore, to illustrate how our general results work on time series, we need to make a choices, and our’s is to work with those time series that are
S
- and
M
-mixing. These two notions of mixing have been introduced by
Berkes et al. (2009)
and
Berkes et al. (2011)
, respectively, and they cover many time series (linear and non-linear). Very importantly, it has also turned out that verifying
S
- and
M
-mixing conditions is often easier than verifying classical mixing conditions.
Berkes et al. (2009)
;
Berkes et al. (2011)
provide illuminating discussions of these matters with accompanying examples and references.

8.1
S
-mixing and statements (
3.23
), (
5.11
) and (
6.10
)

We follow
Berkes et al. (2009)
and say that a time series
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
S
-mixing if it satisfies the following two conditions:

(A)

For any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a random variable
\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}
such that the property

(8.1)

\mathbb{P}\big(|X_{t}-\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}|\geq\gamma_{m}\big)\leq\delta_{m}

holds for some deterministic sequences
\gamma_{m}\to 0
and
\delta_{m}\to 0
.

(B)

For any disjoint intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{r}\subset\mathbb{Z}
of integers and for any positive integers
m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
, the vectors
(\Upsilon_{t}^{(m_{1})},t\in I_{1}),\dots,(\Upsilon_{t}^{(m_{r})},t\in I_{r})
are independent, provided that the separation between the pairs
I_{i}
and
I_{j}
is greater than
m_{i}+m_{j}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq r
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(I_{i},I_{j})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\inf\big\{|a-b|,a\in I_{i},b\in I_{j}\big\}

(8.2)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle m_{i}+m_{j}.

We can clearly see why
S
-mixing has turned out to be such an attractive notion, in particular from the applications point of view: it is based on various portions of the time series
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and not on mathematical constructs such as
\sigma
-algebras, as is the case with many classical mixing notions. The only challenge with
S
-mixing is that one needs to construct random variables
\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}
, but
Berkes et al. (2009)
suggest several recipes for accomplishing this task. To gain intuition on the matter, we next follow one of the recipes and show that the causal autoregressive of order
1
time series is
S
-mixing.

Example 8.1

(AR(1) is
S
-mixing)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a causal AR(1) time series, that is,

X_{t}=\varphi X_{t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}

for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
|\varphi|<1
is a constant and
(\varepsilon_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a mean-zero white noise with finite marginal variances
\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{t})<\infty
. In addition, we assume that
\varepsilon_{t}
’s are iid random variables, thus making
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
strictly stationary. The representation

(8.3)

X_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{i}\varepsilon_{t-i}

holds for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Denote

(8.4)

\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\varphi^{i}\varepsilon_{t-i}.

Condition
(B)
of
S
-mixing is satisfied because the two random vectors

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{s-m_{i}},\dots,\varepsilon_{s}),\quad s\in I_{i},

\displaystyle(\varepsilon_{t-m_{j}},\dots,\varepsilon_{t}),\quad t\in I_{j},

do not overlap and are therefore independent whenever
\mathrm{dist}(I_{i},I_{j})>\max\{m_{i},m_{j}\}
, and so definitely when
\mathrm{dist}(I_{i},I_{j})>m_{i}+m_{j}
. To check condition
(A)
, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(|X_{t}-\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}|\geq\gamma_{m}\big)

\displaystyle\leq{1\over\gamma_{m}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg(\bigg(\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\varphi^{i}\varepsilon_{t-i}\bigg)^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle={\varphi^{2(m+1)}\over\gamma_{m}^{2}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}\over 1-\varphi^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\delta_{m},

where the bound is due to Markov’s inequality.
For example, if set
\gamma_{m}=m^{-a}
for any constant
a>0
, then we have
\delta_{m}=O(m^{-A})
when
m\to\infty
for any constant
A>0
. In fact, for
\gamma_{m}
given above, the decay of
\delta_{m}
is exponential.

The following theorem is a very special case of
Berkes et al. (2009, Theorem 2, p. 1303)
, but it is exactly what we currently need.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a strictly stationary and
S
-mixing time series whose marginal cdf
F
is Lipschitz continuous of order
\theta>0
, that is, there is a constant
c>0
such that

(8.5)

\big|F(x)-F(y)\big|\leq c|x-y|^{\theta}

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, assume that condition
(A)
is satisfied with

(8.6)

\gamma_{m}={1\over m^{A/\theta}}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\delta_{m}=O\bigg({1\over m^{A}}\bigg)

for some
A>4
. Then

(8.7)

\sqrt{n}\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|F_{n,\textsc{ts}}(x)-F(x)\big|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

when
n\to\infty
, where
F_{n,\textsc{ts}}
is the empirical cdf based on the observable portion
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
of the time series
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

Note 8.1
.

If the marginal cdf
F
of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
has a bounded pdf, then condition (
8.5
) is satisfied with
\theta=1
, and so

\gamma_{m}=m^{-A}
in this case.

We can now formulate the following corollaries to Theorem
8.1
.

Corollary 8.1
.

Let all the conditions of Theorem
8.1
be satisfied.
If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
and the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the point
p
, then statement (
3.23
) holds, that is,

(8.8)

\sqrt{n}\,\left(\int_{p}^{1}F_{n,\textsc{ts}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\\
=\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\textsc{upper}}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left(h_{\textsc{upper}}(X)\right)\bigg)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where

h_{\textsc{upper}}(x)=\big(x-F^{-1}(p)\big)^{+}.

The moment condition
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
is sufficient to have
o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
in asymptotic equation (
8.8
), but a stronger moment condition is surely needed to enable the normalized sum on the right-hand side of equation (
8.8
) to converge to a non-degenerate limit. An analogous note applies to the next corollary.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let all the conditions of Theorem
8.1
be satisfied.
If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}_{1}
and the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the point
p
, then statement (
5.11
) holds, that is,

(8.9)

\sqrt{n}\,\left(\int_{0}^{p}F_{n,\textsc{ts}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\\
=\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\textsc{lower}}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left(h_{\textsc{lower}}(X)\right)\bigg)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where

h_{\textsc{lower}}(x)=\big(F^{-1}(p)-x\big)^{+}.

Our final corollary concerns with the middle-layer integral.

Corollary 8.3
.

Let all the conditions of Theorem
8.1
be satisfied.
If the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, then statement (
6.10
) holds, that is,

(8.10)

\sqrt{n}\,\left(\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n,\textsc{ts}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\\
=\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\textsc{middle}}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left(h_{\textsc{middle}}(X)\right)\bigg)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where

h_{\textsc{middle}}(X)=\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-x\big)^{+}-\big(F^{-1}(p_{1})-x\big)^{+}.

Corollary
8.3
does not require any moment assumption because the random variables
X_{i}
are transformed by the function
h_{\textsc{middle}}
that is bounded. Incidentally, note that the three functions
h_{\textsc{upper}}
,
h_{\textsc{lower}}
and
h_{\textsc{middle}}
are Lipschitz continuous of order
1
, which will be an important property in next Section
8.2
.

8.2
M
-mixing and statements (
3.24
), (
5.12
) and (
6.11
)

In view of the results of the previous section, in order to establish asymptotic distributions of the centered and
\sqrt{n}
-normalized integrals of quantiles, i.e., to establish statements (
3.24
), (
5.12
) and (
6.11
), we are left to verify the validity of
statements (
3.25
), (
5.13
) and (
6.12
). That is, in view of Corollaries
8.1
–
8.3
, we need to show

(8.11)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\textsc{upper}}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left(h_{\textsc{upper}}(X)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{upper}}(p),

(8.12)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\textsc{lower}}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left(h_{\textsc{lower}}(X)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}-\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{lower}}(p),

(8.13)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{\textsc{middle}}(X_{i})-\mathbb{E}\left(h_{\textsc{middle}}(X)\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}-\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{middle}}(p_{1},p_{2}),

where the functions

h_{\textsc{upper}}
,
h_{\textsc{lower}}
and
h_{\textsc{middle}}
are defined in Corollaries
8.1
–
8.3
. Obviously, the random variables in the sums need to have at least finite second moments, and the forms of the functions
h_{\textsc{upper}}
and
h_{\textsc{lower}}
translate this requirement into the conditions
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{2}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}_{2}
, respectively. Hence, unlike in Section
8.1
where we worked with indicators and thus employed the probability-based notion of
S
-mixing, we now need to work with a moment-based notion of mixing. This leads us to the notion of
M
-mixing introduced by
Berkes et al. (2011)
.

Namely, following
Berkes et al. (2011)
, we say that a time series
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
is
M_{p}
-mixing for some
p\geq 1
if it satisfies the following two conditions:

(C)

For any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a random variable
\Psi_{t}^{(m)}
such that the bound

(8.14)

\left(\mathbb{E}\big(|X_{t}-\Psi_{t}^{(m)}|^{p}\big)\right)^{1/p}\leq\varrho_{m}

holds for some deterministic sequence
\varrho_{m}\to 0
.

(D)

For any disjoint intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{r}\subset\mathbb{Z}
of integers and for any positive integers
m_{1},\dots,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
, the vectors
(\Psi_{t}^{(m_{1})},t\in I_{1}),\dots,(\Psi_{t}^{(m_{r})},t\in I_{r})
are independent provided that

(8.15)

\mathrm{dist}(I_{i},I_{j})>\max\{m_{i},m_{j}\},

where the definition of
\mathrm{dist}(I_{i},I_{j})
is given in condition
(B)
of
S
-mixing.

Note that condition
(C)
implicitly requires
X_{t}
to have a finite
p^{\textrm{th}}
moment, that is, the cdf of
X_{t}
must be in the class
\mathcal{F}_{p}=\mathcal{F}^{-}_{p}\cap\mathcal{F}^{+}_{p}
. As to the random variable
\Psi_{t}^{(m)}
postulated in the condition,

Berkes et al. (2011)
offer several recipes for constructing it. The following example illustrates the notion and how to verify it.

Example 8.2

(AR(1) is
M_{p}
-mixing)
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be the same AR(1) time series as in Example
8.1
. In addition, assume that
\varepsilon_{t}
’s have (identical) finite
p^{\textrm{th}}
moments
\mathbb{E}(|\varepsilon_{t}|^{p})<\infty
for some
p\geq 2
. Hence, representation (
8.3
) holds. Denote

\Psi_{t}^{(m)}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\varphi^{i}\varepsilon_{t-i},

which is the same as
\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}
defined by equation (
8.4
). (In general,
\Psi_{t}^{(m)}
and
\Upsilon_{t}^{(m)}
do not need to be the same.)
Since
\mathrm{dist}(I_{i},I_{j})>\max\{m_{i},m_{j}\}
, condition
(D)
of
M_{p}
-mixing is satisfied, just like condition
(B)
of
S
-mixing is. To check condition
(C)
, we write

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\big(|X_{t}-\Psi_{t}^{(m)}|^{p}\big)\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\mathbb{E}\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\varphi^{i}\varepsilon_{t-i}\bigg|^{p}\bigg)\Bigg)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big(\big|\varphi^{i}\varepsilon_{t-i}\big|^{p}\big)\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty}|\varphi|^{i}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big(\big|\varepsilon_{0}\big|^{p}\big)\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle={|\varphi|^{m+1}\over 1-|\varphi|}\Big(\mathbb{E}\big(\big|\varepsilon_{0}\big|^{p}\big)\Big)^{1/p}

\displaystyle=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\varrho_{m},

where the bound is due to Minkowski’s inequality.
Hence,
\varrho_{m}=O(m^{-A})
when
m\to\infty
for any constant
A>0
. In fact, the decay of
\varrho_{m}
is exponential.

The following theorem is a very special case of
Berkes et al. (2011, Theorem 1, p. 2445)
, but it is exactly what we need.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a strictly stationary and
M_{p}
-mixing time series for some
p>2
and with

(8.16)

\varrho_{m}=O\bigg({1\over m^{A}}\bigg),

where

(8.17)

A>\max\bigg\{1,{p-2\over 2\eta}\bigg(1-{1+\eta\over p}\bigg)\bigg\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad{1+\eta\over p}<{1\over 2}.

Then, when
n\to\infty
,

(8.18)

\sqrt{n}\,\bigg({1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left(X\right)\bigg)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,\nu^{2})

with the asymptotic variance

\nu^{2}=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}\big(X_{0},X_{h}\big).

Since the functions
h_{\textsc{upper}}
,
h_{\textsc{lower}}
and
h_{\textsc{middle}}
are Lipschitz continuous of order
1
, the three transformed time series
(h_{\textsc{upper}}(X_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
,
(h_{\textsc{lower}}(X_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
(h_{\textsc{middle}}(X_{t}))_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
are
M_{p}
-mixing with the same
p
and
\varrho_{m}
as the original time series
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Furthermore, strict stationarity of
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}}
implies strict stationarity of the three transformed time series. Hence, we can now formulate the following corollary.

Corollary 8.4
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
for some
p>2
, and let the conditions of Theorems
8.1
and
8.2
be satisfied.

(1)

If the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the point
p
, then statement (
8.11
) and, therefore, statement (
3.24
) hold with a limiting mean-zero normal random variable
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{upper}}(p)
whose variance is

\nu^{2}_{\textsc{upper}}=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}\big(h_{\textsc{upper}}(X_{0}),h_{\textsc{upper}}(X_{h})\big).

That is, when
n\to\infty
, we have

\sqrt{n}\,\left(\int_{p}^{1}F_{n,\textsc{ts}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\nu^{2}_{\textsc{upper}}\big).

(2)

If the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the point
p
, then statement (
8.12
) and, therefore, statement (
5.12
) hold with a limiting mean-zero normal random variable
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{lower}}(p)
whose variance is

\nu^{2}_{\textsc{lower}}=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}\big(h_{\textsc{lower}}(X_{0}),h_{\textsc{lower}}(X_{h})\big).

That is, when
n\to\infty
, we have

\sqrt{n}\,\left(\int_{0}^{p}F_{n,\textsc{ts}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\nu^{2}_{\textsc{lower}}\big).

(3)

If the quantile function
F^{-1}
is continuous at the points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
, then statement (
8.13
) and, therefore, statement (
6.11
) hold with a limiting mean-zero normal random variable
\mathcal{L}_{\textsc{middle}}(p)
whose variance is

\nu^{2}_{\textsc{middle}}=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Cov}\big(h_{\textsc{middle}}(X_{0}),h_{\textsc{middle}}(X_{h})\big).

That is, when
n\to\infty
, we have

\sqrt{n}\,\left(\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F_{n,\textsc{ts}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\nu^{2}_{\textsc{middle}}\big).

In the three statements of Corollary
8.4
, the asymptotic variances are complex to estimate empirically, and so practically useful assessments of the variances should probably be done using resampling techniques
(
Lahiri, 2003
;
Kreiss and Paparoditis, 2011
, e.g.)
.

9
L
-functionals

Given what we have established in the previous sections, we may now wish to see if those results could be extended to the class of so-called
L
-functionals, whose definition we recall next. Namely, the
L
-functional
L_{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{F}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
is given by the equation

L_{w}(F)=\int_{0}^{1}F^{-1}(u)w(u)\mathrm{d}u,

where
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is a function. For illustrative “actuarial” examples where
L
-functionals arise, we refer to
Wang (1998)
, and
Jones and Zitikis (2003)
, with the latter paper being perhaps the first one to connect actuarial risk measures with
L
-functionals for the sake of developing statistical inference for the risk measures. To see the role of
L
-functionals in the theory of distorted expectations, which are of particular interest when assessing risks in economics, finance and insurance, we refer to
Dhaene et al. (2012)
. Next is a specimen of additional examples that illustrate the
w
’s that arise in statistics, finance, and insurance.

Example 9.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
be the family of normal (i.e., Gaussian) distributions. To assess the scale parameter when location is known
(e.g.,
Serfling, 1980
, p. 269)
, the
L
-functional
L_{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is used with the weight function

w(u)=\Phi^{-1}(u),

where
\Phi
denotes the standard normal cdf.

Example 9.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{F}
be the family of logistic distributions. To assess the location parameter when scale is known
(e.g.,
Serfling, 1980
, p. 269)
, the
L
-functional
L_{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
is used with the weight function

w(u)=6u(1-u).

Example 9.3
.

The Gini Mean Difference (GMD) is the
L
-functional
L_{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{F}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with the weight function

w(u)=4u-2.

For properties and manifold applications of the GMD in economics and finance, we refer to
Yitzhaki and Schechtman (2013)
.

Example 9.4
.

The Tail Gini
(
Furman et al., 2017
, p. 74)
is the
L
-functional
L_{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{F}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with the weight function

w(u)=\frac{\mathds{1}\{p\leq u<1\}}{(1-p)^{2}}\big(4u-2(1+p)\big),

where
p\in[0,1)
is a parameter.

Example 9.5
.

The Gini Shortfall
(
Furman et al., 2017
, p. 75)
is the
L
-functional
L_{w}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{F}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with the weight function

w(u)=\frac{\mathds{1}\{p\leq u<1\}}{(1-p)^{2}}\bigg(1-p+4\lambda\bigg(u-\frac{1+p}{2}\bigg)\bigg),

where
p\in[0,1)
and
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
are parameters.

To give a flavour of the technical path that is often taken in the literature
(e.g.,
Serfling, 1980
, p. 265)
when deriving large-sample statistical properties of
L
-integrals, we write the equations

\displaystyle L_{w}(G)-L_{w}(F)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\Big)w(u)\mathrm{d}u

(9.1)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(K_{w}\big(F(x)\big)-K_{w}\big(G(x)\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}x

(see Appendix
A.6.4
for technical details), where

K_{w}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}w(u)\mathrm{d}u,

assuming that
w
is integrable on
(0,1)
. Note that equation (
9.1
) collapses into equation (
1.6
) when
w(u)\equiv 1
. With an estimator
F_{n}
instead of
G
, we can now establish asymptotic properties of the empirical
L
-estimator
L_{w}(F_{n})
when
n\to\infty
, but this path requires some form of the Taylor expansion applied on the function
K_{w}
, which, in turn, imposes smoothness conditions on the weight function
w
. Although in some applications such conditions are satisfied, in many other applications they are not.

As a way out of the difficulty, we can successfully use our developed theory for integrated quantiles, and the path toward achieving this goal is analogous to that given by equation (
9.1
), although instead of integrating the weight function
w
as in the definition of
K_{w}
, we integrate the quantile function
F^{-1}
. The following example offers an idea how this goal can be achieved.

Example 9.6

(a proof of concept)
.

For simplicity, let
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
, which means that integrals
\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
are finite for every
p\in(0,1)
, and let the weight function
w
be the difference
w_{1}-w_{2}
of two non-decreasing, right-continuous, and non-negative functions
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
. An example of such a weight function
w
would be
w(u)=6u(1-u)
that appears in Example
9.2
. Hence,

\displaystyle L_{w}(F)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}F^{-1}(u)w_{1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{0}^{1}F^{-1}(u)w_{2}(u)\mathrm{d}u

(9.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\int_{w_{1}^{-1}(x)}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\bigg)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigg(\int_{w_{2}^{-1}(x)}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\bigg)\mathrm{d}x,

with the technical details justifying the right-most equation given in Appendix
A.6.5
. Equation (
9.2
) paves a path that connects the results of Section
3
with asymptotic properties of the difference
L_{w}(F_{n})-L_{w}(F)
, where
F_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is a sequence of cdf’s that approximate
F
when
n
increases. Furthermore, via the results of
Dhaene et al. (2012)
, the equation also facilitates the development of statistical inference for distorted expectations.

The following theorem, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.4
, implements the idea of Example
9.6
under minimal assumptions on
F
and
w
, thus enabling to tackle
L
-functionals that arise from a whole spectrum of applications. To formulate the theorem, we use the notation

L_{w,a,b}(F)=\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)w(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Note that
L_{w}(F)=L_{w,a,b}(F)
when
a=0
and
b=1
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let the weight function
w
be non-decreasing and right-continuous on the interval
(a,b)\subseteq(0,1)
. Then, for every cdf
F
for which
L_{w,a,b}(F)
is finite, we have the representation

(9.3)

L_{w,a,b}(F)=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathds{1}\{w(a)<x\}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b\wedge w^{-1}(x)}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x\\
+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{x\leq w(b)\}\Bigg(\int_{a\vee w^{-1}(x)}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x,

where
w^{-1}
is the left-continuous inverse of
w
.

We see from the right-hand side of equation (
9.3
) that when
a=0
and
b=1
, the
L
-integral
L_{w,a,b}(F)
, which is equal to
L_{w}(F)
, can be expressed in terms of integrated quantiles under the assumption that the weight function
w
is non-decreasing on the entire unit interval
(0,1)
, but this is a rather restrictive assumption in many applications. For this reason, we shall show next that all
w
’s of practical relevance can be expressed in terms of linear combinations of non-decreasing functions on various subintervals of
(0,1)
. This reduces
L_{w}(F)
to a linear combinations of
L
-integrals
L_{w,a,b}(F)
with various non-decreasing weight functions
w
over various intervals
(a,b)\subset(0,1)
. To develop statistical inference for the
L
-integrals
L_{w,a,b}(F)
, we use the equation

L_{w,a,b}(G)-L_{w,a,b}(F)=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathds{1}\{w(a)<x\}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b\wedge w^{-1}(x)}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x\\
+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{x\leq w(b)\}\Bigg(\int_{a\vee w^{-1}(x)}^{b}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x

that follows from Theorem
9.1
. Using appropriate results from Sections
3
,
5
, and
6
, we can now convert integrals of
G^{-1}-F^{-1}
into integrals of
F-G
for which asymptotics with
F_{n}
instead of
G
becomes straightforward.

Example 9.7
.

Consider the weight function

w(u)=6u(1-u),

which we have encountered in Example
9.2
. Obviously, we can decompose this weight function as the difference of
6u
and
6u^{2}
on the entire interval
(0,1)
. Alternatively, we can decompose it as the difference of
-6(1-u)^{2}
and
-6(1-u)
on the entire interval
(0,1)
. However, if we adopt the first decomposition, then we need to assume the finiteness of the moment
\mathbb{E}(X^{+})
, and if we adopt the second decomposition, then we need to assume the finiteness of the moment
\mathbb{E}(X^{-})
. Either way, superfluous conditions arise, which are not required for the finiteness of the
L
-integral
L_{w}(F)
because the original weight function
w(u)
converges to
0
when
u\downarrow 0
and
u\uparrow 1
. For this reason, to reduce the problem to non-decreasing functions, we split the interval
[0,1)
into two parts:
[0,1/2)
where the function
w
is increasing, and
[1/2,1)
where the function
w
is decreasing and thus
-w
is increasing. Hence, we can express
w
as the sum of two differences of non-decreasing functions, and this can be done in several ways. One way is to use the equation

(9.4)

w(u)=\mathds{1}\{0\leq u<1/2\}\Big(w_{11}(u)-w_{12}(u)\Big)+\mathds{1}\{1/2\leq u<1\}\Big(w_{21}(u)-w_{22}(u)\Big)

with the functions

\displaystyle w_{11}(u)=w(u)\quad\textrm{and}\quad w_{12}(u)=0,

\displaystyle w_{21}(u)=0\quad\textrm{and}\quad w_{22}(u)=-w(u).

Another way is to use equation (
9.4
) with the functions

\displaystyle w_{11}(u)=6u\quad\textrm{and}\quad w_{12}(u)=6u^{2},

\displaystyle w_{21}(u)=-6(1-u)^{2}\quad\textrm{and}\quad w_{22}(u)=-6(1-u).

Note that irrespective of the way we use, we always have the bounds

\displaystyle|w_{11}(u)|\vee|w_{12}(u)|\leq c|w(u)|\quad\textrm{for all}\quad u\in[0,1/2),

\displaystyle|w_{21}(u)|\vee|w_{22}(u)|\leq c|w(u)|\quad\textrm{for all}\quad u\in[1/2,1),

with some positive constant
c<\infty
. Therefore, no superfluous moment conditions arise when developing statistical inference for the
L
-integrals based on the four functions
w_{ij}
. This concludes Example
9.7
.

Example
9.7
naturally leads to the following condition, which plays a fundamental role in connecting statistical inference for the
L
-integral
L_{w}(F)
with that for integrated quantiles.

(C
w
)

Let the weight function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
be such that, for some integer
K\geq 1
, there is a partition
0=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{K}=1
of the unit interval
(0,1)
and also pairs
(w_{11},w_{12}),\dots,(w_{K1},w_{K2})
of non-decreasing and right-continuous on
(0,1)
functions such that, for a constant
c<\infty
,

(9.5)

\displaystyle|w_{11}(u)|\vee|w_{12}(u)|\leq c|w(u)|\quad\textrm{in a neighbourhood of $0$},

(9.6)

\displaystyle|w_{K1}(u)|\vee|w_{K2}(u)|\leq c|w(u)|\quad\textrm{in a neighbourhood of $1$},

and

(9.7)

w(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}\{a_{k-1}\leq u<a_{k}\}\Big(w_{k1}(u)-w_{k2}(u)\Big).

To appreciate condition
(Cw)
, which may look somewhat complex at first sight, we observe that if the function
w\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is piecewise monotonic, then the assumption is satisfied. All the weight functions that we have encountered in the literature are piecewise monotonic. In the very special case when
w
is monotonic (i.e., either non-decreasing or non-increasing) on the entire domain
(0,1)
of its definition, condition
(Cw)
is satisfied with
K=1
. The following example illustrates the case
K=2
. Interestingly, we shall see from following Theorem
9.2
, whose proof is constructive and given in Appendix
A.5
, that if condition
(Cw)
holds with
K\geq 3
, then the condition can be reduced to the case
K=2
.

Example 9.8
.

Consider the weight function

w(u)=(1-u)^{\beta}

defined for all
u\in[0,1)
and parameterized by
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The function can be expressed as the difference of two non-decreasing functions as follows:

w(u)=\underbrace{\mathds{1}\{\beta\leq 0\}(1-u)^{\beta}}_{w_{1}(u)}-\underbrace{(-1)\mathds{1}\{\beta>0\}(1-u)^{\beta}}_{w_{2}(u)}.

The two functions
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are continuous and non-decreasing, and satisfy the bound

|w_{1}(u)|\vee|w_{2}(u)|\leq|w(u)|

for all
u\in(0,1)
. That is, for the weight function
w
we do not need to split the interval
(0,1)
into parts, and we can therefore set
K=1
.
This concludes Example
9.8
.

Theorem 9.2
.

If condition
(Cw)
is satisfied with
K\geq 3
, then it is also satisfied with
K=2
.

Theorem
9.2
, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.5
, says that there is a constant
a\in[0,1]
and two pairs
(w_{11},w_{12})
and
(w_{21},w_{22})
of non-decreasing and right-continuous functions such that bounds (
9.5
) and (
9.6
) are satisfied and the representation

(9.8)

w(u)=\mathds{1}\{0\leq u<a\}\Big(w_{11}(u)-w_{12}(u)\Big)+\mathds{1}\{a\leq u<1\}\Big(w_{21}(u)-w_{22}(u)\Big)

holds. Earlier in Example
9.7
, we showed that the case
K=2
cannot be generally reduced to
K=1
. In other words, from the theoretical point of view, it is sufficient to consider only the cases
K=1
and
K=2
. From the practical point of view, this minimality property could sometimes be time consuming to realize, but this is not important: we can always work with any
K\geq 1
that we can easily obtain.

10
Conclusion

We have developed a general statistical inference theory for integrals of quantiles. The required conditions are formulated in terms of general approximating sequences of cdf’s
F_{n}
, which are not attached to any specific sampling design or dependence structure. To maximally illuminate the developed theory, we have illustrated the obtained results using simple random sampling, due to everyone’s familiarity with this sampling design and basic statistical results. In particular, we have discussed consistency, bias, and asymptotic normality of various integrals of quantiles and their combinations that arise in risk and economic-inequality measurements: the upside and downside tail-values-at-risk (
\mathrm{TVaR}
and
\mathrm{TV@R}
, respectively), and the Lorenz and Gini curves. Furthermore, we have illustrated how our general results can be used in the case of time series data. To facilitate the appreciation of theoretical results, we have illustrated some of them numerically. Finally, we have linked the herein developed theory for integrated quantiles with topics such as
L
-integrals.

A.1
Auxiliary lemmas

In this appendix we shall derive formulas for, and establish finiteness of, the first and second moments of the random variables
Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)
and
Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)
. We shall need these results in Appendix
A.2
. The use of Fubini’s theorem and distributional equations
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}F^{-1}(U)
and
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G^{-1}(U)
will permeate our considerations, where
U
is a uniform on
[0,1]
random variable and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution.

Lemma A.1
.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
, then the random variable
Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)
has a finite first moment, and the following equations hold:

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)\big)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x

(A.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u-(1-p)F^{-1}(p).

If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{2}
, then
Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)
has a finite second moment, and formula (
3.27
) holds for the variance of
Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)
.

Proof.

We start with the equations

(A.3)

\displaystyle Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)

\displaystyle=\big(X-F^{-1}(p)\big)^{+}

(A.4)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big(F^{-1}(U)-F^{-1}(p)\big)^{+}.

The first moment of the random variable on the right-hand side of equation (
A.4
) is finite whenever
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{1}
. We can now use Fubini’s theorem to interchange the expectation and integration operations on the right-hand side of the equation

\mathbb{E}\big(Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)\big)=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{F^{-1}(p)}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{X>x\}\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

and in this way arrive at equation (
A.1
) because

\mathbb{E}\big(\mathds{1}\{X>x\}\big)=1-F(x)
.
To prove equation (
A.2
), we write

\displaystyle\int_{F^{-1}(p)}^{\infty}\big(1-F(x))\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{F^{-1}(p)}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{X>x\}\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(F^{-1}(U)-F^{-1}(p)\big)^{+}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(F^{-1}(s)-F^{-1}(p)\big)^{+}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{p}^{1}\big(F^{-1}(s)-F^{-1}(p)\big)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s-(1-p)F^{-1}(p).

To prove the second half of the lemma, we first note that in view of equation (
A.4
), the random variable
Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)
has a finite second moment whenever
F\in\mathcal{F}^{+}_{2}
. To prove equation (
3.27
), we use
Fubini’s theorem and have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{\textsc{upper}}(p)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\mathds{1}\{X>x\}\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\bigg(\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(\mathds{1}\{X>x\}-(1-F(x))\big)\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}-F(x)\big)\big(\mathds{1}\{X\leq y\}-F(y)\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(F(x\wedge y)-F(x)F(y)\big)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

This completes the proof of Lemma
A.1
.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}_{1}
, then the random variable
Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)
has a finite first moment, and the following equations hold:

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}F(x)\mathrm{d}x

(A.6)

\displaystyle=pF^{-1}(p)-\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}_{2}
, then
Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)
has a finite second moment, and formula (
5.15
) holds for the variance of
Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)
.

Proof.

We start with the equations

(A.7)

\displaystyle Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)

\displaystyle=\big(F^{-1}(p)-X\big)^{+}

(A.8)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big(F^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(U)\big)^{+}.

The first moment of the random variable on the right-hand side of equation (
A.8
) is finite whenever
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}_{1}
. We can now use Fubini’s theorem to interchange the expectation and integration operations on the right-hand side of the equation

\mathbb{E}\big(Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)\big)=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

and in this way arrive at equation (
A.5
) because

\mathbb{E}\big(\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}\big)=F(x)
.
To prove equation (
A.6
), we write

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}F(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(F^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(U)\big)^{+}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(F^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(u)\big)^{+}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{p}\big(F^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=pF^{-1}(p)-\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u.

To prove the second half of the lemma, we first note that in view of equation (
A.8
), the random variable
Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)
has a finite second moment whenever
F\in\mathcal{F}^{-}_{2}
. To prove equation (
5.15

Fubini’s theorem and have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(Y_{\textsc{lower}}(p)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\big(\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}-F(x)\big)\big(\mathds{1}\{X\leq y\}-F(y)\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\big(F(x\wedge y)-F(x)F(y)\big)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

This completes the proof of Lemma
A.2
.
∎

A.2
Proofs of Theorems
3.1
,
5.1
, and
6.1

The three theorems deal with arbitrary cdf’s
F
and
G
that, at most, are allowed to satisfy only moment conditions, with no further assumptions. It should be noted at the outset that we may find some portions of the following proofs look like simple consequences of integration-by-parts and change-of-variable formulas of calculus, but this would be mathematically inaccurate because, strictly speaking, the quantile function
F^{-1}
is not an ordinary inverse of the cdf
F
, unless the latter is strictly increasing and continuous, which is not necessarily the case in the context of the present paper. Indeed, keeping in mind that our general results need to include, as a special case, the empirical cdf
F_{n,\textsc{srs}}
, which is a step-wise function and thus has flat regions as well as jumps, good care (recall
Wacker (2023)
) needs to be taken to maintain the largest possible class of cdf’s. Hence, only moment-type assumptions are required, as specified in the formulation of theorems.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

By Lemma
A.1
, we have

(A.9)

\int_{p}^{1}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u=(1-p)F^{-1}(p)+\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x.

Since the same equation also holds with
G
instead of
F
, taking the difference between the two equations gives

(A.10)

\int_{p}^{1}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=(1-p)\big(G^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(p)\big)\\
+\int^{\infty}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x-\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x.

Next we express the penultimate integral on the right-hand side of equation (
A.10
) as follows:

(A.11)

\int^{\infty}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x=\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+\int^{\infty}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x,

where the first integral on the right-hand side is equal to
-\int_{F^{-1}(p)}^{G^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x
when
F^{-1}(p)<G^{-1}(p)
. Combining equations (
A.10
) and (
A.11
), we arrive at equation (
3.2
) with the remainder term

\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)=(1-p)\big(F^{-1}(p)-G^{-1}(p)\big)-\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(1-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x

that can be concisely written as the integral on the right-hand side of equation (
3.3
).

To show that
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)
is non-negative, we proceed as follows. If
F^{-1}(p)\geq G^{-1}(p)
, then
x\geq G^{-1}(p)
, which is equivalent to
G(x)\geq p
, and so

\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)=\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-p\big)\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.

If
F^{-1}(p)<G^{-1}(p)
, then
x<G^{-1}(p)
, which is equivalent to
G(x)<p
, and so

\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)=-\int^{G^{-1}(p)}_{F^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-p\big)\mathrm{d}x\geq 0.

Hence, we always have
\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)\geq 0
.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)

\displaystyle=\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(F(x)-p\big)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x-\mathrm{Rem}(p;G,F)

\displaystyle\leq\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x,

with the last inequality holding because
\mathrm{Rem}(p;G,F)\geq 0
. This establishes upper bound (
3.4
). Bound (
3.5
) follows immediately.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

By Lemma
A.2
, we have the equation

(A.12)

\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u=pF^{-1}(p)-\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}F(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Since the same equation also holds with
G
instead of
F
, taking the difference between the two equations gives

(A.13)

\int_{p}^{1}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=p\big(G^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(p)\big)-\int_{-\infty}^{G^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}F(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Next we express the penultimate integral on the right-hand side of equation (
A.13
) as follows:

(A.14)

\int_{-\infty}^{G^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{F^{-1}(p)}^{G^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x,

where the second integral on the right-hand side is equal to
-\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x
when
F^{-1}(p)>G^{-1}(p)
. Combining equations (
A.13
) and (
A.14
), we arrive at equation (
5.2
) with the remainder term

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rem}(p;F,G)

\displaystyle=p\big(G^{-1}(p)-F^{-1}(p)\big)-\int_{F^{-1}(p)}^{G^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-p\big(F^{-1}(p)-G^{-1}(p)\big)+\int^{F^{-1}(p)}_{G^{-1}(p)}G(x)\mathrm{d}x,

that can be concisely written as the integral on the right-hand side of equation (
3.3
).
This completes the proof of Theorem
5.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

We start with the equation

(A.15)

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u=p_{2}F^{-1}(p_{2})-p_{1}F^{-1}(p_{1})-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x,

which can be verified in many different ways, and whose validity does not require any condition on the cdf
F
. For example, we can prove equation (
A.15
) as follows.

Since
F^{-1}(p_{1})
and
F^{-1}(p_{2})
are finite, the integral
\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x
is finite. With
X
denoting a random variable whose cdf is
F
, and also noting that
X
is equal in distribution to
F^{-1}(U)
, where
U
is a uniform on the interval
[0,1]
random variable, we have the equations

\displaystyle\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg(\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\mathds{1}\{X\leq x\}\mathrm{d}x\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\vee X\big)^{+}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big(\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\vee F^{-1}(U)\big)^{+}\Big)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{1}\vee u)\big)^{+}\mathrm{d}u.

Splitting the integral
\int_{0}^{1}
on the right-hand side into the sum of
\int_{0}^{p_{1}}
,
\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}
and
\int_{p_{2}}^{1}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{p_{1}}\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\big)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=p_{1}\big(F^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\big)+(p_{2}-p_{1})F^{-1}(p_{2})-\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=p_{2}F^{-1}(p_{2})-p_{1}F^{-1}(p_{1})-\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Equation (
A.15
) follows. Of course, the same equation also holds with
G
instead of
F
. Hence,

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=p_{2}\big(G^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{2})\big)-p_{1}\big(G^{-1}(p_{1})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\big)\\
\quad-\bigg(\int_{G^{-1}(p_{1})}^{G^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}F(x)\mathrm{d}x\bigg),

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=p_{2}\big(G^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{2})\big)-p_{1}\big(G^{-1}(p_{1})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad-\bigg(\int_{G^{-1}(p_{1})}^{G^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x\bigg).

Since

\int_{G^{-1}(p_{1})}^{G^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{G^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{1})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{G^{-1}(p_{2})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x,

we have

\displaystyle\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=p_{2}\big(G^{-1}(p_{2})-F^{-1}(p_{2})\big)-p_{1}\big(G^{-1}(p_{1})-F^{-1}(p_{1})\big)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{G^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{1})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{G^{-1}(p_{2})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}G(x)\mathrm{d}x.

Combining the terms on the right-hand side of the latter equation, we obtain

\int_{p_{1}}^{p_{2}}\big(G^{-1}(u)-F^{-1}(u)\big)\mathrm{d}u=-\int_{F^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(G(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x\\
+\int_{G^{-1}(p_{1})}^{F^{-1}(p_{1})}\big(p_{1}-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x-\int_{G^{-1}(p_{2})}^{F^{-1}(p_{2})}\big(p_{2}-G(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x.

This establishes equation (
6.2
) and completes the proof of Theorem
6.1
.
∎

A.3
Asymptotic results

Lemma A.3
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mu\neq 0}_{2}
, and let the quantile function
F^{-1}
be continuous at the point
p\in(0,1)
. Then

(A.16)

\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{LC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{LC}(p)\right)=-{1\over\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(p)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where

Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(p)={1\over\mu}\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\big(\mathds{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\}-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+{\mathrm{LC}(p)\over\mu}\big(X_{i}-\mu\big).

Proof.

We start with the generic equation

(A.17)

{\theta_{n}\over\mu_{n}}-{\theta\over\mu}=\underbrace{{1\over\mu}(\theta_{n}-\theta)-{\theta\over\mu^{2}}(\mu_{n}-\mu)}_{\textrm{main term}}+\underbrace{{\theta\over\mu^{2}\mu_{n}}(\mu_{n}-\mu)^{2}-{1\over\mu\mu_{n}}(\theta_{n}-\theta)(\mu_{n}-\mu)}_{\textrm{remainder term}},

which we next use with the following quantities:

•

\mu
is the mean of
F
,

•

\theta
is the lower-layer integral defined by equation (
2.3
),

•

\mu_{n}
is the arithmetic mean
\mu_{n,\textsc{srs}}
given by equation (
5.8
),

•

\theta_{n}
is the estimator
\theta_{n,\textsc{srs}}=\int_{0}^{p}F_{n,\textsc{srs}}^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u
of
\theta
.

By the classical central limit theorem, we have

\sqrt{n}(\mu_{n,\textsc{srs}}-\mu)=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Statement (
7.7
) implies

\sqrt{n}(\theta_{n,\textsc{srs}}-\theta)=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Consequently, the remainder term on the right-hand side of equation (
A.17
) is of the order
o_{\mathbb{P}}(1/\sqrt{n})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{LC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{LC}(p)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left({\theta_{n,\textsc{srs}}\over\mu_{n,\textsc{srs}}}-{\theta\over\mu}\right)

(A.18)

\displaystyle={1\over\mu}\sqrt{n}(\theta_{n,\textsc{srs}}-\theta)-{\theta\over\mu^{2}}\sqrt{n}(\mu_{n,\textsc{srs}}-\mu)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

By equation (
5.11
) with
F_{n,\textsc{srs}}
instead of
F_{n}
, we have

(A.19)

\sqrt{n}(\theta_{n,\textsc{srs}}-\theta)=\sqrt{n}\,\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\big(F(x)-F_{n,\textsc{srs}}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Combining equations (
A.18
) and (
A.19
), we obtain

\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{LC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{LC}(p)\right)=-{1\over\mu}\sqrt{n}\,\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(p)}\big(F_{n,\textsc{srs}}(x)-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x-{\theta\over\mu^{2}}\sqrt{n}(\mu_{n,\textsc{srs}}-\mu)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

which implies statement (
A.16
) and concludes the proof of Lemma
A.3
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}^{\mu\neq 0}_{2}
, and let the quantile function
F^{-1}
be continuous at the points
p\in(0,1)
and
1-p
. Then

\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{GC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{GC}(p)\right)={1\over\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(1-p)+Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(p)\Big)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where

Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(u)={1\over\mu}\int_{-\infty}^{F^{-1}(u)}\big(\mathds{1}\{X_{i}\leq x\}-F(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x+{\mathrm{LC}(u)\over\mu}\big(X_{i}-\mu\big).

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{GC}(p)

\displaystyle=1-{1\over\mu}\left(\int_{0}^{1-p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{p}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right).

\displaystyle=1-\mathrm{LC}(1-p)-\mathrm{LC}(p).

Hence,

\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{GC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{GC}(p)\right)=-\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{LC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(1-p)-\mathrm{LC}(1-p)\right)-\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{LC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{LC}(p)\right),

and with the help of Lemma
A.3
, we obtain

\sqrt{n}\left(\mathrm{GC}_{n,\textsc{srs}}(p)-\mathrm{GC}(p)\right)={1\over\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(1-p)+Y_{i,\mathrm{LC}}(p)\Big)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

which concludes the proof of Lemma
A.4
.
∎

A.4
Proof of Theorem
9.1

We begin the proof by splitting the integral
L_{w,a,b}(F)
into two parts according to whether
w(u)<0
or
w(u)\geq 0
. This gives us the equation

(A.20)

\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)w(u)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)<0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)\geq 0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u.

We shall next analyze the two integrals on the right-hand side separately, showing that they are equal to the respective integrals on the right-hand side of equation (
9.3
).

Pertaining to the first integral on the right-hand side of equation (
A.20
), since
w
is non-decreasing and right-continuous, we have
w(u)<0

if and only if
u<w^{-1}(0)
, and so

\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)<0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u=\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u<w^{-1}(0)\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u.

Furthermore, since
w(u)<0
, we have

\displaystyle w(u)

\displaystyle=-\int_{w(u)}^{0}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathds{1}\{u<w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}x,

where the last equation holds because we have
w(u)<x
if and only if
u<w^{-1}(x)
, due to our assumption that
w
is non-decreasing and right-continuous.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)<0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u<w^{-1}(0)\wedge w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x

(A.21)

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u<w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x,

where right-most equation holds because
w^{-1}
is non-decreasing.
The double integral on the right-hand side of equation (
A.21
) is zero when
w^{-1}(x)\leq a
. Hence, continuing with equation (
A.21
) for only those
x

a<w^{-1}(x)

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)<0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathds{1}\{a<w^{-1}(x)\}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u<w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{-\infty}^{0}\mathds{1}\{a<w^{-1}(x)\}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b\wedge w^{-1}(x)}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x.

Since
w

a<w^{-1}(x)

w(a)<x
, and so equation (
A.22
) gives the first integral on the right-hand side of equation (
9.3
).

A.20

w(u)\geq 0

Furthermore, since
w(u)\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle w(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{w(u)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{u\geq w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}x,

where the last equation holds because
w(u)\geq x
if and only if
u\geq w^{-1}(x)
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)\geq 0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u\geq w^{-1}(0)\vee w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x

(A.23)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u\geq w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x,

where right-most equation holds because
w^{-1}
is non-decreasing.
The double integral on the right-hand side of equation (

w^{-1}(x)>b
. Hence, continuing with equation (
A.23
) for only those
x
that satisfy
w^{-1}(x)\leq b
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{w(u)\geq 0\}w(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{w^{-1}(x)\leq b\}\Bigg(\int_{a}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathds{1}\{u\geq w^{-1}(x)\}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x

(A.24)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}\{w^{-1}(x)\leq b\}\Bigg(\int_{a\vee w^{-1}(x)}^{b}F^{-1}(u)\mathrm{d}u\Bigg)\mathrm{d}x.

Since
w
is non-decreasing and right-continuous, we have that

x\leq w(b)

A.24
) gives the second integral on the right-hand side of equation (
9.3
). This concludes the proof of Theorem
9.1
.

Proof of Theorem
9.2
.

(Cw)

A.6.2
On the validity of statements (

)-(

Using the definition of the empirical quantile function

) is equivalent to

To prove it, we want to find

such that

(A.27)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}-\frac{1}{2}\right|>\epsilon\right)>0.

We set
\epsilon=1/2
and note that statement (
A.27
) follows if we establish at least one of statements (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), but for completeness of the argument, we shall next establish both of them. Using a well-known formula
(e.g.,
David and Nagaraja, 2003
, eq. (2.1.3))
for the cdf of order statistics, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}>\frac{3}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}\leq\frac{3}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(F_{Z}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\right)^{j}\left(1-F_{Z}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)\right)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-j}

(A.28)

\displaystyle=1-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}<\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}<\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(F_{Z}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)^{j}\left(1-F_{Z}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{j}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-j}

(A.29)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}.

We next rearrange the sums of binomials on the right-hand sides of equations (
A.28
) and (
A.29
). When
n
is even,
\lceil n/2\rceil=n/2
and

can be expressed in

(which is odd) terms

\displaystyle 2^{n}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{0}+\binom{n}{1}+\cdots+\binom{n}{\frac{n}{2}-1}+\binom{n}{\frac{n}{2}}+\binom{n}{\frac{n}{2}+1}+\cdots+\binom{n}{n-1}+\binom{n}{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}

\displaystyle 2^{n}

\displaystyle=2\left(\binom{n}{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}+\cdots+\binom{n}{n-1}+\binom{n}{n}\right).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=\lceil n/2\rceil}^{n}\binom{n}{j}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}+\binom{n}{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+1}+\cdots+\binom{n}{n-1}+\binom{n}{n}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n}>\frac{3}{2}\right)
