Why was the treatment discontinued? Reasons for attrition in evidence-based trauma-focused treatments
Authors: Kristin Alve Glad, Vidar Blokhus Ekroll, Karina Egeland, Harald Bækkelund
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating mental disorder. Whereas effective treatments exist, many studies report high dropout rates.Objective:We aimed to get a better understanding of why evidence-based trauma-focused treatments are discontinued, and factors associated with problematic terminations, in a naturalistic setting.Method:Patients (N = 135) in outpatient mental health clinics, receiving evidence-based trauma-focused treatment, self-reported PTSD symptoms throughout their treatments. Additionally, their therapists were asked to fill out a form stating the reason(s) for treatment termination. Based on these two data sources, we classified each case according to whether the termination appeared to be problematic vs. unproblematic. Separate logistic regression analyses were then run to investigate the association between termination category and relevant variables (i.e. sex, age, baseline psychopathology, number of traumas, treatment type, and therapeutic alliance at last measurement time-point).Results:Based on our categorization, most of the treatment terminations (n = 77, 57%) were deemed unproblematic (i.e. according to treatment plan and/or clinically relevant reduction in symptoms), whereas a significant minority (n = 26, 19%) could indicate problematic terminations (i.e. clinically relevant increase in symptoms, unexplained dropout, and/or above cut-off for PTSD at last measurement time-point). Some of the cases that discontinued treatment earlier than planned could be described as early responders rather than as dropouts. Of the variables included in the logistic regression model, only therapeutic alliance was found to be significantly associated with termination category.Conclusions:This study highlights the need for a more nuanced approach to defining patient dropout. Our results also suggest that therapists may reduce the chance of a problematic trauma-focused treatment termination through securing a strong working relationship with the patient.KEYWORDS: PTSD, evidence-based treatment, treatment termination, dropout, early responder

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a debilitating mental disorder resulting from exposure to trauma, including actual or threatened death, physical injury or sexual violence (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). In a study based on 26 population surveys from across 24 countries, Koenen et al. (2017) found the overall lifetime prevalence of PTSD to be approximately 3.9%. Effective treatments exist and are offered to patients suffering from PTSD. These include treatments such as Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR, Shapiro, 2001), cognitive therapy for PTSD (CT-PTSD, Ehlers et al., 2003), Prolonged Exposure (PE, Foa et al., 2009), and cognitive processing therapy for PTSD (Resick et al., 2016). However, several studies (e.g. Garcia et al., 2011) have reported high dropout rates from such treatments, sparking a debate about whether trauma-focused treatments are unacceptable or intolerable for many patients (Imel et al., 2013; Najavits, 2015).

To date, there is no clear and consistent definition of what constitutes a dropout from psychotherapy. Rather, in the extant literature, a variety of definitions have been used, including discontinued treatment prior to the completion of all sessions (Gros et al., 2013), no contact with the patient for a specific number of months, no future appointments, and no termination summary note (Erbes et al., 2009), or leaving treatment prior to achieving predetermined treatment goals (Garcia et al., 2011). In a recent meta-analysis on predictors of dropout in cognitive behavioural therapy with a trauma focus for PTSD in adults, Wright et al. (2024) found that 27% dropped out (i.e. did not complete the post-treatment assessment). In another recent systematic review and meta-analysis, examining dropout rates from RCTs of psychological therapies for PTSD in adults, Lewis et al. (2020) also chose to take the number of participants who had left the study at the point of post-treatment assessment as a proxy-indicator of dropout. They found that the pooled rate of dropout was 16%. Furthermore, despite some earlier studies failing to find such an association (e.g. Imel et al., 2013), Lewis et al. (2020) found a significantly higher dropout rate for therapies with a trauma-focus. A possible explanation for this could be that trauma-focused psychological therapies rely on challenging interventions, such as confrontation of traumatic images, which can be difficult for patients to tolerate (Pitman et al., 1991; Tarrier et al., 1999), and thus may result in higher potential for dropout (Lewis et al., 2020). On the other hand, of note, evidence also suggests that trauma-focused treatments are more effective than non-trauma-focused treatments (Bisson et al., 2019).

The results in the existing literature on factors related to dropout from trauma-focused treatments are inconsistent. For example, whereas several researchers have found a positive association between dropout from PTSD treatment and younger age (for a meta-analysis, see Wright et al., 2024), treatment type (Imel et al., 2013; Kehle-Forbes et al., 2016; Lewis et al., 2020), depressive symptoms (se Haven et al., 2022), and PTSD symptom severity (Garcia et al., 2011), others have not found such associations (for a meta-analysis, see Varker et al., 2021). Of note, in an RCT study exploring therapeutic alliance in PE treatment, Keller et al. (2010) found that early client-rated alliance was associated with overall treatment completion. In a more recent study, exploring the effect of therapeutic alliance on dropout in cognitive processing therapy for PTSD through a randomized implementation effectiveness trial, Sijercic et al. (2021) found that overall alliance, but neither initial alliance, late alliance, nor change in alliance over time, significantly predicted treatment dropout. According to the authors, given that patients with a weaker therapeutic alliance in general seem to be more likely to drop out of psychotherapy (Sharf et al., 2010), the role of alliance should be explored further in trauma treatments.

As pointed out by Szafranski et al. (2017), few studies have examined symptom change among patients who drop out of trauma-focused treatment. This may be partly due to the lack of available data (e.g. only pre- and post-treatment data for treatment completers). Interestingly, however, in a randomized controlled study, testing a modified cognitive processing therapy (MCPT) intervention among interpersonal trauma survivors diagnosed with PTSD, where the number of sessions was determined by patient progress, Galovski et al. (2012) found that 58% of the 50 patients who completed the intervention reached the end-state criteria (i.e. PTSD and depression symptoms below a predetermined cutoff) prior to the full standard 12-session treatment protocol, and thus terminated the treatment. Of note, the authors found that these early completers demonstrated maintenance of treatment benefits at a 3-month follow-up. While preliminary, these findings suggest that a substantial portion of patients may not need to receive the full protocol in order to benefit significantly from the treatment. Even though it has been suggested that early termination in psychotherapy may be associated with risk of deterioration after treatment (Ekroll & Ronnestad, 2018), Schottenbauer et al. (2008) note ‘it is possible that some patients feel that they have improved enough from partial treatment and may drop out not due to failure, but to success’ (p. 139). Relatedly, Szafranski et al. (2017) have argued that it is possible that a portion of dropout from PTSD treatment is related to improvement in symptoms. In line with this, in a more recent RCT study exploring PTSD and depression symptom change in female civilians with PTSD who discontinued psychotherapy, they (ibid.) found that a considerable proportion of the dropout participants (35–55%) displayed clinically significant improvement and/or met good end-state criteria for PTSD or depression. The authors argued that these patients should not be considered dropouts, but that they would be better defined as early treatment responders.

In sum, many patients discontinue their evidence-based trauma-focused treatment before the end of their treatment protocols – and it is critical that we learn more about why (e.g. Lewis et al., 2020; Varker et al., 2021). As argued by Szafranski et al. (2017), for some, treatment discontinuation may be relatively unproblematic, for example, if the patient is an early responder. For others, it may be more problematic, for example, if it is caused by treatment intolerance. To date, few studies have included temporally sensitive measures of outcome, and to the best of our knowledge, no one has asked therapists to report their perception of reasons for PTSD treatment discontinuation. Since patients are often hard to reach after they have left the study, therapist reports, as well as more frequent measures of outcome, could provide us with important knowledge in this regard.

The present study: context and aims

In a recent study on the implementation of evidence-based trauma-focused treatments in naturalistic therapy settings in Norway, we found that very few patients reported an increase in symptoms during treatment, which suggests that the treatments were, in general, well tolerated (Bækkelund et al., 2022). That said, we also found that more than 50% of the recruited patients did not fill out end-of-treatment measurements. Unfortunately, due to a lack of ethical approval, we were prohibited from then asking the therapists why these treatments were terminated, which made it difficult to fully assess the safety and acceptability of the treatments. In subsequent data collections leading up to the present study, however, we applied for and received ethical approval to assess why, according to the therapists, the treatments were terminated.

The overarching aim of the current study was to contribute to a refined understanding of attrition from evidence-based trauma-focused treatments. More specifically, we wanted to gain a better understanding of why patients’ trauma-focused treatment is discontinued, by incorporating the therapists’ point of view as well as data on client-reported outcome. Furthermore, we wanted to identify clinical characteristics that may predict seemingly unproblematic terminations (i.e. according to the treatment plan and/or symptom improvement) vs. problematic terminations (e.g. symptom deterioration, dropout).

Method

The present study is based on data from an ongoing implementation of evidence-based trauma-focused treatments in Norwegian mental health services for adults. The implementation programme is initiated and financed by the Norwegian Ministry of Health and Care Services and led by the Norwegian Centre for Violence and Traumatic Stress Studies (NKVTS). The data for the present study were collected between 2020 and 2022, during the second and third rounds of implementation in 22 different clinics all over Norway. The clinics are regular outpatient clinics, which are part of the specialist health service in Norway. Of note, the clinics have volunteered to participate in the implementation project, based on an expressed need for increased knowledge about trauma treatment. The therapists in the current study (n = 60; 76.7% female, Mage 46.7; SD = 11.3), were largely psychologists (n = 37), but some were nurses (n = 11), social workers (n = 5), medical doctor (n = 1), or other (n = 6), with specialization in mental health. They had worked as a therapist for 11 years on average (SD = 9), and had been at their current workplace 5.7 years (SD = 7). The therapists were taught one of two evidence-based treatments for PTSD: CT-PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Ehlers & Wild, 2015) or EMDR (Shapiro, 2001; Shapiro & Laliotis, 2015) (for more information on the implementation project, see Bækkelund et al., 2022).

Participants and procedures

Patients were recruited by therapists as training-cases in public outpatient clinics, based on therapists' judging them to be candidates for trauma-focused therapy, as well as patients wanting to receive such treatment and to participate in the project. No exclusion criteria were specified, and the only inclusion criterion was that the patient had received a clinical diagnosis of PTSD, according to the ICD-10 criteria, which are used in Norwegian health services. The diagnosis was made by their therapist, before starting the trauma-focused treatment. Therapists were not required to conduct a structured diagnostic interview, but they were provided with screening tools for trauma history and PTSD symptoms (see Measures). Further, all therapists participated in a three-hour-long workshop on trauma and how to diagnose PTSD. They were also instructed on the scoring of the screening measures to diagnose PTSD, including evaluation of trauma and symptom criteria. The therapists received a project iPad (for data collection, supervision, and recording of sessions). All data was stored on a secure server for sensitive data at the University of Oslo. Patients provided informed consent and were encouraged to fill in self-report measures using the iPad before each treatment session. For more details on this study, see Bækkelund et al., (2022). All procedures were approved by the Norwegian Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (139026) and Data Protection Officials at each hospital.

A total of 330 patients were recruited, whereof we had therapists’ reports on reason(s) for treatment termination on 135 (41%). The latter group formed the participants in the present study (for demographics information, see Table 1). There were no significant differences in PTSD scores between included vs. excluded patients, neither at baseline, nor with regard to the amount of change during treatment.

Measures

Reasons for treatment termination. A 10-item checklist was developed for use in the present study, to assess why, according to the therapists, the PTSD treatment was terminated, including ‘according to the treatment plan’, ‘due to deterioration’, and ‘dropout’ (see Table 2). The checklist was developed by the project group, based on feedback from the therapists involved in the implementation project. The therapists could tick off for one or more reason(s) for the treatment termination of each patient.

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms during the last month were assessed using the Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5; Weathers et al., 2013). The therapists were instructed to deliver the PCL-5 before each treatment session, from the first session. PCL-5 is a self-report questionnaire, consisting of 20 items measuring PTSD symptoms, on a 5-point scale ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘extremely’). It has been recommended to use a change of 10 points as a minimum threshold for determining whether an individual has responded to the treatment in a clinically meaningful way, and a cut-off level of 31–33 (we used 32) as a provisional indication of diagnosis (Weathers et al., 2013). The sum score was calculated using all 20 items. There were no missing data on single items. When patients terminated therapy, we used their last available PCL-5 score as an end score. The Norwegian translation of the PCL-5 has previously shown good psychometric properties (Hem et al., 2012). Cronbach's alpha in the present study was .854.

Disturbances in self-organization (DSO) was measured at the first session, using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ, Cloitre et al., 2018). The ITQ is a 12-item self-report measure, assessing symptoms of ICD-11 PTSD and complex PTSD (CPTSD) during the last month. Items are scored on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (‘not at all’) to 4 (‘extremely’). In the present study, we used six items from the ITQ, measuring the three core elements of DSO (i.e. difficulty in interpersonal relationships, negative self-concept, emotion dysregulation). The presence of a symptom is indicated with a Likert score ≥2 (i.e. ‘sometimes’).

General anxiety disorder (GAD) symptoms over the last two weeks were measured using the GAD-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006) at the first session. This is a widely used short self-report questionnaire, consisting of seven items which are scored on a 4-point scale, ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’). The sum score was calculated using all seven items. Cronbach's alpha in the present study was .841.

Depressive symptoms over the last two weeks were measured using the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al., 2001) at the first session. This brief self-report instrument consists of nine items scored on a 4-point scale, ranging from 0 (‘not at all’) to 3 (‘nearly every day’), indicating the frequency of depressive symptoms. The sum score was calculated using all nine items. Cronbach's alpha for the PHQ-9 in the present study was .823.

The Working Alliance Inventory-Short Revised (WAI-SR; Hatcher & Gillaspy, 2006) was used to measure the therapeutic alliance. The therapists were instructed to deliver the WAI-SR to their patients every session, from the second session. WAI-SR is a 12-item self-report questionnaire which measures Bordin’s (1979) Bond, Task and Goal dimensions (four items, respectively). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘never’) to 5 (‘always’). The mean score was calculated using all 12 items. A mean score for each of the three aspects of the therapeutic alliance was also calculated. Cronbach's alpha for the total WAI-SR in the present study was .947, whereas the Cronbach's alpha was .887 for bond, .956 for task, and .884 for goal.

The stressful life events screening questionnaire (SLESQ; Goodman et al., 1998). Lifetime exposure to potentially traumatizing events was measured with a revised version of the SLESQ at the first session. Patients were asked about exposure to 14 different potentially traumatizing events (‘yes’ or ‘no’), including natural disaster, sexual abuse, and interpersonal violence. A 15th item was included to allow the respondents to specify other frightening or horrific experiences they had experienced. The sum score was calculated using all 15 items. Only cases with no missing answers were included.

Type of treatment. The participants received one of two evidence-based treatments for PTSD: CT-PTSD (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Ehlers & Wild, 2015) or EMDR (Shapiro, 2001; Shapiro & Laliotis, 2015). All sessions were in-person. Up to five random recordings of sessions for each therapist were scored for fidelity by trained raters (Author et al., 2022). For EMDR, the raters used an EMDR-specific treatment integrity checklist (16 items), previously used in other EMDR studies (e.g. Covers et al., 2021; Meentken et al., 2021). To evaluate CT-PTSD: A Checklist of Therapist Competency (i.e. an adapted version of the Revised Cognitive Therapist Scale; Blackburn et al., 2001) was used.

Analyses

We wanted to use all relevant information to categorize the patients’ treatment terminations in a way that would be both meaningful with regard to the aims of the study and as realistic as possible. We considered different ways of categorizing, before settling with the one that had the best fit with our data, enabling most cases to be classified according to informative termination categories, without stretching interpretations too far. We allocated cases to categories by using a stepwise procedure, where we gave the highest priority to the information we considered to be most credible, namely, patient-reported outcome. Thus, we started out by categorizing all included cases showing either clinically meaningful improvement (≥10 points decrease) or deterioration (≥10 points increase) on the PCL-5. We classified the former as belonging to an overarching ‘unproblematic’ termination category, as we do not consider termination after clinically meaningful improvement a failure, even when this happens before a planned ending point. These cases were also placed in a subcategory called ‘symptom reduction’, which was further specified as ‘planned’ vs. ‘early responder’, referring to whether the termination happened according to the treatment plan or before. Cases showing clinically meaningful deterioration on PCL-5 were allocated to a corresponding overarching termination category called ‘problematic’, and to a subcategory called ‘deterioration’. After this, we proceeded to categorize remaining (i.e. still uncategorized) cases by drawing more on information from the therapists’ reports about reasons for termination (for an overview of therapist reports, see Table 2). We placed so far uncategorized cases, where therapists had reported termination to be according to plan (without patients reporting deterioration), in a subcategory called ‘planned’, under the ‘unproblematic’ termination category. Then, we classified remaining cases where therapists reported termination being a result of the therapist quitting, or the patient relocating, as belonging to a termination category called ‘external factors’, and subcategories of ‘therapist quit’ and ‘patient relocates’ accordingly. We deemed these terminations as reflecting more random factors outside of the treatments, rather than whether the treatment was experienced as tolerable or not. Furthermore, we classified remaining cases that were reported by therapists to be dropouts as ‘problematic’, and placed them under a subcategory called ‘dropout (unexplained)’. In a final step, we classified still remaining cases, where therapists had reported termination being according to the patient's wish, while the patient at the same time reported symptoms above cut-off for PTSD on PCL-5, as ‘problematic’ and under a subcategory called ‘patient quits with PTSD’. Cases that lacked sufficient information to be placed in either one of the mentioned categories were classified as ‘unknown’.

Our procedure allowed us to classify most cases according to specific termination categories, called ‘unproblematic’, ‘problematic’, and ‘external reason’, while a minority remained classified as ‘unknown’. Stepwise allocation according to priority ensured that each case ended up in only one category, even if therapists in some cases had selected several reasons for termination. Discussion

The aims of the present study were to get a better understanding of why evidence-based trauma-focused treatments are discontinued and to identify factors associated with problematic terminations. In brief, when therapists’ reports of reasons for ending treatment were combined with patient-reported symptom levels, we found that most (57%) of the terminations appeared to be unproblematic, whereas a significant minority (19%) of the cases could indicate problematic terminations. Furthermore, we found that the strength of the therapeutic alliance was significantly related to therapy termination, whereas neither patient characteristics nor the type of treatment showed a significant association.

According to the therapists, the most common reasons for termination from these evidence-based trauma-focused treatments were ‘according to the treatment plan’ and ‘symptom reduction’. In line with this, when also including patient-reported outcome scores, we found that most of the treatment terminations appeared to be unproblematic (i.e. according to treatment plan and/or clinically meaningful reduction in symptoms). Interestingly, whereas about two-thirds of the unproblematic terminations were reported to be ‘planned’ by the therapists, almost one-third were not. Although the latter were not ‘planned’, these patients did report clinically meaningful improvement (i.e. ≥ 10 points decrease on PCL-5), suggesting that they may be ‘early responders’. By using a less nuanced classification procedure, as has been the case in most earlier studies, such cases would rather have been classified as ‘dropouts’. While preliminary, these findings add to existing literature suggesting that a significant proportion of unplanned terminations might entail early treatment response. This could also indicate that a substantial portion of trauma patients do not need to receive the full protocol to benefit significantly from their treatment (e.g. Galovski et al., 2012; Szafranski et al., 2017). Of note, however, some researchers have warned that premature terminations might entail enhanced risk of later deterioration (e.g. Ekroll & Ronnestad, 2018). Future studies, using follow-up measures after termination of therapy, could help illuminate this question further.

Whereas a few early terminations inevitably were related to external factors, a significant minority (19%) of cases showed signs of what could be interpreted as problematic terminations (i.e. clinically relevant increase in symptoms, unexplained dropout, and/or above cut-off for PTSD at last measurement time-point), potentially associated with the therapy provided. These seem to be the cases to target when working towards understanding treatment intolerance and preventing unwanted loss of patients. It is worth noting that only 6% of our cases were considered unexplained dropouts. This is very low compared to results from recent meta-analysis in the field, with pooled dropouts ranging from 16% to 27% (Lewis et al., 2020; Wright et al., 2024). A likely reason for this discrepancy is that we managed to categorize treatment discontinuation in a more nuanced fashion than what has usually been done in the literature, where a rather heterogenous group of cases have often been chunked together as ‘dropout’. In the future, we would encourage others to do the same.

In terms of factors associated with problematic terminations, we found that client-rated therapeutic alliance was significantly associated with termination category. More specifically, clients who reported a low therapeutic alliance at the last measurement were more likely to have a problematic termination compared to patients who reported a strong alliance at the last measurement. Based on this, in line with Keller et al. (2010), we believe that clinicians may find it useful to routinely assess alliance throughout treatment, so that weak or damaged alliances can be addressed. On the other hand, we neither found sex, age, baseline psychopathology, number of traumas, nor treatment type, to be significantly associated with termination category. Thus, our findings suggest that it is difficult to know pre-treatment, based on patient characteristics or the type of trauma-focused treatment offered (i.e. CT-PTSD vs. EMDR), which patients will be at risk of having problematic terminations. Rather, monitoring and working towards building and sustaining a good alliance during therapy shows more promise as a potential route towards reducing problematic terminations. This can either entail creating a stronger bond, ensuring agreement on treatment tasks, ensuring agreement on treatment goals, or a combination of these. In line with this, in a relatively recent study, Beierl et al. (2021) found that higher ratings in the total working alliance score, and all three subscales, as rated by both patients and therapists, were predictive of better outcomes at the end of CT-PTSD treatment. The authors concluded that a good alliance from the beginning may be an important ingredient for effective trauma treatment. Of note, however, Beierl et al. also found a reciprocal relationship between working alliance and PTSD symptom change. More specifically, their results suggested that the therapists’ ratings of the alliance predicted symptom change but were also in itself influenced by their patients’ prior symptom change. In sum, this supports the relevance of the working alliance as both a predictor and a dynamic process during therapy. Interestingly, DeRubeis and Feeley (1990) found that in cognitive therapy of depression, observer-rated early-session alliance did not predict symptom change, but later in the treatment, symptom reduction predicted subsequent alliance ratings. In other words, they found the working alliance to be predicted by, but not predictive of, therapeutic change. Whereas this study was based on a relatively small sample (25 adult outpatients), it does call into question assumptions of causality in the alliance-outcome relationship and highlights the importance of viewing the alliance as a potentially bidirectional process. That is, it is possible that patients’ ongoing experiences of symptom improvement – or lack thereof – influence both their perception of the alliance and their decision to remain in or leave treatment. In line with this, in a recent study exploring working alliance in three different variants of PE among patients with PTSD following childhood abuse, Oprel et al. (2024) concluded that ‘the relationship between alliance and treatment outcome in exposure-based treatments for PTSD is not direct or linear but may be more complicated and may represent some form of a reciprocal effect’ (p. 405). Along these lines, it is possible that the association we found in the current study, between alliance late in the therapy process and treatment termination category may, at least partly, be explained by the patient's improvement.

Study strengths and limitations

This study adds to the existing literature in several ways. It provides a fine-grained description of reasons for termination in evidence-based trauma-focused treatments, incorporating information from both therapists and patients. Furthermore, it is one of the few studies exploring self-reported psychopathology in clients who quit trauma-focused treatment early, and the first to do so in a naturalistic setting. Other strengths are that we did not have any exclusion criteria, which implies that our sample was quite typical of clinical samples seeking treatment for PTSD, and constituted a large number of participants. That said, the study findings should be interpreted considering certain limitations. First, of the more than 300 patients recruited to the larger implementation study, we had therapists’ reports on reasons for ending treatment for less than half. As such, even though these samples were comparable in symptom levels and symptom change, we do not know for sure how representative the reports are for the entire sample. Second, we did not ask the therapists what they believed to be the main reason for the treatment termination, which might have made it easier to interpret the results. Third, many therapists reported ‘other’ as the reason for treatment termination. Unfortunately, these were not given the opportunity to state what these other reasons were. As such, 19% of our sample was placed within an ‘unknown’ termination category, rather than being allocated to more specific categories. Fourth, given that this was an observational study, the true cause of the treatment termination cannot be determined. Finally, it is worth noting that no formal diagnostic procedure to diagnose PTSD was used, and assessment of symptoms was based on self-report only.

Clinical implications and future directions

The present study's findings have important implications. For example, several treatment outcome studies examine symptoms only at the start of treatment as well as at the time of a planned termination, and define dropout as patients who begin, but do not complete, the full treatment protocol (e.g. Gros et al., 2013; Imel et al., 2013). Hence, as noted by Szafranski et al. (2017), and supported by our findings, it is possible that several patients are classified as dropouts, despite improving significantly during treatment. To address these methodological challenges, researchers could administer brief symptom measures, at regular intervals, throughout the treatment (see, e.g. Galovski et al., 2012; Resick et al., 2008). Such data could be used actively during treatment, for example, to pick up and act on signs of deterioration or, as noted by Szafranski et al. (2017), as an indication of whether treatment can be discontinued prior to the predetermined final session of the protocol. The latter can also have implications regarding the classification of dropout, as a subset of patients may not need to receive the full protocol to benefit significantly from the treatment (Galovski et al., 2012). In other words, perhaps some patients need and want less treatment (i.e. they leave when they have reached satisfactory improvement), and would more accurately be classified as early treatment responders. That said, it is also possible that patients who evidence sharp symptom reductions early in treatment, but nevertheless choose to complete the full protocol, may display even greater (Clapp et al., 2016), and possibly longer lasting (e.g. Ekroll & Ronnestad, 2018) symptom reduction. If so, it could still be right for clinicians to focus on motivating patients to stay until the end, even though they experience early symptom improvement. Nevertheless, we would encourage researchers to use fine-grained categories, enabling a more nuanced picture, when describing treatment discontinuation.

Our results also suggest a link between alliance quality and termination category. This implies that therapists might actively reduce the chance of trauma-focused treatments ending with a problematic termination, through securing a strong bond, and/or agreement on treatment goals and tasks, during their therapies.

All in all, in line with Szafranski et al. (2017), we believe that obtaining regular structured feedback about symptom change, and alliance quality, could aid therapists in efforts to reduce rates of problematic terminations, as well as in identifying patients who do not need full treatment protocols. The latter could possibly contribute to reducing waitlists in mental healthcare by eliminating unnecessary sessions (Szafranski et al., 2017). That said, as noted by Szafranski et al. (2017), early responders should ideally also be monitored for some time after treatment, to ensure that their gains are maintained and that their symptoms do not revert to baseline levels.
