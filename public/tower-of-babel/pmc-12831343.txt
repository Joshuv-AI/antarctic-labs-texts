First report on detection and molecular characterization of alphacoronaviruses in the small Indian mongoose (Urva auropunctata)
Authors: Jessica L. Kulberg, Anne A. M. J. Becker, Yashpal S. Malik, Souvik Ghosh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMembers of the species Alphacoronavirus suis/Alphacoronavirus-1 (αCoV-1) are important viral pathogens of canids/felids that exhibit complex evolutionary patterns and have been associated with interspecies transmission events including zoonoses. Mongooses (family Herpestidae, order Carnivora) are scavengers that pose a risk as potential carrier of viral pathogens. To date, there are no reports on the genetic make-up/diversity of CoVs circulating in mongoose populations.MethodsFecal samples obtained from 53 small Indian mongooses (Urva auropunctata) on the Caribbean island of St. Kitts were screened for CoVs using a pan-CoV RT-semi-nested PCR assay and canine αCoV-specific RT-PCR assays. The partial RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) and membrane protein (M) coding sequences (CDS) and the nearly full-length spike (S) protein CDS were determined from the mongoose CoVs and analyzed in the present study.ResultsWe report here high detection rates of αCoVs (30.18%, 16/53 samples) in small Indian mongooses on St. Kitts, indicating that αCoVs might be widely circulating in the island mongoose population. Analysis of the CoV RdRp-, M- and S- CDS revealed significant genetic diversity among the mongoose CoVs, and between mongoose CoVs and other αCoVs, including evidence for at least 2 recombination events (involving S gene), one of which involved the putative receptor binding domain (RBD). Phylogenetically, the mongoose CoVs formed distinct cluster/s within the canine CoV-2 (CCoV-2) lineage and appeared to be more related to CCoV-2b than other αCoVs. Despite differences in the S CDS including those in the RBD, the mongoose CoVs preserved certain features that are characteristic of CCoV-2 (lack of furin cleavage motif at S1/S2 site and presence of cleavage motif at S2’ site) and retained the crucial amino acid residues essential for αCoV binding to the host aminopeptidase N receptor.ConclusionsThe present study is the first to report high detection rates and genetic makeup/diversity of CoVs in mongooses, expanding our knowledge on the host range and complex evolutionary patterns of αCoVs. Considering our findings, the proximity of mongoose to other canids/felids and humans, and cross-species transmission potential of CoVs, large-scale studies on prevalence and genetic diversity of CoVs that might be circulating in different mongoose species/populations, and in-depth investigation of mongoose CoV RBD-host receptor interactions are of utmost importance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-03050-9.

Background

Coronaviruses (CoVs) (family Coronaviridae) are enveloped viruses with a single-strand positive-sense RNA genome (~ 22–36 kb in size) [1, 2]. Among the major CoV structural proteins, the spike (S) protein is responsible for virus attachment and membrane fusion and is a major inducer of host immune responses [1–3]. The S protein consists of two distinct domains, the highly variable S1 subunit (that binds to specific host cellular receptors) and the S2 subunit (mediates virus envelope-cell membrane fusion) [3–5]. The CoV membrane (M) protein is the most abundant of CoV structural proteins and plays a key role in viral assembly and morphogenesis [2, 6]. Among the CoV nonstructural proteins, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) is crucial for transcription and replication of viral genomes [7], and due to its conserved nature, constitutes an important basis (along with CoV 3CLpro, NiRAN, ZBD and HEL1) for the taxonomic classification within the family Coronaviridae [1, 8].

Members of the CoV species Alphacoronavirus suis (formerly known as Alphacoronavirus-1 (αCoV-1)) (subgenus Tegacovirus, genus Alphacoronavirus, subfamily Orthocoronavirinae) include canine CoVs (CCoVs), feline CoVs (FCoVs) and porcine CoVs (transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV) and porcine respiratory CoVs (PRCVs)), which are important animal pathogens [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of CCoVs have been reviewed elsewhere [9]. Based on differences in the S gene, CCoVs have been classified into two genotypes, CCoV-1 and − 2 [9–11]. CCoV-2 consists of reference/classical CCoV strains and have been widely reported since the 1970 s [9–11]. The emergence of TGEV-like CCoVs through TGEV-CCoV recombination events involving the very 5’ end of S gene have resulted in further classification of the CCoV-2 genotype into two subtypes: CCoV-2a (including classical CCoVs) and − 2b (consisting of TGEV-like CCoVs) [9–11]. More recently, phylogenetic analysis of CCoV genomes identified a third CCoV-2 subtype, tentatively designated as CCoV-2v/c, that might have been derived from recombination events between CCoV-1 and − 2 [9, 12, 13]. CCoVs have been shown to be involved in the origin of FCoV-2 (recombination events involving FCoV-1 and CCoV-2) and TGEV (through cross-species transmission of CCoV) [9–11]. Recombination events between CCoV and FCoV have resulted in emergence of hypervirulent αCoVs in domestic dogs and cats [9–11, 14]. Recently, novel CCoV-FCoV recombinant αCoVs have been reported in humans with clinical illness, revealing the zoonotic potential of CCoVs/FCoVs [15, 16]. Taken together, these observations highlight the complex evolutionary pathways (driven by mutations and recombination events) and cross-species transmission potential of CCoVs/FCoVs, resulting in emergence of viruses with altered antigenicity, virulence, and/or host/tissue tropism [9–11, 14–16].

The efficient binding of virus to their host receptor is a crucial determinant of CoV host range and cross-species infection [2, 3]. The S protein receptor binding domain (RBD) of CCoVs, FCoVs and TGEV/PRCVs bind to the host aminopeptidase N (APN) receptor for virus entry into host cells [2, 5, 17–19]. Within the RBD domain, 19 amino acid (aa) residues have been proposed to directly interact with the APN, of which 5 aa residues (R540, G542, Y543, Q545, and W586 in CoV strain HuPn-2018) are crucial for virus binding to APN and conserved among CCoVs, FCoVs and TGEV/PRCVs [17–19]. On the other hand, 23 aa residues in the porcine APN have been shown to directly interact with the PRCV RBD, of which N736, W737 and T378 (the 3 aa residues form a N-linked glycosylation site) are essential for αCoV attachment to host receptor [17, 18, 20]. Interestingly, most of these aa residues, including the 3 essential residues, are conserved in the APNs of domestic and wild carnivores [17, 21]. In-vitro studies have revealed that the domestic cat APN can serve as a receptor for cell entry by CCoV-2, FCoV-2, TGEV and PRCV [22], whilst recently, CCoV-FCoV recombinant viruses have been shown to bind to human APN [19]. These observations indicate that a wide range of host species might be susceptible to CCoV/FCoVs, warranting surveillance studies in wild carnivores [17, 21]. To date, CCoV/CCoV-like viruses have been detected in various wildlife species (African wild dogs, coyotes, Eurasian badgers, foxes, giant pandas, hyenas, jackals, racoons, snow leopards, a tiger and wolves) with/without clinical disease [17, 21, GenBank MN719179 and PV259885].

Mongooses (family Herpestidae, suborder Feliformia, order Carnivora) are small mammals that exhibit scavenging behavior and have been shown to stray into habitats of humans and domestic animals, posing a risk as potential carriers of important viral pathogens, such as rabies virus, hepatitis E virus, Carnivore protoparvovirus 1 and feline panleukopenia virus [23–28]. Among the 34 mongoose species reported so far [29], to date, CoVs have been detected in a single Egyptian mongoose (Herpestes ichneumon) [28], revealing the lack of information on prevalence and genetic make-up/diversity of CoVs that might be circulating in various mongoose species/populations.

The Caribbean Island of St. Kitts has a sizeable population (estimated to be > 45,000) of a single species (the small Indian mongoose, Urva auropunctata) of free-roaming mongooses that were introduced circa 1884 to control rats in sugarcane plantations [24, 30, 31]. The small Indian mongoose is considered an invasive species in St. Kitts that thrive in urban and wild habitats and come in close contact with humans, companion animals (cats and dogs), livestock and other wildlife species (African green monkeys, bats, reptiles and wild birds) [24, 31, 32]. Small Indian mongooses have been recognized as the primary reservoir of rabies virus in at least four Caribbean islands [25]. Although there are no reports on mongoose rabies from St. Kitts, various novel viruses (adenoviruses, astroviruses, circoviruses, cycloviruses, and picobirnaviruses) have been identified in the St. Kitts small Indian mongoose population [32–35]. In the present study, we report high detection rates and molecular characterization of αCoVs in the small Indian mongoose on St. Kitts.

Methods

Sampling

During April-July 2017, eighty-three small Indian mongooses were trapped, euthanized, and sampled during necropsy (under sterile conditions) for a gut microbiome study on the Caribbean island of St. Kitts [31]. The fecal samples were obtained from the rectum/distal colon of the mongooses and stored at −80 o C [31]. The mongoose sampling (conducted as a part of the gut microbiome study) was approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the Ross University School of Veterinary Medicine (RUSVM), St. Kitts and Nevis (IACUC protocol # 17.04.13, dated 13 April 2017) and all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and monitored by the RUSVM IACUC [31]. In the present study, a total of 53 archival mongoose fecal samples were available in sufficient volumes for further analysis. Permission to use the archival mongoose samples for this study was obtained from the RUSVM IACUC (IACUC notification # TSU05.15.2024, dated 15 May 2024).

Amplification of CoV genome

Viral RNA was extracted from the fecal samples using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen Sciences, Germantown, MD, USA), following the instructions described by the manufacturer. The mongoose samples were screened for the presence of CoVs by a pan-CoV RT-semi-nested PCR assay (targeting a short region (~ 420 bp) of the RdRp gene) that has been successfully used to detect viruses representing all 4 genera within the subfamily Orthocoronavirinae [36]. The mongoose samples were further screened using CCoV-specific RT-PCR assays targeting the M gene and S gene (using CCoV-1, −2 and − 2b specific primers) as described previously [37–39]. The CCoV-1 primers amplify a short region (~ 346 bp, corresponding to nucleotide residue (nt) 2611-nt 2956 of CCoV-1 strain Elmo, GenBank AY170345) of the S gene [38], whilst the CCoV-2 primers target ~ 690 bp of 3’ region of the S gene [38]. The CCoV-2b-specific RT-PCR assay (primers CEPol-1 and TGSp-2) target a short region (~ 370 bp) consisting of the 3’ end of ORF1ab and 5’ end of S gene [39]. Additional primers employed to obtain the nearly full-length S coding sequences (CDS) were designed in the present study and are shown in Table S1. The RT-PCRs were carried out using the QIAGEN One-Step RT-PCR Kit (Qiagen Sciences, Germantown, MD, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To avoid contamination, we included sterile water as the negative control in all RT-PCR assays.

Nucleotide sequencing

The purification of PCR products was carried out by the Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), as outlined by the manufacturer. Nucleotide sequences were obtained by the Sanger dideoxy chemistry using the ABI Prism Big Dye Terminator Cycle Sequencing Ready Reaction Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) on an ABI 3730XL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Nucleotide sequences were obtained in both directions.

Sequence analysis

The CoV putative CDS and corresponding deduced aa sequences were determined by the ORF finder (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/, accessed on 5 May 2025), and validated by similarity search using the BLASTN/BLASTX and BLASTP program (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast, accessed on 7 May 2025), respectively. Pairwise sequence identities (%) were determined by BLASTN/P program (using the ‘align two or more sequences option’), or the EMBOSS Needle (using sequence type option ‘DNA’ or ‘protein’, respectively, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/psa/emboss_needle, accessed on 20 June 2025) program. Multiple alignment of nt/aa sequences were performed using the ClustalW program (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw, accessed on 8 July 2025), or the Clustal Omega program (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo, accessed on 8 July 2025) with default parameters.

Phylogenetic analysis was performed by the maximum likelihood (ML) method using MEGA11 software (version 11.0.13) [40], with 1000 bootstrap replicates. For respective ML analysis, the best-fit model of substitution was determined by the ‘Find Best DNA/Protein Models (ML)’ tool embedded in MEGA11. The clustering patterns of the Mon CoVs were confirmed by repeating ML analysis with other mathematical models of substitutions (applying the 2nd and 3rd most appropriate models identified by MEGA11). Recombination analysis was performed by the RDPv5 program, and the findings were corroborated by the SimPlot program (v3.5.1), as described previously [13, 41, 42].

GenBank accession numbers

The GenBank accession numbers for the Mon CoV sequences determined in the study are PV664221-PV664264 and PV893143.

Results and discussion

The present study was based on archival fecal samples collected from the rectum/distal colon of 53 small Indian mongooses on the Caribbean island of St. Kitts in 2017 [31]. The mongooses were trapped on different locations (in wild and urban habitats) of the island [32]. Considering the diversity of CoVs in wildlife [2, 17, 21, 43–51], and the lack of information on CoVs in the mongoose, the small Indian mongoose fecal samples were screened by a pan-CoV RT-semi-nested screening PCR assay (that amplifies a short stretch of the RdRp gene of α-, beta-, gamma- and deltaCoVs) [36]. Sixteen samples (30.18%, n = 53) tested positive for CoVs, which were confirmed by sequencing of the PCR products (Table S2). Compared to the previous study that identified CoVs in small intestinal lymph nodes from a single Egyptian mongoose (1/23 mongooses) in Portugal [28], the high CoV detection rates in the small Indian mongooses on St. Kitts indicated that CoVs might be widely circulating in the island mongoose population, warranting similar studies in other mongoose species and in mongoose populations in other geographical regions. The mongoose CoVs from St. Kitts (Mon CoVs) were detected in fecal samples from apparently healthy animals. Since the present study did not screen mongoose tissue samples, we could not establish if the Mon CoVs were associated with systemic infection in these animals.

The partial RdRp CDS (~ 350 nt long, obtained from the screening PCR products) from the Mon CoVs (except for Mon-12 and − 52, yielded low quality RdRp CDS, phred value < 40) shared nt and deduced aa identities of 88.42–100% and 93.68–100%, respectively, between themselves. The Mon CoV partial RdRp CDS shared nt and deduced aa identities of ~ 92–96% and ~ 95–100%, respectively, with cognate sequences of CCoV-2 strains (and those of FCoV-2 and TGEV). Phylogenetically, the RdRp CDS from Mon CoVs formed five distinct clusters within the CCoV-2 lineage (Fig. 1a).

The Mon samples (n = 48, as CoV RdRp-PCR positive samples Mon-46, −47, −51, −52 and − 58 were exhausted and not included) were also screened with CCoV specific RT-PCR assays targeting short region of the M- [37] and -S genes (CCoV-1 and − 2 genotyping assay) [38]. Eleven and 9 of the RdRp-PCR positive samples yielded the expected amplicons with the M- and S- (with CCoV-2-specific primers) RT-PCR, respectively (Table S2), whilst the other Mon samples tested negative with both the assays. Furthermore, seven of the 9 Mon CCoV-2 positive samples and two other Mon CoV samples (Mon-50 and − 53, that tested positive with the RdRp- and M- RT-PCR assays, and negative with the CCoV-1/−2 RT-PCR assays) tested positive with the CCoV-2b specific RT-PCR assay [39] (Table S2). The partial M CDS of the Mon CoVs shared nt and deduced aa identities of 87.27–99.49% and 88.19–100%, respectively, between themselves, whilst with other CoVs, maximum identities of 90.13–96.50% and 92.13–97.74%, respectively, were observed with cognate sequences of CCoV-2 strains. By phylogenetic analysis of the partial M CDS, the Mon CoVs formed a distinct cluster near the cluster of CCoV-2 strains from dogs in Brazil within the CCoV-2 lineage (Fig. 1b). The partial S CDS (determined using CCoV-2 specific primers [38]) from the Mon CoVs shared nt and deduced aa identities of 84.83–99.98% and 87.96–99.07%, respectively, between themselves, and identities of ~ 86–90% and ~ 88–93%, respectively, with CCoV-2 strains. Phylogenetically, the Mon S sequences formed a distinct cluster near the CCoV-2 lineage, which was supported by high bootstrap values (100%) (Fig. 2a). The Mon CoV sequences obtained from the CCoV-2b PCR amplicons shared nt identities of 80.34–97.77% between themselves, and phylogenetically, were more related to cognate sequences of CCoV-2b and TGEV/PRCV strains (nt identities of ~ 81–86%) than those of other αCoVs (Fig. 2b).

Since the archival Mon samples have been analyzed in previous studies [32–35], most of the Mon CoV positive samples lacked sufficient volumes/were exhausted during the present study, and only Mon-1, −14, −43, −56 and − 65 were available for further analysis (Table S2). In the present study, we could determine the nearly full-length deduced aa sequences of the putative CoV S proteins from Mon-1 (1439 aa in length), −14 (1445 aa), −43 (1456 aa), −56 (1446 aa) and − 65 (1451 aa) (Table S3 and Fig. S1). Interestingly, an additional CoV S CDS (designated as Mon-14-2, 1047 bp in size, 5’ end region of S CDS) was obtained from sample Mon-14 (Fig. S2). The Mon-14-2 sequence shared nt and deduced aa identities of 71.2% and 64.8%, respectively, with cognate sequence of Mon-14 S CDS, revealing the presence of at least 2 different CoV strains in sample Mon-14 (Fig. S2). The nearly full-length deduced aa sequences of the S proteins of the Mon CoV strains shared identities of 80.20–97.60% between themselves, and with other CoVs, shared maximum identities of ~ 80–81% with cognate sequences of CCoV-2b strains, which were comparable to those observed with TGEV strains (~ 78–80% aa identities) (Table S3). By phylogenetic analysis of nearly complete S deduced aa sequences, the Mon CoVs formed a distinct cluster within the CCoV-2 lineage that was located near the CCoV-2b cluster and distantly related to the CCoV-2a/FCoV-2 and CCoV-2v/c clusters (Fig. 3). The N-terminal domain (NTD) of the putative S proteins of Mon CoVs shared deduced aa identities of 58.99–93.84% between themselves, and identities of 64.31–66.91%, 62.37–68.40% and ~ 26–28% with those of CCoV-2b, TGEV and CCoV-2a strains, respectively. Phylogenetically, the NTD sequences of Mon-1, −14, −56 and − 65 grouped into a distinct cluster near that of CCoV-2b strains, whilst Mon-43 branched as an outlier to the larger cluster consisting of Mon CoVs, CCoV-2b and TGEV strains (Fig. S3). The S1 and S2 subunit of the Mon CoV S proteins shared deduced aa identities of 69.07–96.30% and 90.80–99.10%, respectively, between themselves, and S1 identities of ~ 73–75% and ~ 60–61%, respectively, and S2 identities of ~ 88% and ~ 88%, respectively, with CCoV-2b/TGEV and CCoV-2a strains.

Recombination events, especially those occurring in the S gene, have been shown to play important roles in the evolution of CoVs [2, 9–11, 49–51]. In the present study, the nearly full-length S CDS of the Mon CoVs were subjected to recombination analysis, as described previously [13]. The RDPv5 program revealed two statistically relevant recombination events, one each in Mon-14 and Mon-43, which were corroborated by similarity plot recombination analysis using the SimPlot program (v3.5.1) (Fig. S4). Although the CoV S1 subunit is considered as a hotspot for recombination events [2, 13, 52], the putative recombination event involving Mon-14 occurred in the S2 region of S gene (beginning breakpoint: 2948, and ending breakpoint: 4179), with Mon-56 and − 43 as the major and minor parent, respectively (Fig. S4a, b). On the other hand, the Mon-43 putative recombination event involved the CoV RBD in the S1 region (beginning breakpoint: 1195, and ending breakpoint: 2292), with Mon-1 and a CCoV-2a strain (SH32) from hyena [17] as the major and minor parent, respectively (Fig. S4c, d). Recombination events involving the CoV RBD should be monitored, as such events might influence virus affinity to host receptors, resulting in altered host tropism and spillover events [2, 10, 11, 49, 50, 52].

Considering the diversity in S sequences between the Mon CoVs, and between the Mon CoVs and other αCoVs, we evaluated the Mon CoV putative S protein sequences for certain conserved features that are retained in the CCoV strains [5, 9]. The Mon CoV S protein sequences lacked the putative furin cleavage motif (R-R-S/A/V-R-R-S/A) at the S1/S2 activation site (that is present in FCoV-1/CCoV-1 and absent in CCoV-2 [5]), and possessed the distinctive CCoV-2 cleavage motif (K-R-K-Y/F-R-S [5]) at the S2’ activation site, although one and 2 aa mismatches were identified in the Mon-1 (T-R-K-Y-R-S) and − 56/65 (R-R-A/T-Y-R-S) putative S2’ cleavage motif sequences, respectively (Fig. 4a and b). The K967R in Mon-56 and − 65 has been reported in some CoV sequences from canids [GenBank MT114541, OM864519 and OQ540910 among others], whilst K964T in Mon-1, K969A in Mon-56, and K969T in Mon-65 appeared to be unique events. Although implications of mutations in the S2’ cleavage site of CCoV/CCoV-like strains remain to be elucidated so far, modulation of the CoV S2’ appear to correlate with changes in tissue tropism, host range and pathogenesis [53, 54].

Multiple alignment of the Mon CoV RBD sequences with those of other αCoVs is shown in Fig. 5. The Mon-43 putative RBD retained all the 5 crucial aa residues that are essential for CoV binding to host APN, whilst the other Mon CoV RBDs retained four of the 5 aa residues, and presented a R→ L substitution (aa position corresponding to R540 in HuPn-2108) that has been previously reported in some FCoVs and in CoVs from spotted hyenas and a jackal [17] (Fig. 5). Of the 19 aa residues in RBD that directly interact with the APN [17–19], 3/19 aa mismatches were observed among Mon-1, −14, −56 and − 65, whilst 6/19 aa residues in Mon-43 were not shared with any of the other Mon CoVs (Fig. 5 and table S4). On the other hand, 6–8/19 aa mismatches were observed between the Mon CoVs and TGEV/PRCV/CCoV-2b strains (Fig. 5 and table S4). Multiple alignment of the RBD sequences revealed two unique insertions in the Mon CoV RBDs, which were lacking in other αCoVs (Fig. 5). The RBD sequences of Mon-1, −14, −56 and − 65 shared deduced aa identities of 90.41–96.58% between themselves and shared low identities of 68.28–72.41% with that of Mon-43, which might be attributed to the recombination event involving the RBD of Mon-43. With RBD sequences of other CoVs, the Mon CoVs shared deduced aa identities of 71.72–78.08% and 69.66–75.34% with CCoV-2 and TGEV/PRCV, respectively. On the other hand, RBD sequence identities of ~ 94–97% and ~ 90%, respectively, were observed between CCoV-2a and − 2b, and between CCoV-2 and TGEV/PRCV.

Phylogenetically, the mongoose putative APN (GenBank MF101907) clustered near those of hyenas (family Hyaenidae) within the suborder Feliformia (and order Carnivora) (Fig. S5), and shared deduced aa identities of ~ 85%, ~ 83–84%, and ~ 78–79% with APN from members of Hyaenidae, Felidae, and Canidae, respectively. Among the 23 aa residues in the porcine APN that have been shown to directly interact with the PRCV RBD [17, 18], 17 residues, including the 3 essential aa residues (N736, W737 and T378 in porcine APN), were retained in the mongoose APN (Table S4). The mongoose APN shared 17/23 aa residues with the APN sequence from a domestic dog (GenBank NM001146034), whilst 2/6 and 1/6 aa mismatches were identified as conservative and semi-conservative substitutions, respectively (Table S4). Taken together, despite sequence variations, the Mon RBD and the CCoV RBD, and the mongoose APN and the domestic dog APN, shared certain conserved features that are crucial for αCoV-1 binding to host APN receptor. Based on these observations, the susceptibility of wild carnivores to CCoVs/FCoVs [17, 21, 44, 45, 47, 48], and the phylogenetic clustering of the Mon CoVs within the CCoV-2 lineage (Figs. 1−3 and S3), it might be possible that mongooses are susceptible to CoVs circulating in domestic dogs/other carnivores and vice versa. In the present study, 12 of the 16 CoV positive samples were from small Indian mongooses trapped in urban areas, whilst the remaining CoV positive mongooses were from wild habitats. Since St. Kitts has an urban population of stray domestic dogs that come in close contact with the free-roaming mongooses, especially at/near food sources, such as garbage dumps, it would be interesting to investigate the role of the small Indian mongoose as a potential carrier of CoVs for the local canine population.

This is the first study to report high detection rates and molecular characterization of CoVs from the mongoose, expanding our knowledge on the host range and complex evolutionary patterns of CoVs. Based on analysis of the partial RdRp- and M- CDS and partial/nearly complete S CDS, the Mon CoVs were classified as αCoVs (genus Alphacoronavirus suis/αCoV-1) (Figs. 1–3). Phylogenetically, the Mon CoVs formed distinct cluster/s within the CCoV-2 lineage (Figs. 1−3, S3) and appeared to be more related to CCoV-2b (and TGEV) than other CoVs (Figs. 2b and 3, S3; Table S3). The Mon CoV S CDS preserved certain features (at S1/S2 and S2’ cleavage sites) that are characteristic of CCoV-2 and retained the crucial aa residues essential for αCoV binding to host APN receptor (Figs. 4 and 5). However, significant genetic diversity, including differences in the putative RBD and evidence for recombination events (involving the S gene), were observed between the Mon CoVs, and between the Mon CoVs and other αCoVs. Based on these observations, especially the divergence in S protein, it might be possible that the Mon CoVs represent novel viruses within the CCoV-2 lineage, although analysis of the complete CoV genomes would be required to confirm the overall genetic makeup of these viruses. Taken together, our findings warrant further studies on the prevalence, pathogenesis and cross-species transmission potential of CoVs that might be circulating in different mongoose species/populations worldwide.

Although the present study provided important/first time insights into the genetic make-up/diversity of mongoose CoVs, there were limitations: (i) The study was based on a small sample size (53 mongooses) in a tiny island; (ii) Since the study was based on archival fecal samples (collected during a gut microbiome study in 2017), we could not screen tissue samples for CoVs, which would have revealed if the Mon CoVs were associated with pantropism/systemic infection; (iii) Since the archival Mon samples lacked sufficient volumes/were exhausted during the study, we could not perform further analysis, such as determining the full-length CoV genomes, or obtaining the complete CDS of other important CoV genes; and (iv) Considering the interspecies transmission potential of αCoVs, it would have been interesting to compare the CoV genomes from the local canine/feline population with those of the Mon CoVs, since the small Indian mongooses often come in close contact with dogs/cats on St. Kitts. Previously, we had reported CCoV-1 in domestic dogs during a canine parvovirus-2 study from St. Kitts [55]. However, the study was limited to preliminary analysis of the partial M gene, warranting detailed molecular characterization of canine/feline CoVs from the region in the future.

Conclusions

Our findings indicated that CoVs might be widely circulating in the small Indian mongoose population on St. Kitts. The present study provided the first insights into the genetic makeup/diversity of mongoose CoVs, revealing significant genetic diversity (including evidence for at least 2 recombination events in the S gene, one of which involved the RBD) between the Mon CoVs on a small (surface area of 176 square km), isolated geographical region. Based on phylogenetic analysis of the RdRp-, M- and S- CDS, the Mon CoVs formed distinct cluster/s within the CCoV-2 lineage and appeared to be more related to CCoV-2b (and TGEV) than other αCoVs. Despite differences in the S CDS including those in the RBD, the Mon CoVs retained certain features (lack of furin cleavage motif at S1/S2 site and presence of cleavage motif at S2’ site) that are characteristic of CCoV-2 and maintained the essential aa residues crucial for binding of αCoV to the host APN receptor. Considering (i) the findings from this study, (ii) the close proximity of mongooses to humans and other canids/felids in certain geographical regions, (iii) the role of the small Indian mongoose as a carrier of important viral pathogens (Rabies virus) [25], and (iv) the cross-species transmission potential of αCoV-1 (between canids-felids, between wild and domestic canids/felids, and more importantly, between canids/felids and humans [2, 9–11, 14–17, 21, 43–46]), large-scale studies on prevalence and genetic diversity of CoVs that might be circulating in different mongoose species/populations, and in-depth investigation of mongoose CoV RBD-host APN interactions are of utmost importance.
