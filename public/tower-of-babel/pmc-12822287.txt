Attitudes and practices of women’s health specialists regarding fertility treatment for social reasons – a reproductive rights’ survey from Pakistan
Authors: Samia Husain, Sonia Husain, Rubina Izhar, Rahila Imtiaz, Zubaida Masood
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Subfertility is a global health concern; around 12% of couples around the world are considered sub fertile [1]. More than half of these couples reside in South Asia and Sub Saharan Africa [2, 3].

The couples living in low and middle income countries suffer dramatic consequences of involuntary childlessness [4–7]. The stigma is particularly severe for childless women. They are isolated and neglected not only by the spouse but sometimes the whole community. Common sequelae of this childlessness are violence, polygamy and even suicide. Childlessness can no longer be regarded as an individual medical problem in these settings because many families are completely dependent on children for economic survival in south Asia [8].

Despite its importance in the community and the significance of childbearing, treating subfertility continues to remain a low priority area for local health care providers [9]. Most specialists commonly see couples in their clinics but do not initiate any investigations/treatment because the clinical guidelines suggest that a period of twelve months must elapse before a couple is labeled subfertile [10]. However, this may be perceived as too much time for the newlyweds who are mostly given an ultimatum to bear a child [11]. This results in severe distress regarding fertility [12].

Pakistan is a developing country with a patriarchal and polygamous society where married women who are unable to bear a child are commonly divorced and the husband remarries [13]. Such childless women often present to clinics where most of the times the only issue is social pressure and apparently no cause for her delayed fertility can be found. They present within a year and are mostly denied formal treatment and in some cases even investigations before the recommended 12 months lapse [14]. The impact of this problem is profound and healthcare providers dealing with these couples are faced with a dilemma. Social reasons for providing screening and treatment to such couples in developing countries where societal norms demand a couple to bear a child soon are commonly investigated [15, 16]. However, the stance of health care providers on earlier investigations and management of fertility concerns reasoned by societal pressures has not been evaluated before in the region. The existing research focuses heavily on the experience of the subfertile woman and the societal dynamics. The specific and formal evaluation of the healthcare provider’s (gynaecologist/fertility specialist) viewpoint—how they internally navigate the clinical guideline (wait 1 year) versus the intense social pressure to intervene immediately—is the nuance that appears to be missing in a dedicated study. We conducted this study to assess the attitude and practice of gynecologists regarding fertility treatment for social reasons.

Methods

We conducted an online survey on Pakistani gynecologists from 1st March 2021 to 30th September 2021. Snowball sampling technique was used to recruit survey participants. Those who were sent the link were asked to forward the link to other gynecologists. We chose participants from all over Pakistan and we asked participants to send the link to all other potential participants. We calculated the sample size using EpiCalc-2000. The sample size came out to be 384; based on the assumption that proportion of positive attitude would be 50%, level of confidence 95% and precision 5%.We included 464 people in total.

Data collection process

A Google form was used to enhance accessibility of the survey. There were only three inclusion criteria of the survey; (1) Pakistani residents (2) practicing obstetrics and gynaecology with a formal qualification in their specialty and (3) having a special interest in subfertility. All other healthcare professionals were excluded from the analysis. All respondents gave an informed consent to participate in our study. The participation was voluntary and no compensation was given to any respondent.

A self-administered questionnaire was designed for the study after reviewing clinical guidelines for fertility problems. The questions were developed based on literature search and some informal interviews with women who reported for treatment. All questions were discussed with an expert committee formed of two fertility specialists, one gynecologist and one resident in training, input was also taken with couples reporting for treatment. The questionnaire was checked for content and relevance by the authors. We pretested the instrument on 20 consultant gynecologists with a special interest in subfertility; these responses were not included in the final analysis. That pretesting showed that the internal consistency of the attitude (cronbach’s alpha = 0.7) and practice (cronbach’s alpha = 0.678) portions were good. To reduce social desirability bias during the pilot study target professionals were debriefed and asked explicitly which questions they found difficult or felt pressured to answer in a certain way, and adjustment in the wording off questionnaire was based on this feedback. We used projective techniques to frame the questions, employed forgiving language and ensured neutral and balanced wording to avoid leading and judgmental language.

The questionnaire is made up of 4 sections: (I) Demographic characteristics (II) The knowledge section comprised of 6 questions about the use of clomiphene citrate which, according to literature, is the first treatment option commonly used worldwide for couples with unexplained subfertility. (III) The attitude section; included seven questions. Response to each item was recorded on the 5-point Likert scale as follows: (1) strongly agree, (2) agree, (3) undecided, (4), disagree, (5), strongly disagree. (The questions were negatively worded) (IV) Practices section had 5 questions. Response to each item was recorded on the 5-point Likert scale as follows: (1) never, (2) rarely, (3) sometimes, (4) mostly, (5) always. Attitude score was calculated and classified as follows: ≤ 20 negative attitude, 21 and above positive attitude.

Practices regarding subfertility were also scored and graded as good practice with scores 16 and above. Practice scores below 16 are considered suboptimal practice.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software version 23(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The attitude and practice scores were calculated. We conducted bivariate analyses to identify the factors associated with good attitude and good practices. Post stratification chi square test was applied and p value of 0.05 was taken as statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Our study was originally inspired by the patient’s priority to get treatment before 12 months because they undergo significant anxiety and distress due to childlessness. We specifically included questions on referral and attitude of healthcare professionals towards their demand of treatment. Patients were specifically asked at the time of questionnaire development about the key practices and attitudes of specialists towards their demand of investigation and treatment. The published results would be shared with public and local forums in the country to raise awareness.

Results

During the study period we approached 500 specialists and asked them to participate. We received 480 responses; the response rate was 96%. We excluded 16 responses because the respondent though a national was not residing in Pakistan at the moment. We therefore included 464 responses in the study. Our study population comprised of 230(49.6%) gynecologists, 218(47.0%) obstetricians and 16 (3.4%) reproductive endocrinologist. Most of the respondents (83.6%) had some training in subfertility and around 48% had specialized in the diagnosis and treatment of subfertility. Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study population. The mean Attitude score was 17.50 ± 2.42 and the mean practice score was 14.44 ± 3.32. Only 48(10.3%) had a positive attitude towards screening and offering treatment to women before 12 months of marriage. However, 186 (40.1%) had good practices regarding such cases.

Discussion

Main findings

Our study shows that our healthcare providers do not have good practices and positive attitude towards seeking fertility treatment earlier than that recommended for social reasons and pressures on childless couples. Only one tenth (10.8%) had a positive attitude towards screening and offering treatment to such couples and less than one-half (40.1%) had good practices regarding such cases.

Interpretation

Obstetrician-gynecologists are the fourth largest group of physicians and the only specialty that deals with women’s health care issues [17]. Patient centered care is a component of health care quality [18] and is commonly measured to assess the quality of healthcare a patient receives. Because subfertility has a huge impact on economic, emotional and psychological wellbeing of a couple, the importance of patient centered care in this regard cannot be overemphasized. The problem with developing countries is that couples are expected to bear a child soon after marriage and most of the times they are not allowed the liberty to wait for 12 months before seeking medical help, which is the time duration after which fertility treatment is indicated in couples [19]. Due to social reasons and societal pressure, the couple seeks advice from obstetricians and gynecologists; but in most instances is dismissed without treatment. This attitude is contrary to the patient centered care approach.

A study done in Saudi Arabia highlights the practices of women who are denied treatment, they resort to self-medication, use herbal remedies and even go ‘doctors shopping’ [20]. Similarly, Pakistani women resort to fraudsters or impostors or even unqualified practitioners that do not provide adequate dose of medicines and then they lose hope because the medicines are not working. This adds to their emotional burden of women and eventually the man remarries, even before being evaluated. Female spouse subfertility is considered an acceptable reason for polygamy in Saudi Arabia [21].

It is alarming that unqualified individuals tend to communicate well and sometimes provide more information, are easy to access and appear highly responsive [20]. Nearly two thirds of the specialists felt little sympathy, if any, that they cannot prescribe treatment to such patients because of formal guidelines. On the other hand, the majority instructed women that this treatment is not for them at this point in time of their marriage. These practices can be severely disturbing for the woman who has been given an ultimatum to bear a child. The clinical guidelines are meant to be adapted to local scenarios; all guidelines can be customized to suit local needs [22]. In our study, specialists with foreign qualifications were less likely to start any investigations and treatment in such couples. This may be due to self-reported design of the questionnaire. It could also mean that our specialists adhere firmly to clinical guidelines and do not show enough flexibility to tailor it per case when considering the entire context. We are not advocating for treatment and investigation in all scenarios but when patient presents with solid social reasons such as an impending divorce or when severe mental breakdown is suspected, baseline investigations and proof of normality should be provided.

The negative impact of delayed fertility on women has been studied in different countries. Women are subjected to domestic violence in Pakistan if they are unable to bear a child [23]. A study from Iran [24] reports that women with subfertility had “Concerns over life instability” and “being judged by others”. A study from China [25] shows that divorce is twice as likely in couples with subfertility.

In the light of these findings it is fitting to describe the attitude harbored by our specialists as paternalistic and may hinder the quality of care received because our specialists are unlikely to start any treatment on social grounds. These women are under enormous pressure and just lifestyle advice for 12 months duration may not be what meets their needs adequately.

Implementing health guidelines is especially difficult because they often address complex systems and require action from multi-sector government institutions rather than just individual clinicians. This challenge arises when any guideline is applied in a context different from its origin, a scenario known as cross-context implementation. For instance, guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO), designed for a global audience, must be carefully reviewed and customized for a country or sub-national health system. Similarly, local contexts—such as a specific state, province, region, or hospital—must adapt guidelines issued at the national or international level to ensure they are feasible and relevant to their local environment [26, 27]. However, studies have shown that the quality of guideline development, the strength of recommendations, and the applicability of local implementation are uneven [28].

We recommend that the following steps should be taken to tackle this issue. Social grounds for starting fertility treatment in developing countries need to be established, and considered as a special indication in international guidelines. Local guidelines should be revised to include care for such couples. Special training/workshops should be conducted for practitioners to raise awareness and develop a pathway for referral, if the initial point of contact practitioner does not feel strongly about treating these couples. Similar to abortion care, fertility treatment may be started in these scenarios despite the recommendation from elite societies.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of the study is the fact that we conducted a survey on gynecologists/obstetricians who are practicing in a country where social reasons for seeking subfertility treatment are common. The attitude and practices of these practitioners directly affect the quality of care they provide which is a major area of concern and should be assessed. Our study fills this void in literature. Moreover we included practitioners who have local/foreign qualifications in obstetrics and gynaecology to ensure we are targeting the knowledgeable members in the related field of practice.

The major limitation is that the survey was conducted using self-report measures which may lead participants to give responses that are considered socially desirable rather than their actual responses. Secondly the snowballing technique used may lead to selection bias. Our sample has a huge number of practitioners from teaching hospitals and that may affect the generalizability of results.

Conclusion

Our study shows that healthcare providers have negative attitude and suboptimal practices regarding starting fertility treatment for social reasons. This approach can be counterproductive in scenarios where couples are desperate to conceive. A committee should be set up to formulate a national reproductive health policy that takes into account the social differences and deals with these issues accordingly. The recommendations that conflict with local cultural norms and patient preference should be modified in light of the available evidence. Special training/workshops to raise awareness and modify practice are recommended.
