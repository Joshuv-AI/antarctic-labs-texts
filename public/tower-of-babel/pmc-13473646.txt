Intradialytic neuromuscular electrical stimulation improves muscle oxygenation and aerobic capacity in end-stage renal disease: a single-blind, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Amal Machfer, Achraf Ammar, Halil İbrahim Ceylan, Mohamed Amine Bouzid, Ismail Dergaa, Raul Ioan Muntean, Hamdi Chtourou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing hemodialysis (HD) often exhibit substantial impairments in aerobic capacity and skeletal muscle oxidative function, contributing to physical deconditioning and elevated cardiovascular risk. Adherence to traditional exercise programs during HD remains limited due to physical constraints and treatment fatigue. Intradialytic neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) represents a low-effort alternative that may promote peripheral muscle adaptations without imposing significant cardiovascular strain. This study examined the effects of a 12-week intradialytic NMES intervention on aerobic capacity and muscle oxygenation in patients with ESRD. In this single-blind, randomized, controlled pilot trial, 24 male patients with ESRD were assigned to either a neuromuscular electrical stimulation training group (NSTG, n = 12) or a control group (CG, n = 12). The NSTG received quadriceps NMES during HD sessions (40 min/session, three times per week for 12 weeks). Before and after the intervention, participants completed an incremental exercise cycling test to assess peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) and maximal aerobic power (MAP). Muscle oxygenation of the vastus lateralis was measured during exercise using near-infrared spectroscopy, quantifying changes in oxyhemoglobin (ΔO2Hb), deoxyhemoglobin (ΔHHb), and total hemoglobin (ΔTHb). A significant Group × Intervention interaction effect was observed for VO2peak (p < 0.05) and maximal aerobic power (MAP) (p < 0.01), indicating greater improvements in the NSTG compared with the CG. Descriptively, VO2peak increased by 27% in the NSTG, while remaining unchanged in the CG. MAP also increased by 26% in the NSTG (120.5 ± 25.3 to 151.8 ± 20.9 W, p < 0.01) while remaining unchanged in the CG. Within-group analyses indicated increased ΔHHb and ΔO₂Hb at high exercise intensities (≥ 90% VO2peak) following NMES training period (p < 0.05), while no changes were observed in the CG. However, these findings should not be interpreted as formal between-group differences. This pilot randomized controlled trial suggests that a 12-week intradialytic NMES program significantly improved aerobic capacity and skeletal muscle oxygen extraction in patients with ESRD, which may be related to peripheral muscular adaptations rather than central cardiopulmonary changes, although these mechanisms were not directly assessed. This improvement occurred without affecting heart rate, ventilation, or regional muscle blood volume. The findings support NMES as a practical and effective strategy to counteract physical deconditioning during routine hemodialysis. However, given the pilot nature of the study and the limited sample size, these findings should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in larger trials.Trial registration: Pan African Clinical Trial Registry Identifier: PACTR202206634181851 Registered on 21/06/2022. Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) affects more than 2 million individuals globally, with hemodialysis (HD) serving as the predominant form of renal replacement therapy for the majority of these patients1. In addition to the substantial burden of managing renal dysfunction, ESRD is commonly associated with severe complications, including skeletal muscle atrophy, diminished aerobic capacity, and systemic inflammation. These pathophysiological alterations contribute to reduced physical activity and heightened cardiovascular risk, which are major determinants of morbidity and mortality in this population2,3. Targeting these comorbidities is essential, as enhancement in physical function and metabolic health has been demonstrated to improve quality of life and overall outcomes in patients with ESRD4.

HDoffers a unique window for therapeutic intervention, as patients typically spend three to four hours per session, three times a week, in a semi-recumbent position. This setting offers a practical opportunity to implement strategies to enhancemuscle function and metabolic health. However, adherence to conventional exercise programs during hemodialysis is frequently limited by patient fatigue, comorbidities, and logistical constraints5. Consequently, innovative and low-effort interventions that can be incorporated into the dialysis routine are needed.

A major concern in ESRD is the substantial decline in muscle oxidative capacity, largely attributed to the accumulation of uremic toxins, chronic systemic inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction6. This reduction in oxidative capacity impairs physical performance and exacerbates metabolic dysregulation, contributing to a cycle of physical deconditioning and reduced aerobic fitness. Aerobic capacity is commonly assessed as peak O2 uptake (VO2peak)7, measured during maximal incremental exercise which reflects the highest and maximal attainable performance of the integrated oxygen transport and utilization system.Noninvasive in vivo methods such as determining the maximal rate of ATP resynthesis7 or muscle oxygenation8 during aerobic exercise, can reveal impairment in oxygen extraction, mitochondrial oxygen utilization, or both.

VO2peakis a predictor of cardiovascular health and all-cause mortality in both healthy individuals and those with chronic diseases, including ESRD9,10.Therefore, interventions that enhance muscle oxidative capacity and improve VO2peak holdpotentialfor advancing both clinical and functional outcomes in this high-risk population.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) has emerged asa promising alternative to conventional exercise, using low-frequency electrical impulses to activate muscle fibers and induce physiological adaptations similar to those achieved through voluntary training (Gondin, Brocca et al., 2011). Mechanistically, NMES induces non-selective muscle fiber recruitment, which may involve both type I and type II fibers depending on stimulation parameters, enhances mitochondrial enzyme activity, and increases local blood flow, thereby improving oxidative capacity. This technique has been successfully employed in populations with significant physical limitations, such as patients with chronic heart failure or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, to enhance muscle strength, endurance, and metabolic function11,12. In patients with ESRD, existing NMES studies have employed a wide range of stimulation frequencies, from very low to very high, yielding inconsistent results regarding their effectiveness. For example, a randomized trial by Dobsak et al. (2012) demonstrated that intradialytic NMES at 10 Hz significantly improved quadriceps strength and exercise capacity, as assessed by the 6-minute walk test13. In contrast, Schardong et al. (2017) found no significant improvement following an 8-week NMES intervention at 80 Hz14. Our group has previously explored the effects of intradialytic NMES on muscle strength, functional capacity, and postural balance15, as well as neuromuscular fatigue and muscle oxygenation during sustained isometric contractions in ESRD patients16.Collectively, these findings suggest that NMES may serve as a clinically relevant strategy to improve aerobic performance, physical autonomy, and cardiovascular prognosis in patients undergoing hemodialysis. However, its specific application for enhancing muscle oxidative capacity during hemodialysis, remains underexplored.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of NMES during hemodialysis on muscle oxidative capacity and aerobic fitness. VO2peak and NIRS-derived parameters were used as primary outcome measures. We hypothesized that NMES would enhance muscle oxygen utilization and increase VO2peak, offering a feasible and effective intervention to mitigate physical deconditioning in patients with ESRD.

Methods

Participants

A total of 36 patients with ESRD undergoing HD therapy were screened for eligibility based on medical records and recruited from affiliated dialysis centers. During the recruitment period, only male patients who met the eligibility criteria were available and consented to participate; therefore, the study included men exclusively. Eligibility required participants to have been receiving chronic HD therapy for at least 12 months. Exclusion criteria included active coronary artery disease, intradialytic fluid accumulation > 3 L, intradialytic blood pressure ≥ 180 mmHg systolic or ≥ 95 mmHg diastolic, hemoglobin < 9.0 g/dL, and a recent ischemic cardiac event. All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. The study was approved by the Regional Research Ethics Committee (Comité de protection des personnes du sudN° 11/2019), registered in the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR202206634181851), and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

The methodological procedures were adapted from our previous study16, where full details are provided. This study was a randomized, controlled clinical trial reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 201017. Participants were allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the control group (CG) or the neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) training group (NSTG) (Table 1) using block randomization generated via an online random number generator (random.org). Participants in the NSTG were scheduled to complete 36 intradialytic NMES sessions over a 12 weeks, 3 sessions/week. Adherence was defined a priori as completion of ≥ 33 sessions (≥ 90%), allowing a maximum of 3 missed sessions. Attendance was recorded at each dialysis visit. Participants who completed fewer than 33 sessions were deemed non-adherent and excluded from the final analysis; meaning the results are derived from a per-protocol analysis rather than an intention-to-treat analysis. Given the visible nature of the NMES intervention, blinding the participants was not feasible. Baseline and post-intervention assessments were conducted on non-dialysis days, and the final evaluation was performed following the 12-week training protocol.

Admission visit

To ensure stable and reliable performance, participants attended a familiarization session four days before the experiment. During this session they were introduced to the study protocol and testing equipment. Upon arrival at the laboratory, anthropometric measurements were recorded.

Intervention protocol

All patients received standard HD therapy care; however, those in the NSTG additionally underwent intradialytic NMES targeting the quadriceps muscles for both lower limbs. The NMES protocol was designed based on established methodological guidelines for effective muscle strengthening via surface electrical stimulation18. Each participant in the NSTG completed 36 NMES sessions 3 per week over 12 weeks.

Each session lasted 40 min and consisted of 80 isometric NMES-induced contractions of the quadriceps muscles. A 2-minute low-intensity warm-up preceded the first contractions, and a 2-minute cool-down at reduced current intensity followed the final set.Each contraction lasted 10 s, followed by a 20-second rest period. The average stimulation intensity applied during NMES sessions was 34 ± 6 mA. Participants were positioned with their trunks at 120°, and their knees flexed to 60°, to optimize force production during quadriceps activation (where 0° represents full leg extension)19.

NMES contractions were delivered using a portable, programmable stimulator (Genesy 1200 PRO, Globus Italia, Codognè, Italy), which delivered rectangular biphasic pulsed currents at a fixed stimulation frequency of 50 Hz and a pulse duration of 400 µs. Rectangular waves with extended pulse durations (300–400 µs) were selected based on evidence indicating their effectiveness in eliciting robust quadriceps contractions20. A frequency of 50 Hz was chosen because it falls within the range commonly recommended to promote muscle strengthening and oxidative adaptations21. Importantly, the stimulation frequency was not progressivelymodified during the intervention. Rather, the parameter that was adjusted was the current intensity (mA), which was gradually increased within each session until the participant reached the maximal tolerable painless intensity capable of producing a visible and effective quadriceps contraction. Thus, the term ‘highest painless threshold’ refers to the tolerated stimulation intensity, not to the stimulation frequency.

Stimulation was delivered using self-adhesive bipolar electrodes (four per leg, MyoTrode, 5 × 5 cm, Globus Italia SRL). Electrode placement followed a standardized protocol: two negative electrodes were positioned near the proximal insertions of the vastus lateralis (VL) and vastus medialis (VM), over the femoral triangle 1–3 cm below the inguinal ligament, while corresponding positive electrodes were placed as close as possible to the motor points of the VL and VM muscles. Motor points were identified by probing the skin surface to determine the lowest stimulation threshold22. Electrodes remained in throughout the session and were neither removed nor repositioned. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual lifestyle and daily routines throughout the study. All NMES sessions were conducted by trained study assistants under medical supervision to ensure adherence to the protocol and patient safety. All pre- and post-intervention assessments were carried out by a single evaluator who wasblinded to group assignment (single-blind design). Participants could not beblindedbecause the NMES intervention was visible. To ensureassessorblinding, group assignment was concealed by an independent researcher responsible for randomization and delivering the intervention. Additionally, participants were explicitly instructed not to reveal their group assignment during testing sessions, and all assessments took place in a standardized environment without access to intervention-related information. Although all sessions were conducted under medical supervision and stimulation was adjusted based on individual tolerance, muscle cramping was not recorded as a specific adverse event or tolerance outcome during the intervention.

Study outcomes

Baseline anthropometric and clinical assessments

Body weight (kg) was measured using a calibrated digital scale (Seca, Hamburg, Germany) with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes. Height (m) was assessed using a wall-mounted stadiometer to the nearest 0.1 cm. Body mass index (BMI, kg·m⁻²) was calculated as body mass divided by the square of height.

Physical activity level was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form, and expressed as an arbitrary score according to standard scoring procedures23.

Comorbidities were assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index, and the prevalence of type 2diabetes mellitus and hypertension was obtained from medical records and expressed as number and percentage of participants24.

Clinical parameters were extracted from patients’ medical records. Time on dialysis (months) was calculated from the initiation of hemodialysis treatment. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, mL·min⁻¹·1.73 m⁻²) was obtained using standard clinical laboratory equations. Hemoglobin concentration (g·dL⁻¹) was measured from fasting venous blood samples using an automated hematology analyzer.

Resting systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mmHg) were measured using an automated sphygmomanometer after 10 min of seated rest, following standard clinical guidelines. Resting heart rate (bpm) was recorded simultaneously using a heart rate monitor (Polar Electro Oy, Finland).

Dialysis adequacy was assessed using Kt/V, calculated according to standard clinical practice based on urea clearance measurements.

Experimental protocol

Participants arrived at the laboratory at least 2 h after their last meal and were instructed to abstain from caffeine, alcohol, and any pharmacological agents that could potentially influence their performance. Additionally, they were advised to avoid strenuous physical activity for at least 24 h before exercise testing. To minimize circadian variability, all experimental sessions were conducted at the same time of day for each participant.

Assessment of physical capacity

VO2peak and the corresponding maximal aerobic power (MAP) were assessed using an incremental cycling test performed to exhaustion on an electronically braked cycle ergometer (Monark 874E, Switzerland). Following a standardized 5-minute resting period, participants completed a 3-minute warm-up at 30 W. Thereafter, the workload was increased by 25 W every 2 min until exhaustion. Pedaling cadence was maintained at 60 rpm throughout the protocol, and the test was terminated when cadence dropped below 58 rpm despite verbal encouragement. The highest completed stage was immediately followed by an active recovery phase at 60 W, then by 2 min of passive recovery. The exercise test was supervised by a certified physician and trained medical staff. Electrocardiographic monitoring was available on standby. Termination criteria adhered to standard clinical guidelines and included chest pain, severe dyspnea, significant hypotension, or participant request.Perceived exertion was assessed using the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion scale. Participants were familiarized with the scale during the preliminary session. RPE was recorded at the end of the exercise test (i.e., at maximal effort corresponding to 100% of VO2peak). The Borg scale ranges from 6(“no exertion at all”) to 20 (“maximal exertion”), and has been widely used as a valid indicator of subjective exercise intensity in both healthy individuals and clinical populations25.

Cardiopulmonary responses

Cardiopulmonary parameters were continuously monitored throughout the experimental sessions. Heart rate (HR) (beats·min⁻¹) was recorded at rest and during the exercise test using a heart rate monitor (Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland).Pulmonary ventilation (VE) and VO2peak were assessed using a gas-exchange analysis system (FitMate Pro, Cosmed, Rome, Italy)26. While the FitMate Pro system reliably estimates VO₂peak, it does not measure carbon dioxide output (V̇CO₂), respiratory exchange ratio (RER), ventilatory efficiency (VE/V̇CO₂ slope), or anaerobic threshold. As such, these variables were not recorded in this study.

Prior to each testing session, the gas analysis system underwent an automated calibration routine, and the turbine flow meter was periodically calibrated with a 3 L precision syringe, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, to ensure optimal measurement accuracy and reproducibility.

Muscle hemodynamic measurements

Muscle hemodynamics were continuously monitored in real time using near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS; Portamon, Artinis Medical Systems, Gelderland, Netherlands) during the exercise protocol. NIRS is a noninvasive method that assesses tissue oxygenation by transmitting light at continuous wavelengths through paired optodes. The probe was placed over the right VL, positioned at the midpoint between the greater trochanter and the lateral epicondyle, and aligned with the muscle’s longitudinal axis. To ensure measurement stability and reduce external interference, the probes were secured with adhesive tape and covered with a black bandage to block ambient light and minimize optical signal loss. The interoptode distance was set to 40 mm, and data were acquired at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz.

Hemodynamic parameters were derived using the modified Beer–Lambert law, quantifying changes in (i) muscle oxygenation (oxyhemoglobin [∆O2Hb]), (ii) deoxygenation due to O2 extraction (deoxyhemoglobin [∆HHb]), and calculated the changes in regional blood volume (total hemoglobin [∆THb], which was the arithmetical sum of ∆O2Hb and ∆HHb)27. The ArtinisPortamon NIRS device used in this study does not provide Tissue Saturation Index (TSI). To facilitate standardized comparisons, NIRS signals were normalized relative to a 1-minute baseline period recorded immediately before the beginning of the exercise test (arbitrarily defined as zero µM).

The methodological considerations, advantages, and limitations of NIRS in assessing muscle hemodynamics and oxygenation have been extensively documented in the literature (Rooks, Thom et al. 2010).

Statistical analysis

Statistics were calculated using Statistica 12 software. Results are reported as mean ± SD. The sample size was determined a priori and was consistent with the trial registration. The calculation was based on functional/aerobic capacity evidence in ESRD patients receiving NMES. Using published changes in 6-minute walk distance (6MWT) following NMES28, the minimum required sample size was estimated using G*Power (G*power, version 3.1.9.4) with α = 0.05 and power (1–β) = 0.80, resulting in a minimum of 10 participants per group. To account for potential dropouts and non-adherence, we aimed to recruit at least 12 participants per group.

Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using the independent samplest-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. A two-way ANOVA (Group × Intervention [pre vs. post]) was used to analyze cardiopulmonary outcomes (VO2peak, MAP, VE, HR, and RPE), and the primary basis for inference was the Group × Intervention interaction effect. When a significant interaction was observed, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons were performed.

For NIRS-derived variables, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (Exercise intensity × Intervention) was conducted separately within each group at the same relative exercise intensities (10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% of VO2peak). This approach was chosen because the large number of repeated measures across exercise intensities, combined with the limited sample size, reduced the feasibility of a unified model including a group factor. Therefore, NIRS findings should be interpreted cautiously, as direct between-group comparisons were not formally tested within a single model. Partial eta squared (ηp2; ANOVA) was calculated to estimate effect sizes, with values representing small (< 0.13), medium (≥ 0.13, < 0.26) and large (≥ 0.26)29. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically.

Results

Given the pilot nature of the study and the limited sample size, the results should be interpreted as exploratory.

Participants

Six patients were excluded based on predefined criteria: active coronary artery disease (n = 1), interdialytic fluid accumulation > 3 L (n = 1), intradialytic hypertension (systolic ≥ 180 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 95 mmHg; n = 2), hemoglobin < 9.0 g/dL (n = 1), and recent ischemic cardiac event (n = 1). Of the 30 patients who initiated the study procedures, six did not complete the intervention for personal reasons or by voluntary withdrawal.Accordingly, the final analysis was conducted on 24 male patients (NSTG: 38.6 ± 2.5 years; CG: 40.6 ± 3.2 years), with no significant between-group difference in age at baseline (p = 0.87) (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Vastus lateralis hemodynamic and oxygenation

Because NIRS data were analyzed separately within each group, no formal between-group comparisons can be made. Therefore, the following results describe within-group pre–post responses only and should not be used for causal inference between conditions.Our results showed that ΔO2Hb decreased from baseline to the end of exercise in both groups (p < 0.01), and showed a significant Interaction effect (Exercise x Intervention) in the NSTG (p < 0.05; ηp² = 0.4). Discussion

This study aimed to examine the effects of NMES on muscle oxygenation and aerobic capacity in patients with ESRD. Specifically, we investigated the impact of a 12-week NMES intervention during HD sessions on VO2peak, muscle oxygenation (ΔO₂Hb), oxygen extraction (ΔHHb), and regional blood volume (ΔTHb) during a maximal incremental exercise test. The main findings of this pilot trial suggest that NMES improved VO2peak and muscle oxygen extraction during exercise in patients with ESRD. However, these findings should be interpreted within the context of a pilot study and therefore remain preliminary. In addition, findings of the present study are derived from a per-protocol analysis, and the exclusion of non-adherent participants may have introduced attrition bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of the observed effects.

Aerobic fitness

VO2peak is a well-established predictor of cardiovascular health and a key determinant of functional capacity in both healthy individuals and patients with chronic diseases, including ESRD30. In the NSTG, VO₂peak increased by 6.3 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, representing a 27% improvement, surpassing the threshold generally regarded as clinically meaningful in the ESRD population. This increase is consistent with previous studies reporting that NMES enhances aerobic capacity in populations with reduced physical capacity31,32. The observed improvement may reflect a combination of enhanced peripheral oxygen extraction efficiency and increased muscle oxidative capacity, although these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study. Prior research has shown that NMES preferentially activates type I fibers and may stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis (Mancinelli, Toniolo et al., 2019). Although not directly measured in this study, previous literature suggests that NMES may be associated with adaptations such as increased mitochondrial enzyme activity and capillarization, as suggested in previous literature33,34 although these were not directly assessed in the present study.

Interestingly, despite the significant improvement in VO2peak, no changes were observed in VEmaxand HRmax. In the absence of direct measurements of cardiac output or stroke volume, it remains unclear whether central adaptations contributed to the observed improvements in VO2peak. However, the absence of changes in HRmax, together with changes in NIRS-derived variables, may suggest a contribution of peripheral mechanisms; however, central adaptations cannot be excluded. Importantly, the interpretation of cardiopulmonary outcomes was based on the significant Group × Intervention interaction effects, supporting a greater improvement in the NSTG than in the CG.

Muscle oxygenation and hemodynamics

Within-group analyses showed an increase in ΔO₂Hb following the intervention in the NMES group, which may suggest enhanced oxygen delivery during exercise. However, because no formal between-group comparison was performed, this observation should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be attributed definitively to the NMES intervention. This improvement may be attributable to increased capillary density or enhanced microvascular function, although these adaptations were not directly assessed. Supporting this interpretation, previous studies have shown that NMES increases angiogenic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor, and promotes capillarization in skeletal muscle tissue35.

In addition, the increase in ΔHHb observed within the NMES group may indicate a potential improvement in muscle oxygen extraction capacity. However, given the absence of a formal between-group statistical comparison, this finding should be considered exploratory and not interpreted as definitive evidence of an NMES-specific effect. In ESRD, factors, such as reduced microvascular density, muscle atrophy, and mitochondrial dysfunction, contribute to impaired muscle oxygen extraction, ultimately leading to exercise intolerance36. Previous studies have reported that patients with ESRD exhibit a diminished ability to extract oxygen during exercise, often manifesting as early-onset fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance37,38. Therefore, the enhancement in ΔHHb in the NSTG is particularly noteworthy, suggestingthat NMES may help mitigate these physiological impairments. This observation may be related to mechanisms such as improved peripheral oxygen extraction efficiency, potentially associated with upregulation of oxidative enzymes (e.g., citrate synthase, cytochrome c oxidase)as demonstrated in prior NMES studies31,39, although these molecular mechanisms were not assessed in the present study.

Contrary to our expectations, ΔTHb did not change significantly following the intervention. Several plausible explanations may account for this observation. First, the absence of change in ΔTHb could indicate a limited physiological response in blood volume or may reflect technical limitations of NIRS in detecting microvascular changes during incremental exercise. Second, the lack of significant change in ΔTHb suggests that NMES may not have influenced total blood volume in the active muscle, but rather improved tissue oxygen utilization efficiency. This interpretation aligns with findings by Katajiri et al. (2024), who reported that NMES improve microvascular oxygen extraction without necessarily increasing local blood flow40. Third, the unchanged ΔTHb may reflect compensatory cardiovascular system mechanisms commonly observed in ESRD patients. Due to limited cardiac output and impairedmicrovascular responsiveness, patients with ESRD often rely on peripheral adaptations, such as increased capillary density and improved arteriovenous oxygen difference, to maintain oxygen delivery during exercise41. The adaptations induced by NMES are likely tooccur within this framework, highlighting its potential to enhance peripheral hemodynamics in a manner that support systemic cardiovascular limitations.

Clinical implications

Given the well-documented challenges in improving physical function in the ESRD population, NMES is a practical and feasible intervention that can be readily integrated into routine hemodialysis care. The intervention can be applied during standard dialysis sessions without requiring additional time commitment or active patient participation, making it particularly suitable for patients with limited exercise tolerance, fatigue, or comorbidities. Implementation in clinical settings would require minimal equipment, appropriate staff training, and basic supervision to ensure correct electrode placement and progression of stimulation intensity, suggesting good scalability across dialysis centers.

From a clinical perspective, the observed improvements in VO2peak and muscle oxygen extraction are particularly important although their direct impact on clinical outcomes remains to be established. Enhancing aerobic fitness may improve the ability to perform daily activities and reduced physical deconditioning; however, these potential benefits were not directly assessed in the present study.

Although quality of life was not directly assessed in the present study, it is possible that improvements in physical capacity could translate into patient-centered benefits. However, this remains speculative and cannot be inferred from the current data. Future studies should directly assess quality of life using validated instruments (e.g., KDQOL or SF-36) to confirm these potential effects.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 24) limits the statistical study’s statistical power, particularly for secondary outcomes, and increases the risk of both type I and type II errors. Therefore, this study should be considered as a pilot randomized controlled trial. In this context, the magnitude of the observed effects, particularly for aerobic capacity, may be overestimated and should be interpreted with caution. Larger, adequately powered multicenter trials are required to confirm these findings. Second, the study enrolled only male patients, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Sex-related differences in muscle morphology, hormonal profile, and neuromuscular responses may influence adaptations to NMES; therefore, the present results cannot be directly extrapolated to female patients with ESRD. Future studies should include both sexes to determine whether responses to NMES differ between men and women. Third, although improvements in VO2peak and muscle oxygenation were observed, the underlying mechanisms were not directly investigated.Future research should incorporate muscle biopsies and molecular analyses to better elucidate the mechanisms driving these adaptations. Fourth, although HRmax remained unchanged and ΔHHb increased, indicating a predominantly peripheral adaptation, the lack of direct measurements of central responses limits the interpretation of the potential of cardiovascular involvement. Fifth, the metabolic data collected using the FitMate Pro system had notable constraints. Due to protocol limitations, the system could not calculate ΔVO₂/ΔW, did not provide breath-by-breath VO₂ data, and reported VO2peak only relative to body weight. Additionally, it did not capture key variables such as VCO2, respiratory exchange ratio (RER), or anaerobic threshold, limiting the ability to differentiate between central and peripheral contributions to the observed adaptations.Another limitation is that serum potassium levels were not monitored as part of the study protocol. Given the potential influence of potassium balance on muscle function, fatigue, and oxygen utilization in patients undergoing hemodialysis, we cannot exclude the possibility that individual differences in potassium status or dietary compliance may have contributed, in part, to the observed responses. Future studies should include relevant biochemical monitoring, including serum potassium, to better isolate the specific effects of NMES. In addition, although baseline age was comparable between groups, the potential influence of age-related differences in muscle health or integrity on responsiveness to NMES cannot be excluded and should be explored in future studies with larger samples. Moreover, physical activity levels were assessed only at baseline using a standardized questionnaire and were not reassessed following the intervention. Although participants were instructed to maintain their usual lifestyle and not to modify their physical activity habits, we cannot exclude the possibility that changes in daily activity levels may have contributed, in part, to the observed improvements. In addition, the analysis was conducted using a per-protocol approach, as participants who did not meet adherence criteria or withdrew from the study were excluded. This deviates from the intention-to-treat principle recommended for randomized controlled trials and introduces a risk of attrition bias. Specifically, excluding non-adherent participants may lead to an overestimation of the intervention effects, as the analysis is restricted to individuals who were able to complete the protocol. Therefore, the magnitude of the observed effects should be interpreted with caution. Finally, because NMES is visibly apparent intervention, neither participants nor assessors could be blinded. Therefore, the lack of a sham control group may have introduced observation bias and future trials should consider incorporating placebo-controlled designs to mitigate this risk.In addition, although cardiopulmonary outcomes were analyzed based on Group × Intervention interaction effects, NIRS-derived variables were analyzed separately within each group rather than using a unified model including the group factor. This analytical approach was selected because of the limited sample size and the complexity of repeated measures across multiple exercise intensities, but it limits direct between-group comparison and weakens causal inference for the NIRS findings.

Conclusion

This pilot randomized controlled trial suggests that a 12-week intradialytic NMES program may improve aerobic capacity and selected indices of skeletal muscle oxygen utilization in patients with ESRD undergoing hemodialysis. Specifically, NMES was associated with improvements in peak oxygen uptake and maximal aerobic power, together with alterations in NIRS-derived responses observed within the NSTG group; however, given the analytical limitations, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be considered definitive evidence of between-group differences. In addition, the physiological mechanisms underlying these changes were not directly measured; therefore, any interpretation involving peripheral muscular adaptations remains hypothetical. Overall, the present findings support the feasibility and potential clinical relevance of intradialytic NMES, however, these findings remain preliminary and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered randomized controlled trials.
