The use of cold-stored platelets to provide hemostasis in dogs with thrombocytopenia
Authors: Shannon A Monaco, Jillian M Haines, Sarah C Guess, Sybil M Fiedler, K Jane Wardrop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlatelet transfusion can be lifesaving in dogs with severe thrombocytopenia and hemorrhage, but platelet products are limited by short shelf life and contamination risk. Cold-stored platelets (CSP) may provide a practical alternative, but in vivo hemostatic efficacy in dogs is unknown.Hypothesis/ObjectivesEvaluate the clinical hemostatic efficacy and safety of CSP transfusions in thrombocytopenic dogs.AnimalsTwenty-four thrombocytopenic dogs from a veterinary teaching hospital population that received CSP transfusions between March 2022 and October 2024.MethodsRetrospective cohort study. Medical records of dogs receiving platelet concentrate stored at 4 °C for up to 21 days were reviewed. Hemorrhage severity was assessed using a modified Daily Canine Bleeding Assessment Tool (DOGiBAT) score before transfusion and at 1 and 4 h post-transfusion. Primary outcomes included change in bleeding score, clinical response, transfusion reactions, and survival to discharge.ResultsTwenty-four dogs received 27 CSP transfusions. Mean pre-transfusion platelet count was 23 × 103/μL (range, 2-107 × 103/μL) and mean post-transfusion count was 28 × 103/μL (range, 2-100 × 103/μL; P = .84). Mean DOGiBAT score decreased from 3.15 ± 2.4 at baseline to 2.21 ± 2.1 at 4 (mean change, −0.94; P = .002). Clinical improvement occurred in 19 of 27 transfusions (70%). No transfusion reactions were documented. Nineteen of 24 dogs (79%) survived to discharge.Conclusions and clinical importanceCold-stored platelets were well-tolerated and associated with decreased bleeding severity in thrombocytopenic dogs, supporting CSP as a practical platelet transfusion option for clinical hemostatic support.

Introduction

Spontaneous hemorrhage becomes a substantial risk in dogs when platelet counts fall below a critical threshold of 30 000-50 000/μL.1,2 In these cases, platelet transfusion may be lifesaving for active hemorrhage, surgical stabilization, massive transfusion protocols, or prophylaxis before invasive procedures.2,3 Despite these indications, platelet transfusion remains underutilized in veterinary medicine because of limited product availability, lack of standard protocols, and uncertainty regarding efficacy and safety.4

Historically, available canine platelet products include fresh whole blood, platelet-rich plasma (PRP), centrifuged platelet concentrate (PC), apheresis platelets, cryopreserved platelets (CPP), and lyophilized platelets (LP). Each preparation provides unique advantages and limitations, but most are constrained by short shelf life, contamination risk, and the need for specialized equipment.5 Traditionally, PC in veterinary medicine is stored at room temperature (20-24 °C) under constant agitation to preserve function.6 However, room temperature storage is associated with a limited shelf life of approximately 5 days and increased risk of bacterial proliferation.5,7

Cold-stored platelets (CSP), maintained at 1-6 °C, have emerged as a potential alternative. Refrigerated storage may enhance hemostatic function, extend storage duration, decrease bacterial growth, simplify transport, and mitigate storage lesions.8,9 In human medicine, CSP use expanded during the COVID-19 pandemic in response to blood bank shortages. Reports demonstrated adequate hemostasis and no apparent increase in transfusion reactions or infections.10,11 Although CSP exhibit decreased circulatory half-life compared with room temperature platelets, they display enhanced aggregation and faster clot formation, which may offset their shorter survival.9,12–14

Although platelet transfusions have been extensively studied in human transfusion medicine, there is a paucity of data evaluating the in vivo efficacy of CSP in veterinary patients. Most studies in dogs have focused on PC storage characteristics or on alternative products such as CPP or LP. To date, no studies have determined whether CSP provide clinically meaningful hemostasis in thrombocytopenic dogs. Most recently, one study described the use and potential benefits of LP for both thrombocytopenia-associated and non-thrombocytopenia-associated hemorrhage in dogs.15

Our objective was to evaluate the in vivo hemostatic efficacy of CSP in thrombocytopenic dogs. We hypothesized that transfusion of CSP, when clinically indicated, would improve clinical outcomes by providing effective hemostasis. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the hemostatic response of thrombocytopenic dogs to transfused PC that has been stored at 4 °C for up to 21 days using a modified Daily Canine Bleeding Assessment Tool (DOGiBAT) bleeding score, clinical observations, and serial platelet counts.11 A secondary objective was to evaluate the safety of CSP transfusions in dogs.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A retrospective review identified thrombocytopenic dogs at the Washington State University Veterinary Teaching Hospital receiving CSP transfusions from March 2022 through October 2024. Patients were identified from transfusion logs and included if thrombocytopenic and transfused. Patients were excluded if records did not permit assessment of transfusion indication, bleeding score, or outcome.

Data collected included age, breed, sex, body weight, blood type, primary disease, indication for transfusion, pre-transfusion platelet count, hospitalization duration, and survival to discharge. The CSP data included volume, platelet count, collection and transfusion dates, and storage duration.

Platelet product preparation and storage

Platelet concentrates were prepared from 450 mL whole blood by double centrifugation and suspended in 50 mL plasma or 35% plasma and 65% platelet additive solution (Plasma-Lyte A) to a volume of 60 mL.3 After protocol changes, all units were suspended in plasma.3,16,17 Units were stored at 4 °C without agitation for up to 21 days. Dogs received dog erythrocyte antigen (DEA) 1-negative or type-matched units through a 150-200 μm blood product filter dosed at one unit per 10 kg over 30-60 min.5

Monitoring and adverse events

Transfusion decisions were made by the primary clinician. Procedures were performed within 1-2 h of CSP administration. Post-transfusion platelet counts were obtained 1 h later. Adverse events were recorded from medical records; acute and delayed reactions were not distinguished.

Bleeding assessment

Hemorrhage severity was assessed using a modified DOGiBAT score (Table S1).11 Cavitary bleeding was added to the original score and assessed by thoracic and abdominal point-of-care ultrasonography. Change in cavitary fluid was determined subjectively by the clinician. Scores were recorded at baseline, and 1 and 4 h post-transfusion and reconstructed from records when not documented. Details are provided in Appendix.

Clinical response and outcomes

Complete response was defined as resolution of bleeding, a ≥2-point DOGiBAT decrease or both; partial response as improvement without resolution or a ≥1-point decrease; and no response as no improvement. Outcomes included DOGiBAT change, response category, adverse events, and survival to discharge.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 30.0.0.0.18 Normality was assessed visually and by Kolmogorov–Smirnov testing. Normally distributed data are reported as mean ± SD and non-normally distributed data as median (interquartile range [IQR]). Continuous variables were analyzed using repeated-measures 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and linear regression, as appropriate. Missing data were managed by pairwise exclusion. Statistical significance was set at 2-tailed P < .05.

Results

Study population

Twenty-four dogs met inclusion criteria, accounting for 27 transfusion events with CSP concentrate. The mean age was 6.5 years (range, 1-12 years), and the mean body weight was 23.8 kg (range, 3.8-53 kg). The cohort included 13 mixed breed dogs, 2 German shepherd dogs, and 9 dogs of various other breeds, including a miniature schnauzer, English bulldog, Pomeranian, Havanese, Swiss Mountain dog, Boston terrier, golden retriever, Labrador retriever and Australian shepherd. There were 12 male neutered, 1 male intact, 8 female spayed, and 3 female intact dogs. Blood type was available for all dogs. Seventeen of 24 dogs were DEA positive and 7 dogs were DEA negative. Three of the 24 dogs received an additional CSP transfusion.

Immune-mediated thrombocytopenia (ITP) was the most common primary disease process (n = 9), followed by neoplasia (n = 7). Additional primary disease processes included portosystemic shunts (n = 2), ruptured gallbladder mucocele (n = 1), toxin-induced hepatic failure (n = 1), dystocia (n = 1), chronic enteropathy with gastroduodenal ulceration (n = 1), gastric dilatation-volvulus (n = 1), and rodenticide ingestion (n = 1). Four dogs were suspected to have disseminated intravascular coagulation as a complication of their primary disease processes. The CSP transfusions were administered for spontaneous bleeding (n = 9), surgical procedures (n = 6), and minimally invasive procedures (MIP), (eg, peripherally inserted central catheter placement, fine needle aspiration, urinary catheterization; n = 12).

Cold-stored platelet product characteristics

The CSP units had a mean platelet count of 1.62 × 106/μL. Of the 27 transfusion events, 21 were CSP suspended in plasma, 5 were CSP suspended in platelet additive solution (Plasma-Lyte A), and 1 was a mix of both unit types. The mean storage duration of the CSP units before transfusion was 12.4 days (range, 1-21 days).

Platelet dose

Platelet dose was calculated for each transfusion event based on the estimated number of platelets administered relative to patient body surface area. The median calculated platelet dose was 2.27 × 1011 platelets/m2 (range 0.59-5.88 × 1011 platelets/m2).

Additional transfusion products administered

Nine dogs received additional blood products in addition to CSP administration. Packed red blood cells (pRBC) were the most commonly administered product. Three dogs received 1 unit of pRBC concurrently with CSP administration, including 1 dog that also received 3 units of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) intraoperatively. One dog received 1 unit of pRBC approximately 1 h after CSP, and another received 1 unit after CSP (exact timing not specified). Two dogs received 1 unit of pRBC on the same day as CSP, but the precise timing relative to CSP was not available. One dog received multiple pRBC and plasma transfusions on the same day as CSP (timing unavailable) and subsequently received a whole blood transfusion 2 days later.

Plasma products also were administered in several cases. One dog received 1 unit of FFP 3 h before CSP, 1 dog received 3 units of FFP concurrently with CSP, and another received FFP at 6 mL/kg on the same day as CSP (timing unavailable).

In addition, 2 dogs received a LP product (Stable Plate Rx; BodeVet, Rockville, Maryland, 1.5 × 109 particles per mL). In one case, the dog received the LP between 1 and 4 h after CSP was given and therefore the 4 h DOGiBAT score was not included for analysis. In the second case, the LP was given 2 days before the CSP transfusion.

Platelet counts and bleeding scores

Patient pre-transfusion platelet counts ranged from 2 to 107 × 103/μL, with a median platelet count of 17 × 103/μL (IQR, 31 × 103/μL). Post-transfusion platelet counts measured approximately 1 h after administration ranged from 2 to 100 × 103/μL, with a median platelet count of 23.5 × 103/μL (IQR, 33.5 × 103/μL). No significant difference was identified between pre- and post-transfusion results (P = .84). No significant correlation was found between post-platelet count and average platelet storage time (R = 0.334, P = .1).

Of the 24 dogs that were included, 6 were thrombocytopenic but did not have clinical evidence of bleeding based on their modified DOGiBAT score before receiving CSP transfusions. For those with clinical evidence of bleeding, DOGiBAT scores were normally distributed and decreased significantly after CSP administration. The mean baseline score was 3.15 (range, 0-10; SD = 2.73), decreasing to 3.00 (range, 0-10; SD = 2.67) at 1-h post-transfusion and to 2.21 (range, 0-8; SD = 2.23) at 4 h. A significant decrease in bleeding score was observed between baseline and 4-h measurements (P = .002). No significant difference was seen in DOGiBAT scores between baseline and 1 h (P = .424) nor DOGiBAT scores between 1 and 4 h (P = .125). Individual DOGiBAT scores are presented in Table S2.

Clinical response and adverse events

Clinical improvement, defined as decreased or absent spontaneous hemorrhage or absence of procedure-associated bleeding, was documented in 19 of 27 transfusion events (70%) within 4 h. Partial improvement was noted in 7 of 27 events (26%). One case (4%) was classified as indeterminate because of cardiopulmonary arrest soon after transfusion, attributed to suspected neoplasia.

No transfusion reactions or acute adverse events were reported in any of the 27 transfusion events and no infectious, hypersensitivity, or febrile reactions were documented during or after any transfusion. Delayed procedure related bleeding events were suspected to have occurred in 3 dogs. Two dogs with ITP developed suspected hemoabdomen 12-18 h after splenic fine-needle aspiration; one stabilized after an additional CSP transfusion, and one stabilized without further intervention. Additionally, one surgical patient developed mild, self-limiting incisional bleeding 24 h after surgery.

Survival outcomes

Nineteen of 24 dogs (79%) survived to hospital discharge. Baseline and 4-h modified DOGiBAT scores were significantly higher in non-survivors than in survivors (baseline mean ± SD, 5.25 ± 2.7 vs. 2.26 ± 2.1; 4-h mean ± SD, 4.00 ± 2.8 vs. 1.47 ± 1.4; P = .03).

Survival to discharge was not associated with transfusion indication (P = .84). Within the subgroup of dogs with ITP, 6 dogs survived to discharge. Both baseline DOGiBAT score (P = .02) and 4-h DOGiBAT score (P = .002) were significantly associated with survival.

Discussion

Our retrospective study evaluated the clinical effects of transfused CSP concentrate in thrombocytopenic dogs and found that bleeding scores decreased over the first 4 h after transfusion, with most transfusion events accompanied by some degree of clinical improvement. Although platelet counts did not increase significantly after administration, many dogs demonstrated stabilization or a decrease in hemorrhage severity, suggesting that CSP may offer hemostatic support even in the absence of a measurable post-transfusion platelet count increase. These findings contribute preliminary clinical data to a field in which the in vivo effects of CSP in veterinary species have not yet been documented.

The lack of platelet count increase in our study is not unexpected. In both human and veterinary medicine, numerical platelet recovery often fails to reflect clinical response, especially in dogs with ITP, consumptive coagulopathies, or systemic inflammation.11,19 CSP are known to circulate for shorter durations than room temperature units, which likely contributed to the minimal count changes observed in our study.20 However, CSP retain, or in some cases enhance, functional attributes such as aggregation, adhesion, and contractile force.14 Prior work has shown that platelets stored at 4 °C can enhance clot quality compared with room temperature-stored platelets. Specifically, CSP were associated with stronger clot formation and altered fibrin structure, including a more compact fibrin network and increased fibrin branching and crosslinking. This effect was proposed to occur, at least in part, through increased binding of plasma factor XIII to the platelet surface, promoting factor XIII-mediated fibrin stabilization.14 Prior in vitro work has shown that cold storage is associated with production of larger extracellular vesicles over time, with increased extracellular vesicle concentration during prolonged storage. These vesicles may enhance coagulation by providing procoagulant phospholipid surfaces, shortening clot formation time and contributing to improved clot firmness. Although extracellular vesicles were not evaluated in our study, they represent a plausible mechanism by which CSP could support hemostasis without producing a measurable increase in circulating platelet count.21 These functional properties may help explain the improvement in bleeding scores seen in our study, although causation cannot be determined. Although a causal effect cannot be confirmed, the temporal relationship between CSP administration and decreasing bleeding scores suggests that CSP did not impair clot formation and may have supported stabilization in some dogs.

Although platelet transfusion dosing has been evaluated more extensively in human medicine, the optimal platelet dose remains unclear, even in people. In a large, randomized trial, low-, medium-, and high-dose prophylactic platelet transfusions resulted in similar bleeding outcomes, suggesting that higher platelet doses may not necessarily translate into improved clinical benefit.22 In veterinary medicine, available evidence is much more limited, and platelet dosing recommendations for dogs are largely extrapolated from data obtained in humans. In our study, dogs received a variable platelet dose considering the unit per kilogram transfusion protocol used, and our study did not determine the ideal or most effective platelet dose for dogs. Additional prospective studies are needed to determine the platelet dose that provides the largest hemostatic benefit while avoiding unnecessary transfusions.

Equally important is the absence of documented transfusion reactions across all 27 CSP transfusions. Cold storage has been shown to limit the accumulation of leukocyte-derived cytokines and metabolic by-products and to exert a bacteriostatic effect, changes that may decrease the likelihood of febrile or septic transfusion reactions.23 Although retrospective recognition of mild reactions is inherently imperfect, no infectious, febrile, allergic, or hemolytic events were identified in our cohort. These findings provide supportive, but not conclusive, evidence that CSP were clinically well tolerated under the conditions of our study. Similar observations have been reported in human medicine, where CSP use expanded during COVID-19-related platelet shortages without an apparent increase in transfusion complications.10 Establishing a lack of harm is an essential early step when evaluating new platelet storage strategies in veterinary practice.

The association between lower bleeding scores and survival, including within the ITP subset, is consistent with veterinary studies demonstrating that the presence and severity of hemorrhage are important prognostic indicators in dogs with thrombocytopenia and ITP.11,19,24 Although survival is influenced by many factors beyond transfusion choice, this finding highlights the clinical value of structured bleeding assessment tools and reinforces that hemorrhage severity remains a major determinant of outcome regardless of the product used. Prospective studies are warranted to determine whether earlier CSP administration, or more aggressive CSP-based transfusion strategies, can improve outcomes in dogs with severe bleeding.

Three dogs in our cohort developed bleeding after CSP transfusion. Two dogs with ITP developed suspected hemoabdomen 12-18 h after splenic fine-needle aspiration, and one surgical patient developed mild, self-limiting incisional bleeding approximately 24 h after surgery. In each case, bleeding occurred hours after the anticipated hemostatic challenge rather than immediately after product administration, and no infectious, hypersensitivity, or febrile reactions were documented during or after transfusion. Given the expected limited in vivo persistence of CSP, these events may reflect the anticipated duration of effect of CSP in circulation rather than implying lack of efficacy. These cases highlight the importance of transfusion timing and raise the possibility that repeat dosing or adjunctive hemostatic support may be needed in dogs with ongoing bleeding risk.

The administration of additional blood products in a substantial proportion of dogs represents a potential limitation of our study. In several cases, pRBC, plasma products, or whole blood were administered concurrently with or in close proximity to CSP transfusion. The concurrent use of other blood components may have contributed to stabilization through improved oxygen-carrying capacity, coagulation factor replacement, or volume expansion. Two dogs received LP in addition to CSP during hospitalization, which could have affected hospitalization time and survival data. However, in the majority of cases, CSP was the only platelet product administered.

Modification of the DOGiBAT scoring system represents an additional limitation of our study. The original DOGiBAT score does not include a specific category for cavitary or internal bleeding, which was clinically relevant in our patient population because several dogs received CSP in the setting of suspected hemoabdomen, abdominal procedures, or other invasive interventions. Excluding this category may have underestimated clinically relevant hemorrhage, particularly in patients with bleeding that was not externally visible. Assessment of cavitary bleeding was therefore added to better characterize hemorrhage severity and clinical response to CSP transfusion in this population. However, because this modification has not been validated, this factor should be considered when interpreting the results.

Our findings should be interpreted within the context of the study’s design. The retrospective nature of the study limited control over transfusion timing, concurrent interventions, completeness of medical records, and modification of the DOGiBAT scoring system may have introduced some variability. The unweighted scoring system does not fully capture the relative clinical importance of different bleeding sites, and the absence of a contemporaneous room temperature platelet group precludes direct comparison of storage methods. Concurrent anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive medications administered at the time of CSP transfusion may have limited detection of transfusion reactions in 2 dogs. One dog was chronically receiving prednisone (2.6 mg/kg PO q24h) and cyclosporine (10 mg/kg PO q12h), and a second dog had received 2 doses of dexamethasone (0.2 mg/kg IV q24h).

Variation in underlying disease represents an inherent limitation, because hemostatic responses may differ among dogs with immune-mediated platelet destruction, neoplasia, or consumptive coagulopathies. However, this variability reflects real-world clinical practice, where platelet transfusions are used across diverse disease processes and supports the broader applicability of our findings.

Our study suggests that CSP can be safely administered to thrombocytopenic dogs with potential hemostatic benefit. Improvements in bleeding scores, observed clinical responses, and absence of transfusion reactions support CSP’s feasibility as a transfusion option in thrombocytopenic dogs. Overall, CSP represents a safe and practical option for providing hemostasis in thrombocytopenic patients.
