Thermochemical upcycling of banana stem fibers into biochar and activated carbon for rapid, high-capacity removal of methylene blue from water
Authors: S L Athira, K Swarnalatha, Sneha Gautam, Baehyun Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe toxicity and environmental persistence of synthetic dyes present significant challenges for the treatment and disposal of dye-contaminated wastewater. In this context, agricultural residues as bio-based adsorbents are gaining increasing significance due to their lower cost and high adsorption capacity. The feasibility of utilizing banana fibers, which are available in abundance as agriculture waste, and are rich in cellulose and hydroxyl functional groups is investigated as an adsorbent for cationic dye removal in the present study. Low surface area, less reusability potential and low adsorption capacity of raw fibers limits its applicability in adsorption process. Therefore, this study explores the potential of thermochemically upcycled banana fibers in removing methylene blue, a commonly used dye, from aqueous solutions. The technique adopted for upcycling was converting raw banana fiber (RBF) into biochar (BBF) and activated carbon (ABF), thereby enhancing its adsorption capacity. Adsorbent surface characterization was performed using SEM, BET, and FTIR analyses. The thermochemical treatment markedly increased the surface area of the adsorbents, with increases of approximately 183% for BBF and 362% for ABF relative to RBF. Isotherm analyses further revealed that, under optimal conditions, the adsorption capacity increased by 51.01% for BBF and 98.63% for ABF, indicating a significant enhancement in adsorption performance after thermochemical modification. In addition, ABF exhibited superior regeneration stability compared with BBF and RBF. Overall, these findings demonstrate the potential of thermochemical upcycling as a sustainable strategy for valorizing waste banana stem fibers into effective adsorbents for treating dye-contaminated water.Keywords: Natural fiber, Adsorption, Methylene blue, Isotherm, Cationic dyeSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Deterioration of freshwater resources has emerged as one of the top ten global risks over the past decade, as recognized by the World Economic Forum1–3. Industrial effluents are one of the major contributors to water quality deterioration3–5. These effluents typically contain a variety of pollutants, including hazardous chemicals, toxic compounds, biodegradable materials, dissolved solids, and color-forming compounds, all of which present significant environmental challenges and adverse health effects if inadequately treated prior to discharge6–8. Among these pollutants, synthetic dyes are of particular concern because of their chemical stability and persistence, which render them resistant to conventional wastewater treatment techniques9. Many synthetic dyes pose serious environmental and health concerns. Numerous studies have reported that these dyes can be harmful, as they may exhibit toxic, mutagenic, and even carcinogenic effects. Therefore, their presence in water can pose serious threats to both aquatic organisms and humans exposed to contaminated sources10. Among these dyes, methylene blue (MB) is widely used in printing, chemical production, and pharmaceutical applications11,12. MB (C16H18ClN3S) is an aromatic compound with a stable aromatic ring structure, which contributes to its persistence in the environment. Effective removal of MB from wastewater prior to discharge is essential to minimize its negative impacts on ecosystems and public health11,13. Over the years, numerous studies have explored various methods for removing synthetic dyes from wastewater. Among these approaches, adsorption has emerged as one of the most effective and widely used techniques for dye removal14.

The application of conventional adsorbents is often constrained by high production and recovery costs, energy-intensive preparation processes, limited reuse potential, and operational challenges associated with large-scale implementation. Furthermore, the regeneration and disposal of spent adsorbents may result in secondary environmental contamination, thereby diminishing their eco-friendly nature15,16. Consequently, recent research has increasingly focused on the development of cost-effective adsorbents obtained from bioresources and waste materials, including agricultural residues6,12,17,18.

The presence of active surface functional groups in natural fibers makes them well-suited for adsorption-based treatment applications19,20. A wide range of agricultural and biomass-derived materials, including coir fiber21,22, bagasse fly ash,23,24 rice husk biochar25,26, papaya bark fiber27, jute fiber28, fiber aerogels and banana stem–based adsorbents, have demonstrated high removal efficiencies for various pollutants14,29–32. For example, Habimana et al.33 employed both raw and sodium hydroxide-modified banana fibers (BF) for the removal of ammonium and phosphate from slaughterhouse wastewater. Similarly, NaOH–H2O2-modified banana pseudo-stem fibers exhibited a maximum adsorption capacity of 23.57 mg/g for Reactive Red 195 removal at acidic pH29. Efficient bio-based adsorbents synthesized from banana peduncles have been investigated for Pb (II) removal34. Selimin et al.35 evaluated the adsorption performance of a chemically treated banana blossom peel adsorbent (0.1 M HCl and 5% w/v NaOH) for Cr (VI) removal, with the adsorbent exhibiting a wavy, shallow-dented surface. Zn based organic framework was immobilized onto bamboo fiber for MB dye removal and a 77% efficiency was achieved within 20 min of contact time36. Acrylic acid grafted sugarcane bagasse achieved 92% removal efficiency for MB37. Sugarcane bagasse-based biochar was used for the removal of crystal violet (CV) by Kumari et al.38 and obtained a maximum removal efficiency of 97.46% under optimized conditions. Coconut husk-derived partially oxidized graphene oxide was achieved 67.65% MB removal, at pH 10, 0.1 g/L adsorbent dosage and 15 mg/L initial dye concentration, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 14.73 mg/g39. Nipa et al.27 conducted experiments on papaya bark fiber for MB removal and the results shows that it has a maximum adsorption capacity of 66.67 mg/g. The present study aims to explore the effective upcycling of raw waste banana stem fibers using thermochemical techniques to produce sustainable adsorbents for cationic dye removal. These modified fibers are then evaluated for their efficiency in MB removal. Characterization studies, kinetic analyses and regeneration potential were also systematically examined to assess their potential for developing as a cost-effective and environmentally sustainable alternative for dye removal.

Materials and methods

All solutions used in this study were freshly prepared with distilled water to ensure experimental accuracy and consistency. MB (C16H18ClN3S) was selected as the model adsorbate. The solution pH was adjusted using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions and pH was measured using a calibrated pH meter. Raw banana stem fibers (RBF) were collected from a nearby local farm, washed, sundried, and were thermochemically upcycled into biochar from banana fibers (BBF) and activated carbon from banana fibers (ABF) to enhance their adsorptive capacity. All sample collections were conducted with the appropriate permissions from farm owners and in compliance with local guidelines.

Preparation of biosorbents for upcycling

Banana fibers were collected from stems obtained from nearby farms, then sliced, sun-dried, and ground to a particle size range of 2.36–1.18 mm. The processed fibers were subsequently dried under controlled heating at 105 °C for 24 h to remove any residual moisture and were designated as raw banana fiber (RBF). The cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin content of the banana fibers were determined in accordance with40, ASTM E-3503-25 and ASTM D-1166-21, respectively. RBF had a limited pore volume (0.006 cm3/g) and surface area (3.889 m2/g). In order to enhance its adsorption performance, RBF was converted to biochar which facilitate the decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose and enhances carbon content. To further enhance adsorption performance, phosphoric acid activation was also used. The temperatures for biochar and activated carbon preparation were fixed based on thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). This was done by heating the oven-dried (105 °C for 24 h) biomass (1.311 mg) under a nitrogen-controlled atmosphere at a uniform heating rate. The selected pyrolysis temperature was fixed beyond the primary devolatilization zone to ensure complete carbonization and thermal stability of the resulting biochar. BBF was prepared by subjecting oven-dried banana stem pieces to slow thermal decomposition in a muffle furnace, where the material was heated for 1 h41, followed by cooling to room temperature, grinding, sieving to 200 µm, and storage in an airtight container within a desiccator. In contrast, ABF was produced by chemically activating the raw fiber with phosphoric acid, followed by pyrolysis31.

Characterization of adsorbents

Comprehensive characterization of the developed adsorbents was performed to evaluate their surface characteristics, morphology, and pore structure, all of which significantly influence adsorption performance. The point of zero charge (pHpzc) was determined using the pH drift method42. In addition, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Spectrum IR Version 10.6.0), scanning electron microscopy (SEM; ZEISS EVO 18 Research), and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis (Nova Touch lx4) were employed to further characterize the adsorbents.

Adsorption studies

The effects of key operational parameters on adsorption were systematically investigated through optimization experiments43. The variables examined included pH (2–12), contact time (15 min to 3 h), adsorbent dosage (0.2–5 g/L), and initial MB concentration (10–50 mg/L). The samples were then analyzed in a UV–visible spectrophotometer at 665 nm, and the efficiency of the adsorbents for dye removal was subsequently determined21. The experiments were conducted in triplicates and the mean values were used in adsorption studies.

Regeneration and reuse potential

Reusability of an adsorbent is of high significance in case of industrial applications. Thus, to evaluates the stability and practical applicability of the adsorbent in real-world conditions, their regeneration and reuse potential was investigated. The same adsorbent was used multiple times, separated from solution and washed with distilled water thoroughly. These spent adsorbents were then regenerated in 100 mL of KOH solution (0.1 M) and agitated at 150 rpm using an orbital shaker for 60 min. The alkaline medium facilitates the release of adsorbed dye molecules by altering the adsorbent’s surface charge and weakening electrostatic interactions between the dye and the surface44. The adsorption–desorption cycle was repeated over multiple iterations to assess the material’s stability and reusability, and the adsorption efficiency was determined after each regeneration cycle.

Adsorption isotherm study

Adsorption isotherm experiments were conducted on RBF, BBF, and ABF at five initial dye concentrations (10–50 ppm). The equilibrium data were analysed using four widely applied adsorption isotherm models: Langmuir14, Freundlich45, Temkin46 and Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R)47.

Adsorption kinetic models

Kinetic studies were conducted through batch experiments following the same procedures adopted for the isotherm analysis. The adsorption data were fitted to pseudo-first-order and second-order kinetic models48. The role of intraparticle diffusion in governing the overall adsorption process was also evaluated49.

Results and discussion

Chemical composition of raw banana fibers

Banana fibers used are found rich in cellulose (66.20% of the total biomass). The high abundance of hydroxyl functional groups in cellulose can facilitate in the removal of cationic dyes. Hemicellulose, the second most abundant component, was found as 22.30% of the fiber, while lignin content was relatively low (2%). Low lignin content of banana fibers is a significant advantage compared to other biosorbents, as lignin is hydrophobic and can hinder adsorption by blocking active sites and reducing fiber wettability50–52,53. Moreover, minimal lignin content reduces the severity of chemical pretreatment required for fiber purification54, thus enhancing the environmental sustainability and cost-effectiveness of banana fibers as a raw material.

FTIR analysis of adsorbents

The FTIR spectra of RBF, BBF, and ABF prior to adsorption (Fig. 1) were analyzed to identify the surface-associated functional groups55 and to evaluate the influence of thermal and chemical modifications on the fiber chemistry. Furthermore, changes in the spectra observed after adsorption (Fig. 2) were used to evaluate shifts in peak position, intensity, and functional group interactions resulting from the adsorption process. O–H stretching was found at 3300–3400 cm-1 associated with the cellulose, hemicellulose and lignin present in the RBF56–59. The band near 2920–2850 cm-1 corresponds to the C–H stretching vibration56,58,59. A noticeable peak around ~ 1630 cm⁻1 was assigned to C=O stretching of ester groups presents in hemicellulose, while the band near ~ 1400 cm⁻1 was associated with aromatic C=C stretching vibrations56. Reduced peak in BBF near O–H region indicates the loss of hydroxyl group during pyrolysis. Disappearance of C=O stretching in BBF indicates the decomposition of hemicellulose. Carbonization has resulted in formation of aromatic conjugated structures with more stable carbonaceous matrix enriched with sp2 hybridized carbon which is evident from the peak at ~ 1400 cm⁻1 and it contains delocalized π electrons. The band at 900–1200 cm -1 primarily represent C–O stretching and is observed in all adsorbents58,59. But a slight broadening of this peak in ABF indicates the additional contribution from P-O-C and P=O functional groups60. All the three adsorbents show similar functional groups but noticeable changes occurred in peak represents the impact of thermo-chemical modification. O–H and C=O functional groups in adsorbents is advantageous as it induces acidic and basic characteristics to adsorbent which helps in dye adsorption31.

FTIR spectra of adsorbents before adsorption.

FTIR spectra of adsorbents before and after adsorption.

The FTIR spectral profiles of the adsorbent materials were compared with that after MB adsorption (Fig. 2) to evaluate changes in surface functional groups after dye uptake. Following adsorption, noticeable shifts, intensity reductions, and slight peak broadening indicate that these functional groups are involved in electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions with MB molecules61. The more pronounced spectral changes were observed for ABF, especially in the 1600–1000 cm⁻1 region, further confirming stronger adsorbent–adsorbate interactions.

Thermogravimetric (TGA) analysis

TGA and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) analysis were performed to determine the appropriate temperatures for the preparation of BBF and ABF from RBF (Fig. 3). An initial weight loss of 5.07% was observed below ~ 120 °C, indicating the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and light volatiles. This stage does not involve structural decomposition and is characteristic of lignocellulosic biomass.

Results of TGA analysis.

A noticeable reduction in mass occurred between 250 and 380 °C, with a clear DTG peak appearing around 320–330 °C. This stage corresponds to the breakdown of hemicellulose and cellulose, the main structural constituents of banana fibers. The sharp decline in weight within this interval suggests intense devolatilization and the gradual development of a carbon-rich framework. Considering this major decomposition phase, a carbonization temperature in the range of 350–400 °C was deemed appropriate for biochar production, as it allows adequate removal of volatile matter while preserving surface functional groups that contribute to adsorption performance. Above 400 °C, the TGA curve exhibits a slower and more uniform mass decrease up to 800 °C, reflecting the gradual degradation of lignin and the continued aromatization and stabilization of the carbon matrix. The absence of sharp DTG peaks in this temperature range suggests enhanced thermal stability and the development of a more ordered carbon framework. This high-temperature region is also suitable for pore development and surface area enhancement, particularly under activating conditions. Based on the TGA–DTG analysis, biochar was produced at a temperature of 400 °C for a residence time of 1 h. l kg of RBF was mixed with 1 L of ortho phosphoric acid (85 wt%) for 24 h and it is then carbonized at 400 °C for a residence time of 1 h62.

BET analysis on adsorbents studied

The BET analysis results revealed that thermochemical upcycling significantly increased the surface area by 183% and 362% for BBF and ABF, respectively. RBF initially exhibited a very low pore volume, which might have increased markedly during thermal treatment and chemical activation due to the removal of volatile components and the partial degradation of structural constituents31. The nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms of BBF and ABF correspond to a type IV isotherm with a hysteresis loop, according to International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) classification, which is characteristic of biochar and activated carbon materials. In contrast, RBF exhibited a type II isotherm with no significant hysteresis loop, as shown in Fig. 463. Previous studies have reported that biochar naturally develops mesopores because of the inorganic substances present in biomass, and the pre-carbonization step helps preserve these pores even after chemical activation64. Moreover, MB molecules are preferentially adsorbed within mesopores due to their relatively large molecular size65. Consistent with these findings, BET analysis results (Table 1) indicate that the average pore diameters of all adsorbents fall within the mesopore range (2–50 nm), which facilitates MB adsorption64. The high surface area, total pore volume, and average pore diameter of ABF make it an efficient adsorbent for MB dye removal.

Point of zero charge (pHpzc)

The pHₚzc values of all three adsorbents were found to be in the acidic range. The predominance of acidic surface functional groups (Fig. 5) can favour metal complexation and ion exchange in addition to chemisorption and electrostatic attraction66 . A low pHₚzc may not indicate improved adsorption; rather, adsorption efficiency depends on the relationship between the solution pH and pHₚzc, which governs the surface charge of the adsorbent67,68. Upon conversion to biochar (BBF), the pHₚzc decreased significantly to 2.74, suggesting an increase in acidic surface functionalities. This shift may be related to the thermal decomposition of cellulose and hemicellulose during pyrolysis, leading to the evolution of oxygenated acidic groups and enhanced surface oxidation.

Point of zero charge of different adsorbents studied.

In general, a higher abundance of acidic functional groups on activated carbon surfaces results in lower pHₚzc values69,70. However, despite phosphoric acid activation, ABF exhibited a comparatively higher pHₚzc value (4.6) than RBF and BBF, indicating that activation promoted the formation of phosphate-linked structures and stabilized aromatic carbon domains rather than increasing the concentration of freely dissociable acidic groups71,72.

The variation in pHₚzc (Fig. 5) confirms that pyrolysis and phosphoric acid activation significantly alter the surface chemistry of banana fibre-derived adsorbents, thereby influencing their adsorption behaviour in aqueous systems.

Optimization of pH

RBF achieved the highest MB removal efficiency (75%) at pH 5 (Fig. 6). Under acidic to near-neutral conditions (pH ≤ 7), the enhanced removal efficiency of RBF can be attributed to the presence of O–H functional groups73, which facilitate adsorption through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions74.

Optimization of pH for MB removal.

In contrast, BBF and ABF possess more aromatic and graphitic structures with relatively fewer protonated surface sites under acidic conditions, resulting in comparatively lower adsorption efficiencies75,76. BBF achieved optimal adsorption (74%) at a neutral pH of 7. The lower pHₚzc values of the synthesized adsorbents promote negatively charged surface conditions over a broad pH range, thereby enhancing adsorption performance and enabling effective operation even under near-neutral conditions, which is advantageous for real wastewater treatment. Furthermore, the development of aromatic carbon structures, coupled with a balanced surface charge can facilitate π–π interactions and physical adsorption mechanisms77. Thus, ABF exhibited highest MB removal efficiency (81%) at pH 9 due to the presence of negatively charged adsorption sites at higher pH, resulting in stronger electrostatic attraction toward cationic MB ions78,79. The enhanced adsorption performance of ABF can be attributed not solely to its pHₚzc, but to the combined effects of surface charge, functional groups, and enhanced porosity68.

Optimization of adsorbent dosage

The change in removal efficiency due to increasing dosages of various adsorbents is presented in Fig. 7. As adsorbent dosage increases, MB removal efficiency increases due to availability of more active sites. As the available surface area increases, more dye molecules can be effectively captured from the solution. However, once most dye molecules have been adsorbed and the solution concentration becomes low, the system approaches saturation. At this stage, further addition of adsorbent results in minimal improvement in removal efficiency80. Beyond 3 g/L, no significant increase in efficiency was observed as in case of RBF and BBF and hence, is taken as the optimum dosage. For ABF, no further increase in removal efficiency was observed beyond 2 g/L. At the optimum dosages, the removal efficiencies reached 88% and 98% for BBF and ABF, respectively.

Optimization of contact time

For RBF, the removal efficiency increased gradually, reaching approximately 95% after 2.5 h (Fig. 8). Beyond this point, no significant improvement was observed, indicating that equilibrium had been achieved and most available adsorption sites were already occupied.

Optimization of contact time for MB removal.

BBF exhibited a slower but steady increase in removal efficiency, rising from about 74% in the first 30 min to around 97% after 3 h. This trend suggests that adsorption in BBF is mainly controlled by diffusion, with dye molecules gradually migrating from the surface into the internal pores of the biochar.

In contrast, ABF demonstrated much faster adsorption, achieving nearly 99% removal within just 30 min. This rapid and high efficiency is likely due to its larger surface area, well-developed porous structure, and greater number of active adsorption sites81,82. The thermo-chemical upcycling has significantly decreased the contact time and comparable with various other studies reported by83–85.

Effect of initial MB concentration on adsorption

Effect of initial MB concentration on adsorption is shown in Fig. 9. Data points represent mean values obtained from triplicate experiments with error bar representing ± SD (n = 3). Removal efficiency of RBF decreased with increase in initial MB concentration due to limited active sites of the adsorbent86,87. For BBF and ABF, especially ABF, differences across concentrations are not statistically significant as there is overlap of error bars in ABF which indicates that effect of initial MB concentration has less influence on adsorption. Superior and consistent performance in ABF may be due to high surface area and porosity developed due to thermochemical upcycling.

The Freundlich model provided the best fit (R2 = 0.96–0.99) for all adsorbents, indicating a heterogeneous surface adsorption. ABF showed the highest KF value, confirming its superior adsorption capacity91. To elucidate the adsorption mechanism and identify the rate-controlling step for MB uptake onto RBF, BBF, and ABF, the intraparticle diffusion model was applied92. The plots of qt versus t1/2 for all three adsorbents exhibited good linearity (Fig. 10), indicating that pore diffusion alone does not govern the adsorption process and that other mass transfer steps also contribute to the overall rate. The adsorption process occurs in two distinct stages, with each exhibiting different kinetic behaviour. The first stage reflects adsorption occurring on the outer surface of the adsorbent and can be attributed to film diffusion. The second stage of the linear segment corresponds to the slower movement of adsorbate molecules into the internal pores, indicating that pore diffusion serves as the controlling step in the overall adsorption process93,94.

Intraparticle diffusion model of different adsorbents.

For RBF, a high correlation coefficient (0.9667) suggests that the adsorption process is strongly influenced by intraparticle diffusion. Nevertheless, a high intercept value reflects boundary layer (film diffusion) resistance during the initial adsorption stages95,96. During the early contact period, external mass transfer dominates, after which the adsorbate molecules slowly migrate into the inner pore structure of the material, where further adsorption occurs94,96.

Similarly, BBF exhibited a strong linear relationship. The slightly lower slope compared with RBF suggests that intraparticle diffusion occurred more slowly. This variation may result from differences in the pore structure, available surface area, or the surface functional groups of the material. The non-zero intercept further confirms that film diffusion and surface adsorption significantly influence the adsorption kinetics before intraparticle diffusion becomes dominant. In contrast, ABF displayed a nearly horizontal trend, characterized by a very low slope and a comparatively lower R2 value (0.6338). This behavior suggests that intraparticle diffusion contributes minimally to the overall adsorption process for ABF97. The high intercept value indicates a strong boundary-layer effect and rapid surface adsorption, likely due to the abundance of reactive sites on the outer surface96,98.

SEM analysis of adsorbents

The SEM images of the adsorbents before and after adsorption (Fig. 11) show clear changes in their surface morphology. Before adsorption, RBF (Fig. 11 a) had a smooth and compact fibrous surface with very few visible pores, while BBF (Fig. 11b) exhibited a rough and irregular texture with several cavities formed during thermal treatment. In contrast, ABF (Fig. 11c) displayed a well-developed porous structure with cracks and interconnected channels, indicating the formation of additional active sites after chemical activation.

SEM images of adsorbents (a) RBF before adsorption, (b) BBF before adsorption, (c) ABF before adsorption, (d) RBF after adsorption, (e) BBF after adsorption, (f) ABF after adsorption.

After MB adsorption, all samples showed surface coverage and partial pore blockage. The RBF surface appeared smoother, suggesting that adsorption mainly occurred on the external surface (Fig. 11d). BBF showed noticeable pore filling, confirming the diffusion of dye molecules into its porous structure (Fig. 11e). Meanwhile, ABF exhibited a denser morphology with fewer visible pores, indicating extensive occupation of adsorption sites by MB molecules (Fg. 11 f.). Overall, these observations confirm that surface modification improved porosity and increased the availability of adsorption sites, with ABF demonstrating the best adsorption performance.

Regeneration and reusability study

The long-term performance and practical applicability of the adsorbents (RBF, BBF, and ABF) were evaluated through reusability and regeneration studies99. The number of cycles was varied among different adsorbents due to their heterogeneous structure and different optimal conditions for adsorption. The % retention of initial capacity of each adsorbent over multiple cycles were evaluated and is shown in Fig. 12. The regenerated adsorbents demonstrated higher retention capacity than adsorbents reused without regeneration. It may be due to improved surface exposure and activation of previously inaccessible active sites during regeneration100. RBF shows a sharp decline in retention capacity which indicates poor structural stability and limited reusability101,102. BBF showed moderate stability maintaining above 90% retention even at 8th cycle. ABF retained its adsorption capacity above 85% even after 12th cycle. The higher retention capacity of ABF may be due to the increased structural stability resulted from structural modification. The gradual reduction in retention may be attributed to the partial pore blockage, strongly attached dye molecules on the adsorbent surface even after regeneration, partial loss of functional groups103. The results of this study are comparable with previous studies also (32,84). Overall, the ABF exhibited superior adsorption capacity, faster adsorption kinetics, higher surface area, and better reusability indicating that upcycling of raw fibres significantly enhanced their performance. Thus, these findings demonstrate the strong potential of waste banana fibres as an efficient adsorbent in wastewater treatment applications.

Conclusions

This research highlights how discarded banana stem fibres can be transformed and upcycled through thermochemical treatment into effective adsorbent materials capable of removing MB from aqueous solutions. Thermal and chemical activation significantly enhanced the adsorption capacity and efficiency of banana fibre–derived adsorbents. Banana fibers, rich in cellulose, are low-cost, available in plenty, and easily biodegradable, thus making them preferred adsorbents for the removal of contaminants from wastewater. The results of the various characterization techniques are consistent with the observed improvements in adsorption efficiency. The SEM images revealed substantial changes in the material’s surface structure after thermochemical modification. Notably, ABF exhibited a well-developed mesopore network along with increased surface area and total pore volume, as confirmed by BET analysis, suggesting that the treatment process effectively enhanced its structural properties. These morphological improvements are closely related to the adsorption behavior observed in the isotherm analysis. ABF exhibited the highest adsorption capacity (45.05 mg/g) and a stronger affinity for the dye molecules due to the formation of aromatic conjugated structures thus enhancing π-π interactions. In addition, the minimal resistance from intraparticle diffusion for ABF suggested that the overall uptake was largely controlled by rapid surface reactions occurring at the abundant active sites available on the material. SEM images and BET analysis results are also consistent with the experimental studies. ABF exhibits improved regeneration stability in terms of % retention of adsorption capacity. By converting discarded banana fiber through thermal treatment, its surface characteristics and overall usability can be greatly improved, while also promoting a more sustainable approach to waste management. This transformation demonstrates that the material has strong potential as a simple, low-cost solution for removing dyes from contaminated water. Due to its affordability and effective adsorption ability, even after multiple regeneration cycles, it could serve as a promising adsorbent for small-scale water and wastewater treatment plants, especially in community-based or rural areas where advanced and expensive wastewater treatment technologies are often inaccessible. Despite the promising adsorption performance of the developed adsorbent, large-scale industrial application can be challenging and hence more researches are still in demand to meet the issues such as structural and chemical stability of fibers and energy requirement during upcycling.
