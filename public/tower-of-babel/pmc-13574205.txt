Multiphase PHA/PBAT/bacterial cellulose/starch biopolymer films: balancing high-temperature stability and controlled compostability
Authors: Dang Viet Hung, Nguyen Ngoc Diep, Hoang Hai Hien, Phan Huy Hoang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study reports the development of cost effective multiphase biodegradable composite films based on polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA), poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT), starch (St), and bacterial cellulose (BC) prepared via melt blending without the use of chemical compatibilizers. FTIR analysis, together with mechanical, thermal, and morphological characterization, suggested the presence of intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions that contributed to maintaining the structural integrity of the multiphase system. The incorporation of bacterial cellulose and starch increased the initial stiffness and improved the thermo-mechanical stability of the composites, while the quaternary formulation retained 28.7% of its initial elongation after thermal aging at 100 °C. Composting experiments demonstrated that the hydrophilic biofillers promoted accelerated degradation while maintaining adequate thermo-mechanical performance during service. The degradation behavior was satisfactorily described by a modified first-order kinetic model incorporating a residual mass term, with two apparent kinetic stages characterized by different degradation rates. FTIR analysis before and after composting revealed a substantial reduction in ester related functional groups, consistent with extensive ester-bond hydrolysis during degradation. The observed degradation behavior is consistent with the progressive depletion of more readily hydrolysable polymer fractions during composting, although detailed characterization of the residual degraded materials is required to fully elucidate the degradation pathway. Overall, the developed PHA/PBAT/BC/St composites provide a promising biodegradable material platform with balanced thermo-mechanical performance and controlled compostability for sustainable packaging applications.

Introduction

The global demand for plastics has increased markedly due to their versatility, durability, and cost-effectiveness. In 2023, global plastic production reached 413.8 million tons; however, only approximately 9% was recycled, and less than 1% originating from bio-based sources.1 Packaging, which represents the largest application sector, has expanded rapidly as a result of the global shift from reusable to single-use products, leading to a substantial escalation in plastic waste generation.2 This waste stream is dominated by non-biodegradable, fossil-derived polymers such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polyethylene terephthalate (PET). This global crisis of plastic waste has necessitated an urgent transition towards a circular economy. The CE framework seeks to maintain material value within the economy through strategies including mechanical and chemical recycling, the use of renewable feedstocks, energy recovery, and biodegradation.

Within this context, biodegradable polyesters have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional plastics, with polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) and poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) receiving particular attention. PHAs are a family of polyesters biosynthesized by microorganisms as intracellular carbon and energy storage compounds. A key advantage of PHAs is their complete biodegradability under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions, mediated by microbial depolymerases that hydrolyze the polymer into oligomers and monomers.3 Furthermore, the compositional versatility of PHAs enables a wide range of material properties, from rigid and crystalline to flexible and elastomeric.4 For example, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (P3HB) exhibits high crystallinity and brittleness comparable to polypropylene, whereas medium-chain-length PHAs, such as P(3HB-co-3HA), demonstrate improved flexibility and toughness. Despite these advantages, the widespread application of PHAs remains constrained by their high production cost and thermal instability during melt processing.5 In contrast, PBAT is a synthetic biodegradable polyester known for its excellent flexibility, ductility, and processability, making it suitable for applications such as films and packaging materials.6,7 Its mechanical properties can be tuned by varying monomer ratios and molecular weight.8,9 However, the biodegradability of PBAT remains relatively limited, as only a few soil-derived microbial strains (e.g., Bacillus subtilis, Rhodococcus sp., and Stenotrophomonas sp.) are capable of degrading it efficiently.10–12 Consequently, blending PBAT with more biodegradable polymers, such as PHAs, has been recognized as an effective strategy to improve both performance and environmental degradability.6 However, its biodegradation rate in natural environments is relatively slow, primarily due to the limited availability of specific depolymerizing microorganisms capable of efficiently breaking down its aromatic segments. As a result, neither PHA nor PBAT alone fully satisfies the requirements for cost-effective, high-performance, and rapidly biodegradable materials.

To address these limitations, the incorporation of low-cost, renewable fillers such as starch (St) and cellulose-based materials has been extensively explored. These fillers not only reduce material cost but also enhance biodegradability by increasing hydrophilicity and facilitating microbial colonization. Previous studies have predominantly focused on binary or ternary systems, including PHA/starch and PBAT/starch (thermoplastic starch, TPS) blends. Recent research has further demonstrated that the incorporation of natural fillers into biodegradable polymer blends can significantly enhance their performance and end-of-life properties. Among these, bacterial nanocellulose (BNC) and starch are particularly attractive due to their abundance, renewability, low cost, and compatibility with biodegradable matrices. BNC, produced by certain bacterial strains through fermentation, has emerged as an exceptional reinforcing agent due to its high purity, crystallinity, and unique three dimensional nanofibrillar network.13 When incorporated into polymer matrices, BNC can improve tensile strength, thermal stability, and barrier properties while promoting biodegradation due to its hydrophilic and biodegradable nature.14,15 Significantly, ultra thin PHA–BC membranes fabricated by bacterial “entrapment” of PHA particles into the BC network exhibited a Young's modulus 4.25 times higher than neat PHA and oxygen barrier properties superior to PET, despite being eight times thinner. Furthermore, BC incorporated systems allow for the adjustment of surface hydrophilicity and cell adhesion, making them suitable for specialized biomedical scaffolds.16 Starch, on the other hand, is one of the most widely available biopolymers, and its incorporation into polymer blends can reduce production costs, improve compostability, and tailor the degradation rate by increasing hydrophilicity.17,18 Despite these advantages, challenges such as poor interfacial compatibility between hydrophilic fillers and hydrophobic matrices remain, requiring systematic investigations into their effect on polymer–filler interactions. Consequently, significant deterioration in mechanical properties is frequently observed unless compatibilization strategies or chemical modifications are employed. Despite considerable progress, the development of multicomponent biodegradable systems that simultaneously achieve cost reduction, mechanical integrity, and controlled biodegradation remains a significant challenge. In particular, limited attention has been given to the synergistic design of PHA/PBAT-based composites incorporating both starch and cellulose-derived fillers, where interfacial interactions, phase morphology, and degradation behavior can be finely tuned. Therefore, this study aims to develop and systematically investigate a novel multicomponent biodegradable composite system based on PHA, PBAT, starch, and cellulose-derived fillers. The relationships between composition, structure, mechanical performance, and biodegradation behavior are elucidated to provide insights into the design of sustainable bioplastics with balanced properties for practical applications.

Previous studies have primarily focused on binary or ternary biodegradable polymer systems, including PHA/starch, PBAT/TPS, PHA/PBAT, and cellulose-reinforced polyester composites.19–21 These systems have demonstrated improvements in individual properties such as mechanical performance, thermal stability, or biodegradability. However, simultaneously balancing thermo-mechanical durability, controlled compostability, and economic feasibility remains challenging because enhancement of one property is frequently accompanied by deterioration of another. Furthermore, many previously reported systems rely on chemically modified cellulose, reactive compatibilizers, or coupling agents to improve interfacial adhesion, thereby increasing processing complexity and production cost. Consequently, studies on uncompatibilized quaternary biodegradable polymer systems remain relatively limited.

Accordingly, the present work investigates a multiphase PHA/PBAT/starch/bacterial cellulose composite prepared by conventional melt blending without chemical compatibilizers. In this formulation, bacterial cellulose functions as a bio-based reinforcing phase, whereas starch serves as a cost-effective biodegradable component. Rather than maximizing a single material property, the objective is to establish an optimized formulation capable of simultaneously improving thermo-mechanical stability, maintaining controlled compostability, and reducing material cost. To the best of our knowledge, studies on uncompatibilized quaternary PHA/PBAT/starch/bacterial cellulose composites remain very limited. The present work therefore provides further insight into the composition–structure–property relationships governing multiphase biodegradable polymer systems and offers a practical formulation strategy for sustainable packaging materials.

In this work, we investigate an uncompatibilized PHA/PBAT/starch/bacterial cellulose composite prepared by conventional melt blending. Rather than relying on reactive compatibilizers or chemical coupling agents, the present study explores whether naturally occurring intermolecular interactions together with an optimized multiphase composition can provide a balanced combination of thermo-mechanical stability and controlled compostability. The primary objective is not to provide a complete evaluation of commercial packaging performance, but to establish the composition–structure–property relationships governing the thermal, mechanical, morphological, and degradation behavior of multiphase biodegradable polymer systems. Particular attention is given to correlating the degradation kinetics with the evolution of the polymer structure during composting, thereby providing insight into the design of cost-effective biodegradable composites for future sustainable packaging applications.

Material and methods

Material

Poly(hydroxyalkanoate) (PHA) (trade name Ecomann® Bioresin EM30010) was supplied by Shenzhen Ecomann Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangdong, China) with density of 1.24 g cm−3 (ASTM D792), an equilibrium water absorption below 0.40%, a Vicat softening temperature of 70 °C and a melting temperature in the range 130–150 °C. Poly(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT) was supplied by Kingfa Sci.&Tech. Co., Ltd. Bacterial cellulose (BC) and corn starch were purchased from local market.

Preparation of blend films

PHA/BC blend

The PHA/BC blend was prepared by manually mixing PHA and bacterial cellulose 4% (w/w) and was processed using a co-rotating twin screw extruder (LABTECH ENGINEERING COMPANY LTD, Thailand). Prior to extrusion, both components were dried in an oven at 40 °C for 24 hours to remove moisture due to their hydrophilic nature. PHA and BC were pre-mixed before being fed into the main hopper of the extruder at a feed rate of 15 rpm. The processing temperature was set between 130 °C and 160 °C, depending on the functional zone of the extruder. The screw speed was maintained at 120 rpm. The extruded PHA/BC strands were then passed through a chilled water bath and subsequently pelletized.

PBAT/starch blend

The starch blend was prepared using the same procedure as the BC masterbatch, incorporating 30% (w/w) starch into the PBAT matrix.

PHA/PBAT/BC/starch blend

The two previously described blends were compounded using a co-rotating twin-screw extruder at a feed rate of 15 rpm. The screw speed was maintained at 120 rpm, and the processing temperature was varied from 130 °C to 160 °C depending on the functional zones of the extruder.

Characterization method

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were acquired using a Nicolet iS10 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Scienctific, USA). The spectra were obtained from 32 scans, in the wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1, and the resolution of 4 cm−1. Thermal analysis (DSC,TGA). The thermal behaviour and thermal stability of the individual components and the composite films were investigated by simultaneous differential scanning calorimetry and thermogravimetric analysis TGA/DSC 3+ analyzer (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Samples of approximately 5–10 mg were heated from 25 to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate 30 mL min−1). Because the measurements span the thermal-decomposition region, the heat-flow curves were used to identify the characteristic decomposition temperatures (Td), taken at the heat-flow minima, rather than to determine melting or crystallisation enthalpies. The corresponding weight loss (TGA) and derivative (DTG) curves were used to determine the onset and peak degradation temperatures. Contact angle analyses were carried out using a microscope Dino-2Mp-1000X coupled with image analysis software Dino capture 2.0. A drop of water was placed on the surface of each film. The methodology to calculate the contact angle was based on the processing of images by determining the angle formed by the intersection of the liquid–solid interface (drop of water–surface of the film) and the liquid–vapour interface (tangent to the boundary of the drop). The average of eight measurements was reported.

Mechanical properties of material, including tensile strength, elongation at break and Young's modulus, were measured by an instron 5900 universal testing systems (Instron, USA). Five specimens were cut into strips measuring 100 × 10 mm from each sheet and the reported values are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Young's modulus values were recalculated directly from the initial linear region of the stress–strain curves according to ASTM D882. All reported values have been verified. The initial distance between the grips was 80 mm, and the loading speed was 5 mm min−1. The samples were cut into 2 cm × 2 cm pieces and placed into a compost mixture prepared in accordance with the ISO 20200 standard. The entire mixture was transferred into plastic boxes measuring 28 × 21 × 17 cm. The boxes were then incubated under controlled conditions at a temperature of 58 °C and a relative humidity of 70–80% for 90 days or until complete degradation of the samples occurred.

X-ray diffraction (XRD). The crystalline structure of the films was examined using an X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA (Empyrean XRD Malvern Panalytical). Diffractograms were recorded over a 2θ range of 5–60° at a scanning rate of 2° min−1 with a step size of 0.03°. The relative crystallinity index (CI) was estimated from the diffractograms using the area method. After subtraction of the amorphous background by an iterative baseline procedure, the crystallinity index was calculated as the ratio of the integrated area of the crystalline reflections to the total integrated scattered area:CI (%) = (Acrystalline/Atotal) × 100where Atotal is the total area under the diffraction curve and Acrystalline is the area remaining after subtraction of the amorphous halo. Sharp reflections arising from the minor crystalline inorganic phase (e.g. CaCO3) present in the commercial PHA were excluded from the crystalline area so that the reported index reflects only the crystallinity of the polymer matrices. The CI values are reported as a relative index for comparison between samples rather than as an absolute degree of crystallinity.

Results and discussion

Structural morphology and phase interaction

The transition towards a circular economy is an urgent step to address the global plastic waste crisis. Biodegradable polymers such as PHA and PBAT have emerged as promising alternatives. However, PHA is susceptible to molecular weight degradation due to thermal sensitivity during processing, while PBAT exhibits a relatively slow biodegradation rate owing to the lack of specific microorganisms in the environment. To address these limitations, a multi step compounding strategy was employed: starch was pre blended with PBAT to exploit its high melt viscosity for shear driven particle dispersion, while bacterial cellulose (BC) was independently dispersed in PHA, before combining the two to fabricate the final composite films.

The characteristic vibrational modes of the polymer matrices were elucidated using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Fig. 1). Specifically, the 2916–2848 cm−1 region is attributed to the stretching vibrations of methyl –CH3 groups. The absence of new chemical bonds indicates the physical nature of the interfacial interactions between the polymer matrices and the biofillers (starch, BC).

FTIR spectra of the pristine matrices and composite blends.

Notably, although the FTIR spectra of the composites lacked absorption bands for new chemical bonds, they revealed shifts in the hydroxyl and carbonyl absorption bands, suggesting the presence of intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions between PHA, PBAT, starch, and bacterial cellulose. These interactions are considered to contribute to maintaining the structural integrity of the multiphase composite and are consistent with the improved thermo-mechanical properties observed in the mechanical and thermal analyses. Although the FTIR peak shifts are consistent with intermolecular hydrogen bond formation, the degree of interfacial compatibility in multiphase polymer systems is influenced by multiple factors, including phase morphology and chain mobility. Therefore, the present interpretation should be considered together with the thermal, mechanical, and morphological observations rather than FTIR evidence alone. The free hydroxyl –OH groups from bacterial cellulose and starch interacted with the carbonyl CO groups of the PHA/PBAT matrices. Consequently, the absorption band in the 3000–3600 cm−1 region (characteristic of –OH stretching vibrations) of the composites tended to broaden, while the CO peak at 1709 cm−1 exhibited a slight shift towards lower wavenumbers compared to neat PHA and PBAT (Fig. 2). Although hydrogen bonds were formed, the density and strength of these physical interactions were insufficient to overcome the mismatch in surface energy between the hydrophilic filler phase and the hydrophobic polyester matrices. Overall, the interfacial adhesion remained inherently weak. The morphological consequences of this uncompatibility were directly observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 3).

Deconvoluting the –OH stretching vibration band (3700–3000 cm−1) is an effective approach for quantifying the hydrogen bonding networks in polymer composite systems. In starch, this complex hydrogen bonding architecture can be resolved into three components: directly bound water (∼3300 cm−1), intra molecular OH bonds (∼3400 cm−1), and indirectly bound hydration water (∼3500 cm−1).23 Similarly, the –OH band of bacterial cellulose (BC) encompasses three distinct hydrogen bonding states: 2OH⋯O6 intra molecular bonds (∼3455–3480 cm−1), 3OH⋯O5 intra molecular bonds (∼3328–3320 cm−1) that govern longitudinal rigidity, and 6OH⋯O3′ inter molecular bonds (∼3210–3220 cm−1) responsible for the tight packing of microfibril chains.24 For the PHA/PBAT polymer matrix, the spectrum is separated into free –OH groups (∼3550 cm−1), intra molecular hydrogen bonds (∼3450 cm−1), and inter molecular hydrogen bonds (∼3300 cm−1).8 The FTIR spectra revealed slight broadening of the O–H stretching band together with a small shift of the carbonyl absorption toward lower wavenumbers, suggesting the presence of intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions among bacterial cellulose, starch and the polyester matrices. These spectral changes provide indirect evidence that hydrogen bonding may contribute to improved interfacial adhesion. However, FTIR alone cannot conclusively establish phase compatibility, and the observed interactions should be interpreted together with the SEM morphology, contact angle, thermal stability and mechanical performance. Collectively, these results are consistent with enhanced interfacial interactions within the multiphase composite rather than definitive proof of complete phase compatibility.

By applying the internal ratio normalization method to eliminate the mass dilution effect, the micro structure of the PHA/PBAT/BC composite system reveals interfacial interactions. However, note that due to the complex overlapping of multiple vibrational states within the multi component –OH band, the deconvolution method is applied on a comparative basis. The obtained percentage area values are intended to track shifts in structural bonding states, rather than representing absolute concentration values. In the dispersed BC phase, the proportion of the inter molecular network decreased significantly from 88.0% to 64.3%. This reduction suggests that mechanical shear forces during blending effectively disrupted the inter chain hydrogen bonds. Consequently, a substantial number of free –OH groups were liberated, driving an increase in intra molecular OH bonding from 9.0% to 35.5%. Simultaneously, the structure of the PHA/PBAT matrix also shifted significantly from intra molecular bonding (decreasing from 35.6% to 22.9%) to inter molecular bonding (increasing from 6.9% to 19.5%), suggesting that the polymer chains rearranged themselves to form H bonds with the newly released –OH groups from the BC fiber surfaces. These results are in agreement with morphological observations from cross sectional FESEM images of the sample, where BC microfibrils are fairly well dispersed within the polymer matrix.

In the PBAT/PHA/St composite system: the thermoplastic processing completely evaporated the directly bound water in starch (dropping from 29.6% to 0.0%). Notably, the intra molecular OH ratio of starch slightly decreased from 62.2% to 57.0%, indicating that the amylose and amylopectin chains maintained an uncoiled state to achieve greater contact with the matrix phase. The free OH ratio of the PHA/PBAT matrix dropped from 57.5% to 48.1%, with an increase in inter molecular network bonding from 6.9% to 17.4%. This reduction in free OH, coupled with the increase in inter molecular bonding, demonstrates direct hydrogen bonding with the starch chains. Furthermore, the tensile strength of the composite did not exhibit the severe deterioration typically associated with starch retrogradation and agglomeration. Instead, it showed an improvement, thereby affirming the reinforcing role of this physical bonding network.

Cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 3) revealed that bacterial cellulose and starch were generally distributed throughout the polymer matrix without obvious macroscopic agglomeration. However, the present SEM analysis was primarily intended for qualitative assessment of phase morphology rather than quantitative evaluation of filler dispersion or phase-domain statistics. Therefore, the observed morphology suggests reasonably good phase distribution, although local heterogeneity inherent to multiphase biodegradable composites remains visible. While large-scale macroscopic phase separation is effectively prevented by the physical interfacial hydrogen bonding network (as confirmed by FTIR), the inherent thermodynamic differences still result in distinct phase boundaries. Because the objective of this work was to investigate the overall relationship between composition, thermo-mechanical stability and compostability, SEM observations were mainly used to qualitatively evaluate the morphological evolution of the composites. Although quantitative image analysis of filler dispersion, interfacial voids and phase-domain size distributions would provide additional information, the present morphological interpretation is supported by the consistency between the SEM observations and the tensile, contact angle and thermal characterization results.

Regarding the mechanical properties, the incorporation of rigid bacterial cellulose and starch particles into the relatively ductile PBAT-rich matrix inevitably introduces local mechanical heterogeneity. Under tensile loading, these heterogeneous interfaces may restrict polymer chain mobility and contribute to localized stress accumulation, leading to a reduction in elongation at break. However, the detailed crack initiation and propagation mechanisms cannot be conclusively established based on the present observations alone.

The improved tensile strength observed in the composites is likely associated with enhanced interfacial interactions arising from hydrogen-bonding interactions among bacterial cellulose, starch, and the polyester matrices, as suggested by the FTIR analysis. These intermolecular interactions may facilitate stress transfer across the interfaces and contribute to maintaining the structural integrity of the multiphase system. Nevertheless, FTIR provides only indirect evidence of such interactions, and the proposed structure–property relationship should therefore be interpreted together with the SEM observations, thermal behavior, wettability, and mechanical performance rather than as definitive proof of interfacial compatibility.

The absence of reactive compatibilizers or chemical coupling agents was intentionally adopted to preserve the fully bio-based and environmentally benign nature of the developed composites. Consequently, a certain degree of phase heterogeneity is expected in this multiphase system. Despite this limitation, the combined experimental results indicate that the developed formulation achieves a reasonable balance between mechanical integrity, thermal stability, and biodegradability. Further investigation using fracture-surface SEM, quantitative morphological analysis, and advanced interfacial characterization techniques will provide deeper insight into the failure mechanisms governing these biodegradable composites.

Crystalline structure (XRD)

The crystalline structure of the films was examined by X-ray diffraction (Fig. 4). All diffractograms are dominated by a broad amorphous halo centred around 2θ ≈ 20–23°, confirming that the films are predominantly amorphous. Neat PBAT shows only weak, broad reflections at 2θ ≈ 17.6°, 20.6°, 23.2° and 24.9° (d = 5.04, 4.32, 3.84 and 3.58 Å), characteristic of its poorly developed crystalline phase, while neat PHA displays a reflection at 2θ ≈ 23.0° (d = 3.86 Å). In addition, the PHA and PHA/PBAT samples exhibit a set of narrow, high-intensity peaks (2θ ≈ 29.3°, 39.3°, 43.1°, 47.4° and 48.5°; full-width at half-maximum ≈ 0.6–0.9°, apparent crystallite size ≈ 10–13 nm from the Scherrer equation). These reflections are more than one order of magnitude narrower than the polymer reflections, indicating a small crystalline inorganic phase, most likely a calcium carbonate filler or nucleating agent present in the commercial PHA grade, rather than polymer crystallinity; they were therefore excluded when estimating the crystallinity index of the polymer matrices.

X-ray diffraction patterns of the individual components and the composite films, vertically offset for clarity. The narrow reflections in the PHA-containing samples correspond to a minor crystalline inorganic phase (e.g. CaCO3) in the commercial PHA.

The relative crystallinity index (CI), estimated from the ratio of the crystalline peak area to the total scattered area after subtraction of the amorphous background and exclusion of the inorganic reflections, remained low for all samples (Table 1): 18.3% for PBAT and 20.6% for PHA, with the blends ranging from 21.2% to 37.5%. Incorporation of the hydrophilic biofillers progressively reduced the crystallinity of the blends, and the quaternary PHA/PBAT/BC/St composite exhibited one of the lowest indices (21.2%) together with an almost featureless amorphous halo. This suppression of crystalline order upon blending is consistent with intimate interfacial contact between the phases and with disruption of polymer chain packing by starch and bacterial cellulose. The predominantly amorphous character also agrees with the DSC results, which showed no pronounced polymer melting transition, and provides independent structural support for the interfacial interactions inferred from FTIR.

Surface wettability and thermal degradation properties

The incorporation of biofillers induced a significant change in the surface wettability of the materials, as directly evaluated through water contact angle measurements (Fig. 5). As observed, the neat PHA film exhibited the most pronounced hydrophobicity, reaching a contact angle of up to 97.7°, while the pristine PBAT matrix showed a slightly lower value of 90.3°. Notably, this inherently higher hydrophobicity of PHA fundamentally restricts interfacial water penetration, serving as a critical factor contributing to its comparatively slower hydrolytic degradation rate. Upon introducing the biofillers, the contact angles dropped sharply to 88.1° for the PHA/BC system and 84.9° for the PBAT/St system. This marked reduction is directly attributed to the incorporation of hydrophilic components (starch and bacterial cellulose), which introduce an abundance of polar hydroxyl (–OH) groups to the composite surface. The droplet profiles on the film surfaces (Fig. 5) visually confirmed a broader spreading tendency, indicating a definitive shift toward enhanced surface hydrophilicity. Furthermore, to prevent the artificial lowering of the apparent contact angle via capillary suction, measurements were captured almost immediately upon droplet deposition. This methodological precaution ensures that the observed reduction is intrinsically driven by the altered hydrophilic surface chemistry rather than physical absorption.

Images of water droplet morphology and contact angles on the film surfaces.

The enhanced surface hydrophilicity, evidenced by the contact angle measurements, along with the intrinsically lower thermal resistance of the biofillers, significantly influences the overall thermal profile of the composites. This shift is quantitatively evaluated through the onset degradation temperatures Tonset and peak degradation temperatures Tp (Fig. 6). As observed in the TGA curves, neat starch and bacterial cellulose exhibit an initial mass loss below 100 °C due to the evaporation of physically absorbed moisture, reflecting their highly hydrophilic nature. Consequently, the incorporation of these biofillers, which possess significantly lower Tonset values (294.6 °C for St and 305.6 °C for BC) compared to the pristine PHA (365.4 °C) and PBAT (393.8 °C) matrices, systematically shifts the Tonset of the composites to a lower range, such as 360.1 °C for the quaternary PHA/PBAT/BC/St system. Unlike the pristine polymer matrices, which exhibit a stable, flat baseline prior to their main degradation, the composite blends display a noticeable early mass loss. Interestingly, while the FTIR spectra confirmed the disappearance of the 1646 cm−1 band (indicating the absence of free absorbed water after melt blending), this early mass reduction in TGA suggests the volatilization of low molecular weight compounds and strongly confined moisture. These fractions are tightly trapped within the complex multiphase network and only escape when sufficient thermal energy disrupts the physical bonds. Thus, this early thermal degradation is fundamentally driven not only by the intrinsic sensitivity of the carbohydrate chains but also by the release of these confined species, which can facilitate hydrolytic cleavage at elevated temperatures.

(A) TGA and (B) DTG thermograms analyzing the maximum degradation peaks Tp of the material systems.

Crucially, the derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curves exhibit distinct multistep degradation profiles for the composites, reflecting the independent thermal decomposition of each constituent phase. Rather than merging into a single intermediate peak, which would imply a miscible or covalently bonded system, the individual degradation signatures remain pronounced. Specifically, the PHA/BC system displays a broad degradation shoulder within the 300–360 °C range corresponding to the decomposition of BC (Tp = 349.0 °C), followed by the primary degradation peak of the PHA matrix at 409.0 °C. Similarly, the PBAT/St blend reveals an early mass loss stage characteristic of starch decomposition (Tp = 325.0 °C) prior to the ultimate thermal degradation of the PBAT matrix at 421.0 °C.

This multiphase decomposition behaviour is further corroborated by the DSC-coupled heat-flow measurements (Fig. 7). Because the analysis spans 25–600 °C, the recorded transitions correspond to thermal-decomposition events rather than melting or crystallisation; accordingly, only the characteristic decomposition temperatures (Td, taken at the heat-flow minima) are reported, without assigning phase-transition enthalpies. The individual constituents exhibit distinct Td values: starch decomposes earliest (Td = 326 °C), followed by bacterial cellulose (Td = 370 and 429 °C), while the neat polymer matrices decompose at higher temperatures (PHA at Td = 310 °C and 419 °C; PBAT showing a single, sharp event at Td = 420 °C). Crucially, the quaternary PHA/PBAT/BC/St composite reproduces two clearly separated decomposition events, a lower-temperature event at Td = 310 °C, coinciding with the starch and PHA fractions, and a higher-temperature event at Td = 420 °C, coinciding with the PBAT and bacterial-cellulose fractions. The persistence of both events in the blend, rather than their coalescence into a single averaged transition, mirrors the multistep DTG profiles and provides independent evidence that each phase largely retains its intrinsic thermal signature. This supports the proposed two-stage degradation scenario, in which the more labile polysaccharide and PHA fractions are lost first, while the more thermally stable PBAT rich phase persists to higher temperatures.

DSC coupled heat flow thermograms (25–600 °C) of the individual components and the quaternary composite, with the characteristic decomposition temperatures (Td) marked at the heat-flow minima.

This independent, multi stage thermal behavior serves as strong evidence that is consistent with the preceding FTIR and SEM findings. The absence of new covalent linkages as confirmed by the FTIR internal ratio analysis and the presence of interphase observed via SEM dictate that the components form a heterogeneous multiphase system governed primarily by physical interactions (hydrogen bonding). Consequently, instead of forming a thermodynamically homogeneous melt, each phase largely retains its intrinsic thermal characteristics during the high temperature degradation process. While this uncompatibilized morphology exhibits distinct phase boundaries maintained solely by interfacial hydrogen bonds, this specific microstructure is advantageous for the end of life cycle of the material. The heterogeneous interfaces and hydrophilic domains facilitate rapid moisture uptake, thereby serving as the primary driver for an accelerated biodegradation rate. From a practical standpoint, this accelerated degradability is achieved without severely compromising the film forming processability and adequate mechanical performance required for short term packaging applications. This favorable eco-mechanical balance is further validated by the subsequent mechanical evaluation, where the composites demonstrate acceptable tensile strength and sufficient elongation at break to support practical film processing and usage.

Mechanical properties and thermo-mechanical stability under aging

Tensile and thermal aging tests were performed to assess the structural integrity and service life of the composites. Mechanical results derived from the stress strain curves (Fig. 8) revealed intrinsic differences between the polymer matrices.

Stress strain curves of the materials.

The mechanical properties of all films are summarised in Table 2. Results from Fig. 8 indicated that neat PHA was inherently brittle (elongation at break of approximately 13.8%), whereas PBAT was ductile due to the presence of flexible aliphatic chain segments. However, upon adding BC and starch, both the ductility and elongation at break of the materials exhibited a decline. This phenomenon is elucidated by the aforementioned SEM morphology: the pores and interfacial gaps acted as stress concentration points. Under tensile loading, stress transfer was interrupted, leading to localized tearing around the filler particles, which caused the samples to fracture prematurely at low strain levels. Despite the initial compromise in mechanical properties, the composite systems demonstrated superior performance in a thermal aging environment (from 25 °C to 100 °C for 72 hours), as depicted in Fig. 9. As observed, the neat PHA film is brittle, exhibiting a notably low tensile strength (4.79 MPa) and elongation at break (13.8%). In contrast, pristine PBAT demonstrates high ductility (elongation at break of 505.7%) and high tensile strength (31.4 MPa) owing to the presence of its flexible aliphatic chain segments. The PHA/PBAT blend yields a balanced tensile strength of 16.12 MPa and an elongation at break of 412.0%.

However, upon the incorporation of hydrophilic biofillers, the ductility and ultimate tensile strength of the composites exhibited a systematic decline. For instance, compared to the pristine matrix, the quaternary PHA/PBAT/BC/St system experienced a reduction in tensile strength to 8.77 MPa and elongation at break to 155.9%. This mechanical compromise is clearly reflected by the observed FESEM morphology. The thermodynamic incompatibility between the hydrophobic matrix and hydrophilic fillers generates interfacial micro voids and phase gaps. Under tensile loading, these micro defects act as critical stress concentration points, interrupting effective stress transfer across the interphase. Consequently, this induces stress concentration around the fillers, causing the samples to fracture at lower strains. This premature failure leads to a drop in overall toughness (from 41.98 MJ m−3 for the PHA/PBAT matrix to 11.40 MJ m−3 for the quaternary blend), a parameter that represents the total energy absorbed prior to fracture and is determined by integrating the entire area under the stress strain curve.

Despite the reduction in ductility, the incorporation of the rigid starch and bacterial-cellulose fibrils markedly increased the initial stiffness relative to neat PBAT. The Young's modulus rose from 44.8 MPa for pristine PBAT to 79.5 MPa for the quaternary PHA/PBAT/BC/St composite. The highest modulus among the blends was recorded for the ternary PHA/PBAT/St matrix (102.0 MPa), with the bacterial cellulose containing systems (PHA/PBAT/BC 84.4 MPa; quaternary 79.5 MPa) lying between the matrix and neat PBAT. This enhanced stiffness relative to PBAT is attributed to the inherent rigidity of the starch and BC fibrils and to interfacial hydrogen bonding, for which FTIR provides indirect supporting evidence. This physical network effectively restricts the mobility of the polymer chains within the elastic deformation region, thereby enhancing the material's modulus.

Therefore, although the absence of chemical coupling agents sacrifices a degree of elongation at break and strength, the resulting composite still retains sufficient stiffness and mechanical integrity for practical short term packaging applications.

Furthermore, despite this initial compromise in room temperature tensile properties, the composite systems demonstrate remarkable stability under thermal aging conditions. To thoroughly evaluate this long term structural stability, the films were subjected to thermal conditioning at various temperatures (from 25 °C to 100 °C), followed by tensile testing at room temperature. The elongation at break data and the corresponding ductility retention ratios are represented in Fig. 9. To quantitatively evaluate the thermal aging performance, the ductility retention ratio, denoted as R (%), was calculated using the following equation:R (%) = εT/ε25 × 100%where εT is the elongation at break after aging at a specific temperature T, and ε25 is the initial elongation at break at 25 °C.

Analysis of this data reveals two thermo-mechanical trends, demonstrating the importance of the multi phase structure:

First, the elongation at break analysis (ε, Fig. 7a) reveals the premature thermal failure of the pristine matrices, which is prevented by the BC incorporated composite system. The neat PHA film experiences premature thermal failure, as the specimens became too brittle to be handled or mounted on the testing grips (recorded as ε = 0%) after aging at just 60 °C. Conversely, the ductile pristine PBAT matrix displays a sharp drop, retaining 88% of its initial elongation at 70 °C to 14% at 80 °C, ultimately suffering a complete loss of physical integrity at 100 °C. This failure is attributed to severe physical aging and the irreversible loss of macromolecular cohesion under prolonged thermal exposure. Notably, blending the matrix with only starch (PHA/PBAT/St) fails to prevent structural failure at 100 °C. In contrast, the quaternary composite system (PBAT/PHA/St/BC) exhibits a gradual and stable degradation. It not only shields the PHA matrix from early failure at 60 °C but also sustains an elongation at break of approximately 45% (a 74% loss) at the most severe aging condition (100 °C), a point where both the pristine PBAT and the ternary starch system have entirely failed.

Second, the ductility retention ratio (R, Fig. 7b) clearly demonstrates the structural stabilization effect provided by the BC microfibrils and hydrogen bonding network. While the pristine PBAT matrix suffers thermal embrittlement (R = 0%) at 100 °C, the quaternary PBAT/PHA/St/BC composite maintains nearly 30% of its original ductility. This enhanced thermo-mechanical stability can be attributed to the contribution of the network of the bacterial cellulose microfibrils coupled with the interfacial hydrogen bonds.

These findings confirm that while the incorporation of hydrophilic biofillers sacrifices a degree of initial room temperature ductility, this specific multi phase structure is a crucial factor in preventing severe structural degradation at elevated temperatures. This effect ensures that the biocomposites maintain mechanical integrity at high temperatures, making them suitable for practical applications involving thermal exposure, such as hot food or promising material platform for future sustainable packaging applications. Although these findings are encouraging, additional application specific evaluations, including barrier and sealing performance, are required to fully assess their suitability for food-packaging applications.

Biodegradability and the dual functionality of biofillers

Following the evaluation of short term thermo-mechanical stability, the end-of-life behavior of the materials was assessed through two distinct environments: soil burial focusing on mechanical disintegration and controlled composting focusing on mass loss kinetics. Although both processes follow a two stage degradation model, a significant temporal decoupling was observed due to the differing environmental stimuli and evaluation criteria.

The analysis of elongation at break and ductility retention (Fig. 10) demonstrates a selective, two stage disintegration mechanism. This behavior reveals an environment dependent dual functionality of the hydrophilic biofillers (St and BC), successfully fulfilling the target lifecycle requirements for sustainable packaging: maintaining thermo-mechanical stability during service life while exhibiting high susceptibility to biological disintegration upon disposal.

Absolute elongation (a) and ductility retention ratio (b) of the composites during soil burial over time.

Stage 1: moisture induced structural fragmentation (0–7 days).

The initial stage is defined by a rapid loss of macroscopic structural integrity, which is directly induced by moisture absorption. The elongation at break data reveals a marked differentiation between the continuous matrix and the composite systems. The inherent hydrophobicity of the pristine PBAT matrix restricts moisture permeation, resulting in a gradual, linear mechanical decline (retaining 83.1% ductility at day 7 and terminating at 35.8% by day 45), acting as the persistent bulk phase.

Conversely, the bio composites experience a rapid mechanical degradation. Specifically, the ternary PHA/PBAT/St system exhibits a substantial ∼97% reduction in elongation at break within just 7 days, retaining only about 3.0% of its initial ductility. Similarly, the quaternary composite loses approximately 92% of its elongation in the same period. Notably, this pronounced ductility loss is not primarily governed by the rapid enzymatic consumption of the polymer mass (as neat PBAT only degraded by approximately 85%). Instead, the primary mechanism is extensive moisture permeation.

Upon soil burial, the hydrophilic St particles and BC microfibrils rapidly absorb water, inducing localized swelling. This hydration physically destabilizes the interfacial hydrogen bonding network, which previously functioned as the primary stabilizing factor in a dry environment, thereby severing the stress transfer mechanism within the composite. Consequently, the fillers transition from being a “thermo-mechanical stabilizing agent” during use to acting as “moisture pathways” that initiate early structural fragmentation.

Stage 2: bulk hydrolysis and porosity formation (7–45 days).

Following the initial interfacial disruption, the mechanical degradation enters a second stage, marked by a low ductility plateau (fluctuating between 5% and 12%). During this phase, the mechanical reduction accurately reflects the hydrolytic cleavage of the main polymer chains rather than solely interfacial debonding. The material completely loses its macroscopic integrity, transforming into a fragmented porous matrix.

Within this phase, starch acts as a primary degradation initiator. The rapid microbial consumption of starch creates a porous structure, which completely diminishes the mechanical cohesion of the ternary system (elongation at break dropping to 0% by day 45). In contrast, while BC microfibrils also promote moisture diffusion, their network maintains a structural framework, preventing the quaternary system from reaching total mechanical failure (retaining 3.5–5.0% ductility).

Consequently, this two stage mechanism effectively addresses the requirements of sustainable packaging. The initial hydration induced fragmentation rapidly breaks down the macroscopic structure, significantly increasing the exposed surface area, which in turn efficiently facilitates the subsequent bulk hydrolysis in Stage 2. The proposed degradation pathway should therefore be regarded as a mechanistic interpretation based on the combined kinetic and FTIR results, rather than definitive evidence of the underlying molecular degradation mechanism. Further characterization of the degraded residues would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the degradation process.

Visual observation (Fig. 11) showed that the composite samples exhibited surface cracking and fragmentation after 14 days. However, the mass loss rate constant in this stage was relatively low (e.g., k1 of PBAT/PHA/BC/St was 0.0184 day−1). In this initial phase, the degradation is characterized by a microbial induction period. The enzymes began to cleave the polymer chains at the surface level, but the activity was insufficient to generate a massive release of soluble oligomers. Because the initial mass loss is primarily governed by heterogeneous biological colonization and localized surface erosion rather than uniform bulk hydrolysis, the empirical regression coefficient R2 for the first-order model in Stage 1 was naturally lower (ranging from 0.90 to 0.94).

Visual and physical disintegration levels of the film samples under controlled composting conditions over time.

Neat PBAT required up to 35 days to initiate fragmentation and 70 days to degrade entirely, due to the hydrophobic barrier hindering bacterial adhesion. Meanwhile, the micro cracks in the composites exposed the hydrophilic St particles and BC fibers, enabling rapid moisture absorption and providing favorable conditions for initial microbial adhesion. It is essential to elucidate the apparent kinetic mismatch between the two evaluation environments: while mechanical ductility loss occurred rapidly by day 7 in soil, the accelerated mass loss transition was delayed until day 35. This dissimilarity is governed by the different principles of fracture mechanics and transport phenomena. Tensile ductility is sensitive with defects, heavily dependent on localized stress concentrators. The early moisture induced swelling of biofillers creates interfacial micro voids. Even a single void acts as an effective stress amplifier, increasing the applied load beyond the critical fracture stress and causing macroscopic failure at day 7, despite minimal physical material loss. Conversely, mass reduction is a cumulative process requiring a prolonged induction period governed by mass transfer limitations. Before accelerated bulk hydrolysis can initiate, moisture and enzymes must diffuse, facilitate localized surface erosion, and infiltrate deeper into the matrix via capillary action. The experimental degradation profile showed a noticeable increase in degradation rate after approximately 35 days of composting. This apparent transition is consistent with the progressive development of internal transport pathways that facilitate moisture and enzyme penetration into the polymer matrix. However, the transition time should be interpreted as a kinetic characteristic derived from the experimental degradation profile rather than direct evidence of a distinct physicochemical threshold.

Entering Stage 2, the degradation rate accelerated. For the neat PBAT and composite systems, the k2 constants (0.0778 and 0.00735 day−1, respectively) were 3 to 4 times higher than k1. This rapid mass loss occurred when the molecular weight of the polymer was cleaved below a critical threshold, allowing water and enzymes to deeply penetrate the bulk volume. Short oligomer fragments dissolved into the composting medium. The bulk hydrolysis mechanism dominated, causing the mass reduction to closely conform to the first order kinetic equation (R2 > 0.98). Despite the high k2 rate, complete (100%) degradation was not achieved, leaving a disintegration degree of 47% for the quaternary composite at day 90 (Table 3).

To describe the degradation behavior during the 90-day composting period, a modified first-order kinetic model incorporating a residual mass term was employed. This model was selected because complete mineralization was not achieved within the experimental period and therefore provides a more realistic description than the conventional first-order equation. Unlike the conventional first-order model, which assumes complete conversion of the degradable material, the modified model incorporates an asymptotic residual mass term, enabling more realistic description of biodegradable polymer systems that exhibit incomplete degradation during finite composting periods. This approach has been widely adopted for describing degradation processes in polymeric materials where a residual fraction remains after prolonged exposure. The degradation profile could be conveniently described by two apparent kinetic stages characterized by different degradation rates (Table 3 and Fig. 12). It should be noted that this stage division represents an operational kinetic description based on the observed change in degradation rate rather than a discrete physicochemical transition between two independent degradation mechanisms.

First order kinetics evaluation and two stage degradation process of the composite materials.

Several kinetic models have previously been applied to biodegradable polymers, including conventional first-order kinetics, logistic growth models, Avrami-type equations, and empirical sigmoidal models. The conventional first-order model generally assumes continuous exponential degradation until complete conversion, whereas logistic and sigmoidal models are more appropriate for systems exhibiting pronounced induction periods. In the present study, the modified first-order model was selected because it adequately describes the experimental degradation profile while accounting for the residual polymer fraction remaining after 90 days of composting. Furthermore, the model provides physically interpretable parameters while avoiding unnecessary model complexity.

The deceleration in the final phase and the persistence of approximately 17% ester bonds originated from the preferential enzymatic depletion of easily hydrolyzable aliphatic segments (e.g., adipate units). The reduction in degradation rate observed during the later composting period is consistent with the progressive depletion of the more readily hydrolysable ester linkages during the early stage of degradation. However, because the chemical composition of the degraded residues was not directly characterized, this explanation should be regarded as a proposed degradation pathway supported by the FTIR results, degradation kinetics, and published literature rather than definitive experimental confirmation.

Overall, the present results suggest that the incorporation of hydrophilic biofillers contributes to a sequential degradation behavior in which early moisture-assisted structural disruption facilitates the subsequent bulk hydrolysis of the biodegradable matrix. Although the detailed degradation pathway requires further chemical characterization, the combined mechanical, compostability, kinetic, and FTIR results consistently indicate that appropriate biofiller design provides an effective strategy for balancing service-life stability and end-of-life biodegradability. Consequently, the systematic optimization of the ratio, dispersion, and network structure of these biofillers emerges as a critical technical strategy. This approach is fundamental for achieving an optimal balance between thermo-mechanical durability during service life and programmed disintegration during waste management, advancing the development of sustainable packaging.

It should be noted that the apparent transition between Stage 1 and Stage 2 was identified from the change in degradation rate observed in the experimental dataset and the corresponding kinetic fitting. Therefore, the transition should be interpreted as a convenient kinetic description rather than evidence of a distinct physicochemical boundary separating two independent degradation mechanisms.

To elucidate this chemical mechanism, FTIR spectra before and after composting were evaluated (Fig. 13). Post-composting FTIR spectra showed a marked decrease in the carbonyl (1709 cm−1) and ester C–O–C (1300–1100 cm−1) absorption bands together with broadening of the O–H stretching region. These spectral changes are consistent with ester bond hydrolysis and the formation of hydroxyl- and carboxyl-containing degradation products. Nevertheless, FTIR provides indirect evidence of these chemical transformations, and detailed identification of degradation products would require complementary analytical techniques.

Conclusion

In this study, cost-effective multiphase biodegradable composite films based on PHA, PBAT, starch, and bacterial cellulose were successfully developed via melt blending without the use of chemical compatibilizers. The combined FTIR, morphological, thermal, and mechanical results suggest that intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions contributed to maintaining the structural integrity of the multiphase system. The incorporation of bacterial cellulose and starch enhanced the stiffness and thermo-mechanical stability of the composites, while the optimized quaternary formulation retained 28.7% of its initial elongation after thermal aging at 100 °C.

Composting experiments demonstrated that the incorporation of hydrophilic biofillers accelerated degradation while maintaining satisfactory thermo-mechanical performance during service. The degradation behavior was adequately described by a modified first-order kinetic model incorporating a residual mass term, with two apparent kinetic stages characterized by different degradation rates. FTIR analysis before and after composting revealed a substantial reduction in ester-related functional groups, consistent with extensive ester-bond hydrolysis during degradation. The observed degradation behavior is consistent with the progressive depletion of more readily hydrolysable polymer fractions during composting, although further compositional characterization of the degraded residues would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the degradation pathway.

Overall, the present work demonstrates that the synergistic incorporation of bacterial cellulose and starch provides an effective strategy for balancing thermo-mechanical stability and controlled compostability in biodegradable PHA/PBAT-based composites. These findings provide a promising material platform for the future development of sustainable packaging materials, while further evaluation of barrier properties, heat-sealing behavior, and other packaging-related performance will facilitate practical food-packaging applications.
