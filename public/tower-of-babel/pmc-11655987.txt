Global urban and rural settlement dataset from 2000 to 2020
Authors: Zhitao Liu, Sheng Huang, Chuanglin Fang, Luotong Guan, Menghang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate mapping of global urban and rural settlements is crucial for understanding their distinct expansion patterns and ecological impacts. However, existing global datasets focus mainly on urban settlements and ignore the delineation of rural settlements. Therefore, this study proposed a framework for delineating between urban and rural settlements based on dynamic thresholds defined by area and light brightness and constructed the first global 100-meter resolution urban and rural settlements dataset (GURS) spanning from 2000 to 2020, integrating GHS-BUILT-S R2023A, NPP-VIIRS-like nighttime light, and OpenStreetMap data. An accuracy assessment of 44,474 independent samples showed that GURS achieved an overall accuracy of 91.22% with a kappa coefficient of 0.85, outperforming nine multi-scale reference datasets in delineating global urban and rural settlements. GURS offers deep insights into the dynamics of global settlements, facilitating urban-rural comparative studies on socio-economic characteristics, environmental impacts, and governance modes, thereby enhancing the sustainable management of settlements.Subject terms: Geography, Sustainability

Background & Summary

Understanding the spatial and temporal dynamics of global urban and rural settlements is crucial for the Earth sustainability in the Anthropocene era, which is characterized by the increasing influence of human activities on the environment1–4. Settlements, whether urban or rural, represent spatial manifestations of human presence on the earth5, whose expansion significantly influences land-change processes and profoundly impacts ecosystems with lasting consequences6. Furthermore, the United Nations estimates there will be 1.8 billion more people on the earth by the year 20507. This means that the expansion of settlements, coupled with declining settlement density8, is expected to intensify and thus exacerbate the encroachment on agricultural land and ecological habitats9–12. Additionally, different dominant patterns of human activity, architecture, and economy contribute to the development dynamics of urban and rural settlements differently13–15. Thus, more studies are needed to delineate between urban and rural settlements, and further monitor their expansion patterns and environmental impacts on a global scale.

Much research has been focused on the development of global urban settlements16–20 and their impacts on climate change21,22, food security10,23, and biodiversity24,25. Methods for determining urban extent can be broadly classified into two groups according to the type of input remote sensing data. The first category uses traditional remote sensing products like MODIS to extract urban extents primarily by analysing landscape morphology and texture19,26,27. The second category employs NTL data, such as DMSP-OLS NTL and NPP-VIIRS NTL, to define urban extents by synthesizing the intensity and location information from NTL16,17,28. However, given that MODIS and NTL are used as source data, global datasets pertaining to urban settlements predominantly operate at low resolutions ranging from 250 to 1000 m. There is an urgent demand for higher-resolution urban settlement products to accurately capture the dynamics of urban settlement expansion.

Moreover, rural settlements have received relatively little attention, despite the fact that rural settlements are typically closer to key biological habitats, which can have a substantial impact on animal behaviours and stress levels29–31 and more vulnerable to shocks due to poverty and lack of institutional security32. Studies focusing on the identification of rural settlements are limited and often localized, lacking comprehensive global mapping of rural settlements. Specifically, most existing studies employ high-resolution remote sensing imagery, such as SPOT-5 (2.5 m) or Sentinel-1/2 (10 m), for localized assessments33,34 However, these datasets cover only a short period, thus impeding long-term monitoring of rural settlements33. While medium-resolution remote sensing images (30–100 m) can effectively distinguish settlements from permeable areas, leading to various impervious surface and human settlement datasets, including GAIA (Global Artificial Impervious Area)35, GAUD (Global Annual Urban Dynamics)36, GISA2.0 (Global Impervious Surface Area 2.0)37, GISD30 (Global 30 m Impervious Surface Dynamic Dataset)38, and GHS-B (GHS-BUILT-S R2023A)39, their direct interpretation is unable to delineate between urban and rural settlements due to similar urban-rural visual characteristics. Overall, we still know nothing about the size and spatial distribution of rural settlements globally.

To improve global urban settlement mapping resolution and delineate global rural settlements, we developed the first global 100 m urban and rural settlement (GURS) dataset from 2000 to 2020. It can provide novel insights into global settlement patterns and dynamics, laying a foundation for urban-rural comparative studies across various domains such as population and GDP estimation, environmental impact assessment, and sustainable planning and governance of settlement.

Methods

Data collection

Firstly, we utilized GHS-B (https://human-settlement.emergency.copernicus.eu/download.php?ds=bu) as our base dataset for mapping global urban and rural settlements. This dataset identified “any roofed structure built on the ground”, essentially human settlements, and provides their distribution at 100 m resolution, estimated every five years from 1975 to 2030. Importantly, the inclusion of the 2018 10 m data, derived from a multi-source integrated learning set (including GHS-BUILT-S2 R2020A, Facebook settlement delineation, Microsoft, and OSM building delineation) and 10 m Sentinel-2 imagery, significantly enhanced the accuracy of the GHS-B dataset, which is crucial for rural settlement identification5. GHS-B dataset achieves an overall accuracy of 91.54% under binary classification of built-up versus non-built-up surfaces. Therefore, the 100 m GHS-B dataset was selected to map the base extents of global urban and rural settlements and count their “area”.

Secondly, the global extended NPP-VIIRS-like NTL data (https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/YGIVCD) offered crucial “brightness” information for distinguishing urban and rural settlements worldwide. This dataset was created using a novel cross-sensor calibration method employing vegetation indices and autoencoder models, leveraging DMSP-OLS NTL data (2000–2012) and NPP-VIIRS NTL monthly data (2013–2018), ensuring exceptional spatial patterns and temporal consistency40. It has higher spatial resolution than the harmonized global DMSP OLS-like nighttime light dataset 1992-202041, and exhibits reduced overglow and saturation effects. Hence, it was utilized in this study to attribute “brightness” to global urban and rural settlements.

Thirdly, the “place” information from OSM (https://download.geofabrik.de) was used to determine whether a settlement sample was urban or rural. OSM is a freely accessible and editable global map database containing a diverse range of geographic objects (e.g., roads, buildings, land use, points of interest) worldwide42. It relies on contributions from experts, surveyors, cartographers, volunteers, and the general public, with over 2 million contributors as of August 2023 (http://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Stats). This extensive public participation underscores OSM’s significance as a spatial information source for settlements6,43,44. OSM’s feature “place” primarily provides details about populated settlements (e.g., city, suburb, town, village, hamlet). In this study, spatial points of populated settlements from OSM-Place data were utilized as labels for classifying urban and rural settlements. Given that the settlement attributes in OSM-Place data rarely change over time, we used the 2020 OSM-Place data to ensure an adequate sample size.

Finally, this study analysed the performance of GURS in delineating global urban and rural settlements from 2000 to 2020 by comparing it with three global impervious surface datasets, including GAIA (1985–2018)35, GISA2.0 (1972–2019)37, and GISD30 (1985–2020)38, along with three global urban extent datasets, including GUB (Global Urban Boundaries, 2000–2018)20, MGUP (MODIS Global Urban Extent Product, 2001–2018)19, and NTL-UE (Global Annual Urban Extents from Harmonized NTL, 1992–2020)17. GAIA, GISA2, and GISD30, 30 m resolution datasets, are used to evaluate GURS’s performance in mapping settlements and localized rural settlements. GUB, MGUP, and NTL-UE-2020 derived from morphological methods, remote sensing data, and NTL data, respectively, serve as benchmarks for comparing GURS’s performance in mapping urban settlements.

However, there is no global-scale map distinguishing urban and rural settlements. Therefore, this study evaluated GURS’s ability to distinguish urban and rural settlements locally by comparing it with China’s Land Use/Cover Dataset (CLUD). CLUD is the longest-spanning and largest-scale land use/cover dataset distinguishing urban land and rural settlements. Generated in a human-computer digital environment using a unified technical process and classification system45,46, it has been extensively employed in comparative studies of urban and rural areas47–49.

Methodological framework

Globally, urban and rural settlements encompass all roofed buildings used for human habitation and activities, differing notably in size (area) and human activity intensity (brightness). In our study, urban settlements are defined as spatially contiguous areas of roofed buildings, characterized by large sizes and intense human activity, driven by concentrated industries, services, and advanced infrastructure50,51. In contrast, rural settlements consist of spatially contiguous roofed buildings, typically smaller in size, with light human activity, primarily relying on agriculture and natural resources. Therefore, this study proposed an object-oriented methodology for distinguishing urban and rural settlements based on “area” and “brightness” attributes (Fig. 1) and mapped global urban and rural settlements (2000–2020) at 100 m resolution. Firstly, we extracted potential settlement pixels from the GHS-B data and aggregated spatially adjacent pixels into settlement patches, each attributed with “area”. Subsequently, “brightness” of each settlement patch was determined using NTL data. Secondly, urban and rural settlement patch samples were extracted according to OSM-Place points. Urban-rural settlement classification thresholds for area and brightness were established based on the distribution of these samples and all settlement patches were classified into urban and rural settlements according to the thresholds and classification scheme. Finally, multi-temporal urban-rural settlement classification results were updated to maintain spatial-temporal consistency and logical evolution between urban and rural settlements.

Methodological framework for delineating urban and rural settlements.

Identification of potential settlement patches

Mapping potential settlement patches globally involved three main steps.

Step1: the potential settlement pixels were extracted. GHS-B assigned a continuous value from 0 to 10000 to represent the built-up area percentage of a pixel. To determine the potential settlement pixels, we initially calculated the total area of GHS-B pixels with pixel values higher than 0, 500, 750, 1000, 1250, and 1500, respectively, using 375 grids provided by GHSL. Second, we calculated the areas of referenced settlement datasets, 30 m GISD30 and GAIA, using the same grids on the GEE platform. Finally, we extracted the GHS-B pixels whose total area was closest to that of GISD30 and GAIA. Scatter plot fit results (Fig. 2) indicated that the area of GHS-B pixels with a built-up area percentage of 1000 or higher was most comparable to the areas of GISD30 and GAIA. Wang et al.52 found the GHS-B raster with pixel values of 1000 and above had the smallest omission rate when classified as built-up area, aiding in the detection of scattered small rural settlements. Therefore, in this study, GHS-B with pixel values greater than or equal to 1000 were extracted as potential settlements.

Scatterplots of GHS-B at different thresholds of built-up area percentage against GISD30 and GAIA. The x and y axes are their areas in million hectares (Mha).

Step2: settlement pixels were aggregated to settlement patches. In this study, spatially neighbouring pixels were considered as part of a settlement patch, for human settlements tend to be spatially clustered53. Totally, 120 million settlement patches were identified. This approach classified villages connected to cities as urban settlements. Although these villages retain the architectural traits of rural settlements, their close ties to urban centers, access to urban markets and public services, and ongoing urbanization suggest it’s reasonable to regard them as part of urban settlements.

Step3: the fundamental attributes of the settlement patches, “area” and “brightness”, were calculated. The “area” was derived during the aggregation of settlement patches. Subsequently, the NPP-VIIRS-like NTL data were employed to compute the average light radiation values of settlement patches, serving as the “brightness”. While the “area” is effective in distinguishing most urban and rural settlements due to the larger size and higher economic activity intensity of urban settlements54, it may not suffice for distinguishing large rural settlements, particularly common in developing countries, and small urban settlements, especially prevalent in developed countries. As another example, new construction projects on the outskirts of an urban settlement may manifest as other small settlement patches. The “brightness” further aids in differentiating these cases. The complementarity of the “area” and “brightness” enhances the accuracy of urban and rural settlement classification in this study.

Delineation of urban and rural settlements based on “area” and “brightness”

This study established classification thresholds between urban and rural settlements, different from previous approaches that predefined these thresholds based on specific attributes like impervious surface proportion26,55. Instead, it derived these thresholds from statistical distributions of about 3.44 million actual urban and rural settlement samples. The classification process involved three main steps.

Step1: urban and rural settlement patch samples were extracted by matching OSM-Place points with all settlement patches. Due to the uneven distribution of information globally56, the number of OSM-Place points significantly varies across regions. Utilizing a global sample directly may result in classification thresholds biased toward regions with abundant OSM-Place data, thus compromising the thresholds’ generalizability. To address this, we referenced the United Nations Statistics Division’s world division and reorganized subgroups with excessively large settlement data volumes (e.g., splitting “Europe and North America” into “Europe” and “North America”) and subgroups with insufficient data (e.g., combining “Oceania” and “Australia” into “Oceania”). Ultimately, the global countries were divided into nine subregions (see Fig. 3). Subsequently, the study initially extracted urban settlement points (city, suburb) and rural settlement points (village, hamlet, farm) from the OSM-Place points. In the subsequent step, settlement patches and urban/rural settlement points were spatially matched and matched urban and rural settlement patches were selected as the base samples. Finally, the study calculated the minimum density of urban/rural base samples for settlement areas by dividing the number of base samples of urban/rural settlement patches by settlement areas in each of the nine subregions. Patches in the nine subregions were randomly selected based on this density, forming statistical samples of urban/rural settlement patches. Attribute information of the statistical samples provided four key metrics: “urban settlement patch area,” “rural settlement patch area,” “urban settlement patch brightness,” and “rural settlement patch brightness.”

Spatial distribution of the accuracy validation samples from 2000 to 2020. The base map displays the nine global subregions across terrestrial areas. High-resolution Google Earth images provide examples of how urban settlements, rural settlements, and non-settlement areas were visually distinguished.

Step2: thresholds for urban and rural settlement classification were determined by analysing the statistical distributions of “area” and “brightness” of these samples. Initially, we applied the natural logarithm to the sample data to mitigate absolute value discrepancies. Subsequently, cumulative frequency curves for area/brightness of urban settlement patches and complementary cumulative frequency curves for area/brightness of rural settlement patches were plotted. The intersection of these curves represents the point where the difference in occurrence probabilities between urban and rural settlements is maximized, offering the greatest distinction between the two. A point (x,fx) on the cumulative frequency curve for area/brightness of urban settlement indicates the probability fx that an urban settlement has an area or brightness less than x. Similarly, a point (x,gx) on the cumulative frequency curve for area/brightness of rural settlement indicates the probability g(x) that a rural settlement has an area or brightness less than x. We define the difference in occurrence probabilities between urban and rural settlements as the urban-rural distinction, dx. When dx is maximized, using x as the threshold for distinguishing between urban and rural settlements can minimize the classification errors. Since rural settlements tend to have smaller areas and lower brightness values, while urban settlements tend to have larger areas and higher brightness values, fx is consistently smaller than gx. The intersection of the cumulative frequency curve for urban settlements with the complementary cumulative frequency curve for rural settlements occurs at the point where fx+1−g(x) (i.e., 1−dx) is minimized, which corresponds to the point where dx is maximized.

The intersection x indicates the area/brightness classification threshold between urban and rural settlements. To the right of this intersection, the frequency of rural settlements is extremely low while the frequency of urban settlements increases; conversely, to the left of the intersection, the frequency of urban settlements is extremely low while the frequency of rural settlements increases. To ensure threshold robustness, we repeated Step1 and Step2 100 times, averaging the 100 thresholds to obtain the final urban-rural settlement classification thresholds in both area and brightness dimensions from 2000 to 2020 (Fig. 4).

Urban-rural settlement classification thresholds in both area and brightness dimensions. Label number is the 100 times average threshold.

Step3: all settlement patches were classified based on these thresholds, yielding the initial global urban and rural settlement map. This study formulated a classification scheme according to the two-dimensional attributes of settlement patches (area and brightness) and urban-rural settlement classification threshold. Specifically, settlement patches were categorized into four types: urban context, mega village, village, and bright island, using brightness as the x-axis, area as the y-axis, and the classification threshold as the origin.

Furthermore, bright islands were reclassified based on their distance from urban contexts. Those within a certain distance are classified as urban enclaves, while the rest are classified as villages near light sources. Four distance thresholds of 1000, 800, 500, and 300 m were initially set, with the optimal distance threshold determined through subsequent accuracy assessments. Ultimately, a distance threshold of 300 m was found to be the most suitable. Finally, this study categorized urban context and urban enclaves as urban settlements, and mega village, village, and villages near light sources as rural settlements.

Post-processing

We conducted post-processing on the initial urban and rural settlement map to address misclassification from spatial extent mismatches across multi-source data and to establish logically consistent time-series data. In some cases, a few scattered settlement patches near coastal areas were erroneously classified as rural settlements due to the absence of NPP-VIIRS-like NTL data coverage. To rectify this, we first identified coastal settlement patches not covered by NTL data, all of which were initially classified as rural settlements. Subsequently, coastal settlement patches located within 1 km of existing urban settlement patches were reclassified as urban settlements.

To ensure logically consistent time-series data, this study referenced the existing method to rectify inaccuracies in urban-rural settlement classification using contextual spatiotemporal information and urban-rural transformation logic1. The study posited the underlying logic of urban and rural settlement evolution based on the fundamental principles of the urbanization process: the conversion of settlements to non-settlement areas is reversible, allowing settlements to revert to greenfield or cropland, while the conversion of settlements is irreversible, with urban settlements not degrading back to rural settlements. Guided by this logic, the sequence of urban and rural settlements was systematically examined, and settlements that deviated from this logic (i.e., regressing from urban settlements to rural settlements) were corrected.

Accuracy assessment

This study employed an independent validation sample set, constructed from publicly available data and high-resolution remote sensing imagery, to evaluate the accuracy of GURS. We assessed both the spatial accuracy and temporal dynamics accuracy of GURS.

Initially, sample points were randomly generated within GURS, with proportions corresponding to the settlement areas in the nine subregions outlined in Sect. 3.2.1 and on the continent outside GURS. In 2020, 10,000 sample points were generated within GURS (or 500 if the sample points in a region were fewer than 500), and 5,000 sample points (or 300 if the sample points in a region were fewer than 300) were generated for each of the other years, totaling 31,474 sample points. Outside GURS, 1,000 sample points were randomly generated each year, totaling 5,000 sample points.

Secondly, we randomly selected 2,000 sample points from the 6,277 sample points in 2000 and added them to the sample sets for the other years. Overall, 44,474 sample points were used to evaluate the spatial accuracy of GURS (Fig. 3). The 2,000 sample points with consistent spatial locations across all five years were used to evaluate the temporal dynamics accuracy of GURS.

Thirdly, inspired by Liu et al.36, this study overlaid three sets of global long-time-series urban boundary data: GUB, MGUP, and NTL-UE. Areas identified as urban by all three datasets were classified as high-confidence urban areas, while those identified as non-urban were classified as high-confidence rural areas. Areas with inconsistent classifications among the three datasets were classified as uncertain areas. Sample points within GURS that fell in high-confidence urban areas were labeled as urban settlement sample points, those in high-confidence rural areas were labeled as rural settlement sample points, and those in uncertain areas, along with sample points outside GURS, were further labeled as urban/rural settlement sample points (15,127 in total) using high-resolution remote sensing imagery from Google Earth. During this process, residential areas with multi-story buildings, well-organized road networks, and large contiguous areas were classified as urban settlements; residential areas with sparse, low-rise buildings, disorganized road networks, and small contiguous areas were classified as rural settlements; and areas with farmland or natural landscapes, distant from buildings, were classified as non-settlement areas. Specific examples are shown in Fig. 3. Combining publicly available data with high-resolution remote sensing imagery for validation significantly enhanced validation efficiency.

Finally, we calculated the overall accuracy (OA), kappa coefficient (Kappa), F1-score (F1) producer accuracy (PA, a measure of commission error), and user accuracy (UA, a measure of omission error) according to the confusion matrices of the validation samples and our results57.

Data Records

The global 100 m urban and rural settlement dataset (GURS) from 2000 to 2020 is available at https://zenodo.org/records/1116089358. We provided five geo-tiff files named GURS_2000, GURS_2005, GURS_2010, GURS_2015, and GURS_2020, each corresponding to a specific year. The projection coordinate system used is Mollweide. In these geo-tiff files, pixels with a value of 1 represent urban settlements, while pixels with a value of 2 represent rural settlements. These data can be processed by ARCGIS, QGIS, MATLAB, and other tools.

Technical Validation

Accuracy assessment by independent samples

Assessment of spatial accuracy based on 44,474 samples reveals that GURS achieves an overall accuracy of 91.22% and a Kappa coefficient of 0.85 (Table 1). Specifically, across five years (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020), the overall accuracy consistently surpasses 90%, with Kappa coefficients exceeding 0.80. Urban settlements have the lowest F1 score, though it is still as high as 0.89. Rural settlements exhibit the highest user accuracy at 97.65%, primarily because cities with high brightness and large areas are less prone to being misclassified as villages. However, rural settlements near cities may be misclassified as urban enclaves due to urban NTL spillover, leading to a user accuracy of 82.97% for urban settlements and a producer accuracy of 87.07% for rural settlements.

Accuracy assessment by cross-comparisons with regional settlement-related data

Numerous studies have examined the spatial extent of rural settlements at local levels, but there is currently no available global dataset. The GHS-SMOD dataset provides identification of villages and dispersed rural areas globally at a 1 km resolution5, but it includes considerable non-built-up land and can’t represent rural settlements. Consequently, we conduct a local comparison of GURS with CLUD, focusing on area and spatial extent accuracy. To assess the accuracy of urban and rural settlement areas identified by both datasets, we referenced the third National Land Resource Survey (NLRS) of China.

Specifically, this study utilized China’s municipalities, prefectural-level administrative districts, and counties under provincial jurisdiction as statistical units, and excluded the largest and smallest 2.5% of areas to mitigate the impact of outliers. The fitting results of the 2019 NLRS data with the 2020 GURS and 2020 CLUD data are shown in Fig. 5a–d. Both GURS and CLUD accurately identified urban settlement areas in China, with high fitted R2 values of 0.91 for both data. However, the fitted slope of GURS to NLRS was 0.73, primarily because GURS focuses mainly on the floor area of urban settlements, whereas NLRS includes all types of built-up land use area, leading to a higher count. Regarding rural settlements, the fitted R2 and slope of GURS with NLRS were significantly higher than those of CLUD with NLRS, indicating that GURS data has superior area accuracy.

Comparison of GURS and CLUD in area and spatial extent. (a–d) The fitting of GURS and CLUD’s urban and rural settlement areas to those of NLRS of China, respectively. (e) The performance of GURS and CLUD in Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou-Foshan, and Chengdu, China. The spatial extent of each region is 110 km × 110 km. The Google Earth images in 2020 were downloaded from the Google Earth Engine.

Moreover, this study selected four representative regions, Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou-Foshan, and Chengdu, to illustrate the performance of GURS and CLUD in recognizing the spatial extent of urban and rural settlements (Fig. 5e). It was observed that CLUD misidentifies some large construction land patches as rural settlements, though they are actually urban residential complexes and factories, while GURS accurately distinguishes between urban and rural settlements in these areas. Additionally, GURS outperformed CLUD in mapping smaller rural settlements in some areas, particularly on Chongming Island in northern Shanghai. Overall, GURS demonstrates strong performance in delineating urban and rural settlements.

Accuracy assessment by cross-comparisons with global settlement-related data

To assess GURS’s global performance, this study compared it with three global 30 m impervious surface data and three urban extent data. We quantitatively evaluated the area consistency of GURS with these datasets for the years 2000, 2010, and 2020. If reference data for the corresponding year was unavailable, the nearest matchable year was chosen. Figure 6 displays a scatter plot depicting GURS’s proportion of urban and rural settlement (urban settlement) area against the proportion of impervious surface (urban extent) area from the reference dataset. The fitting results of the scatter plots revealed strong agreement between the total settlement area and the urban settlement area identified by GURS and the available data, with R2 values exceeding 0.77. However, the slopes of the fits of GURS with GISD30, GISA2.0, and GAIA consistently remained below 0.8, primarily due to the total area overestimation caused by GURS’s lower resolution. Conversely, the lower resolution of GUB, MGUP, and NTL-UE led to slopes consistently exceeding 1.1 in their fits to GURS.

The x and y axes are the proportion of settlements in each 1° × 1° spatial grid.

Nine representative cities from different global subregions were selected for analysis. Figure 7 presents five cities from Asia and Africa with lower levels of urbanization. It demonstrates that in Beijing, Bangkok, and Cairo, GUB and MGUP tend to misclassify large rural settlements as urban areas compared to GURS. Additionally, GURS, with its improved resolution, mitigates the issue of NTL-UE extending urban edges excessively due to its low resolution (1000 m). Despite its coarser resolution, GURS performs comparably to 30 m impervious surface data in identifying rural settlements. In cities like Beijing, New Delhi, and Cairo, GURS identifies rural settlements to a similar extent as GISD30, GISA2.0, and GAIA, and in Bangkok and Durban, GURS even outperforms impervious surface data. Finally, this study compares the accuracy of GURS, GUB, MGUP, and NTL-UE using the same validation sample set to demonstrate the accuracy of GURS in delineating urban settlements (Table 3). Since GISD30, GISA2.0, and GAIA lack urban/rural attributes, they were not included in the accuracy comparisons. Overall, GURS exhibits the highest performance with an overall accuracy of 92.55% and a kappa coefficient of 0.84, compared to GUB’s 90.41% and 0.79, MGUP’s 89.33% and 0.78, and NTL-UE’s 88.61% and 0.73. Additionally, GURS achieves the highest F1 score for each year.

Usage Notes

While the accuracy assessments and comparisons demonstrate that GURS is effective at depicting global urban and rural settlements, several aspects of GURS data production require improvement. Firstly, the digital divide and socio-economic inequality result in a notable regional bias in OSM data, with more contributors in the Global North than in the Global South59–61. Although we balanced the OSM sample size across nine subregions based on settlement areas, this may have reduced the total sample size for labeling urban-rural settlement patches. Secondly, NTL data fails to accurately capture the true brightness of cities with lights turned off at night62, resulting in the misclassification of these urban settlements as rural settlements. Additionally, differences in resolution and range between NTL data and GHS-B contribute to classification errors. Despite our efforts to mitigate errors through post-processing for range inconsistencies, some coastal urban settlements are still misclassified as rural settlements, particularly in cities with large reclaimed land areas. Finally, identifying spatially neighbouring pixels as settlement patches may misclassify urban-connected rural settlements as urban settlements, lowering classification accuracy. Overall, there’s potential for improving the accuracy of global urban-rural settlement delineation.

In addition, our methodology could be extended and adapted to differentiate between urban and rural settlements using global 30-meter resolution or even higher resolution impervious surface data. However, working with higher-resolution data presents potential challenges that need to be addressed. For instance, our object-based approach may result in an exponential increase in the number of objects (settlement patches) compared to the 100-meter data used in our study, posing challenges for data storage and processing speed. Additionally, new pixel aggregation rules would need to be designed when using higher-resolution data, as aggregating pixels based on adjacency might cause a single settlement to be fragmented into multiple patches. Nevertheless, the application of our methodology for creating higher-resolution global urban-rural settlement maps is a promising avenue for future research.
