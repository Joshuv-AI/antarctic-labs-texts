The honey bee triad: a comprehensive catalogue of phages in the Apis mellifera gut microbiome
Authors: Nikolas Basler, Lina De Smet, George Bouras, Jill Swinnen, Katarzyna Pranga, Corina P D Brussaard, Peter Vandamme, Dirk C de Graaf, Jelle Matthijnssens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHoney bees (Apis mellifera) contribute to crop production and floral biodiversity via pollination, but their health is increasingly challenged by stressors including pathogens, parasites and agricultural practices. Although the honey bee gut microbiome is relatively simple, its phages are not well studied. Here, we conducted a metagenomic study, providing a comprehensive catalogue of honey bee gut phages from 450 virus-enriched samples from 63 hives, across eight European countries, three seasons and three gut sections. We describe a diverse phageome including many phages that appear to belong to novel taxa, as well as a core set of 97 highly prevalent phages. In addition, we identify potential auxiliary metabolic genes, such as a sulfur metabolism gene carried by phages that are predominantly temperate and likely infect mutualistic honey bee core bacteria. This gene is associated with land use around the sampled hives, indicating complex ecological interactions in the tripartite system of the honey bee, its microbiota and the phages therein.Subject terms: Data processing, Microbial ecology, Metagenomics, Bacteriophages, Agroecology

Introduction

Honey bees (Apis mellifera) have been tied to human culture since the Stone Age1. Nowadays, the estimated economic value of their pollination services exceeds that of beeswax and honey 25-fold2–4. While honey bees are indispensable pollinators of many crops, managed colonies suffer from various health challenges. For example, deformed wing virus (DWV) has become the most prevalent and detrimental honey bee pathogen via global expansion of its vector, the mite Varroa destructor5. The microsporidian endoparasite Vairimorpha ceranae (formerly Nosema ceranae) is also linked to decreased colony survival6, especially in combination with pesticide exposure7.

The gut microbiota is an increasingly appreciated contributor to bee health. All individuals share a core set of bacterial species of the genera Gilliamella, Snodgrassella, Lactobacillus, Bombilactobacillus and Bifidobacterium8, although individual workers maintain considerable strain diversity9,10. While mutualism between honey bees and their gut bacteria has become widely acknowledged11, research into viruses (phages) infecting these bacteria has only begun recently12–18. Phages can have a profound impact on their environment by exerting high selection pressures on their bacterial hosts or by altering their phenotypes. Some phages carry auxiliary metabolic genes (AMGs), which may play different roles in the virus’ ecology, depending on its lifestyle. As virulent phages lyse their host cell soon after infection, their AMGs can be a means to exploit host resources for immediate virion production19. Temperate phages, on the other hand, can integrate into their host’s genome as prophages, linking their fitness to that of the bacterium, whereby their AMGs may be collaborative, i.e., convey an advantage for both, and even their animal superhost20,21. Some AMGs occur in various environments around the globe–e.g., the sulfur metabolism gene cysH, which encodes phosphoadenosine phosphosulfate (PAPS) reductase22.

Previous studies on honey bee gut phages have been limited in breadth, impeding a comprehensive characterisation of the phageome and its role in bee homeostasis. We address this knowledge gap by employing a large-scale metagenomic sequencing endeavour and provide a comprehensive description of honey bee gut phages, profiling assembled phage genomes taxonomically and ecologically. We assess the existence of a globally-present core phageome and explore the role of AMGs via their association with land use.

Results

The majority of honey bee phages are undescribed and belong to novel taxa

A total of 150 pools of 10 bees each were collected from 1 apiary per country in Portugal (PT), France (FR), the United Kingdom (UK), Belgium (BE), the Netherlands (NL), Switzerland (CH), Germany (DE) and Romania (RO) in spring, summer and autumn 2020 (Supplementary Data 1). Guts were dissected into midgut, ileum and rectum, yielding 450 samples (Fig. 1). All samples were enriched for viruses by filtration and centrifugation. RNA and DNA was extracted, RNA reverse-transcribed and resulting double-stranded DNA was randomly amplified and Illumina-sequenced (“Methods”). Sequencing reads from all samples were quality-filtered and assembled into contigs before collapsing highly similar contigs, identifying phage genomes, and assessing their completeness.

A total of 51 Mb were assembled into 2343 non-redundant, ≥50%-complete phage genomes, recruiting 1.9 billion (29%) of the trimmed, host-removed reads. After quality filtering, 433 samples with detected phages remained, representing all 150 bee pools. Bacterial sequences were removed from 4 genomes and 1066 genomes (45%) were estimated to be >90% complete, indicating that the vast majority of phages were present as virions, rather than being prophage remnants of bacterial genomes.

As viruses lack a universal marker gene, we used vConTACT3 (ref. 23) for taxonomic classification, which infers viral taxonomy based on gene sharing networks from viruses of known taxonomy. By this, 1393 genomes were assigned to the Caudoviricetes class, representing 100 undescribed (novel) orders (Fig. 2). While 1220 of these 1393 genomes formed 249 novel families, 173 fell into 13 Caudoviricetes families recognized by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV). Another 638 phages shared class-level similarity in vConTACT3 but consisted of 3 ICTV classes with short, single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) genomes: Faserviricetes, Huolimaviricetes and Malgrandaviricetes. The latter contains the family Microviridae (order Petitvirales). As 633 of these 638 phages were classified as Microviridae by geNomad24, we assigned them to an order-level taxon here called microviruses. Following the classification framework by Kirchberger and colleagues25, who propose to elevate the Microviridae family to order-level, we assigned 321 microvirus genomes to 7 of the proposed families. Another ambiguity between class-level similarity in vConTACT3 and ICTV-recognized taxonomy was the case for 11 genomes of the RNA phage classes Vidaverviricetes or Leviviricetes, which remained unclassified on order and family level. Finally, 1 genome was assigned to the filamentous phage class Tokiviricetes, falling into a novel order and family. Overall, 1715 genomes (73%) were assigned to one of 270 groups of family-level similarity, but the majority of those (1221, 71%) currently lack a name and description.

To further assess the novelty of our 2343 phage genomes, we clustered them by ≥70% average nucleotide identity (roughly representing genus-level similarity) with the current INPHARED dataset26—an extensive and curated collection of phage genomes from public databases–supplemented with additional prophage genomes from honey bee gut bacteria27. Only 49 of our genomes were clustered (Supplementary Data 2), 10 of which were core phages (see below) and clustered with prophage genomes from Gilliamella apicola (4), Snodgrassella alvi (2), Lactobacillus melliventris (2) and Bombilactobacillus mellis (2). Of the remaining 39 genomes, 27 clustered with Bifidobacterium asteroides phages previously isolated from honey bees12 and 1 with a phage infecting Paenibacillus larvae, the causative agent of the bee disease American foulbrood. Nine genomes clustered with phages likely originating from the environment (infecting Escherichia, Pantoea, Yersinia, Erwinia, Xantomonas, Klebsiella, Enterobactria and Rhanella), and the remaining 2 genomes with unclassified microviruses.

Honey bees house a core phageome

We hypothesized the existence of a core phageome, equivalent to the core bacteriome. To test this, we conservatively established a phage’s presence in a sample by requiring ≥70% of its genome to be covered by mapped reads. Most of the 2343 phages occurred in a single or a few of the 150 bee pools. However, 3 phages were detected in 135, 137, and 146 pools (90%, 91% and 97%), respectively (Supplementary Data 2). Additionally, most of the phages were site- and therefore country-specific, although 97 occurred in all 8 countries (Fig. 3a). Given that metagenomic sampling captures a random and incomplete subset of the present diversity and that our sampling locations were dispersed over substantial distances, we consider these 97 viruses part of a prokaryotic honey bee core virome, i.e., a core phageome. All core phages belonged to 17 novel Caudoviricetes orders and 94 of them fell into 23 novel families, while 2 were classified as Peduoviridae and 1 as Herelleviridae (Supplementary Data 2). Mean relative abundances of core phages averaged to 22 ± 2.1% (mean ± SEM) within countries (Fig. 3b).

Tools commonly used to predict phage lifestyle (virulent vs. temperate) might systematically underestimate the number of temperate phages in metagenomic datasets. To test the suitability of the novel tool Replidec28, we compared its performance to that of the commonly-used tools BACPHLIP29 and PhaTYP30 using genomes of known virulent and temperate phages (the latter entirely from honey bees, “Methods”). While all tools largely agreed in their correct assignment of virulent phages, Replidec assigned 222 (95%) of the 233 prophage genomes as temperate, whereas BACPHLIP and PhaTYP assigned only 78 (33%) and 175 (75%) genomes as such, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, Replidec’s assignments for incomplete temperate genomes remained consistent, while BACPHLIP’s and PhaTYP’s performance decreased further. We therefore report Replidec’s lifestyle predictions of our phages (see Supplementary Data 2 for predictions of all tools).

As all core phages were caudoviruses, we compared their predicted replication strategy to that of the 1296 non-core Caudoviricetes phages. Most of the non-core phages (776, 60%) and nearly all (92) of the 97 core phages were identified as temperate (Fig. 3c), and a small number in each group–22 (2%) and 3, respectively–was predicted to continuously shed virions from their host without killing it, i.e., follow a chronic lifestyle. It should be noted, however, that Replidec calls a chronic lifestyle based on the detection of inovirus-like genes and should therefore be interpreted cautiously in our context. The remaining 2 virulent core phages were a Herelleviridae member predicted to infect Lactobacillus and a novel-family phage without predicted host, respectively.

We predicted the bacterial hosts of our phages with iPHoP31, which combines several approaches—matches to CRISPR spacer or prophage sequences, k-mer frequency similarities and machine learning algorithms—for genus-level host predictions. The bacterial hosts of 956 (43%) non-core phages could be predicted, mostly as Bifidobacterium (319, 14%) and Lactobacillus (199, 9%, Fig. 3d). Phages infecting the other bacterial core genera (Gilliamella, Bombilactobacillus and Snodgrassella) summed up to 67 (3%) and another 94 (4%) infected other common bee-associated genera (Frischella, Commensalibacter, Bombella and Bartonella–the latter was recently suggested to be renamed Ditibartonella)32. Hosts likely originating from the environment were predicted for 277 (12%) non-core phages. In contrast, 90 of the 97 core phages could be assigned to a host, and almost all (86) were predicted to infect a bacterial core genus, predominantly Gilliamella (35) and Lactobacillus (34), followed by the other core bacteria (17). Two core phages were assigned to Frischella, 1 to Apilactobacillus and 1 to Acinetobacter.

To confirm that our core phages are indeed highly prevalent among honey bees worldwide, we screened publicly available Illumina sequencing datasets from every current A. mellifera phageome study12–17. We detected all 97 core phages in those datasets, expanding their range to 10 countries on 3 continents, despite the sampling of these studies spanning over 11 years (Fig. 4a). Twelve core phages, predicted to infect Lactobacillus (8), Gilliamella (2), Snodgrassella (1) and an unknown host (1), were found in all datasets (Fig. 4b). Core phage retrieval appeared to scale mainly by the number of reads per analysed pool, potentially reflecting the extent of viral diversity sampled. We employed a sequencing effort ~6 times as high as all previous studies combined (Fig. 4a) and re-sampled hives in different seasons, potentially capturing a high proportion of the phage diversity present in those hives. Of note, we detected 88 core phages (range: 0–57, median: 19) in the 49 samples of Ndiaye and colleagues, although these samples did not comprise pools, but single animals, indicating that core phages are prevalent across individual worker bees.

Phage diversity profiles differ between gut parts and correlate with bacterial and bee diversity

To acquire basic ecological profiles of our phages, we calculated intra-sample (alpha) diversities (Hill numbers of Shannon indices) and inter-sample (beta) diversities (pair-wise Bray–Curtis dissimilarities and SNP distances). Alpha diversities gradually increased along the anterior-posterior gut axis; a trend driven by the core phages, even though their genome count was low compared to the total (Fig. 5a). While without apparent pattern, alpha diversities also differed between countries except in core phages, as expected by their definition. Between sampling seasons, alpha diversities remained consistent, indicative of a temporally stable phageome.

Similarly, variation of Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were driven mostly by the gut part the samples stemmed from, and less so by the country of origin (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 2). Again, this effect was strongest when only considering core phages, indicating their specificity for the bee gut, whereas the non-core fraction contains more environmental phages. We continued by testing for geographic and temporal patterns in allelic variation and whether these are linked between phages, bacteria and bees. Variation in pair-wise SNP distances was almost entirely driven by country of origin (Fig. 5c). This was the case in all three mutually-exclusive sets of reads, mapping against the honey bee genome, core bacteria genomes or assembled phage genomes, respectively. Moreover, the resulting SNP distance matrices all correlated with each other (Fig. 5c inset), indicating parallel evolutionary trajectories of the honey bee, its bacterial gut microbiome and the phages therein.

Phage-conferred metabolism is associated with agricultural land use

Next, we sought to explore functional roles of the phageome. To this end, we first refined the completeness of our core phage genomes using COBRA, increasing the length of 33 core phages by an average of 5 kb (18% of their original length) and increasing their completeness estimate by an average of 11 percentage points. Completeness improved for all but one contig, whose estimate decreased from 56–42%, because CheckV compared the refined sequence to a longer (hypothetical) complete genome. This contig was nevertheless retained for further analysis. We then screened all genomes (core and non-core) for AMGs by first employing Colabfold33, utilising AlphaFold2 (ref. 34), to predict the 3-dimensional structure of all phage proteins. Proteins were identified by structure comparison and their functions were inferred with the structural annotation tool Phold35 based on labels from the Prokaryotic Virus Remote Homologous Groups (PHROGs) database36. We also inferred functions using sequence-based homology searches against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database37. Across all 2343 genomes, 71,690 coding sequences were predicted, of which 1208 (2%) were placed into the PHROG category containing potential AMGs, and 38,042 (53%) were assigned to one of the other PHROG categories (Fig. 6a). Of the 1208 potential AMG sequences, 141 were assigned to a KEGG ortholog (KO), 27 of which belonged to a curated set of KOs associated with AMGs22,38 and represented 16 distinct functions. Although not assigned to a KO, 119 additional sequences were annotated with one of these 16 functions and included in further evaluations. Of particular interest were 3 of these 16 functions, as their genes were present in all countries. For simplicity, we hereafter refer to these genes by their inferred functions, and their relative abundances and prevalences denote those of the phages encoding them. These genes encoded PAPS reductase, glucosyltransferase and levanase. However, most of the glucosyltransferase and levanase genes were excluded by strict manual curation in the spirit of suggested guidelines39 (“Methods”), so we focused our further analyses on PAPS reductase. PAPS reductase occurred on 52 contigs of lengths between 16.1 and 83.0 kb (median 39.2 kb) and a median genome completeness of 95.7%. geNomad did not detect bacterial sub-sequences on those contigs and assigned virus scores of ≥0.998. All PAPS reductase-encoding genomes were classified as Caudoviricetes and predicted hosts included core bacteria, namely Gilliamella and Snodgrassella (Fig. 6b). Although these 52 PAPS reductase-carrying genomes represent only 4% of our 1393 Caudoviricetes phages, they occurred in 269 (62%) of our samples, revealing the gene as widely-spread but rare inside phage communities, in line with an auxiliary role.

We applied several mixed-effects models to assess whether land use parameters are associated with PAPS reductase or pathogens (Supplementary Data 4–7). Land use parameters encompassed the cropland area in a 2 km radius around the sampling sites–retrieved from satellite land cover data of the European Union’s Earth observation programme Copernicus–and estimated pesticide usage in these areas based on information from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO). As a correlate for pathogen load, we used previously-generated Ct values40. PAPS reductase relative abundance was negatively linked to cropland area around the sampling sites as well as the estimated total pesticide use and that of fungicides & bactericides, herbicides and plant growth regulators in particular (Fig. 6c). The gene’s relative abundance was reduced by 44–61% between the site with the least versus most land use. Interestingly, while PAPS reductase relative abundance was also negatively associated with two insecticide subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 3), overall insecticide use was linked to an increased probability of PAPS reductase presence in a sample (Fig. 6d).

An additional link to the environment was a strong association of DWV-B abundance with cropland area and an herbicide subgroup, presenting higher viral loads (i.e., lower Ct values in quantitative PCRs) with more intense agricultural land use (Ct reduction 8.30 and 11.18, respectively, Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 3). Similarly, sacbrood virus (SBV) loads were associated with the use of an herbicide subgroup (Ct reduction 5.50, Supplementary Fig. 3). On the other hand, black queen cell virus (BQCV) loads were unexpectedly negatively associated with insecticide use, although to a lesser degree (Ct increase 3.09 and 3.76, respectively, Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 3). None of the tested pathogens’ probabilities of presence were linked to any land-use parameter (Supplementary Data 7), but we note that Vairimorpha ceranae was present in 32 of the 98 qPCR-tested bee pools, including all countries.

Absolute virus counts corroborate metagenomic data

As Illumina sequencing experiments inherently provide relative data, we acquired absolute virus counts by flow cytometry. Midgut and ileum samples proved to be unsuitable for virus enumeration due to prohibitively low signal to noise ratios (data not shown). While the viral load in rectum samples ranged from 17,390 to 824,449 particles per microliter (median 96,920), the interquartile range spanned only a factor of 2 (from 65,244 to 145,723 particles per microliter, Supplementary Fig. 4). As 78% of the phage-mapped sequencing reads originated from rectum samples, this rather narrow range of viral loads lends overall support to our conclusions from the relative metagenomic data. Specifically, alpha and beta diversity calculations based on absolute viral loads corroborate those based on read counts (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

By acting through their bacterial hosts, phages exert a great range of effects on their environments, from influencing oceanic nutrient cycles41 to human pathogen virulence42. Although A. mellifera is an important pollinator, even proposed as a model organism for microbiome research43, its phages are poorly characterised. We addressed this knowledge gap by a large honey bee phage sequencing effort, supported by absolute virus quantification.

A minority of the 2343 assembled genomes could be assigned to known taxonomic families and only 49 were similar to previously published sequences, highlighting that honey bees house a specific and under-studied phageome. Regardless, we were able to place our phages into 270 family-level groups, enabling downstream analyses. Unlike other bee virome studies, we identified a considerable number of microviruses, likely due to our ssDNA-including laboratory procedures and the omission of restrictive length filters on assembled genomes imposed by others12,15,16. Almost none of the microviruses’ hosts could be predicted though, leaving their role in the honey bee gut unclear.

The existence of a core phageome in honey bees has been speculated upon before13, postulated based only on shared family-level taxonomy16 and doubted when demanding universal prevalence15. While our definition, based on presence in all 8 sampled countries, might appear broad, the vast phage diversity prohibits its exhaustive capturing. This is illustrated by an unsaturated accumulation curve of observed phages over our samples, despite our deep sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 6). In fact, when taking known bacterial strain diversity as a proxy10, our 10-bee pools would contain approximately 60–70% of a hive’s phage diversity. Our deep sequencing enabled the assembly of 97 core phages whose presence we confirmed in the raw data of previous studies, including one utilising individual bees. These data were sampled over more than a decade and from a vast geographic range, indicating that core phages are likely present worldwide in almost all hives, potentially in nearly all honey bee workers.

Similar to reports from bacteria10, core phage distributions showed little to no variation between locations and seasons (Fig. 5a, b). However, alpha diversities increased along the anterior-posterior gut axis (Fig. 5a). As per the definition of the Shannon index, this must be driven by increasingly similar abundances, as almost all core phages were present in all gut parts (Supplementary Fig. 7), suggesting specialised roles of different core phages along the gut. This is likely in relation to differently abundant core bacteria along the gut axis. The midgut is known to house very few core bacteria, the ileum is dominated by Gilliamella, Snodgrassella, Lactobacillus and Bombilactobacillus and the rectum by Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus and Bombilactobacillus8. However, the relative abundances of core phages infecting Snodgrassella or Bombilactobacillus were overall very low and Gilliamella phages made up a significant proportion of core phages in the rectum (Fig. 3d), indicating that phages may play a more prominent role for some core bacteria (especially Gilliamella) than others. SNP profiles were location specific (Fig. 5c), likely due to the generally high bacterial strain diversity in individual worker bees10. In line with recent findings on bacteria-phage interaction in the honey bee gut17, SNP distance matrices deduced from our sequencing reads of phages, bacteria and bees were correlated. This further supports the notion that the reportedly non-random bacterial strain composition in the bee gut is in part shaped by phages9.

Cropland represents a low-biodiversity landscape that can be detrimental for bee health44. Indeed, we found associations with DWV-B, indicating worse health in areas with more surrounding cropland (Fig. 6e). Pesticides can additionally challenge the bees’ health and microbiome, including substances not targeted at insects45, with grossly understudied sublethal and combined effects46. The association of insecticide use with less BQCV (Fig. 6e) is therefore counter-intuitive. However, the effect size is small and unlike DWV, BQCV loads in worker bees generally do not correlate with disease state47.

AMGs of temperate phages often confer benefits for their bacterial hosts. AMG-encoding phages of mutualistic bacteria would therefore also benefit the bee superhost. Interestingly, bee sulfur metabolism can be disrupted in settings of pesticide and Vairimorpha exposure7. Vairimorpha was prevalent in our samples and the relative abundances of PAPS reductase (which is involved in sulfur metabolism) was decreased in areas with more cropland and pesticide use (Fig. 6c). It is tempting to speculate that PAPS reductase could be a phage-mediated microbial indicator or promotor of bee health, similar to previous findings showing a phage-encoded PAPS reductase gene (cysH) to be enriched in centenarian humans whose gut phageome is thought to contribute to their longevity21. The opposite directions in associations between insecticide use on one hand and PAPS reductase probability of presence (increase) and relative abundances (decrease) on the other hand (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 3) then likely reflect a survivorship bias: Insecticides are highly lethal to bees, only survived by healthy individuals. As a health indicator or promotor, phage-encoded PAPS reductase would be found predominantly in healthy bees. It would therefore be observed more frequently after insecticide exposure, but in surviving bees, its abundance would decline as the bees’ health is deteriorated.

Concerns regarding virus enrichment to introduce a bias towards virulent phages16,27 seem unjustified, given that most caudovirus phages in our virus-enriched dataset were predicted to be temperate. This also indicates that spontaneous prophage induction48 is common in the honey bee gut. The prediction of 2 virulent core phages is remarkable with respect to their maintenance over such distances and time scales, but may also reflect a limitation in the prediction software or a general lack of knowledge about the diversity of phage replication strategies.

Although we employed up-to-date tools and conservative scrutiny, several limitations of our approach remain: While our laboratory procedure (NetoVIR protocol) is optimised for minimal bias, it is known to select against giant viruses and the use of beads during homogenisation may have affected potential enveloped phages more than others. Further, assignments of our phage genomes to taxonomic ranks, hosts and lifestyles as well as the structures of their proteins are based on predictive in silico models, which have to balance sensitivity and specificity. While experimental validation would be desirable, especially for the function of PAPS reductase, bacterial homologs of this gene have been studied in detail (e.g., refs. 49–51), and collective evidence in the context of phages (e.g., refs. 21,52–54) strongly suggests it as a true AMG. Lastly, the links between land use and PAPS reductase or pathogen load are associative rather than causal and call for further investigation. While many experimental studies elucidate the role of honey bee gut bacteria in health and disease—also in the context of land use and pesticide exposure–contributions of the phageome have so far been overlooked.

The work presented here highlights the complex ecological role of phages in the honey bee gut microbiome, their globally present core and responsiveness to the bee’s environment. Although phages ultimately kill the bacteria they infect, phage-bacterium interactions can be far more intricate than that of typical pathogen-host pairs55. With PAPS reductase, we identified an AMG in viruses infecting bacterial mutualists (Gilliamella and Snodgrassella) of the honey bee, suggesting a collaborative role in this tripartite community of honey bee, bacteria and phages.

Methods

Sample collection, dissection and virus enrichment

One-hundred-and-fifty bee pools of 10 worker bees each were collected from 6 to 10 hives (63 hives in total) of single apiaries by partners in PT, FR, UK, BE, NL, CH, DE, and RO in the context of the B-GOOD project40 and the ITN VIROINF56 during spring, summer and autumn 2020, except for CH, where no bees were acquired in spring (Supplementary Data 1). Collected bees were immediately killed on dry ice and transported to the lab to be stored at −80 °C. For dissection, bees were slowly thawed under a sterile laminar flow bench where their guts were removed and sectioned into midgut, ileum and rectum using separate sterile tools before the gut parts were collected in cooled PBS. The total of 450 samples were homogenized (MINILYS, Bertin Technologies) at 3000 rpm for 1 min with 2.8 mm zirconium oxide beads (Precellys) and virus particles were enriched as per the NetoVIR protocol57. This protocol is designed to retrieve viral genomes of all molecule types (single-stranded/double-stranded, linear/circular, DNA/RNA) while introducing minimal bias for any particular viral groups. To achieve this, homogenates were centrifuged at relatively low speed (17,000 × g) for 3 min to sediment debris before supernatants were passed through PES filters with rather large (0.8 µm) pores to remove the majority of bacterial and eukaryotic cells, while retaining all but the largest known viruses. Filtrates were depleted of free-floating nucleic acids by digestion with benzonase (Millipore) and micrococcal nuclease (NEB). Aliquots of 120 µl each were then separated for virus particle counting and metagenomic sequencing, respectively (see below). Blank controls without gut homogenate were processed in the same way.

DNA/RNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

Virus-enriched homogenates were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) were extracted using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, but without carrier RNA. Extracts underwent reverse transcription and random amplification (WTA2, Merck) and the resulting double-stranded (complementary) DNA was processed into Illumina sequencing libraries using the Nextera XT kit (Illumina) before sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), generating 22 ± 5.2 million read pairs of 150 bp length per library (mean ± SD, range 10–49 million, Supplementary Data 1).

Virus particle counting

We acquired absolute virus counts via flow cytometry as developed by Brussaard and colleagues58. Specifically, to fix virus particles, 120 µl of virus-enriched homogenates (see above) were mixed with glutaraldehyde (EM-grade, 25%, Merck) to a final concentration of 0.5% in a final volume of 240 µl and then incubated at 4 °C for 15 min, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further use. Series of twofold and tenfold dilutions with final volumes of 500 µl each were prepared in filter-sterilised Tris-EDTA before staining nucleic acids with SYBR Green I (Merck). Virus particles were enumerated using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) with a 488 nm argon laser and trigger on green fluorescence. Events inside virus-typical gates of green fluorescence intensity versus side scatter (Supplementary Fig. 4) were enumerated with CellQuest pro (BD Biosciences) and FCS Express (De Novo software). Five blank Tris-EDTA solutions were processed between all samples and events recorded in the last blank were subtracted from the virus counts of the following sample. Viral load was calculated from the counts of events, taking the overall dilution of the initial sample homogenate, the flow rate of the cytometer and the measurement duration into account.

Metagenome assembly

Raw sequencing reads were pre-processed and assembled using the ViPER pipeline59 v.2.1, relying on biopython60 (v.1.81), diamond61 (v.2.1.8), fastqc (v.0.12.1), krona62 (v.2.8.1), multiqc (v.1.14), pybedtools63,64 (v.0.9.0), pysam (v.0.21.0), python (v.3.10.12), quast65 (v.5.2.0), seqkit66 (v.2.5.1), openpyxl (v.3.1.2), sqlite (v.3.41.2) and others (see below). Specifically, duplicate reads were collapsed using the clumpify.sh script from bbmap67 v.39.01, adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed off with Trimmomatic68 v.0.39, before mapping against the honey bee genome (GCF_003254395.2) with bowtie2 (ref. 69) v.2.5.1. To avoid assemblies of honey bee sequences, only unmapped reads as well as random subsets of 1 and 10% thereof were assembled using metaSPAdes70 v.3.15.5. Resulting contigs were screened with geNomad24 v.1.8.0 and CheckV71 v.1.0.1 to trim off bacterial sequences from any proviral genomes and to exclude potentially chimeric contigs, respectively. To collapse redundancy, contigs from the different (sub-) sets were clustered using BLAST+72 v.2.14.0 and CheckV’s anicalc.py and aniclust.py scripts according to the MIUViG guideline73, i.e., ≥95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) over ≥85% length of the shorter contig. To divide the computational burden, this was first done per sample and then across all samples, retaining the longest contig per cluster as representative. The novelty of the assembled genomes was assessed by clustering the representative contigs together with the INPHARED dataset26 v.14 (14 April 2025)–supplemented with prophage genomes from honey bees27 which were not included in the INPHARED dataset–with ≥70% ANI over ≥85% length, roughly representing genus-level similarity.

Phage identification, genome completeness estimation and relative abundance

Cluster-representative contigs with an estimated genome completeness of ≥50% (CheckV) were passed to geNomad and their identification was looked up in the ICTV’s Virus Metadata Resource table74 v.19 (MSL37). Contigs were retained as a non-redundant phage genome set if they either belonged to groups which listed bacteria in the column Host Source or were unclassified by geNomad. Contigs classified by geNomad as Picobirnaviridae were also retained if an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase was detected in an online BLASTx against the nr database (8 February 2026) and despite completeness estimates of <50%, as their genomes are segmented. Trimmed, host-removed reads (see above) from each individual sample were mapped against these phage contigs with bwa-mem2 (ref. 75) v.2.2.1. Counts of mapped reads per contig were recorded with samtools76 v.1.17, but conservatively set to 0 if <70% of a contig was covered by reads or its average depth of coverage was <1 to avoid spurious detection of phages. The resulting read counts were first normalized by the contig lengths and then by the total number of reads per sample to acquire relative abundances. All statistical analyses and data visualisations were performed in RStudio77 v.2023.12.1.402 running R78 v.4.3.1 and utilising packages tidyverse (v.2.0.0), renv (v.1.1.4), patchwork (v.1.3.0), forcats (v.1.0.0), ggforce (v.0.4.2), gggenomes (v.1.0.1), ggrepel (v.0.9.6), ggtext (v.0.1.2), ggtree (v.3.10.1), ggVennDiagram (v.1.5.2), readxl (v.1.4.5), stringi (v.1.8.7), tidytext (v.0.4.2) and others (see below). Contigs identified as contaminants by the package decontam79 (v.1.22; relying on phyloseq v.1.46.0) were excluded. Presence of core phages was checked in all publicly available datasets from studies that performed virus-enriched metagenomic sequencing of honey bees12–17 by trimming, mapping to the phage genomes and filtering the respective raw Illumina reads from NCBI’s sequence read archive (SRA, Supplementary Data 8) as described above. All data were downloaded using the tools ncbi-datasets-cli v.16.6.0, entrez-direct v.21.6, and sra-tools v.3.1.1. In case those studies also sequenced non-enriched samples, only the reads from virus-enriched samples were analysed. Overlapping presence of core contigs in different datasets was visualised with the R package UpSetR (v.1.4.0).

Taxonomic classification, host and lifestyle prediction

To improve geNomad’s taxonomic assignment of phage genomes, we utilized vConTACT3 (ref. 23) v.3.0.0.b38 for its gene sharing-based classification. In case several taxa were equally valid, the taxon in concordance with higher ranks was chosen to ensure a consistent phylogenetic lineage for all genomes. Phages assigned to the class Faserviricetes|Huolimaviricetes|Malgrandaviricetes by vConTACT3 and to the family Microviridae by geNomad were given the order microviruses and classified by Kirchberger et al.‘s gene sharing framework25, as the authors’ recent proposal for the phylogeny of microviruses is not yet reflected in the ICTV’s current taxonomy.

To predict the bacterial hosts of our phage genomes, the suite iPHoP31 v.1.3.3 was used (confidence setting ≥90%), which integrates the orthogonal approaches of several tools80–83, utilising matches to CRISPR spacer or prophage sequences, k-mer frequency similarities and machine learning algorithms. For lifestyle predictions, we relied on Replidec28 v.0.2.3.1, employing a naïve Bayes classifier, which is less sensitive to incompleteness of genomes compared to other tools. As Replidec is not peer-reviewed yet, we compared its performance to that of the commonly used tools BACPHLIP29 and PhaTYP30, the latter via their web interface (https://phage.ee.cityu.edu.hk/phabox, 7 February 2026). For temperate phages, we downloaded a set of prophage genomes recently described in honey bee gut core bacteria27 and filtered for the 233 genomes designated as full. For comparison, we acquired a list of all 164 Caudoviricetes genomes described as virulent from the PhageDive database84 of the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures that had a Genbank accession number (as of 6 December 2025) and supplemented this list with 10 virulent phages which were previously isolated from honey bees12. To simulate genome incompleteness, we symmetrically cropped all of these sequences on both ends to 83%, 67% and 50% of their original length.

Diversity profiles

Shannon indices and Bray–Curtis dissimilarities were computed using the R package vegan (v.2.6–10) separately for core, non-core and all phages by summing mapped read counts per family, ignoring unclassified genomes, and randomly selecting the same total number of reads for each sample. This total was set based on observed gaps in counts between samples (1073 for core phages, 1694 for non-core phages and 1548 for all phages, Supplementary Fig. 8), and samples with fewer mapped reads were ignored. This process was repeated 1000 times, averaging diversity metrics over the entire rarefaction, as suggested by Patrick D. Schloss85, using the R package furrr (v.0.3.1). Shannon diversities were converted into effective number of species (Hill numbers) and tested for differences using a Kruskal–Wallis test, post-hoc followed by a two-sided Wilcoxon ranked sum test using R packages ggpubr (v.0.6.0), multcompView (v.0.1–10) and rstatix (v.0.7.2). Bray–Curtis dissimilarities and the effect of different metadata variables on the observed variation were determined and tested for significant contribution in an adapted distance-based redundancy analysis using the R packages RLdbRDA (v.0.1.2) and ape (v.5.8-1) and PCoA plots were colourized with RColorBrewer (v.1.1-3).

As the virus enrichment (see above) was performed under rather relaxed conditions, the sequencing data also contained sufficient honey bee and bacterial reads for analysis. Using SKA86 v.1.0, we could therefore calculate pairwise SNP distances between samples on 3 mutually-exclusive subsets of trimmed reads, mapping to (i) the honey bee genome, (ii) a representative collection of honey bee core bacteria genomes (Supplementary Data 9) and (iii) the phage genomes. In all cases, reads were merged per bee pool. SKA reports split k-mers of length 31 with a coverage of ≥4 and a polymorphic middle base with a frequency of ≥0.2. Only one allele per site and sample remained, in effect satisfying the tool’s assumption for haploidy. The SNP distance between two samples is defined as the proportion of shared k-mers whose middle bases differ between the samples. Note that this definition as per SKA’s source code is different to the one given in the paper. Resulting distance matrices were subjected to a distance-based redundancy analysis and tested for correlation with a Mantel test.

Contig completeness refinement and functional annotation

To increase the completeness of the 97 core genome assemblies, COBRA54 v.1.2.3 was utilized, before passing all phage genomes to Pharokka87,88 v.1.7.1 for sequence homology-based functional gene annotation. Predicted gene sequences were subjected to 3D structure prediction with AlphaFold2 (ref. 34) utilising ColabFold33 followed by structure-based phage annotation with Phold33,35,36,87,89,90 v.0.2.0 for more sensitive functional gene annotation and assignment to PHROG36 categories. To acquire a list of metabolic genes coded by phages, we performed a GhostKOALA91 search on the KEGG’s website with all gene sequences annotated to the PHROG category moron, auxiliary metabolic gene and host takeover. Genes assigned to a KO were further screened to (i) be included in a list of curated phage metabolic KOs22,38 (ii) exclude genes on contig edges (unless their genome is 100% complete) as they might be bacterial, (iii) include only genes if between their own position and both contig edges there is at least one other gene assigned to a PHROG category (including unknown function and other) to further ensure viral context, (iv) exclude genes if their nearest annotated neighbouring genes on both sides are structural (PHROG categories head and packaging, connector and tail) as this would be unexpected for true AMGs, and (v) include only genes if 2 of the 3 top-scoring hits in the structure-based searches agreed in their specific annotation to exclude genes potentially misannotated due to shared domains. For each gene, the relative abundances of phages carrying that gene were summed per sample to acquire relative gene abundances.

Quantitative PCR of honey bee pathogens

Ct values of BQCV, SBV, DWV-B, acute bee paralysis virus (ABPV), Vairimorpha ceranae and chronic bee paralysis virus (CBPV) were generated previously40, taking separate worker bees from the same hives and at the same timepoints as described above, i.e., animals equivalent to our bee pools. These qPCRs were performed by the national reference laboratories in BE (Sciensano) and DE (Friedrich-Loeffler-Institut) according to the official guidelines of the European Union (EU) Reference Laboratory for Bee Health (ANSES).

Association of metabolic genes with land use and pathogen load

The crop land area in a 2 km radius around all sampling locations in 2019 was derived from satellite land cover data of the EU’s Earth observation programme Copernicus92. Data for country-wide pesticide usage and total cropland area were downloaded from the FAO2 to estimate the amounts of different pesticides applied around the sampling locations in 2019 (Supplementary Data 10). These estimates included the total amounts of all pesticides and groups thereof, namely herbicides, insecticides, fungicides & bactericides and plant growth regulators, as well as 7, 4, and 6 subgroups of herbicides, insecticides and fungicides & bactericides, respectively. Together with the cropland area, we therefore obtained a total of 23 land-use parameters. Linear and logistic mixed-effects models were constructed with the R packages lme4 (v.1.1-37) and lmerTest (v.3.1-3), accounting for the hierarchical structure of our data by defining gut part (midgut, ileum, rectum) and season (spring, summer, autumn) as fixed effects and hive ID (63 hives) with a random intercept. Linear models were used to test the quantitative association of log-transformed relative abundances of PAPS reductase with land-use parameters and with pathogen Ct values. Logistic models were used to test the effect of the land-use parameters on the probability of PAPS reductase or pathogen presence. BQCV, SBV and DWV-B Ct values were tested quantitatively, because most (100%, 99%, and 95%, respectively) qPCR tests were positive. ABPV, V. ceranae and CBPV were tested in logistic models for their presence/absence, because an intermediate proportion (59%, 33%, and 23%, respectively) of qPCR tests were positive. P-values were separately adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method for all tests of relative PAPS reductase abundance vs. land-use parameters, PAPS reductase presence vs. land-use parameters, pathogen Ct values vs. land-use parameters and pathogen presence vs. land-use parameters. We confirmed that the assumptions for all models were met by inspecting fitted values versus raw residuals with the R package DHARMa v.0.4.7.
