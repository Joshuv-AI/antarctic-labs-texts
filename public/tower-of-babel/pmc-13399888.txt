Hospital course and duration of oxygen supplementation in children with unoperated intracardiac posttricuspid left-to-right shunts: A prospective observational study
Authors: Nimisha Sharma, Sakshi Sachdeva, Dinesh Kumar, Lokesh Sharma, Munish Guleria, Hema Gupta Mittal, Dheeraj Deo Bhatt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Children with left-to-right (L->R) shunts often need hospital admission and oxygen supplementation due to intercurrent respiratory infection and worsening heart failure. The duration of oxygen requirement in these children is often unknown. This study was done to determine the hospital course of children with unoperated posttricuspid L->R shunts admitted to a hospital with a need for supplemental oxygen. The primary objective was to find the proportion of children requiring prolonged oxygen supplementation (>1 month).Methods:In this prospective observational study, 122 children with a median (interquartile range) age of 5 (3–8.75) months, having a hemodynamically significant posttricuspid L->R shunt and a need for supplemental oxygen, were enrolled. N terminal B type natriuretic peptide (NT pro-BNP) and C-reactive protein were measured at baseline, 2-, and 3-weeks postenrollment. Decongestive measures, antibiotics, and supportive measures were given, and patients were followed up during their hospitalization.Results:22.1% patients died, and 12.3% had prolonged oxygen requirement. 65.5% of admissions and 66.6% of deaths occurred between September and December. At the time of enrollment, NT pro-BNP above age-related cut-off was found in < 20% of patients. On multivariate analysis, the need for mechanical ventilation at admission and the need for escalation of mode of oxygen delivery were independently associated with mortality.Conclusion:Children with hemodynamically significant posttricuspid L->R shunts admitted to a hospital with a need for supplemental oxygen have an unfavorable short-term outcome. A substantial proportion of children needing oxygen supplementation succumbed. Seasonal variation in admission and mortality was seen. NT pro-BNP was elevated in a minority of patients. These findings may have important clinical implications in the management.

INTRODUCTION

Children with unoperated posttricuspid left-to-right shunt are frequently admitted to the hospital for respiratory illnesses. Decreased compliance of the lungs and increased left atrial pressure due to increased pulmonary blood flow make these children vulnerable to respiratory infections.[1] Even a mild respiratory infection tips their precarious balance and pushes them toward acute decompensated heart failure. In comparison to children without an underlying heart disease, these children tend to have a longer duration of hospital stay and higher mortality during admission for respiratory illnesses.[2345]

Despite some evidence to the contrary, surgery is often delayed due to the perceived increased risk of mortality due to cardiac surgery during an ongoing infection.[67] While recovering from respiratory illnesses and waiting to be weaned from oxygen before corrective surgery, these children face the dual burden of uncontrolled heart failure and risk of recurrent infections. Continued prolonged oxygen requirement in such a scenario may result from persistent pneumonia, pulmonary edema, atelectasis, reactive airway disease, intra or extraluminal bronchial compression, mucosal edema, or even a vascular ring.

There is a paucity of data regarding the incidence and causes of prolonged oxygen requirement in children with unoperated congenital heart disease (CHD) who are admitted to a hospital with a need for supplemental oxygen.

This study was done with the primary objective of finding the incidence of prolonged oxygen requirement (>1 month) in children with hemodynamically significant posttricuspid left-to-right shunt, admitted with a need for supplemental oxygen.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a prospective observational study, conducted in the Division of Pediatric Cardiology of a Tertiary Care Hospital between August 2022 and February 2024.

Definitions of terms

Hemodynamically significant posttricuspid left-to-right shunt

It was defined based on the shunt size in posttricuspid lesions, expressed as Qp/Qs ratio on echocardiography. A Qp/Qs ratio of more than 1.5 was taken as hemodynamically significant shunt.

Respiratory distress

This was defined as fast breathing of > 60/min (0–1 month) >50/min in (1–12 months) >40/min (12–24 months) and/or grunting, wheeze, stridor, nasal flaring, head bobbing, chest retractions, sweating, and using accessory muscles of respiration.

Pneumonia

Respiratory distress with radiological evidence of consolidation, peribronchial nodules, ground-glass opacity, or patchy infiltrates.

Persistent pneumonia

Persistence of symptoms and radiological abnormalities for more than 1 month.

Escalation of oxygen therapy

Change of the mode of oxygen delivery from nasal prong (NP) to continuous positive airway pressure/high flow nasal cannula (CPAP/HFNC) to mechanical ventilation (MV). Escalation of the mode of therapy was at the discretion of the treating physician.

Oxygen supplementation

It was given for hypoxia (saturation <90% or PaO2 <60 mmHg in room air).

After enrollment, complete blood count, serum electrolytes, renal function test, chest X-ray, blood culture, C-reactive protein (CRP), and N-terminal B-type natriuretic peptide (NT pro-BNP) levels were measured in all patients, along with the assessment of their heart failure severity with Ross score. Ross score, CRP, and NT pro-BNP levels were again measured at the beginning of 2nd and 3rd weeks.

NT pro-BNP was measured using an Enzyme Immunoassay kit (AO Vector-Best A-9102). Age-related cutoffs were determined using the cut-offs given by Kiess et al.[8] Echocardiographic measurements and Z scores of LA and LV were calculated using Z scores of cardiac structures published by Pettersen et al.[9] The severity of congestive heart failure was graded using the Ross score.[10]

Additional investigations such as contrast-enhanced computed tomography chest and bronchoscopy were done based on the individual requirements of the patients, depending on the hemodynamic stability of the patients. Decongestive measures in the form of intravenous (IV) furosemide were given to all patients. Blood transfusion was given with a target hemoglobin of 7–10 g/dl, considering their oxygen requirement and hemodynamic status. Choice of IV antibiotics and antiviral (Tamiflu) was as per clinicians’ discretion.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation for the study was based on an expected proportion of subjects with prolonged supplemental oxygen requirement as 50% as per the formula: N = (Zα2 × PQ)/δ2. Where N is the sample size, Zα = 1.96, δ = 0.10 (10%), and Q = 1 − P.

Since it was presumed initially that approximately 100 patients would be available for inclusion in the study, applying finite population correction, a sample size of 60 was calculated, considering an attrition of 20%. However, a total of 122 patients were enrolled in the study.

Statistical data analysis

The presentation of the categorical variables was done in the form of number and percentage (%). Quantitative data were presented as the means ± standard deviation and as median with 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range [IQR]). The data normality was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Quantitative variables were analyzed using independent t-test/Mann–Whitney test. Associations of qualitative variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test/Fisher’s exact test. In cases in which the data were not normal, nonparametric tests were used. Multivariate logistic regression was used to find out significant risk factors of prolonged oxygen requirement and mortality. Data entry was done in the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and the final analysis was done with the use of Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, IBM manufacturer, Chicago, IL, USA, version 25.0. For statistical significance, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Institutional ethical approval was taken, and the study followed the good practice guideline advocated by Helsinki.

Computed tomographic (CT) scan of the chest was done in 12 of the 15 patients with persistent oxygen requirement. It revealed the presence of consolidation/collapse in all the patients. There was no evidence of tracheo-bronchial compression in any of the 12 patients. Bronchoscopy was carried out in 8 of the 15 patients with persistent oxygen requirement. It was also suggestive of infection and corroborated the CT findings. There was no airway stenosis/compression, mucus plug, or vascular ring reported by these additional investigations.

One-fifth (22.1%, n = 27) of patients died during the study period. Fifteen (12.3%) patients in the study had prolonged oxygen requirements (more than 30 days) due to persistent pneumonia. Marked seasonal variation in admissions and deaths was seen in this study as 65.5% of admissions and 66.6% of deaths occurred between September and December. On multivariate analysis, need for MV (odds ratio [OR]: 128.657, P = 0.012) at admission and need for escalation of the mode of oxygen delivery (OR: 16.323, P = 0.014) were independently associated with mortality [Supplementary Table 2]. On multivariate analysis, none of the clinical, biochemical, hematological, or echocardiography parameters were predictive of the need for prolonged oxygen therapy [Supplementary Table 3].

DISCUSSION

Since no prior study has prospectively evaluated the duration of oxygen supplementation needed in children with unoperated CHD, it is difficult to directly compare the results of our study with other studies. The duration of supplemental oxygen therapy in the current study is longer than that needed by children admitted with RSV bronchiolitis in the absence of underlying heart disease. Proesmans et al. reported that the median IQR duration of oxygen supplementation required by infants without heart disease admitted for RSV bronchiolitis is 3 (1–7) days.[11] Median (IQR) duration of hospital stay in the current study was 10.5 (7–16.7) days and was comparable to other studies in children with CHDs admitted with respiratory illnesses.[12131415] This suggests that compared to children without heart diseases, those with CHDs need prolonged hospitalization as well as prolonged duration of oxygen supplementation.

There was high in-hospital mortality (22.1% n = 27/122) in our study, which is comparable to other studies from lower resource settings but markedly higher than Western data.[1415161718] Thirty-one patients required MV (7 at enrollment, 24 subsequently escalated to MV), and 49 required escalation of the mode of respiratory support. On multivariate analysis, the need for MV and the need for uptitration of the initial mode of oxygen therapy were the only predictors of mortality.

Mortality rates for children with heart disease appear to vary according to the setting. Children who are admitted to the pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) in low-resource settings have reported even higher mortality than in the present study.[1819] Mohsin et al., from Pakistan, reported that among 34 patients with unoperated CHD admitted to PICU, there was 59% hospital mortality. Children having CHDs with increased pulmonary blood flow constituted 74% of their population.[18] Similarly, Banothu et al. published their experience of treating unoperated CHD in their PICU from New Delhi, India.[19] In their retrospective study of 41 patients, the overall mortality was 50%. Among those who were not operated upon, the mortality rate reached 58.8%. The most common lesion in their study was ventricular septal defect (VSD) (38.8%).

In contrast, in-hospital mortality in the Western world is much lower.[16] Medrano et al. published the outcome of their epidemiologic observational study from Spain. Among the 354 subjects with hemodynamically significant CHD who were admitted due to community-acquired respiratory infection, the mortality rate was only 1.98%. Like the current study, most of their study subjects were also < 12 months, and there was a high prevalence of malnutrition (nearly half of them had weight for age < 3rd centile). However, there were some important differences which could account for the huge difference in mortality. First, 51.9% patients in their study had undergone some form of surgical intervention; second, more than 80% of their subjects had received prophylaxis against RSV infection, and finally malnutrition was more severe and more prevalent in our study (all our patients had weight, length, and weight for length < 3rd centile). Recurrent hospitalization, malnutrition, and most importantly, lack of access to early cardiac surgery are important contributors to the high mortality seen in low-resource settings.

Only one-fifth of children in the current study had NT pro-BNP levels above the normal cut-offs at admission, despite all of them having a hemodynamically significant left-to-right shunt. Previous studies have suggested that NT pro-BNP level correlates with the severity of heart failure in children with CHDs.[202122232425] In our study, we did not assess the correlation between the severity of heart failure to NT pro-BNP levels, as most of our patients had moderate heart failure.

There are limited data on the ability of NT pro-BNP to predict outcome in children with left-to-right shunt lesions. A few studies have suggested that NT pro-BNP may have a role in predicting prognosis in children with CHDs. In a study by Chowdhury et al.,[21] 88 children with unoperated CHD (mostly VSDs), median NT pro-BNP levels were higher in those who died versus those who survived. However, in this study, the NT pro-BNP was measured in a point-of-care assay, and they did not report on the proportion of children with NT pro-BNP level above the age-related cut-off. In another study by Palm et al., the Z log of NT pro-BNP was found to be a good prognostic marker for major adverse cardiovascular events in children with a variety of CHDs.[25] However, unlike the present study, only a small proportion of their patients had an unoperated left-to-right shunt lesion.

In the present study, median NT pro-BNP levels at admission were higher in the group which had prolonged oxygen requirement or mortality. In univariate analysis, NT pro-BNP was associated with mortality as well as prolonged oxygen requirement. However, in multivariate analysis, serum levels of this biomarker were not a predictor of either mortality or prolonged oxygen requirement. Further studies are required to clarify the utility of NT pro-BNP to predict outcome in children with CHD.

In this study, all the patients who had prolonged oxygen requirement had persistent pneumonia. The average CRP values at baseline, at the end of the 2nd week, and at the end of the 3rd week were 5.97 ± 7.46 mg/dl, 8.24 ± 9.4 mg/dl, and 8.89 ± 9.93 mg/dl, respectively. This suggests the persistence of some form of infection in the cohort at 3rd week of admission. The chest X-ray, CT, and bronchoscopy findings also corroborated these findings as they also demonstrated the presence of persistent pneumonia.

Although previous studies have reported that 20%–30% of children with persistent pneumonia have an underlying CHD.[26272829] To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has prospectively assessed the incidence of persistent pneumonia in children with unoperated CHD. There was a striking seasonal variation in admission as well as mortality, with 65.5% (n = 80/122) of admissions and 66.6% (n = 18/27) of the deaths occurring from September to December. This period also coincides with the RSV season in Northern India.[17] Future studies specifically focusing on the spectrum of viral illnesses in children with heart disease from our region will help policymakers to assess the role of immunoprophylaxis against RSV infection in our region.

CONCLUSIONS

Twelve percent (15/122) of children with hemodynamically significant posttricuspid left-to-right shunt admitted with oxygen requirement had prolonged oxygen requirement for 30 days or more, due to persistent pneumonia. There was a high in-hospital mortality of 22%. All children had severe malnutrition. The predictors of mortality were the need for MV or the need for escalation of oxygen therapy during the admission. Less than one-fifth of the subjects had elevated NT pro-BNP levels at the time of enrollment. NT pro-BNP levels at admission did not predict mortality or prolonged oxygen requirement.

Limitations

Many patients were already on various heart failure medications at the time of enrolment, and these were continued during the hospitalization. Up-titration of diuretics, use of heart failure medications, antibiotics or antiviral agents, and the choice of the mode of oxygen delivery were decided by treating physicians and were not protocolized, therefore, could have affected the duration of oxygen requirement and/or mortality.
