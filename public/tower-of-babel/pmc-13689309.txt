Ferro-aging: a potential driver of degenerative bone and joint diseases
Authors: Songlin Liang, Shang Gao, Fumin Wang, Xintian Qu, Luming Kong, Chenmoji Wang, Nianhu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDegenerative bone and joint diseases (DBJDs), including osteoporosis (OP), osteoarthritis (OA), and intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD), are chronic age-related disorders characterized by progressive destruction of bone and joint tissues and loss of function, posing a heavy global health burden. Emerging evidence indicates that iron dyshomeostasis, chronic lipid peroxidation, and cellular senescence are common pathological features shared by these diseases. The newly discovered concept of ferro-aging—a non-lethal, persistent cellular state driven by sub-lethal lipid peroxidation that leads to cellular dysfunction and a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)—provides a unified mechanistic framework linking iron metabolism to age-related degeneration. This review systematically summarizes the molecular basis of ferro-aging, with a focus on the central role of ACSL4 in promoting membrane lipid remodeling and sustaining chronic lipid peroxidation. We discuss how ACSL4-associated ferro-aging contributes to the pathogenesis of OP, OA, and IVDD by disrupting local microenvironmental homeostasis, driving metabolic reprogramming, amplifying inflammatory SASP signaling, and impairing the function of key cell types such as osteoblasts (OBs), chondrocytes, and nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs). The differential response to the same ferro-aging program across different tissues determines the distinct clinical phenotypes of these diseases. Finally, we highlight potential intervention strategies targeting the ACSL4-ferro-aging axis and future research directions, offering new insights for the precision treatment of degenerative bone and joint diseases.Keywords: Ferro-aging, ACSL4, Lipid peroxidation, Cellular senescence, DBJDs

Introduction

Degenerative bone and joint diseases (DBJDs) are a class of chronic disorders characterized by progressive destruction of bone and joint tissue architecture and loss of function, primarily including osteoporosis (OP), osteoarthritis (OA), and intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD). Epidemiological studies have shown that OA affected 595 million people in 2020 and is projected to reach 1.10 billion by 2050 [1]; low bone mineral density—the core pathological feature of OP—accounted for 477,000 deaths and 17.2 million DALYs in 2020 [2]; and low back pain, the primary manifestation of IVDD, ranked as the eighth leading cause of global DALYs in 2022 [3]. With the accelerating aging of the global population, the prevalence of DBJDs continues to rise, making them an important public health issue that affects the quality of life of the elderly and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden [4–7]. OP, OA, and IVDD not only frequently coexist in the same patient but also share multiple risk factors, including aging, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic disorders [8, 9]. From a pathophysiological perspective, the development and progression of DBJDs essentially reflect the disruption of dynamic homeostasis among different cell types within the tissue microenvironment [10]. Therefore, in-depth elucidation of their common molecular drivers is of great significance for developing novel preventive and therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, with increasing research on ferroptosis, abnormal iron metabolism has gradually been recognized as an important pathological basis for aging-related diseases. Iron is an essential trace element for oxygen transport, energy metabolism, and DNA synthesis. However, excess free iron can generate large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the Fenton reaction. This induces lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and cellular dysfunction [11–13]. Traditionally, iron overload has been thought to promote tissue damage primarily by triggering ferroptosis [14, 15]; Nevertheless, recent studies have proposed a new concept distinct from ferroptosis—ferro-aging [16]. Ferro-aging is not an acute form of cell death but rather a chronic pre-pathological state characterized by persistent lipid peroxidation and progressive cellular dysfunction. Although cells in the ferro-aging state retain membrane integrity, they can sustain irreversible damage and continuously affect the local microenvironment through the formation of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), ultimately contributing to tissue functional decline and systemic aging [17].

Although accumulating evidence has confirmed that iron metabolism disorders, lipid peroxidation, and cellular senescence are involved in the pathological processes of OP, OA, and IVDD, research on the role of ferro-aging in degenerative bone diseases is still in its early stages. In particular, as a newly proposed concept, the functional definition, molecular characteristics, tissue specificity, and relationship of ferro-aging with other cell fates such as ferroptosis have not yet been fully elucidated. Therefore, re-examining the common pathological mechanisms of degenerative bone diseases from the new perspective of ferro-aging may help establish a unified theoretical framework linking abnormal iron metabolism, cellular senescence, and microenvironmental remodeling.

Based on this, the present review focuses on ferro-aging, systematically summarizing its molecular basis and potential connections with OP, OA, and IVDD, with an emphasis on how ferro-aging participates in disease progression by affecting the homeostasis of bone and joint tissue microenvironments. The goal is to provide a new theoretical foundation for a deeper understanding of the shared pathological mechanisms of degenerative bone diseases and for future precision intervention strategies.

Ferro-aging as a bridge between iron homeostasis imbalance and bone microenvironment aging

Bone is a highly dynamic organ, the homeostasis of which depends on the precise coordination among osteoblasts (OBs), osteoclasts (OCs), osteocytes, and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). The normal functions of these cells are jointly regulated by trace elements such as iron, calcium, and phosphorus, as well as by various endocrine factors [18]. Iron participates in oxygen transport, mitochondrial respiration, DNA synthesis, and multiple enzymatic reactions, serving as an important cofactor for maintaining energy metabolism and bone remodeling activities in bone cells. However, the redox activity of iron also confers its potential toxicity. When iron homeostasis is disrupted, excess free iron can induce oxidative stress and cellular damage, further affecting skeletal health and disease progression [19].

The body primarily maintains systemic iron homeostasis through the hepcidin-ferroportin (FPN) axis. Hepcidin secreted by the liver induces FPN degradation, thereby limiting intestinal iron absorption and macrophage iron release, and regulating the availability of circulating iron. Hepcidin deficiency can lead to systemic iron overload accompanied by decreased bone mechanical performance. Studies have shown that hepcidin-deficient mice exhibit significantly reduced maximum cortical bone load and maximum bending stress, along with elevated levels of the bone resorption marker CTX-1, suggesting that iron accumulation promotes bone loss by enhancing OC activity [20]. These results indicate that hepcidin is not only a core regulator of systemic iron homeostasis but also an important node in maintaining bone turnover balance.

In addition to the hepcidin-FPN axis, there is extensive signaling crosstalk between iron metabolism and bone metabolism. BMP signaling is involved in both the transcriptional regulation of hepcidin and the differentiation of OBs. Erythroferrone (ERFE) can suppress hepcidin expression by binding to BMP family members, thereby increasing iron availability to support erythropoiesis [21]. ERFE is abundantly expressed in OBs, and its deficiency leads to a low bone mass phenotype accompanied by increased bone resorption and compensatory enhancement of bone formation. Furthermore, calcitonin can enhance hepatic hepcidin transcription by promoting BMP6 expression [22]. These studies suggest that bone metabolism and iron metabolism are not independent of each other but form a complex regulatory network through the hepcidin-FPN axis and the BMP-ERFE network.

With aging, this bone-iron homeostasis network gradually becomes imbalanced. The aged bone marrow microenvironment exhibits features such as chronic inflammation, metabolic disorders, and mitochondrial dysfunction, accompanied by uncoupling of bone formation and resorption. At the cellular level, aged BMSCs display mitochondrial functional defects, including decreased membrane potential, reduced ATP production, and diminished osteogenic potential [23]. There is a close relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and iron metabolism disorders: on the one hand, excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) can destabilize proteins involved in iron homeostasis; on the other hand, iron accumulation further exacerbates oxidative stress, forming a continuously amplifying injury loop. Moreover, in a mouse model with specific knockout of Sod2 in OB precursor cells, impaired mtROS clearance leads to reduced bone mass, impaired mitochondrial respiration, and intracellular NAD+ depletion, indicating that mitochondrial oxidative stress is an important driver of bone microenvironment aging [24].

Single-cell RNA sequencing studies further demonstrate that mtROS not only induce classical senescence markers. They also promote functional reprogramming of osteogenic lineage cells. This occurs through interference with parathyroid hormone (PTH) signaling, protein homeostasis, and intracellular iron metabolism regulation [24]. Therefore, in the aged bone microenvironment, persistent oxidative stress, NAD+ depletion, and iron homeostasis dysregulation together constitute an important basis for long-term cellular functional imbalance and provide permissive conditions for the subsequent occurrence of ferro-aging.

Of note, iron homeostasis abnormalities in bone tissue do not always manifest as an increase in total iron content but exhibit significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity. Large-scale imaging studies have shown that with advancing age, the vertebral bone marrow fat fraction gradually increases, whereas the measured bone marrow iron concentration paradoxically decreases; osteoporotic patients also have lower bone marrow iron concentrations than healthy individuals [25]. This finding suggests that bone marrow adipose infiltration may interfere with iron concentration measurements, and relying solely on total iron content may not accurately reflect local iron burden. In contrast, the rapidly aging OXYS rat model displays abnormal accumulation of iron and phosphorus in bone tissue, a decreased calcium-to-phosphorus ratio, and mineralization defects [26]. Electron paramagnetic resonance studies have further revealed the presence of more abnormal coordination complexes between iron and bone matrix in the femoral cortical bone of elderly women [27]. These results indicate that the spatial distribution and chemical state of local iron may better reflect iron-related damage in aged bone tissue than total iron content.

Iron overload diseases provide additional evidence linking iron homeostasis imbalance to bone damage. Patients with thalassemia-induced bone disease (TIBD) often suffer from severe systemic iron overload due to long-term blood transfusions and compensatory increased iron absorption, and they develop bone loss and microarchitectural destruction at an early stage [28, 29]. Clinical studies have shown that elevated liver iron concentration is an important risk factor for OP and endocrine complications in these patients [30]. Notably, even after receiving optimized iron chelation therapy, some patients still exhibit significant decreases in bone mineral density, and their phenotype does not fully correlate with peripheral serum ferritin levels. This observation further suggests that peripheral circulatory iron parameters may not accurately reflect local iron burden or the associated damage in bone tissue [31].

In summary, iron homeostasis imbalance in the bone microenvironment can arise either from systemic iron dysregulation or be driven by aging-related mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and local metabolic remodeling. Its pathological consequences are not limited to acute cellular injury but tend to create a persistently low-grade oxidative stress state. Based on this understanding, elucidating the relationship between iron homeostasis imbalance and bone microenvironment aging will help to further clarify the mechanisms by which ferro-aging contributes to the development and progression of degenerative bone and joint diseases (Fig. 1).

Ferro-aging: a potential pathological axis in DBJDs

From acute ferroptosis to chronic ferro-aging: threshold-dependent cell fate determination

During the progression of degenerative bone and joint diseases, iron homeostasis imbalance not only serves as a trigger for cellular damage but also drives cells into a chronic pre-pathological state, namely ferro-aging, through lipid peroxidation. Traditional research has largely focused on acute iron-induced injury—ferroptosis—in which, under conditions of glutathione depletion and collapse of the GPX4 antioxidant axis, lipid peroxidation rapidly breaches the membrane tolerance threshold, ultimately leading to membrane disruption and cell death [32–35]. However, in the chronic, low-intensity iron load environment characteristic of degenerative bone and joint diseases, cells do not necessarily undergo immediate acute death; instead, they are more likely to enter a ferro-aging state marked by cell cycle arrest, metabolic reprogramming, and enhanced SASP.

This fate divergence can be explained by the “threshold-dependent fate model” [36–38]. ACSL4 preferentially catalyzes the conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs)—such as arachidonic acid and adrenic acid—into their acyl-CoA derivatives. These derivatives are then incorporated into membrane phospholipids in coordination with LPCAT3. This process increases the susceptibility of cellular membranes to oxidative damage [39–42]. On this basis, whether a cell ultimately undergoes ferroptosis or ferro-aging depends on the dynamic balance between pro-oxidative stress and antioxidant buffering capacity. Systems such as GPX4, FSP1, DHODH, and GCH1/BH4 collectively determine the cellular tolerance to lipid peroxidation [43–45]. When oxidative stress exceeds the membrane damage threshold, cells undergo ferroptosis. By contrast, when lipid peroxidation persists at sub-lethal levels, it acts as a sustained stress signal. This activates ATM/ATR-mediated DNA damage responses and senescence pathways—such as p53/p21 and p16—thereby driving cells into a stable ferro-aging state [46, 47].

Thus, ferroptosis and ferro-aging are not mutually exclusive cell fates but rather sequential outcomes of the same lipid peroxidation program operating under different intensities and timescales of damage. Acute ferroptosis helps eliminate severely damaged cells, whereas ferro-aging represents a chronic pathological state in which cells survive long-term but become functionally aberrant [38]. ACSL4-associated metabolic reprogramming network in ferro-aging

Upon entering the ferro-aging state, intracellular changes are no longer limited to passive accumulation of oxidative damage but manifest as metabolic reprogramming involving iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, mitochondrial function, and autophagy. This state allows cells to maintain basic survival under stress conditions while gradually losing their normal functions.

First, aberrant iron metabolism provides a continuous oxidative stress that sustains lipid peroxidation. In ferro-aging cells, upregulation of transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) and decreased FPN function lead to expansion of the labile iron pool, thereby enhancing Fenton reactions and ROS generation [51, 52]. Concomitantly, ACSL4 activation not only increases the proportion of oxidation-sensitive PUFAs in membrane phospholipids but also shifts arachidonic acid metabolism toward the production of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators such as PGE2, LTB4, and 12-HETE, thereby linking lipid peroxidation to inflammatory responses [53, 54].

Second, multiple feedback mechanisms contribute to the maintenance of the ferro-aging state. Chronic oxidative stress induces mitochondrial dysfunction, further amplifying intracellular oxidative pressure [55]. Reduced Parkin expression leads to impaired ubiquitination and degradation of ACSL4, resulting in its abnormal accumulation [56, 57]. ROS can also activate PKCβII, promoting phosphorylation of ACSL4 at Thr328 and enhancing its enzymatic activity, thus forming a positive feedback loop of “ROS–PKCβII–ACSL4–lipid peroxidation–ROS”. Furthermore, aberrantly enhanced NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy releases free iron, further expanding the labile iron pool and intensifying lipid peroxidation [58, 59].

The subcellular localization of ACSL4 may also influence its functional output. ACSL4 can localize to the endoplasmic reticulum, mitochondria-associated membranes, and the surface of lipid droplets [60]. Among these, ACSL4 at mitochondria-associated membranes may participate in lipid exchange between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum and exacerbate oxidative stress; ACSL4 on lipid droplets is involved in the regulation of lipid storage and the generation of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators [61]. These findings suggest that ACSL4 is not merely a lipid metabolic enzyme but may also coordinate iron metabolism, lipid metabolism, and mitochondrial function through distinct subcellular localizations.

In summary, ferro-aging is a complex pathological state centered on ACSL4-associated lipid metabolic abnormalities, accompanied by iron homeostasis imbalance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and autophagic dysregulation [36, 62]. Together, these alterations may sustain the damage-associated survival state of cells and potentially lay the foundation for subsequent aberrant intercellular communication and impaired tissue function (Fig. 2).

Pathological amplification of ferro-aging: from metabolic reprogramming to SASP-mediated tissue damage

The pathological significance of ferro-aging lies not only in the functional decline of the affected cells themselves but also in their ability to influence surrounding tissues through an altered secretory phenotype. Upon entering the ferro-aging state, cells release SASP factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and various matrix metalloproteinases [51, 63]. These secreted factors can alter the proliferation, differentiation, and metabolic status of neighboring cells, thereby extending local damage from individual cells to the tissue level [16].

ACSL4-associated lipid metabolic abnormalities also confer a pro-inflammatory lipid signature on ferro-aging cells. For example, bioactive lipid mediators such as PGE2 and LTB4 can promote SASP factor expression through pathways such as NF-κB and STAT3, forming an “inflammatory lipid–NF-κB–SASP” signaling axis [64, 65]. This not only allows lipid peroxidation to act as an intracellular damage signal but also converts it into an intercellular inflammatory communication signal.

SASP and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) jointly participate in the propagation of tissue damage. On the one hand, paracrine factors such as IL-1α and TGF-β can activate DNA damage responses and the p53/p21 pathway in neighboring cells, inducing secondary senescence [66]. On the other hand, DAMPs such as mtDNA and HMGB1 released from damaged mitochondria activate TLRs and the cGAS-STING innate immune pathway, promoting inflammatory responses and immune cell activation [67, 68]. During this process, increased expression of matrix-degrading molecules such as ADAMTS and MMPs further disrupts extracellular matrix homeostasis [69, 70].

Thus, ACSL4-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming not only shapes the intracellular state of ferro-aging cells but also influences surrounding cells and tissue structures through SASP, pro-inflammatory lipid mediators, and DAMPs. This characteristic distinguishes ferro-aging from simple cell death and aligns it more closely with a chronic pathological process capable of persistently affecting tissue homeostasis [16].

Cross-organ implications of the ACSL4-lipid peroxidation axis

It is important to note that ACSL4 has recently been defined as a core executor of ferro-aging [16]—not merely as a regulator of ferroptosis or lipid metabolism, but as a molecular hub that integrates iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and cellular senescence into a unified program. Although the observation of ACSL4-driven lipid peroxidation across different tissues does not constitute direct evidence of ferro-aging, these findings do raise a testable hypothesis that ferro-aging may operate in the pathogenesis of aging-related diseases driven by iron metabolic dysregulation.

In the cardiovascular system, decreased Parkin expression in aged cardiomyocytes leads to abnormal ACSL4 accumulation, promoting lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial dysfunction, whereas exercise intervention improves the aging phenotype by restoring the Parkin-ACSL4 axis [56, 71]. In Alzheimer’s disease, iron deposition and ACSL4 upregulation enhance the susceptibility of neuronal membrane lipids to oxidation and are associated with synaptic dysfunction [72–74]. Under chronic iron load conditions, neurons are more likely to exhibit functional decline with mitochondrial damage and energy deficits rather than immediate ferroptosis. In the tumor field, ACSL4 has been extensively studied for its role in determining ferroptosis sensitivity. Although these findings are primarily interpreted in the context of ferroptosis regulation, they collectively underscore ACSL4 as a central metabolic node that integrates lipid metabolism with cell fate decisions under oxidative stress—a property that may extend beyond ferroptosis to other iron-dependent processes such as ferro-aging [75–77]. Although these studies were not designed to distinguish ferro-aging from ferroptosis, they collectively demonstrate that ACSL4-driven lipid peroxidation is a recurring feature in age-related tissue dysfunction across multiple organ systems (Fig. 3).

In addition, independent lines of evidence have linked iron metabolic dysregulation and ACSL4-associated senescence to skeletal tissues [78, 79]. In OP, OA, and IVDD, ACSL4 upregulation has been reported in osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs), respectively.

These findings suggest that the ACSL4-mediated ferro-aging network may constitute a shared molecular basis for multiple aging-related diseases. This network does not necessarily lead to overt ferroptosis, but may instead drive senescence—a hallmark feature common to DBJDs and the aforementioned diseases—with their specific manifestations determined by differences in tissue environment and cell type.

Collectively, given that ACSL4 is a core executor of ferro-aging, and that ACSL4-driven lipid peroxidation is present in both DBJDs and other aging-related diseases, it is reasonable to hypothesize that ferro-aging may represent a shared pathological mechanism. Tissue-specific manifestations likely arise from differences in cell type, microenvironment, and the intensity and duration of lipid peroxidation. Direct testing of this hypothesis in skeletal tissues remains an important direction for future investigation.

Ferro-aging: a potential common pathological basis for DBJDs

OP, OA, and IVDD affect bone, joint, and intervertebral disc tissues, respectively, and each presents with distinct clinical manifestations and histological characteristics. Nonetheless, accumulating evidence indicates that these three age-related degenerative diseases share common pathological alterations, including local iron accumulation, enhanced chronic lipid peroxidation, and accumulation of senescent cells [34, 51].

Specifically, iron deposition, lipid peroxidation, and inflammatory factor release have been observed in osteoblasts and osteocytes in OP [79]; in OA, chondrocytes exhibit iron homeostasis imbalance and lipid metabolic abnormalities that promote matrix-degrading molecule expression [80, 81]; and in IVDD, nucleus pulposus (NP) and cartilage endplate (CEP) display persistent iron metabolic abnormalities that progressively disrupt intervertebral disc homeostasis [82]. Despite these tissue-specific manifestations, persistent iron homeostasis imbalance, oxidative damage, and cellular senescence represent common pathological features across all three diseases.

OP, OA, and IVDD share ferro-aging-related molecular alterations, yet their clinical manifestations diverge. This can be attributed in part to the tissue-context-dependent paracrine effects of senescent cell-derived SASP. Specifically, senescent mesenchymal stromal cells have been shown to exert distinct paracrine effects depending on the cellular context [83]. Thus, while the core ferro-aging program may be conserved across tissues, the downstream SASP composition and its impact on tissue homeostasis are shaped by the unique cellular and extracellular context of each tissue. This provides a mechanistic explanation for why the same ferro-aging framework yields bone loss in OP, cartilage degradation in OA, and structural disruption in IVDD.

These cross-tissue commonalities suggest that different degenerative bone and joint diseases may share similar pathological drivers. Factors such as aging, estrogen deficiency, and abnormal mechanical loading can perturb cellular iron homeostasis, promote expansion of the labile iron pool, and provide sustained driving forces for lipid peroxidation [34, 35, 84]. Meanwhile, multiple signaling pathways associated with degenerative diseases, including p53, Nrf2, AMPK, and Hippo-YAP, can participate in lipid metabolic remodeling by regulating ACSL4 expression or activity. Under persistent oxidative stress, cells gradually develop metabolic abnormalities and functional decline, ultimately affecting tissue homeostasis [53, 78, 85].

Therefore, ferro-aging may represent a common pathological program that links iron homeostasis imbalance, cellular dysfunction, and tissue structural degeneration across DBJDs, providing a unified theoretical framework for understanding these diseases (Fig. 4).

DBJDs: From Iron Overload to Bone Aging

Ferro-aging drives bone remodeling imbalance in OP

OP is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, with its core pathology lying in the imbalance between bone formation and bone resorption [86, 87]. Accumulating evidence indicates that iron metabolism disorder is an important risk factor for OP, particularly in postmenopausal women and the elderly, where a significant correlation exists between iron accumulation and bone loss [88, 89]. Epidemiological studies have found that elevated serum ferritin levels are closely associated with decreased bone mineral density and increased fracture risk, an association that is especially pronounced in women over 45 years of age [90, 91].

With aging, senescent cells gradually accumulate in bone tissue and affect local bone metabolism through the SASP, manifesting as reduced bone formation, enhanced bone resorption, and decreased osteogenic capacity of BMSCs [92]. The ferro-aging framework described above suggests that ACSL4-mediated chronic lipid peroxidation may contribute to senescent cell dysfunction and bone remodeling imbalance [16].

OBs are the primary effector cells of bone formation and are among the most sensitive target cells to iron load [93]. After entering cells via transferrin receptor (TfR) and divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), iron ions cause expansion of the labile iron pool (LIP), which in turn generates large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induces lipid peroxidation [94, 95]. In ovariectomized (OVX) mouse bone tissue, studies have observed marked iron deposition and enhanced lipid peroxidation, accompanied by decreased GPX4 expression and impaired antioxidant defense systems [96]. ACSL4-mediated remodeling of polyunsaturated fatty acids increases the susceptibility of cell membranes to oxidative damage, further exacerbating chronic lipid peroxidation burden [97]. This long-term low-grade oxidative stress can activate classical senescence signaling pathways such as p53/p21 and p16 [79], driving OBs to exit the cell cycle, along with decreased alkaline phosphatase activity and weakened osteogenic capacity [98, 99]. Senescent OBs generally also exhibit reduced proliferative capacity, mineralization ability, and expression of osteogenesis-related proteins, thereby affecting bone microstructural integrity and bone homeostasis maintenance [100]. At the same time, iron accumulation can inhibit the canonical Wnt signaling pathway, hindering OB differentiation [94]. In addition to the possibility that iron-load-induced oxidative stress and lipid metabolic abnormalities may weaken mature OBs, alterations in BMSC fate also represent a critical link in the development of OP. Iron load significantly affects the metabolic status of BMSCs. It induces an ‘osteogenic-adipogenic lineage shift’ in these cells. Mechanistically, Steap4—a NADPH-dependent metalloreductase—promotes the conversion of Fe3+ to Fe2+. This increases intracellular Fe2+ accumulation and ROS generation, and upregulates CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein β (C/EBPβ) expression [101]. In turn, C/EBPβ binds to the Steap4 promoter, forming a Steap4/Fe2+-ROS/C/EBPβ positive feedback loop that accelerates lipid damage and cellular senescence, ultimately promoting BMSC differentiation toward adipocytes while inhibiting their osteogenic capacity. Furthermore, mitochondrial ferritin (FTMT) expression is elevated under iron load conditions, and FTMT inhibits the initiation of mitophagy by binding to PINK1, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and cellular senescence, thereby compromising the osteogenic potential of BMSCs [102].

Age-related BMSC senescence is also accompanied by increased expression of p16 and p21 as well as elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [103, 104]. With advancing age, the expression of FOXP1 and NRF2 decreases, and the regenerative capacity and antioxidant defense ability of BMSCs are correspondingly diminished. Downregulation of FOXP1 promotes bone loss, whereas restoring its expression helps maintain osteogenic potential [105]. These findings suggest that long-term oxidative stress induced by iron homeostasis imbalance not only impairs cellular homeostasis but may also promote bone marrow adiposity and insufficient bone formation by altering stem cell lineage fate.

Beyond intrinsic aging and local iron overload, systemic metabolic conditions also play a critical role in driving BMSC senescence and, consequently, ferro-aging in the bone microenvironment. Obesity, a well-established risk factor for both OP and OA, has been shown to induce senescence in bone marrow MSCs even in young animals. Mechanistically, metabolic stressors such as excess free fatty acids and chronic low-grade inflammation associated with obesity promote oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage in BMSCs, thereby accelerating their entry into senescence independently of chronological age [106]. This finding supports the notion that systemic metabolic conditions can prime the bone microenvironment for ferro-aging, even in the absence of advanced chronological aging. Therefore, metabolic stressors such as obesity may act as an upstream accelerator of ferro-aging in bone, complementing the effects of age-related iron accumulation and estrogen deficiency.

Ferro-aging-related alterations can also affect bone metabolism through paracrine mechanisms. OBs and BMSCs that have entered a senescent state can release IL-6, TNF-α, RANKL, and various chemokines and matrix-modulating molecules, thereby disrupting normal bone remodeling balance [51, 107]. Previous studies have shown that conditioned medium from senescent cells increases the survival rate of OC precursor cells and promotes OCs formation [108, 109], while the inflammatory SASP further enhances OCs differentiation and bone resorption. In an iron-overload OVX model, urinary deoxypyridinoline excretion is increased, accompanied by elevated levels of osteopontin and TGF-β1, indicating enhanced bone resorption activity [110]. Therefore, bone loss not only results from insufficient bone formation but is also closely associated with a pro-resorptive state promoted by paracrine signals from senescent cells. Given that ferro-aging is defined as a senescence state, it is conceptually expected that ferro-aging cells in bone tissue would similarly engage SASP-mediated paracrine signaling; direct experimental confirmation in OP-specific ferro-aging models, however, remains an important future direction.

Osteocytes, as the most abundant terminally differentiated cells in bone tissue, also exhibit increased p16 expression with aging. Senescent osteocytes release multiple SASP components and display diminished mechanosensing ability and intercellular communication, thereby compromising bone homeostasis maintenance. On the other hand, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) generated during iron load and chronic lipid peroxidation can activate local innate immune responses, promote the expression of inflammatory factors, and create a chronic inflammatory environment favorable for OC differentiation [70]. Collectively, these changes indicate that OP is not caused by dysfunction of a single cell type but involves multi-level interactions among OBs, BMSCs, osteocytes, and OCs precursors [111].

In summary, iron metabolism disorder may drive the shift of bone remodeling balance toward a pro-resorptive state by inducing functional decline of OBs, promoting the osteogenic-adipogenic imbalance in BMSCs, enhancing inflammatory paracrine signals, and increasing bone resorption activity. Accordingly, ACSL4-associated chronic lipid peroxidation and cellular senescence processes may provide important mechanistic clues for understanding the development and progression of OP under iron load conditions.

Involvement of ferro-aging in whole-joint homeostasis disruption in OA

OA is a whole-joint disease involving cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, and periarticular soft tissues, with its core pathology being the chronic disruption of joint tissue homeostasis [112]. A large number of clinical and basic studies have shown that iron metabolism disorder is an important risk factor promoting the development and progression of OA [113]. Studies have found that elevated serum iron levels and transferrin saturation are positively correlated with OA risk, and patients with hereditary hemochromatosis often present with early-onset bilateral hip OA [114]. Animal experiments have further confirmed that systemic iron overload increases iron deposition in knee joint cartilage and the infrapatellar fat pad, exacerbating cartilage degeneration and arthritis progression; conversely, iron restriction or administration of iron chelators significantly delays cartilage damage [115–117]. These lines of evidence suggest that chronic iron loading may create a pathological environment conducive to chronic lipid peroxidation in joint tissues, progressively affecting the functions of chondrocytes and synovial cells.

In OA cartilage, iron homeostasis imbalance is mainly characterized by TfR1 upregulation and decreased ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) expression. These changes lead to increased intracellular iron accumulation [118]. Consequently, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are induced. The resulting mitochondrial DNA release activates the cGAS/STING inflammatory pathway, which accelerates chondrocyte degeneration [119]. At the same time, reduced FTH1 expression weakens the cellular buffering capacity for iron ions, making chondrocytes more susceptible to oxidative damage. Under iron loading conditions, ROS generated by the Fenton reaction attack polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) in membrane phospholipids, inducing lipid peroxidation [120, 121]. As a key enzyme in long-chain fatty acid metabolism, ACSL4 preferentially activates arachidonic acid (AA) and adrenic acid (AdA), promoting the formation of oxidation-prone phospholipids, and is a core executor determining cellular susceptibility to lipid peroxidation [122–125].

Chondrocyte senescence is a critical event in OA progression. Age-related chondrocytes commonly exhibit classical senescence features, including increased expression of p16 and p21, accumulation of DNA damage, and permanent cell cycle arrest [126]. Contrary to the traditional view that senescent chondrocytes merely lose activity, these cells undergo aberrant reprogramming from a homeostatic phenotype toward a hypertrophic-like phenotype [127], accompanied by increased matrix degradation, abnormal calcification, and angiogenesis [128, 129]. Mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation activate NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, further promoting the expression of RUNX2 and MMP13 [130, 131]. Moreover, reduced autophagic flux leads to accumulation of damaged mitochondria and misfolded proteins, exacerbating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [132, 133]. These changes suggest that iron-load-related oxidative damage may participate in the transition of chondrocytes from a senescent state to a hypertrophic-like phenotype.

Although some damaged chondrocytes exhibit a clear tendency toward lipid peroxidation, they can upregulate excitatory amino acid transporter 1 (EAAT1) to increase intracellular glutamate levels, thereby compensatorily enhancing glutathione system activity and avoiding spontaneous membrane rupture [134]. This compensatory mechanism allows damaged chondrocytes to remain viable [135, 136]. In addition to IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, senescent chondrocytes also express hypertrophy-related factors such as MMP13, RUNX2, and COL10A1 [137, 138], thus promoting cartilage matrix degradation and maintenance of the degenerative state. Furthermore, the chronic kidney disease-associated uremic toxin indoxyl sulfate induces chondrocyte lipid peroxidation and functional decline through the calpain-1/IL-1α signaling axis by promoting calcium influx and iron accumulation [139], further illustrating that iron metabolism disorders and oxidative stress participate in the propagation of joint degeneration. Although these observations are derived from classical senescence models, they are conceptually relevant to ferro-aging—given that ferro-aging is inherently a senescence state—and support the hypothesis that ferro-aging chondrocytes may similarly drive matrix degradation through SASP-related mechanisms.

Multiple signaling pathways are involved in regulating chondrocyte lipid peroxidation and ferro-aging-related alterations. Nuclear protein 1 (Nupr1) expression is significantly elevated in OA cartilage, and Nupr1 knockdown alleviates lipid peroxidation and delays OA progression by suppressing ACSL4 and p53 expression and restoring GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels [140]. FBXW7 exacerbates lipid damage and promotes OA lesion development by promoting SLC7A11 degradation [141]. Mitochondria-localized NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) induces mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress by impairing the GRP78/GPX4 protective axis, and inhibition of NOX4 partially reverses this pathological process [142]. Collectively, these studies suggest that the ACSL4-centered lipid peroxidation network may be a potential molecular basis for chondrocyte functional degeneration.

In addition to chondrocytes, synovial tissue also participates in OA progression. Bioinformatics analyses have identified a series of key genes related to iron homeostasis, cellular stress, and inflammatory responses, including ATF3, IL6, CDKN1A, IL1B, and JUN, which are significantly enriched in OA synovitis and closely associated with immune cell infiltration [69, 143]. During this process, JUN, ATF3, and CDKN1A are co-overexpressed in both cartilage and synovial tissues, suggesting that they may share similar stress and senescence programs. Iron deposition in the joint cavity also induces a senescence-like phenotypic transition in synovial macrophages. These damaged macrophages release large amounts of SASP factors and, through paracrine actions, induce secondary lipid peroxidation damage in periarticular tissues, thereby promoting OA-related muscle atrophy and extra-articular lesions [144]. At the same time, synovial fibroblasts under iron overload conditions secrete various inflammatory mediators, exacerbating cartilage matrix degradation and synovial hyperplasia [112]. Therefore, ferro-aging-related alterations in OA are not limited to chondrocytes but involve pathological interactions among cartilage, synovium, and periarticular tissues.

Age-related chondrocytes are also accompanied by epigenetic changes, including abnormal DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA dysregulation [145, 146].These changes stabilize the expression of RUNX2, COL10A1, and MMP13, and reduce the ability of chondrocytes to restore homeostasis, thereby promoting long-term maintenance of the degenerative state.

In summary, OA may not be a simple cartilage wear-and-tear disease but rather a whole-joint degenerative disease accompanied by abnormal iron homeostasis, chronic lipid peroxidation, aberrant chondrocyte fate, and synovial inflammation. During this process, interactions among chondrocytes, synovial macrophages, and synovial fibroblasts jointly drive the dysregulation of joint tissue homeostasis, providing a new perspective for understanding the cellular and molecular basis of persistent OA progression.

Ferro-aging drives NP-CEP microenvironment imbalance in IVDD

IVDD is the primary pathological basis of chronic low back pain, and its development involves multiple processes including extracellular matrix degradation, cellular senescence, and tissue homeostasis disruption [82, 147, 148]. Abnormal iron metabolism is considered an important risk factor promoting IVDD progression. Clinical sample analyses have revealed that iron content in degenerated intervertebral disc tissue is significantly higher than that in normal tissue and is positively correlated with the Pfirrmann imaging grade, suggesting that the degree of iron accumulation is closely associated with the severity of degeneration [149]. Under conditions of long-standing inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress, upregulation of transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1) leads to gradual accumulation of free iron in NPCs and cartilage endplate cells (CEPCs). The lipid peroxidation thus induced does not necessarily immediately cause cell death but may instead drive cells into a ferro-aging state characterized by functional decline, metabolic reprogramming, and a high secretory phenotype. Meanwhile, mitochondrial damage and release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) activate the cGAS-STING inflammatory pathway, further enhancing local degenerative signals [67]. ACSL4 is a key executor that determines cellular susceptibility to lipid peroxidation. It promotes the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids—such as arachidonic acid and adrenic acid—into membrane phospholipid remodeling. This increases the accumulation of oxidized lipids [150, 151]. Therefore, in IVDD tissues, ACSL4-associated chronic lipid peroxidation may represent an important link connecting abnormal iron homeostasis to NPCs senescence.

The intervertebral disc is characterized by low oxygen, low nutrition, and low cell turnover, which makes local cells more likely to maintain a damage-associated survival state under long-term iron load rather than undergoing rapid and complete cell death. Studies have shown that oxidative stress impairs the iron export capacity of NPCs by inhibiting the expression of the iron exporter ferroportin (FPN), leading to intracellular iron accumulation; conversely, nuclear translocation of metal regulatory transcription factor 1 (MTF1) restores FPN expression, thereby alleviating iron load and cellular senescence [152]. Furthermore, GLS1/NFS1-mediated glutamine metabolic reprogramming and the HACE1-Nrf2/ARE antioxidant pathway help maintain iron homeostasis and alleviate oxidative stress, thereby delaying functional degeneration of NPCs and extracellular matrix degradation [153, 154]. These studies suggest that impaired iron export, insufficient antioxidant defense, and metabolic adaptation imbalance jointly participate in the functional decline of NPCs.

Recent single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed that as IVDD progresses, NPCs gradually transition from a homeostatic phenotype toward fibrotic and hypertrophic phenotypes, accompanied by enhanced glycolysis. Abnormal lactate accumulation in degenerated tissues not only reflects a change in metabolic pattern but also promotes ACSL4 transcription through histone H3K18 lactylation and directly mediates lactylation of ACSL4 at lysine residues, thereby enhancing its stability and lipid peroxidation sensitivity [155]. Moreover, decreased expression of the deacetylase SIRT3 impairs the delactylation capacity of ACSL4, allowing chronic lipid peroxidation to be sustained [156]. Thus, the “glycolytic reprogramming - lactate accumulation - ACSL4 activation” axis may constitute an important metabolic basis for the long-term persistence of ferro-aging in NPCs.

In addition to lipid metabolic reprogramming, autophagy system abnormalities also represent an important mechanism maintaining the ferro-aging state in NPCs. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses have shown that degenerated NPC subsets are enriched with numerous genes related to iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation. Among these, CRISPLD2 deficiency exacerbates inflammation through the miR-548I/IL17A axis, accelerating cellular functional exhaustion [157]. The ROS-p53-p21 signaling axis has also been confirmed as an important pathway driving NPCs into a senescent state, and ROS clearance significantly reverses cell cycle arrest and matrix metabolic abnormalities [158]. Recent studies have found that reduced chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) function may be a key factor sustaining ferro-aging in NPCs. In degenerated NP tissues, the expression of the CMA core receptor LAMP2A is decreased, accompanied by elevated levels of senescence markers such as p53, p21, and p16 [159]. Studies have confirmed that ACSL4 is an important substrate of the CMA system in NPCs. HSPA8 recognizes acetylated ACSL4 and mediates its entry into lysosomes for degradation via LAMP2A [160, 161]. KAT2B-mediated acetylation at multiple lysine sites enhances the binding between ACSL4 and HSPA8, thereby promoting ACSL4 clearance and alleviating lipid peroxidation damage [162]. Therefore, abnormal ACSL4 accumulation resulting from impaired CMA function may be an important molecular basis maintaining metabolic abnormalities and senescence in NPCs. In addition, expression of the autophagy-related genes TP53 and SESN2 is aberrant in degenerated NPCs [163], suggesting complex regulatory interactions among iron homeostasis disruption, autophagy dysfunction, and abnormal ACSL4 accumulation.

Not only NP but also the CEP, which serves as the gateway for nutritional exchange in the intervertebral disc, plays an important role in the development and progression of IVDD when its function becomes abnormal. Studies have shown that under inflammatory and oxidative stress stimulation, HIF-2α enhances iron influx by transcriptionally activating TfR1, and the ensuing increase in iron load further induces mitochondrial damage and mtDNA release, activating the cGAS-STING pathway [67]. Meanwhile, the YAP/TEAD1 signaling axis promotes NCOA4 expression, enhances ferritinophagy, and releases free iron, thereby aggravating lipid peroxidation damage [58]. Furthermore, the iron-binding protein lactoferrin (LTF) is markedly downregulated in degenerated endplate tissues, and its loss promotes endplate cell calcification, cellular senescence, and extracellular matrix degradation, with the JAK2/STAT3 pathway involved in this regulatory process [164]. These changes impair endplate barrier function and nutritional supply capacity, and gradually lead to a state of high secretory activity and functional decline in CEPCs.

Of note, NPCs and CEPCs do not degenerate in isolation. CEPCs calcification and barrier dysfunction restrict nutrient diffusion, while inflammatory mediators and matrix-degrading factors released by NPCs further exacerbate endplate inflammation and iron homeostasis abnormalities. Senescent cells release IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and various matrix-degrading enzymes, forming a typical SASP. These inflammatory mediators not only promote extracellular matrix degradation and local inflammatory responses but also induce secondary lipid peroxidation damage in neighboring cells through paracrine actions. mtDNA release and cGAS-STING pathway activation further enhance innate immune responses, deepening the pathological interactions among NPCs, CEPCs, and local immune cells [165]. In the context of IVDD, where NPCs and CEPCs display multiple features of senescence—including cell cycle arrest and expression of p53/p21/p16—it is plausible that ferro-aging cells in the disc contribute to local pathology via SASP-mediated paracrine mechanisms.

In summary, IVDD can be understood as a process of tissue homeostasis disruption closely associated with abnormal iron homeostasis, ACSL4-associated chronic lipid peroxidation, metabolic reprogramming, autophagy dysfunction, and pathological CEP-NP interactions. Among these, lactylation modification and chaperone-mediated autophagy imbalance affect ACSL4 activity at the levels of metabolic regulation and protein homeostasis, respectively, suggesting that the ACSL4 ferro-aging axis may participate in the persistent progression of IVDD. Further dissection of this process will help deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying IVDD and provide potential strategies for precision intervention in DBJDs (Table 2) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Ferro-aging as a new pathological paradigm linking ferroptosis and cellular senescence

Traditionally, ferroptosis and cellular senescence have been viewed as two interrelated yet distinct cell fate endpoints. The former is characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and irreversible membrane damage, ultimately leading to cell death [19]. The latter is centered on permanent cell cycle arrest, metabolic reprogramming, and SASP formation [169]. However, recent studies have revealed significant overlap between the two processes in terms of reactive oxygen species accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage responses, and inflammatory signaling activation [16, 100, 170], suggesting that they are not isolated events but may lie at different stages of the same pathological spectrum.

In this context, the concept of ferro-aging provides a new theoretical interface for understanding the intrinsic connection between ferroptosis and cellular senescence. Unlike the rapid membrane rupture and cell clearance that occur in acute ferroptosis, chronic, sub-lethal levels of lipid peroxidation tend to drive cells into a stable state characterized by functional decline, metabolic reprogramming, and a high secretory phenotype. Although such cells retain basic metabolic activity and membrane integrity, their physiological functions are profoundly altered, and they can continuously affect the surrounding tissue microenvironment through the SASP. Based on these observations, we can preliminarily summarize three defining features of ferro-aging. First, it is not an acute cell death event but rather a chronic state of functional decline in which cells survive with sustained damage. Second, it is driven by ACSL4-mediated persistent lipid peroxidation as its molecular engine, rather than sporadic oxidative injury. Third, it converts intrinsic cellular damage into intercellular pathological signals through SASP formation, thereby generating tissue-level destructive effects. These three features collectively establish ferro-aging as a distinct biological state independent of both ferroptosis and classical senescence, rather than a variant of either.

ACSL4-mediated membrane lipid remodeling plays a key role in this process [33, 53, 78]. As a key metabolic regulator determining the oxidative susceptibility of cell membranes, ACSL4 maintains lipid peroxidation by promoting the incorporation of arachidonic acid and adrenic acid into membrane phospholipids. When the intensity of oxidative stress exceeds the cellular tolerance threshold, cells enter a classical ferroptosis program; under long-term low-grade oxidative stress, they are more likely to transition into a ferro-aging state. Therefore, ferro-aging can be regarded as an important pathological link between ferroptosis and cellular senescence, with its essence being a state of functional homeostasis imbalance potentially driven by chronic lipid peroxidation.

Based on this understanding, the widespread cellular dysfunction and tissue homeostasis disruption observed in degenerative diseases may not primarily originate from cell death itself, but rather be closely associated with the accumulation of cells in a “damage-associated survival state.” This perspective breaks through the traditional “cell death-centric” explanatory framework, offers a new view of the progressive pathological evolution of chronic degenerative diseases, and suggests that future intervention strategies should pay more attention to reversing or eliminating this persistently dysfunctional state.

Ferro-aging may drive the progression of DBJDs

As discussed above, although OP, OA, and IVDD exhibit marked differences in tissue structure, predominant cell types, and clinical manifestations, accumulating evidence suggests that these three diseases may share similar pathological bases during disease progression.

In OP, iron homeostasis imbalance leads to functional decline of OBs and BMSCs, accompanied by an osteogenic-adipogenic differentiation imbalance and enhanced inflammatory responses in bone tissue. In OA, chondrocytes and synovial cells are chronically exposed to a lipid peroxidation environment, gradually forming a chronic inflammatory state characterized by SASP release and tissue homeostasis disruption [128]. In IVDD, NPCs and CEPCs interact through oxidative stress, autophagy abnormalities, and innate immune signaling, jointly promoting continuous degeneration [171].

Although these diseases occur in different tissues, existing evidence indicates that they all share common features including abnormal iron homeostasis, chronic lipid peroxidation, and cellular ferro-aging. Ferro-aging may not only participate in disease initiation but also drive disease progression. In contrast to traditional views that emphasize single cell death events, the ferro-aging theory focuses more on the cumulative effects of long-term damage-associated cell survival and tissue homeostasis imbalance, thereby providing a unified explanatory framework for the intrinsic connections among different degenerative bone and joint diseases.

Limitations and future directions

Although existing studies have preliminarily suggested that ferro-aging may play a driving role in aging-related diseases such as DBJDs, as an emerging field and concept, further refinement of the ferro-aging framework still faces considerable limitations.

First, while ferro-aging has been formally defined and mechanistically characterized in a recent landmark study [16], its core molecular criteria are beginning to be explored in the specific musculoskeletal disease contexts covered by this review, and broader validation across these settings represents a priority for future research. Furthermore, the discovery of ferro-aging has largely been based on non-human primate models and in vitro cell systems; therefore, whether the core molecular criteria of this state—including the specific threshold of ACSL4-driven lipid peroxidation, the characteristic gene expression profile, and its reversibility in vivo—are fully applicable to human skeletal tissues remains to be independently validated. In a related vein, much of the evidence linking ferro-aging to OP, OA, and IVDD is currently indirect. Although iron overload, ferroptosis, oxidative stress, and classical senescence have each been extensively documented in these diseases by independent studies, direct experimental evidence demonstrating that the pathological changes observed in DBJDs are specifically attributable to ferro-aging—rather than to ferroptosis or classical senescence—remains limited. Nevertheless, the conceptual framework itself—by integrating existing findings—has already demonstrated heuristic value in offering a new perspective on the common mechanisms of DBJDs.

Secondly, the ferro-aging network may exhibit significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity across different tissues and cell types. OBs, chondrocytes, synovial cells, and NPCs differ in iron homeostasis regulation, lipid metabolism, and antioxidant capacity, and the activation mode and extent of ferro-aging may not be identical across different diseases. In-depth analysis of its tissue specificity and dynamic regulatory patterns will help identify critical nodes in the progression of degenerative bone and joint diseases.

A further issue that warrants attention is that if ferroptosis and ferro-aging represent a continuous pathological spectrum, can chronic lipid peroxidation drive dynamic switching between ferro-aging and ferroptosis in cells? Moreover, while senescence is a core component of ferro-aging, specific biomarkers that can clearly distinguish ferro-aging from ferroptosis or oxidative stress-associated senescence in situ are currently lacking. The distinct threshold conditions governing such switching remain to be elucidated.

With the continuous development of technologies such as single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, lipidomics, and metabolomics [172], it is expected that the dynamic characteristics of the ACSL4 ferro-aging network will be resolved with greater spatiotemporal precision, and a systemic regulatory map of the development and progression of degenerative bone and joint diseases will be constructed. This may also enable the identification of key ferro-aging hubs beyond ACSL4. It is foreseeable that this gradual shift from single-pathway studies to multi-level, multi-omics integrative analyses will propel research on the mechanisms of ferro-aging-driven degenerative bone and joint diseases toward a systems biology direction.

Potential intervention strategies guided by ferro-aging

Traditional treatments for degenerative bone and joint diseases have largely focused on symptom relief and delaying structural destruction, but their intervention in refractory cellular functional imbalance and microenvironmental abnormalities is limited. With the proposal of the ferro-aging theory, it is foreseeable that intervention strategies aimed at restoring iron homeostasis and rebuilding the tissue microenvironment will gradually be incorporated into the research framework.

Current studies suggest that multi-level regulation targeting abnormal iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and the senescent microenvironment may be of great significance. On the one hand, oxidative stress can be alleviated by modulating the iron metabolic network, inhibiting ferritinophagy, and reducing the labile iron pool level [115, 117, 173]. On the other hand, restoring the function of the GPX4 and System Xc⁻ antioxidant systems to enhance the cellular capacity to clear lipid peroxidation also helps block the ferro-aging process [174, 175].

Of note, ACSL4, as the most critical node currently linking iron homeostasis imbalance and chronic lipid peroxidation in ferro-aging, may become an important target for future intervention in degenerative bone and joint diseases. Inhibiting ACSL4 activity not only reduces the susceptibility of cell membranes to oxidative damage but may also simultaneously affect SASP release and inflammatory amplification, thereby achieving multi-level regulation of disease progression. Potential modulators relevant to ferro-aging, ferroptosis, and senescence

Furthermore, the emerging senolytics and senomorphics targeting strategies [176, 177] offer new possibilities for restoring tissue homeostasis. Markedly different from previous studies that focused primarily on the ferroptotic outcome of iron metabolic dysregulation, these findings shift the emphasis toward the aging process itself during iron metabolism disturbance. Inhibiting ferroptosis alone cannot fully address the pathological consequences; rather, rescuing the senescent state induced by iron dysregulation represents a more robust strategy for maintaining tissue homeostasis. Therefore, in the future, combining multiple strategies such as iron homeostasis regulation, antioxidant defense, ACSL4 inhibition, and senescent microenvironment remodeling may break through the limitations of traditional single-target therapies and provide new directions for precision treatment of degenerative bone and joint diseases.

In summary, simply inhibiting ferroptosis is insufficient. A systematic regulation centered on the ACSL4-based ferro-aging axis—targeting chronic lipid peroxidation and microenvironmental imbalance—may better align with the slowly progressive nature of degenerative bone and joint diseases. This approach is expected to become an important theoretical foundation for future prevention and treatment strategies.

Conclusion and perspectives

Emerging evidence suggests that chronic iron-dependent lipid peroxidation may not necessarily culminate in ferroptotic cell death, but instead drive cells toward a persistent dysfunctional state characterized by impaired homeostasis and altered intercellular communication. The concept of ferro-aging provides a novel perspective that links ferroptosis with cellular senescence and expands our understanding of how chronic oxidative stress contributes to tissue degeneration.

In this review, we propose that ACSL4-mediated lipid remodeling and sustained lipid peroxidation represent a central mechanism underlying ferro-aging. Rather than being restricted to a specific cell type or disease entity, the ACSL4 ferro-aging axis may constitute a common pathological basis shared by degenerative skeletal disorders. This framework provides a unified explanation for the progressive nature of these diseases and highlights the importance of chronic microenvironmental disequilibrium during disease evolution.

Although many questions remain to be addressed, advances in single-cell and multi-omics technologies are expected to further elucidate the spatiotemporal characteristics of ferro-aging. A better understanding of the ACSL4 ferro-aging network may ultimately facilitate the development of mechanism-based therapeutic strategies and provide new opportunities for the prevention and treatment of degenerative skeletal diseases.
