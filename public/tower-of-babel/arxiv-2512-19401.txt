Undecidability of theories of semirings with fixed points
Anupam Das 1, Abhishek De 2, Stepan L. Kuznetsov 3

Abstract
. In this work we prove the undecidability (and Σ 1 0 \Sigma^{0}_{1} -completeness) of several theories of semirings with fixed points. The generality of our results stems from recursion theoretic methods, namely the technique of effective inseperability . Our result applies to many theories proposed in the literature, including Conway μ \mu -semirings, Park μ \mu -semirings, and Chomsky algebras.

2.1.
Semirings with fixed points

For generality of our results, we shall work with a minimal theory of semirings with fixed points, before considering specific examples such as (idempotent) Park
\mu
-semirings and Conway
\mu
-semirings later.

Definition 3

(Basic axioms)
.

The equational theory
\mu\mathsf{S}
of
\mu
-expressions is given by all the following axioms:

(i)

(0,1,+,\cdot)
form a semiring:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}e+(f+g)&(e+f)+g\\
e+f&f+e\\
e+0&e\end{array}\quad\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}e(fg)&(ef)g\\
e1&e\\
1e&e\\
e0&0\\
0e&0\end{array}\quad\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}e(f+g)&ef+eg\\
(e+f)g&eg+fg\end{array}

(ii)

\mu xe(x)
is a fixed point of
x\mapsto e(x)
:

\mu xe(x)=e(\mu xe(x))

Note that
\mu\mathsf{S}
does
not
insist that
\mu xe(x)
is a
least
fixed point, which would require non-equational axioms.
Instead, notice that the axiomatisation above is finite and equational.

Remark 4

(On
\mu
-congruence)
.

Note that
\mu\mathsf{S}
does not admit the
\mu
-congruence
rule:

e(x)=f(x)

\mu xe(x)=\mu xf(x)

.
This is often built into the definition of various classes of ‘
\mu
-semirings’ in the literature
[
7
,
8
,
20
]
, but we shall not exploit this here.
This means that, even if two operators are (provably) equivalent, the fixed points named by their closure under
\mu
may be different.

For instance we do not have in
\mu\mathsf{S}
that
\mu x(a+x)=\mu x(x+a)
.
This is consistent with the fact that we are not axiomatising
\mu xe(x)
to be a
least
fixed point, but just
some
fixed point.
So, e.g., we can tweak the model
\mathcal{L}
to satisfy
\mu x(a+x)=a
but
\mu x(x+a)=a+b
.
Of course only the former is the
least
fixed point, but the resulting structure remains a model of
\mu\mathsf{S}
.

Construing expressions in a higher-order syntax, where
\mu
is a higher-order constant rather than a binder, the lack of
\mu
-congruence may be seen as saying that
\mu
does not behave
extensionally
.
In practice, in this work, this means we must be careful to not carry out reasoning
under
a
\mu
unless justified.

Remark 5

(Comparison to
\mu
-semirings)
.

A
\mu
-semiring
, introduced in
[
7
]
, is defined just like
\mu\mathsf{S}
except:

•

\mu
-semirings must satisfy
strong

\mu
-congruence:

\forall x\,e(x)=f(x)\rightarrow\mu xe(x)=\mu xf(x)
; and,

•

\mu
-semirings do not require
\mu xe(x)
to be a fixed point, i.e. axiom (
ii
) is omitted.

Thus our system
\mu\mathsf{S}
is, strictly speaking, incomparable with general
\mu
-semirings, though it will still underlie extensions considered later.

\mu\mathsf{S}
should rather be considered a basic theory of ‘semirings with fixed points’, namely the theory of those
\mu
-semirings in which
\mu xe(x)
always denotes a fixed point of
x\mapsto e(x)
, albeit not necessarily the
least
fixed point, and albeit not extensionally so (see previous
Remark

4
).

We omit
\mu
-congruence for two main reasons: (a) we do not need it to obtain our ultimate undecidability results, rendering them strictly more general; and (b) the axiomatisation
\mu\mathsf{S}
remains purely equational, and so could be of independent interest.

We shall soon present
\mu
-semirings equipped with an order, so as to axiomatise the
leastness
of fixed points denoted by
\mu
, in particular satisfying the ‘strong’ congruence principle mentioned in the example above.
Before that let us recall a class of models of
\mu\mathsf{S}
that is covered by our results:

Definition 6

(
[
7
]
)
.

A
Conway
\mu
-semiring
is a
\mu
-semiring further satisfying:

(1)

\mu xe(f(x))=e(\mu xf(e(x)))

(2)

\mu x\mu ye(x,y)=\mu xe(x,x)

Note that the fixed point axiom (

, and so Conway

Conway

as a minimal theory in which certain normal forms of context-free grammars may be formally obtained, in particular

We shall not develop its metatheory any further here, as our results about Conway

2.2.
Equational systems and context-free grammars

Before considering further extensions of
\mu\mathsf{S}
, let us revisit the interpretation of
\mu
-expressions as context-free languages.
It is known that Conway
\mu
-semirings can ‘solve’ context-free grammars (CFGs), but we shall recast (and improve) this result within
\mu\mathsf{S}
here.

An
equational system
is a set
\mathcal{E}(\vec{X})=\{X_{i}=f_{i}(\vec{X})\}_{i<n}
.
By recursively applying
Bekić’s Lemma
(
[
1
]
) we can solve such systems in
\mu\mathsf{S}
:

Proposition 7
.

Fix an equational system
\mathcal{E}(\vec{X})=\{X_{i}=f_{i}(\vec{X})\}_{i<n}
.
There are (closed) expressions
\vec{e}
s.t.
\mu\mathsf{S}\vdash\mathcal{E}(\vec{e})
.

Proof.

By induction on
n
:

•

When
n=0
the statement is vacuously true.

•

Fix an equational system
\{X_{i}=f_{i}(\vec{X},X_{n})\}_{i\leq n}
, where
\vec{X}=X_{0},\dots,X_{n-1}
.
Write
e_{n}^{\prime}(\vec{X}):=\mu X_{n}f_{n}(\vec{X},X_{n})
.
Now, by inductive hypothesis, let
\vec{e}
be solutions of
\{X_{i}=f_{i}(\vec{X},e_{n}^{\prime}(\vec{X}))\}_{i<n}
, i.e. such that
\mu\mathsf{S}\vdash e_{i}=f_{i}(\vec{e},e_{n}^{\prime}(\vec{e}))
, for all
i<n
.
It suffices now to show that
e_{n}:=e_{n}^{\prime}(\vec{e})
is a solution for
X_{n}
, i.e.
\mu\mathsf{S}\vdash e_{n}=f_{n}(\vec{e},e_{n})
, for which we reason as follows:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }ll}e_{n}&e_{n}^{\prime}(\vec{e})&\text{by definition of $e_{n}$}\\
&\mu X_{n}f_{n}(\vec{e},X_{n})&\text{by definition of $e_{n}^{\prime}$}\\
&f_{n}(\vec{e},\mu X_{n}f_{n}(\vec{e},X_{n}))&\text{by fixed point axiom \eqref{item:fixed-point-axioms}}\\
&f_{n}(\vec{e},e_{n})&\text{by definition of $e_{n}$}\qed\end{array}

Recalling
Remark

4
, note that solutions to an equational system are not necessarily unique, as
\mu\mathsf{S}
does not insist that
\mu
computes
least
fixed points.

Of course, we can use the above result to ‘solve’ CFGs too.

Corollary 8
.

Fix a CFG
\{X_{i}\Rightarrow f_{ij}(\vec{X})\}_{i<n,j}
.
There are
\mu
-expressions
\vec{e}=e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}
s.t.
\mu\mathsf{S}\vdash e_{i}=\sum_{j}f_{ij}({\vec{e}})
for all
i<n
.

3.
Effective inseparability of two problems on CFGs

Before presenting our main results on the undecidability of various theories of (idempotent) semirings with fixed points, we shall take a detour through the recursion theory of problems on CFGs.

3.1.
Two problems on CFGs

For our arguments, we shall use
effective inseparability
of two algorithmic problems for context-free grammars (CFG).
We shall consider only a special class of CFGs which we call
productive
ones. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the set of all terminal symbols and let
\mathcal{V}
be the set of all non-terminal ones.
As usual we shall assume among the non-terminals a designated
start
symbol
S\in\mathcal{V}
.

Definition 14

(Productive CFGs)
.

A CFG is
productive
if, for every production rule
X\Rightarrow\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{n}
, some
\alpha_{i}
is a terminal, i.e. some
\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
.

A typical example of productive CFG is a Greibach grammar without
\varepsilon
-productions. In Greibach grammars, we always have
\alpha_{1}\in\mathcal{A}
. Thus, by Greibach’s normalisation theorem
[
9
]
, one shows that any context-free language without the empty word is generated by a productive CFG. On the other hand, the empty word may never be generated by such a grammar.
In the sequel we shall restrict our attention to productive CFGs.

The first problem we are going to consider is the
totality
problem (i.e., the question whether a given productive CFG generates all non-empty words over
\mathcal{A}
). The second one is more specific. It could be called “the grammar is total for obvious reasons,” or
regularly total
.
Formally:

Definition 15

((Regular) totality)
.

A productive CFG is
total
, if it generates (from
S
) all non-empty words over
\mathcal{A}
.

A productive CFG
G
is
regularly total
, if it includes two designated non-terminals
Y,T\in\mathcal{V}
such that:

•

For each
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
G
includes all productions of the form:

\displaystyle S\Rightarrow aYT

\displaystyle T\Rightarrow a

\displaystyle T\Rightarrow aT

•

There is some
n>0
such that:

(1)

S
generates all nonempty words of length
\leq n+1
;

(2)

Y
generates all words of length
n
.

It is not hard to see that
every regulary total productive CFG is total:

Proposition 16

(Regular totality implies totality)
.

Every regularly total productive CFG is total.

Proof.

Let
G
be a regularly total productive CFG and let
\vec{a}
be a nonempty word:

•

If
|\vec{a}|\leq n+1
then it is generated from
S
by definition of regular totality.

•

If
|\vec{a}|>n+1
then write
\vec{a}=a\vec{b}\vec{c}
, for some
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
|\vec{b}|=n
and
|\vec{c}|>0
.
Then
S\Rightarrow aYT
and
Y
generates
\vec{b}
, by assumption, and
T
generates
\vec{c}
(since it trivially generates
every
nonempty word). ∎

The algorithmic complexity of regular totality is dual to that of totality: while the latter is known to be
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-complete, the former belongs to
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
(and, as we shall see below, is also complete in this class).

In what follows, we tacitly identify productive CFGs with their Gödel numbers under a reasonable encoding.
We write
\mathbf{R}
for the set of (Gödel numbers of) productive CFGs which are regularly total and
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
for the set of (Gödel numbers of) productive CFGs which are not total.

3.2.
Effective inseperability by reduction

We recall, following
[
19
]
, the notion of effective inseparability and the results which allows it to be used for proving
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-completeness of logical theories.

Fix some acceptable enumeration
\{f_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of the one-input partial recursive functions on
\mathbb{N}
,
and let us write
W_{n}
for “the
n
th
r.e. set,” i.e.
\mathsf{dom}(f_{n})
, the domain of
f_{n}
.

Definition 17
.

A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
are
effectively inseparable
if:

•

A\cap B=\varnothing
; and,

•

there is a partial recursive function
h(x,y)
s.t., whenever
W_{u}\supseteq A
and
W_{v}\supseteq B
with
W_{u}\cap W_{v}=\varnothing
, we have
h(u,v)\downarrow\,\notin W_{u}\cup W_{v}
.

We shall call such
h(x,y)
a
productive function
for
A,B
.

Given
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, a set
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}

separates

A
from
B
(or is a
separator
) if
C\supseteq A
but nonetheless
C\cap B=\varnothing
.
In the Definition above, informally,
h
uniformly falsifies any attempt to separate
A
from
B
by a decidable set.
Intuitively, we can see
\lambda v\,h(u,v)
here as a ‘proof’ that any such
W_{u}
is not decidable: it is a recursive function that computes counterexamples to the statement that
W_{u}
has an r.e. complement.

The following result is a corollary of a theorem by Myhill
[
22
]
, as shown in
[
23
, Exercise 11-14]
:

Theorem 18
.

If
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
are effectively inseparable r.e. sets and
C
is an r.e. set separating them then
C
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-complete.

Note in the Theorem above, and generally in the sequel, we shall not be precise about whether
C
separates
A
from
B
or vice-versa, WLoG by symmetry of the statement.

Effective inseparability can be propagated by a (very weak) kind of reduction.
Given
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
A\cap B=\varnothing
and
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime}=\varnothing
, an
e.i.-reduction
from
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
to
(A,B)
is a total computable function
f
such that:

(1)

f(A)\subseteq A^{\prime}

(2)

f(B)\subseteq B^{\prime}

Such maps are indeed appropriate for reducing questions of effective inseperability:

Proposition 19
.

Suppose
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
are effectively inseparable, and

A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
A^{\prime}\cap B^{\prime}=\varnothing
.
If
f
is an e.i.-reduction from
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
to
(A,B)

then
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are also effectively inseparable.

Proof.

The preimage of an r.e. set under a total computable mapping is itself r.e.
Indeed we have
f^{-1}(W_{n})=\mathsf{dom}(f_{n}\circ f)
, and we can even effectively compute from
n
the index of the r.e. function
f_{n}\circ f
, say by a total recursive function
g
, so that always
f^{-1}(W_{n})=W_{g(n)}
.

Now, let
h
be a productive function for
A,B
and let us define a productive function
h^{\prime}
for
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
as follows:

h^{\prime}(u,v)=f(h(g(u),g(v)))

Clearly
h^{\prime}
is a partial recursive function.
To show it is productive for
A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
, suppose

W_{u}\supseteq A^{\prime}
,
W_{v}\supseteq B^{\prime}
with

W_{u}\cap W_{v}=\varnothing
and let us show that
h^{\prime}(u,v)\notin W_{u}\cup W_{v}
.
First we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}W_{g(u)}&=&f^{-1}(W_{u})&\text{by definition of $g$}\\
&\supseteq&f^{-1}(A^{\prime})&\text{since $W_{u}\supseteq A^{\prime}$}\\
&\supseteq&A&\text{since $f(A)\subseteq A^{\prime}$}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr W_{g(v)}&=&f^{-1}(W_{v})&\text{by definition of $g$}\\
&\supseteq&f^{-1}(B^{\prime})&\text{since $W_{v}\supseteq B^{\prime}$}\\
&\supseteq&B&\text{since $f(B)\subseteq B^{\prime}$}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\cr W_{g(u)}\cap W_{g(v)}&=&f^{-1}(W_{u})\cap f^{-1}(W_{v})&\text{by definition of $g$}\\
&=&f^{-1}(W_{u}\cap W_{v})&\text{since preimages preserve intersections}\\
&=&f^{-1}(\varnothing)&\text{since $W_{u}\cap W_{v}=\varnothing$}\\
&=&\varnothing\end{array}

From here we have:

\begin{array}[]{rcll}h(g(u),g(v))&\notin&W_{g(u)}\cup W_{g(v)}&\text{since $h$ is productive for $A,B$}\\
&\notin&f^{-1}(W_{u})\cup f^{-1}(W_{v})&\text{by definition of $g$}\\
&\notin&f^{-1}(W_{u}\cup W_{v})&\text{since preimages preserve unions}\end{array}

Hence
h^{\prime}(u,v)=f(h(g(u),g(v))\notin W_{u}\cup W_{v}
, as required.
∎

3.3.
Effective inseperability of
\mathbf{R}
and
\bar{\mathbf{T}}

The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 20
.

The sets
\mathbf{R}
and
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
are effectively inseparable.

This subsection is devoted to proving this result.
We proceed via a reduction from a more well-known pair of effectively inseparable problems: halting and looping for Turing machines.
Throughout this section we shall work with one-input deterministic Turing machines that include, besides the initial and final state, also a designated

state
q_{c}
with rules that force the machine to remain at this state once it is reached:
(q_{c},a)\Rightarrow(q_{c},a,N)
for any letter

of the tape alphabet.

We say that a machine
M
loops on an input

if

reaches

when running on

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\{(\text{\rm\tt M},x)\mid\mbox{$\text{\rm\tt M}$ loops on input $x$}\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\{(\text{\rm\tt M},x)\mid\mbox{$\text{\rm\tt M}$ halts on input $x$}\}

Again, we construe both these sets as subsets of
\mathbb{N}
by implicitly identifying pairs
(\text{\rm\tt M},x)
with their codes.
The following fact is folklore (see, e.g.,
[
23
, Exercise 7-55d]
[
19

Proposition 21
.

The sets
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{H}
are effectively inseparable.

Now we can prove the effective inseparability of
\mathbf{R}
and
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
by reduction, in the sense of Proposition
19
, to the effective inseparability of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{H}
.

Let us start by briefly recalling some ideas from the standard proof of
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-hardness for the totality problem for CFGs, as presented in Kozen’s textbook
[
15
]
.
The

of a Turing machine

(if it exists) is the word
\#\kappa_{0}\#\kappa_{1}\#\ldots\#\kappa_{n}\#
, where
\kappa_{0}
is the initial configuration

\kappa_{i+1}
is the configuration which is the immediate successor of
\kappa_{i}
, and
\kappa_{n}
is a final configuration. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the alphabet in which configurations are written down (which includes the internal alphabet of
M
, its set of states, and the additional symbol
\#

We may readily define a (computable) reducing function, mapping each pair
(\text{\rm\tt M},x)
to a CFG which generates all non-empty words except for the halting protocol (if it exists). Thus, this grammar is total if and only if
M
does not halt on
x
.

We shall modify this construction a bit, following
[
19
]
, in order to take care of looping.
All non-empty words which are not the halting protocol must belong to one of the following three classes:

(I)

words of length greater than 1, beginning with
\#
, which cannot be even a prefix of the halting protocol: this includes words not beginning with
\#\kappa_{0}\#
; words where there is not a correct configuration between two
\#

\#\kappa\#\kappa^{\prime}\#
where
\kappa^{\prime}
is not the immediate successor of
\kappa

;

(II)

words of length greater than 1, beginning with
\#
, and not ending with
\#\kappa_{F}\#
where
\kappa_{F}
is a final configuration (such a word could be an incomplete protocol—a prefix of the halting protocol);

(III)

words beginning with a letter different from
\#
, and the one-letter word
\#
.

Note that these classes are not necessarily disjoint: a word can belong to more than one of them.

Write (I)
′
and (II)
′
for the sets of words from (I) and (II), respectively, with the first symbol
\#
removed.
The standard construction shows that both (I)
′
and (II)
′
are context-free. Denote the grammars generating (I)
′
and (II)
′
by
G_{1}
and
G_{2}

′
and (II)
′
are
\varepsilon
-free. Moreover,
G_{1}
and

are computable from
(\text{\rm\tt M},x)

[
19
, Section 2.1]
.

We assume
G_{1}
and

have disjoint sets of non-terminals, writing
Y
and
Z
for their start symbols, respectively.
Now define the grammar

obtained from the union of

and

along with the following additional productions:

\displaystyle S\Rightarrow aYT

for each

\displaystyle T\Rightarrow aT

for each
a\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle T\Rightarrow a

for each
a\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle S\Rightarrow\#Y

\displaystyle S\Rightarrow\#Z

\displaystyle S\Rightarrow aT

for each

\displaystyle S\Rightarrow a

for each
a\in\mathcal{A}

Here
S
and

are fresh non-terminals, and

is the new start symbol of

.
Notice that the first three productions are exactly as in
Definition

for regular totality.

Proposition 22
.

G_{\text{\rm\tt M},x}
generates all nonempty words over
\mathcal{A}
, except the halting protocol of
M
on
x
(if it exists).

Proof.

For words from (I) or (II) we start by firing
S\Rightarrow\#Y
and
S\Rightarrow\#T
, respectively, and continue as in
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
, respectively.
Words from (III) are generated by the final two rules. Note that this analysis accounts for
all
words generated by runs beginning with the final four rules from the start symbol
S
.

It remains to verify that the halting protocol cannot be generated by runs beginning with the first rule

.
However, any such run generates only words not beginning with
\#
and words with a prefix from (I); by definition, no such word could be the halting protocol.
∎

We can now define an appropriate

:

(3)

f\colon(\text{\rm\tt M},x)\mapsto G_{\text{\rm\tt M},x}

Of course, this function is total and computable.
Moreover it allows us to deduce effective inseparability of
\mathbf{R}
and
\bar{\mathbf{T}}
from that of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{H}
:

Proposition 23
.

The function

in (

(\mathbf{R},\bar{\mathbf{T}})
to
(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{H})
.

Proof.

Clearly
\mathbf{R}\cap\bar{\mathbf{T}}=\varnothing
.
So we need to show the following two properties:

•

if
(\text{\rm\tt M},x)\in\mathbf{C}
, then
f((\text{\rm\tt M},x))=G_{\text{\rm\tt M},x}\in\mathbf{R}
;

•

if
(\text{\rm\tt M},x)\in\mathbf{H}
, then
f((\text{\rm\tt M},x))=G_{\text{\rm\tt M},x}\in\bar{\mathbf{T}}
.

The second property follows immediately from
Proposition

, since the halting protocol cannot be generated by

. The first property is more involved.

Let
M

and let

be the length of an (incomplete) protocol of

on

which reaches a configuration where

is in state

. We shall show that

is regularly total, according to the given

.
That is, we will show that:

(1)

generates all nonempty words of length
\leq n+1
.

(2)

Y
generates all words of length
n
.

For (1), since

does not halt on

generates all nonempty words, by

generates all words of length

.

For (2), we need to show that any word

of length

belongs to (I)

.
For this, even if

is a prefix of the (infinite) protocol of

∎

.

Follows immediately from

,

and

.
∎

4.
Undecidability of (extensions of)

This section is devoted to a proof of our first main result:

.

Any r.e. extension of

is

We shall actually strengthen this result in the next section to avoid the need for idempotency (and even identity), by a sort of reduction, but we present this weaker version to illustrate the main ideas in a simpler setting.

4.1.
Some intermediate lemmata

To prove

Fix a CFG

When

5.
The case without idempotency

In this section we shall strengthen the main result of the previous section to cover theories of semirings that are not necessarily idempotent.

5.1.
Identity-free

Write
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}

The

5.2.
An idempotent interpretation of productive expressions

Temporarily write

e^{\prime}
is an idempotent:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }ll}e^{\prime}&e+\mu y(e^{\prime}+y)&\text{by unfolding $e^{\prime}$, by axiom \eqref{item:fixed-point-axioms}}\\
&e+e^{\prime}+\mu y(e^{\prime}+y)&\text{by unfolding $\mu y(e^{\prime}+y)$, by axiom \eqref{item:fixed-point-axioms}}\\
&(e+\mu y(e^{\prime}+y))+e^{\prime}&\text{by associativity and commutativity of $+$}\\
&e^{\prime}+e^{\prime}&\text{by the first line}\end{array}

In the presence of an order
e^{\prime}
is also an upper bound of
e
, and in the presence of induction
e^{\prime}
is in fact the
least
idempotent upper bound of
e
.
In particular in
\mathcal{L}
(and any other idempotent model) we have simply
e^{\prime}=e
:

•

On one hand, we have
\mathcal{L}(e^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(e+\mu y(e^{\prime}+y))
, and so
\mathcal{L}(e^{\prime})\supseteq\mathcal{L}(e)
.

•

On the other hand, we have that
e
solves the matrix of
e^{\prime}
:

–

Clearly
\mathcal{L}(\mu y(e+y))=e
: as above we have
\mathcal{L}(\mu y(e+y))=\mathcal{L}(e+\mu y(e+y))\supseteq\mathcal{L}(e)
; we also have that
e
solves the matrix of
\mu y(e+y)
in
\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}(e)=\mathcal{L}(e+e)
, thanks to idempotency.

–

Thus we have
e=e+e=e+\mu y(e+y)
in
\mathcal{L}
, again by idempotency.

Thus indeed
\mathcal{L}(e^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(e)
, as
e^{\prime}
must be a least solution.

Definition 30

(Idempotent interpretation)
.

For
1
-free expressions
e
, define
e^{i}
as follows:

•

a^{i}:=a^{\prime}=\mu x(a+\mu y(x+y))

•

\cdot^{i}
commutes with everything else. I.e.,

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ \df\ }l}0^{i}\ :=&0\\
x^{i}\ :=&x\\
(e+f)^{i}\ :=&e^{i}+f^{i}\\
(ef)^{i}\ :=&e^{i}f^{i}\\
(\mu xe)^{i}\ :=&\mu xe^{i}\end{array}

As previously argued it is not hard to see that:

Proposition 31
.

\mathcal{L}(e^{i})=\mathcal{L}(e)
.

Proof sketch.

By induction on the structure of
e
. In fact, we must strengthen the statement to
\mathcal{L}(e^{i}(\vec{A}))=\mathcal{L}(e(\vec{A}))
for all languages
\vec{A}
.
The case when
e
is some
a\in\mathcal{A}
follows from the argument above.
Every other case is immediate, with the
\mu
-case following by the strong
\mu
-congruence principle in
\mathcal{L}
:
\forall x\,e(x)=f(x)\rightarrow\mu xe(x)=\mu xf(x)
.
∎

Moreover,
\cdot^{i}
induces a bona fide interpretation of identity-free
\mu
-semirings:

Proposition 32
.

\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\vdash e=f\implies\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\vdash e^{i}=f^{i}
.

To see this, simply replace every expression
e
in a
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
proof by
e^{i}
. It remains to verify that the axioms and rules remain correct, which is routine.

Finally, thanks to the fact that idempotent elements of a semiring form an ideal, cf.
12
, we have idempotency of the image of
\cdot^{i}
on productive expressions provably in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
:

Lemma 33
.

If
e(\vec{x})
is productive then
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\vdash e^{i}(\vec{f})+e^{i}(\vec{f})=e^{i}(\vec{f})
for all
\vec{f}
.

Proof.

By induction on the structure of
e(\vec{x})
, according to the grammar of productive expressions in (
5
).
Working in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
we have:

•

If
e(\vec{x})
is
0
then:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }ll}0^{i}+0^{i}&0+0^{i}&\text{by definition of $0^{i}$}\\
&0^{i}&\text{since $0$ is an additive unit}\end{array}

•

If
e(\vec{x})
is some
a\in\mathcal{A}
then
a^{i}+a^{i}=a^{i}
by the argument at the beginning of this subsection.

•

If
e(\vec{x})
is
p(\vec{x})+q(\vec{x})
then:

\begin{array}[]{rll}&(p^{i}(\vec{f})+q^{i}(\vec{f}))+(p^{i}(\vec{f})+q^{i}(\vec{f}))&\\
=&(p^{i}(\vec{f})+p^{i}(\vec{f}))+(q^{i}(\vec{f})+q^{i}(\vec{f}))&\text{by associativity and commutativity of $+$}\\
=&p^{i}(\vec{f})+q^{i}(\vec{f})&\text{by inductive hypothesis for $p(\vec{x})$ and $q(\vec{x})$}\end{array}

•

If
e(\vec{x})
is
p(\vec{x})g(\vec{x})
then:

\begin{array}[]{rll}&p^{i}(\vec{f})g^{i}(\vec{f})+p^{i}(\vec{f})g^{i}(\vec{f})&\\
=&(p^{i}(\vec{f})+p^{i}(\vec{f}))g^{i}(\vec{f})&\text{by distributivity}\\
=&p^{i}(\vec{f})g^{i}(\vec{f})&\text{by inductive hypothesis for $p(\vec{x})$}\end{array}

•

If
e(\vec{x})
is
g(\vec{x})p(\vec{x})
then the argument is symmetric to above.

•

If
e(\vec{x})
is
\mu xp(x,\vec{x})
then:

\begin{array}[]{rll}&\mu xp^{i}(x,\vec{f})+\mu xp^{i}(x,\vec{f})\\
=&p^{i}(\mu xp^{i}(x,\vec{f}),\vec{f})+p^{i}(\mu xp^{i}(x,\vec{f}),\vec{f})&\text{by $\mu$-unfolding, axiom \eqref{item:fixed-point-axioms}}\\
=&p^{i}(\mu xp^{i}(x,\vec{f}),\vec{f})&\text{by inductive hypothesis for $p(x,\vec{x})$}\qed\end{array}

Note that, the above result notwithstanding,
e^{i}
is typically not productive, even if
e
is. In particular
a^{i}
is not productive, even though
a
is.

Notice that,
Proposition

32
notwithstanding,
\cdot^{i}
does not necessarily interpret
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}+\mathsf{idem}
into
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
. This is because, a priori, a proof in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}+\mathsf{idem}
may exploit idempotency of expressions not in the image of
\cdot^{i}
on productive expressions.
For this reason, in what follows,
we shall exploit the fact that we carefully kept track of the use of idempotency in our arguments in the previous section.

5.3.
Undecidability of
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
(and extensions)

Recall
\top:=\mu x\left(\sum\mathcal{A}+\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}ax\right)
.
By a similar argument to
Proposition

27
in the previous section we can obtain:

Proposition 34
.

Let
G\in\mathbf{R}
and let
e_{X}
be productive solutions of each of its non-terminals
X
in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
, cf.
Proposition

29
, with
e_{T}=\top
.
Then
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\vdash\top^{i}\leq e_{S}^{i}
.

For this we need appropriate versions of the two intermediate results we employed,
Lemmas

25
and
26
, in the previous section.
Their proofs are morally similar to the previous ones, but we must be careful to state them appropriately in the absence of idempotency, exploiting the refined results of this section on productive CFGs and
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}

Lemma 35
.

Fix a productive CFG
G
and write
e_{X}
for (any) corresponding productive solutions in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
of each non-terminal
X
of
G
, cf.
Proposition

29
.
If
\vec{a}\in\mathcal{L}(X)
in
G
then
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\vdash\vec{a}^{i}\leq e^{i}_{X}
.

Proof.

By induction on the derivation tree of
\vec{a}
from
X
in
G
. Working in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
:

•

If
X\to\vec{a}
then we have
\vec{a}+g=e_{X}
for some
g
by
Proposition

29
.
Thus
\vec{a}^{i}+g^{i}=e^{i}_{X}
by
Proposition

32
, and so
\vec{a}^{i}\leq e_{X}^{i}
by idempotency of
\vec{a}^{i}
,
Lemma

33
.

•

The inductive step is the same as in the proof of
Lemma

25
. ∎

Lemma 36
.

For
n>0
, we have
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\vdash\top^{i}=\sum\limits_{|\vec{a}|=1}^{n}\vec{a}^{i}+\sum\limits_{|\vec{a}|=n}\vec{a}^{i}\top^{i}
.

Proof.

The proof of
Lemma

26
does not use any identity axioms, and so goes through already in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
.
The result now follows by
Proposition

32
.
∎

We are now ready to prove the result we stated at the beginning of this section:

Proof of
Proposition

34
.

The proof goes through just like the proof of
Proposition

27
, only replacing all (productive) expressions by their image under
\cdot^{i}
.
All appeals to
Lemmas

25
and
26
are replaced by
Lemmas

35
and
36
respectively.
Note that, by inspection of the proof, the only uses of idempotency (marked in
purple
) are on productive expressions, whose image under
\cdot^{i}
is indeed provably idempotent in
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
by
Lemma

33
.
In particular, the expressions
e_{Y}
and
e_{S}
are productive by assumption.
∎

5.4.
Putting it together

Finally we arrive at our promised strengthening of
Theorem

24
:

Theorem 37
.

Any r.e. extension of
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
sound for
\mathcal{L}
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-complete.

The proof is similar to that of
Theorem

24
, only being more careful about handling the
\cdot^{i}
translation.

Proof.

Like in the proof of
Theorem

24
, let us assume that all CFGs have start symbol
S
and write
e^{G}_{X}
for the productive solutions of nonterminals
X
in a productive grammar
G
obtained by
Proposition

29
.
Let
\mathsf{U}\supseteq\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}
be r.e. with
\mathcal{L}\models\mathsf{U}
.
Now we write
\mathsf{Tot}(\mathsf{U}):=\{G\text{ productive}:\mathsf{U}\vdash\top^{i}\leq(e^{G}_{S})^{i}\}
. We have:

•

\mathsf{Tot}(\mathsf{U})\cap\bar{\mathbf{T}}=\varnothing
, by assumption that
\mathcal{L}\models\mathsf{U}
and by
Proposition

31
.

•

\mathbf{R}\subseteq\mathsf{Tot}(\mathsf{U})
, since
\mu\mathsf{S}_{\not{\hskip 0.81949pt1}}\subseteq\mathsf{U}
and by
Proposition

34
.

Thus by
Theorems

18
and
20
we have that
\mathsf{Tot}(\mathsf{U})
is
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-complete.
Again, by the obvious computable reduction from
\mathsf{Tot}(\mathsf{U})
to
\mathsf{U}
, we conclude as required.
∎

Corollary 38
.

The equational theories of Conway
\mu
-semirings and Park
\mu
-semirings are undecidable, and moreover
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-complete.
The same holds even for identity-free versions of these theories.

6.
Conclusions

In this work we addressed the question of (un)decidability of the equational theories of various classes of semirings with fixed points.
Using recursion theoretic methods we showed that a large class of such theories are undecidable.
These results account for many of the
\mu
-semiring theories proposed in previous works, cf.
[
21
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

Conway semirings were proposed in
[
6
,
7
]
as a basic finite equational theory that is nonetheless powerful enough to carry out reduction to Greibach normal form.
Our basic system
\mu\mathsf{S}
is weaker/more general than Conway semirings, and is also finitely equationally axiomatised.
It would be interesting to examine if it may have any further utility, other than as a lower bound for the range of our undecidability results herein.
For instance, are there results of formal language theory that are validated already in
\mu\mathsf{S}
?

Finally our results do not cover pure
\mu
-semirings, where
\mu xe(x)
is not necessarily a fixed point. The (un)decidability of its equational theory is, as far as we know, open.
