Comparative Assessment of Efficacy of “BLUEM” Active Oxygen Gel and COE-PAK Dressing on Postoperative Healing After Surgical Depigmentation—A Split Mouth Study
Authors: Jaishree Garg, Somya Tiwari, Saurabh Sinha, Tamim Ahmad, Indrani Bharadwaj, Radhika Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Dark-colored gingiva is an esthetic concern that cannot be ignored, especially when accompanied by a high lip line or gummy smile. Gingival depigmentation is a periodontal plastic surgical procedure, wherein the hyperpigmentation is removed or reduced by various techniques.Materials and Methods:This split-mouth randomized clinical trial was conducted on 20 patients aged between 18 and 45 years with maxillary physiological gingival pigmentation classes (II), (III), and (IV) according to the Dummett–Gupta Oral Pigmentation Index (40 treated sites). The maxilla was randomly divided into two symmetrical parts—from the right second premolar to the midline and from the midline to the left second premolar—to receive either BlueM gel or COE-PAK as a dressing after surgical depigmentation with a scalpel. Indices assessed were healing index and re-epithelization index with toluidine blue, and the follow-up period was one month.Results:The result of the study demonstrated that pain score and re-epithelization score were less in the test group (BlueM gel) as compared to the control group (COE-PAK) and was statistically significant.Conclusion:It can be concluded that BlueM gel can also be a better alternative as a periodontal dressing compared to COE-PAK due to its properties of better healing and decreasing postoperative pain.

INTRODUCTION

Gingival health and appearance are essential for an aesthetically pleasing smile, and the presence of pigmented gingiva can detract from one’s overall facial esthetics, often leading individuals to seek cosmetic correction.[1] Gingival hyperpigmentation, commonly referred to as physiological or racial pigmentation, is a benign condition frequently observed in individuals with darker skin types and is influenced by genetic traits.[2] The pigmentation arises due to endogenous pigments, such as melanin, melanoid, carotene, and hemoglobin derivatives, with melanin produced by melanoblasts being the primary contributor. External factors, including habits like tobacco smoking, can exacerbate gingival pigmentation.[3]

To address such concerns, gingival depigmentation is performed as a periodontal plastic surgical procedure aimed at improving smile esthetics.[4] Various techniques have been developed for this purpose, such as gingivectomy, gingivectomy with free gingival grafts, electrosurgery, cryosurgery, application of chemical agents (e.g. phenol and alcohol), mechanical abrasion using diamond burs, and laser-based treatments including Nd: YAG, semiconductor diode, and CO₂ lasers.[5] Despite the availability of modern tools, the scalpel technique remains widely preferred for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility under local anesthesia.

Scalpel-based depigmentation involves removing the pigmented gingival epithelium along with a thin layer of connective tissue, thereby exposing a raw wound that heals by secondary intention. This necessitates the use of periodontal dressings to protect the wound, reduce discomfort, and prevent infection. Traditional dressings, like COE-PAK, have limitations, including cytotoxicity and delayed healing due to plaque retention. Newer alternatives such as BlueM gel—containing active oxygen from honey enzymes and sodium perborate—are emerging, showing promising therapeutic effects, including enhanced healing, neovascularization, and antimicrobial activity, thereby opening avenues for improved postoperative care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This clinical study included 24 systemically healthy individuals aged between 18 and 40 years, all presenting with mild-to-moderate gingival pigmentation (Dummett–Gupta Oral Pigmentation Index scores 3–4) and 3–4 mm of gingival display. Participants were excluded if they were smokers, pregnant or lactating women, had a history of depigmentation procedures, or were taking medications affecting healing. All participants provided informed consent in their native language, and demographic data were recorded. Patients were randomly divided into two groups: the test group (n = 12) received BlueM gel, and the control group (n = 12) received non-eugenol periodontal dressing.

All procedures were performed by a single operator under aseptic conditions. The maxillary anterior gingiva between the second premolars was divided into two symmetrical halves. After topical anesthesia with 2% lidocaine, a no. 15 scalpel blade was used to remove the pigmented gingival epithelium and underlying connective tissue until complete depigmentation was achieved. Hemostasis was maintained with sterile gauze. One half of the arch was treated with BlueM gel (test) and the other with COE-PAK dressing (control) using a split-mouth design. Patients in the test group also applied BlueM gel thrice daily at home. Follow-up visits were conducted at 24 hours and seven days postoperatively to assess healing and remove the COE-PAK.

Three clinical parameters were recorded: the Dummett–Gupta Oral Pigmentation Index, the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain (assessed on day 1 and day 7), and the Landry Healing Index to evaluate tissue recovery. Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS Version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA, 2011).

RESULTS

At 24 hours, the test group had a mean visual analog pain score (VAS) of 8.08 ± 1.24, while the control group had 8.83 ± 0.94. The difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.109). On the 7th day, the test group’s mean visual analog pain score (VAS) further decreased to 0 ± 0, while the control group remained at 1.75 ± 0.42, showing a significant difference (P = 0.001). Initially, pain levels were comparable in both groups. Over time, the test group experienced a significantly greater reduction in pain compared to the control group. This suggests that the intervention used in the test group was more effective in reducing pain [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Scalpel depigmentation remains the gold standard for managing gingival pigmentation due to its simplicity and effectiveness. However, it often leads to intra- and postoperative bleeding and discomfort due to the open surgical wound, which heals by secondary intention. Such wounds typically require a periodontal dressing for 7–10 days to minimize pain, protect the site, and support healing.[1] Postoperative discomfort is influenced by the extent of tissue manipulation and the wound healing time, necessitating an ideal dressing that can maintain a moist environment, control exudate, and provide a barrier against microbial contamination.[2]

Oxygen therapy has been shown to significantly enhance wound healing by promoting angiogenesis, fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, and bacterial clearance. BlueM gel, which releases active oxygen through the breakdown of sodium perborate and honey enzymes, has demonstrated efficacy in selectively eliminating anaerobic pathogens and supporting a healing-compatible oral environment.[3] Its components release low concentrations of hydrogen peroxide and boric acid, stimulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and fibroblast growth factor (FGF), which aid in tissue regeneration.[4]

In this split-mouth study, patients reported significantly less postoperative pain with BlueM gel compared to COE-PAK, supporting its anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial benefits.[5] Enhanced re-epithelization was observed in the BlueM group at one week postoperatively, though no significant difference was noted by one month. BlueM gel’s ability to stimulate neovascularization and fibroblast proliferation likely contributed to this outcome, as corroborated by earlier studies.[6] In contrast, COE-PAK functions merely as a mechanical barrier with no direct biological effect on healing. While findings are promising, the limited sample size underscores the need for further large-scale, long-term clinical investigations to validate the broader applications of BlueM gel in oral wound management.[7]

CONCLUSION

Based on the results of this clinical study, it was concluded that the use of BlueM gel following the surgical scalpel technique for depigmentation enhances wound healing, accelerates re-epithelization, and reduces postoperative pain more effectively than applying the traditional COE-PAK™ dressing. However, further more extensive and long-term follow-up studies are required.
