Introduction

Anxiety and fear have been found to be the most common reasons for a child's lack of cooperation during dental treatment.1 Children's incapacity to cope with frightening dental stimuli frequently emerges as behavior management difficulties, which can then impair the quality of care and hinder the outcome of treatment for both the child and pediatric dentist.2,6 Anxiety about dental injections is the most commonly cited reason for dental anxiety, followed by extraction and drilling of teeth.7 Anxious patients need about 20% more chair time than low-anxiety patients.8 According to a survey conducted by the Association of Pediatric Dentistry Diplomates, children in the age-groups of 2–9 years require more vigorous physical behavioral management procedures than other age-groups.9

Measurement of dental anxiety in children is important not just for the delivery of high-quality clinical care but also for research. Dentists use behavior management procedures that are time- and cost-effective, ultimately producing satisfactory results with the least amount of time and effort.10

“Video modeling” or “behavioral modeling” is a technique that involves the demonstration of desired behaviors, outcomes, and attitudes through active, visual representations. It can be effective in reducing anxiety while preparing patients for procedures, teaching coping strategies, providing information, and facilitating self-care behaviors.11 Bandura et al.12 stated that a skillfully designed, attention-grabbing video could be developed and used to eliminate or prevent fears and anxieties in children. Modeling has not been widely practiced as a routine behavior management technique, even though its role in reducing dental fear and anxiety has been well-documented in the past.13,14 Additionally, there is a dearth of literature and randomized controlled trials on video modeling, especially in young children, so more studies should be conducted on video modeling alone as preparation for dental care in children.

According to the findings of the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD)15 on the need to study nonpharmacologic behavior management techniques employing various clinical protocols and considering the importance of video modeling in pediatric dentistry, the present study was undertaken to comparatively evaluate the three different modeling videos on dental anxiety of 3–6-year-old children requiring treatment under LA.

Subjects and Methods

Fifty-six patients in the age-group of 3–6 years visiting the Outpatient Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, requiring treatment under mandibular LA, were selected for the study. After the study design was formed, Ethical and Institutional Research Committee approval was obtained (CTRI/2022/10/046746).

This was a parallel, randomized controlled trial. The study was carried out in pediatric dental patients meeting the inclusion criteria and was considered for the present study. Participating children were assigned randomly into four groups (14 each) by simple random sampling technique using the lottery method.

Group B—Modeling video showing restorative procedure without LA.

Group C—Modeling video showing restorative procedure preceded by LA.

Group D—Control group without video modeling.

Group D—Control group without video modeling.

Allocation concealment was done using the sequentially numbered opaque sealed envelope (SNOSE) method. This was a single-blinded study; only the investigator was blinded to the treatment groups.

Venham's Index (modified 6-point scale according to Venham)16,18

Score Criteria 0Relaxed: Smiling, being willing to converse, and displaying behavior desired by the dentist.1Uneasy: Concerned, able to cooperate, tense facial expressions, hands that are down or partially raised, and a brief protest to show discomfort.2Tense: Anxiety is evident in tone of voice, questions, and responses; during a stressful procedure, the child complies with the request to cooperate despite verbal protest, crying, and tense, raised hands that are not very disruptive.3Reluctant: Crying, vocal protest, attempting to halt the procedure with hands, and treatment is difficult.4Interference: Protests interrupt the process, general sobbing, and body movements that occasionally require physical restraint.5Out of contact: Physical restraint is necessary when there is hard, loud crying, screaming, cursing, and an inability to listen.

First visit: The patients coming to the outpatient department (OPD) were examined, and the children meeting the inclusion criteria were selected for the study. Parents were informed about the study. Written consent was signed by the legal guardian of the child, and verbal assent was obtained from the participating children.

Second visit: During this visit, the participants were randomly assigned to a control group and one of the three video modeling intervention groups by the dental assistant, who was not involved in the treatment procedure. Each group consisted of 14 children. Before watching the video, the dental anxiety score of each participating child in the video modeling intervention groups was determined using the Modified 6-point anxiety scale according to Venham (Venham's Index). After that, each participant was shown a video of the allotted group on a projector by the second dental assistant, who was also not involved in the further treatment procedure. Three interventional videos were used for the study: a modeling video showing oral hygiene instructions, a modeling video showing a restorative procedure without LA, and a modeling video showing a restorative procedure preceded by LA. The duration of each video was 3–5 minutes. The peer model in the video was a 4.5-year-old male child who was unfamiliar to all other child participants, showing cooperative behavior throughout the video and being verbally reinforced by the dentist. All three interventional videos featured the same dentist and the same peer model. The video was filmed in the same clinic space where study data was collected. The dentist in the modeling videos and the one who performed the treatment procedure requiring LA were the same. The accompanying guardian/parent was allowed to be present while the video was being watched, but they were instructed not to influence the child or comment on anything. No video was shown to the participants in the control group.

After completion of the video, the dental anxiety score of the child was determined again. Thus, for the modeling groups, the anxiety score of the child at the first two intervals, that is, “before watching video” and “after watching video,” was recorded in the projector room. Then, the child was escorted to the clinical area, and the tell-show-do approach and standardized dialogs were incorporated by the dentist for all participating children in all the groups. After that, the LA procedure was carried out on the child. The anxiety scores were taken before the start of the LA procedure, during LA administration, and after the administration of LA using Venham's Index for all the groups.

The anxiety score of the child was recorded five times throughout the entire visit for the three modeling video groups: before watching the video, after watching the video, before administration of LA, while administration of LA, and after administration of LA. For the control group, the anxiety score of the child was recorded only three times, as no video was shown to participants in this group: before administration of LA, while administration of LA, and after administration of LA. All the patients were treated by the same pediatric dentist, who was unaware of the subject's experimental groups, and the same dentist recorded the anxiety scores of the child.

Statistical Analysis

Intragroup comparison was done by analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by pairwise comparison done by Tukey's post hoc test. The five intervals within the modeling groups and the three intervals within the control group were compared for anxiety scores by repeated measures ANOVA, followed by pairwise comparison by Bonferroni's post hoc test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all the above tests. The software used was Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 19.

Discussion

Dental anxiety is a state of apprehension that something dreadful is going to happen in relation to dental treatment, and it is coupled with a sense of losing control. Detecting and assessing dental anxiety among child patients with a valid method of measurement is crucial, as it is identified as one of the most significant problems in pediatric dentistry.19,20 The purpose of resolving a child's anxiety about dental procedures is both to reduce the immediate fear and prevent it from continuing into adulthood.21,22

The most recommended techniques for modifying children's behavior during dentistry have involved various forms of pre-exposure to the dental environment and procedures. One such behavior management technique is modeling.1,21 Bandura established that modeling or learning by observation works not only for the acquisition of new behaviors but also for reducing undesirable behavior.12 Modeling is of two types: live or participant modeling and audiovisual modeling.23,24 Filmed modeling does not require a live model; it can also be easily incorporated in a dental clinic or at home, which reduces the dentist's professional time, and it can attract a child's attention easily. Several studies have evaluated the efficiency of modeling through a film in the reduction of a child's dental anxiety, including those by Machen and Johnson (1974),25 Melamed et al. (1975),26 Fields and Pinkham (1976),21 Melamed et al. (1978),27 Rouleau et al. (1981),28 and Klingman et al. (1984).29 Audiovisual modeling encourages pleasant memories and positive attitudes, resulting in less anxious and more cooperative behavior in child patients toward the dental experience.19

In the present study, children between 3 and 6 years old were included, as this age-group is an important consideration in assessing the effectiveness of modeling. This is in accordance with previous studies conducted by Machen and Johnson (1974),25 Fields and Pinkham et al. (1976),21 and Hine et al. (2019).30

Venham's 6-point anxiety scale was used in this study for recording anxiety scores, as this rating procedure is reliable, valid, and easily integrated into clinical or research activities. It also potentially provides a much-needed standard tool for behavioral assessment in pedodontics research.31 According to Melamed et al. (1978),27 children with no prior experience did best when given prior exposure to either a long demonstration tape or a short version with a peer model. Therefore, in this study, children with no previous dental experience were included.

In the present study, the effect of three different modeling videos i.e., oral hygiene instruction video, video showing restoration without LA, video showing restoration with LA, and a control group without video modeling on the dental anxiety of children was evaluated. This study was conducted to observe whether a child's anxiety is reduced by watching any video, or if the child should be shown a video that demonstrates the exact treatment procedure that the child is going to face. In this study, one videoclip included the LA procedure, and one videoclip did not. The effect of these videos on the child's anxiety and behavior while undergoing treatment was important, as only those children who required LA were included in the study. Additionally, anxiety of children was recorded before, during, and after the administration of LA, which is significant.

A statistically significant (p < 0.05) difference was found between group A (oral hygiene instruction video) and group C (video showing restoration with LA), indicating that the anxiety of the children was reduced significantly in group C as compared to group A. Participants from group C may have paid attention to the video, remembered what had been observed, and tried to reproduce the same behavior as the child model, which made observational learning effective. This might be the reason for the reduction in anxiety among participants from group C.

The control group showed a significant difference in anxiety score as compared to all other three modeling groups at “while giving LA.” This suggested that there was a significant reduction in the anxiety of participating children during the administration of LA after watching the modeling video, especially in groups B and C. Comparatively, there was an increase in disruptive behavior among children in group D (control group) during the administration of LA, as no modeling video was incorporated in this group. These results demonstrated that modeling video effectively reduced the anxiety of children requiring LA. Al-Namankany et al. (2014)32 conducted a similar type of study to investigate if the use of video would decrease anxiety surrounding the delivery of LA, and the authors concluded that video modeling was effective at reducing dental anxiety at the time of LA application for the modeling group.

It is noteworthy that the mean anxiety score at “while giving LA” (2.07) was higher than the mean anxiety at “before watching video” (1.79) for group A. This may be because children in this group watched an oral hygiene instruction video and may not have become familiar with the dental situation during the administration of LA.

Figure 1 displayed that participating children showed more anxiety at the first interval, that is, “before watching video.” Then, there was a consistent reduction in the anxiety of the child at the next two intervals, proving the significance of the video modeling technique. The fact that the children watched the film immediately before the actual dental work may have enhanced its effectiveness because of the aroused state of the individual. During the administration of LA, the anxiety increased again, as there was actual needle penetration, which is a painful procedure. However, it is notable that at “after giving LA,” the anxiety reduced again, as the LA procedure was completed.

The reduction of anxiety in group C may be attributable to the viewing of a filmed model depicting positive behavior during dental treatment, especially during the administration of LA, and was, in turn, verbally and materially reinforced for cooperation in the modeling film. Exposure to the modeling film may have made the children more accustomed to the sights, sounds, and procedures they would face. As a result, the threat of the unknown was diminished among these children, which may have reduced their anxiety and negative reactions toward an unfamiliar situation, such as an anesthetic injection, through fear extinction. However, it is significant that the anxiety again reduced at the last interval, that is, “after giving LA,” which may be because the child could sense relief from the completion of the LA procedure, as the participating children were relating this to the modeling video.
