Relatively lower BMI-adjusted calf circumference is associated with metabolically unhealthy phenotype in non-obese adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Xiaotong Wang, Na Ta, Shan Hu, Yawen Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO) individuals are at higher risk of cardiovascular diseases, while not receiving sufficient attention. This study was conducted to explore the association between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO in non-obese US adults using the NHANES database.MethodsA total of 9,628 non-obese US adults (48.01% female and 51.99% male) were included in this cross-sectional study. The metabolically unhealthy phenotype was defined as ≥ 2 components of metabolic abnormalities. BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted skeletal muscle mass (SMM) were divided into quartiles. Weighted multiple logistic regression model was used to evaluate the study aims. The relation between BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM was tested by Spearman correlation.ResultsIn the multivariate analysis, compared with the lowest quartile of BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM, OR (95%CI) of the highest quartile was 0.30 (0.25, 0.35) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001) and 0.39 (0.29, 0.53) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001), respectively. BMI-adjusted CC was positively correlated with BMI-adjusted SMM for all participants (r = 0.48). A weak non-linear association was observed in BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO (P = 0.047). Similar results were observed in sex- and BMI-specific subgroup and sensitivity analysis.ConclusionBMI-adjusted CC was inversely associated with MUNO, suggesting more attention should be paid to monitoring BMI-adjusted CC for the public to ease the disease burden, especially for people without obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20974-z.Keywords: Calf circumference, Metabolically unhealthy, Skeletal muscle mass, Body mass index, Non-obese

Background

Metabolic abnormalities, such as dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and increased blood pressure, are vital determinants of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) which are the leading cause of death globally [1, 2]. Metabolic abnormalities are often regarded as obesity-associated diseases [3]. Nevertheless, a substantial proportion (around 17.2%~34.4%) of individuals also have metabolic abnormalities despite being non-obese, referred to as metabolically unhealthy non-obese (MUNO) [4–7]. Similar to obese ones, MUNO individuals were found to have a higher risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), CVDs, cancer, and mortality, compared with metabolically healthy non-obese (MHNO) ones [4, 8]. However, MUNO individuals have not been given sufficient attention for preventing non-communicable diseases (NCDs) with a focus on the obese group. In-depth investigations into identifying MUNO may provide important clues for early prevention of NCDs mentioned above.

Skeletal muscle plays a key role in regulating glucose and lipid homeostasis [9]. Low skeletal muscle mass (SMM) and intramuscular lipid accumulation could lead to insulin resistance (IR) and metabolic diseases [9]. Additionally, MUNO individuals were reported to have lower muscle mass and poorer muscle quality than MHNO individuals [7, 10]. Thus, the assessment of SMM may have an important implication for distinguishing different metabolic phenotypes. Recently, calf circumference (CC), as a feasible, easily accessed, and invasive tool, has been proposed as a sensitive indicator of SMM and peripheral subcutaneous fat [11]. Besides, evidence has shown that CC was closely related to IR, carotid atherosclerosis, CVDs, frailty, and mortality [12–15]. Considering a variation of subcutaneous adipose tissue accumulation across different levels of adiposity, BMI-adjusted CC, rather than raw CC, was recommended to evaluate its associations with health outcomes, such as falls, cardiometabolic syndrome, and mortality [16]. Collectively, it can be speculated that BMI-adjusted CC might be useful for screening MUNO individuals.

To date, limited cross-sectional studies with small samples reported on the association between CC and metabolic abnormalities [17–20]. Yet, their results were inconsistent, which may partly lie in discrepancies in participants’ characteristics and CC adjustment. Moreover, some important confounders were not considered, like physical activity and energy intake [21, 22]. Notably, the utility of BMI-adjusted CC in identifying MUNO individuals in the US has not yet been explored.

Therefore, the present study was conducted to clarify the relationship between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO in US adults. Besides, to further verify the potential association, SMM assessed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) was also taken into account.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data used in this cross-sectional study was from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected from 1999 to 2006. Five different types of data, including Demographics, Dietary, Examination, Laboratory, and Questionnaire information were contained in the NHANES database. The protocols of NHANES 1999–2004 and 2005–2006 survey cycles were approved by the Institutional Review Board/Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, CDC (Protocol #98 − 12 and Protocol #2005-06). All participants provided written informed consent. The NHANES dataset is available at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/ContinuousNhanes/Default.aspx.

Study population

Participants aged 18 years and over, with BMI between 18.5 kg/m2 and 30 kg/m2 were included. Participants were excluded if they had missing data about determining parameters of MUNO, CC (n = 330), or SMM (n = 1,228). Participants with pre-existing cancer (n = 865), or CVDs (n = 799), or with pregnancy (none) were further excluded. No outliers for CC measurements were identified according to the IQR method with a threshold of 3. Finally, 9,628 participants were included (Fig. 1).

Measurement of CC and leg SMM

Participants had body shape assessed using a combination of anthropometric imaging methods. When CC was measured, participants sat with their right calf relaxed. The maximal CC was measured on a perpendicular plane to the long axis on the right calf, to the nearest 0.1 cm. Detailed information of measurement can be obtained at https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/1999-2000/manuals/bm.pdf (Page 3–26). All body composition measures were performed using whole-body DXA scans (Hologic Scanner, QDR-4500, Bedford, MA). The whole-body scans were performed under standard conditions and a detailed description can be found in the NHANES procedures manual (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/1999-2000/manuals/bm.pdf). The average of left leg lean excluding bone mineral content and right leg lean excluding bone mineral content was used as leg SMM in the present study. Missing and invalid DXA data from NHANES 1999–2006 were already imputed using multiple-imputation methodology. Detailed information is available at the official website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/Dxa/Dxa.aspx).

BMI was calculated as weight (kg) / height (m2) and expressed as kg/m2. BMI-adjusted CC was calculated as CC (cm) / BMI (kg/m2), and BMI-adjusted SMM was calculated as SMM (kg) / BMI (kg/m2) [23]. BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM were included in the final analysis.

Outcome ascertainment

Participants who met two or more of the following four criteria were classified as metabolically unhealthy [24–26]: (1) elevated blood pressure, systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg or ever having been diagnosed with hypertension or use of antihypertensive drugs; (2) impaired glycemic control, fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.0% or 2-h postload glucose (2 h-PG) from an oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) or ever having been diagnosed with diabetes or use of antidiabetic drugs; (3) elevated triglyceride (TG), TG ≥ 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or use of lipid-lowering drugs; (4) reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), HDL‐C < 40 mg/dL (1.04 mmol/L) for men or < 50 mg/dL (1.29 mmol/L) for women or ever having been diagnosed with hypercholesterolemia or use of lipid‐lowering drugs. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [27, 28]. Combined with BMI and metabolic status, participants who were both metabolically unhealthy and non-obese were defined as MUNO. All the laboratory variates including total cholesterol (TC), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting insulin and the above variates were measured according to the NHANES Laboratory Procedures Manual. Homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as [FBG (mmol/L) * fasting insulin (µU/mL)] / 22.5 [29].

Measurement of covariates

Information on demographics, history of diseases and medicine, smoking, physical activity, and nutritional factors were acquired by questionnaires. The dietary intake data during one 24-hour period were used to estimate the total energy intake (TEI, kcal), alcohol consumption and healthy eating index (HEI) 2015 [30]. HEI ranges from 0 to 100, and higher HEI represents better diet quality [31]. Race/ethnicity was divided into the following four categories: Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and others. The marital status was classified as married/cohabiting and else. Education level was classified into under high school, high school or equivalent, at least some college and others. Ratio of family income to poverty (PIR) was classified as under 1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and up to 3.5 [32]. Physical activity was categorized as inactive group (< 150 min/week of moderate-intensity activity or < 75 min/week of vigorous-intensity activity) and active group (≥ 150 min/week of moderate-intensity activity or ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous-intensity activity) according to The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans [33]. Smoking status was classified as current smoker, former smoker and non-smoker. Participants were labeled as non-drinker, moderate drinker and heavy drinker. TEI and HEI were divided into quartiles for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 15.0) software. Since NHANES is a stratified, multi-stage, and complex sample design survey, all statistical analyses in this study used appropriate sample weights associated with the NHANES data. Baseline characteristics of participants were presented as number and survey-weighted percentage (%) for categorical variables, and as survey-weighted mean and standard error for normally distributed continuous variables, else as survey-weighted median and interquartile range. Weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO. BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM were divided into quartiles (BMI-adjusted CC: Q1, < 1.38; Q2, 1.38 to 1.47; Q3, 1.47 to 1.58; Q4, ≥ 1.58; BMI-adjusted SMM: Q1, < 0.25; Q2, 0.25 to 0.30; Q3, 0.30 to 0.36; Q4, ≥ 0.36), and Q1 was used as a reference. Model 1 was the crude model. Model 2 was adjusted for sex and age. Model 3 was further adjusted for race/ethnicity, marital status, PIR, physical activity, education, smoking, drinking and TEI. Missing values of covariates were treated as dummy variables. Besides, the relationship between BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM was tested by Spearman correlation. Non-linear associations between BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO were explored using restrict cubic spline logistic regression with 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles.

Since sex differences in BMI-adjusted CC were identified, sex-specific subgroup analysis was conducted. Additionally, according to BMI, participants were divided into two groups (normal weight, overweight) and included in the subgroup analysis. For robustness of results, sensitivity analysis was conducted after multiple imputation for covariates including marital status, physical activity, and TEI. Additionally, considering the important role of nutrition [34], HEI was additionally adjusted in the regression models in sensitivity analysis. P < 0.05 was considered significant for all tests.

Results

Baseline characteristics of included participants

Among the included 9,628 participants, 4,453 (48.01%) was female, 5,175 (51.99%) was male. The weighted median (interquartile) of age was 40.00 (30.00, 52.00) years old. As shown in Tables 1, 3,380 (33.47%) participants were metabolically unhealthy. Compared with metabolically healthy non-obese participants, those with MUNO tended to be older, male, White, married and cohabiting, less educated, ever smoker, non-drinker and have higher PIR (all P < 0.01). Moreover, MUNO ones tended to have higher BMI, TC, LDL-C, TG, SBP, DBP, FBG, 2 h-PG, HbA1c, fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, HEI and CC, but lower HDL-C, BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM (all P < 0.01). The weighted median (interquartile) of BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM were 1.45 (1.37, 1.53) and 0.30 (0.24, 0.35) among MUNO participants, respectively.

Association of BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM with MUNO

Table 2 presents the results of logistic regression analysis evaluating the association between BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO. After multiple adjustment, significant negative associations were observed between BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO. Compared with the lowest quartile of BMI-adjustment CC, OR (95% CI) of the highest quartile was 0.30 (0.25, 0.35) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001). Compared with the lowest quartile of BMI-adjustment SMM, OR (95%CI) of the highest quartile was 0.39 (0.29, 0.53) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001). The correlation scatterplot between BMI-adjusted CC and BMI-adjusted SMM was presented in Fig. 2. There was a positive correlation for all participants (Spearman correlation r = 0.48), and BMI-adjusted CC alone explained 65% and 65% of BMI-adjusted SMM variability for males and females, 49% and 56% of BMI-adjusted SMM variability for normal weight and overweight participants, respectively.

Restrict cubic spline was used to explore potential non-linear association between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO. We observed a downward trend of MUNO prevalence with the increment of BMI-adjusted CC and a significant non-linear association was observed (P = 0.047). Similar non-linear trend was observed in males (P = 0.01) while not in female group (P = 0.28), or the strata of BMI (normal weight, P = 0.27; overweight, P = 0.24) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Non-linear association was also observed in the association between BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO in the entire population (P = 0.01), across the strata of sex (female: P = 0.03; male: P < 0.0001) and in the normal weight group (P = 0.03), while not in the overweight group (P = 0.29) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

The associations between BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO across the strata of sex and BMI are summarized separately in Table 3 and Supplementary Table 1. For male, female and normal weight groups, the negative associations remained stable after multiple adjustment. Among overweight participants, compared with the lowest quartile of BMI-adjustment CC, OR (95%CI) of the highest quartile was 0.64 (0.50, 0.83) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.01), while no significant association was observed in the association between BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO [OR (95%CI) highest vs. lowest: 0.77 (0.53, 1.13) (P = 0.19, P for trend = 0.31)].

Sensitivity analysis

After multiple imputation for missing covariates, negative associations of BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM with MUNO remained stable (Supplementary Table 2). Compared with the lowest quartile of BMI-adjustment CC, OR (95%CI) of the highest quartile was 0.31 (0.26, 0.36) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001). Similarly, BMI-adjusted SMM was also associated with MUNO inversely. The OR (95%CI) in the highest quartile compared with that in the lowest quartile was 0.41 (0.31, 0.56) (P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001). After further adjusting HEI, the associations between BMI-adjusted CC, BMI-adjusted SMM and MUNO remained stable (all P < 0.001, P for trend < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we provided the evidence for the first time that BMI-adjusted CC were negatively associated with MUNO in non-obese US adults using NHANES database. The protective role of BMI-adjusted CC remained significantly in sex-specific subgroup analysis and regardless of being normal weight or overweight. All these results illustrated BMI-adjusted CC may play an important role in identifying MUNO population.

MUNO can contribute to an elevated risk of NCDs such as CVDs [35]. The increasing burden of MUNO demonstrates an immediate need to find a useful tool for early identification of those at high risk. CC, as an anthropometric measurement with advantages of its feasibility, ease of performance and non-invasiveness, can reflect a change in fat-free weight and is a sensitive indicator of SMM and peripheral subcutaneous fat in adults [36]. It has been recommended as a muscle indicator for sarcopenia case finding and a component of sarcopenia screening tools [37, 38]. Since raw CC measurements were prone to error as a consequence of not accounting for subcutaneous adipose tissue across different levels of adiposity [16], BMI-adjusted CC was used in this study. Similar to our results, previous evidence showed CC was inversely associated with prevalent hypertension, dysglycaemia and dyslipidaemia in 486 middle-aged and elderly Chinese women [17]. Instead of using the raw CC, Takamura et al. found weight-adjusted CC was negatively associated with metabolic parameters (e.g., SBP, DBP, FBG) and IR indices among 195 Japanese aged 40–59 [19]. Another study found participants with higher waist-to-CC ratio had higher risk of metabolic syndrome (MetS) among 684 Korean men aged > 40 [18]. Nevertheless, no significant association was observed between CC and MetS in 387 obese Turkish [20]. The discrepancy may partly result from differences in CC adjustment and demographic characteristics. Indeed, some important limitations still existed in those studies, such as limited sample, and no adjustment for some important confounders. Furthermore, no evidence investigated the association of BMI-adjusted CC with metabolic phenotypes focusing on non-obese adults in the US. Our findings shed a new light on the important role of BMI-adjusted CC in healthcare practices. Notably, a non-linear association was observed in the associations between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO. A plateau effect seemed to exist, which needs further large-scale population-based study with wider distribution to clarify.

Although the underlying mechanism remains unclear, skeletal muscle seems to play a vital role in the association between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO. Skeletal muscle has been confirmed that it could influence insulin secretion, which participates in the pathophysiology of blood glucose metabolism [39]. The loss of insulin-like growth factor 1 sensitization in skeletal muscle could lead to IR [40]. Additionally, myokines including irisin and myonectin secreted by skeletal mass can regulate metabolism [41]. Abnormal secretion of these myokines was involved in the abnormal blood glucose, lipid and pressure metabolism [42, 43].

CC is a substitute for lower-body adipose and a credible screening measure. Increased levels of free fatty acids (FFA) can lead to lipid accumulation [44]. Recently, amounting evidence demonstrated lower-body subcutaneous fat played a pivotal role in accumulation of FFA in adipose tissue [45, 46]. The accumulation of FFA is thought to occur in skeletal muscle by altering the subcellular localization of diacylglycerols and ceramides, which could accelerate peripheral inflammation and damage [47]. Moreover, previous studies reported FFA was also related to oxidative stress, which is one of the independent risk factors of metabolic disease [48, 49]. Higher BMI-adjusted CC in non-obese participants may reflect higher BMI-adjusted SMM and lower accumulation of fat. Therefore, the lower risk of peripheral inflammation, damage and oxidative stress led by lower FFA accumulation might partly explain potential protective effect of BMI-adjusted CC on MUNO.

Almost 90% twitch muscle in the soleus muscle of the triceps surae is slow twitch muscle fiber [50]. A low proportion of slow twitch muscle fibres can cause high vascular resistance, and further develop hypertension [51, 52]. Compared with fast twitch muscle fibers, the capillary density of slow twitch muscle is higher [53]. Capillary density was positively associated with muscular blood flow and inversely associated with resting heart rate and blood pressure [54, 55]. We can speculate participants with lower BMI-adjusted CC have lower proportion of slow twitch muscle fibres, lower capillary density in soleus muscle and higher resting heart rate [17, 56], which may lead to high risk of CVDs [57]. Additionally, venous return, as an important part of blood circulation, was dependent on muscle pumping closely related to soleus muscle and CC [58, 59]. Therefore, compared with higher BMI-adjusted CC, lower BMI-adjusted CC may be accompanied by lower pumping and venous return, which were also independent risk factors of CVDs and can further explain the observed associations partly.

Overall, our analysis reveals a clear functional effect of BMI-adjusted CC on MUNO in a nationally represented population for the first time. Muscle mass assessed by the golden standard-DXA was additionally include in analysis to further confirm the potential association. Besides, contrary to previous studies, we controlled for potential cofounders as much as possible, such as sociodemographic variables, physical activity and total energy intake. However, this study also has several limitations. Firstly, as a cross-sectional study, the direction of causation between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO can not be unraveled. Secondly, there may be age-specific effects exist between BMI-adjusted CC and MUNO. Considering the restricted statistical power, further stratified analysis across strata of age was not performed. Thirdly, due to a lack of muscle function measurement data like muscle strength, only muscle mass was used in the present study. Since muscle strength can reflect muscle function and has been shown to have a strong correlation with CVDs and mortality [60], further researches underlying the association between BMI-adjusted CC, muscle strength and MUNO are necessary. Above all, further prospective cohort studies based on a large-scale population are needed to confirm and clarify the causal associations.

Conclusions

Our study showed BMI-adjusted CC was inversely associated with MUNO. These findings may signify significant public health implications with the proportion of MUNO increasing in most countries. It is essential to focus on the community-dwelling population, even if BMI is within the normal range. Those with lower BMI-adjusted CC should be identified and informed for well-timed MUNO risk prevention.
