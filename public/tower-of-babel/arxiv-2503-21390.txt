Vertex 𝑭 \boldsymbol{F} -Algebras and Their Associated Lie Algebra
Markus UPMEIER

Abstract
Vertex F F -algebras are a deformation of the concept of an ordinary vertex algebra in which the additive formal group law is replaced by an arbitrary formal group law F F . The main theorem of this paper constructs a Lie algebra from a vertex F F -algebra – for the additive formal group law, this extends Borcherds’ well-known construction for ordinary vertex algebras. Our construction involves the new concept of an F F -residue and some other new algebraic concepts, which are deformations of familiar concepts for the special case of an additive formal group law.

1
Introduction and results

Vertex algebras, popularized in mathematics by Borcherds
[
1
]
, provide an algebraic framework for understanding two-dimensional conformal field theories and play a fundamental role in representation theory. A feature of vertex algebras is that their axioms implicitly rely on the additive formal group law
F(z,w)=z+w
. Vertex
F
-algebras, originally introduced by Li
[
12
]
, generalize vertex algebras by replacing the additive formal group by an arbitrary formal group law
F(z,w)
.

A fundamental theorem of Borcherds
[
1
]
, which has important applications to representation theory, states that every vertex algebra determines a Lie algebra. The main result of this paper extends this result and shows that every vertex
F
-algebra also determines a Lie algebra. However, the usual construction of the Lie bracket in the vertex algebra setting, via the residue of the state-to-field correspondence, does not directly apply to vertex
F
-algebras. To overcome this difficulty, we introduce a deformation of the concept of a residue, the
F
-residue
\Res_{z=0}^{F}
. Establishing the Lie algebra structure in the generalized setting requires significantly more technical work than in the classical case.

The following result is our main theorem, and it is proven in Section
4.3
.

Theorem
.

Let
(V,\mathbbm{1},\mathcal{S},Y)
be a vertex
F
-algebra
(
see Definition
4.1
)
. Then

[a,b]=\Res_{z=0}^{F}Y(a,z)b{\rm d}z

defines a Lie bracket on the quotient
V/\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathcal{S}^{(n)}(V)
.

Besides the potential applications to representation theory, there is further motivation for generalizing the concept of a vertex algebra stemming from enumerative geometry: recently, Joyce
[
8
,
9
]
has shown that vertex algebras play a central role in enumerative geometry, where the Lie algebra associated to the ordinary homology
H_{*}(\mathcal{M})
of a moduli stack
\mathcal{M}
(for example, the stack of perfect complexes over a projective variety). The Lie bracket is used to formulate wall-crossing formulas of enumerative invariants. The study of enumerative invariants in generalized homology
E_{*}(\mathcal{M})
is becoming increasingly popular (most notably, the theory of
K
-theoretic enumerative invariants). For complex oriented generalized homology theories
E_{*}
with formal group law
F
, similar constructions show that
E_{*}(\mathcal{M})
is naturally a vertex
F
-algebra. Indeed, in
[
4
]
the author and Gross proved that the generalized homology of an
H
-space (plus some extra data) naturally has a vertex
F
-algebra structure. This extends Joyce’s construction to generalized homology. While a comprehensive theory of wall-crossing has not yet been developed for generalized enumerative invariants (for progress in the case of
K
-theory, see Liu
[
13
]
), it may well be expected that the wall-crossing formula will use the Lie bracket constructed in this paper.

The proof of our main theorem, as well as a better understanding of vertex
F
-algebras, requires the development of various deformations of familiar concepts (
F
-binomial coefficients,
F
-delta distributions,
F
-residues, and
F
-hyperderivatives), developed in Section
3
. These constructions, which appear to be new to the literature, provide the tools for generalizing results for ordinary vertex algebras to vertex
F
-algebras. Section
2
reviews some background of formal groups and establishes certain properties we will use later. In Section
4
, we define vertex
F
-algebras (our definition is equivalent to that of Li
[
12
]
), prove some meromorphicity properties, and prove our main theorem.

We use the following notation.

•

R
a commutative ring with unit,

•

R\big[z^{\pm 1}\big]
the ring of Laurent polynomials,

•

R\llbracket z\rrbracket
the ring of formal power series
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}z^{i}
,

•

R\big\llbracket z^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
the abelian group of bilateral Laurent series
\sum_{i=-\infty}^{+\infty}a_{i}z^{i}
(note that
R\big\llbracket z^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
is
not
a ring, but only has a partially defined product),

•

R(\!(z)\!)
the ring of meromorphic series having a pole at
0
.

2
Background on formal groups

All rings
R
are assumed to be commutative and unital. A
formal group law
over
R
is a formal power series
F(z,w)\in R\llbracket z,w\rrbracket
satisfying

\displaystyle F(z,w)=F(w,z)=z+w+O(zw),\qquad F(z,F(w,v))=F(F(z,w),v).

(2.1)

There is a unique
inverse

\iota(z)=-z+O\bigl(z^{2}\bigr)\in R\llbracket z\rrbracket
such that
F(z,\iota z)=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Our definition includes a choice of coordinate
z
for the formal group.

Example 2.2
.

Over
R=\mathbb{Z}[s]
, we have the formal group law

F_{s}(z,w)=z+w+s\cdot zw.

(2.2)

We view
F_{s}
as a family of group laws over the affine line. At
s=0
, it specializes to the
additive group law

F_{a}
and at
s=1
to the
multiplicative group law

F_{m}
. All polynomial formal group laws are of the form (
2.2
), and other examples must involve infinite series.

Example 2.3
.

Over the ring of modular forms
R=\mathbb{Z}\big[\frac{1}{2},\epsilon,\delta\big]
, we have the
elliptic formal group law

F_{ell}(z,w)=\frac{z\sqrt{S(w)}+w\sqrt{S(z)}}{1-\epsilon z^{2}w^{2}},\qquad S(z)=1-2\delta z^{2}+\epsilon z^{4}.

For
\epsilon=\delta^{2}
it specializes to the group law

\frac{z+w}{1+\delta zw}

(for
\delta=1
equivalent to
L
-genus) and for
\epsilon=0
it specializes to

z\sqrt{1-2\delta w^{2}}+w\sqrt{1-2\delta z^{2}}

(for
\delta=-1/2
equivalent to
\hat{A}
-genus).

Example 2.4
.

Let
p
be a prime number and fix a positive power
q=p^{h}
. Set
\phi_{p}(z)=z+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}p^{-n}z^{q^{n}}
. In
[
5
]
, it is shown that

F_{p}(z,w)=\phi_{p}^{-1}(\phi_{p}(z)+\phi_{p}(w))

has integer coefficients and thus defines a formal group law over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Using the notation
F^{m,n}=\frac{\partial^{m+n}}{\partial z^{m}\partial w^{n}}F
for derivatives, the axioms imply

\displaystyle F^{m,n}(z,w)=F^{n,m}(w,z),\qquad F^{m,0}(z,0)=F^{0,m}(0,z)=\delta_{m,1}.

The associativity law is the most interesting axiom. By differentiating it, one obtains many complicated identities, for example

F^{0,1}(z,w)F^{1,0}(0,w)=F^{1,0}(z,w)F^{0,1}(z,0).

(2.3)

Identities of this kind will be important later, so we now introduce a systematic method for proving these based on Lazard’s theorem. We first discuss the invariant
1
-form and the logarithm of a formal group law.

The
invariant differential
of a formal group law
F(z,w)
is the unique
1
-form

\theta_{F}=p_{F}(z){\rm d}z,\qquad p_{F}(z)\in R\llbracket z\rrbracket,

(2.4)

satisfying

p_{F}(0)=1
,
F^{*}(\theta_{F})=\pi_{1}^{*}(\theta_{F})+\pi_{2}^{*}(\theta_{F})
.
Equivalently,

p_{F}(F(z,w))\bigl(F^{1,0}(z,w){\rm d}z+F^{0,1}(z,w){\rm d}w\bigr)=p_{F}(z){\rm d}z+p_{F}(w){\rm d}w.

(2.5)

Using this characterization of
\theta_{F}
one verifies

-\iota^{*}(\theta_{F})=\theta_{F}.

(2.6)

One checks
p_{F}(z)=F^{0,1}(z,0)^{-1}
, which is well-defined as
F^{0,1}(z,0)=1+\cdots
.

Suppose now that
\mathbb{Q}\subset R
. Then every formal group law has a unique (formal)
logarithm

\phi\in R\llbracket z\rrbracket
satisfying

\displaystyle\phi(F(z,w))=\phi(z)+\phi(w),\qquad\phi(0)=0,\qquad\phi^{\prime}(0)=1.

(2.7)

Indeed, taking
\partial/\partial w
of (
2.7
) and setting
w=0
we find that
\phi^{\prime}(z){\rm d}z=\theta_{F}
is the invariant
1
-form which, since
\mathbb{Q}\subset R
, has a primitive
\theta_{F}=d\phi
. For this reason, we write
p_{F}(z)=\phi^{\prime}(z)
with the caveat that
\phi(z)
is only defined if
\mathbb{Q}\subset R
.
The composition inverse
\phi^{-1}(x)\in R\llbracket x\rrbracket
is called the
exponential
.

Example 2.5
.

For
s\neq 0
, the logarithm of
F_{s}
is
s^{-1}\log(1+sz)
. For
F_{ell}
, the logarithm is the functional inverse of the indefinite elliptic integral
\int S(z)^{-1/2}{\rm d}z
.

Example 2.6
.

The logarithm in Example
2.4
is the series
\phi(z)
defined there. For
p=q
, we have

p_{F}(z)^{-1}=F^{0,1}(z,0)=\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(z)}=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+z^{p^{k}}}.

Hence the coefficient of
z^{d}
in
p_{F}(z)^{-1}
is the sum of
(-1)^{k}
over all partitions
p^{n_{1}}+\dots+p^{n_{k}}=d
into positive powers of the prime
p
.

Using variables as coefficients defines the
universal formal group law

F_{L}=\sum_{n,m\geqslant 0}a_{n,m}z^{n}w^{m}

over the
Lazard ring

L
, the quotient of the polynomials
\mathbb{Z}[a_{n,m}]
modulo all the relations contained in (
2.1
). By construction, every formal group law
F
is obtained from
F_{L}
by reduction of coefficients
F=u_{*}(F_{L})
along a unique ring homomorphism
u\colon L\to R
.

Theorem 2.7

(Lazard
[
10
]
)
.

L\cong\mathbb{Z}[p_{1},p_{2},\dots]
is a polynomial ring over the integers. In particular,
L
is torsion-free.

This implies that it suffices to prove statements about formal group laws over the rationals, where one may restrict to laws of the form

F(z,w)=\phi^{-1}(\phi(z)+\phi(w)).

(2.8)

Using this method, (
2.3
) has a simple chain rule proof. Moreover, the following proposition would be very difficult to prove without this new method.

Proposition 2.8
.

The series
G(z,w)\in R\llbracket z,w\rrbracket
defined by

F(z,\iota w)=G(z,w)\cdot(z-w)

(2.9)

is a unit and converges on the diagonal to

G(z,z)=\phi^{\prime}(z).

(2.10)

Proof.

The coefficients of
G(z,w)
in (
2.9
) satisfy a recursion which is easily solved inductively. The main point is to prove (
2.10
) for the group law (
2.8
). Set

\psi(x)=\frac{\phi^{-1}(x)}{x}=1+O(x).

Then

G(z,w)=\frac{\phi^{-1}(\phi(z)-\phi(w))}{z-w}=\psi(\phi(z)-\phi(w))\cdot\frac{\phi(z)-\phi(w)}{z-w}.

Substituting
w=z
, the first factor is
\psi(0)=1
and the second is
\phi^{\prime}(z)
.
∎

Formal calculus and
\boldsymbol{F}
-residues

3.1
Bilateral and Laurent series

Let
R\big\llbracket z_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,z_{n}^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
be the space of
bilateral Laurent series

f=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}}z_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{i_{n}}

(3.1)

in variables
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
. This is an abelian group under addition, but the product is only partially defined. The product of (
3.1
) with

g=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}z_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{j_{n}}

is said to
converge
if each of the coefficients in

fg=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}\Biggl(\sum_{i_{1}+j_{1}=k_{1},\dots,i_{n}+j_{n}=k_{n}}a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}}b_{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}\Biggr)z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{k_{n}}

reduces to a sum with only finitely many non-zero terms. By
[
11
, p. 24]
, this product is associative if all products
fg
,
gh
,
(fg)h
,
f(gh)
converge and the triple product converges.

Similarly, we say that
f(z,w)=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i,j}w^{i}z^{j}\in R\llbracket z,w\rrbracket

converges on the diagonal
if each of the coefficients in

f(z,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Biggl(\sum_{i+j=n}a_{i,j}\Biggr)z^{n}

reduces to a sum with only finitely many non-zero terms.

We say that (
3.1
) is a
formal Laurent series
if
a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}}=0
for all but finitely many negative indices. The subspace of formal Laurent series

R(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)

is a ring since all products converge. For
f\in R(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
, we say also that
f
is
meromorphic
in the variables
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
. We have the subring

R\llbracket\underline{z}_{1},\dots,\underline{z}_{n}\rrbracket

of
formal power series
which are also said to be
holomorphic
in
\underline{z}_{1},\dots,\underline{z}_{n}
. It is useful to underline holomorphic variables in some contexts below.

Remark 3.1
.

This generalizes to series with coefficients in an
R
-module
M
. Then
R(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
acts on
M(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
. We leave this extension to the reader.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f=\sum_{n\geqslant N}a_{n}z^{n}\in R(\!(z)\!)
with lowest coefficient
a_{N}\neq 0
. Then
f
is invertible in
R(\!(z)\!)
if and only if
a_{N}
is invertible in
R
, with inverse in
z^{N}R\llbracket z\rrbracket
. In particular, all integer powers
f^{n}\in R(\!(z)\!)
are defined in this case.

Proof.

Suppose that
a_{N}
is invertible and factor
f=a_{N}z^{N}(1+zg)
for
g\in R\llbracket z\rrbracket
. Formally applying Newton’s binomial theorem, we define

f^{n}=a_{N}^{n}z^{nN}(1+zg)^{n}=a_{N}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{n}{k}z^{nN+k}g^{k}.

(3.2)

Clearly,
f^{n}\cdot f^{m}=f^{n+m}
by the binomial identity
\sum_{j=k+\ell}\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{\ell}=\binom{n+m}{j}
and
f^{n}\in z^{nN}R\llbracket z\rrbracket
. Putting
n=1
,
m=-1
, shows that
f(z)
is invertible.
∎

3.2
Expansions

Expansion maps play a central role in modern formulations of vertex algebras, so we briefly review them here.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
be tuples of formal variables, where singleton brackets will be dropped from the notation. We view the iterate Laurent series ring as a subset of the space of bilateral series:

R(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\!)\cdots(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)\subset R\big\llbracket x_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,x_{m}^{\pm 1},\dots,z_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,z_{n}^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket.

Let
j_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}
be the natural inclusion of
R(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\dots,z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
, the un-iterated Laurent ring, into the ring
R(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\!)\cdots(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
. Moreover, let
S_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}\subset R(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\dots,z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
be the multiplicative set consisting of those Laurent series whose image under
j_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}
is invertible in the iterated Laurent ring.

The
expansion map

i_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}
is the localization of the map
j_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}
at
{S_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}}
shown in the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hskip-85.35826ptR(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\dots,z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\!)\cdots(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}^{-1}R(\!(x_{1},\dots,x_{m},\dots,z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}}

Example 3.4
.

Let
F(z,w)
be a formal group law. The images of the series
F(z,w)
,
F(z,\iota w)
,
F(\iota z,w)
,
\iota F(z,w)
in
R(\!(z)\!)(\!(w)\!)
are invertible because their lowest coefficients are the units
z,\iota(z)
in
R(\!(z)\!)
. Therefore,
F(z,w)
,
F(z,\iota w)
,
F(\iota z,w)
,
\iota F(z,w)
are in
S_{z,w}
. Hence there are well-defined integer powers

i_{z,w}F(z,w)^{n},\qquad i_{z,w}F(\iota z,w)^{n},\qquad i_{z,w}F(z,\iota w)^{n},\qquad i_{z,w}(\iota F(z,w))^{n},\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z},

which are elements of
R(\!(z)\!)(\!(w)\!)\subset R\big\llbracket z^{\pm 1},w^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
. These are computed by first viewing
F(z,w)
,
F(z,\iota w)
,
F(\iota z,w)
,
\iota F(z,w)
as elements of the iterated ring
R(\!(z)\!)(\!(w)\!)
and then forming the
n
-th power there.

Remark 3.5
.

For ordinary vertex algebras, the expansion maps
R(\!(z,w)\!)[z-w]^{-1}\to R(\!(z)\!)(\!(w)\!)
are defined on the localization by a single element
z-w
. For vertex
F
-algebras, it becomes necessary to localize
F(z,w)
,
F(z,\iota w)
,
F(\iota z,w)
,
\iota F(z,w)
. In the case of several variables, even more complicated expressions in
F
must be localized (for example, in the proof of Proposition
3.19
). Since these expressions are difficult to list systematically, we define the expansion maps on the universal localization, for example,
i_{z,w}\colon S_{z,w}^{-1}R(\!(z,w)\!)\to R(\!(z)\!)(\!(w)\!)
.

We can also include holomorphic variables which we indicate by an underline. Note that expansion maps preserve products,

i_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}(fg)=i_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}(f)\cdot i_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}),\dots,(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})}(g).

Remark 3.6
.

The grouping of variables is important, because the algebra in
R(\!(z_{1})\!)(\!(z_{2})\!)
and
{R(\!(z_{2})\!)(\!(z_{1})\!)}
is different. For example, the elements
i_{z_{1},z_{2}}(z_{1}\pm z_{2})^{n}
and
i_{z_{2},z_{1}}(z_{1}\pm z_{2})^{n}
are different in
R\big\llbracket z_{1}^{\pm 1},z_{2}^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
when
n<0
. Expansion maps specify the ring in which the algebraic operations are performed. For holomorphic variables, the grouping is unimportant since

R\llbracket\underline{z}_{1}\rrbracket\llbracket\underline{z}_{2}\rrbracket=R\llbracket\underline{z}_{2}\rrbracket\llbracket\underline{z}_{1}\rrbracket=R\llbracket\underline{z}_{1},\underline{z}_{2}\rrbracket.

Hence
i_{\underline{z}_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}=i_{\underline{z}_{2},\underline{z}_{1}}
.

Note that
R(\!(z_{1},z_{2})\!)=R(\!(z_{1})\!)(\!(z_{2})\!)\cap R(\!(z_{2})\!)(\!(z_{1})\!)
is a proper intersection since, for example,
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z_{1}^{-n}z_{2}^{n}\in R(\!(z_{1})\!)(\!(z_{2})\!)\setminus R(\!(z_{1},z_{2})\!)
. On the intersection, we have

i_{z_{1},z_{2}}f=i_{z_{2},z_{1}}f,\qquad\forall f\in R(\!(z_{1},z_{2})\!).

(3.3)

In particular,
R\big\llbracket z_{1}^{\pm 1},z_{2}^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
is an
R(\!(z_{1},z_{2})\!)
-module and expansions are linear,

i_{z_{1},z_{2}}(f\cdot g)=f\cdot i_{z_{1},z_{2}}(g),\qquad\forall f\in R(\!(z_{1},z_{2})\!),\ g\in R\big\llbracket z_{1}^{\pm 1},z_{2}^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket.

(3.4)

Remark 3.7
.

Traditionally, the expansion maps are combined with (
3.2
) and
i_{z_{1},z_{2}}(z_{1}\pm z_{2})^{n}
is defined as an explicit series. This places the emphasis on properties of binomial coefficients, which are actually just laws of exponentiation.

3.3
Filtrations

To determine which substitutions are well-defined, we will introduce topologies on iterate Laurent rings. These will all be induced by filtrations.

Definition 3.8
.

A
filtration
on a ring
A
is a decreasing sequence of subgroups

\displaystyle\cdots\supset A_{(n)}\supset A_{(n+1)}\supset\cdots,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(3.5)

such that
1\in A_{(0)}
and
A_{(n)}\cdot A_{(m)}\subset A_{(n+m)}
. A filtration is said to be
exhaustive
if
\bigcup A_{(n)}=A
and
separated
if
\bigcap A_{(n)}=\{0\}
. We will always assume that filtrations are separated.

There is a unique Hausdorff topology on a filtered ring for which addition and multiplication are continuous and for which (
3.5
) is a fundamental system of neighborhoods of zero. This topology is induced by the uniform structure given by the pseudometric

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}0&{\iff}x-y\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{(n)},\\
2^{-n}&{\iff}x-y\in A_{(n)}\setminus A_{(n+1)},\\
+\infty&{\iff}x-y\notin\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}A_{(n)}.\end{cases}

A filtered ring is said to be
complete
if it is complete as a uniform space. A
morphism
of filtered rings is a ring homomorphism
f\colon A\to B
such that
f(A_{(n)})\subset B_{(n)}
for all
n
. Filtered maps are uniformly continuous.

Example 3.9
.

Let
\mathfrak{a}\triangleleft A
be an ideal. The
\mathfrak{a}
-adic filtration
on
A
is defined by
A_{(n)}=\mathfrak{a}^{n}
for
{n>0}
and
A_{(n)}=A
for
n\leqslant 0
. If
\mathfrak{a}=(0)
, this is the
trivial filtration
on
A
, which induces the discrete topology.

For example,
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
carries the
(z)
-adic filtration where
A\llbracket z\rrbracket_{(n)}=z^{n}A\llbracket z\rrbracket
for
n\geqslant 0
. We also call the filtration on
A(\!(z)\!)
defined by
A(\!(z)\!)_{(n)}=z^{n}A\llbracket z\rrbracket
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
the
(z)
-adic filtration
, although
(z)
is not an ideal in
A(\!(z)\!)
.

3.4
Substitutions

The following is the main technical result for defining substitutions. Parts (a)–(c) extend
[
2
, Chapter III, Section 2.6]
to the case where
A_{(0)}\neq A
.

Proposition 3.10
.

(a)

Let
A
be a filtered ring. Then

A(\!(z)\!)_{(n)}=\Bigl\{\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i}z^{i}\,\Big|\,a_{i}\in A_{(n-i)}\Bigr\},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(3.6)

defines a filtration on the Laurent ring
A(\!(z)\!)
. If
A
is separated, then so is
A(\!(z)\!)
and the power series ring
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
is a closed subspace with filtration

A\llbracket z\rrbracket_{(n)}=A(\!(z)\!)_{(n)}\cap A\llbracket z\rrbracket.

(3.7)

(2)

If
A
is complete, then so are
A(\!(z)\!)
and
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
.

(3)

If
A_{(0)}=A
, then the polynomials
A[z]
are dense in
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
and the Laurent polynomials
A\big[z^{\pm 1}\big]
are dense in
A(\!(z)\!)
.

(4)

(
Universal properties
)
For each filtered morphism
f\colon A\to B
into a complete ring
B
and each invertible element
b\in B_{(1)}
, there is a unique filtered morphism
\bar{f}\colon A(\!(z)\!)\to B
with
{\bar{f}|_{A}=f}
and
\bar{f}(z)=b
. Here
A(\!(z)\!)
is given the filtration generated by (
3.6
) and the
(z)
-adic filtration.
Pictorially,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(\!(z)\!)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces z\mapsto b}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

(3.8)

Similarly for multivariable Laurent rings
A(\!(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\!)
and also for power series rings, where we drop the invertibility condition.

Proof.

(a) It is easy to check that (
3.6
) defines a filtration of
A(\!(z)\!)
, which is separated if the original filtration is so. Moreover,
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
is a closed subspace.

(b) To prove that
A(\!(z)\!)
is complete, let
f_{n}(z)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i,n}z^{i}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a Cauchy sequence. For each
N
, there is
n_{0}
such that for all
n,m\geqslant n_{0}
and
i
we have

\forall n,m\geqslant n_{0}\colon\ a_{i,n}-a_{i,m}\in A_{(N-i)}.

(3.9)

In particular, for each fixed
i
the sequence
(a_{i,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy in
A
, hence converges to some
{a_{i}\in A}
. We may then take
m\to\infty
in (
3.9
) and get

\forall n\geqslant n_{0}\colon\ a_{i,n}-a_{i}\in A_{(N-i)}.

This proves that
f_{n}(z)\to\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{i}z^{i}
as
n\to\infty
.

(c) If
A_{(0)}=A
, then we have
az^{i}\in A\llbracket z\rrbracket_{(i)}
for any
a\in A
. Hence the truncation
\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}a_{i}z^{i}\in A[z]
is in the
A_{(N)}
-neighborhood of
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}z^{i}
, showing that the polynomials are dense. Similarly for Laurent polynomials.

(d) We prove the universal property for
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
. By the universal property of polynomial rings,
(f,b)
uniquely induce a ring homomorphism

A[z]\longrightarrow B,\qquad z\longmapsto b.

(3.10)

First, put the trivial filtration on
A
. Then (
3.10
) is filtered for the
(z)
-adic topology since
{b\in B_{(1)}}
. Since
B
is complete, we may extend to a filtered map
\bar{f}
on the completion
A\llbracket z\rrbracket
. Observe that
\bar{f}
is also automatically filtered for (
3.6
). Hence
\bar{f}
is filtered for the generated filtration. Conversely, if
\bar{f}
is filtered for the generated filtration, then it is also filtered for the
(z)
-adic filtration for which
A[z]
is dense. This proves uniqueness. Similarly for Laurent series.∎

Remark 3.11
.

If
A_{(0)}\neq A
, the polynomials
A[x]
are not dense in
A\llbracket x\rrbracket
and the filtration in the universal property differs from (
3.7
).

Applying (
3.6
) inductively starting with the trivial filtration on
R
, we obtain the
complete filtration
on iterate Laurent rings
R(\!(x_{1})\!)\cdots(\!(x_{m})\!)
, where to simplify notation we work in the single variable case. For
h(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
, to be in positive complete filtration means that each monomial occurring in

has strictly positive total degree. We also have the
generated filtration
on
R(\!(x_{1})\!)\cdots(\!(x_{m})\!)
, obtained by using the generated filtration inductively at each step.

Corollary 3.12
.

Let
f_{i}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in R(\!(y_{1})\!)\cdots(\!(y_{n})\!)

For example, there are mutually inverse bijections

Moreover, the universal property (

3.5
\boldsymbol{F}
-binomial coefficients

Let
F(z,w)
be a formal group law. As observed in Example
3.4
, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
there are well-defined integer powers

i_{z,w}F(z,w)^{n},\qquad i_{z,w}F(\iota z,w)^{n},\qquad i_{z,w}F(z,\iota w)^{n},\qquad i_{z,w}(\iota F(z,w))^{n}.

Definition 3.14
.

The coefficients of the expansion
i_{z,w}F(z,w)^{n}
are called the
F-binomial coefficients
, so by definition

i_{z,w}F(z,w)^{n}=\sum_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}\binom{n}{i,j}_{\!\!F}z^{i}w^{j},\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(3.14)

Proposition 3.15
.

The
F
-binomial coefficients satisfy the following identities:

\displaystyle\binom{n}{i,j}_{\!\!F}=0\qquad\text{if}\ j<0\ \text{or}\ i+j<n,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{i,0}_{\!\!F}=\delta_{i}^{m},\qquad\binom{n}{i,j}_{\!\!F}=\binom{n}{j,i}_{\!\!F}\qquad\text{if}\ n\geqslant 0,

\displaystyle\binom{m+n}{r,s}_{\!\!F}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+k=r\\
j+\ell=s\end{subarray}}\binom{m}{i,j}_{\!\!F}\binom{n}{k,\ell}_{\!\!F}.

Example 3.16
.

For the one-parameter group law (
2.2
), we have

\binom{n}{i,j}_{\!\!F_{s}}=\binom{n}{j}\binom{j}{i+j-n}s^{i+j-n}.

In particular, for the additive group law (
s=0
), we recover the ordinary binomial coefficients
\binom{n}{i,j}_{\!\!F_{a}}=\binom{n}{j}
if
i+j=n
and
\binom{n}{i,j}_{\!\!F_{a}}=0
else. For the multiplicative group law, put
s=1
.

3.6
\boldsymbol{F}
-delta distributions

Definition 3.17
.

The group law
F
-delta distribution
is

z^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)=i_{z,\underline{w}}F(z,\iota w)^{-1}-i_{w,\underline{z}}F(z,\iota w)^{-1}.

Example 3.18
.

For the additive group law, we get the classical distribution

z^{-1}\delta_{F_{a}}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)=i_{z,\underline{w}}(z-w)^{-1}-i_{w,\underline{z}}(z-w)^{-1}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}w^{n}z^{-n-1}.

(3.15)

More generally, for the one-parameter formal group law (
2.2
), we have

\delta_{F_{s}}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)=(1+sw)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}w^{n}z^{-n}.

Proposition 3.19
.

(a)

The F-delta distribution is supported on the diagonal,

\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)f(z)=\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)f(w),\qquad\forall f\in R(\!(z)\!).

(3.16)

More generally, if
f(z,w)\in R(\!(z,w)\!)
converges on the diagonal,

\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)f(z,w)=\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)f(w,w).

(3.17)

(2)

We have the
F
-Jacobi identity
,

\displaystyle i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}z_{0}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{z_{0}}\right)-i_{z_{2},\underline{z}_{1}}z_{0}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{z_{0}}\right)=i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{2}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{0})}{z_{2}}\right).

(3)

We have

i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{2}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{0})}{z_{2}}\right)=i_{z_{2},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{1}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{0},z_{2})}{z_{1}}\right).

Proof.

(a) The identities (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) are well-known for the classical delta distribution. By (
2.9
) and (
3.4
), the delta distributions are related by

\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)=G(z,w)^{-1}\delta_{F_{a}}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right).

(3.18)

Therefore, (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) follow directly from the classical case.

(b) Formally, the first term of the Jacobi identity is the substitution

i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}z_{0}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{z_{0}}\right)=z_{0}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z_{0}}\right)\Big|_{w^{\pm 1}\to i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}F(z_{1},\iota\underline{z}_{2})^{\pm 1}}.

Expanding the definition of
\delta_{F}
and using functoriality of substitution, this gives

\displaystyle i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}z_{0}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{z_{0}}\right)={}

\displaystyle i_{z_{0},\underline{w}}F(z_{0},\iota\underline{w})^{-1}\big|_{\underline{w}\to i_{\underline{z}_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}F(\underline{z}_{1},\iota\underline{z}_{2})}

\displaystyle-i_{w,\underline{z}_{0}}F(\underline{z}_{0},\iota w)^{-1}\big|_{w^{\pm 1}\to i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{2}}F(z_{1},\iota\underline{z}_{2})^{\pm 1}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle i_{z_{0},(\underline{z}_{1},\underline{z}_{2})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}-i_{z_{1},(\underline{z}_{2},\underline{z}_{0})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}.

In the same way,

\displaystyle i_{z_{2},\underline{z}_{1}}z_{0}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{z_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=i_{z_{0},(\underline{z}_{2},\underline{z}_{1})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}-i_{z_{2},(\underline{z}_{1},\underline{z}_{0})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1},

\displaystyle i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{2}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{0})}{z_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=i_{z_{2},(\underline{z}_{1},\underline{z}_{0})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}-i_{z_{1},(\underline{z}_{0},\underline{z}_{2})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}.

Observe that by (
3.3
) each term appears twice in the last three equations. Hence the terms cancel in pairs, which proves the
F
-Jacobi identity.

(c) Notice that

i_{z_{2},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{1}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{0},z_{2})}{z_{1}}\right)=i_{z_{1},(\underline{z}_{0},\underline{z}_{2})}F(z_{1},\iota z_{0},\iota z_{2})^{-1}-i_{z_{2},(\underline{z}_{0},\underline{z}_{1})}F(z_{1},\iota z_{0},\iota z_{2})^{-1}

can be multiplied by the holomorphic
\frac{F(\iota z_{0},z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})}
which may be exchanged with the expansion. Hence

\displaystyle i_{z_{2},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{1}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{0},z_{2})}{z_{1}}\right)\cdot\frac{F(\iota z_{0},z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad=i_{z_{1},(\underline{z}_{0},\underline{z}_{2})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}-i_{z_{2},(\underline{z}_{0},\underline{z}_{1})}F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{\eqref{swap-expansion}}{=}-i_{z_{1},\underline{z}_{0}}z_{2}^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{F(z_{1},\iota z_{0})}{z_{2}}\right).

Finally, using (
3.16
) put
z_{1}=F(z_{0},z_{2})
into
\frac{F(\iota z_{0},z_{1},\iota z_{2})}{F(z_{0},\iota z_{1},z_{2})}
on the left to get
1
.
∎

By (
3.17
), we may put
z=w
in (
3.18
) and use (
2.10
) to get

\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right)\phi^{\prime}(z)=\delta_{F_{a}}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right).

(3.19)

Observe here that
\phi^{\prime}(z)=F^{0,1}(z,0)^{-1}
makes sense and (
3.19
) holds over an arbitrary ring
R
. There is a proof of the
F

3.7
\boldsymbol{F}
-residues

Recall that the
residue
of a formal Laurent series
f(z)=\sum a_{n}z^{n}\in R\big\llbracket z^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
is defined as
\Res_{z=0}f(z){\rm d}z=a_{-1}
. We have the identities (see
[
14
, Chapter 13]
)

\displaystyle\Res_{z=0}f^{(n)}(z){\rm d}z=0,\forall n\geqslant 1,\qquad\Res_{z=0}f^{\prime}(z)g(z){\rm d}z=-\Res_{z=0}f(z)g^{\prime}(z){\rm d}z,

\displaystyle\Res_{w=0}f(h(w))h^{\prime}(w){\rm d}w=\Res_{z=0}f(z){\rm d}z.

(3.20)

Here
f,g\in R\big\llbracket z^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
are formal Laurent series and
h
is a (holomorphic) formal power series with
{h(0)=0}
and
h^{\prime}(0)\in R^{\times}
.

This terminology can be extended to formal group laws
F(z,w)
as follows. Recall the invariant
1
-form
\theta_{F}
from (
2.4
). Then the
F-residue
is defined as

\Res_{z=0}^{F}f(z){\rm d}z=\Res_{z=0}f(z)\theta_{F}
.
Assuming the existence of a logarithm,

\displaystyle\Res_{z=0}^{F}f(z){\rm d}z=\Res_{x=0}f(x){\rm d}x,\qquad\text{where}\quad f(x)=f\bigl(\phi^{-1}(x)\bigr).

(3.21)

From (
2.6
), we have

\Res_{z=0}^{F}f(\iota(z)){\rm d}z=-\Res_{z=0}^{F}f(z){\rm d}z.

(3.22)

From (
3.19
) combined with (
3.15
) and (
3.16
), we find

\Res_{z=0}^{F}z^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right){\rm d}z=\Res_{w=0}^{F}z^{-1}\delta_{F}\left(\frac{w}{z}\right){\rm d}w=1.

3.8
\boldsymbol{F}
-hyperderivatives

Generalizing Hasse’s number-theoretic notion to formal group laws, define the
F-hyperderivative
of
f\in R(\!(z)\!)
,
\mathcal{S}^{F}_{n}f
,
n\geqslant 0
, by expanding the substitution

i_{z,\underline{w}}f(F(z,w))=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\bigl(\mathcal{S}^{F}_{n}f\bigr)(z)w^{n}.

(3.23)

Hence
\mathcal{S}^{F}_{n}f(0)
picks out the
n
-th coefficient of
f
, and we think of
\mathcal{S}^{F}_{n}f
as a substitute for
(n!)^{-1}f^{(n)}(z)
. One easily shows

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{0}^{F}f\bigr)(z)=f(z),\qquad\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{F}f\bigr)(z)\theta_{F}=f^{\prime}(z){\rm d}z,\qquad\mathcal{S}_{n}^{F}(\lambda f+g)=\lambda\mathcal{S}_{n}(f)+\mathcal{S}_{n}(g),

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}^{F}(f\cdot g)=\sum_{i+j=n}\mathcal{S}_{i}^{F}(f)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{j}^{F}(g),

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{m}^{F}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{F}(f)=\mathcal{S}_{n}^{F}\mathcal{S}_{m}^{F}(f)=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\binom{k}{m,n}_{\!\!F}\mathcal{S}_{k}^{F}(f).

(3.25)

For the additive group law, (
3.25
) implies
\bigl(\mathcal{S}_{1}^{F_{a}}\bigr)^{\circ n}=n!\cdot\mathcal{S}^{F_{a}}_{n}
, which shows the importance of considering higher hyperderivatives in the presence of torsion. Equation (
3.25
) follows from the associativity of
F
.

Comparing (
3.14
) and (
3.23
), we find

\mathcal{S}_{j}^{F}(z^{m})=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\binom{m}{i,j}_{\!\!F}z^{i}.

From this, we see that (
3.25
) implies

\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\binom{m}{i,s}_{\!\!F}\binom{i}{j,r}_{\!\!F}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\binom{m}{i,r}_{\!\!F}\binom{i}{j,s}_{\!\!F}=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\binom{k}{r,s}_{\!\!F}\binom{m}{j,i}_{\!\!F}

for all
r,s,m,j\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 3.20
.

For all
n\geqslant 1
, we have

\displaystyle\Res^{F}_{z=0}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{F}f(z){\rm d}z=0,

(3.26)

\displaystyle\Res^{F}_{z=0}\mathcal{S}_{n}^{F}f(z)\cdot g(z){\rm d}z=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Res_{z=0}^{F}\bigl[\mathcal{S}_{n-j}^{F}f(z)\cdot\mathcal{S}_{j}^{F}g(z)\bigr]{\rm d}z.

(3.27)

Proof.

Summing (
3.26
) times
w^{n}
over
n
, we see by (
3.23
) that it suffices to show

\Res^{F}_{z=0}i_{z,\underline{w}}f(F(z,w)){\rm d}z=\Res^{F}_{z=0}f(z){\rm d}z.

We will apply the substitution rule (
3.20
) to the power series
h(z)=F(z,w)-w
with coefficients in the ring
R\llbracket w\rrbracket
. This corresponds to viewing
w
as a fixed constant. Putting
{\rm d}w=0
in (
2.5
) gives
p_{F}(F(z,w))F^{1,0}(z,w)=p_{F}(z)
. Clearly,
h(0)=0
and
h^{\prime}(0)=F^{1,0}(0,w)=p_{F}(w)^{-1}
is a unit, so the hypotheses for (
3.20
) are satisfied. Applying this and unravelling the definition gives

\displaystyle\Res^{F}_{z=0}i_{z,\underline{w}}f(F(z,w)){\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\Res_{z=0}i_{z,\underline{w}}f(F(z,w))p_{F}(z){\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\Res_{z=0}i_{z,\underline{w}}f(F(z,w))p_{F}(F(z,w))F^{1,0}(z,w){\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\Res_{z=0}i_{z,\underline{w}}f(h(z)+w)p_{F}(h(z)+w)h^{\prime}(z){\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\Res_{v=0}i_{v,\underline{w}}f(v+w)p_{F}(v+w){\rm d}v.

Let

g(z)=f(z)p_{F}(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}z^{n}\in R(\!(z)\!).

It remains to prove that

\Res_{v=0}i_{v,\underline{w}}g(v+w){\rm d}v=\Res_{z=0}g(z){\rm d}z,

which is a straight-forward computation

\Res_{v=0}i_{v,\underline{w}}g(v+w){\rm d}v=\Res_{v=0}\sum_{n,k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{n}\binom{n}{k}v^{n-k}w^{k}{\rm d}v=a_{-1},

where we have used that
\binom{n}{n+1}=0
for
n\neq-1
and
\binom{-1}{0}=1
.

Finally, combining (
3.24
) with (
3.26
) gives (
3.27
).

Theorem 3.21
.

For all
f\in R\big\llbracket x_{0}^{\pm 1},x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}^{\pm 1}\big\rrbracket
such that the substitutions
f(x_{1}-x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})
,
f(x_{0},x_{1},x_{1}-x_{0})
converge in the algebraic sense,

\displaystyle\Res_{x_{1}=0}\Res_{x_{2}=0}i_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1}-x_{2},x_{1},x_{2}){\rm d}x_{2}{\rm d}x_{1}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad-}{}=\Res_{x_{1}=0}\Res_{x_{0}=0}i_{x_{1},x_{0}}f(x_{0},x_{1},x_{1}-x_{0}){\rm d}x_{0}{\rm d}x_{1}.

(3.28)

More generally,

\displaystyle\Res^{F}_{z_{1}=0}\Res^{F}_{z_{2}=0}i_{z_{1},z_{2}}f(F(z_{1},\iota(z_{2})),z_{1},z_{2}){\rm d}z_{2}{\rm d}z_{1}

(3.29)

Proof.

It suffices to check (
3.28
) for
f=x_{0}^{a}x_{1}^{b}x_{2}^{c}
, where

\displaystyle i_{x_{1},x_{2}}f(x_{1}-x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(-1)^{k}\binom{a}{k}x_{1}^{a+b-k}x_{2}^{c+k},

\displaystyle i_{x_{2},x_{1}}f(x_{1}-x_{2},x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(-1)^{a+k}\binom{a}{k}x_{1}^{b+k}x_{2}^{a+c-k},

\displaystyle i_{x_{1},x_{0}}f(x_{0},x_{1},x_{1}-x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geqslant 0}(-1)^{k}\binom{c}{k}x_{0}^{a+k}x_{1}^{b+c-k}.

Taking residues, we see that (

for all

It suffices to prove (

) for the universal formal group law, where we may assume (

). In this case, we can use (

) to reduce (

) to (

) for the function

, where

,

∎

-algebras and their Lie algebras

4.1
Axioms

Definition 4.1
.

A
vertex
F
-algebra
over a formal group law

•

•

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(z)\colon V\longrightarrow V\llbracket z\rrbracket,\qquad\mathcal{S}(z)a=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{S}^{(n)}(a)z^{n},

•

an

\displaystyle V\otimes_{R}V\longrightarrow V(\!(z)\!),\qquad a\otimes b\longmapsto Y(a,z)b,

\displaystyle Y(a,z)b=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(n)}(b)z^{-n-1},\qquad a_{(n)}(b)=0\qquad\text{for}\ n\gg 0.

The following axioms are required:

(1)

Vacuum & creation:

is holomorphic for all

\displaystyle Y(a,z)\mathbbm{1}|_{z=0}=a,\qquad Y(\mathbbm{1},z)=\id_{V}.

(4.3)

(2)

-translation covariance:
for all

, we have

\displaystyle Y(\mathcal{S}(w)(a),z)b=i_{z,w}Y(a,F(z,w))b,\qquad\mathcal{S}(z)\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}.

In (

), we have used the substitution (

,

\mathcal{S}(0)=\id_{V}
.

(3)

Weak

-associativity:
for all
a,b,c\in V
, there exists

with

F(z,w)^{N}Y(Y(a,z)b,w)c=F(z,w)^{N}i_{z,w}Y(a,F(z,w))Y(b,w)c,

(4.5)

using the substitution

from (

(4)

(4.6)

Putting

into (

(4.7)

Remark 4.2

The axioms are slightly redundant. For example, (

Remark 4.3
.

For an ordinary vertex algebra, one usually works with the translation operator

, see
[
11
]
. The shift operator then is

Remark 4.4
.

Huang
[
6
]
has given a geometric interpretation of ordinary vertex algebras in terms of punctured Riemann spheres. For elliptic formal group laws, are vertex
F
-algebras analogously interpreted over an elliptic curve?

The author thanks an anonymous referee for pointing out that, in Huang’s geometric interpretation of ordinary vertex algebras, conformal invariance plays a crucial role. In the genus one setting, an invariant construction requires not only the vertex algebra itself but also its modules and intertwining operators, whose matrix coefficients need not be meromorphic. Full modular invariance is presently established only in the
C_{2}
-cofinite (rational) case due to Huang
[

]
, and more recently in certain

-cofinite settings in work of Creutzig–McRae–Yang
[

. It therefore remains unclear whether a theory of vertex

4.2

The meromorphicity assumption in (

Proposition 4.5
.

For all
a,b\in V
in a vertex

-algebra,

\displaystyle i_{z,w}Y(a,F(z,w))\circ\mathcal{S}(w)b

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(w)\circ Y(a,z)b.

Proof.

Putting

into (

for some

. Since

by (

) and (

∎

Here is the fundamental meromorphicity property of vertex

-algebras.

Proposition 4.6

For each

\displaystyle i_{z,w}f_{a,b,c}(z,w)

\displaystyle=i_{z,w}Y(a,F(z,w))Y(b,w)c.

Using (

4.3
Construction of Lie bracket

Theorem 4.9
.

The formula

[a,b]=\Res_{z=0}^{F}Y(a,z)b{\rm d}z

(4.20)

defines a Lie bracket on the quotient
V/\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathcal{S}^{(n)}(V)
.

Proof.

We first show that (
4.20
) descends to the quotient by proving

\Res^{F}_{z=0}Y\bigl(\mathcal{S}^{(m)}(a),z\bigr)b{\rm d}z=\frac{1}{m!}\left.\frac{d^{m}}{{\rm d}w^{m}}\right|_{w=0}\Res_{z=0}^{F}Y(\mathcal{S}(w)(a),z)b{\rm d}z=0

(4.21)

for
m\geqslant 1
. This will also prove

\Res_{z=0}\biggl(\frac{Y(a,z)\mathcal{S}^{(m)}(b)}{F^{1,0}(0,z)}\biggr){\rm d}z\in\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathcal{S}^{(n)}(V),

since by (
4.4
), (
4.8
) we have

\displaystyle Y(a,z)\circ\mathcal{S}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}(w)\circ Y(a,F(z,\iota(w)))=\mathcal{S}(w)\circ Y(\mathcal{S}(\iota(w))a,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\geqslant 0}\mathcal{S}^{(k)}\circ Y\big(\mathcal{S}^{(\ell)}(a),z\big)w^{k}\iota(w)^{\ell},

which expresses
Y(a,z)\mathcal{S}^{(m)}(b)
for
m\geqslant 1
as a sum of terms with
k\geqslant 1
or with
\ell\geqslant 1
. The terms with
k\geqslant 1
belong to
\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\mathcal{S}^{(n)}(V)
, while the terms with
\ell\geqslant 1
are covered by (
4.21
).

We show (
4.21
) by applying Theorem
3.20
. Since

\displaystyle\Res_{z=0}^{F}Y(\mathcal{S}(w)a,z)b{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\Res_{z=0}^{F}Y(a,F(z,w))b{\rm d}z=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\bigl(\Res_{z=0}^{F}\mathcal{S}_{n}Y(a,z)b\bigr)w^{n}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\Res_{z=0}^{F}Y(a,z)b{\rm d}z,

the series is constant in
w
, as required.

Clearly the bracket is bilinear. We prove the Jacobi identity. Using the notation of Proposition
4.6
, set
f_{a,b,c}(z,v,w)=\frac{p_{a,b,c}(z,w)}{v^{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle i_{v,w}[f_{a,b,c}(z,v,w)|_{z\to F(v,\iota(w))}]

\displaystyle=Y(a,v)Y(b,w)c,

\displaystyle i_{w,v}[f_{a,b,c}(z,v,w)|_{z\to F(v,\iota(w))}]

\displaystyle=Y(b,w)Y(a,v)c.

On the other hand, putting
w=F(v,\iota(z))
into (
4.14
) implies the first step in

i_{v,z}[f_{a,b,c}(z,v,w)|_{w\to F(v,\iota(z))}]=i_{v,z}\frac{p_{b,a,c}(\iota(z),v)}{F(\iota(z),v)^{N}}\overset{\eqref{s3eqn12}}{=}Y(Y(b,\iota z)a,v)c.

Putting these calculations into the formula (
3.29
) for iterated
F
-residues with
(z_{0},z_{1},z_{2})\to(z,v,w)
and using (
3.22
) yields a version of the Jacobi identity

[a,[b,c]]-[b,[a,c]]=-[[b,a],c].

Assuming a trivial center (meaning that
[d,c]=0
for all

implies
d=0
), this Jacobi identity implies also the skew symmetry of the Lie bracket. In general, (

) and (

Remark 4.10
.

For ordinary homology, the moduli space of the quiver given by the Dynkin diagram of a Lie algebra recovers the original Lie algebra. What happens in the case of elliptic homology?

4.4
Example: Heisenberg vertex
\boldsymbol{F}
-algebra

The simplest new examples are generated by a single vertex operator. These are the Heisenberg vertex
F
-algebras. Define a central extension

0\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow H_{F}\longrightarrow R(\!(t)\!)\longrightarrow 0

of the commutative Lie algebra
R(\!(t)\!)
by the cocycle

c(f,g)=\Res^{F}_{z=0}\mathcal{S}_{1}^{F}(f)\cdot g=\Res_{z=0}f^{\prime}g.

Notice (
3.24
) here. Set
b_{n}=t^{n}
. Then

[b_{n},b_{m}]=n\delta_{n,-m},

(4.22)

so we have obtained the ordinary
Heisenberg Lie algebra
except for having replaced
\mathbb{C}
by the ring
R
. Define the space of states

V=R[b_{-1},b_{-2},\dots]
.
Write
\mathbbm{1}\in V
for the constant series
1
, the

. Define the vertex operator

b(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{n}z^{-n-1},

where

b_{n}\colon\ V\longrightarrow V,\qquad b_{n}=\begin{cases}b_{n}\cdot(-)&(n<0),\\
n\dfrac{\partial}{\partial b_{-n}}&(n\geqslant 0).\end{cases}

These operators satisfy the commutation rules (
4.22
). We seek an
F
-translation operator

\mathcal{S}(z)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\mathcal{S}^{(n)}z^{n},\qquad\mathcal{S}^{(n)}\colon\ V\longrightarrow V,

satisfying
\mathcal{S}(z)\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
and

[\mathcal{S}(z),b(w)]=b(F(z,w))
.
These conditions actually determine
\mathcal{S}(z)
completely. Written in components,

\big[\mathcal{S}^{(n)},b_{m}\big]=\text{coefficient of $w^{m}$ in }\bigl(\mathcal{S}^{(n)}_{F}b\bigr)(w).

This can be applied inductively to a state
b_{j_{1}}\cdots b_{j_{k}}
until we reach
\mathcal{S}^{(n)}\mathbbm{1}=0
.
