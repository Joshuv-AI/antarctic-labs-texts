Association between weight-adjusted waist and incidence of low back pain: evidence from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study
Authors: Xueru Shen, Dexin Hu, Yixing Lu, Lixin Zhao, Hua Yuan, Xiaolong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsThe weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), a novel adiposity metric, provides precise assessment of abdominal fat accumulation. This longitudinal study aims to examine the association between WWI and incident low back pain (LBP) risk in middle-aged and older Chinese adults.MethodsData from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) (2011–2020) were utilized. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess WWI’s association with incident LBP, while restricted cubic splines (RCS) modeled their potential nonlinear relationship. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to estimate LBP-free probabilities across WWI strata. Sensitivity analyses included multiple imputation and subgroup analyses.ResultsA total of 3,912 individuals were included in the study. After full adjustment for covariates, each 1-unit increase in WWI within the highest WWI quintile was associated with an 18.6% elevated risk of incident LBP (HR = 1.186, 95% CI: 1.057–1.331). Further analysis revealed a J-shaped association between WWI and incident LBP risk, whereby each 1-unit increment above 10.56 corresponded to a significant 9.6% risk increase (HR = 1.096, 95% CI: 1.026–1.171). A lower probability of remaining LBP-free was observed in the highest WWI quintile group (Q5) relative to other groups (p = 0.00018). Additionally, no statistically significant interaction effects were observed across subgroups (all interaction P > 0.05).Discussions & ConclusionsIn the Q5, WWI demonstrated an independent association with incident LBP. A critical risk threshold of WWI = 10.56 was established, suggesting the potential of WWI as a screening tool.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-026-03351-1.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP), the most prevalent musculoskeletal disorder worldwide, imposes a substantial societal burden [1], with years lived with disability (YLDs) attributable to LBP having increased by 54% a trend primarily driven by population growth and aging demographics [2]. Notably, the global aging process is accelerating, with the population aged ≥ 65 years projected to increase to 1.5 billion by 2050 [3]. This demographic shift is anticipated to further elevate the prevalence of chronic LBP [4]. Of particular concern is the current global obesity prevalence rate of 25.3% among older adults, which continues to demonstrate an upward trajectory [5]. Substantial evidence has established a robust association between obesity and both the incidence and progression of LBP [6–8]. Consequently, the development of simple yet effective adiposity assessment tools for middle-aged and older populations carries significant implications for early LBP risk screening and preventive intervention.

Obesity (defined as excessive adipose tissue accumulation) is an established risk factor for multiple chronic conditions [9]. Current evidence demonstrates that increased hip circumference, elevated total body fat mass, and higher body fat percentage are all significantly associated with heightened LBP risk [10], with abdominal adiposity showing particularly strong associations with chronic musculoskeletal pain [11]. However, conventional adiposity metrics such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) exhibit notable limitations in assessing fat distribution and metabolic risk: BMI fails to discriminate between muscle and adipose tissue while being susceptible to confounding factors, whereas WC varies substantially with age, height, and ethnic differences [12–14]. These inherent deficiencies have driven the development of more clinically meaningful composite indices.

The WWI is calculated as WC (cm) divided by the square root of body weight (kg). This novel composite metric integrates the advantages of both BMI and WC while eliminating their ambiguous correlation [15, 16], thereby enabling more precise assessment of abdominal adiposity accumulation and fat distribution patterns [17]. Substantial evidence has established the robust health-predictive value of WWI [16]. It has been validated as a reliable indicator for predicting cardiometabolic disease incidence and mortality - for instance, findings from Ding et al. [18]. demonstrated that elevated WWI (≥ 11.2 cm/√kg) was associated with increased risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in Southern China. Notably, the clinical relevance of WWI continues to expand across multiple health domains. Significant associations have been identified with adult sarcopenia, hypertension, and familial hypercholesterolemia [19–22]. These findings underscore WWI’s unique advantage as an integrative biomarker for capturing systemic health impairments related to central obesity and metabolic dysregulation. Furthermore, compared with emerging indicators [23] such as waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and body roundness index (BRI), the WWI incorporates body weight parameters, enabling it to better distinguish between the relative contributions of muscle and fat within body composition.

However, current research on the relationship between WWI and LBP remains very limited. Beyond a cross-sectional study [24] suggesting a significant positive association between the two, only one retrospective cohort study [25] has preliminarily explored their longitudinal association. Notably, existing studies have primarily focused on U.S. populations, and there is currently no longitudinal research investigating the relationship between WWI and the risk of new LBP among middle-aged and older adults in China.

To address this knowledge gap, the current study utilizes nationally representative data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to prospectively assess this relationship. The findings are expected to provide novel scientific evidence for developing early screening protocols and preventive strategies within primary healthcare systems. Ultimately, this research aims to enhance quality of life in aging populations and reduce the substantial public health burden attributable to LBP.

Methods

Study population

The study utilized data from CHARLS, a nationally representative cohort conducted by the National School of Development at Peking University. This longitudinal investigation encompassed 28 Chinese provinces, with baseline data collected in 2011 through stratified multistage probability sampling that enrolled 17,705 participants. Follow-up assessments were subsequently performed at biennial intervals (2013–2014, 2015–2016, 2017–2018, and 2019–2020) over a 9-year period, utilizing face-to-face computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI) for data collection [26].

All participants enrolled in the national baseline survey were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) age < 45 years; (2) pre-existing LBP at baseline; (3) missing weight or waist circumference data; (4) incomplete covariate information (sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and lifestyle factors); (5) loss to follow-up; or (6) extreme WWI values (< 8 or > 14) [27, 28]. Ultimately, subjects aged ≥ 45 years with complete data were included in the longitudinal analysis (Fig. 1). The original study protocol was approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (Approval No. IRB00001052-11015), with written informed consent obtained from all participants [29]. This secondary analysis of de-identified data complies with the ethical standards for non-interventional research under the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of exposure

The WWI was calculated as waist circumference (cm) divided by the square root of body weight (kg). WC measurements were obtained using standardized protocols: participants stood upright while trained examiners horizontally positioned a flexible tape measure at the umbilical level, maintaining skin contact without compression. Measurements were recorded at end-expiratory breath hold. Body weight was measured using an Omron™ HN-286 scale [26], with participants standing barefoot in the center of the platform after removing heavy outerwear and personal items; stable readings were documented. All anthropometric procedures were performed in strict accordance with standardized operating protocols [30].

Assessment of outcomes

In this study, incident LBP was established as the primary outcome variable, with diagnosis based on a standardized self-report protocol. Participants were initially asked: “Are you currently experiencing pain in any part of your body?” Affirmative responders were then required to identify pain locations using standardized anatomical illustration cards. Cases were only classified as LBP-positive when the lower back region was specifically marked [31]. This assessment methodology ensured diagnostic consistency.

Assessment of covariates

Covariate selection was guided by clinical relevance and existing scientific literature. Multiple covariates were systematically evaluated, encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and lifestyle factors [32]. Data were collected using standardized questionnaires to assess sociodemographic factors including age, sex, education level (no formal education, primary or lower secondary, junior or senior high school and above), marital status (married/cohabiting, others including separated, divorced, widowed, never married), as well as health and lifestyle factors including smoking status, alcohol consumption, chronic diseases, and physical activity (PA). Smoking status was determined by responses to: (1) “Have you ever chewed tobacco, smoked pipes, hand-rolled cigarettes, or smoked manufactured cigarettes/cigars?” (2) “Do you currently maintain this habit or have you completely quit?” [32], and was categorized into three groups: former, never, and current smokers. Alcohol consumption was classified into two groups based on drinking frequency in the past year: non-drinkers (less than once per month) and drinkers (more than once per month) [32]. Chronic diseases mainly included hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases (referring to stroke and heart disease in this study), and dyslipidemia. Chronic diseases were identified through self-reported diagnoses. PA was categorized into two groups according to WHO recommendations: sufficient physical activity (more than 150 minutes of moderate activity or more than 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week) and insufficient physical activity (less than 150 minutes of moderate activity per week) [33]. This comprehensive analysis of covariates contributes to a thorough understanding and interpretation of the study findings.

Statistical analysis

In the baseline characteristics of the study population, all normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages). Differences between individuals with and without LBP were assessed using independent samples Student’s t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. WWI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and in quintiles, with hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident LBP risk calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. Three progressively adjusted Cox models were constructed: Model 1 (adjusted for age and sex); Model 2 (further adjusted for education level, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and PA); and Model 3 (fully adjusted for all covariates). The proportional hazards assumption was verified through examination of Schoenfeld residual plots. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) models were employed to explore potential nonlinear dose-response relationships between WWI and incident LBP risk. Finally, Kaplan-Meier curves were employed to visualize LBP-free probabilities across different WWI quintiles. Sensitivity analyses included multiple imputation for missing covariate values and subgroup analyses to evaluate result robustness. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (http://www.R-project.org, R Foundation) with two-tailed p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. This comprehensive analytical approach facilitates thorough interpretation of study findings.

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of 3,912 participants stratified by WWI quintiles. The cohort had a mean age of 58.6 ± 8.9 years, with 2,309 (59%) female participants. During the 9-year follow-up period, 3,388 incident LBP cases (86.6%) were documented. Participants in higher WWI quintiles were characterized by: advanced age, greater female predominance, lower educational attainment, and higher marriage prevalence. And the proportion of affected individuals with hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and dyslipidemia showed progressively increasing trends with higher WWI quintiles. All demographic characteristics showed statistically significant intergroup differences (P < 0.001).

Longitudinal association between baseline WWI (2011–2020) and incident LBP

To examine the longitudinal association between WWI and incident LBP, Cox proportional hazards models were employed (Table 2). Before model implementation, the proportional hazards were verified primarily through Schoenfeld residual testing (Supplementary Fig. 1(Online Resource 1)). Results demonstrated that residuals showed no temporal patterns, confirming time-independent residuals, stable variable effects, fulfillment of the proportional hazards, and model stability. When WWI was analyzed as a continuous variable, results from the Cox proportional hazards model showed a positive association between WWI and incident LBP in the unadjusted model. However, this association disappeared after covariate adjustment. Therefore, we treated WWI as a categorical variable to perform knot point analysis to examine whether there was a nonlinear association between WWI and incident LBP.

In Fig. 2, RCS regression was employed to visualize the association between WWI and incident LBP risk. After adjusting for all covariates listed, a nonlinear relationship was identified (P = 0.015), exhibiting a J-shaped association with a knot point at 10.56. Beyond this knot value, WWI showed a significant positive correlation with incident LBP risk (HR = 1.096; 95% CI: 1.026–1.171; P = 0.0066) (Supplementary Table 1(Online Resource 3)), indicating a 9.6% increased risk of incident LBP per 1-unit WWI increment. No significant association was observed below the knot value (HR = 0.854; 95% CI: 0.709–1.027; P = 0.094).

When WWI was analyzed as a categorical variable, the second quintile group (Q2) contained the knot point. We therefore used Q2 as the reference group for subsequent analyses, unadjusted models revealed significantly elevated incident LBP risk in the highest WWI quintile (Q5) versus the second quintile (Q2) (HR = 1.254, 95% CI: 1.127–1.395). This association persisted following covariate adjustment. In the fully adjusted Model 3, each 1-unit increase in WWI within the highest quintile was associated with an 18.6% higher risk of future LBP among Chinese middle-aged and older adults compared to Q2 (HR = 1.186, 95% CI: 1.057–1.331) (Table 2).

Kaplan-Meier analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2(Online Resource 2)) demonstrated that different WWI levels significantly influenced LBP-free survival probabilities in middle-aged and elderly Chinese populations (p = 0.00018). A declining trend in LBP-free survival probabilities was observed across all WWI quintile groups with prolonged follow-up. Notably, the Q5 group exhibited the most rapid descent in survival curves, indicating the highest risk of incident LBP. The risk table, which displays the number of individuals at risk at each follow-up timepoint, revealed a progressive reduction in population numbers across all WWI groups over time.

Sensitivity analysis

To enhance the generalizability of our results, sensitivity analyses were performed using two approaches. First, multiple imputation was applied to handle missing covariates; the results (Supplementary Table 2(Online Resource 4)) showed that the direction and significance of the WWI-LBP association remained unchanged after imputation. Second, subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of the WWI-LBP relationship across strata defined by age, gender, education level, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and PA (Fig. 3). The results demonstrated no statistically significant interaction effects in any subgroup (all interaction P > 0.05). Specifically, the results from all subgroup analyses showed increasing LBP prevalence with higher WWI values, confirming the robustness of our primary findings. Furthermore, in certain subgroups (e.g., females, individuals aged ≥ 60 years), the observed point estimates of HRs appeared higher, suggesting a potential trend; future studies with larger sample sizes are required to confirm this.

Discussion

This study represents the first cohort investigation examining the longitudinal association between WWI and incident LBP risk over a 9-year period in Chinese middle-aged and older adults. The core finding demonstrates that among 3,912 baseline participants followed for 9 years, each 1-unit WWI increase in the highest quintile was associated with an 18.6% higher risk of incident LBP (HR = 1.186, 95%CI:1.057–1.331) after full covariate adjustment, with this association persisting in sensitivity analyses. This is consistent with the findings of Zhang W et al. [25]. Further analysis identified a critical knot point at WWI = 10.56 cm/√kg, when WWI > 10.56 cm/√kg, its increase is associated with a higher risk of developing LBP episodes over the subsequent 9 years. This finding differs from the results reported by Zhang W et al. [25], and such a discrepancy may stem from potential cultural or regional influences. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that the survival curve of the highest WWI quintile group (Q5) demonstrated the most rapid decline with prolonged follow-up duration These findings suggest that WWI may serve as a simple, effective, and abdominal fat distribution-integrated indicator, which could be suitable for primary health care institutions to conduct simplified early screening aimed at identifying high-risk populations for LBP. A clinical alert threshold of WWI = 10.56 cm/√kg was established. It provides scientific rationale and actionable targets for implementing targeted early screening and preventive interventions, thereby facilitating the reduction of this significant public health burden.

Our study revealed that after full adjustment for covariates, each 1-unit increase in WWI was associated with an 18.6% elevated risk of incident LBP (HR = 1.186, 95%CI:1.057–1.331) in the highest quintile group. These findings are consistent with previous cross-sectional studies [24]. This cross-sectional study revealed a significant positive association between WWI and LBP. Moreover, the results align with existing research evidence. For instance, a Korean cohort study demonstrated that abdominal obesity was significantly associated with LBP occurrence among women aged ≥ 50 years [34]. However, existing studies have predominantly focused on the association between BMI [7, 35] and LBP, while BMI has been recognized as an indicator of fat mass with low sensitivity [36] that cannot accurately reflect fat distribution characteristics. Although waist circumference can partially reflect body composition, it has been demonstrated to be incapable of precisely capturing metabolic risks related to visceral fat accumulation in individuals [37, 38]. By contrast, the WWI has been shown to more precisely quantify abdominal fat distribution. When compared with either BMI or waist circumference alone, WWI has been found to better reflect the distribution and accumulation of abdominal fat, which may be more mechanistically relevant to the pathogenesis of LBP (e.g., increased mechanical load, chronic inflammation). Furthermore, compared with emerging indicators [23] such as waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and body roundness index (BRI), the computational simplicity of WWI and its direct responsiveness to weight changes also afford it potential advantages for clinical practice and dynamic risk assessment.

The potential mechanisms by which abdominal obesity contributes to low back pain are as follows: First, abdominal obesity may also contribute to low back pain through increased mechanical loading on the spine. For example, it may directly act on intervertebral discs, significantly increasing disc pressure [39]. Second, abdominal obesity refers to the proliferation and hypertrophy of adipocytes, which may lead to abnormal secretion of both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory factors [40, 41]. Combined with abdominal fat accumulation, this exacerbates systemic inflammatory responses, thereby inducing whole-body inflammation that aggravates LBP. Cytokines [42] secreted by visceral fat (such as TNF-α, IL-6, and leptin) can reach lumbar tissues through blood circulation. Animal studies have shown a subset of these cytokines can activate the NF-κB pathway in disc cells, promote matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs) expression [43, 44], and accelerate disc matrix degradation (including type II collagen breakdown) [45], ultimately leading to LBP. In summary, abdominal fat accumulation not only increases mechanical load on the spine, but may also accelerate disc degeneration through systemic chronic inflammation and direct molecular effects, thereby promoting the onset and progression of LBP. In clinical practice, WWI can be calculated through simple measurements (WC and body weight), enabling rapid identification of individuals at risk of inflammatory-metabolic disorders and spinal overload. These individuals should be prioritized as the target population for LBP prevention interventions. Intervention measures may be implemented for this group. Recommended measures for this group may include: mechanical load intervention through personalized core muscle training combined with scientific weight‑loss plans; implementation of an anti‑inflammatory dietary pattern (e.g., increasing triterpenoids [46], lectins, etc.) together with regular exercise to lower pro‑inflammatory factor levels [47]; and for those with concurrent metabolic abnormalities (e.g., diabetes), intensification of glucose and lipid control (e.g., use of metformin, statins) to inhibit adipokine (leptin, adiponectin)‑mediated intervertebral disc matrix degradation (e.g., type‑II collagen disruption).

Further research discovered a J-shaped nonlinear association between the WWI and incident low back pain (knot point: 10.56). Above the knot point (WWI > 10.56), each 1-unit WWI increment was associated with a significant 9.6% (95% CI: 1.026–1.171) increase in incident LBP risk. The mechanisms may involve elevated spinal mechanical loading due to abdominal obesity, aligning with Canadian cohort studies showing a 20 kg spinal load increase per 5 cm WC enlargement at equivalent body weight [48]. No significant association was observed below the knot point (WWI < 10.56 cm/√kg), suggesting this range may represent a safe threshold where moderate loading could promote adaptive strengthening of intervertebral discs and paraspinal muscles, enhancing compressive resistance without increasing LBP risk. Therefore, for individuals with WWI < 10.56 cm/√kg, routine health education is advised. For Chinese middle-aged and elderly individuals reaching or exceeding this threshold value, interventions (e.g., weight reduction, anti-inflammatory diets) should be implemented with the objective of reducing WWI below the knot point. Additionally, health education emphasizing abdominal obesity risks and regular LBP symptom monitoring should be conducted to reduce disability risk at its source.

Furthermore, throughout the follow-up period, the highest WWI quintile group consistently exhibited significantly lower LBP-free probabilities compared to other groups. Combining with the knot point of 10.56 cm/√kg, WWI ≥ 10.56 cm/√kg is recommended as a clinical alert threshold. These findings indicate that WWI assessment may be integrated into community health service systems or regular health examinations for middle-aged and elderly populations, with prioritized resource allocation to individuals exceeding the clinical alert threshold. This approach ensures that high-risk populations receive sustained attention and timely management, thereby maximizing prevention of LBP incidence and progression. These findings serve as important evidence for the establishment of LBP risk monitoring programs.

This study has several strengths: first, it is the first cohort study conducted over 9 years in a middle‑aged and older population aimed at exploring the longitudinal association between WWI and the occurrence of LBP; second, it suggests the potential of WWI as a screening tool, allowing early identification of individuals at high risk for LBP through simple instruments (e.g., a tape measure and a weighing scale), which may be suitable for primary care settings with relatively limited medical resources. However, this study also has limitations: the diagnosis of LBP was based on self‑report, which may lead to misclassification due to recall bias or individual perception differences; although demographic, lifestyle, and chronic disease factors were adjusted for, potential confounders such as occupational physical load, psychological status, and dietary structure were not included; furthermore, only baseline WWI was assessed without dynamic tracking of its changes. Therefore, future research should conduct cross‑cultural comparisons based on international aging databases to validate the generalizability of the WWI threshold, incorporate occupational exposure history, psychological assessments, and dietary factors to build a more comprehensive predictive model, examine the relationship between WWI and the severity of LBP, and design randomized controlled trials to evaluate the impact of lifestyle interventions that reduce WWI on LBP incidence, thereby promoting clinical translation and application.

Conclusion

WWI emerged as an independent predictor of incident LBP. Establishing WWI = 10.56 as an objective screening threshold may enable a simplified clinical pathway (“waist circumference measurement → weight assessment → WWI calculation → threshold comparison”) for primary healthcare systems. This may approach permits community health centers to identify high-risk individuals using low-cost tools. The ultimate objective is to improve quality of life in middle-aged and to provide evidence-based foundations for primary prevention of low back pain.
