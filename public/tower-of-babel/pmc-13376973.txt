Low-power design and implementation of an enhanced I2C bus controller using open lane: from RTL to GDSII
Authors: Gavaskar K, Arun S, Anbumani V, Sathya Narayanan Velmurugan, Mathankumar M
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit) protocol is a widely adopted two-wire serial communication. Interface controllers are becoming increasingly important in the embedded and Internet of Things (IoT) domains as power savings and tiny communication technologies improve. The work presented here develops and implements an optimised I2C bus controller that significantly reduces power and silicon area without impacting functionality. The controller was designed in Verilog HDL and synthesised with the Open Lane ASIC design pipeline and the Sky130 PDK. It includes enhanced methods such as highly precise clock gating and optimised finite state machines. The recommended controller is successfully created and implemented in Verilog using the OpenLane RTL-to-GDSII flow and the Sky130 CMOS technology library. These support high-speed I2C modes and enable autonomous master-slave communication with minor CPU involvement. Compared to traditional designs, the suggested approach reduces synthesis power by 40.55% and area by 9.8%. Functional accuracy and timeliness were demonstrated using Model Sim and Vivado, while backend verification was secured using DRC, LVS, and XOR checks. This study presents a reliable, silicon-compatible I2C controller designed for integration in high-density and energy-constrained VLSI devices.Keywords: I2C controller, Low-power design, Area optimization, RTL-to-GDSII, Open laneSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With the advent of ubiquitous computing and networked systems, efficient design of on-chip communication interfaces has emerged as a key parameter in system-on-chip (SoC) and embedded systems design. Amidst the scores of serial communications protocols, Inter- Integrated Circuit (I²C) is still a favored option because it is simple to implement, pins are few in number, and it is fully compatible with both multi-master and multi-slave topologies1,2. In spite of these benefits, traditional I²C controllers suffer from numerous performance and design hurdles such as high-power consumption, poor area efficiency, and CPU-intensive reliance for protocol state management3. These constraints limit their suitability in power-constrained applications like battery-operated IoT devices, medical instrumentation, and wearable technology4,5, where power efficiency and silicon footprint compactness are not negotiable.

This paper overcomes the above-mentioned constraints by proposing a low power, area-efficient, and functionally improved I²C bus controller, designed in an end-to-end ASIC design flow. The proposed controller is micro architecturally designed for minimizing dynamic power through clock gating, enhancing protocol autonomy through special master and slave finite state machines (FSMs)6, and reducing resource overhead with an effective RTL structure. A comparative analysis against current controllers, specifically the GD32F1 × 0 and Mini I²C designs, proves that the new design has up to 40.55% power saving and 9.8% area reduction, validated by stringent physical design phases and backend validation procedures7.

The design is implemented in Verilog HDL and synthesized through the open-source Open Lane RTL-to-GDSII flow, mapping to the Sky130 130 nm process node, thus supporting reproducibility and openness to the open hardware community8. Functional verification is accomplished with Model Sim and Xilinx Vivado, whereas the backend design is checked with Layout Versus Schematic (LVS), Design Rule Checks (DRC), and XOR checks, which ascertain the layout integrity and manufacturability of the new design9. Additionally, exhaustive power and area analyses are made across several phases of the flow synthesis, placement, routing, and clock tree synthesis (CTS) in order to estimate optimization gains9.

The finite state machine (FSM) of the suggested I2C controller uses conditional state transitions and control flags to manage unusual circumstances including missed acknowledgement, bus busy conditions, and data transmission delays. Before proceeding to the next step, the controller verifies the acknowledge signal after transferring the slave address or data. If the acknowledge message is not acquired, the FSM reaches the stop or idle mode to safely terminate the transmission. Bus disruptions and erroneous data transfer are prevented by performing this10.

In order to handle bus issues, waiting conditions, the FSM features delay phases where the chip halts the operation until the required condition is fulfilled, such as data register ready or bus available. In these cases, the controller stops the clock and fails to continue until a valid indication is received12. The FSM reverts to a safe state, such as START, STOP, or IDLE, if the anticipated response is not detected. Although the previous description was concise, the suggested controller can reliably manage aberrant circumstances because of these dependent checks and wait states.

This work contributes to the ongoing evolution of energy-efficient, open-source and scalable hardware communication protocols. The simulation results and a detailed analysis are covered in Sect. 4. Important conclusions and future directions are presented in the last section of the work.

Review of existing I²C controller architectures

The I²C protocol has grown into a base of embedded communication because it is low pin count, easy to implement and multi- master capable. With the increasing requirement for ultra-low-power and area- constrained digital systems, it has become necessary to revisit the conventional I²C controller architecture.

GD32F1 × 0 I²C controller

The 32-bit ARM Cortex-M3 microcontroller line from GigaDevice includes the GD32F1 × 0 I²C controller, which provides a dependable and excellent I²C protocol implementation. It can send data at 100 kbps (standard mode), 400 kbps (fast mode), and 1 Mbps (fast-plus mode) according to I²C specification version 6.0. Clock stretching, automatic ACK/NACK signal creation, and support for two device addresses are further helpful characteristics of this controller12.

A power breakdown evaluation was performed in both 100 kbps typical mode and 400 kbps fast mode to determine how power is used in various design components. Three components account for the majority of the overall power: sequential/associated logic power (registers and FSM), logic of combination power, and clock tree power. The total the use of power is lower in standard mode (100 kbps) since there is less changing activity. Active power is increased, particularly in the clock tree, when the clock toggles more frequently in fast mode (400 kbps). According to the synthesis summary, the clock tree uses the most power in both modes since the clock signal is active for the majority of the process. Nevertheless, the clock is turned off for idle blocks since the suggested architecture uses precise clock gating, which lowers needless power usage.

Although logic of combination and FSM-related logic use fewer kW overall, the impact rises in fast mode because of increased shifting action during data transit. Additionally, only when activated do related logic components like registers and control signals use power, which aids in lowering overall power consumption during idle and wait states. Because the clock gating is implemented at a smaller block level and the simpler FSM minimizes logic switching, the suggested controller exhibits lower clock tree power when compared to conventional systems. Consequently, it is evident from the power decomposition chart for both 100 kbps and 400 kbps phases that the suggested design improves power efficiency by lowering clock power and minimising pointless logic action13.

The GD32F1 × 0 architecture depicted in Fig. 1 incorporates a bus interface unit (BIU), an FSM-based control unit, and a programmable clock generation unit into its multilevel control system14. By utilising interrupt-driven signalling and enabling DMA (Direct Memory Access), the processor lowers CPU participation during high-speed transactions. Slave communication can be throttled using clock stretching, which is essential for sensor-based systems where data readiness is delayed. Synthesis and layout using the Open Lane toolchain targeting the SkyWater 130 nm process (Sky130 PDK) reveal a substantial logic footprint, with 510 logic cells and a silicon area of approximately 5456.48 μm²15. Power analysis across the design flow reports a consumption of 6.09 × 10⁻⁴ W post-synthesis and increases marginally through placement and routing stages. The design passes all standard backend checks, including DRC, LVS, and XOR, validating its tape-out readiness16 that demonstrated in Fig. 2. However, its relatively high area and power consumption underscore the limitations of general-purpose controller reuse in low-power system-on-chip (SoC) platforms, especially those targeting battery-operated IoT nodes17. Mini I²C controller

A simplified, customised version of the I2C protocol that prioritises power and volume savings is the Mini I2C controller. In order to greatly reduce CPU interaction and enable self-sufficient interactions, this system uses two dedicated FSMs that handle master and slave activities18. The MINI I2C controller’s design view is displayed in Fig. 5. Although it lacks sophisticated correction of errors and protection capabilities, it includes basic error detection methods (ACK/NACK checks and conditional data processing).

Xilinx Vivado was used to evaluate it on FPGA tools before Open Lane with Sky130 PDK was used to convert it to an ASIC flow19. In terms of physical implementation, the design significantly outperforms the GD32F1x0 design by achieving a decreased gate number of 488 logic cells and occupying 5125.69 μm². This simplicity is also reflected in power use, which was reported at 4.11 × 10⁻¹ W during synthesis and showed only slight increases at backend phases20–22. The design is contained in GDSII and LEF files and successfully sign-off with clean DRC, LVS and XOR reports, confirming its production preparedness. This design is ideal for integration into power-sensitive devices such as wearables, IoT sensor nodes, automotive subsystems and edge devices.

Functional architecture

The specified I2C controller is made simpler by two FSMs for Master and Slave modes, which also improve testing and flexibility. Transmission and reception via polling modes are supported by the common I2CDAT register. SCL/SDA control scheme with three state buffers and drain emulation ensures I2C compliant. A clock gating element saves energy by shutting off modules that aren’t in use, according to Open Lane CTS. Interrupt logic enables efficient and low in power execution by alerting the CPU to significant events. In general, the design achieves a balance between utility, efficiency, and protocol compliance27.

Physical design integration

Targeting the SkyWater 130 nm (Sky130_fd_sc_hd) process node, the Open Lane physical ASIC design path was used to execute the backend concept and physical realisation of the suggested I2C controller. In order to translate the high-level register swap logic into a technology-specific gate netlist optimised for area and power usage, the RTL generated in Verilog HDL conducted logic syntheses using Yosys in conjunction with ABC (And-Inverter Graph-based logic optimisation).

Floor planning and standard cell placement were carried out after synthesis to guarantee uniform logic unit allocation and minimum routing latency. To optimise timing paths related to the bidirectional data (SDA) and clock (SCL) lines, particular focus was placed on the spatial arrangement of control logic, clock networks, and I/O buffers. After that, a balanced clock distribution network was built using Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS). Importantly, during CTS, clock gating logic which was implemented at the RTL level was maintained and respected, guaranteeing that idle devices did not receive any superfluous clock signals, hence lowering variable power usage.

Global and detailed routing engines were used for routing, which reduced setup and hold time constraints and guaranteed low-latency connection links. Typical physical testing procedures, such as Design Rule Check (DRC), Layout vs. Schematic (LVS) comparison, and XOR checking, were used in after routing assessment. These steps verified that the ultimate layout was able to tape-out, functionally comparable to the synthesised netlist, and free of design rule violations. Functional validation and simulation

Both the behavioural and gate stages of the suggested I2C controller underwent thorough functional evaluation. Model Sim was used for the RTL modelling, and Xilinx Vivado was used for timing validation, stimulus production and pattern examination. A comprehensive assessment of protocol compliance was made possible by the test bench’s ability to simulate both master and slave exchanges on a shared I2C bus28.

A number of stringent tests were performed on the controller, such as Generation and Detection of START and STOP Conditions, Testing of the controller’s capacity to accurately assert and recognise START and STOP alerts, and assuring correct I2C operation start and end29.

The recommended controller does not solely reuse conventional I2C topologies in order to minimise unnecessary switching behaviour during the idle, start, stop, and acknowledgement phases of the I2C protocol. Rather, it starts with a reduced FSM framework that has optimised state encoding and minimal state shifts. This decrease instantly reduces changing power use and logic space by guaranteeing that only the required control line is active at every oscillation and removing unnecessary intermediary states that are commonly present in conventional systems.

Figs. 9 and 10 show the LEF and GDSII files for the planned design. The simulation demonstrated precise state changes and timing relationships in accordance with the I2C protocol standard. Results revealed that the controller could easily convert between master and slave roles, maintaining synchronization and data integrity throughout. Furthermore, it was confirmed that logic optimisations and physical design changes did not generate functional anomalies by comparing the simulation outputs exactly with post-synthesis functional behaviour. The accuracy, dependability, and silicon readiness of the suggested I²C controller architecture are highlighted by this dual-level validation, which combines RTL simulation with physical design consistency.

The optimized finite state machine (FSM) of the suggested I2C controller is created by eliminating superfluous states and streamlining the control function. Hardware complication is increased in typical designs since each operation, including start, location transmitting, transmitting data, wait, and stop condition, is performed using distinct states. Instead of generating several states, the suggested system combines and controls related processes using enable signals. In order to express each state with fewer bits, efficient state encoding is also employed. This simplification lowers switching activity, latency and power consumption by reducing the quantity of logic gates and flip-flops. According to current systems where the FSM is built straight from the protocol steps without optimization, this increases the controller’s efficiency.

In the work suggested, “fine-grained clock gating” refers to controlling the clock signal at the level of tiny internal blocks, such as registers, state machines, and control units, rather than gating the clock for the entire controller. The clock in many current I2C controllers operates constantly throughout the module, even when certain components are not in use, which raises dynamic power usage. In the suggested architecture, the clock is only turned on when data transmission or reception is necessary, and the FSM produces enable signals to switch off the clock for inactive blocks during idle and wait states. It is known as fine-grained clock gating because it helps cut down on needless switching power by controlling the clock at a lower level.

The combination of fine-grained clock gating and simpler FSM inside the controller architecture is the primary novelty of the claimed approach. The suggested approach concentrates on lowering hardware activity under idle situations, whereas existing designs primarily concentrate on proper protocol execution. As a consequence, compared to the current GD32F1 × 0 and MINI I2C designs, the suggested I2C controller obtains less power consumption and smaller space, which is confirmed by the synthesis and physical design findings.

In order to ensure correct placement and routing through the backend design stage, the pin definitions, port locations and macro boundaries of the suggested layout are displayed in the LEF file. The core area division, standard cell placement and routing arrangement produced following the backend design step are displayed in the GDSII layout. The layout view verifies that the chosen technology library was successfully used to create the controller. These outcomes verify that the suggested design is appropriately executed and optimised at the level of physical layout.

Conclusion

A powerful and compact I2C bus controller was engineered and put into use in this research. In comparison to current implementations like GD32F1 × 0 and Mini I2C, the suggested design achieves considerable savings in power consumption (up to 40.55%) and silicon area (up to 9.8%) by utilising clock gating techniques, modular FSM driven control and improved data path synthesis. The overall implementation, including correct handling of start/stop situations, 7-bit addressing, bidirectional data transfer and ACK/NACK signalling, is confirmed by workable computations with Model Sim and Vivado. However, in order to have a small footprint, the design purposefully leaves out sophisticated capabilities like inbuilt security, fast I2C modes, and multi-master disputes.
