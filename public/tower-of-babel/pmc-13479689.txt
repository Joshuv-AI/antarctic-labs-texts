Innovations in cardiac device therapy in the era of advanced rhythm management: implantable defibrillators and conduction system pacing
Authors: Margarida Pujol-Lopez, Roderick Tung, Lluis Mont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac device therapy has significantly evolved since the introduction of the first implantable pacemaker and the subsequent development of the implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD). ICDs are highly effective; however, their main weakness lies in lead-related complications. To avoid the need for venous access and the complications associated with transvenous leads, a fully subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) system was developed. Despite these advancements, the S-ICD system is limited by its inability to provide bradycardia pacing and antitachycardia pacing. This limitation prompted the development of a modular cardiac rhythm management system, integrating a new leadless pacemaker with an S-ICD that uses unidirectional communication to command the pacemaker to deliver antitachycardia pacing.Conduction system pacing, including His bundle pacing and left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP), has emerged as a physiological alternative to biventricular resynchronisation therapy and conventional pacing, pending results of large clinical trials. LBBAP offers superior electrical parameters and long-term performance compared with His bundle pacing. The capability to provide defibrillation via the same lead used for LBBAP represents an unresolved challenge that is currently under ongoing research.This state-of-the-art review presents the latest developments and innovations in cardiac device therapy, offering a comprehensive overview of current technologies that increasingly enable therapy to be tailored to individual patient needs.Keywords: Tachycardia, Ventricular; Defibrillators, Implantable; Pacemaker, Artificial

Introduction

The complete implantation of a pacemaker in 1958 by the cardiac surgeon Åke Senning at the Karolinska Hospital—using a device designed by Rune Elmqvist with epicardial wires connected to a rechargeable generator placed in the abdominal wall—is considered the birth of modern pacemaker therapy.1 2 Michel Mirowski, with co-inventor Morton Mower, revolutionised the care of patients at risk of sudden death with the design of the implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD).3 In 1980, the first human implant occurred4 and the device was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 1985.3 More than 65 years of evolution in cardiac device therapy have delivered unique contributions to improving patient outcomes, reducing procedural complications and advancing cardiac rhythm management (figure 1A).

Highly sophisticated pacemaker and defibrillator devices are now in widespread use, thanks to numerous clinical scientists and engineers who have introduced technological advances to improve patient outcomes. Given the complications associated with transvenous ICDs, both short-term and long-term, the subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD) was developed in 2010.5 To overcome the absence of pacing capabilities in the S-ICD, a system comprising a leadless pacemaker (LP) in wireless communication with an S-ICD was developed6 (figure 1B).

In the field of cardiac pacing, the major factors motivating the search for alternatives to conventional pacing are, on one hand, that right ventricular (RV) pacing causes dyssynchrony7 and pacing-induced cardiomyopathy;8 9 and on the other hand, that the ventricular activation pattern during biventricular pacing (BiVP)—characterised by slow cell-to-cell conduction from epicardium to endocardium—is non-physiological,10 and the non-responder rate persists at approximately 30%.11 Moreover, BiVP provides an activation characterised by epicardial onset in the left ventricle (LV), with two main foci of activation, LV and RV. Given this scenario, conduction system pacing (CSP) was developed to offer a more physiological and homogeneous activation.

This manuscript provides a comprehensive overview of the latest innovations in cardiac device therapy, including the S-ICD, modular communicative leadless pacing–defibrillator system, extravascular ICD (EV-ICD) and CSP.

Subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator

Clinical rationale

Since the first transvenous ICD implants,4 robust evidence from high-quality randomised controlled trials has supported their use across various patient populations, including cardiac arrest survivors, patients with ventricular tachycardia (VT) and structural heart disease, and those with significant LV dysfunction.12 Transvenous ICDs are associated with a significant number of complications, of which many are related to the lead; the transvenous lead is the Achilles heel of these devices.13 The risk of complications is present both during the implantation procedure (pneumothorax/haemothorax, lead dislodgement, cardiac perforation/pericardial tamponade) and during long-term follow-up (lead malfunction, endovascular infection, venous thrombosis/occlusion).3

Results from a large healthcare database14 showed that 5.3% of the study cohort had a mechanical complication from the ICD lead, whereas 1.9% had an infectious complication involving the ICD lead over a mean follow-up of 2.3 years. The risk of mechanical failure of transvenous ICD leads was 25% at 10 years.14 Moreover, if transvenous lead extraction is required, there is a risk of major complications, urgent cardiac surgery and a 0.9% risk of in-hospital deaths during lead extraction procedures.15 To eliminate the need for venous access, an entirely subcutaneous system was introduced in 2010.5

Fundamentals

The S-ICD consists of a subcutaneous pulse generator implanted in the midaxillary position on the left side of the thoracic wall, and a parasternal subcutaneous lead with an 8 cm coil16 (figure 1B). The current third-generation device is magnetic resonance compatible, has an atrial fibrillation monitor and incorporates a novel algorithm aimed at minimising inappropriate therapy (SMART Pass (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA)).16

Efficacy and safety

In patients with an indication for an ICD but no indication for pacing, the PRAETORIAN trial17 showed that the S-ICD was non-inferior to the transvenous ICD with respect to device-related complications and inappropriate shocks. The UNTOUCHED study18 was a non-randomised study that enrolled primary prevention patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤35% and no pacing indication; it showed freedom from inappropriate shocks at 17-month follow-up of 95.9%, and a conversion success rate for appropriate episodes of 98.4%. The ATLAS trial19 found a significant reduction in perioperative lead-related complications, which occurred in 0.4% of S-ICD and 4.8% of transvenous ICD implants. The S-ICD postapproval study20 real-world multicentre registry, with a 4.2-year median follow-up, showed 98.4% shock efficacy for VT or ventricular fibrillation; S-ICD-related and electrode-related complication-free rates were 93.4% and 99.3%, respectively.

The recently published PRAETORIAN-XL21 trial demonstrated no significant difference between the S-ICD and transvenous ICD in all device-related complications during long-term follow-up (median follow-up 87.5 months). (online supplemental material table 1).

Target population

The S-ICD may be preferred in patients with limited vascular access, previous device infections or high infection risk12 such as dialysis, immunodeficiency and prosthetic heart valves. Modular pacing–defibrillator system

Clinical rationale

Defibrillator shocks are associated with a higher risk of death, and the number of shocks contributes to this risk, whereas antitachycardia pacing (ATP) does not appear to increase mortality risk.24 25 ATP has been shown to reduce the number of unnecessary and inappropriate shocks and improve patient quality of life and device longevity.26–28 Since the S-ICD cannot deliver ATP, it is contraindicated in patients who present with recurrent sustained monomorphic VT treatable with ATP.12 22

The recently published APPRAISE ATP trial29 demonstrated that a single burst of ATP prior to shock delivery reduced the relative risk of time to first all-cause shock by 28% in patients with primary prevention ICD. However, the reduction in first all-cause ICD shock did not extend to a reduction in all-cause shock burden or mortality. The benefit of ATP may be limited and might be reserved for patients who experience VT. This would justify S-ICD implantation and adding an LP only if ATP or bradycardia pacing is required (modular pacing–defibrillator system).

Fundamentals

After preclinical studies in animal models,30 31 a modular pacing–defibrillator system was designed to deliver ATP without the risks associated with transvenous leads. This modular cardiac rhythm management (mCRM) system consists of an LP in wireless communication with an S-ICD (Boston Scientific) (figure 1B).

The LP is a rate-responsive, single-chamber RV pacemaker (32 mm long and 6 mm in diameter) with active fixation to the endocardium via active-fixation nitinol tines.32 The LP is implanted under fluoroscopy through a 21F inner diameter introducer sheath and can be retrieved by a dedicated retrieval catheter.32

The pacing–defibrillator system relies on unidirectional communication from the S-ICD to the LP for ATP delivery.6 When device therapy is warranted to treat a ventricular arrhythmia, the S-ICD has one-way communication capabilities to transmit an ATP request signal to the LP. The modality used for interdevice communication is galvanic coupling or conducted communication.33 34 Through galvanic coupling, one implanted device transmits low-energy electrical signals, which are detected by the receiving device.35

The technology was developed for both de novo implants and patients with an existing S-ICD, eliminating the need to explant and replace the pulse generator. To use pre-existing hardware, the existing circuitry within the S-ICD pulse generator (originally designed to assess shock circuit integrity) was repurposed to deliver bursts of approximately 25 kHz pulses from the electrode’s shock coil to the S-ICD pulse generator can.35 These pulses are sent in a proprietary pattern that can be recognised by the LP.35 The S-ICD messages are sent coincident with the sensed R-wave to avoid oversensing and provide built-in redundancy to avoid the risk of failed communication.

ATP can be programmed with the external programmer. The S-ICD continuously monitors the ATP therapy results. If the ATP successfully terminates the tachycardia and returns the heart to a normal rhythm, the S-ICD will continue monitoring, and no shock will be delivered. If VT persists, the S-ICD will deliver shock therapy.

Efficacy and safety

The MODULAR ATP trial tested the safety and efficacy of the mCRM system in a multinational, single-group study that included 282 patients.6 32 In that trial, LP in wireless communication with S-ICD exceeded prespecified performance goals for freedom from major LP-related complications (97.5%), successful communication between the LP and the S-ICD (98.8%) and percentage of patients with a pacing threshold of up to 2 V at 0.4 ms at 6 months (97.4%)6 (figure 2) (online supplemental material table 1).

Target population

There is currently insufficient evidence to formulate guideline recommendations. Larger studies in diverse patient populations are needed. The MODULAR ATP trial6 excluded patients indicated for dual-chamber pacing or cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT), those with other implanted devices that could interfere with the LP system, patients requiring rate-responsive pacing and individuals who were entirely pacemaker-dependent.

Extravascular implantable cardioverter-defibrillator

Clinical rationale

The EV-ICD system (Aurora EV-ICD, Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA) is another technological innovation designed to provide the benefits of an EV-ICD while delivering pause-prevention pacing and ATP. The S-ICD lead requires a high current for pacing and defibrillation because of its extracardiac location, necessitating a larger device.37 The EV-ICD uses a novel substernal lead placement to address these limitations.38

Fundamentals

The EV-ICD pulse generator is implanted along the patient’s left midaxillary line. The lead is positioned substernally using a tunnelling tool, with placement guided by fluoroscopy. The lead has an epsilon shape with two pace/sense electrodes and two defibrillation coil segments (4 cm each) that are tied together for defibrillation purposes to form an overall 8 cm defibrillation coil.38

Efficacy and safety

The EV-ICD pivotal study, a prospective, multicentre, single-group, non-randomised study,37 showed that the EV-ICD could be implanted safely and that it effectively terminated acute ventricular arrhythmias, exceeding the prespecified efficacy and safety criteria. The primary efficacy endpoint—successful defibrillation at implantation—was achieved in 98.7% of patients, while 92.6% of patients remained free from major system or procedure-related complications at 6 months.37 At a mean follow-up of 30.6 months,39 the EV-ICD system terminated spontaneous ventricular arrhythmias with a high rate of ATP and defibrillation therapy success (77% and 100%, respectively) and a low major complication rate.

The Enlighten study40 is a prospective, postmarket registry study designed to assess the performance of the EV-ICD system in real-world clinical practice and its safety at 5-year follow-up. Preliminary results40 show a high success rate for terminating induced ventricular arrhythmia episodes at implant and a low rate of periprocedural major complications at discharge, comparable with the premarket experience (online supplemental material table 1).

Target population

There is currently insufficient evidence to formulate guideline recommendations for the EV-ICD. This device is not indicated in patients who require bradycardia pacing or CRT, as well as in those with a history of prior sternotomy.39

Knowledge gaps in modern defibrillator therapy

It is important to emphasise that, although new technologies have generated considerable enthusiasm, a cautious approach is warranted, as the available evidence is substantially more limited compared with endovascular ICDs and long-term follow-up data remain scarce. For EV-ICD studies, defibrillation testing may not accurately reflect the clinical shock efficacy, and the authors of the articles cited acknowledge that the study population was younger than typical ICD recipients.37 39 40 Currently, no randomised or long-term evidence is available for the EV-ICD or for the modular pacing–defibrillator system; therefore, guidelines have not yet defined the specific patient population for which these technologies are indicated.

In contrast, the evidence regarding S-ICDs is extensive (online supplemental material table 1). The safety and efficacy of the S-ICD are non-inferior to transvenous devices, with low rates of inappropriate shocks and complications.17–20 Furthermore, these benefits are maintained over long-term follow-up,21 41 supporting the S-ICD as a reliable and effective alternative to traditional transvenous defibrillators. However, gaps remain in long-term follow-up data, partly because the studies and informed consent were not originally designed for extended observation and also due to the low inclusion of women, constituting less than 20% of participants in the PRAETORIAN-XL trial21 and 28% in the EFFORTLESS study.41

Conduction system pacing

A more physiological alternative to biventricular and RV pacing has been sought for years. Although BiVP partially corrects dyssynchronous contraction,42 it yields significantly longer activation times compared with the intact conduction system, and 30% of patients are clinical non-responders. Regarding RV pacing, it could be harmful in a considerable number of patients: the incidence of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy is estimated to be around 9% at 12-month follow-up43 and 19%–23% with longer follow-up.44 45 In this scenario, His bundle pacing (HBP) and left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) have arisen as a possible new resynchronisation therapy or pacing modality.

His bundle pacing

HBP is considered the most physiological pacing method, as it engages the native conduction system, resulting in simultaneous activation of both ventricles. Using computational modelling, Meiburg et al 46 showed that HBP was theoretically the superior CSP mode, leading to the most synchronous electrical activation and homogeneous distribution of mechanical work.

In 1967, Scherlag et al 47 described successful acute His bundle stimulation in a canine model, with preserved normal sequential ventricular activation. In 1977, Narula48 reported that HBP distal to the lesion normalised the QRS in patients with left bundle branch block (LBBB) due to synchronous impulse conduction through both bundle branches, right and left.

However, it was not until 2000 that Deshmukh et al 49 published the first series on permanent HBP, demonstrating that HBP is a feasible approach in patients with chronic atrial fibrillation and dilated cardiomyopathy, leading to reduced LV dimensions and improved cardiac function. In 2005, Moriña et al described for the first time that HBP may be a possible alternative to BiVP in cases of difficult coronary sinus access.50

Since then, several observational studies51 and a few randomised clinical trials52–54 (table 2, figure 3A) have been published with encouraging preliminary results. However, technical difficulties in achieving the target pacing site, unsatisfactory electrical lead parameters (eg, increase in pacing thresholds over time) and the inability to correct infra-Hisian or more distal conduction disease limit the adoption of HBP as a standard alternative to BiVP.55 The His-SYNC53 trial showed a 48% crossover from HBP to BiVP; one-half of patients exhibited intraventricular conduction delay (intact Purkinje activation), which is unlikely to be corrected by HBP.56

The ALTERNATIVE-AF57 crossover trial included 50 patients with persistent atrial fibrillation and LVEF ≤40%; all patients underwent atrioventricular (AV) node ablation and received both HBP and BiVP. Patients were randomised to either HBP or BiVP for 9 months, then were switched to the alternative pacing modality for the next 9 months. HBP delivered a modest but significant improvement in LVEF compared with BiVP in patients with AV node ablation.

In a registry of 2342 patients, the lead complication rate for HBP and LBBAP was 6.3% and 2.5%, respectively, at 36-month follow-up.58 Kron et al 59 reported that LBBAP pacing could offer the best of HBP (safe and effective physiological pacing) with the long-term durability of traditional RV pacing leads. The more favourable electrical parameters and fewer lead-related complications that have been reported led to the general adoption of LBBAP over HBP.58

Left bundle branch area pacing

LBBAP includes left bundle branch pacing (LBBP), left fascicular and LV septal pacing. In 2017, Huang et al 60 discovered LBBP during HBP implantation in a patient with heart failure and LBBB. High pacing output failed to correct the LBBB with HBP, and advancing the pacing lead approximately 15 mm toward the ventricular side resulted in successful engagement of the left bundle branch and marked the discovery of LBBP.60 In addition to making His-Purkinje pacing more applicable by reducing the failure rate, LBBP provides more space for AV node ablation and may help to avoid later deterioration at the proximal His bundle or AV node caused by the progression of AV conduction delay.60

Since 2017, we have witnessed an explosion of studies and a global expansion of CSP, supported by large observational studies61–63 and only a few randomised trials, but showing very promising results. Regarding available trials on CRT indication, the LEVEL-AT trial64 showed non-inferiority of CSP (4 patients with HBP, 31 LBBP) compared with BiVP, decreasing left ventricular activation time. LBBP-Resync65 and His-optimised CRT (HOT-CRT)66 trials showed LBBP superiority, increasing LVEF at 6-month follow-up. A secondary analysis of the LEVEL-AT trial demonstrated that CSP and BiVP obtained a comparable correction of dyssynchrony, improvement in LVEF and reduction in LV end-systolic volume at 12-month follow-up.67 The recently published CONSYST-CRT trial68 showed that CSP was non-inferior to BiVP in achieving clinical and echocardiographic response, suggesting that CSP may be an alternative to BiVP (table 2, figure 3A). The CONSYST trial is the only published trial for CRT indication with a clinical endpoint, but it should be noted that this is a combined endpoint.

HBP and LBBP are inherently limited in restoring physiological activation in patients with diffuse distal conduction disease (intact Purkinje activation) or intramyocardial disease.56 69 In this context, HOT-CRT or left bundle branch-optimised CRT (LOT-CRT)70 emerged as a potential strategy to provide better results in patients who present with a very wide QRS or obtain suboptimal response with HBP or LBBP. HOT-CRT and LOT-CRT consist of combining an electrode in the His or left bundle position, respectively, with a coronary sinus lead.

The CSPOT study,71 in a CRT cohort with advanced conduction disease, found that LOT-CRT and BiVP provided greater acute haemodynamic benefit than LBBAP. Patients who are more likely to benefit from LOT-CRT are those with suboptimal LBBAP, wider QRS or ischaemic cardiomyopathy.71

In addition to CRT trials, three recent trials have provided new insights into patients with AV block. The STAY trial72 randomised 75 patients with a high-degree AV block and preserved or mildly deteriorated LVEF (>40%) to RV apical pacing versus CSP. At 6 months, the RV pacing group showed a greater decline in LVEF (mean difference −5.8%, p<0.01) and a higher rate of heart failure-related admissions compared with the CSP group. In the Prague CSP trial,73 patients with pacemaker indication due to the AV conduction disease and LVEF >45% showed a smaller LVEF decline with CSP compared with RV pacing after 1 year (−2% vs −4%, p=0.03). Finally, the recently published CSPACE74 trial demonstrated the superiority of CSP over RV septal pacing in achieving improved clinical outcomes.

His bundle pacing and left bundle branch pacing in the guidelines

HBP was referenced in the American guidelines (2018) as a plausible option in patients with AV block and permanent pacing indication with LVEF between 36% and 50%, who were expected to require ventricular pacing more than 40% of the time.75 European guidelines (2021) supported HBP in patients with failed coronary sinus lead implant or in those with AV block and LVEF >40% who were anticipated to have >20% ventricular pacing.76

It was not until 2023 with the Heart Rhythm Society guideline on cardiac physiological pacing for the avoidance and mitigation of heart failure,77 followed in 2025 by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) clinical consensus statement on indications for CSP 55 that specific recommendations regarding LBBAP have been introduced (table 1B, table 3). It should be noted that the ESC document represents a consensus statement rather than a formal guideline. Leadless pacing

Current technical developments aim to achieve leadless CSP78 and to reduce the lead burden.79 Leadless pacing reached its first milestone in 2025 with the publication of a first-in-human acute feasibility study evaluating the safety and performance of a novel helix-based LP for LBBAP in 10 patients78. The study demonstrated successful acute implantation of the device with clinically acceptable intraprocedural electrical performance and safety.

Defibrillation from a LBBAP lead

The delivery of defibrillation through the same lead used for LBBAP represents a novel, unresolved challenge that is currently under investigation. If a single lead cannot both pace and defibrillate, an additional lead is required, with the associated procedural risks and potential complications.

The Lead Evaluation for Defibrillation and Reliability in LBBAP (NCT04863664) study is currently underway. It was designed to confirm the safety and defibrillation efficacy of a novel, lumenless, small-diameter defibrillation lead80 (OmniaSecure, Medtronic) when placed in the LBBAP location in ICD and LOT-CRT patient population. To date, only 3-month results have been reported.81 This is an elegant and simple approach that uses a single lead to provide both defibrillation and physiological pacing.79 82

Knowledge gaps in conduction system pacing

We are entering a new era of cardiac pacing; however, we are still in the early stages of implementing new technologies. No randomised studies are currently available for LOT-CRT or HOT-CRT. Leadless LBBAP pacing has only just begun to emerge. The largest randomised trials investigating CSP for CRT barely exceed 100 patients each, with follow-up periods of 1 year or less. These CSP studies have been conducted in high-volume centres with extensive expertise; whether widespread implementation of LBBAP in less experienced centres will yield similar results remains unknown. The proliferation of novel technologies generates enthusiasm, but that should not overshadow the limited clinical experience supporting them. High-quality randomised controlled trials are crucial to rigorously evaluate their safety, efficacy and long-term outcomes prior to widespread clinical adoption. Large ongoing randomised trials such as Left vs Left (NCT05650658) and PROTECT-HF (NCT05815745), when results become available, may help address the remaining unanswered questions regarding the clinical benefits of CSP.

Much of the current effort is directed towards demonstrating the potential benefits of CSP, as physiological pacing should, in theory, always be more effective at resynchronising than pacing the myocardium via cell-to-cell conduction (figure 3B). However, in clinical practice, we deal with patients with diverse characteristics (different types of blocks, intact Purkinje activation, extensive scarring, septal infarctions, etc). In the era of precision medicine, rather than replacing BiVP with CSP for all patients, it would be preferable to apply evidence-based criteria to select the most appropriate therapy for each individual.

Conclusion

The latest technological developments in the field of cardiac pacing and defibrillation may allow us to adapt to the specific needs of each patient, depending on their underlying pathology, baseline characteristics and risk of arrhythmic events. Therapy personalisation is still in its early stages, both in terms of pacing modality and the need for ICD therapy according to scar burden. We are still far from being able to anticipate the optimal treatment for each patient. Nonetheless, we are progressively moving closer, and randomised clinical trials will help bridge the gap.
