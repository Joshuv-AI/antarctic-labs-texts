Addressing geographical disparities in the allocation of hospital human resources: an examination of health inequalities in Iran

Background

Achieving equity in healthcare and ensuring equal access to medical services across diverse socio-economic groups remain pivotal goals for health policy worldwide [1]. These objectives are particularly critical in the realm of healthcare resource allocation, as emphasized by the World Health Organization (WHO). Equitable distribution of human resources is among the most pressing dimensions of healthcare equity [2]. Human resources are fundamental to the delivery of effective healthcare services, directly influencing both service quality and patient outcomes.

In many healthcare systems, including Iran’s, significant challenges arise due to the uneven distribution of specialized staff, support personnel, and the general healthcare workforce [3]. This imbalance often extends beyond mere numbers, affecting performance, specialties, and service delivery methods, leading to inequities in access and quality of care [4, 5]. As hospitals are the backbone of healthcare service provision, these disparities in human resource distribution undermine their ability to meet organizational goals, ensure continuity, and deliver efficient patient care [6]. Therefore, addressing the fair distribution of healthcare workers is essential for optimizing healthcare system performance and achieving universal health coverage. Disparities in human resource distribution can take various forms—geographical, occupational, institutional, or gender-based—making it a multifaceted issue [7, 8]. Despite healthcare reforms in many countries, including Iran, these inequities persist, raising serious concerns among healthcare planners and policymakers. For instance, research in the United States has shown a clear correlation between hospital resource levels, i.e., the number of nurses, residents, and technical staff per bed—and reduced hospital mortality rates, illustrating the critical impact of equitable resource distribution on health outcomes [9].

In Iran, while significant strides have been made in improving health indicators, there remains a stark contrast in healthcare access between urban and rural areas. The country’s vast geographical expanse has led to a concentration of physicians and healthcare providers in major cities, leaving rural and less populated regions underserved [10]. This imbalance poses a pressing public health challenge, as rural populations often experience higher healthcare needs yet face greater barriers to accessing medical services. In response, the Iranian government has made substantial efforts to address these disparities by establishing targeted healthcare policies aimed at improving resource allocation, particularly in underserved areas. However, much work remains to achieve equity in human resource distribution, which is crucial for enhancing the overall effectiveness and fairness of the healthcare system [11].

To date, the policy for the distribution of the healthcare workforce in Iran appears to require further development. Human resource allocation continues to rely on traditional methods, which may contribute to an uneven distribution and pose potential challenges in delivering healthcare services and meeting the population’s health needs. Given the critical role of accurate data on workforce distribution as a key hospital resource in planning and policymaking to improve healthcare outcomes, this study aims to assess inequalities in the distribution of hospital human resources and their association with hospital mortality rates across the provinces of Iran.

Methods

This study utilized a cross-sectional quantitative design to analyze inequality in the distribution of hospital human resources, both within provinces and across the country. The primary aim was to assess the disparities in resource allocation and their potential impact on healthcare outcomes. Data were collected for the year 2022, the most recent year for which comprehensive data were available through the Hospital Statistics and Information System (AVAB), a nationally recognized database.

Study design and setting

The study covered 31 provinces across Iran, analyzing data from 383 districts, which represent a comprehensive national-level examination of human resource distribution in the healthcare sector. This wide geographical coverage ensures that the analysis accounts for regional differences in health infrastructure, population density, and healthcare demands.

Study variables

The study focuses on three key variables related to human resources in hospitals: “Number of physician per beds (Physician)”, “Number of nurse per beds (Nurses)”, and “Number of other. Staff per bed (Other. Staff)”. These variables were selected as they are critical indicators of healthcare system efficiency, directly impacting patient care quality, hospital capacity, and overall health outcomes [12, 13].

Data sources and collection

The primary data source for this study was the AVAB system, maintained by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MoHME). This system is a repository for hospital-level data, including human resources, infrastructure, and service provision, and is regularly updated to reflect changes in healthcare system capacity.

Data collection

The data collection process involved several key steps:Data extraction: hospital resource data were extracted from the AVAB system for all 383 districts. This included physician, nurse, and staff counts, as well as the number of available hospital beds.Data organization: extracted data were systematically organized into a Microsoft Excel file. The data were categorized by province, district, and variable type (physician, nurse, other staff).Data cleaning: a four-month data management process was conducted to ensure data accuracy. Outliers, missing data points, and inconsistencies were identified and corrected. Data verification was performed by cross-referencing provincial data with local university records and district-level healthcare statistics.

Data validity and reliability

Given the reliance on official government data, the sources were deemed highly reliable. However, to further ensure data accuracy, we performed a validation process. The hospital resource data for a randomly selected subset of provinces were cross-verified with data from universities of medical sciences responsible for healthcare management in those provinces. This comparison involved detailed checks for each variable (physicians, nurses, and other staff) to ensure consistency and reliability across the dataset.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using two complementary approaches: simple descriptive statistics and more advanced inequality measurement methods. To provide a broad overview of human resource distribution, basic statistics (mean, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation) were calculated for each province. These statistics allowed for initial comparisons between provinces and the identification of regions with extreme resource shortages or surpluses.

To provide a straightforward metric of interprovincial inequality, we calculated the relative ratio (RR) for each human resource indicator per bed in each province. The RR was computed by dividing the value of each indicator in a given province by the highest value observed among all provinces for that indicator, which serves as the reference point. An RR closer to 1 indicates proximity to the best-resourced province, while lower values highlight more pronounced disparities in workforce allocation. This measure complements the more comprehensive Gini coefficient analyses by offering an intuitive comparison of provincial gaps in healthcare staffing.

The Gini coefficient, a widely recognized measure of inequality, was used to provide a more precise understanding of resource disparities. The Gini coefficient is a standard metric in inequality research, with values ranging from 0 to 1. A Gini value of 0 represents perfect equality (i.e., resources are evenly distributed across all provinces), while a value closer to 1 indicates significant inequality. Based on previous studies, Gini values below 0.2 were classified as low inequality, while values above 0.4 were classified as severe inequality [14, 15].

To assess the relationship between human resource distribution and hospital mortality rates, we used correlation analysis. Key mortality indicators, including overall hospital mortality, inpatient mortality, and mortality in specialized units (ICU, NICU, PICU),1 were correlated with the distribution of physicians, nurses, and other staff per bed. A Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated to determine the strength and direction of the relationships. Additionally, multivariate regression analysis was performed to control for confounding factors, i.e., hospital size and regional economic conditions, providing a clearer picture of how inequalities in human resources impact mortality outcomes.

All statistical analyses, including calculation of descriptive statistics, RR, Gini coefficients, and regression models, were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) with relevant packages, i.e., ‘ineq’ and ‘reldist’, complemented by data management and cleaning in Microsoft Excel 365.

Interpretation and sensitivity analysis

To further validate the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted. Different inequality measurement techniques (e.g., the relative risk ratio) were applied to test the robustness of the Gini coefficient findings. Additionally, stratified analyses were conducted by region (urban vs. rural provinces) to determine whether inequalities were more pronounced in specific geographic areas.

Discussion

Considering the importance of equitable distribution of human resources to improve the health outcomes of health systems; this study measures geographical disparities in the allocation of the geographic distribution of Physicians, Nurses and Other staff in the country.

Based on results of the study; although, the trend of geographical inequalities in distribution of health manpower showed that the highest value of the Gini coefficient was belonged to physician (0.291) and the lowest level of inequality belonged to nurses (0.298). Nurses and physicians had very similar levels of inequality and both groups experienced moderate but notable disparities in distribution. Potoulos Teodorakis and colleagues also investigated the inequality in the distribution of general practitioners in Albania using the Gini coefficient and the Robin Hood index. Based on their findings; a decreasing trend in the inequality of distribution of GPs in Albania has been observed between 2000 and 2004. According to the authors, reallocation strategies for general practitioners in Albania can be the main reason for the reduction of inequality in primary care workforce distribution [16]. Similarly, Tao Zhang and colleagues suggested that there was inequality in the geographic distribution of health resources. The Gini coefficients were greater than 0.7 in the geographic distribution of institutions, health workers and beds in both the hospital and the primary care sectors that indicates high levels of inequality [17]. In another study, authors showed that the Theil indices of the indicators, except for technicians and physicians, exhibited decreasing trends in hospitals and primary care sectors in China [18].

Unlike the present study, in his study, Mikael Monga showed that there are significant inequalities in the per capita distribution of health workers in different regions of Tanzania, so that the population quintile with the lowest per capita health workers constitutes only 8% of all health workers, while the quintile with the majority of health workers makes up 46% and the inequality in urban and rural areas is quite noticeable. More importantly, when under-five mortality is used as an indicator of healthcare needs, distributional inequalities in Tanzania become more apparent [19]. Also, Farhad Lotfi in his study showed that the distribution of the health sector resources between Iran’s provinces was relatively equal [20]: Tuvia Horev and colleagues showed that physician distribution has become less equitable, while hospital-beds’ equity has increased. Physicians’ distribution exhibited a geographic trend, becoming more equitable in the West of the country [9].

Moreover, our study indicated inequitable distribution of resources among different regions of the country. Like our study, Justyna Rój showed that there are some noticeable differences between macro-regions in the equity level of healthcare human resources distribution in Poland [21]. In another study, Enhong Dong and colleagues showed that healthcare resources increased across Shanghai districts from 2010 to 2016. The central districts had higher ratios on healthcare resource indicators, and faster growth in the bed and nurse indicators. The Theil of the indicators, except for doctors in hospitals, all exhibited descending trends [22].

Moreover, the results of the study showed that there was no significant relationship between the inequality in the number of nursing staff working per hospital bed and hospital mortality. This could be explained by several factors. First, unmeasured confounding factors, i.e., differences in hospital infrastructure, referral patterns, socio-economic status of patients, and variations in case severity, may overshadow the direct impact of nursing staff ratios on mortality. Second, unique features of Iran’s healthcare system, including compulsory service programs for medical graduates and the widespread referral of complex cases to tertiary hospitals in major cities, may buffer the mortality effects of unequal staffing in peripheral areas. Third, cultural and social dynamics, i.e., strong family involvement in caregiving during hospital stays, may mitigate some of the adverse consequences of staff shortages, particularly in nursing care. Unlike the present study, several studies indicated the relationship between the number of nursing staff and patient mortality. For example, in their study, Bond C. et al. investigated the relationship between the characteristics of the hospital and the professional healthcare staff with the hospital mortality rate, and the results of their study indicated the existence of a negative relationship between the number of nurses and hospital mortality, which means that the more the number of nurses per occupied hospital bed decreased, the more the mortality rate of hospitalized patients increased [23]. Also, Jack Needleman and his colleagues showed in their study that there is a significant relationship between the death of hospitalized patients and the increase in the working hours of nurses in each shift; the authors of the study emphasized the necessity of proportionality between the number of patients and the number of nurses and their working hours [13].

In another study, Peter Griffiths et al. have sought to investigate the relationship between mortality and nursing staffing in English hospital centers considering health and medical support staff. In this study, two data sets were examined: administrative data from 137 acute disease centers and a sample of 2917 nurses. The results of the study showed that high bed occupancy and a low number of doctors are related to a high mortality rate. The death rate of patients in hospitals where there were ten patients for every nurse was higher than the death rate in hospitals where there was one nurse for every six patients. These results have been found in the case of surgical patients with less severity [24]. Also, Linda Aiken and colleagues in their study showed that in hospitals that had a low evaluation degree, the decrease or increase in education and the number of nurses had no effect on the treatment outcomes, i.e., the death of patients, but in high-level hospitals, the decrease or increase in education and the number of nurses had an effect on the death of patients [25].

The results of the present study showed that there is a relative relationship between the inequality in the number of active doctors per bed and hospital mortality in 2023. Sudhir Anand and his colleagues stated in their study that the inequality in the distribution of professional manpower is related to the maternal mortality rate, the infant mortality rate, and the under-five mortality rate. This relationship is significantly significant, especially for doctors [26].

In addition to international evidence, the findings of this study can be compared with previous research conducted in Iran, where substantial work has already examined inequalities in health resource distribution. For example, Lotfi et al. (2018) reported relatively equal distribution of healthcare resources, including human resources, across provinces [20], which contrasts with our results showing moderate disparities, particularly among physicians. Similarly, Mirsaeid Seyed Javad et al. (2013) highlighted uneven staffing among Tehran University of Medical Sciences hospitals [11], supporting our conclusion that resource allocation remains inconsistent even within a single province. Other provincial-level studies, i.e., those by Mostafavi et al. (2015) in West Azerbaijan [8] and Mosavizadeh et al. (2008) in Kohgiluyeh and Boyer-Ahmad [7], also documented marked imbalances in the availability of specialist physicians and hospital beds. Taken together, these findings suggest that although some national-level studies have reported relative equity, localized investigations consistently reveal meaningful disparities, which align more closely with the present study. This underscores the importance of examining inequalities at both national and sub-national levels to obtain a comprehensive picture of human resource distribution in Iran.

This study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships and observe temporal trends in human resource distribution and hospital mortality. Future studies employing longitudinal designs are necessary to assess cause–effect dynamics and better evaluate the impact of health policies over time.

Conclusions

Human resources in hospitals are key determinants of healthcare quality, with the availability of physicians, nurses, and support staff directly influencing patient care outcomes. This study highlights the significant disparities in the distribution of hospital human resources across Iran’s provinces, with pronounced inequality in the allocation of physicians, nurses, and other staff. The Gini coefficients indicate notable disparities, particularly among physicians, where some provinces experience far better staffing levels compared to others. These inequalities pose a critical challenge to equitable healthcare access and quality, especially in rural and underserved regions. Addressing these inequities requires ongoing evaluation and the adoption of more effective human resource distribution policies. Targeted interventions, including incentivizing healthcare professionals to work in underserved areas and improving resource allocation models, are essential to enhance the fairness and efficiency of Iran’s healthcare system. Further research should focus on the long-term impact of these policies and explore innovative approaches to mitigate existing disparities.
