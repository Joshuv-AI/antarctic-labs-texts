Improving Sepsis Mortality Prediction With Machine Learning Using Full Region Synthetic Sampling Approach
Authors: Ibrahim A. Amory, Parviz Rashidi Khazaee, Saleh Yousefi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSepsis is a life‐threatening condition and remains a leading cause of mortality in Intensive Care Units (ICUs). Accurate mortality prediction is crucial for optimizing ICU resource allocation. However, severe class imbalance in ICU datasets hampers the generalization performance of machine learning models.MethodsThis study proposes a Full Region Synthetic Sampling Approach (FRSSA), a novel data augmentation method that dynamically balances the minority class distribution based on regional density. Additionally, we introduce Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Tuning (ASST), an optimization‐based strategy that adjusts augmentation weights to enhance model performance. To evaluate model fairness and clinical utility, we propose the Balanced Performance Score (BPS), which integrates accuracy, precision, and recall for personalized ICU risk assessment. Also, we compare two augmentation strategies: (1) Pre‐Splitting: Augmentation occurs before data set splitting, and (2) Post‐Splitting: Augmentation is applied only to the training set to ensure fair evaluation. We utilize publicly available ICU datasets from both the MIMIC‐IV and eICU‐CRD databases and evaluate the performance of Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM models, with hyperparameters optimized using RandomizedSearchCV on the training set.ResultsThe Pre‐Splitting strategy achieved 89.64% accuracy and an Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) of 0.968, but posed a risk of test‐set contamination. In contrast, the Post‐Splitting strategy yielded 78.31% accuracy and an AUROC of 0.7241, ensuring better real‐world generalization. The ASST mechanism optimally balanced 65% interpolation (FRSSA) and 35% expansion (FRSSA), reducing false positive rates and enhancing model fairness.ConclusionFRSSA preserves regional data distribution by generating synthetic samples near the imbalanced regions. ASST dynamically adjusts augmentation ratios to maximize classification performance and generalization. The BPS score‐fusion metric offers a flexible evaluation framework, accommodating varying clinical priorities in ICU settings. Our findings demonstrate that Post‐Splitting augmentation with FRSSA and ASST produces a fairer and more reliable ICU mortality prediction model.

Introduction

Sepsis is a life‐threatening condition and a dysregulated immune response to infection, resulting in organ dysfunction and increased mortality in intensive care units (ICU). The ability to predict sepsis mortality accurately and promptly can enhance care for patients, as medical interventions can be expedited. We note that the complexity of clinical data and the imbalance between survivors and non‐survivors pose challenges for consistently reliable prediction models [1].

Research on statistical techniques for sepsis mortality and predictive approaches in machine learning has also been quite popular. Jiayi Gao et al. presented several ML tools on the MIMIC‐IV data set on sepsis disease, using SMOTE for data augmentation and achieving an AUROC of 0.94 and SHAP analysis highlighting SOFA scores as significant predictors [2]. Kong et al. implemented GB Machines and LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression on MIMIC‐III, cervical cancer prediction, achieving an AUROC of 0.845, but lacked data augmentation strategies [3]. Moor et al. reviewed a wide range of predictive ML models across sepsis disease datasets, emphasizing variability in definitions, with AUROCs ranging from 0.78 to 0.99 [4]. Weng et al. developed the POSMI (The Prediction of Sepsis Mortality in ICU) score using LR, achieving an AUROC of 0.831, outperforming SOFA and APACHE IV, though limited by geographic validation [5]. Su et al. utilized SMOTE with RF and XGB for mortality prediction but relied on single‐center data [6]. Zeng et al. proposed a blending model, achieving an AUROC of 0.806, though constrained by a lack of augmentation techniques [7]. Rahman et al. used SMOTE‐TOMEK LINK in a stacking‐based classifier, achieving an AUROC of 0.99, highlighting SHAP's interpretability, yet relying on MIMIC‐III [8]. Jeon et al. used MLP for pneumonia mortality prediction with an AUROC of 0.838, constrained by single‐center data [9]. Zhang et al. employed XGB with SHAP, achieving an AUROC of 0.94, emphasizing biomarkers, though requiring external validation [10].

In imbalanced datasets, traditional approaches to handling class imbalance, such as SMOTE and ADASYN, have been widely used to overcome the imbalance and improve ML model performance. However, when applying these methods, the distribution of the minority class is not taken into account. Most existing work uses either SMOTE or ADASYN, which may result in suboptimal augmentation ratios and increased overfitting risk in certain regions of data. However, many previous studies employ Pre‐Splitting augmentation, which artificially inflates performance metrics as there is contamination between the test and training set (as opposed to Post‐Splitting augmentation, which offers a more realistic evaluation of performance) [11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

To tackle these problems, we present the Full Region Synthetic Sampling Approach (FRSSA), a novel augmentation paradigm that adaptively samples synthetic samples over the entire data distribution. Once a region is identified, FRSSA adaptively decides whether to use interpolation‐based augmentation (for dense regions) or density‐aware expansion (for sparse regions), thus avoiding over‐representing pieces of the original data set with synthetic points.

We also present Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Tuning (ASST), a self‐optimizing framework for tuning augmentation ratios used in FRSSA. Instead of using augmentation evenly, ASST adaptively adjusts the ratio of interpolation and expansion augmentation to maximize classification performance. ASST is controlled by an optimization function that considers accuracy, precision, and recall, and produces a perfect augmentation for each data set setup.

To enable better model evaluation, we introduce the Balanced Performance Score(BPS), a new measure that combines accuracy, precision, and recall in a single weighted equation. In contrast to traditional evaluation metrics that focus only on accuracy or AUROC, BPS enables adaptive weighting of classification trade‐offs based on ICU decision‐making priorities. This ensures that models can be truly optimized based on whether reducing false positives(FP) or false negatives(FN) is more clinically important.

To assess these contributions, we use Pre‐Splitting Augmentation (applying the augmentation before splitting the training/test data), contrasted with Post‐Splitting Augmentation (applying the augmentation only to the training set to preserve the unbiased evaluation of the model). The set of machine learning models is RF [20]., Decision Tree [21], GB [22], XGB [23], LGBM [9], Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) [24], and Support Vector Machines (SVM) [17]. The Hyperparameter optimization using RandomizedSearchCV [23, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32] ensures the best configuration for each model.

The rest of this study is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the materials and methods. Section 3 describes the findings. Section 4 discusses performance, compares results from previous studies, highlights limitations, and proposes future research directions. Section 5 concludes the paper.

Materials and Methods

Data Set and Preprocessing

This study uses publicly available data concerning ICU patients from the MIMIC‐IV database. As discussed in the previous study [2]. To ensure consistent data and eliminate missing values, we used the pre‐cleaned data set. This included dealing with missing values, handling categorical variables, eliminating duplicates, and creating new features, all of which improved the reliability of the data for predictive modeling.

The hospital_expire_flag is the main target label, indicating in‐hospital mortality as a binary indicator (0 = survival, 1 = mortality) with 5,201 patients. One important challenge with this data set is that there is a significant class imbalance (which is very common in ICU datasets), with far fewer mortality cases than survival cases. This could introduce bias in the prediction, affecting modeling performance. Toa address this, a structured augmentation framework was established meaningful data representation and class distribution were improved, leading to more robust and generalizable machine learning models.

In addition, to validate the effectiveness and generalizability of the proposed methods, an external data set from the eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU‐CRD) [33, 34, 35] was also used. After rigorous preprocessing and filtering to include only sepsis‐related cases, a final cohort of 35,491 patients was obtained. This external validation demonstrates that the developed models and augmentation strategies perform consistently well across different datasets, further confirming their robustness and applicability in real‐world clinical scenarios.

Data Augmentation

Data augmentation is a stochastic preprocessing technique that is used to increase the generalization capabilities of machine learning models without collectingm more real samples from the world. Especially in predictive modeling, and especially when the number of instances is imbalanced, augmentation allows for compensating for the fact that there are not enough training instances for an underrepresented class in the data set. This structured augmentation process enhances model fairness, robustness, and real‐world implications, facilitating the transition of learning from controlled to open environmentsa and leading to more reliable and clinically relevant predictions.

Full Region Synthetic Sampling Approach (FRSSA)

To mitigate the impact of imbalance without losing the holistic structure of the data, we propose a novel and unique Data Augmentation algorithm: the Full Region Synthetic Sampling Approach (FRSSA), which adaptively constructs samples based on the density of minority instances. In contrast with conventionala augmentation techniques, which adhere to fixed oversampling restrictions in the feature space, FRSSA examines a restricted subset of the feature space and incrementally modifies the placement of samples in the feature space to promote more realistic synthetic data generation [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47].

Mathematical Formulation of FRSSA

FRSSA is a density‐aware augmentation method that generates synthetic samples based on the local structure of the minority class. Formally, this method is defined as the following: Xnew=λXnearest+(1−λ)Xtarget+δf(D,Xtarget)

Where:

Xnew = Newly generated synthetic sample.

Xnearest = Closest minority class neighbor.

Xtarget = Minority class instance being augmented.

λ = Dynamic sampling weight based on feature space density.

δ= Adaptive dispersion factor ensuring structural consistency.

f(D,Xtarget) = Function modifying sample spread based on regional density.

D = Density measure of the local region surrounding Xtarget.

FRSSA differs from the conventional augmentation techniques, which assume that the minority class is uniformly distributed across the feature space. Rather, it examines the attributes of home neighborhoods and leverages adaptive transformations to position samples more accurately and evenly.

Implementation of Frssa

Following a structured methodology, the FRSSA approach, implemented to ensure effective sample generation and avoid unnecessary redundancy in synthetic data, maximized data utility. The key steps are as follows: 1.Data set Partitioning: The data set was preprocessed and scaled to define the system between the different feature distributions.2.Neighborhood Density Estimation: Provided with an unbalanced data set, the 75th percentile of all minority class distances computed concerning k‐nearest neighbors (k‐NN) was used to establish density thresholds.3.Sample Generation Based on Density Analysis: Regions with high density were assigned low dispersion values δ to maintain their natural structure.Sparse regions were assigned higher dispersion values δ to encourage diversity and prevent overfitting. 4.Final Data Balancing: The newly generated samples were combined with the original training data, and the resulting data set was manually classified accordingly to preserve proportionality.

Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Tuning (ASST)

Although FRSSA generates balanced synthetic samples, it is difficult to find the best augmentation weight (λ) for synthetic samples. The common methodology is to use the same ratio of interpolation and expansion augmentation, which is not necessarily optimal. To address this problem, we present Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Tuning (ASST), a self‐adaptive framework that tunes FRSSA augmentation parameters by utilizing model confidence scores.

Mathematical Formulation of ASST

ASST adaptively regulates FRSSA augmentation ratio runs dynamically based on model uncertainty and sample diversity: λopt=argminλ∑i=1N11+e−Ci×Di

λopt = Optimized augmentation weight dynamically selected by ASST.

Ci = Model confidence score for each sample (logits from classifier output).

Di = Density measure of each sample's local region.

e = Exponential function to normalize confidence values.

N = Total number of samples.

ASST Works in FRSSA Optimization

Evaluates Model Confidence: Ci is calculated per minority‐class instance to understand how “uncertain” the model is in its classification.

Assigns Dynamic Augmentation Weights: Instances with low confidence scores Ci are assigned higher augmentation ratios, ensuring that more synthetic samples are generated where the model struggles.

Balances Data Density: The density measure Di ensures that ASST does not over‐augment regions that are already well‐represented.

Optimizes λ Selection: ASST selects the λ that minimizes the uncertainty‐weighted augmentation imbalance.

Experimental Design

To test the effect of augmentation techniques on model performance and generalizability, two augmentation strategies were compared:

Pre‐Splitting Augmentation Strategy

In this strategy, the FRSSA was used over the entire data set before dividing it into training and testing sets. By doing so, all the augmented data were present in both the training and testing sets, as illustrated in Figure 2a. During this technique development, we conduct an evaluation to improve benchmarking results, ^but incur the risk of test‐set contamination, a well‐known source of model generalization over‐estimation.

(a) Pre‐Splitting augmentation strategy, (b) Post‐Splitting augmentation strategy.

Post‐Splitting Augmentation Strategy

In this strategy, we implemented FRSSA on the training data only, followed by dividing the data set into training and testing subsets, as outlined in Figure 2b. This maintains the integrity of the test set in the real world and gives a much more accurate view of the model's generalization performance.

Machine Learning Models

In this respect, to compare the efficiency of these hybrid augmentation strategies, different ML models are employed, such as RF, DT, GB, XGB, LGBM, MLP, and SVM. Each of these was chosen because of its distinct nature, from interpretability to robustness when handling imbalanced data [48, 49].

RandomSearchCV [50] was used to efficiently search for the best hyperparameter values within predefined ranges. For the RF classifier, key hyperparameters such as the number of estimators (100 to 500), maximum tree depth (10 to None), minimum samples required at each split (2, 5, 10), minimum samples per leaf (1, 2, 4), and the use of bootstrap sampling (True or False) were systematically optimized. Models that were not included in the tuning process used default parameter values to ensure a fair baseline for comparison [51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57].

Balanced Performance Score (BPS)

Being traditional metrics, accuracy and AUROC do not directly target ICU‐specific clinical priorities. To address this, we introduce the Balanced Performance Score (BPS) which takes into account accuracy, precision, and recall, forming a performance evaluation framework that can be tailored to specific needs —whether the goal is to minimize false positives (FPs) or false negatives (FNs)— BPS adjusts accordingly by adding more weight to Precision (b) or Recall (c). The formal mathematical description of BPS is: BPS=a.A+b.P+c.R

Where: a, b, c = Adjustable weight coefficients allowing clinicians to prioritize different aspects of classification performance.A, P, R = Accuracy, Precision, and Recall, respectively.

Results

This study highlights the importance of an augmentation strategy for dealing with class imbalance and ensuring models of ICU mortality maintain generalizability. Pre‐Splitting Augmentation was chosen to be compared against previous works, applying augmentation to the data set before its split. This approach is at risk of test set contamination and thus might lead to overly rosy performance evaluation metrics. To overcome this limitation, Post‐Splitting Augmentation was introduced, where augmentation was applied only to the training set while retaining the exactness of the test data to simulate the real‐world data.

Clinical Interpretability of SHAP Results

The SHAP analysis identified avg_urineoutput, resp_rate_mean, and coma as the most impactful features for predicting ICU mortality due to sepsis (Figure 6). These findings are clinically plausible and vital. For instance, urine output is a crucial parameter for assessing kidney function and perfusion in patients. Decreased urine output is a common feature of multiorgan dysfunction, and lower urine volume has been linked to a worse outcome in septic patients. In respiratory failure caused by sepsis, the respiratory rate is often one of the earliest changes observed in the patient's vital signs. The coma feature (derived from the Glasgow Coma Scale or equivalent metrics) indicates neurological status, and patients with an altered consciousness level have a substantially worse outcome. These results are consistent with common ICU decision‐making strategies and clinical rules, and verify the model's interpretability as well as suggestibility for actual critical care practice.

Discussion

Predicting sepsis mortality for patients in an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is a critical challenge. Thus, this study proposes a novel augmentation strategy known as Full Region Synthetic Sampling Approach (FRSSA), which enables accurate prediction of sepsis mortality. Different from any method of traditional augmentation, FRSSA utilizes a region‐based augmentation strategy, where the synthetic sample generation is accelerated or reduced based on the data density and model uncertainty around the region. Furthermore, we investigate two augmentation strategies, Pre‐Splitting and Post‐Splitting, for their influence on the test set integrity and performance in real‐world scenarios.

Gao et al. [2] Utilized SMOTE to address class imbalance, but their approach did not incorporate region‐specific augmentation or compare augmentation strategies in terms of test set generalization. Kong et al. [3] Applied Gradient Boosting Machines on imbalanced datasets without augmentation, leading to suboptimal predictive performance. Similarly, Moor et al. [4] Identified high variability in feature selection and evaluation methodologies across studies, reinforcing the need for standardized augmentation and evaluation frameworks in ICU predictive modeling.

Effectiveness of FRSSA With Post‐Splitting Augmentation

The results from Table 6 indicate the insight gained from FRSSA in the Post‐Splitting Augmentation process, where augmentation was solely performed on the training set, keeping the original class distribution in the evaluation set. The baseline model (non‐augmented) reached 0.768 for accuracy, 0.5 for precision, and 0.066 for recall, an indication of a strong model bias towards the majority class.

We perform dynamic optimization of augmentation weights by ASST (Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Tuning). The optimal performance was found at an interpolation weight of 0.65 and an expansion weight of 0.35, producing an accuracy of 78.31% (95% CI: [76.05%, 81.42%]) and a precision of 0.6596. While previous works simply adopted fixed augmentation ratios, ASST, as an example of FRSSA, utilizes adaptive FRSSA parameters, which prove to enhance the test set accuracy significantly.

As a result, the highest recall (0.3029) was found at interpolation weight 0.00 and expansion weight 1.00 (as seen in Table 6), which demonstrates that increasing the expansion factor improves recall. High recall is significant in predicting sepsis mortality, in which minimizing false negatives (FN) is critical to ensure that critically ill patients receive appropriate treatment promptly.

To further validate the generalizability of the proposed methods, the FRSSA framework with ASST weight optimization was evaluated on the eICU‐CRD data set, and the results are summarized in Table 7. The baseline model without augmentation achieved an accuracy of 0.8618, a precision of 0.6553, and a recall of only 0.1477, again demonstrating a strong bias toward the majority class and poor sensitivity in identifying mortality cases. With ASST weight optimization applied, significant improvements were observed across multiple configurations. The highest accuracy (0.8697) was achieved at an interpolation weight of 0.05 and expansion weight of 0.95, along with a precision of 0.7005 and a recall of 0.1918, showing an improvement in specificity and sensitivity compared to the baseline.

These results confirm that the trends observed in MIMIC‐IV (Table 6) are consistently reflected in the eICU‐CRD data set (Table 7), supporting the robustness and generalizability of the proposed FRSSA and ASST methods across different clinical datasets. Moreover, these findings highlight the importance of our proposed Balanced Performance Score (BPS) in effectively evaluating model performance under such critical clinical scenarios.

Importance of BPS for Balancing Model Performance

Because FP and FN are differently harmful in the ICU context, model performance should be evaluated in this context. In some circumstances, decreasing FP is essential to optimize ICU use, whereas in other circumstances, decreasing FN is paramount to avoid neglecting patients at high risk of deterioration. The importance of our proposed Balanced Performance Score (BPS) is here.

Limitations and Future Works

Although FRSSA and ASST have demonstrated their effectiveness in enhancing class balance and improving generalizability, this study remains limited by the use of publicly available datasets (MIMIC‐IV and eICU‐CRD), which, while comprehensive, may not fully capture variations across all clinical environments. However, the inclusion of the eICU‐CRD data set provided a crucial external validation step, confirming the robustness and applicability of the proposed methods across different ICU populations.

Despite these validations, further external testing using independent datasets from additional institutions or geographic regions is necessary to assess the broader applicability of the proposed methods fully. Future work will focus on expanding validation efforts across more diverse clinical settings to confirm model generalization further.

Integrating FRSSA with GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks) can also be applied to induce further robustness of the models by boosting feature diversity, especially in minority clinical cases. It is also essential to study domain adaptation techniques to ensure that such performance generalizes across diverse ICU settings. In Vol 2′, the BPS could be fine‐tuned through collaboration with ICU clinicians to configure precision and recall weightings according to local clinical priorities. These improvements are expected to reinforce the real‐world applicability of FRSSA in predicting sepsis mortality.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a full‐region synthetic sampling approach (FRSSA) as a novel augmentation approach for imbalance issues in sepsis mortality prediction. These synthetic samples are generated dynamically using the density position of the sample in the feature space, allowing FRSSA to maintain the structural integrity of the data set while augmenting scarce data points. Adaptive Synthetic Sampling Tuning (ASST) not only enhances augmentation but also fine‐tunes it by creating samples based on model certainty and regional density. Furthermore, Balanced Performance Score (BPS) offers a versatile performance metric that allows for focusing on what matters clinically by adjusting the accuracy‐precision‐recall trade‐off. Key differences in performance and generalization were observed through a comparative analysis of Pre‐Splitting and Post‐Splitting augmentation strategies. In the Pre‐Splitting strategy in which FRSSA was applied, before the data set was divided into training and test sets, Random Forest (RF) had an accuracy of 89.64% and an ROC‐AUC of 0.968, indicating a remarkable predictive capacity. Cross‐validation verified that this approach was stable, obtaining an accuracy of 89.64% (95% CI: [0.8826, 0.9120]) and a mean ROC‐AUC of 96.8% (95% CI: [0.9615, 0.9760]). But this technique leads to test set contamination, possibly falsely inflating model performance. Alternatively, in Post‐Splitting augmentation, where FRSSA has been applied only on the training set, the strength of the test set is preserved, which is indicative of the actual performance of the model. Using custom ASST weight optimization, an optimal augmentation ratio (0.65 interpolation, 0.35 expansion) led to 78.31% accuracy with a ROC‐AUC of 0.7241, keeping precision (0.6596) and recall balanced, at the expense of overfitting. This is further evidenced by the Confusion Matrix output, highlighting that Post‐Splitting augmentation is more applicable due to its efficacy in reducing false positives in the ICU decision. The results showcase an augmentation‐agnostic effect that should be carefully considered when attempting to derive strategies from real‐world applications, especially in the critical care setting of the ICU, where the use of predictive models shapes clinical decision‐making.
