A note on defining positive definite functions
Lucas da Cunha Godoy, Marcos Oliveira Prates, Fernando Andrés Quintana

Abstract
A fundamental requirement in spatial statistics is that covariance functions are positive definite. While many positive definite functions are known for Euclidean spaces, their positive definiteness may not extend to non-Euclidean spaces. We present a way to derive valid positive definite functions for spatial statistics on non-Euclidean geometries. Our approach leverages overlooked results due to Schoenberg, 1938 to establish conditions under which covariance functions that are valid on Euclidean spaces remain valid on the domain of interest. This approach provides a more accessible and direct framework for spatial statisticians with diverse backgrounds. Keywords: Isometric embedding; Isotropic covariance functions; Spatial statistics.



1
Introduction

Positive definite functions are fundamental in various areas of statistics,
including statistical learning, time series analysis, and spatial and
spatiotemporal statistics. For instance, the covariance function of random
fields must be positive-definite (PD) to ensure their
validity
(
Stein, 1999
, Ch. 2.1)
. Gaussian Processes (GPs), a
popular class of random fields with attractive analytical properties, are
completely characterized by a PD covariance function along with a mean
function (often taken to be constant). The analytical simplicity and flexibility
of GPs have turned them into a popular tool for spatial generalized mixed
effects models
(
Diggle et al., 1998
)
, spatially varying regression
coefficients
(
Gelfand et al., 2003
)
, addressing spatial misalignment
issues
(
Banerjee and Gelfand, 2002
)
, modeling non-linear
relationships
(
Williams and Rasmussen, 2006
)
, and many other applications.

While many PD functions are known for
\mathbb{R}^{p}
equipped with the Euclidean
distance
(
Cressie, 1993
, Ch. 2.5.1)
, their positive definiteness
may not extend to non-Euclidean spaces
(
Curriero, 2006
)
. In fact, a
function that is PD on
\mathbb{R}^{1}
may not even be PD on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, although the
reverse is true
(
Cressie, 1993
, p. 22)
. Recently, there has been a
growing interest in PD functions to characterize GPs on Riemannian
manifolds
(
Borovitskiy et al., 2020
;
Li et al., 2023
)
. The particular case of
the sphere has been extensively studied
(
Banerjee, 2005
;
Gneiting, 2013
;
Guinness and Fuentes, 2016
;
Porcu et al., 2021
)
. Some
functions exhibit the interesting property of being PD on both the

p-1
-dimensional sphere (
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
) and
p
-dimensional
Euclidean space
(
Porcu et al., 2016
)
.

We present a way to derive valid PD functions for spatial statistics on
non-Euclidean geometries. Existing methods often rely on Harmonic
Analysis
(
Berg et al., 1984
)
, which may be unfamiliar to practitioners
confronted with data on non-Euclidean spaces. As an alternative, we leverage
overlooked results due
Schoenberg, 1938

(
Wells and Williams, 1975
, also available
in)
to establish conditions under which covariance
functions that are valid on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
remain valid on the domain of interest. This
approach provides a more accessible and direct framework for spatial
statisticians of diverse backgrounds.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2

presents the definitions necessary for the upcoming results. In
Section
3
, we present the main results, and
Section
4
illustrates its application with examples. Finally,
we conclude with a discussion in Section
5
.

2
Notation and Terminology

We begin defining the necessary concepts. Let
D
be an index set and
d
a
real-valued function on
D\times D
. For any
r,s,t\in D
,
d
is called a
metric if the following postulates hold:
d(s,t)=d(t,s)
(symmetry),

d(t,t)=0
and
d(s,t)\geq 0
(nonnegativeness),

d(s,t)\leq d(s,r)+d(r,t)
(triangle inequality), and
d(s,t)=0

implies
s=t
(positiveness). A function
d
satisfying only the symmetry
and nonnegativeness postulates is called a quasi-metric. If triangle inequality
also holds, then
d
is a semi-metric. A (quasi-, semi-) metric space,

(D,d)
, is an index set
D
equipped with a (quasi-, semi-) metric
d
.

A function
g\,:\,D\times D\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is conditionally negative
definite (CND), if it satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}b_{i}b_{j}g(s_{i},s_{j})\leq 0,

(1)

for
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\in D
and any real numbers

b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}
such that
\sum_{i}b_{i}=0

(
Rao, 1984
)
. On the other
hand, a PD function
r\,:\,D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
must satisfy

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}r(s_{i},s_{j})\geq 0,

for any real numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
. Lastly, a real function
r
defined on
non-negative real numbers is radial positive definite (RPD) on the (quasi-)
metric space
(D,d)
provided that
r
is continuous and

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}r(d(s_{i},s_{j}))\geq 0,

(2)

for real numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
and
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\in D
. It should
be noted that isotropic covariance functions represent a special case within the
broader class of RPD functions.

Isometric embeddings, a special class of mappings between spaces, are crucial to
establish our main result. An isometric embedding preserves the distances
between points in the original space when mapped to the target space. More
formally, a space
(D,d)
can be isometrically embedded in another space

(D^{\ast},d^{\ast})
if there exists a map
\phi\,:\,D\to D^{\ast}
such that

d^{\ast}(\phi(s),\phi(t))=d(s,t),

for any
s,t\in D

(
Wells and Williams, 1975
)
.

A Hilbert space is a vector space endowed with an inner product. In fact, there
exists an equivalence between a
L_{2}
space and real Hilbert space

H

(
Schoenberg, 1938
)
. The
L_{2}
space can be viewed as a special
case of a broader class of metric spaces, called
\alpha
-norm metric spaces. We
denote by
L_{\alpha}
a normed metric space on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
equipped with the following
metric:

\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\rVert_{\alpha}={\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lvert x_{i}-y_{i}\rvert^{\alpha}\right)}^{1/\alpha},

where
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. This metric quantifies the distance between
vectors
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We call the metric above the

L_{\alpha}
metric. When
\alpha=2
, we recover the familiar Euclidean distance.

3
Results

The following theorem is a known result derived in
Schoenberg, 1938
,
and is also stated in
Wells and Williams, 1975
.

Theorem 1
.

A metric space
(D,d)
can be isometrically embedded in a Hilbert space
if and only if
d^{2}
is CND.

For a proof, we refer to Theorem 2.4 in
Wells and Williams, 1975
. An
interesting consequence from Theorem
1
is the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
(D,d)
be a metric space such that
d
is CND. Then,

\exp\{-\kappa d^{\nu}\}
is RPD for
\nu\in(0,1]
and
\kappa>0
.

Proof.

If
d
is CND, Theorem
1
ensures that
(D,d^{1/2})
can be
isometrically embedded in a Hilbert space
H
. Consequently, there exists a
map
\phi:D\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

d(s,t)=\lVert\phi(s)-\phi(t)\rVert_{2}
for all
s,t\in D
. By
Corollary 4.8 of
Wells and Williams, 1975
, the function

\exp\{-\kappa\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2}^{2\nu}\}
is RPD on
L_{2}
for

\nu\in(0,1]
. Therefore, for any set of points

s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\in D
and real numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}\exp\{-\kappa d(s_{i},s_{j})^{\nu}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}a_{j}\exp\{-\kappa\lVert\phi(s_{i})-\phi(s_{j})\rVert_{2}^{2\nu}\}\geq 0.

This establishes that
\exp\{-\kappa d^{\nu}\}
is RPD on
(D,d)
.
∎

Corollary
1
establishes that the powered exponential function with
power between zero and one is always positive definite on metric spaces equipped
with CND metrics (on the original scale). This has significant implications for
constructing valid covariance functions in non-Euclidean spaces.

Theorem
2
extends Theorem
1
in the context of RPD
functions and show necessary conditions for constructing valid isotropic
covariance functions on non-Euclidean spaces equipped with CND metrics.

Theorem 2
.

Let
D
be a non-Euclidean space and
d
be a CND metric. Then, any RPD
function in
L_{2}
(in any dimension) is also RPD on
(D,d^{1/2})
.

Proof.

From Theorem
1
, we have that
(D,d^{1/2})
is a metric space
that can be isometrically embedded in
H
, and, thus, in
L_{2}
due to
their equivalence
(
Rao, 1984
)
. Therefore, RPD functions on

p
-dimensional
L_{2}
for any
p
are also RPD on
(D,d^{1/2})
.
∎

The practical implications of the above results for spatial statistics have been
largely overlooked. These results provide many candidate PD functions, such as
those belonging to the Matérn and rational quadratic families, to any domain
equipped with a CND metric. However, their main application currently lies in
the definition of RPD functions based on
\alpha
-norms
(
Curriero, 2006
)
.

While verifying that a function is CND can be challenging, several established
results can aid this process. For instance, any
\alpha
-norm
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is CND
(
Richards, 1985
)
. In higher dimensions (i.e.,

\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
p\geq 3
),
L_{\alpha}
metrics are CND
for
\alpha\in(0,2]
. Furthermore, Theorem 3.6 in
Meckes, 2013

provides two particularly relevant cases for spatial statistics: First, if a
metric
d
is CND, then so is
d^{\nu}
, for
\nu\in(0,1]
; second, the Great
Circle (GC) distance on round spheres is CND.

4
Examples

The Matérn covariance function is a popular choice for modeling spatial data
due to its flexibility and interpretability. It is an isotropic covariance (and,
consequently, RPD) function, defined as:

r(s_{1},s_{2};\theta)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2^{\nu-1}\Gamma(\nu)}(\kappa\lVert s_{1}-s_{2}\rVert_{2})^{\nu}\mathcal{K}_{\nu}(\kappa\lVert s_{1}-s_{2}\rVert_{2}),

(3)

where
\sigma^{2}
represents the marginal variance, controlling the overall
variability of the spatial process.
\nu
is a smoothness parameter such that
higher values of
\nu
correspond to smoother spatial processes.

\mathcal{K}_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of
order
\nu
. The parameter
\kappa
is a scale parameter, inversely related to the
range of spatial dependence. In practice, we often reparameterize the
Matérn function in terms of the practical range, given by

\rho=\sqrt{8\nu}/\kappa
. The practical range (
\rho
) has a more intuitive
interpretation: it represents the approximate distance at which the spatial
correlation between two points drops to approximately
0.1
. The Matérn
function is RPD on
(\mathbb{R}^{p},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{2})
for any dimension

p

(
Bevilacqua et al., 2019
)
.

The following examples illustrate the correlation structures induced by the
Matérn family when applied to the square-root of a CND metric. For each
example, simulated datasets were used to empirically assess the positive
definiteness of the resulting covariance matrices, providing practical support
for the theoretical results presented in Theorem
1
. Specifically, we
evaluated the Matérn-induced covariance matrix with commonly used values for
the smoothness parameter (
\nu\in\{0.5,1.5,2.5\}
), and a practical range
(
\rho
) spanning weak to strong spatial dependence. In all scenarios, we fixed

\sigma^{2}=1
. This simplification is justified by the fact that the product of a
non-negative constant and a PD function is always PD.

4.1
PD functions and
L_{\alpha}
distances

The previous section established the CND property of
L_{\alpha}
metrics on

\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Theorem
1
implies that functions of the Euclidean distance,
which are RPD in any dimension, are also RPD when evaluated at the square root
of any
L_{\alpha}
metric.

We generated a regular
25\times 25
grid spanning the unit square,

[0,1]\times[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We then calculated the distance matrices induced by
the
L_{1}
and
L_{10}
norms. These distance matrices were transformed by
taking their element-wise square root. We used the resulting matrices as input
to the Matérn covariance function, with the parameter combinations pointed
out in Section
4
. This setup allows for empirical investigation
of the positive definiteness arising from combining the Matérn function and
the square root of
L_{\alpha}
metrics.

Figure
1
displays the smallest eigenvalue of each resulting
covariance matrix (
\lambda_{1}
). The panel columns in the figure correspond to the
three distinct smoothness (
\nu
) values considered, while the rows depict the
two different norms. The positive definiteness of a matrix implies that all its
eigenvalues are positive. The fact that
\lambda_{1}>0
in all cases empirically
verifies the PD of the constructed covariance matrices and thus provides
practical evidence consistent with Theorem
2
results.

Figure 1:
Smallest eigenvalue (
\lambda_{1}
) for each correlation matrix generated
using the Matérn function and the square root of the
L_{\alpha}
distances. All
values are positive, indicating positive definiteness.

4.2
Sphere and the Great Circle distance

The GC (or geodetic) distance between points
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
is
the length of the arc of a great circle joining the two points. In other words,
the distance is simply the sphere’s radius multiplied by the angle between the
vectors
\bm{O}s_{1}
and
\bm{O}s_{2}
, where
\bm{O}
represents
sphere’s origin
(
Banerjee, 2005
)
. Theorem 3.6 in

Meckes, 2013
proves that the GC distance is CND on
spheres. Therefore, by Theorem
2
, functions of the Euclidean distance,
which are RPD in any dimension, are also RPD when evaluated at the square root
the GC distance. We illustrate this with the Matérn covariance function,
examining its behavior in this context.

We generated a regular
50\times 50
grid spanning the unit sphere of dimension 2,

\mathbb{S}^{2}=\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,\lVert s\rVert_{2}=1
}. We
computed the GC distance matrix and applied the square root to each of its
elements. As in the previous example, these distances were used as input to the
Matérn covariance function to produce correlation matrices. This setup allows
us to empirically investigate the positive definiteness arising from the
combination of the Matérn function and the square root of the GC distance.

Figure
2
plots the smallest eigenvalue (
\lambda_{1}
) of the
Matérn covariance matrices generated using the square root of the GC
distance. The combinations of parameters are analogous to the previous
example. In all cases,
\lambda_{1}>0
, empirically supporting the positive
definiteness of the resulting covariance matrices and providing practical
evidence consistent with Theorem
2
.

Figure 2:
Smallest eigenvalues (
\lambda_{1}
) of correlation matrices generated
using the Matérn function with the square root of the GC distance, for

\nu\in{0.5,1.5,2.5}
. Positive values provide evidence of positive
definiteness.

5
Discussion

The results presented in this paper have significant implications for
constructing valid covariance functions in Euclidean and non-Euclidean
spaces. By leveraging Schoenberg’s overlooked results, we have established a
more accessible and direct framework for spatial statisticians with varying
levels of mathematical training. Theorem
1
establishes sufficient
conditions for embedding a metric space into a Hilbert space. Consequently, it
also determines the conditions under which valid covariance functions in
Euclidean spaces remain applicable in a given domain. Using
Theorem
1
, Theorem
2
provides a direct way to construct PD
functions in non-Euclidean spaces. Moreover, Corollary
1
sheds light
on why the powered exponential (with
\nu\in(0,1]
) covariance function is RPD
on the sphere equipped with the GC distance
(
Gneiting, 2013
)
.

The examples in Section
4
demonstrated how to apply the
theoretical results to different scenarios. The empirical assessment of the
positive definiteness of the resulting covariance matrices offered practical
support for the theoretical findings. Although the current study provides a
comprehensive analysis of the construction of valid covariance functions in
Euclidean and non-Euclidean spaces, future research must investigate the
properties of such functions. For example, study the impact of the smoothness of
the resulting functions in the domain of interest.
