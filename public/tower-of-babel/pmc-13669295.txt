GLP-1 receptor agonists and dental sedation: a potential blind spot in preoperative assessment
Authors: Toru Yamamoto, Naotaka Kishimoto
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have transitioned rapidly from specialized diabetes therapy to broader application in obesity and weight management. With this expansion, perioperative concerns have emerged regarding delayed gastric emptying, residual gastric contents, regurgitation, and the risk of pulmonary aspiration during general anesthesia or deep sedation. Recent multidisciplinary recommendations have outlined perioperative strategies for patients receiving GLP-1 RAs, addressing fasting protocols, medication management, gastrointestinal symptoms, and risk stratification [1]. However, this issue may also warrant specific attention in the context of deep dental sedation and dental general anesthesia, in which protective airway reflexes may be impaired or the airway may remain unsecured. Minimal and moderate sedation are not the primary focus of this Letter, although unintended progression to a deeper level of sedation remains possible.

Dental anesthesia and sedation are frequently performed in ambulatory settings and may be perceived as less invasive than hospital-based surgery. This may create a potential vulnerability in preoperative assessment. Patients receiving GLP-1 RAs for weight management, including treatment obtained through private or online services, may not consider themselves medically complex and may not regard these injections as medications that should be reported before anesthesia. In addition, private prescriptions or non-hospital-based weight-management treatments may not always appear in routine medical records or medication lists.

The clinical relevance of this issue is supported by recent evidence. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 randomized and observational studies encompassing 2143 adult patients undergoing procedures under anesthesia or sedation, demonstrated that GLP-1 RA use was associated with increased preprocedural gastrointestinal symptoms and elevated residual gastric content despite adherence to fasting guidelines [2]. These findings do not indicate that all patients receiving GLP-1 RAs are unsuitable for sedation; rather, they underscore the importance of identifying such patients during preoperative assessment. Pulmonary aspiration and regurgitation have been reported in patients receiving semaglutide for weight loss despite adherence to standard fasting protocols, further underscoring the potential clinical consequences of incomplete medication histories [3].

A balanced interpretation remains essential. In a large cohort study that included 366,476 adults undergoing common surgical procedures, preoperative GLP-1 RA use was not associated with significantly increased odds of postoperative aspiration pneumonia after adjusting for patient characteristics, comorbidities, and surgical factors [4]. Importantly, postoperative aspiration pneumonia represents a distinct outcome from residual gastric contents, regurgitation, or aspiration occurring at induction of anesthesia or during deep sedation. Therefore, the absence of increased postoperative aspiration pneumonia does not necessarily exclude increased residual gastric contents or regurgitation risk in selected patients. The practical objective should therefore emphasize risk identification and individualized perioperative planning rather than automatic discontinuation or case cancellation.

For dental anesthesia and sedation providers, the clinical implication is clear: GLP-1 RAs should not be regarded solely as antidiabetic medications. Their increasing use for weight management, including among ambulatory patients who otherwise appear uncomplicated, necessitates direct inquiry during preoperative assessment. The Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency has advised that patients receiving GLP-1 receptor agonists or dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 receptor agonists may retain residual gastric contents despite standard preoperative fasting when undergoing general anesthesia or deep sedation. Furthermore, patients using these agents for weight management through private or online services may not readily disclose their use unless specifically asked [5].

Preoperative questionnaires and interviews for dental sedation or general anesthesia should explicitly include GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide, and lixisenatide, as well as the dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist, tirzepatide. Screening instruments may need to include not only “antidiabetic medication,” but also categories such as “weight-loss injections,” “obesity treatment,” “appetite-control medications,” and “medications obtained through private or online weight-management services.” Additional risk assessments should incorporate treatment indication, phase of therapy, dose escalation status, timing of the last dose, gastrointestinal symptoms (including nausea, vomiting, bloating, abdominal pain, and reflux), fasting status, planned depth of sedation, and airway management considerations.

Deep dental sedation and dental general anesthesia present unique features that may amplify the consequences of regurgitation, including intraoral water spray, blood, and saliva, restricted airway access, mouth-opening devices, and shared access between the operator and the anesthetic team. These characteristics do not mandate discontinuation of GLP-1 RAs in all patients; rather, they emphasize the importance of identifying GLP-1 RA use before sedation, assessing aspiration risk, and adapting the anesthetic plan when clinically indicated.

The key point is not necessarily that all patients should discontinue GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy; rather, dental anesthesia and sedation providers should consider revising their screening questions to address this potential blind spot before deep dental sedation or dental general anesthesia (Fig. 1).
