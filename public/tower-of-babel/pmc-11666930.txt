Development of targeted safety hazard management plans utilizing multidimensional association rule mining
Authors: Xingbang Qiang, Guoqing Li, Yuksel Asli Sari, Chunchao Fan, Jie Hou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInvestigating hidden hazards and implementing closed-loop management are essential strategies for accident prevention in the mining industry. This study tackles a key challenge in applying association rule mining to the development of hazard management plans for underground mines. The current approach mainly focuses on hazard description data, often underutilizing critical information such as hazard time and location. To address this, we integrate topic mining with association rule mining to uncover intrinsic association patterns among various attributes of mine safety hazards. Through a systematic analysis of standardized mining hazard attributes, five key analytical dimensions were identified: Hazard Type, Level, Time, Location, and Responsible Units. A topic mining model, utilizing the Biterm Topic Model, was constructed to reduce dimensionality and aggregate hazard description data. Evaluation indicators such as Standard Lift and Difference Degree were proposed, resulting in a multidimensional association rule mining model for mining safety hazards. In this research, 1387 valid rules were extracted based on hazard inspection data from an underground gold mine in China. The analysis revealed relatively strong associations between hazard location and hazard type, responsible unit, as well as hazard level, with association degrees of 1.934, 1.412, and 1.240, respectively. Additionally, 15 rules with a high degree of differentiation were identified to explore interesting correlations among different attributes. Based on this, corresponding control measures and improvement plans were developed for 19 locations. The results demonstrate that a multidimensional partition-based association rule mining approach for mining safety hazards can significantly enhance the specificity of safety training and improve the efficiency of safety hazard investigation.Keywords: Mine safety, Safety hazard, Association rule mining, Topic mining

Introduction

Underground mining operations possess a unique working environment and production characteristics. The workplaces are typically influenced by various natural factors such as geological structures, rock layer stability, groundwater systems, and toxic gases. Additionally, the long hours worked by personnel in enclosed, confined, and humid environments also increase the risk of safety accidents. The combined effects of these factors result in a highly complex and uncertain safety situation for underground metal mines, making safety management an important business for mining enterprises. Currently, risk pre-control has become an important method for reducing mining accidents and improving the level of mine safety, with the focus on identifying, assessing, and managing potential unstable factors that could lead to accidents [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) in the United States has defined the process of hazard identification and assessment, which includes the collection of hazard information in the workplace, hazard identification, the establishment of risk control plans, and periodic confirmation of the effectiveness of measures to manage mining safety hazards [5]. The Canadian Centre for Occupational Health and Safety has clarified the identification methods for safety hazards in the production process, as well as control measures and investigation plans. Similarly, the Minerals Industry Safety and Health Risk Management Guideline of Australia has proposed comparable control methods. In China, a combination of Risk Tiered Control and Hazard Inspection and Governance has been proposed to achieve risk prevention and control. It can be seen that the risk management of mining safety accidents based on hidden hazard investigation is widely applied. In these schemes, targeted interventions must be implemented based on the results of hazard investigations. This approach minimizes potential hazards and reduces the risk of accidents. However, the analyses of safety hazard data by some mining enterprises rely on business intelligence tools for quantifying statistics according to the location and time period of hazard occurrences [6]. This approach may lack specificity and fail to meet the demands for improving investigation efficiency due to the absence of interaction analysis among different attributes. Therefore, more effective methods are required to identify key points in hazard management, achieve targeted training, and implement special rectification measures to effectively reduce the number of hazards.

Analyzing historical data to obtain valuable information that can improve existing measures and technologies is considered an important way to further enhance management levels and control efficiency [7,8]. Therefore, data mining technology such as Classification, Clustering, Association Rule Mining and Regression has been widely applied to improve the efficiency of safety management [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Among these methods, association rule mining is capable of uncovering interesting associations and potential patterns between item sets that are implicit and potentially valuable for decision-making within large datasets. The concept was first proposed by Agrawal et al. [13], who also developed the Apriori algorithm, which remains the most widely used algorithm in this field. The Apriori algorithm employs a breadth-first search and a Hash tree structure to efficiently count candidate item sets. It generates candidate item sets of length k from item sets of length k−1. Subsequently, it prunes candidates that contain infrequent sub-patterns. In the field of safety management, association rule mining technology is widely applied, primarily in sectors such as transportation [14,15], healthcare [16], construction [[17], [18], [19]], chemical industry [20], subway systems [21], steel mills [22], and power transmission [23], with the data for these studies mainly derived from accident case reports in the respective industries. Some researchers have also studied the application of association rule mining techniques concerning safety hazard inspection data. Rossy Armyn [24] used association rule mining methods to study instances of unsafe behavior in construction projects, identifying common unsafe behaviors related to major production accidents. The research findings can serve as guiding information for optimizing the content of employee safety training. Lipeng Fu et al. [25] used association rule analysis to identify that the human and organizational factors related to the owner and designer, as well as social environmental factors, are the core sources of risk, which provides an optimized approach for the safety management of deep foundation pit projects (DFPP) in subway construction. Ruihua Xu [26] conducted a scientific prediction and early warning of errors and violations by air traffic controllers using correlation analysis methods, enabling the prediction of unsafe behaviors in air traffic control. Guo et al. [27] employed an association rule mining model to analyze the distribution patterns of hidden hazards across temporal and spatial dimensions, identifying critical risks such as improper use of safety equipment and the improper storage of explosives. This approach has enhanced the efficiency of hazard management in mining operations.

At present, most of the research on association rule mining for safety hazard investigation data is limited to hazard description data, lacking the utilization of fields such as investigation time, investigation location, and penalty amount. The reason for this is that, unlike accident reports, hidden hazard investigation data focuses more on the routine identification of potential accident hazards. As a result, this leads to a larger volume of data, with many records being duplicated, which lowers the density of valuable information within the dataset. Additionally, the use of more colloquial language in these records makes the analysis and mining process more complex. Therefore, the primary challenge in effectively utilizing hazard investigation data is how to normalize the personalized descriptions of hazards. Topic mining model is based on the pattern of co-occurrence of words in documents. It provides the topics of documents in the form of a probability distribution through a series of processes including preprocessing, modeling, and similarity calculation, which can achieve the collection and dimension reduction of text data [28]. To date, topic mining model has been applied in the field of safety management. Donghui Shi et al. [29] has used topic mining technology to transform event description text data into model input, employing data stream algorithms to incrementally build and test risk factor classification models, overcoming obstacles such as labor-intensive manual identification of risk factors and data scarcity. Kuhn KD et al. [30] applied structural topic modeling to the Aviation Safety Reporting System data, revealing previously unreported connections and highlighting fuel pump, tank, and landing gear issues. Ideally, these results can be used to prioritize when planning future safety studies. Tan, TJ et al. [31] employed Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic analysis to identify hazardous topics and feature words from 3900 historical hazard records to assist in hazard investigations. The prior parameters were set to α = 50/14 and β = 0.1, ultimately identifying 14 risk topics, including Transportation Equipment, Air Duct Hazard, Pipeline Hazard, Roller Hazard, etc. These topic results were then used as input for a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model, providing an innovative approach to efficiently discovering and utilizing concealed danger information in coal mines for accident prevention.

In summary, due to the limitations of unstructured text data, existing research on safety hazard control strategies based on association rule mining primarily focuses on hazard description data. This focus limits the integration of information from other dimensions, thereby hindering the development of more comprehensive and precise hazard strategies, which are essential for ensuring the efficiency and accuracy of hazard control measures. Therefore, this study combines the topic mining model with the association rule mining model to analyze valuable association relationships between different attributes of mining safety hazards. This paper's original contributions include: ① The five key attributes for safety hazard analysis—hazard type, level, time, location, and responsible unit—have been determined, and the methods for vectorizing each attribute have been established. ② To explore associations among these attributes, analysis indicators such as Standard Lift and the Difference Degree in association rules were proposed. ③ Finally, the associations among different safety hazard attributes were analyzed visually using methods such as heat maps and stacked column charts. Additionally, specific and targeted control measures were provided. Overall, this study facilitates the development of focused preventive measures and forms the basis for safety training programs, thereby enhancing mine safety management.

Current studies primarily concentrate on the description fields of safety hazards, utilizing segmented results as datasets for association rule mining. This approach necessitates a meticulously constructed segmentation lexicon. Furthermore, due to the colloquial nature of safety hazard descriptions, the segmented results tend to be extensive, leading to fewer effective associations and issues related to dimensionality explosion. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to establish key analysis attributes for safety hazards, enabling the comprehensive and detailed utilization of hazard inspection data. Through extensive field practice, consultations with on-site safety management personnel, and a review of relevant literature [[33], [34], [35], [36]], this study identified five key analytical attributes of mining safety hazards: Hazard Type, Level, Time, Location, and Responsible Units. Each attribute constitutes an analytical dimension of the safety hazard. The reasons for selecting each attribute and the corresponding analysis are outlined as follows.

In hazard record data, the hazard description stands out as the crucial information for identifying the specificity of hazard records and serves as the fundamental dataset in current research. The first issue that this study addresses is the need for dimensionality reduction and the normalization of hazard description fields. It involves using concise and representative type information to encapsulate the specific content of hazard descriptions. For instance, incidents like a fractured roof, loose rocks, and cracks could be categorized under roof management issues.

Method of constructing hazard type dimension

This study utilizes a topic mining model to cluster and reduce the dimensionality of safety hazard description data, determining the types of hazards that are prone to occur in the actual production process. The Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) and Biterm Topic Model (BTM) are both popular techniques for topic modeling, each with distinct advantages and disadvantages [37]. LDA is a well-established method that is intuitive and robust for longer documents, making it easier to understand how topics relate to documents. Nonetheless, LDA struggles with very short texts due to its reliance on word distributions across longer documents and requires users to predefine the number of topics, which can lead to suboptimal results if not estimated correctly. Conversely, BTM is particularly effective for short texts, as it focuses on word co-occurrences, capturing contextual relationships between words (Biterms) to produce coherent and semantically rich topics. However, its complexity and sensitivity to parameter choices can pose challenges for implementation and interpretation. The choice between the two models depends on the nature of the text data, and the desired outcomes of the topic modeling process [38]. Due to the descriptions of safety hazards in hazard investigation data often being presented as short texts (with each record containing no more than 80 words), the limitation on text content length can reduce the efficiency of the LDA model and the reliability of the topic mining results. Therefore, this study employs the BTM model for mining hazard-related topics.

The BTM model's modeling process includes three steps: ① Constructing a corpus (set of words for conducting topic mining) using the extracted word pairs from the text to be modeled; ② Calculating suitable parameters for the model; ③ Training the BTM based on the parameters to obtain the word distribution and topic distribution within the established corpus.

Multidimensional association rule mining model

Association rule mining technology can uncover interesting associations and correlations between item sets that are implicit and potentially valuable for decision-making from large datasets. Revealing the potential associations between various dimensions of information on mine safety hazards plays a significant role in improving the accuracy of hazard source identification and the efficiency of hidden hazard investigation.

(3)Selection of Association Rule Mining Algorithms

Selection of Association Rule Mining Algorithms

The most widely used association rule mining algorithm is the Apriori algorithm, with many subsequent algorithms being improvements upon it. These include the Direct Hashing and Pruning (DHP) algorithm based on hashing technology, the Partition algorithm based on the division method, the Sampling algorithm based on the sampling method, the Max-miner algorithm based on maximum frequent item sets, and the FP-Growth algorithm based on frequent patterns. Among these, the FP-Growth algorithm stands out for its performance and scalability. It compresses the original data using the FP-Tree data structure, eliminating the need to generate candidate item sets, and achieves efficient mining of frequent item sets by scanning the database only twice. Differences in intra-dimensional association rules

For safety hazards, the association rules under different category constraints within the same dimension may vary. For example, the association rules formed by datasets divided by different seasons may differ, allowing for the analysis of specific types of hazards in different categories. Therefore, it is necessary to construct an evaluation method for the differences between association rules to compare and filter the rules mined from different datasets.

(1)Construction of the Association Rule Difference Analysis Model

Construction of the Association Rule Difference Analysis Model

Let the set of rules in the rule knowledge base be Rb, and the set of newly discovered rules be Rn, where the number of rules in Rb is | Rb |, and the number of rules in Rn is | Rn |. w(p,q) represents the degree of difference between the newly discovered rules Rn and the rules Rb in the knowledge base, with w(p) and w(q) representing the difference in the antecedent and consequent between rules Rb and Rn. To measure the degree of difference between the antecedents of two rules, it is necessary to compare the differences between the constituent elements of the rules. Let the set of elements in the antecedent of rule Rb be P, and the set of elements in the antecedent of rule Rn be Q. Then, the difference between any element Pk in P and an element in Q is denoted as difk. If they are of different dimensions, the difference value difk is recorded as 1; if they are of the same dimension but different categories, the difference value is recorded as 0.5; if they are the same, it is recorded as 0. By comparing the elements in P and Q, the difference in the antecedent between rules Rb and Rn can be calculated as w(p)=∑k=1|P|difk|P|, where |P| is the number of elements in the antecedent of rule Rb. Similarly, the difference in the consequent can be determined as w(q)=∑k=1|Q|difk|Q|, where |Q| is the number of elements in the consequent of rule Rn. When determining the final degree of difference between rule Rb and rule Rn, it is necessary to consider the difference in both the antecedent and consequent since they are equally important. Therefore, referring to the calculation process of the F-test, the harmonic mean of the differences in the antecedent and consequent is calculated to ensure the balance between the two in the calculation process. Construction of Hazard Type Dimension

A custom dictionary was employed for the text segmentation of hazard description data. The custom dictionary includes not only a semantic word segmentation library and a Stopword library but also a mapping dictionary. The main function of the mapping dictionary is to replace some typographical errors, colloquial terms, and synonyms in the original records with standard descriptions, further enhancing the accuracy and reliability of safety topic data mining. The word segmentation process was carried out using the Jieba library. The segmentation results were then subjected to term frequency statistics and TF-IDF weighting to achieve vectorization of the text. Subsequently, the Gibbs sampling algorithm was used for parameter estimation of the BTM to obtain the final topic mining results.

During the process of topic mining, the number of hazard topics K is incremented by a step size of 5, with an empirical value of α set as 50/K and β set to 0.01. Within the interval [20,30] where the optimal number of topics is likely to occur, a finer calculation step size is used. The resulting calculations are shown in Fig. 4. It can be observed from the figure that the optimal number of topics is determined to be 26 at the point where the perplexity is lowest and an inflection point occurs.

The Trend of perplexity as the number of topics changes.

Although the BTM model is more effective for mining topics in short texts, the scarcity of information in individual hazard records makes it challenging to accurately interpret the extracted topics. Through discussions with experts in the field, the corpora under the 26 topics were interpreted individually. Considering the actual production conditions of underground mines, high-frequency topic words were manually identified and incorporated into the original text based on their contribution. After evaluating the results and merging semantically similar topics, 14 effective analysis topics were ultimately formed. As shown in Table 2.

Results and analysis

Association rule mining results

Setting appropriate thresholds for Support and Confidence is key to the effectiveness of the association rule mining results. Currently, when performing association rule mining, parameter settings are mainly based on the multiple experiments and result analyses. To avoid omitting some potential associated factors during the association rule mining process, lower thresholds are generally set. By aggregating hazard issues through topic mining, this study reduces the total number of item categories, allowing for the use of extremely low indicator values. This enables the extraction of a comprehensive set of association rules between attributes and supports the calculation of inter-dimensional relationships. The results can then be filtered based on indicator values as necessary. This means adopting a strategy of mining first and then pruning according to the results. When constructing the association rule mining model, the mlxtend library was used. In theory, performing full-scale association rule mining could generate 57,456 two-dimensional association rules. However, the actual mining result yielded only 1387 rules, indicating that a large number of categories do not have co-occurrence relationships. Intra-dimensional association relationship difference analysis

Each category within a dimension is designated as a classification label, dividing the dataset into two parts: one comprising records associated with the label, and the other containing all data not associated with the label. Association rule mining is conducted separately on these two datasets, with the latter mining results serving as the basic rule base. The Difference Degree of the former rule set relative to the basic rule base is then studied. For example, for the analysis of the Unit dimension, all records corresponding to the Outsourced Units are taken as dataset a, and the records corresponding to the In-House Units and Management Units are taken as dataset b. Association rule mining is conducted on datasets a and b separately to obtain Ra and Rb. Then, the Difference Degree of the rules in Ra relative to the rules in Rb is calculated. By repeating the above operation for all categories within all dimensions, special rules for all categories can be obtained. However, in the example above, only dataset Ra will contain rules related to the Outsourced Units, and these rules will all have a higher degree of difference compared to dataset Rb. However, these rules do not meet the objective of the Difference Degree analysis, so in practice, the rules in Ra that are related to the category used as the Label for dataset division were removed. After experimentation, it was found that there are fewer high-difference-degree rules that can be screened when the number of antecedent and consequent items in the association rules is limited to 1. Therefore, the number of items in the association rules was increased, setting the relationship between antecedent and consequent to 3, and the results were filtered based on Lift and Confidence, ultimately yielding the Difference Degree analysis results as shown in Table 5.

The association network diagram serves as a visual tool to delineate both the shared attributes among various categories within a given dimension and the distinct traits that exhibit a heightened level of association. Within this graphical representation, categories exclusively linked to the central node are indicative of a more pronounced associative relationship when juxtaposed with other nodes. Conversely, categories that maintain connections with several central nodes are deemed to possess a commonality across those nodes. Leveraging the association network facilitates a deeper analysis of the hazard characteristics inherent to each categorical subset, thereby enhancing the precision and efficacy of hazard identification and assessment methodologies.

Limitations of the research

This study uses an underground gold mine as a case to develop a multidimensional association rule mining model aimed at formulating safety hazard management strategies. The model's topic mining component can be adapted and applied to other contexts, with appropriate adjustments for local conditions or specific datasets. Additionally, the multidimensional association rule mining aspect shows strong generalizability and applicability to structured data with fixed fields. In future work, we plan to extend the research to evaluate the generalizability of these results across a wider range of cases and different environments. However, certain limitations remain.

First, although this study incorporates data such as hazard location and time into the development of hazard management strategies, the full potential of the available data has not been fully realized. Certain key fields remain underexplored and underutilized. For instance, variations in working conditions and personnel status across different shifts may influence the occurrence and manifestation of hazards. These variations could be leveraged to tailor hazard control strategies to specific working periods. Additionally, data on corrective action status is invaluable. Understanding the progress of hazard remediation can provide insights into the effectiveness and timeliness of safety management measures, offering opportunities for further refinement of safety strategies. Future research will focus on addressing these underutilized data fields to enhance the multidimensional analysis of safety hazards.

Second, this study relies solely on the presence of penalties as an indicator of hazard severity, which is clearly insufficient. In fact, the magnitude of penalties plays a crucial role in assessing the risk posed by a hazard, as higher penalties typically indicate more severe hazards that could threaten both company operations and employee safety. Furthermore, the evaluation of hazard significance should consider additional factors, such as the duration of corrective actions, the frequency of hazard occurrences, and the potential losses involved. In future research, we aim to develop a more comprehensive and precise system for assessing hazard severity, thereby improving the accuracy of hazard evaluations and supporting the development of more scientifically sound and robust safety management strategies.

Finally, safety hazards are dynamic processes, characterized by their generation, evolution, and mitigation over time. Thus, developing a real-time analysis and evaluation approach is crucial for ensuring the timeliness and effectiveness of strategy development and hazard assessment. The current methodology, which primarily relies on static data analysis, may fail to capture the dynamic changes in safety hazards in real-time. This could result in delays in safety management strategies, limiting their effectiveness in preventing and controlling accidents. Future studies will explore the use of real-time data collection and analysis techniques to establish a dynamic system for monitoring and evaluating safety hazards, enabling timely adjustments to safety management strategies and enhancing the overall precision and effectiveness of hazard control.

Conclusion

Addressing the issue that the current association rule mining based on safety hazard investigation data is limited to the description field, resulting in underutilization of data, this study applies topic mining, data cleaning, and association rule mining to the in-depth excavation of mining safety hazard information based on an analysis of applicability. An association rule mining model for mining hazards is constructed based on the FP-Growth algorithm, excavating the internal co-occurrence and association patterns between hazards that trigger safety accidents, which effectively guides the work of safety hazard inspection and improves the efficiency of safety hazard investigation. Specifically, the following conclusions have been drawn.

(1)Five attributes have been identified for analyzing safety hazards in mines: Type, Time, Location, Level, and Responsible Unit. Topic modeling methods, such as BTM, are effective for standardizing safety hazard description fields and providing a foundation for analyzing associations between different dimensions. In this study, a topic modeling algorithm was used to identify 14 distinct hazard types. Additionally, the vectorization methods for other dimensions were analyzed, offering valuable insights for similar analyses.
