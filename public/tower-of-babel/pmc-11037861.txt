The Impact of Whole Egg Consumption on Bone Accrual in Healthy Children: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Lauren M. Coheley, Mengyun Yu, Xianyan Chen, Patrick J. O’Connor, Kirk S. Kealey, Emma M. Laing, Assaf Oshri, Alicia K. Marand, Julia M. Lance, Joseph M. Kindler, Richard D. Lewis
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Dietary interventions designed to examine the role of nutrition on childhood bone accrual have often focused on the role of individual micronutrients (eg, calcium, vitamin D, and zinc) and macronutrients (eg, protein). The osteogenic benefits of whole foods, such as eggs, are not well understood despite eggs being a source of high-quality nutrients and bioactive compounds known to positively influence bone. A significant positive cross-sectional association between whole egg consumption and tibia cortical bone mass has recently been shown in young children; however, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have not been conducted. This study is a double-blind RCT in male and female children ages 9–13 years of different ancestries, designed to determine if consuming food products with whole eggs (equivalent to 8–10 eggs/wk) versus foods with milk or gelatin (placebo) over a 9-month period will improve measures of bone strength. Total body less head (TBLH) and lumbar spine bone mineral content (BMC) and areal bone mineral density (aBMD) were assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). DXA Z-scores were computed using published pediatric growth charts and were adjusted for height-for-age Z-score (HAZ). Mid-tibia cortical volumetric BMD, BMC, cortical area, total bone area, cortical thickness, and strength strain index were measured using peripheral quantitative computed tomography. Overall, there were no significant intervention effects for any bone outcomes. The increase in spine BMCHAZ Z-score in the egg group versus the gelatin group approached significance (p = 0.07). Significant time effects in TBLH aBMDHAZ Z-score occurred as all groups decreased over 9 months (p < 0.03). Most tibia cortical bone outcomes increased over time (all p < 0.02), but changes did not differ across intervention groups. Whole eggs provide important nutritional benefits for children, but the bone responses to consumption of 8–10 eggs/wk over a period of 9 months in children entering the early stages of puberty were small.

Introduction

Childhood is a critical period for rapid bone elongation, areal bone expansion, and bone mineral accrual, with approximately 40% of adult bone mineral content (BMC) acquired during the 4 years surrounding peak height velocity.(1,2) Areal bone mineral density (aBMD) Z-scores track remarkably during childhood,(3) suggesting that effective lifestyle interventions that improve aBMD during the growing years could serve as preventive strategies for reducing osteoporotic fractures in adulthood. To date, most dietary interventions and prospective observational studies designed to examine the influence of nutrition on aBMD in children have focused on individual micronutrients such as calcium, vitamin D, and zinc,(4) macronutrients such as protein,(5,6) or food groups such as dairy products.(7,8) To date, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in children have been conducted to test whether a single food item, primarily milk or soluble corn fiber, modifies bone.(9–13) Three of the milk RCTs found improvements in total body BMC and aBMD (1%–3%)(9,13) or hip (1%) and spine aBMD (1%).(11)

Whether single food items other than milk positively impact bone in children and adolescents is not known. One food product of interest has been whole eggs. The nutritional composition of whole eggs should support bone health, as eggs contain 6 g of high-quality protein per one large egg and the essential micronutrients vitamin D and zinc. Moreover, the bioactive compounds, lutein and zeaxanthin, are found in eggs, and these carotenoids possess anti-inflammatory properties in certain populations.(14) High intakes of lutein and zeaxanthin have been shown to lower the risk for osteoporosis in a study from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2005–2018.(15)

Whether whole eggs improve bone strength and reduce fractures is unclear, given that the limited studies published to date are observational and have only considered egg intake in groupings with other whole foods and not independently. For example, Inose and colleagues(16) reported that mothers who consumed a food group that included eggs, dairy products, and milk compared with mothers who consumed food groups containing vegetables and fruits had greater Z-scores for tibia cortical speed of sound, a surrogate measure for cortical bone strength.(16) In older adults, Zeng and colleagues(17) showed that intake of red meat, but not poultry or eggs, was associated with a higher risk for skeletal fractures. These observational studies are hypothesis-generating with respect to the potential for a positive impact of egg intake on bone health, but their study designs do not allow for ascertaining the degree to which whole eggs independently contribute to the bone and fracture findings.

To our knowledge, the only study to date that has examined associations between whole egg intake on childhood bone was published by our group.(18) We found that egg intake was positively associated with radius cortical BMC (Ct.BMC) and that the association between egg intake and Ct.BMC was partially mediated through lean body mass (LBM), an important predictor of skeletal strength in children.(18) This study supports the hypothesis that whole eggs may have a positive impact on bone, but its cross-sectional design limits the interpretation and generalizability of these results. The aim of the present study was to determine using a double-blind RCT if daily consumption of food products containing formulated whole egg powder used as a meal/snack replacement over 9 months improves measures of bone strength at the total body, spine, and tibia compared with similar food products formulated with milk powder or gelatin (placebo). Given that eggs contain bioactive compounds unique to other high-quality protein sources and are thought to modulate inflammation, a secondary aim was to examine the impact of the intervention on biomarkers of inflammation.

Participants and Methods

Study design and participant characteristics

The Skeletal and Cognitive Effects of Nutrition from Eggs (SCENE) study was a double-blind RCT assessing the impact of dried whole egg intake versus dried whole milk and gelatin powder (placebo) intakes on pediatric bone outcomes and cognitive function in apparently healthy adolescent boys and girls, ages 9–13 years, and of various ancestries. The cognitive outcomes have been reported elsewhere.(19) An a priori power analysis was conducted to determine the sample size. To conservatively estimate our sample size, we used a biomarker of inflammation instead of a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) bone mineral density outcome. Preliminary data collected in our laboratory and evidence from other laboratories suggest large effect sizes for the associations between level of egg consumption and biomarkers of inflammation.(20–25) Using these effect size statistics with expected mean differences between egg and milk and gelatin groups that would be clinically relevant, we estimated the sample size needed for this study (via the G × Power program). To achieve a sufficient power (1-β < 0.80) for each outcome measure, a sample size of at least 32 per condition group, for a total sample size of 96, is necessary. Our prior nutrition RCTs, ranging from 16 weeks to 2 years, have a retention and compliance rate of 82% to 95%. We expected a similar compliance rate in this proposed study, and assuming a conservative 20% sample size loss due to anticipated attrition, a starting sample size of 60 subjects in each condition group (N = 180) would preserve power of the study design.

Boys and girls were recruited in the Athens, GA, USA, area via e-mail announcements, social media (ie, Facebook and Instagram), US mail, and study flyers placed at strategic locations around the area (eg, in schools, doctors’ offices, and community centers). Interested participants called the Bone and Body Composition Laboratory at The University of Georgia for an initial telephone screen to assess age and exclusion criteria, including presence of menarche (for females), growth disorders/chronic diseases (eg, cerebral palsy) known to impact bone development, or the use of medications (eg, corticosteroids) known to impact bone metabolism. If children met the telephone screening criteria for participation, they were scheduled to come into the laboratory for their first visit, which included determination of body mass index (BMI). Randomization into the three treatment groups was accomplished using 12 separate randomization lists or blocks using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The blocks were created for the 12 stratums to account for sex (female and male), ancestry (Black, White, and other ethnicities such as Asian and Hispanic), and BMI-for-age percentiles (above and below the 85% BMI for age percentile) (2 × 3 × 2). A list of 51 participants were generated for each block, more than what would be needed for the trial to account for potential dropout. One researcher not involved in the study implementation generated the allocation sequence and provided it as a spreadsheet to the research coordinator. The research coordinator enrolled and randomly assigned all participants to the intervention whole egg (n = 65), milk powder (n = 61), or gelatin placebo (n = 56) groups. The research coordinator, principal investigator, co-investigators, research assistants, and participants and their parents/guardians were blinded to the intervention assignment. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram in Figure 1 summarizes the participant screening and enrollment procedures, allocation, and dropouts. The Institutional Review Board for Human Subjects at the University of Georgia approved all study procedures, including COVID-19 protocols implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic. Participant informed assent and parental informed permission were obtained before all testing procedures. The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as 2521RC296228.

Development of food products

The Food Product Innovation and Commercialization Center at the University of Georgia, Griffin Campus, developed the SCENE food products using whole egg powder, whole milk powder, or unflavored gelatin powder (placebo) in the different products. A variety of palatable food products were developed to mask the flavor of the egg, milk, and gelatin protein, and facilitate adherence to the interventions across the 9-month study period. A list of common food items consumed by children was reviewed by one registered dietitian nutritionist (RDN), and five of the most popular items were chosen for inclusion. The five product choices per treatment group consisted of a breakfast (ie, waffle and pancake), a lunch/dinner (ie, macaroni and cheese), and two dessert items (ie, ice cream and brownie). Prototypes were first developed using commercial brands as the base material when feasible, and the egg powder, whole milk powder, or gelatin powder ingredients were added into each of the five foods. After prototype development, these 15 food products (five per treatment group) were tested for consumer acceptability. Each product was evaluated by a minimum of 75 children within the study age group (ie, 9–13 years of age) to determine acceptability at the University of Georgia’s Sensory Laboratory in Griffin, GA. Children were assigned to an individual tasting booth for consumer testing. They were provided one product at a time and asked to answer several questions regarding overall product acceptability and product attributes. Participants were provided condiments if desired (eg, syrup and butter for waffles and pancakes). The overall acceptability scale used was 0–7, with a score of 4.0 being “so-so” and a score of 7 being “loving it.” Specific attribute data for the 15 products leveraged cluster analysis by product type, and all consumer data were subjected to statistical analyses to measure the degree of differences (ANOVA). All 15 food products were determined acceptable and were utilized in the SCENE study. Final product formulations were slightly modified after the sensory testing and no significant differences in acceptability across groups were identified. The products were designed to be isocaloric in nature and only varied in the amount of whole egg, milk, and gelatin powder they provided. SCENE study participants were provided preparation instructions based on consumer feedback for texture (eg, toast waffles to produce a crisper mouthfeel). The ingredients and macronutrient composition of the 15 food products are shown in Supplemental Tables S1–S3.

Intervention

Participants and their parents/guardians met with one of two RDNs to discuss how to incorporate their designated food products into their current diet with the goal of consuming at least 10 food products per week for the 9-month duration as a substitute for other similar products in the diet. The same five product choices were provided per treatment group including two breakfast items (ie, waffle and pancake), one lunch/dinner item (ie, macaroni and cheese), and two dessert items (ie, brownie and ice cream). Depending on the assigned products selected, consuming 10 of the egg- or milk-based food products per week is the equivalent to adding approximately 8 large or 10 medium whole eggs or 7 servings of milk, respectively, to the diet. Compliance to the intervention was determined from monthly compliance calendars. Precoded compliance calendars were provided and collected on a monthly basis. The participants and their parents/guardians were instructed to record the number of food products the participant consumed each week on the calendar with the goal of 10 food products consumed per week. The compliance calendars were either returned in-person to the research laboratory or mailed to the lab coordinator. Compliance was determined as a percentage of total food product consumption over each participant’s study duration divided by the maximum food products that could have been consumed in that same period, assuming participants consumed 10 products per week as instructed (ie, as instructed: 10 products/wk × 36 weeks = 360 products). Participants and their parents/guardians picked up their food products from the Bone and Body Composition Lab on either a monthly or bimonthly basis, depending upon their freezer storage capacity at home. Each participant was provided a large cooler to haul foods from the Bone and Body Composition freezer to their home freezer. During the COVID-19 pandemic, participants and their parents/guardians picked up their foods at varying lengths of time depending upon their storage capacity at home. Members of the Bone and Body Composition lab also delivered foods to participants’ homes to provide non-contact delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Sexual maturation rating and anthropometrics

Sexual maturation rating (SMR) stage was determined by self-assessment of breast development in females and genitals in males using the criteria described by Tanner.(26,27) Anthropometric measurements were conducted at baseline and at 4.5 and 9 months. Weight was measured to the nearest 0.5 kg using an electronic scale. Standing height was measured within 0.5 cm using a wall-mounted stadiometer. Participants were measured in light outdoor clothing after the removal of their shoes. Body mass index (kg/m2) was determined and BMI-for-age-percentile scores were calculated using the 2000 Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) growth charts.(28) BMI and height for age Z-scores (HAZ) were calculated using CDC growth charts.(28) Single-measure intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and test–retest coefficients of variation (CVs) were determined previously from standing height (0.99 and 0.4%) and body weight (0.99 and 1.4%) in females aged 6–10 years (n = 10) who were measured twice over a 2-week period by the same researcher in our lab.(29)

Body composition and bone measurements by DXA

Total body less head (TBLH) and lumbar spine aBMD (g/cm2) and BMC (g) were determined using DXA (Hologic Discovery A, Waltham, MA, USA). Fat mass (FM), percent fat, and LBM were determined from the total body scans. LBM index (LBMI) and FM index (FMI) Z-scores were calculated using reference data sets from NHANES.(30) TBLH and lumbar spine BMC and aBMD Z-scores were calculated using previously published pediatric growth charts from the Bone Mineral Density Childhood Study (BMDCS).(30) All BMC and aBMD Z-scores were adjusted for HAZ using the method described by Zemel and colleagues.(30) Any scan with significant movement or incorrect reference line placement was removed from the study (n = 20 scans). ICCs were calculated for body composition in females in our lab, ages 5–8 years (n = 10), scanned twice over 7 days (all ≥0.98).(29)

Tibia cortical bone measurements

Peripheral quantitative computed tomography (pQCT) was used to assess tibia and radius cortical bone using Stratec XCT 3000 version 9 (Stratec Medizintechnik GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany). For the purposes of this article, only the tibia data are reported. One operator performed baseline and 9-month scans. At the start of each testing day, a cortical bone phantom specific to the pQCT with known properties was scanned and the variation in phantom measures differed by <1%. Participants were placed sitting upright with their self-reported non-dominant leg in the center of the pQCT gantry. A single tomographic slice at the 38% and 66% site relative to the distal growth plate was obtained. Cort mode 1 (threshold, 710 mg/cm3) was used to obtain cortical volumetric bone mineral density (CrtDen, mg/cm3), cortical bone mineral content (CrtBMC, mg), and cortical area (CrtArea, mm2) and to define the outermost edge of the bone. Peel mode 2 (threshold, 400 mg/cm3) was used to separate the cancellous and cortical bone compartments. Cortical thickness (CrtTh, mm), periosteal perimeter (Ps.Pm, mm), and endosteal perimeter (Es.Pm, mm) were also measured. This same threshold was used to calculate polar-strength strain index (SSI, mm3), which is derived from CrtDen, section modulus, and normal physiological bone density that is estimated at 12,000 mg/mm3. Five healthy females (ages 18–24 years) were scanned in our lab to determine test–retest reliability.(31) One-way random effects model and single-measure ICCs for all pQCT variables were R ≥ 0.97. All scans were reviewed for severity of movement. A movement ratio was used to quantify the severity of the movement and the ratio is the percent movement artifacts/bone area.(32) Any scans that had a ratio exceeding 0.25 indicated excessive movement and were not included in the analyses. A total of 57 scans had excessive movement and were not used.

Dietary assessment

To assess average daily protein, fat, carbohydrate, energy, calcium, and vitamin D intakes, three-day diet records(33) were completed at home by participants and their parents/guardians on two week-days and one weekend day. Before data analysis, if there were questions regarding the diet records, the research staff would contact the parents and children to clarify the diet record. A total of 182 diet records were analyzed at baseline, 123 at 4.5 months, and 77 at 9 months. The diet records were analyzed using the Nutrition Data Systems for Research (NDSR) software version 16 (Nutrition Coordinating Center [NCC], Minneapolis, MN, USA). Quality control was assessed by comparing a random sample of foods listed on the three-day for a subject to foods entered for that same subject in the NDSR software. The percentage error in transferring dietary data from the paper document to the computer document was determined. The average percentage errors (number of errors/food products sampled) for approximately 100 foods at baseline, 4.5 months, and 9 months was 2.97%, 0.32%, and 0.65%, respectively.

Bone physical activity questionnaire

Past and current physical activity were measured by the Bone-Specific Physical Activity (BPAQ)(34) questionnaire. Researchers in the Bone and Body Composition Laboratory interviewed participants to ask about specific sports or physical activities that they participated in regularly during the prior 12 months at baseline and prior 9 months at the end of the trial and to indicate the frequency in sessions per week. An algorithm was then used to determine the “current bone-specific physical activity” by combining the information above with estimates of the effective load stimuli based on published estimates of ground reaction forces of the different activities.(34) A one-way random effects model single-measure ICC was calculated for past BPAQ (ICC = 0.96) in a sample of females (n = 17) aged 20–27 years.(34)

Serum measurements

Blood serum samples were collected in the morning after an overnight fast and were stored in a −80°C freezer until the time of the analysis. Total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and triglycerides were measured with SIRRUS analyzer (Stanbio Laboratory, Boerne, TX, USA). High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) was measured on a SIRRUS analyzer (Stanbio Laboratory) using Pointe Scientific (Canton, MI, USA) turbidometric reagent. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-alpha) were measured in duplicate using MesoScale Discovery (Rockville, MD, USA) Human V-Plex Proinflammatory Panel I kits. All analyses were conducted at the University of Alabama (UAB) Metabolism Core, which is supported by the UAB Diabetes Research Center (P3oDKO79626) and the UAB Center for Clinical and Translational Science (UL1TR003096).

Statistics

Descriptive statistics are reported as means and standard deviations (SD). The effects of the intervention were tested using mixed-effects models. The fixed effects consisted of the main effects of the three treatments and time, a two-level factor at baseline and 9 months. The two-way interactions of treatment and time were included in the TBLH, lumbar spine, and tibia bone models. For TBLH and lumbar spine, adjustments were made for four potentially confounding variables: baseline values of SMR stage, baseline calcium intakes, baseline LBMI Z-score, an index based on age- and sex-specific reference data for LBM, and baseline current BPAQ. Tibia cortical bone outcomes were adjusted for SMR stage, age, leg length, ancestry, and sex. Significant egg, milk, or gelatin intervention effects were evaluated using contrast tests in post hoc analyses to compare the difference in the changes of DXA- and pQCT-derived bone outcomes from baseline to 9 months. Effect sizes (d) for paired samples across 9 months were calculated by dividing the mean difference in post minus baseline unadjusted gain scores by the standard deviation of the differences. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Dunnett method was used to adjust the p values for multiple comparison tests. Dropouts were included in the models as were two participants who had missing baseline data scores. All analyses were conducted (R version 4.1.2) with packages including mice, Imer, emmeans, and ImerTest, which were used for missing data imputation, mixed-effects models, contrasts, and tests for mixed-effects models.(35) Seven participants who completed the study failed to return their compliance calendars, and therefore, the analysis reported for compliance used a sample size that was reduced by three in the placebo group, three in the milk group, and one in the egg group.

Results

Baseline

Baseline characteristics of the 182 enrolled participants are provided in Table 1. The SMR stage distribution for participants at baseline was stage 1, 23%; stages 2–3, 63%; stage 4, 10%; and stage 5, 3%. Seven female participants started their menstrual cycle during the study: 2 in the whole egg group, 2 in the milk group, and 3 in the gelatin group. Across all groups, there were no significant baseline differences in age, SMR stage, sex, ancestry, anthropometric measurements, body composition, BMCHAZ or aBMDHAZ Z-scores, cortical bone outcomes, dietary intakes, current BPAQ, serum lipids, or biomarkers of inflammation. Of the 182 participants enrolled in the study, 122 completed the 9-month trial for a retention rate of 67%. The overall compliance rate was 63%, and there were no differences in compliance among the three intervention groups, with compliance being 58%, 63%, and 68% for the egg, milk, and gelatin groups, respectively. Baseline characteristics were compared between participants who completed the study and those who dropped out, and there were no statistical differences observed between the groups.

Total body less head and spine BMC and aBMD

Table 2 presents mean and SD for TBLH and lumbar spine aBMD and BMCHAZ Z-scores at baseline and 9 months. Supplemental Tables S4 and S4.1 present additional statistical results, including confidence intervals and effect sizes. After controlling for baseline values of SMR stage, calcium intakes, LBMI Z-score, and current BPAQ, there was a significant negative time effect for TBLH BMCHAZ Z-score in the milk group (p = 0.02; Fig. 2) and TBLH BMDHAZ Z-scores for all groups (all p < 03; Fig. 3). No significant time × group interactions occurred for TBLH BMCHAZ or aBMDHAZ Z-scores. There was a significant time effect in spine BMCHAZ Z-score in the egg group (p = 0.003; Fig. 4) but not in the milk or gelatin groups. No time × group interactions were observed for BMCHAZ Z-score, though the change in spine BMCHAZ Z-score from baseline to 9 months in the egg group compared with the gelatin group approached significance (p = 0.04; Dunnett’s adjusted p = 0.07; Fig. 4). There were no significant time effects or time × group interactions for spine BMDHAZ Z-scores (Fig. 5).

Tibia cortical bone

Table 3 presents mean and SD of the tibia bone outcomes at baseline and 9 months. Supplemental Tables S5 and S5.1 present additional statistical data including confidence intervals and effect sizes. For all groups, after controlling for SMR stage, age, leg length, ancestry, and sex, there were significant time effects observed with increases in 38% Ct.Area (all p < 0.0001), 38% Ct. BMC (all p < 0.0001), 38% Ct.Th (all p < 0.0001), 38% SSI (all p < 0.0001; Fig. 6), 66% Ct.Area (all p < 0.0001), 66% Ct.BMC (all p < 0.0001), and 66% SSI (all p < 0.0001; Fig. 7), and muscle area (p = 0.0001 to 0.014). No significant time × group interactions were observed for any tibia bone measure. The increases in 38% SSI in the milk group and 66% SSI in the egg group were slightly less than those in the gelatin group, approaching significance with p value of 0.081 (Dunnett’s adjusted p = 0.148) and 0.057 (Dunnett’s adjusted p = 0.106), respectively.

Discussion

The aim of this RCT was to determine whether the consumption of food products containing formulated whole egg powder used as a meal/snack replacement over 9 months improves childhood TBLH and spine BMCHAZ and aBMDHAZ and tibia cortical bone outcomes compared with the consumption of similar food products prepared with either whole milk powder or unflavored gelatin powder (placebo). The primary finding is that the intervention of increased consumption of egg-containing products over 9 months in children had no statistically significant effects on TBLH or lumbar spine aBMDHAZ and BMCHAZ Z-scores or tibia cortical bone parameters. Though not statistically significant, increases in spine BMCHAZ Z-score during the 9-month intervention in the egg group versus the gelatin group approached significance. There were significant time effects such that over 9 months, there was a significant decline in TBLH BMCHAZ Z-score for the milk group and significant declines in TBLH aBMDHAZ Z-score for all groups. Lumbar spine BMCHAZ increased over time in the egg group but not the milk or gelatin groups. Most tibia cortical bone outcomes increased over time in all groups. There were no significant changes in biomarkers of inflammation.

The current study is the first RCT assessing the impact of whole eggs independently, and not as part of a group of food pairing, on bone outcomes in apparently healthy children in the early stages of puberty. The null findings in the SCENE trial may be related to multiple factors, including baseline dietary calcium intakes. For example, positive BMD responses to dairy consumption have been reported in trials in which participants had low baseline calcium intakes.(36) Mean baseline calcium intakes of participants in the SCENE trial were 900–950 mg/d compared with the 400–750 mg/d with either a milk(9,13) or milk powder trial.(11) Calcium intakes in the aforementioned studies were increased through fortification of high-temperature milk(9) or provision of sufficient milk product(11,13) to achieve daily calcium intakes of 560 mg/d and 400–1300 mg/d, respectively, above baseline intakes. The authors observed significant gains in total body BMC and aBMD (1%–3%)(9,13) and aBMD at the hip (1%) and spine (1.5%).(11) We did not provide additional calcium through supplementation in the current study, and it is possible that participant intakes for this threshold nutrient, lower than the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) of 1300 mg/d, attenuated the potential effects of the intervention on BMC accrual. The food products used in this trial, assuming participants consumed 10 products/wk, would have provided 1400–1700 mgCa/wk or about 200–250 mg/d. Participants were instructed to use the experimental food products in place of similar food products in their diets, and mean intakes of calcium at baseline did not change throughout the study for any group. Vogel and colleagues(8) reported that participants increasing calcium intake from 700 mg/d to approximately 1000 mg/d (control) had similar bone responses to participants increasing intakes from 700 to 1500 mg (intervention group), indicating that the mean calcium intakes in our sudy (~900 mg) may have been satisfactory.

The 9-month time frame for the present study was partially selected based on the pediatric position paper recommendations issued by the International Society of Clinical Densitometry (www.ISCD.org) suggesting a minimum interval of 6–12 months between DXA scans. Unlike the current study, the two prior milk trials had longer study durations of 12–24 months.(9,11) The timing and magnitude of peak height and BMC accrual varies depending on ancestry and sex, and because of our diverse participants, a longer study duration might have been warranted. The age of spine peak bone BMC velocity occurs near 12.0–12.5 years of age for females and 14.0 for males, depending on ancestry.(2) A longer duration intervention for our RCT may have better captured the peak lumbar spine BMC velocity and the significant bone mineralization that occurs in late adolescence after peak height velocity is achieved.(2) In an older group of females, 15–16 years of age, Merrilees and colleagues(37) demonstrated that 2 years of a dairy food intervention to achieve 1100 mg calcium intake per day led to significant increases in trochanter (4.6%), lumbar spine (1.5%), and femoral neck aBMD (4.8%). However, additional food product intervention trials of 1–2 years in duration failed to observe significant bone changes.(10,12)

One key factor for the disparate bone results in the current trial and two of the milk studies(9,11) could be the initial health status of participants. Participants in two feeding trials with milk(9,11) or cheese(10) were vitamin D deficient at the onset of the study, particularly girls of Asian ancestry with serum 25(OH) D < 20 nmol/L.(9) The children in our study were apparently healthy, though we did not assess serum 25(OH)D concentrations. A previous report from our lab in male and female children of similar age living in the same community/geographic region as the current RCT showed mean 25(OH)D concentrations ranging from 60 and 80 nmol/L.(38) Other calcium absorption, bone, and vitamin D trials have demonstrated null intervention responses in children with serum 25(OH)D values >50 nmol/L.(39) The health status of the children participating in the trial may have contributed to the lack of intervention response in the biomarkers of inflammation. A large number of CRP samples were classified as undetectable, so statistical analysis of this biomarker was not possible. The inflammatory biomarker response to egg intake is likely population-specific,(14) with minimal effects expected in healthy children.

The overall compliance rate of 63% in the current trial was lower than expected. The two milk trials showing positive bone results were conducted solely or partially in the school setting and the investigators estimated compliance to be 99%–100%.(9,11) Compliance rates for at-home food product interventions ranged from 58% for the in-home portion of the milk powder trial(11) to 70% for a cheese-feeding study and 75%–85% for a soluble corn fiber intervention.(12) Participants in the present study collected their food products either in the laboratory by parents or received home deliveries made by our research team. Considering the extensive efforts made by both parents and staff to ensure that participants had sufficient food products for the intervention, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, the overall compliance rate seemed acceptable and underscores one challenge of conducting an RCT of the type described here in children.(40)

Recruitment of children within the age range of 9–13 years was intended to identify children entering the early stages of puberty defined by SMR stages 2–3 for breast development for females and genital development for males. Of the 182 children enrolled in the study, 115 were classified at baseline in SMR stages 2–3. The magnitude and timing of peak BMC accrual is influenced by sex and ancestry,(2) and the age range of 9–13 years captured boys and girls of various ancestries approaching peak BMC accrual during the course of the 9-month intervention. Pediatric bone strength depends not only on material properties of bone captured by DXA but also the structural properties using pQCT. Significant increases in Ct.Th, Ct.Area, and SSI at the 38% and Ct.Area, SSI, and muscle area at the 66% site are consistent with the bone growth experienced by children in the early stages of puberty.

Unexpectantly, there were significant declines for all groups in TBLH BMDHAZ Z-scores. The decline in TBLH BMDHAZ Z-scores in growing children could have been related to several factors. For example, Faulkner and colleagues(41) showed before peak height velocity (PHV), but not after, a dissociation of peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) velocity and peak bone area velocity in boys and girls, resulting in a decrease in size-adjusted total body aBMD. The majority of children in the current trial at baseline (86%) would have been classified at a SMR stage 1–3, which is consistent with a stage that occurs before the onset of PHV. During the 9-month trial, even though some participants likely reached PHV, a dissociation of PBMC velocity and peak bone area velocity might have contributed to the decrease in TBLH BMDHAZ Z-scores. Further, height-adjustment methods have been reported to be less accurate in adjusting for height in mid-pubertal children,(30,42) a time consistent with the SMR stages of the majority of our participants. Hence, height may not be accounted for in its entirety when tracking TBLH BMDHAZ Z-scores during this time. The decrease in TBLH BMDHAZ Z-scores may also have been attributable to an earlier timing of pubertal development. Children with obesity and children of African American ancestry enter puberty ahead of their peers,(43) and the more rapid rate of height and BMC accrual typical of the pubertal growth period may have slowed by the onset of the 9-month trial.

A statistically significant result in this study was the increase over time in the egg group lumbar spine BMCHAZ Z-score without significant time effects in the milk or gelatin groups. The increases in spine BMCHAZ Z-score with the egg intervention (compared with the gelatin group) approached significance. The effect size of 0.34 for the increase in BMCHAZ Z-score with the egg intervention, combined with the p = 0.07 after adjustment for Family-wise error, suggest that the increase in BMCHAZ Z-score with eggs is a meaningful outcome. However, future studies would benefit from exploring this idea further using shorter and more controlled egg-feeding study designs, assessment of compliance using plasma lutein and zeaxanthin and macular pigment optical density, and measurement of bone biomarkers as the primary outcomes. If future studies explore this area, the use of shorter and more controlled egg-feeding study designs, the assessment of compliance using plasma lutein and zeaxanthin and macular pigment optical density, and the measurement of bone biomarkers as the primary outcomes may be a more satisfactory approach.

There are several strengths of this study. First, the participants in the RCT were boys and girls of multiple ancestries, so the results may be more applicable to the general population than studies with narrower inclusion criteria. The BMDCS(2,30) data underscore the importance of sex and ancestry on bone mineral accretion and the timing of peak bone mineral gains in relation to height growth. The other trials included only females, presumably White or of Asian ancestry. Second, the multiple food products formulated for this trial were tested before the study in a sample of children similar in age to the participants, and they included a variety of tasteful foods created in an effort to reduce consumer fatigue over the 9-month intervention. Moreover, to ascertain if whole eggs—and not just high-quality protein—would impact bone, the study design included comparison conditions involving food products containing dried milk, another high-quality protein food, and gelatin, a poor-quality protein, lacking or deficient in certain essential amino acids (placebo). All food products contained similar quantities of protein (Supplemental Tables S1–S3). The nutrient composition of eggs is unique in that they contain the bioactive compounds lutein and zeaxanthin, and greater intakes have been reported to be associated with lower fracture risks in adults.(15) Until 2015, dietary recommendations have limited whole eggs because of concerns of eggs’ high cholesterol content that may increase risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD).(44–46) In 2015, taking into account the newer scientific evidence that dietary cholesterol does not promote risk for CVD, the 2015–2020 Dietary Guidelines removed the dietary restriction on high-cholesterol foods like eggs. Additionally, the 2020–2025 Dietary Guidelines specifically recommend nutrient-rich eggs for inclusion in a healthy diet. In the current study, serum lipids were assessed and confirmed that in children 9–13 years of age, increased whole egg intake of 8–10 eggs/wk had no impact on serum lipid levels. Mean 9-month serum total cholesterol, HDL, and calculated LDL levels in the egg group were 168, 56, and 93 mg/dL, respectively, with no significant intervention or time effects. Because of the heterogeneity of the HDL-C and LDL-C particles, it would have been worthwhile to have assessed the subfractions of these particles because there is some evidence in adults that eggs favorably alter specific subfraction function.(47)

Despite the many strengths to this study, this RCT is not without limitations. Although the participants represented a diverse group of children, they were recruited in the Athens, GA, USA, area, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. One example of this is the vitamin D status of participants living in the southeastern United States at 34 degrees N latitude. This latitude is associated with less vitamin D deficiency compared with participants living at more northern latitudes,(48) and because initial health status of participants can impact the bone response to interventions, this could be an important consideration. One unexpected complication experienced during the trial was the COVID-19 pandemic. Inperson testing for this RCT was discontinued from March 2020 to June 2020, but food deliveries and pickups were allowed to continue. Before resuming the study, standard operating procedures were revised for COVID-19 and approved by the institutional review board. As a consequence, the study duration was extended beyond 9 months for a few participants. Our study protocol allowed for a study duration of 8–10 months. After the COVID-19 modifications, 2 participants completed the study after 13 months, 2 after 12 months, and 4 after 11 months. Finally, food product compliance assessment using compliance diaries or calendars is subject to participants over- or underreporting food items. No objective measure of compliance was used in this RCT. Assessment of plasma concentrations of lutein and zeaxanthin or determination of macular pigment optical density would provide good objective measures of compliance with egg intake.(49)

In summary, the early stages of puberty represent a time characterized by the lengthening of bones, significant bone mineralization, and increases in bone dimensions and strength. With the timing and magnitude of these coordinated processes influenced by multiple factors including sex and ancestry, it is a challenge intervening in children during the early stages of puberty. Increased consumption of whole eggs or milk powder over 9 months had no significant effects on TBLH or lumbar spine aBMDHAZ and BMCHAZ Z-scores, tibia cortical bone parameters, or biomarkers of inflammation. The null findings in the SCENE trial with respect to these outcomes may be partially associated with enrolling a diverse group of apparently healthy children, a relatively short follow-up period, and a lower than expected compliance with the intervention.
