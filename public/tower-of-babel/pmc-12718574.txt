Mass SARS-CoV-2 testing in US correctional facilities during the pandemic: a longitudinal analysis
Authors: Charlotte Kim, Byron S Kennedy, Mary Lansing, Robert P Richeson, Matt Hitchings, Albert I Ko, Margaret L Lind
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionReal-world evidence on the utility of SARS-CoV-2 mass screening in correctional facilities remains limited. The Connecticut Department of Correction (CTDOC) implemented an adaptive testing programme involving serial mass screening with reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR). Data from this programme offer a unique opportunity to evaluate mass screening throughout the pandemic.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal analysis of people tested as part of mass screening between November 2020 and May 2023 while housed in a CTDOC cell. We estimated the time to test result and the average number of exposures averted by isolating residents who tested positive. Averted exposures were calculated by comparing exposures in the 14 days following a positive versus negative test.ResultsAmong the 600 477 recorded tests, collected among 29 728 residents, 177 845 were collected during mass screening and included. Median time from test collection to result notification was 2 days (IQR: 1–3) for positives and 1 day (IQR: 1–2) for negatives. Among residents who tested positive during mass screening, the average number of susceptible cellmates declined from 1.23 on the test day to 0.16 by day 8; at the cell block level, susceptible contacts fell from 79.5 to 0.19 by day 9. These declines corresponded with an average of 0.65 (95% CI: 0.62 to 0.67) cell and up to 66.9 (95% CI: 64.7 to 69.1) cell block exposures adverted per day per positive case. However, between testing and result notification, residents who tested positive had an average of 1.19 (95% CI: 1.15 to 1.23) susceptible cellmates and 73.0 (95% CI: 71.4 to 74.6) susceptible cell block contacts.ConclusionsMass screening in correctional facilities can reduce exposures to respiratory pathogens, such as SARS-CoV-2, through timely identification and isolation. However, test delays, limited isolation capacity and missed infections allow ongoing transmission even in well-resourced settings, suggesting that testing alone is unlikely to fully mitigate transmission in correctional facilities.

Introduction

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic beginning in early 2020, many countries implemented non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), which encompassed school and business closures, stay-at-home guidelines and testing for SARS-CoV-2.1 A growing body of mathematical modelling and real-world data has suggested that NPIs helped mitigate infection spread. However, there is limited real-world data evaluating testing programmes, especially when implemented within high-risk congregate living spaces, and scepticism about the value of such programmes persists.27

In the USA, a significant challenge in controlling the COVID-19 pandemic was curbing infection spread within congregate settings—including long-term care facilities and correctional facilities—which were inherently susceptible to outbreaks given the density of their housing environments.7 8 The Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) issued tailored infection control guidance for congregate settings, including correctional facilities, recommending testing symptomatic residents, close contacts (people who spent ≥15 min within six feet of infected persons) and isolating confirmed cases. While routine (mass) screening was considered valuable for early detection, it was recommended primarily for unvaccinated people and during periods of high community transmission.9 Early evidence regarding the effectiveness of mass testing within correction facilities, however, is striking—facilities which implemented at least one round of mass testing early in the pandemic identified over 12 times more cases than facilities which conducted symptom-based testing and contact tracing alone.10

Despite early findings highlighting the benefits of mass screening, limited data have been published on how mass screening was conducted within correctional facilities throughout the pandemic.1012 Further, the value of mass screening as a tool to minimise SARS-CoV-2 exposures remains unclear. This study documents how the Connecticut Department of Correction (CTDOC), a state operated system comprising 11 prisons and five jails as of 1 March 2020, implemented an adaptive testing programme comprising biweekly mass screening, contact tracing and symptom-based testing and evaluates the utility of mass screening followed by isolation as a tool to mitigate infection spread within correctional facilities.

Materials and methods

We conducted a longitudinal analysis among people housed in a CTDOC facility who underwent mass screening between 21 November 2020 and 31 May 2023. Residents were identified using CTDOC-maintained facility and COVID-19 surveillance databases. To increase our ability to identify SARS-CoV-2 exposures, we limited the sample to people who were housed in a cell at the time of testing. Using the CTDOC databases, we extracted demographic (age, race/ethnicity and gender), housing, SARS-CoV-2 testing and COVID-19 vaccination data for each identified resident. Testing records were supplemented with records provided by Quest Diagnostics (CTDOC’s primary laboratory partner for SARS-CoV-2 PCR processing).

Residents were followed for the 14 days following test collection to assess the impact of mass screening followed by isolation on potential SARS-CoV-2 exposures. To ensure our sample included only unique positive testing events, we excluded positive tests that were collected in the 14 days following a prior positive test. Further, to prevent duplicated follow-up time, follow-up was censored if another mass screening test was collected within 14 days while the resident was housed in a cell. Sex was determined at the time of intake from government identification documents. Housing data included facility, block and room name for each day of incarceration. The recorded testing data included the date of test collection, the date of test notification (if reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) and the reason for testing. This study was exempt from review by the Yale University Institutional Review Board as a public health surveillance activity (2000031675). We affirm that this manuscript is a transparent account of the study reported without omission of important findings.

Testing strategies and isolation protocol

Starting March 2020, the CTDOC began testing symptomatic residents and their contacts. In alignment with CDC recommendations, contacts were defined as residents who spent at least 15 min within 6 feet of an infected resident.9 In July 2020, the CTDOC implemented serial mass screening (conducted at least monthly) and in March 2022, they modified their strategy to be an adaptive schedule informed by community and facility burden, whereby mass screening was initiated when predefined internal criteria were met (figure 1; online supplemental section: Connecticut Department of Correction SARS-CoV-2 Testing Policies). Prior to 21 November 2020, all testing was conducted using RT-PCR. As the reason for testing was not recorded, all tests collected prior to this date were classified as ‘reason not ascertained’. After 21 November 2020, all mass screening was conducted by RT-PCRs, and rapid antigen tests (primary BinaxNOW) were used for all additional reasons for testing. Mass screening participation did not vary by comorbidity or medical vulnerability, as all residents—except those recently testing positive—were tested. The exclusion window following a positive result followed Connecticut’s Reopen Guidelines and was consistent with CDC correctional facility guidance.

Residents who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 were placed in isolation in accordance with Connecticut’s Reopen Guidelines and CDC recommendations for correctional facilities. During the first wave of the pandemic, symptomatic adult males were isolated in the CTDOC Medical Isolation Unit at Northern Correctional Institution. On 16 September 2020, this unit was relocated to MacDougall-Walker Correctional Institution. Both Northern and MacDougall-Walker Medical Isolation Units were equipped with cells with solid walls and doors as well as efficient ventilation and triage areas.13 Isolation at MacDougall-Walker consisted of individual isolation. During periods of high COVID-19 case volumes, asymptomatic adult males were isolated within their own facilities, either individually or in pooled (cohorted) arrangements, done in collaboration with Connecticut Department of Public Health guidance. During periods of low COVID-19 case volume, both asymptomatic and symptomatic males were isolated at MacDougall-Walker. Residents receiving specialised non–COVID-19 medical care (eg, dialysis or high-acuity mental health treatment) were isolated within their existing treatment units to maintain continuity of care (eg, Garner Correctional Institution for high-acuity mental health). Women and youth were isolated within their respective facilities, York Correctional Institution and Manson Youth Institution.

RT-PCR processing

Most RT-PCRs collected as part of CTDOC testing were processed at Quest Diagnostics. Following Emergency Use Authorisation from the Food and Drug Administration on 17 March 2020, Quest Diagnostics analysed SARS-CoV-2 specimens using their SARS-CoV-2 RNA, Qualitative Real-Time RT-PCR, a RT-PCR test that detects SARS-CoV-2 RNA for pooled samples of up to four individual specimens.14 While pooled testing requires individual testing of each specimen within a positive pool, it has been shown to be highly accurate and to increase throughput and conserve resources.15

Statistical analysis

Mass screening throughout the pandemic

To show how mass screening was performed within the CTDOC during the study period, we visually summarised reported SARS-CoV-2 tests and positive SARS-CoV-2 tests by whether they were performed as part of mass screening. We also assessed frequency of testing as part of mass screening by calculating the proportion of residents with a recorded mass screening test in the last month and in the last 2 weeks, for each day of the study. Finally, we summarised resident demographic, vaccination and prior infection history characteristics by the presence of recorded test.

Resident characteristics at the time of mass screening test collection

We summarised the demographic, vaccination and prior infection history characteristics for people tested as part of mass screening while housed in cells by test result. Categorical variables were summarised with counts and percentages, and continuous variables were summarised using medians and first to third quartiles.

Time to mass screening test result and the number of SARS-CoV-2 exposure events during processing

Because mass screening was performed with RT-PCRs, we examined whether tests collected as part of mass screening demonstrated laboratory-related delays by estimating the median time from mass screening test collection (among residents housed in cells) to result notification. Additionally, to examine if time to test notification varied over the course of the pandemic, we estimated the 14-day rolling average time to test notification over the study period. To assess if the time to notification differed by test result, both analyses were stratified by result (positive vs negative).

For each resident who tested positive as part of mass screening while housed in cells, we estimated the average number of susceptible residents who resided in the same cell or cell block as the infected resident for the days between test collection and test result notification. We defined a susceptible contact as a resident who had no recorded infections in the past 90 days. Due to the absence of social network data, we assumed complete universal mixing within a cell block and defined the number of susceptible contacts within a cell block as the number of susceptible residents within the same cell block.

Impact of mass screening followed by isolation on potential SARS-CoV-2 transmission events

To assess how mass screening followed by isolation impacted potential transmission within a cell, we estimated the average number of averted exposures per day over the 14 days following positive mass screening test collection. First, for each day following test collection, we calculated the change in the number of susceptible contacts by subtracting the number of contacts on that day from the number on the day of test collection. We defined a susceptible contact as a cellmate of the infected resident who had no recorded SARS-CoV-2 in the past 90 days. To isolate the effect of mass screening followed by isolation, we used linear regression to estimate how changes in the number of susceptible contacts differed following positive and negative test collection. Our model included an outcome of susceptible contacts, a primary exposure of test result (positive or negative), and a modifying factor of time (days since test collect). This approach accounts for changes in the number of contacts resulting from routine movement and unrelated infection detection.

Next, we estimated the upper bound of the impact of mass screening followed by isolation within a cell block. In the absence of social network data, we assumed complete universal mixing within a cell block and, in alignment with CTDOC policy, assumed residents were restricted to their rooms (isolated) for the duration of the infection starting the day after positive test notification. Thus, we defined the number of susceptible contacts within a cell block as the number of residents within the same cell block who had no recorded infection in the prior 90 days until the day after positive test notification. Starting the day after positive test notification (day 1), we defined the number of susceptible contacts within a cell block as the number of susceptible contacts within a cell. We then calculated the number of averted contacts using the same approach as we used to estimate the number of averted contacts within a cell.

We defined our follow-up as 14 days due to early CDC quarantine recommendations.16 However, this is likely an overestimate of the average duration of infection and, as a sensitivity analysis, we performed the described analyses but limited the follow-up to 7 days.

Infection capture evaluation

As a secondary analysis, we examined whether infections were likely missed throughout the pandemic by assessing whether recorded prior infection proportions aligned with expected prior infection proportions. To do so, we estimated, for each day, the average proportion of residents tested as part of mass screening while housed in a cell who had a recorded prior infection using a Poisson regression with an exposure of calendar day modelled using a natural cubic spline. We compared this to seroprevalence estimates from US blood donors (online supplemental table 1). While this analysis was limited to people who were tested at part of mass screening on a given day, prior infection was defined as any prior infection—not one identified through mass screening. To reduce the risk of missed prior infections, we restricted our sample to people who were incarcerated since the beginning of the pandemic. Further, we removed biases resulting from differences in vaccination rates by limiting the comparison to unvaccinated persons. All analyses were performed in R V.4.3.1.

Patient and public involvement

This work was performed using retrospective, observation data. Neither patients nor the public were involved when conducting the study.

Results

Overview of mass screening

Between 1 March 2020 and 31 May 2023, 600 477 SARS-CoV-2 tests were collected among 29 728 residents of a CTDOC facility (figure 1). The majority of the tests and positive tests were collected as part of mass screening (proportion of all tests: 67.9%; positive tests: 47.2%; figure 1A,B). The median per cent of residents tested as part of mass screening in the last month between January 2021 and March 2023 was 79.7% (IQR: 75.1%–87.3%; figure 1C). The average test positivity of tests collected as part of mass screening was 1.7%.

Resident characteristics at the time of mass screening test collection

Among the 29 728 residents with a recorded SARS-CoV-2 test, 15 623 had a test collected as part of mass screening while they were housed within a cell. Of the 178 714 tests collected as part of mass screening among residents of cells, 764 were inconclusive and 105 positive tests were collected within 14 days of another positive test and therefore excluded. This left 177 845 tests collected among 15 614 residents, of which 2548 were positive (online supplemental figure 1). Included tests were collected among residents with a median age of 36 (IQR: 28–45), 7.3% were collected from women and 45.1% were collected among residents identified as non-Hispanic Black. Demographically, residents who contributed a positive test collected as part of mass screening were similar to residents who contributed a negative test; however, residents with a positive test were more likely to be unvaccinated and less likely to have had a prior infection than residents with a negative test (table 1).

Time to mass screening test result and the number of SARS-CoV-2 exposure events during processing

The median time to result notification for a positive test, collected as part of mass screening while people were housed in cells, was 2 days (IQR: 1–3). For a negative result, the median time to notifications was 1 day (IQR: 1–2, figure 2). This varied over the course of the pandemic, with the median time to negative test result notification exceeding the median time to positive test result notification during the infection peak, aligning with the winter wave of 2021–2022. For each resident who tested positive as part of mass screening, the average number of susceptible residents that resided in the same cell or cell block on the days between test collection and test result notification was 1.19 (95% CI: 1.15 to 1.23) and up to 73.0 (95% CI: 71.4 to 74.6), respectively.

Impact of mass screening followed by isolation on potential SARS-CoV-2 transmission events

Among residents who tested positive during mass screening, the average number of susceptible cellmates dropped from 1.23 on the day of testing to 0.16 by day 8 (figure 3A; online supplemental table 2). At the cell block level, susceptible contacts declined from 79.46 to 0.19 by day 9 (figure 3B; online supplemental table 2). In contrast, among those who tested negative, the average number of susceptibles declined from 1.17 to 0.94 and 94.69 to 94.17 over the 14 days within a cell and cell block, respectively (figure 3A,B; online supplemental table 2). These differences in the number of susceptible contacts following positive and negative test collection translated to an average of 0.65 (95% CI: 0.62 to 0.67) averted cell exposures per day and up to 66.9 (95% CI: 64.7 to 69.1) averted cell block exposures per day (figure 3B,C; online supplemental table 2). From our sensitivity analyses in which follow-up was limited to 7 days, the average averted exposures per day within cells and cell blocks were 0.53 (95% CI: 0.50 to 0.56) and 54.6 (95% CI: 52.4 to 56.9), respectively.

Discussion

Our analysis suggests that mass screening followed by isolation prevented further transmission of SARS-CoV-2, both within the cell and the rest of the cell block. On average, isolation following positive test collection averted 0.65 cell exposures per day and up to 66.9 averted cell block exposures per day. Although the latter estimate assumes universal mixing and perfect isolation adherence—likely representing an upper bound—these findings highlight the potential of mass screening followed by isolation as a tool to reduce transmission in high-density congregate settings such as correctional facilities. However, certain limitations likely reduced the full impact of these testing efforts.

As the literature has largely described single rounds of mass SARS-CoV-2 testing for US carceral facilities, particularly for the first few months of the pandemic, the advantage of our data set lies in its coverage, covering the fall of 2020 through the end of the pandemic (May 2023). This was important as population immunity grew and newer variants presented with fewer or milder symptoms.18 19 The sheer volume of mass screening performed at the CTDOC—as well as improvement in other infection control measures that were put in place as our understanding of the virus evolved—likely explains the low test positivity (1.7%) in comparison to other reports capturing ‘one-off’ testing from early in the pandemic.10

Our study is one of the few to evaluate potential structural or operational barriers to enacting routine mass screening for this vulnerable correctional population, which limited the ability of the CTDOC’s mass screening strategy to mitigate infection spread. First, the persistence of close contacts within cells for up to 2 weeks after positive test collection suggests that correctional settings may lack the capacity to isolate all individuals. Pooled or cohorted isolation, in which multiple people testing positive are housed together, may be considered for facilities that have exhausted all single-occupancy rooms. Second, while PCR-based mass screening facilitated accurate testing, our data suggest that it is subject to laboratory processing delays, which were most notable for positive results and during periods of high infection rates, likely due to pooled testing. Such delays slowed the initiation of isolation, resulting in continued exposures between infected and susceptible residents. Together, these challenges highlight how even well-resourced testing strategies may be limited by practical constraints in implementation.

Finally, while the dense housing conditions of congregate settings, such as long-term-care and correctional facilities, increase transmission risk,20 21 our data revealed a lower prior infection proportion than general US population seroprevalence estimates among unvaccinated people.17 Although the CIs overlapped, the lower point estimate was unexpected given the inherently higher transmission risk in these densely populated environments. This pattern may therefore reflect missed infections rather than actual reduced risk, despite consistent, high levels of testing.

This analysis is subject to limitations. Reasons for testing were not recorded for tests before 21 November 2020, restricting us from examining mass screening early in the pandemic. Symptom data were not routinely reported for tests collected as part of mass screening and we were unable to determine if mass screening captured infections in the absence of symptoms. Data on comorbidities or medical vulnerabilities were not available and we were unable to stratify any part of this analysis by such factors. While all residents were granted the right to refuse SARS-CoV-2 testing, any refusal was not included in our data set. Additionally, in the absence of CTDOC-specific seroprevalence data, we relied on general population estimates which allowed us to examine the likelihood of missed infections but not the quantity. Without social network data, we also assumed universal mixing within residents of the same cell block and compliance with isolation policies. This produced an upper estimate of the number of (1) cell block exposures between test collection and result notification and (2) averted cell block exposures. Given the assumption that isolation was nearly impossible to enforce in dormitory settings, our study was unable to comment on mass screening in these types of congregate living situations. Finally, testing data for CTDOC employees and their relationships to residents was not available in this study.

Our study provides real-world evidence on the effectiveness and practicality of a comprehensive and adaptive COVID-19 testing strategy in high-risk correctional environments. We found that consistent mass screening paired with isolation was associated with meaningful reductions in potential exposures at the cell and cell block exposures, reinforcing the value of mass screening within highly dense housing settings as a proactive public health tool. However, observed operational limitations—such as limited isolation capacity, delays in test result processing and potential missed infection—likely reduced the full impact of this well-resourced testing effort. Other questions to explore in future studies include the impact of mass screening compared with other triggers for testing, such as contact tracing and/or screening based on the presence of symptoms.
