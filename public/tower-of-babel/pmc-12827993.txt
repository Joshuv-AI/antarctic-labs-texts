Obstructive sleep apnea increases heartbeat-evoked potentials and reorganizes Granger-causal coupling between cortical interoception and autonomic variability during wakefulness
Authors: Mahsa Alidadi, Maryam Mohebbi, Parisa Adimi Naghan, Babak Mohammadzadeh Asl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) is a systemic disorder with far-reaching neuro-cardiac consequences that extend beyond sleep. While autonomic and cortical dysregulation in OSA have been studied, the temporal interactions between cortical interoceptive processing and autonomic variability during wakefulness remain poorly understood. This study investigates the severity-dependent alterations in cortical response to cardiac activity and the directionality of HEP–HRV temporal interactions in 366 adults stratified by OSA severity. We analyzed Heartbeat-Evoked Potentials (HEPs) derived from wakeful electroencephalography (EEG) as a marker of cortical interoception and examined their coupling dynamics with heart rate variability (HRV) features using Granger Causality (GC). Our results reveal a significant, monotonic increase in HEP amplitude over central (C3/C4, 320–480 ms) and frontal (F3/F4, 540–680 ms) regions with escalating OSA severity (p < 0.001), indicating heightened cortical sensitivity to cardiac afferent signals. Concurrently, HRV analysis shows progressive autonomic dysfunction, marked by reduced RMSSD, SDNN, SD1, and SD2 in severe OSA (p < 0.01). Crucially, GC analysis uncovers a severity-dependent reorganization of temporal predictive influence: while control individuals exhibit relatively balanced bidirectional interactions, severe OSA displays a predominant strength-based HRV \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document} HEP influence, particularly in frontal regions and parasympathetic-related metrics. These GC-derived findings reflect altered temporal dynamics between cortical interoceptive responses and autonomic variability. Overall, the results demonstrate that OSA induces a persistent, severity-dependent disruption of cortical–autonomic integration during wakefulness, positioning HEP amplitude and GC-based temporal coupling measures as promising non-invasive biomarkers for assessing the systemic neurological impact of OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) is a serious and prevalent sleep-related breathing disorder, affecting 2–14% of adults globally, with prevalence rising sharply with age and exceeding 50% in populations older than 65 years1. OSA is characterized by recurrent episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in intermittent hypoxia, repeated arousals, and fragmented sleep2. These nocturnal disturbances have wide-ranging systemic consequences, including increased risks of hypertension, coronary artery disease, and stroke3,4, as well as neurocognitive impairments affecting attention, memory, and executive function, accompanied by excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) and mood disturbances3,4.

Although OSA is traditionally regarded as a nighttime disorder, emerging evidence shows that its physiological impact extends well into wakefulness. Persistent daytime abnormalities in both brain activity and autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulation suggest that OSA represents a state-independent pathology rather than a purely sleep-bound condition3–9. During wakefulness, electroencephalography (EEG) studies consistently reveal cortical slowing–elevated delta and theta relative to alpha and beta power–most prominently in frontal regions3,5,10,11. This slowing correlates with nocturnal oxygen desaturation and contributes to daytime cognitive deficits, impaired vigilance, and reduced driving performance11–13.

Parallel to these cortical alterations, daytime autonomic dysfunction is a well-established feature of OSA. Heart rate variability (HRV) analyses consistently demonstrate reduced overall variability, diminished vagal tone, and impaired baroreflex sensitivity, reflecting chronic sympathovagal imbalance6–9,14,15. Importantly, HRV markers assessed during wakefulness have shown diagnostic value, with several studies reporting their ability to predict OSA severity and apnea–hypopnea index (AHI)(the number of apneic events per hour of sleep). While extensive research has characterized these cortical and autonomic disturbances, far less is known about how OSA affects the brain’s processing of internal physiological signals–its interoceptive function–during wakefulness.

Interoception, the brain’s capacity to sense and integrate internal bodily states, plays a fundamental role in autonomic regulation, emotional processing, and self-awareness. A key neural marker of interoception is the heartbeat-evoked potential (HEP), an EEG-derived signal time-locked to cardiac activity16,17. Although its precise physiological generators remain under investigation, converging evidence implicates a distributed network including the insular cortex, anterior and posterior cingulate cortices, somatosensory regions, prefrontal cortex, and brainstem structures such as the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS)18,19. These areas receive baroreceptive input via vagal and glossopharyngeal pathways and contribute to blood pressure regulation through the baroreflex loop. In OSA, repeated cycles of hypoxia and arousals may disrupt this baroreceptive feedback pathway, heightening sympathetic activity, reducing vagal tone, and altering cortical processing of cardiovascular signals—all factors that could modify HEP amplitude and spatial distribution. Supporting this interpretation, sleep-based studies have reported significantly elevated HEP amplitudes in OSA patients, particularly over central regions20,21, suggesting that interoceptive alterations may persist across states.

Beyond physiological mechanisms, HEP amplitude is modulated by various psychological and pathological conditions, including emotional arousal22, stress23, depression24, and nightmare disorder16. Despite this diverse sensitivity, no study to date has systematically examined HEP during wakefulness in OSA, nor explored how the brain’s interoceptive processing interacts dynamically with autonomic activity during this state.

Understanding these interactions requires moving beyond univariate analyses of isolated EEG or electrocardiogram (ECG) signals. Instead, a multivariate framework is necessary to characterize the bidirectional, time-dependent relationships within the cortical–autonomic system. Granger causality (GC) analysis provides such a framework by quantifying whether past fluctuations in one physiological process statistically improve the prediction of another25. Applied to the HEP–HRV relationship, GC captures cortical-to-cardiac (HEP\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HRV) and cardiac-to-cortical (HRV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HEP) predictive influences, offering insight into the temporal organization of interoceptive–autonomic coupling. Although GC methods have previously been applied to study brain–heart interactions in OSA, existing work has primarily focused on sleep or has not used HEP as a marker of interoception.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study investigates cortical interoceptive processing and directional brain–heart interactions during wakefulness in a large clinical cohort (N = 366). We hypothesize that (i) HEP amplitude varies systematically across OSA severity levels, reflecting alterations in interoceptive processing, and (ii) the directionality of HEP–HRV coupling differs across control, mild, moderate, and severe OSA groups. Here, directionality represents temporal predictive influence derived from GC, without implying direct anatomical efferent or afferent pathways. The primary outcome of this study is the characterization of HEP amplitude across severity levels, and the secondary outcome is the quantification of directional cortical–autonomic coupling using GC during wakefulness.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The dataset used in this study was collected at the Sleep Clinic of Masih Daneshvari Hospital, Tehran, as part of an ongoing clinical research program that began in April 2020. Data acquisition was performed under the direct supervision of a board-certified pulmonologist specializing in sleep disorders. The study population consisted of 366 adult patients (mean age: 57.96 ± 13.04 years; 50.93% male) who were referred to the clinic with symptoms suggestive of sleep-disordered breathing, including severe snoring, excessive daytime sleepiness, frequent nocturnal awakenings, and chronic fatigue.

Participants were categorized into four groups based on the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), following AASM guidelines26: control (AHI < 5), mild (5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} AHI < 15), moderate (15 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} AHI < 30), and severe OSA (AHI \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 30). A small number of subjects were excluded during initial quality screening due to excessive artifacts, poor recording quality, or insufficient data length; these individuals were not included in the final dataset. All remaining participants exhibited predominantly obstructive respiratory events, with minimal evidence of central sleep apnea. Final diagnostic labels were confirmed through manual scoring of full-night PSG recordings by a board-certified pulmonologist and sleep specialist.

To minimize confounding influences on autonomic or cortical activity, participants were instructed to abstain from tobacco use, caffeine intake, and medications affecting cardiovascular or neural function (e.g., beta-blockers, antidepressants) for at least 12 hours prior to the study. Individuals with neurological, cardiovascular, or metabolic disorders, as well as those receiving chronic medications influencing ANS or cortical activity, were excluded. All subjects underwent an overnight in-lab polysomnography (PSG) study using a standardized clinical recording system. The recorded channels included EEG (C4–A1, C3–A2, O1–A2, O2–A1, F3–A2, F4–A1), electrooculography (EOG) (LOC–A2, ROC–A1), chin and leg EMG, and ECG signals obtained from chest leads I and II, all sampled at 200 Hz. Respiratory signals, including nasal/oral airflow and thoracic and abdominal effort, were sampled at 10 Hz, while oxygen saturation, plethysmography, pulse rate, and body position were recorded at 1 Hz.

All signals were acquired synchronously within the same PSG hardware platform, ensuring perfect temporal alignment via the system’s internal clock and eliminating the need for any external synchronization triggers. Raw PSG recordings were stored in EDF format and annotated using Sleepware G3 software. Demographic information (age, sex, neck circumference) was stored separately in anonymized form. For the present study, all artifact-free EEG channels and Lead I of the ECG were used for subsequent analyses.

The research protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University (Approval ID: IR.MODARES.REC.1404.100). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

ECG and EEG pre-processing

A rigorous multi-stage preprocessing pipeline was implemented to ensure high-quality EEG and ECG signals for reliable HEP extraction. This pipeline effectively suppressed residual artifacts–including those frequently observed in occipital derivations –and yielded clean, stable signals suitable for downstream analyses.

For each subject, a 5-minute artifact-free wakefulness segment containing simultaneously recorded EEG (six channels) and ECG (Lead I) was first selected. To guarantee consistent data quality, segment selection followed a combined quantitative–visual quality control procedure. ECG quality was evaluated using a QRS template-matching metric27 to verify stable beat morphology, after which all detected R-peaks were visually inspected by an experienced researcher to confirm accurate detection and the absence of ectopic beats. EEG channels were similarly inspected to exclude intervals containing muscular bursts, electrode pops, or low-amplitude noise. Only segments satisfying both quantitative and expert visual criteria were retained.

Following segment selection, EEG signals were re-referenced to the averaged mastoids (A1–A2) and both EEG and ECG underwent 50 Hz notch filtering to remove power-line interference. A zero-phase Butterworth band-pass filter (0.1–35 Hz) was then applied to suppress slow drifts and high-frequency noise while preserving physiologically meaningful activity for HEP and HRV analysis.

A key challenge in processing wakefulness EEG is the removal of eye-blink artifacts. Although multiple channels were recorded, the limited channel count and short recording duration rendered Independent Component Analysis (ICA) impractical. To address this, we employed the Variational Mode Extraction–Discrete Wavelet Transform (VME–DWT) algorithm28, a two-stage denoising procedure in which blink-contaminated intervals are first identified through Variational Mode Extraction and subsequently cleaned using automated wavelet-based decomposition. This approach allowed precise and efficient suppression of ocular artifacts without distorting underlying cortical activity. After preprocessing, each subject’s dataset consisted of six clean 5-minute EEG signals (C3, C4, O1, O2, F3, F4) recorded simultaneously with a clean 5-minute ECG Lead I signal.

HEP extraction

HEPs were extracted from EEG signals time-locked to ECG R-peaks, following the procedure illustrated in Fig. 1. R-peaks were detected using the Pan–Tompkins algorithm29 (Fig. 1.a), and all detections were subsequently verified through visual inspection to ensure precise peak localization. For each validated R-peak, an EEG epoch extending from –200 ms to 800 ms was extracted. Baseline correction was applied by subtracting the mean amplitude of the –200 to 0 ms interval to remove slow drifts and DC offsets. A critical consideration in HEP analysis is the attenuation of cardiac field artifacts (CFAs), which arise from the spread of the heart’s intrinsic electrical activity to the scalp30. Given the limited number of channels available in this dataset, spatial filtering techniques such as ICA or Principal Component Analysis (PCA) were not feasible. To minimize CFA contamination, the analysis focused on a latency window from 300 to 700 ms after the R-peak, which lies beyond the end of the T-wave and sufficiently prior to the next cardiac cycle. Participants with consistently short RR intervals (i.e., <700 ms) were excluded to ensure temporal separation between consecutive heartbeats. Across all included subjects, the mean heart rate during wakefulness was 67.4 ± 8.9 bpm, corresponding to an RR interval of approximately 890 ms, confirming that the selected window remained free of activity from the ongoing cardiac cycle. Respiratory rates (14.8 ± 2.5 breaths/min) further indicated low likelihood of respiration-related contamination within this interval.

This 300–700 ms window has been widely used in the literature to suppress residual CFA contributions while preserving the cortical components of the HEP20. Importantly, the HEP in this latency range exhibits a spatially broad and diffuse topography, enabling reliable extraction even with a limited number of scalp electrodes. After artifact rejection, an average of 349 ± 86 clean epochs per subject contributed to HEP estimation. The mean number of retained epochs was comparable across severity groups: 366 in the Control group, 357 in Mild OSA, 350 in Moderate OSA, and 340 in Severe OSA.

For all processing steps–including R-peak alignment, epoch extraction, artifact mitigation, and waveform visualization–the open-source NeuroHEP Python package31, developed by the authors, was used. This package provides a standardized and reproducible framework for HEP analysis across large cohorts.

HRV feature extraction

HRV offers a comprehensive window into ANS function. Beyond simple inspection of RR intervals, extracting multiple HRV features enables a more detailed characterization of both parasympathetic and sympathetic influences on cardiac dynamics. Accordingly, HRV metrics were derived from the same 5-minute preprocessed ECG segments used for HEP extraction. Features were computed using Kubios HRV software (version 4.1.2.1; Kubios Oy, Kuopio, Finland), a validated and widely adopted platform for clinical and research applications.

Time-domain features included SDNN, the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals, which reflects overall HRV, and RMSSD, the root mean square of successive differences, a robust marker of short-term parasympathetic modulation. In the frequency domain, high-frequency (HF) power (0.15–0.40 Hz) was extracted as an index of vagally mediated cardiac control. Nonlinear cardiac dynamics were quantified using the Poincaré plot, in which each RR interval is plotted against the subsequent interval. An ellipse is fitted to this distribution: SD1 captures variability perpendicular to the line of identity (short-term variability), whereas SD2 reflects variability along the line of identity (long-term variability). These nonlinear indices provide complementary information beyond traditional linear metrics. Statistical analysis and Granger causality analysis

Normality of continuous demographic variables (age and BMI) was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because both variables violated normality assumptions, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied across the four OSA severity groups, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Gender distribution was evaluated using the Chi-square test of independence. These analyses ensured appropriate statistical handling of both continuous and categorical demographic characteristics.

Group differences in HEP waveforms were first examined between the control group (AHI < 5, N=31) and all participants with OSA (AHI\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}5, N=335) across all six EEG channels (C3, C4, F3, F4, O1, O2). A non-parametric cluster-based permutation test32 was used to identify significant temporal clusters. Two-tailed independent t-tests were performed at each time point, and samples with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document} were grouped into clusters based on temporal adjacency. Cluster statistics were computed as the sum of t-values, and statistical significance was determined using a null distribution generated by 1,024 random label shuffles. Clusters exceeding the 95th percentile were considered significant. All analyses were restricted to the CFA-minimal HEP window (300–700 ms). To verify that this window was free from cardiac contamination, the same cluster-based testing procedure was applied to the time-locked ECG waveform, confirming the effectiveness of the CFA-minimal approach.

Next, HEP amplitudes were compared across the four OSA severity groups (Control: N=31, Mild: N=85, Moderate: N=106, Severe: N=144) using the significant temporal windows identified in the preceding step (e.g., C3/C4 320-480 ms and F3/F4 540-680 ms). To test for monotonic progression of HEP amplitude with increasing severity, the Jonckheere–Terpstra (JT) test33 was applied to each electrode. Kendall’s Tau34 was additionally computed to quantify the direction and strength of these ordered trends, with values ranging from –1 (perfectly decreasing) to +1 (perfectly increasing).

To complement these univariate analyses and jointly assess spatial and severity effects, mean HEP amplitudes extracted from each electrode-specific significant temporal window were entered into a two-factor ANOVA with the factors Severity and Electrode. This model evaluated both the topographical organization of HEPs and the influence of disease severity. Because time points were already averaged within each significant window, time was not included as an additional factor. When required, Tukey-adjusted post-hoc comparisons were applied.

HRV metrics were analyzed across the same four severity groups. As HRV features (time-domain, frequency-domain, and nonlinear indices) exhibited heterogeneous distributional profiles, each feature was individually tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed features were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey-adjusted post-hoc tests. Non-normal features were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test, and significant group effects were followed by Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction. This hybrid statistical strategy ensured rigorous, distribution-appropriate group comparisons across all HRV metrics. All statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 27).

To characterize the directional coupling between cortical interoceptive processing and autonomic dynamics across OSA severity levels, we employed a bivariate GC framework applied to time-resolved EEG- and ECG-derived features during wakefulness. GC evaluates whether the inclusion of past values of one signal improves the prediction of another, thereby estimating the temporal directionality of functional interactions25. Because GC requires sufficiently long and locally stationary time series, each subject’s 5-minute wake epoch was segmented into overlapping 30-second windows with a 25-second overlap (5-second step), yielding 55 windows per modality (Fig. 3.a). This window length balanced the requirement for local stationarity with the need to retain a physiologically meaningful number of heartbeats and HEP epochs per segment. Autonomic fluctuations do not exceed the HF band (0.15–0.40 Hz), and a 30-second window captures this range fully35. (b) For each segment, RR intervals are extracted from ECG segments, and HEP amplitudes are computed from time-locked EEG epochs within statistically significant post-R-peak windows ([320–480] ms for C3/C4, [540–680] ms for F3/F4). (c) Time sequences of HRV features and HEP amplitudes are constructed across all segments, forming input sequences for bidirectional bivariate GC analysis to infer the temporal directionality of HEP–HRV interactions.

From each EEG window, HEP waveforms were extracted as described in Section 2.3. Epochs time-locked to R-peaks were averaged and reduced to scalar amplitudes by computing the mean value within the individually derived CFA-minimal time windows identified via cluster-based permutation testing (Fig. 3.b). This procedure yielded four HEP amplitude time series per subject (C3, C4, F3, F4). In parallel, HRV metrics (SDNN, RMSSD, HF, SD1, SD2) were computed from each ECG window. Because physiological interpretation of HRV features is not appropriate at 30-second durations, these measures were treated as ultra-short dynamic proxies enabling construction of HRV time series suitable for autoregressive modeling.

Both EEG and ECG were sampled at 200 Hz. After segmentation, one HEP and one HRV feature were extracted every 5 seconds, yielding synchronized time series with a uniform sampling rate of 0.2 Hz. This strict temporal alignment ensured equivalent temporal resolution across modalities and permitted valid bivariate autoregressive modeling.

For each participant, this procedure generated four HEP time series and five HRV time series of equal length (Fig. 3.c). The GC from y to x (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$y \rightarrow x$$\end{document}) is quantified as:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_{y \rightarrow x} = \ln \left( \frac{\operatorname {Var}(\varepsilon _{x,\text {cond}})}{\operatorname {Var}(\varepsilon _{x,\text {uncond}})} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\operatorname {Var}(\varepsilon _{x,\text {uncond}})$$\end{document} is the variance of the prediction error of x using only its own past values (univariate model), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\operatorname {Var}(\varepsilon _{x,\text {cond}})$$\end{document} is the variance when past values of y are also included (bivariate model). A significant \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{y \rightarrow x} > 0$$\end{document} indicates that y Granger-causes x, meaning the past of y improves the prediction of x.

Throughout this study, GC directionality was interpreted solely as temporal predictive influence. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\textrm{HEP} \rightarrow \textrm{HRV}}$$\end{document} reflects the extent to which cortical interoceptive activity predicts subsequent autonomic fluctuations, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\textrm{HRV} \rightarrow \textrm{HEP}}$$\end{document} describes how autonomic patterns predict later cortical responses to cardiac afferent signals. These estimates capture temporal organization without implying physiological efferent or afferent pathways. Bidirectional GC was computed for each HEP–HRV pair.

The optimal VAR model order p was selected for each pair using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) across candidate lags 1–5. Statistical significance was determined using a permutation-based bootstrap: one time series was randomly time-shuffled 1,024 times to produce a null distribution of GC values. Observed GC values exceeding the 95th percentile were deemed significant (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document}, uncorrected). Multiple comparisons across channels and features were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. All GC computations were performed using the statsmodels Python package (version 0.14.4).

To extend GC analysis beyond the binary determination of significance, three secondary indices were computed to quantify directional dominance, interaction magnitude, and temporal organization. For each subject and each HEP–HRV feature pair, a directionality index was defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _{\textrm{Dir}} = S_{\textrm{HEP}\rightarrow \textrm{HRV}} - S_{\textrm{HRV}\rightarrow \textrm{HEP}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{\textrm{HEP}\rightarrow \textrm{HRV}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{\textrm{HRV}\rightarrow \textrm{HEP}}$$\end{document} represent the binary significance outcomes of the two GC directions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S \in \{0,1\}$$\end{document}). This index therefore ranges from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1$$\end{document}, capturing subject-level directional dominance. Because binary significance alone does not reflect the magnitude of predictive influence, the raw GC \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F$$\end{document}-statistics were used to compute a strength-difference metric:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _{\textrm{Strength}} = F_{\textrm{HRV}\rightarrow \textrm{HEP}} - F_{\textrm{HEP}\rightarrow \textrm{HRV}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where positive values indicate stronger autonomic-to-cortical predictive coupling. To characterize the temporal structure of directional interactions, the optimal predictive lag associated with each significant GC effect was extracted. A lag-difference index was defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta _{\textrm{Lag}} = \ell _{\textrm{HRV}\rightarrow \textrm{HEP}} - \ell _{\textrm{HEP}\rightarrow \textrm{HRV}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\textrm{HRV}\rightarrow \textrm{HEP}}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell _{\textrm{HEP}\rightarrow \textrm{HRV}}$$\end{document} denote the VAR-derived optimal lags for each direction. To statistically compare the three indices across OSA severity levels, each metric was first tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since all indices (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Dir}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Strength}}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Lag}}$$\end{document}) violated normality assumptions, non-parametric group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed—when appropriate—by Dunn’s post-hoc tests with Bonferroni adjustment. All multiple comparisons across feature pairs and metrics were controlled using FDR correction.

Results

Group differences in demographic variables were examined to assess whether age, BMI, or gender varied across the four OSA severity levels (Control, Mild, Moderate, Severe). Kruskal–Wallis tests identified significant group effects for both age (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{2}=18.11$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.001$$\end{document}) and BMI (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{2}=22.47$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.001$$\end{document}). Post-hoc comparisons showed that participants with severe OSA were older than those with moderate OSA (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=0.001$$\end{document}). BMI was significantly higher in all OSA groups relative to controls (all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.01$$\end{document}). Gender distribution did not differ across groups (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^{2}=6.74$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=0.081$$\end{document}). These characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Cluster-based permutation testing revealed two temporally and spatially distinct windows in which HEP amplitudes differed significantly between controls and OSA patients. Over central electrodes (C3, C4), a significant cluster emerged between 320–480 ms; over frontal electrodes (F3, F4), a second significant cluster appeared between 540–680 ms (cluster-level \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.001$$\end{document} for both). No significant effects were found at occipital sites.

Across both significant windows, HEP amplitudes demonstrated severity-dependent increases, with progressively larger responses observed in more severe OSA groups. Discussion

This study provides a detailed characterization of the sustained disruption of cortical–autonomic interactions in OSA during wakefulness. By jointly analyzing HEPs, autonomic indices derived from HRV, and their temporal predictive coupling via GC, we demonstrate that OSA is not merely a sleep-related respiratory disorder but a chronic, systemic condition whose neural and autonomic consequences persist into wakefulness. Across a large cohort (N = 366), three severity-dependent patterns emerged: (1) a monotonic increase in cortical responsiveness to cardiac afferent signals, (2) progressive autonomic imbalance with declining parasympathetic tone, and (3) a severity-dependent reorganization of HEP–HRV temporal interactions, most pronounced in severe OSA.

The most robust neural finding was the steady increase in HEP amplitude across severity levels, observed over central and frontal electrodes within two distinct post-R-peak intervals (320–480 ms for C3/C4; 540–680 ms for F3/F4). This pattern reflects a graded increase in cortical sensitivity rather than categorical group differences. Because HEPs index the cortical response to cardiac afferent input, heightened amplitudes are interpreted as increased cortical reactivity to internal bodily signals. This interpretation is consistent with prior findings showing elevated HEP amplitude in OSA during sleep, especially over central regions21, and our results extend these observations into wakefulness. The two-factor ANOVA further confirmed a main effect of severity on HEP amplitude, while the lack of significant Tukey pairwise differences aligns with the monotonic but subtle trends captured by JT and Kendall analyses.

Physiologically, sustained cortical hyper-responsivity during wakefulness may arise from the cumulative effects of intermittent hypoxia, recurrent arousals, sympathetic surges, and reductions in baroreflex and vagal modulation. These mechanisms can sensitize interoceptive cortical regions over time. Supporting this interpretation, heightened HEP amplitudes have been reported in other high-arousal contexts, such as nightmare disorder during REM sleep16, suggesting diminished interoceptive filtering. Conversely, reduced HEP amplitudes in pediatric sleep-disordered breathing17 may reflect developmental differences in sensory gating. These observations position our findings within a broader theoretical frame of interoceptive hyper-responsivity in adult OSA.

Cardiovascular alterations characteristic of OSA also likely contribute to the HEP trends. Cycles of intermittent hypoxia produce large blood pressure fluctuations36 and chronic sympathetic activation37, modifying the cardiac afferent signals that reach the brain. Reduced baroreflex sensitivity38 alters the timing and strength of vagal afferents ascending toward insular and cingulate interoceptive regions39. These pathways help explain the observed HEP increases, which reflect these modified cardiovascular inputs.

Parallel to cortical hyper-reactivity, our findings show clear autonomic dysregulation. Parasympathetic HRV indices (RMSSD, SD1) declined steadily with increasing OSA severity, consistent with previous reports of reduced waking vagal tone in OSA6,7. Nonlinear indices showed similar patterns, while frequency-domain metrics exhibited weaker group differences, consistent with prior observations7. As illustrated in Figure 2, the compact, clustered distribution in Poincaré plots of severe OSA patients reflects diminished parasympathetic modulation and reduced autonomic flexibility.

Our GC results clarify how these cortical and autonomic alterations interact. In controls, HEP–HRV coupling was relatively balanced in both directions, consistent with stable bidirectional interplay between cortical interoception and autonomic variability. Mild and moderate OSA showed subtle deviations, as reflected by small negative shifts in the directionality index (Table 6), indicating slightly increased HEP\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HRV influence but without substantial restructuring of coupling patterns.

In severe OSA, however, the temporal structure of coupling changed markedly. HRV fluctuations more often improved the prediction of HEP amplitudes than the reverse direction, consistent with the strikingly elevated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Strength}}$$\end{document} seen only in this group. This reflects a predominance of HRV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HEP temporal predictive influence absent in lower severities. These effects were most apparent over frontal electrodes and in parasympathetic-linked HRV features (HF, RMSSD), suggesting heightened cortical sensitivity to autonomic variability within affective and regulatory prefrontal–cingulate circuits40.

The timing of these interactions provided further insight. Although the modal GC lag was 1 across all groups, indicating generally fast cortico–autonomic coupling, mean lags showed subtle directional differences. The lag-difference index (Table 6) showed a significant difference between severe and mild OSA, driven by shorter HRV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HEP predictive horizons in severe OSA. This suggests a disruption of the temporal organization of cortical–autonomic interactions in advanced disease. Other between-group differences in lag were small and not statistically significant.

Together, the three GC-derived indices offer a coherent view of the progressive reshaping of cortical–autonomic coupling with OSA severity. Early in the severity spectrum, the negative shift in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Dir}}$$\end{document} suggests modestly increased HEP\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HRV influence–possibly reflecting compensatory cortical engagement with autonomic regulation. As severity increases, this reciprocal structure weakens. In severe OSA, the large positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Strength}}$$\end{document} indicates disproportionately stronger HRV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HEP effects, consistent with chronic autonomic dysregulation in which exaggerated cardiovascular fluctuations exert dominant autonomic-driven influence on cortical interoceptive responses. The negative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta _{\textrm{Lag}}$$\end{document} in severe OSA, indicating shorter-latency HRV\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rightarrow$$\end{document}HEP effects, further supports this interpretation. Altogether, these patterns suggest a severity-dependent trajectory: early compensatory cortical involvement, reduced reciprocal coordination in moderate OSA, and dominant, fast autonomic drive in severe OSA.

While this study provides important insights into neuro-cardiac interactions in OSA, certain limitations offer valuable opportunities for future research. One key limitation is the cross-sectional design, which provides a snapshot of neuro-cardiac interactions across varying OSA severity levels at a single time point. While this design is useful for examining relationships within a defined timeframe, it limits the ability to track temporal changes and establish causal links. Longitudinal studies would allow for a more dynamic understanding of how HEPs and HEP-HRV interactions evolve over time, particularly in response to interventions such as CPAP therapy. Although gender distribution did not differ significantly across groups, both age and BMI showed significant increases with OSA severity. These variables may partially confound the observed associations between OSA and neuro-cardiac measures. Future studies should include age- and BMI-matched groups or statistically control for these covariates to isolate the specific effects of OSA on cortical and autonomic function.

An important consideration is the timing of the wakefulness recordings, which were conducted prior to sleep. Although efforts were made to minimize potential biases from residual sleepiness or fatigue, the timing of these recordings may have influenced the cortical–autonomic interactions observed. Future studies could consider recording wakefulness data at different times of day, particularly when participants are at their peak alertness or during midday, to minimize the impact of sleepiness and ensure a more accurate reflection of neuro-cardiac dynamics during optimal wakefulness. Another promising avenue for future work is to adapt the HEP–HRV GC framework to NREM and REM sleep. The pronounced shifts in both autonomic regulation and cortical dynamics across sleep stages may uncover patterns of HEP–HRV directional coupling that cannot be captured during wakefulness alone, and thus would meaningfully complement the wake-based findings of the present study. The control group size (31 participants) in this study was relatively small in comparison to the OSA severity groups, which may have affected the statistical power of the results. Expanding the size of the control group and ensuring a more balanced distribution across the OSA severity levels would enhance the reliability of the conclusions regarding the impact of OSA on cortical–autonomic dynamics. A more balanced sex distribution would also be necessary to enable meaningful examination of potential sex-related effects.

The use of only six EEG channels limits the ability to accurately localize cortical processes involved in interoception. High-density EEG could provide more precise mapping of regions responsible for processing cardiac signals. Additionally, the limited number of channels made preprocessing more challenging, as advanced methods, such as ICA, require a higher number of channels for effective artifact removal. These limitations may affect the robustness of the results and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, the neurophysiological biomarkers identified, such as HEP amplitude and Granger Causality-based directional coupling, demonstrate strong potential. However, further validation in larger, independent cohorts is essential to confirm the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, the incorporation of machine learning techniques could enhance the diagnostic and prognostic capabilities of these biomarkers, offering the potential for developing non-invasive tools to monitor OSA severity and its response to treatment in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study shows that OSA is associated with a robust and severity-dependent disruption of cortical–autonomic coupling that remains evident during wakefulness. Across the severity spectrum, we identified a consistent triad of abnormalities: (1) increased cortical sensitivity to cardiac afferent signals, reflected in the monotonic rise of HEP amplitudes over central and frontal regions; (2) reduced autonomic flexibility, expressed as progressive declines in parasympathetic HRV indices; and (3) a reorganization of HEP–HRV temporal interactions, captured by GC analyses. A key finding is the shift from the balanced bidirectional temporal predictive influence observed in controls toward a pattern in which HRV fluctuations more strongly predict subsequent HEP variability in severe OSA. Together, these results indicate not a reversal of physiological directionality, but a change in the statistical architecture of cortico–autonomic coupling, with severe OSA showing faster and disproportionately stronger autonomically-driven influence on cortical interoceptive processing. The convergence of heightened cortical reactivity, reduced vagal modulation, and altered temporal coupling suggests that as OSA worsens, cortical responses to cardiac-related input become increasingly reactive and less regulated. The presence of these abnormalities during wakefulness emphasizes that the dysregulation is state-independent, extending beyond sleep and reflecting a persistent alteration of interoceptive and autonomic dynamics. Overall, these findings position OSA as a disorder with broad neural–autonomic consequences, not limited to respiratory impairment. They also demonstrate that HEP amplitude and GC-based measures of temporal coupling offer promising non-invasive markers of OSA severity and its systemic physiological impact, providing a foundation for improved monitoring and future interventional research.
