ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux restrains oxidative stress and neuroinflammation after spinal cord injury
Authors: Yuqi Shao, Jiayun Liu, Jiangyu Zhao, Kaixiao Xue, Xincan Wu, Heng Cao, Rui Bao, Qiancheng Sun, Yuanzhen Zhang, Peng Gao, Mingran Luo, Jian Chen, Guoyong Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal cord injury (SCI) triggers a persistent inflammatory microenvironment that contributes to secondary tissue damage and neurological dysfunction. However, the metabolic mechanisms sustaining activation of lesion-associated phagocytes remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify lipid-laden microglia/macrophages as a metabolically stressed inflammatory state that emerges after SCI and investigate the role of lipid efflux in regulating this process. Single-nucleus and immune-enriched transcriptomic analyses revealed coordinated upregulation of cholesterol transport and inflammatory pathways in lesion-associated phagocytes. Among lipid transporters, the ATP-binding cassette transporter ABCA1 was consistently induced across post-injury stages. Conditional deletion of ABCA1 in Cx3cr1-lineage cells led to excessive lipid droplet accumulation, enhanced reactive oxygen species production, sustained pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and impaired locomotor recovery following SCI. To therapeutically target this pathway, we performed structure-guided virtual screening and identified the small molecule Z231 as an ABCA1-binding compound. Pharmacological activation of ABCA1 reduced lipid accumulation, suppressed oxidative stress and inflammatory gene expression, and partially restored mitochondrial metabolic balance in microglia exposed to myelin debris. In vivo, systemic Z231 administration attenuated inflammatory signaling and improved functional recovery after SCI. Together, these findings identify ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux as a key regulator of microglial metabolic stress and neuroinflammation after spinal cord injury, and suggest that targeting lipid handling pathways may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for SCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03859-3.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Lipid efflux, Lipid-laden microglia/macrophages, ABCA1, Redox–inflammatory coupling

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) triggers a complex secondary injury cascade that extends far beyond the initial mechanical trauma. Progressive neuroinflammation, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and metabolic disturbances collectively shape a hostile microenvironment that severely restricts tissue repair and functional recovery [1–4]. Among the diverse cellular components involved, microglia and infiltrating macrophages play central roles in coordinating inflammatory responses and debris clearance. Although their inflammatory signaling pathways have been extensively investigated, accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic stress derived from myelin degradation products critically influences their functional phenotypes [2, 5, 6]. However, how lipid metabolic imbalance integrates with redox dysregulation to drive persistent inflammation after SCI remains poorly defined.

Myelin debris is highly enriched in cholesterol and complex lipids, imposing a substantial lipid burden on lesion-associated microglia/macrophages. Inefficient lipid processing leads to intracellular lipid droplet accumulation, generating foam-like immune cells that resemble pathological populations described in neurodegenerative and cardiovascular disorders and other diseases [5, 7–9]. Lipid droplets are now recognized as active regulators of immune and redox signaling rather than inert storage organelles [9]. Excessive lipid droplet accumulation can enhance mitochondrial dysfunction, amplify ROS production, and promote sustained pro-inflammatory cytokine release, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing metabolic–oxidative–inflammatory loop [9–11]. In the context of SCI, whether lipid droplet accumulation merely reflects myelin debris clearance or actively drives secondary injury progression has remained unresolved [12–15]. In this study, we identify a distinct population of injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages (ILLMs) that exhibit coordinated enrichment of lipid storage, oxidative stress, and inflammatory programs, suggesting that lipid metabolic failure constitutes a core pathological feature of chronic post-injury neuroinflammation.

ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) is a key regulator of cellular lipid efflux and cholesterol homeostasis [16–18]. In peripheral macrophages, ABCA1 limits foam cell formation and restrains inflammatory amplification [19]. In the central nervous system, ABCA1 has been implicated in lipid transport and neurodegenerative processes [20, 21]; however, its role in SCI-associated microglial/macrophage lipid remodeling remains largely unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that ABCA1 is essential for resolving myelin-derived lipid burden in lesion-associated microglia/macrophages. Loss of ABCA1 exacerbated lipid droplet accumulation, impaired myelin debris clearance, and intensified ROS generation and inflammatory signaling, ultimately resulting in aggravated tissue damage and poorer functional recovery. These findings position ABCA1 as a critical metabolic checkpoint linking lipid homeostasis to redox and inflammatory regulation in SCI.

To explore the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway, we identified a small-molecule ABCA1 activator, Z231, through structure-guided virtual screening and functional validation. Z231 effectively promoted ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux, reduced lipid droplet accumulation, suppressed ROS production, and attenuated inflammatory responses in ILLMs. Importantly, Z231 restored metabolic reprogramming toward a reparative phenotype and significantly improved tissue repair and motor recovery in vivo. Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed that Z231 stably binds to the site3 pocket of ABCA1 at the transmembrane–extracellular domain coupling interface, suggesting an allosteric mechanism that favors transporter conformational dynamics and lipid efflux efficiency. Collectively, our study establishes a loss-of-function versus gain-of-function mechanistic framework in which ABCA1 governs lipid-driven redox and inflammatory remodeling of ILLMs. These findings identify ABCA1 activation as a promising immune–metabolic therapeutic strategy for SCI and highlight lipid metabolic regulation as a central determinant of post-injury redox balance and neurological repair.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University and were performed according to institutional guidelines. Male C57BL/6J mice and genetically modified strains were housed in a controlled environment at the Nanjing Medical University Animal Facility, maintained at 22 °C, with 60% humidity and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to food and water. C57BL/6J male wild-type mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River. ABCA1flox/flox and Cx3cr1CreER mice were obtained from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. Conditional ABCA1 knockout mice (ABCA1f/fCx3cr1CreER+/−) and their controls (ABCA1f/fCx3cr1CreER−/−) were generated by crossing ABCA1flox/flox mice with Cx3cr1CreER mice. For ABCA1 deletion, mice received intraperitoneal injections of tamoxifen (20 mg mL-1; HY-13757 A, MCE) for 7 consecutive days. All animals were housed in groups of 3–5 per cage. Efforts were made to minimize suffering and reduce animal usage.

SCI model

Spinal cord injury (SCI) was induced in 8-week-old male mice. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and a midline incision was made to expose the T9-T10 level of the spine. The vertebral lamina was removed to expose the spinal cord. A controlled contusion injury was applied at the center of the exposed spinal cord using a spinal impactor (68099ll device, RWD, Shenzhen, China), delivering an 80 kdyn force to induce SCI. The SHAM group underwent the same surgical procedure, but without spinal cord impact. Post-surgical recovery included gentle warming and manual bladder expression twice daily to maintain hydration and prevent urinary retention and infections. Mice were treated with subcutaneous injections of penicillin (40000 U 20 g− 1; HY-B1463, MCE) for 3 consecutive days to prevent infection.

Primary microglia and Huma HMC3 cell line cultures

T-75 flasks were coated with poly-l-lysine (100 µg mL− 1; BS198-100, Biosharp) for 2 h at 37 °C, followed by three rinses with PBS and air drying prior to use. Primary microglia were isolated from neonatal C57BL/6J mice within 24 h after birth. Briefly, brains were rapidly harvested under sterile conditions and carefully stripped of meninges and visible blood vessels. The cortical tissues were minced into small fragments in prechilled DMEM/F-12 medium (KGL1205-500, KeyGEN BioTECH) and enzymatically digested with 0.25% trypsin (KGL2102-100, KeyGEN BioTECH) at 37 °C for 20 min with gentle agitation. Digestion was terminated by adding complete culture medium (DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10099141c, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (KGL2303-100, KeyGEN BioTECH)), followed by gentle trituration to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was transferred into centrifuge tubes and spun at 350 × g for 5 min. After removal of the supernatant, the pellet was resuspended in complete culture medium and subsequently filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer twice to eliminate residual debris. The cell suspension was seeded onto T75 flasks. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, and the medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. After 12–14 days, when mixed glial cultures reached confluence, microglia were detached by shaking the flasks at 180 rpm for 90 min at 37 °C. The supernatant containing suspended microglia was collected, centrifuged at 350 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in fresh complete medium. Cells were then plated onto poly-l-lysine–coated plates for subsequent experiments. The HMC3 human microglia cell line was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (CL-0620, Wuhan, China) and maintained under the same culture conditions as primary microglia.

Myelin debris isolation

Myelin debris was isolated from adult C57BL/6J mouse brains using a discontinuous sucrose-gradient protocol adapted from established methods and optimized according to our laboratory workflow [22–24]. Adult mouse brains were rapidly removed on ice, minced into small fragments, and homogenized in cold 0.32 M sucrose prepared in Tris-HCl buffer. The homogenate was brought to a final volume of 90 mL with 0.32 M sucrose and carefully layered onto 20 mL of 0.83 M sucrose in ultracentrifuge tubes to form a discontinuous gradient. Samples were centrifuged at 24,000 rpm for 45 min at 4 °C using a pre-cooled ultracentrifuge rotor with slow acceleration and deceleration. The myelin-enriched fraction was collected from the interface between the 0.32 M and 0.83 M sucrose layers.The collected interphase material was transferred to fresh tubes, adjusted to 35 mL with Tris-HCl buffer, and gently re-homogenized to disperse aggregates. The suspension was then redistributed into ultracentrifuge tubes, balanced with Tris-HCl buffer, and centrifuged again at 24,000 rpm for 45 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellets were resuspended in Tris-HCl buffer and subjected to an additional wash under the same centrifugation conditions. Final pellets were resuspended in sterile PBS and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. No hypo-osmotic shock step was used. Instead, contaminating cellular material was reduced by repeated density-gradient separation and isotonic washing in Tris-HCl buffer and PBS. The wet pellet weight was recorded only to estimate the yield of each preparation. The final concentration of myelin debris suspension was adjusted based on wet weight. After the final centrifugation step, the supernatant was carefully removed, and the myelin pellet was briefly blotted to remove excess buffer. The wet weight of the pellet was measured using a precision analytical balance. The pellet was then resuspended in sterile PBS to a final concentration of 100 mg/ml. To ensure consistency between preparations, protein concentration of representative samples was additionally measured a BCA protein assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Aliquots were stored at − 80 °C until use. For cell treatment, myelin debris was diluted to the indicated final concentration before use.

Primary microglia and Huma HMC3 cell line treatment

Primary microglia and Huma HMC3 cell were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 24-well plates or 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then exposed to myelin debris at a final concentration of 5 µg mL− 1 and incubated for 24 h to establish lipid-loading conditions. After stimulation, cells were harvested for subsequent analyses, including flow cytometry, quantitative real-time PCR, immunofluorescence staining, and CCK-8 assays. For pharmacological intervention experiments, microglia were first treated with myelin debris for 24 h, followed by three washes with PBS to remove residual myelin. Cells were then incubated with the indicated drugs at different concentrations for an additional 24 h before further experimental analyses.

Immunofluorescence staining and ROS detection

Cultured microglia or frozen spinal cord sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature and rinsed three times with PBS. Samples were blocked with QuickBlock™ Immunostaining Blocking Buffer (P0260, Beyotime) and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-IBA1 (1:200; Abcam, ab289874), anti-dMBP (1:200; Sigma-Aldrich, ab5864), and anti-CD68 (1:200; Abcam, ab53444), anti-ABCA1 (1:200; NOVUS, NB400-105), and anti-ABCG1 (1:200; NOVUS, NB400-132), and anti-Galectin-3 (1:200; Abcam, ab76245). After washing, samples were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark: Donkey anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluo 594 (1:200; Invitrogen, A21207), Donkey anti-Rat Alexa Fluo 555 (1:200; Invitrogen, A48270), Donkey anti-Goat Alexa Fluor 647 (1:200; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 705605003). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime) or Hoechst (62249, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For lipid droplet visualization, samples were incubated with BODIPY (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, D2191) for 15 min at room temperature prior to nuclear staining.

Intracellular oxidative stress in cultured microglia was evaluated using CellROX™ (C10422, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and MitoSOX™ (M36008, Thermo Fisher Scientific) fluorescent probes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were incubated with the corresponding probes diluted in PBS or HBSS medium at 37 °C under protected light conditions. After incubation, cells were gently washed with PBS to remove excess dye and immediately subjected to fluorescence imaging.

For detection of ROS in spinal cord fresh frozen sections, a commercial ROS detection kit (BB-470516, BestBio) based on DHE fluorescent probe was applied according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Frozen sections were incubated with the diluted probe solution at 37 °C in the dark for 60 min, followed by PBS washing and coverslip mounting and immediately subjected to fluorescence imaging.

All fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica THUNDER DMi8 inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica) under identical acquisition settings. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity, positive area, and cell counts was performed using ImageJ v1.41 software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis

Primary microglia were harvested after the indicated treatments, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in staining buffer for flow cytometric analysis. Lipid droplet content was assessed using BODIPY staining, whereas intracellular oxidative stress and mitochondrial superoxide production were evaluated using CellROX™ and MitoSOX™ reagents, respectively. Fluorescent staining procedures were performed according to the same protocols described in the immunofluorescence staining section. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and immediately analyzed using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer operated with CytExpert v2.4 software (Beckman Coulter FC600, USA). Forward and side scatter parameters were used to gate viable single-cell populations, and fluorescence intensities were collected using identical acquisition settings for all groups. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software (Tree Star, USA). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified to assess relative lipid droplet accumulation and ROS levels in microglia.

Virtual screening and molecular docking visualization

Structure-based virtual screening was performed using the cryo-EM structure of human ABCA1 (PDB ID: 7TBW). Protein preparation was conducted in Schrödinger Maestro 12.8 (Schrödinger, USA), including hydrogen addition, side-chain optimization, and restrained energy minimization. Residue E1245 located in site 3 was selected as the center of the docking grid. CNS Library from MedChemExpress were prepared using LigPrep to generate optimized three-dimensional conformations. Molecular docking was carried out using Glide with a hierarchical workflow consisting of HTVS, SP, and XP precision modes. The CNS Library was directly docked using XP mode. Compounds were ranked based on Glide docking scores and binding poses. Top candidates were further filtered according to key residue interactions, binding orientation stability, and structural diversity. Docking conformations of selected compounds were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (v2.5, Schrödinger, USA). Hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and spatial orientation within the ABCA1 binding pocket were examined to determine interaction patterns.

Affinity detection of Z231 binding to ABCA1

The direct interaction between human ABCA1 protein and Z2312316994 was evaluated using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and microscale thermophoresis (MST). For SPR analysis, ABCA1 protein (HY-P701357, MCE) was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip (BR100530, Cytiva) using standard amine-coupling chemistry on a Biacore 8 K system (Cytiva, USA). Z2312316994 was serially diluted and injected over the protein-coated surface in PBS-P+ buffer containing 5% DMSO. Association and dissociation phases were recorded, and solvent correction was applied. Binding kinetics were globally fitted using a 1:1 Langmuir model with Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Cytiva, USA) to obtain kinetic and equilibrium dissociation constants.

For MST analysis, fluorescently labeled ABCA1 protein was incubated with a serial dilution of Z2312316994 and loaded into standard capillaries. Thermophoretic movement was measured using a Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper Technologies, Germany). Both assays were independently repeated at least three times, and consistent KD values obtained from SPR and MST were used to confirm the direct and specific interaction between Z2312316994 and ABCA1.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (C6005, New Cell & Molecular Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Microglia were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight (1 × 104 cells well− 1). After the indicated treatments, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer. Blank wells containing medium and CCK-8 without cells were used for background subtraction. Cell viability was calculated as: Cell viability (%) = (As − Ab) / (Ac − Ab) × 100%. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells and spinal cord tissues using a rapid RNA extraction kit (RN001, Yishan Biotech) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples with adequate purity were diluted to uniform concentrations with RNase-free water. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (R323, Vazyme). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q712, Vazyme) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. All primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

Seahorse XF analysis of cellular bioenergetics

Microglia metabolic function was evaluated using a Seahorse XFe96 extracellular flux analyzer. OCR and ECAR were measured using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (103015-100, Agilent) and Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit (103344-100, Agilent), respectively. Cells were seeded in XF96 plates and cultured overnight (1 × 104 cells well− 1). Before measurement, culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium supplemented with 17.5 mM glucose, 0.5 mM pyruvate, and 2 mM glutamine, followed by equilibration at 37 °C in a non-CO₂ incubator. OCR and ECAR were measured under basal conditions and after sequential injection of mitochondrial modulators (1.5 µM Oligomycin, 2 µM FCCP, 0.5 µM Rot/AA for OCR; 0.5 µM Rot/AA, 0.5 mM 2-DG for ECAR). OCR reflected mitochondrial respiratory capacity, whereas ECAR served as an indicator of glycolytic activity. ATP production rates were determined using the Seahorse XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay Kit (103592-100, Agilent). 1.5 µM Oligomycin and 0.5 µM Rot/AA were sequentially injected to calculate mitochondrial and glycolytic ATP production based on OCR and ECAR changes. Following completion, cell counts were determined using a Cytation imaging system (BioTek, USA). All parameters were normalized to cell number. Data acquisition and calculations were performed using Seahorse Wave software (v2.6, Agilent).

Behavioral assessments

Neurological function recovery of mice was evaluated using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS). Assessments were performed 1 day before injury and at 1, 3, 7, 30, and 42 days after SCI by two blinded investigators. Scores were assigned according to standard criteria, and the mean value per animal was used for analysis.

Hindlimb EMG signals were recorded using a PowerLab system (PL2604, ADInstruments, Australia) with LabChart software (v8.1.31, ADInstruments). At designated time points, EMG activity was collected from hindlimb muscles under standardized stimulation conditions (0.2 ms pulse width, 1.5 mA current, 1 Hz repeat rate). Peak-to-peak amplitude and latency were quantified and normalized for intergroup comparisons.

Automated gait analysis was performed using the CatWalk system (Noldus Information Technology, Netherlands). Mice completed multiple uninterrupted runs at a consistent speed. Valid runs were selected automatically, and spatiotemporal gait parameters were extracted for quantitative analysis.

Skin overlying defined bony landmarks was shaved and labeled with markers. Locomotion was recorded using a high-speed camera (4 K, 120 fps). Videos were processed in Anaconda3 (Anaconda Inc., USA) for machine-learning–based marker detection. The tracked coordinates were decoded in Visual Studio Code (Microsoft Corporation, USA) to reconstruct limb motion as an interconnected rigid-segment model. Joint kinematics and trajectory parameters were subsequently calculated.

All behavioral analyses were conducted in a blinded manner, and data were summarized per animal before statistical comparison.

Toxicity assessment

Acute toxicity of Z231 was evaluated using a stepwise dose-escalation approach. An up-and-down procedure was first applied, in which mice received a single dose up to 2000 mg/kg, and were monitored for clinical signs of acute toxicity and mortality. No obvious toxic manifestations were observed at this limit dose. Subsequently, mice were administered Z231 at 10, 50, 100, or 200 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis of cardiac, hepatic, and renal function markers, including CK, ALT, AST, BUN, and creatinine. In parallel, heart, liver, and kidney tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and subjected to routine H&E staining for histopathological evaluation.

Molecule dynamics

Molecular dynamics simulations were performed using AMBER 24 (University of California, USA). The ABCA1–Z2312316994 complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer and neutralized with counterions. Ligand parameters were generated using Antechamber (AmberTools 24, USA) with the GAFF force field and AM1-BCC charges. After energy minimization, the system was heated to 300 K, equilibrated under NVT and NPT conditions, and subjected to a 100-ns production run. Long-range electrostatics were treated using the particle mesh Ewald method, and hydrogen bonds were constrained with the SHAKE algorithm using a 2-fs time step. Trajectory analyses were performed using CPPTRAJ (AmberTools 24, USA), with visualization in VMD 1.9.4 (USA) and PyMOL 2.5.0 (Schrödinger, USA). RMSD, RMSF, radius of gyration, hydrogen-bond occupancy, and solvent-accessible surface area were calculated. Binding free energies were estimated using the MM/GBSA method.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing

A 4 mm length of spinal cord tissue encompassing the lesion core was collected from SHAM mice and from SCI mice at 1, 3, 7, 14, 30, and 42 days post-injury. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing was performed with technical support from OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, spinal cord samples were processed to generate a clean nuclear suspension following the recommended workflow for nuclei isolation for 10x Genomics 3′ gene expression assays, including gentle tissue disruption, selective membrane lysis, debris removal, and filtration to minimize aggregates. Nuclei were counted, assessed for integrity, and adjusted to the target concentration prior to loading. Nuclei were encapsulated into gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs) using the Chromium instrument (10x Genomics), enabling nucleus-specific barcoding and UMI labeling. Reverse transcription was performed within GEMs, followed by emulsion breaking, cDNA amplification, and construction of 3′ gene expression libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were quality controlled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform using a standard paired-end configuration compatible with 10 × 3′ gene expression libraries.

CD45 + immune-enriched single-cell RNA sequencing

Immune-enriched scRNA-seq was conducted with technical support from Singleron Biotechnologies (Nanjing, China). Mice at defined time points were euthanized and transcardially perfused with cold saline. A 4-mm spinal cord segment centered on the lesion was dissected, rinsed in cold PBS, and dissociated using a Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (T) (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) to generate a single-cell suspension. Cells were passed through a 70-µm strainer, pelleted, and resuspended in 30% Percoll. The suspension was gently layered onto 70% Percoll and centrifuged (500 × g, 30 min, 4 °C) to remove myelin and tissue debris. Cells at the interface were collected and washed twice in pre-chilled staining buffer (HBSS supplemented with 1% FBS and 2 mM EDTA). For immune enrichment, cells were incubated with anti-CD45 antibody on ice in the dark for 30 min, washed three times with PBS containing 1% FBS, and resuspended for sorting. CD45⁺ cells were sorted on a CytoFLEX SRT flow sorter (Beckman Coulter, USA). Viability was assessed using Countstar Rigel S2 (Countstar, China), and only samples with viability > 90% were advanced for library preparation. Single-cell libraries were generated using the GEXSCOPE workflow (Singleron), including single-cell capture on SCOPE-chips, cell lysis, mRNA capture by barcoded beads, cDNA amplification, and library construction following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform with paired-end reads using a configuration recommended for GEXSCOPE transcriptome libraries.

Bioinformatic analysis

Raw data from snRNA-seq and CD45 + scRNA-seq libraries were processed using manufacturer-recommended pipelines (Cell Ranger, 10x Genomics; Singleron pipeline) to generate gene–barcode matrices. Downstream analyses were performed in Seurat (R). Low-quality cells/nuclei were filtered based on gene content and mitochondrial transcripts. Data were normalized, highly variable genes were selected, and principal component analysis was conducted. Batch effects across samples were corrected where necessary, followed by shared nearest-neighbor clustering. UMAP and PHATE were used for visualization. Cell identities were assigned according to canonical markers and reference atlases. Differential expression analysis used the Wilcoxon test, with |log₂FC| > 0.58 and adjusted q < 0.05 defining significance. GO/KEGG enrichment and gene-set activity scoring, including lipid transport, cholesterol efflux, and oxidative stress programs, were performed using standard implementations. Lineage-specific subclustering was carried out for microglia and macrophages.

Results

Delayed lipid droplet accumulation follows myelin degradation in phagocytic microglia/macrophages after SCI

To characterize the temporal relationship between myelin clearance and lipid droplet formation after spinal cord injury, immunofluorescence staining was performed at multiple time points post-injury. Lipid droplets were visualized by BODIPY staining, while microglia/macrophages were identified by IBA1 labeling. Representative images demonstrated minimal BODIPY signal in the Sham group and at early time points after injury (1 and 3 dpi). In contrast, BODIPY+ lipid droplets became clearly detectable at 7 dpi, reached a maximal level at 14 dpi, and subsequently declined at 30 dpi, with a further reduction observed at 42 dpi (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis of BODIPY+ area and integrated fluorescence intensity confirmed this temporal pattern (Fig. 1B). To evaluate myelin degradation dynamics, degraded myelin basic protein (dMBP) was examined in parallel. dMBP immunoreactivity increased rapidly at 1 dpi and peaked at 3 dpi. Thereafter, dMBP levels gradually declined beginning at 7 dpi, were markedly reduced at 14 dpi, and continued to decrease at 30 dpi, with minimal residual signal detectable at 42 dpi (Fig. 1C). Quantitative measurements of dMBP+ area and integrated fluorescence intensity were consistent with these consequences (Fig. 1D). Comparative temporal analysis revealed a clear dissociation between myelin degradation and lipid droplet accumulation, indicating a delayed but persistent lipid burden in phagocytic IBA1 + microglia/macrophages after SCI.

To further assess the activation status of phagocytes during lipid and myelin handling, CD68 was used as a marker of activated microglia/macrophages and co-stained with either BODIPY or dMBP (Fig. S1A, B). Representative images demonstrated that CD68 + cells were consistently observed at time points when lipid droplets remained detectable. Similarly, CD68 expression persisted during the period of myelin degradation and subsequent clearance. Together, these findings suggest that phagocytes sustain an activated state during myelin clearance, and that lipid droplet accumulation constitutes a central metabolic stimulus for activation after SCI. Accordingly, we define this population as injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages, a distinct phagocytic state marked by persistent intracellular lipid burden following injury.

Given that lipid-laden phagocytes are commonly associated with pro-inflammatory phenotypes, the expression of inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines was examined at 7, 14, and 42 dpi, corresponding to the initiation of lipid droplet accumulation, peak lipid burden, and lipid decline phases, respectively. Heatmap analysis and quantitative PCR analysis revealed that pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα, were markedly upregulated at 7 dpi, followed by a gradual reduction at 14 dpi. Although their expression levels decreased at 42 dpi, they remained significantly elevated compared with the Sham group. In contrast, the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10 exhibited a distinct temporal pattern. Both cytokines were elevated at 7 dpi but showed a pronounced reduction at 14 dpi, followed by a further decrease at 42 dpi (Fig. 1E, F). The decline in anti-inflammatory mediators coincided with sustained expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Notably, this inflammatory profile temporally paralleled the accumulation and persistence of lipid droplets in activated phagocytes, indicating a close association between intracellular lipid burden and inflammatory status in ILLMs after SCI.

Single-cell and immune-enriched transcriptomics reveal lipid efflux–associated microglia/macrophage states after SCI

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) was performed on spinal cord tissues collected from sham and SCI tissues across multiple post-injury stages. UMAP embedding of 187,212 nuclei resolved 14 major cell classes (Fig. 2A). Cell-type identities were supported by canonical marker expression (Fig. S2A), and the top-marker heatmap together with inter-cluster correlation analysis indicated clear separation among annotated populations, consistent with high-quality clustering (Fig. S2B, C). Quantification of cell-type proportions across time further showed prominent temporal remodeling of the myeloid compartment after injury, with microglia and macrophages displaying marked stage-dependent shifts (Fig. 2B).

Guided by our histological observation of lipid droplet accumulation after SCI (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1), we next focused on lipid-handling programs across major spinal cord populations. We selected the principal resident parenchymal and stromal populations (e.g., microglia/macrophages, oligodendrocytes/OPCs, astrocytes, endothelial cells and fibroblasts) because these cell classes collectively represent the primary sources concise drivers of lipid uptake/processing, myelin-derived lipid turnover, and inflammatory signaling in the lesion microenvironment. Gene-set scoring revealed that microglia and macrophages showed the most prominent induction of cholesterol efflux/handling signatures, including positive regulation of cholesterol efflux, cellular response to cholesterol, and regulation of cholesterol metabolic process, with clear time-dependent patterns across post-injury stages (Fig. 2C). These data suggest that lesion-associated phagocytes engage coordinated lipid processing and export programs during SCI evolution.

We then asked whether lipid efflux remodeling in microglia is coupled to redox/inflammatory programs over time. GO enrichment of microglial DEGs (SCI vs. SHAM) showed significant enrichment of phospholipid/cholesterol efflux, lipid/cholesterol transport, and response to reactive oxygen species across stages, while inflammatory response–related terms were most pronounced at 14–30 dpi and diminished by 42 dpi (Fig. S2D). Consistently, KEGG analysis highlighted enrichment of Cholesterol metabolism, ABC transporters, NOD-like receptor signaling, Cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and Antigen processing and presentation across time points (Fig. 2D). A parallel analytical strategy applied to macrophages revealed highly concordant enrichment of lipid efflux/transport, oxidative stress response, and inflammatory pathways, again displaying stage-dependent dynamics (Fig. 2E and Fig. S2E). Together, these analyses indicate that microglia and macrophages mount broadly similar lipid–redox–inflammatory transcriptional programs after SCI. Because ABC transporters emerged as a core enriched pathway linked to lipid export, we specifically interrogated DEGs within the ABC transporter pathway. Volcano plots restricted to ABC transporter genes showed robust post-injury induction of ABCA1 and ABCG1 in both microglia and macrophages across comparisons (Fig. 2F, G), nominating these transporters as principal candidates associated with post-SCI lipid efflux remodeling.

To further resolve heterogeneity within the immune compartment, we complemented snRNA-seq with CD45⁺ cell-enriched single-cell RNA-seq from SHAM, 7 dpi, 14 dpi, and 30 dpi tissues. We used snRNA-seq initially because it is well suited for spinal cord tissue with dissociation sensitivity and allows consistent profiling across injury stages; however, nuclei-based capture can reduce cytoplasmic transcript representation and may limit resolution of immune activation states. Therefore, we used CD45⁺ enrichment to improve immune-cell coverage and refine myeloid substructure. The four selected time points were prioritized to capture the early-to-subacute peaks of lipid/inflammatory remodeling suggested by our enrichment analyses, while omitting 42 dpi where multiple immune signatures trend toward stabilization in the snRNA-seq comparisons. Re-clustering of 59,095 CD45⁺ cells yielded 10 immune cell classes (Fig. 2H), with cell-type proportions showing dynamic shifts after SCI and highlighting microglia/macrophages as major populations of interest (Fig. 2I). Immune-cell annotation was supported by marker dot plots and top-marker heatmaps (Fig. S2F, G), confirming robust clustering quality.

Subclustering of myeloid lineages further resolved three microglial states (MG1–MG3) and five macrophage states (MP1–MP5) (Fig. 2J). Functional diversity across these subclusters was supported by pathway differences (Fig. S2H). Importantly, temporal composition analysis showed that MG2 expanded prominently after SCI, while MP1 emerged predominantly post-injury (Fig. 2K). Lipid efflux gene-set scoring across subclusters revealed that MG2 and MP1 displayed the strongest elevation of the Cholesterol Efflux signature over time (Fig. 2L). To visualize where key efflux regulators concentrate within the myeloid manifolds, we mapped gene expression density of ABCA1 and ABCG1 on the PHATE embedding; both genes showed higher density within the MG2 and MP1 regions (Fig. 2M). Notably, MG2 and MP1 displayed relatively higher cholesterol efflux gene-set scores and increased ABCA/ABCG1 expression density compared with other myeloid states. To describe this transcriptional tendency while avoiding functional presumption, we collectively refer to these populations as cholesterol efflux–associated myeloid cells (CE-M), including microglial (CE-MG) and macrophage (CE-MP) subsets. This nomenclature reflects their relative enrichment within the efflux-related transcriptional landscape observed in our dataset rather than implying a uniquely defined or dominant role.

Given the consistent induction of ABCA1/ABCG1 at the pathway level, we next compared their temporal expression patterns using feature plots. ABCA1 showed broader and more prominent enrichment across injury-associated myeloid states than ABCG1 in both microglia and macrophages (Fig. S2I, J), aligning with the DEG patterns in ABC transporter analyses (Fig. 2F, G). Consistently, immunofluorescence in injured spinal cord sections showed stronger ABCA1 signal relative to ABCG1 within IBA1 + phagocytes after SCI (Fig. S2K). Collectively, these analyses indicate that MG2 and MP1 represent myeloid states relatively associated with higher cholesterol efflux transcriptional signatures following SCI. Among genes contributing to this program, ABCA1 displayed consistent induction across time points and subclusters, making it a rational candidate for functional interrogation.

To further establish a direct link between the histologically defined injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages (ILLMs) and the transcriptionally defined cholesterol efflux–associated myeloid (CE-M) states, we sought to identify molecular features that bridge these two levels of analysis. Among genes enriched in CE-M subclusters, we noted that Lgals3 (encoding Galectin-3, the β-galactoside-binding lectin protein) was prominently expressed in both MG2 microglia and MP1 macrophages (Fig. 2N). To validate this observation at the tissue level, we performed immunofluorescence staining for Galectin-3 in spinal cord sections across injury stages. In the SHAM group, Galectin-3 expression was minimal within the spinal cord. In contrast, following SCI, robust Galectin-3 immunoreactivity was detected in IBA1 + microglia/macrophages within the lesion core at 7, 14, and 30 dpi (Fig. S2L). The temporal emergence and persistence of Lgals3 + IBA1+ cells closely paralleled the accumulation of lipid droplets observed in Fig. 1, supporting their association with lipid-laden phagocyte states in vivo. Together, these findings link ILLMs to CE-M transcriptional subclusters, showing that they largely correspond to Lgals3-enriched MG2 and MP1 populations. This integration distinguishes ILLMs from generic foam-like microglia by their injury-specific temporal dynamics and coordinated lipid efflux–redox–inflammatory signatures after SCI.

Given the consistent enrichment of ABCA1 within CE-M populations, we next sought to determine whether ABCA1 plays a functional role in regulating lipid handling and the associated inflammatory responses in these injury-associated myeloid states.

ABCA1 deletion exacerbates lipid droplet burden and impairs functional recovery

Before investigating the functional role of ABCA1 in vivo, we first examined its protein-level expression and cellular localization in the injured spinal cord. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed minimal ABCA1 signal in the spinal cord of sham-operated mice. In contrast, following spinal cord injury, ABCA1 expression was markedly induced within the lesion core, with robust signal detected at 7, 14, and 30 days post-injury (Fig. S3A). To determine the cellular source of ABCA1 expression, we performed double immunostaining with microglial/macrophage markers. ABCA1 signal was found to co-localize extensively with IBA1 + cells, indicating expression in microglia/macrophages. Consistently, co-staining with CD68 further confirmed that ABCA1 is enriched in activated phagocytic cells within the lesion area. These observations demonstrate that ABCA1 is specifically upregulated in lesion-associated microglia/macrophages at the protein level following SCI, supporting its potential role in regulating lipid handling within this cellular compartment and establishing a foundation for subsequent functional analyses.

Next, to investigate the functional role of ABCA1 in regulating lipid metabolism after SCI, Cx3cr1CreER+/- mice were crossed with ABCA1flox/flox mice to generate conditional knockout animals (ABCA1f/fCx3cr1CreER+/-; referred to as ABCA1-CKO). Littermate ABCA1f/fCx3cr1CreER-/- mice were used as controls (referred to as ABCA1f/f). Successful genotyping of both Abca1flox/flox and Cx3cr1CreER alleles was confirmed by PCR electrophoresis (Fig. S3B, C). SCI was subsequently induced, and spinal cord tissues were collected at 7, 14, 30, and 42 dpi.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed markedly increased lipid droplet accumulation in ABCA1-CKO mice compared with ABCA1f/f mice at all examined post-injury time points (Fig. 3A), as confirmed by significantly elevated BODIPY+ area and fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3B), indicating impaired lipid clearance in the absence of ABCA1. Consistently, dMBP/IBA1 co-staining showed progressive myelin debris resolution in ABCA1f/f mice, whereas substantial dMBP immunoreactivity persisted in ABCA1-CKO mice even at 42 dpi (Fig. 3C, D), suggesting delayed myelin clearance associated with defective lipid handling. CD68 co-staining further demonstrated sustained microglia/macrophage activation in ABCA1-CKO mice in parallel with persistent lipid droplets and dMBP signal (Fig. S3D, E).

Given transcriptomic enrichment of oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways, ROS levels were examined and found to be significantly elevated in ABCA1-CKO mice, particularly at 30 and 42 dpi (Fig. S3F, G). In parallel, expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, remained increased in ABCA1-CKO mice across post-injury stages (Fig. S3H), indicating exacerbated oxidative and inflammatory responses following ABCA1 deletion.

Functional consequences of ABCA1 deletion were assessed using behavioral, electrophysiological, and kinematic analyses. Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scoring demonstrated significantly impaired locomotor recovery in SCI-ABCA1-CKO mice compared with SCI-ABCA1f/f mice throughout the post-injury period (Fig. 3E). Electrophysiological assessment by hindlimb electromyography (EMG) revealed reduced amplitude and prolonged latency in SCI-ABCA1-CKO mice relative to SCI-ABCA1f/f mice at 30 and 42 dpi, when EMG responses became reliably detectable (Fig. 3F). Quantitative analysis, expressed as percentages relative to SHAM-ABCA1f/f mice, confirmed significantly decreased amplitude and increased latency in SCI-ABCA1-CKO mice (Fig. 3G). Gait analysis using the CatWalk system revealed pronounced footprint overlap, dragging, and reduced print clarity in SCI mice, with more severe abnormalities observed in SCI-ABCA1-CKO mice at both 30 and 42 dpi (Fig. 3H). Principal component analysis of multiple gait parameters demonstrated clear separation among experimental groups, with the first principal component capturing injury- and genotype-dependent differences (Fig. 3I, K). Base of support, stride length, run duration, run average speed, hindlimb swing, and body speed showed the strongest associations with PC1. Quantitative analysis revealed persistently increased base of support and reduced stride length in ABCA1-CKO mice compared with ABCA1f/f mice at corresponding time points (Fig. 3J, L and Fig. S3I). To further quantify limb kinematics, motion capture analysis was performed by labeling key hindlimb landmarks and recording movement using high-speed videography (4 K, 120 fps) (Fig. 3M). Pose estimation and trajectory tracking revealed reduced joint excursion and more pronounced limb dragging in SCI-ABCA1-CKO mice compared with SCI-ABCA1f/f mice (Fig. 3N). Quantitative analysis of kinematic parameters demonstrated increased paw drag and reduced swing amplitude and step height in SCI-ABCA1-CKO mice at both time points (Fig. 3O, P). Collectively, these behavioral, electrophysiological, and kinematic data demonstrate that conditional deletion of ABCA1 in CX3CR1-lineage mice is associated with impaired functional recovery after SCI.

Virtual screening identifies a candidate ABCA1-targeting small molecule that reduces lipid droplet accumulation

Given the central role of ABCA1 in phagocyte lipid efflux and the persistence of lipid droplet–laden phagocytes after SCI, we sought to identify a small molecule that directly engages ABCA1 rather than indirectly modulating its expression via upstream nuclear receptor pathways. A structure-guided virtual screening workflow was implemented (Fig. 4A) using a CNS-focused compound library to prioritize blood–brain barrier permeability and the human ABCA1 structure (PDB ID: 7TBW) for docking-based screening. Recent cryo-EM studies reveal that ABCA1 contains a distinctive extracellular domain architecture featuring a lateral-access lipid entry model, supporting the feasibility of small-molecule modulation of lipid export. Accordingly, we focused on the Schrödinger-predicted site3 pocket, centered near E1245, to enrich for compounds predicted to allosterically modulate ABCA1 activity rather than sterically occlude the lipid translocation pathway. The top-ranked 200 compounds were shortlisted and the top 10 candidates were advanced to cell-based screening (Fig. 4A and Table S1).

Primary microglia were used for functional validation to better recapitulate the lipid-handling phenotype of ILLMs observed in vivo. In pilot experiments using the BV2 microglial cell line, we observed only limited lipid droplet accumulation following myelin debris (MD) exposure, in contrast to primary microglia, which robustly developed lipid droplets under the same conditions. These observations indicate that primary microglia more faithfully recapitulate the lipid-handling phenotype of ILLMs observed in vivo, and were therefore used for subsequent functional analyses. This difference likely reflects the known limitations of immortalized microglial cell lines, which often exhibit altered metabolic and phagocytic properties compared to primary microglia. Among the ten candidates tested, Z2312316994 (Z231) produced the most pronounced reduction in MD-induced lipid droplets, whereas solvent control (MD + DMSO) had no effect (Fig. 4B and Fig. S4A). Quantitative analysis confirmed that Z231 significantly reduced both lipid droplet number per cell and integrated BODIPY fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4C). Flow cytometric analysis provided an orthogonal readout, demonstrating a leftward shift in BODIPY fluorescence distribution and a significant reduction in mean fluorescence intensity following Z231 treatment (Fig. 4D, E). Together, these data indicate that Z231 attenuates intracellular lipid droplet accumulation in MD-challenged primary microglia.

To assess whether the lipid-modulating effect of Z231 extends to human cells, we performed parallel experiments using the human microglial cell line HMC3. BODIPY staining revealed that Z231 treatment markedly reduced MD-induced lipid droplet accumulation in HMC3 cells compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. S4B). These results indicate that the lipid-lowering effect of Z231 is reproducible in human microglia-like cells, supporting its potential relevance across species. Consistent with known differences among immortalized microglial cell lines, lipid droplet formation capacity may vary between models, which could account for the distinct responses observed in BV2 and HMC3 cells under similar conditions.

Cytotoxicity profiling revealed that Z231 exhibited an IC₅₀ of 30.76µM (Fig. 4F and Fig. S4C). Acute toxicity assessment using an up-and-down procedure showed no overt adverse effects at 2000 mg/kg. Consistently, mice treated with escalating doses of Z231 (10–200 mg/kg) displayed no significant alterations in serum biochemical indices of cardiac, hepatic, or renal injury, nor histopathological abnormalities in major organs (Fig. S4D, E), supporting its in vivo tolerability.

Structural analysis revealed that Z231 occupies the predicted site3 pocket and forms hydrogen-bond and salt-bridge interactions with GLU1246 and GLU2108 (Fig. 4G, H), consistent with stabilization of a conformationally responsive interface rather than direct obstruction of the lipid translocation route. Biophysical assays further confirmed direct target engagement, with SPR and MST measurements indicating micromolar-range binding affinities (Kd = 5.71 × 10⁻⁶ M and 1.28 ± 0.11 µM, respectively; Fig. 4I, J). Meanwhile, dose-ranging studies established 5µM for in vitro assays (using primary microglia) and 100 mg/kg for in vivo experiments as effective conditions for subsequent mechanistic and efficacy analyses. Notably, at this dose, Z231 treatment reduced lipid droplet accumulation within the lesion area in wild-type mice, as assessed by BODIPY staining (Fig. S4F, G).

Finally, To determine whether the lipid-lowering effect of Z231 is dependent on ABCA1, we next evaluated its activity in vivo using ABCA1 conditional knockout (ABCA1-CKO) mice. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that, Z231 treatment failed to reduce lipid droplet accumulation in ABCA1-CKO mice, as assessed by BODIPY labeling within the lesion area (Fig. 4K). These findings indicate that the lipid-lowering effect of Z231 requires the presence of ABCA1, supporting the notion that its activity is functionally dependent on ABCA1 in vivo.

Z231 activates ABCA1 to reverse lipid-induced microglial stress and inflammation

Following identification of Z231 as an ABCA1-binding compound that reduces lipid droplet burden, we next examined whether pharmacological enhancement of lipid handling modulates oxidative stress and inflammatory programs highlighted by our single-cell analyses. Primary microglia were exposed to MD to model lipid loading characteristic of ILLMs in vivo, followed by treatment with vehicle or Z231.

Live-cell CellROX staining revealed low basal ROS levels in control group, whereas MD stimulation induced a marked increase in global ROS, consistent with metabolic stress under lipid-loading conditions (Fig. 5A). This MD-induced ROS elevation was unaffected by solvent control but was substantially attenuated by Z231, concomitant with reduced BODIPY+ lipid droplets (Fig. 5A, B). Flow cytometric analysis provided an orthogonal validation, demonstrating a leftward shift and reduced MFI of CellROX signal following Z231 treatment (Fig. 5C, D). Given the central role of mitochondria in lipid-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial ROS was assessed using MitoSOX. MD strongly increased mitochondrial ROS, whereas Z231 significantly reduced MitoSOX fluorescence by both imaging and flow cytometry (Fig. 5E–H). Together, these data indicate that Z231 suppresses both global and mitochondrial ROS in lipid-loaded microglia.

We next assessed whether Z231 modulates the inflammatory transcriptional response associated with lipid burden. Heatmap analysis revealed that MD stimulation induced pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines while suppressing anti-inflammatory mediators, a profile partially reversed by Z231 (Fig. 5I). qPCR confirmed that MD increased IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and reduced IL-4 and IL-10 expression, whereas Z231 significantly counteracted these changes (Fig. 5J). In parallel, MD-induced chemokines involved in inflammatory recruitment, including CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL10, were all reduced by Z231 treatment (Fig. 5I and Fig. S5A). These results indicate that pharmacological reduction of lipid burden is accompanied by coordinated attenuation of pro-inflammatory and chemotactic programs.

Because oxidative stress and inflammation are tightly coupled to metabolic remodeling, we next examined bioenergetic consequences using Seahorse assays. MD stimulation reduced mitochondrial respiratory capacity, as reflected by diminished maximal oxygen consumption rate (OCR), whereas Z231 partially restored respiratory reserve without significantly altering basal respiration or ATP production (Fig. 5K, L). In contrast, MD promoted a shift toward glycolytic metabolism, evidenced by increased extracellular acidification rate (PER), basal glycolysis, and compensatory glycolysis; these changes were partially reversed by Z231 (Fig. S5 B–D). ATP rate analysis further demonstrated that MD increased reliance on glycolytic ATP production at the expense of mitochondrial ATP, resulting in a reduced ATP Rate Index, which was partially normalized by Z231 (Fig. S5E, F). Energetic mapping illustrated a shift from mitochondrial respiration toward glycolysis under MD conditions, with Z231 restoring a more balanced metabolic profile (Fig. S5G). These in vitro findings demonstrate that myelin debris drives primary microglia into an ILLM-like state characterized by lipid droplet accumulation, elevated global and mitochondrial ROS, inflammatory and chemotactic activation, and a metabolic shift toward glycolytic ATP dependence. Importantly, ABCA1 activation by Z231 is sufficient to reverse these hallmarks under lipid-loading conditions. Whether such metabolic reprogramming can be achieved within the complex microenvironment of the injured spinal cord and translate into tissue preservation and functional recovery was therefore next evaluated in vivo.

Z231 promotes resolution of lipid droplet burden in ILLMs and improves functional recovery after SCI

To evaluate whether pharmacological activation of ABCA1 reshapes lipid handling in vivo, 8-week-old C57BL/6J mice were assigned to SHAM, SCI, SCI + DMSO, or SCI + Z231 groups and analyzed at 7, 14, 30, and 42 dpi. In untreated and vehicle-treated SCI mice, BODIPY staining revealed pronounced lipid droplet accumulation within lesion-associated IBA1 + microglia/macrophages, which gradually declined over time but remained evident into chronic stages. In contrast, Z231 administration consistently reduced lipid droplet signal across post-injury stages, indicating enhanced resolution of intracellular lipid burden in injury-activated phagocytes in vivo (Fig. 6A). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly decreased BODIPY+ area and integrated fluorescence intensity in the SCI+Z231 group compared with SCI and SCI+DMSO controls (Fig. 6B), supporting a sustained effect of ABCA1 activation on lipid clearance.

Because inefficient processing of myelin-derived lipids contributes to prolonged phagocyte activation, we next examined degraded dMBP within IBA1 + cells. Across all groups, dMBP signal progressively declined with time, consistent with gradual myelin debris removal after SCI (Fig. 6C). Z231-treated mice exhibited reduced dMBP immunoreactivity relative to SCI and SCI+ DMSO groups, with a statistically significant reduction in dMBP+ area at 14 dpi, while later time points showed a consistent but non-significant downward trend (Fig. 6D). These findings suggest that pharmacological enhancement of ABCA1 primarily alleviates intracellular lipid burden and may secondarily facilitate myelin debris resolution, with the most detectable impact during the subacute phase.

To determine whether improved lipid handling translated into altered phagocyte activation, CD68 expression was assessed. In SCI and vehicle-treated mice, CD68 remained prominently expressed, indicating sustained activation of microglia/macrophages during prolonged debris and lipid processing. Z231 treatment was associated with reduced CD68 signal in parallel with decreased lipid droplets and dMBP, with differences becoming more apparent at 30 and 42 dpi (Fig. S6 A, B). This temporal pattern suggests that resolution of phagocyte activation follows a delayed trajectory and becomes evident during chronic stages as lipid stress is progressively relieved.

Given the close coupling between lipid overload, oxidative stress, and inflammation, we next assessed ROS levels in spinal cord tissue. SCI induced robust ROS accumulation that persisted across time points in SCI and SCI+DMSO groups. Z231 treatment reduced ROS fluorescence at each analyzed stage (Fig. S6C), and quantitative analysis demonstrated significantly lower ROS intensity in the SCI+Z231 group compared with both control groups at all time points (Fig. S6D). Consistent with reduced oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokine profiles were also attenuated. Heatmap analysis showed lower expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in SCI+Z231 mice relative to SCI and SCI+DMSO groups throughout the post-injury period (Fig. S6E), indicating suppression of sustained inflammatory signaling in vivo.

Functional outcomes were evaluated longitudinally. Z231-treated mice exhibited improved locomotor recovery compared with SCI and SCI+DMSO groups, as reflected by higher BMS scores during the recovery course (Fig. 6E). Electrophysiological assessment at 30 and 42 dpi revealed increased EMG amplitude and shortened latency in the SCI+Z231 group, indicating improved neuromuscular conduction (Fig. 6F, G). Gait analysis further demonstrated that Z231 mitigated footprint overlap, dragging, and reduced print clarity observed in SCI mice, particularly during later stages (Fig. 6H). Principal component analysis of gait parameters showed separation of experimental groups along PC1, and Z231 partially improved representative gait features such as base of support and stride length, although recovery varied across individual parameters (Fig. 6I-L and Fig. S6F). Finally, kinematic analysis based on multi-joint tracking revealed improved joint excursion, reduced paw drag, increased limb swing amplitude, and greater step height in Z231-treated mice (Fig. 6M-O), indicating enhanced hindlimb coordination. Together, these in vivo data demonstrate that pharmacological activation of ABCA1 by Z231 reduces lipid droplet burden in injury-activated microglia/macrophages, dampens oxidative and inflammatory stress, and promotes functional recovery after SCI. Importantly, the therapeutic effects emerge progressively and are most evident during subacute-to-chronic phases, consistent with the slow kinetics of lipid clearance and metabolic reprogramming in the injured spinal cord.

Molecular dynamic support stable binding of Z231 to ABCA1 and reveal a compact, stabilized conformational ensemble

To evaluate the dynamic stability and conformational consequences of Z231 binding, all-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed for the ABCA1–Z231 complex over a 100 ns trajectory. Recent cryo-EM studies indicate that ABCA1 operates through ATP-driven conformational coupling between transmembrane helices and extracellular domains to support lipid extraction and export. Structural analyses further suggest that charged interfaces at the transmembrane–extracellular junction, including the site3 region surrounding conserved acidic residues E1245/E1246, act as regulatory coupling hubs that modulate transport efficiency rather than forming the lipid pathway itself. Engagement of this region by small molecules could therefore plausibly enhance ABCA1 activity through allosteric stabilization of efflux-competent conformations. Ligand stability was first assessed by monitoring ligand RMSD. Z231 rapidly equilibrated within the binding pocket during the initial 5–10 ns and subsequently fluctuated around a stable plateau of approximately 0.13–0.15 nm for the remainder of the simulation (Fig. 7A), indicating persistent retention within the binding site without dissociation or large positional drift. Representative trajectory snapshots further confirmed stable ligand positioning across time points without dissociation (Fig. S7A). Consistent with stable ligand engagement, analysis of protein backbone RMSD revealed that the ABCA1–Z231 complex exhibited reduced global conformational deviation compared with apo ABCA1 throughout the simulation (Fig. 7B), suggesting that ligand binding is associated with increased overall structural stability. At the residue level, RMSF profiling demonstrated that while most regions of ABCA1 remained within low fluctuation ranges (< 0.2 nm), several flexible segments showed attenuated mobility in the ligand-bound complex relative to the apo state (Fig. 7C), indicating localized dampening of conformational dynamics upon Z231 association. Measures of structural compactness further supported this stabilization effect. The radius of gyration (Rg) of the ABCA1–Z231 complex was consistently lower and less variable than that of apo ABCA1 (Fig. 7D), accompanied by a sustained reduction in solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) (Fig. 7E). Together, these metrics indicate that Z231 binding promotes a more compact and solvent-shielded protein architecture. Persistent polar interactions were observed throughout the trajectory, with the number of hydrogen bonds between ABCA1 and Z231 predominantly distributed between two and five and showing a modest increase during later stages of the simulation (Fig. 7F), supporting durable ligand–protein contacts. Binding energetics were further quantified by MM/GBSA analysis using the equilibrated 90–100 ns trajectory window. The predicted binding free energy (ΔGbind) was − 23.00 ± 1.44 kcal/mol, driven primarily by favorable electrostatic and van der Waals contributions (Fig. 7G). Per-residue energy decomposition identified several residues within the binding pocket as major contributors to ligand stabilization, with GLU2105 providing the strongest energetic contribution, along with additional stabilizing inputs from neighboring acidic and hydrophobic residues. Finally, free-energy landscape analysis constructed from RMSD and Rg coordinates revealed that the ABCA1–Z231 complex converged toward a dominant low-energy basin characterized by relatively higher RMSD but reduced Rg (Fig. 7H). This pattern indicates convergence to a compact and thermodynamically favorable conformational ensemble rather than frequent transitions between multiple metastable states. Collectively, these simulations demonstrate that Z231 binding stabilizes ABCA1 conformational dynamics, reduces global and local flexibility, and favors a compact structural state, providing a mechanistic basis for the functional enhancement of ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux observed in subsequent cellular and in vivo analyses.

Z231 activates ABCA1 to reverse lipid-laden ILLM phenotypes and promote spinal cord repair after injury

Schematic illustration of the proposed mechanism by which the small-molecule compound Z231 modulates spinal cord repair through ABCA1-mediated lipid handling in injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages (Fig. 8A).

Following spinal cord injury, accumulation of myelin debris promotes excessive lipid uptake by microglia/macrophages, leading to the emergence of lipid-laden ILLMs characterized by intracellular lipid droplet accumulation, increased ROS production, and sustained pro-inflammatory signaling. This metabolic–inflammatory coupling is associated with secondary tissue damage and impaired functional recovery. Z231 directly engages ABCA1 and enhances its ATP-dependent lipid efflux activity, thereby facilitating intracellular lipid clearance in ILLMs. ABCA1 activation is accompanied by a reduction in lipid droplet burden, attenuation of ROS generation and inflammatory signaling, and a shift toward a more reparative metabolic state. The coordinated restoration of lipid homeostasis and redox balance contributes to alleviation of the pro-inflammatory microenvironment and supports tissue repair. Consequently, Z231-mediated ABCA1 activation is associated with improved lesion microenvironment remodeling, reduced secondary injury, and enhanced motor functional recovery following spinal cord injury.

This schematic highlights ABCA1 as a central metabolic regulator in ILLMs linking lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation, and identifies Z231 as a pharmacological modulator of ABCA1 with therapeutic relevance for spinal cord injury.

Discussion

Spinal cord injury represents one of the most devastating neurological disorders, in which secondary injury processes rather than the primary trauma largely determine long-term neurological outcome [1, 25, 26]. In this study, we identify a lipid–redox–inflammatory coupling that evolves over time after spinal cord injury (SCI) and demonstrate that ABCA1 serves as a central metabolic checkpoint attenuating this pathogenic cascade in injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages. By integrating temporal histology, single-nucleus transcriptomics, genetic loss-of-function, pharmacological gain-of-function, and molecular modeling, we provide convergent evidence that delayed yet persistent lipid droplet accumulation in lesion-associated phagocytes sustains oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, thereby contributing to secondary injury progression and functional impairment. Importantly, pharmacological activation of ABCA1 reverses this maladaptive state and promotes neurological recovery, establishing lipid efflux as a therapeutically tractable target in SCI.

Previous studies have established that myelin debris is rapidly phagocytosed by microglia and macrophages after SCI and is considered a major source of lipid overload in lesion-associated phagocytes [4, 5, 27, 28]. However, the temporal relationship between myelin clearance and intracellular lipid handling remains poorly defined. A key conceptual advance of this work lies in the temporal separation between myelin debris clearance and lipid droplet resolution. dMBP rose rapidly after injury and declined during later stages, reflecting progressive debris removal. In contrast, lipid droplets appeared with a delay, peaked during the subacute phase, and persisted into chronic stages even when dMBP was largely resolved. This temporal dissociation indicates that lipid droplets do not simply mirror phagocytic uptake of myelin but instead represent an intracellular metabolic bottleneck in lipid processing and export. However, the temporal dissociation between myelin degradation and lipid droplet accumulation, while suggestive of altered lipid handling, does not by itself establish impaired cholesterol efflux. Instead, this phenomenon may reflect a combination of increased lipid uptake, limitations in intracellular processing, and constraints in lipid clearance efficiency, including potentially insufficient cholesterol efflux, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Recent studies have similarly reported that lipid droplet accumulation in phagocytes can persist independently of extracellular lipid sources, reflecting impaired lipid efflux and intracellular metabolic stress [28–30]. Such persistence suggests that ILLMs retain a “lipid memory” of myelin overload that outlasts the physical presence of debris. This observation has important implications for secondary injury biology. While inflammatory responses are often attributed to continuous antigenic or damage signals, our data suggest that unresolved intracellular lipid stress may represent a persistent pathogenic condition that accompanies or sustains chronic inflammatory activation. And this founding agrees with the previous studies that intracellular lipid overload can act as a persistent driver of inflammatory activation in phagocytes, even after the initial tissue damage has subsided [8, 30, 31]. Indeed, CD68 expression and inflammatory gene signatures remained elevated during the window of lipid droplet persistence. These findings support a model in which lipid droplet accumulation provides a sustained metabolic stimulus that maintains phagocyte activation independently of ongoing tissue breakdown.

The temporal overlap between lipid droplet persistence, ROS elevation, and inflammatory cytokine expression highlights a tight coupling between lipid metabolism and redox biology in ILLMs. Lipid overload is known to perturb mitochondrial membrane composition and electron transport efficiency, promoting superoxide generation and oxidative damage. In turn, ROS can activate inflammatory transcriptional programs and further impair mitochondrial function, forming a self-reinforcing loop [9, 31–33]. Our in vivo ROS staining and in vitro CellROX/MitoSOX data support this reciprocal amplification model. Notably, anti-inflammatory mediators declined over time, whereas pro-inflammatory cytokines remained elevated, indicating a failure of inflammatory resolution. This imbalance may reflect the inability of lipid-laden phagocytes to transition toward reparative phenotypes. Thus, lipid droplet accumulation is not merely a metabolic phenotype but represents a critical upstream regulator of redox and inflammatory homeostasis in SCI.

Single-nucleus RNA sequencing provided an unbiased framework to relate the temporal histological phenotypes to transcriptional programs in lesion-associated cells. Consistent with prior single-cell and bulk transcriptomic studies of central nervous system diseases, lesion-associated phagocytes undergo extensive metabolic and inflammatory reprogramming over time, particularly in pathways related to lipid handling and redox stress [8, 34, 35]. In our dataset, both microglia and macrophages showed coordinated enrichment of lipid transport, cholesterol efflux, ROS response, and inflammatory pathways across post-injury stages. Notably, these signatures suggest that lipid metabolic remodeling represents a broadly shared component of the lesion-associated phagocyte response. This pattern aligns with previous observations from neurodegeneration and demyelinating disease, where cholesterol efflux machinery is robustly induced in lipid-engorged phagocytes as an adaptive response to excessive myelin-derived lipid burden [20, 36, 37]. These findings link ILLMs to CE-M transcriptional subclusters defined by high Lgals3 expression, suggesting that ILLMs represent an injury-induced metabolic state enriched with CE-M subsets rather than a distinct lineage. Unlike generic foam-like microglia or lipid-laden microglia arising in other pathological contexts, ILLMs are distinguished by their context-specific temporal dynamics and the coordinated lipid efflux–redox–inflammatory features that characterize CE-M populations. While this Lgals3-associated metabolic signature highlights a subset-enriched state, it occurs within a broader landscape of lipid remodeling shared across lesion-associated phagocytes. In this context, induction of efflux-related genes likely reflects an attempt to mitigate intracellular lipid stress and maintain cellular homeostasis under conditions of sustained lipid uptake. Among lipid efflux genes, ABCA1 emerged as a prominently upregulated and consistently expressed transporter across time points and subclusters. This is mechanistically relevant because ABCA1 mediates the initial step of lipid export [38–41]. Its sustained induction is therefore compatible with a compensatory efflux response in injury-associated phagocytes. Notably, ABCG1 was also identified as a potential candidate in our analysis, consistent with its established role in cholesterol efflux and lipid homeostasis in myeloid cells. ABCA1 and ABCG1 mediate complementary steps in cholesterol efflux, with ABCA1 primarily facilitating cholesterol transfer to ApoA-I, while ABCG1 promotes efflux to more mature HDL particles. Given the consistent and robust upregulation of ABCA1 across subclusters in our dataset, together with its established role in initiating cholesterol efflux, we prioritized ABCA1 for further mechanistic and functional investigation. Also, immunofluorescence analysis suggested a more prominent induction of ABCA1 compared with ABCG1 in lesion-associated phagocytes following SCI. Nevertheless, ABCG1 may also contribute to lipid handling and inflammatory regulation in SCI by facilitating cholesterol efflux to mature HDL particles, thereby cooperating with ABCA1 to maintain lipid homeostasis in lesion-associated phagocytes. However, the persistence of lipid droplets despite ABCA1 upregulation indicates that transcriptional activation of efflux pathways alone may be insufficient to fully normalize lipid handling in the injured spinal cord microenvironment, potentially due to limitations in efflux capacity, extracellular lipid acceptor availability, or concurrent metabolic constraints. Importantly, sustained intracellular lipid burden is not merely a passive metabolic feature but has been linked to cellular dysfunction, including oxidative stress and inflammatory activation. Notably, recent work from our group supports a mechanistic link between lipid accumulation and downstream inflammatory signaling in spinal cord injury. In this context, macropinocytosis-driven myelin uptake promotes foamy macrophage formation, leading to cholesterol overload, ROS generation, and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. While our current study does not directly establish this sequence, these findings provide a biologically plausible framework in which impaired cholesterol efflux may sustain coupled lipid, redox, and inflammatory alterations in lesion-associated phagocytes [4].

The ABCA1 conditional knockout model provided direct causal evidence linking ABCA1 activity to ILLM phenotype and SCI outcome. Previous works have implicated cholesterol efflux pathways, particularly ABCA1, as critical regulators of macrophage and microglial metabolic homeostasis under lipid-rich conditions, including demyelination and neurodegeneration [16, 18, 42, 43]. Loss of ABCA1 exacerbated lipid droplet accumulation, delayed myelin debris resolution, sustained CD68 activation, and amplified ROS and inflammatory signals. These cellular abnormalities translated into worsened electrophysiological conduction, gait coordination, and overall functional recovery. These findings demonstrate that ABCA1 is not a passive marker of lipid handling but an active determinant of phagocyte metabolic state and injury trajectory. Mechanistically, ABCA1 deficiency likely traps phagocytes in a lipid-overloaded, oxidative, and inflammatory state, preventing effective transition toward resolution and repair. This establishes a direct link between lipid efflux failure and chronic secondary injury. The identification of Z231 as a direct ABCA1 activator provides a critical translational extension of our findings. In contrast to nuclear receptor agonists that indirectly upregulate ABCA1 transcription, Z231 directly enhances ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux. In myelin debris–challenged microglia, Z231 reduced lipid droplet accumulation, suppressed cellular and mitochondrial ROS, attenuated inflammatory and chemokine programs, and partially restored mitochondrial respiratory capacity while reducing glycolytic dependence. A similar reduction in myelin debris–induced lipid droplet accumulation was also observed in the human microglial cell line HMC3, supporting the cross-species relevance of this effect. These effects indicate that lipid efflux lies upstream of broader redox and bioenergetic remodeling in injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages (ILLMs).

Molecular dynamics simulations provide a structural rationale for Z231 activity. Recent cryo-EM and structural analyses of ABCA1 have shown that the interface between transmembrane helices and extracellular domains undergoes coordinated rearrangements during the ATP-driven transport cycle and plays a key role in coupling substrate translocation to conformational transitions [44–46]. In this context, Z231 was found to stably occupy a pocket near E1245/E1246 at the transmembrane–extracellular domain interface, stabilizing ABCA1 conformational dynamics and promoting a more compact structural ensemble. This region has been implicated in long-range allosteric communication within ABCA transporters, suggesting that Z231 enhances lipid efflux by facilitating productive domain coordination rather than directly obstructing substrate pathways [45, 47–50]. Importantly, functional conclusions in this study rely primarily on genetic and pharmacological evidence, with molecular simulations serving as mechanistic context rather than standalone proof.

This work identifies injury-activated lipid-laden microglia/macrophages (ILLMs) as a metabolically defined pathological state integrating lipid overload, oxidative stress, and inflammation, and establishes ABCA1 as a central, druggable checkpoint governing this state. But several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CX3CR1-lineage targeting does not fully distinguish resident microglia from infiltrating macrophages, and future studies using more refined lineage-tracing strategies will be required to resolve their respective contributions. Second, although our data demonstrate coordinated changes in lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation following ABCA1 modulation, they do not establish a definitive causal relationship among these processes. Thus, the observed lipid–redox–inflammatory alterations should be interpreted as coupled rather than strictly sequential, and further studies are needed to dissect their mechanistic interdependence. Third, the efficacy of ABCA1-mediated lipid efflux may depend on microenvironmental factors, including the availability of extracellular lipid acceptors within the injured spinal cord, which warrants further investigation. Finally, while Z231 shows therapeutic potential, its long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, and optimal dosing require systematic evaluation, and its precise binding interface and allosteric activation mechanism remain to be defined by future structural studies.

In conclusion, our study identifies ABCA1 as a central metabolic checkpoint that constrains myelin lipid overload–driven ILLM formation and the coupled lipid–redox–inflammatory after SCI. Genetic deletion of ABCA1 exacerbates pathology, whereas pharmacological activation with Z231 reverses these defects and promotes functional recovery, positioning ABCA1 as a promising immune–metabolic therapeutic target for spinal cord injury.
