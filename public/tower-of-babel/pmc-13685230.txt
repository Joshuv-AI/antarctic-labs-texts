Visit-to-visit variability and longitudinal change of the triglyceride-glucose index are associated with early carotid plaque progression
Authors: Pan Tang, Min Cui, Ru Li, Feifei Jiang, Xiang Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, but the clinical relevance of its longitudinal dynamics remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether visit-to-visit variability in TyG (TyG-CV) and longitudinal change in TyG (TyG-Slope) were associated with early carotid plaque progression and improved risk prediction beyond conventional factors and mean TyG.MethodsIn this single-centre retrospective longitudinal cohort, 1,519 adults aged 18–65 years who completed three annual examinations from 2023 to 2025 were included. Fasting triglycerides and fasting plasma glucose were measured from overnight fasting venous blood samples using standardized laboratory assays, and the TyG index was calculated at each visit. Carotid plaque and plaque area were assessed by standardized carotid ultrasonography. TyG-CV was calculated as SD/mean × 100%, and TyG-Slope was estimated as the annualized linear change in TyG across the three examinations. Plaque progression was defined as incident plaque in 2025 or an increase in total plaque area of ≥ 0.05 cm² from 2023 to 2025.ResultsPlaque progression occurred in 384 participants (25.3%) and was more frequent in higher TyG-CV quartiles. In the fully adjusted model, both TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were independently associated with plaque progression (per 1-SD increase: OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.13–1.45; and OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.42, respectively), whereas mean TyG was not. Restricted cubic spline analyses showed significant overall associations for both dynamic metrics, without statistically significant evidence of non-linearity. Adding spline-modelled TyG-CV or TyG-Slope was associated with modest improvements in discrimination and reclassification in internal model evaluation, while the model including both dynamic metrics achieved the highest predictive performance among the evaluated models.ConclusionsTyG-CV and TyG-Slope were independently associated with early carotid plaque progression and provided incremental predictive information beyond conventional factors and mean TyG in this single-centre health-screening cohort. External validation is needed before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06330-6.

Background

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Despite major advances in controlling traditional risk factors, many cardiovascular events still occur in individuals whose blood pressure, lipid levels, and glucose appear well controlled, highlighting cardiometabolic residual risk that is not fully captured by conventional assessments based on static measurements [3]. Because atherosclerosis develops silently over years [4, 5], early identification of individuals at heightened subclinical risk is central to effective primary prevention. Carotid ultrasonography provides a practical, non-invasive window into subclinical atherosclerosis [6], and carotid plaque progression has been widely used as an early marker of cardiometabolic vascular injury in population settings [7].

Insulin resistance is a central pathophysiological driver of atherogenesis, contributing to dyslipidemia, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic low-grade inflammation [8, 9]. Direct measurement of insulin resistance, however, is impractical in routine clinical and health-screening settings [10, 11]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting triglycerides and fasting plasma glucose [12], has emerged as a simple surrogate marker of insulin resistance and systemic metabolic status. It has been linked to arterial stiffness, carotid plaque, coronary disease, and cardiovascular outcomes across diverse populations [13–16].

Most prior studies have relied on a single TyG measurement or static summaries such as baseline or mean values [17–20]. However, triglycerides and glucose vary substantially within individuals over time due to aging, lifestyle modification, intercurrent illness, and biological variability. These temporal patterns may reflect longitudinal variation in triglyceride-glucose levels and changes in metabolic risk profiles that are not captured by static indicators [21–24]. Consequently, recent investigations have begun to examine longitudinal TyG features, including visit-to-visit variability and long-term slope, capturing visit-to-visit variation and directional change, respectively [25–27]. Although dynamic TyG measures have been linked to incident cardiovascular events and mortality [18, 28, 29], evidence remains limited regarding their association with early structural atherosclerotic progression assessed by serial carotid imaging. In particular, it is uncertain whether TyG variability (TyG-CV) and long-term change (TyG-Slope) provide information beyond conventional risk factors and average TyG levels.

In this retrospective cohort of adults undergoing routine health examinations, we characterized TyG dynamics using TyG-CV and TyG-Slope and examined their associations with carotid plaque progression. We further evaluated whether incorporating these dynamic metrics provides incremental predictive information beyond conventional cardiometabolic factors and mean TyG.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using routine health examination data collected in three calendar years, from 2023 to 2025. The annual examinations included physical assessment, laboratory testing, and carotid ultrasonography. Adults aged 18–65 years were eligible if they underwent one health examination in each calendar year during the study period, with at least 3 months between consecutive visits. Fasting triglycerides, fasting plasma glucose, and carotid ultrasound data were required at each visit.

Among 128,080 individuals who underwent health examinations during the study period, 21,659 met the initial screening criteria after restriction to the eligible age range, availability of basic clinical information, and completion of three follow-up records. Participants were then excluded if key clinical information was incomplete, including blood pressure, lipid profile, glucose measures, smoking status, or medical history. We also excluded individuals with a prior diagnosis of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including coronary heart disease or ischemic stroke. Additional exclusion criteria included malignancy, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, thyroid disease, other conditions likely to substantially affect metabolic parameters, and missing triglyceride, fasting plasma glucose, or carotid ultrasound data at any visit. Because the analysis required complete exposure, outcome, and covariate information across the three annual examinations, participants with incomplete longitudinal follow-up were excluded, and the final analysis was conducted using a complete-case approach. Missing data were defined as missingness in any key exposure, outcome, or covariate variable required for the three-year longitudinal analysis. Multiple imputation was not performed because missingness frequently involved key longitudinal exposure or outcome measurements, including triglycerides, fasting plasma glucose, or carotid ultrasound data, for which imputation would require strong assumptions and might introduce additional uncertainty.

After these exclusions, 1,519 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Two sensitivity analyses were performed: one excluding participants with diabetes or hypertension, and another excluding those with triglycerides ≥ 5.6 mmol/L.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. Given the retrospective and observational nature of the study and the use of existing clinical data, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2025YJSLL-75). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Laboratory measurements and calculation of TyG-related indices

Anthropometric measures and blood pressure were obtained using standard procedures. Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of at least 8 h [30], and analyzed in the central clinical laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. Serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL-C, and HDL-C were measured using enzymatic colorimetric assays, and fasting plasma glucose was measured using the glucose oxidase-peroxidase method. The laboratory participated in routine internal quality control and external quality assessment programs throughout the study period.

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was calculated as ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL) / 2] [31]. TG and FPG were converted from mmol/L to mg/dL before calculating TyG. TyG was calculated at each examination. TyG-mean was the arithmetic mean across the three examinations [28], and TyG-CV was calculated as SD/mean × 100% [32]. TyG-Slope was estimated as the individual-specific linear trend of TyG across the three annual examinations using ordinary least squares regression, with time coded as 0, 1, and 2 for 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively. The resulting slope represents the average annual change in TyG over the study period. Although exact examination dates were not available for all participants, each participant completed one examination in each calendar year, and consecutive visits were separated by at least 3 months. Therefore, equally spaced time coding was considered a reasonable annualized approximation and was unlikely to materially influence the magnitude or interpretation of the estimated slope.

Assessment of carotid plaque and definition of plaque progression

Carotid ultrasonography was performed using a Versana Premier Plus ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) by trained sonographers according to a standardized protocol recommended by the American Society of Echocardiography [6]. Sonographers performed the examinations as part of routine health screening and were not involved in the calculation of TyG metrics or the statistical analyses. Because TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were derived retrospectively after data extraction, sonographers were not aware of the derived TyG metrics or the study hypotheses at the time of ultrasound assessment. All examinations followed the same institutional scanning protocol, and image acquisition and measurement procedures were subject to routine departmental quality-control procedures. Previous ultrasound reports could be reviewed when clinically necessary to facilitate consistency in the longitudinal assessment of carotid plaque burden.

To assess measurement reproducibility, 30 participants were randomly selected. TPA measurements were independently assessed by a second experienced sonographer. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs). Plaque was identified using established criteria [6]. Plaque area was quantified for the left and right carotid arteries, and TPA was calculated as the sum of plaque areas from both sides [33].

Plaque progression was defined a priori as either (i) incident plaque at the 2025 examination among participants without plaque in 2023, or (ii) an absolute increase in TPA of ≥ 0.05 cm² between 2023 and 2025 among those with plaque at baseline [33]. The absolute change was calculated as ΔTPA = TPA_2025 − TPA_2023. We prespecified an absolute increase in TPA of ≥ 0.05 cm² to define progression, consistent with previous longitudinal studies using carotid plaque area measurements [33]. This threshold has been used to distinguish meaningful plaque progression from minor measurement variation in serial ultrasound assessments [33]. Participants meeting either criterion were classified as having plaque progression.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as number (percentage). We used Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ² test for categorical variables.

Associations between TyG-related metrics and plaque progression were assessed using logistic regression. TyG-mean, TyG-CV, and TyG-Slope were analyzed per 1-SD increase and reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Four logistic regression models were constructed sequentially. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 was additionally adjusted for body mass index (BMI), smoking history, systolic blood pressure (SBP), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), history of diabetes, and history of hypertension. Model 4 included all covariates in Model 3 with further mutual adjustment for TyG-mean, TyG-CV, and TyG-Slope. Given the close relationship among TyG-derived metrics, collinearity diagnostics were performed before multivariable modelling. Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated among TyG-mean, TyG-CV, and TyG-Slope. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were further examined in the fully adjusted model including conventional covariates and the three TyG-derived metrics. A VIF value > 5 was considered suggestive of potentially important multicollinearity.

We used restricted cubic splines to evaluate potential non-linear associations [34, 35] (4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles and the median value used as the reference). The distribution of TyG-Slope was additionally visualized using a histogram with a kernel density curve, with reference lines indicating zero slope and the median TyG-Slope value.

For the joint association analysis, TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were classified as low or high according to their median values, with cutoffs of 2.50% for TyG-CV and 0.0065 TyG units/year for TyG-Slope. Participants were then cross-classified into four groups: low TyG-CV/low TyG-Slope, low TyG-CV/high TyG-Slope, high TyG-CV/low TyG-Slope, and high TyG-CV/high TyG-Slope. The low TyG-CV/low TyG-Slope group was used as the reference category. To evaluate whether the joint association exceeded that expected from the individual effects of each metric, multiplicative interaction was assessed by including a cross-product term between dichotomized TyG-CV and TyG-Slope in the logistic regression models. In subgroup analyses, potential effect modification was further examined in separate models by including product terms between each TyG metric and prespecified subgroup variables (age group, sex, BMI category, diabetes status, and hypertension status).

For incremental prediction, we compared nested models. The base model included age, sex, BMI, smoking status, SBP, LDL-C, HDL-C, diabetes, hypertension, and TyG-mean. TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were then added separately and jointly, and were modeled using spline terms with 4 degrees of freedom. Discrimination and reclassification were assessed using the AUC, category-free (continuous) NRI, and IDI [36, 37]. Uncertainty was estimated by bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations); 95% confidence intervals were derived from the empirical bootstrap distributions, and two-sided P values were obtained from bootstrap-based tests. Model calibration was assessed using Brier scores and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Calibration curves were generated by grouping participants into deciles of predicted risk and comparing the mean predicted risk with the observed event rate in each decile. These performance metrics represent internal model evaluation and may be subject to optimism; therefore, external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by age, sex, BMI, and diabetes and hypertension status. Sensitivity analyses repeated the main models after excluding participants with diabetes or hypertension, and after excluding those with triglycerides ≥ 5.6 mmol/L [23].

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population according to TyG-CV quartiles

Baseline characteristics according to TyG-CV quartiles are presented in Table 1. Prior to the main analyses, measurement reproducibility was assessed in a randomly selected subset of 30 participants. The interobserver ICC for total plaque area (TPA) measurement was 0.911 (95% CI 0.822–0.956), indicating excellent agreement. The cohort included 1,519 participants, with a mean age of 47.40 ± 8.98 years, and 49.3% were women. Age and sex were comparable across TyG-CV quartiles (P = 0.401 and P = 0.484, respectively). Participants with higher TyG-CV generally had a less favorable metabolic profile. Compared with those in Q1, participants in Q4 had a higher BMI (25.02 ± 3.52 vs. 24.41 ± 3.41; P = 0.005) and a higher prevalence of diabetes (11.8% vs. 5.8%; P = 0.030). TG levels were higher in the upper TyG-CV quartiles, with median values ranging from 1.36 (1.04–2.01) to 1.60 (1.03–2.54); P = 0.004, whereas HDL-C levels were lower among participants with higher TyG-CV (P = 0.087). Baseline TyG and 3-year mean TyG were higher in the higher TyG-CV quartiles (both P < 0.001), while TyG-Slope did not differ significantly across quartiles (P = 0.354). Plaque progression was more frequently observed in participants with higher TyG-CV, with rates of 20.0% in Q1 and 31.8% in Q4; P = 0.002.

Associations of TyG dynamic metrics with carotid plaque progression

Multivariable logistic regression analyses are summarized in Table 2. TyG-CV showed a robust association with carotid plaque progression across all models; in the fully adjusted model with mutual adjustment of TyG metrics, each 1-SD increase in TyG-CV was associated with a 28% higher odds of progression (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.13–1.45; P < 0.001). TyG-Slope was also independently associated with progression after full adjustment (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.42; P < 0.001). In contrast, the association for TyG-mean was attenuated after multivariable adjustment and was no longer significant in Model 4 (OR 1.04, 95% CI 0.89–1.21; P = 0.603).

Collinearity diagnostics did not indicate severe multicollinearity among the TyG-derived metrics. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were 0.197 for TyG-mean with TyG-CV, -0.040 for TyG-mean with TyG-Slope, and − 0.037 for TyG-CV with TyG-Slope. Similar patterns were observed using Spearman correlation coefficients. The VIF values for TyG-mean, TyG-CV, and TyG-Slope were 1.648, 1.045, and 1.019, respectively, and the maximum VIF among all variables in the fully adjusted model was 1.799. These findings suggest that multicollinearity was unlikely to materially influence the mutually adjusted estimates (Table S1). The distribution of TyG-Slope values is shown in Fig. S2. The median TyG-Slope was 0.0065 TyG units/year, which was also used as the cutoff for the joint classification analysis .

Restricted cubic spline analyses showed a significant positive association between TyG-CV and plaque progression without evidence of non-linearity (P-overall = 0.001; P-non-linear = 0.976). For TyG-Slope, a positive association was also observed (P-overall < 0.001), with a steeper increase in risk at higher positive slope values, although formal evidence for non-linearity did not reach statistical significance (P-non-linear = 0.069) (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that both greater TyG variability and upward longitudinal changes in TyG are associated with a higher risk of carotid plaque progression.

Combined effect of TyG-CV and TyG-Slope on carotid plaque

Table 3 shows the joint categories of TyG-CV and TyG-Slope. Using the low CV/low slope group as the reference (N = 379; 71 events, 18.7%), plaque progression was more common when only one component was elevated (low CV/high slope: 26.1% [99/380]; high CV/low slope: 25.1% [95/379]). After full adjustment, however, these two groups were not statistically different from the reference (OR 1.29, 95% CI 0.89–1.86; and OR 1.27, 95% CI 0.88–1.84).

Consistency of the associations in subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses examining potential effect modification by demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Fig. 3. Overall, the associations of TyG-CV and TyG-Slope with carotid plaque progression were directionally consistent across most subgroups. For TyG-CV, the subgroup results suggested possible effect modification by age and sex. The association appeared stronger in participants younger than the median age compared with those at or above the median age (P for interaction = 0.044) and was more pronounced in women than in men (P for interaction = 0.009). Interactions by BMI, diabetes status, and hypertension status were not statistically significant. Although the point estimate among participants with diabetes was lower than that among those without diabetes, this estimate was imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited sample size and event count.

Incremental value of TyG dynamic metrics for risk prediction

The incremental predictive performance of adding TyG dynamic metrics is presented in Table 4; Fig. 4. The base model (Model A) showed an AUC of 0.713. Adding spline-modelled TyG-CV (Model B) resulted in a modest improvement in discrimination (AUC 0.722, P for ΔAUC = 0.004), together with better reclassification (continuous NRI 0.1507, P = 0.016) and a small but significant increase in IDI (0.0102, P = 0.002). When spline-modelled TyG-Slope was added (Model C), discrimination improved further (AUC 0.730, P for ΔAUC = 0.004), with larger gains in continuous NRI (0.2132, P = 0.006) and IDI (0.0188, P < 0.001). When both TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were included (Model D), discrimination was similar to that observed with TyG-Slope alone (AUC 0.734 vs. 0.730), indicating only marginal additional improvement in AUC. However, continuous NRI (0.1942, P = 0.008) and IDI (0.0217, P < 0.001) remained statistically significant. Given the modest changes in AUC and the known tendency of continuous NRI to overestimate predictive gain, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of incremental rather than substantial improvement in predictive performance.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses are shown in Table 5. Overall, the associations between TyG dynamics and plaque progression were similar to the main findings. In the full cohort (N = 1,519; 384 events), both TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were independently associated with progression (TyG-CV: OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.13–1.45; P < 0.001; TyG-Slope: OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.42; P < 0.001). When participants with diabetes or hypertension were excluded (N = 1,042; 206 events), the associations remained significant and were slightly stronger (TyG-CV: OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.12–1.55; P = 0.001; TyG-Slope: OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.15–1.60; P < 0.001). After excluding individuals with triglycerides ≥ 5.6 mmol/L (N = 1,479; 373 events), TyG-CV remained robust (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.15–1.49; P < 0.001), and the association for TyG-Slope remained statistically significant, although slightly attenuated (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.07–1.37; P = 0.003).

Discussion

In this retrospective longitudinal cohort of adults undergoing routine health examinations, both TyG-CV and TyG-Slope were independently associated with early carotid plaque progression, whereas mean TyG lost significance after multivariable and mutual adjustment. These findings suggest that visit-to-visit TyG variability and upward TyG change may provide vascular risk-related information beyond static TyG burden. Rather than reflecting a single exposure level, TyG dynamics appear to reflect longitudinal changes in triglyceride-glucose burden that are associated with structural atherosclerotic progression [28, 32, 38]. The attenuation of TyG-mean after mutual adjustment should not be interpreted as evidence against the importance of average metabolic burden. Rather, it may reflect overlap among related TyG-derived metrics and suggests that variability and directional change provide additional information beyond the average level.

Restricted cubic spline analyses suggested a broadly linear association between TyG-CV and plaque progression. For TyG-Slope, risk appeared to increase more steeply at higher positive values, although formal evidence for non-linearity did not reach statistical significance. Previous studies have reported potential non-linear associations between TyG-related metrics and carotid atherosclerosis [16, 19]. In the present study, these findings suggest that TyG-CV and TyG-Slope may capture different aspects of longitudinal triglyceride-glucose dynamics related to plaque progression. Biologically, higher TyG-CV reflects larger swings in glucose and triglyceride metabolism. Such swings have been associated with intermittent oxidative stress and episodes of endothelial dysfunction [39–42], and repeated exposures may help maintain inflammatory activation, potentially through durable molecular changes [43], thereby supporting leukocyte/macrophage recruitment and early plaque growth [44]. Regarding TyG-Slope, excess risk was largely confined to positive values, suggesting that an upward TyG trajectory reflects sustained metabolic deterioration. Similar long-term TyG patterns have been associated with carotid atherosclerosis progression and with greater arterial stiffness [17]. In contrast to short-term variability, TyG-Slope captures the direction of change and may better represent cumulative exposure to insulin resistance; consistent with this, longitudinal increases and greater accumulated TyG burden have been linked to higher incident cardiovascular risk [27, 29]. One possible explanation is that an increasing TyG trajectory may reflect worsening metabolic status, including insulin resistance, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and chronic low-grade inflammation, which may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and plaque development [8, 9, 44]. In our cohort, the highest risk was observed when a positive TyG-Slope coexisted with high TyG-CV, indicating that marked volatility superimposed on long-term worsening may be particularly adverse [28].

Joint classification added an intuitive layer of risk stratification. Although plaque progression was descriptively more frequent when either TyG-CV or TyG-Slope was elevated, the strongest and most consistent signal was seen when both were elevated: after full adjustment, only the high CV/high slope group remained independently associated with progression, while the “single-high” groups were not clearly different from the reference. This suggests that focusing on static TyG levels, or on one dynamic feature in isolation, may miss individuals in whom instability and sustained worsening co-occur. The absence of a significant multiplicative interaction suggests that these two dynamic features may contribute additively rather than synergistically to plaque progression risk.

Stronger associations observed among younger participants may reflect the greater relative contribution of metabolic dysfunction to early vascular injury before the accumulation of competing age-related risk factors. Subgroup analyses suggested that the associations of TyG-CV and TyG-Slope with plaque progression were more pronounced among younger participants and women, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of multiple comparisons and limited precision in some strata. The apparently stronger associations observed in women may warrant further investigation. Women in midlife often experience marked cardiometabolic changes during the menopausal transition, including changes in fat distribution, insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, and vascular function [45]. These changes may limit the ability of single-time-point metabolic measurements to fully capture early vascular risk. In support of this view, recent evidence from the SWAN Study showed that, among midlife women with metabolic syndrome, HDL-C status contributed to the prediction of incident diabetes but did not independently distinguish higher carotid intima-media thickness after accounting for other metabolic components [46]. Therefore, longitudinal TyG variability and directional worsening may be particularly informative during a period of cardiometabolic transition. However, menopausal status and reproductive hormone data were not available in our cohort, and the sex-specific findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Sensitivity analyses generally supported the main findings. Associations were slightly stronger after excluding participants with diabetes or hypertension, suggesting that the observed relationships were not solely driven by these established cardiometabolic conditions. After excluding individuals with triglycerides ≥ 5.6 mmol/L, both TyG-CV and TyG-Slope remained significantly associated with plaque progression, although the effect estimate for TyG-Slope was slightly attenuated. This attenuation may indicate that directional worsening in TyG is more sensitive to extreme metabolic trajectories than visit-to-visit variability. Nevertheless, this pattern suggests that the associations of TyG dynamic metrics were not driven exclusively by individuals with severe hypertriglyceridemia and supports the robustness of the primary findings.

From a practical standpoint, fasting triglycerides and glucose are routinely measured in health-check programs, allowing TyG dynamics to be derived without additional testing. Incorporating TyG-Slope and TyG-CV was associated with modest improvements in discrimination and reclassification beyond conventional risk factors and mean TyG, suggesting that dynamic metabolic profiling may provide complementary information beyond conventional risk factors. Nevertheless, these prediction results should be interpreted with caution. Although discrimination, reclassification, and calibration were assessed in the present study, the analyses were based on internal apparent model performance in a single cohort. In particular, the Hosmer-Lemeshow results for models including TyG dynamic metrics suggested possible calibration deviation, indicating that external validation and, if necessary, model recalibration are needed before clinical implementation.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, because this was an observational retrospective study, causal inference cannot be established, and residual confounding remains possible. Information on lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering, and antihypertensive medication use, as well as detailed socioeconomic status, was not systematically available in the routine health examination database; therefore, potential confounding by treatment status and socioeconomic factors could not be fully addressed. Second, requiring participants to have three consecutive annual health examinations may have introduced selection or survivorship bias, as individuals with regular follow-up may differ from those with incomplete examinations in health awareness, access to care, or underlying cardiometabolic risk. Third, TyG-Slope was estimated from three annual measurements using equally coded time intervals. Although this approach provides a practical and reproducible approximation of longitudinal change in routine health-screening settings, estimation based on only three measurements may be sensitive to short-term fluctuations and may not fully capture complex or non-linear TyG trajectories. More complex longitudinal approaches, such as mixed-effects models or latent trajectory modelling, were not applied because only three annual TyG measurements were available for each participant. These methods may be more informative in cohorts with more frequent measurements and exact visit dates. Fourth, although plaque measurements demonstrated excellent interobserver agreement in a reproducibility assessment (ICC 0.911), some degree of measurement variability inherent to ultrasound-based plaque assessment cannot be completely excluded. Fifth, this was a single-centre Chinese health-screening cohort. The findings may not be directly generalisable to Western populations, rural populations, community-based cohorts, or patients with established cardiovascular disease, and validation in diverse populations is warranted. The stronger associations observed in women should also be interpreted cautiously, because menopausal status and reproductive hormone data were not collected. Finally, prediction analyses were based on internal apparent model performance. Although discrimination, reclassification, and calibration were assessed, the calibration findings suggest that external validation and possible recalibration are needed before clinical implementation. Longer follow-up is also required to determine whether these dynamic TyG features improve prediction of clinical cardiovascular events.

Overall, TyG-CV and TyG-Slope appear to capture complementary aspects of early plaque progression and may provide additional information for risk assessment in routine screening. External validation and prospective studies are needed to confirm their clinical utility.

Conclusions

In this health-screening cohort, visit-to-visit variability and longitudinal change in the TyG index were independently associated with early carotid plaque progression, whereas mean TyG levels were not independently predictive after full adjustment. These findings suggest that visit-to-visit TyG variability and sustained upward TyG change may better reflect early atherosclerotic risk than static measures alone. Incorporating longitudinal TyG metrics may provide additional information for early risk stratification in routine preventive care, although external validation and prospective studies are required before clinical implementation.
