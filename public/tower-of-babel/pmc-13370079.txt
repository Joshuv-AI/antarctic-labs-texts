Trochlear ridge reconstruction using a patient-specific three-dimensionally printed titanium implant in a dog with severe unilateral trochlear deformity and complex femoral deformity: a long-term follow-up case report
Authors: Na-Yeong JE, Kyu-won KANG, Sun Young KIM, Byung-Jae KANG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 4-year-old spayed female beagle presented with intermittent lameness and external rotation of the left hind limb after distal femoral fracture repair. Imaging revealed severe lateral trochlear deformity with complex deformities of the left femur. Trochlear ridge reconstruction and corrective osteotomy were performed using patient-specific titanium trochlear ridge implant and 3D-printed surgical guides. Postoperative images confirmed the proper femoral alignment and patellar positioning. The lameness resolved, and full weight-bearing was achieved within two months. Normal limb function was maintained for 24 months without implant failure or recurrence. At the 36-month telephone follow-up, the owner reported sustained improvement in limb function and quality of life. This case demonstrates successful long-term outcomes of trochlear ridge reconstruction using a patient-specific 3D-printed titanium implant.

Trochlear deformity is characterized by an abnormally shaped trochlear groove, presenting as shallow, flat, or dome-shaped [1, 3]. In dogs, abnormal trochlear morphology is a contributing factor, with a bidirectional relationship between patellar luxation and trochlear groove abnormalities [20]. Trochlear morphology in dogs can be objectively evaluated using computed tomography (CT) based measurements such as femoral trochlear groove angle (FTGA). The FTGA is smaller in dogs without medial patellar luxation than in those with medial patellar luxation; it also helps assess disease severity and guide surgical decision-making [15].

Trochlear deformity associated with patellar luxation is commonly managed with surgical intervention. Trochleoplasty has been used to deepen the trochlear groove in patellar luxation cases [12]. Recently, polyethylene ridges and patellar groove replacements were introduced to augment trochlear ridge height and replace damaged femoropatellar joints with favorable outcomes [7, 8, 10, 16]. In cases accompanied by femoral deformities, corrective osteotomy should be considered as part of the overall surgical strategy [22]. Despite the availability of various surgical techniques and implants, severe defects in the trochlear ridge with deformities may necessitate patient-specific prostheses to restore the unique anatomical and functional characteristics of the joint. Reports of patient-specific trochlear ridge prostheses for treating trochlear deformity and patellar luxation in dogs exist, but include only short-term follow-up, limiting the assessment of implant durability and long-term functional outcomes [17, 19].

This report describes the surgical technique and long-term outcomes of a one-step trochlear ridge reconstruction using three dimensionally (3D) printed patient-specific titanium implant combined with femoral osteotomy in a patient with severe trochlear deformity and a complex femoral deformity.

A 4-year-old spayed female beagle weighing 11 kg presented with intermittent non-weight-bearing lameness in the left hind limb. The patient had undergone surgical repair of a left distal femoral fracture at 5 months of age. Over the subsequent three years, progressive external rotation of the left hind limb during ambulation and intermittent non-weight-bearing lameness were observed.

Radiographic assessment identified a severe left femoral deformity characterized by external torsion, valgus, mild shortening, and a residual implant from the prior fracture repair in the distal femur (Fig. 1B, 1C). CT scans (Aquilion 64, Toshiba Medical Systems, Carrollton, TX, USA) of both hind limbs were acquired using a helical scan mode with the following parameters: 120 kVp, 200 mA; slice thickness, 0.5 mm; tube rotation time, 1 sec; pitch, 0.5; and matrix size, 512 × 512. The anatomical lateral distal femoral angle (aLDFA) of the left femur was measured at 67°, indicating a femoral valgus deformity, and the femoral anteversion angle was measured at −20°, suggesting a severe femoral torsional deformity. The lateral trochlear ridge exhibited a marked depression forming a cliff-like deformity, whereas the medial ridge remained intact (Fig. 1D).

FTGA was measured using a CT DICOM file viewer (Osirix version 2.7; Pixmeo SARL, Geneva, Switzerland), following previously reported methods [15]. FTGA was measured in the transverse plane at the proximal one-fourth (P25) and one-half (P50) levels corresponding to the deepest regions of the trochlea (Fig. 2C, 2D) [18]. The mean FTGA values at P25 and P50 were 124.1° on the right side (P25: 133.7°, P50: 114.5°) and 158.4° on the left side (P25: 147.8°, P50: 169.0°), respectively. The left side value exceeded the normal range (119.2 ± 5.6°) reported for medium-to-large dogs [15]. Therefore, lateral trochlear deformity and complex deformities of the left femur were diagnosed. Although assessment of lateral patellar luxation was limited on physical examination, CT revealed a severe abnormal trochlear morphology with complete absence of the lateral trochlear ridge. Given the chronically lateralized quadriceps mechanism and the lack of a physical lateral trochlear barrier, femoral realignment alone was expected to predispose the patella to lateral instability. Surgical planning included 1) corrective osteotomy to realign the femoral axis and 2) trochlear ridge reconstruction using a patient-specific titanium implant to augment the lateral trochlear ridge.

The CT images were imported into 3-D Slicer (version 5.8.1, http://www.slicer.org) for segmentation and STL file generation. The bone structure segmentation threshold was set to 200 Hounsfield units. The STL files were imported into computer-aided design software (3DS Max, Autodesk Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA) to model the distal femoral osteotomy (DFO) guide and patient-specific trochlear ridge implant. Corrective osteotomy was initially planned to address left femoral torsional and valgus deformities. A 3D simulation was performed to plan the correction of the femoral alignment. The osteotomy location was determined based on center of rotation of angulation (CORA) principles by identifying the intersection of the proximal and distal anatomical axis [21]. Following 3D simulation to achieve the desired limb alignment, with the goal of restoring colinear alignment from the femur to the distal extremity, the spatial relationship and axis of the proximal and distal segments were defined and mapped back onto the original preoperative 3D model. A patient-specific osteotomy guide was designed using a surface-matching approach and positioned on the craniomedial cortical surface of the distal femur, with the medial ridge contour used as the primary positional landmark (Fig. 3B). Using the same design principles and workflow as previously reported [11], a patient-specific reduction guide for fragment apposition and a compression guide designed to generate interfragmentary compression through an oblique pin trajectory were additionally created (Fig. 3C).

The lateral trochlear ridge implant was designed considering its positional relationship with a DFO plate. The normal right femur was mirrored and the mirrored trochlea was aligned with the dysplastic left trochlea. Boolean subtraction generated an anatomically accurate left lateral trochlear ridge reconstruction, incorporating a 0.2 mm clearance. The implant aimed to achieve a final sulcus angle and trochlear depth of 124° and 5.3 mm, respectively, with a minimum thickness of 1.5 mm with reference to the contralateral limb. Locking-screw fixation was selected to achieve rigid implant fixation. Four 3D-modeled screw holes were designed to accommodate 2.0-mm locking screws (Arix Vet, Jeil Medical Co., Seoul, Korea), ensuring stable implant fixation (Fig. 3D, 3E).

The guides were 3D-printed (Pixel One, Zerone, Seongnam, Korea) using biocompatible urethane acrylate resin (SG-Clear, Dentis, Daegu, Korea), and the trochlear ridge implant was fabricated from Ti6Al4V titanium alloy using a Selective Laser Melting printer (MetalSys150, Winforsys Co., Ltd., Suwon, Korea). The titanium implants were polished to prevent friction and oxidation. Femoral bone models were generated from CT data and fabricated using a 3D printer (Shuffle XL, Phrozen, Hsinchu, Taiwan) with resin material (Harpiks, Zerone). The dog was premedicated with remifentanil (1 μg/kg, IV; Remiva, Hana Pharm Co., Hwaseong, Korea), ketamine (0.5 mg/kg, IV; Ketamine, Huons Co., Seongnam, Korea), and midazolam (0.1 mg/kg, IV; Midazolam, Bukwang Pharmaceutical Co., Seoul, Korea) for preemptive analgesia. Anesthesia was induced with alfaxalone (2 mg/kg, IV, Alfaxan, Jurox Pty Ltd., Rutherford, Australia) and maintained with an isoflurane-oxygen mixture (Ifran Liq; Hana Pharm Co.). A constant rate infusion of remifentanil (0.01 mg/kg/hr), midazolam (0.02 mg/kg/hr), and ketamine (0.3 mg/kg/hr) combination was administered perioperatively. Cefazolin (33 mg/kg, IV; Cefazolin, Chong Kun Dang Pharmaceutical Co., Seoul, Korea) was administered as preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis and repeated every 120 min. The affected limb was prepared aseptically by clipping and scrubbing. The dog was positioned in dorsal recumbency and the limb was draped using the 4-quarter method.

A lateral parapatellar skin incision was made and extended proximally along the cranial border of the femur. The cranial border of the biceps femoris muscle was incised, and the two previously implanted K-wires were removed from the lateral side of the distal femur. An incision was made between the cranial and caudal parts of the sartorius muscle and the medial aspect of the femur was exposed via blunt dissection. Subsequently, the quadriceps muscle group was dissected from the cranial aspect and retracted laterally. The K-wire obtained during the previous surgery was removed from the medial side of the distal femur. The 3D-printed patient-specific saw guide was positioned on the craniomedial surface of the femur and secured with 1.0 mm and 1.6 mm K-wires. Osteotomy was performed using an oscillating saw according to guidelines (Fig. 4E). Bone alignment was achieved using customized reduction and compression guides (Fig. 4F). Trochlear inspection revealed asymmetric deformity with degenerative changes in the lateral trochlear ridge. Lateral patellar luxation was confirmed by manual manipulation. The patient-specific trochlear ridge implant was placed in its pre-planned position and secured with four 2.0 mm locking screws (Arix Vet, Jeil Medical Co.) (Fig. 4G, 4H). Proper patellar tracking within the trochlear groove was confirmed during flexion-extension and internal-external rotations of the stifle joint. Finally, the osteotomy was stabilized with 2.7 mm distal femoral osteotomy plate laterally and a 2.0 mm locking plate medially (Arix Vet; Jeil Medical Co.). After copious saline lavage, the cortical and cancellous bone removed from the osteotomy was morselized and packed around the osteotomy site, and routine layered closure was performed.

This was a case of lateral trochlear ridge reconstruction combined with complex femoral deformity correction using a patient-specific 3D-printed titanium implant and surgical guide, which successfully restored limb function, improved alignment, and enhanced the quality of life without complications over a 36-month follow-up.

In this case, severe valgus and torsional deformities of the distal femur were considered the primary issue. The abnormal trochlear morphology was interpreted as an acquired condition secondary to torsional deformity caused by malunion following distal femoral fracture repair at an early age, rather than congenital trochlear dysplasia. Accordingly, DFO was planned and performed as the primary treatment, leading to improved limb alignment and restoration of weight-bearing function postoperatively.

However, preoperative imaging findings indicated a high risk of lateral patellar instability following femoral realignment, suggesting that deformity correction alone would be inadequate. In the presence of a severe trochlear ridge defect, realignment alone was not enough to ensure patellar stability, as confirmed by intraoperative assessment, which required additional trochlear ridge reconstruction. Therefore, various surgical options were considered to restore trochlear ridge height and provide structural support for the patella.

Conventional trochleoplasty techniques were unsuitable in the present case because of the asymmetric trochlear morphology with an intact medial trochlear ridge and an underdeveloped lateral ridge. Polyethylene sulcal ridge implants were inadequate in this case, as the fixed height of each implant size limited their ability to restore sufficient ridge height in markedly eroded or cliff-like trochlear morphology. Furthermore, patellar groove replacement is considered excessively invasive given medial ridge preservation. Consequently, a patient-specific 3D-printed implant was designed to reconstruct the lateral trochlear ridge, while preserving the native medial trochlear ridge.

Patient-specific trochlear ridge implants offer advantages such as enabling precise anatomical reconstruction while preserving the native articular cartilage. Unlike earlier methods that required two-step procedures or hand-molded components, which pose the risks of inaccurate placement and operator dependence [17, 19], the implant used in this study was designed through 3D modeling to fit directly onto the bone without additional guides or osteotomy. Intraoperatively, its precise fit eliminates the need for intra-articular sculpting or groove preparation, reduces surgical time, and minimizes trauma to articular structures.

Furthermore, fixation stability was enhanced by incorporating locking screw holes into the titanium implant. Previous in vitro studies have shown that 3D-printed locking holes with identical design and manufacturing processes provide mechanical stability comparable to conventional locking systems [13], and in vivo studies using a rabbit model have demonstrated significantly greater primary fixation stability with locking fixation compared to non-locking constructs [14]. Although this report describes a single case, no evidence of screw loosening was observed during the 24-month follow-up.

A titanium alloy was used to fabricate implants for articular surface reconstruction. Titanium has demonstrated favorable outcomes in partial joint replacement of the shoulder and wrist, with reported pain relief and functional recovery in humans over 12–38 months [2, 5]. Nonetheless, long-term wear and corrosion remain as concerns for articular applications. In this study, polishing improved wear resistance [9], and advanced surface treatments were not applied due to cost and customization complexity. No implant-related complications were observed after 24 months. Nevertheless, continued monitoring is warranted to detect potential long-term issues.

In conclusion, patient-specific titanium implants enabled successful unilateral trochlear ridge reconstruction. These implants are effective in treating trochlear deformity and patellar luxation, enabling anatomical restoration of the trochlear ridge, preserving normal cartilage, enhancing surgical convenience, and improving functional outcomes. Importantly, this method is not a definitive treatment but allows for potential future surgical interventions and can be effectively combined with other procedures if needed.
