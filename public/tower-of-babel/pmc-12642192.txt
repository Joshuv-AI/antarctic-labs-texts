The impact of non-invasive brain-computer interface technology on the therapeutic effect of patients with spinal cord injury: a summary of evidence based on meta-analysis
Authors: Zhuojing Sun, Song Hu, Jiaju Zhu, Zijun Ye, Mingchen Ma, Guodong Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study is to systematically evaluate the effects of non-invasive brain-computer interface technology on motor and sensory functions and daily living abilities of patients with spinal cord injuries. In addition, the study will investigate the related modifying factors. Ultimately, the study will provide evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice.MethodsA systematic search was conducted on PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Wiley Online Library, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Data Resource System, and VIP Database for relevant literature from database inception to February 2025. The quality of the studies was assessed using Review Manager 5.4, with the risk of bias visually represented. The presence of publication bias was assessed through the utilization of the “metafor” package (version 4.6-0) in R (version 4.4.1). The certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the GRADE framework.ResultsA total of 9 papers were included, including 4 randomized controlled trials and 5 self-controlled trials with 109 spinal cord injury patients. Compared with the control group, the non-invasive brain-computer interface intervention had a significant impact on patients’ motor function (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: [0.35,1.09], P < 0.01, I2 = 0%, medium level of evidence), sensory function (SMD = 0.95, 95% CI: [0.43,1.48], P < 0.01, I2 = 0%, medium level of evidence), activities of daily living (SMD = 0.85, 95% CI: [0.46,1.24], P < 0.01, I2 = 0%, low level of evidence) reached statistical significance. Subgroup analyses showed that for the current summary of evidence, noninvasive brain-computer interface interventions in patients with subacute stage spinal cord injuries showed statistically stronger effects on motor function, sensory function, and ability to perform activities of daily living than in patients with slow chronic stage spinal cord injuries.ConclusionAs far as the existing literature is concerned, non-invasive brain-computer interface technology shows the potential to improve motor and sensory functioning as well as the ability to perform activities of daily living in patients with spinal cord injury. However, the conclusions are preliminary and hypothetical, and as the current evidence for non-invasive BCI interventions for people with spinal cord injury remains limited, this paper does not recommend the application of the conclusions to clinical practice until future large-sample RCTs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01766-x.

Introduction

Spinal Cord Injury (SCI) has a global impact on more than 22 million patients, and new cases are estimated to be between 250,000 and 500,000 per year, creating a serious burden and pressure on society and the economy [1, 2]. It is estimated that approximately 1.275 million individuals in the United States are paralyzed as a result of spinal cord injuries, with annual care costs projected to range from $30,000 to $60,000 [3, 4]. According to the “International Standard for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury” (ISNCSCI), the severity of SCI is classified into five categories, known as the American Spinal Injury Association (AIS) Impairment Scale grades A to E, with grade A representing the most severe level of impairment. Specifically, individuals classified as AIS A exhibit a complete loss of both sensory and motor function below the neurological level of injury. Those categorized as AIS B retain some degree of sensory function below the injury level but demonstrate no voluntary motor function. Patients classified as AIS C and D exhibit varying degrees of preserved motor function, although these functions remain impaired. AIS E indicates normal sensory and motor function [5]. This will greatly limit the patient’s ability to perform activities of daily living, such as further balance impairment due to sensory loss, or inability to walk, stand, or reduced cardiorespiratory endurance due to impaired motor function, and the degree of recovery will depend on the severity of the spinal cord tissue injury [6, 7]. In addition to the direct consequences of SCI, it can lead to a variety of complications, including autonomic nerve dysfunction, neurogenic shock, muscle atrophy, urinary incontinence, and pressure ulcers. Furthermore, patients may experience elevated psychological issues, such as post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and depression [8–11]. To date, there remains no definitive cure for SCI. Over the past few decades, neural stem cell transplantation and biomaterial scaffolds have been recognized as promising strategies for the treatment of SCI; however, the level of recovery achieved still falls short of expectations [12, 13]. While traditional therapies such as surgical interventions have been shown to improve recovery from SCI, the results are also highly heterogeneous and may lead to serious consequences, so the community has been looking for better treatment modalities to improve the outcome of rehabilitation of patients after SCI [14, 15].

Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) technology, as a cutting-edge new interaction technology, has attracted much attention in various fields such as neuroscience, clinical rehabilitation, and bioengineering, and has been rapidly developed in recent years, and a number of meta-analyses have confirmed that this technology has a good effect on the rehabilitation of stroke patients, who are also suffering from central nervous system injury [16–18]. This technology offers a method for interaction between the brain and a computer. The signal collection device captures the patient’s brain signals, processes and amplifies them through filtering, feature extraction, and classification of brain waves or signals. Subsequently, these signals are converted into a recognizable form by a converter. Upon receiving the signal, the effector executes the corresponding function, ultimately fulfilling the patient’s intention. The working principle can be broadly categorized into four stages: (1) Signal acquisition (2) Preprocessing (3) Feature extraction (4) Equipment control [19, 20]. BCI can be categorized into three types based on the signal acquisition method: invasive, semi-invasive, and non-invasive BCI. However, invasive and semi-invasive BCI present several limitations, including risks associated with implantation, weak signal acquisition, stability challenges, and issues related to energy supply. In contrast, non-invasive BCI are safer, more convenient, and offer a diverse range of monitoring instruments. Consequently, they have emerged as one of the most promising research avenues within the BCI field, with significant application potential in both the mass market and clinical rehabilitation sectors [21–23].

Given that the core of BCI’s mechanism lies in bypassing damaged neural pathways, directly decoding brain intentions and driving external devices or inducing neuroplasticity, it allows it to show great potential for application in the field of SCI rehabilitation as well. SCI leads to disruption of signaling pathways between the brain and somatic effectors, severely impairing the patient’s motor and sensory functions and activities of daily living (ADL). Non-invasive BCI techniques could theoretically provide patients with SCI with (1) alternative control pathways: improving motor function through direct use of brain signal control aids (e.g., exoskeletons, wheelchairs, computers) or functional electrical stimulation (FES); (2) closed-loop neurofeedback training: potentially improving impaired motor control by potentially facilitating cortical reorganization and reinforcement of residual pathways through real-time feedback; and (3) Sensory substitution or feedback: enhancing perceptual abilities or providing sensory feedback to improve the interaction experience. Therefore, quantitatively assessing the specific rehabilitative effects of BCI interventions on core functional domains of motor function (e.g., upper/lower extremity motor control, muscle strength), sensory function (e.g., somatosensory, nociceptive), and ADLs (e.g., self-care, tool use) for patients with SCI is key to evaluating their clinical value [24]. To date, there are few experimental studies applying non-invasive BCI to SCI patients and observing its efficacy, usually with insufficient single sample size, and most of them are self-controlled trials, with a small percentage of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), so integrating previous studies through meta-analysis is necessary [25]. The Meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effect of non-invasive brain-computer interface technology on various aspects of functional rehabilitation of spinal cord injury patients, to provide a systematic scientific basis for clinical practice and to provide corresponding recommendations for subsequent research in this field.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [26]. The study protocol was prospectively registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251026140.

Literature search strategy

The search platforms included Pubmed, Web of Science, Scopus, Wiley Online Library, Cochrane Library, China Knowledge Network, Wanfang Data Resource System, and VIP databases to search for relevant literature from the time of library construction to February 2025, with the last search on February 25, 2025. Use Boolean logic operators to integrate the following terms for a comprehensive search: [(brain-computer interface) OR (brain computer interface) OR (BCI) OR (brain- machine interface) OR (brain machine interface) OR (BMI) OR (user computer interface) OR (human machine interface)] AND [(spinal cord injury) OR (spinal cord trauma) OR (spinal cord lesion) OR (spinal cord laceration) OR (spinal cord damage) OR (spinal cord contusion)]. In addition, we reviewed the included meta-analyses and reviews to complement the search .

Literature screening and data extraction

Literature management and elimination of duplicates was performed using EndNote 20 software, then irrelevant literature was eliminated by reading titles and abstracts, and then literature that did not meet the criteria was eliminated after reading the full text according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a process that was performed independently by two researchers. The coefficient of agreement (Cohen’s kappa) was 0.837 ≥ 0.81, indicating good reliability [27]. In case of disagreement, the decision was made after discussion with a third researcher.

Data Extraction: (1) First author, year and nationality; (2) Study design and sample size; (3) Age, number and grouping of the sample; (4) BCI grading characteristics and patient course; (5) Outcome indicators.

Risk of bias evaluation

Using the ‘risk of bias assessment’ tool developed by the Cochrane Collaboration, there are seven dimensions: (1) Whether or not the method of allocation is randomized; (2) Allocation concealment; (3) Blinding of participants and realizers; (4) Blindness to outcome assessment; (5) Completeness of the outcome data; (6) Selective reporting of research findings; (7) The presence of other biases.

Statistical analysis

The forest and funnel plots were done through Review Manager 5.4 software. According to the Cochrane Handbook, when a meta-analysis extracts multiple studies of the same metric for a single document, it will result in multiple comparisons of the control group, which ultimately leads to an unjustified amplification of its weight, and therefore the weight needs to be controlled by dividing the number of passes by the number of comparisons [28]. The indicators included in this study were continuous variables but not measured in a uniform way, so Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) and its 95% Confidence Interval (CI) were used as the effect indicators. Because SMD uses a standard deviation scale with no unit of measurement, effect sizes were categorized as small (± 0.2), medium (± 0.5), and large (± 0.8) as suggested by HOPKINS et al. [29]. If the 95% CI contained 0, it indicated that there was no statistical significance in the comparison of the two interventions. Using the I2 value as an indicator of heterogeneity, when P >0.1 and I2 < 50%, the study was considered homogeneous and a fixed-effects model was used, and vice versa, a random-effects model was used. Sensitivity analysis will be performed based on leave1out summarization to test whether the results are stable, followed by subgroup analysis to explore the source of heterogeneity, mainly based on the study population (patient disease duration) and study type (length of single BCI intervention, study design) as the moderating variables, and funnel plots to judge whether there is publication bias in the included literature. Sensitivity analyses were performed using RStudio software version 2023.09.1 + 494 (R software version 4.4.1) metafor package (version 4.6-0) and publication bias tests were performed for all indicators using Egger’s operation and Begg’s rank correlation test, and publication bias was considered to be present if the test results showed P < 0.05, and vice versa (P ≥ 0.05) then it was considered that it did not exist.

Assessment of the level of evidence

The Grade of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method was used to evaluate the level of evidence, which was categorized into four levels: high, medium, low and very low [30]. The main contents are: The main components were: (1) Limitations of the study: downgraded one level if it included a non-randomized controlled trial; (2) Inconsistency of results: if heterogeneity (I2) >25%, downgrade by one level; (3) Indirectness: whether the indicator tested in the article reflects the actual effect of the competency, and if not, the level is degraded; (4) Precision: whether the effect size changes when removing a study with the highest weight, and if so, down one level; (5) Publication bias: one level down if Egger’s operation or Begg’s rank correlation test showed P < 0.05.

Motor function

A total of 9 papers (13 data sets) were included, and the heterogeneity test showed that no heterogeneity existed (I2 = 0%, P > 0.05), which was analyzed using a fixed-effects model. Meta-analysis showed a significant difference between the two groups (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: [0.35,1.09], P < 0.01), see Fig. 3. After removing one of the highest weighted data, the results remained significant (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: [0.33,1.12], P < 0.01, I2 = 0%). The results remained significant after removal of the self-controlled trials (SMD = 0.92, 95% CI: [0.45,1.39], P < 0.01; I2 = 0%). The analysis was re-run using a random -effects model, and the results did not shift. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Material S1.

Meta-analysis showed a significant difference between the two groups (SMD = 0.95, 95% CI: [0.43,1.48], P < 0.01), see Fig. 4. The analysis was re-run using a random -effects model, and the results did not shift. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Material S1.

Subgroup analysis showed that when SCI patients were in the subacute phase (SMD = 0.97, 95% CI: [0.44,1.51], P < 0.01) and received BCI intervention, the difference in the effect of restoration of sensory function was statistically significant compared to that in the chronic phase (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI: [-2,3.04], P > 0.05, I2 = 0%); The 2 literature types were both RCTs and the length of a single intervention was > 30 min, which did not qualify for subgroup analyses; the results of the remaining subgroup analyses are shown in Table 2.

ADL

A total of 6 papers were included, and the heterogeneity test showed that no heterogeneity existed (I2 = 0%, P > 0.05), which was analyzed using a fixed-effects model. Meta-analysis showed a significant difference between the two groups (SMD = 0.85, 95% CI: [0.46,1.24], P < 0.01), see Fig. 5. After removing one of the highest weighted data, the difference between the two groups would not be significant (SMD = 0.52, 95% CI: [-0.03,1.08], P > 0.05, I2 = 0%). After removal of the self-controlled trials, I2 was elevated to 65%, but there was still a significant difference between the two groups (SMD = 0.98, 95% CI: [0.48,1.48], P < 0.01; I2 = 65%), and it is worth noting that this result would not be significantly different after reanalysis using a random-effects model (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: [-0.4,1.84], P > 0.05, I2 = 65%), while the remaining results were not transformed. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Supplementary Material S1.

Subgroup analyses showed that patients with SCI in the subacute phase (SMD = 1.03, 95% CI: [0.55,1.5], P < 0.01; I2 = 37%) who received the BCI intervention showed improvement in activities of daily living compared with those in the chronic phase (SMD = 0.49, 95% CI: [-0.2,1.18], P > 0.05, I2 = 0%), and the difference was was statistically significant; The duration of a single BCI intervention ≤ 30 min (SMD = 0.38, 95% CI: [-0.44,1.2], P > 0.05, I2 = 0%) versus > 30 min (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI: [-0.11,1.41], P > 0.05, I2 = 0%) was not statistically significant in either group; Both the randomized controlled trial group (SMD = 0.98, 95% CI: [0.48,1.48], P < 0.01; I2 = 65%) and the self-controlled trial group (SMD = 0.66, 95% CI: [0.03,1.28], P < 0.05; I2 = 0%) reached statistical significance for the improvement in the ability to perform activities of daily living of the patients with SCI, see Table 2.

Discussion

Limb dysfunction remaining after central nervous system injury has always been the focus of clinical rehabilitation. Although SCI has led to a disruption in the connectivity of the spinal cord, the brain is still capable of generating the corresponding motor commands, which lays the foundation for the use of brain-computer interface technology in it, which is capable of analyzing neuronal signals in the cerebral cortex and reading the patient’s motor awareness, ultimately achieving the purpose of realizing the patient’s basic daily activities [31, 32]. The results of several previous case studies have demonstrated the potential of the BCI technique to promote recovery in patients with SCI [33, 34].

This Meta-analysis included 9 papers with a total sample size of 109 participants to investigate the effect of non-invasive brain-computer interface technology on the rehabilitation efficacy of patients with spinal cord injuries. The available evidence suggests that non-invasive BCIs have a significant effect on the spinal cord injury patients’ motor (SMD = 0.72, 95% CI: [0.35,1.09], moderate level of evidence), sensory function (SMD = 0.95, 95% CI: [0.43,1.48], medium level of evidence) and ability to perform activities of daily living (SMD = 0.85, 95% CI: [0.46,1.24], low level of evidence) achieved statistically significant improvements. Sensitivity analyses showed a shift in the results of the sensory function and ability to perform activities of daily living indicators after removing the highest weighted data. Whereas none of the results shifted after removing the self-controlled trials, the ability to function in daily living results would not be significant after reanalysis using a random -effects model. Subgroup analyses showed that non-invasive brain-computer interface technology improved motor and sensory functions and activities of daily living in patients with subacute SCI to a statistically significant level; Single BCI intervention duration ≤ 30 min versus > 30 min did not reach statistical significance in terms of functional improvement in motor function and activities of daily living ability of the patients; Randomized controlled trials will be more significant compared to self-controlled trials, while 6 papers reported no adverse events (remaining 3 unspecified). It is worth noting that the studies and sample sizes included in this paper are small, so the limited preliminary evidence from this Meta-analysis suggests that non-invasive BCI may have potential for treatment outcomes in patients with SCI, but this is largely based on exploratory analyses and a very small number of studies. This finding should be viewed as an important but much-needed “research hypothesis” rather than a basis for recommendations for current clinical practice. Future needs are for large-scale, high-quality RCTs validated specifically for this population and outcome.

The 9 studies integrated in this Meta-analysis used a total of 7 different assessment tools (BBS [35], ASIA motor score [35], MMT [35], LEMS [36–38], ROM [39, 40], GRASSP [41, 42], UEMS [43]), and the results all reported a possible positive effect of non-invasive BCI interventions on the motor abilities of patients with spinal cord injury. In the study by Z. Cui et al. [38], seven patients had a mean LEMS score improvement of 2.6 ± 0.8 points after 3 to 7 weeks of BCI intervention, the mean lower extremity muscle recovery was 8.5 times higher than before training, and all AIS A and B patients achieved AIS C at the end of the intervention, which was further confirmed to be a statistically significant effect by the present Meta-analysis. This clinical observation is consistent with the theoretical mechanism that the BCI technique aims to promote the recovery of motor function by inducing neuroplasticity or providing alternative control pathways [44]. After spinal cord injury, the central nervous system will exhibit adaptive reorganization of structure and function (neuroplasticity), and several animal experiments have previously demonstrated that surviving axons can spontaneously form new neural circuits [45, 46]. In contrast, residual corticospinal tract fibers have been shown to be a key factor in spontaneous motor recovery after SCI [47], while a study by R. van den Brand et al. [48] confirmed that active training intentions after SCI will activate corticospinal neurons, which will be significantly more effective than passive guided rehabilitation training. The BCI pushes this principle to a higher level of precision by decoding the intention to move in real time and triggering exoskeleton gait or FES-induced muscle contraction, forming a closed loop of “intention-action-feedback” [49]. It is important to note that the lack of long-term follow-up in all the studies included in this article (8 studies were assessed immediately post-intervention and 1 study had a follow-up at 8 weeks after the intervention) seriously hampers the judgment of the durability of BCI efficacy, and future stratified follow-up studies with multiple time windows are urgently needed to explore the trajectory of BCI efficacy and individualized maintenance regimens. At the same time, only 1 of the 9 studies included in this review reported allocation concealment and blinding, and the remaining studies were at high risk of selection, allocation, and concealment bias, which may lead to subjective outcome exaggeration and systematic overestimation of results. Therefore, the positive effects observed in this review may be partially or entirely driven by bias and need to be interpreted with caution, suggesting a cautious approach to the efficacy of this intervention in clinical decision-making.

2 studies reported the effects of non-invasive BCI on sensory function in patients, confirming the experimental findings of M. A. L. Nicolelis et al. [37]. In their study, four patients improved their pain scores by an average of 4.75 (3.75 in the control group) and their touch scores by 9.5 (4.75 in the control group) after a 14-week BCI combined with an exercise intervention (and only exercise intervention in the control group), and three patients achieved AIS grade C criteria. Although the specific mechanism of BCI intervention is still unclear, the current view is that it may be related to the function of BCI to enhance neuroplasticity and neural pathway reconstruction to compensate for the impaired sensory-motor nerves in the brain [50, 51]. Spinal cord injury disrupts sensory-motor circuits, resulting in impaired sensory function, but this does not mean that the transmission of sensory information is completely disrupted. Recent evidence suggests that even in the most severe complete SCI, sensory information from the skin below the plane of injury can be transmitted via the upstream pathway in 50% of patients, a type known as “Sensory Discomplete”. Although patients themselves do not perceive tactile input from the skin, sensory signals are equally transmitted to sensory areas of the brain, which may be related to residual sensory nerves [52–55]. Meanwhile, the study of Chen et al. [25] concluded that non-invasive BCI intervention allowed the decoded sensory instructions to be delivered to the corresponding neural pathways through spinal cord electrodes or peripheral nerve stimulators to activate the sensory neurons so that the patient could produce sensation, and the somatosensory feedback system transmitted the sensory information back to the brain, forming a closed-loop neurofeedback mechanism, which ultimately restored the sensory-motor function gradually [56].

The 6 studies reported the effect on patients’ ability to perform activities of daily living, and the combined analysis showed significance, which is in line with the study by H. Zhang et al. [57]. The ultimate goal of rehabilitation for SCI patients is to improve their ability to perform daily living tasks, and a study by L. I. Jovanovic et al. [42] reported that after 30 BCI interventions, three out of five patients showed significant improvement, with a mean SCIM self-care score improvement of 7.33 ± 5.73. In a study by S. Shokur et al. [58], after a BCI intervention for up to 28 months in seven patients with SCI, their quality of life level improved from 71.4 points at month 7 to 85.7 points at month 28, while the anxiety and depression domains improved by 10.7 points, and the social and environmental domains did not find any significant improvement. Individuals with SCI tend to experience greater stress, and one study found that the prevalence of mental illness in adults with SCI would be as high as 59.1% compared to healthy adults (30.9%) [59, 60]. Meanwhile, the results of a Meta-analysis showed that the incidence of depression in SCI patients is expected to be 22.2% [61], and the function of the prefrontal cortex of the brain will be limited when individuals are subjected to such a high-pressure situation, which will directly affect the ability to allocate and focus attention, and at the same time, the efficiency of its utilization will also be reduced, which will make it difficult for the patients to sustain the BCI treatment [62, 63]. There is still controversy regarding BCI interventions on psychological domains such as depression and anxiety in patients with SCI [64, 65], and the emergence of psychological problems is a confounding factor that is usually determined by a combination of aspects, which undeniably negatively affects the completion of various tasks such as movement and imagery in patients [66, 67]. It is also important to note that the results will no longer be significant after the use of the random effects model, suggesting that the observed positive results may be magnified by a combination of small sample size, between-study variation, or bias, rather than true efficacy. Meanwhile, the GRADE evidence grading results show a low quality of evidence result for ability to perform daily living, i.e., we have limited confidence in the effect value and the true value is likely to be very different from the estimated value. The reasons for this result may be (1) Different outcome indicators, this paper included the Spinal Cord Injury Independence Measure (SCIM) and the Barthel Index (BI) to assess ADLs, although both indicators can respond well to the ADLs of patients [68, 69], they do not have the same degree of focus, the SCIM is specially designed for spinal cord injury patients to assess their independence in dealing with dysfunction specific to spinal cord injury, while the BI assesses basic activities of daily living. management, while the BI assesses basic activities of daily living (ADL) and is applicable to a wide range of geriatric, chronically ill, neurologically ill, or generally rehabilitated patients. (2) Different experimental designs, the current experimental studies on non-invasive BCI in patients with SCI are still preliminary and exploratory, so different design schemes were used in each study. (3) Different levels of impairment, the 6 included items contained patients with different levels of impairment (AIS A:3, AIS B:44, AIS C:41, AIS D:4), and it will be more difficult to improve ADLs in patients with high cervical spinal cord injuries [70]. All of the above factors may affect the final intervention effect, in the future research need to standardize the BCI experimental design as well as for different levels of damage SCI patients to develop a specialized experimental program, at the same time can explore the psychological factors on the mediating role of the BCI intervention and the specific performance.

Results of subgroup analyses showed that the efficacy of BCI interventions may be better in patients with SCI in the subacute phase than in the chronic phase. The time since injury and the degree of SCI are crucial in the choice of treatment strategy, and a follow-up study found that the period of rehabilitation of postoperative patients was a significant predictor of patients’ long-term motor function [71], and that patients in the chronic phase may have a slightly poorer functional rehabilitation outcome than those in the subacute phase [72], which is in line with the results of a study by R. Rupp [73]. Existing evidence tends to support that the subacute period is a better window for improving comprehensive rehabilitation outcomes in patients with SCI; however, it is prudent to emphasize that this conclusion is an exploratory one based on a small sample size study, and its actual clinical significance may be limited, and it needs to be validated and supported by larger, high-quality studies. It is important to note that the non-invasive BCI can be used as an assistive technique despite the fact that the potential for recovery of motor function may be limited in patients in the chronic phase. In a experiment by M. K. H. Al-Taleb et al. [74], 12 of 15 chronic patients achieved statistically significant pain reduction after BCI intervention. At the same time, this was a home BCI usability study that was finalized with 9 patients reporting high satisfaction (4.5/5), which offers the possibility that future patients will be less dependent on caregivers and long-term care systems [75]. Finally, in evaluating the place of noninvasive BCI in SCI rehabilitation, one potential limitation needs to be recognized: other neuromodulation techniques (e.g., TMS, tDCS, SCS, etc.) have shown potential for symptomatic improvement in both the acute and chronic phases of SCI [76–78]. Therefore, the applicability, evidence base, and individual patient needs of BCI versus other modes of neuromodulation should be considered in clinical decision-making, especially in the non-subacute phase or when BCI is not effective, in order to develop an optimal individualized rehabilitation plan.

Limitations

The following limitations exist in this study: (1) The small number of relevant extant experimental literature, which in turn led to the inclusion of a small sample size in this paper (only 109), suggests that caution must be exercised in interpreting the relevant findings. (2) This Meta-analysis incorporated different study designs (RCT, self-controlled), which will limit the robustness of the conclusions. (3) The quality of the included literature, with only one paper explicitly mentioning allocation concealment and blinding, and 3 studies not mentioning the presence or absence of adverse reactions, will directly affect the quality of this Meta-analysis. (4) The literature included in this paper is all in Chinese or English, which will have some language bias. Therefore, more high-quality RTC studies are needed in the future to validate the reproducibility of this article while follow-up is needed to explore the real benefits and long-term efficacy of non-invasive BCI, and the current issue of BCI interventions for the mental health of patients with SCI is still questionable, and still needs to be improved and validated by subsequent studies.

Conclusion

This study assessed the impact of a non-invasive brain-computer interface intervention on patients with spinal cord injury through quantitative analysis. Preliminary available results suggest that non-invasive BCI may show potential for improvement compared to conventional rehabilitation in motor function, sensory function and activities of daily living ability, with patients in the subacute phase likely to show better improvement than those in the chronic phase.

The above findings need to be interpreted with caution given the differences in study design and level of evidence in the existing literature. Future studies could explore in depth the long-term efficacy of non-invasive BCI interventions and examine the impact of relevant psychological factors on efficacy.
