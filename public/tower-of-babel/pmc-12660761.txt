Strong metal-support interaction of Ru-NPs/WCx boosts alkaline hydrogen evolution and oxidation for alkaline fuel cells and electrolyzers
Authors: Panpan Sun, Run Jiang, Zelong Qiao, Shiqing Huang, Shitao Wang, Dapeng Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDeveloping Pt-free catalysts for alkaline hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) for anion-exchange membrane fuel cells (AEMFCs) is still a big problem. Herein, we report a WCx supported Ru nanoparticles (Ru-NPs/WCx) electrocatalyst. The Ru-NPs/WCx-based H2-air AEMFC not only delivers in the mass activity (MA) (3.13 mgPGM-1@0.65 V) and specific peak power density (2.55 W mgPGM-1) at low total metal loadings, but also exhibits more than 210 h durability. Besides, the Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMWE only needs 1.73 V to reach an industrial current density of 1 A cm-2. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations uncover that the strong interaction of Ru-NPs and WCx support results in the electron-deficient state of Ru-NPs, which simultaneously optimizes the adsorption of intermediates, thus enhancing both HOR and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) performances. In short, designing the support with strong interaction with metal can contribute to the development of durable alkaline HOR electrocatalysts.Subject terms: Fuel cells, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

The sustainable hydrogen cycle has been considered as the foundation of the ultimate hydrogen society1–5. The alkaline AEMWEs and AEMFCs are potentially alternative technologies to the proton exchange membrane water electrolysis and fuel cells towards the renewable hydrogen cycle owing to its accessibility of adopting Pt-free electrocatalysts, possessing prospectively low cost advantages6–18. Unfortunately, the intrinsic kinetics of HER and HOR decreases by about 2 ~ 3 orders of magnitude when switching the electrolyte from acid to base environment19–23, leading to relatively poor performance and much higher Pt loadings. Thus, to achieve the applications of AEMWEs and AEMFCs, exploring high-performance, low-cost and long-term durable Pt-free hydrogen electrocatalysts in alkaline condition is extremely desirable.

Ruthenium (Ru) presents a viable alternative to Pt, ascribed to its relative cheapness (~ 4% cost of Pt)20,24,25 as well as similar catalytic properties. Nevertheless, pure Ru nanoparticles (NPs) possess a much higher hydrogen binding energy (HBE) than Pt24,26, which impedes their HOR/HER catalytic activity. Especially, in HOR, the metallic Ru-based catalysts cannot maintain adequate oxide-free surface for binding with Had intermediates with elevating the anodic potential windows, because it is prone to passivation when the anodic working potential > 0.1 V (vs RHE)27,28. Consequently, the Ru-based anodic electrocatalysts usually suffer from the rapid activity loss when the anode potential window is ~0.3 V (vs. RHE)28–30. The unavoidable surface oxidation of Ru makes it inappropriate for practical application in fuel cells, because the cell polarity reversal, which triggered by H2 starvation, happens inevitably during cold start-up or shut-off processes of the fuel cells6,31,32. Therefore, simultaneously enhancing the activity and stability of Ru-based HOR materials is greatly significant.

In this regard, researchers have proposed various approaches to control and optimize the surface-interface chemistry through engineering the composition, size, surface structure and support to boost the catalytic performance of Ru-based materials for alkaline HOR/ HER19,20,29,33–36. These strategies can actually enhance the activity and durability of the Ru-based electrocatalysts in alkaline HOR/HER. Among them, the substrate engineering is an effective approach owing to their electronic metal-support strong interaction (EMSI) via the electrons transferred between the metal NPs and support20,37, which not only stabilizes the metallic species, but also modulates the electronic structure of active sites to optimize the adsorption of intermediates (such as *H and *OH)27,33,38, and thus improves its catalytic ability. A series of nano-supports such as metal hydroxide38, nitrides24, oxides27,39, phosphides40 and porous carbon materials20, have been used to anchor active Ru sites. The carbon-based materials have been commonly used as the carrier for developing HOR/ HER catalysts ascribed to their advantages of high electrical conductivity and porous structure23. However, these porous carbon supports always suffer from carbon corrosion, because the loading of PGMs can trigger the supported NPs to fall off from the carbon substrates during the reaction41,42, therefore inducing catalyst inactivation. Thus, searching for a more durable substrate is crucial, and may be a viable way to simultaneously promote the catalytic activity and stability for HOR/ HER.

Previous studies indicate that transition metal carbides (TMCs) own the unique physicochemical properties, including high conductivity, metal-like electronic properties, as well as superior chemical corrosion resistance, and have been developed as stable and conductive supports for electrochemical energy conversions43,44. In particular, the tungsten carbide (WCx), a distinct member of TMCs, possesses superior conductivity and robust thermal stability, which endow with versatile applications for diverse catalytic reactions43–46. Therefore, the TMCs may be excellent substrates to anchor Ru NPs via the EMSI.

In this study, we adopt WCx as the support to stabilize Ru NPs and prepare a high-performance Ru-NPs/WCx hydrogen electrocatalyst for both HOR/HER. Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits high HOR performance with MA of 6.5 A mgRu-1 at 50 mV, which is ca. 10-fold of the Pt/C. Using Ru-NPs/WCx as anode, the resulting H2-air AEMFC delivers a high peak power density (PPD) of 0.62 W cm-2, specific PPD (3.13 A mgPGM-1 @ 0.65 V) and MA (2.55 W mgPGM-1), as well as simultaneously exhibits more than 210 h durability. Moreover, Ru-NPs/WCx also presents prominent HER performance. The Ru-NPs/WCx-assembled AEMWE only needs a low cell voltage of 1.73 V to deliver an industrial current density (1.0 A cm-2) and can maintain more than 150 h at 500 mA cm-2 without obvious decay. The high performance of Ru-NPs/WCx is attributed to the strong interaction of Ru and WCx, and their EMSI modulates the surrounding electronic structure of Ru sites, which optimizes the adsorption of intermediates and boosts the alkaline HOR/ HER processes.

Results

Catalyst synthesis and structural characterization

The typical synthesis route of Ru-NPs/WCx is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. Firstly, 5 mmol dopamine hydrochloride (DA) was dispersed in DI water. Then the DA-solution was adjusted to pH~2 by adding 1 M HCl. After that, 0.5 mmol RuCl3 was added into the DA solution and mixed well by agitation under 0 °C to form crimson DA-Ru solution. Subsequently, 0.1 M Na2WO4 solution dropwisely added into the DA-Ru solution with continuous stirring over 4.0 h. The metal-organic compounds with flower-like morphology assembled by nanosheets or flakes DA-Ru-W precursor (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b) were centrifugated, washed and then dried at 60 °C. Finally, the Ru-NPs/WCx catalysts supported on the carbon matrix was acquired by calcinating the DA-Ru-W precursor at 900 °C for 2.0 h with 2 ° min-1 under Ar atmosphere. For comparison, the WCx (Supplementary Fig. 2) and Ru/C (Vulcan XC-72R supported Ru NPs, Supplementary Fig. 3) were also synthesized, respectively. The detailed processes were illustrated in the Supporting Information. As shown in Fig. 1b, Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits good crystallinity of WC (PDF#51-0939) and W2C (PDF#35-0776) phases, consistent with previous study44. No recognizable diffraction peaks of metallic Ru can be found within the detection limits on account of the small size or low content of Ru in the Ru-NPs/WCx (Fig. 1b). To optimize the content of Ru in Ru/WCx catalysts, we further conducted the control experiments by varying the amount of RuCl3 such as 0.25 mmol, 0.5 mmol and 0.75 mmol in the DA-Ru-W precursor synthesis. Experimental details were also provided in the method section. The pyrolyzed products were denoted as Ru-NPs/WCx-1 (0.25 mmol RuCl3), Ru-NPs/WCx (0.5 mmol RuCl3) and Ru-NPs/WCx-2 (0.75 mmol RuCl3) (Supplementary Fig. 4), respectively. ICP-OES results reveal that the amounts of Ru in Ru/WCx are 1.38, 2.88 and 4.92 wt% (Supplementary Table 1) for Ru-NPs/WCx-1, Ru-NPs/WCx and Ru-NPs/WCx-2, respectively. The latter HOR test shows that the Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits the highest HOR activity among three samples (Supplementary Fig. 13). Even after normalizing kinetic current density (jk) at 50 mV based on the Ru mass loading on the electrode, the Ru-NPs/WCx also possesses the highest jk,m (6.5 A mg-1), among the Ru-NPs/WCx-1 (1.2 A mg-1) and Ru-NPs/WCx-2 (0.054 A mg-1) (Supplementary Table 4), indicating that 2.88 wt% Ru amount in the Ru-NPs/WCx was the optimal addition amount. Therefore, the subsequent results were focused on the Ru-NPs/WCx catalyst. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) demonstrates that Ru-NPs/WCx maintains the microspherical morphology of the DA-Ru-W precursor (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Supplementary Fig. 5) and high-angle annular dark-field STEM (HAADF-STEM) (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 6a) images clearly demonstrate that the Ru-NPs/WCx are composed of WCx NPs uniformly distributed on carbon sheets. The enlarged HAADF-STEM and corresponding Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images reveal the Ru NPs spreading on the edge of WCx nanocrystals support (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). As depicted in Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. 6b, the distinct interplanar distance of 0.255 nm and 0.259 nm matches with (100) plane of WC and W2C, respectively, which is in alignment with the findings derived from XRD. Notably, it is unavoidable that some smaller WCx NPs can be also found on the carbon matrix in the Ru-NPs/WCx system, which is similar to the previously reported literatures43. As the individual WCx NPs shows insufficient catalytic activity and durability for the HOR/HER45, the interest in possible active sites for the HOR/HER is mainly focused on the Ru-NPs/WCx. The order lattice fringes with spacing of 0.214 nm ((200) plane) is clearly distinguished on Ru NPs region (Fig. 1e, f). Moreover, the HAADF-STEM and electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) were further conducted to verify the Ru-NPs/WCx at atom resolution (Fig. 1f, g). Figure 1g shows the EELS spectra of Ru-M2, 3 along the line scan for the three positions (from the edge to the interior of WCx nanocrystals) from Ru to WCx. According to the presence or absence of Ru-M2, 3 and Ru-M2,3 signal intensity changes in the three regions, it is found that Ru NPs mainly anchored on the WCx surface and edge, which is in favor of exposing more metal active sites. Furthermore, the corresponding EELS mapping images (Supplementary Fig. 8) confirm that the Ru-NPs are dispersed on the WCx surface. The EDS mapping also distinctively confirms the WCx surface is mainly covered with Ru species (Supplementary Fig. 9). The N2 sorption analysis reveals that the Ru-NPs/WCx catalyst exhibits a high specific surface area (322.1 m2 g-1, Supplementary Table 2) due to the presence of amorphous carbon sheet, which is relatively lower than WCx (362.3 m2 g-1, Supplementary Table 2), owing to the Ru NPs existence. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 10a, Ru-NPs/WCx is mesopore-dominant, as testified by the representative type IV with a H3-type hysteresis loop47 at the relative pressure P/P0 of 0.4 ~ 1.0. Pore size distribution exhibits that more mesoporous structures are mainly distributed at 2 ~ 4 nm, compared with WCx (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Table 2), which is in favor of the electrochemical behavior44,48,49.

To confirm the compositions and surface valence-electron properties of the as-obtained catalysts, the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were carried out. The XPS patterns verify the expected elements of C, Ru, W and O in the Ru-NPs/WCx (Supplementary Fig. 11). Figure 2a shows the Ru 3p spectra of Ru-NPs/WCx and Ru/C, where Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits a positive peak shift of 0.15 eV in the Ru 3p binding energy (BE) of metallic Ru0 compared to Ru/C. This positive peak shift also appears in Ru 3 d spectra (Supplementary Fig. 12), indicating that electron transforms from metallic Ru to the WCx support. The high-resolution W 4 f spectra were studied to detect the oxidation state of W. The two peaks located at 32.0 and 34.2 eV are ascribed to 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 orbital of W-C50, respectively. The other two peaks are centered at 35.8 and 38.0 eV possibly from the inevitable surface oxidation of WCx. Apparently, W 4 f shows a negative-shift in the BE of W-C 4f7/2 peak of ~ 0.10 eV for Ru-NPs/WCx compared to WCx (Fig. 2b), further demonstrating the existence of EMSI in Ru-NPs/WCx. As shown in X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra (Fig. 2c), the absorption edge of Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits an apparent positive shift relative to Ru foil (inset in Fig. 2c), which further confirms the electron transfer from Ru to WCx owing to the strong interaction. Additionally, the Fourier transforms of the extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectra (FT-EXAFS) of Ru-NPs/WCx (Fig. 2d) exhibits a predominant coordination peak located at 2.45 Å corresponding to the Ru-Ru coordination, and the weak peak at 1.50 Å, ascribed to the Ru-C scattering path owing to the interaction between Ru NPs and the WC support (Supplementary Table 3). The coordination environment of Ru in Ru-NPs/WCx can also be verified by the EXAFS wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 2e–g).

It is reported that the lower work function (WF) indicates a higher d-band center and electron-delocalization capability23, which is beneficial for electron transfer from the inside of the catalyst to the surface in the electrochemical process51. Fig. 2h presents the WF described by ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS), where the secondary electron cutoff energy (Ecutoff) exhibits an increase from 17.76 eV for WCx to 18.36 eV for Ru-NPs/WCx. Accordingly, the calculated WF of Ru-NPs/WCx is 3.68 eV, much smaller than that of WCx (4.19 eV, Fig. 2i), suggesting the lower energy barrier of electron transfer from the surface of catalysts to reactant and intermediates. Meanwhile, the valence band maximum position (EVBM) of Ru-NPs/WCx shifts negatively by 0.42 eV than Ru/C (Fig. 2h), suggesting that the d-band center of Ru-NPs/WCx upshifts over Ru/C51. Thus, the above data demonstrate that Ru-NPs/WCx possesses suitable adsorption energies for intermediates (such as H* and OH*), which is in favor of speeding up the Volmer step of HOR/ HER1.

HOR and AEMFCs performance

The catalytic performance of HOR was firstly evaluated by employing a rotating disk electrode in a standard three-electrode configuration in H2-saturated 0.1 M KOH. For comparison, the commercial 20% Pt/C, home-made Ru/C and WCx were also assessed. Figure 3a exhibits the polarization curve of Ru-NPs/WCx recorded under a rotating speed of 1600 rpm, where a significant current response appears over 0 V (vs. reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE)) in H2-saturated alkaline system, suggesting that the anodic current is mainly derived from the H2 oxidation rather than electric double layer charging by comparing with the negligible HOR current in Ar-saturated alkaline electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 14a). Actually, the Ru-NPs/WCx presents a higher HOR activity than other catalysts (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 14b). The current density of Ru-NPs/WCx is 2.46 mA cm-2 at 50 mV, which is much higher than those of Pt/C (1.87 mA cm-2) and Ru/C (1.61 mA cm-2). It is noted that the Ru/C catalyst quickly lost its activity when the anode potential is above ~0.1 V (Fig. 3a). This performance degradation is caused by the oxidation of metallic Ru species in Ru/C, resulting in the disappearance of metallic Ru active sites27, suggesting that Ru/C catalysts tolerate a rather limited anode potential, which would limit the power output of the resulting fuel cells. In contrast, the current density of Ru-NPs/WCx has no obvious drop up to 0.28 V, suggesting that the EMSI in Ru-NPs/WCx possibly plays an important role in boosting the antioxidation capacity of metallic Ru species. Moreover, the reaction kinetics were also studied by employing the HOR polarization curves as a function of the rotating speed from 400 to 2500 rpm, where the limiting current density grows with increase of rotation rate owning to the improved mass transfer (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 15). By fitting the reverse of current density (j-1) at 50 mV and the reciprocal of square root rotation rate (ω-1/2), a straight line for Ru-NPs/WCx was obtained with a slope of 4.03 cm2 mA-1 s-1/2 (Fig. 3c), which well matches with the theoretical value (4.87 cm2 mA-1 s-1/2) for 2e- transfer HOR pathway52. Fig. 3d presents the Tafel plots derived by the Koutecky-Levich formula, where Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits the highest kinetic current density (jk) among four samples. jk of Ru-NPs/WCx reaches 45.6 mA cm-2 at 50 mV (Supplementary Fig. 16), which is 7.0 and 10.6 times of Pt/C (6.5 mA cm-2) and Ru/C (4.3 mA cm-2), respectively. Figure 3e, Supplementary Fig. 16 also show that the Ru-NPs/WCx possesses a higher geometric exchange current density (j0) of 3.36 mA cm-2 than those of Pt/C (2.0 mA cm-2) and Ru/C (1.6 mA cm-2), further verifying high HOR performance of Ru-NPs/WCx induced by the metal-support strong interaction.

After normalizing jk based on the precious metal mass loading on the electrode (jk, m), the Ru-NPs/WCx presents a jk, m of 6.5 A mgRu-1 at 50 mV, which is ca. 10.0- and 15.1-folds of Pt/C (0.65 A mgPt-1) and Ru/C (0.43 A mgRu-1), surpassing almost all the advanced PGM-based HOR electrocatalysts (Supplementary Table 4). Meanwhile, the j0,s (normalized by the electrochemical active surface areas (ECSA)), which is determined by Cu-UPD method (Supplementary Fig. 17) of Ru-NPs/WCx, is 1.5 mA cmECSA-2, larger than that of Pt/C (0.57 mA cmECSA-2) and Ru/C (1.2 mA cmECSA-2) (Supplementary Table 5), further verifying the key role of the catalyst-support strong interaction of Ru-NPs/WCx in boosting the HOR process.

The durability and CO-tolerance ability of Ru-NPs/WCx were further investigated. Figure 3f shows that the Ru-NPs/WCx presents a negligible potential decay after 1000 cycles in comparison with Pt/C (Supplementary Fig. 18a) and Ru/C (Supplementary Fig. 18b). Meanwhile, the inset in Fig. 3f shows that the j0 retention of Ru-NPs/WCx is ca. 98%, while they are only 63% for Pt/C (Supplementary Fig. 18c) and 43% for Ru/C (Supplementary Fig. 18d), suggesting that Ru-NPs/WCx is a long-term stability catalyst for HOR. After 20000 s continuous test at 100 mV (Supplementary Fig. 19), the Ru-NPs/WCx can still retain ca. 80% of its original current density, which is much higher than that of Pt/C (60%) and Ru/C (26.5%). By increasing the Ru-NPs/WCx loading amount to 80 μg on carbon paper, Ru-NPs/WCx maintains 25 h durability test with only ~11% activity loss, far surpassing those of Pt/C and Ru/C (Fig. 3g), which may be attributed to the metal-support strong interaction in Ru-NPs/WCx, effectively inhibiting the detachment or oxidation of Ru NPs during durability tests. Moreover, the Ru-NPs/WCx delivers much higher CO tolerance than Pt/C and Ru/C during the HOR (Fig. 3h), and the limiting current density of Ru-NPs/WCx after introducing 1000 ppm CO only presents a slight decrease, demonstrating its good tolerance and resistance to CO. The current density of Ru-NPs/WCx still maintains ~ 94.2% at 100 mV after 2000 s with 1000 ppm CO impurity (Supplementary Fig. 20), while the Ru/C only maintains ~85% under the same test conditions. All above results imply that the Ru-NPs/WCx possesses high HOR performance including high activity, long-term stability as well as CO-tolerance capability, which could be intrinsically related to the metal-support strong interaction between Ru NPs and WCx support.

The high HOR mass activity of Ru-NPs/WCx encourages us to further assemble AEMFC with a low loading of Ru. Figure 4 displays the performance of AEMFC with Ru-NPs/WCx (0.043 mgRu cm-2) as anode and a commercial Pt/C (0.2 mgPt cm-2) as cathode. The open circuit of Ru-NPs/WCx reaches up to 1.04 V in both H2-O2 and H2-Air (CO2-free), respectively. And the Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMFC not only delivers a high PPD of 0.75 W cm-2 in H2-O2 and 0.62 W cm-2 in H2-Air (CO2-free) (Fig. 4a), but also presents a high current density of 1.0 A cm-2 in H2-O2 and 0.76 A cm-2 in H2-Air (CO2-free) at 0.65 V (typical operating potential for automotive applications), which are comparable with the high-loading commercial PtRu/C (0.1 mgPtRu cm-2) and Pt/C (0.1 mgPt cm-2) anode AEMFC and even better at higher operating potentials (Supplementary Figs. 21, 22), again verifying its high HOR activity of Ru-NPs/WCx in practical application. Even after seven consecutive tests, the AEMFC performances remain unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 23). The MA and specific PPD normalized by the total platinum group metal (PGM) mass are 4.12 A mgPGM-1 (Supplementary Fig. 24a) and 3.09 W mgPGM-1 (Supplementary Fig. 24b) in H2-O2 system, and they are 3.13 A mgPGM-1 (Supplementary Fig. 24c) and 2.55 W mgPGM-1 in H2-Air (CO2-free) condition (Supplementary Fig. 24d), respectively, indicating that the Ru-NPs/WCx as anode catalysts in AEMFC is competitive (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 24, Table 6). In particular, the MA and specific PPD of Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMFC are about 1.8 or 1.7 times of recently reported Ru-CrOx@CN catalyst in both H2-O2 and H2-Air (CO2-free) system1, suggesting a great potential for practical application of Ru-NPs/WCx in AEMFCs.

The stability of Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMFC was also evaluated in H2-Air (CO2-free) at 250 mA cm-2 and H2-O2 at 400 mA cm-2, respectively. Figure 4b shows that the Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMFC delivers the high stability with only 0.9 mV h-1 voltage decay rate after continuously operating over 210 h, which is a competitive performance with such a low anode loading (0.043 mgRu cm-2, Supplementary Table 6). On the contrary, Ru/C shows fast declines (voltage decay rate: 8.5 mV h-1) after continuous operation for only 20 h. The good durability of Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMFCs was also achieved in the H2-O2 condition and it runs for 100 h at 400 mA cm-2 (Supplementary Fig. 25).

To further confirm the durability of Ru-NPs/WCx, the structural composition, morphology and surface chemical states of Ru-NPs/WCx after AEMFC durability tests were analyzed by XRD, TEM and XPS. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 26, XRD and TEM patterns uncover that the composition and morphology still remain intact without apparent changes after durability tests. Moreover, the Ru 3p spectra of Ru-NPs/WCx before and after fuel cell measurement confirm its good surface chemical stability during the continuous AEMFC operation (Supplementary Fig. 27). Meanwhile, the W 4 f spectra show that the peak strength of the W6+ (the surface passivating layer forming WOx) keep almost unchanged after AEMFC durability test due to strong anti-oxidation of WCx substrates, indicating that the better oxidation-resistance behavior of WCx makes Ru-NPs/WC catalyst have good durability in AEMFC test. Furthermore, the Ru-NPs/WCx also maintains most AEMFC performance under H2/100 ppm CO feeding even the anode with lower Ru loading of 0.03 mgRu cm-2 (Fig. 4c). The AEMFC can still deliver a current density of 739 mA cm-2 at 0.65 V and a PPD of 490 mW cm-2 (506 mW cm-2 as before), respectively, suggesting the advantage of Ru-NPs/WCx in the AEMFC working condition employing crude H2 feedstock. All the above-mentioned results demonstrate that the Ru-NPs/WCx material presents good HOR activity and long-term stability as well CO-tolerance in AEMFCs.

HER and AEMWE performance

Besides high HOR performance, the Ru-NPs/WCx, as a bifunctional catalyst, also presents good alkaline HER performance (Fig. 5). Among four samples, the Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits a higher HER activity with an overpotential of 16 mV (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Fig. 28a) to reach the current density of 10 mA cm-2, which surpasses Pt/C (65 mV), Ru/C (96 mV) and WCx (172 mV). Moreover, the MA of Ru-NPs/WCx reaches 18.8 A mg Ru-1 (Fig. 5c) at the overpotential of 100 mV, which is 10.9 and 17.8 times of Pt/C (1.73 A mg Pt-1) and Ru/C (1.06 A mg Ru-1), respectively, demonstrating its high HER activity. The Ru-NPs/WCx deservedly ranks on the list of state-of-the-art PGM-based electrocatalysts in terms of overpotential and MA (Supplementary Table 7). The Tafel slope of Ru-NPs/WCx is only 31.1 mV dec-1 (Fig. 5d), obviously smaller than Pt/C (83.6 mV dec-1), Ru/C (85.1 mV dec-1) and WCx (88.9 mV dec-1), implying the faster reaction dynamics of Ru-NPs/WCx in alkaline media46. To evaluate the HER specific activity of the samples, we studied the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) employing the Cu underpotential stripping deposition (Cuupd) method in Supplementary Fig. 17. The Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits HER specific activity of 0.15 mA cmECSA-2 at 100 mV overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 28b), which is about 2.1 and 7.5 times higher than that of Pt/C (0.07 mA cmECSA-2) and Ru/C (0.02 mA cmECSA-2), respectively, indicating intrinsically enhanced HER activities on the Ru-NPs/WCx catalyst due to metal-support strong interaction between Ru NPs and WCx support. The Ru-NPs/WCx delivers a high turnover frequency (TOF) of 21.03 s-1 at an overpotential of 20 mV (Fig. 5f), which is 10.1 times of Ru/C (2.09 s-1), indicating its high HER intrinsic activity. The Faradaic efficiency of Ru-NPs/WCx is approximately 100% (Supplementary Fig. 29), confirming high HER selectivity. Moreover, no obvious overpotential increase can be observed for Ru-NPs/WCx after continuous chronopotentiometry measurement at current density of 100 mA cm-2 for 20 h (Supplementary Fig. 30). Apparently, Pt/C and Ru/C catalysts exhibit visible increase of overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 30) under the same condition, indicating that the Ru-NPs/WCx possesses not only good HER activity but also long-term stability. To further assess the application potential of Ru-NPs/WCx as cathode for electrolyzing water, we also synthesized NiFe-LDH (Supplementary Fig. 31) as anode to assemble the AEMWE. Figure 5g shows that the Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMWE only requires cell voltages of 1.73 and 1.93 V to reach the industrial-scale current density of 1.0 and 2.0 A cm-2, respectively, which is much lower than Pt/C | | NiFe-LDH system (1.8 V @ 1.0 A cm-2 and 2.1 V @ 2.0 A cm-2). Importantly, the AEMWE device with a low Ru-NPs/WCx loading of 0.043 mgRu cm-2 presents high MA of 44.0 A mgRu-1 at working potential of 1.8 V, about 11-folds higher than that of Pt/C (inset in Fig. 5h). Moreover, the Ru-NPs/WCx || NiFe-LDH system also presents a prominent durability with over 150 h continuous operation at a current density of 500 mA cm-2 (Fig. 5h). Compared with recently reported PGM-based materials (Supplementary Table 8), the Ru-NPs/WCx presents comprehensive advantages for AEMWE from the aspects of cell voltage, MA and durability, verifying its potential for applying in AEMWE for green H2 production.

Mechanism insights

To uncover the intrinsic catalytic mechanism of Ru-NPs/WCx for HOR/HER, a series of experiments and theoretical calculations were conducted. The operando electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) plots illustrate that Ru-NPs/WCx has a smaller semicircle than that of Pt/C under the same voltages from 0 to -150 mV (Supplementary Fig. 32, Table 9), which indicates the rapid electro/proton transfers of Ru-NPs/WCx in the HOR/HER processes45. Compared with Pt/C, the rapid decrease of Bode phase plots of Ru-NPs/WCx in the low-frequency regions (Fig. 6a, b) also proves the accelerated charge transfer at the catalyst interface, motivating the reaction dynamics of HOR/ HER. Figure 6c shows that the underpotential deposited hydrogen (Hupd) peak of Ru-NPs/WCx (~0.128 V) has a slight negative shift than Ru/C ( ~ 0.134 V), meaning a weaker HBE for Ru-NPs/WCx. Due to the strong interaction between Ru NPs and WCx substrate, the Ru-H bond energy is reduced. To further probe OH adsorption behavior on the catalyst surface, the CO-stripping voltammetry measurement were also carried out, and the CO stripping peak of Ru-NPs/WCx negatively shifts to 0.593 V (Fig. 6d), compared to Ru/C (0.688 V), which means that the order of OHBE is Ru-NPs/WCx > Ru/C. This is also in accordance with the results of zeta potential tests (Supplementary Fig. 33), i.e., the potential of Ru-NPs/WCx is much more negatively (-24.2 mV) than that of Ru/C (-5.58 mV), which refers to the increase of negative charge on the surface, implying the strengthened adsorption of OH species53. Additionally, the adsorption ability of CO was further monitored by CO-TPD. Accordance with the CO-stripping voltammetry experiment and zeta potential, the CO desorption peak of Ru-NPs/WCx (454 °C) exhibits slightly lower temperature than Ru/C (460 °C) (Supplementary Fig. 34), suggesting the weakened CO adsorption on Ru-NPs/WCx27, which explains the reason why Ru-NPs/WCx has a better tolerance and resistance to CO in the RDE (Fig. 3h) and AEMFC tests (Fig. 4e). Above results demonstrate that the weakened HBE and enhanced OHBE on Ru-NPs/WCx catalyst surface optimize the adsorption Had and OHad intermediates, which therefore boosts the HOR/ HER performance in alkaline condition.

DFT calculations were further used to reveal the origin of high performance of Ru-NPs/WCx for alkaline HOR/HER. Based on the experimental results (Fig. 1c), we constructed the Ru crystal surface model (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 35) and Ru-NPs/WCx-M interface models (Supplementary Fig. 36–43, Supplementary data 1). Previous investigations have demonstrated that both H and OH adsorption can effectively influence the alkaline HOR/HER activities. Therefore, we simultaneously considered two commonly existing mechanisms, the HBE (*H binding energy) and the OHBE mechanisms (*OH binding energy, also known as the bifunctional mechanism). The HBE mechanism only considers the dissociation of H2 on the catalyst surface to form *H, which then combines with OH- in the solution to generate H2O (Supplementary Fig. 44,blue arrow process), while the OHBE mechanism suggests that OH- species also need to be adsorbed on the catalyst surface to form *OH, then *H and *OH combine on the catalyst surface to form H2O (Supplementary Fig. 37, green arrow process). The two mechanisms usually co-exist in alkaline HOR/ HER processes, so we calculated both their free energy diagrams. Among all Ru-NPs/WCx-M configurations, Ru-NPs/WCx (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 45) exhibits the most significant HOR performance. Therefore, we believe that this configuration is the main active structure in Ru-NPs/WCx. Figure 6g, h and Supplementary Table 12 show that the potential-determining step (PDS) of HBE mechanism is *H + OH- → * + H2O for both Ru and Ru-NPs/WCx catalysts, while the PDS of OHBE mechanism is *H + *OH → 2* + H2O. Thus, for Ru and Ru-NPs/WCx catalysts, the generation of H2O is the key step in both HBE and OHBE mechanisms. Figure 6i shows that the Ru-NPs/WCx surface has a significantly weaker *H adsorption capability than the Ru surface, and adsorption energy of *H increases to –0.106 eV (Ru-NPs/WCx) from –0.552 eV (Ru); while the Ru-NPs/WCx surface also has stronger *OH adsorption capability, and adsorption energy of *OH decreases to 0.050 eV (Ru-NPs/WCx) from 0.138 eV (Ru). Obviously, the introduction of WC efficiently optimizes the HOR/HER processes, leading to favorable alkaline HOR/HER activities via speeded up the Volmer kinetics, which caused the ηHBE and ηOHBE decrease to 0.106 V and 0.056 V from 0.552 V (Ru-NPs/WCx, Fig. 6j, Supplementary Table 12) and 0.414 V (Ru, Fig. 6j, Supplementary Table 12), respectively.

Furthermore, we also calculated the electron structure of Ru and Ru-NPs/WCx to disclose the intrinsic mechanism of the activity enhancement. The charge density difference diagram of Ru-NPs/WCx (Fig. 6k) shows that WCx will attract the electrons of Ru, thereby increasing the oxidation state of Ru, resulting in weakened adsorption strength for positively charged H, which is also in line with the HUPD (Fig. 6c) and XPS results (Fig. 2a). The COHP (Crystal Orbital Hamilton Population) diagram intuitively shows that the both energy levels, and the peak area of the bonding orbitals of H-Ru-NPs/WCx is significantly smaller than that of H-Ru, and the anti-bonding orbital energy level of H-Ru-NPs/WCx is lower than that of H-Ru, indicating that the electron withdrawing effect of WCx pulls more anti-bonding electrons below the Fermi level54 when Ru-NPs/WCx bonds with *H. The decrease in the peak area of bonding orbitals and the increase in electron filling of antibonding orbitals are the origins of the weakening of Ru-NPs/WCx for *H adsorption. The ICOHP (integral COHP) data also quantitatively confirmed this result. The lower value of -ICOHP generally represents the lower bonding strength55–57. The *H peak area of H-Ru bonding orbital significantly reduced to 0.435 from 0.720 (Fig. 6l). In addition, the electron withdrawing effect of WCx leads to the increase in the valence of Ru, which is the origin of the enhanced adsorption of negatively charged OH species, consistent with the CO-stripping results (Fig. 6d). Overall, the electron withdrawing effect of WCx reduces the electron density of Ru in Ru-NPs/WCx, increasing its valence state, weakening *H adsorption and enhancing *OH adsorption. The weakening of H adsorption simultaneously boosts the HOR/ HER performance via both HBE and OHBE pathways, while the enhancement of *OH allows more reactants to proceed along OHBE pathway, further improving its HOR performance.

Discussion

In summary, we have successfully prepared the Ru-NPs/WCx electrocatalysts with the metal-support strong interaction for both alkaline HOR and HER. Ru-NPs/WCx exhibits mass activity about 10 times of Pt/C at 50 mV in alkaline HOR and only needs overpotential of 16 mV at 10 mA cm-2 in alkaline HER. The Ru-NPs/WCx-assembled H2-air (CO2-free) AEMFC not only delivers a high mass activity (3.13 mgPGM-1@0.65 V) and specific peak power density (2.55 W mgPGM-1), but also maintains more than 210 h durability. Moreover, the Ru-NPs/WCx-based AEMWE only needs 1.73 V to reach the Ampere-level current density (1 A cm-2), and can run more than 150 h at 500 mA cm-2 with negligible degradation. The good performance of Ru-NPs/WCx is rooted in the strong interaction of Ru NPs and the WCx support, where the electron transfer from Ru NPs to WCx efficiently modulates the local environment of Ru sites, and optimize the adsorption of intermediates, therefore boosting the HOR and HER processes. In short, designing the support with strong interaction with metal is the key to developing durable electrocatalysts for different reactions, including alkaline HOR/HER and acidic oxygen reduction/evolution reactions.

Methods

Chemicals

All chemicals were used as received. Ruthenium trichloride (RuCl3, Ru ≥ 37%), Dopamine hydrochloride (DA, ≥ 98%), sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O, ≥ 98%), isopropanol (C3H8O, ≥ 99.7%), ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, ≥ 99%), urea (CO(NH2)2, ≥ 99%) and potassium hydroxide (KOH, 99.99 %) were obtained from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.0 ~ 38.0%) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O, ≥ 98%) and Nafion D521 ( ≈ 5%) were purchased from Thermo Scientific. Commercial Pt/C (20 wt% or 40 wt% Pt) were received from Johnson Matthey. Gas diffusion layers (SGL 29BC) were obtained from Suzhou Sinero Technology Co., Ltd. The anion exchange membrane (PiperION™ A20) and the ionomer were bought from Versogen. All aqueous solutions were prepared with DI water produced from Millipore purification system.

Synthesis of Ru-NPs/WCx and WCx

5 mmol (0.948 g) dopamine hydrochloride (DA) was dissolved in 50 m L DI water. Then the dopamine solution was adjusted to pH~2 by 1 M HCl. After that, 0.5 mmol (103.7 mg) RuCl3 was added into the DA solution and mixed well by stirring under 0 °C ice-water mixture. Subsequently, 0.l M Na2WO4·2H2O was dropped slowly into the DA-Ru solution and then stirred for 4.0 h. The precipitation was then collected and dried overnight. Then the DA-W-Ru precursor was pyrolyzed at 900 °C for 2.0 h with the heating rate at 2 °C min-1 under Ar atmosphere. For comparison, WCx was also synthesized without the addition of RuCl3.

Synthesis of Ru-NPs/WCx-1 and Ru-NPs/WCx-2

Ru-NPs/WCx-1 and Ru-NPs/WCx-2 were obtained by the same method as Ru-NPs/WCx catalyst, except employing amount of 0.25 mmol and 0.75 mmol RuCl3 in the DA-W-Ru precursor.

Synthesis of Ru/C

90 mg of carbon black (Vulcan XC-72R) was added into 40 mL absolute ethanol under constant stirring and ultrasonic dispersion for 1.0 h. Then 10 mL 1 M RuCl3 ethanol solution was dropwisely added into the carbon black solution under continuously stirring for 3.0 h. After that the solution was then maintained at 80 °C and put on a heating plate. Finally, the products were pyrolyzed at 800 °C for 2.0 h under Ar atmosphere.

Synthesis of NiFe-LDH

Ni(NO3)2·6H2O (0.2990 g), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (0.1385 g) and CO(NH2)2 (0.1287 g) were dissolved in 15 mL DI. Subsequently, the mixed solution and Ni foam (2.25 × 3.0 cm2) were transferred to Teflon autoclave and then heated at 120 °C for 12 h to obtain NiFe layered double hydroxide nanosheets on NF, named as NiFe-LDH.

Structural characterization

The XRD patterns were characterized by X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV). The SEM was performed with JEOL JMS-6700F. The TEM images were recorded by FEI Talos F200X with an electron acceleration energy of 200 kV. The atomic structures of the catalysts were characterized using an ARM-200CF (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV and equipped with double spherical aberration (Cs) correctors. XPS measurements were conducted on Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer using Al Ka irradiation. The inductively coupled plasma emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) measurement was conducted out on a Aglient-5110. N2 sorption isotherms were measured by the HD88 system (Quantachrome Instruments) at 77 K and the porosity parameters were calculated using the software QuadraWin (version 6.0). CO-TPD measurements were carried out Bel Cata II+ BelMass. The ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) analysis were conducted on ThermoFisher Nexsa ESCALAB 250Xi spectrophotometer. The XAFS spectra at Ru K-edge were obtained under ambient conditions in transmission mode at beamline BL14W1 of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). Data analysis for XAFS were analyzed by Athena and Artemis software.

Electrochemical measurements

All the electrochemical measurements were performed in a three-electrode system using a CHI760e electrochemical workstation (Shanghai Chenhua Co. Ltd.) in H2-saturated 0.1 M KOH electrolyte (pH = 13.0 ± 0.01). The fresh 0.1 M electrolyte is prepared by dissolving 2.805 g of KOH (99.99%) in 500 mL of volumetric flask. The electrolyte was prepared 1.0 h before the test stored at room temperature, and protected from light. A rotation disk electrode (RDE) with a glassy carbon (GC) disk of 5 mm in diameter was employed as working electrodes, the saturated calomel electrode (SCE) and a graphite rod were used as reference and counter electrodes, respectively. 5 mg of the catalysts and 2.5 mg Vulcan XC-72 were dispersed in 150 μL DI, 300 μL absolute ethanol and 50 μL Nafion solution (5 wt%) by sonication for at least 0.5 h to form a homogeneous catalyst ink. Then the well-dispersed ink was dipped onto the glassy carbon electrode (GCE has been polished to a smooth surface with 0.05 µm γ-Al2O3) and dried at air to form a catalyst film with a loading of 7 μg cm-2 (determined by ICP-OES). Cycle voltammetry (CV) was texted in 0.1 M KOH saturated with Ar at a scan rate of 50 mV s-1. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were conducted in H2-saturated 0.1 M KOH at a rotating speed of 1600 rpm with a sweep speed of 2 mV s-1 and an 100% iR correction. HER tests were conducted in a 1.0 M KOH aqueous with a rotating speed of 1600 rpm with a sweep speed of 5 mV s-1 and an 85% iR correction. The EIS was measured at 0.0 V, -0.025 V, -0.05 V, -0.075 V, -0.10 V, -0.125 V and -0.15 V with an amplitude voltage of 5 mV in a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz. The long-term stability was obtained by chronoamperometry tests were performed without iR compensation. The Faradaic efficiency was determined by gathering H2 with employed 4 cm×4 cm PiperION™ A20 (Membrane thickness: 20 μm) membrane at a constant current of 50 mA cm-2 with area of 1.0 cm2 (25 °C, 1 atm). The zero point of the RHE was determined by the equilibrium potential of HOR/HER of 20% Pt/C catalyst in H2-saturated electrolyte. Anion exchange membrane fuel cell (AEMFC) tests

In AEMFCs, PiperION™ A20 (Membrane thickness: 20 μm) and PiperionION A5 were applied as the anion-exchange membrane and ionomer, respectively. The SGL-29 BC was used as gas diffusion layer. Ru-NPs/WCx was used as anode catalyst and commercial Pt/C (40 wt%, Johnson Matthey) as cathode catalyst. The catalyst ink was prepared by ultrasonically dispersing the catalysts and PiperionION A5 (5 wt% in ethanol) into water and isopropanol (1:25 v/v) for 2.0 h. Then the anode catalyst ink and cathode catalyst were sprayed onto the two sides of PiperION™ A20 membrane to fabricate a catalyst-coated membrane (CCM) with the electrode area of 5 cm2. The CCM was immersed into 3 M KOH solution for 2.0 h and then rinsed thoroughly with DI to remove all excess KOH. The MEA was measured by a fuel cell test station (Scribner 850e) equipped with a backpressure module. The anode, cathode humidifier temperature and cell temperature were 80 °C. Both anode and cathode relative humidity were 100%. The back pressure was set as 200 kPa. The flow rates of H2-O2/ Air (CO2-free) were 1.0 and 1.5 L min-1, respectively. All MEA measurements were obtained without the iR-compensation.

Anion exchange membrane water electrolyzers (AEMWE) tests

The AEMWE (Suzhou Sinero Co., LTD) was assembled with an anode (2.25 cm) and cathode (2.25 cm), and anion exchange membrane (AEM) PiperION™ A80 (80 μm). Ru-NPs/WCx or commercial Pt/C (40 wt%) ink is sprayed onto one side of the AEM, in which the noble metal loading mass of catalyst was 0.043 mgRu cm-2 and 0.5 mgPt cm-2, respectively. Then the obtained membrane electrode was immersed into 3 M KOH solution for 2.0 h before use. The home-made self-supporting NiFe-LDH was employed anode electrocatalyst. Ti felt was applied as a cathode porous transport layer (PTL). Finally, NiFe-LDH || PiperION™ A80 || cathode electrocatalysts || PTL was assembled with the bipolar plates using a torque wrench 5 Nm to preparing AEMWE single cell. The AEMWE measurement was operated at 80 °C with peristaltic pump circulating 1.0 M KOH at a flow rate of 5 mL min-1. I-V curves were collected from 1.23 to 2.2 V at a scan rate of 5 mV s-1. The durability test was assessed via chronopotentiometry at a current density of 500 mA cm-2. All electrolyzer measurements were obtained without the iR-compensation.

DFT calculations

The calculations based on the first principles under the framework of spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT), using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) code58. The Generalized gradient approximation (GGA) method with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE)59 was adopted as the exchange-correlation functional. The projection enhanced wave (PAW) pseudopotential60 was used to describe ionic nuclei. The plane-wave cut-off energy was set to 500 eV. The convergence threshold of iteration in self-consistent field (SCF) was set to 10–5 eV per atom for energy and 0.02 eV Å–1 for the force. The Becke-Jonson damping (DFT-D3) method61,62 was used for weak vdW corrections. A vacuum slab of more than 20 Å was inserted in the z direction for all models to avoid the influence of the interaction between adjacent periodic units. The Lobster software63 was used to perform the COHP analysis64, and obtain the bonding and anti-bonding information. A Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid was used to sample the Brillouin zone, detailed values are shown in Supplementary Table 13. VASP-sol package was used to simulate the solvent environment, where the dielectric constant was set to 80. The computational hydrogen electrode model proposed by Nørskov et al. 65. was employed to calculate the activity of the catalyst.
