Key factors influencing retention in care among people living with HIV in Turkey: a 5-year retrospective analysis
Authors: Gursel Ersan, Emel Erogul, Firat Servan Fidan, Ilker Odemis, Sabri Atalay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to identify the factors influencing retention in care among individuals living with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), with the goal of emphasising improved treatment compliance and health outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective, single-centre study included 300 individuals living with HIV who had been followed for at least two years. Patients who adhered to their follow-ups at three-month intervals for two years after their initial appointment and subsequently attended follow-ups at least twice a year for at least six months thereafter were considered to have “retention in care”. We evaluated the effects of several factors, including age, sex, time to the first visit after diagnosis, use of telehealth services, daily pill count, dose frequency, and the time interval between the first visit and the commencement of treatment, on follow-up outcomes.ResultsThe study population (mean age of 36.8 ± 11.91 years) included 269 (89.7%) males. Overall, there were 258 cases (86%) in which the time between diagnosis confirmation and the first visit was less than one month. Moreover, 227 individuals (75.6%) retained in care. The utilisation of telehealth services had a significant effect on follow-up consistency (p < 0.001). Age, sex, time to the first visit after diagnosis, daily pill count, dose frequency, and the time interval to treatment initiation did not affect retention in care.ConclusionsTelehealth services have shown a substantial correlation with retention in HIV care and should be integrated into care models for better patient outcomes.Keywords: HIV linkage, HIV treatment adherence, HIV care

Background

Currently, HIV infection has become a chronic and manageable disease [1]. People living with HIV need to be referred to a treatment centre as soon as possible (known as linkage to care). Linkage can be achieved in two ways. In active linkage, an appointment is scheduled at the treatment centre, after which an individual authorised in case management is notified, and contact is maintained with the diagnosed individual until the linkage is completed. In passive linkage, the newly diagnosed person is asked to make an appointment, with only the name and contact information of the treatment centre being provided. Compared with passive linkages, active linkages are more effective at establishing care connections. An initial appointment that exceeds one month after diagnosis is considered to be a delayed linkage [2–4]. Factors contributing to a delayed first appointment may include refusal to accept the disease, lack of social security, long appointment times, workload, psychological distress, and substance use. Stigma, immigration, and language barriers are also important factors [5–10].

There are two fundamental concepts related to HIV treatment management: retention in care and adherence to treatment. However, these concepts are often misinterpreted. Retention in care refers to the individual living with HIV who attends scheduled appointments within the healthcare system after diagnosis and who continues with the follow-up and care process. Conversely, treatment adherence refers to the consistent use of antiretroviral therapy at the recommended dose and the suppression of HIV RNA levels. Overall, retention in care refers to maintaining the patient in the system, whereas treatment adherence refers to the patient’s proper use of their medication [11].

The factors that hinder treatment adherence are very similar to those that impede retention in care. Specifically, a person’s age, occupation, social environment, housing situation, and length of treatment can influence retention in care. The structure of the treatment centre is also an essential factor. In centres where institutional identity is prominent and the entire staff (including both physicians and nonphysicians) collectively work together, the percentage of patients remaining in treatment is greater. Moreover, the relationships that centres establish with individuals living with HIV are required to consistently prioritise a problem-solving approach and encourage constructive interaction. The appropriateness of the antiretroviral therapy (ART) regimen also facilitates retention in care. Adherence to both treatment and follow-up has been observed to be more difficult for young people and those who have been prescribed medications for a long period of time, thereby resulting in a loss of motivation [5–10].

Consistent HIV care is a central component of effective long-term management for people living with HIV. This adherence is crucial for achieving optimal health outcomes and enhancing overall well-being. An experienced centre is required to manage treatment with follow-up investigations, which include the determination of any interruptions in ART use, the performance of laboratory monitoring, and the evaluation of the individual’s physical, mental, and social status at each visit [12].

Consistent follow-ups for individuals living with HIV contribute to increased treatment adherence, which subsequently contributes to sustainable viral suppression, a reduced risk of HIV transmission, a decreased likelihood of drug resistance, improved quality of life, and enhanced overall health [13]. The American HIV guidelines recommend simplifying the regimen by reducing drug load and/or dose frequency, as well as selecting molecules that are well tolerated and demonstrate low long-term toxicity; focusing attention on drug-drug interactions and the need for concomitant food intake; selecting appropriate ART during pregnancy or when pregnancy is desired; using donor drugs; and implementing steps to reduce costs [14].

In our country, as in many other countries, retention in care continues to pose challenges despite major improvements in HIV diagnosis and treatment infrastructure. People’s situations and requirements can change considerably over time. Understanding the determinants is essential for informing programmatic strategies and optimising national HIV responses. There are various ways to measure retention, including those based on attended visits over a defined period and those based on missed visits. Both approaches are valid and independently predict survival. Constancy measures (e.g., at least two visits that are at least 90 days apart over 1 year, or at least one visit every 6 months over the last 2 years) can be used as clinic quality assurance measures (12–14).

Although there is an increasing amount of international evidence regarding linkage to care, adherence, and retention in HIV treatment, there is a lack of data from Turkey that specifically examines the factors affecting retention in care. Moreover, there has been no investigation on the impact of telehealth services on retention in HIV care in this setting. Addressing these gaps is vital for improving national HIV programs and service delivery in middle-income countries, which are trying to boost their HIV care systems.

This study aimed to determine the factors that affect retention in care among people living with HIV in Turkey.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective, single-centre study involved 300 patients living with HIV who visited the Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology outpatient clinic from April 2018 to April 2023.

Study population and eligibility criteria

A total of 300 adults living with HIV were included. Participants were eligible if they were aged ≥ 18 years, initiated ART at our centre, and had at least two years of follow-up after their first visit. Exclusion criteria included follow-up duration < 2 years, lack of data in electronic medical records, death, and transfer to another facility.

Definitions

Delayed presentation: Patients with first appointments that were more than one month overdue were classified as having “delayed presentation”.

Retention in care: Individuals who returned for follow-up at three-month intervals for two years following their initial appointment and subsequently at least twice a year for a minimum of six-month intervals thereafter were considered to achieve “retention in care”.

Telehealth system

Telehealth system was offered to patients who had maintained suppressed viral loads after their first year of follow-up. The primary purpose of the telehealth service was to manage prescriptions between two in-person appointments, in which physical examinations and laboratory tests were conducted. Healthcare providers ensured that remote consultations were not consecutively scheduled.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we examined the factors that may influence retention in care among individuals living with HIV via logistic regression analysis. The identified dependent variable included regular follow-up, whereas the independent variables included sex, age, time elapsed until the first visit following diagnosis, utilisation of telehealth services, daily pill counts, frequency of daily dosing, and duration until treatment commencement after the initial visit.

The results were analysed by using a 95% confidence interval (CI), with a significance threshold established at p < 0.05. The data were analysed by using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27).

Results

The characteristics of the study population

The study population included 300 patients, with a mean age of 36.8 ± 11.91 years being reported. Among the total population, 269 male patients (89.7%) were observed. A total of 258 cases (86%) demonstrated an interval of less than one month between the confirmation of the diagnosis and the scheduling of the first appointment. Currently, 227 patients (75.6%) are actively undergoing regular follow-ups and monitoring.

Evaluation of follow-up patterns and underlying causes of irregular attendance

In a review of 73 patients with irregular follow-up, we identified several factors that led patients to stop attending the follow-up. Specifically, the most common reason included the lack of general health insurance, which affected 24.6% of the participants (n = 18). Many individuals also struggled with mental health issues, with 21.9% of the participants (n = 16) reporting depression and other psychological disorders. Another significant factor involved a low degree of education, which affected 19.1% of the participants (n = 4), thereby making it more challenging for some individuals to manage their healthcare issues. Additionally, 13.7% of the participants (n = 10) were unable to accept their diagnosis, which affected their willingness to seek care. Side effects from antiretroviral therapy (ART) occurred in 6.8% of the patients (n = 5), whereas 5.5% (n = 4) reported issues related to substance use. Work commitments also played a role, with 2.8% of the participants (n = 2) reporting that their jobs limited their ability to attend follow-ups. Legal issues were reported by 2.8% of the participants (n = 2), thus indicating that some individuals experienced barriers due to legal convictions. Finally, homelessness affected 1.4% of the participants (n = 1), and another 1.4% (n = 1) identified as immigrants, thereby demonstrating how various challenges can prevent consistent healthcare engagement (Fig. 1).

Retention in care based on demographic factors

Overall, 77.3% (n = 208) of men and 61.3% (n = 19) of women participated in regular follow-up. Among the included patients, 37% (n = 111) were individuals aged 30 years or younger, 47.3% (n = 142) were between 30 and 50 years of age, and 15.7% (n = 47) were 50 years or older. The rates of attending regular follow-ups according to age groups were 75.6% (n = 84) for those 30 years or younger, 73.2% (n = 104) for those between 30 and 50 years, and 85.1% (n = 40) for those 50 years or older. Neither sex nor age had a significant effect on regularly attending follow-up appointments (p = 0.208 and p = 0.868, respectively).

Effect of the timing of initial presentation

Among the individuals who participated in follow-up assessments, 77.1% (n = 199) scheduled their postdiagnosis appointments within one month, whereas 22.9% (n = 28) opted for appointments to occur more than one month later. The analysis indicated that there were no statistically significant differences in follow-up outcomes based on the timing of these appointments (p = 0.373).

Telehealth utilisation and retention in care

In our study, 177 individuals (59%) utilised telehealth services and achieved a follow-up rate of 87%. In contrast, the follow-up rate of those who did not receive telehealth was 59.3%. The analysis revealed a significant effect of telehealth on regular follow-up (OR: 4.58, 95% CI: 2.60–8.08,p < 0.001), with individuals without these services being approximately 4.5 times more likely to miss follow-ups, thereby underscoring the importance of telehealth services.

Timing of ART initiation

The percentages of cases in which treatment was initiated on the same day, within one week, and after one week of diagnosis were 25.6% (n = 77), 40.7% (n = 122), and 33.7% (n = 101), respectively. The follow-up rates for these aforementioned groups were 79% (n = 64), 76.2% (n = 93), and 72% (n = 72), respectively. The analysis revealed that the interval from diagnosis to the commencement of treatment did not significantly affect follow-up adherence (p = 0.335).

ART schedule, pill burden, and dosage frequency

In this study, 52.7% of the patients (n = 158) received a single-tablet regimen, 36.7% (n = 110) received a two-tablet regimen, and 10.6% (n = 32) received a three-tablet regimen. Among the participants who maintained regular follow-ups, the proportions who adhered to a single tablet, two tablets, and three tablets were 76.6% (n = 121), 70.9% (n = 78), and 87.5% (n = 28), respectively. The influence of the number of pills on the consistency of regular follow-up was not statistically significant (p = 0.158).

Moreover, 294 patients utilised the ART regimen as a daily single dose, and the rate of regular follow-up was 76.2% (n = 224). Furthermore, six patients utilised a twice-daily regimen, and the rate of regular follow-up was 50% (n = 3). The dose frequency did not significantly affect adherence to follow-up (p = 0.076).

Multivariable analysis

Telehealth utilisation emerged as the strongest factor correlated with retention in care. None of the other variables—including sex, age, delayed presentation, pill burden, dosing frequency, or timing of ART initiation—demonstrated statistically significant associations with retention in care.

Discussion

To ensure that an individual living with HIV effectively initiates treatment, it is critical to arrange an initial evaluation by an infectious disease specialist or a physician experienced in HIV care as promptly as possible (ideally within one month of diagnosis). Guidelines recommend that individuals living with HIV consult with their HIV care team within one week of diagnosis and consider implementing a same-day test and treatment strategy (when feasible) [15, 16]. In HIV surveillance studies conducted in the United States, 81.6% of individuals diagnosed with HIV applied to a treatment centre within the first month after their diagnosis in 2022. The target rate for 2025 is set at 95% [17]. In contrast, a study in Uganda revealed that only 53% of 928 newly diagnosed HIV cases were linked to care within one month [18]. Additionally, a study conducted in South Africa reported that 85% of 343 newly diagnosed HIV patients made their first appointment within three months of their diagnosis; however, the follow-up rate for these patients was only 46% [19]. A meta-analysis from Europe revealed that factors such as HIV transmission through heterosexual contact or intravenous drug use, as well as young age, low education level, and feeling well at the time of diagnosis, contributed to delays in linking to care after diagnosis [20]. This meta-analysis (which was limited to 12 studies) estimated a rate of 85% (95% CI: 75–93%) for a first appointment within three months. In our study, 86% of the patients received a specialist evaluation within one month of confirmation of an HIV diagnosis. The reasons underlying the delayed referrals remain unclear. Our analysis revealed no significant difference in the regularity of follow-up appointments between those who were promptly referred and those who experienced delays after their diagnosis (p = 0.373).

According to the 2022 HIV surveillance report in the United States, the authors reported that among the approximately 1.2 million individuals living with HIV, 87% had received a diagnosis, 66% were receiving HIV treatment, and 47% continued to adhere to their HIV treatment regimen [17]. After the initial appointment, it is essential to regularly assess adherence to antiretroviral therapy and clinic appointments. Barriers to adherence need to be regularly evaluated both prior to initiating antiretroviral therapy (ART) and during treatment. Individuals living with HIV who encounter challenges in adhering to appointments or ART should be provided with adherence support that emphasises a constructive, collaborative, nonjudgmental, and problem-solving approach. Each individual requires to be evaluated according to their unique needs and the obstacles that they encounter in obtaining care. This evaluation encompasses the modification of ART to increase dosage simplicity or minimise adverse effects; the provision of flexible appointment scheduling; the identification of resources to assist with treatment expenses; and the provision of support for transportation, nutrition, housing, stigma, substance use, or psychological distress [4]. Tarfa et al. [21] reported that the most important obstacles to initial contact and retention in treatment after diagnosis included stigma, substance abuse, psychological problems, homelessness, and health insurance problems. In our study, 227 (75.6%) patients continued to receive regular follow-ups. The primary factor contributing to irregular follow-up involved a lack of general health insurance, which highlights a significant barrier to access to essential health services. Additionally, the presence of psychological disorders indicates the profound impact of mental health on adherence to treatment; moreover, low levels of education suggest that educational disparities contribute to difficulties in navigating healthcare systems, refusal to accept the diagnosis, adverse effects associated with ART, substance use, demanding workloads, legal conviction, homelessness, and immigration status.

These findings underscore the challenges that individuals experience in maintaining consistent medical follow-ups and highlight the necessity for tailored interventions to effectively support their healthcare needs.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the use of telehealth services in HIV care plays a critical role in maintaining regular follow-up. Previous studies have primarily investigated the impact of telehealth services on ART adherence. Esmaeili et al. [22] conducted a meta-analysis and revealed that telehealth applications substantially enhanced adherence to ART, with a risk ratio of 1.18 being reported (95% confidence interval: 1.03–1.35, p < 0.05). Similarly, Smith et al. [23] indicated that clinical meetings held via videoconferencing yielded higher viral suppression rates compared to traditional face-to-face meetings, with rates of 78.3% versus 74.1% being reported, respectively (risk ratio: 1.06,95% confidence interval: 1.01–1.11). A systematic review on adherence to follow-up highlighted the benefits of telemedicine and digital support [24]. Moreover, a study conducted at a centre in Chicago revealed that the rate of appointment attendance was higher for telehealth services than for in-person visits (78.9% vs. 61.9%, respectively) [25]. Ridgway and colleagues revealed that the adoption of telehealth services (especially during the COVID-19 pandemic) resulted in a higher rate of retention in care among individuals living with HIV [26]. In our study, we determined that attaining telehealth services was significantly associated with improved follow-up retention (p < 0.001). Our findings highlight the important role of remote healthcare applications (which have demonstrated notable momentum during the COVID-19 pandemic) in HIV care.

Several factors may explain the observed link between telehealth utilization and improved retention in HIV care. Telehealth can reduce perceived or experienced stigma by enabling patients to receive care in a private setting rather than in a clinic. It also reduces logistical challenges such as travel time, transportation expenses, and time taken away from work, which are known barriers to consistent retention in care. Moreover, flexible scheduling and the ease of communicating with healthcare providers via remote platforms may enhance the consistency of care. Prior studies have similarly indicated that convenience, privacy, and improved communication are crucial facilitators of retention in telehealth-supported HIV care models (22–26).

Hemmige et al. [27] conducted a study involving 1,028 individuals living with HIV who had beenreceiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) for the first time between 2008 and 2011. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and outcomes of single-tablet regimens (STRs) with those of multiple-tablet regimens (MTRs). The 12-month follow-up results demonstrated that 80.7% of the participants adhered to the STR, whereas 72.7% adhered to the MTR. Moreover, individuals who received the STR were approximately 49% more likely to maintain care than individuals who received the MTR (adjusted odds ratio: 1.49). In our study, no statistically significant association was observed between the number of tablets and consistent follow-up. This result can be attributed to the sample size, characteristics of the patient population, and effects of structural and social factors.

Bor et al. [28] reported that retention in treatment at 12 months was 11.7% greater after switching to a fixed single-dose (FDC) regimen, especially among those patients in the early stages of disease. Several studies have primarily focused on single daily dose regimens and treatment adherence. In our study, the effect of the number of daily doses on follow-up retention was not significant (OR: 3.13, 95% CI: 0.006–1.296, p = 0.076). Although an odds ratio greater than one suggests a propensity towards a less frequent daily dosage, the extensive confidence interval and lack of statistical significance indicate that this correlation is not conclusive.

Koenig et al. [29] reported that initiating HIV treatment on the same day as diagnosis resulted in a 17% improvement in retention and a 13% improvement in viral suppression. This study emphasised that initiating HIV treatment on the same day after diagnosis increased hope, optimism, and a general sense of commitment to the healthcare system among patients, which is important for retention [29]. A study conducted in Ethiopia compared the 6- and 12-month retention rates in individuals who initiated ART on the same day after HIV diagnosis with those who initiated treatment > 7 days after diagnosis [30]. At 6 months, the retention rates were 82% for those who initiated ART on the same day, 89.4% for those who initiated ART after the seventh day, and at 12 months 75.8% and 82%, respectively. In conclusion, although initiating ART on the same day provides rapid access to treatment, retention rates are lower at 6 and 12 months. In our study, no significant relationship was demonstrated between the time to initiation of treatment after HIV diagnosis and consistent follow-up.

In our country, studies have primarily focused on treatment adherence. A small number of studies have reported varying adherence rates. In a study conducted by the ACTHIV-IST group [31] involving 224 patients, the adherence rate was observed to be 85%, with forgetfulness being identified as the primary reason for nonadherence. In another study conducted by Ceylan et al. [32], ART adherence was reported to be 61% among 158 individuals living with HIV. Significant correlations were observed between adherence and knowledge about ART, social security, and duration of treatment. No studies have reported on the follow-up of HIV treatment in our country.

Globally, it is estimated that 20–30% of patients living with HIV will experience at least one instance of treatment interruption within the first two years of initiating antiretroviral therapy. This interruption refers to a period during which patients may be unable to consistently adhere to their prescribed medication regimen. This problem is not unique to HIV patients. Notably, those patients receiving treatments for diabetes, psychiatric disorders, and hypertension may also experience disruptions in adherence to treatment, thus highlighting the general difficulty of long-term engagement in healthcare services [33].

In conclusion, our study revealed that the implementation of telehealth services was strongly correlated with retention in care. The risk of irregular follow-up was 4.5 times greater for patients who did not utilise telehealth services compared to those who did use these services (OR: 4.58, 95% CI: 2.60–8.08, p < 0.001). Other variables, such as sex, age, time to presentation after diagnosis, daily dose, tablet count, and time to treatment initiation, appeared to not be related to retention in care. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating telehealth services into HIV care models and their essential role in ensuring retention in care, particularly for those patients who experience challenges in accessing in-person visits.

Limitations of the study

The inclusion criterion requiring a minimum of two years of follow-up appears to introduce selection bias. We have acknowledged this as a limitation of the study. Although this study identified several reasons for loss to follow-up, such as lack of insurance, depression, and low education level, these variables were derived from retrospectively recorded data. They could not be quantitatively incorporated into the regression analysis, which represents a limitation of the study. Future research combining quantitative modelling with qualitative methods could provide deeper insight into the psychosocial and structural factors influencing retention in HIV care.
