Peer effect of environmental information disclosure behavior in China:Empirical test based on "Real green" and "Fake green"
Authors: Chunxia Gu, Meixia Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The quality of corporate environmental information disclosure is concerned by all sectors of society. Using data on Chinese listed A-share companies in heavy polluting industries from 2015 to 2022, this study examines whether the "quantity" and "quality" dimensions of corporate environmental information disclosure have peer effects in the same region. Further, it compares the mechanism and economic consequences of the "real green" and "fake green" behaviors. The empirical results show that environmental information disclosure has peer effect in both "quantity" and "quality", and the "real green" behavior and "fake green" behavior also exist. Two underlying mechanisms are identified: social learning and competitive imitation mechanisms. Further analysis reveals that the peer effect of "Real green" behavior is derived from the competitive imitation mechanism, and that of "Fake green" behavior is derived from the social learning mechanism. Overall, the peer effect of environmental information disclosure quality weakens the green innovation ability. Further, shows that the peer effect of "Real green" behavior has a positive U-shaped impact on green innovation. Research attention and environmental regulation play a certain role in regulating the peer effect on the quality of environmental information disclosure.

Introduction

The environmental information disclosure system, as a standard requirement for enterprises to practice the concept of green development, has become an important way for enterprises to communicate environmental protection beliefs and promote transformation and development [1]. According to reputation and value creation theories, improving the level of environmental information disclosure is in line with the social emphasis on environmental protection, helps enterprises build a green reputation [2], increases enterprise value [3], and eases financing constraints [4]. Today, environmental information disclosure still remains voluntary in China. Furthermore, many disclosures still lack standardization in terms of the disclosed information. Hence, enterprises do practice "greenwashing" behaviors using selective disclosure and symbolic representation to whitewash their green performance [5]. For instance, firms often use vague summaries or provide insufficient data backing their claims. Hence, the quality of environmental information disclosure in China remains uneven.

On the one hand, companies' decision-making is largely driven by heir own strategic choices. Most current studies on improving corporate environmental information disclosure quality are based on external regulation and internal governance. On the other hand, there is still little consideration of the possible mutual and cross-impact of environmental information disclosure with other enterprises in the same region; this is also termed the peer effect [6].

Essentially, the peer effect emphasizes that individuals' behavioral choices and outcomes are not completely independent, but are influenced by peer behavior. This reflects the individual's response to changes in the surrounding social environment. In recent years, peer effect theories have also been widely used to explain the convergence of behavior among firms [7]. This view expands the connotation of traditional classical economic theory [8]. In fact, the environmental disclosure of listed companies in China has shown an increasing trend year by year. Is there also a phenomenon of imitative peer effect? Due to the lack of a unified and effective environmental information disclosure evaluation system in China, it is biased to blindly study the economic consequences of environmental information disclosure on companies. This is an essential question that this study seeks to answer in the Chinese context.

Most studies focus on the peer effect of business decision making[[9], [10], [11]]. Studies on the peer effect of environmental information disclosure behavior focus on disclosure format [12], content [13], results [14], and mechanism [6]. Furthermore, scholars have discussed the peer effect of disclosures from the perspective of corporate social responsibility, and ESG, respectively[[15], [16], [17]]. Others have verified that the peer effect also exists in the "greenwashing" behavior of companies[18,19].

Based on the review of domestic and foreign literature, it can be found that most of the existing literature studies on the peer effect of corporate environmental information disclosure behavior focus on verifying its existence and mechanism analysis. However, few studies consider different environmental information disclosure quality and highlight the economic consequences. In real-world scenarios, the majority of enterprises are more inclined to disclose comprehensive environmental information and provide supporting data. However, extant research on "greenwashing", scholars frequently overlook the investigation and emphasis on this "Real green" behavior, leading to a partial generalization that undermines the confidence of certain "Real green" enterprises. Measuring environmental information disclosure quality is challenging and complex. High-quality information disclosure is a kind of good behavior, which can bring numerous positive effects to corporate management. However, merely adopting this perspective is not sufficient, as it can ignore the negative effects of poor information quality, such as "greenwashing"; similarly, "greenwashing" is a negative behavior, and if only from the perspective of "greenwashing" to study the impact of green information disclosure quality is also one-sided, so it cannot test the positive effect of higher green information disclosure quality. Therefore, this study posits that both "Real green" behaviors and "greenwashing" practices should be examined. A comparative analysis between these two can more effectively illuminate the distinct economic consequences arising from varying environmental information disclosure behaviors, particularly in the context of China's current green development landscape. Such a comparative study holds significant practical implications. Furthermore, to make a more intuitive comparison, this study terms "greenwashing" as "Fake green". Essentially, "Real green" behaviors are defined as the corporate environmental information disclosure of a substantial number of verifiable and substantial disclosures. Whereas "Fake green" behaviors are characterized by the disclosure of a limited number of environmental information items or symbolic disclosures accompanied by vague statements. Given the lack of comparative analysis of "Real green" and "Fake green" behaviors within the academic realm, this study posits the following research question: Are there peer effects in "Real green" and "Fake green" behaviors? If so, how is this peer effect formed? What are the economic consequences of the peer effect of different environmental disclosure qualities on corporate green innovation?

To answer this question, this study focuses on the environmental information disclosure behavior of heavy polluting listed companies in China from 2015 to 2022, combines the adequacy, reliability and comparability of the quality of information disclosure. Specifically, drawing on the value creation, reputation, and signal transmission theories, among others, this study analyses various causes of the peer effect of the selected firms' environmental information disclosure behavior in terms of the quantity and quality of these disclosures and puts the research focus more on the quality perspective. Furthermore, it examines the differences between the peer effects of "Real green" and "Fake green" behaviors. Then, it discusses the competitive imitation mechanism for the "Real green" behavior and social learning mechanism for the "Fake green" behavior, and distinguishes between the different economic consequences. Finally, this study further discusses the positive moderating effect of research reports on the peer effect of corporate environmental information disclosure behavior and the negative moderating effect of environmental regulation on the peer effect, so as to expand the external influencing factors of the peer effect.

This study makes several contributions: (1) This study differentiates the quality of disclosures from the perspectives of real and fake green behaviors, and provides a novel comparative analysis of the peer effects. (2) This study highlights two mechanisms driving the peer effect: social learning and competitive imitation mechanisms. These mechanisms apply for real and fake green behaviors, respectively. Thus, this study sheds further light on the transmission path of "corporate behavior-information transfer-economic benefits" and offers valuables insights for promoting the deep-level governance of enterprises; (3) Finally, this study considers the economic consequences of the peer effect by analyzing its influence on corporate green innovation, which further enriches the topics related to the study of the economic consequences of the peer effect.

The remainder of this article is structured as follows: Section 2 introduces the institutional background of environmental information disclosure in China. Section 3 develops the hypotheses. Section 4 describes the data and construction of the peer effect. Section 5 reports the baseline results of the local peer effects of environmental information disclosure and discusses some formation channels, economic consequences, and robustness tests. Section 6 undertakes several further analyses. Finally, section 7 presents the conclusions of this study.

Institutional background

China's green information disclosure system is still on its way. In 2002, China promulgated the Law of the People's Republic of China on the Promotion of Cleaner Production, and later issued a lot of documents requiring heavy polluting enterprises to disclose their relevant environmental information and performance. However, due to the lack of supervision, the effectiveness of environmental information disclosure is not significant. In 2014, the Measures for Self-Monitoring and Information Disclosure of Key National Monitoring Enterprises were promulgated, with the regulator setting out a detailed system of rewards and penalties for the presentation and content of corporate environmental information, and also expanding the target subjects, alleviating the lack of enforcement by the regulator in the past. In 2015, the promulgation of the Environmental Protection Law of the People's Republic of China and the Measures for the Disclosure of Environmental Information of Enterprises and Institutions further clarified the specific ways and relevant contents of environmental information disclosure, which further improved the quality of corporate environmental information disclosure. In 2018, the China Securities Regulatory Commission established the ESG Disclosure Framework Guidelines, but overall, China's environmental information disclosure is still in the stage of voluntary disclosure, and the quality is mixed. The 2020 "dual carbon" target marks the deepening of China's exploration of a green economy, and environmental information disclosure has become a signal and mechanism for demonstrating good corporate green behavior and implementing social responsibility. In 2022, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment officially implemented the Management Measures for the Legal Disclosure of Enterprise Environmental Information, which clarified the content and form of relevant environmental information that heavy polluting enterprises should disclose, but there is no certain uniformity in terms of indicators and norms, which gives enterprises some opportunities to take advantages to "greenwashing". It deepens the information barrier between listed enterprises and stakeholders, which is not conducive to information transparency and further governance of listed companies. The Guidance on Sustainability Reporting for Listed Companies in 2024 has standardized the issues and directions of the sustainability information disclosure of SSE enterprises, served as the basis for the compilation of corporate sustainability reports, and provided qualitative methods and references for improving the quality of environmental information disclosure. The Guide for the Compilation of Sustainable Development Reports for Listed Companies further provides a systematic and quantitative method, marking the formal formation of China's sustainable development report compilation system, but it still lacks certain specific norms for the guidance and evaluation of external investors.

Although some international organizations and standardization bodies have formulated corresponding international ESG disclosure standards, their application and popularity are still limited as China is still in the voluntary disclosure stage. Some enterprises have the risk of "greenwashing" in the process of ESG information disclosure, which makes the environmental information disclosure behavior confused with "Real green" and "Fake green". China has not yet established a unified evaluation index for the quality of environmental information disclosure at the national level, and different accounting methods and standards may lead to large differences in accounting results, which may make it more difficult for SMEs to carry out environmental information management. The connection between the legal disclosure system of environmental information and the environmental credit evaluation and green finance systems needs to be strengthened.

Hypotheses

Existence hypothesis of peer effect of environmental information disclosure

Analysis on the existence of peer effect

Environmental information disclosure, as a new field that enterprises focus on in recent years, has opened up a new path of value creation for enterprises, but at the same time, there are certain market risks. In corporate governance, the peer effect indicates that a certain behavior adopted by one or more enterprises in a certain group encourages other enterprises to adopt the same behavior, eventually fostering imitation and convergence among organizations [6]. The network relationship formed by the close geographical location provides more convenience for learning and imitation among enterprises [19]. The enterprise adjusts its decision according to the behavior of other enterprises in the same region, which manifests as the peer group behavior in the same region.

In China, the current strong government supervision [20], social concern, and media opinion [21] make it necessary for heavy polluting enterprises to make due contributions to environmental protection and social responsibility. To obtain further development momentum and financial support, an increasing number of heavy polluting enterprises are expressing their determination and attention to environmental protection by disclosing more environmental information and issuing independent disclosure carries, which has improved the level of environmental information disclosure.

The economic development, government supervision, and cultural environment faced by enterprises in the same region all have a certain degree of similarity. In response to the regional government's regulatory requirements, managements need to think deeply about what environmental information they need to disclose and to what extent. However, as environmental information disclosure remains voluntary in China, and lacks strict and unified disclosure standards and guidelines, the level of environmental information disclosure among enterprises is uneven. In this context, the decision-making of other companies in the same region has a very important reference value. When a firm's management realizes that other enterprises are able to promote their own growth, create value, improve their external reputation, and transmit the signal of having a good reputation by improving the level of environmental information disclosure, they tend to use their behavior as a reference system to imitate and learn.

Drawing on this reasoning based on the value creation, reputation, and signal transmission theories, among others, this study analyses various factors leading to the peer effect of the environmental information disclosure behavior of heavy polluting enterprises, and posits the following hypothesis.H1The environmental information disclosure behavior of heavily polluting enterprises has a peer effect in the same region.

The environmental information disclosure behavior of heavily polluting enterprises has a peer effect in the same region.

A comparative analysis of the existence of peer effects between "real green" behavior and "fake green" behavior

Driven by China's goal of "carbon peaking and carbon neutrality," an increasing number of Chinese enterprises are undertaking green environmental protection activities, actively disclosing environmental information, and effectively fulfilling their environmental responsibilities [22]. However, China currently lacks a unified evaluation institutional framework and standard for environmental information disclosure [23]. As a result, the content of environmental information disclosure among enterprises is not standardized and the quality is uneven. Greenwashing is quite prominent in this context. For instance, many enterprises choose to hide negative news, such as enterprise-related environmental accidents and environmental penalties, or adopt partial descriptions and obscure expressions to whitewash their environmental performance so that they can achieve their own economic goals [24].

Owing to the production characteristics of heavy polluting enterprises, external investors and financial institutions pay more attention to their environmental behavior [25]. Consequently, these enterprises are under pressure to adhere to green expectations. However, they may find it difficult because of the need to invest substantial time, money, and energy in environmental protection [26]. This creates more motivation to greenwash. Based on this justification for the "legitimacy" of enterprise survival, some heavy polluting enterprises may choose to pursue short-term interests and whitewash the content of environmental information disclosure, creating a false impression of the enterprise's environmental performance. Consequently, some investors who lack the necessary judgement and discernment will be confused by the "greenwashing", which will result in blind investment that ultimately confuse the public.

With policy guidance and encouragement, companies are accelerating their pursuit to achieve environmental performance. However, the individual behavior of enterprises is not only influenced by their own factors but also by the behavioral decisions of other enterprises in the same group. On the one hand, based on value creation theory, enterprises that really want to achieve practical environmental performance to obtain real, long-term, sound profit return and create value will perhaps take motivation from their regional peers with better environmental performance, including on disclosures. This can include improving the quality of their environmental information disclosure, adjusting the quantity, expanding the scope and carrier of disclosure, and increasing green investment thus showing a certain degree of peer effect.

On the other hand, drawing on the legitimacy and reputation theories, to improve the public's impression of them, and regain the faith of society and rating agencies, enterprises with a poor quality of environmental information disclosure, substandard environmental performance, and those who have suffered from environmental penalties may imitate the "greenwashing" behaviors of their regional enterprises to make their performance look more environmentally friendly and compliant, achieving a "false prosperity" in the short term, thus demonstrating a certain degree of peer effect.

Based on the above analysis, this study proposes the following hypotheses.H1a"Real green" behavior has a peer effect.H1b"Fake green" behavior has a peer effect.

"Real green" behavior has a peer effect.

"Fake green" behavior has a peer effect.

Underlying mechanisms

As an important part of the green development of enterprises, the disclosure of corporate environmental information broadens a new way of value creation for enterprises, but at the same time, it will also carry a certain degree of market risk. Existing suggests [27] that when firms make important decisions, they usually need to learn and imitate the decision-making experiences of other firms to reduce the cost and risk of the decisions. Among them, the lack of positive decision-making will cause enterprises to get into trouble and pay a high price. Hence, enterprises of the peer group in the same region may tend to exchange and imitate relevant information. Here, enterprises with better performance will form an "industry benchmark" for individual enterprises to use as a reference, thereby inducing the peer effect. Lieberman and Asaba [7] reviewed the literature and established a theoretical framework underlying the imitative behavior of firms. They classified the motives of imitation behavior of firms into two significant categories: information learning and rival-based theories. Information learning theory suggests that when the relationship between actions and outcomes is highly uncertain, firm managers prefer to make decisions with the help of external information and reduce the uncertainty of outcomes by imitating the behavior of other firms. Competition theory suggests that firms imitate the behavior of other firms to protect market share or hedge against competitors' aggressive market behavior, thus maintaining competitive parity with competitors. Competition-motivated imitation usually occurs among firms with similar resource endowments and market positions, which are usually evenly matched competitors whose market shares and profits are easily eroded by their competitors. In such an environment, firms pursue differentiated strategies with high risk and, thus, prefer to adopt homogeneous strategies to alleviate competitive pressure and reduce risk. Next, this study discusses the peer effect of environmental information disclosure behavior from the social learning and competitive imitation mechanisms, and considering real and fake green behaviors.

Mechanism analysis

At present, environmental information disclosure has become a fashion trend in the industry, the more popular the environmental information disclosure behavior of enterprises in the same region, the more common the learning and imitation behavior of the peer group of enterprises. To cater to the trend of information disclosure and avoid the risk posed by environmental uncertainty, most enterprises will choose to imitate the behavior of model companies in their peer group. Generally, enterprises with larger scale and better economic performance are more likely to become target enterprises. Consequently, the types, contents, and levels of reports issued by them, as well as their decisions and strategies will receive great attention. Meanwhile, learning from model enterprises can help some enterprises save decision-making costs, and reduce trial and error costs, thus accelerating the learning process of enterprises and narrowing the information gap with leading enterprises. In other words, a certain degree of social learning mechanism is formed. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis.H2The peer effect of environmental information disclosure behavior is partly due to the social learning mechanism, and compared with the leading enterprises, the peer effect of non-leading enterprises is more significant.According to the dynamic competition theory, the mutual interaction in the competitive environment will prompt enterprises to react to the behavior of competitors, thus preventing the establishment of competitor barriers and the loss of their own advantages [28]. Improving the quality of environmental information disclosure can bring many benefits to enterprises: On the one hand, enterprises with better environmental performance can increase corporate investment, obtain excess returns, and limit the negative impact of environmental uncertainty. On the other hand, such enterprises can build a really good reputation, further promote their financing ability, and achieve their long-term development. When the level of environmental information disclosure of all enterprises in the same region is at a higher level or approaching high quality, the pressure of normative and cognitive legitimacies of enterprises will simultaneously increase, which will push them into a state of competition. To maintain the competitive position in the market and reduce their own pressure, managers will choose to imitate the environmental information disclosure strategy of their peers. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypothesis.H3The peer effect of the environmental information disclosure behavior is partly due to the competitive imitation mechanism. The higher the degree of competition, the more significant the peer effect of environmental information disclosure behaviors.

Mechanism analysis of the peer effect of "real green" and "fake green" behaviors

The emergence of "Fake green" behavior reduces the quality of corporate disclosure of relevant environmental information. Based on the external reputation theory and legitimacy analysis, to obtain short-term economic returns, establish corporate reputation, help companies regain real income, and reduce penalties for environmental protection violations, many companies adopt "Fake green" behavior to cater to government environmental regulations and social environmental concerns, thereby consolidating their legitimacy. Notably, leading enterprises have greater influence and strong information advantages. Hence, once non-leading enterprises in the same region observe the "Fake green" behavior of leading enterprises, the former will encourage managers to imitate such "Fake green" behaviors, and then whitewash the environmental information disclosure. This can reduce the quality of environmental information disclosure of enterprises, finally resulting in convergence within the peer group. This reflects the social learning mechanism of the "Fake green" behavior peer effect.

Meanwhile, the more intense the competitive environment is, the more intense the tendency to compare with each other will be. Based on the value creation theory, enterprises with high-quality environmental information disclosure can win social recognition, institutional ratings, and government subsidies and encouragement in the long run. Consequently, they are more able to further increase environmental protection investment and promote other enterprises to obtain long-term, sound, and exact value returns, thereby promoting sustainable development. When the enterprises in the peer group notice the numerous benefits brought by the "Real green" behavior, in order to improve their own competitive advantage, they will choose to imitate the target enterprises to maintain their own status, thus forming a peer effect. This reflects the competitive imitation mechanism of the "Real green" behavior peer effect.

Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypotheses.H4aBased on the social learning mechanism, there is a peer effect of "Fake green" behavior.H4bBased on the imitative competition mechanism, there is a peer effect of "Real green" behavior.

Based on the social learning mechanism, there is a peer effect of "Fake green" behavior.

Based on the imitative competition mechanism, there is a peer effect of "Real green" behavior.

The impact of the peer effect of environmental information disclosure quality on green innovation

Theoretically, it is uncertain whether the peer effect of environmental information disclosure quality can enhance corporate green innovation. Environmental protection and the green innovation of enterprises are cost expenditures [16]. Given the limited resources and executives' attention, when enterprises pay more attention to environmental information disclosure owing to peer effect, they may correspondingly reduce other expenditures. Meanwhile, green innovation, as a kind of expenditure with high risk, high cost, and uncertain benefits, may be delayed or reduced all together. Concurrently, the higher the peer effect of environmental information disclosure of enterprises, the more willing enterprises are about meeting the requirements and evaluation standards of the government, society, and rating agencies through environmental information disclosure. This may weaken their market understanding and grasp, thus reducing or even ignoring the importance of green innovation. Based on the signal transmission theory, owing to the existence of "Fake green", the peer effect of corporate environmental information disclosure behavior is most likely a whitewashing behavior to cater to the social attention to the environmental protection actions of heavy polluting enterprises and conform to the trend of green development to attract investors. Here, the peer effect of this "Fake green" behavior makes it more costly to cover up, and enterprises do not have extra energy and funds to carry out green innovation. Together, this can eventually manifest as an inhibition of green innovation. Research design

Sample

According to the 16 heavy polluting industries identified by the Ministry of Environmental Protection in the Guidelines for Environmental Information Disclosure of Listed Companies (2010), 784 Chinese heavy polluting enterprises listed on Shanghai and Shenzhen A-shares from 2015 to 2022 are selected.

The samples are processed according to the following criteria: (1) financial and insurance listed companies are excluded; (2) ST, ST∗ and other types of companies are excluded; (3) The listed companies with missing relevant data are excluded; (4) To eliminate the influence of outliers, the main continuous variables were winsorized at the 1 % and 99 % percentiles. This yields a total sample of 5780. Among them, the environmental information disclosure and quality of environmental information disclosure come from the Annual Report, Social Responsibility Report, Sustainability Report, Environmental Report, and ESG Report released by the enterprise, which are manually extracted and sorted by using content analysis method.

Variable definition

Independent variables

The independent variable is the behavior of environmental information disclosure, which is divided into three levels.

a. Based on the quantity of disclosure, the variable environmental information disclosure (EID) is constructed. This is measured using the total number of environmental information disclosed by enterprises regarding a total of 28 items.

b. From the perspective of quality, the study constructs two variables: the quality of environmental information disclosure (GreenQ) and enterprise "Real green" behavior (Real).

Following Wang [30], this study draws on the contents of environmental information disclosure for heavy polluting enterprises stipulated in the Measures for the Administration of Legal Disclosure of Corporate Environmental Information, and distinguishes between different carriers such as Annual Reports, Social Responsibility Reports, Sustainability Reports, Environmental Reports and ESG Reports and uses a total 28 specific items, from four aspects of "governance and structure," "process and control," "input and output," and "Law-abiding and compliance" are used to establish an indicator system for enterprise green information disclosure, as shown in Table 1. This study refers to the disclosure with specific data and provisions as substantive disclosure. Meanwhile, more vague statements, objectives, and descriptions without supporting data are referred to as symbolic statements. The content analysis method is used to score the relevant matters of environmental information disclosure. "Yes" is assigned a value of 1 and 2 according to the degree of disclosure, and "No" is assigned a value of 0. Accordingly, the scores of substantive and symbolic disclosures are obtained, respectively. In the scoring process, each sample is scored by two people, respectively. The final score is retained when the agreement between the two raters reaches more than 90 % in the trial evaluation stage. The measurement method of enterprise environmental information disclosure quality (GreenQ) is differentiated according to the substantive and symbolic disclosure of environmental information by enterprises:

Drawing on Huang [31], this study uses the ratio of undisclosed items of enterprises to all items that should be disclosed (28) to measure the degree of selective disclosure:

Selective Disclosure (GWLS) = 100 × (1- Number of disclosed items/number of items to be disclosed)

Next, the ratio of the number of symbolic disclosures to the number of disclosed items is used to measure the degree of declarative manipulation:

Degree of declarative manipulation (GWLE) = 100 × (number of symbolic disclosures/number of disclosed items)

Finally, the greenwashing degree (GWL) of each enterprise is calculated using the geometric mean:GWL=GWLS×GWLEGreenQ=100−GWL

The calculation of the "greenwashing" value is very likely to neutralize the quality of information disclosure of enterprises. Hence, this study measures "Real green" behavior and "Fake green" behavior according to the substantive degree of disclosure content of enterprises as follows:

First, according to the content of environmental information disclosed by enterprises, the median of the total number of disclosed enterprises is first selected. If it exceeds the median, it is recorded as "more disclosure"; otherwise, it is recorded as "less disclosure". Second, the median of substantive disclosure is selected. If it exceeds the median, it is recorded as "more substantive disclosure"; otherwise, it is recorded as "more symbolic disclosure". Then, the collected disclosure content is divided into four levels, namely: "more disclosure and more substantive," "more disclosure and more symbolic," "less disclosure and more substantive," and "less disclosure and more symbolic."

Among them, "more disclosure and more substantive" represents the "Real green" behavior with better environmental performance and higher quality of green information disclosure. "More disclosure and more symbolic," "less disclosure and more substantive," and "less disclosure and more symbolic" correspond to the two ways of "greenwashing" of heavily polluting enterprises: selective disclosure and expressive manipulation. Hence, these represent "Fake green" behavior with poor environmental performance and low quality of green information disclosure.

Dependent variable

The dependent variable is the environmental information disclosure behavior of the peer enterprises. Referring to Zhang [16], EID-loc and GreenQ-loc are used to measure the average level of environmental information disclosure quantity and quality of the enterprise, and all other companies in the same region in the same year, respectively. The regions are divided according to seven methods as Northeast, Northwest, North China, Central China, East China, Southwest, South China.

Control variables

Following Li and Shen et al. [17,32], the control variables include company size (Size), corporate profitability (ROA), corporate debt capacity (LEV), corporate growth (Growth) and TobinQ. The model also controls the industry fixed effect and year fixed effect. Based on the characteristics of the peer effect and combined with the above studies, this study also controls the relevant characteristics of peer firms, including the average size, average profitability, average debt capacity, average growth, and average Tobin's Q value of peer firms.

Economic consequences

Environmental disclosure quality peer index (TGreenQ). Following Aerts et al. [33], a peer index of environmental information disclosure quality is constructed. First, the dispersion of GreenQ is calculated according to equation (1), and then the peer index TGreenQ is calculated according to equation (2).(1)DisGreenQ=[GreenQia−mean(GreenQia)SD(GreenQa)]

The mean (GreenQia) and SD (GreenQa) represent the mean and standard deviation of GreenQ for all other companies in the same region, respectively.(2)TGreenQia=max(DisGreenQa)−DisGreenQia

TGreenQ is the difference between the maximum GreenQ dispersion degree and GreenQ dispersion degree of each company. The larger the index, the higher the degree of GreenQ dispersion degree in the same region.

Green innovation (GreenI). Following Wang [34] and He [35], respectively, this study uses green technology innovation (GTI) and green innovation quality (GIQ) to measure green innovation. GTI is measured by adding one to the sum of the number of green invention and green utility model patents independently obtained by the enterprise in the year, and then finally taking the logarithm. GIQ is the natural logarithm of the number of green patent applications plus one.

Model construction

The peer effect test of environmental information disclosure behavior

To verify the peer effect on the "quantity" of environmental information disclosure, model (3) is established:(3)EID=α0+β1EID−loc+β2CONTR+∑Year+∑Ind+ε

To verify the peer effect on the "quality" of environmental information disclosure, model (4) is established:(4)GreenQ=α+0βG1reenQ−loc+β2CONTR+∑Year+∑Ind+ε

This study examines the peer effects of "Real green" and "Fake green" behaviors of enterprises. According to the above grouping method, the "more disclosure and more substantive" of environmental information of enterprises are regarded as "Real green" behavior, and the others are "Fake green" behavior. The responses of "Real green" and "Fake green" behaviors to the environmental information disclosure quality of the target company are each tested. If the coefficient of "Real green" behavior is significantly positive, hypothesis H1a is supported; if the coefficient of "Fake green" behavior is significantly positive, hypothesis H1b is supported.

Mechanism test

To explore the formation mechanism of peer effect of corporate environmental information disclosure behavior, this study refers to Chen [19], and measures the size and return on equity of enterprises. The enterprises ranked in the top 30 % in the region are regarded as the leading enterprises, while the others are regarded as non-leading enterprises. Then, the responses of leading and non-leading enterprises to the environmental information disclosure behavior of regional peers are examined. If firms imitate the behavior of other firms mainly based on the information learning motive, non-leading firms are more likely to imitate the environmental information disclosure behavior of the leading firms. That is, if the coefficient is significantly positive for non-leading firms but not for the group of leading firms, then hypothesis H3 is supported. The results show that the peer effect of environmental information disclosure behavior of enterprises is to some extent driven by the social learning mechanism. Then, the above groups are compared in "Real green" and "Fake green" behaviors, respectively. If the coefficient of the non-leading group in the "Fake green" behavior is more significant, hypothesis H4a is supported.

Next, the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) is used to measure the degree of market competition. Then, the annual median of market competition degree is used to test models (3)–(4). When the HHI is less than the median value, it is classified as the high-competition group; otherwise, it is the low-competition group. If the coefficient in the high-competition group is significantly positive and the significance is greater than that in the low-competition group, then the peer effect is partly due to the competitive imitation mechanism, assuming that H3 is confirmed. Then, the above groups are compared in the "Real green" behavior and the "Fake green" behavior. If the coefficient of the high-competition group in the "Real green" behavior is more significant, then hypothesis H4b is supported.

Analysis of the economic consequences of peer effect

To validate the effects of quantitative and qualitative peer effects of environmental information disclosure on green innovation, respectively, models (5) and (6) are established:(5)GreenI=α+0βT1EID−loc+β2CONTR+∑Year+∑Ind+ε(6)GreenI=α+0βT1GreenQ−loc+β2CONTR+∑Year+∑Ind+ε

To further verify whether the peer effect of environmental information disclosure quality has a positive U-shaped impact on enterprise green innovation, model (7) is established:(7)GreenI=α+0βT1GreenQ−loc+βT2GreenQ−loc2+β3CONTR+∑Year+∑Ind+ε

Then, the above groups are compared in the "Real green" behavior and the "Fake green" behavior. If the coefficient of the square of the "Real green" behavior is significantly positive, hypothesis H5 is supported.

Robustness tests

Replacing key variables

Replace the independent variable

For the robustness analysis, this study first substitutes independent variables and uses EID-ind, GreenQ-ind, EID-locind and GreenQ-locind to indicate the peer effect of the quantity and quality of environmental information disclosure in different regions of the same industry and same region of the same industry.

Replacing the dependent variable

Following Walker and Wan [36], this study measures the degree of "greenwashing" of enterprises using the ratio of the contents of enterprises' symbolic disclosure to substantive disclosure. Considering that some enterprises only have symbolic behavior but no substantive behavior, 1 is added to the denominator.Degreeofgreenwashing(GWL)=∑i=1nXi∑i=1nYi+1

The higher the degree of "greenwashing" of an enterprise, the lower the quality of its green information disclosure, namely:

Green Disclosure Quality (GreenQ) = 1-GWL.

To ensure the reliability of the results, the GreenQ was normalized between 0 and 1.

The overall findings remain unchanged, indicating that the baseline regression results are robust.

Lag effect analysis

To further explore the influence of the peer effect of the previous period on the peer effect of the current period, the peer variables of quantity and quality of environmental information disclosure are lagged by one to two periods each. Again, the main conclusions hold.

Propensity score matching method

Following Wang [30], this study takes heavy polluting listed companies with higher and lower environmental information disclosure qualities as the treatment and control groups, respectively, in a propensity score matching approach. The above control variables serve as the matching variables. The one-to-one nearest neighbor no return principle is adopted for matching to eliminate the selection bias of environmental information disclosure quality. Then, the matching samples are used to test the benchmark regression model. The results reveal that the peer effect of the quantity and quality of environmental information disclosure holds, which further supports the robustness of the conclusions. Due to space limitations, the robustness test results are not presented here.

Moderating test

Corporate decision making is strongly influenced by the external environment. In particular, the greater the number of analyst research reports on peer companies, the higher the transparency of local information. This may adversely affect the peer level of environmental information disclosure behavior. Moreover, there are regional differences in the intensity of environmental regulation by local governments. The higher the intensity of local environmental regulations, the stricter the government's requirements on the environmental performance of enterprises. Consequently, the enterprises will make more efforts to meet the requirements, and hence, the more genuine its environmental protection behaviors will be. This is highly likely to weaken the peer effect of environmental information disclosure behavior.

Table 9 shows the moderating test results. Columns (1)–(4) show the moderating effect of research report (Report) on the peer effect. In the peer effect of the same region, the quantity and quality of environmental information disclosures are multiplied with the research report variable, respectively. The regression coefficients of the cross-multiplication term and quantity and quality of environmental information disclosure are 0.004 and significant at the 1 % level. Conclusion

Understanding the motivators for companies to engage in environmental information disclosure behavior practices is crucial to promote China's green ambitions. With the help of the environmental information to be disclosed by enterprises as stipulated in the Guidelines on Environmental Information Disclosure of Listed Companies, this paper constructs the environmental information disclosure evaluation indexes of listed enterprises to verify whether there is a peer effect of environmental information disclosure. The results showed a significant peer effect. Moreover, both the "Real green" behavior and the "Fake green" behavior have peer effects. The peer effect of firms' environmental disclosure quality is formed through social learning and competitive imitation mechanisms. Meanwhile, the peer effect of "Real green" behavior is formed more through the competitive imitation mechanism, while that of "Fake green" behavior is formed more through the social learning mechanism. Finally, regarding the economic consequences of the peer effect, this effect weakens enterprises' green innovation. Furthermore, compared with the peer effect of "Fake green" behavior, the influence of "Real green" behavior on green innovation is positive U-shaped. The findings of this paper have implications for promoting the improvement and healthy development of the environmental information disclosure system in China.

Based on the conclusion above, focusing on three subjects (Enterprises, Government and Public), this paper makes the following recommendations.1.Enterprises should implement standard requirements and pay more attention to environmental information disclosure, strive to be "Real green", realize the long-term development of enterprises, and overcome the short-sightedness caused by "Fake green". Enterprises with limited experience in information disclosure can choose to learn from the disclosures of leading enterprises in the same region. However, even while doing so, they must be practical and realistic according to their own enterprise situation, build a governance linkage mechanism, and closely link enterprise environmental information disclosure with enterprise green innovation and their own characteristics, so as to achieve truly green development.2.The government should strengthen the importance of environmental information disclosure, push ahead with mandatory disclosures, establish appropriate environmental information disclosure standards, benchmark with international environmental information disclosure standards, and accelerate the process of internationalization of environmental information. Setting up specialized environmental protection inspection departments to supervise and rectify. On the one hand, it should encourage enterprises with poor environmental information disclosure to improve their disclosure level by learning from "Real green" enterprises. On the other hand, it should push capable and mature enterprises to enhance the authenticity and personalization of their environmental information disclosure according to their own characteristics.3.The public should improve their own environmental awareness, strengthen their judgment, accurately identify the environmental performance of enterprises, and learn to distinguish between "Real green" and "Fake green" behavior to avoid uninformed investments. Investors can improve their ability to analyze corporate reports, not only focusing on the description of the text, but also discern the actual operation of the enterprise, actual investment and achievements, and pay more attention to the role of media supervision. For example, the positive media can be used to "Real green" behavior to carry out a certain amount of publicity and education, and "Fake green" behavior to carry out a certain amount of exposure, so as to form a benign social atmosphere of public supervision.

Overall, this study provides valuable evidence on the peer effect of corporate environmental information disclosure in the same region in both "quantitative" and "qualitative" aspects, and explains its economic consequences by comparing "Real green" behavior with "Fake green" behavior. Importantly, incorporating such interactive disclosure behavior within the industry into company-specific or industry-wide disclosure models may be an interesting future research avenue. This study is based on manually collected disclosures, which may have some subjectivity. More objective and standardized information disclosure guidelines are needed. Another area of further research can be the economic consequences of the peer effect. And scholars are encouraged to strengthen the comparisons between "Real green" behavior and "Fake green" behavior to expand the relevant economic consequences.
