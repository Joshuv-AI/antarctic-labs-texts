Nursing students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zeynep Yıldırım, Bahar Çiftçi, Hatice Etyemez, Mehmet Mehdi Çetin, Hatice Tiryaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine nursing students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education (measured by the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale) and to identify factors associated with perceived effectiveness scores.MethodsThe study included 1228 nursing students enrolled at Atatürk University. A sample of 235 students who met the inclusion criteria agreed to participate. Data were collected online using the “Sociodemographic Form” and the “Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale.”ResultsThe mean total score on the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale was 64.98 ± 10.43 (range 21–84), above the theoretical midpoint (52.5). In multivariable linear regression, perceived course efficiency, satisfaction with nursing, and family structure were associated with perceived effectiveness scores (p < 0.05). Because normative cut-offs for this instrument are limited, scores were interpreted relative to the theoretical midpoint.ConclusionNursing students reported perceived effectiveness of ethics education above the theoretical midpoint. Perceived course efficiency, satisfaction with nursing, and family structure were associated with higher perceived effectiveness scores. These findings reflect self-reported perceptions in a cross-sectional design and should not be interpreted as evidence of objective ethical competence or causal effects.

Introduction

As healthcare systems continue to expand and patient expectations for high-quality nursing care grow, nurses face increasingly complex ethical challenges that require thoughtful and informed decision-making. These challenges are often rooted in competing priorities, resource constraints, and the need to balance professional obligations with patient-centred care. Educating nurses is therefore crucial for mitigating these issues and equipping them with the tools to navigate ethical dilemmas effectively [1]. However, despite the recognized importance of ethics education, evidence regarding its perceived effectiveness and the factors influencing these perceptions among nursing students remains limited. Ethics education is essential for all healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, as it helps them recognise, analyse, and address ethical issues. By providing a structured framework, ethics education is intended to support nursing students’ understanding of ethical principles, enhance their confidence in ethical decision-making, and reduce potential conflicts [2]. Through this training, students may develop a deeper understanding of ethical principles, such as autonomy, beneficence, and justice, which may support more informed decision-making that prioritises patient welfare. Conversely, nurses with inadequate ethics education often struggle with moral decision-making in clinical settings, hindering their ability to advocate for patients and address ethical concerns [3]. These struggles contribute to moral distress, low job satisfaction, burnout, and high turnover rates, ultimately compromising the quality of patient care. Therefore, ensuring robust ethics education during nursing training is vital for individual professional development and maintaining the integrity and effectiveness of healthcare systems [4].

Education and training programs are crucial for enhancing nurses’ ethical sensitivity and ability to make informed decisions in complex situations. Such programs enable nurses to recognise the ethical dimensions of clinical situations and respond effectively, even in challenging circumstances. Ethics education fosters critical thinking and analytical skills, enabling nurses to evaluate ethical dilemmas and make well-informed decisions [5, 6]. Additionally, well-designed training programs can equip nurses with the knowledge and skills to access and utilise current resources, such as institutional policies, ethical guidelines, and decision-making frameworks, to address potential ethical problems. These resources provide nurses with a foundation to navigate complex ethical scenarios with confidence while ensuring adherence to professional standards. To maximise their association, education programs should incorporate active teaching methods, such as case analyses, role-playing, and simulations, which engage participants in practical, real-world scenarios. Recent evidence also suggests that case-based and reflective ethics education can support the development of moral competence and ethical reasoning in nursing students [7]. This approach may enhance students’ engagement with ethics-related reflection and strengthen their ability to discuss ethical issues in daily practice [5, 6].

Nurses can make ethical decisions only if they recognise their moral values, which guide their understanding of right and wrong in patient care. These values, shaped by cultural, personal, and professional experiences, provide a foundation for addressing ethical dilemmas. Ethics education helps students identify and refine these values, enabling them to navigate complex clinical situations with confidence. By integrating reflective exercises and case-based learning, nursing programs ensure students connect their moral values to real-world practice, enabling sound ethical decision-making [8]. Ethics education for nurses should include content and methods that help them acquire clinical decision-making, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills. Case analysis is an effective method for understanding ethical values [2]. Few studies have examined nursing students’ perceptions of the effectiveness of ethics education and the factors associated with these perceived outcomes. This study was informed by established moral development and ethical decision-making perspectives, including Kohlberg’s stages of moral development and Rest’s Four-Component Model, which emphasise moral awareness, reasoning, motivation, and implementation [9–11]. These frameworks primarily interpret ethics education outcomes as students’ developmental and cognitive–motivational processes. Accordingly, the present study focuses on students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education rather than objectively measured ethical performance [10]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess nursing students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education using a validated self-report scale and to examine factors associated with these perceptions. In addition, this study contributes conceptually by distinguishing perceived ethical preparedness from objectively assessed ethical competence within the framework of ethics education. Unlike previous studies, this research specifically focuses on perceived ethical preparedness rather than objective competence, thereby addressing an underexplored dimension of ethics education in nursing.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design to examine associations between nursing students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education and selected sociodemographic/educational characteristics. Because data were collected at a single time point, findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal effects. The reporting of this study was guided by the STROBE Checklist, as recommended by the Equator Network, to enhance transparency and methodological rigour. The dependent variable in this study was students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education, as measured by the total score of the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale, and does not represent ethical competence, ethical sensitivity, or observed ethical behaviour.

Population and sample

The population of this study consisted of 1,228 nursing students enrolled at Atatürk University’s Faculty of Nursing during the 2019–2020 academic year. A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for a multiple linear regression model (fixed model, R² deviation from zero). The analysis assumed an effect size of f² = 0.40 (large effect according to Cohen), an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power (1–β) of 0.99, and eight predictors, resulting in a required minimum sample size of 214 participants. The effect size (f² = 0.40) was selected based on a conservative assumption of a potentially strong association, in line with commonly used conventions in power analysis for regression models [12–14]. However, it should be acknowledged that this assumption may overestimate the true effect size, and therefore the required sample size should be interpreted with caution.

A higher statistical power (0.99) was selected to reduce the risk of Type II error when examining multiple categorical predictors. To ensure a sufficient sample size, 300 students were contacted; 65 declined to participate, resulting in a final sample of 235 participants. Inclusion criteria were being 18 years or older, enrolled in the 2019–2020 academic year, in junior or senior year status, having a student identification number ending in an odd digit, and providing voluntary consent. Students whose identification numbers ended in an odd digit were invited as part of a systematic recruitment strategy to facilitate online data collection and prevent duplicate invitations. Student identification numbers are assigned sequentially by the university administration and are not related to students’ academic performance, demographic characteristics, or perceptions of ethics education. Therefore, this criterion was considered an operational and practical approach rather than a theoretically driven selection mechanism. However, because this approach does not constitute true random sampling, it may limit the representativeness of the target population and introduce potential selection bias. In addition, voluntary online participation may further restrict representativeness, as students with a pre-existing interest in ethics-related topics may have been more likely to participate. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and generalizability may be limited.

Data collection tools

In this study, data were collected using two tools: the Sociodemographic Form and the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale. These tools were carefully selected to comprehensively evaluate participants’ demographic characteristics, academic backgrounds, and perceptions of ethics education.

Sociodemographic form

The Sociodemographic Form was developed by the researchers to collect descriptive information about nursing students. The form included variables such as gender, family structure, willingness to choose nursing as a profession, satisfaction with nursing, participation in social and scientific activities, perceived efficiency of the ethics in nursing course, and reading of personal development books. These variables were selected to provide contextual information for interpreting students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education. The Sociodemographic Form enabled a comprehensive understanding of participants’ educational and personal characteristics relevant to the study. Perceived course efficiency was measured as a single self-report item in the Sociodemographic Form (yes/no), based on students’ subjective evaluation of the ethics in nursing course.

Students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education scale

Vynckier et al. initially developed the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale to assess nursing students’ perceptions of the effectiveness of ethics education and its contribution to ethical awareness, reasoning, and behavioural intentions [15]. Although the scale includes domains related to ethical awareness, reasoning, and behavioural intentions, it measures students’ subjective perceptions of the effectiveness of ethics education rather than objectively assessed ethical competence. The scale was later adapted into Turkish and validated by Üstün et al., ensuring its applicability and relevance in Turkey [16].

Purpose

The scale aims to assess nursing students’ perceptions of the effectiveness of ethics education, particularly its perceived contribution to moral self-development, ethical reasoning/decision-making, and ethical behaviour development.

Scoring

The scale uses a 4-point Likert format, with total scores ranging from 21 to 84. Higher scores indicate greater perceived effectiveness of ethics education, reflecting students’ self-reported perceptions of the effectiveness of ethics education. This scoring approach reflects students’ self-reported perceptions across the three domains. In the present study, the internal consistency of the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale was high (Cronbach’s α = 0.87).

Origin and usage of the tools

The researchers developed the Sociodemographic Form for this study to gather detailed demographic and academic data. In contrast, the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale is a validated tool widely recognized in the literature. Created by Vynckier et al., the scale was adapted and validated for Turkish nursing students by Üstün et al., ensuring its reliability and cultural relevance. This distinction ensures transparency regarding the origins and validity of the tools used in the study.

Rationale for tool selection

The selection of these tools aligns strongly with the study’s objectives. The Sociodemographic Form provided contextual data for evaluating the participants’ perceptions of ethics education. The Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale was selected for its high validity and reliability, and for its comprehensive assessment of perceived effectiveness. Its multidimensional structure enabled a detailed evaluation of students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education, making it an ideal instrument for this research. Additionally, this scale is recognised in the literature as a robust and widely accepted tool for measuring the effectiveness of ethics education.

Ethics in nursing course

Participants had completed a 28-hour ethics course in nursing during their second year of study. The course was conducted over 14 weeks, with weekly 2-hour sessions for cohorts of approximately 80 students. The primary goal of this course was to equip students with the knowledge and skills necessary to address ethical dilemmas and make informed ethical decisions in their professional practice. The course content included theoretical foundations, such as deontological theory and ethical principles, as well as healthcare ethics, providing a framework for understanding and analyzing ethical issues in healthcare. The course also addressed common ethical challenges in healthcare, including patient rights, confidentiality, and ethical violations. National and international nursing ethics codes were examined, with an emphasis on their application in clinical settings. Active learning strategies were employed throughout the course to foster engagement and critical thinking. These included group discussions, where students analyzed real-life or simulated case studies; collaborative problem-solving; in-depth case study analyses; problem-based learning to develop creative solutions; and film reviews and reflections to encourage a more profound understanding. These methods were used to promote engagement, reflection, and discussion of real-world ethical dilemmas in nursing practice.

Data collection procedure

Data was collected between January and February 2020 through an online survey hosted on Google Docs. Eligible participants received survey links via SMS. The survey was designed to prevent duplicate entries by enabling the “submit only once” option. Completing the study took approximately 8–10 min per participant. All data were anonymized and securely stored to ensure confidentiality and ethical compliance.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participants’ characteristics and scale scores, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Inferential statistics were also employed, utilizing t-tests and ANOVA to compare group differences. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine associations between the independent variables and the dependent variable (total score of the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale), which reflects self-reported perceptions. The primary outcome for regression analysis was defined as the total scale score to ensure model stability and avoid overparameterization. Although the outcome variable was derived from a Likert-type scale, the total score (sum of 21 items) was treated as a continuous variable. This approach is commonly accepted when composite scores comprise multiple items and are approximately normally distributed. In the present study, the distribution of total scores was approximately normal, supporting the use of parametric methods, including linear regression. In addition, the relatively large sample size further supports the robustness of parametric analyses under these conditions. A significance level of p < 0.05 was applied in all statistical analyses. No formal correction for multiple testing was applied to the univariate analyses presented in Table 2, as these comparisons were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. In this study, regression analyses were used to estimate the strength and direction of statistical associations between explanatory variables and perceived effectiveness scores; these analyses do not establish causality. Categorical variables were dummy-coded, and the sign of the coefficients reflects the reference category and coding scheme (e.g., Yes/No coding). Negative coefficients indicate lower predicted perceived effectiveness scores than those for the reference category (coded as 0). Therefore, the direction (positive/negative) of the coefficients should be interpreted with reference to the coding and reference groups. For clarity, the coding scheme (reference categories) for each categorical predictor is provided in Table 3 footnote. Prior to regression analysis, key assumptions were evaluated. The normality of residuals was assessed using histograms and normal probability plots, which indicated an approximately normal distribution. Linearity and homoscedasticity were examined through scatterplots of standardized residuals against predicted values, which indicated no major violations. In addition, influential observations were evaluated using standardized residuals and Cook’s distance values, and no influential outliers were detected. All standardized residuals were within ± 3, and Cook’s distance values were below 1.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Erzurum Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine Ethical Board (Approval Date: January 16, 2020, Approval Number: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/24). Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants who voluntarily agreed to take part in the study. Before participation, detailed information was provided regarding the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and the ethical safeguards in place. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the protection of participants’ rights, confidentiality, and well-being throughout the study.

Discussion

Because the outcome was self-reported perceived effectiveness, we interpret findings as associations in students’ appraisals of ethics education, not as evidence of objective competence or causal effects. Overall, nursing students reported perceived effectiveness scores above the scale midpoint, indicating relatively higher self-reported perceptions on this instrument rather than objective evidence of educational effectiveness. However, scores above the theoretical midpoint should be interpreted with caution, as they do not necessarily indicate true educational effectiveness in the absence of established normative benchmarks or cut-off values. In addition, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the potential overrepresentation of students with a greater interest in ethics may have inflated perception scores, thereby limiting the interpretability of the “above midpoint” result. Variables that were not statistically significant in the regression model are mentioned only for contextual comparison and are not interpreted as meaningful predictors of perceived effectiveness. Using the same instrument, Vynckier et al. reported that the scale captures students’ self-appraisal of ethics education across domains, supporting interpretation of higher scores as perceived gains rather than observed competence [15]. Recent studies discuss the intended outcomes of ethics education (e.g., reflective practice and ethics-related learning processes); however, in the present study, these are captured only as students’ self-reported perceived gains on the Students’ Perceived Effectiveness of Ethics Education Scale [7, 17–19]. Prior studies suggest that students’ evaluations of ethics education can vary by instructional approach and learning context.

The strongest association in the adjusted model was students’ perception that the ethics course was efficient. Although the strongest predictor appears conceptually related to the outcome, it reflects students’ evaluation of course structure and quality rather than the outcome construct itself, indicating a related but distinct dimension. This likely reflects a coherence between perceived course quality and perceived learning gains, and may also capture general positive appraisal tendencies inherent to self-report measures. This association may also partly reflect common-method variance inherent in self-reported measures rather than a purely substantive relationship. Prior studies suggest that students’ perceptions of ethics education may vary by instructional design (e.g., discussion-based approaches, case analyses, simulations) and by the learning environment [20, 21]. Interactive and reflective methods may shape how students experience and evaluate ethics education, which is directly relevant to perceived effectiveness outcomes [22]. In this study, such influence is reflected only through students’ subjective appraisals captured by the scale. Accordingly, we interpret this association strictly as a co-occurrence between students’ appraisal of course efficiency and their reported perceived effectiveness scores. Although perceived course efficiency was retained in the model due to its relevance to students’ evaluations of the educational context, its conceptual proximity to the outcome should be carefully considered, and future studies may benefit from re-estimating models without this variable to confirm the robustness of the findings.

In our regression model, family structure (nuclear vs. non-nuclear) was associated with perceived effectiveness scores. This finding should be interpreted as a co-occurrence: students from nuclear families reported higher perceived gains from ethics education. These interpretations are speculative and were not directly measured in the present study; therefore, this finding should be interpreted as an observed association rather than an explanatory mechanism. Sociocultural context may shape how students appraise ethics education and report perceived gains on self-report instruments; prior work also emphasizes that values and educational structures are culturally embedded, which may influence students’ evaluations of ethics teaching [23]. Therefore, ethics education may benefit from incorporating sociocultural dimensions while maintaining alignment with universal ethical principles.

Satisfaction with nursing was associated with higher perceived effectiveness scores. This association may reflect co-occurring positive evaluations rather than a directional relationship, and reverse causation or shared positivity bias cannot be excluded [24, 25]. Participation in social and scientific activities showed higher scores in univariate comparisons; however, these variables were not significant in the regression model and are not interpreted as independent correlates of perceived effectiveness in the adjusted model. The finding that students who engaged in personal development reading scored higher on Personal Moral Development highlights the role of self-directed learning and reflection in shaping perceived ethical growth, as reflected in students’ self-reported scores on this scale rather than objectively assessed competence [22, 26]. Overall, the findings suggest that students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education (as captured by the scale’s domains) co-occurs with selected educational, personal, and sociocultural characteristics. Therefore, interpretations beyond the observed associations should be considered cautiously, as the study design does not allow conclusions about underlying mechanisms or directional effects.

These associations may be understood through moral development perspectives (e.g., Kohlberg; Rest), which emphasise how learners interpret and internalise ethics-related learning experiences. In this study, such processes are reflected as perceived gains captured by the scale. Rather than claiming direct theoretical advancement, the present study contributes contextually by identifying factors associated with students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education. By focusing on perceived outcomes and integrating motivational and sociocultural dimensions, the study offers an empirically grounded perspective, limited to statistically observed associations in students’ self-reported perceptions within this context. Although these findings do not imply causality, they may inform curriculum planning by identifying factors that co-occur with higher perceived effectiveness. Although subscale scores were reported descriptively, the regression analysis focused on total scores to provide a more parsimonious and statistically stable model. Subscale-level multivariable analyses were beyond the scope of the present study and warrant future research. Preliminary exploration at the subscale level indicated broadly similar patterns; however, detailed modelling of subscale-specific outcomes was beyond the scope of the present study. Future research may benefit from modelling subscale-specific outcomes and employing longitudinal or experimental designs to provide a more comprehensive understanding using objective assessments. These findings may be explained by the role of experiential and structured learning processes in shaping students’ ethical awareness. From a theoretical perspective, ethics education should be understood not only as knowledge transmission but also as a process influencing students’ subjective readiness and professional identity formation. These findings also provide practical implications for nursing education by highlighting the importance of integrating experiential, reflective, and student-centred approaches to enhance perceived ethical preparedness. Additionally, the conceptual proximity between the perceived efficiency of the course and the outcome variable may introduce construct overlap and common-method bias, which should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting associated perceptions rather than a distinct independent effect. In addition, the use of self-reported measures for both predictor and outcome variables may have increased the likelihood of common-method bias. The main contribution of this study is not to demonstrate objective educational effectiveness, but to identify factors associated with nursing students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education in a context where this dimension has been less frequently examined.

Study limitations

This study relied on a single self-report instrument, which may limit the depth and reliability of the findings and increase the possibility of response bias. The regression model explored basic associations but did not examine potential mediating or moderating variables that could further clarify the mechanisms underlying students’ self-reported perceptions regarding the perceived effectiveness of ethics education (i.e., perceived moral self-development, perceived ethical reasoning/decision-making, and perceived behavioural intentions). In addition, the descriptive and cross-sectional design prevents any causal inference regarding the effectiveness of ethics education. Future research should consider longitudinal or experimental designs, incorporate objective assessments of ethical behavior, and apply more advanced analytical models to strengthen methodological rigor. Furthermore, data were collected from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Expanding future studies to multiple institutions could provide a more diverse and representative sample. Because the sample was drawn from a single nursing faculty within a specific cultural and institutional context in Turkey, the findings may not generalise to other institutions or countries with different ethics curricula, clinical learning environments, or sociocultural norms. In addition, online recruitment may have introduced self-selection bias, as students who were more interested in ethics education were more likely to participate. Most importantly, the study relied exclusively on self-reported perceptions and did not include objective assessments (e.g., observed ethical decision-making performance or behavioural outcomes), which limits conclusions to perceived effectiveness. Additionally, the conceptual proximity between the perceived efficiency of the course and the outcome variable may introduce construct overlap and common-method bias, which should be considered as a limitation when interpreting the strength of this association. The use of a systematic recruitment strategy based on student identification numbers (odd-digit criterion) and voluntary online participation may have limited full representativeness and should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. In addition, multiple univariate comparisons were conducted without formal adjustment, which may increase the risk of Type I error; therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

In addition, the a priori power analysis was based on a large effect size (f² = 0.40) and high statistical power (0.99), which may have resulted in an underestimation of the required sample size. Therefore, the adequacy of the sample size should be interpreted cautiously. Although a high statistical power (0.99) was selected to minimise Type II error, this value exceeds commonly recommended thresholds (e.g., 0.80–0.95) and is likely to have further reduced the estimated required sample size. Despite this, the final sample size exceeded the minimum required number and provided sufficient statistical power to detect associations in the regression model.

Conclusion

Students reported relatively higher perceived effectiveness scores on the scale; however, these findings reflect self-reported perceptions rather than objective evidence of educational effectiveness. Family structure, satisfaction with nursing, and the perceived efficiency of the ‘Ethics in Nursing’ course were significantly associated with higher perceived effectiveness scores. Given the cross-sectional design and the self-reported nature of the outcome, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

These findings suggest that aspects related to the educational context, such as course structure and perceived quality, may be associated with students’ perceived effectiveness of ethics education. Future research should incorporate objective, longitudinal assessments to more accurately evaluate educational outcomes.
