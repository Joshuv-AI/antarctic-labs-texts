Drug Delivery Bias in No-Reflow
Authors: Marco Stracqualursi, Alberto Cereda, Francesca Croce, Andrea Baldo
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the network meta-analysis by Oliveri et al,1 which clarifies the relative efficacy of intracoronary agents for no-reflow, yet leaves open a more fundamental question: is the observed effect determined by the drug itself, or by how it is delivered?

The authors should be commended for a rigorous synthesis of randomized evidence. Their mechanistic framework, distinguishing between local microvascular and systemic myocardial effects, is particularly insightful.1

However, these agents can be administered through markedly different strategies, including systemic intravenous infusion, guide catheter delivery, balloon-based infusion, or distal superselective administration with microcatheters.2,3 Across the included trials, delivery approaches were heterogeneous, neither randomized nor consistently reported, rendering their independent contribution difficult to disentangle from pharmacologic effects. Notably, the route of administration may substantially influence the therapeutic window of vasodilators, calcium channel blockers, and beta-agonists, modulating the balance between local efficacy and systemic exposure.

This raises the possibility of a drug delivery bias, whereby differences in local drug concentration within the microcirculation, as well as systemic distribution, shape the apparent efficacy of a given agent.

Such a perspective challenges current paradigms in no-reflow: what matters may not only be which drug is selected, but which delivery strategy allows it to reach—and act upon—the target microvasculature.

Future studies should directly compare distal vs proximal intracoronary delivery under standardized conditions. Until then, what we interpret as drug efficacy may, at least in part, reflect how—and where—the drug is delivered.
