Methylglyoxal potentiates palmitic acid-induced endothelial dysfunction and atherogenesis in human endothelial cells
Authors: Banafsheh Yalameha, Alireza Nourazarian, Reza Rahbarghazi, Delara Laghousi, Fatemeh Khaki-Khatibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndothelial dysfunction is a key pathological mechanism in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, and methylglyoxal (MGO) has been identified as a major contributor to endothelial cell damage through its overabundance. In this study, the effects of MGO on atherosclerotic human endothelial cells were investigated.MethodHuman Umbilical Vein Endothelial cells (HUVECs) were first pre-treated with 1 mM palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h to induce lipotoxic conditions and then treated with 60 and 400 µM MGO for an additional 24 h. A total of six experimental groups were included: Control, 60 µM MGO, 400 µM MGO, PA, PA + 60 µM MGO, and PA + 400 µM MGO. Intracellular lipid accumulation was investigated using Oil-Red O staining. Apoptotic changes were assessed by flow cytometry, while nitric oxide (NO) levels were measured using the Griess assay. Gene expression patterns associated with angiogenesis, inflammation, lipoprotein, and cholesterol metabolism were investigated by real-time PCR.ResultsBased on findings, the distribution of lipid droplets was detected exclusively in the PA-treated group, while no such accumulation was observed in the MGO (60 and 400 µM)-treated groups. Furthermore, co-treatment with PA-MGO (60 and 400 µM) significantly reduced apoptosis, while PA with 60 µM MGO elevated NO levels (p < 0.05). HUVECs’ exposure to MGO remarkably upregulated levels of LPL, LPA, IL-8, and IFN-γ (p < 0.05), whereas IL-6 levels were elevated only in the PA-MGO group (p < 0.05). The PA-MGO combination significantly altered the expression of angiogenesis-related genes (p < 0.05).ConclusionsMGO dose-dependently exacerbated the harmful effects of PA on HUVECs through increased inflammatory responses, impaired angiogenesis, and induction of nitrosative stress. Additionally, high concentrations of MGO altered the expression of genes related to lipid metabolism, including LPL and LP(a). Overall, the results indicate that MGO and PA may play a synergistic role in the occurrence of inflammation, lipid and angiogenesis disorders, and endothelial damage associated with CVDs.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Endothelial cells, Methylglyoxal, Palmitic acid, Vascular injury

Background

During the last decades, the prevalence of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) in different societies has contributed to high rates of mortality in the clinical setting [1]. Among different etiologies involved in the development and progression of CVDs, atherosclerosis (AS) is the main predisposing factor that increases the possibility of CVDs. AS is induced by the accumulation of lipids in the luminal surface of the vascular wall, followed by fibrotic changes and pathological calcium deposition [2]. Upon the involvement and activation of the intima layer and the recruitment of different immune system components, the vessels become narrowed or completely obstructed [3]. From a molecular aspect, the excessive production and accumulation of certain metabolites, such as advanced glycation end products (AGEs), have been proven to contribute to endothelial cell (EC) dysfunction [4, 5]. It has been thought that methylglyoxal (MGO), an AGE precursor, has a crucial role in cardiovascular events. This compound is an α-ketoaldehyde byproduct and is produced by a glycolytic pathway or non-enzymatic degradation of carbohydrates [6]. Previous data have shown that MGO disrupts vascular endothelial function via dysregulating pathways related to inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, cell death, etc. Recent evidence showed that the injection of MGO in Apoe−/− mice advances the atherosclerotic plaques in the aorta and coincides with the upregulation of inflammatory factors and the recruitment of circulating immune cells [7].

Furthermore, MGO reduced cell viability and VEGFR-2 expression, which was associated with impaired tube formation in ECs. Other evidence has indicated that MGO and its derived AGEs interrupt mitochondrial function, induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, and activate NADPH oxidase [8]. In line with these statements, Jarisarapurin et al. reported that MGO impairs endothelial cell function (EA.hy926) through ROS generation, inflammatory signaling, and activation of the JNK/p38/apoptosis pathway [9]. Furthermore, a new advanced study reports that exposing H9C2 cells to PA and high glucose suppresses SRSF3 expression, blocks KRAS expression, and therefore inhibits PI3K/AKT and NF-κB pathways, while upregulating BAX expression [10]. It is also possible that modification of arginine and, to a lesser extent, lysine leads to the production of MGO-originated AGEs. The generation of other derivatives, such as cyclic hydroimidazolones, is also possible by the direct reaction of MGO with the guanidine group of arginine [6]. Thus, MGO can influence the bioactivity of various proteins directly following interaction with amino acid residues. AGE-modified proteins can affect dynamic growth and cell-to-cell interaction by attaching to the receptor for AGE (RAGE) [11]. These features indicate that pathological conditions and metabolic disorders with a simultaneous increase of MGO can increase the possibility of CVDs [12–14]. Despite extensive experiments on the role of MGO and MGO-derived AGEs in the pathogenesis of CVDs, there remains to gain a deeper insight and address the exact underlying molecular mechanism leading to EC dysfunction. Hence, the current study aimed to investigate MGO-mediated vascular damage using a lipid accumulation model induced with palmitic acid (PA) in HUVECs, representing an atherosclerotic HUVECs model. Different parameters such as lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, nitrosative stress, inflammation, and dynamic EC growth were monitored. Whether and how co-treatment of human ECs with MGO and PA can foster endothelial dysfunction was monitored in the present study. It seems that data from the current experiment can help us assess the exact role of MGO in the reduction/increase of AS-induced injury in human ECs.

Methods

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from the National Cell Bank of Iran. HUVECs were cultured in DMEM/F12(Gibco)supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biosera) and 1% Pen-Strep (Biosera) inside a Memmert incubator. The cells were subcultured using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Biosera) solution at a confluency level of 70–80%. Cells in passages 3–5 were utilized for all experiments.

Preparation of a PA solution

For in vitro analyses, albumin (Alb)-PA conjugates were prepared according to previous protocols [15]. To prepare a 1 mM PA solution, 2.267 g of bovine serum albumin (Cat no: A9418-5G; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in a 150 mM NaCl solution, maintained at a temperature of 37 °C to ensure complete dissolution. The process was continued with the addition of 30.6 mg PA (Cat no: P0500; Sigma-Aldrich), and the mixture was heated to 70 °C to yield a homogeneous solution. The procedure was continued with gentle agitation of the prepared solution at 40 °C for 1 h to facilitate the Alb-PA conjugation process. The pH value was adjusted to 7.4 and sterilized using 0.22-µm microfilters.

MTT assay

The Methyl Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide (MTT) assay was used to determine the viability of the HUVECs. For this purpose, cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were plated in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h under standard conditions. In this study, HUVECs were exposed to different doses of MGO (Cat no: 67028; Sigma-Aldrich) ranging from 60 to 1000 µM for 24 h in a culture medium containing 1% FBS. After that, the supernatants were discarded, and the cells were incubated with 200 µl of MTT (0.5 mg/ml) solution (Cat no: M5655; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 4 hours. After removing the MTT solution and replacing it with a 100 µl DMSO (Merck) solution, the final OD values were read at an absorbance of 570 nm (BioTek, USA), and the survival rate was expressed as a percentage of the control HUVECs.

Treatment protocol

To assess the possible detrimental or stimulatory effects of MGO on atherosclerotic HUVECs, cells were randomly allocated into six groups as follows: Control, 60 µM MGO, 400 µM MGO, 1 mM PA, 1 mM PA + 60 µM MGO, and 1 mM PA + 400 µM MGO. Then, HUVECs were plated at an initial density of 3.0 × 10 5 cells per well in 6-well plates and treated according to the treatment protocol for 24 h.

Detection of intracellular lipid content

To show the possible regulatory effects of MGO on intracellular lipid contents in HUVECs, cells were stained with an Oil-Red O (ORO) solution. To this end, treated HUVECs in different experimental groups were fixed using a pre-cooled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 20 min at 4 °C. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), cells were incubated with 0.5% ORO solution for 30 min. After several PBS washes, the existence of red-colored intracellular deposits was monitored using an inverted microscope (Labomed).

Monitoring apoptotic changes in HUVECs using flow cytometry

The apoptotic changes in treated HUVECs were monitored using flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, after treatment, the cells were detached and washed twice with 1X binding buffer. Then, they were incubated with 1 µL FITC-conjugated Annexin V (PADZA, Iran) for 20 min at 4 °C. Eventually, the HUVECs were resuspended in 500 µL PBS and analyzed using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer and FlowJo software version 10.6.1.

Nitric Oxide (NO) assay

The Griess assay was used to determine nitrosative stress (NO content) in treated HUVECs as described previously [16]. After the completion of incubation time, supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 15,000 g for 5 min to remove any influencing particles. Then, 200 µL of supernatant was mixed with 20 µL of Griess A solution and incubated for 10 min, followed by the addition of 5 µL of Griess B solution. After 20 min, the colorimetric changes were monitored using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA) at 540 nm. Data are presented as the units of NO3−concentration in nM, and sodium nitrite (NaNO2) was applied to prepare a standard.

Real-time PCR analysis

To monitor the angiogenesis behavior, inflammation status, lipoprotein, and cholesterol metabolism of HUVECs, total RNA contents of treated HUVECs were extracted using Trizol reagent (Cat no: YT9066; Yekta Tajhiz Azma). Following the detection of RNA levels using a NanoDrop UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), cDNAs were synthesized using a cDNA synthesis kit (Cat no: YT4500; Yekta Tajhiz Azma, Iran). Primers were designed using Oligo primer analysis software (Version 7.0) (Tables 1 and 2, and 3). RealQ Plus 2x Master Mix Green without ROX (Cat no: A323402-25; Ampliqon) with the Light Cycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) was used to measure the expression rate. The fold changes were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method after normalization to housekeeping genes (18 S rRNA, GAPDH, ACTB, and B2M). Differences of more than ± 2-fold change in expression were regarded as cut-off values with P values < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Data (mean ± SD) were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.) by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of MGO on ECs viability

Our results demonstrated that exposing HUVECs to increasing doses of MGO, ranging from 60 to 1000 µM, can contribute to different patterns in cell survival rates (Fig. 1). Based on the data, incubating HUVECs with 60 µM MGO resulted in a slight increase in HUVEC viability, without statistically significant differences compared to the control group (p > 0.05). By increasing the dose of MGO, lower cell survival rates were obtained compared to the 60 µM MGO group. Treatment of HUVECs with 400 and 1000 µM MGO led to a significant reduction in survival rate compared to the 60 µM MGO-treated HUVECs. However, these features were not significantly related to the non-treated control cells. For subsequent analyses, doses of 60 and 400 µM MGO were selected.

Monitoring the effect of MGO at different doses (60, 80, 100, 200, 400, and 1000 µM) on HUVECs viability after 24 h. Data showed that higher MGO doses resulted in lower viability compared to lower doses. A significant reduction in survival rate was observed in HUVECs treated with 400 and 1000 µM MGO compared to the 60 µM MGO group. In this study, 60 and 400 µM MGO were selected for other analyses. Bright-field images revealed prominent morphological alterations, including the loss of cell-to-cell connections, at higher MGO doses, particularly between 200 to 1000 µM MGO. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc. *p < 0.05. MGO, Methylglyoxal

MGO did not alter intracellular lipids in atherosclerotic ECs

Oil Red O staining revealed a significant accumulation of lipid droplets, as evidenced by the presence of numerous red-stained cells in 1mM PA-treated HUVECs (Fig. 2). Bright-field images indicated that the central part of each cell is surrounded by numerous micron-sized lipid rims. These findings confirmed the efficiency of our experimental protocol in the induction of atherosclerotic changes in human ECs. The incubation of PA-treated HUVECs with 60 and 400 µM MGO did not alter the distribution of lipid droplets (Fig. 2). Based on our findings, slight changes in the intracellular content of lipid droplets have been documented in the PA plus MGO groups compared to the PA-treated cells.

MGO reduced the apoptosis rate in atherosclerotic HUVECs

As determined by flow cytometry, treatment of HUVECs with PA meaningfully enhanced the proportion of apoptotic cells (7.35 ± 3.9%) compared to the control cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 3). Notably, treatment of HUVECs with 60 and 400 µM MGO increased apoptosis in HUVECs compared to the control group; however, this increase was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Treatment of HUVECs with 60 and 400 µM MGO pre-exposed to PA led to the reduction of apoptosis as compared to the PA group (p < 0.05; Fig. 3), in which non-significant changes were obtained in PA + MGO groups related to the non-treated control HUVECs.

MGO can influence the nitrosative stress in HUVECs

Data indicated that treatment of HUVECs with 1 mM PA led to a significant increase in intracellular NO contents in comparison with the control HUVECs (p < 0.05; Fig. 4A). Interestingly, it was found that treatment of PA-treated HUVECs with 60 µM MGO resulted in a significant increase in NO compared to the control and 60 µM MGO groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 4A). In contrast to the 60 µM MGO group, the intracellular levels of NO were statistically insignificant in the 400 µM MGO group compared to the control cells. Unlike intracellular NO changes, no statistically significant differences were obtained in terms of supernatant NO levels in all experimental groups (p > 0.05; Fig. 4B).

MGO changed the expression of angiogenesis-related genes

In this study, the gene expression of VEGF, FGF-2, TSP-1, and PEDF was monitored in atherosclerotic HUVECs treated with MGO (Fig. 5A-D). Based on our data, treatment of atherosclerotic HUVECs with 60 µM MGO resulted in a significant increase in VEGF expression compared to 400 µM MGO-treated cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 5A). HUVECs treated with PA + MGO (400 µM) significantly elevated mRNA levels of FGF-2 compared to PA alone and MGO 60 and 400 µM (p < 0.01), and PA + MGO 60 µM (p < 0.05) groups (Fig. 5B). Noteworthy, it was found that the transcription of TSP-1 was increased in HUVECs exposed to the combination of PA + 400 µM MGO compared to MGO 60 and 400 µM (p < 0.01) and PA + MGO (60 µM) (p < 0.05) groups (Fig. 5D). However, no statistically significant differences were found in terms of PEDF gene expression among all experimental groups (p > 0.05; Fig. 5C).

MGO can increase the inflammation in atherosclerotic ECs

To investigate the relationship of MGO and PA + MGO with vascular inflammation status in ECs, the expression of several specific inflammatory genes (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) was analyzed by Real-time PCR. We found that the expression of genes such as IL-8 (3.25-fold) and IFN-γ (3.14-fold) was increased in the MGO 60 µM group (Table 4, p < 0.05). Based on our data, 1 mM PA did not exert any significant effects on the expression of all genes when compared to the non-treated HUVECs (p > 0.05). The exposure of HUVECs to the combination of PA and 60 and 400 µM MGO led to the profound up-regulation of IL-8 (~ 3 to 4-fold) and IL-6 (~ 4 to 5-fold) compared to the control cells (Table 4, p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found in the expression of IL-1β among all groups compared to the control cells (Table 4, p > 0.05).

Discussion

There is substantial evidence that MGO is implicated in the development of vascular complications and AS, underscoring its role in EDs [17, 18]. Despite the widespread hypothesis that elevated levels of MGO promote EDs [19], lower concentrations may lead to adaptive or even protective responses [20]. Thus, we assessed the effect of MGO on the dynamic growth and angiogenesis potential of atherosclerotic human ECs pre-treated with PA. The present study is the first to investigate the combined effects of MGO and PA on ECs. The simultaneous exposure of both agents may offer novel insights into how they may affect cellular mechanisms associated with AS in a synergistic or additive manner. This novel approach is based on the fact that several stressors may be responsible for ECs’ damage under pathological conditions.

MGO and cell death

Our data showed that MGO can influence the viability of HUVECs in a dose-dependent manner, with 400 and 1000 µM MGO exerting toxic effects compared to the control group. Braun et al. found morphological changes in ECS during 24 h after treatment with 1600 µM MGO, while another study indicated the potency of MGO in ECs after 5 h in the presence of 800 µM MGO [21, 22]. Consistent with the present data, high doses of MGO can influence the bioactivity of ECs through various mechanisms, including inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress [9]. Even though a non-toxic dose of MGO used in the present study did not alter the survival rate after 48 h. Cells were also exposed to 1 mM PA for 24 h, followed by treatment with 60 and 400 µM MGO for the next 24 h to induce atherosclerotic HUVECs. The existence of numerous red-colored lipid droplets in the cytoplasm of PA-treated HUVECs confirmed the accumulation of saturated fatty acids inside the cells, indicating the induction of atherosclerotic HUVECs [15]. Moreover, it was found that cells treated with PA elevated the apoptotic cell rate compared with MGO 60 and 400 µM alone and co-combined with MGO groups. These data show the increase in apoptotic changes in vascular cells in response to AS-like pathologies. In fact, PA can trigger cell death by producing ROS, inducing the MAPK/JNK and protein kinase C (PKC) pathways, and activating ER-specific markers such as CHOP and ATF4 [23, 24]. Meanwhile, prolonged exposure to high glucose levels or MGO, as well as elevated palmitate levels, can disrupt Ca2+ homeostasis [25]. In other words, severe Ca2+ imbalance may induce apoptosis due to ER stress in response to pathological conditions [26]. In this regard, a recent study demonstrated that PA caused a more significant decline in Ca2+-ATPase activity and cell viability than MGO in a model of INS-1E beta, suggesting it is a stronger inhibitor [27]. Interestingly, we found that PA + MGO can reduce the rate of apoptotic cells compared to the PA-treated HUVECs. It seems that co-exposure of HUVECs to PA and MGO has decreased the number of cells before inducing apoptosis, resulting in a reduction in the apoptotic rate of cells. However, several factors may influence MGO and PA cytotoxicity, including duration of exposure, concentration, and type of cells [27].

MGO and nitrosative stress

In the present research, NO levels were increased in HUVECs treated with PA alone and in co-treatment with MGO at 60 µM. The potency of HUVECs to alter NO levels has not been evident so far after exposure to combined PA and MGO. Nevertheless, evidence has shown that MGO and AGEs derived from MGO promote mitochondrial dysfunction, ER stress, and activation of NADPH oxidase, increasing ROS production [8].In response to MGO-induced ROS, the p38 MAPK, JNK, and Akt signaling pathways are activated, leading to an NF-κB-mediated inflammatory reaction through the production of products, including COX-2 (at 2 h) and iNOS (at 6 h). Through this mechanism, MGO may upregulate iNOS expression, thereby enhancing NO production under inflammatory conditions [9]. Meanwhile, MGO-induced O2•− reacts with NO to form ONOO−, which causes significant oxidative damage to endothelial function [6]. Consequently, MGO seems to stimulate NO production through iNOS and eNOS, as well as induce severe oxidative stress in ECs, which may influence both physiological responses and the pathophysiology of vascular diseases.

MGO and the angiogenesis pathway

There is evidence that MGO can disrupt angiogenesis and wound healing, leading to complications related to the vascular system [28]. Based on these documents, we investigated the expression of angiogenesis (VEGF and FGF-2) and anti-angiogenesis (TSP-1 and PEDF) genes in atherosclerotic HUVECs alone and co-treated with MGO. The findings indicated that MGO can induce angiogenesis in PA-incubated HUVECs through overexpressing VEGF and FGF-2. It has been demonstrated that MGO enhances the expression of VEGF in HMrSV5 cells through the activation of the p38 MAPK pathway in a dose-dependent manner [29]. MGO also stimulates the production of VEGF while suppressing PDGF-BB, potentially causing angiogenesis disruption in the peritoneal membrane [30]. It appears that MGO, combined with PA, may transiently trigger stress-response pathways, such as NF-κB and HIF-1α, resulting in increased production of VEGF, FGF-2, and angiopoietin [6]. Nevertheless, in our study, the expression level of TSP-1, an anti-angiogenic factor, was enhanced in atherosclerotic HUVECs incubated with MGO (400 µM), in line with the study by Lee and colleagues. They declared that MGO at a high dose (1.0 mM) suppresses migration, proliferation, and tube formation in ECs by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby activating anti-angiogenesis [31]. Similar findings have also been reported, indicating that MGO-derived AGE/RAGE signaling induces a reduction in VEGFR-2 and VEGFA expression, thereby impairing the angiogenic potential of EPCs [32, 33]. Thus, it appears that MGO and PA can act synergistically to amplify both angiogenic and anti-angiogenic factors simultaneously, reflecting a potentially pathological cellular environment. In addition, a dual modulatory process may occur in conditions such as cancer, diabetes, or neurodegeneration, where microenvironments fluctuate between promoting and suppressing vascularization to maintain tissue homeostasis.

MGO and inflammatory responses

Several factors can contribute to the development of AS and other vascular disorders, including vascular inflammation. On the other hand, pro-inflammatory responses due to MGO are capable of affecting endothelial function [34]. As a consequence, we examined the effect of MGO, both alone and in combination with PA, on inflammatory factors in HUVECs. The findings indicated that the combination of PA and MGO, particularly at a dose of 400 µM, led to an increase in the expression levels of pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-8 and IL-6, in HUVECs. A significant upregulation of IL-8 and IFN-γ mRNA levels was also observed following exposure to MGO at 60 µM, highlighting that MGO may independently contribute to an inflammatory state. A growing body of evidence suggests that MGO, either directly or by promoting the formation of AGEs, initiates inflammatory responses in ECs [35]. It has been reported that AGE/RAGE interactions phosphorylate MAPK, such as ERK1/2 and p38, leading to abnormal activation of NF-κB and subsequently inflammation [36]. It was also demonstrated that MGO (0.8 mM)-induced ROS upregulates cleaved Caspase 3 and GSDME-NT formation in HUVECs, thereby releasing inflammatory mediators (IL-1β, IL-18, and LDH) following pyroptosis [37]. Additionally, exposure of HUVECs to 200 µM MGO for 24 h could enhance levels of IL-1β and IL-6, reduce Akt phosphorylation, and inhibit the Nrf2/HO-1 axis [38]. Meanwhile, inflammatory pathways could mediate either indirectly through ER stress and mitochondrial ROS or directly through receptors such as TLR4, CD36, and FFAR1 [39]. Thus, it suggests that PA + MGO treatment of ECs might synergistically promote pro-inflammatory factors and pathways related to pyroptosis and oxidative stress.

MGO and lipid metabolism

It is well known that lipid excess or hyperlipidemia is a risk factor for lipotoxicity, which contributes to lipid accumulation and subsequent vascular complications [40]. Based on the gene expression data, the upregulation of ITGA2 and LP (a) genes was increased in the HUVECs following PA treatment. LP(a), as an altered form of LDL, has been shown to cross the endothelial barrier and remain in the arterial wall, promoting the development of atherosclerotic plaques [41]. Besides, ITGA2 is involved in regulating cellular adhesion and surface-mediated signaling as well as endothelial adhesion [42]. Based on evidence, free fatty acids (FFAs), such as PA, have diverse effects on lipid metabolism, endothelial damage, and lipid deposition [43, 44]. PA was demonstrated to induce endothelial lipotoxicity and upregulate the lectin-like oxidized LDL receptor 1 in HUVECs [45]. Furthermore, VSMCs treated with 300 µmol/L PA for 48 h showed a significant increase in the levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) [46]. As a result, PA negatively impacts vascular health by increasing lipid accumulation and vascular dysfunction. In the current study, ECs also over-expressed the LPL and LP (a) levels in response to MGO (400 µM) exposure, suggesting an exacerbation of endothelial disorders. It has been proposed that MGO-induced modifications in plasma lipoproteins are responsible for diabetes complications [47]. For instance, alteration of Arg18 of apoB100 by MG-H1 elevates LDL particles’ affinity for vascular walls and triggers pro-inflammatory events, foam cell formation, and atherogenic plaque development [14]. An experiment conducted by Chilelli et al. found that adipocytes exposed to chronic glycation, e.g., MGO, upregulate LPL expression and subsequently increase triglyceride synthesis [48]. While increased LPL expression may appear beneficial for lipid metabolism, in the context of chronic glycation and AGEs, this change is likely to promote AS rather than protect it.

Study limitations

One of the limitations of the present research was the assessment of PA and MGO effects over a single period (24 h). Considering the chronic nature of AS and endothelial dysfunction, assessing these compounds over a more extended period of time may provide a more holistic understanding of their cumulative and dynamic effects. There was also a limitation in that there was no in vivo validation of the experiment. Further validation in animal models or advanced systems is necessary to apply these findings to physiological conditions. Various time points and chronic exposure protocols should be included in future studies, along with in vivo validation, to enhance the clinical relevance of the findings. Additionally, future research should employ “multi-omics” approaches, including proteomics and genomics (PCR-array), to exactly uncover molecular and metabolic changes and activated signaling pathways relevant to metabolism, angiogenesis, and inflammation.

Conclusion

The findings of the present experiment demonstrated that MGO can exacerbate the atherosclerotic effects of PA on human ECs, mainly via increasing inflammatory status (e.g., IL-6 and IL-8 upregulation), elevating intracellular NO levels indicative of nitrosative stress, and impairing angiogenesis. Furthermore, MGO altered the expression of lipid-related genes, such as LPL and LP(a), indicating a possible impact on endothelial lipid metabolism. While these data do not establish direct mechanistic pathways, they suggest that MGO may not mitigate PA-induced endothelial injury but instead exacerbate it. Thus, the results of this study indicate that MGO and saturated fatty acids, such as PA, are pathologically synergistic in the progression of metabolic and inflammatory disorders associated with CVDs.
