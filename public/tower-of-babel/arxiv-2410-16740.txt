Lyman- α \alpha forest power spectrum and its cross-correlation with dark matter halos in different astrophysical models
Koichiro Nakashima, Atsushi J. Nishizawa, Kentaro Nagamine, Yuri Oku, Ikkoh Shimizu

Abstract
The Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly}\alpha forest, a series of HI absorption lines in the quasar spectra, is a powerful tool for probing the large-scale structure of the intergalactic medium. Its three-dimensional (3D) correlation and cross-correlations with quasars allow precise measurements of the baryon acoustic oscillation feature and redshift space distortions at redshifts z > 2 z>2 . Understanding small-scale astrophysical phenomena, such as star formation and feedback, is crucial for full-shape analyses. In this study, we measure the 3D auto-power spectrum of the Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly}\alpha forest and its cross-power spectrum with halos using hydrodynamic simulations from the GADGET3-OSAKA code, which includes models for star formation and supernova feedback. Across five astrophysical models, we find significant deviations from the Fiducial model, with 5 − 10 % 5-10\,\% differences for wavenumbers k > 2 ​ h ​ Mpc − 1 k>2\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1} in the Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly}\alpha auto-power spectrum. The Ly α × \mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\, halo cross-power spectra show even larger deviations, exceeding 10 % 10\,\% in some cases. Using the fitting models of Arinyo-i-Prats et al. (2015) and Givans et al. (2022) , we jointly fit the Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly}\alpha auto- and Ly ​ α \mathrm{Ly}\alpha × \times halo cross-power spectra, and assess the accuracy of the estimated f ​ σ 8 f\sigma_{8} parameter by comparing it with the ground truth from the simulations, while varying the maximum wavenumber k max k_{\mathrm{max}} and minimum halo mass M h M_{h} . Our results demonstrate that the extended model of Givans et al. (2022) is highly effective in reproducing f ​ σ 8 f\sigma_{8} at k max ≤ 3.0 ​ h ​ Mpc − 1 k_{\mathrm{max}}\leq 3.0\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1} for M h > 10 10.5 ​ M ⊙ M_{h}>10^{10.5}M_{\odot} , and remains robust against astrophysical uncertainties.

Introduction

In recent decades, cosmological research has predominantly focused on the
\Lambda
CDM (Lambda Cold Dark Matter) model, spurred by the discovery of the universe’s accelerated expansion. The
\Lambda
CDM model incorporates the concept of dark energy, with significant research efforts aimed at understanding its properties. The accelerated expansion is directly observed through the redshift dependence of distances and expansion rates, notably demonstrated by luminosity distances to Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia). This phenomenon, first reported in studies by
Riess
et al. (1998)
and
Perlmutter
et al. (1999)
, has driven further extensive exploration.

Large-scale structure surveys, such as galaxy surveys, are powerful tools for constraining the parameters of the
\Lambda
CDM model. These surveys offer three-dimensional clustering information by measuring spectroscopic redshifts. The baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) scale, first measured by
Eisenstein
et al. (2005)
and
Cole
et al. (2005)
, serves as a critical standard ruler
(
Seo &
Eisenstein, 2005
, e.g.)
. It provides constraints on the expansion history of the universe by offering distances and expansion rates normalized to the sound horizon, observable through the BAO feature in the matter correlation function
(
Aubourg
et al., 2015
, e.g.)
.
Redshift space distortions (RSD)
(
Sargent &
Turner, 1977
;
Kaiser, 1987
;
Hamilton, 1992
;
Cole
et al., 2005
;
Hamilton, 1998
, RSD; e.g.)
, observed as anisotropies in galaxy clustering caused by the radial components of galaxy peculiar velocities, reveal the growth rate of structure in the universe. Various redshift surveys have constrained the RSD parameter
\beta\equiv f/b
, where
f=d\ln{D}/d\ln{a}
represents the linear growth rate, defined as the logarithmic derivative of the linear growth factor
D
by the scale factor.

The Lyman alpha forest (Ly
\alpha
forest), the absorption features in the quasar’s spectrum, was first noted by
Lynds (1971)
and studied by
Sargent et al. (1980)
and high-resolution spectroscopy have provided rich data over the two decades
(
Weymann
et al., 1981
;
Cowie
et al., 1995
;
Rauch, 1998
, e.g.)
. Alongside those observational endeavours, cosmological hydrodynamic simulations have been instrumental in enhancing our comprehension of the characteristics of the Ly
\alpha
forest clouds
(
Cen et al., 1994
;
Hernquist et al., 1996
;
Miralda-Escudé et al., 1996
;
Croft et al., 1998
;
Zhang et al., 1997
;
Zhang et al., 1998
)
.

The Ly
\alpha
forest serves as a pivotal tool for revealing the large-scale structure of the intergalactic medium (IGM), which probes cosmic expansion through the BAO feature and the growth of cosmic structures via redshift-space distortions (RSD) at higher redshifts (
z\geq 2
), surpassing the reach of previous galaxy surveys.

Over the past decade, the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey
(
Dawson
et al., 2013
, BOSS;)
and its extension
(
Dawson
et al., 2016
, eBOSS;)
, have been pivotal in advancing our understanding of BAO. Together, BOSS and eBOSS have amassed over 341,000 quasar spectra at redshifts
z>1.77
.
Notably, the first detections of BAO from the Ly
\alpha
auto-correlation function and its cross-correlation with quasars were made with data from BOSS DR9 data
(
Busca, N. G.
et al., 2013
;
Slosar
et al., 2013
;
Kirkby
et al., 2013
)
and DR11
(
Font-Ribera
et al., 2014
)
, respectively.
Leveraging these extensive datasets, researchers successfully measured the 3D auto-correlation of the Ly
\alpha
forest in quasars at
z>2.1
and its 3D cross-correlation with quasar positions. This comprehensive analysis has enabled the detection of BAO around
z=2.3
, showcasing the power of increasingly larger datasets in advancing our knowledge of cosmic structures.

On the other hand, the RSD analysis using only the Ly
\alpha
auto-correlation has not been performed, primarily due to the degeneracy of the growth rate
f
with an unknown velocity gradient bias
(
Seljak, 2012
)
. This problematic bias originates from the two-point correlation of the flux map, which undergoes nonlinear distortion relative to optical depth.

Recently,
Cuceu et al. (2021)
proposed a joint analysis of
3\times 2
-pt correlation functions (
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
,
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times
QSO, and QSO
\,\times\,
QSO) to measure the product of the growth rate and the amplitude of matter fluctuations,
f\sigma_{8}
. Note, however, that Ly
\alpha
auto-correlation still has the degeneracy of the growth rate
f
with an unknown bias and
f
is measured mainly by cross-correlation and QSO auto-correlation. Additionally, they emphasized that for precise measurement of the growth rate, the modelling of the cross-correlation function must be accurate.

Givans
et al. (2022)
used the Sherwood suite of hydrodynamic simulations
(
Bolton et al., 2017
)
and presented the measurements of the 3D power spectrum of Ly
\alpha
auto- and of its cross-correlation with halos. They compared the fitting function approach to the perturbation theory approach as a description of the 3D power spectrum beyond linear scales. They demonstrated that the fitting function proposed by
Arinyo-i-Prats et al. (2015)
can predict a 3D power spectrum with residuals smaller than
5\%
on scales
k<10\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
. Furthermore, they measured Ly
\alpha
cross-correlation with only massive halos (
M_{\mathrm{halo}}>10^{11.5}M_{\odot}
), and it had less power than the correlation with all halos, which suggests the need to create a fitting function describing the strong loss of cross-correlation on small scales. They proposed a new model (called the ‘Extended model’ in their paper) that can improve the fits, especially at
k\sim 1\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
compared to the Kaiser model. However, we note that the L160_N2048 model from the Sherwood simulation does not include self-shielding effect (i.e. optically thin approximation), and uses
Quick-Lya
which removes high-density gas in dark matter halos in order to avoid heavy computational load.

The primary objective of this paper is to assess the robustness of
f\sigma_{8}
measurements, with a specific focus on including small-scale correlations and the impact of varying feedback models and self-shielding effects.
We utilize outputs from the GADGET3-OSAKA cosmological smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH) code
(
Aoyama et al., 2017
;
Shimizu et al., 2019
)
, conducting simulations with and without supernova (SN) feedback, along with different treatments of UV background (UVB) radiation and self-shielding corrections. These simulation sets were also analyzed in
Nagamine
et al. (2021)
.
By employing a variety of models within our simulation dataset and avoiding the use of the
Quick-Lya
approximation, which overlooks detailed astrophysical processes at smaller scales, we aim to elucidate the critical roles of astrophysical processes, including star formation, SN feedback, and the self-shielding effect.

As a precursor to cosmological analysis by the Subaru Prime Focus Spectrograph (PFS), we examine the three-dimensional power spectrum of
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
auto- and cross-correlation with halos and perform parameter estimation by fitting those correlations. We note that we adopt only the fitting function approach, rather than the perturbation theory approach, due to our focus on particularly small scales where the density fluctuations of gas are fully non-linear and influenced by astrophysical processes. Additionally, we do not address the non-linear model of the halo auto-power spectrum, as our hydrodynamical simulations are not the best tool to discuss it.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2.1
, we describe the details of our cosmological hydrodynamic simulations. The method for generating the Ly
\alpha
forest data set and halo density distribution are presented in Section
2.2
and
2.3
, respectively. In Section
3
, we review existing models of the Ly
\alpha
auto-power spectrum and the Ly
\alpha
-halo cross-power spectrum. Section
3.3
introduces the methods of fitting analysis and parameter estimation. In Section
4
, we present the power spectrum measurements, compare them with different astrophysical models, estimate parameters, and provide the results of
f\sigma_{8}
measurements through the joint fitting of the Ly
\alpha
auto-power spectrum and
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times
halo cross-power spectrum. We then discuss the validity of the fitting models in relation to the
f\sigma_{8}
estimation. Finally, we summarize our findings and conclude in Section
5
.

2
Simulations and Methods

In this section, we describe the detailed setup of our cosmological hydrodynamical simulation and the method of constructing the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
forest data.

2.1
Cosmological Hydrodynamic Simulations

We use the cosmological SPH code GADGET3-OSAKA
(
Aoyama et al., 2017
;
Shimizu et al., 2019
;
Nagamine
et al., 2021
)
, which is a revised version of GADGET-3
(originally described by
Springel, 2005
, as GADGET-2)
.
It includes treatment for star formation, SN feedback, dust formation and evolution
(
Aoyama et al., 2017
;
Shimizu et al., 2019
)
. Our code also incorporates several significant enhancements, such as the density-independent, pressure-entropy formation of SPH
(
Hopkins, 2013
;
Saitoh &
Makino, 2013
)
, the time-step limiter
(
Saitoh &
Makino, 2009
)
, and a quintic spline kernel
(
Morris, 1996
)
.

Following
Nagamine
et al. (2021)
, we employ the same simulation dataset across various physical models, as detailed in Table
2.2
.
Among these models, the self-shielding from the UVB is notably considered for its potential impact on the Ly
\alpha
forest. The simulation incorporating self-shielding is referred to as the “Shield" model, while its counterpart without self-shielding is designated as the “Fiducial" model.

In the “NoFB" model, SN feedback is not included to investigate the influence of the Osaka feedback model on the Ly
\alpha
forest statistics.
The constant-velocity galactic wind model by
Springel &
Hernquist (2003)
is used in the “CW" model.
The UVB model of
Faucher-Giguère et al. (2009)
is used in the “FG09" model instead of
Haardt &
Madau (2012, hereafter HM12)
model to assess the impacts arising from variations in the UVB model.

We use box sizes of
100\,h^{-1}
Mpc and
2\times 512^{3}
particles, where Mpc stands for comoving mega-parsecs.
The adopted cosmological parameters are
(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}},\Omega_{\mathrm{dm}},\Omega_{\mathrm{b}},\sigma_{8},h,n_{s})=(0.3089,0.2603,0.04860,0.8150,0.6774,0.9667)
from
Planck
Collaboration et al. (2016)
.

2.2
Simulated Ly
\alpha
forest Data Set

For the Ly
\alpha
forest analysis, we follow the same methodology as in
Nagamine
et al. (2021)
.
We first compute the physical quantities at
i
-th pixel at three dimensional coordinate
\bm{x}_{i}
as

\displaystyle A_{\mathrm{pixel}}(\bm{x}_{i})=\sum_{n}\frac{m_{n}}{\rho_{n}}A_{n}{W}(r_{in},h_{n}),

(1)

where sum models over all particle and
A_{n}
represents physical quantities assigned to the
n
-th particle, such as hydrogen density
n_{\mathrm{HI}}
, velocity
v
, and temperature
T
,

m_{n}
is the gas particle mass,
\rho_{n}
is the gas density,
h_{n}
is the smoothing length of the particle and
r_{in}=|\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{q}_{n}|
is the distance between the pixel
\bm{x}_{i}
and particle position
\bm{q}_{n}
.
For the kernel function, we use the following cubic spline kernel
(
Monaghan &
Lattanzio, 1985
)
:

W^{\mathrm{SPH}}(r,h)=\frac{8}{\pi h^{3}}\begin{cases}1-\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{r}{h/2}\right)^{2}+\frac{3}{4}\left(\frac{r}{h/2}\right)^{3}&0<r<h/2\\
\frac{1}{4}\left(2-\frac{r}{h/2}\right)^{3}&h/2<r<h\\
0&h<r\end{cases}.

(2)

So far, we use the single index of
i
for uniquely identifying the pixel position for simplicity, but let us rewrite the coordinate as
\bm{x}=(x_{i},y_{j},z_{k})
. We assume, without losing the generality, that the
z
is the line of sight direction (LoS).
Then, we use the
A_{\mathrm{pixel}}(\bm{x}_{i})
values to compute the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
optical depth
\tau
at any LoS position of
z
but on the grid
(x_{i},y_{j})
,

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}(z)=\frac{\pi{e}^{2}}{m_{e}{c}}f\sum_{k}\phi(z,z_{k}|T_{ijk},v_{ijk})n_{\mathrm{HI}}(x_{i},y_{j},z_{k})dl,

(3)

where
f
and
n_{\mathrm{HI}}
are absorption oscillator strength, and HI number density. The pixel length,
dl
, is set to a constant,
L_{\mathrm{box}}/N_{\mathrm{pixel}}\sim 0.1h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}
. The function
\phi
is the Voigt profile which represents the line profile of the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
absorption. We can obtain it by averaging the Lorentzian profile for a single atom over a collection of atoms with a Maxwellian distribution of thermal velocities. The gas temperature
T
characterizes the thermal broadening of spectral lines due to the Doppler effect, and the peculiar velocity
v
distorted the spatial distribution of absorption lines due to the Doppler shift in addition to the redshift caused by the cosmological expansion. Here, we utilize the fitting formula of
Tasitsiomi (2006)
which is accurate for temperatures
T>2\mathrm{K}
, rather than direct integration.

The observed
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
transmitted flux can be written as
F(z)=\exp(-\tau(z))
. Finally, we adjust the mean transmitted flux fraction
\bar{F}(z)
corresponding to the intensity of the ionizing background. The simulated transmitted flux is normalized to the observed value of
\bar{F}(z)
. Here, we determine
\bar{F}
so that the effective optical depth
\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)=-\ln\langle\bar{F}(z)\rangle
is identical to that of
Becker et al. (2013)
which is calculated by

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)=\tau_{0}\left(\frac{1+z}{1+z_{0}}\right)^{\beta}+C,

(4)

and we use their best-fitting parameters
[\tau_{0},\beta,C]=[0.751,2.90,-0.132]
for
z_{0}=3.5
. They ensured that this analytic fit closely matches the binned
\tau_{\mathrm{eff}}(z)
at all redshifts, while scaling C to literature values around
z\sim 2
.
As we put the subscription of
ij
in equation (
3
),
F
can be generally written as
F(\bm{x})
.

2.3
Halo density distribution

Our simulations include a halo catalog constructed with a friends-of-friends algorithm.
We measure the halo mass function in our simulation and find that it agrees with the theoretical prediction
(
Sheth &
Tormen, 1999
)
for halo masses
10^{10}\lesssim M_{h}/M_{\odot}\lesssim 10^{13}
.
Halo samples are assigned on the grid using a nearest-grid-point interpolation. To measure the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha

\times
halo cross-spectrum, we create a
1024^{3}
density field of halo same as the 3D distribution of transmitted flux
F
.

The halos in this paper are assumed to be quasars and galaxies in actual observations. The halo distribution should be given in redshift space with a view to measuring the structural growth rate by RSD analysis. Positions of each halo in real space
(x_{r},y_{r},z_{r})
are shifted to in redshift space
(x_{s},y_{s},z_{s})
, assuming
z_{r}
and
z_{s}
are the LoS position, according to the following formula:

\displaystyle(x_{s},y_{s},z_{s})=\left(x_{r},y_{r},z_{r}+\frac{v_{\mathrm{pec}}}{aH}\right),

(5)

where
a
is the scale factor,
H
is the Hubble parameter and
v_{\mathrm{pec}}
is the peculiar velocity in LoS direction. The rightmost panels in Fig.
1
show a 2D slice of the halo distribution. Comparing this with the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
flux in the left three panels, we observe an anti-correlation between halo number density and Ly
\alpha
flux. Consequently, the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,
halo cross-power spectrum is negative on most wavenumber scales.

3
Model of the power spectrum

3.1
Ly
\alpha
auto power spectrum

The transmitted flux
F
is the ratio of the observed flux to the continuum flux emitted by the background source. The fluctuation of the transmitted flux
F
is defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{F}=\frac{F}{\bar{F}(z)}-1,

(6)

where
\bar{F}
is the mean value of transmitted flux for the same redshift.

The linear expression of the
\delta_{F}
field in redshift space is

\displaystyle\delta_{F}=b_{F}\,\delta_{\mathrm{m}}+b_{\eta}\,\eta,

(7)

where
\delta_{\mathrm{m}}
is the matter density fluctuation, and

\displaystyle\eta=-\frac{1}{aH}\frac{\partial{v}_{\mathrm{los}}}{\partial{x_{\mathrm{los}}}},

(8)

is the dimensionless gradient of the peculiar velocity
v_{\mathrm{los}}
along the LOS. Here,
x_{\mathrm{los}}
is the comoving coordinate,
a
is the scale factor, and
H
is the Hubble parameter. In Fourier space, equation (
8
) is transformed as
\eta=f\mu^{2}\delta_{\mathrm{m}}
, where
f
is the growth rate and
\mu
is the cosine of the angle between the Fourier mode
\bf{k}
and the LOS. The linear power spectrum of
\delta_{F}
in redshift space can be written as

\displaystyle P_{F}(k,\mu)=b^{2}_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})^{2}P_{\mathrm{L}}(k),

(9)

where
\beta_{F}=b_{\eta}{f}/b_{F}
is the RSD parameter and
P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)
is the linear matter power spectrum.

Multiplicative corrections to the linear model are useful to express non-linearity on small scales as below:

\displaystyle P_{F}(k,\mu)=b^{2}_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})^{2}P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)D_{\mathrm{NL}}(k,\mu),

(10)

and
McDonald (2003)
presented the parameterized correction term
D_{\mathrm{NL}}(k,\mu)
with eight free parameters that could fit the flux power spectrum down to
k=10\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
.
Arinyo-i-Prats et al. (2015)
also introduced their fitting function with six parameters as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{NL}}(k,\mu)=\exp\left\{[q_{1}\Delta^{2}(k)+q_{2}\Delta^{4}(k)]\left[1-\left(\frac{k}{k_{v}}\right)^{a_{v}}\mu^{b_{v}}\right]-\left(\frac{k}{k_{p}}\right)^{2}\right\},

(11)

where
\Delta^{2}(k)=k^{3}P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)/2\pi^{2}
is the dimensionless linear matter power spectrum. We will call equations (
10
) and (
11
) as the
Arinyo
model. Here,
Arinyo-i-Prats et al. (2015)
and
Givans
et al. (2022)
mentioned that the
q_{2}
parameter in this model was unnecessary. We also find that the best-fit value for
q_{2}
is very close to zero. Therefore, we ignore this second term, and the number of free parameters is reduced to five.

3.2
Ly
\alpha\,\times\,
halo cross power spectrum

Before considering the model of the cross-power spectrum, we introduce that of the halo auto power spectrum. In a linear regime, the standard model of the halo auto power spectrum in redshift space, which was presented by
Kaiser (1987)
is

\displaystyle P_{h}(k,\mu)=(b_{h}+f\mu^{2})^{2}P_{\mathrm{L}}(k),

(12)

where
b_{h}
is the linear halo bias which serves as a proportionality factor connecting fluctuations in halo number density to fluctuations in dark matter mass density, and it proves to be a reliable approximation on large scales.

The model of the Ly
\alpha

\times
halo cross-power spectrum was first developed by
Givans
et al. (2022)
. On large scales,
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
forest and halo auto-power spectrum follow the Kaiser model (eq. (
10
) and (
12
)), and
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha

\times
halo cross-power spectrum is expected to be expressed as the geometric mean of the Kaiser models for both tracers:

\displaystyle P_{\times,{\rm L}}(k,\mu)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{P_{F}(k,\mu)P_{h}(k,\mu)}

(13)

\displaystyle=b_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})(b_{h}+f\mu^{2})P_{\mathrm{L}}(k).

(14)

Similarly, the flux component of the non-linear correction can be modelled as the square root of equation (
11
) from the
Arinyo
model:

\displaystyle P_{\times}(k,\mu)=b_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})(b_{h}+f\mu^{2})P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{NL}}(k,\mu)}.

(15)

They referred to this equation as the
Minimum
model, and we use the same name. Then,
Givans
et al. (2022)
proposed that massive halos hosting quasars are less correlated with the Ly
\alpha
forest, making it challenging to fit the cross-correlation on small scales. As a result, they developed a more complex model:

\displaystyle P_{\times}(k,\mu)=b_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})(b_{h}+f\mu^{2})P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{NL}}(k,\mu)}D_{\mathrm{M}}(k,\mu),

(16)

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{M}}(k,\mu)=\exp\left[(\alpha+\gamma\mu^{2})\Delta^{2}(k)-(k\mu\nu)^{4}\right],

(17)

where
\alpha
,
\gamma
,
\nu
are the free parameters. Equations (
16
) and (
17
) are used in our main analysis in Section
4.3
and are referred to as the
Givans
model.

On the other hand, observations could measure
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
cross-correlation with quasars, etc., rather than halos. Quasar non-linear velocities, commonly known as the ’Fingers of God’ (FoG) effect, is an important non-linear effect for the quasar auto- and cross-correlation with
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
forest. Following
Percival &
White (2009)
, we also use

\displaystyle P_{\times}(k,\mu)=b_{F}(1+\beta_{F}\mu^{2})(b_{h}+f\mu^{2})P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)\sqrt{F_{\mathrm{FoG}}(k,\mu)},

(18)

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{FoG}}(k,\mu)=\frac{1}{1+(k\mu\sigma_{v})^{2}},

(19)

where
\sigma_{v}
is a free parameter representing the rms velocity dispersion. This model has been used for quasar redshift surveys and their forecast
(
du Mas des
Bourboux et al., 2020
;
Cuceu et al., 2021
;
DESI
Collaboration et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, and it also accounts for statistical quasar redshift errors. We refer to this equation as the
FoG
model and use it for the fitting of the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha

\times
halo cross-power spectrum to estimate
f\sigma_{8}
.

To compute the linear matter spectrum
P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)
, we use CAMB code with input parameters fixed to their values adopted in our simulation. When performing a fitting analysis, free parameters for the linear RSD are
b_{F}\sigma_{8}
,
b_{h}\sigma_{8}
and
f\sigma_{8}
because
P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)\propto\sigma_{8}^{2}
.

4.4
, we present the results of the
f\sigma_{8}
estimation using equations (
15
), (
16
) and (
18
), comparing the outcomes from each to evaluate the validity of these fitting models. We summarize these fitting models in Table

.

3.3
Fitting Analysis

We fit models by Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) sampling with
emcee
package
(
Foreman-Mackey et al., 2013
)
to minimize the pseudo-
\chi^{2}
function:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}(\bm{\theta})=\sum_{i,j}\frac{[P^{\mathrm{sim}}(k_{i},\mu_{j})-P^{\mathrm{model}}(k_{i},\mu_{j}|\bm{\theta})]^{2}}{\sigma_{ij}^{2}},

(20)

where
P^{\mathrm{sim}}(k_{i},\mu_{j})
is the measured power spectrum for the
i-
th
k
and
j-
th
\mu
bin,
P^{\mathrm{model}}(k=k_{i},\mu=\mu_{j})
is the model power spectrum given in Section
3
and
\sigma_{ij}
is the statistical uncertainty on
P^{\mathrm{sim}}(k_{i},\mu_{j})
. In the following, however,
P(k_{i},\mu_{j})
will be written simply as
P_{i}
.We perform two types of fitting analysis in our work: only
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
auto-power spectrum and joint fitting of 2
\times
2-pt correlation (
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
and
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,
halo).
In the former, we minimize the following pseudo-
\chi^{2}
function:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{F}(\bm{\theta}_{F})=\sum_{i}\frac{\left[P^{\mathrm{sim}}_{F,i}-P_{F,i}^{\mathrm{model}}(\bm{\theta}_{F})\right]^{2}}{\sigma_{F,i}^{2}},

(21)

where the model of
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
auto-power spectrum
P_{F}^{\mathrm{model}}
is given by the equation (
10
) and (
11
) with seven free parameters

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{F}=\{b_{F},\beta_{F},q_{1},k_{p},k_{v}^{a_{v}},a_{v},b_{v}\},

(22)

using wavenumbers
k_{i}
up to
k_{\mathrm{max}}=10\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
.

In the latter, we minimize the following pseudo-
\chi^{2}
function:

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{joint}}(\bm{\theta}_{\times})=\chi^{2}_{F}(\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{F})+\chi^{2}_{\times}(\bm{\theta}_{\times}),

(23)

where

\displaystyle\chi^{2}_{\times}(\bm{\theta}_{\times})=\sum_{i}\frac{\left[P^{\mathrm{sim}}_{\times,i}-P_{\times,i}^{\mathrm{model}}(\bm{\theta}_{\times})\right]^{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{\times,i}},

(24)

using
k_{i}
up to
k_{\mathrm{max}}=1,3,5\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
. The models of cross power spectrum
P_{\times}^{\mathrm{model}}
are given by the equation (
15
)- (
19
) with free parameters

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{F}

\displaystyle=\{b_{F},\beta_{F},q_{1},k_{v}^{a_{v}},a_{v},b_{v}\}

(25)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{\times}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{F},\{b_{h}\sigma_{8},f\sigma_{8}\},\bm{\theta}_{\rm\times,NL}\}

(26)

where
\bm{\theta}_{\rm\times,NL}
is the additional parameter in non-linear correction;
Minimum
model has no
\bm{\theta}_{\rm\times,NL}
,
Givans
model has
\bm{\theta}_{\rm\times,NL}=\{\alpha,\gamma,\nu\}
and
FoG
model has
\bm{\theta}_{\rm\times,NL}=\{\sigma_{v}\}
. We note that other fundamental cosmological parameters related to
P_{\mathrm{L}}(k)
are fixed. When we perform the joint fitting, the parameters
\bm{\theta}^{\prime}_{F}
are shared with
\bm{\theta}_{\times}
and we remove the
k_{p}
parameter because we find the best-fit value for
k_{p}
diverges to infinity on the scale of
k\leq 5\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
.

In this work, the uncertainty
\sigma_{i}
is defined as the inverse of the measurement uncertainty of the power spectrum:

\displaystyle\sigma_{F,i}=P_{F,i}^{\mathrm{sim}}\left[{N_{i}}^{-1/2}+\epsilon\right].

(27)

When the density field is a Gaussian random field, the variance of the power spectrum can be estimated by the number
N_{i}
of Fourier modes contributing to each bin
(
Jeong &
Komatsu, 2009
)
.
However, the high-
k
data points with exceptionally small error bars (thanks to high
N_{i}=N(k_{i},\mu_{i})
) will dominate in the standard fit as elaborated in
McDonald (2003)
;
Arinyo-i-Prats et al. (2015)
. Following these earlier works, our approach involves adjusting the standard weighting system, incorporating a noise floor
\epsilon=0.05
.

Next, we estimate the measurements uncertainty of
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,
halo cross-power spectrum
\sigma_{\times,i}
, following the appendix B in
Font-Ribera
et al. (2014)
.
First, we define the weight of the halo auto-power spectrum as

\displaystyle\sigma_{h,i}=\left[P^{\mathrm{sim}}_{h,i}+n_{h}^{-1}\right]{N_{i}}^{-1/2},

(28)

where
n_{h}
is the number density of the halo. The statistical uncertainty of
{P_{\times,i}^{\mathrm{sim}}}
is derived using
{P_{\times,i}^{\mathrm{sim}}}
itself,
\sigma_{F,i}
and
\sigma_{h,i}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\times,i}=\left[\left(P_{\times,i}^{\mathrm{sim}}\right)^{2}{N_{i}}^{-1}+\sigma_{F,i}\sigma_{h,i}\right]^{1/2}.

(29)

We note that these weights are merely estimators, and it is preferable to measure the covariance directly from the simulated data. However, measuring the covariance matrix will require a larger simulation box, which will be addressed in a future study.

4.1.3
Parameter estimation

We fit the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
auto-power spectrum using equations (
10
) and (
11

) as free parameters as described in Section

The linear bias parameter
b_{F}
decides the amplitude of the power spectrum. We note that the fluctuation of the transmitted flux
\delta_{F}
has anti-correlation with the density fluctuations of dark matter
\delta_{\mathrm{DM}}
in the linear regime, resulting in a negative bias parameter. The RSD parameter
\beta_{F}
characterizes
\mu
-dependence of the normalization of
P_{F}(k,\mu)
. Mode estimation of these two parameters shows overall consistency within
\sim 1\sigma
error among different astrophysical models. We see some deviations of
b_{F}
and
\beta_{F}
in each model from the Fiducial model in Fig.
4
; however, these are not necessarily consistent with the deviations of power spectrum on large scales (at small
k
) in Fig.
3
. This is because these linear parameters are degenerate with certain nuisance parameters in the fitting function (
11
) as our analysis does not sufficiently include large-scale modes
>100\,h^{-1}\mathrm{Mpc}
. For instance,
b_{F}
is degenerate with
q_{1}
and
\beta_{F}
is degenerate with
a_{v}
.
As a result, differences in the small-scale power spectrum can affect the linear parameters due to their degeneracy with nuisance parameters.

The parameter
q_{1}
is enhanced for CW and Shield models over other models. This is consistent with the stronger power of these two models observed in the left panel of Fig.
3
. The high
q_{1}
value of Shield model can be explained as the clumping structure of HI gas resulting from the self-
shielding effects.
The parameter
k_{p}
is higher for the Shield model over other models, suggesting the Jeans smoothing occurs at slightly smaller scale than other models. However, the parameter
k_{p}
just captures the
\mu
-independent suppression of the power. A reasonable explanation for the smaller suppression of power in the Shield model compared to the Fiducial model is that the HI gas remains un-ionized, leading to larger fluctuation amplitudes.
Parameters
k^{a_{v}}_{v}
,
a_{v}
and
b_{v}
capture the
\mu
-dependence on small scales. We do not find any significant differences in
k^{a_{v}}_{v}
and
a_{v}
among different models within
1\sigma
errors, whereas the Shield model shows significantly lower
b_{v}
value than other models. It implies a weaker
\mu
dependence on small scales, although we do not delve into the physical origin of it, since those are marginalized and not that important for the latter cosmological parameter estimation. However, it is worth noting that if we aim to constrain the astrophysical model using
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
power spectrum,
b_{\nu}
parameter would be informative.

5
Conclusions

In this study, we present measurements of the anisotropic
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
power spectrum and its cross-power spectrum with halos derived from a suite of cosmological hydrodynamic simulations encompassing five different astrophysical models.
As shown in Fig.
2
, the measured
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
auto power spectrum is fairly well fitted by equation (
10
) and the overall behaviour is consistent with the previous works
(
Arinyo-i-Prats et al., 2015
;
Givans
et al., 2022
, e.g.)
.
The impact of astrophysical model differences on the power spectrum becomes noticeable at smaller scales. As depicted in Fig.
3
, all models, except the Shield model, deviate from the Fiducial model by roughly
5\,\%
up to
k=10\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
. We offer the physical interpretations of some of these behaviours, such as the enhanced power in the Shield model for
\mu=0.03
, which can be attributed to the clumping structure of HI gas due to the self-shielding effects.
In Fig.
4
, we compare the best-fitting parameters across the five models.
For instance, the parameters
q_{1}
and
k_{p}
for the Shield model show significantly higher values compared to the other models, also due to the clumping structure of HI gas.

We also investigate the impact of different astrophysical models on the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,
halo cross-power spectrum, as shown in Fig.
6
. We find that the relative differences of
P_{\times}
among the different astrophysical models, exceeding 10%, are larger compared to those of
P_{F}
, as shown in Fig.
3
. However, as shown in Section
4.3
, the estimation of
f\sigma_{8}
using the
Givans
model is not impacted by these astrophysical uncertainties.

Next, we perform a joint fitting of the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
auto- and the
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha\,\times\,
halo cross-power spectrum using
Givans
model to estimate the growth rate parameter
f\sigma_{8}
. Fig.
7
shows the 2D contour plot of the parameter estimation resulting from the joint fitting. We observed the negative correlations between
b_{h}\sigma_{8}
and
\alpha
, as well as between
f\sigma_{8}
and
\gamma
, where
\alpha
and
\gamma
are the nuisance parameters in the
Givans
model. Refining this non-linear model of the cross-power spectrum to minimize the parameter degeneracies could improve the precision of the
f\sigma_{8}
estimation.

Finally, we conduct a fitting analysis under various conditions (varying
k_{\mathrm{max}}
and
M_{h}
) to assess the accuracy of
f\sigma_{8}
determination. As shown in Fig.
8
, the
Givans
model proves to be more robust to accurately reproduce
f\sigma_{8}
in most cases, except when
M_{h}>10^{10.5}M_{\odot}
at
k_{\mathrm{max}}=5.0\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
. In contrast, both the
Minimum
and
FoG
models do not provide accurate estimates. We also find that the
Givans
and
FoG
models can determine
f\sigma_{8}
without being affected by astrophysical uncertainties. However, despite the overall success of the
Givans
model, the statistical uncertainty in
f\sigma_{8}
estimation is significantly larger than that of other models. Therefore, the
FoG
model emerges as the best option for
M_{h}>10^{10.5}M_{\odot}
sample at
k_{\mathrm{max}}=1.0\,h\mathrm{Mpc}^{-1}
.

Our results support the assertion by
Givans
et al. (2022)
that an additional correction term (eq.
17
) is necessary when fitting
\mathrm{Ly}\alpha
cross-power spectrum with massive halos (
M_{h}>10^{11.5}M_{\odot}
). In contrast,
Cuceu et al. (2021)
adopted the
FoG
model in their forecast of RSD analysis and argued that using a smaller
r_{\mathrm{min}}
, the minimum separations used for fits, would improve
f\sigma_{8}
constraints. However, since massive halos host quasars, including small-scale data could actually hinder accurate estimates.
