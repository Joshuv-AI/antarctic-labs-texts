Improving Factuality in LLMs via Inference-Time Knowledge Graph Construction
Shanglin Wu, Lihui Liu, Jinho D. Choi, Kai Shu

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) often struggle with producing factually consistent answers due to limitations in their parametric memory. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) methods address this issue by incorporating external knowledge from trusted sources at inference time. However, such methods typically treat knowledge as unstructured text, which limits their ability to support compositional reasoning and identify factual inconsistencies. To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel framework that dynamically constructs and expands knowledge graphs (KGs) during inference, integrating both internal knowledge extracted from LLMs and external information retrieved from external sources. Our method begins by extracting a seed KG from the question via prompting, followed by iterative expansion using the LLM’s latent knowledge. The graph is then selectively refined through external retrieval, enhancing factual coverage and correcting inaccuracies. We evaluate our approach on three diverse factual QA benchmarks, demonstrating consistent improvements in factual accuracy, answer precision, and interpretability over baseline prompting and static KG-augmented methods. Our findings suggest that inference-time KG construction is a promising direction for enhancing LLM factuality in a structured, interpretable, and scalable manner. The code for this paper is available at https://github.com/ShanglinWu/autokg.git .

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding

Resent research indicates that even for relatively straightforward factual retrieval tasks, LLMs struggle with reliably grounding their responses
Huang et al. 2025
.

To this end, recent works reveal the effectiveness of merging structured data, especially knowledge graphs (KGs) at inference time to enhance LLMs’ factuality
Pan et al. 2024
Yao et al. 2023
Sun et al. 2023
. KGs represent facts as interconnected triples of entities and relations, offering explicit structure that supports compositional reasoning, and mitigates hallucination. Instead of relying purely on unstructured parametric memory, reasoning over structured KG enables LLMs to access and combine factual knowledge systematically. The graph-based representation of knowledge facilitates the identification of knowledge deficiencies
Zheng et al. 2023
, provides the potential to expand and refine the knowledge graph precisely during inference time, enabling more accurate answers to factual questions.

Many existing methods rely on pre-constructed knowledge graphs derived from static knowledge bases as their primary source of factual information
Sen et al. 2023
;
Baek et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023
. While these external sources are typically well-curated and offer domain-specific knowledge, constructing and maintaining them demands substantial human effort. This often limits scalability and adaptability, especially in fast-evolving knowledge contexts. In contrast, LLMs are trained on massive text corpora, which endow them with broad but implicit knowledge across diverse topics
Gekhman et al. 2025
. Although their internal knowledge bases may be less comprehensive or precise than curated external sources, they offer a key advantage: knowledge extraction from LLMs can be performed on-demand with minimal manual intervention, allowing for rapid, dynamic construction of knowledge graphs tailored to specific inference tasks. This contrast raises a natural question:

Can we combine the strengths of both paradigms leveraging the customizability and reliability of external knowledge bases with the adaptability and efficiency of LLM-derived knowledge graphs?
Such a hybrid approach could enable real-time KG construction and expansion during inference-time, improving LLMs’ factual reasoning ability while minimizing human labor.

To illustrate the core motivation and methodological differences, Figure

Compared to unstructured CoT reasoning, KGs make the reasoning process more explicit and interpretable. Moreover, incorporating external knowledge allows the model to revise missing or inaccurate triplets, thereby improving factual coverage and answer accuracy.

In this paper, we address the challenge of improving the factual reliability of LLMs by leveraging structured knowledge representations. We propose a novel pipeline that constructs and expands knowledge graphs dynamically during inference-time, drawing from both internal LLM representations and external knowledge bases. The main contributions of our work are:

•

Inference-Time Knowledge Graph Construction:
We present a method to extract factual triplets directly from LLMs’ internal knowledge through prompting, design an inference-time expansion strategy that iteratively extends the graph with relevant entities and relations. Our method is guided by the model’s evolving reasoning needs, thereby enhancing coverage and contextual relevance.

•

Hybrid Knowledge Integration:
We explore a unified approach that combines internal LLM-derived knowledge with curated external knowledge bases via knowledge graphs. This hybrid setup enhances factual grounding by compensating for gaps in either source.

•

Empirical Validation on Factual QA Tasks:
We evaluate our approach on multiple factual question answering benchmarks, demonstrating consistent improvements in answer accuracy and factual consistency.

Related Work

2.1
Construct KG by LLMs

Recent advances in LLMs have led to growing interest in using them for knowledge graph construction. Traditional KG construction is a labor-intensive task requiring extensive domain expertise, whereas LLMs offer the potential to automate this process by leveraging their pre-trained knowledge and natural language understanding.
Kommineni et al. 2024
introduce a semi-automatic pipeline where an LLM generates competency questions to guide ontology construction and triple extraction from academic texts.
Nie et al. 2024
employ chain-of-thought prompting to improve relation extraction consistency using predefined schemas, while enhancing graph quality through ontology alignment.
Xu et al. 2024
present Generate-on-Graph (GoG), a training-free method where an LLM dynamically augments incomplete KGs during question answering by injecting new triples based on its world knowledge. For data-scarce settings,
Li et al. 2024
propose FKGC, which synthesizes plausible triples for rare relations and distills them into smaller models with consistency checks.

LLM-based KG construction has also been explored in various domain-specific and QA-driven contexts.
Bai et al. 2025
construct a 2-million-node KG (KG-FM) from over 100,000 research papers using Qwen2-72B, enabling accurate QA through KG-augmented inference. Meanwhile,
Ding et al. 2024
propose pipelines for theme-specific and cybersecurity KGs, guided by domain-specific ontologies to enhance structure and accuracy. Complementing these text-based approaches,
Cohen et al. 2023
propose LMCRAWL, a method for extracting knowledge graphs directly from the internal knowledge of LLMs. Starting from seed entities, the model is prompted to recursively generate related triples, building a structured KG without relying on external corpora. Their results show that LLMs encode a substantial amount of factual knowledge that can be surfaced with high precision. Together, these works reflect a broader shift toward LLM-centric, structure-aware pipelines that combine prompt design, schema constraints, and minimal supervision to extract and utilize structured knowledge for tasks such as question answering and KG completion.

2.2
Knowledge Graphs for Factual QA

Recent works have highlighted the effectiveness of KGs in enhancing factual question answering.
Pan et al. 2024
propose a roadmap for unifying LLMs and KGs, arguing that structured, verified facts provided by KGs help mitigate hallucinations and ensure factual consistency. They demonstrate that retrieving from KGs supplements the parametric memory of LLMs and supports more accurate factual retrieval.
Yao et al. 2023
and
Sun et al. 2023
emphasize that the explicit graph structure enables compositional, multi-hop reasoning by linking related facts through traversable paths. Their studies show that reasoning over KGs naturally facilitates complex inference steps that are difficult for LLMs relying solely on free-text generation. Empirically,
Ma et al. 2025
introduce Debate-on-Graph (DoG), a KG-augmented reasoning framework, achieving a 23.7% improvement in accuracy over prior methods on WebQuestions. Their results highlight that incorporating KG-based reasoning loops leads to more systematic, interpretable, and fact-grounded QA performance.

2.3
Factual Question Answering

Factual question answering has been widely explored across different data sources and reasoning formats. Despite recent breakthroughs in LLMs, state-of-the-art models still face notable limitations in producing consistent and verifiable factual responses across QA benchmarks. These shortcomings have renewed interest in structured approaches such as knowledge graph-based QA to improve factual accuracy and reasoning transparency. Factual QA benchmarks are generally classified into three categories according to knowledge source:
Knowledge Base QA (KBQA)
focuses on answering questions from curated knowledge bases such as Freebase and Wikidata. Datasets like SimpleQuestions
Bordes et al. 2015
and WebQuestionsSP
Yih et al. 2016
provide natural language questions mapped to single-hop and multi-hop facts, while ComplexWebQuestions
Talmor and Berant 2018
and LC-QuAD 2.0
Dubey et al. 2019
introduce complex logical, comparative, and numerical queries.
Document-based QA
requires answering questions using evidence from unstructured text. HotpotQA
Yang et al. 2018
is a widely used multi-hop benchmark where models must synthesize information across multiple paragraphs, while ConditionalQA
Sun and et al. 2022
includes questions whose answers depend on specific conditions mentioned in context. DROP
Dua et al. 2019
poses challenges involving numerical, comparative, and logical reasoning.
Expert-curated QA
datasets, such as SimpleQA
Wei 2024
, are designed to assess factual precision under adversarial and high-accuracy settings. SimpleQA consists of simple fact-seeking questions with verifiable answers, carefully curated by human annotators and adversarially tested against GPT-4.

Existing KG-based QA methods typically rely on either (i) manually curated knowledge bases, or (ii) extraction of entities and relations only from the LLM’s parametric knowledge.
In contrast, our approach introduces two novel components:
(1) an automated pipeline that guides the LLM to iteratively construct and expand the knowledge graph from the input question without requiring predefined schemas, and
(2) an external grounding mechanism that integrates information from open-domain retrieval (Wikipedia, Google Search) to correct or expand incomplete or incorrect triplets. Formally, a Knowledge Graph (KG) is represented as
G=(s,r,o)\subseteq E\times R\times E
, where

R

(s,r,o)
consists of a subject-relation-object structure, where
s,o\in E
and
r\in R
. Additionally, we use
Q
to represent a given question, and
LM
denote a language model.

Graph Expansion:

G_{0}

External Retrieval:
When leveraging the knowledge graph to answer questions, the LLM may encounter incorrect or incomplete triplets, either containing factually incorrect entities (incorrect) or lacking sufficient detail and thus requiring further expansion to reach the correct answer (incomplete). To address this, the LLM modifies or expands the graph
G_{1}

(s,r,o)

G^{*}

Answering on the Graph:
The final step involves the LLM generating an answer to the question
Q

G^{*}
.

3.1
Graph Initialization

Given a question
Q
, the LLM is first prompted to extract relevant entities and relations. Specifically, the model is instructed:
"Extract meaningful entities and relations from the given question in order to answer it."
The LLM then returns a set of triplets that capture the core focus and informational intent of
Q
. These triplets are utilized to initialize the knowledge graph
G_{0}
, which forms the basis for the subsequent expansion process.

3.3
External Retrieval

Although the LLM can attempt to answer the original question
Q
using only the information encoded in the knowledge graph
G_{1}
, its internal knowledge base may be insufficient to generate a fully accurate answer. To address this limitation and enhance the factuality of the LLM’s responses, we introduce an external retrieval mechanism that incorporates knowledge from Wikipedia and Google Search.

The LLM is allowed to iteratively improve the knowledge graph up to a limited number of times. In each iteration, the LLM selects a triplet from the graph and chooses one of two possible actions:
Correct
or
Expand
.
Correct
refers to improving the accuracy of a triplet that may contain errors, while
Expand
involves adding new related triplets based on the existing ones. Specifically, we construct a search query from the selected triplet
(s,r,o)
in the form of ’s, r, o’, and retrieve the top
k
most relevant search results using the BM25 algorithm
Robertson et al. 2009
. The search context is then provided to the LLM, which returns the corrected or expanded triplet(s), which are merged into the graph. By leveraging external information, the LLM refines and updates the knowledge graph, resulting in an improved graph
G^{*}
.

3.4
Answering on the Graph

Once the knowledge graph
G^{*}
has been constructed and refined, the LLM is prompted to generate an answer to the question
Q
based on the final graph. This structured representation ensures that the LLM’s response is grounded in verifiable facts, whether they are internally derived or externally validated. All the relevant prompts used during this process are provided in Appendix
B

4.1
Datasets

The test questions should be simple and concise, with answers consisting of a single entity. This ensures that the module can accurately interpret the question, allowing the evaluation to focus on factual grounding. Considering the diversity of knowledge sources and the broad coverage of topics and entities, we select three representative datasets from Section
2.3
, encompassing Knowledge Base Question Answering (KBQA), Document-based QA, and Expert-curated QA.
Complex WebQuestions(CWQ)
Talmor and Berant 2018
is a dataset designed to evaluate the ability of question answering systems to decompose complex questions into simpler sub-questions and provide answers. It contains 34,689 complex questions, each paired with a SPARQL query executable against Freebase.
HotpotQA
Yang et al. 2018
is a large-scale dataset consisting of 113,000 crowd-sourced questions, each requiring reasoning over multiple supporting documents from the English Wikipedia. All questions have sentence-level supporting facts provided.
SimpleQA
Wei 2024
is a benchmark comprising 4,326 short, fact-seeking questions. Each question is designed to have a single, indisputable answer, and responses are graded as correct, incorrect, or not attempted. The dataset thus serves as a concise yet rigorous testbed for evaluating LLMs’ factual precision in QA systems.

4.2
Experiment Settings

In our experiments, we evaluate the performance of various models on CWQ-test, (3,519 questions), HotpotQA-dev(7,405 questions), and SimpleQA (4,326 questions). For HotpotQA-dev, we only provide questions to LLM without the support contexts. The evaluation metrics include Exact Match (EM) and LLM-assisted judge accuracy (Acc) on HotpotQA and CWQ, for SimpleQA we utilize simple-evals tools. For the LLM-assisted judge, we utilize GPT-4o-mini. The models under evaluation are GPT-4o, Deepseek-v3, Gemini-2.5-flash, Qwen2.5-32B-instruct, and Llama-4-scout. Main Results

Table

Internal), and (c) knowledge graph augmentation with external retrieval (KG w. External). These three settings reflect increasing levels of structure and factual grounding, progressing from purely unstructured reasoning to explicitly constructed and verifiable knowledge representations. Across all models and datasets, we observe a consistent and substantial improvement in performance when external retrieval is incorporated into the knowledge graph construction. This highlights the robustness and generality of our proposed approach. Specifically, our method yields significant gains over the standard CoT prompting baseline. For instance, Deepseek-V3 achieves a 12% absolute improvement in exact match on CWQ, and Llama-4-scout sees a remarkable 25.4% increase in accuracy on SimpleQA. These results are particularly notable given the diverse nature of the datasets.

However, the KG w. Internal Knowledge setting — where knowledge graphs are constructed solely from the LLM’s internal generations — does not consistently improve model performance. In some cases, it even leads to degradation. For example, GPT-4o achieves lower accuracy on SimpleQA under this setting compared to CoT prompting. This observation suggests that while constructing KGs from internal knowledge can help elicit factual content, the process is not robust enough to ensure consistent gains. Moreover, performance disparities are especially pronounced on SimpleQA. Notably, Qwen2.5-32B and LLama-4-scout both achieve under 10% accuracy when relying solely on internal knowledge. This underscores a critical limitation: although LLMs encode a vast amount of implicit factual knowledge, such knowledge is often incomplete, imprecise, or difficult to extract reliably.

By using knowledge graphs as a mediator between internal representations and external knowledge sources, our method raises the performance of smaller models to be on par with much larger ones. For instance, Llama-4-scout, when equipped with external KG retrieval, even surpasses both Deepseek-V3 and Gemini-2.5-flash on SimpleQA — despite having significantly fewer parameters. This demonstrates the potential of external KG augmentation to bridge the capability gap across LLMs of varying scales and settings.

Limitations of Internal Knowledge Alone.

To better understand the contribution of external retrieval in improving knowledge coverage of knowledge graphs, we compute the recall of the constructed graphs—i.e., the proportion of groundtruth appear in the graphs. The results are shown in Table
2
. Across all datasets and models, the recall consistently improves when external sources are integrated into the graph construction pipeline. For instance, on the SimpleQA dataset, Llama-4-scout shows a substantial recall increase from 9.3% to 41.2%, a gain of +31.9 points. Similarly, Qwen2.5-32B improves by +28.8 points on SimpleQA. These gains are particularly prominent on SimpleQA, a dataset with sparse but precise fact requirements, which highlights the brittleness of relying solely on internal LLM knowledge. Even for stronger models like GPT-4o and Deepseek-V3, external retrieval leads to consistent improvements across CWQ and HotpotQA. These findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on internal LLM knowledge and demonstrate the substantial benefits brought by external retrieval. The structured nature of knowledge graphs allows external retrieval to operate at the granularity of individual triplets, making the retrieval process more fine-grained and controllable. This design enables the system to achieve significant performance gains with minimal retrieval overhead, offering an efficient and scalable pathway to enhance factual coverage in LLM-based QA.

4.4
Ablation Study

Effect of number of hops

HotpotQA is a multi-hop factual QA dataset in which each question is annotated with the number of reasoning hops required to arrive at the correct answer. To further investigate how this reasoning complexity impacts performance, we analyze the KG w. External method by examining both LLM-judged answer accuracy and the size of the constructed knowledge graphs, measured by the number of triplets per graph. As shown in Figure
3
, we observe a clear trend: as the number of hops increases, the average graph size grows while the accuracy of the model’s answers declines.

This behavior suggests that more complex questions, which require multi-hop reasoning across multiple facts, lead to generate larger initial graphs in an attempt to capture all potentially relevant entities and relations. These larger initial graphs, when further expanded and contribute to the overall increase in graph size. However, the expanded graph introduces more candidate paths and entities, which makes it increasingly difficult for the model to identify the correct answer node with high precision. The decline in accuracy reflects this challenge, revealing a trade-off between broader coverage through larger graphs and the precision required for accurate factual retrieval.

Effect of model scale

We further investigate the relationship between model size, accuracy, and graph recall, as shown in Figure
4
. We select three models with varying sizes: Llama-4-scout (17B), Qwen2.5 (32B), and Deepseek-v3 (671B). Deepseek-v3 achieves the highest accuracy and graph recall across all hop levels, demonstrating the advantage of a larger internal knowledge base. Notably, it also exhibits the largest gap between accuracy and recall, indicating that, given a fixed amount of information in the graph, Deepseek-v3 is more effective at activating relevant internal knowledge. In contrast, Qwen2.5 performs the weakest, and as the hop count increases, its graph recall even surpasses its accuracy, suggesting a high sensitivity to reasoning difficulty and a relatively weak ability to leverage graph structure. Llama-4-scout shows greater robustness to hop complexity increase, indicating stronger capability in retrieving useful information from the graph in multi-hop scenarios.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we present a pipeline for inference-time knowledge graph construction that integrates LLM-derived and external knowledge. This approach improves factual accuracy, interpretability, and robustness in QA, with experiments showing its effectiveness in bridging knowledge gaps and supporting multi-hop reasoning.

6
Limitations

Several limitations remain for our work: (a) When constructing knowledge graphs from the LLM’s internal knowledge base, there is a risk of generating hallucinated content. Such noise can propagate through the graph and lead to incorrect answers. (b) A noticeable gap persists between graph recall and exact match performance, indicating that current graph retrieval mechanisms are still suboptimal. Improving the accuracy and relevance of graph construction and retrieval may further enhance overall performance.

7
Ethical Consideration

Our work focuses on enhancing factual grounding in LLM-based QA systems.
Potential benefits include improved trustworthiness in applications like education, healthcare, and legal domains. However, misapplication might lead to overreliance on automated answers or misuse in misinformation contexts.
We mitigate these risks by grounding responses in verifiable external sources and making reasoning chains interpretable. We also ensure that any dataset used complies with licensing and privacy regulations.
Finally, all datasets used in our study are publicly released for academic research. HotpotQA is distributed under Apache License 2.0, Complex WebQuestions (CWQ) is released under the Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike (CC-BY-SA) license, and SimpleQA is distributed under the MIT License. We do not collect or redistribute any data from private or user-generated sources. Any content retrieved externally (e.g., via Google Search or Wikipedia) is used only at inference time for model augmentation and is not retained, repackaged, or released. Such use aligns with fair use principles for academic research.

You are an AI assistant for grading a factual question-answering task. You will receive a question, a model’s prediction, and one or more correct answers. Your goal is to judge whether the prediction is factually aligned with any of the ground truths.

Do not require an exact wording match. Accept synonyms, abbreviations, paraphrases, or contextually correct answers. Even if the prediction is only part of the full ground truth, or includes extra information, consider it correct as long as the key factual content is accurate.

If multiple ground truths are given, matching any of them is enough. Use common sense and background knowledge. Only mark the prediction wrong if it clearly contradicts the facts.

Question:

[question]

Prediction:

[prediction]

Ground Truth(s):

[ground_truth]

Explain your reasoning, and end your response on a new line with only "Yes" or "No" (without quotes)
