The effect of adding pancreatin to standard otilinium bromide and simethicone treatment in type 2 diabetes mellitus patients with irritable bowel syndrome
Authors: Hulusi Can Karpuzcu, Beril Turan Erdoğan, Çağdaş Erdoğan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to assess the impact of adding pancreatin (a pancreatic enzyme derivative) to standard treatment on patients diagnosed with Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM). IBS and T2DM frequently coexist, leading to significant gastrointestinal symptoms and a decrease in quality of life. Gastrointestinal symptoms arising in T2DM patients present challenges in management for both clinicians and patients. Standard treatments may not fully address these symptoms. Recent studies suggest that pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy may offer additional benefits. This study investigates whether adding pancreatin to standard treatment can improve gastrointestinal outcomes in this patient population. Conducted as a prospective observational study, the research involved patients diagnosed with IBS according to Rome IV criteria and having concomitant T2DM. The patients were divided into two groups: one receiving standard dual treatment (otilinium bromide + simethicone) and the other receiving a triple therapy including a pancreatin derivative in addition to the standard treatment. Various clinical scores and measurements, including Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Bristol Stool Chart (BSC), Irritable Bowel Syndrome-Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS), and Irritable Bowel Syndrome Quality of Life Instrument (IBS-QOL), were assessed before and after treatment. The study comprised 121 patients, with a median age of 41 years (interquartile range [IQR] 38–48), of whom approximately 70.2% were female. Fifty-eight patients (47.9%) received dual therapy, while sixty-three patients (52.1%) received triple therapy. Before treatment, both groups exhibited similar pre-treatment Bristol stool scale results: normal (39.7% and 49.2%) and constipation (37.9% and 31.7%) in the dual and triple therapy subgroups, respectively (p > 0.05). Post-treatment, the triple therapy group showed a significantly higher proportion of normal cases on the Bristol stool scale (82.5%) compared to the dual therapy group (44.8%) (p < 0.001). After treatment, the triple therapy group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in VAS score (p < 0.001), IBS-SSS (p < 0.001), and IBS-QOL (p < 0.001) compared to the dual therapy group. There were no significant differences between groups in BMI, HbA1c levels, duration of diabetes, or diabetes treatment at baseline (p > 0.05 for all parameters). The findings suggest that adding pancreatin to standard therapies significantly enhanced the quality of life for patients with both IBS and T2DM, demonstrating improvements across various symptom measurements and indicating the potential benefits of this supplementary therapy in managing gastrointestinal symptoms in this population. Further studies are warranted to explore its broader clinical implications and mechanisms of action.

Introduction

The intricate relationship between Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) presents overlapping symptoms and challenges that significantly impact patients’ quality of life and gastrointestinal health. IBS, recognized as a prevalent functional gastrointestinal disorder affecting approximately 11% of the global population1, presents with distressing symptoms such as chronic abdominal pain and disruptive changes in bowel habits. On a parallel front, individuals diagnosed with T2DM encounter a heightened susceptibility to various gastrointestinal complications that encompass dysmotility issues throughout the gastrointestinal (GI) tract2. This encompasses a spectrum from esophageal dysmotility to compromised gastric and intestinal motility, often leading to delayed gastric emptying, dysmotility along the small bowel, colon, and rectum, and complications such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

Apart from diabetes-related motility disorders such as diabetic gastroparesis, it is well recognized that T2DM patients have a variety of gastrointestinal symptoms that may overlap with IBS symptoms and complicate clinical management. Studies have notably highlighted that individuals with T2DM frequently report a range of gastrointestinal symptoms, including dysphagia, reflux, retrosternal burning, abdominal discomfort, early satiety, as well as episodes of nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation3. Notably, Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI), a condition sharing symptomatic similarities with IBS, manifests through symptoms like abdominal pain, bloating, excessive gas, and diarrhea. Its prevalence stands notably higher, reported between 28 and 54% among individuals diagnosed with T2DM4. Additionally, a specific study by Leeds et al. has indicated the presence of exocrine pancreatic insufficiency in about 6% of IBS patients5.

A variety of treatment options are available for IBS, including dietary modifications, antispasmodic drugs, probiotics, tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, and behavioral therapies6. Otilonium is a quaternary ammonium compound that exhibits antispasmodic properties. It inhibits T-type calcium channels, thereby inducing relaxation of the gastrointestinal smooth muscles. Furthermore, otilonium has been demonstrated to bind to tachykinin NK2 receptors, thereby inhibiting a primary contractile agent and reducing the activation of afferent nerves responsible for transmitting sensory signals from the periphery to the central nervous system. Due to their distinctive physico-chemical characteristics, otilonium compounds exhibit minimal absorption into the systemic circulation, with the majority remaining in the gastrointestinal tract, where they exert their muscle relaxant effects through local action7. Otilonium bromide has been demonstrated to be an efficacious treatment for abdominal discomfort and bloating in patients with IBS, and it has been shown to provide long-term protection against relapse8. Simethicone is an antifoaming agent that has been in use for approximately 60 years. It reduces the surface tension of gas in the intestines, allowing small gas bubbles to coalesce. Its efficacy in the treatment of functional dyspepsia is well established9. These properties enable simethicone to facilitate the passage of gas and alleviate bloating symptoms. However, the effect of simethicone on IBS symptoms other than gas and bloating when used alone is limited, and it is typically employed as part of combination therapies. In a meta-analysis conducted by Martinez and colleagues, which included 2,585 patients, the combination of simethicone and otilonium also demonstrated a notable improvement in global symptoms in the treatment of IBS10. This combination therapy is recognized as a standard treatment option for managing IBS symptoms.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) is a common treatment for improving nutrient absorption and associated symptoms, particularly in patients with pancreatic exocrine insufficiency. This treatment enhances fat and protein absorption, leading to a reduction in fecal fat and nitrogen excretion. Consequently, it has been shown to have beneficial effects, including the alleviation of gastrointestinal symptoms and an improvement in quality of life11.

The objective of this study was to investigate the occurrence of pancreatic enzyme abnormalities following the consumption of a high-fat meal in patients diagnosed with functional dyspepsia (FD) and IBS-FD overlap, as defined by the Rome IV criteria. The study also sought to examine the potential role of duodenal inflammation and protease-activated receptor (PAR) expression in this phenomenon. It was proposed that pancreatic enzyme abnormalities and PAR2 expression in degranulated eosinophil infiltrations in the duodenum may contribute to the pathophysiology of patients with IBS-FD overlap12. This study indicates that PAR2 activation may result from pancreatic enzyme abnormalities in IBS-FD patients, and thus duodenal inflammation resulting from eosinophil infiltration may lead to symptoms in IBS-FD patients.

In addressing gastrointestinal concerns linked with T2DM and IBS, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy stands as a vital intervention. This therapy primarily aims to counteract malabsorption symptoms and rectify digestive disorders13. Extensive research has shown its contributions toward glycemic control in diabetes patients while concurrently improving various gastrointestinal symptoms. This therapeutic approach is advocated not solely for its glycemic benefits but also for its positive impact on alleviating abdominal symptoms, enhancing serum vitamin D levels, and rectifying nutritional imbalances among diabetic populations14.

Moreover, studies have indicated the efficacy of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy in ameliorating stool frequency, stool consistency, and alleviating abdominal pain among IBS patients5. These findings collectively underscore the potential merits of exploring pancreatin, a derivative of pancreatic enzymes, as an adjunct to standard treatments in individuals grappling with the complexities of both IBS and T2DM. The management of gastrointestinal symptoms in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) presents a significant challenge due to the complex pathophysiology of both conditions. The aim of our study is precisely to investigate the potential effects and benefits of incorporating pancreatin into the standard therapeutic regimen for patients navigating the challenging intersection of IBS and T2DM. The hypothesis is that the combined effects of these three agents may result in superior outcomes in the management of gastrointestinal symptoms in patients with T2DM and IBS.

Methods

Study design and patient population

Our study was designed as a prospective observational study. Patients older than 18 years who presented at the gastroenterology outpatient clinic of Ankara Etlik City Hospital between November 2023 and February 2024 were evaluated for eligibility for the study. Patients diagnosed with IBS according to Rome IV criteria and patients with concomitant T2DM were included in the study sequentially.

One group (n = 58) was started on standard dual treatment (otilinium bromide + simethicone) and the other group (n = 63) was started on a pancreatic enzyme derivative (pancreatin) in addition to standard treatment.

Patients in the dual treatment group received 40 mg otilinium bromide + 80 mg simethicone tablets three times a day 20 min before meals. Patients in the triple treatment group received 40 mg otilinium bromide + 80 mg simethicone tablets three times a day 20 min before meals. In addition to this treatment, pancreatin treatment in tablets containing 5500 IU amylase + 6500 IU lipase + 400 protease was started as two tablets three times a day with meals.

Exclusion criteria were as follows Under 18 years of age, pregnant women, puerperium, patients with known organic GI system disease, patients with GI system malignancy, patients who have undergone GI system surgery, patients with inflammatory bowel disease, patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus, patients with diagnosed GI system motility disorder (including diabetic gastroparesis), patients who do not use the initiated treatment appropriately, having GI alarm findings, having a history of overt chronic pancreatitis.

Ethics declarations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ankara Etlik City Hospital (Approval No. AEŞH-EK1-2023-494), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians prior to their inclusion in the study. This research adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Baseline and follow-up measurements

The data from two patient visits, four weeks apart, were analyzed. We evaluated the scores on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), the Bristol Stool Chart (BSC), the Irritable Bowel Syndrome -Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS) and the Irritable Bowel Syndrome Quality of Life Instrument (IBS-QOL) before and after treatment (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were made by using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). The normality of the distribution of numerical variables was analyzed via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. After, normally distributed numerical variables were given as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons were made via the Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed numerical variables were expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and intergroup comparisons were made via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were defined as frequency (percentage), and intergroup comparisons were made via the Chi-Square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Clinical scores and categorical variables before and after treatment were compared for each subgroup by using the Paired sample t-test or the Wilcoxon test or McNemar test, as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Patients receiving dual therapy had a significantly longer duration of T2DM than those receiving triple therapy (8 (5-10.25) years and 6 (4–9) years, respectively, p = 0.016). The most used T2DM drug in the study group and subgroups was metformin, followed by Dipeptidyl Peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors (Table 1).

The most common type of IBS observed in both the study group and subgroups was constipation. Upon analyzing the clinical scores of patients undergoing dual and triple therapy, the pre-treatment Bristol stool scale results were normal (n = 23 (39.7%) and n = 31 (49.2%), respectively) and constipation (n = 22 (37.9%) and n = 20 (31.7%), respectively) in both subgroups and there was no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05). After treatment, the dual treatment group had 26 (44.8%) normal cases, 19 (32.8%) cases of constipation, and no cases of diarrhea. In contrast, the triple treatment group had 52 (82.5%) normal cases, 11 (17.5%) cases of constipation, and no cases of diarrhea. This difference between the groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001). There was no statistically significant difference in the other scoring in the pre-treatment evaluations (p > 0.05, for all parameters). After treatment, both the VAS score and IBS-SSS were significantly lower in the triple treatment group than in the dual treatment group. Moreover, the triple treatment group showed a statistically significant increase in the IBS-QOL score across all measurements compared to the dual treatment group (p < 0.001 for all parameters). No statistically significant difference was found between the groups in other data (p > 0.05 for all parameters) (Table 1).

Discussion

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) stand as prevalent conditions within communities, often intertwined with gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms seen frequently in T2DM patients. The complicating factor of Exocrine Pancreatic Insufficiency (EPI) adds a layer of complexity for clinicians managing both T2DM and IBS. However, the definitive role of EPI therapies in alleviating GI symptoms and enhancing the quality of life for individuals contending with both IBS and T2DM remains uncertain. Our study set out to investigate the impact of pancreatin treatment, coupled with established therapies known for their efficacy in managing IBS and T2DM. Through comprehensive assessments, we observed substantial enhancements across various parameters, showcasing significant improvements in Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Irritable Bowel Syndrome - Severity Scoring System (IBS-SSS), and Irritable Bowel Syndrome Quality of Life Instrument (IBS-QOL) scores. This translated to an overall remarkable improvement in the quality of life for individuals affected by these conditions.

Within the study cohort and its subgroups, constipation emerged as the predominant subtype of Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS). Prior to treatment, the Bristol stool scale predominantly indicated normal stool consistency, followed by constipation as the second most prevalent result. Remarkably, the triple treatment intervention led to the complete alleviation of diarrhea symptoms in the respective patient group. Furthermore, post-treatment evaluations revealed a notable increase in the frequency of normal stool consistency among individuals receiving the triple therapy regimen, indicating a tangible improvement in bowel habits and consistency following this specific treatment approach. These findings underscore the substantial impact and efficacy of the triple therapy intervention in resolving not only diarrhea but also in promoting more normalized stool patterns among the study participants with IBS.

In a study conducted in patients with IBS with diarrhea (IBS-D) according to Rome II criteria (n = 314) and investigating the presence of EPI with fecal elastase measurements, the frequency of EPI in patients with IBS-D was reported to be approximately 6%5. Emmanuel et al. retrospectively evaluated more than 3500 IBS patients according to Rome III criteria and showed that fecal biomarkers can give abnormal results between 5 and 13%15. In a study investigating the presence of undiagnosed EPI in patients with functional GI disease, Talley et al. found that 4.6% (n = 10) of 218 patients had low fecal elastase16. In patients with T2DM, the situation is different and EPI has been reported to have a high prevalence of 28–54%4. Symptoms such as bloating, nausea and diarrhea are common problems in diabetic patients. These symptoms may have many causes such as autonomic neuropathy, concomitant celiac disease, concomitant EPI and anti-diabetic drug side effects. In a large systematic review by Zsóri et al. in 2018, it was emphasized that although pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy in patients with T2DM has some benefits on glycemic control, this has not reached a sufficient level of evidence and that treatment should be given more for its effects such as improvement of abdominal symptoms and serum vitamin D levels14. A recent large review reported that the prevalence of EPI is higher in patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) compared to T2DM17. This may be related to the long disease duration seen in T1DM patients. However, in a study conducted in newly diagnosed adult diabetic patients, it was reported that magnetic resonance imaging measurements revealed that pancreatic volume indices were about 26% less than healthy volunteers18. There is also a study showing that pancreatic steatosis may be a cause of EPI19. In our study, although the duration of diabetes disease was significantly shorter in the group receiving pancreatin-containing triple therapy, it is noteworthy that there was a significant improvement after treatment. This may be related to the onset of EPI findings starting from the diagnosis of diabetes especially in our overweight patient population with a body mass index > 25 in patients with pancreatic steatosis. Although there was a statistically significant difference, there was no clinically significant difference between the duration of diabetes in the groups (8 years in the dual treatment group and 6 years in the triple treatment group). It cannot be ruled out that the duration of diabetes may contribute to the treatment outcomes, but the significant differences in all parameters of the scoring systems cannot be explained by the duration of diabetes alone.

There are several studies in the literature showing the benefit of pancreatic enzyme replacement in IBS patients. In a study by Money et al. evaluating 49 patients, it was shown that the administration of pancreatic lipase to post-prandial IBS patients with diarrhea improved symptoms such as abdominal cramping, bloating, global pain, and number of stools20. However, the presence of concomitant diabetes was not examined in this study. In the previously mentioned study, which reported a prevalence of EPI of around 6%, improvement in stool consistency, stool frequency and abdominal pain was reported after pancreatic enzyme replacement5. However, in this study, the diabetic population was not specifically analyzed, and quality of life scales were not used. An earlier study by Suarez et al. investigated the effects of pancreatin supplementation after a high-fat meal in 18 healthy volunteers. In this study, it was shown that postprandial gas and bloating complaints seen after a fatty meal can be reduced with pancreatin supplementation21. This suggests that pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy may improve some dyspeptic complaints independent of the underlying overt pancreatic disease. In a recent study by Olmos et al., 140 patients were evaluated using current scoring systems such as Rome IV criteria, fecal elastase values and IBS-SSS and the prevalence of EPI in IBS-D patients was investigated. In this study, the prevalence of EPI was found to be around 5% and it was reported that stool frequency, stool consistency, abdominal pain symptoms and IBS-SSS scores improved after pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy22. However, only 12.9% of the patients in this study were reported to be diabetic and no evaluation was made about the diabetic subgroup. In 2018, Graham et al. reviewed studies over the past 60 years and emphasized that various enzyme supplements have been known to be beneficial in the treatment of postprandial symptoms seen in IBS for many years, but studies using validated scoring systems are needed to prove it with definitive evidence23. As reported in the same review, it was found that lipase was not used in the appropriate form and dose in the content of multi-enzyme supplements claimed to be successful and it was thought that success may be related to other enzymes other than lipase. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first detailed study evaluating a treatment containing an appropriate form of lipase in patients with IBS and T2DM using quality scales and new scoring systems.

As summarized above, most studies in the literature examining the relationship between IBS and EPI were designed in IBS patients with diarrhea-dominant symptoms. However, in our study, IBS-D patients were found to be around 20% according to the Bristol stool scale before treatment. In the group receiving triple therapy, diarrhea symptoms significantly regressed after treatment, in line with previous studies. In addition, unlike other studies, a positive response to treatment was observed in the groups with normal Bristol stool scale and constipation. This suggests that pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy may be beneficial in the presence of early diabetic EPI in whom EPI symptoms have not yet fully manifested.

Studies investigating the frequency of EPI in IBS patients are usually designed based on fecal elastase measurements. The enzyme elastase is produced in the pancreas and is readily detectable in feces. Although it has many clinical practical advantages over gold standard tests in the diagnosis of EPI, fecal elastase measurements have some disadvantages. Since fecal elastase measurements are based on concentration, it is naturally detected at lower concentrations in the case of watery stool. This increases false positive rates, especially in IBS-D patients24. A meta-analysis examining fecal elastase measurements in the diagnosis of EPI showed that although the false negative rate of this test was 1.1%, the false positive rate could be up to 11%25. Considering all this information about fecal elastase, it shows that the EPI rates detected in studies conducted in IBS patients in the literature may be lower. Therefore, we have doubts about the proven benefit of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapies in IBS patients without a diagnosis of diabetes.

While our study did not extensively delve into pancreatic diseases within our patient cohort, we rigorously examined their medical backgrounds, encompassing inquiries related to chronic alcohol consumption, a history of chronic gallstones, atypical pain incongruent with typical IBS symptoms, and a comprehensive family medical history. Patients exhibiting suggestive symptoms of overt chronic pancreatitis were deliberately excluded from our study to maintain cohort homogeneity. However, our study demonstrated several strengths, including the comprehensive assessment of patients utilizing robust quality scales and novel scoring systems pre- and post-treatment. Adhering to the latest Rome IV classification criteria, our study was also attentive to potential confounding factors such as HbA1c levels and body mass index, ensuring their consistent distribution within the patient cohort. Moreover, our study employed standardized evaluation forms and recommended treatment doses, contributing to the reliability of our findings. Notably, our study represents the pioneering effort, to the best of our knowledge, in investigating the IBS population specifically within the realm of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), enriching the scientific understanding in this understudied intersection of conditions.

The precise pathophysiologic basis of the ameliorative effect of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy on IBS symptoms remains unclear. Microinflammation, eosinophil and mast cell infiltration play a significant role in the pathogenesis, particularly in patients with functional dyspepsia26. Agawa et al. proposed that elevated trypsin levels may be a contributing factor to duodenal inflammation through PAR2-mediated degranulated eosinophils, as evidenced by the examination of duodenal samples from patients with pancreatic enzyme abnormalities using real-time PCR and immunohistochemistry staining techniques27. In a study by Futagami et al. involving 93 patients in an Asian population, it was proposed that PAR2 expression in degranulated eosinophil infiltrations in the duodenum resulting from pancreatic enzyme abnormalities after a high-fat diet may play a role in the pathophysiology of patients with IBS-FD overlap12. This study revealed that the observed changes were more pronounced in patients with IBS-FD overlap compared to those with isolated FD. In conclusion, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy may alleviate IBS symptoms and improve patients’ quality of life by correcting digestive problems caused by dysfunctional enzyme production of the pancreas and reducing duodenal inflammation. It may also prevent exacerbation of IBS symptoms by reducing postprandial complaints of gas and bloating after fatty meals. Further studies, both at the molecular level and clinically, are required to elucidate these pathophysiological bases.

Our study has limitations as we did not conduct extensive research on pancreatic diseases among our patient group. However, we thoroughly investigated their medical history, including inquiries regarding chronic alcohol use, a history of chronic gallstones, atypical pain that was inconsistent with IBS symptoms, their family medical history, and patients who displayed symptoms suggestive of overt chronic pancreatitis were excluded. Due to the observational design of our study, there is concern about bias between the treatment groups. However, by evaluating the patients using various quality scales and new scoring systems that allow for comprehensive assessment both before and after treatment, we aimed to achieve a high degree of standardization in treatment responses, thereby minimizing these concerns. The single-center nature of our study limits the generalizability of the results. The strengths of our study lie in the evaluation of patients using quality scales and new scoring systems both before and after treatment, adherence to the latest classification criteria of Rome IV, awareness of potential confounding characteristics like HbA1c and body mass index and their uniform distribution, use of standardized forms and recommended treatment doses, and being the first to our knowledge to study the IBS population with T2DM.

Conclusion

Our study unraveled the intricate relationship between Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) and Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), revealing the potential benefits of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy. We observed significant improvements in patients’ well-being, as evidenced by enhanced scores on various quality-of-life measures. Notably, constipation emerged as the predominant IBS subtype, and the triple treatment regimen effectively resolved diarrhea while promoting normalized stool consistency, even in patients without evident symptoms. Although our study had limitations in exploring pancreatic diseases fully, our meticulous screening process and adherence to rigorous assessment criteria strengthen the credibility of our findings. This pioneering effort sheds light on a promising therapeutic pathway for individuals navigating the complexities of IBS and T2DM, offering hope for tailored treatment strategies in this understudied realm.
