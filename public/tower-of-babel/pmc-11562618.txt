Familial Mediterranean Fever in Childhood
Authors: Rabia Miray Kisla Ekinci, Elif Kilic Konte, Nergis Akay, Umit Gul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is the most prevalent monogenic autoinflammatory disorder, characterized by recurrent fever and serositis. It primarily affects individuals of Mediterranean descent, including Arabs, Armenians, Turks, and Jews. The Mediterranean fever (MEFV) gene, responsible for FMF, was discovered in 1997. Biallelic pathogenic variants lead to excessive activation of the pyrin inflammasome, resulting in inflammation.Clinical manifestations include recurrent fever, abdominal pain, and joint involvement, with attacks typically lasting 12-72 hours. Diagnosis relies on clinical criteria and is supported by genetic testing. Colchicine is the primary treatment to reduce attack frequency and prevent the complications like renal amyloidosis.Despite advancements in understanding FMF, including its genetic basis and treatment options, challenges remain in distinguishing it from other autoinflammatory diseases. Co-existing conditions such as juvenile idiopathic arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease are common among FMF patients. Ongoing research should aim to clarify the development of the disease, enhance diagnostic accuracy, and address its clinical presentation and genetic variability, with a focus on identifying new genetic mutations and epigenetic factors that contribute to its pathogenesis.

Introduction

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF), known as the most common monogenic autoinflammatory disorder worldwide, was first described in the early 20th century. Familial Mediterranean fever is characterized by self-limiting attacks of recurrent fever, accompanied by serositis, substantially starting at the childhood. After the first definitions, such as “toxemia of endogenous origin” and “benign paroxysmal peritonitis,” the periodicity of the disease attacks was emphasized by Reimann in 1948.1 Afterwards, in 1958, Heller recognized the aggregation of the FMF cases in the Mediterranean basin, including the ethnicities, Arabs, Armenians, Turks, and Jews, and therefore named the disease as “familial Mediterranean fever.”2

The effect of colchicine was first described in 1972, indicating that colchicine decreased the attack frequency.3 Afterwards, it was suggested that colchicine also reduces the risk of renal amyloidosis, which was the most common complication of FMF if left untreated.4 Moreover, in 1997, Mediterranean fever (MEFV) gene, coding the pyrin/marenostrin protein, was identified as the gene responsible for the FMF disease by the French and International study groups. After this discovery, it was formerly thought that FMF causes a homogeneous phenotype, inherited by an autosomal recessive pattern of loss-of function biallelic variants in MEFV gene.5,6 However, we now recognize that this discovery was only the tip of the iceberg since there were many patients with only 1 pathogenic variant in the MEFV gene, resulting in a milder phenotype of FMF. Moreover, recent studies suggested that pyrin has its inflammasome, which causes caspase activated interleukin 1β (IL1β) maturation in the presence of pathogenic gain-of function variants.7 Although there are still many unknown aspects of the disease, today’s clinicians are better to be able to diagnose and manage FMF more effectively than before, with the up-to-date recommendations and treatment options.

This review aimed to provide up-to-date information on the disease follow-up to clinicians in general pediatrics and pediatric rheumatology for pediatric patients with FMF by evaluating the current information on the pathogenesis, clinical findings, differential diagnosis and treatment of FMF disease. We conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed/MEDLINE and Scopus using the keywords “familial Mediterranean fever,” “childhood,” and “autoinflammatory diseases” from the inception of the databases to September 16, 2024, in line with the recommended approach for narrative reviews. Our search was limited to English-language articles.

Epidemiology

Once it was hypothesized that FMF originated from the middle east and spread from there, since the highest frequency of the cases and carrier rates are encountered in ethnicities once lived in Mesopotamia more than 3000 years ago8 After the discovery of the MEFV gene in 1997, large cohorts of FMF patients from Turkey, Israel and Armenia have been reported so far. Epidemiological studies show higher carrier rates and disease frequency in these ethnicities, while only a few cases have been reported from other parts of the world.9-11

The estimated prevalence of FMF is reported as 1/1000 in Israel, whereas 1/500 in Armenia. The carrier rate is approximately 1/5-1/7 in these populations.9,12 In Turkey, prevalence is higher in Black Sea and Central Anatolia regions, reaching up to 1/395 in Sivas.13,14

The first inflammatory attack of FMF is usually experienced in childhood, in 90% of patients before the age of 20.15 Patients whose symptoms start before 20 years of age have more severe disease and M694V variant.16 The mean age at disease onset was reported as 9.6 years, while the diagnosis was delayed for approximately 7 years.10 There is no gender predilection for the development of FMF, and gender does not affect the disease onset age and disease activity.10,16

Etiology and Pathogenesis

Familial Mediterranean fever is a monogenic autoinflammatory disease caused by pathogenic variants in the MEFV gene. Pyrin protein, encoded by MEFV gene, is a cytoplasmic protein expressed in monocytes, granulocytes, dendritic cells, and synovial fibroblasts. Pathogenic variants in the MEFV gene, especially in exon 10, cause excessive activation of pyrin inflammasome and caspase 1 activation, thus oversecreting of IL1β.7 The pyrin protein encoded by the MEFV gene consists of 5 components: PYD or PYRIN domain, bZIP transcription factor domain, B-box zinc finger, α-helix coiled-coil domain, and a C-terminal B30.2 domain. Pyrin protein is phosphorylated and inactive under normal conditions. However, as a result of bacterial toxin stimulation or pathogenic mutation in the MEFV gene (mostly seen in B30.2 domain), the pyrin protein is dephosphorylated and activated. Activated pyrin protein (especially with PYD domain) is able to combine with ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein with a caspase recruitment domain) and Caspase 1 to form the pyrin inflammasome. As a result of the activation of the pyrin inflammasome, the IL1β precursor is converted into active IL1-β and released in excessive amounts.17,18

There is increasing evidence that environmental factors may significantly influence the development and clinical manifestations of FMF through epigenetic modifications. Variants in other genes associated with various inflammatory pathways, along with epigenetic changes, may account for the variability in symptom severity, age at disease onset, and the emergence of accompanying inflammatory conditions and comorbidities among FMF patients, even among individuals within the same family sharing the same genotype.19-22

Genetics

To date, there are more than 300 variants, reported in MEFV gene, which consists of 10 exons, located in the short arm of the 16thchromosome. These variants are listed in the Infevers Database, and categorized as “Pathogenic,” “Likely Pathogenic,” “Variant of Unknown Significance” (VUS), “Likely Benign” and “Benign,” according to the pathogenicity scores.23,24 Although there are also pathogenic variants in other exons, most of the cases have the founder mutations, located in the exon 10 of MEFV gene, coding the C-terminal B30.2 domain of pyrin. These are M694V, M680I, M694I and V726A. In Turkey, large cohorts revealed that the most common variant is M694V, encountered in 43-55% of the children with FMF.23,25,26 Formerly, the clinicians could only perform targeted sanger sequencing for a few pathogenic variants, currently next generation sequencing enabled to screen for all variants in all exons. This improvement led to more accurate diagnosis of FMF patients, but also may increase the overdiagnosis with identification of VUS. Therefore, it should be kept in mind that diagnosis of FMF is based on clinical manifestations and supported by the presence of pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in MEFV gene analysis.27

An important issue is the E148Q variant, a VUS located in exon 2 is more frequently encountered in healthy subjects and patients in far east countries, where FMF is scarce and sometimes not related to the disease.28 Therefore, even in the homozygosity, E148Q should not lead to FMF diagnosis until other possible diagnosis are excluded.27

There are diverse phenotype-genotype correlations among different ethnicities. For instance, while M694V and M680I are usually linked to more severe phenotype, the presence of joint involvement, and risk for amyloidosis in Turkey, a study from Algeria showed that M694I is the genotype with the highest amyloidosis risk.10 In a study conducted by Beshlawy et al,29 it was found that among 500 FMF patients, those with the homozygous or heterozygous M694V and V726A genotypes, or the M694V/V726A genotype, are at a higher risk of experiencing fever attacks and abdominal pain compared to FMF patients with other genotypes. Moreover, patients carrying the M694V/M680I genotype have the highest risk of developing a severe phenotype. Additionally, the study revealed that the M694V variant followed by the V726A was the most common association with HSP, and the presence of the M694V variant was linked to a significantly higher response rate compared to patients carrying the M680I variant.

Clinical Manifestations

Characteristic manifestations of FMF are recurrent fever, accompanied by serositis, usually lasting 12-72 hours. These attacks are self-limiting, and the patient is well between these febrile attacks. Attacks of FMF may be precipitated by infections, starving, surgery, vigorous exercise, and menstruation.23,26,30 Fever is sometimes the sole symptom in a minority of FMF patients. Otherwise, the most common accompanying symptom is abdominal pain, generally diffuse due to the peritonitis in the 86%-92% of children with FMF. Seldomly, it can be restricted in the right lower quadrant, which may resemble appendicitis, and require diagnostic laparotomy.10,23,25,26 A history of appendectomy prior to FMF diagnosis has been identified in 5% of children.25

A study involving 854 patients with FMF demonstrated that those with early-onset FMF, specifically findings occurring before the age of 2, exhibited a more severe disease phenotype. Additionally, the study identified that M694V and M680I homozygous mutations are prevalent among early-onset patients, who consequently required higher doses of colchicine for effective management of their condition.31

Joint involvement during fever episodes is also common, especially arthralgia (45%-64%) is more encountered than arthritis (11%-47%).23,25,26 Arthritis of FMF is generally monoarticular, non-destructive, and responsive to short courses of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). It usually involves large joints of the lower extremities and is related to M694V mutations in our country.25,26,30,32 Besides, erysipelas-like erythema, which means red, swollen, and tender skin around a large joint, may be present in 6%-30% of children with FMF and resembles septic arthritis. A more insidious musculoskeletal symptom is exertional leg pain, usually occur in the calf after a vigorous exercise in the evening, is encountered in 22%-66% of the children with FMF.24-26

A relatively new entity was recently described in FMF is protracted febrile myalgia syndrome (PFMS). It is characterized by severe disabling myalgia, accompanied by fever, and elevated acute phase reactants (APRs), lasting at least 5 days in the absence of occult myositis or elevation of muscle enzymes. Protracted febrile myalgia syndrome is reported in 0.5%-1.6% of the children with FMF, of which most have homozygote M694V genotype.25,26,33

Pleuritis, pericarditis, and resultant chest pain are less common in children than in adults. Its frequency is 5%-20% and a diagnosis of pericarditis has been established in only 0.6% in children with FMF.24-26 Besides, nausea, constipation, diarrhea, and acute scrotal edema are less common manifestations of FMF in children.24-26,34

Laboratory Work-Up

During inflammatory attacks, acute phase responses (APRs), including erythroid sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein, fibrinogen, and serum amyloid A (SAA), are usually elevated. Besides, complete blood count often reveals leucocytosis and thrombocytosis during these episodes.26,35 Laboratory results are non-specific and cannot discriminate FMF from other causes of inflammation, including infections.

Synovial fluid from the arthritis of an FMF patient is also inflammatory but sterile, with elevated leukocyte count.36 Diagnosis should be made based on the whole picture since inflammatory arthritis can also be seen in septic arthritis and juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA).

Urine analysis should be made during febrile attacks accompanying abdominal pain, especially in younger children to identify urinary tract infections before considering FMF diagnosis. In FMF patients, regular urine sampling for dip-stick test and microalbumin/creatinine ratio is recommended to indicate the development of renal amyloidosis.26,33

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of FMF is used to be made only by clinical suspicion and determination of a favorable response to colchicine before the discovery of MEFV gene and the widespread use of genetic testing. The first diagnostic criteria, Tel Hashomer, which was further modified by Livneh had been used to diagnose FMF by scoring the presence of clinical items and the colchicine response.37 In 2009, Turkish criteria was proposed by Yalcinkaya and Ozen, which is more practical with higher sensitivity but unacceptable rates of specificity.38-40 According to the latter, the presence of at least 2 following items is sufficient for FMF diagnosis: (1) fever, (2) abdominal pain, (3) chest pain, (4) Arthritis (must be lasting 6-72 hours, at least 3 attacks for items 1-4), (5) exertional leg pain, and (6) family history for FMF.38

Over time, it became more problematic to distinguish FMF from other rare monogenic autoinflammatory diseases (AIDs). Eurofever and Paediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organisation (PRINTO) proposed a new classification criterion for each common monogenic autoinflammatory diseases. For FMF, in the case of the available genetic testing, the diagnosis is reasonable within 2 circumstances: in the presence or absence of a confirmatory MEFV genotype (Table 1).41 The study by Sag et al42 compared the sensitivity and specificity of the new Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria, Tel Hashomer and Yalcinkaya-Ozen criteria in FMF patients. According to the study, the sensitivity of the new Eurofever/PRINTO criteria is better due to clinical manifestations with genotype, but the specificity is poorer due to ethnicity data with higher misclassifications compared to Tel Hashomer and Yalcinkaya-Ozen criteria. Another comparison study revealed that Tel Hashomer criteria and Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria reaches the highest specificity for FMF to differentiate it from other AIDs, which may lead to more appropriate diagnosis of other AIDs in pediatric population.40

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis of FMF should be handled in 2 parts. Firstly, during an inflammatory attack, systemic or localized infections, acute abdomen requiring surgery, and malignancies should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients with fever, abdominal pain, and/or arthralgia. After that, if the febrile episode is self-limited or in the case of a history of recurrent fever, the clinician should keep in mind other causes of recurrent fever, including immunodeficiencies, cyclic neutropenia, inflammatory bowel diseases, and other AIDs, mainly including periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, adenitis (PFAPA) syndrome, mevalonate kinase deficiency (MKD), TNF receptor-associated periodic syndrome (TRAPS) and cryopyrin-associated periodic syndrome (CAPS). If the fever is subtle or absent, porphyria, Fabry disease, and adrenal failure should be considered in differential diagnosis.41,43

Periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis, adenitis syndrome is characterized by clinical periodicity and attacks of cryptic tonsillitis, aphthous stomatitis, and cervical lymphadenopathy. Additionally, skin rashes accompanying fever can help distinguish between hereditary periodic fevers. Conditions, where maculopapular rashes co-occur with cervical lymphadenopathy, should raise suspicion for MKD, while those accompanied by periorbital edema and myalgia suggest TRAPS. In cases where recurrent urticaria is present, CAPS should be considered.44 The classification criteria recommended by Eurofever/PRINTO for the differential diagnosis of autoinflammatory diseases have shown over 95% specificity and sensitivity when applied based on genetic and clinical features, enhancing the accuracy of disease diagnosis.41

Common Diseases Co-Existing with FMF

Other than the classical presentation of FMF patients, we are now aware of a substantial part of patients suffering from other inflammatory diseases who experience them more severely than the patients without FMF. On some occasions, the diagnosis of these disorders may precede the diagnosis of FMF. In large pediatric FMF cohorts, immunoglobulin A vasculitis/Henoch–Schoenlein purpura (HSP), JIA (particularly spondyloarthropathies), polyarteritis nodosa (PAN), Behcet’s disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and multiple sclerosis were the most common co-existing diseases among pediatric FMF patients.45-49 Moreover, it is postulated that the risk of JIA, HSP, and PAN can increase 24, 62, and 112-fold in FMF patients, compared to normal population.46 Even febrile seizures can be encountered in children with FMF than normal population.50 Diagnosis of FMF may be challenging due to the overlap of clinical features with other autoinflammatory and autoimmune diseases like PFAPA syndrome, systemic JIA, Behcet’s disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, or acute rheumatic fever.51

Moreover, PFAPA is one of the most common causes of recurrent fever and is classified within the autoinflammatory diseases without an identifiable genetic cause. Although it is not difficult to discriminate typical attacks of fever with pharyngitis and aphthous ulcers from typical presentation of FMF, we detect MEFV gene variants, even M694V homozygosity in PFAPA patients in clinical practice. MEditerranean FeVer variants are encountered in almost half of the PFAPA patients, and these patients respond better to colchicine than the ones without. Therefore, in endemic regions, it is also recommended to screen the MEFV gene in patients with PFAPA symptoms.52-56 Presence of regular attacks, oral aphthous, pharyngitis and attack duration of 5-7 days typically suggest PFAPA rather than FMF.57

Treatment

The first step of treatment of FMF is managing acute febrile attacks by rest and analgesic drugs. Moreover, since its first introduction in 1972, colchicine is the primary drug of choice in FMF patients. Colchicine may decrease the attack frequency, duration, and severity of the FMF attacks and overwhelm the subclinical inflammation between attacks, thus preventing the patient from developing amyloidosis.4 The mechanism of action occurs via binding to microtubules, inhibiting microtubule polymerization, thus both neutrophil chemotaxis and pyrin inflammation leading to the inhibition of IL-1 secretion.58 Colchicine is eliminated through the liver, and although usually well-tolerated, transient elevations of transaminases may occur. Two-folds elevation in transaminases require dose reduction and further investigation for other causes.33 Other common side effects are gastrointestinal, including abdominal pain, nausea, and diarrhea, which predominantly occur at the initiation and dose-increment of the drug.59 Moreover, although clinically silent, mild leukopenia may occur in 10% of the children receiving daily 0.5-2 mg colchicine.60

The recommended daily colchicine dosage in children is 0.03-0.07 mg/kg. However, it can cause side effects in patients with hepatic and renal impairment or concomitant use of the drugs interacting with cytochrome P450 enzyme.61 Colchicine toxicity tend to occur with colchicine consumption higher than 0.1-0.2 mg/kg and may be fatal. This is because it causes bone marrow depression and multiorgan failure, and it is challenging to eliminate the drug due to its wide distribution.62

In the absence of favourable response to colchicine treatment, the clinicians should be aware if the patient is adherent to the treatment. There are many factors, which may affect colchicine adherence, including sociodemographic factors, economical aspects, and the presence of drug intolerance. It is noteworthy that adherence to colchicine is insufficient in more than half of the patients, however the rate of insufficient adherence did not differ between colchicine-resistant and colchicine-responsive FMF patients in a recent study.63

Colchicine resistance, encountered in approximately 5% of FMF patients, is defined as one or more attacks per month under a maximally tolerated dose of colchicine for the last 6 months.26,33 In colchicine-resistant patients, changing coated colchicine (available form in Turkey) to compressed colchicine may be preferred before considering anti-IL1 agents.64

Following the elucidation of pyrin’s role in synthesizing interleukin-1 (IL-1), the use of anti-IL-1 agents in patients with colchicine-resistant familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) has become increasingly prevalent.33 The first agent employed for this purpose, Anakinra, functions as an IL-1 receptor antagonist and has demonstrated efficacy in preventing serositis attacks in colchicine-resistant patients, leading to improvements in renal function in cases of amyloidosis.65,66 The most common side effects associated with Anakinra include injection site reactions and challenges associated with daily administration. In contrast, Canakinumab, which has a longer half-life and is administered subcutaneously monthly, is a fully humanized monoclonal antibody of the IgG1 class specifically targeting IL-1β. Its long-term efficacy and safety have been reported in various clinical studies.67-70

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels are elevated in the serum of FMF patients during acute attacks. In this context, tocilizumab—a humanized monoclonal anti-IL-6 receptor antibody capable of binding to soluble and membrane receptors—has been utilized in clinical investigations involving FMF patients. By downregulating IL-6 synthesis, tocilizumab potentially suppresses the production of serum amyloid A (SAA). Notably, its efficacy in alleviating attacks in adult FMF patients has been demonstrated.71 The long-term safety of tocilizumab is currently under investigation in a multicenter, placebo-controlled, randomized, double-blind study involving colchicine-resistant FMF patients aged 12 to 75 years. Although no statistically significant difference was observed in the primary endpoint (the number of fever attacks), tocilizumab was found to be more effective than the placebo group for most secondary endpoints.72

Prognosis and Follow-Up

Amyloidosis occurs by the accumulation of AA amyloid in the tissues, and it is the most devastating complication of FMF. It was previously reported to occur in approximately 13% of FMF patients, of which the risk was increased by the positive family history of amyloidosis and delay in the diagnosis.10 Before the introduction of colchicine therapy, FMF represented a considerable public health crisis, particularly in the Eastern Mediterranean region. Due to the improvement in the genetics, diagnosis, and management of patients with FMF, the frequency of amyloidosis decreased. Recent pediatric cohorts report the amyloidosis frequency as 0.1%-0.3% in children with FMF.25,26 To avoid the development of amyloidosis in FMF patients, it is strictly suggested to control the unprovoked inflammatory attacks and minimize subclinical inflammation between attacks. In the case of subclinical inflammation and persistent attacks, colchicine dosage should be increased, and if colchicine intolerance or resistance occurs, anti-IL1 agents should be provided.33

Other potential complications of FMF include destructive arthritis, adhesions, infertility, and vasculitis. Fortunately, these complications are rare, and recent studies indicate that with long-term treatment, both the complication rates and mortality associated with FMF are comparable to those observed in age-matched healthy controls.73

The disease activity tools are helpful for determining if the disease is stable and the response to the treatment is satisfactory. There are several tools, including the Pras, Mor, and FMF50, the utility of these tools for determining disease activity.74-76 The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) also recommends the auto-inflammatory disease activity index (AIDAI), a practical diary for monitoring disease activity by assessing the symptoms of patients on a daily basis.33,77

There is a limited number of studies regarding the transition of childhood-onset rheumatic diseases to adulthood. Recently, this increasingly significant topic has led to the publication of standards and recommendations by EULAR/PReS (European League Against Rheumatism/Pediatric Rheumatology European Society).78 These recommendations encompass several key elements, including high-quality multidisciplinary care initiated during early adolescence, the integral role of a transition coordinator, transition policies and protocols, effective communication, transfer documentation, an open electronic platform for resource access, appropriate education for pediatric and adult healthcare teams, secure funding for the continuation of treatments and services in adult rheumatology, and the necessity for increased evidence to inform best practices. In a study conducted by Elhani et al involving 111 patients, it was found that the subgroup of familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) patients who were unable to transition to adulthood exhibited poorer disease control.79

Addressing quality of life (QoL) considerations for children with familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is essential for comprehensive care. Understanding the multifaceted challenges posed by the disease, which includes physical symptoms, educational barriers, and psychosocial outcomes, can aid healthcare providers, educators, and families in developing targeted interventions that enhance the overall well-being of affected children. The Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), commonly used to monitor chronic illnesses, is an appropriate tool for assessing general health status and specific disease conditions. It measures QoL across physical, emotional, social, and academic functioning domains. The literature indicates that pediatric patients with FMF tend to have lower educational performance and quality of life.80,81 Physicians caring for FMF patients should be cognizant of the QoL changes in managing these patients.

Analysis of MEFV should be recommended for all patients who meet the Eurofever clinical criteria in a multi-ethnic population. However, this decision is influenced mainly by the country of origin. In the Eastern Mediterranean countries, we perform MEFV analysis in almost all patients with recurrent non-infectious febrile attacks. Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) should also be considered in individuals with a significant acute-phase response in the absence of infection or patients with an atypical course of rheumatic diseases. For instance, MEFV analysis is recommended in cases of recurrent Henoch–Schoenlein purpura (HSP), in patients with oligoarticular juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) who exhibit persistently elevated acute-phase responses, or in children presenting atypical clinical manifestations associated with PFAPA syndrome (such as serositis attacks, lack of response to adenotonsillectomy, and late-onset age).23

Global Perspectives

The disease course and management of FMF might differ in various healthcare settings globally, particularly in resource-limited areas. The effect of settlement was previously investigated by Ozen et al82 and was revealed that FMF patients immigrated from Eastern Mediterranean countries to western Europe had milder disease activity than the patients continued to live in their countries. The disease activity may directly reflect disease management however, as the primary medication, colchicine is an inexpensive and easily accessible drug and biological agents are necessitated in a small proportion of the patients. Therefore, we think that this difference particularly may be due to the discrepancy of socioeconomic status and environmental factors, by affecting general health.

Conclusion

Currently, FMF is the monogenic autoinflammatory disease, which we know most about the pathogenesis, genetics, clinical manifestations, and outcome. However, there are still missing information about the pathogenesis and diverse effects of MEFV variants on the FMF phenotype. In the future, further studies should be performed to clarify the whole pathogenesis, including additional genetic and environmental factors, and identifying markers for discriminating an FMF attack from other causes of fever and/or pain, especially in children.

Ongoing research should aim to clarify the development of the disease, enhance diagnostic accuracy, and address its clinical presentation and genetic variability, with a focus on identifying new genetic mutations and epigenetic factors that contribute to its pathogenesis. A more detailed examination of risk factors and the biomarkers used for this purpose will contribute to the clinical management of the disease, minimizing the prognosis and the risk of complications. There is a need for more in-depth research on the monitoring of asymptomatic and symptomatic carriers, as well as on the treatment processes for symptomatic carriers and additional factors that contribute to complications such as amyloidosis. Furthermore, future studies should concentrate on developing more effective treatment modalities for patients who do not respond to colchicine.
