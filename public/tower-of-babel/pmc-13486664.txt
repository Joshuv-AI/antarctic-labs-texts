Multi-sensor observations and modeling of wave swash on a low-tide terrace beach
Authors: Harold Diaz, Julien Boucharel, Patrick Marchesiello, Rafael Almar, Yves Soufflet, Rachid Benshila, Duong Hai Thuan, Nguyen Trung Viet, Luis Pedro Almeida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the first application of the non-hydrostatic CROCO model to simulate surf- and swash-zone hydrodynamics over a week-long field experiment, combining LiDAR, camera, and ADCP observations. This approach enables validation of cross-shore wave transformation and run-up dynamics, showing that CROCO reproduces dominant swash components and extreme run-up events. Sensitivity analyses reveal a nonlinear coupling between and : small changes in period (≈ 1 s) significantly affect swash response, while increasing amplifies this sensitivity. Run-up increases with tide under low-energy conditions but decreases under high-energy conditions, reflecting interactions between incident and infragravity waves over low-tide terrace beaches. Frequency spreading, negligible at low energy, becomes important at higher energy levels. Model limitations include underestimation of infragravity tidal modulation, simplified bottom friction in the swash zone, and the absence of directional spreading (2DV configuration). Despite these, adjustments to and cross-shore comparisons confirm the robustness of key results. Overall, findings emphasize the combined role of wave energy, spectral spread, and water level, as well as depth-controlled morphology (e.g. surf-zone slope), in run-up prediction. The study demonstrates CROCO’s potential for near–real-time applications and supports the development of site-specific, data-driven run-up parameterizations for coastal hazard assessment.Keywords: Run-up, Swash, CROCO, LiDAR, CameraSubject terms: Natural hazards, Ocean sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Approximately 10% of the global population lives in coastal areas located less than 10 meters above present sea level1. These regions are already experiencing the consequences of sea-level rise2,3, which increasingly threaten coastal ecosystems and human communities. Beyond this gradual rise, coastal flooding is expected to intensify due to more frequent and severe extreme sea-level events, driven by storm surges and wave processes. In particular, waves can contribute to a rise of several meters to total water levels during extreme storm conditions4–8. Wave run-up, defined as the maximum vertical excursion of the waterline produced by individual waves above the mean sea level, is one of the primary mechanisms driving extreme coastal water level, and potentially overtopping events and flooding7,9,10. At particularly vulnerable sandy coasts, it encloses the complex interactions between offshore wave conditions, nearshore transformation processes, and local beach morphology. Although empirical11–24, and numerical models have provided useful estimates of extreme water levels, developing a universally accurate formula remains a challenge due to the complexity of processes involved25–27.

In particular, numerous attempts to develop such models for wave run-up have faced persistent challenges arising from various limitations, including the scarcity of comprehensive field data, site-specific variability in coastal morphology and hydrodynamics, short experimental durations that fail to capture representative conditions, measurement uncertainties and unaccounted physical processes11–24. Despite these difficulties, such efforts have produced a broad range of empirical and semi-empirical run-up parameterizations17. However, the diversity of coastal environments and forcing conditions currently makes it unrealistic to define a single, universally applicable formulation capable of accurately representing all settings and their particularities. Specifically, one major issue arises from the diversity of beach types and their dynamic regimes, which exert a strong environmental control on wave run-up. Beaches are commonly classified according to their morphodynamic state, ranging from dissipative to reflective, with a wide range of intermediate states in between28,29. This classification is often quantified using the Iribarren number (ξ), or the surf similarity parameter30, which combines wave steepness and beach slope to describe the degree of energy dissipation across the surf zone. Low Iribarren numbers correspond to gently sloping, dissipative beaches with extensive breaking zones, while high values indicate steep, reflective beaches where waves can directly impact the shore. From Hunt (1959)11 to more recent studies, wave run-up has typically been related to offshore wave forcing in combination with local nearshore morphodynamics (see17 for a recent comprehensive review). However, depending on the type and regime of the sandy beach under study, relying solely on these parameters can oversimplify and undermine the understanding of wave run-up complexity, especially where non-linear and site-specific processes dominate.

In addition, beaches characterized by low-tide terraces (LTT) — often found in tropical environments and subject to strong tidal modulation of the nearshore zone — have historically received far less attention in run-up research than the “classic” dissipative or reflective beach types to the knowledge of the authors. The scarcity of field detailed studies in these settings is especially true when considering beaches that are tidally controlled and feature sandbars or low-tide terraces31,32. In such environments, energy dissipation patterns can be dominated by changing water-depth over the terrace and by shifts in the surf–swash transition zone; this modulation of the swash zone response strongly affects run-up extreme values and their variability27,32–35. One the most affected procces by this dissipation patterns are infragravity waves. Infragravity (IG) waves, defined as low-frequency surface waves below 0.04–0.05 Hz, plays an important role in runup excursions, particularly through their contribution to the swash IG component. As described by36, they originate from short-wave groupiness and evolve through shoaling, dissipation, and reflection, under strong control of beach morphology. However, there is no clear unanimity in the literature regarding the dominant controls on the IG contribution to swash dynamics. For example16, highlight a strong dependence on foreshore slope and morphodynamic state via the fall velocity parameter, while37 suggest limited breaking influence under low-energy conditions and38emphasize frequency-dependent dissipation. Tidal effects also remain debated39: report enhanced swash modulation with tide, whereas40 find opposite trends linked to sandbar-controlled breaking. Likewise, wave spectral influences are inconsistent, with41 and42 associating greater frequency spread with increased IG swash, while43 show this relationship can reverse under highly dissipative conditions. These discrepancies underline the strong context-dependence of the IG swash component. For these reasons, using hydrodynamic information acquired directly in the near-shore or swash region — rather than relying solely on offshore forcing parameters — appears much more appropriate for run-up prediction in LTT environments32,33. Nevertheless, acquiring such near-shore data is not without cost or risk: measurements in the swash and terrace zone involve complex instrumentation under highly dynamic conditions, and expose both equipment and personnel to hazards. To overcome these difficulties, two complementary approaches have emerged as effective alternatives for characterizing near-shore hydrodynamics and run-up processes: numerical modeling and remote sensing.

Recent advances in numerical modeling have enabled the development of non-hydrostatic, wave-resolving models capable of accurately simulating nearshore dynamics and run-up levels44–48. These improvements, combined with increasing computational power and high-resolution forcing data, now allow the generation of extensive, spatially and temporally detailed data sets at lower cost and risk than traditional field measurements. In particular, phase-resolving models have shown strong ability to capture the full variability of run-up, surpassing earlier phase-averaged methods that were limited to estimating only its extreme excursion levels19,49–54.

In parallel, remote sensing technologies have considerably broadened the range of coastal environments that can be monitored–from sandy and gravel beaches to rocky coasts, mixed sediment settings, and coral reef systems15,17,20,55–57. Video monitoring systems have long been used to estimate wave breaking, swash excursions, and shoreline evolution, with recent stereo-video developments improving spatial coverage and accuracy58–60. LiDAR systems have likewise proven effective for resolving free-surface elevations and morphological changes at high spatio-temporal resolutions, though measurements can be affected by foam and suspended sediment during breaking events32,32,61–64. Other remote sensing tools, including infrared sensors, X-band radars, stereo camera arrays, or combinations of those, have also been successfully applied to nearshore wave and run-up monitoring66–69.

In this work, we integrate numerical modeling and remote sensing to enhance the characterization and parameterization of wave run-up at a LTT beach in Nha Trang, Vietnam, where a comprehensive field campaign was conducted in 201532,70. Specifically, we employ and validate the non-hydrostatic model CROCO (Coastal and Regional Ocean COmmunity model)46,47,71–73 to simulate near- and inner-shore dynamics under observed forcing conditions. Once validated, sensitivity experiments are conducted to identify the key hydrodynamic parameters controlling run-up in this type of beach environment.

Inner-shore hydrodynamics and beach state during the experiment

A field experiment was conducted from 26 November to 4 December 2015 at Nha Trang Beach, a semi-enclosed sandy beach in southeast Vietnam32,70. The site experiences a monsoon-driven wave climate and occasional tropical cyclone events, producing highly variable hydrodynamic and sedimentary conditions74,75. The experiment focused on the northern sector of the bay, characterized by a steep foreshore and a narrow LTT, to investigate short-term coastal morphodynamic responses under wave forcing76. Measurements were obtained along a cross-shore transect using a combination of in-situ and remote sensing instruments. Offshore wave and current conditions were monitored with an acoustic Doppler current profiler (ADCP), while nearshore hydrodynamics and morphological changes were captured using a 2D LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) system and repeated RTK-GPS (Real Time Kinematics-Global Positioning System) topographic surveys. The LiDAR provided high-resolution observations of swash dynamics and beach morphology, allowing detailed analysis of shoreline and profile evolution. Further details on the experimental set-up and data processing are provided in the Methods section and Fig. 1.

Swash hydrodynamics

We next evaluate how the offshore bulk wave parameters transform within the near-shore and surf zone into wave setup, run-up, and swash components, using both the LiDAR observations and the CROCO simulations. The wave setup (<η>, Fig. 3a), defined as the mean coastal water level about which run-up oscillates (see Methods), is strongly correlated with the tide during the peak-energy period (27–29 November), with almost no variation occurring near high tide. After November 29th, however, this tidal modulation becomes notably weaker. CROCO slightly overestimates the setup, with a bias of approximately 17 cm. The model also under-represents the tidal modulation, especially weaker modulation at low tide and stronger modulation at high tide, which results in a moderate overall correlation coefficient for setup (≈ 0.5). The extreme run-up (, the estimated extreme run-up from wave setup and swash variability, and the discrete extreme run-up , a wave-by-wave representative maximum excursion; see Fig. 3b–f and Methods–Swash metrics) also exhibits a clear tidal dependence, reflecting tidal controls on nearshore wave conditions and swash–surf-zone interactions. The swash peak and mean periods are strongly correlated with offshore wave energy measured by the ADCP, with a slight modulation of the mean period near low tide (see Supplementary Information, Fig. S7o). In contrast, the zero-crossing period shows no variation around high tide (see Supplementary Information, Fig. S7p–q).

Key hydrodynamic drivers of swash/run-up

Having validated the model’s ability to realistically reproduce wave transformation from offshore to the nearshore, surf, and swash zones, we designed a set of sensitivity experiments (Table 2) to assess how extreme run-up and swash statistics respond to variations in key hydrodynamic forcing parameters. In particular, simulations were performed using different combinations of significant wave height (), peak period (), the JONSWAP frequency-spread parameter (γ), and still water level (). Parameter ranges were chosen to be consistent with the observed site conditions and were extended beyond what was observed during the 7-day observation period to explore more energetic wave scenarios and assess the robustness of the CROCO simulations. The JONSWAP frequency-spreading parameter (γ) was varied from 1 to 40, while γ = 30 provided the best fit to the observed ADCP spectra.

Discussion and conclusion

This study presents the first application of the non-hydrostatic CROCO model to simulate surf- and swash-zone hydrodynamics over a continuous, week-long field experiment. Our combination of multi-sensor observations and fully non-hydrostatic modeling offers an uncommon opportunity to assess nearshore dynamics across event to multi-day timescales. The coordinated use of LiDAR, camera imagery, and ADCP measurements enabled a detailed validation of the model’s ability to reproduce the cross-shore transformation of wave energy and the ensuing run-up dynamics. CROCO successfully captures the dominant swash components as well as extreme run-up events, and highlights the influence of total energy (combined effect of and ), still water level, frequency spreading in forcing and swash components response, particularly in the infragravity frequency band.

A CROCO sensitivity analysis using wave-forcing conditions outside the experimental range, as presented in this study, highlights the interdependence between and . Small changes in wave period (≈1 s) can strongly influence the development of as wave height varies, likely due to nonlinear interactions between infragravity waves and bathymetry. Conversely, doubling increases the sensitivity of swash to wave period beyond certain thresholds (here, s) and change completely its nonlinear relationship. The analysis also provides converging evidence for the relationship between total incident energy and tidal modulation, consistent with previous findings39,40. Under low-energy conditions, runup increases with sea level, primarily due to an increase of the infragravity component. In contrast, at higher energy levels, the opposite trend is observed. The mechanisms governing how infragravity bands respond to the combined effects of incident energy and still water level on low-tide terrace (LTT) beaches remain unclear and require further investigation. A plausible hypothesis involves nonlinear infragravity interactions within the terrace, where variations in still water level modulate both the breaking location and the cross-shore extent of infragravity wave development. This also suggests that changes in LTT width may influence these interactions, potentially leading to resonance-like behavior. Additionally, the sensitivity analysis indicates that frequency spreading has a negligible effect on swash response under low energy conditions. However, at higher energy levels, this effect becomes more pronounced, with broader spectra enhancing the infragravity contribution, again linking total incident energy and spectral characteristics. Overall, the results point to a triad of dominant hydrodynamic controls on swash—incident wave energy, spectral spreading, and still water level—that should not be considered independently. Their combined influence, together with site-specific morphological features such as LTTs or sandbars, should be further investigated. This finding were also supported by observation data where the incorporation of a depth controlled parameter () into runup parameterization improves correlations. These results represents a promising avenue for future research for intermediate state type of beaches, to be explored through further modeling and validated with in situ observations.

Previous studies on intermediate beaches have highlighted the central role of depth-controlled processes and the importance of local morphology in regulating swash dynamics (e.g16,33,39,42.,). Our findings are consistent with this body of work: the beach’s transition toward a LTT state shifts swash behavior and modifies the relative contribution of incident and infragravity motions, demonstrating the need for flexible parameterizations that can accommodate such morphological changes.

Several limitations of the 2DV (two-dimensional vertical) model have been identified. Purely 2DV, shore-normal models often overestimate the local reflected wave amplitude. In a realistic 3D setting, some of the energy returned by the 2DV model as local reflection could be converted into trapped edge waves and carried away alongshore78. Here, reflection nodes in the cross-shore domain, visible in the mean water level and significant wave height profiles (see supplementary information Fig. S2), would only be expected for slopes steeper than 1:962 and they therefore likely contribute to model–data discrepancies. When running simulations using a fixed (non-evolving) mean profile, these reflections distort wave peak periods in the surf zone (correlation ≈ –0.37, RMSE ≈ 4 s), which in turn degrades run-up predictions (correlation ≈ –0.23, RMSE ≈ 0.55 m).

The model further underestimates the tidal modulation of infragravity waves in the surf zone. LiDAR observations made 30 m offshore show that the high-/low-tide energy ratio in the infragravity band can double, and that at high tide the low-frequency component can contribute nearly half of the total wave height (). Part of the observed infragravity signal can be attributed to variations along the shoreline absent from the 2DV model, not only to progressive edge waves, as we have discussed, but also to surf eddies and rip currents, some of which are in the infragravity range46,79. Observation of beach cusps during the study in Nha Trang tends to suggest that alongshore variations were indeed significant80. 2DV limitations include the fact that directional spreading is not directly taken into account. This can affect how infragravity waves are released and develop along the alongshore dimension, directly influencing runup excursions. Other possible explanations for model-data discrepancy in the surf zone include insufficient infragravity generation due to excessive reflection or an incomplete representation of bottom friction, although this would also affect the swash zone. Nevertheless, sensitivity tests using the formulation for 81, within realistic grain-size ranges for Nha Trang (0.4–1 mm82), produced increases in infragravity height (≈10 cm, or 30 %), but did not fully account for the observed discrepancies. Similarly, altering the shoreface slope seaward of the LTT—between its outermost point and the offshore boundary of the domain (−1200 m)—had little effect on the infragravity energy of the surf zone. Only unrealistically flat slopes—which are inconsistent with camera-based slope estimates70,83 —produced large (≈50%) increases. Thus, while bottom friction and slope changes affect infragravity energy, they alone cannot explain the RMSE levels between CROCO and LiDAR data, which more likely result from 2DV limitations.

These comparisons are made possible only through the dense in situ and remote sensing array deployed during the experiment. Obtaining accurate, time-varying foreshore and surf-zone slopes remains challenging in real-world contexts, and the ability of simple parameterizations to predict extreme run-up on LTT beaches will depend heavily on access to reliable morphological information.

Despite these limitations, the study highlights the strengths of a multi-source approach that combines LiDAR, camera, and ADCP observations with numerical modeling. This methodology increases confidence in the results and supports the development of simplified, locally calibrated run-up parameterizations for practical coastal management. CROCO’s ability to perform rapid and cost-effective near–real-time simulations during storm events demonstrates its potential both as an operational tool and as a means to elucidate the hydro-morphodynamic mechanisms controlling extreme runup excursions.

Ultimately, the results highlight the dominant role of depth-controlled parameters-surf-zone slope—in run-up parameterizations for LTT beaches. High reflection at high tide increases coupling between surf and swash zones, and this interaction is better captured when morphodynamic changes (e.g., foreshore terrace development) are incorporated confirming the former observations by84. Conversely, imposing a fixed profile leads to significant biases in surf-zone wave period and run-up.

Looking ahead, model development should prioritize improving swash-zone physics—particularly dissipation and reflection—either through a fully 3-D framework or, more economically, by incorporating a parameterized alongshore radiation sink for reflected energy within the 2DV model, complemented by dynamically varying bottom friction or enhanced infragravity-generation schemes. Expanding datasets from continuous remote monitoring could support generalized parameterizations across a broader range of beach types. Additionally, CROCO-based scenario simulations, accounting for the hydrodynamic triad of incident wave energy, spectral spreading, and still water level, in combination with varying morphological features, could help identify robust, site-specific controls on swash dynamics and facilitate the development of data-driven relationships using machine learning. Together, these advances would enable robust, site-specific run-up predictions for operational coastal hazard assessment.

Methods

Study site

We used an extensive data set of wave observations acquired from 2015/11/27 to 2015/12/04 in Nha Trang, Vietnam in the Context of COASTVAR project32,70,85. Nha Trang sandy beach is located within a semi-enclosed, northeast-southwest oriented bay on the southeastern coast of Vietnam. Partial protection of summer monsoon waves from southeast is provided by a cluster of islands. In contrast, during the winter monsoon, the bay is more exposed to long-period, high-energy waves from the northeast(see 1 a))

The regional wave climate exhibits pronounced seasonal variability. Offshore, the annual mean significant wave height () is about 0.95 m, with a corresponding mean peak period of 6.2 s. During the fall and winter months (October–April), average increases to approximately 1.2 m and to 6.8 s, while during spring and summer (May–September) conditions are milder, with around 0.6 m and less than 5 s. Tides in the area are mixed (diurnal and semi-diurnal) with a micro tidal range varying from 0.4 m to a maximum of 1.7 m70.

Beach morphology is characterized by a steep foreshore (slopes ≈ [0.1–0.2]) and narrow, flat low-tide terrace (slope ≈ 0.01) approximately 10–40 m wide. Alongshore grain size variability is present ranging from 0.1 mm to 0.4 mm from north to south.

Materials

Three main instruments were used for the present work: 2D LiDAR, installed on a 3.5 m tower on top of the berm. An ADCP, located 1 km offshore from the LiDAR position and Video Cameras located 15 m above beach level (see 1 a) b)). On the other hand CROCO (Coastal and Regional Ocean COmmunity model) model was used to perform numerical simulations.

The ADCP located 1 km (15 m depth) offshore of the LiDAR position allows the full offshore wave conditions to be available and used for comparison with those observed directly inshore by the LiDAR free-surface measurements. In addition, the ADCP data were used as direct hydrodynamic forcing for numerical runs using the model CROCO.

Since May 2013 a video monitoring system has been installed, consisting of two high-resolution VIVOTEK IP7361 mounted at ≈ 14 m above the still water level (see 1 a)). Image rectification to real-world coordinates was done using RTK-GPS ground control points estimating intrinsic camera parameters (focal distance and distortion coefficients) minimizing GPS re-projection errors. Videos at 2 Hz were retreated in order to generate spatial-temporal time-stacks accordingly with LiDAR geographic position.

LiDAR 2D measurements were made using a SICK LMS511 instrument on a 3.5 m pole above the high part of the beach (see 1b) inset). The LMS511 captures continuous cross-shore elevation profiles of the water and beach surface and by beams projected in 190° scan range with angular resolution from 0.042° to 1° and up to 100 Hz scanning frequency, allowing high-temporal-resolution profiling of swash and surf-zone processes. The effective measurement range goes from 0.2 up to 80 m depending on target reflectivity and atmospheric conditions32,80,86.

Post processing

The raw LiDAR data needs to be treated in order to obtain hydrodynamic and topographic information. First linear space and time re-interpolation is applied into a 1 cm spatial resolution grid every 0.2 seconds, expanding 60 meters, going 50 meters offshore, and 10 meters onshore of the LiDAR installed tower (see 1 b)). Cross-section domain selection was made based on visual inspection of data observations to guarantee free surface returns from the LiDAR. Data are then separated in the so-called static (sand/ground) and (water/foam) dynamic part, following the methodology proposed by32,63, the last, use a variance threshold criterion over a given time window. For this work components were separated using a threshold of 10e-4 over a time window of 10 seconds.

Hydrodynamics can be obtained at every point of the interpolated grid. In this study still water level and waves characteristics were extracted every 30 minutes at 30 m offshore from LiDAR position. This point was chosen to minimize temporal dispersion in the LiDAR measurements. While spatial validation is desirable, high-frequency signal quality and breakpoint variability limit its use. This allows us to have the morphological variations of the upper part of the beach at regular time intervals. Simulations

CROCO (Coastal and Regional Ocean COmmunity model) is a non-hydrostatic, free-surface, terrain-following model developed around the regional oceanic modeling system87,88. In its non-hydrostatic (non-Boussinesq) version46,47,71–73,89, it resolves the full set of Navier-Stokes equations, including the momentum and continuity equations, free-surface kinematic relation and the transport equation of tracers if any (heat, salt or any type of passive tracer). In this version, CROCO is able to resolve individual wave propagation, shoaling and breaker-induced circulation. CROCO is discretized on an Arakawa C-grid with finite difference methods and a split-explicit treatment of slow and fast modes (acoustic waves and shallow-water motions). The time discretization follows a third-order predictor-corrector scheme. No breaking parameter is used in CROCO, breaking waves are treated as bores with a shock-capturing advection scheme (WENO5), which transfers steep wave energy to the mean currents, while part of the breaking wave energy is transferred to unresolved turbulence via a k-ε closure model4646,47,71–73.

Waves are generated using the wavemaker implemented in CROCO73, adapting its general 3D form to the 2D equations presented here, i.e., a two–dimensional vertical plane (x,z) with free surface η (x,t) and horizontal velocity u(x,z,t). The wave direction is considered shore-normal (θ =0 and no directional spread), all alongshore dependence is removed and the alongshore velocity component is zero.
