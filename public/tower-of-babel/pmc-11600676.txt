Selenium levels in soil and tea as affected by soil properties in Jiangxi Province, China
Authors: Fahui Jiang, Yan Wu, Mahbub Ul Islam, Xinfeng Jiang, Binqiang Wang, Shaolang He, Xiaobing Lin, Yongming Sun, Guohui Chen, Xi Chen, Shangshu Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Selenium (Se) is an essential element for humans and animals, and Se deficiency-related diseases are a significant global health concern. Tea may help ameliorate Se deficiencies. However, the mechanisms of natural Se enrichment in tea remain poorly understood, particularly in high-Se soils, such as those in Jiangxi Province. This study conducted a comprehensive field survey of 67 soil and tea samples from Jiangxi, a major tea production region in China, to analysis spatial variation in Se concentrations and identify key driving mechanisms. The average soil Se concentration across Jiangxi was 0.44 mg kg− 1, exceeding both global (0.15 mg kg− 1) and Chinese (0.29 mg kg− 1) averages. Soil Se content was significantly influenced by soil organic matter (SOM), total potassium (K), iron (FeOX) and aluminium (AlOX) oxides, and tea planting duration, contributing 15%, 6%, 20%, 22%, and 10%, respectively. Soil Se levels increased with SOM and planting duration, but decreased with total K content. Conversely, the average Se content in tea leaves was only 0.10 mg kg− 1, and primarily driven by soil Se (29%), followed by FeOX (13%), AlOX (15%), SOM (5%), available K (5%), as well as inputs of nitrogen and K fertilizer (6% and 5% respectively), each. Partial least squares models identified four key pathways in which environment and human management practices, directly or via interactions with soil properties (SOM, K, FeOX, AlOX), influenced Se transfer from soil to tea leaves. Overall, our findings indicate that tea cultivation is more suitable in areas with high soil Se, such as central Jiangxi Province, and suggest that tea Se content can be enhanced by increasing levels of soil Se, SOM, K, FeOX, and AlOX.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05844-5.

Introduction

Global selenium (Se) deficiency currently occurs in previously unaffected regions where severe afflictions are thus emerging, such as Keshan disease [1, 2], infertility [3], and cognitive impairment [4]. Se is an essential element for humans and animals as it is vital for multiple fundamental functions in various physiological processes [5, 6]. For example, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anti-aging, and longevity functions depend on anti-oxidative effects and support of thyroid hormone synthesis and metabolism [7]. However, human Se intake is frequently insufficient, i.e., less than the recommended 40 μg per day [8].

The predominant source of Se in humans is the daily diet. However, modern food plans comprise very few Se-rich plants where mainly include the four main crops wheat, maize, soybean, and rice, which are Se non-accumulators, as are most vegetables, with Se concentrations typically < 1 mg kg− 1 [9, 10]. Therefore, artificial cultivation of Se-accumulating plants may help address the issue of Se deficiency. Some Se-enriched products such as Brazil nut, broccoli, spirulina, and particular fungi are presently cultivated, however, the current production areas are rather limited [11]. Tea has a marked potential to enrich Se (∼ 48% of 35 tea samples were higher Se than the Chinese industry standard; Fig. 1) and may thus help address large-scale Se deficiency challenges [12]. Sodium selenite is frequently directly consumed by humans with other foods [13], however, organic-bound Se, which is metabolized from sodium selenite by tea plants, is a more effective and safe form of Se. One considerable advantage of tea Se supplementation is the large market, as tea is frequently consumed throughout the world and is widely distributed in China, India, Kenya, and Sri Lanka [14]. Tea beverages have thus become an important replacement for dietary ingestion of Se; therefore, research on the enrichment of Se by tea is important and urgent.

Previous studies confirmed that Se levels in plants strongly depend on soil Se content [15–17]. However, most soils show poor Se content, with average concentrations of only 0.4, 0.39, 0.51, 0.23, 0.3, and 1.04 mg kg− 1 globally, in southern India, Japan, Iran, Sweden, and Scotland, respectively [18–22]. In China, approximately 72% of mainland areas are Se-deficient with Se levels < 0.125 mg kg− 1. These areas are distributed from the northeast to the southwest, including the Taihang Mountains, the Qin Ling Mountain, the Loess Plateau, and the eastern part of the Tibetan Plateau [23, 24]. Therefore, Se fertilisers have been examined, developed, and applied to increase soil Se levels [25]. However, practical applicability of Se fertiliser is limited owing to its high costs and low willingness of farmers to apply it, even though scientific studies produced some useful conclusions, such as plant Se levels increasing with the amount of Se fertiliser input, and this process is affected by soil pH, redox potential, iron and aluminium content, and soil organic matter (SOM) [26, 27]. Thus, one potential avenue to address the challenge of global Se deficiency is large-scale cultivation of tea plants in regions with high Se levels in soils.

Globally, China is the largest tea producer, with 54% of the tea plantation area and 40% of the tea production [28]. Jiangxi is a major tea production region in China, which also exhibits a high level of soil Se content, according to surveys [29]. Here, we chose Jiangxi province to study the status and mechanisms of soil Se transfer into tea in a natural environment where no exogenous Se is applied to test our hypotheses. Our main objectives were (1) to assess Se levels and their spatial distribution in soils and teas in Jiangxi province, China; (2) to determine whether and how key factors such as the local environment, soil properties, and management affect Se enrichment in soil and tea; and (3) to test how key factors interact directly and indirectly with soil and tea Se concentrations. To this end, we conducted an intensive field survey using 67 soil and tea samples from Jiangxi and measured total Se content, soil physiochemical properties, fertiliser application schemes, topography, and climate. Furthermore, we created random-forest models to evaluate the relative importance of differential factors, tested their relationship through regression analysis, and then developed partial least squares models to estimate their direct and indirect effects.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area was located in the main region of green tea production in China, covering approximately 16.69 × 104 km2, representing approximately 73% of the tea plantations of Jiangxi province (24°29′14″–30°04′43″ N; 113°34′18″–118°28′56″ E; Fig. 2). The local climate is a humid subtropical monsoon climate with 1,341–1,943 mm annual mean precipitation, 42% of which occurs from April to June. The average annual air temperature is 16.3–19.5 °C, which decreases by approximately 3 °C from the north to the south of the research area; the annual cumulative temperature varies (by > 10 °C) from 5,621 to 6,066 °C, and the annual accumulated sunshine hours amount to 1,637 h. The region comprises hilly and mountainous landscapes, with high mountains to the south, east, and west. Elevations above sea level vary widely, with a minimum of 20 m and maximum of 2,157.8 m. The soil parent materials in the study area were predominantly granite, quartzose sandstone, quaternary red clay, mudstone, sandy shale, and limestone, which produced various soil types, including red soil, yellow soil, yellow-brown soil, and paddy soil. Red soil, with low pH (< 5.5) and organic carbon matter (< 2%), was the most common soil type at approximately 64.8% of the area. Yellow soil ranked second, with 10% of the entire area, mainly from 700 to 1,200 m elevation. The tea cultivation area in Jiangxi province was 10.36 × 104 ha in 2018, according to a previous study [28]. The tea types of 19 contained in this study were showed in Supplementary table S1.

Field sampling

Field sampling was conducted between late March and early June, 2021. The sample sites were selected among major tea plantations in Jiangxi province. The basic information obtained by interviewing local farmers included tea types, planting duration, and annual fertiliser input with regard to the amounts of nitrogen (N), phosphorous (P), and potassium (K), as well as the type and amount of chemicals or manure. A hand-held GPS device (Unistrong, G120BD, China) was used to record altitude, slope aspect, and coordinates of each experiment site. An angle locator (DEGUQMNT, MNT1111804, Germany) was used to record the slope. Mean annual precipitation and mean annual air temperature data from 2006 to 2021 were obtained from the “Resource and Environment Science and Data Center”, and soil parent materials and types were classified according to a survey provided by “Nanjing Institute of Soil Science, Chinese Academy of Sciences, 10.11666/00073. ver1.db”, based on the respective location.

In total, 67 paired soil and tea samples were collected from 67 major tea plantations using a mixing-point approach. At each plantation, a minimum of five sampling plots, each measuring 300 × 300 m, were established based on plantation size, and a bulk soil sample of approximately 500 g was collected from the surface layer (0–20 cm) using a stainless steel auger (5 cm diameter). From each plot, a mixed tea sample (approximately 500 g) including one bud and the nearest two leaves per plant was collected during soil sampling. The tea samples were treated immediately following the local tea production protocol, i.e., drying at 100 °C for 1 h, followed by storage in a sealed container until laboratory analyses. Before chemical analyses, the tea samples were crushed to fine powder using a disintegrator (LC-GD-FSJ01, China, Nanjing). Soil samples were air-dried in the shade after removing plant residues and other impurities and were then ground and passed through a sieve with 2 mm mesh size. The sieved soil samples were used to measure soil pH and size fractions (sand, silt, and clay). An aliquot of each sieved sample was further ground to pass through a 0.15-mm-mesh sieve for measuring total soil Se, SOM, total N, total P, total K, available N, available P, and available K. All instruments were made of wood instead of ironwork to avoid sample contamination due to the tools’ Se.

Chemical analyses

The concentrations of total Se in the soil and tea leaves were measured using the method described by [30–32]. Briefly, approximately 0.5 g of each soil sample was placed in a polytetrafluoroethylene crucible, and a mixed solution of HNO3, HClO4, and HF (10 mL, vol: vol: vol 8:1:2) was added, followed be digestion through oil-bathing (165–175 °C) until any white smoke was exhausted; that is, the solution was pale yellow and viscous. After cooling, 5 mL HCl (HCl: H2O 1:1) was added and the mixture was boiled for 1 min to completely convert Se6+ toSe4+. Similarly, approximately 0.5 g of the tea sample was added to a mixed acid solution of HNO3 and HClO4 (vol: vol, 4:1) followed by boiling in the same state, and then reduced by HCl (HCl: H2O 1:1). Thereafter, the mixture solutions containing soil and tea samples were subjected to atomic fluorescence spectroscopy to measure the total Se content (AF-610 A, China, Beijing). To ensure data quality, standard reference materials for calcareous soil (GBW07402) and tea (GSV-4) were obtained from the Institute of Geophysical and Geochemical Exploration, Geological Survey of China. The corresponding recovery rates ranged from 92.56 to 106.25% for soil and 91.67–99.56% for tea. The relative standard deviations were 0.34–3.67% and 1.12–4.96%, respectively, demonstrating a high level of accuracy and precision in the measurements, which are well within acceptable limits.

Following parameters were all measured with the method of Lu [33]. The soil pH was determined in deionized water at a soil: solution ratio of 1:2.5 using an FE20K device (Mettler-Toledo, Switzerland). SOM was measured through wet oxidation using potassium dichromate redox colorimetry. Total soil N was measured using an elemental analyser (Vario EL Cube, Germany). Available soil N was determined using the alkali hydrolysis diffusion method. Samples for measuring soil total P and available P were pretreated with H2SO4 + HClO4 solution and 0.5 M NaHCO3, respectively, followed by measurement through molybdenum colorimetry. Soil total K and available K were assessed through extraction with NaOH and NH4OAc, measurement using a flare photometer (Kowa FM500, Kowa Company, Ltd). Soil amorphous iron oxide (AFeOX) and amorphous aluminum oxide (AAlOX) were extracted using an acidic ammonium oxalate buffer solution (pH = 3.17). Soil free iron oxide (CFeOX) and free aluminum oxide (CAlOX) in the soil were extracted using the DCB method (sodium dithionite-citrate-bicarbonate). The concentrations of iron and aluminum in the extraction solutions were determined using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (Avio 200, PerkinElmer, Singapore). Soil particle size fractions, i.e. sand (50–2000 μm), silt (2–50 μm), and clay (< 2 μm), were analysed using a particle size analyser (LS 13,320, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The statistic information of measurement soil and tea properties were showed in the Table 1.

Statistical analyses

The Se accumulation ability of tea from soil (i.e. tea Se enrichment) reflected by the response ratio of tea Se content to soil Se content, was calculated as follows:

where Se.coe is the Se enrichment coefficient of tea, Tea.Se is the Se content in tea, and Soil.Se is the Se content of soil.

The effects of various factors on soil Se and tea Se enrichment were evaluated by fitting a random-forest regression model using R software (version 4.1.1) with the software package “party” and the parameters “mtry” = 4 and “ntree” = 1,000 (Liu et al. 2019). The Gini coefficient computed from the random-forest structure was calculated to indicate the relative importance (%) of the respective characteristic [34]. Then, the relationship between soil and tea Se content and several important factors was analysed through linear or non-linear regression using Origin software (Origin Pro 9.0).

Direct and indirect effects of the factors on soil Se, tea Se enrichment, and tea Se content were estimated using through partial least squares modelling with the R package “vegan” (version 3.6.3). Two latent variables, that is, environment (i.e., various slope, elevation, precipitation and temperature) and management (i.e., nitrogen input, phosphorus input, potassium input, and planting duration) were considered to affected soil (i.e., pH, SOM, sand, clay, total N, available N, total P, available P, total K and available K) first, and then influenced the soil Se content and tea Se enrichment (i.e., Se.cone). The contents of Se in the tea was final effected. Before modelling, all data were tested and normalized (making sure the data is in a standard normal distribution with a mean value of 0 and standard deviation of 1) [35].

All other statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2013 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmont, WA, USA).

Results

Enrichment of soil and tea Se

Overall, in Jiangxi Province, Se concentrations in soil and tea leaves followed a normal distribution (Fig. 3), with regional averages of 0.44 ± 0.16 mg kg− 1 and 0.10 ± 0.05 mg kg− 1, respectively, based on 67 samples from major tea cultivation areas without exogenous Se supplementation (Fig. 2; Table 1). Specifically, soil Se content in tea cultivation areas ranged from 0.19 mg kg− 1 to 0.89 mg kg− 1, with 51% of observations exceeding 0.40 mg kg− 1 (Table 1; Fig. 3). Spatially, the highest soil Se content was observed in the centre of Jiangxi at 0.58 mg kg− 1, followed by the northeast at 0.42 mg kg− 1while the north and south areas were relatively low, averaging 0.31 mg kg− 1 and 0.29 mg kg− 1, respectively. Furthermore, Se content in tea varied from 0.03 mg kg− 1 to 0.27 mg kg− 1, with 58% of observations below than the average of 0.1 mg kg− 1 (Table 1; Fig. 3). Consistent with soil results, the highest tea Se levels occurred in the central Jiangxi, and lower levels at the north (0.11 mg kg− 1), northeastern (0.06 mg kg− 1), and southern borders (0.06 mg kg− 1; Fig. 2). Generally, Se levels in soil and tea were highly scattered, with high coefficients variation of 51.8% and 35.6%, respectively (Table 1).

Impact of various factors on soil Se

Multiple random forest models revealed that soil SOM, AFeOX, and CAlOX were the most important factors influencing Se accumulation in the red soil of tea cultivation areas in Jiangxi Province. These models considered 29 influencing parameters, including human management, local environment, and soil properties (Fig. 4a). The relative importance of the top three factors was 15%, 20%, and 22%, respectively. Additionally, soil Se enrichment was affected by total soil potassium content (K), AAlOX, and tea planting duration, contributing 6%, 8%, and 10%, respectively (Fig. 4a). In detail, soil Se accumulation significantly increased with soil SOM (y = 0.0036 x + 0.32, R2 = 0.2, P < 0.001; Fig. 5a), AFeOX (y = 0.0062 x + 0.27, R2 = 0.24, P < 0.053; Fig. 5c), AAlOX (y = 0.057 x + 0.25, R2 = 0.47, P < 0.0015; Fig. 5d), and duration (y = 0.0032 x + 0.37, R2 = 0.14, P = 0.001; Fig. 5f). Soil Se content increased with soil CAlOX when its concentration was low, but decreased when CAlOX content was too high CAlOX (y = -0.44 × 2 − 17.53 x + 0.16, R2 = 0.24, P = 0.0076; Fig. 5e). Conversely, soil Se content significantly decreased with soil total K content (y = -0.0018 x + 0.56, R2 = 0.13, P < 0.05; Fig. 5b).

Impact of various factors on tea Se

For tea Se accumulation, the most important factor was the Se content in the corresponding soils of tea planting, with a relative contribution of 29% in the random forest models (Fig. 3b). Consequently, soil CFeOX and CAlOX content also contributed significantly to tea Se enrichment, by 9% and 15%, respectively. Furthermore, soil SOM (5%), available potassium (5%), input of P fertilizer (6%), and input of K amount (5%) contributed to tea Se enrichment, albeit to a lesser extent than other influencing factors (Fig. 3b). Specifically, there was a significant linear correlation between the Se contents in soils and tea leaves, described by the equation Y = 0.15 X + 0.032 (R2 = 0.2, P < 0.001, n = 67; Fig. 6f). Furthermore, soil SOM (y = 6.28 x + 0.078, R2 = 0.046, P = 0.04), soil available K content (y = 0.039 x + 0.074, R2 = 0.36, P = 0.02), and soil CFeOX (y = 0.019 x + 0.063, R2 = 0.39, P = 0.0023) were also positively correlated with tea Se content (Fig. 6a, b, and c). Similarly to soil CAlOX contributing to soil Se content, the Se concentration in tea leaves also increased when CAlOX content was low and decreased when CAlOX content was high (Fig. 6d). Relative to the influence of soil potassium on soil Se content, the accumulation of Se in tea leaves also significantly decreased with the amount of input potassium fertilizer (y = 0.0023 × 2 – 0.0044x + 0.12, R2 = 0.47, P < 0.001; Fig. 6e).

Discussion

Enrichment of soil and tea Se

Currently, in Jiangxi Province, Se levels in tea plantation soils (represented by the blue areas in Fig. 2) are very high. The regional average Se concentration (0.44 mg kg− 1) aligns closely with findings from [36]. In red soil regions. Notably, 51% of the observations in our study exceed the Se-rich soil classification standard threshold of 0.40 mg kg− 1 in China [29]. Furthermore, these values are higher than the Chinese average of 0.29 mg kg− 1 and the global average of 0.15 mg kg− 1 [29, 37]. However, Se concentrations in tea were lower than expected based on the high levels in soil samples (Fig. 2). Despite this, similar Se distribution patterns were observed in both soils and tea leaves, with tea Se content strongly varying with soil Se content from the centre of Jiangxi to the north, northeast, and south (Fig. 2). To explain these variations and uncover the mechanisms of soil Se enrichment, we analysed the effects of various factors on soil and tea Se in the following.

Impact of various factors on soil Se

The spatial distribution and enrichment of soil Se in Jiangxi Province are strongly influenced by various factors, including the local environment, human management, and soil properties (Fig. 4). Among these, soil properties—particularly SOM, soil potassium, and oxides of iron and aluminium — contributed the most to the current Se distribution pattern in major tea planting areas in Jiangxi, compared to climate, topography, and human fertilizer application (Fig. 4). Previous studies revealed that soil Se levels were largely determined by soil parent material [38–40], due to soil parent material was the initial source of Se in the soil [41]. However, the contribution of soil parent material to soil Se accumulation is low, in present study (Fig. 4). This is likely because the soil in the region, primarily developed from Quaternary red clay, has similar initial Se compositions [41]. Additionally, intense leaching and acidification have further depleted most of the Se [42]. This also partly explained the reaction of soil Se content to SOM, and iron and aluminium oxides. Our observation revealed a significantly positive correlation between soil Se accumulation and soil SOM, AFeOX, AAlOX and CAlOX content (Fig. 5a, c, d, and e). The associated mechanism is that soil SOM and iron and aluminium oxides strongly binding Se when it is separated from soil minerals or parent materials. Several studies have shown this process; for instance, Abrams and Coppin found that soil organic matter absorbed 40-50% of free Se species, while Lu and Supriatin reported 95% of Se bound to SOM in Se-poor Dutch agricultural soils [40, 43–45]. Borsig investigated the immobilization mechanisms of Se uptake into iron oxides, while Jordan confirmed the adsorption process of Se onto nano-transition alumina at both macroscopic and molecular levels [46, 47]. Ultimately, our results also revealed a similar spatial pattern of soil Se, soil SOM, and iron and aluminium oxides, with high levels observed in the centre of Jiangxi, followed by the northeast, north, and south areas (Figs. 2 and 5).

Conversely, a strong antagonistic interaction between soil Se and potassium was observed in this study (Fig. 5b), consistent with findings by Yu in Fujian Provinces [48]. This effect may be attributed to Se predominantly existing in soil as selenite (Se4+), which shares the same positive charge as potassium (K+) ions, thereby creating antagonistic interactions [49].

Impact of various factors on tea Se

The accumulation of Se in plants significantly increased with soil Se content in both natural environments (without Se fertilizer) and under artificial Se supplementation conditions [13, 25, 38]. Furthermore, our random forest model confirmed this result, with soil Se content contributing mostly and significantly by 29% to tea Se accumulation (Fig. 4b). A significant (P < 0.001) positive and linear relationship between soil Se and tea Se content was also quantified as Y = 0.15 X + 0.032 (Fig. 6f; [50]). Nonetheless, while Se levels in soils often determine the potential uptake of Se into plants, the plant-availability of soil Se is highly influenced by various factors such as local climate, human management, and particularly soil properties (Fig. 4b). Previous research revealed a negative correlation between the bioavailability of Se and the contents of SOM, and Fe/Mn oxides in specific soils with plant cultivation [26, 44]. However, in the present study, Se concentrations significantly increased with soil SOM, available potassium, CFeOX, and CAlOX content (Fig. 6). We believe that SOM-bound and Fe/Mn/Al oxide-bound Se are less available to plants owing to their strong binding to soil components. However, our survey indicated that regions in Jiangxi with high SOM and Fe/Al oxide content are more likely to produce tea with high Se levels. Similarly, Wang et al. also reported that SOM-bound Se content was positively correlated with plant Se content in maize [51]. The possible reason is that soils achieved a higher Se content were often contained a higher SOM, and Fe/Al oxide (data not show), thus created a positive correlation between them (Fig. 6).

Furthermore, the accumulation of selenium (Se) in tea leaves is also strongly influenced by various soil nutrient elements, particularly potassium (K) (Fig. 4b). Unlike the observed decrease in soil Se accumulation with increasing soil total K content (Fig. 5b), tea Se accumulation was positively associated with soil available K content (Fig. 6b). However, Se accumulation in tea decreased following the application of K fertilizer (Fig. 6e). This is likely because the bioavailability of soil Se and K to tea plants is similarly affected by soil properties such as pH, redox potential (Eh), soil texture, and the presence of iron oxides/hydroxides and organic matter [52]. For example, the availability of both soil Se and K tends to increase with rising soil pH [53]. However, excessive application of K fertilizer can drive antagonistic interactions, limiting Se uptake by tea plants [49]. It is also important to note that crop type, soil characteristics, environmental conditions, and fertilizer type can significantly influence these interactions [54]. Additionally, nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers frequently enhance plant uptake of soil Se [55].

Potential mechanism of soil Se transport into tea leaves

To gain a comprehensive understanding of the response of tea Se to soil Se, we conducted an analysis using the partial least squares modelling, as shown in Fig. 7. The final models explained 96% of tea Se content change and 49% of soil Se (GOF = 0.6588, Fig. 7). Among all factors included in the current study, SOM, total and available K, AFeOX and CFeOX, AAlOX and CAlOX were the most important variables with positive effects on soil and tea Se content, directly or through interaction with other factors. Specifically, in red soil of Jiangxi Province, at least four pathways were recognized significantly by our models for environment influenced on Se accumulation in tea. First, local environment (slope, elevation, precipitation, and temperature) had a significant indirect effect on soil Se content via SOM (environment to SOM path coefficient = 0.37, P < 0.001; SOM to soil Se path coefficient = 0.43, P < 0.001), and then significantly effects on the transportation of soil Se to tea leaves (Soil Se to tea Se path coefficient = 0.64, P < 0.001). Second, environmental effects on soil total/available potassium influence soil Se (environment to potassium path coefficient = 0.30, P < 0.001; potassium to soil Se path coefficient = 0.17, P < 0.05), ultimately affecting tea Se content (coefficient = 0.64, P < 0.001). Third and fourth, the uptake of soil Se by tea plants is indirectly influenced by the environment through its impact on soil iron oxides (environment to FeOX path coefficient = 0.42, P < 0.001; FeOX to soil Se path coefficient = 0.31, P < 0.05; soil Se to tea Se path coefficient = 0.64, P < 0.001) and aluminum oxides (environment to AlOX path coefficient = 0.39, P < 0.001; AlOX to soil Se path coefficient = 0.42, P < 0.05; soil Se to tea Se path coefficient = 0.64, P < 0.001; Fig. 7).

Furthermore, four similar pathways of human management effects on tea Se enrichment were observed in the models (Fig. 7). First, management practices (such as the amount of applied nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium fertilizers, and the duration of tea cultivation) influence SOM, which in turn affects soil Se and ultimately tea Se. Second, management effects on soil total/available phosphorus effect on soil Se, ultimately affecting tea Se content. The other two pathways were management indirectly influenced soil and tea Se through the iron and aluminum oxides respectively in Jiangxi Province. Conclusions

Our large-scale field survey in Jiangxi Province, China, reveals that while selenium levels in tea plant soils are high, selenium accumulation in tea leaves remains lower than anticipated. This study demonstrates that soil selenium content is the primary driver of selenium levels in tea plants, though it is modulated by factors such as local environmental conditions, human management practices, and specific soil properties, particularly soil SOM, potassium, and iron and aluminum oxides. Spatially, these soil properties enhance the potential for selenium uptake by tea plants, thus affecting selenium concentrations in tea leaves. The pathways identified, along with the partial least squares models developed, highlight the direct and indirect influences of the environment, management, and soil characteristics on selenium accumulation. Our findings offer a comprehensive framework to better predict and manage selenium levels in tea production systems, informing strategies for optimal selenium bioavailability in tea crops.
