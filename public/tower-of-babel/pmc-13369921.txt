Prediction of the axial capacity of various polygon-type section concrete-filled steel (CFST) columns utilising deep neural network and machine learning regressor
Authors: Yue Chen, Haytham F. Isleem, Bagas R. Subchan, Mohammad Khishe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The advantages of concrete-filled steel tubular (CFST) have made it a popular choice for structural applications. For structural design and safety, the precise estimation of the axial capacity of the CFST is a crucial element. However, design codes currently cover only conventional CFST parts, such as square, circular, and rectangular sections, and aren’t applicable to polygonal sections, such as hexagonal, octagonal, etc. In this study, four data-driven models were developed to predict the axial capacity of polygonal CFST columns, including an optimized multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network, Support Vector Regressor (SVR), Random Forest Regressor (RFR), and Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR). This study uses a dataset containing 835 experimental and numerical test samples from the literature. All samples will be analysed using machine learning models, and the best predictors will be determined. For further evaluation, ML model predictions will be compared to existing design codes (such as Eurocode 4, ANSI/AISC 360, Chinese Code GB50936, etc.) and proposed equations from the literature. The results showed that DNN was the best predictor and outstanding (which can be applied to all polygonal CFST) when compared to design codes and proposed equations from the literature. Finally, a GUI-based polygonal CFST application predictor is offered to integrate engineering applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49985-z.

Introduction

Concrete-filled steel tubular (CFST) is a composite structural system consisting of an outer steel tube filled with concrete. It is currently receiving a lot of attention due to its advantages, including high ductility and stiffness, high strength, high seismic absorbance, and high fire resistance1–3. CFST can also serve as permanent formwork and primary reinforcement, which reduces construction time and labour4. The use of CFST is no longer restricted to square, rectangular, or circular shape sections; it may now be applied to a wide variety of shape sections, including triangular, pentagonal, hexagonal, heptagonal, and octagonal CFST, or polygonal CFST. Since current design codes, such as Eurocode 4 (EC4)5, and ANSI/AISC 3606 only offering simplified square and circular sections, other shapes of CFST lack a trustworthy and precise method for determining their axial capacity7.

Over recent years, there have been several experimental and numerical studies on the behaviour of CFST columns with a variety of shapes and geometrical aspects, among them Chen and Chan8 studied octagonal CFST utilising the circumcircle approach of EC4 and ANSI/AISC 360, as well as the Chinese code GB 50,936 − 2014, and obtained excellent predictions. Ayough et al.9 also explored hexagonal and octagonal CFST utilising EC4 (using the square and circle CFST equations), and the ANSI/AISC 360. As a result, ANSI/AISC 360 forecasts with less accuracy than EC4 (with the mean values of Nu, exp/Nu, and pre of 1.09 using the square equation, and 0.96 and 0.99 using the circular equation for hexagonal and octagonal, respectively). However, research has yet to establish the use of machine learning to predict the axial capacity of polygonal CFST, in contrast to design codes and proposed formulas in the literature.

The limitations of the design regulations for calculating axial capacity of CFST are outlined in Table 1. AISC 360 − 16 and Eurocode 4 limit the concrete compressive strength, denoted by (f’c) the steel yield strength, denoted by (fy), whereas ACI 318 limits only the concrete compressive strength and does not impose a direct limit on steel strength. In comparison, GB50936 established restrictions for both f’c and fy. AISC 360 established limits for f’c between 21 and 69 MPa, fy of steel composites below 525 MPa, and fy of reinforcing bars below 550 MPa. ACI318 established restrictions for f’c above 17.2 MPa. Eurocode 4 restricts f’c at 20–50 MPa and fy to 230–460 MPa. Meanwhile, GB50936 established restrictions for f’c below 60 MPa and fy below 500 MPa.

Compared with traditional approaches, machine learning (ML) methods are more efficient, as conventional methods are time-consuming and expensive10. Researchers have undertaken extensive studies on machine learning to predict axial CFST. Zhang et al.11 studied predictors of circular CFST reinforced with basalt fiber-reinforced polymer (BFRP) axial capacity using Linear Regression (LR), Support Vector Regression (SVR), Bayesian Ridge Regression (BRR), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, and CatBoost algorithms. It was discovered that LR, SVR, and BRR had accuracy R2s of approximately 0.81, while CatBoost had the highest at 0.98, and RF and XGBoost had R2s of 0.97. Lai et al.12 studied circular CFST using ML models, and the best predictors were Natural Gradient Boosting (NGBoost), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Wang et al.13 explored rectangular CFST using ANN and obtained an R² accuracy of 0.97. However, the research covers only circular and rectangular CFST, with no studies on polygonal CFST. Very recent studies have shown that advanced machine-learning and deep-learning models can also predict the capacity of other non-conventional CFST and closely related composite columns, including round-ended CFST columns and elliptical concrete-filled steel tubular short columns1,4.

Recent studies have further confirmed the effectiveness of data-driven methods for predicting the axial capacity of steel–concrete composite coluns. Bayesian optimization has been used to improve machine-learning hyperparameter selection for CFST axial-capacity prediction14. ANN, GEP, and MLR have been applied to recycled-aggregate concrete-filled circular steel tube columns15, while ANN, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting approaches have been used for high-strength CFST columns16. Metaheuristic-assisted neural-network models have also been reported for square concrete-filled steel tube columns17 and for concrete–steel columns more broadly18, showing that optimization-based learning frameworks can improve predictive accuracy. Recent hybrid studies have also combined machine learning with finite-element modelling for concrete-filled PVC tube columns under uniaxial eccentric compression and for elliptical FRP-confined concrete columns under axial compression, further supporting the use of data-driven frameworks for composite-column capacity assessment2,3. Similar soft-computing strategies have also been extended to GFRP-reinforced concrete columns19. Machine learning has also been extended to elliptical FRP-concrete-steel double-skin columns for axial load-capacity prediction, indicating that accurate data-driven strength estimation is feasible even for more complex composite-column configurations20. Despite these advances, studies focused specifically on polygon-type CFST columns remain very limited8,9,19,22,42,55.

Aside from that, there is a neural network method known as a Deep Neural Network (DNN) that has more hidden layers between the input and output, which improves neural network learning and mapping skills and outperforms ANNs. DNN is a viable option for fault tolerance and robustness21,22. Xu et al.23 conducted a recent study utilising a DNN to forecast stress-strain curves for ultra-high-performance concrete (UHPC). They found that the results were exceptionally accurate in complex studies. As an outcome, the authors will propose using DNN for machine learning analysis.

Thus, this study uses machine learning models, including the Deep Neural Network (DNN), Support Vector Regressor (SVR), Random Forest Regressor (RFR), and Gradient Boosting Regressor (GBR), to examine the axial capacity of polygonal CFST. All ML models will be assessed to determine the best predictor of polygonal CFST. ML models will be compared with design codes and proposed formulas from the current literature to make a comprehensive decision. Lastly, an application-based GUI predictor for polygonal CFST is developed, integrating engineering applications to simplify prediction.

Machine learning approach

Data collection and preprocessing

Dataset collection

The dataset utilized in this study comprises 835 samples from 41 studies8,9,24–62, meticulously compiled from existing literature and finite element method (FEM) analyses in the current study. This comprehensive dataset encompasses a range of input variables (X1 to X14) and a single output variable (Y), each playing a critical role in predicting the load-carrying capacity of composite columns. Table 1 provides a detailed overview of these variables, including their names, units, and descriptions. All sample details were illustrated in Fig. 1.

Because the database combines published experimental records and finite element (FE) results generated in the current study, leakage control was treated as a key requirement. Each sample was tagged byits source study, and FE samples from the current investigation were additionally grouped by parametric family so that closely related cases derived from the same base configuration remained in a single group. All train-validation splits were then performed at the group level rather than at the individual-row level. This grouping strategy prevented similar specimen families or FE variants from appearing simultaneously in training and validation subsets.

Overview of dataset variables.

Fig. 1Sample details illustration of this study.

Sample details illustration of this study.

The input variables were selected to represent the main geometric, material, and loading parameters governing the axial behavior of polygonal concrete-filled steel tubular columns. X1 denotes the compressive strength of standard concrete cylinders. X2–X7 describe the specimen geometry, including column height, cross-sectional perimeter, polygon angles, base width, and section depth. X8–X13 characterize the steel components and inner hollow section through the thickness and yield strength of the outer and inner steel tubes, the depth of the inner steel tube, and the area of the inner void. X14 represents the eccentric loading ratio and captures non-uniform loading conditions that commonly arise in practice.

The output variable, Y, is the load-carrying capacity in kN. Together, these variables provide a physically meaningful description of each specimen and include the main factors affecting axial strength. This variable set supports the development of a robust prediction model for polygonal CFST columns with different shapes, material strengths, and loading conditions. This careful selection and detailed characterization of variables facilitates the creation of an accurate and reliable dataset, which is essential for the successful application of finite element methods and machine learning approaches to predict structural performance.

Dataset analysis

A comprehensive analysis of the dataset presented in Supplementary Table S1 was conducted to understand the underlying characteristics and relationships among the variables. Table 2 presents a detailed statistical description of each variable, including measures such as mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values, quartiles, skewness, and kurtosis. The statistical analysis reveals significant variability across the variables.

For instance, X1 has a mean of 50.4 MPa and a standard deviation of 26.6 MPa, indicating a broad range of concrete strengths in the dataset. The X2 shows considerable variation, with values ranging from 248.8 mm to 3810 mm and a high standard deviation of 491.99 mm, suggesting diverse column sizes are represented. The X3 also displays substantial variability, reflecting the inclusion of various column shapes and sizes. Angles X4 and X5 have skewness of -1.34 and − 1.26, respectively, indicating left-skewed distributions, with most data points clustering toward higher angle values. This is consistent with typical column designs, in which angles are often standardized within a specific range. Geometric dimensions such as X6 and X7 exhibit positive skewness (0.69 and 0.84, respectively), suggesting that while most columns have smaller base and depth dimensions, there are instances of significantly larger sizes. Material properties, including X8 to X13, show varying degrees of skewness and kurtosis, highlighting the diversity in steel tube thicknesses, yield strengths, and hollow section areas. Notably, X13 and X14 exhibit extreme skewness and kurtosis, indicating the presence of outliers and a heavy-tailed distribution.

Statistical description analysis of the dataset.

Figure 2 displays histogram distributions for various variables in the dataset, providing a visual representation of each variable’s density over specified ranges. This visualization is essential for understanding the distribution characteristics, such as central tendencies, spread, and the presence of outliers or skewness. Variables X1, X2, X3, X6, and X7 demonstrate a range of distribution shapes, with some showing significant skewness. For example, X2 and X12 exhibit pronounced right skewness, which is consistent with their high skewness values of 2.2687 and 2.1253, respectively, as noted in Table 2. This characteristic suggests heavy tails and peakedness, distinct from a normal distribution. The histograms corroborate this by showing a long tail extending toward higher values.

Variables X4 and X5 exhibit left-skewed distributions, as evidenced by their negative skewness values of -1.3403 and − 1.2625. The histograms for these variables indicate that most data points are concentrated at higher values, with fewer occurrences of lower values, aligning with the quartile measurements listed in Table 2. Variables X8 and X9 also exhibit right-skewed distributions, with histograms showing longer right tails. This aligns with their skewness and kurtosis values, indicating a concentration of data points near a mode with fewer outliers extending toward larger values.

Variables X10 and X11, notable for their extreme positive skewness and high kurtosis, suggest the presence of significant outliers or a non-uniform distribution in which most data points cluster near zero, while some extend to much larger values. Their histograms underscore this distribution pattern, with the majority of data concentrated near zero and sporadic larger values appearing infrequently. Lastly, the output variable Y shows considerable variability and skewness of 1.4619, with its histogram illustrating a broad spread of values, peaking at lower ranges and then tapering off. This pattern matches its high standard deviation and range as reported in Table 2.

The architecture of the proposed DNN comprises multiple hidden layers, each containing a substantial number of neurons to ensure sufficient capacity for learning intricate patterns from the input variables.

Hyperparameter optimization

Hyperparameter optimization was applied to all predictive models considered in this study. In this study, we employ Bayesian Optimization67 optimization was used to identify the best-performing hyperparameter settings within the training data of each outer fold, thereby avoiding optimistic bias during model selection.

Bayesian Optimization is a sequential model-based optimization method that builds a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective function and uses it to select the most promising hyperparameters to evaluate in the true objective function. Unlike grid search, which exhaustively explores the hyperparameter space, or random search, which samples hyperparameters randomly, Bayesian Optimization strategically balances exploration and exploitation to identify optimal hyperparameters with fewer evaluations. This efficiency is particularly advantageous given the computationally intensive nature of training DNNs.

The Bayesian Optimization process begins with the selection of a prior distribution over the objective function, typically modeled using a Gaussian Process (GP). The GP serves as the surrogate model, capturing our beliefs about the objective function based on the observed data. Mathematically, the GP is defined by its mean function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m\left(x\right)$$\end{document} and covariance function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k\left(x,{x}^{{\prime\:}}\right)$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:x$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}^{{\prime\:}}\:$$\end{document}represent different hyperparameter configurations as given by Eq. As hyperparameter evaluations are performed, the GP is updated with the observed performance metrics, refining the surrogate model’s predictions. (5).5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{EI}\left(x\right)=\mathbb{E}\:\left[\mathrm{max}\left(0,f\left(x\right)-f\left({x}^{+}\right)\right)\right]$$\end{document}

The hyperparameter configuration that maximizes the acquisition function is selected for the subsequent evaluation, and the process iterates until a predefined stopping criterion is met, such as a maximum number of iterations or convergence of the objective function.

In the context of this study, the hyperparameters optimized using Bayesian optimization include number of hidden layers (determines the depth of the DNN, influencing its capacity to capture complex patterns), number of neurons per layer (affects the model’s ability to learn representations of varying complexity), learning rate (controls the step size during the optimization process, impacting convergence speed and stability), dropout rate (mitigates overfitting by randomly deactivating neurons during training), batch size (influences the gradient estimation and training dynamics).

The optimization workflow commenced with an initial exploration phase, where a diverse set of hyperparameter configurations was evaluated to establish a foundational understanding of the objective function. Subsequently, Bayesian Optimization iteratively refined the search based on the surrogate model’s predictions and the acquisition function’s guidance. This method allowed for efficient identification of hyperparameter settings that yielded optimal performance metrics, specifically minimizing the MSE and maximizing the R2 on the validation dataset.

To implement Bayesian Optimization, we utilized the scikit-optimize library, which provides robust tools for sequential model-based optimization. The optimization was constrained within reasonable bounds for each hyperparameter to ensure computational feasibility and practical relevance. For instance, the number of hidden layers was restricted between 2 and 5, neurons per layer between 10 and 100, and the learning rate between 1 × 10− 5 and 1 × 10− 1.

Model training and validation

The training, tuning, and validation of all predictive models were conducted using a nested grouped cross-validation framework. To achieve a robust evaluation, the study employs a 5-fold cross-validation strategy coupled with comprehensive validation metrics, namely the R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE.

Model training with 5-fold cross-validation

Model development followed a nested grouped cross-validation workflow to obtain an unbiased estimate of predictive performance. First, the full dataset was divided using an outer 5-fold GroupKFold procedure, where grouping was defined by source study and FE parametric family. In each outer iteration, one grouped fold was held out for testing and the remaining grouped folds were used for model development.

Within each outer-training partition, hyperparameter tuning was carried out using an inner grouped cross-validation loop. The same workflow was applied to all four models considered in this study, namely the MLP neural network, Support Vector Regressor, Random Forest Regressor, and Gradient Boosting Regressor, thereby ensuring a fair comparison. After selecting the best hyperparameter configuration from the inner loop, the corresponding model was retrained on the full outer-training data and evaluated on the untouched outer-test fold.

Final model performance was reported by aggregating the outer-fold results using R2, RMSE, MAE, and MAPE. The actual-versus-predicted plots were generated from out-of-fold predictions collected across the outer test folds. Complete step wise steps is depicted in Fig. 12.

Feature importance

Understanding the relative importance of input features is essential for interpreting the DNN model’s predictions and for gaining insights into the underlying factors that influence the load carrying capacity of composite columns. In this study, we employ SHAP analysis68,69 to quantify and visualize the contribution of each feature to the model’s output. SHAP is a game-theoretic approach that assigns each feature an importance value for a particular prediction, providing both global and local interpretability of the model.

SHAP values are grounded in cooperative game theory, where each feature is considered a “player” contributing to the “game” of making a prediction. The SHAP value for a feature represents the average marginal contribution of that feature across all possible feature coalitions. Mathematically, the SHAP value for a feature iii is given by Eq. (10).10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\phi\:}_{i}\left(f\right)={\sum\:}_{S\subseteq\:N\setminus\:\left\{i\right\}}\frac{\left|S\right|!\left(\left|N\right|-\left|S\right|-1\right)!}{\left|N\right|!}\left[f\left(S\cup\:\left\{i\right\}\right)-f\left(S\right)\right]$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:f$$\end{document} is the predictive model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} is the set of all features, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S$$\end{document} is a subset of features excluding iii. This equation ensures that the SHAP values satisfy desirable properties such as local accuracy, missingness, and consistency, making them a robust choice for feature importance analysis.

The methodology for applying SHAP analysis in this study involves several key steps. First, the trained DNN model is integrated with the SHAP library, which leverages the model’s prediction capabilities to compute SHAP values for each feature across the dataset. Given the complexity of DNNs, which consist of multiple hidden layers and nonlinear activations, SHAP effectively decomposes the model’s predictions into contributions from each input feature, despite the model’s inherent opacity.

Once the SHAP values are computed, they are aggregated to derive global feature importance metrics. This aggregation involves averaging the absolute SHAP values across all samples, providing a ranking of features based on their overall impact on the model’s predictions. Features with higher average SHAP values are deemed more influential in determining the load carrying capacity. Additionally, SHAP summary plots and dependence plots are generated to visualize the distribution and interaction of SHAP values, offering deeper insights into how specific features affect the predictions across different data points.

The regularization parameter alpha was set to 0.01 to prevent overfitting by penalizing large weights. A constant learning rate (learning_rate) with an initial value of 0.01 ensures a steady and controlled update of weights during training. Finally, a generous max_iter of 1500 iterations guarantees sufficient training epochs for the network to converge to an optimal solution.

The optimization process, leveraging Bayesian Optimization, systematically explored the hyperparameter spaces to identify configurations that minimize the MSE and enhance the model’s predictive accuracy. This method proved to be highly effective, as evidenced by the superior performance metrics attained by each model post-optimization, particularly the DNN model. The chosen hyperparameters not only improved individual model performances but also contributed to the overall robustness and generalizability of the predictions.

Feature importance analysis

Understanding the influence of various features on the predictive models is crucial for interpreting model behavior and enhancing decision-making processes. In this study, SHAP values were employed to determine the impact of each feature on the output of the GBR and RFR. The SHAP summary plots for both models are illustrated in Figs. 20 and 21, providing a visual interpretation of feature contributions across individual predictions.

Gradient boosting regressor feature importance

Figure 20 presents the SHAP summary plot for the Gradient Boosting Regressor. This visualization clearly depicts the distribution of the impact each feature has on the model output, with the color scale indicating feature value (red high, blue low). It is evident from the plot that X3 (Perimeter of the column cross-section) and X8 (Thickness of the outer steel tube) are the most influential features, showing substantial positive and negative impacts on the model output, respectively. High values of X3 tend to increase the predicted load carrying capacity significantly, which is intuitive as larger perimeters typically suggest greater structural strength.

Fig. 20SHAP summary plot of GBR model.

SHAP summary plot of GBR model.

Conversely, increased values of X8 generally lead to a decrease in the load carrying capacity. This might indicate that thicker outer tubes could be introducing stiffness that does not proportionally contribute to bearing more load under the conditions simulated. Other significant features such as X1 (Compressive strength of standard concrete cylinders) and X6 (Base of the column cross-section) also show notable variability in their effects, suggesting complex interactions between the material properties and geometric dimensions of the columns.

Random forest regressor feature importance

Figure 21 displays the SHAP summary plot for the Random Forest Regressor. Similar to the GBR, X3 appears as a predominant feature, reinforcing the importance of the perimeter in determining the structural capacity of the columns. The plot also highlights the significant role of X7 (Depth of the column cross-section) and X6, with high values generally correlating with increased predictive outputs. This aligns with structural engineering principles where deeper and broader columns can support greater loads.

Fig. 21SHAP summary plot of the RFR model.

SHAP summary plot of the RFR model.

In contrast to the GBR, the RFR demonstrates a more distributed influence across features, indicating a model that relies on a broader array of inputs to make predictions. This behavior is typical of Random Forest models, which integrate diverse decision paths across many decision trees, thereby utilizing a broader range of feature information.

The comparison between Figs. 20 and 21 reveals distinct patterns of feature importance between the two models, underscoring the different mechanisms by which these algorithms process the same input data. While GBR shows concentrated effects from fewer features, RFR distributes importance more evenly among a larger set of features. This distinction can guide practical applications: for scenarios where specific features are known to be more reliable or easier to measure, GBR might be preferable due to its focused sensitivity. Conversely, RFR might be more suitable for applications requiring robustness to variations across a broader set of features.

In summary, the SHAP analysis provides a compelling narrative about how different features drive complex models’ predictions in the context of structural engineering. By understanding these dynamics, engineers and researchers can better design experiments, interpret model predictions, and make informed decisions about structural design and assessment. The insights drawn from these analyses not only enhance trust in the machine learning models but also enrich our understanding of the fundamental factors influencing composite column behavior.

Graphical user interface

The Graphical User Interface (GUI) of the application, as depicted in Fig. 22, serves as a user-friendly platform enabling users to easily input parameters, predict outcomes, and visualize the structure of the data inputs and outputs. This section delves into the design and functionality of the GUI, emphasizing its integral role in facilitating the practical application of the machine learning model developed to predict the load carrying capacity of composite columns.

The GUI layout is divided into two main sections: the input panel on the left and the information panel on the right. The input panel consists of a series of text fields where users can enter values for each of the 14 features (X1 to X14) that influence the prediction model. These features include compressive strength of standard concrete cylinders, height of the column, perimeter of the column cross-section, and other geometric and material properties that are critical in calculating the column’s load carrying capacity.

Each input field is clearly labeled with the corresponding variable name and unit (e.g., X1 (MPa), X2 (mm)), which not only aids in data entry but also reduces potential errors due to unit mismatches or misinterpretations. After inputting the data, the user can initiate the prediction by clicking the ‘Predict’ button. This triggers the backend predictive workflow to process the inputs using the same preprocessing pipeline adopted during model development and then pass them through the trained optimized MLP neural-network model selected as the best performer in the comparative analysis to generate the predicted load-carrying capacity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y$$\end{document}), which is displayed in a popup message box.

For transparency, the comparative benchmark models remain available in the archived code package, but the default GUI prediction corresponds to the final selected MLP model reported in Tables 4 and 5.

The information panel, shown on the right side of Fig. 22, provides a detailed overview of each variable used in the model. It lists the variable name, unit, and a brief description, thereby educating the user about the nature of each input and its relevance to the prediction task. This section is particularly useful for new users or stakeholders who may not be familiar with the specific terminologies or the structural engineering domain, as it enhances transparency and understanding of how the model makes its predictions.

Upon submission of the input values, the prediction result is immediately displayed in a dialog box, as seen in the screenshot where the predicted load carrying capacity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y$$\end{document} is shown. The prediction, expressed in kilonewtons (kN), is based on the user-provided inputs, processed through the predictive model. This instant feedback loop makes it practical for engineers or decision-makers to use the application in real-time scenarios, such as during planning stages of construction projects or in educational settings for instructional purposes.

The GUI is intended as a practical implementation tool and does not replace the methodological details presented in Sect. 2.1–2.5. One can reproduce the prediction workflow directly from the manuscript through the reported dataset variables, preprocessing procedure, DNN architecture, hyperparameter optimization settings, and validation protocol. To further support transparency and reuse, the developed GUI, trained model files, and source code are provided through the Code and GUI Availability statement.

Conclusions

This study developed and compared four data-driven models for predicting the axial capacity of polygon-type concrete-filled steel tubular columns using a dataset of 835 samples collected from 41 studies. Among the evaluated models, the DNN delivered the best overall performance under the adopted validation framework, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}^{2}=0.9979$$\end{document}, RMSE = 362.83 kN, MAE = 253.07 kN, and MAPE = 6.61%. These results were substantially better than those of SVR, RFR, and GBR, which confirms the stronger ability of the DNN to capture the nonlinear interaction among geometric, material, and loading variables.

The interpretation analysis further showed that the perimeter of the column cross-section and the thickness of the outer steel tube were among the most influential predictors, while section depth and base dimensions also made strong contributions to capacity estimation. The comparison with existing design-code equations and published formulas showed that the proposed framework provides broader applicability across polygonal CFST shapes, where conventional provisions remain limited or shape-specific. The developed GUI translates the final optimized model into a practical prediction tool for engineering use. Overall, the study demonstrates that the proposed DNN-based framework provides an accurate and practical approach for axial-capacity prediction of polygon-type CFST columns.
