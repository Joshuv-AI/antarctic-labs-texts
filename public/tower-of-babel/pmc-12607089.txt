Prevalence of pressure ulcer and associated factors in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bekalu Mekonen Belay, Yeshiambaw Eshetie, Masresha Kassaw Ewunetu, Yirgalem Abere, Mengistu Ewunetu, Zemenu Addis Alem, Fetene Nigussie Tarekegn, Bantalem Tilaye Atinafu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure ulcers remain common in both hospitals and in the community settings, leading to chronic wounds that are difficult to treat.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the prevalence of pressure ulcer and associated factors in Ethiopia.MethodsThis review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A comprehensive search was conducted across electronic databases, including MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, Google Scholar, and PubMed from March 20 and April 6, 2025. The quality of each included article was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute's critical appraisal tool. Data were extracted using Microsoft Excel and analyzed using STATA version 17.0. A random effects model was employed to calculate the pooled effect size. The I2 test was used to assess the heterogeneity among the included studies. Additionally, a funnel plot and Egger’s regression test were used to assess publication bias. Sensitivity analysis were conducted.ResultsThis study included twelve articles comprising a total of 3319 participants. The overall pooled prevalence of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia was 10.93% (95% CI: 7.57%–14.29%), with significant heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 92.09%, P < 0.001). The prevalence may be overestimated due to potential publication bias. This systematic review and meta-analysis, identified the following factors as a predictors of pressure ulcers: being bedridden (AOR, 4.75, 95% CI 2.53–6.97); being diabetic (AOR, 3.28, 95% CI, 1.61–4.94); friction (AOR, 6.75, 95% CI, 2.15–11.36); hospital stay > 21 days (AOR, 4.9, 95% CI, 3.46–6.33); lack of frequent change in position (AOR, 5.96, 95% CI, 2.1–9.81); and sensory disorders (AOR, 2.93, 95% CI, 2.36–3.51).ConclusionThis study revealed a substantial burden of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia and identified several significant predictors for their development. This findings underscore the importance of implementing prevention and early detection strategy by clinicians and policymakers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25114-9.Keywords: Pressure ulcer, Bedsore, Pressure injury, Decubitus ulcer, Ethiopia

Introduction

A pressure ulcer (PU) also known as a bed sore or pressure injury; is an area of localized damage to the skin and underlying tissue caused by prolonged pressure and friction, particularly in bony regions of the body [1]. PU remain common in both hospital and community settings, often leading to a chronic wounds that are difficult to treat [2]. The development of PU among hospitalized patients is often linked to gaps in nursing care, such as inadequate skin care, infrequent repositioning, and insufficient documentation of risk factors [3].

Multiple chronic diseases, along with complicating factors such as immobility, tissue ischemia, and undernutrition, contribute to the development of PU in community settings, hospitals, and nursing facilities [4]. The risk factors most frequently associated with the development of PU include patient age, length of hospital stay, chronic conditions such as diabetes [5], and impaired mobility or sensation [6]. Patients undergoing orthopedic procedures are completely immobile on the operating table and unable to respond to bodily sensations, which can lead to tissue injury, particularly in the heel and pelvic areas [7].

The prevalence of PU varies significantly across different care setting [8]. The prevalence and incidence of PU remained relatively stable between 1990 and 2017, with no significant improvement observed [9], while regions with a high-middle socio demographic index (SDI) experienced an increase risk [10]. The global prevalence of PU is 14.8% [11], while it is 11% in Africa [12]. In Ethiopia however, the prevalence ranges from 3.4% [13] to 25.3% [14]. The burden of PU was significant in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, due to inadequate healthcare infrastructure and a shortage of medical personnel [15].

Although it is preventable, pressure ulcers are common adverse events and represent a serious health concern, not only their impact on patient morbidity and mortality [16], but also because of the high costs to treat a severe PU [17] especially, in country with limited accessibility to healthcare institutions that provide curative services [18]. It has significant physical, social, and psychological impacts that can greatly affect quality of life and often in results poor outcome [6].

Although there is a growing number of individual reports on the prevalence of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia, the data have not been systematically synthesized to date, with only primary studies available. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the prevalence of pressure ulcers and their associated predictors in Ethiopia. This study is critical for identifying risk factors and informing the development of effective prevention strategies. Additionally, it provides valuable insights for policymakers and future researchers to understand trends and guide evidence-based interventions.

Method

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) statement guideline for systematic reviews and meta-analysis and PRISMA-A for the abstract [19] (Supplementary 1). The review protocol identified by CRD420251030116 has been registered in an international prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Population (P)

Admitted patients.

Exposure (E)

Factors associated with pressure ulcer and at least two times reported as significant factors (demographic, clinical, behavioral, or treatment-related factors).

Comparator (C)

Patients without the specified exposures.

Outcome (O)

Pressure ulcer.

Language

Only studies reported in the English language were considered.

Study design

Observational studies.

Setting

Studies conducted in Ethiopia.

Exclusion criteria

Studies conducted outside Ethiopia, publications in languages other than English, qualitative studies, review articles and case reports were excluded. In addition, articles that could not be accessed despite attempts to contact the corresponding author were also excluded.

Study screening and selection

All citations identified through the search strategy were exported to EndNote X9, and duplicate records were removed. The titles and abstracts of the retrieved articles were independently screened by two authors (BT and FN) for eligibility. Based on the titles and abstracts, studies were classified as included, excluded, and undecided. We then obtained the full texts of all the included and the undecided studies to confirm inclusion or exclusion. Any discrepancies between the two authors were resolved through discussion, with a third author (ZA) consulted when necessary. Screening and selection were conducted manually.

Outcome variables

The main outcome of this review was prevalence of pressure ulcer identified in the original paper.

The secondary outcome was predictors of pressure ulcers, defined as any demographic, clinical, behavioral, or treatment-related factors that demonstrated a statistically significant association with the development of pressure ulcers in the included studies (Being bed ridden, having DM, poor position changing, longer length of stay in hospital (> 21 days), presence of sensory problem and presence of friction). To enhance the validity of the study, only factors reported in at least two primary studies were included in the pooled analysis.

Databases and search strategy

Electronic databases, MEDLINE, EMBASE, CINAHL, Scopus, Google Scholar, and PubMed were searched using population, exposure, context, and outcome (PECO) terms. The search was held from March 20 to April 6, 2025. The search was done by using the keywords “prevalence or magnitude or incidence,” and “bed sore or pressure ulcer or pressure injury or decubitus ulcer,” and or predictors or associated factors or risk factor or determinants “and “Ethiopia"(supplementary 2).

Quality assessment

For this review, Endnote version X9 (Thomson Reuters, Philadelphia, USA) software was used to remove duplicate studies. Two investigators (BM & YA) assessed the quality of eligible articles using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical appraisal tools. Eleven [13, 14, 20–28] cross sectional studies and one [29] cohort study were assessed using the JBI checklist for cross-sectional and cohort study designs respectively. This quality appraisal tool has options of yes, no, unclear, and not applicable; 1 is for yes and 0 for others. Of the eleven cross-sectional studies, nine of them scored seven of the eight questions (87.5%, low risk) and two studies scored six of the eight (75%, low risk). Similarly, a cohort studies, scored nine of 11 questions (81.8%, low risk). As a result, the included studies in this meta-analysis had no considerable risk of bias as they scored greater than or equal to 60% of the assessment criteria (Table 1) and (Supplementary 3).

Data collection and analysis

First, significant data (prevalence PU, Study area, Sample size, study design, mean age, author, publication year, tool and associated predictors with CI and AOR) was extracted by two authors (BT and FN) from each original study and entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Other author (BM) take the responsibility to check for the presence of any disagreement. Any discrepancies between the two authors were resolved through discussion. Only predictor variables that were identified as statistically significant in at least two studies were considered in this review (supplementary 4). Then STATA 17.0 was used for statistical analysis. Publication bias was checked by the Egger’s weighted regression test and funnel plot for pooled prevalence of pressure ulcer. Then, trim and fill analysis was employed to manage publication bias. Furthermore, sensitivity was conducted using the leave-one-out method to evaluate the influence of a particular study. As the test statistic showed significant heterogeneity among included studies (I2 = 92.09%, p < 0.001), the random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled prevalence at 95% CI. For the predictors of pressure ulcers, the summary measure was the pooled adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% CI.

Heterogeneity handling

The heterogeneity across the recorded studies was evaluated by using the I2 test and its p-values, with I2 values of below 25, 25–75, and above 75% demonstrating low, medium, and high heterogeneity, respectively. As the test statistic showed significant heterogeneity among studies, the random-effects model was used to determine the pooled estimate at 95% CI and presented with a forest plot.

Results

Search results

The database search of literature yielded a total of 302 primary articles, of which 133 were excluded due to duplication. Then, 131 articles were excluded after abstract screening due to ineligible objectives and populations. Of the remaining 38 articles, eight were inaccessible for full text review. As a result, only 30 full-text articles were reviewed, but 18 were excluded for being conducted outside the study area (i.e., outside Ethiopia). Finally, 12 studies comprising a total of 3,319 participants were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram showing screening and selection process of articles for systematic review and meta-analysis, 2025

Characteristics of included study

Twelve articles [13, 14, 20–29] were included in this study to explore the prevalence of pressure ulcers and their predictors in Ethiopia. Eleven of the included articles were institution-based cross sectional studies, while one employed a retrospective cohort study design [29]. Regarding the assessment tool, seven articles used the Braden Scale for pressure ulcer risk assessment. All studies included in this review were published between 2014 and 2024 (Table 1).

Pooled prevalence of pressure ulcer in Ethiopia

A wide variation in prevalence of pressure ulcer were observed across the included studies in this systematic review and meta-analysis. The lowest prevalence (3.0%) was reported in a study conducted in Addis Ababa [21], while the highest prevalence (25.3%) was reported in a study from Debre Berhan [14]. In this study, the pooled prevalence of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia was estimated at 10.93% (95% CI: 7.57%–14.29%), with significant heterogeneity observed (I2 = 92.09%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Pooled prevalence of pressure ulcer in Ethiopia 2025

Subgroup analysis

Due to higher heterogeneity, subgroup analysis was done based on the mean sample size categorized less than the mean sample (< 272) and greater than mean sample (> 272) to find its source. Studies with a sample size less than the mean (< 272) showed high heterogeneity (I2 = 89.96%, p < 0.001), while those with a sample size greater than the mean (> 272) exhibited lower heterogeneity (I2 = 72.22%, p < 0.001).

According to the subgroup analysis, the pooled prevalence of pressure ulcers in studies with a sample size greater than the mean was 37.03%, which is higher than the overall pooled prevalence (Fig. 3).

Forest plot demonstrating subgroup analysis based on mean sample size

Publication bias

In this meta-analysis, a funnel plot and egger’s regression test were employed to assess the presence of publication bias. The results indicated the presence of publication bias, as the funnel plot showed asymmetry and egger’s regression test was significant (p < 0.05). For this reason, a trim and fill analysis was employed. The trim-and-fill analysis identified three potentially missing studies. After adjusting for these, the pooled prevalence decreased to 7.12%, suggesting possible publication bias (Fig. 4).

Publication bias of the included studies after and before trim and fill analysis

Sensitivity analysis

To investigate whether any small study effect influenced the overall effect size, we performed a Leave one out sensitivity analysis. The result showed no single study significantly affected the pooled estimated. Therefore, the findings of this meta-analysis appear to be fairly consistent (Fig. 5).

Sensitivity test for prevalence of pressure ulcer in Ethiopia, 2025

Predictors of pressure ulcer in Ethiopia

This review identified several factors significantly associated with the occurrence of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia. These included being bedridden, having diabetes mellitus, infrequent position changes, a hospital stay longer than 21 days, the presence of sensory impairment, and the presence of friction (Fig. 6).

Forest plot illustrating factors associated with pressure ulcer in Ethiopia, 2025

Three studies [13, 25, 27] were included to examine the association between being bedridden and the development of pressure ulcers. Based on the random-effects model, bedridden patients had a 4.75 times higher risk of developing pressure ulcers compared to non-bedridden patients (95% CI: 2.53–6.97; I2 = 91.82%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Two studies [22, 27] evaluated the association between having diabetes mellitus and the risk of pressure ulcers. The pooled estimate showed that patients with diabetes had a 3.28 times higher risk of developing pressure ulcers compared to non-diabetic patients (95% CI: 1.61–4.94; I2 = 90.56%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Five studies [22, 23, 25–27] examined the effect of friction on the development of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia. According to the random-effects model, patients exposed to friction were 6.75 times more likely to develop pressure ulcers compared to those without friction (95% CI: 2.15–11.36; I2 = 98.66%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Length of hospital stay was another predictor of pressure ulcer. Four studies [23–25, 27] examined its effect. Patients who stayed longer than 21 days had a 4.9 times higher risk of developing pressure ulcers compared to those who stayed 21 days or less (95% CI: 3.46–6.33; I2 = 84.6%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Lack of frequent position changes among hospitalized patients was one of the determinants of pressure ulcer occurrence in this review. This factor was evaluated by three studies [22, 26, 27], and the pooled estimate indicated that patients who did not change position frequently had a 5.96 times higher risk of developing pressure ulcers compared to those who did (95% CI: 2.10–9.81; I2 = 97.83%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Presence of sensory disorder also influenced the development pressure ulcer which was evaluated by two studies [23, 27]. The pooled estimate showed that patients with sensory disorders had a 2.93 times higher likelihood of developing pressure ulcers compared to those without sensory disorders (95% CI: 2.36–3.51; I2 = 15.14%; P < 0.001) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Pressure ulcers increase the workload of nurses by up to 50% [30]. It also causes physical debilitation, pain, and prolongs the length of hospital stay [31]. This systematic review and meta-analysis will give the pooled estimates of pressure ulcers and their predictors in Ethiopia, which gives invaluable information to policymakers, health planners, and the clinician.

The finding of this study revealed that the pooled prevalence of pressure ulcers was 10.93% (95% CI 7.57%−14.29%), which was inconsistent with the review report done in Africa (11%) [12], Turkey (12.7%) [32], and Indonesia (8.0%) [33]. However, it was lower than the global reports (14.8%) [11], the Norwegian (18.2%) [34] and Iranian study (19.59%) [35]. It was higher than the report of China (4.8%) [36] and Italy [37]. This variation may reflect differences in healthcare quality, patient monitoring systems, reporting practices, and resource availability. Pressure ulcer decreased as the economy developed and countries’ healthcare system performances improved [38]. These results underscore the need for targeted interventions, healthcare worker training, and policy development, along with the implementation of a national patient safety program [39], to reduce the burden of pressure ulcers and improve patient outcomes in Ethiopia.

This review indicated individuals with bedridden patients have 4.75 times higher risk to develop pressure ulcers compared to non-bedridden patients. This is consistent with a similar study conducted in the USA, which found that patients unable to self-ambulate were at higher risk of developing pressure ulcers [40]. Persons with disabilities may exhibit a wide range of functional limitations, further increasing their risk of pressure ulcer development [41]. This finding highlights the critical need for targeted preventive strategies for bedridden patients, including frequent repositioning, use of pressure-relieving devices, and close skin monitoring [42].

Those patients who stayed longer than 21 days were 4.9 times at a higher risk of developing a pressure ulcer than those who stayed shorter than 21 days. The result was in agreement with other similar studies in Qatar [43], Pakistan [44] and Finland [45]. Patients who remain hospitalized for longer durations are typically more severely ill or have multiple comorbidities, which can impair circulation, nutrition, and skin integrity factors that contribute to pressure ulcer formation [46]. The finding suggests that patients with extended hospitalization require closer monitoring and early preventive interventions.

In this review, diabetic patients were 3.28 times higher risk of developing pressure ulcers than non-diabetics. This was inconsistent with similar studies in New York [47], Iran [48] and Norway [49]. Pre-existing diabetes increases the risk of pressure ulcers due to impaired sensation from peripheral neuropathy, reduced blood flow from peripheral arterial disease, and poor wound healing caused by high blood sugar levels, which damage nerves and blood vessels [50]. furthermore, the finding emphasize the need for comprehensive prevention strategies and caregiver education to reduce the burden of pressure ulcers, particularly among diabetic patients [51].

Patients with friction were 6.75 times more likely to develop pressure ulcers than those without friction. This was inconsistent with findings from Pakistan [44] and Kenya [52]. A combination of shear and friction while lying at an incline may affect underlying capillary beds and contribute to local tissue hypoxia [6]. The friction phenomenon is associated with shear deformations and stresses on the skin surface, within skin layers and subcutaneously [53].

Patients with a sensory disorder had a 2.93 times higher chance of developing a pressure ulcer as compared to those without a sensory disorder. The result was in agreement with similar studies conducted in Turkey [54] and Pakistan [55]. Sensory nerve disorders reduce pain perception, making patients unaware of prolonged pressure on the skin, which increases the risk of pressure ulcer formation. Clinicians should prioritize early risk assessment and consistent preventive measures like skin checks, and caregiver education to reduce pressure ulcer incidence [2], especially among patients with sensory deficits.

Changing the client's position during hospitalization plays a crucial role in reducing the risk of pressure ulcers [56]. This review also revealed that lack of frequent changing of position was 5.96 times an increased risk of pressure ulcer compared to those with frequent change of position, which was in line with a similar review report [57]. This highlights the critical importance of routine position changes in preventing pressure ulcers, as frequent repositioning reduces pressure incidence [58–61].

Strengths and limitations of the study

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first review to assess both prevalence and predictors of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia. Articles included in this review were good quality.

This systematic review and meta-analysis has limitations, as it is based solely on articles written in English. Other limitation of this meta-analysis is the potential variability in the confounders used in the multivariable adjustment models across the included primary studies. The study had significant heterogeneity. This meta-analysis was subjected to publication bias, which could have led to an overestimation of the true prevalence.

Conclusion and recommendation

The current review revealed a substantial burden of pressure ulcers in Ethiopia. Moreover, this review revealed that being bedridden, having DM, poor position changing, longer length of stay in hospital (> 21 days), presence of a sensory problem and presence of friction were statistically significant factors for pressure ulcers.

Clinicians should remain vigilant in identifying patients at high risk for pressure ulcers and implement routine repositioning and thorough skin assessments for prevention and early detection.

Policymakers should prioritize the development and implementation of a pressure ulcer prevention and early detection strategy. It is recommended that resources should be allocated for training regarding prevention of PU.

For future researchers, it is suggested to address non-English studies. It is recommended to see changes over time using a longitudinal study.
