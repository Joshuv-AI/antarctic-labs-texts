Primary adrenocortical insufficiency in patients with AIDS in Wuhan, China: 3 cases report and literature review
Authors: Hongfu Zhai, Shi Zou, Yuting Tan, Mengmeng Wu, Shihui Song, Mingqi Luo, Xiaoyan Wu, Ke Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary adrenal insufficiency (PAI) is a common endocrine complication in AIDS patients, but the diagnosis is often delayed due to the atypical clinic symptoms, which may result in a life-threatening adrenal crisis.ResultIn this report we present the clinical data of three patients with AIDS and PAI, summarizing the etiology, clinical characteristics, treatment and prognosis. All enrolled three patients were male, aged between 32 and 54 years. Their CD4+ T lymphocyte count (CD4 count) were 58 cells/µL, 378 cells/µL and 35 cells/µL respectively. Two patients had cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection and one had tuberculosis (TB), which were considered to be the main etiologies for PAI. The reported cases exhibited nonspecific clinical symptoms, including fatigue, weight loss, vomiting, diarrhea, anorexia, and fever. All three patients had skin and mucous membrane hyperpigmentation, and two experienced adrenal crisis characterized by intractable hypotension and hyponatremia. Laboratory results revealed low cortisol and higher adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) in serum. All patients responded well to glucocorticoid replacement therapy. By follow-up, their CD4 counts increased steadily.ConclusionsIn patients with AIDS, especially those with opportunistic infection such as CMV infection or TB, PAI should be considered when symptoms of glucocorticoid deficiency are present. Conversely, in AIDS patients diagnosed with PAI, potential underlying infections such as CMV or TB should be actively investigated.

Introduction

Primary adrenocortical insufficiency (PAI) is an important endocrine complication in patients with acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). It is characterized by glucocorticoid deficiency with or without associated mineralocorticoid dysfunction. Although PAI is not rare in patients with AIDS, its diagnosis remains challenging due to the non-specific symptoms [1, 2]. Therefore, it is crucial to improve the diagnosis and treatment for PAI among patients with AIDS. However, so far the limited cases were reported [3, 4]. To address this gap, we report the clinical characteristics of three patients with AIDS and PAI, aiming to highlight the clinical presentation and management of PAI in AIDS patients, and to help prevent life-threatening adrenal crisis.

Case data

Case1

A 32-year-old man with complaints of intermittent fever for 10 days was admitted. He was diagnosed with AIDS one month ago and did not receive antiretroviral therapy (ART). Physical examination only showed thrush on the tongue. Laboratory tests revealed a CD4+ T lymphocyte count (CD4 count) of 58 cells/µL (normal range 345–2350 cells/µL). The chest CT scan showed high-density patchy opacities. He was treated with antibiotics for pulmonary infection with no improvement in symptoms. Then the patient was started on diagnostic anti-tuberculosis (anti-TB) treatment, but his fever did not improve. Then the patient appeared fatigue, nausea and vomiting symptoms after anti-TB treatment, which were considered to be drug-related side effects. Further blood tests for both IgM and IgG of cytomegalovirus (CMV) were positive. Anti-TB treatment was stopped and ganciclovir was provided. After treatment with ganciclovir, the patient’s temperature turned to normal, and ART was subsequently initiated. However, the patient’s nausea and vomiting were worsened. The intractable hypotension and hyponatremia were observed, with the lowest blood sodium level recorded at 119.2 mmol/L (normal range135-145mmol/L). Physical examination revealed generalised hyperpigmentation involving the face, oral mucosa and the palmar crease (Fig. 1A and B), with persistent hypotension (supine blood pressure 85/60 mmHg). The CT scan of the adrenal glands showed atrophy of the right adrenal and enlargement of the left adrenal (Fig. 1C). Significantly decreased serum cortisol levels were detected. The results showed serum cortisol levels were 2.49 ug/dl at 8:00, 2.46 ug/dl at 16:00, and 3.14 ug/dl at 00:00. The serum aldosterone and angiotensin levels were normal (Table 1). The patient was finally diagnosed as PAI caused by CMV infection and oral prednisone (5 mg/day) was provided. After one month of treatment, the patient’s nausea and vomiting were significantly alleviated, skin hyperpigmentation subsided, both blood pressure and serum sodium levels gradually returned to normal levels. At follow-up six months later, the CD4 count had increased to 666 cells/µL.

Case2

A 54-year-old man was admitted with a 3-year history of skin hyperpigmentation, which had worsened over the past 3 months. He was diagnosed with AIDS 6 years ago, and had been receiving ART for 4 years. In addition, he was diagnosed with adrenal TB 1 year ago and did not receive anti-TB treatment. Physical examination revealed hyperpigmentation on the whole body, especially on the face, tongue and oral mucosa. Laboratory tests revealed a CD4 count of 378 cells/µL, and the interferon-γ release assay was positive. The level of serum cortisol decreased, the serum cortisol levels were 2.2ug/dl at 8:00, 1.7 ug/dl at 16:00, and 1.26 ug/dl at 00:00, while the adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels were increased, at 568.8pg/ml at 8:00, 312.4pg/ml at 16:00, 191.9pg/ml at 00:00 (Table 1). The serum aldosterone and angiotensinogen levels were within the normal range. CT scan of the adrenal revealed bilateral adrenal enlargement with multiple nodules (Fig. 1D). Finally, the patient was diagnosed with PAI caused by adrenal TB. Anti-tuberculosis treatment was initiated in combination with oral prednisone at a dose of 20 mg/day as replacement therapy. After one month of treatment, the patient’s symptoms significantly improved, and the dosage of prednisone was gradually tapered to 5 mg/d for maintenance.

Case3

A 35-year-old man was admitted with a 2-week history of nausea, vomiting, weakness, and anorexia. He was diagnosed with AIDS one month ago, and did not receive ART yet. Physical examination found apathy, cold limbs, with persistent hypotension (supine blood pressure 84/53 mmHg). Hyperpigmentation could be seen all over the body, especially on the face, the end of the hands, and the extended side of the forearms. Laboratory tests revealed CD4 count of 35 cells/µL, hyponatremia (serum sodium 115mmol/L; normal range 135-145mmol/L). Tests also showed significantly decreased serum cortisol levels:: 2.17ug/dl at 8:00, 2.42 ug/dl at 16:00, and 2.58 ug/dl at 00:00, while the ACTH levels were elevated, at 188pg/ml at 8:00, 142.01pg/ml at 16:00, 210.76pg/ml at 00:00 (Table 1). Serum aldosterone and angiotensinogen levels were within normal ranges. In addition, both CMV-IgM and CMV-IgG antibodies tested positive. CT scan of the adrenal showed thickening of the left adrenal and calcification of the right adrenal. The patient was finally diagnosed with PAI complicated by adrenal crisis. Treatment included intravenous stress dose steroids (100 mg every 8 h), fluid resuscitation, and electrolyte correction. After 4 days, his symptoms improved, and serum sodium levels normalized. Therapy was then transitioned to oral prednisone (7.5 mg/day). At six-month follow-up, the symptoms of the patient were relieved, and the CD4 count increased to 336 cells/µL.

In our study, all enrolled patients were male. Two patients had CD4 count < 100 cells/µL, only one of them received ART. All patients had one or more opportunistic infections (OIs) at presentation, including CMV infection in two cases and TB in one case, which were considered as the main etiologies of PAI among AIDS patient.

All three cases had non-specific symptoms such as weakness, lethargy, weight loss, etc. In addition, two cases exhibited manifestations of adrenal crisis, such as gastrointestinal symptoms, hypotension and hyponatremia. Notably, all three patients showed hyperpigmentation on the face and extremities. Two of them had hyperpigmentation involving the tongue, oral mucosa and nail. Severe electrolyte disturbances were observed in two cases. All three cases showed decreased plasma cortisol level at 8:00 am and two exhibited significantly increased in ACTH levels.

Imaging changes of the adrenal glands were observed in all cases, including adrenal atrophy, calcification and nodules. Following glucocorticoid replacement therapy, the symptoms of all patients improved markedly, and the levels of cortisol increased gradually. In the follow-up, the CD4 count of all patients were increased steadily.

Discussion

Previous studies have suggested that adrenal insufficiency of varying degrees is present in approximately 60% of patients with AIDS at autopsy [5]. It is characterized by glucocorticoid deficiency with or without mineralocorticoid abnormalities [6]. In the pre-AIDS era, up to 17% of patients exhibited an inadequate response to plasma cortisol, whereas this percentage increases to as high as 54% in those with AIDS [7]. Some clinical studies have demonstrated that the dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is directly proportional to the level of immune deficiency in HIV-infected patients [8]. However, the clinical diagnosis of PAI inpatient with AIDS is challenging due to its low incidence, lack of specific symptoms, usually overlap with the manifestations of AIDS itself. Some studies suggested the symptoms only become apparent when adrenal function is impaired by more than 80% [9, 10]. Therefore, summarizing and reporting the clinical characteristics of AIDS patients with PAI is helpful for improving the diagnosis and treatment of PAI.

The clinical manifestations of PAI among AIDS patients are nonspecific. In the early stage of the disease, patients usually present with non-specific symptoms such as fever, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, weight loss and muscle and joint pain [1, 11]. All three cases in our study presented with weakness and wasting, two had fever and gastrointestinal symptoms. The symptoms are often atypical and may be mistaken for medication side effects, leading to potential oversight. It may even progress to an adrenal crisis characterized by dehydration, refractory hypotension and uncorrectable hyponatremia. Some studies have showed that AIDS patients are more prone to adrenal crisis than the general population due to reduced cortisol reserves [12, 13].Our case 1 was admitted for recurrent fever, and given empirical anti-TB treatment. After treatment, the patient developed nausea and vomiting, which were initially misinterpreted as adverse drug reactions. PAI was not diagnosed until manifestations of adrenal crisis such as severe hypotension and electrolyte disturbance appeared. Therefore, for AIDS patients with unexplained fatigue, nausea, vomiting, and low blood pressure, PAI should be considered. For all patients enrolled in our study, physical examinations revealed hyperpigmentation, which could be a clue for diagnosis of PAI among AIDS patients. Skin hyperpigmentation is a specific sign indicative of PAI, consistent with previous reports [12, 14].

The etiologies for PAI vary across different regions. In developed countries, autoimmune destruction of the adrenal glands is the most common cause [15]. While in China, infections such as mycobacterium tuberculosis(MTB), viruses(including herpes simplex virus, cytomegalovirus) and fungi (such as Pneumocystis jirovecii) are more common [7]. For AIDS patients, OIs is the most common reason for PAI, especially in CMV and MTB infection. Of these, CMV is the most important pathogen [16]. Studies have showed that the prevalence of CMV infection among AIDS patients without ART is as high as 93% [11]. The risk of CMV infection in the adrenal gland increases with the severity of immunodeficiency and typically occurs in AIDS patients with the CD4 count less than 50 cells/µL. Besides, adrenal gland is a common site of extrapulmonary TB. PAI caused by adrenal TB is due to destruction of the adrenal glands by caseous necrosis [17], which is also common in China for the high burden of TB. It also often occurs during the AIDS phase, especially when the CD4 count is below 100 cells/µL [18]. Among our 3 cases, 2 had CMV infection and 1 had TB, consistent with previous studies. Therefore, in AIDS patients with PAI, OIs such as infection of CMV and TB should be actively considered and treated alongside managing the adrenal insufficiency symptoms.

As mentioned above, the diagnosis of primary adrenocortical insufficiency (PAI) in patients with AIDS is a critical clinical challenge. Due to low immunity, chronic inflammation and stress for a long time, reserve function may be insufficient even if basal cortisol is normal. Therefore, Serum cortisol can be used more in initial screening. The morning serum cortisol level below 3 µg/dl should raise suspicion of AI. Besides, ACTH stimulation experiments are needed to assess adrenal cortical reserve function. Plasma cortisol was measured again 30 and 60 min after intravenous or intramuscular injection of 250 µg of adrenocorticotropic hormone analogue (cosyntropin or tetracosactrin), and the peak cortisol after 30 min was still less than 18 µg/l suggestive of abnormality, indicating inadequate adrenal reserve [10]. It is well-established that OIs is the most prevalent cause of PAI in AIDS patients. Thus, multiple laboratory approaches are required to confirm the diagnosis and identify the underlying etiology. In the case of OIs opportunistic infections such as CMV infection or TB, IHC (immunohistochemistry) can substantially enhance the sensitivity and specificity of pathogen detection. For instance, IHC using monoclonal antibodies against CMV immediate-early antigens can detect infected cells that may be missed on routine hematoxylin and eosin [19]. While ISH (In situ hybridization) technology can also be used to detect viral genomes, such as CMV DNA, providing direct evidence of viral invasion of the adrenal gland [20]. The infiltration of pathogens triggers a local inflammatory response that, combined with direct viral cytotoxicity, leads to the downregulation of a suite of essential enzymes, including CYP11B1, CYP21A2, STAR, and HSD3B2. Study found through PCR and RT-PCR analysis of adrenal tissue from AIDS patients that the expression of CYP21A2, a key enzyme in the final step of glucocorticoid synthesis, was severely reduced in the presence of CMV infection and extensive [21]. STAR, which mediates the initial and rate-limiting steps of steroidogenesis, and HSD3B2, an enzyme crucial for adrenal steroid synthesis, have also been shown to be susceptible to cytokine-mediated inhibition, resulting in a deficiency in adrenal hormone [22]. Detection targeting the etiology and pathogenesis not only strengthens the diagnosis but also clarifies the complex pathophysiology, providing potential targets for future research.

Therapy for PAI is divided into symptomatic and etiological treatment [23, 24]. Firstly, glucocorticoid replacement therapy should be started as soon as possible. If adrenal crisis is suspected, cortisol and aggressive fluid resuscitation is recommended. Secondly, pathogenic treatment should be provided. In recent years, study suggested that the dose of glucocorticoid replacement therapy for AIDS patients with PAI should exceed the physiological level [13]. However, some studies have indicated that higher doses of hormones may affect the effectiveness of ART [23, 24]. Other studies have pointed out that long-term hormone replacement therapy in AIDS patients may lead to hormone-related adverse effects such as osteoporosis, abnormal fat metabolism, and immunosuppression, which may also affect the efficacy of ART [25]. In Case 3, the patient was given a high dose of hydrocortisone (300 mg/d) due to adrenal crisis, then the physiological dosage of corticosteroid was provided. The other two cases received physiological doses of corticosteroids as maintenance therapy. Their treatment was effective and the CD4 count increased steadily, which suggested that physiological doses of glucocorticoid therapy was effective and the efficacy of ART was not affected.

It’s worth noting that some antiretroviral medicine, for example, protease inhibitor [26], could act as inhibitors of human cytochrome P450 3A4. This inhibition restricts the metabolism of fluticasone and triamcinolone in the liver, prolongs the duration of supraphysiological doses of glucocorticoids, exacerbates the inhibitory effect on the HPA axis, and ultimately contributes to secondary adrenal insufficiency.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PAI is often overlooked or misdiagnosed due to its nonspecific clinical symptoms among patients with AIDS. In AIDS patient with symptoms of glucocorticoid deficiency, PAI should be considered. Infectious etiologies, especially for CMV and MTB should be firstly considered. In AIDS patient with CMV infection and TB, PAI should be monitored as a potential complication.
