Comparison of health care utilisation and spending among National Health Insurance and Medical Aid beneficiaries in Korea
Authors: Agnus M Kim, Doojin Ryu, Jin Yong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the effect of out-of-pocket expenses on health service use is important for the financial sustainability of health care systems. This study compared health service use and spending between National Health Insurance (NHI) and Medical Aid (MA) beneficiaries in Korea.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective ecological study with a repeated cross-sectional design using nationwide health insurance claims data covering the NHI and MA beneficiaries in Korea from 2011 to 2023. We examined the demographic composition of the two groups and their health care utilisation by age. We compared per capita spending between the NHI and MA beneficiaries for common diagnoses. Lastly, we assessed changes in per capita health care spending and utilisation between the two groups over the study period by estimating average annual percent change.ResultsIn 2023, inpatient spending among MA beneficiaries (USD 3,011) was more than five times as high as that of NHI beneficiaries (USD 576), and the average length of stay among MA beneficiaries was 87.9 days, about 4.8 times that of NHI beneficiaries (18.4 days). The gap was most pronounced among male MA beneficiaries aged 30–49, with inpatient spending 13 to 14 times that of the NHI group and length of stay showing a similarly large difference. Outpatient and pharmacy spending and utilisation were also more than twice as high among MA beneficiaries. Elevated spending among MA beneficiaries was especially evident for musculoskeletal conditions and chronic mental health conditions. While both groups showed significant increases in spending and utilisation from 2011 to 2023, MA beneficiaries consistently had higher per capita spending (MA to NHI ratio, 3.3–3.6), with a fivefold gap in inpatient care.ConclusionsThe substantial gap in health care utilisation between NHI and MA beneficiaries highlights the need to investigate potential overuse. Developing strategies to limit unnecessary health service use while maintaining access is a crucial issue for ensuring a sustainable health care system.Keywords: Copayment, Korea, Medical Aid, Moral hazard, National health insurance, Out-of-pocket

Background

Since the adoption of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948, which recognized health as a human right [1], the concept of health as a right has been widely acknowledged and incorporated into legislation in many countries [2]. The concept established access to health services as a right for individuals and a duty for governments. Many nations have implemented measures to guarantee a certain standard of health for their populations and have developed their own systems to provide health services to those in disadvantaged conditions.

However, health services have characteristics that make their provision more complex than that of other basic human needs. First, it is difficult to establish absolute criteria regarding the amount and types of services that should be provided [3, 4]. Differences in values when prioritizing services [5, 6], combined with rapid advances in health care technology, make it challenging to define fixed standards, and these standards continue to evolve over time [7, 8]. Second, the need for health services arises unpredictably [9]. Except for some preventive measures, the provision of health services is largely “ex post.” While crude estimations are possible at the population level based on past utilisation patterns, it is impossible to predict, at the individual level, how much or what type of health care a person will require [10].

Thirdly, and most importantly, health services are prone to overuse [11]. The uncertainty in medicine tends to work in favor of more services for both patients and health care providers for the sake of safety for both sides. Moreover, the absence of explicit criteria for treatment choices gives service providers considerable discretion in decision-making [12]. When this discretion is combined with providers’ profit motives, it can lead to overuse of health services [13, 14]. Most of all, the propensity for overuse is related to the fact that the cost of health services is often not directly borne by the user. Due to the risk of high-cost events, health services are predominantly financed through insurance systems, and such third-party payment reduces the insured individuals’ incentive to economize on service use [15, 16].

The involvement of third-party payers is particularly significant regarding the overuse of health services [17], given the increasing support for health service provision by governments. In many countries, governments are directly or indirectly subsidizing services to reduce the financial burden on individuals in pursuit of universal health coverage [18]. Reduced out-of-pocket payments lower barriers to access, consequently increasing the risk of overuse [19, 20]. While the risk of overuse is inherent in all systems that alleviate the financial burden, it is particularly relevant to health care support for disadvantaged populations [21]. Whereas most countries, regardless of their income level, employ diverse payment methods to achieve universal health coverage, they commonly apply preferential financial terms to disadvantaged populations, often in the form of reduced or no out-of-pocket costs [22–24]. As important as achieving universal health coverage, ensuring the system’s financial sustainability is equally crucial. Therefore, understanding how utilisation varies according to out-of-pocket costs is essential for developing strategies to maintain the sustainability of the system. In this respect, the preferential conditions for disadvantaged populations provide a good opportunity to examine this change.

The health care system in Korea serves as a suitable setting to examine how the method of health service provision affects population-level utilisation. Korea has a well-established National Health Insurance (NHI) system, covering 97% of the population. The remaining 3% of the population [25], who are socio-economically disadvantaged, are covered by Medical Aid (MA). MA provides full coverage for hospitalization and near-full coverage for outpatient services, with no or only nominal out-of-pocket payment [26]. While making up 3% of the total population, MA beneficiaries accounted for approximately 9.2% of total health care expenditure [27, 28], indicating that an MA beneficiary incurs more than three times the cost of an NHI beneficiary. Given Korea’s universal coverage under the National Health Insurance and the already high frequency of health care use among the general population compared with other countries, the even higher utilisation among MA beneficiaries suggests potential unwarranted use of health care.

From both a policy and theoretical perspective, the use of health services by disadvantaged populations who receive greater government support than the general population is an important subject of study. The difference in health care utilisation between NHI and MA beneficiaries has been examined in a number of studies [29–32]. However, most of the prior studies, though some accounted for demographic characteristics, did not conduct an in-depth comparison of factors related to health care use, such as population structure and disease prevalence. This study was conducted to compare the use of health services between MA and NHI beneficiaries in Korea. We compared inpatient and outpatient care between the two groups by age groups and common diagnoses. Lastly, we assessed changes in per capita health care spending and utilisation between the two groups over the study period.

Methods

National health Insurance and Medical Aid

The health care system in the Republic of Korea is primarily operated by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). The NHI is a universal, compulsory, single-payer social insurance system covering 97% of Korea’s population. It is funded mostly by contributions (85% as of 2023), with less than 15% (11% as of 2023) supported by government subsidies and tobacco surtaxes [33]. NHI beneficiaries pay a monthly contribution, with the method of calculation and payment differing by employment status: for the employee-insured, the contribution rate is fixed (7.09% as of 2025), and the amount is split equally between employer and employee and paid via payroll deduction, while the contribution of the self-employed is calculated at the household level based on income and assets [34]. Except in certain cases, such as no coverage (e.g., outpatient visits to tertiary hospitals) or full coverage (e.g., childbirth and infant inpatient care), beneficiaries are responsible for coinsurance. Coverage rates, typically 80% for inpatient care and 70% for primary outpatient care, vary by the type of institution, service category, and, in some cases, age group [26].

While the NHI covers the general population, MA is a tax-funded social assistance program for low-income individuals, covering about 3% of the population. MA is not a separate, state-run medical service; it operates within the same private-dominant health care delivery system as the NHI. Providers treat MA patients in the same facilities as NHI patients and are reimbursed by the government on a fee-for-service basis. Eligibility is determined by income, assets, family support, and other conditions recognized by the state as warranting government assistance. Beneficiaries are identified through a means test and classified into two categories: Type 1 includes individuals who are incapable of work, in circumstances deemed equivalent to incapacity, or subject to other legally defined conditions requiring state support, while Type 2 comprises all other eligible beneficiaries who do not meet the criteria for Type 1. For Type 1 beneficiaries, who accounted for about 77% of all MA beneficiaries in 2023, inpatient care is fully covered, and outpatient care requires only a small fixed copayment. For Type 2 beneficiaries, coverage is 90% for inpatient care and 85% for outpatient care, with only a nominal copayment for primary outpatient visits [35].

Data

We used nationwide health insurance claims data for the NHI and MA beneficiaries from 2011 to 2023 [27, 28]. The NHID covers the entire Korean population, encompassing both NHI and MA beneficiaries. The data provide information on demographics, socioeconomic status, and health care utilisation, including inpatient, outpatient, and pharmacy care, diagnoses coded by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), and prescription drug records. Exchange rates used to convert Korean won to U.S. dollars (USD) for the period 2011–2023 were obtained from KOSIS, based on data from the Bank of Korea and the International Monetary Fund [36].

Utilisation measures

Health care utilisation was measured using per capita spending and service use, categorized by inpatient and outpatient care. Spending was defined as the total amount, including both NHI/MA-covered payments and out-of-pocket payments. Spending measured in Korean won was converted to U.S. dollars using the average exchange rate of each year, following the standard for multi-year currency conversion suggested by the International Monetary Fund and international finance literature [37, 38]. Per capita spending was calculated using the total number of beneficiaries in each group as the denominator.

Per capita utilisation measures also used the total number of beneficiaries as the denominator, except for inpatient length of stay (LOS). Outpatient utilisation was measured as the annual number of visits per capita, while inpatient utilisation was assessed using LOS, defined as the average number of hospitalization days per admitted patient. Accordingly, the denominator for LOS was the number of hospitalized patients rather than the total beneficiary population used for all other utilisation indicators. These measures encompassed all types of health care facilities, including clinics and hospitals, as well as specialised facilities such as long-term care and psychiatric hospitals. Per capita total utilisation days were calculated as the sum of hospitalization days and outpatient visits divided by the number of beneficiaries. For the temporal analysis of health care utilisation from 2011 to 2023, spending and utilisation for pharmacy services were calculated. Per capita pharmacy utilisation was defined as the total number of claims divided by the number of beneficiaries in each group.

Statistical analysis

We conducted a retrospective ecological study with a repeated cross-sectional design. We primarily compared the demographic composition of NHI and MA beneficiaries in 2023, stratified by age group and sex, using Pearson’s chi-square test. We compared the per capita spending and utilisation measures for inpatient and outpatient care between NHI and MA beneficiaries by age group in 2023. We also compared the per capita expenditure between the two groups for the 20 most common diseases, ranked by prevalence and total spending, respectively.

Lastly, we compared health care utilisation between the two groups, measured by spending and service use over the period 2011–2023, for inpatient, outpatient, and pharmacy care. To assess the temporal trends in annual per capita spending and utilisation measures over the 13-year period, we analysed aggregate data from the National Health Insurance Service. The statistical significance of the trends was evaluated using simple linear regression analysis, with the calendar year as the independent variable. Additionally, to quantify the rate of increase, the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) was calculated based on the compound annual growth rate formula: AAPC = (ValueEnd/ValueStart)(1/n) − 1. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Boramae Medical Center (IRB No. 07–2017-123).

Results

The age and sex distribution of NHI and MA beneficiaries in 2023 is presented in Table 1 and Fig. 1. The age distribution differed significantly between the NHI and MA groups in both males and females. The MA beneficiaries had a significantly higher proportion of older adults than NHI beneficiaries. While MA beneficiaries account for about 2.9% of the total population, their proportion was higher among those aged 60 and above, at 4.6%, 6.3%, and 8.8% in the 60s, 70s, and 80+ age groups, respectively. Age groups under 50 showed a lower proportion of MA beneficiaries, and the 0–9 and 20–39 age groups had a noticeably low proportion, at less than half of the overall 2.9%. Regarding sex ratio, MA beneficiaries had a lower proportion of males compared to NHI beneficiaries, a pattern consistent across most age groups. However, among those aged 50–69, the male-to-female ratio in the MA group was 1.3, compared to 1.0 in the NHI group.

Spending and utilisation of inpatient and outpatient care among National health Insurance and Medical Aid beneficiaries by age group, 2023

In contrast with NHI beneficiaries, whose per capita spending was lower in inpatient care (USD 576) than in outpatient care (USD 729), per capita inpatient spending among MA beneficiaries was USD 3,011, higher than outpatient spending at USD 1,739. Overall, per capita inpatient spending among MA beneficiaries was about 5.2 times that of NHI beneficiaries. In contrast with children and the very elderly (80+), who showed comparatively smaller gaps relative to NHI beneficiaries, per capita inpatient spending among MA beneficiaries was about eight to nine times that of NHI beneficiaries in the 30–59 age groups. The inpatient spending gap was far greater among males, with MA beneficiaries aged 30–49 spending 13 to 14 times that of the NHI group. Per capita outpatient spending among MA beneficiaries (USD 1,739) was 2.4 times that among NHI beneficiaries, a smaller gap compared with inpatient spending. As in inpatient spending, the gap was larger among males and middle-aged adults.

The average annual number of utilisation days among MA beneficiaries was 64, approximately three times that of NHI beneficiaries. As in the case of spending, the gap between MA and NHI beneficiaries in utilisation days was far larger for inpatient LOS, and the difference tended to be greater among males. The average LOS among MA beneficiaries was 87.9 days, about 4.8 times that of NHI beneficiaries. Middle-aged male MA beneficiaries showed the most prominent difference, with values 14.0 and 11.5 times those of NHI beneficiaries in the 30–39 and 40–49 age groups, respectively. The average number of outpatient visits among MA beneficiaries was 35.8, about 1.9 times that of NHI beneficiaries. While MA beneficiaries in the 1–9 age group exhibited higher per capita spending, consistent with other age groups, their average number of outpatient visits (24.5) was 0.8 times that of NHI beneficiaries. Among those aged 80 and above, there was only a small difference between the two groups.

The top 20 diagnoses ranked by prevalence among NHI and MA beneficiaries are presented in Table 3. Colours were assigned to each rank of NHI beneficiaries, with corresponding disease groups in the MA group shown in the same colour. NHI- or MA-only groups are not colour-coded. The prevalence of inpatient conditions differed notably between the two groups. Among NHI beneficiaries, common conditions included senile cataract, emergency visits for U07 (primarily Coronavirus disease 2019 related), pneumonia, unspecified infectious gastroenteritis, and intervertebral disc disorders, each affecting roughly 0.4–0.6% of the population. In contrast, MA beneficiaries tended to show higher prevalence rates for mental health-related chronic conditions and infection-related conditions, with prevalence ranging from 1.0% to 2.5%. The most common outpatient conditions of the two groups differed less than inpatient conditions, with gingivitis and periodontal diseases, along with acute bronchitis, being the two most common conditions in both groups. However, while NHI beneficiaries showed a higher prevalence of infection-related conditions, MA beneficiaries exhibited a higher prevalence of chronic diseases such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, back pain, and osteoarthritis.

Table 4 lists the top 20 diagnoses ranked by share of spending among NHI and MA beneficiaries. Regarding inpatient spending, Alzheimer’s disease with dementia, cerebral infarction, knee osteoarthritis, pneumonia, and hemiplegia ranked among the top 10 diagnoses in both groups. However, compared with the NHI group, the MA group had fewer cancer-related conditions (“encounter for other medical care”). When compared by per capita spending, MA beneficiaries showed higher spending than NHI beneficiaries in most diagnoses in both prevalence rank and total expenditure rank. In terms of outpatient spending, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and gingivitis and periodontal diseases were the leading conditions among NHI beneficiaries, followed by other common chronic conditions. In contrast, MA beneficiaries showed the highest spending on chronic kidney disease (12.7%). Although chronic conditions such as back pain, hypertension, osteoarthritis, and type 2 diabetes mellitus ranked high among both NHI and MA beneficiaries, the MA group carried a larger burden of mental health conditions such as schizophrenia and depression. Regarding per capita spending, MA beneficiaries showed higher spending than NHI beneficiaries in musculoskeletal conditions but tended to have lower per capita spending in several other diagnoses.

Table 5 and Figure 2 compare per capita spending and utilisation of inpatient care, outpatient care, and pharmacy among NHI and MA beneficiaries from 2011 to 2023. Both groups showed a significant increase in per capita inpatient and outpatient spending, with a higher AAPC in the MA group than in the NHI group. MA beneficiaries consistently incurred much higher per capita total spending throughout the study period, with the MA to NHI ratio ranging from 3.3 to 3.6. The gap was most distinct in inpatient care spending, where MA per capita spending was about five times that of the NHI group. The ratios for outpatient and pharmacy spending were smaller than for inpatient care (roughly 2.3–2.5 times for outpatient care and 2.4–2.8 times for pharmacy). The MA–NHI gap was also observed in utilisation measures. The largest gap was in inpatient LOS, with MA beneficiaries recording about five times that of NHI beneficiaries for most of the period. Outpatient visits and pharmacy claims among MA beneficiaries were approximately twice those among NHI beneficiaries.

Discussion

This study compared the health care utilisation between NHI and MA beneficiaries in Korea. MA beneficiaries demonstrated markedly higher per capita spending and service use, particularly for inpatient care, with the gap most pronounced in middle-aged adults and among males. Inpatient spending among MA beneficiaries was more than five times as high as that of NHI beneficiaries, with LOS about five times greater. Outpatient care and pharmacy utilisation were also higher among MA recipients. Elevated spending among MA beneficiaries was especially evident for musculoskeletal conditions and chronic mental health conditions. While both groups showed significant increases in spending and utilisation from 2011 to 2023, MA beneficiaries consistently had higher per capita spending (ratio, 3.3–3.6), with a fivefold gap in inpatient care.

Our findings show that the higher spending and utilisation among MA beneficiaries in Korea, which may be attributable to a higher proportion of the elderly population, persisted even when analysed by age group and were more pronounced among middle-aged adults than among the elderly. The longer LOS among MA beneficiaries is primarily related to hospitalization in long-term care hospitals [39] and may also be influenced by system-level factors, including the availability of long-term inpatient care and fee-for-service reimbursement. The high prevalence of chronic mental health conditions and the long duration of psychiatric hospitalization in Korea may further contribute to this pattern [40]. These findings are indicative of potential overuse. Only the MA beneficiaries in the 1–9 age group showed lower outpatient visits, despite their higher per capita spending compared to their NHI counterparts, consistent with other age groups. While the higher spending among the MA children, despite their lower utilisation, may indicate a need for more intensive treatment due to delayed medical intervention, it could also reflect a parental preference for high-cost tertiary providers or expensive services. This is consistent with patterns observed in other age groups, where the MA-to-NHI ratio for spending is consistently higher than that for utilisation.

Compared to NHI beneficiaries, MA beneficiaries demonstrated a higher prevalence and share of spending for severe chronic and mental health conditions in inpatient settings, alongside elevated per capita spending for common conditions, particularly musculoskeletal disorders. This pattern was distinct in outpatient care, where MA beneficiaries showed higher prevalence and total spending for common chronic conditions. While per capita spending was markedly higher among MA beneficiaries for musculoskeletal conditions, spending for other conditions was similar to or lower than that of NHI beneficiaries, indicating that the higher overall outpatient spending among MA beneficiaries is largely attributable to musculoskeletal utilisation. The findings suggest that the differences in utilisation patterns between the two groups cannot be explained by the disease patterns alone and may reflect behavioral and structural factors.

This study demonstrated a striking gap between NHI and MA beneficiaries in health care utilisation, raising concerns that part of this gap may reflect utilisation beyond what would be expected based on health needs alone. While MA recipients generally have poorer health and a higher burden of chronic and mental health conditions, the magnitude of observed differences, particularly the fivefold gaps in inpatient spending and LOS and their concentration among middle-aged men, appears difficult to explain fully by health needs alone. Higher inpatient spending relative to outpatient spending among MA beneficiaries, in contrast to the pattern in NHI, is consistent with the possibility of social hospitalization or system-driven reliance on inpatient care. The larger gap in middle-aged adults may also reflect factors such as labor market exclusion, mental health burden, or limited access to community-based care, in addition to differences in care-seeking behavior. The substantially higher per capita spending on musculoskeletal conditions among MA beneficiaries points to the possibility of excess utilisation.

The persistent gap in health care spending and utilisation between MA and NHI beneficiaries over the past decade suggests the lack of measures to regulate utilisation among MA beneficiaries, particularly given Korea’s already high baseline level of health care use. Although previous studies reported that the Community-Based Case Management program, initiated in 2003 to promote rational use of medical services among MA beneficiaries, reduced excessive health care use [41, 42], our findings indicate that its long-term impact has been limited. Given the rising overall medical expenditure [43], the disproportionate increase in spending among MA beneficiaries necessitates more rigorous policy oversight.

Health care utilisation among the general population in Korea is disproportionately high compared to other countries. The country records the longest average length of hospital stay and the highest number of physician consultations among OECD countries, 2.4 and 2.2 times the OECD averages, respectively [44]. Given this excessive use of health care prevalent in the general population, the far higher utilisation among the MA beneficiaries strongly indicates that it is unlikely to be explained solely by medical needs. This aligns with previous studies showing the limited role of medical needs in explaining MA overuse [31, 45, 46]. This is also supported by a previous study, which found significantly lower health care utilisation among the poor not enrolled in MA compared to those enrolled [47].

Developing measures to address potential unnecessary use of services, while maintaining essential support, is critical for system sustainability. Potential interventions for the MA program include strengthened cost-sharing mechanisms, such as higher copayments, coinsurance, and utilisation caps, as well as improved monitoring or incentives to promote appropriate care use. Providing preventive care and supporting health-promoting behaviors may reduce health service use by improving the baseline health status of beneficiaries. The integration of MA into NHI can also be considered. While the premiums are subsidized, mandatory coinsurance in the NHI could serve to discourage overutilisation. Moreover, given that the current difficulty in overseeing MA utilisation partly stems from administrative fragmentation across various governmental levels and organisations, integrating these functions into a single entity could offer a fundamental solution.

The relationship between out-of-pocket expenses and higher utilisation demonstrated in this study is not confined to the impoverished population who pay nominal amounts. It can be applied to all individuals covered by health insurance, as long as the perceived financial burden of the out-of-pocket expense is lower than the anticipated benefit from health service use. Although this phenomenon, often discussed in the context of moral hazard, can occur in any context where health insurance is involved [48, 49], it is particularly relevant in national health insurance systems. The large risk pool and predominantly public orientation of such systems can diminish price sensitivity for both the insured and the insurer. While national health insurance is regarded as the standard model for achieving universal health coverage, identifying effective measures to address the risk of moral hazard would be a critical step toward ensuring the long-term sustainability of health care systems.

There are some limitations to this study. First, there may be differences in health status between NHI and MA beneficiaries. Those in disadvantaged conditions are more likely to be in worse health, which can influence health service utilisation [50, 51], but these factors were not fully accounted for in our analysis. Additionally, as revealed in our study, a larger proportion of MA beneficiaries have mental health conditions [52]. The prevalence of psychiatric disorders among MA beneficiaries is estimated at 34%, approximately four times that of NHI beneficiaries [27, 28, 53]. Inpatient days and inpatient spending for psychiatric conditions account for 52 and 27%, respectively, of total inpatient days and spending among MA beneficiaries [27, 28]. However, a previous study that compared the two groups after adjusting for health conditions and excluding patients with mental health disorders found that differences in health care spending and utilisation still existed and were even more pronounced in the lower morbidity group [46]. In addition, previous studies showing that demographic and health characteristics play only a negligible role in explaining overuse among MA beneficiaries [31, 45] also support the view that the overuse is related to structural factors. Second, this study was a population-level analysis, and its ecological design precludes making definitive causal inferences about individual moral hazard or the exact proportion of utilisation that is truly “unnecessary.” An individually matched comparison would be more appropriate to quantitatively assess the effect of out-of-pocket expenses on health service use.

Conclusions

Health is now widely recognized as a right to be protected and supported by the state. Government support for health service utilisation is increasingly considered necessary. However, the inherent uncertainty and unpredictability of health care, combined with insurance coverage, make publicly supported services susceptible to higher levels of utilisation. The higher utilisation of health services among MA beneficiaries compared with NHI beneficiaries in Korea suggests a potential association between lower out-of-pocket expenses and increased use. In line with the view that moral hazard is a possible mechanism in insured health systems, our findings suggest the importance of exploring policy approaches to address potentially unwarranted utilisation. Determining how to address potential overuse while ensuring access to health care remains a critical issue for achieving a sustainable health care system.
