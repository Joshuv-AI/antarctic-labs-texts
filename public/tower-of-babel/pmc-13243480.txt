Metabolic adaptation drives self-organization during skin organoid morphogenesis
Authors: Jingwei Jiang, Weiwei Liu, Mengyue Wang, Dehuan Wang, Wang Wu, Man Zhang, Siyi Zhou, Yang Xiao, Xia Lin, Xiao Xiang, Weiyi An, Hengguang Zhao, Rixing Zhan, Li Yang, Kaiyong Cai, Gaoxing Luo, Cheng-Ming Chuong, Mingxing Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-organization in organoid morphogenesis involves the coordinated arrangement of interacting cells into higher-order structures, yet the underlying principles remain elusive. Here, we investigate how epidermal and dermal cells respond distinctively to elevated levels of hypoxia during skin organoid morphogenesis that largely resembles the skin development during embryogenesis. We unveil that autonomously generated hypoxic environment-induced metabolic adaptation drives the transition from coalesced spheroids to a planarized structure in skin organoids through the following three levels. Hif1a-mediated anaerobic metabolism positions epidermal cells in the liquid phase of the cultures under lower oxygen levels, facilitating tissue phase separation of the epidermal layer from the dermal layer. Hypoxia-driven activation of lysosomal hydrolases eliminates suprabasal keratin debris during planar epidermis formation. Fibroblasts adjacent to the basal epidermis have differential metabolic adaptation to hypoxia, which exhibit enhanced retinoid metabolism and become putative papillary dermis. Together, these hypoxia-induced metabolic adaptations contribute to reconstructing skin architecture similar to physiological development. Our findings highlight the ability of hypoxia-induced metabolic alteration to trigger varied cellular responses, leading to self-organizing coalesced spheroids-to-planar topological transformations and the restoration of tissue homeostasis.Subject terms: Cell signalling, Skin stem cells, Morphogenesis

Introduction

The self-organization of organoids is a process in which cells interact with the local environment and arrange themselves into high-order patterns without top-down signaling1,2. During embryonic development, tissue architectures emerge as the embryonic development unfolds. The behaviors of individual cells alone cannot explain the fundamental nature of organoid morphogenesis; these emergent behaviors arise from the collective actions of cell groups3,4. The complete structure of patterned organoids requires both cell fate determination and coordinated cell movement. Given that organoid cells encounter environments different from those in embryonic development, it is crucial to identify the principles governing self-organization in organoid morphogenesis. Here, we hypothesize that a generic morphogenesis module consists of three components: a sensor that detects environmental changes, a modulator that adjusts response thresholds, and an executer that carries out the morphogenetic process. Through the interactions of these components, a new state is achieved (Fig. 1a). We further hypothesize that self-organization can occur by linking a series of such morphogenesis modules, where the outcome of one module establishes the initial condition for the next.

We have observed that dissociated skin cells can undergo self-organization in vitro to form planar skin structures5–8. While previous studies on skin organoids have demonstrated the formation of hair follicle-like appendages from spheroid-like structures9–11, our skin organoids generate hair follicles from a self-organizing planar skin. Therefore, to create organoid systems that closely resemble the structure and function of native skin, it is important to analyze the sequential morphological transitions from the dissociated epidermal and dermal cells to the planar hair-bearing skin. Recently, we have demonstrated two major transitional processes: the polarization of cell aggregates to form epidermal-dermal coupled spheroids and the coalescence of these spheroids to form double-layered flat sacs. Both processes involve the initiation of biochemical events triggered by biomechanical forces. However, the conversion from the double-layered flat sac to single-layered planar skin remains puzzling to us.

Single-cell RNA analyses of this stage gave us clues that a hypoxia-induced metabolic switch might be involved. Sensing and adapting to hypoxic environments are two of the most critical challenges for mammalian development12,13. One main challenge in growing organoids is their small size and incomplete differentiation, which is often attributed to the lack of blood vessels and consequent low oxygen levels14. For organoids to develop structurally and functionally, they must adapt to organize and differentiate under hypoxic conditions. However, little is known about the cellular dynamics mediating hypoxic adaptation during organoid morphogenesis. The self-organization of skin cells elucidates how diverse cell populations navigate hypoxic conditions to form complex organ structures, likely driven by hypoxia-related epigenetic memory within these cells15. Therefore, a pivotal question is how cellular clusters navigate hypoxic gradients and drive their progression to form planar skin. Adaptation to hypoxia provides a prerequisite for the further development of organoids, paralleling embryonic skin maturation16. Oxygen levels also influence metabolic reactions17. We hypothesize that cells in hypoxic environments metabolize and survive through different metabolic pathways. These metabolic differences can cause not only cell fate choices but also induce collective morphogenetic movements. Understanding this heterogeneity in cellular metabolism will provide significant insight into organoid engineering.

In this study, we utilized a self-organizing skin organoid system to investigate how epidermal and dermal cells adapt to a hypoxic environment, while simultaneously employing hypoxia to facilitate the formation of structural and functional skin-like constructs. By employing multi-omics analyses, cellular hypoxia detection, cell movement observation, transgenic approaches, and metabolic reprogramming, we identified two distinct oxygen tension gradients within the organoid system, namely, the spheroid compartments and the air–liquid interface. This differential hypoxic microenvironment facilitated phase separation between epidermal and dermal cells. Epidermal cells tend to reside in more hypoxic niches, ascribed to their ability to adapt to low oxygen through glycolytic metabolism. These epidermal cells closer to the hypoxic niche are more prone to differentiate into suprabasal cells, fostering the hierarchical organization of quasi-organ epidermal layers through this environmental selection process, while retinol metabolism guides specific dermal cells for spatial positioning to the epidermal-dermal junction to support basal membrane formation. Together, they drive the process of planar skin formation.

Results

Generation of the hypoxic environment during planar skin formation

Skin development and skin organoid morphogenesis are orchestrated through the intricate interplay between dermal and epidermal cells, both culminating in the establishment of a planar architecture11,18. In the air–liquid interface culture system, dissociated epidermal and dermal cells are initially positioned in the upper chamber of the transwell. Following a series of cellular movements, these cells self-organize into higher-dimensional structures. During this process, we observed that epidermal cells undergo a transition from an epidermal spheroid-like structure (coalescence stage) to planarized skin (planarization stage) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a)5,8. To further examine the dynamics of epidermal cell movement during planarization, we employed live imaging to visualize the behavior of K14GFP+ cells in the latter stages of epidermal organoid culture. Top-view and side-view imaging revealed that K14GFP+ cells self-organize into an epidermal layer, positioned on the liquid side of the culture system. This arrangement facilitates phase separation from dermal cells, resulting in a structure reminiscent of physiological skin (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Movies 1 and 2).

Immunostaining and H&E staining showed that the skin spheroids on D4 transformed into a planarized structure on D7, with the epidermal cells located in the lower layer, while the dermal cells resided in the upper layer of the culture system. Fluorescent microscopic images with dermal cell markers (VIM, TNC, Col I) clearly differentiate fibroblasts into two groups: one group is closely adjacent to epidermal cells, while the other group is situated at a distance from these cells. At the dermo-epidermal junction, there exists a complete basement membrane structure (Laminin positive), mirroring the characteristics of real skin (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Using Herovici and H&E staining, we unexpectedly observed a gradual disappearance of keratin debris within the competent morphogenetic unit (CMU) generated during the polarization stage7 (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1b). The transplantation assay revealed that the planarized (D7) skin cultures regenerated significantly more hair follicles than the spheroids-like (D4) skin cultures did (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1c). We speculate that the lower number of hair follicles from the spheroids-like culture may be due to the inward growing hair that failed to sprout out from the epidermis9,10.

To uncover the molecular basis of the planarization process, we conducted RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on D4 and D7 cultures (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1d) and found a remarkable enrichment of the HIF-1 signaling pathway in the planarized (D7) skin cultures (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1e). We then performed scRNA-seq to analyze differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in epidermal cells during development (P0 VS E14.5, Supplementary Fig. 1f) and found a similar enrichment of genes related to glycolysis and gluconeogenesis processes in epidermal cells (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Glycolysis is known to be the preferred metabolic process of the epidermis under anaerobic conditions19. Considering the inherent variations in oxygen tension present in 3D culture, we speculate that this cellular self-organization process may be driven by local hypoxic conditions. Based on these findings, we postulated that oxygen levels influence epidermal cell behaviors. Using a probe that detects the hypoxia levels (Fig. 1f, g), we observed a significantly lower oxygen level in the suprabasal epidermis at E18 compared to E14.5 skin (Fig. 1h). Similarly, in skin organoids, a progressively hypoxic phenotype was noted from the dermal layer to the basal and suprabasal layers of the epidermis (Fig. 1h). Moreover, as skin organoid cultivation progressed, the hypoxia levels within the system increased (Fig. 1g, h). We also discovered that the liquid phase, where epidermal cells resided near the culture medium, exhibited lower oxygen levels than the air phase, where dermal cells faced the air (Fig. 1g, h). These findings suggest the presence of two distinct zones of oxygen levels in the two 3D organoid culture systems: (1) in the spheroid culture: lower oxygen tension within the spheroid compared to higher oxygen in its external environment, and (2) in the planarized skin culture: lower oxygen levels in the liquid phase compared to the oxygen-richer air phase (Fig. 1i). To further understand the morphogenesis of skin organoids, we evaluated the differential hypoxic gradients across these two layers, which may serve as driving factors for this process.

Multi-omics analysis reveals enhanced hypoxia signaling in epidermal cells during skin organoid planarization

RT-qPCR results showed that the mRNA expression of Hif1a, Epas1 (Hif2a), Hmox1, Gys1, and Tgfa was upregulated on D7 (Fig. 2a). We then performed ATAC-seq to compare the differentially active motifs between D7 and D4 cultures where Hif1a ranked third among the motif-binding proteins (Fig. 2b). Additionally, the chromatin region of Hif1a was significantly more open and accessible on D7 compared to D4 (Fig. 2c). To understand the cellular heterogeneity under hypoxic conditions between skin spheroids and planar skin, we isolated individual viable single cells from D4 and D7 skin organoids for scRNA-seq analysis. We identified sixteen distinct clusters, including the five fibroblast clusters and two epidermal clusters. Based on their conserved marker genes (Figs. 2d, e and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). To verify the expression levels of hypoxia-related genes in organoid cultures, we created a hypoxia gene module of 24 hypoxia-related genes in Seurat for the subsequent scoring (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The scoring results demonstrate that epidermal cell clusters (SBC and BC) exhibited significantly higher hypoxia scores compared to fibroblast clusters (FB1–FB5) (Supplementary Fig. 2d, e). Notably, hypoxia scores in SBC and BC were elevated at D7 relative to D4 (Fig. 2f), suggesting enhanced hypoxic conditions in epidermal cells at the later developmental stage, which aligns with our hypothesis (Fig. 1h). By querying the core regulatory region of Hif1a using the gene regulatory network (GRN) algorithm, we observed that epidermal cells exhibited more active GRN intensity (Fig. 2g). Further subdivision of the epidermal cells identified three suprabasal cell subsets (SBC1, SBC2, and SBC3). Differentiation trajectory analysis showed that BC is the initial differentiation point, followed by SBC1, SBC2, and SBC3 sequentially (Fig. 2h). These cell populations may indicate different levels of structural organization within the physiological skin layer. Notably, Hif1a expression exhibited a progressive upregulation from BC to SBC3 (Fig. 2i), which was paralleled by a concurrent elevation in hypoxia scores (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Additionally, Hif1a and its target genes were also upregulated in epidermal cells in D7 cultures compared to D4 cultures (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. 2g). These results suggest that Hif1a is involved in the transformation process from coalescence to planarization.

Hif1a drives the topological transformation of epidermal cell collectives during skin organoid planarization

Hif1a and Hif2a are transcription factors integral to cellular adaptation and regulation under hypoxic conditions20. RNA-seq demonstrated a continual increase in both Hif1a and Hif2a from D0 to D7 (Fig. 3a). The expression levels of Hif1a were found to be higher than those of Hif2a, inferring a prolonged role for Hif1a in the process of organoid morphogenesis. scRNA-seq analysis and immunostaining demonstrated that Hif1a was predominantly expressed in epidermal cells, showing an increasing trend in expression levels over the cultivation period (D0, D2, D4, and D7) (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Wholemount immunostaining also indicated increased Hif1a expression in the inner layer of the epidermal aggregate at D4 (Fig. 3c). Hif1a expression was primarily expressed in the lower epidermal layers in the D7 culture (Fig. 3b). However, the level of Hif2a in epidermal cells remained consistently low and even decreased with the progression of the culture period (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Hence, Hif1a was selected as the potential key regulator of skin organoid morphogenesis for further functional validation.

Cultures treated with the HIF1A inhibitor KC7F2 failed to planarize (Fig. 3d). Subsequently, we generated K14cre-Hif1aflox/flox mice (Supplementary Fig. 3d) to specifically delete Hif1a in epidermal cells and used newborn mice to generate skin organoids. To validate epidermal-specific Hif1a knockout (K14+), qRT-PCR revealed a robust 72% decrease in Hif1a transcripts, while immunostaining confirmed near-complete protein depletion within epidermal compartments (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Crucially, neither transcriptional (RT-qPCR) nor translational (immunostaining) analyses detected compensatory Hif2a upregulation across epidermal subpopulations in cKO models (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Deletion of Hif1a in the epidermis delayed the planarization of epidermal cells in skin organoids and resulted in decreased hair regeneration after transplantation into the skin of nude mice (Fig. 3d). Given the potential influence of hypoxia on skin organoid self-organization, we systematically assessed their morphogenesis across an oxygen gradient (0.1–5% O2). We found that 0.1% O2 compromised dermal–epidermal adhesion. Although 1% O2 permitted adhesion and planar tissue formation, it sustained a reduced fibroblast pool. Epidermal planarization was incomplete at 5% O2 (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Consequently, 2% O2 was selected as the optimal condition, robustly supporting adhesion, fibroblast maintenance, and full epidermal morphogenesis. Incubating skin organoids in a hypoxic environment with 2% oxygen accelerated epidermal planarization compared to the controls (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 3g). Transplanting hypoxia-cultured organoids into the backs of nude mice resulted in increased hair follicle regeneration (Fig. 3e). To distinguish whether the enhanced hair-regenerative capacity upon transplantation is attributable to the planarized architecture or to HIF1A activation, we pharmacologically activated the pathway in non-planarized skin organoids at the time of grafting (D3) using two independent small-molecule agonists: ML228, a HIF pathway activator, and DMOG, a HIF1A stabilizer (Supplementary Fig. 3h). Exogenous activation of HIF1 did not enhance hair follicle regeneration. In these non-planarized organoids, hair follicles predominantly developed into intradermal epidermal cysts. By contrast, hair follicles regenerated from planarized organoids (D7) emerged from within the organized, planar skin structure. These results indicate that the planarized architecture itself, rather than HIF1A activation, is the primary determinant of successful hair follicle regeneration.

RNA-seq analysis of hypoxia-cultured organoids revealed that, compared to the controls, hypoxia culture activated metabolic pathways, including glycolysis and the HIF-1 signaling pathway (Fig. 3f, g). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of terms related to ATP synthesis and skin development (Supplementary Fig. 3i). These findings further suggest that oxygen levels influence skin organoid self-organization by regulating HIF-1-related metabolic processes. indicating temporal enhancement of glucose acquisition capacity in developing epithelial lineages. To functionally interrogate Hif1a’s role in glycolytic regulation, we conducted extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurements. Pharmacological inhibition of HIF1A resulted in significantly diminished ECAR levels compared to control organoids (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 3j), establishing that Hif1a activity is essential for maintaining glycolytic throughput during epidermal morphogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 3j). To dynamically monitor glucose utilization during skin organoid morphogenesis, we implemented 2-NBDG fluorescent tracing. Quantitative analysis revealed progressive intensification of glucose uptake signals from D4 to D7 of self-organization. Strikingly, epidermal compartments demonstrated predominant 2-NBDG accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 3k). To specifically validate HIF1α binding in our model, we performed Hif1a ChIP-seq on D4 skin organoids (Fig. 3i). Enrichment analysis of the captured peaks revealed significant associations with the HIF-1 signaling pathway and, notably, with glycolysis (Fig. 3j). Among the genes within these enriched pathways were key glycolytic enzymes, including Eno1 and Pgam1. Motif analysis of HIF1A binding sites revealed significant enrichment of the canonical hypoxia response element (RCGTG), confirming the specificity of the ChIP-seq dataset (Fig. 3k). Integration with our ATAC-seq data showed that these binding sites predominantly localized to accessible chromatin regions, supporting their functional relevance (Fig. 3k). Notably, known motif analysis of the Eno1 and Pgam1 promoter sequences identified HIF1A as the top-ranked transcription factor binding motif for both genes (Fig. 3l), indicating that HIF1A directly regulates their transcription. Together, these results suggest that Hif1a promotes the self-organization of planar skin organoids by modulating glycolysis, thereby enhancing their capacity to induce hair follicle regeneration (Supplementary Fig. 3l).

The glycolysis process is required to drive the planarization process

Under hypoxic conditions, the metabolic activity of the glycolytic pathway in organoid systems is typically enhanced. Glucose, the primary energy source for mammalian cells, fuels both glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle21. However, organoid adaptation to hypoxic conditions requires hypoxic energy metabolism17. To assess metabolic changes, we collected D4 and D7 samples for metabolic profiling (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Combined with RNA-seq data, enrichment analysis on metabolites upregulated in D7 cultures showed that glycolysis and gluconeogenesis pathways were ranked highest (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 4b). The significant increase in glycolytic-related metabolites, including G6P, 3PG, lactate, glucose, FEB, and PEP, suggests that organoid morphogenesis is accompanied by increased hypoxic metabolism (Fig. 4c).

To verify hypoxia metabolism levels at the single-cell scale, we evaluated the glycolytic gene activity across all cell populations by setting up a gene set from the GSEA database. The results showed that the glycolysis scores of SBC and BC were ranked first and second, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). RT-qPCR also revealed higher expression of glycolysis-related genes in D7 compared to D4 cultures (Supplementary Fig. 4e). The glycolytic product lactate was significantly higher on D7 compared to D4 (Supplementary Fig. 4f). To validate the differential oxygen consumption between dermal and epidermal compartments, we FACS-sorted cells from D4 organoids and assessed their metabolic profiles using Seahorse analysis. The results (Fig. 4d) revealed that dermal fibroblasts possess a significantly higher OCR than epidermal keratinocytes, thereby establishing their role as the primary consumers of oxygen and generators of the hypoxic niche within the organoids. To further characterize the metabolic heterogeneity of dermal cells and epidermal cells, we used mitochondrial membrane potential probes to observe differences in oxidative phosphorylation metabolism. The results showed that dermal cells had stronger fluorescence intensity compared to epidermal cells. Conversely, the fluorescence intensity of pH probes was higher in epidermal cells, indicating different metabolic patterns between dermal and epidermal cells (Fig. 4e). To probe potential pH differences between the dermal and epidermal microenvironments, we performed ratiometric pH imaging on D4 and D7 skin organoid sections using the fluorescent dye SNARF-1 AM (Fig. 4f). Measurements revealed a pH range of 7.2–7.4 at D4 and 7.1–7.3 at D7. As both ranges lie above the typical acidotic threshold (pH < 7.0), we conclude that the observed phenotypic responses are likely driven by hypoxia rather than extracellular acidosis.

Next, we used small molecule inhibitors Rotenone and 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2DG) to perturb oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic metabolism, respectively. The results showed that no planarized skin was observed in the 2DG-treated group compared to the control at D7 (Fig. 4g). Since Hif1a is involved in regulating the anaerobic glycolysis pathway to help cells or tissues adapt to low oxygen environments22, we selected Hif1a+ and Hif1a- cells in the epidermis and compared their DEGs using scRNA-seq data. Hif1a+ cells specifically expressed Pkm, Ldha, Pgk1, Mif, Eno1, and Pgam1, all of which encode rate-limiting enzymes in glycolysis (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). Immunostaining showed that these genes were highly expressed in epidermal cells, whose expression levels gradually increased during culture progression (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 4i). Inhibition of ENO1 or PGAM1 led to the blockade of planarization process in culture and decreased hair regeneration upon transplantation (Fig. 4i), reduced lactate and ATP production (Fig. 4j). Specifically, we assessed the expression levels of the glucose transporter gene Slc2a1 (Gult1) and the lactate transporter gene Mct1, revealing higher expression in epidermal cell clusters, with a notable increase from D4 to D7. Additionally, we investigated the spatiotemporal regulation of pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle by monitoring two critical metabolic nodes: the pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit (Pdhae1, the rate-limiting catalytic component of the PDH complex) and pyruvate carboxylase (Pc). ScRNA-seq uncovered a compartmentalized expression pattern, with PDH transcripts significantly enriched in dermal cell clusters compared to epidermal populations. Immunostaining validation further localized PDH protein predominantly within fibroblast lineages (Supplementary Fig. 4j), suggesting a metabolic bifurcation. In this scenario, fibroblasts engage in oxidative phosphorylation via PDH-mediated channeling of pyruvate into the TCA cycle, while epidermal progenitors exhibit glycolytic dependency, bypassing mitochondrial oxidation. These data confirm that glycolysis is essential for the planarization process during skin organoid self-organization.

Hypoxia activates lysosomes to hydrolyze keratin debris

Keratinocytes express hyperproliferation-associated cytokeratins and then undergo apoptosis in the suprabasal layers, forming keratinous debris. Through immunostaining and single-cell lineage analysis, our findings reveal that the SBC3 cluster exhibits a high expression of KLK7, representing the most distal aspect of epidermal differentiation (Fig. 2h, i)7. Since the keratin debris is located in the innermost epithelial spheroids, we investigated the biological processes of the innermost epidermal cells (SBC3) adjacent to the keratin debris. As expected, pathways including skin development, epidermis development, and keratinocyte differentiation were highly enriched in the SBC3 group (Fig. 5a). This suggests that, similar to the physiological conditions in the skin, the differentiation of suprabasal cells contributes to the formation of the cornified layer and the generation of keratin debris. Next, we explored the mechanisms of keratin debris elimination during skin organoid planarization. RNA-seq analysis revealed that the expression of the lysosomal hydrolase family was significantly increased at D7 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Then, we constructed a gene set related to lysosomal function to score for the lysosomal activation status at the single-cell level (Supplementary Fig. 5b). We found that both epidermal and partial dermal subpopulations (except FB3 and FB4) scored higher than the other cell types (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 5c). It is worth mentioning that FB1 also showed a high level of lysosomal activation score, which may be related to the maintenance of extracellular matrix homeostasis23.

Epithelial cells can act as phagocytes to remove adjacent epithelial cell debris24. Hypoxia can trigger autophagy via lysosomes25. To verify the impact of hypoxia on the elimination of keratin debris, we subjected organoid cultures to low-oxygen conditions or treated them with KC7F2 to inhibit HIF1A function. Notably, the volume of keratin debris under the low-oxygen culture conditions consistently exhibited a lower level compared to the control. Conversely, the KC7F2-treated group consistently displayed a higher volume of keratin debris (Supplementary Fig. 5d). To further demonstrate that hypoxia promotes the clearance of keratin debris via the lysosomal pathway, we examined lysosomes in the skin organoids using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) after hypoxic or normoxic culture. We discovered that the number of phagocytosed lysosomes was greater in the suprabasal epidermal cells in hypoxia cultures than in normoxic cultures (Fig. 5d). LysoTracker probe detection assay showed significantly more lysosomal signals in hypoxic cultures compared to normoxic cultures (Supplementary Fig. 5e). RT-qPCR and immunostaining showed that hypoxia culture significantly increased the expression of lysosomal-related genes on both D4 and D7 (Fig. 5e and Supplementary Fig. 5f). To directly test if an acidic microenvironment is required for the observed lysosomal response, we repeated the hypoxia treatments in the presence of HEPES buffer to maintain the culture medium at a constant pH of 7.4. Comparative analysis revealed that: The expression level of the lysosomal marker LAMP2 in the HEPES-buffered hypoxia group showed no significant difference compared to the standard hypoxia group (Supplementary Fig. 5g). Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis of the lysosomal protease genes Ctsl and Ctsd confirmed that their hypoxia-induced upregulation was not attenuated by pH stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 5h). These combined results demonstrate that the pH in our organoid model does not reach an acidotic range under hypoxia. More importantly, the persistence of the lysosomal and cathepsin response under pH-buffered hypoxia conditions provides direct evidence that the activation is primarily driven by low oxygen tension itself, rather than by a secondary acidosis.

The cathepsin (CTS) family can activate glutaminyl transferase-1 (GGT1) through hydrolysis and then promote the cross-linking of keratinized membrane proteins, ultimately inducing epidermal differentiation and renewal26. The cathepsin genes are highly expressed in the suprabasal cells of the epidermal spheroids (Fig. 5f, g). Inhibition of the hydrolytic enzymes CTSL and CTSD resulted in more keratin debris generation within the epidermal spheroids on D7 compared to the control (Fig. 5h), which also inhibited the planarization process in organoid culture and hair regeneration upon transplantation (Fig. 5i). These findings suggest that a hypoxic environment can activate lysosomes, which eliminate keratin debris.

Hypoxia triggers heterogeneous retinol metabolism in fibroblasts

Histologically, the dermis has two main layers: the papillary dermis and the reticular dermis27. To investigate the organizational processes and heterogeneity of dermal fibroblasts in the skin organoid, we used scRNA-seq to extract all dermal cells (Pdgfra+ and Vim+), which then were divided into five sub-populations (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 6a) and confirmed their identity using their specific markers by immunostaining (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 6b). Our findings revealed that the marker gene Col3a1 for FB1 and the marker gene Dpt for FB2 are expressed around the dermal cells closer to the epidermis. The marker genes Spp1, Fabp4, and Lum for FB3, FB4, and FB5, respectively, are predominantly expressed in the distal region of the epidermis (Fig. 6b, c). Among these, the cell population with molecular resemblance to the upper dermis was predominantly distributed in FB1, accounting for 31.7% of all dermal cells on D7 (Supplementary Fig. 6c).

To further analyze the functions of different subgroups of dermal fibroblasts, we conducted an enrichment analysis using the top fifty marker genes from each cell group. The results revealed that FB1 and FB2 are primarily involved in the organization of the extracellular matrix. FB4 may potentially function as pro-inflammatory fibroblasts. FB3 and FB5 exhibited enrichment in biological processes related to platelet activation and calcium ion response, suggesting their possible identity as reticular fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 6d). The cell correlation analysis further revealed that FB1 and FB2 share more similarities (Fig. 6d). As such, we grouped FB1 and FB2 together and FB3–5 as another subset of fibroblasts. To determine which of these two cell groups is the potential precursor for papillary fibroblasts, we scored them according to a chosen gene set specific to papillary fibroblasts. We found that the most papillary fibroblast gene signature in the FB1 region (Fig. 6e), suggesting that these cells are the potential precursors for papillary fibroblasts.

The heterogeneity of dermal cells can be determined by differences in metabolic levels28. We compared the differences in metabolic selection between the two types of dermal cells using the R package scMetabolism. The results showed that the retinol metabolic pathway was significantly enriched in FB1 cells (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 6e). Given FB1’s spatial proximity to the epidermal layer, it’s reasonable to assume they experience lower oxygen levels than other dermal cells do, thus leading to elevated HIF1A expression. As expected, FB1 showed the highest Hif1a expression across all five FB groups (Fig. 6g). To further investigate the relationship between Hif1a activation and metabolism, we selected fibroblasts with high Hif1a expression (relative expression level > 1) in all fibroblast clusters, and performed pathway enrichment analysis. Notably, the results showed significant enrichment in the retinol metabolism pathway (Fig. 6g), providing further support to our hypothesis. Additionally, Rara, Rxra, Rbp4, and Cyp26b1, genes pertaining to retinol metabolism, were also specifically expressed in FB1 (Fig. 6h). Nevertheless, we observed a continuous decrease in the expression of Rara and Rbp4 in the epidermal cells (D0–D4), while FB1 exhibited continuous upregulation and the rest of the dermal cell populations exhibited downregulation (Fig. 6i). This suggests that the metabolism of retinoic acid plays a conserved role in the self-organization process of the skin organoid.

We also examined the expression of Rara and Rbp4 in developing murine skin using scRNA-seq analysis. Intriguingly, we discovered a compelling co-expression between Rara, Rbp4, and characteristic markers of papillary fibroblasts, including Trps1 and Bmp4. Notably, we observed upregulated Rara and Rbp4 expression on P0, as opposed to those on E14.5 (Fig. 6j), suggesting that Rara and Rbp4 may be involved in the development and maturation of papillary fibroblasts in mice. A deficiency of vitamin A, which is a metabolite of retinol, can trigger alterations in the structure and makeup of the basement membrane. However, this aberration can be alleviated by supplementation of retinoic acid29, implying involvement of retinol metabolites produced by papillary dermal cells in the formation of the basement membrane. To further validate the ECM-based interaction between FB1 and BC in maintaining skin structural stability, we utilized the ECM-Receptor cell interaction analysis module of CellChat and CellCall to compare the interactions among five clusters of dermal cells and basal epidermal cells. We observed that FB1-BC exhibited the highest number and strongest intensity of interactions (Supplementary Fig. 6f). Specifically, the analysis of ligand-receptor pairs indicated that the interactions between FB1 and BC were primarily based on laminin pathway (Supplementary Fig. 6g). This suggests the presence of interactions between the upper dermis and basal epidermal cells, with this chemically mediated interaction potentially being a major determinant of spatial positioning for dermal fibroblast cells.

Immunostaining showed that RARA and RBP4 were significantly upregulated at the epidermal-dermal junction, with RBP4 exhibiting the highest expression level (Fig. 7a). The inhibition of RBP4 resulted in a reduction in the synthesis of collagen at the dermal-epidermal junction (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Fig. 7a). The stability of the basement membrane was disrupted after the inhibition of RBP4, but this process could be reversed by exogenous supplementation of retinoic acid (Fig. 7c). In addition, inhibition of retinoid metabolism has been shown to impact the formation of cutaneous appendages in a manner that is distinct from exogenous supplementation of retinoic acid, which does not affect epidermal stratification (Fig. 7c, d and Supplementary Fig. 7b). Exogenous addition of all-trans-retinoic acid (atRA) in the skin organoid culture system increased the proliferative activity of epidermal cells (Fig. 7d), while the volume of keratin debris also decreased accordingly (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). We observed a 51% reduction in hair follicle regeneration in transplanted cutaneous organoids with inhibition of retinoid metabolism (Fig. 7d). These results suggest that fibroblasts with a strong retinoid metabolism capability are spatially positioned closer to the basal cells of the epidermis. This proximity is crucial for the formation of the basement membrane, which serves as a critical link between the dermis and the epidermis, and hair regeneration during skin organogenesis. To determine whether retinoid metabolites from dermal fibroblasts influence glycolysis in epidermal cells, we performed RT-qPCR. The results revealed that atRA upregulated Aldh3b1 and Ldha, the latter being critical for sustaining NAD+ regeneration under hypoxic conditions. Conversely, inhibition of RBP4 (which blocks retinol transport) downregulated Aldh3b1 and Gpi, while Hk3, Pgk1, and Tpi1 remained unaffected. This selective modulation of early glycolytic steps and terminal lactate production (Ldha) suggests a tissue-specific mechanism by which retinoid signaling may fine-tune ATP generation through discrete metabolic nodes during the epidermal stratification of skin organoids (Fig.7e).

Altogether, we have discovered a morphogenesis module in which hypoxia acts as the driver, activating Hif1a as the sensor, to promote the adaptation of epidermal and dermal cells to low oxygen through glycolysis and retinol metabolism, respectively, and the subsequent assembly into planar skin organoids (Fig. 7f).

Discussion

Organoids demonstrate the capacity to emulate organ development with a series of self-organizing processes. Using newborn mouse skin organoids, we have shown a series of self-organizing processes and morphological transitions from dissociated epidermal and dermal cells to reconstituted skin5. Furthermore, we now attempt to dissect self-organization into a series of morphogenesis modules and identify the sensor, modulator, and executer of each module (Fig. 1a). We and others have shown that mechano-chemical coupling can trigger morphological transitions in organoid morphogenesis. Here, our study delves into how hypoxia-mediated metabolism adaptation affects skin organoid self-organization. We demonstrate hypoxia as the driver, HIF1a as the sensor, and HIF1a-mediated glycolysis as the key metabolic pathway for epidermal cells under low oxygen levels, while retinol metabolism determines the heterogeneous nature of the dermis during the planarization of skin organoids. Surprisingly, these metabolism-driven processes can transform the flat sac into planar skin and support the hypothesis that metabolism adaptation can also work as a driving force for self-organization in organoid morphogenesis (Fig. 7f).

Adaptation to hypoxia is a fundamental biological process in embryonic development, tissue repair, cancer, and ischemic diseases30,31. Interestingly, we discovered that the self-organization process of organoids can also be driven by hypoxia. This is particularly evident in the phase separation of mixed dermal and epidermal cells, resulting in the formation of corresponding layers that resemble the physiologically developed skin. To transform the spherical cysts into fully functional organoids, hypoxic conditions may drive cellular interactions, cell movement, and differentiation. The dermal layer receives ample oxygen supply from an extensive network of blood vessels, while the supply of oxygen to the epidermis is relatively insufficient. Besides, certain cutaneous appendages exist in moderate to severe hypoxic environments32. We found that both the binding affinity of hypoxia probes and the scoring of Hif1a GRN are consistently higher in the epidermal clusters compared to the dermal clusters (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 2g). Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that the difference in oxygen levels between epidermal and dermal cells may drive the phase transition, ultimately forming a planarized skin. At the stage of spheroid coalescence, the skin organoids exhibit a heterogeneous hypoxic microenvironment, which is accompanied by associated molecular events, including Hif1a, to compensate for hypoxia. Considering the inherent aerobic differences between dermal and epidermal cells, we speculate that it is the natural divergence between them that leads to the phase separation of both cell types under hypoxia.

Metabolic reprogramming refers to the alteration of cellular metabolism pathways to meet the energy and biomass demands of rapidly dividing and differentiating cells33. Hif1a activation induces a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis34. During the planarization process, we observed an increase in glycolysis levels in the culture system. Given the continuously decreasing oxygen levels in the system, this indicates that skin cells adapt to low oxygen levels in the environment by switching to the glycolytic pathway. Under hypoxic conditions, the heart increasingly relies on glucose oxidation for energy production. Intriguingly, in contrast, the brain, kidneys, and liver exhibit enhanced uptake and oxidation of fatty acids35. These findings strongly suggest that different organs possess the remarkable ability to adapt to hypoxia through distinct modes of energy metabolism, which appears to be a manifestation of epigenetic memory derived from the developmental process36. The process of planarization in skin organoids can be understood as the movement of epidermal cells towards a lower-oxygen liquid phase, which involves two main processes: hypoxia sensing and the directional movement towards areas with lower oxygen concentrations. Similarly, the hypoxic environment after a skin wound triggers keratinocyte migration, aiding in re-epithelialization37,38.

Current skin substitute, composed of the dermis and epidermis, functions solely as a barrier and does not regenerate skin appendages15. However, self-organized skin organoids can induce hair follicles and other appendages, implying the coupling between dermal and epidermal cells that activate subsequent developmental events. Thus, in addition to the classical WNT/BMP/SHH morphogen levels, reciprocity also occurs at the metabolic level. The plasticity of metabolic states plays a role in the activation of stem cells during the transition of the hair follicle cycle39,40. The retinol metabolism pathway is a dynamic process involving the conversion of retinol, retinene, and retinoic acid to sulfonic acid. The papillary dermis metabolically processes retinol and retinene efficiently. Additionally, retinoic acid can maintain the proliferative activity of adjacent basal epidermal cells and support the differentiation of upper epidermal cells. Injection of retinoic acid has been shown to increase the thickness of collagen bundles in papillary fibroblasts41, consistent with our findings (Supplementary Fig. 7a). We propose that the enhanced retinoic acid metabolism in these fibroblasts leads to their spatial positioning closer to the epidermis, thus forming the papillary dermal layer of the skin organoids. Collagen secreted by papillary fibroblasts provides structural stability for the skin tissue and interacts with basal epidermal cells, maintaining skin homeostasis. This interplay between dermal and epidermal cells involves the exchange and balance of nutrients, signaling molecules, and other factors required for proper cellular function and tissue maintenance42,43. Through this cooperative metabolic relationship, dermal and epidermal cells can achieve homeostasis and support the formation of a well-organized skin architecture. Beyond the oxygen gradient, the physiological CO2 gradient across skin from ~5% in the dermis to atmospheric levels is also crucial, as it shapes a distinct CO2 microenvironment that likely guides self-organization. However, a limitation of our study is that the standard submerged culture, maintained at a uniform 5% CO₂, cannot replicate this steep transcutaneous gradient due to the rapid diffusion of CO₂ in aqueous media. This precludes analysis of its specific contribution to organoid morphogenesis in the present model.

In conclusion, we suggest that metabolic adaptation under hypoxic conditions drives large-scale morphogenetic movement leading to tissue organization. We showed that epidermal cells tend to engage in Hif1a-mediated glycolytic metabolism towards the liquid phase within the air–liquid culture system, ultimately leading to stratification with the dermis. Guided by retinoic acid, a metabolite of retinol in dermal cells, dermal and epidermal cells coupled and re-established a stable basement membrane, resulting in a planarized, reconstituted skin architecture. Our research offers insights into the biophysical drivers of self-organization in organoid morphogenesis and its potential applications in regenerative medicine.

Method

Mice

Experimental mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. For the skin organoid culture, ICR newborn mice within 24 h of birth were used for sampling. K14-Cre (Stock No: 004782) and Hif1a-flox/flox (Stock No: 007561) mice were procured from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity (50 ± 10%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Following breeding, genomic DNA from tail biopsies was isolated for genotyping. A PCR-based strategy was employed using the following primers targeting the Hif1a floxed allele:

Forward: 5’-TGC ATG TGT ATG GGT GTT TTG-3’

Reverse: 5’-GAA AAC TGT CTG TAA CTT CAT TTC C-3’

PCR amplification was performed under standard conditions (annealing temperature: 58 °C, 35 cycles). The expected amplicon sizes were 490 bp for the floxed allele. Mice homozygous for the Hif1a-flox allele and positive for K14-Cre (confirmed via Cre-specific primers) were selected as conditional knockout models for subsequent experiments. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Experimental Animal Center of Chongqing University (approval no. CQCH-LAE-A0000202055).

Skin organoid culture

To extract the full-thickness back skin, ophthalmic scissors were used, ensuring the gentle removal of any residual adipose tissue. The back skin specimens were then carefully laid flat on a 10 cm culture dish, with approximately 8 pieces per dish, and exposed to 20 mL of 0.25% trypsin solution (Sigma, # T4549) overnight. The following day, the dermis and epidermis were separated using pointed tweezers, and collagenase (Worthington, # LS004197) was used to digest the collagen in a water bath set to 37 °C for 30 min. After digestion and filtration, epidermal and dermal cells were blended in a 1:9 ratio, resulting in a high concentration (approximately 5 × 107 cells/well). The cell mixture was then added to a 12-well transwell cell culture system (JET, #TCS016012), with 700 µL of DMEM/F12 medium (Corning, # 10092018) added to the bottom of the culture system. The medium was changed every other day. Detailed information regarding the culture media, small molecules, and recombinant proteins can be found in Supplementary Data 1. The HIF pathway activator ML228 was applied at a concentration of 10 μM, and the HIF1A stabilizer DMOG was used at 100 μM from day 0 to day 3. KC7F2 was administered at 40 μM, the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG at 20 mM, and the oxidative phosphorylation inhibitor rotenone at 10 μM. Inhibitors targeting ENO1, PGAM1, CTSL, and CTSD were each used at 10 μM. All-trans retinoic acid was applied at 500 μM, and the RBP4 inhibitor at 5 μM. These small-molecule treatments were carried out from day 3 to day 7.

Time-lapse live imaging

The time-lapse live imaging system utilized a two-photon microscope at China Medical University Hospital. K14GFP+ cells were cultured on a glass plate, which was positioned on a heating platform maintained at 37 °C. Image segmentation and 4D trajectory tracking were performed using Imaris v9.0 (Bitplane, Zurich, Switzerland) with the surface creation wizard and autoregressive motion algorithms. Tracks with a track duration less than 10 min or displacement less than 5μm were excluded from further analysis.

Single-cell isolation for scRNA-seq

For scRNA-seq, skin organoids were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using Accutase (STEMCELL Technologies) at 37 °C for 10 min, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer (Corning), and resuspended in PBS containing 0.04% BSA (Thermo Fisher, #AM2616) at a density of 1.0 × 106 cells/mL. Cell viability (>95%) was confirmed by trypan blue exclusion (Sigma). Single live cells were isolated via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using a BD FACSAria v8.0.2 (BD Biosciences) with a 100 μm nozzle and sheath pressure of 20 psi, gating on FSC-A/SSC-A and excluding PI-positive dead cells. Approximately 6500–8000 cells per channel were loaded onto a 10× Genomics Chromium Controller X using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Kit v3.1, targeting 65-70% capture efficiency. GEM generation and cDNA synthesis were performed on a ProFlex PCR System (Thermo Fisher) with reverse transcription at 53 °C for 45 min. Libraries were quantified using a Qubit 4.0 (≥3.5 ng/μL; Thermo Fisher) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150, 50000 reads/cell). Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger v7.1.0 (10× Genomics), aligned to the mm10 genome (GENCODE VM25), with quality control thresholds: 500-6,000 genes/cell, mitochondrial gene content <15%, and doublet detection via DoubletFinder v2.0.3. Batch correction was performed using Harmony v1.0 (theta = 2, lambda = 0.5) to integrate 1 biological replicate.

Quality control and downstream analysis of scRNA-seq data

Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were processed using Cell Ranger v7.1.0 (10× Genomics) with the cellranger count pipeline. Reads were aligned to the mm10 reference genome (GENCODE VM25 annotation) using the STAR aligner, and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts were quantified. Initial quality control metrics included filtering cells with <500 or >6000 detected genes, mitochondrial gene content >15%, and removal of doublets identified by DoubletFinder v2.0.3 (threshold: 1.5 × the expected doublet rate per sample). Processed count matrices were imported into Seurat v4.3.0 for downstream analysis. Data normalization was performed using SCTransform to correct for sequencing depth variability, while regressing out mitochondrial gene effects. Highly variable genes (2000 features) were selected using the vst method. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on scaled data, and significant PCs (determined by elbow plot and JackStraw test, p < 0.001) were used for UMAP dimensionality reduction (parameters: n.neighbors = 30, min.dist = 0.3). Cell clusters were identified via the Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.8) and annotated using marker genes from published skin development atlases. Differential gene expression analysis between clusters was performed using the FindAllMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, log2 fold-change > 0.25, adjusted p < 0.05). Batch effects across biological replicates were corrected using Harmony v1.0 prior to final visualization.

RNA-seq

RNA-seq data of skin organoids were downloaded from the GEO database. A comparison of the D4 and D7 data was conducted to find clues to the formation of planarized skin organoids. Firstly, the raw data were normalized, and then the gene expression values were log2-transformed. For differential analysis, gene expression values between D7 and D4 were determined using the limma package. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 1 were used as thresholds to identify significant differences in gene expression. Differentially expressed gene enrichment and functional annotation analysis were performed using the R package clusterProfiler 4.0.

Immunofluorescence staining

Skin organoids were fixed with freshly prepared 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich 158127 in PBS, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 24 h under gentle agitation. Specimens were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%, 75%, 85%, 95%, 100%, 1 h per concentration), embedded in paraffin wax (Paraplast X-TRA, Leica 39601006) using a tissue processor (Leica ASP300), and sectioned into 6 μm serial slices with a rotary microtome (Leica RM2255) mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides. Prior to staining, sections underwent deparaffinization in xylene (3 × 5 min), rehydration through descending ethanol gradients (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%, 50%, 2 min each), and antigen retrieval in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min using a microwave processor (Milestone RHS-2). Blocking was performed with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma A7906) and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies against the following targets were applied and incubated overnight (16–18 h) at 4 °C in a humidified chamber: BMP4 (Beyotime, AF6312, 1:100), Collagen I (Arigobio, ARG21965, 1:200), Collagen III (Proteintech, 22734-1-AP, 1:200), Collagen VI (Proteintech, 17023-1-AP, 1:200), Collagen XVII (Beyotime, AF1078, 1:200), CTSD (Beyotime, AF1645, 1:100), CTSL (Bioss, BS-1508R, 1:200), DPT (Abcam, EPR22845-83, 1:200), E-cadherin (Beyotime, AF0138, 1:300), ENO1 (Beyotime, AF5162, 1:200), FABP4 (ABclonal, A0232, 1:200), FABP5 (Abcam, EPR22552-64, 1:200), GLUT1 (Beyotime, AF1015, 1:100), HIF-1α (Cayman, 10006421, 1:200), HIF-2α (Abcam, BL-95-1A2, 1:200), KLK7 (Zenbio, 120906, 1:200), KRT14 (Boster, A01432, 1:500), LAMININ (Abcam, ab11575, 1:200), LAMP1 (Beyotime, AF7353, 1:200), LAMP2 (Bioss, BS-2379R, 1:200), LDHA (Affinity, DF6280, 1:200), Lumican (LUM; Abcam, EPR8898(2), 1:200), MCT1 (Beyotime, AF7440, 1:200), MGP (Proteintech, 10734-1-AP, 1:200), MIF (Abcam, EPR18149-128, 1:200), MMP3 (Abcam, EP1186Y, 1:500), P63 (GeneTex, GTX102425, 1:500), PC (Beyotime, AG2807, 1:200), P-Cadherin (R&D Systems, AF761, 1:200), PDHE1 (Beyotime, AG2839, 1:200), PGAM1 (Beyotime, AG2856, 1:200), PGK1 (Beyotime, AF1825, 1:200), PKM (Proteintech, 15822-1-AP, 1:200), RARA (Abcam, EPR23871-271, 1:100), RBP4 (Proteintech, 11774-1-AP, 1:200), SPP1 (Beyotime, AF1651, 1:200), Tenascin C (TNC; Beyotime, AF1651, 1:500), and Vimentin (VIM; Beyotime, AF0318, 1:500). Primary antibodies against target proteins see detailed information on company, catalog number, and dilution in Supplementary Data 2. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with species-matched Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, 1:500 dilution) for 2 h in the dark. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using either DAPI (1 μg/mL, 5 min) or propidium iodide (PI, 2 μg/mL, 10 min). Slides were coverslipped with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen P36961) and cured for 24 h at room temperature. Confocal imaging was conducted on a Leica TCS SP8 system.

Lysosome staining

The samples were embedded in an optimum cutting temperature (OCT) compound and then cooled in liquid nitrogen. To explore the distribution of lysosomes in skin organoid cultures, the samples were immediately cryosectioned and incubated with lysosome staining probes (Lyso-Tracker Red). The nuclei were stained with DAPI.

PH probe staining

Intracellular pH was determined using the pHrodo™ Red AM Intracellular pH Indicator (Invitrogen). The frozen section method was the same as described above. The samples were quickly washed with PBS solution, and then incubated with the pH probe (5 μM) at room temperature, protected from light, for 30 min. PowerLoad™ reagent was used to facilitate the cellular loading of AM esters. The nuclei were stained with DAPI afterward.

To assess spatial pH distribution, 10 μm cryosections of D4 and D7 skin organoids were stained with the ratiometric pH indicator SNARF-1 AM (MedChemExpress, #HY-D1093). Sections were incubated with 5 μM SNARF-1 AM (prepared in serum-free medium) for 1 h at 37 °C in a humidified chamber, followed by three washes with warm PBS to remove extracellular dye. After a 15 min equilibration in fresh medium, live imaging was performed on a laser scanning confocal microscope using 488 nm excitation. Emission was collected simultaneously in two channels: 580 nm (acidic pH-sensitive) and 640 nm (alkaline pH-sensitive). A calibration curve was generated in situ using parallel sections equilibrated with high K+/nigeratin buffers of known pH (6.8–7.6). The fluorescence intensity ratio (640 nm/580 nm) was calculated for each pixel and converted to absolute pH values based on the calibration. Quantitative analysis was performed on n = 7 organoids per time point; data are presented as mean ± SD, and statistical significance was determined using an unpaired t-test.

Hyproxyprobe staining

To investigate hypoxia levels in skin organoids, the Hypoxyprobe™ RED 549 Kit (pimonidazole hydrochloride, Hypoxyprobe, #HP7-1000Kit) was used to stain hypoxic cells. All samples were sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a freezing microtome. The sections were then incubated with pimonidazole at room temperature for 30 min. Within hypoxic cells, the pimonidazole is reduced and activated, forming stable adducts with thiol (sulfhydryl) groups in proteins, peptides, and amino acids. These adducts were subsequently labeled with fluorescent secondary antibodies and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. The samples were then observed and photographed under a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8).

Transplantation in nude mice

To test the in vivo organization of skin organoids and the efficiency of inducing hair regrowth, we performed nude mouse transplantation experiments. Briefly, the procedure is as follows: First, pentobarbital sodium and analgesics were administered intraperitoneally. A full-thickness skin wound of approximately 0.8 mm in diameter was then made on both sides of the back of the nude mouse. Skin organoids were cut from the chamber, and one side of the organoid was attached to the wound. The wound was then sutured with surgical sutures, locally treated with bacteriostatic agents, and protected with gauze.

Metabolic profiling and analysis

Organoid samples were collected on days 4 and 7 of culture, transferred to 1.5 mL sterile centrifuge tubes, and washed three times with ice-cold PBS. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of 20% acetonitrile and vortexed for 30 s. Cell disruption was performed using a cell disruptor (300 W power, 5 s pulses, 5 cycles) in an ice-water bath to ensure complete lysis.

Metabolite profiling was carried out using an Agilent 1290 UHPLC system coupled to a Sciex TripleTOF 6600 Q-TOF mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters) maintained at 40 °C. Mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B), with a constant flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Gradient elution was programmed as follows: 5% B for 1 min, linear increase to 95% B over 20 min, and holding at 95% B for 5 min. The Q-TOF system was operated in both positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI) modes with the following parameters: ion source temperature 550 °C, spray voltage ± 4.5 kV, declustering potential 80 V, and TOF-MS scans acquired over an m/z range of 50–1500 at a resolution of 30,000.

For GC-MS analysis, samples were analyzed using a DB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent) with electron impact ionization (70 eV). The oven temperature program was set as follows: initial temperature 50 °C (held for 1 min), followed by ramping to 300 °C at 5 °C/min. Raw metabolomics data were processed using XCMS (v3.8.2) with probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) to minimize technical variation. Metabolites were annotated by matching MS1–MS2 spectra against the HMDB and KEGG databases. Statistical significance was defined by fold change > 2, FDR-adjusted p < 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1 in orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) models. The relative abundance of all identified metabolites is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

ATAC-seq and analysis

For the ATAC-seq assay, nuclei were isolated from D4 and D7 skin organoids and subjected to tagmentation using the Illumina TDE1 transposase (37 °C for 30 min) to fragment open chromatin regions and ligate sequencing adapters. Libraries were amplified (5–12 PCR cycles), purified with AMPure XP beads, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired-end reads). Raw reads were quality-checked with FastQC (v0.11.9), trimmed for low-quality bases (Phred score < 30) and adapter sequences using Trim Galore (v0.6.7), and deduplicated with Picard (v2.27.4). Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm10 (GRCm38 assembly) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4), retaining uniquely mapped reads (MAPQ ≥ 30) and excluding mitochondrial DNA alignments. Open chromatin regions were identified with MACS2 (v2.2.7.1, q-value < 0.05, fragment extension size: 200 bp). Peaks were annotated to genomic features using ChIPseeker (v1.30.3), and transcription factor binding motifs were predicted via HOMER (v4.11, threshold: p < 1 × 10−5). Differentially accessible regions (DARs) between D4 and D7 were defined using DESeq2 (v1.34.0) with thresholds of adjusted p-value (FDR < 0.05) and |log2 fold change | > 1.

H&E staining and quantification of keratin content

For H&E staining, 7 μm-thick formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized through three xylene washes (5 min each) and rehydrated via a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%, 2 min each). Sections were stained with Harris-modified hematoxylin solution (Solarbio, # G1140, China) at room temperature for 3 min. Nuclear differentiation was achieved using 1% acid ethanol (0.5% HCl in 70% ethanol) for 10 sec, followed by bleaching in Scott’s tap water substitute (0.35% NaHCO3 + 2% MgSO4) for 1 min. Cytoplasmic counterstaining was performed with eosin Y alcoholic solution (Solarbio G1120, 0.5% w/v in 95% ethanol) for 30 s. After dehydration through an ascending ethanol series (95% ×2, 100% ×2) and xylene clearing (3 × 5 min), sections were mounted with neutral balsam (Solarbio G8590). Brightfield Imaging was conducted using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-L upright microscope (Nikon Instruments, Japan).

Lactic acid content detection

Cultured skin organoid tissues were collected in centrifuge tubes, and cell counting was performed. Approximately 5 × 106 cells were added to 1 mL of lactic acid extraction buffer 1 (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cells were then subjected to sonication in an ice bath (300 W power, 3 s on, 7 s off, total duration of 3 min). The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000×g for 10 min. The supernatant (0.8 mL) was collected and slowly mixed with 0.15 mL of extraction buffer 2 while gently vortexing until no air bubbles were produced. After a second centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000×g for 10 min, the supernatant was taken for measurement. The lactate content was determined by measuring the absorbance at 570 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader, and the lactate concentration of the samples was obtained by comparison with a standard curve.

Quantitative RT-PCR

The total RNA of the skin organoid was isolated using MagZol reagent (Magen, cat. R4801-01, China). Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was carried out using the HiScript Q RT Kit (Yeasen, cat.11141ES60, China) through reverse transcription. Following that, RT-qPCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 RT-PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the SYBR Green Qpcr Master Mix with low ROX (Bimake, China) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The reaction mixture contained 0.5 μL of forward and reverse mouse primers, as specified in Supplementary Data 4. The obtained values were normalized using GAPDH as a reference gene.

Seahorse for skin organoids

Skin organoids at differentiation D4, D6, and D7 were divided into control (untreated) and iHIF1A groups, with the latter treated with 40 μM KC7F2 (HIF1A inhibitor) for 24 h prior to analysis. Organoids were dissociated into single cells, seeded at 5000 cells/well into Agilent Seahorse XFp microplates, and equilibrated in XF Base Medium (10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, pH 7.4). Mitochondrial stress tests were performed using sequential injections of oligomycin (1.5 μM), FCCP (1.0 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (0.5 μM each) through the Agilent Seahorse XFp system. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ECAR were measured in real-time, normalized to total protein content, and analyzed by two-way ANOVA. Vehicle controls (0.1% DMSO) and technical replicates (n = 3 wells/group) ensured data reliability.

FACS

To isolate distinct cellular compartments from skin organoids for downstream analysis, FACS was performed based on established surface marker profiles. Epidermal keratinocytes were isolated by sorting viable, single cells double-positive for CD49f (α6-integrin) and EpCAM. Dermal fibroblasts were enriched using a lineage-negative strategy, where viable, single cells negative for CD31, CD45, Ter119, and EpCAM, denoted as Lin− (CD31− CD45− Ter119− EpCAM−), were collected. The purity of the sorted populations was confirmed post-sort by re-analyzing an aliquot of sorted cells and/or by performing quantitative PCR for cell-type-specific genes (Krt14 for epidermal cells and Col1a1 for dermal fibroblasts).

2-NBDG glucose uptake assay

To assess glucose uptake, cells/organoids were first subjected to serum and glucose deprivation by incubation in glucose/serum-free DMEM (Thermo Fisher) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 2 h to minimize basal metabolic interference. For inhibitor controls, parallel samples were pretreated with 10 μM cytochalasin B (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min to block glucose transporter activity. Fresh 2-NBDG working solution (100 μM in glucose-free medium, Thermo Fisher #N13195) was then applied, followed by incubation under light-protected conditions at 37 °C for 30 min. Negative controls included energy-dependent uptake inhibition (4 °C incubation) and competitive suppression using 20 mM unlabeled D-glucose. Reactions were terminated by three rapid washes with ice-cold PBS to preserve fluorescence integrity. Cryosectioned samples (8 μm thickness) were immediately imaged via confocal microscopy with uniform exposure parameters across experimental groups.

ChIP-seq for skin organoids

First, the skin organ is fixed using formaldehyde and subjected to ultrasonic lysis to release the chromatin from the cells. Next, 10 μL of anti-Hif1a antibody (Abcam, #ab179483) is added to the cell lysate to specifically recognize the target protein, followed by overnight incubation and the addition of Protein A agarose to remove non-specifically bound components. The purified chromatin fragments are then subjected to enzymatic treatment to cut them into shorter DNA fragments. Subsequently, the cut DNA fragments are converted into sequencing libraries, and high-throughput sequencing is performed to obtain information about the interactions between chromatin and specific proteins within individual cells. Finally, the raw data is subjected to quality control using FastQC, and mapping is conducted using Bowtie2 (v2.4.4), to align the sequences with a reference genome, resulting in a SAM file. The SAM file is subsequently sorted using Samtools, and duplicates and filters are applied to obtain the final BAM file for further analysis. Additionally, the callpeak function in MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) is utilized to identify regions of chromatin enrichment due to specific protein binding, generating a narrow peak file in BED format, which is further visualized in the R environment.

TEM for lysosome

The organoid sample was treated with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 12 h, then treated with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in Durcupan (Sigma-Aldrich). Next, the sample was cut into 60 nm sections, mounted on a Cu grid, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The morphological structure of the sample was observed by electron microscopy (JEM-1200EX, JEOL Corporation of Japan). Lysosomal counting involved randomly selecting 6 fields of view (5000×) under the electron microscope from top left to bottom right. Three professionals counted the total lysosomes in 2 μm2 areas in the electron microscopy images, and the average count was calculated.

Statistical information

All experiments were performed with at least three biologically independent replicates, with similar results obtained across independent experiments for all representative images. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 19.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD (as indicated in figure legends). A two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. For omics datasets, significance was assessed using appropriate methods (Wald test, hypergeometric test) with Benjamini–Hochberg correction. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical parameters are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
