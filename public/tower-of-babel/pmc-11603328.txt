Mechanical forces inducing oxaliplatin resistance in pancreatic cancer can be targeted by autophagy inhibition
Authors: Maria Kalli, Fotios Mpekris, Antonia Charalambous, Christina Michael, Chrystalla Stylianou, Chrysovalantis Voutouri, Andreas G Hadjigeorgiou, Antonia Papoui, John D Martin, Triantafyllos Stylianopoulos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with limited treatment options and poor prognosis. A common characteristic among pancreatic cancer patients is the biomechanically altered tumor microenvironment (TME), which among others is responsible for the elevated mechanical stresses in the tumor interior. Although significant research has elucidated the effect of mechanical stress on cancer cell proliferation and migration, it has not yet been investigated how it could affect cancer cell drug sensitivity. Here, we demonstrated that mechanical stress triggers autophagy activation, correlated with increased resistance to oxaliplatin treatment in pancreatic cancer cells. Our results demonstrate that inhibition of autophagy using hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) enhanced the oxaliplatin-induced apoptotic cell death in pancreatic cancer cells exposed to mechanical stress. The combined treatment of HCQ with losartan, a known modulator of mechanical abnormalities in tumors, synergistically enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of oxaliplatin in murine pancreatic tumor models. Furthermore, our study revealed that the use of HCQ enhanced the efficacy of losartan to alleviate mechanical stress levels and restore blood vessel integrity beyond its role in autophagy modulation. These findings underscore the potential of co-targeting mechanical stresses and autophagy as a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome drug resistance and increase chemotherapy efficacy.Subject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Multicellular systems, Autophagy

Introduction

Despite recent improvements in pancreatic cancer therapy, prognosis remains poor, the 5-year survival rate is less than 5%, and over 75% of patients are diagnosed with locally advanced disease or metastasis1. Currently, surgical resection combined with treatments employing chemotherapeutic agents, such as gemcitabine, 5-fluorouracil (5FU), or the more recently developed FOLFIRINOX, along with radiation, is used for the cure of pancreatic cancer patients. However, at the time of diagnosis, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDACs) are already unresectable, metastasized, and chemo-resistant.

In fact, PDAC is among the most resistant to chemotherapy tumor types in large part due to biomechanical abnormalities within their tumor microenvironment (TME)2–6. Pancreatic tumors are characterized by an abundant, fibrotic, and stiff extracellular matrix (ECM) that is composed of cellular components, such as fibroblasts, vascular cells, immune cells, and an abundance of acellular components, including collagen I and hyaluronan. As the growing tumor pushes the surrounding host tissue, a reciprocal compressive force is applied from the host tissue to the tumor to resist its expansion. This leads to the development and accumulation of mechanical stress within the tumor, termed solid stress7–13. At the macroscale level, the dense ECM of PDACs, along with the development of mechanical compressive forces, lead to blood and lymphatic vessel collapse that severely impairs the transport of nutrients, oxygen, therapeutic agents, and cells inside the tumor14. Hence, pharmacological approaches targeting components of the ECM, such as collagen and/or hyaluronan, have been suggested to increase drug delivery and improve therapeutic outcomes15.

In this direction, repurposing of drugs with anti‐fibrotic properties has been shown to increase drug delivery in a variety of pancreatic and other fibrotic murine tumor models16–23. Importantly, the angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) losartan, a common antihypertensive drug, has been found to effectively alleviate solid stress and stiffness in murine PDAC and ovarian tumor models by reprogramming cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), restoring partially blood vessels function, and significantly improving overall survival17,24. Losartan was further tested in a phase II clinical trial in combination with chemoradiation, and it was found to significantly increase the resection rate of tumors in locally advanced pancreatic cancer patients25. Currently, it is being tested in combination with chemo-immunotherapy in a multi-center clinical study (clinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT03563248). However, preclinical data indicate that pancreatic tumors have 80% of their blood vessels collapsed, and even after losartan treatment, 50% of vessels remain unchanged17. Unfortunately, the losartan dose cannot be raised to decompress more vessels because of its strong anti-hypertensive effects, while the remaining compressed vessels suggest that elevated mechanical forces remain even in losartan-treated tumors. Therefore, there is a clear clinical need for the development of novel alternative therapeutic strategies to be combined with losartan to improve therapy in PDAC patients.

To this end, we need to consider that mechanical cues activate mechanotransduction-triggered pathways that regulate multiple cellular processes, such as cell proliferation, invasion, and survival26–31. In fact, cancer cells that grow within a biomechanically altered microenvironment need to maintain their integrity and adapt to a mechanically compressive environment. In recent work, we performed a large-scale proteomic assay to reveal mechanical stress-induced signaling pathways that promote cytoskeletal contraction and increase the migration of pancreatic cancer cells32. We also found that mechanical stresses can inhibit cell cycle progression and apoptosis. As most chemotherapeutics target actively proliferating cancer cells, it is reasonable to question if these mechanically regulated mechanisms could affect cancer cell drug sensitivity. This hypothesis is supported by recent findings showing that high compressive stress generated during tumor spheroid growth in a confined agarose matrix led to decreased cell proliferation, which caused resistance to gemcitabine33, whereas mechanical compression applied on ovarian cancer cells promoted chemoresistance, which was linked with CDC42 expression26. However, the exact mechanisms that are regulated under mechanical stress to promote drug resistance in PDAC are not yet defined.

Here, we utilize data from our previously conducted proteomic assay to validate that mechanical compression upregulates autophagy as an adaptive mechanism in pancreatic cancer cells. This adaptation allows the cells to resist the applied mechanical stresses, sustaining their homeostasis and promoting survival. Building upon these findings, our subsequent experiments demonstrate that mechanical compression hampers the efficacy of oxaliplatin (a component of FOLFIRINOX), while pharmacological inhibition of autophagy using hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) enhances oxaliplatin-induced apoptotic cell death in pancreatic cancer cells. Moreover, we present evidence that the treatment of pancreatic tumor-bearing mice with HCQ, in combination with the mechanotherapeutic losartan, significantly improves the efficacy of oxaliplatin through TME- and vessel-normalization. Our results strongly suggest that the combined treatment of HCQ with losartan can impair the mechanically induced chemoresistance in pancreatic tumors and improve therapeutic effects. These findings underscore the need for a deeper understanding of how mechanical stresses within the TME compromise the effectiveness of existing treatments for pancreatic cancer patients.

Results

Compression-induced mechanical stress promotes autophagy in pancreatic cancer cells

We previously conducted a large proteomic assay using protein extracts of MIA PaCa-2 cells that were compressed at 4 mmHg for 16 h, to identify mechanotransduction pathways related to cell migration32. We have used human MIA PaCa-2 cells, as they harbor key mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes such as KRAS, TP53, and CDKN2A, which are common in pancreatic cancer patients. In the current study, we integrated data from this previous work, encompassing a 16-h timeframe, with new observations derived from 48 h of compression. Changes in the expression of 425 proteins from 16- and 48 h of compression were processed and visualized by principal component analysis (PCA). This analysis is a linear dimensionality reduction technique used to transform high-dimensional data into a lower-dimensional space preserving the original variance34. By identifying the principal components, this analysis revealed the structure and patterns within the data, to better understand the contribution of time and compression as two different variables on protein expression patterns. Our data was visually represented in a 3D space, defined by the first three principal components, which collectively capture over 80% of the total variance (Suppl. Figure 1a). The remaining principal components contributed less than 5% of the total variance, as illustrated in Suppl. Figure 1b, which showcases the distribution of variance across each component. Upon comparing the experimental conditions (0- vs 4 mmHg and 16 vs 48 h), a discernible influence of pressure on protein expression becomes evident, leading to distinct segregation of data into two groups corresponding to varying pressure levels (0 and 4 mmHg). This observation is further substantiated by the results of an ANOVA test, particularly for the 2nd principal component (Suppl. Table 2). The duration of the applied load did not emerge as a significant factor affecting protein expression. To delve deeper into the impact of pressure on protein expression, we focused on the 30 proteins with the highest principal component coefficients for the 2nd principal component and the 20 proteins for the 1st principal component—both primarily influenced by mechanical stress, resulting in a total of 43 proteins. Utilizing these proteins, we calculated the relative expression differences attributed to pressure, and the results are visually presented in a heatmap as shown in Fig. 1a. We observed that mechanical stress maintains the activation of actin cytoskeleton regulators (e.g., Cytokeratin 19), stress mediators (e.g., GRP78/HSP70, HSP60) and mechanosensors (e.g., PAR1), as well as apoptosis resistance markers (e.g., PAI-1, CD171) up to 48 h post-compression. At the same time, proteins related to protein synthesis and cell cycle progression were downregulated (e.g., PLK1, 4E-BP1, and Cyclin B1) as previously discussed32. These changes led us to hypothesize that cells are adapted to the applied force and resist death. This was supported by the upregulation of the microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3- alpha and -beta (LC3A-B) expression (indicated with red font color in the heatmap), which is the most well-known marker of autophagy. Autophagy is a physiological process induced by nutrient starvation or cellular stress, leading to the recycling of damaged or unused organelles and intracellular compounds35, fueling cellular metabolic and energy pathways. According to the pertinent literature, autophagy could be a stress-induced cell adaptation mechanism36,37 that enables cells to survive under compression38,39.

To test this, we first performed immunofluorescent staining to examine the formation of LC3B puncta (small, dot-like structures) and LC3B colocalization with p62, which is an autophagy substrate indicative of active autophagy40, as representative markers for autophagic vacuole formation and progression in compressed MIA PaCa-2 cells40,41. Image analysis revealed an increase in LC3B puncta per cell, higher overall LC3B expression, and greater colocalization with p62 in compressed cells, indicating enhanced autophagic vacuole formation (Fig. 1b–e). We next set to confirm the upregulation of LC3B and p62 mRNA and protein expression levels. MIA PaCa-2 cells were compressed at 4 mmHg for 16 h, and total RNA extracts were analyzed by qPCR to indicate that the mRNA expression of both genes is upregulated in compressed cells (Fig. 1f). Subsequently, the protein levels of both genes were analyzed by western blotting, where LC3B is detected as two different forms; the LC3BII indicates the lipidated form of LC3B, which is present on the membrane of autophagic vacuoles, and the LC3BI that represents the free-, cytosolic form of LC3B. Typically, an increased LC3BII/I ratio, along with a reduction in p62 levels, is indicative of enhanced autophagy41. To this end, we analyzed the levels of these markers by western blotting and observed an increased LC3BII/I ratio in compressed cells, with either unchanged or reduced p62 expression (Fig. 1g). Similar results were obtained for human PANC1, murine KPC and PAN02 PDAC cells, which were subjected to different levels of compression for 24 h and analyzed via qPCR and western blotting for LC3B and p62 expression (Suppl. Fig. 1c–h).

Since the expression of autophagy markers can also be increased when autophagic degradation is inhibited, we next aimed to measure autophagic progression (flux) in compressed cells upon autophagy inhibition. To achieve this, we used the FDA-approved drug hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), which blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion, impairing the autophagic process, and is currently being tested in clinical trials for PDAC patients41–44. The concentration of the inhibitor was selected based on a dose-dependent treatment of MIA PaCa-2 cells and subsequent analysis of the LC3BII/I ratio and p62 protein expression (Suppl. Fig. 2d). Control and compressed MIA PaCa-2 cells were treated with 10 μΜ HCQ and analyzed for the expression levels of LC3B and p62. We observed that the LC3BII/I ratio increased significantly upon HCQ treatment, with the strongest effect being observed in compressed cells, while p62 levels were not significantly altered (Suppl. Fig. 1i). These findings indicate that, although MIA PaCa-2 cells exhibit basal levels of autophagy, mechanical compression significantly enhances autophagic flux.

Finally, to investigate whether mechanical compression induces apoptosis, we analyzed the expression of apoptotic and anti-apoptotic markers. Cleaved caspase 3, a key marker of apoptosis, was not detected in either control or compressed cells. However, the expression of anti-apoptotic markers, Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, was elevated in compressed cells compared to control cells, suggesting that these cells resist the stress induced by mechanical compression through anti-apoptotic mechanisms (Fig. 1h).

Compression-induced autophagy impairs the efficacy of oxaliplatin in pancreatic cancer cells

After validating autophagy as a mechanism induced by mechanical stress, we next investigated its potential impact on the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapy. The first-line chemotherapy for PDAC patients is FOLFIRINOX, which is currently being tested in combination with HCQ (NCT04911816, ClinicalTrials.gov) and comprises three main chemotherapeutic agents—5-FU, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin—MIA PaCa-2 cells were compressed in the presence of each drug separately to identify how mechanical compression affects the efficacy of each agent. The concentration of each drug was selected according to IC50 values and was adjusted accordingly for each experimental condition (Suppl. Fig. 2a). Apoptotic cell death was analyzed by flow cytometry assay, with a particular focus on late apoptosis, as this stage of apoptosis provides a more accurate assessment of sustained, irreversible cell death45,46. Our results revealed that mechanical compression slightly increases the late apoptosis of untreated cells and strongly induces the apoptosis of 5FU-treated cells. The treatment of cells with irinotecan did not show any difference in the late apoptosis between control and compressed cells, while the effect of oxaliplatin was strongly delayed in compressed cells, as revealed by the lower percentage of late apoptotic cells in compressed (22.6%) relative to uncompressed cells (30.21%) (Fig. 2a). This effect was subsequently supported by a cell viability assay, where oxaliplatin decreased the viability of uncompressed cells by almost 50%, while it had no effect in cells under compression (Fig. 2b). To examine whether autophagy is induced in the oxaliplatin-treated compressed cells, we performed western blotting and observed that the LC3BII/I ratio did not change significantly between oxaliplatin-treated and untreated control and compressed cells. However, the p62 expression was markedly reduced in oxaliplatin-treated cells, suggesting enhanced autophagic degradation (Fig. 2c). To validate this observation, we analyzed the same autophagy markers in the presence of the autophagy inhibitor HCQ. In both control and compressed cells, co-treatment with oxaliplatin and HCQ resulted in an increased LC3BII/I ratio. Notably, there was a significant accumulation of p62 in compressed cells under these conditions, confirming that oxaliplatin increases autophagic degradation, with a stronger effect being observed in compressed cells (Suppl. Fig. 3a). These results were also confirmed by immunofluorescent staining of LC3B and p62, which showed increased overlap of these markers in both the untreated and oxaliplatin-treated compressed cells (Fig. 2d). Based on the delayed apoptosis of oxaliplatin-treated compressed cells, along with the increased autophagic degradation under these conditions, we next set to identify whether mechanical stress induces autophagy to promote apoptosis resistance in oxaliplatin-treated pancreatic cancer cells.

Pharmacologic inhibition of autophagy increases oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis in compressed pancreatic cancer cells

To determine whether compression impairs the efficacy of oxaliplatin due to autophagy activation, we inhibited autophagy in pancreatic cancer cells using HCQ and subsequently assessed apoptotic markers expression. Cells were compressed in the presence of 10 μΜ HCQ and/or 100 μg/ml oxaliplatin for 24 h, and it was found that the oxaliplatin-induced apoptotic cell death was impaired in response to compression as indicated by the protein levels of active caspase 3, analyzed by a multiplex apoptosis assay (Fig. 3a), supporting the results of Annexin V/PI flow cytometry analysis shown in Fig. 2a. Most importantly, the addition of HCQ in oxaliplatin-treated cells, strongly increased apoptotic cell death in both control- and compressed cells, as indicated by the expression of active Caspase 3. The expression of Bcl-xL-Bak, Mcl-1-Bak, and survivin did not significantly alter in any treatment condition. The expression of cleaved Caspase 3 was subsequently confirmed by western blotting and immunofluorescent staining, which showed that the combination of HCQ with oxaliplatin increased the apoptotic cell death in compressed cells as compared to the respective oxaliplatin-only-treated cells (Fig. 3b–d). Similar results were obtained when KPC cells were exposed to different levels of compression and treated with oxaliplatin (100 μg/mL) and HCQ (10 μΜ) (Suppl. Fig. 3b). The concentration of oxaliplatin and HCQ for KPC cells was selected according to the IC50 value and subsequent analysis of cleaved caspase 3, LC3B and p62 protein expression by western blotting (Suppl. Fig. 2b, c, e). Collectively, these results suggest that autophagy is regulated by mechanical compression to enable cells to resist oxaliplatin-induced apoptotic cell death. Moreover, the fact that compressed cells exhibited reduced apoptosis as compared to control cells under oxaliplatin treatment, suggests that a positive feedback loop is created between mechanical stress and autophagy activation, impairing the efficacy of therapeutic regimens.

A combination of HCQ with losartan increases the anti-tumor efficacy of oxaliplatin in pancreatic tumor models

Based on the above observations, we next set out to investigate whether the addition of losartan - which reduces mechanical stress levels by normalizing the TME- to HCQ and oxaliplatin treatment regimen, can improve therapeutic effects in pancreatic tumors. We pretreated mice bearing MIA PaCa-2 or KPC tumors with daily 40 mg/kg losartan (i.p.) for 3 days prior to chemotherapy administration to normalize TME and restore vessel functionality17 and until the end of the treatment protocol. At the same time, HCQ was administered at 50 mg/kg to block the autophagic process prior to the administration of chemotherapy, which was administered in two cycles of 5 mg/kg47 every three days (Fig. 4a, Suppl. Fig. 4a). We observed that the addition of HCQ or losartan increased oxaliplatin efficacy as compared to oxaliplatin monotherapy, however, their combined treatment resulted in the greatest decrease in the growth of MIA PaCa-2 tumors (Fig. 4b, c, Suppl. Fig. 4b). Although in KPC bearing mice the triple therapy did not significantly reduced tumor volume as compared to the groups treated with oxaliplatin and HCQ or losartan (Fig. 4d), the tumor mass of the triple treatment was significantly reduced for both tumor models (Fig. 4e, f). Importantly, the anti-tumor effect of the combined treatment was not mediated by an antitumor activity of HCQ or losartan, since neither their monotherapy nor their combined treatment resulted in any difference in KPC tumor volume (Suppl. Fig. 4e). Subsequent classification of mice indicated that in the combination therapy group of HCQ + losartan + oxaliplatin, all MIA PaCa-2 and KPC tumor-bearing mice responded to triple treatment (Fig. 4g, h). The classification was defined by their relative tumor volume change48. Finally, no statistically significant changes in the mouse mass of both the MIA PaCa-2- and KPC-tumor-bearing mice were observed (Suppl. Fig. 4c, d).

HCQ supports the losartan-induced normalization of the TME

Next, we set out to confirm that losartan normalized the tumor stroma by reducing collagen and hyaluronan levels and making the tumors softer, which are indications of alleviation of mechanical stresses. According to our in vitro experiments, the reduction of mechanical stresses could also block the compression-induced apoptosis resistance of pancreatic cancer cells. To study changes in the elastic properties of the tumors, we monitored tumor stiffness in mice undergoing losartan treatment with shear wave elastography (SWE) (Fig. 5a). Before the start of losartan treatment (day 17 post-implantation of MIA PaCa-2 cells) tumors from all groups exhibited no change in the average elastic modulus, which was ~25 kPa. After 3 days of treatment and just prior to the administration of the first cycle of oxaliplatin (Day 19 post-implantation), the elastic modulus values measured with SWE were significantly lower in mice treated with losartan (~15 kPa) or HCQ (~20 kPa) and even lesser with HCQ + losartan (~10 kPa) compared to the untreated tumors (~30 kPa) (Fig. 5b). The elastic modulus of these groups was also significantly reduced as compared to day 17 prior the administration of any treatment. To verify the reduction in tumor stiffness, we also assessed the hyaluronan and collagen content in these tumors, which are related directly to mechanical stress levels7,8,49 and they are targeted by losartan. Using fluorescence immunostaining, we found a significant decrease in both hyaluronan binding protein 1 (HABP1) and collagen I protein levels when both MIA PaCa-2- and KPC-bearing mice were treated with losartan, HCQ or HCQ + losartan daily (Fig. 6a–f, Suppl. Fig. 5a, b).

Given the reduction in tumor stiffness, we next reasoned that decreased vascular perfusion would be restored by losartan treatment because of vessel decompression. Indeed, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) imaging shows an increase in the tumor covered by the contrast agent in mice treated with losartan or HCQ + losartan prior to the administration of the first cycle of oxaliplatin (Fig. 5c, d). However, only the combined treatment regimen (HCQ + losartan) resulted in a significant increase in the perfusion of tumors between days 17 and 19, suggesting that HCQ could enhance the effect of losartan. This is supported by the observation that HCQ-only-treated KPC tumors exhibited increased perfusion, which was further enhanced when losartan was added to the treatment regimen (Suppl. Fig. 4f). These results indicate that the combined therapy of HCQ and losartan is more effective in improving tumor perfusion than either agent alone. Next, to verify the structural restoration of blood vessels by losartan treatment, we performed immunostaining of the endothelial cells using the anti-CD31 antibody and pericytes using anti-alpha-smooth muscle actin (aSMA) (Fig. 6g–i). We observed that administration of losartan alone or in combination with HCQ and oxaliplatin in MIA PaCa-2 and KPC tumor-bearing mice increased the pericyte coverage of the tumor vasculature that fortifies the vessel walls, as indicated by the colocalization of the a-SMA and CD31, compared to the untreated control group. Similar results were observed when HCQ and losartan were administered as monotherapies. Although HCQ alone did not significantly increase pericyte coverage, its addition to losartan treatment, significantly restored vessel structure compared to losartan alone (Suppl. Fig. 5c). In line with these, quantification of vessel open lumen fraction using CD31 staining of both MIA PaCa-2 and KPC tumor tissues, revealed that vessels were decompressed when mice were treated with oxaliplatin and losartan, with or without HCQ (Suppl. Fig. 5d, e). In fact, HCQ monotherapy tends to increase vessel decompression and improve vessel structure by increasing pericyte coverage, however only when it was combined with losartan, led to a significantly stronger effect as compared to losartan monotherapy (Suppl. Fig. 5f). Finally, when losartan or HCQ were combined with oxaliplatin, they reduced the total number of cells positive for aSMA expression with a greater difference in the triple therapy regimen (Fig. 6j, k). Since HCQ and losartan were also found to independently decrease aSMA-positive cells (Suppl. Fig. 5g), and as aSMA is the most common marker for activated fibroblasts (CAFs), these results suggest that losartan and HCQ, either administered separately or in combination, reduce the number of CAFs in the respective tumors.

HCQ effectively impairs autophagy and increases oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis in vivo

Finally, we confirmed the in vivo efficacy of HCQ to block autophagy and increase the oxaliplatin-induced cell death as observed in vitro. We performed immunofluorescent staining against LC3B and p62 in tumor tissue sections. We observed a significant reduction in the co-localization of LC3B and p62 in HCQ-treated groups, verifying the successful blockade of the autophagic process (Fig. 7a, b). Most importantly, analysis of cleaved Caspase 3 protein levels in tumor protein extracts analyzed by western blotting revealed that the combination of HCQ with losartan strongly increases the oxaliplatin-induced apoptosis in tumor cells (Fig. 7c, d). In MIA PaCa-2 tumor samples, treatment with losartan enhances oxaliplatin-induced apoptotic cell death, and the combined use of losartan with HCQ further amplifies apoptosis, with nearly double the effect. Both HCQ and losartan independently increase PARP1 cleavage; however, this effect is not evident in the triple therapy group (HCQ + Los. + Oxal.), possibly due to the time point of tumor excision. It is likely that in the triple therapy regimen, the remaining tumor cells are in earlier apoptotic stages compared to other treatment conditions. Additionally, anti-apoptotic markers are reduced across all treatments compared to the untreated control, with the most significant decrease observed in the triple therapy MIA PaCa-2 tumors. In KPC tumor samples, cleaved caspase-3 is markedly elevated in the triple therapy group, though no significant change in PARP1 cleavage is detected. Interestingly, the untreated KPC tumors show lower anti-apoptotic marker expression as compared to MIA PaCa-2 tumors, which increases with any treatment, particularly in the triple therapy group. This suggests that KPC tumor cells may be attempting to balance between apoptosis and survival. Overall, while MIA PaCa-2 and KPC models exhibit different anti-apoptotic marker expressions, these differences cannot be directly compared due to the intrinsic variation between the two models. However, our results suggest that the combination of HCQ, losartan, and oxaliplatin significantly increases caspase-3 cleavage and leads to a strong reduction in tumor growth, supporting the potential of this drug combination to overcome oxaliplatin resistance in both models. While this effect was more pronounced in KPC-bearing mice compared to MIA PaCa-2-bearing mice, our results suggest that the addition of losartan is essential for effectively blocking the autophagic process and facilitating oxaliplatin-induced cancer cell death, likely through improved perfusion that enhances HCQ delivery and efficacy.

Discussion

Mechanical stress exerts a dual role within the TME; it can directly regulate cellular responses of several cell types that are present in TME, including cancer cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts through mechanotransduction50–52, and indirectly compress the tumor vasculature, impairing the delivery of nutrients, oxygen, and therapeutic agents into the tumor53. Hence, in response to the elevated mechanical stress, cancer cells activate various adaptive mechanisms to promote their survival and proliferation29,33 and increase their migratory potential28,32,54,55. In a recent approach employing mathematical modeling, it was shown that confinement could reduce the proliferation of pancreatic tumor cells and increase drug resistance through Erk signaling activation33. To this end, we utilized our previously published data from a large proteomic assay32 to examine whether mechanical stress could be implicated in pancreatic cancer cell chemoresistance and the mechanism involved. Our analysis revealed changes in protein expression as a response to mechanical compression, which are maintained for up to 48 h. Focusing on drug resistance-related proteins, we observed an upregulation of LC3, which is one of the most common markers for autophagy activation and it has been recently shown to be triggered by compressive stress through p38 MAPK signaling activation37. In the context of chemotherapy, autophagy has been implicated in mediating resistance by facilitating the clearance of damaged organelles and promoting cell survival under stress conditions38,56. To this end, after validating the compression-induced autophagy upregulation and enhanced anti-apoptosis in pancreatic cancer cells, we next examined whether these events are implicated in chemoresistance. To test our hypothesis, we treated compressed cells with the main chemotherapeutic agents that compose FOLFIRINOX, which is currently being tested in combination with the autophagy inhibitor HCQ (NCT04911816, ClinicalTrials.gov) for the treatment of PDAC patients. Focusing on late apoptosis, as it reflects the execution phase where cells are undergoing irreversible changes leading to cell death45,46, we revealed that compressed cells exhibited a slight increase in late apoptosis as compared to control cells. However, the percentage of late apoptotic cells was relatively low (9% in control compared to 13.5% in compressed cells) compared to a total number of cells, which may be below the detection threshold of cleaved caspase-3 in western blotting. In addition, we revealed that oxaliplatin treatment was significantly impaired in inducing late apoptosis in compressed cells compared to other treatments, as shown by the fold change in the percentage of late apoptotic cells (3-fold increase in late apoptosis of oxaliplatin-treated control cells compared to 1.7-fold increase in oxaliplatin-treated compressed cells, Fig. 2). This finding suggests that compression impacts the ability of oxaliplatin to drive cells towards terminal apoptosis, while under these conditions, autophagy was shown to be strongly upregulated. While oxaliplatin also increases autophagy in non-compressed cells, this could reflect that the cells might require autophagy to resist the cytotoxic effects of oxaliplatin consistent with previous studies employing other tumor types56,57. However, in compressed cells where autophagy levels are elevated prior to the administration of any treatment, the efficacy of oxaliplatin in inducing cell death is significantly impaired. This is likely due to the increased autophagic flux and anti-apoptosis driven by compression, which enhances cellular resistance to cell death and thus reduces the effectiveness of oxaliplatin. Notably, the inhibition of autophagy by HCQ, results in a dramatic increase in cleaved caspase-3 levels (3-fold increase of cleaved caspase-3 in compressed compared to 1.3-fold in uncompressed cells). These results suggest that autophagy may have a dual role; while basal levels of autophagy could be necessary for several cellular processes, under stress conditions, such as chemotherapy or mechanical compression, autophagy may serve as a protective mechanism against stress-induced cell death. Indeed, according to the pertinent literature, autophagy has been shown to promote oxaliplatin resistance in colorectal cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma58,59, while in pancreatic cancer, it has been shown that blocking endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-induced autophagy activation using chloroquine, increased gemcitabine efficacy both in vitro and in vivo60. Thus, our results provide compelling evidence that mechanical stress serves as a critical inducer of autophagy, which in turn functions as a protective mechanism promoting apoptosis resistance.

Based on the in vitro results and pertinent literature37, a positive feedback loop could be developed between mechanical stresses and autophagy that could impede the mechanism of action of HCQ in pancreatic cancer. Thus, we hypothesized that combined treatment of HCQ and oxaliplatin would be supported by a stress-alleviation strategy, such as the use of losartan. Apart from its effect on restoring the biomechanical properties of TME, losartan was shown to regulate autophagy in vascular smooth muscle cells to promote their hypertrophy61 and was combined with HCQ in patients with nephropathy62. Hence, we next set to examine the effect of a therapeutic strategy combining oxaliplatin with HCQ and losartan in pancreatic tumor models. We observed that oxaliplatin monotherapy did not significantly reduce tumor volume, however the addition of HCQ or losartan greatly improved its therapeutic efficacy. Most importantly, their combined treatment resulted in the strongest effect on tumor growth of MIA PaCa-2 tumors. Using ultrasound imaging, we examined their effect on TME normalization and observed that administration of losartan alone or in combination with HCQ significantly reduced the elastic modulus and improved the perfusion of MIA PaCa-2 tumors, as shown in previous studies17. We also observed that pretreatment with HCQ alone prior to the administration of oxaliplatin showed a significant reduction in the elastic modulus and an increasing trend in perfusion, with a stronger effect in KPC tumors. These results are in accordance with previous studies supporting that HCQ can be used for the treatment of skin fibrosis diseases63 or to reduce tumor stroma fibrosis in pancreatic tumors64. This was suggested to be mediated through the induction of fibroblast cell death, which is the major contributor to collagen production and, hence, to tumor tissue fibrosis64. These data could be an explanation for the reduction of aSMA positive cells observed in HCQ-treated tumor sections. Also HCQ was shown to be implicated in vessel normalization, independent from its role in autophagy, further supporting the increasing trend of perfusion in HCQ-treated tumors65. These results support a currently active phase I clinical trial employing losartan, HCQ, and paricalcitol as three stroma-modifying drugs that could enhance the therapeutic effect of chemo- and radio-therapy in PDAC patients (NCT05365893). However, while HCQ monotherapy was shown to positively impact vessel function, further research is needed to determine whether this effect is linked to autophagy inhibition in stromal or cancer cells of pancreatic tumors. Finally, we validated the efficacy of HCQ to block autophagy as revealed by a reduction in the co-localization of LC3B and p62 on tumor tissue sections. Regarding oxaliplatin efficacy, we observed an increase in apoptosis when HCQ was administered, however the strongest effect was observed when losartan was added to the therapeutic regimen. These findings highlight the importance of alleviating mechanical stress levels, not only to increase drug delivery but also to block the potential positive feedback loop between mechanical stresses and intracellular mechanisms that lead to chemoresistance.

In conclusion, our study elucidates the role of mechanical stress-induced autophagy in mediating chemotherapy resistance in pancreatic cancer and provides preclinical evidence supporting the therapeutic efficacy of targeting autophagy and reducing mechanical stress levels to enhance chemotherapy response (Fig. 7e). However, our study is limited by the short-term observations on the effects of the combined treatment regimen. Long-term consequences, such as tumor recurrence and overall survival studies, would be important areas for future research to further solidify our findings. Finally, further investigation into the underlying molecular mechanisms of how mechanical stresses regulate chemoresistance and clinical validation of these findings are warranted to translate these findings into improved therapeutic strategies for pancreatic cancer patients.

Methods

Cell culture

MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1, and Pan02 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). KRasG12D, Trp53R172H, and Pdx-1Cre (KPC) cells were purchased from CancerTools.org. Cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 1% antibiotic/antimycotic, and incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Cells were tested for mycoplasma prior to the experiments.

Animal care and mouse models

Xenograft pancreatic tumor models were generated by subcutaneous implantation of 2.5 × 106 MiaPaCa-2 cells resuspended in 40 μl of serum-free medium into 10-week-old NOD/ SCID mice. For the pancreatic syngeneic tumor model, 8-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were first implanted subcutaneously with 1 × 106 KPC cells and allowed to generate tumors for 12 days. When tumors became palpable, they were collected, and each tumor was cut into 1–3 mm3 pieces using a surgical blade. Next, laparotomy was performed, and the pancreas was gently excised. One tumor piece was sutured into the pancreas of each mouse using a 7-0 prolene stitch. After tumor implantation, the pancreas was carefully returned to the peritoneal cavity, and both the abdomen and the skin were closed with a 6-0 and 4-0 prolene suture, respectively. An equal number of male and female mice was employed, and we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

The experiments were conducted in accordance with the animal welfare regulations and guidelines of the European Union (European Directive 2010/63/EE and Cyprus Legislation for the protection and welfare of animals, Laws 1994–2013) under a license acquired and approved (CY/EXP/PR.L14/2019, CY/EXP/PR.L15/2019, CY/EXP/PR.L03/2020) by the Cyprus Veterinary Services committee, the Cyprus national authority for monitoring the welfare of animals in research.

The inhibitory effect of each treatment on primary tumor growth was monitored by tumor volume measurements. For the ectopic, xenograft model, planar dimensions (x, y) of the tumor were measured every 2–3 days using a digital caliper, and the tumor volume was estimated from the volume of an ellipsoid and assuming that the third dimension, z, is equal to √xy. The maximal tumor burden of 1200 mm3, as approved by the ethics committee, was not exceeded. For the orthotopic, syngeneic tumor model, ultrasound imaging (B mode) was performed to measure the dimensions (x,y,z) and the tumor volume was estimated from the volume of an ellipsoid.

Ultrasound shear wave elastography and contrast-enhanced ultrasound

Assessment of tumor elastic modulus and perfusion was measured via shear wave elastography (SWE) and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), respectively, in MIA PaCa-2 bearing mice. For SWE, the Philips EPIQ Elite scanner with the eL18-4 linear array transducer was employed22,23,66–69. The settings that were used were: frequency 10 MHz, power 52%, B-mode gain 22 dB, dynamic range 62 dB. SWE was performed at two different planes for each tumor and the average value of both planes was used for our analysis. To confirm the efficacy of losartan, ultrasound elastography measurements occurred before losartan and HCQ treatments (day 17 for MIA PaCa-2 and day 5 for KPC), to determine the baseline level of tissue stiffness, and after three days of treatment, before the 1st chemotherapy cycle.

For CEUS, the same EPIQ Elite system was employed with the linear array transducer L12-5. Tumor perfusion was assessed by bolus injection of 8 μl Sonovue/Lumason (in the USA) contrast agent (Bracco, Geneva, Switzerland). Before each ultrasound application, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of Avertin (200 mg/kg). Power modulation nonlinear imaging at 4 MHz and with a mechanical index of 0.06 in a contrast side-by-side mode (nonlinear bubble image and conventional fundamental) was used. The focus was placed slightly below the tumor, and the gain in the bubble image was set such that there was a subtle noise level across the image. Image analysis was performed offline with quantification analysis software (QLAB, Phillips Medical Systems). For the analysis, we calculated the normalized perfused area at the time when the image intensity reaches its peak, as the number of pixels with microbubble signals divided by the total number of pixels of the tumor ROI.

Animal treatment protocols

Losartan (40 mg/kg17, Selleckchem, #S1359) and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) (50 mg/kg60,70,71), Selleckchem, # S4430) were administered daily by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection when tumors reached an average size of ~100 mm3 from day 17 in MIA PaCa-2 and ~50 mm3 from day 5 in KPC post-implantation. Two cycles of oxaliplatin (5 mg/kg47,72) (AqVida GmbH, Germany) were administered by intravenous (i.v.) injection, starting 3 days post-Losartan and HCQ treatment. Control mice were treated with PBS.

In vitro application of mechanical compression and cell treatments

For the application of a defined and controlled compression on cancer cells, our previously described transmembrane pressure device was employed27. In brief, 24 h prior to compression, cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells in the upper chamber of a 24 mm diameter transwell insert with 0.4 μm pores (Greiner Bio-one). A piston with adjustable weight was added on the top of a cell monolayer that was covered with a 2% agarose cushion and placed in a transwell insert, to apply 4 mmHg of compressive stress on MIA PaCa-2 or 0-, 2-, 4-, 8 mmHg on KPC cells for 16-, 24-, or 48 h. Control cells were covered with an agarose cushion only. In order to examine the effect of FOLFIRINOX components, (5-FU, Irinotecan, and Oxaliplatin) on the viability of pancreatic cancer cells under mechanical compression, MIA PaCa-2 cells were seeded in DMEM-containing transwell inserts and then were subjected to compression in the presence of 20 μg/mL 5FU, 60 μg/mL irinotecan, and 100 μg/mL oxaliplatin for 24 h. Control cells were treated with an equal volume of solvent. The concentration of each drug was selected according to the IC50 curves (Suppl. Fig. 2), and it was adjusted according to cell concentration and the duration of each experiment as indicated in each figure caption. For optimal concentration and effect of HCQ in vitro, MIA PaCa-2 and KPC cells were treated with increasing concentrations of HCQ for 24 h. Then, protein extraction and western blotting were performed for the analysis of LC3B and p62 expression. For Oxaliplatin + HCQ co-treatments, MIA PaCa-2 and KPC cells were compressed for 24 h in the presence of 10 μΜ HCQ and 100 μg/mL oxaliplatin.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cancer cells using Trizol (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was then transcribed to cDNA using Superscript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen), and cDNA (1:10) was employed for quantification of gene expression by real-time PCR using β-actin as reference gene. All primers used are shown in Suppl. Table 1. Quantification of relative gene expression was performed using the ΔΔCt method, where data were log-transformed and analyzed using standard methodology.

Protein extraction and immunoblotting

Whole protein cell lysates were extracted using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Tumor tissues were then sonicated to allow efficient protein extraction. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Pierce), and about 20–40 μg of protein were separated on a 10 or 12% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane which was then blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-Tween 20 buffer and incubated with anti-LC3B (#ab51520, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-p62 (#ab56416, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-Caspase 3 (#ab13847, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-Bcl2 (#ab182858, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-Bcl-xL (#ab32370, Abcam, 1:1000) and anti-PARP1 (#ab191217, Abcam, 1:1000). Antibodies against β-tubulin (#E7-S, DSHB, 1:5000), GAPDH (#2118, Cell Signaling) or β-actin (#A2228, Merck, 1:5000) were used as loading controls. Secondary antibodies (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L), #65-6120, Invitrogen; m-IgGk BP-HRP, #sc-516102, Santa Cruz) were detected with enhanced chemiluminescent system (#34095, Pierce) using the Alliance gel documentation system (Uvitec, Cambridge). Images were analyzed in ImageJ. Color was discarded, and images were converted to grayscale. In some cases, contrast was used in the entire blot to enhance image clarity. No other image manipulation was performed. Uncropped images of Western Blotting used in this article can be found in Suppl. Material.

Cell viability assay (Resazurin)

Cancer cells were subjected to a cell viability test post-compression and/or treatment using Resazurin reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. % Difference in Resazurin reduction was calculated in triplicates for each condition. The data from the control, (untreated and uncompressed) group were considered to represent 100% cell viability, and the viability of the other groups was evaluated relative to this benchmark. In some cases, relative fold change was calculated. Optical density was read with a Microplate Reader (Rayto Life and Analytical Sciences Co., China) at a wavelength of 490 nm.

IC50 of chemotherapeutic drugs

MIA PaCa-2 and KPC cells were seeded in 96-well culture plates at a density of 10,000 cells/well. After a 48-h incubation period, a graded concentration of 5FU (BENDA), Irinotecan (Venus Pharma GmbH) and oxaliplatin were added to each well containing MIA PaCa-2 cells and incubated for 48 h followed by Resazurin assay. For KPC cells, only oxaliplatin was added. Viability as a function of log (drug concentration) was fit to a 4-parameter logistic model in GraphPad Prism 10.0 to determine the IC50 of each drug. The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values represented drug concentrations that decreased the mean absorbance at 490 nm to 50% of that in the untreated control well.

Annexin V/PI flow cytometry

The percentage of cells actively undergoing apoptosis was determined by flow cytometry using an Annexin V/PI assay kit (#640914, BioLegend) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were washed with PBS and then harvested with trypsin and counted. After counting, cells were then resuspended in a binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/ml. Next, 5 μl of Annexin V and 10 μl of PI were added, and the cells were incubated at room temperature for at least 15 min in the dark. After incubation, the percentage of apoptotic cells was analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSLyric, BD Biosciences). The gating strategy for Annexin V+ and PI+ cells is shown in Suppl. Figure 3c.

Analysis of apoptotic cell death via multiplex proteomic assay

Compressed- and/or treated cells were washed with cold sterile PBS, centrifuged, and resuspended in LDB (lysate dilution buffer) containing 1× HALT protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 14,000 rpm. Protein quantification was performed using BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Apoptotic markers were investigated using Bio-Plex Pro RBM Apoptosis Assays panel 3 (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the kit’s manufacturer. In brief, protein samples were mixed with capture beads and incubated for 1 h. After washing, the detection antibodies were added, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h. The streptavidin-PE was subsequently added, and the sample was incubated for another 30 min. Samples were then washed and analyzed using the Bio-Plex® 200 instrument (BioRad). Data were analyzed with Bio-Plex Manager software (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) and then were illustrated in a heatmap representing the mean fluorescent intensity values using GraphPad Prism 10.0.

RPPA data analysis

The Reverse Phase Protein Array (RPPA) analysis was conducted using MIA PaCa-2 cells subjected to either 0.0 or 4.0 mmHg pressure for 16 or 48 h, resulting in four distinct experimental groups (0 mmHg and 16 h, 0 mmHg and 48 h, 4 mmHg and 16 h, and 4 mmHg and 48 h). Each experimental condition was replicated three times, generating a total of 12 observations per condition, encompassing measurements for 425 different proteins. To unravel the impact of various experimental conditions, including different pressure loads and durations, all RPPA data were collated for subsequent analysis. Principal components analysis (PCA) was employed to project the dataset into a 3D space defined by the first three principal components. This approach enabled visualization of the overall dataset and facilitated an exploration of the effects associated with different experimental conditions. Furthermore, PCA was instrumental in identifying proteins exhibiting the highest variation within the dataset, thereby guiding the selection of key candidates for further analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining of cells

After proper compression and treatment, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized in PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100, and blocked with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 1% BSA. Cells were then stained with anti-LC3B (#NB 100–220, Novus Biologicals, 1:200), anti-p62 (#00008878-M01, Novus Biologicals, 1:400) or anti-cleaved Caspase 3 (#9661S, Cell Signaling), 1:100), diluted in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Alexa 488-donkey anti-mouse (#20014-1, Biotium) or Alexa 555-donkey anti-rabbit (#20033, Biotium) antibodies were used as secondary antibodies.

Fluorescence immunohistochemistry of tumor tissues

Tumors were removed, washed twice in 1× PBS for 10 min, and incubated with 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. The fixative was aspirated, and samples were washed twice in 1× PBS for 10 min. Tissue samples were dehydrated in successive alcohol steps and xylene followed by paraffin embedding. Serial sections (7 μm) of paraffin-embedded tissues were produced using the microtome (Accu-Cut SRM 200 Rotary Microtome, SAKURA), flattened out into the water and allowed to dry overnight at 37 °C. Sections were then deparaffinized and rehydrated as routinely processed for histology.

For hyaluronan detection, tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval (microwave heat treatment with Trisodium Citrate, pH 6.0, for 20 min), washed with 1× TBS-0.025% Tween 20 (TBS-T), and incubated in blocking solution (PBS containing 15% FBS, 1.5% BSA and 0.3% Tween 20) for 2 h at room temperature (RT). For murine tumor tissues, sections were incubated with endogenous blocking goat mouse IgG (#ab6668, Abcam) to block the endogenous IgG prior to primary antibody incubation. Next, slides were incubated with anti-biotinylated hyaluronan binding protein (b-HABP) (AMS.HKD-BC41, amsbio 1:100) overnight at 4 °C. Hyaluronan was detected following incubation with streptavidin-FITC conjugate (SA1001, Invitrogen 1:1000) and nuclei with DAPI stain (Sigma, 1:100 of 1 mg/ml stock) at RT for 2 h in the dark. For Collagen I detection, tumor sections were immunostained with rabbit anti-Collagen I (ab21286, Abcam, 1:100), for autophagy examination, with the anti-LC3B (#NB 100-220, Novus Biologicals, 1:200) and anti-p62 (#00008878-M01, Novus Biologicals, 1:400) primary antibodies, while for the pericyte coverage examination, with the anti-a-SMA (#ab7817, Abcam 1:100) and anti-CD31 (#ab182981, Abcam, 1:200) primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Next day, slides were washed with 1× PBS-0.025% Tween 20 (PBS-T) and incubated with Alexa Fluor-555 anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (#20033, Biotium, 1:400) or Alexa 488-donkey anti-mouse (#20014-1, Biotium, 1:400) secondary antibodies along with DAPI stain for 2 h at room temperature (RT) in the dark. Sections were then mounted on microscope slides using the ProLong gold antifade mountant (Invitrogen) and covered with a glass coverslip.

Image acquisition and analysis

All Immunofluorescence images were acquired with a STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope (Leica, Germany) using the Leica Application Suite X software. The same settings were applied in the image acquisition across the samples of various treatment groups. The images were analyzed using custom and built-in algorithms in MATLAB (MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). The quantification of the area fraction of each fluorescence signal was obtained as the ratio of the number of positive pixels to the number of DAPI-positive or total image pixels. For LC3B puncta, the total number of high-intensity dots per field was quantified and divided by the total number of cells. Vessel lumen was calculated from the CD31 images based on previous work17,22. Specifically, the procedure of the identification of the open lumen vessels was automated in the MATLAB code. The CD31+ staining that forms a loop was considered to be a vessel and the ratio of the long per short axis of a cross-section fit to an oval was taken, when this ratio was less than 3 the vessel considered open lumen vessel.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Two-way analyses of variants (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s- or Sidak’s multiple comparison, ordinary one-way ANOVA, or unpaired t-test were used as indicated in each figure legend. Only statistically significant differences, along with the exact P values, are displayed in the figures. A P value of less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sample size and repetition of each experiment are indicated in corresponding figure captions. Data are presented as means with standard errors.
