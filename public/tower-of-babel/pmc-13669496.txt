Strategic driving of the cancer-immunity cycle in HCC: roles of immune, molecular-targeted, and locoregional therapies
Authors: Hideki Iwamoto, Hironori Koga, Takumi Kawaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treatment has entered the immunotherapy era, characterized by multiple systemic and locoregional therapeutic options. Understanding how each modality modulates the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is essential to optimize treatment strategies. The cancer-immunity cycle provides a conceptual basis for antitumor immunity, consisting of a priming phase involving antigen release and T cell activation, and an effector phase characterized by cytotoxic T cell-mediated tumor elimination. Immune checkpoint inhibitor-based combinations play a central role in HCC treatment. Anti-programmed death ligand-1 plus anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy primarily activates the effector phase by reducing regulatory T cells and macrophages and enhancing cytotoxic T cell infiltration. In contrast, cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 blockade-based regimens primarily promote the priming phase by activating CD4+ T cells and dendritic cells. Molecular-targeted agents also modulate the TIME. VEGF inhibition commonly reduces immunosuppressive cell populations, lenvatinib enhances cytotoxic T cell activation through fibroblast growth factor inhibition, and cabozantinib promotes dendritic cell-mediated immune activation via AXL inhibition. Locoregional therapies, including transarterial therapies, stereotactic body radiotherapy, and transarterial radioembolization with yttrium 90, induce tumor injury, release tumor-associated antigens, and activate inflammatory signaling. In this review, we propose the concept of “strategic driving of the cancer-immunity cycle,” in which treatment selection and sequencing are guided by their ability to activate distinct phases of the cycle. This concept may provide a rational strategy to maximize antitumor efficacy in HCC.

INTRODUCTION

Before the approval of sorafenib in 2008, effective systemic therapy options for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) were extremely limited. Until then, hepatic resection and radiofrequency ablation had been used as curative treatments, whereas transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy (HAIC), and radiation therapy had served as locoregional treatments according to tumor stage and liver function. The introduction of sorafenib established the first systemic therapy to demonstrate a survival benefit for unresectable HCC and initiated a new treatment paradigm.1,2

Subsequently, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as regorafenib, lenvatinib, and cabozantinib expanded systemic therapy options by targeting tumor angiogenesis, while immune-based combinations including atezolizumab plus bevacizumab (Atez+Bev), durvalumab plus tremelimumab (STRIDE), and nivolumab plus ipilimumab (Nivo+Ipi) transformed HCC management into the immunotherapy era, characterized by increasingly diverse therapeutic options with distinct mechanisms of action. 3

Combination immunotherapy is now established as a first-line standard of care for HCC, and increasing attention has been directed toward the cancer-immunity cycle, which is central to achieving effective antitumor immunity.4,5 The cycle consists of a priming phase (step 1 to 3), encompassing antigen release, uptake and presentation by antigen-presenting cells, and T cell activation, followed by an effector phase (step 4 to 7), involving trafficking of cytotoxic T cells to the tumor, their infiltration, recognition of malignant cells, and immunological elimination.6

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) plays a critical role in regulating whether this cycle functions effectively. The balance between immune-active and immunosuppressive components determines it. An immune-cold phenotype, dominated by immunosuppressive cells, is typical in HCC, whereas an immune-hot phenotype, characterized by enriched immune-active cells, can sustain the cancer-immunity cycle.7 Recent studies have suggested that acquired resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy may involve a transition from an initially immune-hot TIME to a cold state, reflecting adaptive immune evasion. 8 Converting a cold TIME into a hot state and reactivating the cancer-immunity cycle is therefore essential for optimizing immunotherapy outcomes.9 To improve clarity, the seven steps of the cancer-immunity cycle, the main immune cells involved in each step, and related therapeutic modalities in HCC are summarized in Table 1.

The current immunotherapy era poses a fundamental clinical challenge, as the increasing number of systemic agents and the availability of various locoregional approaches demand more complex treatment decisions. Clinicians need to consider the optimal sequence of systemic therapies and determine the appropriate timing for incorporating locoregional therapies to achieve the best therapeutic strategy. To address this challenge, we propose a perspective of strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle, in which subsequent treatment choices are guided by how each modality can reactivate or sustain the cancer-immunity cycle. This review discusses treatment strategies aimed at maximizing antitumor efficacy from this perspective.

STRATEGIC DRIVING OF CANCER-IMMUNITY CYCLE BY DISTINCT ICI-BASED COMBINATIONS

Overview of current ICI-based regimens

Current ICI-based regimens for HCC are broadly classified into two categories. One is the combination of an ICI with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) blockade, represented by Atez+Bev, and the other is a cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4)-blockade-based regimen, such as the STRIDE regimen and Nivo+Ipi.10,11 Both have now become standard first-line options and are central to current systemic therapy.

Both regimens activate antitumor immunity, inducing an immune-hot TIME characterized by increased infiltration of CD8+ T cells and enhanced immune effector activity, including granzyme B-positive cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells. However, the mechanisms by which they drive this immune activation are distinct, reflecting different pathways of TIME modulation (Fig. 1).

Effector phase activation through combined VEGF and PD-L1 blockade

VEGF signaling has a profound immunosuppressive role within the TIME. VEGF-A promotes the recruitment and expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and promotes M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, thereby contributing to immune evasion.12 Voron et al.12 demonstrated that VEGF-A upregulates exhaustion markers, such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), TIM-3, and CTLA-4, on CD8+ T cells, thereby directly suppressing their effector functions.

We also evaluated the TIME in a syngeneic HCC mouse model treated with Atez+Bev in our recent research.13 Consistent with these mechanistic insights, Atez+Bev markedly reduced intratumoral Tregs and F4/80+ macrophages, and significantly increased CD8+ T cells and granzyme B-positive effector cells.

VEGF blockade also normalizes tumor vasculature, alleviating endothelial cell anergy and improving lymphocyte trafficking.14 These immunological and vascular changes collectively reprogram the TIME toward an immune-hot state and activate the effector phase of the cancer-immunity cycle by promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity.

Priming phase activation through CTLA-4 blockade-based regimens

In contrast, CTLA-4 blockade primarily acts on the priming phase of the cancer-immunity cycle. CTLA-4 competes with CD28 for binding to CD80/86 on antigen-presenting cells, thereby suppressing early T cell activation.15 Blockade of CT-LA-4 relieves this inhibition, enhancing co-stimulatory signaling and promoting dendritic cell maturation.

In our syngeneic HCC mouse model, a CTLA-4 blockade-based regimen increased CD4+ helper T cells and dendritic cells, consistent with these mechanistic findings.13 This activation of CD4+ T cells licenses dendritic cells and induces interleukin (IL)-21 production, which enhances CD8+ T cell priming and differentiation into cytotoxic effectors.16 These immunological changes underlie the robust CD8+ T cell infiltration and enhanced granzyme B expression observed with CTLA-4 blockade-based regimens.

Triplet therapy combining CTLA-4, VEGF, and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade to drive the cancer-immunity cycle

Atez+Bev predominantly activates the effector phase by suppressing immunosuppressive cell populations and promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration, whereas CTLA-4 blockade-based regimens activate the priming phase through activation of CD4+ T cells and dendritic cells. From the perspective of the cancer-immunity cycle, combining these two approaches can appropriately drive both the priming and effector phases, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity. Therefore, triplet therapy, defined as the combination of VEGF, CTLA-4, and PD-1/programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) blockade, can effectively drive both the priming and effector phases of the cancer-immunity cycle, achieving more com-prehensive antitumor immune activation.

In our recent report,13 we demonstrated that the sequential use of a PD-L1 plus VEGF blockade regimen and a CTLA-4 blockade-based regimen in a syngeneic HCC mouse model resulted in significantly greater infiltration of CD8+ T cells and higher granzyme B expression than either treatment alone. This enhanced immune activation appeared to arise from complementary effects on the TIME, as the limited priming activity of the PD-L1 plus VEGF blockade regimen and the relatively weak reduction of immunosuppressive cells with the CTLA-4 blockade-based regimen were mutually compensated, thereby supporting the concept of triplet therapy.

Clinical validation of this strategy is already underway. The TRIPLET-HCC trial (NCT05665348) is currently evaluating Atez+Bev combined with ipilimumab in the first-line setting for advanced HCC, aiming to determine whether simultaneous activation of the priming and effector phases can translate into improved survival outcomes.17

One question raised by this strategy is the optimal sequence of administration. In theory, initiating therapy with CTLA-4 blockade may be preferable to prime the immune system before effector activation. However, in our study, no significant differences in immune activation were observed between CTLA-4 first and Atez+Bev first sequences.13 This lack of difference may be explained by the long half-lives of ICIs. Their immunological effects likely persist across treatment periods and can attenuate sequence-specific differences.

One important implication of these findings is that distinct ICI-based regimens exert complementary effects on the TIME, with PD-L1 plus VEGF blockade primarily activating the effector phase and CTLA-4 blockade-based regimen enhancing the priming phase. Integrating these approaches can therefore drive the cancer-immunity cycle more effectively, providing a strong rationale for triplet therapy as a strategy to maximize antitumor immunity in HCC.

Building on these findings, several ongoing trials are further evaluating CTLA-4-containing triplet strategies in advanced HCC. ARTEMIDE-HCC01 is a phase III randomized study evaluating rilvegostomig, a PD-1/TIGIT bispecific antibody, in combination with bevacizumab with or without tremelimumab as first-line treatment for advanced HCC (NCT06921785). ROSETTA HCC-206 is an open-label, multicenter, randomized phase 1/2 study evaluating pumitamig alone or in combination with ipilimumab as first-line treatment for advanced or unresectable HCC (NCT07291076). These ongoing studies reflect continued interest in incorporating CTLA-4 blockade into next-generation combination strategies to enhance antitumor immunity in HCC.

STRATEGIC DRIVING OF CANCER-IMMUNITY CYCLE BY MOLECULAR-TARGETED AGENTS

Common TIME modulation through VEGF inhibition

VEGF inhibition constitutes the fundamental immunological basis shared by molecular-targeted agents that strategically drive the cancer-immunity cycle in HCC. Sorafenib, regorafenib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib, and the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR2) monoclonal antibody ramucirumab share a common anti-angiogenic effect, resulting in tumor growth suppression. Increasing attention has been directed to the immunomodulatory effects of molecular-targeted agents in addition to their anti-angiogenic activity.18

Recently, we systematically evaluated TIME alterations induced by sorafenib, regorafenib, lenvatinib, cabozantinib, and ramucirumab in preclinical HCC models (Fig. 1). Across all five agents, a consistent reduction in Tregs and F4/80+ macrophages was observed.19 These drugs share VEGF inhibition as a common pharmacological profile. VEGF signaling plays a pivotal role in maintaining an immune-cold TIME by promoting Treg recruitment and M2 polarization of tumor-associated macrophages. Therefore, inhibition of VEGF signaling consistently reduces Tregs and F4/80+ macrophages. This shift alleviates immunosuppressive pressure and primes the TIME for immune activation.

In our preclinical study, the conversion to an immune-hot TIME, characterized by enhanced T cell infiltration and immune activation, is not uniformly achieved across all five agents. The magnitude and qualitative nature of these immunological changes differ according to the additional signaling pathways targeted by each drug, as described in the following sections.

Lenvatinib-driven effector phase activation

Lenvatinib exerts unique immunomodulatory effects beyond its VEGF inhibition, strategically driving the cancer-immunity cycle at the effector phase. In addition to blocking VEGF receptors, lenvatinib inhibits fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling, which plays a critical role in shaping the TIME (Fig. 1). FGF-driven signaling has been implicated in maintaining an immune-cold TIME by sustaining tumor-associated macrophages and promoting Treg accumulation.20 Its blockade therefore provides a dual benefit of anti-angiogenic and direct immune-modulating effects.

Preclinical and clinical findings have demonstrated that lenvatinib markedly enhances CD8+ cytotoxic T cell infiltration and activation within the TIME.21 Our preclinical HCC models revealed significant increases in CD8+ T cells and granzyme B-positive effector cells following lenvatinib administration, and similar immunological changes were reproduced by a specific FGF receptor inhibitor, confirming the involvement of FGF signaling blockade in these effects.19 Clinical evidence supports these preclinical observations. Yamada et al.22 reported an increased population of granzyme K-positive CD8+ T cells in lenvatinib-treated HCC tissues, suggesting robust effector T cell activation mediated by CXCL9 induction. In addition, Yamauchi et al.23 demonstrated that lenvatinib reduced immunosuppressive infiltrates, including Tregs, and thereby enhanced antitumor immunity.

Collectively, these findings highlight lenvatinib as a molecular-targeted agent that not only alleviates immunosuppressive pressure but also directly drives the effector phase of the cancer-immunity cycle by facilitating cytotoxic T cell recruitment and activation, distinguishing it from other VEGF-targeted therapies.

Cabozantinib-driven priming phase activation

Cabozantinib exhibits unique immunomodulatory effects within the TIME. Cabozantinib inhibits VEGFR2, MET, and AXL; among these, AXL plays a crucial role in immune modulation. AXL and MET signaling have been implicated in HCC progression, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, invasion, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and resistance to antiangiogenic therapy.24 AXL signaling maintains an immune-cold TIME by enhancing Treg activity, inducing immunosuppressive cytokine secretion, and impairing dendritic cell differentiation. Inhibition of AXL reduces immunosuppressive cells and restores antigen-presenting cell function, facilitating T cell activation.25

In our preclinical HCC models, cabozantinib significantly increased intratumoral dendritic cells, a change not observed with other VEGF-targeted agents.19 These findings indicate that cabozantinib promotes dendritic cell recruitment and maturation, thereby strengthening early immune activation corresponding to the priming phase of the cancer-immunity cycle (Fig. 1).

Tanaka et al.26 reported that PD-L1 and AXL are co-localized in HCC cells and that anti-PD-L1 antibody treatment can paradoxically activate AXL signaling, promoting tumor proliferation and immune evasion. Additionally, AXL inhibition has been shown to induce molecular changes favorable to an immune-hot TIME, including increased expression of immune-activating molecules, such as interferon-γ, and decreased levels of immunosuppressive cytokines, such as transforming growth factor-β and IL-10.27

As a whole, cabozantinib reprograms the TIME by increasing dendritic cells, improving antigen presentation, and suppressing AXL-mediated immunosuppression, thereby driving the cancer-immunity cycle by reinforcing early immune activation.

STRATEGIC DRIVING OF CANCER-IMMUNITY CYCLE BY LOCOREGIONAL TREATMENTS

Role of TACE in the cancer-immunity cycle

Among the various locoregional treatments for HCC, transarterial therapy is particularly suitable for driving the cancer-immunity cycle because it is established as a standard treatment for intermediate and advanced stages and its treatment intensity can be flexibly adjusted, making it appropriate for combination strategies. 28 TACE effectively initiates step 1 of the cancer-immunity cycle by inducing tumor-associated antigen release through tumor necrosis.29

Recent studies have suggested that TACE contributes to the conversion of an immune-cold TIME into an immune-hot TIME by enhancing tumor-antigen release and triggering inflammatory signaling (Fig. 2). Tumor necrosis after TACE induces the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and heat shock proteins (HSP70 and HSP90), which activate and mature dendritic cells, thereby facilitating antigen presentation.30 These events are accompanied by increased production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α, which promote the recruitment of effector T cells into the tumor. Clinical observations are consistent with these molecular findings. Increased infiltration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and PD-1+ CD8+ T cells, along with decreased regulatory T cell populations, have been demonstrated in HCC tissues after TACE, and significantly higher PD-1 and PD-L1 expression was also reported compared with untreated controls.31

Altogether, TACE drives the cancer-immunity cycle primarily through step 1 by releasing tumor-associated antigens and inducing inflammatory signaling, thereby remodeling the TIME toward an immunogenic state and establishing a foundation for enhanced responsiveness to subsequent immunotherapies.

Recent multicenter clinical data further support the clinical relevance of adding intra-arterial therapy to immunotherapy in patients with unresectable HCC. In a Taiwan multicenter cohort study, the addition of intra-arterial therapy, including TACE and HAIC, to first-line immunotherapy was associated with a higher objective response rate, longer duration of response, and improved overall survival (OS) compared with immunotherapy alone.32 After propensity score matching, the objective response rate was 46.7% in the intra-arterial therapy group and 26.3% in the immunotherapy-alone group, and the median duration of response was 19.0 weeks and 11.0 weeks, respectively. The addition of intra-arterial therapy was also independently associated with improved OS. Because TACE is a major intra-arterial treatment for HCC, these findings support the rationale for integrating TACE with ICI-based therapy.

Role of HAIC in the cancer-immunity cycle

HAIC modulates the TIME and may have the potential to activate antitumor immune responses.33 Our recent work has provided additional insight into the immunological effects of HAIC. We developed a modified HAIC regimen, New FP, which means consisting of continuous arterial infusion of 5-fluorouracil combined with ethiodized oil (lipiodol)-suspended cisplatin. The mild embolic effect of ethiodized oil leads to partial arterial flow blockade and extensive tumor necrosis, enhancing tumor-antigen release. Mechanistic analyses revealed significant upregulation of cancer-associated antigen marker major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I and PD-L1 expression, accompanied by increased infiltration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in New FP-treated tumors.34 New FP therapy increased serum HMGB1, a representative DAMP, and its elevation correlated with longer progression-free survival (PFS), indicating effective induction of step 1 in the cancer-immunity cycle.

The clinical significance of these immunological changes was also demonstrated. The efficacy of Atez+Bev was significantly improved in patients who received New FP as a prior treatment, with objective response rates markedly higher than in those without New FP pretreatment. This finding indicates that HAIC, particularly New FP, may prime the TIME to enhance the efficacy of subsequent ICIs.

In summary, these findings suggest that HAIC has the potential to drive the cancer-immunity cycle, with New FP showing strong immunomodulatory effects that may enhance responsiveness to immunotherapy. From the perspective of strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle, HAIC should be reconsidered as an important locoregional option capable of reshaping the TIME.

Other locoregional therapies: SBRT and TARE/Y90

Other locoregional therapies also influence the cancer-immunity cycle. Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) can induce immunogenic tumor cell death, cancer antigen release, and inflammatory signaling, thereby potentially promoting step 1 of the cancer-immunity cycle. In HCC, recent clinical studies have suggested that SBRT combined with immunotherapy may improve therapeutic outcomes compared with SBRT alone, and that SBRT, TACE, and immunotherapy can reshape the systemic tumor immune environment in patients with unresectable HCC.35,36 These findings support the biological and clinical rationale for integrating SBRT into ICI-based strategies.

Selective internal radiation therapy, also known as transarterial radioembolization (TARE) using yttrium-90 (Y90), can also influence the cancer-immunity cycle through radiation-induced tumor injury. Chew et al.37 reported that Y90 radioembolization induced both local and systemic immune activation in HCC, including increased antigen-presenting cells and activation of T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and natural killer T (NKT) cells. These findings suggest that TARE/Y90 may contribute not only to local tumor control but also to immune activation, providing a biological rationale for combination with ICI-based therapy. A recent review also summarized the biological rationale, clinical evidence, and future directions for combining TARE/Y90 with immunotherapy in HCC.38 However, clinical evidence directly demonstrating that SBRT- or TARE/Y90-induced TIME modulation enhances the efficacy of ICI-based therapy in HCC remains limited.

Clinical outcome evidence supporting TIME-modulating combination and sequential strategies

The clinical relevance of TIME modulation should ultimately be evaluated by whether it is associated with improved therapeutic outcomes, including objective response, PFS, and OS. Several clinical studies have begun to support the potential value of combining or sequencing ICI-based therapy with locoregional treatments or molecular-targeted agents.

For locoregional treatment combined with ICI-based therapy, phase 3 trials have provided important clinical evidence for TACE-based strategies. In the EMERALD-1 trial, TACE combined with durvalumab plus bevacizumab significantly improved PFS compared with TACE alone in patients with unresectable HCC amenable to embolization.39 Similarly, the LEAP-012 trial showed that TACE combined with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab resulted in a significant and clinically meaningful improvement in PFS compared with TACE plus placebo in patients with unresectable, non-metastatic HCC.40 For HAIC-based combination therapy, the TRIPLET study evaluated HAIC-FOLFOX combined with camrelizumab and apatinib in patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage C HCC, supporting the clinical feasibility and antitumor activity of HAIC combined with ICI-based therapy.41 These clinical data suggest that locoregional treatment combined with ICI-based systemic therapy can improve clinical outcomes in selected patients with HCC.

Clinical observations also support the potential value of sequential strategies. Our previous study showed that HAIC/New FP therapy before Atex+Bev was associated with a higher objective response rate and longer PFS and OS than Atex+Bev without prior HAIC/New FP therapy.34 Ito et al.42 also reported a potential association between prior molecular-targeted therapy and the efficacy of subsequent STRIDE in a multicenter real-world study. In their later-line cohort, patients who received molecular-targeted agents before STRIDE showed an objective response rate of 54.5%, a disease control rate of 63.6%, and a median PFS of 6.7 months. These clinical observations suggest that prior locoregional or systemic therapy may influence the efficacy of subsequent ICI-based regimens.

The clinical impact of TKI plus anti-PD-1 combinations should also be considered when discussing TIME-modulating combination strategies. Although TKI plus anti-PD-1 therapy may enhance antitumor immunity by suppressing angiogenesis-related immunosuppression, clinical trial outcomes may be influenced by multiple factors, including the balance between efficacy and toxicity, the specific agents used, and the therapeutic efficacy of the control regimen. In LEAP-002, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab did not meet the prespecified statistical significance thresholds for OS and PFS compared with lenvatinib alone, whereas CARES-310 demonstrated significant improvements in both PFS and OS with camrelizumab plus rivoceranib compared with sorafenib.43,44 These findings suggest that TKI plus anti-PD-1 therapy is biologically rational, but its clinical benefit should be interpreted according to the treatment context and trial design.

Together, these findings support the clinical relevance of combination and sequential strategies involving locoregional treatments, molecular-targeted agents, and ICI-based therapy. However, high-level prospective evidence directly demonstrating that TIME modulation induced by locoregional treatments or molecular-targeted agents enhances the efficacy of immunotherapy remains insufficient. Therefore, further prospective studies incorporating immune profiling and predefined clinical endpoints are needed to determine whether treatment-induced TIME modulation can reproducibly improve response, PFS, and OS.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES IN STRATEGIC DRIVING OF CANCER-IMMUNITY CYCLE

Optimization of TACE procedures for strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle

TACE initiates ctep 1 of the cancer-immunity cycle by inducing tumor necrosis and releasing tumor-associated antigens. However, it remains unclear whether conventional TACE (C-TACE) or drug-eluting bead TACE (DEB-TACE) more effectively converts the TIME into an immune-hot state, and which cytotoxic agents most strongly induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) and promote T cell priming. Clarifying these issues is essential to maximize the potential of TACE as a strategic driver of the cancer-immunity cycle.

C-TACE induces strong ischemic necrosis and robust DAMPs release, promoting the priming phase of the cancer-immunity cycle, but excessive ischemia may increase immunosuppressive cytokines, shifting the TIME to an immune-cold state.45 In contrast, DEB-TACE achieves more controlled necrosis and has been associated with higher ICD, greater CD8+ T cell infiltration, and increased PD-L1 expression, suggesting superior potential to remodel the TIME into an immune-hot state and enhance ICI responsiveness.

Among chemotherapeutic agents, anthracyclines such as epirubicin and doxorubicin are strong ICD inducers, mediating calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 and ATP release that facilitate dendritic cell activation and T cell priming.46 Cisplatin enhances immune activation through upregulation of MHC class I and ICAM-1 and reduction of immunosuppressive cells, contributing to increased cytotoxic T cell activity.47 Determining which TACE procedure and cytotoxic agent most effectively drive the cancer-immunity cycle requires prospective clinical trials incorporating TIME analyses before and after treatment.

Whether TIME modulations by molecular-targeted agents and transarterial therapy truly drive the cancer-immunity cycle

This review has highlighted that molecular-targeted agents and transarterial therapy can modulate the TIME and may act as potential drivers of the cancer-immunity cycle. However, whether these TIME modulations truly drive the cancer-immunity cycle remains to be clarified. The immune activation induced by these treatments primarily results from molecular signaling inhibition or necrosis-mediated immune cell recruitment, which may differ from immune activation driven directly by tumor antigen recognition in the cancer-immunity cycle. It therefore remains uncertain whether these therapies consistently enhance the efficacy of combination immunotherapy. Suzuki et al.34 reported that prior New FP therapy significantly improved the efficacy of Atez+Bev, indicating that the presence or absence of pretreatment may influence the outcome of subsequent combination immunotherapy. Whether the efficacy of post-combination immunotherapy treatments is influenced by prior exposure to combination immunotherapy should also be investigated in future studies. Establishing high-quality prospective evidence will be essential to validate these concepts and to complete the concept of strategic driving of the cancer-immunity cycle.

Decoding TIME for strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle

Understanding individual TIME profiles is essential to select treatments that can most effectively drive the cancer-immunity cycle. Current treatment decisions still rely on population-based data, which may not capture the heterogeneity of TIME. Personalized assessment is therefore required to realize truly strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle.

Etiology-specific differences also represent an important component of TIME heterogeneity. This is particularly relevant in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)-driven HCC. Pfister et al.48 reported that MASH-related HCC showed impaired immune surveillance and reduced responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy, despite the accumulation of activated and exhausted CD8+ PD-1+ T cells. These findings suggest that immune- cell infiltration alone does not necessarily indicate an effective antitumor TIME, and that etiology-specific TIME features may partly explain heterogeneity in ICI responsiveness.

Tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) are another important component for decoding antitumor immunity in the TIME. TLS are ectopic lymphoid aggregates composed mainly of B cells, T cells, dendritic cells, and high endothelial venules, and may support local antigen presentation and T cell priming within or around tumors. In HCC, recent studies have shown that TLS are associated with superior pathological response, improved relapse- free survival, and immunotherapy responsiveness.49,50 Therefore, TLS may serve as a tissue-based biomarker reflecting an immune-active TIME and should be considered when evaluating ICI responsiveness and strategies to drive the cancer-immunity cycle.

Biopsy remains the most direct method but is invasive and unsuitable for repeated evaluation. Blood-based biomarkers such as cell-free DNA, circulating tumor DNA, and exosomal microRNAs may allow less invasive and real-time monitoring of TIME changes, as suggested by recent studies showing correlations between circulating immune-related gene signatures and treatment responses in HCC.51 In clinical practice for HCC, computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging are the most accessible tools for characterizing tumor biology. Utilizing these imaging modalities for radiomics or even radiogenomics, combined with artificial intelligence (AI)-based analysis, may allow non-invasive decoding of TIME. Recent radiomics studies have demonstrated that imaging-derived features can predict immune phenotypes, including CD8+ T cell infiltration, and may thus provide surrogate biomarkers for immune-hot or immune-cold TIME states.52 Such approaches could complement liquid biopsy and provide a comprehensive, minimally invasive strategy to guide treatment decisions. Prospective studies linking these TIME profiling methods with clinical outcomes are required to establish evidence-based strategies and to complete the concept of strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle.

CONCLUSIONS

This review highlights how diverse therapeutic modalities, including ICIs, molecular-targeted agents, and locoregional treatments, modulate the TIME and contribute to the cancer-immunity cycle (Fig. 3). The concept of strategic driving of cancer-immunity cycle provides a scientific basis for optimizing treatment sequences by selecting modalities that best reactivate or sustain this cycle. Understanding the distinct immunological effects of each modality further supports a rationale for drug selection. Future research should focus on establishing high-quality evidence to validate this concept and on integrating personalized TIME profiling to guide treatment decisions. Such an approach may ultimately maximize antitumor efficacy and improve outcomes in patients with HCC.
