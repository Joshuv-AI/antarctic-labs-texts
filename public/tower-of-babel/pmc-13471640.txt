Continuity Therapy for couples living with acquired brain injury: a non-randomised uncontrolled feasibility study
Authors: Gerard A. Riley, Urszula Gajewska, Barbara Hagger, Asweeni Jansdin Raj, Natasha Yasmin, Giles Yeates
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrain injuries can have a significant negative impact on marriages/partnerships. Available interventions either require specialists trained in relationship therapy (which most rehabilitation services have no access to) or offer more structured manualised approaches that are restricted in scope and have generally led to only small improvements. Continuity Therapy was developed to provide a manualised approach that can be delivered by brain injury clinicians without specialist training in relationship therapy, and that addresses a broader range of relationship issues. Study aims were to complete a preliminary evaluation of the benefits of the therapy to determine whether progression to a randomised controlled trial is justified; to obtain feedback that will be used to refine the therapy; and to evaluate selection criteria and patient-reported outcome measures.MethodsFifteen couples living with brain injury received the therapy. They completed questionnaires about their relationship and psychological wellbeing before and after the therapy, and at 3-month follow-up, and rated how helpful they found the therapy. They and the therapists were also interviewed (or provided written feedback) about their experience of the therapy. Criteria about progression to an RCT were established in terms of numbers showing statistically reliable improvement on relationship questionnaires, ratings of helpfulness, and non-completion rates.ResultsProgression criteria were met. Although not everyone benefitted, participants showed a mean large effect improvement on post-therapy questionnaires about their relationship, and this was sustained at 3 months. They showed a mean moderate effect improvement in their psychological wellbeing, again sustained at 3 months. Couples and therapists made suggestions about improvements to the therapy, the circumstances of couples that may impact the effectiveness of the therapy (relevant to selection criteria), and the suitability of outcome measures.ConclusionsEffect sizes should be treated with caution because of the limitations of the study. Nevertheless, the therapy showed sufficient promise to support progression to an RCT. A broader range of outcome measures is required to capture the benefits of the therapy for the relationship. Refinement of the selection criteria is also needed.Trial registrationThe study was registered with ISRCTN: The UK’s Clinical Study Registry (registration number—93611293) on 6.6.2023.

Background

Although it is not always the case [1], acquired brain injuries such as traumatic brain injury and stroke often have a negative impact on marriages and partnerships [2, 3]. For example, a recent study in Denmark reported that the rate of divorce for couples living with brain injury was approximately 150% of the rate for the general population [4]; approximately half of the injured and non-injured partners in a US sample reported significant levels of dissatisfaction about their relationship [5]; and, in a UK sample, non-injured partners rated their current relationship, on average, as significantly worse than the pre-injury relationship [1].

Good relationships contribute to happiness and quality of life, and promote better psychological wellbeing [6, 7]. Brain injuries can have a negative impact on the psychological wellbeing of both the injured and the non-injured partner [8, 9] and so the protective effect of a strong relationship is particularly important in this context. Better family relationships are also associated with better rehabilitation outcomes for the person with the injury [10–12]. Given these benefits, couples living with brain injury should have access to effective support to help them maintain a good relationship.

Relationship-focused interventions in brain injury fall into three broad categories [13]. The first involves the use of therapies developed for use with the general population, such as systemic family therapy [14, 15] and emotion-focused therapy [16, 17]. An issue with these therapies is that the person delivering the therapy needs to be trained in the approach, and few brain injury services have access to clinicians with these specialist skills [17, 18]. The second category comprises interventions that have been developed specifically for brain injury and focus on a particular issue within the relationship, such as sexual functioning [19]. Although valuable, these interventions only address one aspect of the relationship and their focus tends to be on the functionality of the behaviour rather than its relational significance [13]. The third category encompasses interventions developed specifically for brain injury that provide more general support for relationships (i.e. they address more than one relationship issue). Included in this category are interventions such as Couples Caring and Relating with Empathy After Brain Injury [20] and Therapeutic Couples Intervention [21]. These interventions are broadly within the cognitive-behavioural tradition and incorporate components such as education, skills training, coping strategies, and goal setting. Although they deal with more than one relationship issue, each one still focuses on a relatively restricted range of issues, meaning that they may not address a sufficient number of the relationship issues affecting individual couples [13]. A recent review of these therapies also reported disappointing outcomes: Outcomes on 25 out of 28 relationship questionnaires reported across seven randomised controlled trials were classified as showing no improvement or only a small effect improvement [13].

To address these issues, an intervention labelled Continuity Therapy has been developed. The therapy aims to provide more comprehensive coverage of relationship issues but, at the same time, to do this in a structured manualised format that makes it deliverable by rehabilitation clinicians without specialist training in working with couples and families.

The primary aim of this feasibility study was to obtain some preliminary evidence about the benefits of the therapy in order to inform a decision about whether the therapy merits being evaluated in a randomised controlled trial. A small sample of couples received the therapy. It was delivered by clinical psychologists with experience of working in brain injury but without specialist training in working with couples and families. To evaluate potential benefits, couples completed questionnaires before and after therapy, and, along with the therapists, provided feedback about perceived benefits. Other aims of the study were to refine the therapy using participant and therapist feedback, and to evaluate selection criteria and patient-reported outcome measures in preparation for a controlled trial.

Method

Continuity Therapy

Continuity Therapy is based on Continuity Theory which proposes that adjustment to life transitions and disruptive major life events is more successful when there is greater continuity between life before and after the event [22–24]. Continuity comprises both internal and external continuity. Internal continuity refers to the experience of the self as continuous with the self before the disruptive event, and thereby the persistence of ways of understanding events and managing life. If these are lost, life can become more stressful. There may also be a sense of loss for one’s previous self. External continuity refers to the persistence of external elements such as one’s environment, employment, social and leisure activities, social relationships, and generally how one lives life. External continuity can help promote internal continuity, but it is also the source of meaning and value in one’s life. Losing it will undermine psychological wellbeing if sources of meaning and value are not replaced [25, 26].

Continuity Therapy is based on extending these principles to the disruption in the relationship caused by brain injury, and aims to promote internal and external continuity in the relationship. Internal discontinuity means the relationship is no longer experienced as the continuation of the loving relationship that existed before the injury, but as a new and different kind of relationship. Ways of understanding and relating to the other person are lost. External discontinuity means changes in how the couple interact and spend their time together. Ways of being together that gave meaning and value to their relationship and that bonded them as a couple are lost. There is some research to support these ideas. Specifically, there is evidence that many non-injured partners experience discontinuity in the relationship and that this has a negative impact [27–30]. For such partners, the person with the injury is experienced as a stranger. The relationship no longer feels like a marriage or partnership but one which is dominated by the giving and receiving of care. The sense of togetherness, partnership, and equality are lost. Feelings of love and intimacy are replaced by other feelings such as protectiveness, emotional detachment, or even dislike. Discontinuity is associated with lower ratings of the general quality of the relationship [30], with providing poorer quality of care [29, 31], and with experiencing the caring role as burdensome [29].

To promote internal and external continuity in the relationship, the therapy draws on couple therapies within the cognitive-behavioural tradition [32, 33] and Narrative Family Therapy [34]. Cognitive Behavioural Couples Therapy includes an emphasis on understanding patterns of interaction; and through goal setting, changing behavioural patterns that undermine the relationship and promoting ones that foster it. Narrative Family Therapy is based on the idea that, when people are faced with difficult life events, negative narratives focused on those events come to dominate their emotional experience and their way of understanding and dealing with life generally; and that people can be helped by developing richer more complex narratives that incorporate all aspects of their life and personal history, both positive and negative [14, 34]. Continuity Therapy includes the aim of helping couples develop richer narratives about their life together that enhance the sense of continuity between the past, present, and future. An example of a specific technique incorporated from Narrative Family Therapy is the tree of life [35] that can be used as an optional method for structuring some of the discussions within the therapy.

Drawing on these ideas, Continuity Therapy has two main components. In the first, couples are taken through various aspects of their relationship and life together, and they reflect on how it was before the injury, what has changed since the injury, and why it has changed. These aspects include such things as times and activities when they felt close, how they expressed warmth and affection, communication, mutual support, decisions and partnership, and dealing with disagreements. As part of this process, the couple are also asked to identify relationship values—aspects of the relationship that were important to them, and that made the relationship work as well as it did. The couple then set themselves goals to repair changes that they identify as unwanted. The goals focus on how they interact in everyday life, and the couple are encouraged to use how things were before the injury as a guide to choosing the goals. If it is not possible to reinstate pre-injury activities in an exact form, the couple are encouraged to think of alternative activities that reflect the relationship values that made this activity important. The second component focuses on the development of a richer and more positive narrative that highlights continuities in themselves and their relationship. Couples are encouraged to reflect on what has not changed; on their enduring strengths and resources as individuals and as a couple; on any ways in which they or their life have changed for the better as a result of the injury; and on how the injury fits into their life story, encompassing their past and future together. As a way of consolidating this alternative narrative [36], couples are given the option of engaging in a creative arts activity that expresses their story. Examples include making a video or putting together a montage of photographs of their life. This is done by couples outside sessions, but the end product is discussed within session.

In two previous case-study reports, we described preliminary versions of the two main components of Continuity Therapy. One study focused on behavioural change [37] and the other on developing more positive narratives [38]. Participants in these studies showed some improvements following the therapy on questionnaires about their relationship and psychological wellbeing. Feedback from the participants and the experience of delivering the therapy was used to further refine the therapy and to develop a manual containing instructions for delivery of the therapy that was used in the present study. The manual gives some scope for flexibility in that, rather than following a fixed session-by-session curriculum, the focus is on working through the processes described in the previous paragraph in an order that suits the individual couple.

Research team

The core research team consisted of three clinical psychologists, a trainee clinical psychologist, and two couples with lived experience of brain injury. The psychologists and the trainee provided the therapy. Another member of the team, who was not a clinical psychologist and was not involved in delivering the therapy, took informed consent, administered questionnaires, and conducted interviews.

Recruitment and sample size

Couples were recruited through two National Health Service brain injury rehabilitation services in the UK and through four charities providing support for people living with brain injury (Headway, SameYou, Brainkind, and Different Strokes). Potential participants were contacted by a member of the research team either after they responded to advertisements about the study, or after staff working within a service had identified an eligible couple and obtained their consent for the research team to make contact.

To participate, couples were required to be living together at the time of the study and to have lived together for at least 2 years prior to the injury. Both partners were required to have the capacity to give informed consent and be capable of taking a meaningful part in discussions about their relationship conducted in English. Potential participants were advised that the therapy would not be suitable if their pre-injury relationship had been poor, if their current relationship was in crisis and under immediate risk of breaking down, or if they had severe mental health difficulties. These criteria were not formally assessed, and it was left to the discretion of potential participants to decide if they met the criteria. Staff working within services were asked not to involve anyone when they had serious concerns about their relationship or mental health. All types of acquired brain injury were eligible. It was a requirement that the injury occurred at least 1 year before participation, but no more than 10 years previously.

Some justification of these selection criteria is needed. The requirement about the pre-injury relationship is based on the underlying aim of the therapy to reconnect with the pre-injury relationship. This would not be helpful if that relationship was poor. The criteria about the relationship or mental health of the couple being in serious difficulty were based on ethical concerns. Such difficulties would need to be addressed in a more direct way, rather than hoping that an untried therapy would resolve the difficulties. The criterion about the length of time since the injury was based on feedback from participants in the earlier case studies [37, 38] and discussion with the couples living with brain injury on the research team. Reconnecting with a pre-injury relationship was considered challenging if the gap is too long and alternative ways of being together have become too established. Too short a gap was also considered an issue because the injury can be so disruptive to other aspects of life that relationship issues may not be as apparent to the couple in the first year and the couple may not be able to give their attention and time to repairing their relationship.

Formal power analysis is not appropriate for most feasibility studies because they are not hypothesis-driven [39]. It has been suggested that 12 participants per group is a useful rule-of-thumb in feasibility studies where there is no other clear basis on which to justify sample size [40]. In anticipation of a 20% drop-out rate, the aim was therefore to recruit at least 15 couples.

Outcome measures

Participants completed questionnaires about their relationship and about their own psychological wellbeing at three time points—before the therapy, after the therapy, and at 3-month follow-up. Measures of wellbeing were included because of the general link between good relationships and wellbeing [6, 7] and because of the specific predictions of Continuity Theory that continuity facilitates less stressful adjustment to challenging life events [22]. At the same time as completing the post-therapy questionnaires, participants also rated how helpful they had found the therapy on a 7-point scale from 1 (‘not at all helpful’) through 4 (‘quite helpful’) to 7 (‘very helpful’) (no verbal label was applied to the other numbers).

Selection of the questionnaires was based on the aims and rationale of the therapy; prior experience and participant feedback about questionnaires used in the two previous case-study reports [37, 38]; and discussions within the research team, including the two couples living with brain injury. A general consideration was the need to balance the risk of overburdening participants with too many questionnaires and the requirement for a comprehensive evaluation of expected benefits.

In terms of questionnaires about the relationship, the Birmingham Relationship Continuity Measure provides a measure of the non-injured partner’s experience of continuity within the relationship and, provided that the pre-injury relationship was satisfactory, a measure of the general quality of the relationship. It was included because of its direct relevance to the aims and rationale of the therapy. The Index of Marital Satisfaction was selected because it was considered that, compared to other questionnaires, it provides a broader coverage of different aspects of the relationship, including items that addressed processes targeted by the therapy (such as doing valued activities together).

In terms of measures of psychological wellbeing, the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale was included as a general measure of the positive aspects of wellbeing, and the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale as a general measure of distress. Research indicates that measuring positive wellbeing and distress provides more comprehensive coverage because, although related, the two are distinct [54]. The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale was selected for partners with an injury because previous research has shown that it is significantly negatively correlated with a sense of disruption and discontinuity in self-identity [55]. Non-injured partners also completed the Head Injury Behaviour Rating Scale. This asks about whether challenging emotional and behavioural changes have occurred in the person with the brain injury, and, if they have occurred, how much distress they cause to the family member. It was not anticipated that the number of changes would alter over the course of therapy and so the questionnaire served primarily as a measure of the emotional wellbeing of the non-injured partner. It was included because previous research has indicated that perceptions of continuity in the relationship are associated with non-injured partners showing better understanding and management of emotional and behavioural changes, and being less distressed by them [27, 29, 31].

To provide a measure of the severity of the injury, non-injured partners also completed the 34 core items of the informant version of the European Brain Injury Questionnaire [53]. Potential scores range from 34 to 102, with higher scores indicating that the person with the injury has more problems and experiences them more often.

Procedure

A member of the research team met with interested couples to explain the therapy, to evaluate their suitability, and to provide an opportunity to ask questions. For those who decided to take part, a second meeting was arranged at which informed consent was taken and the pre-therapy questionnaires completed.

Prior to delivering the intervention, the therapists read through the manual and met up with the lead author to discuss any points of uncertainty. Four different therapists provided the therapy, following instructions contained in the manual. Two were experienced clinical psychologists, one had recently qualified, and the fourth was in training. Each therapist met up with another therapist on a regular basis to provide and/or receive clinical supervision. In eight cases, the therapy was delivered face-to-face and in the remaining eight it was delivered online. Delivery of the therapy required between 8 and 12 sessions, each lasting 60 to 120 min. The therapists all had experience of working in brain injury and were instructed to monitor the understanding and engagement of the injured partner and make suitable adjustments, such as repeating points arising from the discussion, summarising what had been discussed, providing written summaries, reducing the length of the session, and taking breaks. The earlier sessions were delivered weekly, but there were longer intervals between later sessions. This was to allow couples more time between sessions to work on goals that they had set themselves. For most couples, the therapy was completed within a 3-month period, although in two cases there was an enforced break in therapy due to personal illness.

The intention was to complete the post-therapy questionnaires and rating scale within 2 weeks of the end of therapy and the follow-up questionnaires at least 3 months after the end of therapy. In some cases, the 2-week deadline was missed because of difficulties in arranging a meeting.

Following completion of the post-therapy questionnaires, participants gave feedback about their experience of the therapy. They were given the choice of being interviewed or providing written feedback. Couples who chose an interview were interviewed together. Those who provided written feedback were given the choice of providing this individually or jointly. The interviews and request for written feedback followed a structured format in that the same fixed set of questions were asked, although there was also an opportunity to give feedback on any other issues the participants felt were relevant. Questions addressed their general experience of the therapy, what they found helpful, what was less helpful, suggestions for improvements to the therapy, and their opinions on whether any of their personal circumstances may have impacted on the effectiveness of the therapy. The therapists were also interviewed about the same topics.

Informed consent, pre-therapy questionnaires, and interviews were all conducted by a member of the team who was not involved in delivery of the therapy. This was to avoid the possibility that a relationship with a therapist that the couple had established with their therapist influenced questionnaire responses and the response to interview questions. The intention was that all post-therapy and follow-up questionnaires should be administered by the same member of the team. In some cases, this was not possible, and so participants were asked to email or post completed questionnaires to the research team, again to avoid participants completing the questionnaires in the presence of their therapist.

Data analysis

Whether the progression criteria were satisfied was determined by inspecting frequencies within the data. The Reliable Change Index [56] was used to determine whether participants showed statistically reliable improvements on the relationship questionnaires. The index indicates the probability that a change in scores reflects genuine change rather than the unreliability of the measure. The index was set at 1.96, meaning that there was less than 5% probability of a change score above the index being due to measurement error. Use of the index also allowed an exploration of variability across participants in the benefits obtained from the therapy.

Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were also calculated as a means of evaluating whether the therapy showed sufficient promise to merit evaluation in an RCT. Effects were calculated separately for the injured and non-injured partners, and both an immediate effect (post-therapy scores minus pre-therapy scores) and a follow-up one (follow-up scores minus pre-therapy scores) were determined. To provide context for the interpretation of these effect sizes, paired sample t-tests for these outcomes were also completed. These should not be interpreted as inferential tests of hypotheses. In addition, to explore general impact, four overall means were calculated: the mean of all immediate effects across all participants (i.e. both injured and non-injured partners together) for all relationship questionnaires; the mean of all immediate effects for all psychological wellbeing questionnaires; the mean of all follow-up effects for all relationship questionnaires; and the mean of all follow-up effects for all psychological wellbeing questionnaires.

Cohen’s d was calculated using formula 10 provided by Lakens [57]. Cohen’s d is biased if the data are not normally distributed or the variances of the two sets of scores are not equal [58], and these assumptions are also made when applying t-tests. Checks for normality and homogeneity of variance were therefore conducted. The ratio of the two variances was calculated and a ratio above 2.0 was considered problematic [59]. Normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test and inspection of the normal Q-Q plots.

Feedback from therapists and participants was analysed using framework analysis, following the steps described by Klingberg et al. [60]. The focus was on a ‘small q’ qualitative approach which aims to describe and summarise rather than interpret [61]. Framework analysis provides the opportunity to use the questions that were asked in the interview as the initial thematic framework of categories. Feedback from all sources was grouped into these categories. Subthemes within these categories were developed as appropriate. The analysis was conducted by GR and a check of its credibility was carried out by GY who evaluated whether the summaries were sufficiently grounded in the data.

Results

Participants

Sixteen couples gave their consent but one couple withdrew for personal reasons after one therapy session. All 15 of the remaining couples attended all the therapy sessions and all provided a full set of pre- and post-therapy questionnaire data. However, four of the injured partners and five of the non-injured partners failed to complete the follow-up questionnaires.

Of the 15 couples who received the full course of therapy, all were in heterosexual relationships, and all were of White ethnicity apart from one person of Asian ethnicity and one who preferred not to say. Three couples were resident in the USA, and the rest were from across the UK. Nine of the injured partners were male and six were female. The mean age was 51 for the injured partners (range 26 to 80) and 49 for the non-injured partners (range 27 to 71). The mean time they had lived together was 21 years (range 4 to 45). Eight of the brain injuries were traumatic brain injury, five were stroke, and two were other types of injury. The mean time since injury was 34 months (range 12 to 100).

Overall effect and ratings

Distributions were checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. All variance ratios were below the benchmark of 2.0. None of the variable distributions of the injured partners showed significant departure from normality, but those for the non-injured partners showed mild-to-moderate departures on the pre-therapy and follow-up scores on the Index of Marital Satisfaction, the post-therapy Emotion Regulation, and the follow-up Birmingham Relationship Continuity Measure. Effect sizes based on these variables should therefore be treated with more caution. On the 7-point rating scale of how helpful the participant had found the therapy, the mean rating for injured participants was 5.7 (range 1.0 to 7.0; median = 6.0) and for non-injured participants it was 6.0 (range 4.0 to 7.0; median = 7.0). In terms of the decision criteria about progressing to a controlled evaluation, all of the non-injured participants and all but one of the injured participants rated the therapy as helpful (i.e. a score of 4 or more).

Individual analysis

Another decision criterion about progressing to an RCT concerned the number showing statistically reliable improvement on at least one relationship questionnaire when comparing pre- and post-therapy scores. Nine of the injured participants and six of the non-injured participants showed reliable improvement (at 95% level of probability) in how they rated their relationship.

Examination of reliable change also indicated more general variation in how much benefit individuals derived from the therapy. In terms of the immediate outcome, three of the non-injured partners and six of the injured partners failed to show reliable improvement on any of the questionnaires (at 95% level of probability). In terms of the follow-up outcome, all the non-injured partners showed reliable improvement on at least one questionnaire, but three of the injured partners failed to show reliable improvement on any of the questionnaires. There was no clear correspondence between the participants’ overall helpfulness rating and whether they showed reliable improvement. For example, the maximum rating of 7 was given by all three non-injured partners who failed to show immediate reliable improvement on any questionnaire.

Qualitative feedback

Perceived benefits

Interview feedback from participants highlighted benefits from achieving specific aims of the therapy such as shifting from the care-focused dynamic in the relationship, feeling a closer emotional connection, repairing changes in communication patterns, appreciating the positives in their life together, and helping them to move on with their life and plan their future together. More general benefits were also appreciated, such as the opportunity to process difficult emotions in a safe space, the opportunity to discuss and reflect on issues that were otherwise neglected, and a better understanding of the impact of the injury. Participants generally reported that they had benefitted from receiving the therapy, although they varied in terms of how much they benefitted and one injured partner said he had not benefitted at all.

Feedback from the therapists highlighted the value of addressing certain issues targeted by the therapy. Therapists reported that many of the participating couples had difficulties in managing the way in which care was given and received in the relationship. Although a high level of support had been required earlier on in the recovery process, non-injured partners sometimes experienced problems in making the transition away from a care-based relationship back to a partnership, and continued to provide the same level of care and supervision even though it was no longer necessary. This was a frequent source of friction within the relationship because the injured partner felt they were being needlessly monitored and restricted. Another important benefit highlighted by the therapists was addressing the changes that occur in the activities that couples do together that they enjoyed and valued before the injury, that reflected the values that underpinned their relationship, and that helped them bond as a couple. Some couples had stopped doing many of these activities following the brain injury and were doing few things together that they enjoyed as a couple. When they were asked to reflect on the reasons for this, there was often no compelling reason why the activities had stopped. Restoration of the activities was perceived as making an important contribution to progress.

Areas for improvement

Several areas for improvement in the therapy were suggested by participants. Some repetitiveness and rigidity in the delivery of the therapy was noted, and it was felt by some that the therapy needed to be more tailored to the needs and wishes of the individual couple. This was also noted in some feedback from therapists. Although some couples found the tree of life device helpful, others perceived it as ‘childish’ and preferred not to use it. Willingness to engage in creative activities to consolidate the alternative narrative also varied. Some participants suggested that there needed to be more information at the start and throughout the therapy about what was going to happen in future sessions, and that more written summaries of what had been discussed would be helpful. Participants without prior experience of psychological therapy felt particularly uncertain about what was happening.

Factors impacting on benefits

Other feedback concerned personal circumstances that may have hindered the effectiveness of the therapy. A large component of the therapy involves the couple setting goals focused on changing how they interact on a daily basis. Therapists reported that, for various reasons, some couples struggled to set goals or to work on goals they had set themselves, and they seemed to benefit less from the therapy. Reasons for this struggle included having to deal with other stressful issues that made it difficult to focus on making changes to their relationship; and cognitive difficulties on the part of the person with the injury that made it more difficult to appreciate the need for behavioural change or made it difficult to implement the changes. In one case, a poor pre-injury relationship also hindered the therapy because there was less guidance as to what the couple valued or aspired to in their relationship, and they found it difficult to set goals for themselves. Finally, both participants and therapists commented on the importance of delivering the therapy at the right stage of recovering from a brain injury. It was felt that there was a risk of delivering the therapy too soon, when there was a focus on other aspects of rehabilitation such as physical recovery or when there was less appreciation of the impact of the injury, particularly its impact on the relationship. Conversely, it was also felt that the therapy could come too late because it was more difficult to change behaviours and perceptions that had become fixed over several years and more difficult to reconnect with a past that felt far away. Remembering too far back was a particular issue for some of the injured participants.

Outcome measures

Therapists also fed back about the evaluation of outcome. The relationship questionnaires were considered to be insensitive to some of the benefits of the therapy. It was also reported that some of the injured participants rated their relationship on the questionnaires too positively, and that their scores were not a valid representation of difficulties within the relationship. For example, one of the injured participants gave his relationship a perfect score on the Index of Marital Satisfaction pre-therapy. This did not correspond to the therapist’s perceptions of the relationship or to the rating given by his partner (a score of 42, above the cut-off for a ‘clinically significant problem’). In this particular case, the positivity appeared to relate to the participant’s relatively limited awareness of changes brought about by the brain injury.

Discussion

Progression to RCT

The primary aim of the study was to obtain some preliminary evidence about the potential benefits of the therapy in order to inform a decision about whether it merits further evaluation in an RCT. The findings suggest that the therapy does merit further evaluation. The three decision criteria for progressing to a trial were met. More than half of the injured participants showed reliable improvement in how they rated their relationship when comparing pre- and post-therapy scores. All but one participant rated the therapy as helpful, and only one couple who started the therapy did not complete the full course. In the interviews, both therapists and participants detailed ways in which the therapy had been beneficial.

Effect sizes also suggested that the therapy merits further evaluation. Both injured and non-injured partners showed large improvements immediately after therapy and at 3-month follow-up on the relationship questionnaires, with the exception of the immediate effect on the Index of Marital Satisfaction for non-injured partners which showed a moderate improvement. Also, the wellbeing questionnaires all showed at least moderate improvements except for the follow-up Behaviour Regulation and Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Wellbeing Scale for the non-injured partners. These effect sizes should be treated with caution because of the low sample size and the consequently wide confidence intervals and low precision for the estimates (see Table 4).

Other study aims

Findings highlighted several issues related to the selection criteria. Feedback from the therapists indicated that some couples struggled to implement change in their everyday life because of other ongoing stresses in their life (e.g. health problems) or difficulties in making behavioural change due to the brain injury. Selection criteria relating to these issues may be a useful addition in an RCT. For one couple, a poor pre-injury relationship also interfered with the effectiveness of the therapy. Although there was a criterion relating to this, it was left to the couple’s own discretion to say whether they met the criterion. A more formal assessment of this would be useful. Another selection criterion was that the injury occurred at least 1 year before participation, but no more than 10 years previously. Feedback suggested that the therapy is, indeed, best delivered at least 1 year after the injury, but that 10 years may be too long and this needs to be reduced, perhaps to 5 years. Although no formal evaluation of injury severity was completed, evidence from the European Brain Injury Questionnaire implied that most, if not all, of the injured participants fell into the moderate-to-severe range. Having a severe injury should not preclude people from taking part in the therapy and the selection criterion used for the study (relating to the person’s ability to take a meaningful part in discussions about their relationship) may represent a fairer way of judging suitability.

Findings highlighted some issues relating to the questionnaires used. Feedback from the therapists suggested that some of the non-injured participants were too positive in rating their relationship and that the relationship questionnaires were relatively insensitive to some of the benefits of the therapy. These issues may help explain the finding that there was no clear correspondence between the participants’ overall helpfulness rating and whether they showed reliable improvement on the relationship questionnaires. In an RCT, it may be beneficial to use a broader range of relationship questionnaires, covering more aspects of the relationship. Excess positivity may be a more intractable problem. More effort may be required to encourage participants to be honest in their answers on questionnaires, but this will not be effective against limited awareness of change resulting from cognitive impairments inflicted by the brain injury.

Participants also suggested some refinements to the therapy. Although there are optional elements (the tree of life and the creative activity) and the order of addressing topics is done in a way that suits the couple, some felt that there was still room for the therapy to be more tailored to the needs and wishes of the individual couple. Rather than covering all topics, it may be preferable to focus on those identified as particularly relevant by the couple. It was also suggested that there needed to be more information at the start and throughout the therapy about what was going to happen in future sessions, and that more written summaries of what had been discussed would be helpful. Although therapists were instructed to monitor the understanding and engagement of the injured partner and make suitable adjustments, it may be preferable to formalise, within the manual instructions, ways of enhancing understanding and engagement, including the regular use of written summaries to compensate for cognitive difficulties.

Limitations

Conclusions about the potential effectiveness of the therapy can only be tentative. There was no control group and, because of the small sample size, the estimates of effect size have wide confidence intervals and lack precision. The progression criteria were also somewhat arbitrary. It proved difficult to find guidance in previous literature about setting appropriate criteria for evaluating the potential effectiveness of therapies. There are also potential sources of bias that may have inflated effect size. There were no formal checks of treatment fidelity. Supervision of each therapist provided an opportunity to detect whether the therapist was departing from the manual, but more formal checks would have provided better assurance of fidelity. Although the questionnaires and interviews were administered by a member of the research team who was not otherwise engaged in delivering the therapy, the data may have been influenced by participants’ wishes not to be critical of the therapists or too negative about their relationship. Interviewing the couple about the therapy together, or their submission of joint written feedback, may similarly have been influenced by a desire to avoid negativity about the relationship in front of their partner. On the other hand, there were sources of bias that may have led to underestimation of effect size. As noted earlier, the relationship questionnaires were felt to be insensitive to some of the benefits of the therapy and some injured participants appeared to rate their relationship too positively at the pre-therapy stage, meaning that there was little scope for them to show improvement.

The sample was self-selected and it is unclear how representative they are of the population. Certainly, non-White and non-heterosexual communities were underrepresented. Information about the number of couples approached by staff within services was not collected, and information about the number who declined to participate after meeting with a researcher was not retained. Because the selection criteria were not formally assessed, some of the participating couples may not have been suitable for inclusion (e.g. the couple who appear to have had a poor pre-injury relationship).

There are also some issues about the delivery of the therapy. The aim is to provide a relationship therapy that can be delivered by a broad range of clinicians working in rehabilitation settings, not just those with specialist training in relationship therapy. Although none of the therapists had such specialist training, they were clinical psychologists or a trainee clinical psychologist. Further evaluation would be needed to show that the therapy can be satisfactorily delivered by other brain injury clinicians such as occupational therapists and social workers. The study involved both online and face-to-face therapy. The sample size was insufficient to determine if this made any difference, but the interviews with therapists did not highlight any major difficulties or concerns about online delivery. Evidence about CBT-type therapies suggests that online delivery is not less effective, and that online delivery is favoured by many clients because of its practical benefits [63].

The other feasibility issues addressed by the study were the selection criteria and the outcome measures. Feedback from participants and therapist has suggested some changes to these before progression to an RCT. There may have been some advantage in setting pre-determined criteria to evaluate these issues and using more structured methods for deciding whether changes would be required. For example, participants and therapists could have been asked to formally rate how well the relationship questionnaires mapped on to benefits from the therapy, and the decision about needing to change could have been based on a pre-determined criterion about these ratings. However, such an approach would rely on prejudging what was relevant and there are advantages in the approach that was taken of being responsive to issues raised by therapists and participants based on their experience of the therapy. The feasibility of other components could also have been assessed, such as treatment fidelity, recruitment inclusivity, and recruitment rate. However, assessing recruitment issues would not have been that useful because a different strategy is planned for an RCT whereby clinicians working within UK NHS rehabilitation services would recruit directly from service caseloads and steps would be taken to improve sample inclusivity.

Conclusion

Brain injury frequently creates relationship difficulties and services need to address these. A good relationship is important for happiness and wellbeing, and for the recovery of the person with the injury. Continuity Therapy has the potential to provide an effective and practical option, and a randomised controlled trial of the intervention is merited. However, evidence about the benefits is only preliminary and the study has indicated the need for some refinements to the therapy and the methodology before proceeding.
