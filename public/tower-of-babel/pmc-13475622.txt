Effect of paediatric lung ultrasound on antibiotic prescriptions in hospitalised children and adolescents with lower respiratory tract infections: protocol for a randomised, sham-controlled trial (PLUS-AP trial)
Authors: Dima Khreis, Mohamad Hassan, Maysaa Slika, Hawraa Raoof, Rim Hamideh, Ziyad R Mahfoud, Ali Ismail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) remain a major global health problem and a leading cause of childhood morbidity and mortality worldwide. Inappropriate antibiotic use for predominantly viral LRTIs is common and contributes substantially to the public health threat of antimicrobial resistance. Lung ultrasound (LUS) is a radiation-free, bedside imaging tool with high diagnostic accuracy for paediatric pneumonia. We hypothesise that integrating LUS into the care of children hospitalised with LRTIs can improve antibiotic decision-making and safely reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions.Methods and analysisThe PLUS-AP trial is an ongoing phase III, single-centre, blinded, parallel-group randomised controlled superiority trial conducted at a tertiary hospital in Beirut, Lebanon. Children aged 3 months to 18 years hospitalised with LRTI are randomly assigned (1:1) to LUS-guided management or to standard care with a sham lung ultrasound. In the intervention group, LUS is performed within 24 hours of admission and repeated around 48 hours later or before discharge, and the findings are shared with the treating team to inform management. The control group receives standard clinical evaluation, including chest X-ray as indicated, plus a non-diagnostic sham lung ultrasound. The primary outcome is antibiotic prescription at hospital discharge. Secondary outcomes are hospital length of stay; intensive care unit admission, chest tube insertion, mortality, hospital readmission, frequency of chest radiography performance and antibiotic use for LRTIs, all assessed within 4 weeks after enrolment. A total of 176 participants (88 per arm) will provide adequate power to detect a clinically important reduction in antibiotic prescribing, and analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle with one planned interim analysis at 50% recruitment.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol has been approved by the Institutional Review Board at the American University of Beirut (BIO-2024-0304). Written informed consent (and assent, when applicable) will be obtained for all participants. A Data and Safety Monitoring Committee will oversee patient safety and review interim results. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, scientific conferences and targeted outreach to clinical and public health stakeholders to facilitate translation of the findings into routine practice.Trial registration numberNCT06876766.

Introduction

Lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) remain a substantial global health concern, with pneumonia still a leading cause of childhood mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 2 As global child mortality continues to decline, preventable deaths from LRTIs persist, especially in settings with limited access to timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment.1 2 The reduction in LRTI-related mortality over recent decades is partly attributable to immunisation against key bacterial pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae type b, alongside improved supportive care.3 4 Yet children under 5 years of age continue to have the highest hospitalisation rates for pneumonia, and residual morbidity and mortality remain substantial, particularly in low- and middle-income settings where LRTIs are highly prevalent.1 2 5

Large studies have shown that viral pathogens account for the majority of paediatric LRTIs, whereas bacterial infections represent a smaller proportion among hospitalised children.6–8 Distinguishing bacterial from viral LRTIs in routine practice remains a major challenge and has direct implications for antibiotic use and antimicrobial resistance.6 8 The latest paediatric community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) guidelines emphasise restricting antibiotics to children with suspected or confirmed bacterial infection and advise against routine antibiotic therapy when a viral aetiology is established.7 Despite this, inappropriate antibiotic prescriptions in children with viral LRTIs are common, contributing to unnecessary drug exposure and the global threat of antimicrobial resistance.8 9 Diagnostic uncertainty, limited access to rapid microbiological testing and concern about missing serious bacterial infection all drive empiric antibiotic use, even when the probability of bacterial pneumonia is low.5 7 8 This pattern is particularly problematic in LMICs, where the burden of LRTIs is high and antimicrobial resistance is rapidly emerging.1 2 8

Imaging plays a central role in the assessment of children with suspected pneumonia, but traditional chest radiography has important limitations.5 Chest X-ray (CXR) exposes children to ionising radiation and may be difficult to interpret, especially in early or mild disease.10 11 Repeated imaging further increases cumulative radiation exposure, which is of particular concern in young children given their longer life expectancy and greater radiosensitivity.11 Over the past decade, lung ultrasound (LUS) has emerged as a promising diagnostic alternative that is radiation-free, portable and feasible at the bedside in both emergency and inpatient settings.10 12 Multiple meta-analyses and systematic reviews have shown that LUS has high diagnostic accuracy for paediatric pneumonia, with reported sensitivities around 95–96% and specificities between 93% and 98%, often comparable or superior to CXR.13–16 International guidelines and expert consensus statements now recognise LUS as a valuable tool for the evaluation of respiratory disease in critically ill neonates and children and support its integration into clinical practice.17

Beyond detecting the presence of pneumonia, LUS may also assist in differentiating between viral and bacterial aetiologies.18 19 Several paediatric studies have reported that bacterial CAP is more commonly associated with large, focal and often solitary consolidations, whereas viral infections and bronchiolitis more frequently show bilateral B-lines and small subpleural consolidations.18–22 Prospective studies have demonstrated that LUS patterns, when integrated alongside epidemiological, clinical and laboratory data, can improve aetiological classification compared with imaging alone.23 24 In line with this, a recent prospective cohort study from our centre reported high diagnostic accuracy of LUS for classifying the aetiology of respiratory symptoms in hospitalised children.25 However, while the diagnostic performance of LUS for pneumonia is well established, there is still limited evidence on whether incorporating LUS into routine care can safely reduce unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions in hospitalised children with LRTIs.9 16 Addressing this gap is essential to inform public health strategies that aim to optimise antibiotic use while preserving patient safety, particularly in low- and middle-income settings.1 9

The PLUS-AP trial is designed to fill this evidence gap. We aim to determine whether implementing a paediatric LUS algorithm at the point of care can safely reduce antibiotic exposure in children and adolescents hospitalised with LRTIs, compared with standard care. This article presents the detailed protocol of the trial, following the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines. We hypothesise that the addition of LUS to standard care in hospitalised children with LRTIs as compared with sham lung ultrasound (SLUS) with standard care will result in a notable reduction in the rate of antibiotic prescriptions, compared with standard of care, without increasing the risk of complications or treatment failures.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The PLUS-AP trial is an ongoing phase III, single-centre, blinded, parallel-group randomised controlled superiority trial with two parallel groups and a 1:1 allocation ratio. The trial compares LUS-guided management with standard care (including an SLUS) with a primary endpoint of antibiotic prescription at the time of hospital discharge. The study follows a single-blind design: participants and their families are unaware of group allocation, whereas treating clinicians manage patients according to their assigned group (with or without access to LUS results), so full blinding of providers is not feasible. Outcome assessment and data analysis will be conducted blinded to treatment allocation to minimise bias. Randomisation is performed using a computer-generated permuted-block sequence with a 1:1 allocation ratio, with allocation concealment maintained until the point of enrolment.

The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on 10 March 2025 (NCT06876766), before enrolment of the first participant. Recruitment began in April 2025 and is ongoing, with study completion anticipated in June 2027.

Study setting

The study is conducted at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC), a large tertiary academic teaching hospital located in Beirut, Lebanon. AUBMC serves a diverse urban population, including Lebanese nationals, refugees, displaced individuals and patients referred from across the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. The medical centre provides comprehensive paediatric emergency, inpatient and intensive care services and has an established paediatric LUS programme. This setting offers the necessary clinical volume, imaging expertise and infrastructural capacity to evaluate whether integrating LUS into routine inpatient care can reduce unnecessary antibiotic use among children hospitalised with LRTIs.

Recruitment and consent

Prior to trial initiation, the PI circulated the institutional review board (IRB)-approved protocol to all paediatric attending physicians and presented the study during a divisional meeting to ensure familiarity with trial procedures, clarify operational aspects, obtain feedback and secure agreement for the research team to approach eligible patients.

During the trial, the research team (excluding the PI, who will remain blinded to chest X-ray findings) will screen daily admissions and emergency department boards for new paediatric LRTI cases to identify potentially eligible participants. Families will first be approached to obtain verbal permission to review the child’s electronic health record to confirm eligibility. Once eligibility is verified, the study will be explained in detail and written informed consent will be obtained from a parent or legal guardian, with child assent when applicable. Treating physicians will be notified of patient enrolment after consent is completed. Screening and enrolment will continue until the target sample size is reached, with an anticipated recruitment period of approximately 18 months.

Randomisation and blinding

After consent, participants will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to the LUS arm or the control (SLUS) arm using a computer-generated permuted-block randomisation sequence with variable block sizes. The allocation sequence is generated and held by an independent allocator who is not involved in recruitment, clinical care, data collection or outcome assessment. The randomisation sequence is stored in a secure, password-protected file accessible only to the independent allocator. Allocation concealment is ensured by releasing the assignment only after the participant has been enrolled. Following consent, the enrolling investigator contacts the independent allocator, provides the participant’s study ID and receives the treatment allocation, which is then implemented.

Participants and their families remain blinded to group assignment; all children undergo an ultrasound procedure, but they are unaware of whether it is a diagnostic LUS or an SLUS. The ultrasound operator cannot be blinded to the procedure type but is blinded to chest radiograph findings and does not participate in clinical decision-making. Treating physicians in the intervention arm receive LUS findings, whereas those in the control arm receive no ultrasound information; thus, clinician blinding is not feasible. To minimise bias, the primary endpoint is objectively defined and data entry and analysis are conducted by personnel blinded to allocation. Emergency unblinding procedures are not required, as treating clinicians and the ultrasound operator are already aware of group assignment and no scenario necessitates revealing allocation to blinded staff.

Intervention: lung ultrasound versus sham lung ultrasound

Eligible participants will be randomised to one of two groups: the LUS arm or the SLUS control arm. All ultrasound procedures—LUS or SLUS—will be performed exclusively by the PI, an experienced clinician credentialed in paediatric LUS with over 5 years of experience. This approach was chosen to maximise standardisation of image acquisition and interpretation and minimise interoperator variability during the trial. The PI remains blinded to CXR findings throughout the study.

The assigned intervention (LUS or SLUS) will be completed as soon as feasible following enrolment and always within 24 hours, with a second ultrasound performed 48 hours later or earlier if the child is discharged before that time. Before performing the scan, the PI will explain the procedure to the family without disclosing whether a real or SLUS is being conducted and without sharing any diagnostic findings.

LUS arm

In the LUS group, the PI will perform a complete LUS following a standardised scanning protocol. Adequate imaging depth—at least two times the distance from the skin to the pleural line—will be used to ensure visualisation of artefacts such as A-lines. Each lung will be examined in both longitudinal and transverse orientations at three anatomical lines: the anterior midclavicular line, lateral mid-axillary line and posterior paraspinal line, for a total of six scanning zones, following the method described by Copetti and Cattarossi.26 All LUS findings will be recorded on a dedicated data collection form and interpreted immediately. The PI will communicate the results and impression (viral vs bacterial LRTI features) to the treating attending physician, who will determine appropriate management, including decisions regarding antibiotics.

SLUS arm

In the SLUS group, the PI will scan the same anatomical zones for the same duration but will intentionally use a shallow depth (1 cm) to prevent visualisation of lung structures or artefacts, thereby mimicking the appearance of an ultrasound examination without providing diagnostic information. No findings will be communicated to the treating team.

Modification or discontinuation of interventions

There are no criteria for discontinuing or modifying the assigned intervention, as the procedures involve minimal risk and are performed only two times in a short time window. No specific adherence strategies are required, as all interventions occur at the bedside by the PI without requiring ongoing participant action.

Concomitant care

All aspects of standard inpatient care for LRTIs are permitted in both arms, including laboratory investigation, supportive management and imaging as clinically indicated (including CXR when ordered by the treating physician). The only restriction is that no diagnostic LUS may be performed for participants assigned to the control arm outside of the study protocol.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined based on the primary hypothesis that LUS-guided management will reduce antibiotic prescriptions from approximately 60–35% in the LUS arm—a 25-percentage-point absolute reduction without adjunct biomarkers, as a clinically meaningful effect size based on the anticipated improvement in diagnostic accuracy and antibiotic stewardship. The baseline antibiotic prescription rate of 60% was derived from local institutional practice patterns and is consistent with the high rates of antibiotic use reported among children hospitalised with lower respiratory tract infections in LMICs.27 Because the primary endpoint is assessed at hospital discharge, only minimal loss to follow-up is expected.

Using SPSS (independent proportions test) with 80% power, α=0.05 and a projected 5% loss to follow-up, the required sample size was 66 participants per group (132 total). Given that antibiotic prescribing varies among the 11 attending paediatricians, clustering by physician was incorporated into the calculation. With no directly comparable trials available, we adopted a conservative intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.03, consistent with values reported in similar designs. Using the design-effect formula DE=1 + (M–1) × ICC, with an average cluster size of 12 patients, the design effect was 1.33. Applying this to the initial sample estimate yielded an adjusted total of 176 participants (88 per arm).

Data collection

Data will be collected at baseline, during hospitalisation and throughout the 4-week follow-up period. All enrolled participants will undergo either LUS or SLUS within 24 hours of enrolment, followed by a repeat scan approximately 48 hours later or before discharge, whichever comes first.

LUS examinations will be performed exclusively by the PI, using a Philips CX50 portable machine (linear L12–3 probe, lung preset). Both upper and lower lung zones will be examined in each orientation, as shown in figure 1. Each lung will be scanned in longitudinal and transverse orientations across anterior, lateral and posterior zones following the Copetti and Cattarossi method.26 Image depth will be set to at least two times the pleural-line distance to ensure adequate visualisation of A-lines and artefacts. In the SLUS arm, a shallow depth (1 cm) will be intentionally used to prevent visualisation of lung pathology while maintaining the appearance of a standard examination. LUS score will be calculated using the standardised scoring criteria provided in the data collection sheet (online supplemental file 1). All captured perspectives will be saved, with each recorded video clip lasting 8 s and archived for quality assurance and study documentation purposes.

Following discharge, outcome forms will be completed at 1, 2, 3 and 4 weeks via telephone to document secondary outcomes, including ICU transfers, chest tube insertion, readmissions, mortality, antibiotic exposure (type and duration) and number of chest radiographs (online supplemental file 2).

The study site will make all reasonable efforts to maintain follow-up; loss to follow-up is expected to be <5%.

Data management

All clinical data, LUS findings and outcome forms will be entered into Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, password-protected electronic data capture system. REDCap provides real-time validation, audit trails and controlled access. Only authorised study personnel will have access to identifiable data. Paper consent forms and data collection sheets will be stored in locked cabinets in the PI’s office, with restricted access. Electronic data will be stored on password-protected institutional computers. Coded allocation labels (‘A’ for SLUS, ‘B’ for LUS) will be used for data analysis; the biostatistician conducting the analysis will remain blinded to treatment assignment.

Data cleaning procedures will include retrieval of missing information during follow-up calls.

Study documents will be retained for 10 years following publication, after which physical forms will be securely destroyed.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics variables will be summarised using means and SD for numerical variables, and frequency distribution for the categorical variables. Those characteristics will be checked visually for any sizeable imbalances. The primary analysis will include comparing the primary outcome, the rate of antibiotic prescriptions, between the two study arms using a univariable generalised estimation equation (GEE) to adjust for the potential clustering effect. Unadjusted OR and the 95% CI will be presented. In addition, GEE will be used to obtain relative risk and relative risk reduction along with 95% CI. For the secondary analysis, we will adjust the primary analysis based on the imbalances in the demographic variables using multivariable GEE and obtain OR and 95% CI. Length of stay in the hospital and frequency of CXR performance by 4 weeks after enrolment are numeric secondary variables. Those will be compared between the two study arms using mixed-effects models to adjust for clustering effect. Rates of ICU admission, chest tube insertion, mortality, antibiotic use for LRTIs and hospital readmission within 4 weeks after enrolment are categorical secondary outcomes and will be analysed the same methodology used for the primary outcome. The analysis will be conducted using the latest version of IBM-SPSS or similar software. Two-sided p values with a significance level of 0.05 will be used for all tests. The analyses will be conducted by a professional academic statistician who will be blinded to the study groups. Table 2 summarises the statistical tests for each outcome.

No subgroup analyses are planned, and all primary analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle to evaluate the effectiveness of LUS compared with SLUS. For the primary outcome, a sensitivity analysis will be conducted by which missing values will be imputed to the mode values based on the physician handling the case. No other imputation for missing data will be done. An interim analysis of the primary endpoint will be conducted once 50% of participants have been randomised and completed the 4-week follow-up, using the Peto stopping boundary (p<0.01).

Data and safety monitoring

An independent Data and Safety Monitoring Committee (DSMC) composed of senior experts in paediatrics, neonatology and biostatistics will oversee participant safety throughout the trial. The DSMC will review one planned interim analysis—performed at 50% enrolment by a statistician blinded to allocation—along with any additional safety information it deems necessary. LUS is a radiation-free, non-invasive technique with no expected procedure-related harms; nevertheless, all adverse events and LRTI-related complications will be monitored and reported as required by the IRB. Based on their assessments, the DSMC will provide recommendations to the steering committee regarding continuation, modification or early termination of the trial.

Ethics and dissemination

This study protocol was approved by the American University of Beirut (BIO-2024-0304). The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06876766). All procedures will adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Any important protocol modifications will be submitted to the IRB and updated in the trial registry as required.

The risk to participants is considered minimal. LUS is non-invasive and radiation-free. The primary potential risk is delayed antibiotic treatment in a misclassified case of bacterial pneumonia; however, this is mitigated by close clinical monitoring and the planned repeat LUS at 48 hours (or sooner if any deterioration) to promptly detect and treat any bacterial infection. A DSMC of independent experts will monitor trial safety and outcomes at regular intervals. Any serious adverse events will be reported immediately to the IRB and DSMC, which has the authority to halt the trial for safety concerns.

Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants’ parents or guardians (with child assent as appropriate) (online supplemental files 3–5). Participation is voluntary and families may withdraw at any time. Confidentiality will be maintained by using coded study IDs and secure data storage; only the research team will access identifiable information, and all results will be reported in aggregate without personal identifiers.

Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and presentations at scientific conferences. Additionally, results will be communicated to clinicians and public health stakeholders. By introducing a practical diagnostic approach through the implementation of LUS into standard care, this trial may support more rational antibiotic use in children hospitalised with LRTIs and inform future guideline development.
