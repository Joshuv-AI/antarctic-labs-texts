Accurate and Transferable Pauli Exchange-Repulsion for Molecules with the Anisotropic Valence Density Overlap Model
Dahvyd Wing, Alexandre Tkatchenko

Abstract
Pauli exchange-repulsion is the dominant short-range intermolecular interaction and it is an essential component of molecular force fields. Current approaches to modeling Pauli repulsion in molecular force fields often rely on over 20 atom types to achieve chemical accuracy. The number of parameters in these approaches hampers the development of force fields with quantum-chemical accuracy that are transferable across many chemical systems. We present the anisotropic valence density overlap (AVDO) model for exchange-repulsion. The model produces sub-kcal/mol accuracy for dimers of organic molecules from the S101x7 dataset, a representative set of the most common biologically relevant intermolecular interactions, and for acene dimers of increasing size. It uses a single universal parameter, related to an atomic cross-sectional area, that is transferable across chemical systems. Given recent progress in machine learning the electronic density, this model offers a promising path toward high-accuracy, next-generation machine-learned force fields.



Classical force fields (FFs) of organic molecules have been indispensable in molecular biology, drug design, crystal structure prediction, and many other fields
Dauber-Osguthorpe and Hagler 2019
;
Hagler 2019
. In order to make FFs more accurate, better models for intermolecular interactions such as Pauli repulsion, electrostatics, induction, and dispersion are being explored
Hagler 2019
;
Jing et al. 2019
;
Stone 2013a
. This has led to an increase in the number of model parameters
Babin et al. 2013
;
Liu et al. 2019
such that overfitting becomes a challenge that can limit the transferability of these models beyond the conformations and systems represented in the training data set. FFs fit solely on total energies or bulk properties are particularly susceptible, as the fitted parameters often adopt unphysical values that rely on error cancellations with limited transferability.

One strategy for improving the transferability of FFs is to fit individual FF terms to the corresponding components of symmetry-adapted perturbation theory (SAPT)
McDaniel and Schmidt 2013
;
Van Vleet et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Schriber et al. 2021
, a method that calculates dimer interaction energies as the sum of exchange-repulsion, electrostatic, induction, and dispersion interactions
Jeziorski et al. 1994
;
Stone 2013a
. Although most SAPT variants are not applicable to predict macroscopic properties, studies have demonstrated that model parameters fitted on SAPT can be used as Bayesian priors in a subsequent global fit to higher-level reference data
Vandenbrande et al. 2017
;
Rackers et al. 2021
;
Schriber et al. 2021
. Thus, we adopt the perspective of evaluating a component of an FF by how well it reproduces its corresponding SAPT component.

SAPT analysis indicates that Pauli repulsion is not only a critical component of an FF
Stone 2013a
, but also the largest source of error in modern FFs
Van Vleet et al. 2016
;
Vandenbrande et al. 2017
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Schriber et al. 2021
. Hence, there has been a renewed interest in approximating it accurately
Rackers and Ponder 2019
;
Chung and Ponder 2024a
. In benchmark dimer datasets exchange-repulsion is often the single largest energy component and has the largest variance
Szalewicz and Jeziorski 2022
;
Parker et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Sparrow et al. 2021
. Macroscopic properties of condensed molecular systems, such as the density, are determined by the balance between Pauli repulsion and attractive intermolecular interactions. It is also a key factor in determining the stacking conformation of
\pi-\pi
interactions in proteins
Carter-Fenk et al. 2023
and the preference of linear conformations of halogen sigma bonds
Stone 2013b
, which are prevalent in drug design because they increase drug specificity
Xu et al. 2014
. Additionally, developing more accurate exchange-repulsion models is also relevant for machine-learned force fields
Unke et al. 2021a
, which often incorporate short-range exchange-repulsion models
Kabylda et al. 2025
;
Bereau et al. 2018
;
Schriber et al. 2021
, and may benefit from more transferable and accurate models of exchange repulsion.

Approximating Pauli repulsion in an accurate and transferable way is a longstanding challenge
Stone 2013a
;
Rackers and Ponder 2019
. The most common Pauli repulsion models used in FFs are simple Lennard-Jones models
Lennard-Jones 1931
and Born-Mayer models, which use an exponential term for each atom
Born and Mayer 1932
;
Buckingham 1938
. The fact that these models are isotropic, whereas an atom centered model of exchange repulsion should be anisotropic
Stone and Price 1988
;
Wheatley and Price 1990
;
Day and Price 2003
;
Totton et al. 2010
;
Stone 2013a
;
Elking et al. 2010
;
Van Vleet et al. 2018
;
Rackers and Ponder 2019
;
Chung and Ponder 2024b
, and the fact that these models have the wrong distance dependence
Van Vleet et al. 2016
means that they must be fit per molecule and often rely on error cancelation with other terms in the FF to achieve accuracy
Van Vleet et al. 2016
.

The density overlap model
Kita et al. 1976
;
Kim et al. 1981
, an empirical model, calculates the exchange-repulsion energy between two molecules as

E_{exch}=K\int\rho_{A}\rho_{B}\differential[3]{r},

(1)

where
K
is a fitted parameter and
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}
are the densities of molecules
A
and
B
. It captures the anisotropy of the exchange-repulsion energy
Wheatley and Price 1990
;
Day and Price 2003
;
Misquitta et al. 2008
;
Elking et al. 2010
;
Totton et al. 2010
;
Tafipolsky and Ansorg 2016
;
Rackers and Ponder 2019
and has a better description of the distance dependence than Lennard-Jones and Born-Mayer models
Van Vleet et al. 2016
. However, like the Lennard-Jones and Born-Mayer models, it is challenging to find transferable parameters for density overlap models. There is no single universal
K
parameter that works for all molecules
Gavezzotti 2003
;
Söderhjelm et al. 2006
and instead
K
must be fit per dimer pair to reach sub-kcal/mol accuracy for small molecules,
Piquemal et al. 2006
;
Elking et al. 2010
;
Bygrave et al. 2012
;
Vandenbrande et al. 2017
, with values varying by 20–30%
Kim et al. 1981
;
Piquemal et al. 2006
;
Elking et al. 2010
;
Ihm et al. 1990
;
Nobeli et al. 1998
. The density can be separated into atom-centered partial densities
Lillestolen and Wheatley 2009
;
Verstraelen et al. 2016
so that different atom type parameters (typically 20-30 atom types) can be used with simple combination rules
Mitchell and Price 2000
;
Van Vleet et al. 2016
;
Rackers and Ponder 2019
. However, the fact that the parameter for a single element can vary by as much as a factor of 2 suggests that transferability is limited to similar molecules, or even that every molecule must be fit individually
Van Vleet et al. 2016
;
Chung and Ponder 2024a
. The large number of parameters in these density overlap models hinders their transferability and the ability to fit them on high-accuracy
ab initio
methods.

In this Letter, we show that removing the density from core/semicore molecular orbitals reduces the variation in the
K
parameter of the density overlap model. This enables us to use a model with a single universal parameter to achieve sub-kcal/mol accuracy on a benchmark dataset of small organic molecules. We then show that the model extends to elements and atom types not present in the training dataset and to larger acene dimers.

First, we illustrate the general behavior of partial density overlap models by examining F
2
and uracil homodimers. We fit
K

We observe that the exchange-repulsion PES changes only slightly for density overlap models using different partial densities. The mean relative difference between the all-electron model and the valence model is 6%. However, removing the contribution of the low-energy orbitals to the density overlap lowers the total overlap by 30% for the valence model, as demonstrated by the commensurate increase in the value of
K
for the valence model. Since the distance dependence of the density overlap between the low-energy molecular orbitals of one molecule and the highest occupied orbitals of the other (the largest part of the overlap that is neglected) is similar to the distance dependence of the valence density overlap, a constant prefactor can effectively account for the missing contributions within this restricted distance regime. Importantly, while the PES remains largely unaltered, we observe that the difference between the fitted
K
parameters of the two dimers decreases by using the valence density. This illustrates the possibility that using a partial density can increase the transferability of the density overlap model.

Second, we test the accuracy of partial density overlap models that use a single universal
K
parameter. Each partial density model is fit on a subset of the S101x7 dataset
Wang et al. 2015
, a dataset containing 101 dimers each sampled at 7 intermolecular distances along a dissociation curve. For the fitting procedure, we use the S66 subset
Rezác et al. 2011
which contains 66 combinations of 14 small, closed-shell molecules composed of hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen that sample the most common noncovalent interactions in biomolecules. The remaining five neutral molecule dimers containing hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen, together with the five fluorinated hydrocarbon dimers in S101x7, form a test set used to evaluate whether the model is transferable to new molecules. We neglect the configurations which have a distance of 0.7
R_{eq}
, where
R_{eq}
is the equilibrium dimer distance, for all dimers as these configurations have interaction energies that are 30 kcal/mol above the minima and are thus not accessible at room temperature.

The results are presented in Table

We see that the models which remove the core electrons, the 2s electrons of oxygen, or the 2s electrons of oxygen and nitrogen all have lower root mean square errors (RMSE) than the all-electron density. The model with the oxygen and nitrogen 2s orbitals removed has the best results, and we call this model the anisotropic valence density overlap (AVDO) model. The AVDO model is nearly twice as accurate as the all-electron model and has a relative RMSE of 7% for equilibrium geometries with respect to the standard deviation of the SAPT(DFT) reference. When applied to the test set, we see that the model maintains its accuracy, with a relative RMSE of 9%. This central result demonstrates that AVDO is not only accurate, but also transferable to molecules containing atom types, such as fluorine, that are not included in the training data set.

Further analyzing the results, we see in Table
2
that the error for all the density overlap models increases as the intermolecular distance decreases. Although density overlap models have the same exponential dependence as the exchange repulsion energy, they have the wrong polynomial prefactor
Misquitta and Stone 2016
;
Rackers and Ponder 2019
and thus will not be accurate on the entire domain of intermolecular distances (see the SM for tests using Boltzmann weighting in the fitting procedure to compensate for this). As to whether certain types of molecules give larger errors, we note that dimers with two hydrogen bonds have large relative errors and have the largest absolute errors. The dimers with two hydrogen bonds have smaller intermolecular distances than the other dimers and thus have large errors due to the aforementioned distance dependence error of density overlap models. In particular, the acetic acid dimer has a relative RMSE of 12% when the intermolecular distance is 0.8
R_{eq}
(this is the point that is farthest to the upper right corner in Fig.
3
). The relative RMSE for this dimer decreases to 2% at
R_{eq}
.

To put these results in the context of other exchange-repulsion models, the overlap repulsion model in the AMOEBA force field
Rackers and Ponder 2019
;
Chung and Ponder 2024a
yields an RMSE of 0.4 kcal/mol for equilibrium configurations of the full s101x7 dataset using 26 atom types, for a total of 78 fitted parameters. The isotropic Slater-ISA density overlap model
Van Vleet et al. 2016
used a dataset of smaller organic molecules than the ones in the S101x7 dataset and has an RMSE of 0.2 kcal/mol in the attractive region using 36 atom type parameters. Thus, our model drastically reduces the number of fitted parameters and is transferable to new molecules at the cost of being slightly less accurate near equilibrium and about 1 kcal/mol worse for highly repulsive configurations

Having shown that the AVDO model is transferable across a broad range of organic molecules, we now discuss the reason for this transferability. As shown in the SM, the distribution of optimal
K
parameters for each dimer is narrower for the AVDO model than for the all-electron model. A simple explanation for the transferability is that removing low-energy orbitals based on the elements in the molecule acts as an implicit atom type which modifies the overlap density for each pair of atoms. However, this simple explanation does not explain why the number of orbitals removed for each atom type matches chemical intuition. To gain more insight about errors in the density overlap model, we first consider noble gas homodimers starting from the orbital overlap model
Murrell et al. 1965
;
Rackers and Ponder 2019
,

E_{exch}=\frac{K_{2}}{r_{AB}}\sum_{ab}\absolutevalue{ \bra{\psi_a }\ket{\psi_b}}^{2},

where
r_{AB}
is the interatomic distance and
K_{2}
is a unitless parameter that is nearly equal to one
Murrell et al. 1965
. This approximation is motivated by the form of the SAPT exchange-repulsion energy, Eq.
4
. Using the mean-geometric inequality (assuming that the wavefunctions are real),

\bigg(\int\psi_{a}\psi_{b}\differential[3]{r}\bigg)^{2}\leq V\int\psi_{a}^{2}\psi_{b}^{2}\differential[3]{r},

(5)

where
V
is the volume of the integral, we find that

\frac{K_{2}}{r_{AB}}\sum_{ab}\absolutevalue{ \bra{\psi_a }\ket{\psi_b}}^{2}\leq\frac{K_{2}V}{r_{AB}}\int\rho_{A}\rho_{B}\differential{r}.

(6)

The more orbital pairs with zero overlap due to nodes in the wavefunction, the more the density overlap overestimates the orbital overlap approximation
Söderhjelm et al. 2006
;
Henrichsmeyer et al. 2023
. Since heavier atoms have more orbitals, the density overlap will overestimate the orbital overlap approximation more. For this reason, we speculate that removing the overlaps of the core orbitals lowers the density overlap in a way that systematically cancels with this overestimation.

Eq.
6
also provides an explanation for the magnitude of the value of the
K
parameter, which is 32.4 Bohr
2
for the all-electron density overlap model. We approximate the volume
V
as a cylinder between two atoms with a radius defined by the atom’s van der Waals radius,
V=\pi r_{vdW}^{2}r_{AB}
. Since
K\approx K_{2}V/r_{AB}
and
K_{2}\approx 1
,
K
is approximately the cross-sectional area of an atom. Using a van der Waals radius of 2.8 Bohr yields
K\approx 25
Bohr
2
. This explanation can be extended to molecules by starting with localized Boys orbitals
Boys 1960
rather than molecular orbitals in Eq.
5
. The resulting inequality is the same as Eq.
6
, where
V
can still be approximated as a cylinder between two atoms rather than the volume of the overlap region between the two molecules.

As to the computational expense and possible applications of the AVDO model, in principle, the AVDO model provides the exchange-repulsion PES at the cost of a single DFT calculation for the electron density for each monomer, though here a DFT calculation is done for every configuration because we use dimer basis sets (see the SM for results of similar accuracy using lighter, monomer basis sets). Recently, machine learning methods have been developed that can predict the electron density
Brockherde et al. 2017
;
Bogojeski et al. 2018
;
Grisafi et al. 2018
;
Fabrizio et al. 2019
, with state-of-the-art models achieving an integrated absolute error in the density of 0.002 per an electron on molecules from the QM9 dataset
Ramakrishnan et al. 2014
;
Jørgensen and Bhowmik 2022
which were not present in the training dataset
Jørgensen and Bhowmik 2022
;
Qiao et al. 2022
;
Koker et al. 2024
;
Fu et al. 2024
. In a future publication, we will show that the AVDO model can take advantage of these recent developments so that no DFT calculation is needed. An added benefit of using a machine-learned model is that it can represent the density using a density-fitting basis
Brockherde et al. 2017
;
Grisafi et al. 2018
;
Unke et al. 2021b
;
Qiao et al. 2022
;
Rackers et al. 2023
;
Fu et al. 2024
and this enables the efficient calculation of the density overlap. We estimate that with a density-fit basis, the AVDO model would be on par with the cost of machine-learned potentials (about 3 orders of magnitude slower than a Born-Mayer model). Given this cost, we think that this model will be useful for molecular docking, geometry optimization, and the generation of datasets for machine-learning. As to whether this model can be fit on higher accuracy reference methods, we find that the parameter
K
is relatively insensitive to changes in the density tail due to different levels of theory (see Fig. 3 in the SM). It is also known that MP2 exchange-repulsion energies are proportional to Hartree-Fock exchange-repulsion energies
Hodges and Wheatley 2000
and, additionally, it has been shown that CCSD densities can be predicted by machine-learned models
Rackers et al. 2023
. This indicates that the model has the potential to be used/fitted on higher accuracy ab-initio methods.

In conclusion, we show that using the valence density instead of the all-electron density enables us to create a model for Pauli exchange-repulsion using a single universal fitted parameter: the anisotropic valence density overlap (AVDO) model. The AVDO model achieves sub-kcal/mol accuracy for near equilibrium dimers, even for molecules containing “atom types” that are absent from its training dataset and evidence suggests that the AVDO model is applicable to larger molecules as well. These advantages come at the cost of being more expensive than simpler models of exchange-repulsion. The AVDO model provides insight into how to improve the transferability of these other models and, more importantly, the model can be integrated in a machine-learned (ML) force field framework, where the additional cost is relatively moderate and is far outweighed by the benefit of having a model with excellent transferability. Integrating the AVDO model with an ML model for the density would make the AVDO model fully predictive. This approach has the advantage that it replaces machine learning dimer interaction energies with monomer densities, resulting in a significant reduction in the size of the dataset needed. This work brings us one step closer to transferable, high-accuracy force fields for biological applications such as drug discovery and for generating high-accuracy, synthetic data for ML force fields.
