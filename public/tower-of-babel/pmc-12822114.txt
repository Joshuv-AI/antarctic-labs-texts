Co-delivery of camptothecin and SH3RF3-AS1 SiRNA for glioma synergistic therapy
Authors: Shun Wang, Zhaochao Nie, Lei Liang, Jing Zou, Zhiyue Zhang, Donghai Wang, Chuncheng Qu, Cheng Wang, Jun Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent treatment strategies for high-grade gliomas have a limited overall patient benefit. Here, Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) of glioma was conducted to screen the lncRNA regulated by m6A modification in glioma, and provide an innovative chemotherapeutic strategy to improved the therapeutic efficacy.MethodsTo elucidate the impact and the molecular mechanism of ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1/SH3RF3 regulation on glioma malignancy, proliferation phenotypic assays, MeRIP-qPCR, RIP-qPCR and western blotting were used. Expressions and clinical significances of ALKBH5, SH3RF3-AS1, and SH3RF3 were assessed in tissue chip. Furthermore, we designed a nanodrug (NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251) for the targeted co-delivery of CPT (targeting type Ⅰ topoisomerase) and si-SH3RF3-AS1 (inhibiting SH3RF3, a Rac1-JNK activator) in glioma treatment.ResultsSH3RF3-AS1 was demethylated and overexpressed in glioma. We elucidated the mechanistic link between ALKBH5-mediated N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification and SH3RF3-AS1 expression, and the cis-regulation of SH3RF3-AS1 to SH3RF3. Clinically, the ALKBH5, SH3RF3-AS1, and SH3RF3 expression increased with increasing glioma grade, high SH3RF3-AS1 expression correlated with unfavorable 5-year overall survival and progression-free survival in a cohort of 148 glioma patients. SH3RF3-AS1 drived glioma progression and promoted chemoresistance to camptothecin (CPT). We designed a nanodrug (NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251) for the targeted co-delivery of CPT (targeting type Ⅰ topoisomerase) and si-SH3RF3-AS1 (inhibiting SH3RF3, a Rac1-JNK activator) in glioma treatment, and it effectively suppressed glioma cell growth both in vivo and in vitro models.ConclusionOur findings provide valuable insights into the roles of m6A demethylation-mediated upregulation of SH3RF3-AS1 in supporting glioma proliferation and demonstrate the potential of NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 as an innovative chemotherapeutic strategy for glioma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04119-9.

Background

Gliomas are highly invasive and neurologically destructive and are considered to be among the deadliest human cancers. Current treatment strategies for high-grade gliomas have a limited overall patient benefit, and the survival time is typically measured in months [1]. The deeply infiltrative nature of glioma cells, ineffective delivery of chemotherapeutic agents across the blood-brain barrier, and associated dose-limiting systemic toxicities remain major causes of patient mortality. Effective drug therapy after successful surgery is one of the most effective methods for treating malignant glioma. A possible strategy may be combined treatment with chemotherapeutic drugs and RNA drugs inhibiting cell proliferation.

Major biological roles of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) impacted metabolism, oncogenesis, and other activities. And epigenetic regulation is among the main mechanisms regulating ncRNA expression and tissue specificity. Indeed, epigenetic treatment was a promising therapeutic approach for these aggressive tumors [2]. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most common form of RNA modification and influences the processing, expression, nuclear export, translation and even decay of RNA transcripts [3]. Discoveries made via methylated RNA immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (MeRIP-seq) suggest that m6A modification patterns can predict the histologic grade, abundance of infiltrating immune cells and genetic variation, thus playing a key role in the early diagnosis, targeted therapy and prognostic evaluation of gliomas [4–7]. It was reported that m6A methylation was reduced in glioma tissues and that increasing the m6A level via methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) overexpression in glioma cell lines could impair the proliferation and migration while increasing the apoptosis of these cells [8]. The m6A demethylase alkB homolog 5 (ALKBH5) is highly expressed in glioblastoma stem-like cells (GSCs) and maintains GSC tumorigenicity [9]. ALKBH5 expression has also been found to be significantly positively correlated with the immune cell composition and immune signatures [10]. However, the extent and effect of m6A-mediated regulation of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) expression on glioma progression, especially in terms of chemotherapeutic efficacy, have not been fully elucidated.

Topoisomerases, classified as type Ⅰ and type Ⅱ topoisomerases, are ubiquitous nuclear enzymes involved in regulating the topological state of DNA [11]. They are expedient and strategic molecular targets of anticancer drugs. Topoisomerase I inhibitors, such as irinotecan (camptothecin-11, CPT-11), have been used in glioma treatment. Recent clinical trials have shown major antitumor activity in patients with recurrent glioblastoma when the antiangiogenic drug bevacizumab is combined with CPT-11 [12]. Rac1 might be a major factor in the regulation of type Ⅱ topoisomerases [13]. Activation of the Rac1-JNK signaling pathway was found to suppress apoptosis and promote the proliferation of glioma cells [14]. Mialon demonstrated the cooperation of the JNK pathway and topoisomerase Ⅰ in the positive regulation of HT-1080 cell proliferation [15]. SH3RF3 was found to act as a scaffold for a multiprotein complex that transduces signals from GTP-loaded Rac1 to activate JNK [16]. Targeting the SH3RF3/Rac1/JNK axis might thus be an effective method for treating malignant glioma.

Here, we reveal abnormalities in the m6A epitranscriptome in glioma via MeRIP-seq, and our results emphasize that ALKBH5 mediates the upregulation of SH3RF3-AS1 for its stabilization and that SH3RF3-AS1 further exacerbates the malignant biological behavior of glioma cells through upregulation of SH3RF3. Moreover, SH3RF3-AS1 upregulation results in CPT-11 resistance. Treatment with a nanodrug incorporating si-SH3RF3-AS1 (which inhibits SH3RF3, a Rac1-JNK activator) and CPT (which inhibits topoisomerase Ⅰ) (named NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251) constitutes a foundation for glioma therapy.

Results

MeRIP-seq revealed that SH3RF3-AS1 is a glioma-specific target for m6A-mediated regulation

To construct a transcriptome-wide m6A map of glioma tissues, we performed m6A-seq analysis of three paired tumor and normal tissues (GEO: GSE277894). MeRIP-seq analysis identified 24324 m6A peaks in normal tissues and 23351 m6A peaks in glioma tissues. The m6A signals occurred primarily near the coding (CDSs) and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs) of mRNA transcripts in both normal and tumor tissues.

Specifically, MeRIP-seq analysis showed that m6A peaks were most abundant near the CDS (34.7%) and 3’UTR (37.3%), followed by the stop codon (19.8%), in normal tissues. Similarly, in tumor tissues, m6A peaks were most abundant near the CDS (37.7%) and 3’UTR (34.5%), followed by the stop codon (20.2%) (Fig. 1A and B). To determine whether the m6A peaks were located at RRACH (where R represents a purine base, A represents an m6A-modified base and H represents a nonguanine base) consensus sequences, the top 1000 enriched peaks in glioma and normal tissues were examined, and highly enriched, conserved consensus motifs were identified in both cell types (Fig. 1C). MeRIP-seq analysis identified 4470 differentially m6A modified peaks between glioma tissues and normal tissues. Peaks in protein-coding transcripts accounted for a major percentage of the identified peaks (93.94%; 4199); peaks in lncRNA transcripts, for 1.41% (63); and peaks in pseudogenes, for 0.58% (26); in addition, 4.07% of the total sequenced peaks were in other types of transcripts or unannotated transcripts. Most of the genes exhibited increased m6A levels (Fig. 1D), and m6A modifications were also identified in previously reported glioma-associated lncRNAs and mRNAs (Table S3). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of glioma tissue indicated that transcription and regulation of transcription were the terms most strongly enriched in these differentially m6A modified genes (Fig. 1E), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed enrichment in the pathways proteoglycans in cancer, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum and cancer signaling pathway (Fig. 1F). Correlation analysis between genes with differential m6A modification and genes with differential RNA expression was performed, and the 10 genes with the largest differences in m6A peaks (scored in terms of the |log2FC| value) are shown in Fig. 1G. SH3RF3-AS1 was the only glioma-specific lncRNA target. Using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) tool, we assessed the peak position and peak annotation of SH3RF3-AS1 (Fig. 1H) and determined that SH3RF3-AS1 had only one significant m6A peak, in the middle of the exon.

The regulatory role of ALKBH5 in SH3RF3-AS1 demethylation

Next, MeRIP-PCR analysis with specific primers targeting the potential m6A sites was performed to confirm the abundance of m6A in SH3RF3-AS1 in glioma and normal tissues (Fig. 2A). Significant inhibition of m6A modification was observed in glioma tissues (4.85 vs. 9.16, P = 0.03) (m6A site: chr2:109,128,916), and SH3RF3-AS1 was hypomethylated in glioma tissues.

ALKBH5 is a principal demethylase involved in the removal of m6A modifications. To investigate whether ALKBH5 affects SH3RF3-AS1 m6A modification, the interaction between SH3RF3-AS1 and ALKBH5 was investigated via RIP. ALKBH5 noticeably enriched SH3RF3-AS1 (6.33 vs. 0.44, P = 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Since SH3RF3-AS1 could thus be a direct substrate of ALKBH5, we investigated the mechanism by which ALKBH5 regulates SH3RF3-AS1 expression. We first measured the change in total SH3RF3-AS1 expression upon ALKBH5 overexpression. Both U251 cells and primary glioma G1 cells overexpressing ALKBH5 displayed approximately 20–30% greater expression of SH3RF3-AS1 than the corresponding control cells (Fig. 2C). m6A modification has been reported to modulate the stability of RNAs, including lncRNAs. Furthermore, in the RNA stability assay, we found that ALKBH5 overexpression induced a decrease in the degradation rate of SH3RF3-AS1 after 90 min of actinomycin D treatment (84.8% vs. 80.3%, P = 0.02) (Fig. 2D).

SH3RF3-AS1 upregulated SH3RF3 in glioma

We initially evaluated the subcellular localization of SH3RF3-AS1 in U87 and U251 cells. As shown in Fig. 2E, SH3RF3-AS1 was distributed in both the nucleus and cytoplasm but mainly in the nucleus, suggesting that it functions posttranscriptional modulation. To identify the RNA that interacts with SH3RF3-AS1, bioinformatics analysis based on TCGA data was performed, and the potential target genes of SH3RF3-AS1 included 6 trans-acting genes and 1 cis-acting gene. As shown in Table S5, SH3RF3-AS1 expression was positively correlated with SH3RF3 mRNA expression (r = 0.73, P < 0.001) in high-grade glioma. In a further study, we focused on the mechanism by which SH3RF3-AS1 regulates SH3RF3 in cis based on their sequence complementarity.

To determine the biological function of the ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1/SH3RF3 axis in glioma, two siRNAs targeting SH3RF3-AS1, an ALKBH5 overexpression vector, and an SH3RF3-AS1 overexpression vector were used (verification of changes in expression is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1A). SH3RF3-AS1 siRNA transfection significantly inhibited the expression of SH3RF3 in U251 cells, SH3RF3-AS1 overexpression resulted in SH3RF3 mRNA upregulation, and SH3RF3-AS1 knockdown resulted in SH3RF3 mRNA downregulation (Fig. 2F). SH3RF3-AS1 overexpression induced a decrease in the degradation rate of SH3RF3 mRNA after 4 h of actinomycin D treatment (61.5% vs. 31.7%, P = 0.04) (Fig. 2G). Taken together, these data reveal that SH3RF3-AS1 facilitates SH3RF3 expression by maintaining the stability of SH3RF3 mRNA.

ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1/SH3RF3 overexpression resulted in a poor prognosis in glioma patients

Thereafter, the ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1/SH3RF3 expression levels were measured in 148 glioma tissues via a tissue array. The clinical characteristics of the corresponding glioma patients are shown in Table 1. ALKBH5 protein expression was greater in WHO grade Ⅲ tissues than in WHO grade Ⅰ and Ⅱ tissues (grade Ⅲ vs. Ⅰ: 73.70 vs. 33.80, P < 0.001; grade Ⅲ vs. Ⅱ: 73.70 vs. 63.92, P = 0.005), and also greater in WHO grade Ⅳ tissues than in WHO grade Ⅰ tissues (70.10 vs. 33.80, P = 0.009). ALKBH5 expression was significantly greater in high-grade glioma (grades Ⅲ and Ⅳ) samples than in low-grade glioma (grades Ⅰ and Ⅱ) samples (73.00 vs. 57.56, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). SH3RF3-AS1 expression was significantly greater in high-grade glioma samples than in low-grade glioma samples (16.55 vs. 12.54, P = 0.002). Moreover, as shown in Fig. 3A, SH3RF3-AS1 expression was significantly increased in grade Ⅲ glioma samples compared with grade Ⅰ (19.43 vs. 10.90, P < 0.0001) or grade Ⅱ (19.43 vs. 14.11, P = 0.003) glioma samples. No significant difference in SH3RF3-AS1 expression was observed between grade Ⅳ and grade Ⅲ (14.11 vs. 19.43, P > 0.05) glioma samples, although the SH3RF3-AS1 level was correlated with WHO grade (r = 0.23, P = 0.006). Furthermore, SH3RF3 expression was significantly greater in high-grade glioma samples than in low-grade glioma samples (5.01 vs. 1.57, P = 0.03). As shown in Fig. 3A, compared with grade Ⅰ glioma tissues, grade Ⅲ glioma tissues demonstrated a significant increase in the SH3RF3 level (4.84 vs. 1.47, P = 0.04). No significant difference in SH3RF3 expression was observed between grade Ⅳ and grade Ⅲ glioma samples (4.84 vs. 4.32, P > 0.05), although the SH3RF3 level was correlated with WHO grade (r = 0.25, P = 0.002). Next, we investigated the role of ALKBH5 in 5-year overall survival and progression-free survival in patients with glioma using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. An ALKBH5 expression level higher than the median value in glioma samples was associated with decreased progression-free survival (PFS) relative to an ALKBH5 expression level lower than the median value (P = 0.04, Fig. 3B), but ALKBH5 expression was not associated with overall survival (OS) (P > 0.05). Moreover, the ALKBH5 expression level was correlated with WHO grade (r = 0.30, P < 0.001) and the SH3RF3-AS1 expression level (r = 0.19, P = 0.02). The percentage of SH3RF3-AS1-positive cells in tissues with low ALKBH5 expression was lower than that in tissues with high ALKBH5 expression (13.84 vs. 15.80, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). Next, we investigated the role of SH3RF3-AS1 and SH3RF3 in 5-year overall survival and progression-free survival in glioma patients using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Compared with expression levels lower than the median value, SH3RF3-AS1 and SH3RF3 expression levels higher than the median value were associated with shorter OS and PFS times (P = 0.04, P = 0.009; P = 0.04, P = 0.01; Fig. 3D and E). Typical images of immunofluorescence (IF) staining (ALKBH5 and SH3RF3) and in situ hybridization assay (SH3RF3-AS1) are shown in Fig. 3F.

Moreover, we conducted multivariate Cox regression analysis using SH3RF3-AS1 expression, ALKBH5 expression and clinical variables for all glioma patients, and the analysis identified high SH3RF3-AS1 expression and high tumor grade as independent factors for overall survival after adjustment for age, sex, and Ki67, EGFR, and PDL1 expression (Table S6). Overall, our results indicate that SH3RF3-AS1 is an independent prognostic factor for glioma and that the ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1 axis plays an important role in glioma.

The ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1 axis promotes cell proliferation and CPT-11 resistance in glioma cells

In U251 cells and primary glioma G1 cells, overexpression of ALKBH5 and SH3RF3-AS1 increased cell growth and increased DNA replication, and loss of SH3RF3-AS1 in glioma cells attenuated cell proliferation and DNA replication (Fig. 4A). We analyzed cell cycle progression by flow cytometry. The percentage of cells in S phase was increased after ALKBH5 or SH3RF3-AS1 was overexpressed in U251 cells (ALKBH5: 53.2% vs. 44.73%, P = 0.0002; SH3RF3-AS1: 52.05% vs. 44.73%, P = 0.004) and primary glioma G1 cells (ALKBH5: 44.36% vs. 33.64%, P = 0.002; SH3RF3-AS1: 37.68% vs. 33.64%, P > 0.05); accordingly, the percentage of cells in G0/G1 phase were obviously decreased (Fig. 4B). Moreover, cell cycle analysis revealed that knockdown of SH3RF3-AS1 increased the percentage of G0/G1-phase cells among both U251 cells (si-SH3RF3-AS1-1: 60.97% vs. 57.36%, P = 0.01; si-SH3RF3-AS1-2: 68.38% vs. 57.36%, P = 0.0005) and primary glioma G1 cells (si-SH3RF3-AS1-1: 68.26% vs. 57.03%, P = 0.007; si-SH3RF3-AS1-2: 63.60% vs. 57.03%, P = 0.03), whereas it decreased the percentage of cells in S phase (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these results suggest that ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1 critically upregulates glioma cell proliferation. Moreover, relatively low IC50 values for CPT-11 were observed in SH3RF3-AS1-depleted primary glioma G1 cells and U251 cells (Fig. S1B), while SH3RF3-AS1 overexpression resulted in high IC50 values for CPT-11 (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these data indicate that SH3RF3-AS1 knockdown might enhance therapeutic sensitivity to CPT-11 in glioma.

Inhibitory effects of NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 on glioma

We further prepared NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 to achieve targeted codelivery of CPT and si-SH3RF3-AS1 to enhance the bioavailability and efficacy of the drug/gene combination approach. An agarose gel block experiment was used to verify whether the siRNA was successfully carried by the PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT nanoparticles (NPs) prepared by the process described above. As shown in Fig. S1C, this siRNA could be completely compressed compared with free siRNA at a ratio of 20:1 (w/w). The size, zeta potential and morphology of the PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA@U251 particles were evaluated via dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA@U251 had a particle size of 200.9 ± 4.371 nm and a uniform distribution with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.316 ± 0.088 (Fig. 5A, Table S7). The final siRNA concentration was fixed at 50 nM, and the CPT concentration was 20 µM. First, U251 cells were treated with PBS, NanoPCPT@U251, SH3RF3-AS1 siRNA-single nanoparticles (NanoPCPT/siRNA-1@U251 or NanoPCPT/siRNA-2@U251), or SH3RF3-AS1 siRNA-mixed nanoparticles (NanoPCPT/siRNA-1 + 2@U251) for 24 h. A CCK-8 assay was then performed to determine the inhibitory effects of the different nanoparticles. The SH3RF3-AS1 siRNA-mixed nanoparticles exhibited a greater inhibitory effect on proliferation than the SH3RF3-AS1 siRNA-single nanoparticles (Fig. 5B). Second, U251 and G1 cells were treated with PBS, Nano-siRNA@U251, free CPT, NanoPCPT@U251 or NanoPCPT/siRNA(-mixed)@U251 for 24 h. Codelivery of CPT and si-SH3RF3-AS1 had the greatest inhibitory effect on the proliferation of U251 and G1 cells (Fig. 5C). In addition, the direct inhibitory effect on tumor cell growth depended mainly on CPT, and SH3RF3-AS1 siRNA had a synergistic effect on proliferation inhibition in vitro.

To analyze the effect of NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 in vivo, U251 luciferase cells were intracranially injected into nude mice, and PBS, Nano@U251, free CPT, NanoPCPT@U251, Nano-siRNA@U251 and NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 were administered to the mice beginning 7 days after cell injection. In vivo imaging of the nude mice revealed that at 7 days after modeling, the nude mice in all six groups exhibited luminescence in vivo, without a significant difference in the luminescence intensity. At 28 days after modeling, the bioluminescence intensity was increased in the mice in all six groups (Fig. 5D and E). We observed that monotherapy with NanoPCPT@U251 or Nano-siRNA@U251 inhibited tumor growth. In contrast, compared with either monotherapy, NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 significantly inhibited tumor growth. H&E staining revealed that the tumor tissues in the PBS and Nano@U251 groups were denser than those in the NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 and Nano-siRNA@U251 groups, while the tumors in the NanoPCPT@U251, Nano-siRNA@U251, and NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 groups displayed more defined borders (Fig. 5F). We observed lower expression of SH3RF3 and Rac1 in Nano-siRNA@U251-treated tissues than in Nano@U251-treated tissues (Fig. 5G). Overall, the results of the in vivo studies corroborated the in vitro observations.

Discussion

Gliomas are virtually incurable because they cannot be cured by surgical resection alone, and the toxicity of current chemotherapies and radiation therapies limits the doses that can be used. Therefore, identifying new tumor-selective therapies to improve clinical outcomes is urgently needed [17]. Molecular biomarkers related to glioma malignancy may have prognostic value for predicting survival outcomes and therapeutic responses. For example, the m6A mRNA methylation machinery might be a promising therapeutic target for glioblastoma, as knockdown of METTL3 or METTL14 dramatically promoted the growth and self-renewal of human GSCs as well as tumorigenesis, while overexpression of METTL3 or inhibition of FTO suppressed GSC growth and self-renewal [5]. ALKBH5 was the second demethylase to be discovered. The latest research indicates that the m6A demethylase ALKBH5 is highly expressed in GSCs and that silencing ALKBH5 suppresses the proliferation of patient-derived GSCs [9]. This finding is consistent with our findings. The m6A level in mRNA is significantly reduced in cells overexpressing ALKBH5, and ALKBH5 affects mRNA export and assembly processes [18]. Herein, we found that ALKBH5 demethylated and upregulated SH3RF3-AS1, SH3RF3-AS1 regulated SH3RF3 in cis, and the ALKBH5/SH3RF3-AS1/SH3RF3 axis promoted cell proliferation. This discovery is expected to contribute to improving the prognosis and treatment of glioma.

Classically, the modification of proteins by ubiquitin is mediated by enzymes that catalyze ubiquitin activation (E1s), conjugation (E2s) and ligation to protein targets (E3s), as well as by deubiquitinating enzymes. Alterations in E3s result in changes in the proteolytic ubiquitin-proteasome system, protein quality control, protein trafficking and other ubiquitin-driven pathways and affects fundamental biological processes that are linked to human diseases. E3 ubiquitin ligases have been linked to the regulation of Rac1 and are implicated mainly in the proteasome-mediated degradation of active Rac1. However, the E3 ligase TRAF6 was suggested to activate Rac1 via K63-linked polyubiquitination at K16 [19]. SH3RF3 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that mediates Rac1 ubiquitination and JNK activation [16]. A strong positive correlation was also detected between the activity and ubiquitination level of Rac1 [20]. The Ras homologous family of small guanosine triphosphate-binding enzymes (GTPases) is critical for cell migration and proliferation. Regulating RAC1 is a feasible strategy to improve glioblastoma treatment [21]. Genetic interference with Rac1 has been reported to potentially enhance the inhibitory effect of erlotinib on glioblastoma cell proliferation [22]. Therefore, SH3RF3-AS1 promotes glioma cell proliferation by regulating SH3RF3/Rac1. Combination therapies against multiple targets can reduce the probability of resistance development as well as side effects, owing to the ability to use lower doses of each agent, given the synergistic effect of the combinations. CPT analogs that act on type Ⅰ topoisomerases are the final line of therapy for temozolomide-resistant glioma patients. Here, we used si-SH3RF3-AS1 (which inhibits Rac1-JNK activation and targets type Ⅱ topoisomerases) in combination with CPT (which targets type I topoisomerases) to treat glioma. It was also verified that a nanodrug incorporating si-SH3RF3-AS1 and CPT effectively inhibited the growth of glioma cells in vivo and in vitro.

Invasive glioma usually disrupts the integrity of the BBB, allowing the delivery of drugs to brain tumors; however, sufficient targeting ability is still urgently needed to improve drug accumulation in glioma tumors. Among the recently emerging targeting techniques, coating drug-encapsulated nanoparticles with homotypic cancer cell membranes has been shown to be one of the most efficient strategies to specifically target tumor cells. In our study, PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA nanoparticles were coated with U251 cell membranes to endow nanoparticles with targeting ability. Moreover, triblock copolymeric micelles carrying the chemotherapeutic drug camptothecin and a siRNA (PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA) achieved on-demand drug release by responding to reduction stimuli, with no significant changes observed in the body weights of NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251-treated mice.

Methods

MeRIP-seq and data analysis

Surgical samples were obtained from glioma patients and patients with traumatic head injury (normal tissues). To assess the difference in the m6A-modified transcriptome between glioma tissues and normal tissues, m6A-meRIP followed by high-throughput sequencing of 3 paired glioma and normal tissues (Table S1) was performed. MeRIP-seq and data analysis were performed by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd.

MeRIP-PCR analysis

MeRIP was performed using a Magna Methylated RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit m6A Kit (Merck Millipore, USA). All the experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After RNA purification, RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) were performed. The primers used for RT-qPCR target amplification are listed in Table S2.

Cell culture

The human GBM cell line U251 and primary glioma cells were used in this study. The human GBM cell lines U251 and U251-luc were kindly provided by the Department of Neurosurgery, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University. U251 cells were routinely cultured at 37℃ in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Primary cultures of G1 glioma cells were established by cutting fresh brain tumor tissues into 1–2 mm, mechanically disrupting the pieces, plating the dispersed cells into T25 flasks, and incubating the cells in DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37℃. The medium was changed every 2–3 days. The cells were split at 80–90% confluence. The cells were authenticated by Giemsa staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (Supplementary Fig. S1D). A portion of the cells was frozen.

Plasmids, SiRNAs and antibodies

The SH3RF3-AS1 and ALKBH5 expression vectors were constructed using the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. Two siRNAs targeting SH3RF3-AS1 (si-SH3RF3-AS1-1 and si-SH3RF3-AS1-2) and a negative control siRNA (NC) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The siRNA sequences used are listed in Table S2. Anti-Rac1 (Merck, clone 23A8, Germany), anti-SH3RF3 (Abcam, ab184977, USA), and anti-ALKBH5 (Abcam, ab195377, USA) were purchased from the indicated suppliers.

Tissue chip analysis and RNAscope® assay

SH3RF3 and ALKBH5 expressions of glioma patients were evaluated detection using multicolor immunofluorescence on tissue chip (Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd., China). The SH3RF3-AS1 expressions of glioma patients was detected by RNAscope assay, the in situ tissue chip hybridization assay of SH3RF3-AS1 was performed by Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd., with an RNAscope kit and the probe of SH3RF3-AS1 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, USA).

Drug sensitivity assessment

The indicated cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells/well. After overnight incubation, the cells were continuously exposed to increasing concentrations of irinotecan (CPT-11; Qilu Pharmaceutical, China) for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed with a CCK-8 assay to determine the IC50 value.

Synthesis of PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA

Synthesis of PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT was in the Supporting Information. PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT (8.3 mg) was dissolved in DMSO (0.5 mL) to yield a homogeneous solution. Subsequently, siRNA (4 OD) was dissolved in DMSO (0.5 mL). The above two solutions were added dropwise to 4 mL of deionized water, and the resulting solution was sonicated for 30 min. The solution was dialyzed overnight against deionized water to remove DMSO.

Characterization of PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT binding to SiRNA

Sodium acetate buffer (pH = 3.7) containing different concentrations of micelles was mixed with the aqueous siRNA solution to obtain the desired polymer/siRNA mass ratio (5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 or 60). The mixture was then vortexed well for 30 s and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow the formation of complexes by electrostatic interactions. To evaluate the ability of the micelles to condense siRNA, complexes with different composition ratios were analyzed by separation by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis at 80 mV under native conditions for 40 min, followed by observation of the migration of the complexes using a Bio-Rad imaging system.

Acquisition of cell membranes

U251 cells were collected, washed three times in PBS, dispersed in the appropriate volume of hypotonic buffer solution and resuspended in 3 mL of hypotonic buffer solution (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH = 7.5), 10 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM PMSF) at −80 ℃. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 5 min and was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h. The resulting precipitate was collected and washed once with PBS. This cell membrane precipitate was collected and was then resuspended and solubilized in PBS. The cell membrane solution was stored at −80 ℃ for backup. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4 ℃.

Synthesis of PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA@U251

Freshly prepared cell membrane fragments were solubilized in PBS buffer and mixed with freshly prepared PEG-PDBAMA-PCPT/siRNA at a 1:1 ratio, and the resulting solution was physically extruded through a porous 400 nm polycarbonate membrane 10 times using a liposome extruder. The product was centrifuged and washed three times with ultrapure water to remove excess cell membrane and was then lyophilized.

In vivo analysis using the U251 orthotopic glioma mouse model

BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. To establish a glioblastoma xenograft model, U251 human glioma cells were intracranially implanted into BALB/c nude mice, and 3 × 105 U251-luc cells were carefully inoculated into the brains of mice (male, 4 weeks old) with a microliter syringe. The growth of orthotopic U251-luc tumors was monitored by bioluminescence imaging (BLI). The day that each mouse was implanted with glioma cells was designated day 0. At weeks one and four, tumor progression in vivo was measured via bioluminescence imaging after intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg luciferin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai). Five minutes after luciferin injection, the signal was detected, and images were acquired with an IVIS Lumina series II ex vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, USA). Nude mice bearing U251-luc cells were randomly divided into six groups (n = 7 mice/group). Starting on day 7, the mice received PBS, Nano@U251, free CPT, NanoPCPT@U251, Nano-siRNA@U251 or NanoPCPT/siRNA@U251 via intraperitoneal injection every three days Additionally, the mice were weighed before drug administration. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed on the basis of BLI.

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney U test or Student’s t test was used to determine the significance of differences between 2 groups. Correlations were identified using Spearman rank correlation analysis. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to generate survival curves. The median values were used as cutoff values to discriminate between patients with high and low expression. To assess the relative risk associated with each factor, Cox regression analyses were performed. All tests were two-sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26 and GraphPad Prism 8.
