What factors affect acceptance attitudes towards vaccination amidst a major public health crisis? A national cross-sectional study
Authors: Li Nan Cheng, Jianhui Kong, Xiao Feng Xie, Feng Ying Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesVaccine hesitancy persists as a significant global health concern, especially during public health crises. This study aimed to explore factors influencing vaccination acceptance during a major public health crisis and establish a model of influencing factors.DesignA cross-sectional study.MethodsAn anonymous cross-sectional survey was conducted online, encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, anxiety levels, personal risk perception and attitudes towards vaccination acceptance. Data analysis was performed using t-tests, one-way analysis of variance and multiple linear regression techniques in IBM SPSS Statistics V.25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).ResultsA total of 3921 participants participated in the survey. The mean acceptance attitudes were above the moderate level (M=4.02, SD=0.51), the mean risk perception score was below the moderate level (M=2.66, SD=0.58), and the mean score on the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale was also below the moderate level (M=2.05, SD=0.53). The results showed that internal variables (gender, age, marital status, professional background, region) and external variables (average family income, distance from home to vaccination site, family medical background) were statistically significant (p<0.05). Gender, region, distance from home to the vaccination site, anxiety, family medical background, risk coping, emotional perception and psychological representation of unusual severity were identified as significant predictors of vaccination acceptance attitudes, with the model explaining 35% of the variance (R²= 0.35, adjusted R²= 0.33). Based on these results, we constructed a theoretical model of influencing factors for vaccine acceptance attitudes.ConclusionsDespite increased acceptance of vaccination during major public health events, factors that both support and hinder acceptance remain. Identifying these factors allows for effective disease control and informs the development of public health strategies and crisis response measures.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Health informatics, Health Equity, Health & safety, Health Surveys

Introduction

Public health crises, characterised by their abrupt and unpredictable nature, can have profound impacts on societies and global healthcare systems. These crises, often triggered by acute infectious disease outbreaks, natural disasters or unforeseen events, overwhelm healthcare infrastructures, leading to delayed provision of routine care and increased mortality rates.1 In such contexts, public attitudes towards vaccination play a pivotal role in disease control and prevention since effective vaccination campaigns are indispensable for mitigating the spread and severity of infectious diseases.2

The acceptance of vaccination is crucial during pandemics and widespread outbreaks.3 Extensive research has unequivocally demonstrated the critical benefits of vaccination, including the reduction in rates of disability, hospitalisations, healthcare costs and mortality.4 5 However, despite these well-documented advantages, vaccine hesitancy remains a significant barrier to public health efforts.6 7 Even the WHO identified vaccine hesitancy as one of the top 10 global health threats in 2019.3 8 9

The acute infectious disease crisis has further intensified vaccine hesitancy, directly affecting vaccine acceptance.10 During the acute respiratory infectious disease pandemic, vaccine acceptance has varied significantly across different regions worldwide.11 Despite the availability of vaccines, the ongoing reluctance or refusal to vaccinate remains prominent. Therefore, to improve the vaccination acceptance rates of acute infectious diseases, it is crucial to understand the factors influencing vaccine acceptance, particularly during large-scale public health crises.

This study was motivated by the urgent need to explore and address the various factors shaping vaccine acceptance attitudes during public health crises. These factors are complex and dynamic. Reports indicate that low vaccination acceptance rates have been observed among young people, females, non-medical students and even some healthcare workers.11 Vaccine acceptance is also influenced by individual beliefs, socioeconomic factors, trust in healthcare systems, communication strategies and the perceived severity of the crisis, posing significant challenges to disease management and control.12,14 Additionally, the availability and accessibility of vaccines, along with public health policies and mandates, further shape public acceptance.13 15 Among these, the most immediate factors influencing vaccine acceptance are risk perception and anxiety, which are modifiable variables during major public health crises.

Studies have shown that risk perception is a key predictor of vaccination acceptance, with individuals who perceive a high or very high risk of infection being more likely to get vaccinated promptly rather than delaying vaccination.14,17 And, anxiety can predict negative attitudes towards vaccination. Sources of anxiety include misinformation about vaccines, concerns over vaccine safety and benefits, fears of commercial profiteering, a preference for natural immunity, personal health concerns and societal pressures. These anxieties can significantly influence decision-making behaviours regarding vaccination.18 By identifying the factors influencing vaccination attitudes, we aim to guide targeted interventions that strengthen public health resilience against emerging threats. Additionally, it is important to consider factors that may be overlooked due to cultural and institutional differences across countries.

Given the complexity of these factors and their potential interactions, conducting a national cross-sectional study provides a comprehensive approach to examining vaccination acceptance attitudes during public health crises. This study mainly aimed to explore the factors affecting vaccination acceptance attitudes amidst major public health crises and establish an influencing factor model. Through understanding these factors, tailored interventions can address vaccine hesitancy and bolster public health resilience against emerging threats.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional design, following the STROBE cross-sectional reporting guidelines.19 Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Prior to the questionnaire survey, participants were presented with two initial questions regarding their agreement to participate in the survey and their consent to be part of an academic study. Only respondents who affirmed these questions were included in the final analysis, ensuring informed consent was obtained.

Data collection

Data collection occurred from June to August 2021 via an online survey conducted on the Sojump platform, the largest online survey platform in China. We employed a self-selection online survey method with a non-probability sampling approach, using social network links for participant recruitment.20 The links were distributed through various online channels, such as social media platforms (eg, WeChat) and email lists, inviting adults aged 18 years or older living in China to participate. Participants voluntarily chose to take part by clicking on the shared survey link.

Quality control

Prior to the main survey, a preliminary investigation was conducted with 30 selected participants to assess the completion time, wording and clarity of the questionnaire. Based on the feedback received, several adjustments were made to improve the instrument’s clarity and effectiveness. For example, some complex terms were simplified to ensure better comprehension, and ambiguous questions were reworded to avoid misinterpretation. Additionally, the structure of certain questions was refined to reduce response bias and improve the flow of the questionnaire, which is consistent with best practices in survey design outlined in the literature.17 Confidentiality and anonymity were ensured through the following measures: (1) Informed consent: participants were informed of the study’s purpose, their rights and the confidentiality procedures, with the option to withdraw at any time. (2) Data collection: no personally identifiable information (eg, names, contact details, IP addresses) was collected, and all responses were anonymized. (3) Secure storage: data were stored on a password-protected server, accessible only to the research team, in accordance with data protection guidelines.

Sample and participants

We calculated the sample size according to multivariate analysis criteria, requiring it to be 5–10 times the number of variables.21 With 53 questionnaire items and 12 demographic characteristics, the sample size needed ranged from 390 to 780, accounting for a 20% non-response rate to ensure reliability and representativeness. Of the 4021 adults who participated via the Questionnaire Star platform, 100 (2.5%) were excluded due to: (a) incomplete responses, (b) participants under 18, (c) lack of consent for the initial questions and (d) consistent selection of the same response options. Thus, 3921 eligible surveys were analysed, meeting the reliability and representativeness criteria.

Instruments

The questionnaire covered demographic details (age, gender, minority, region, marital status, education, income, profession, medical payment methods), risk perception, anxiety and vaccination acceptance attitudes.

Xi et al (2020)22 developed and validated the COVID-19 Risk Perception Scale (PRCPS), providing the foundation for the PRCPS, integrating cognitive–empirical self-theory and the common sense model.22 It comprises nine items across three factors: emotional perception, cognitive judgement and severity representation. PRCPS demonstrates good reliability (Cronbach’s α=0.87), content validity and convergent and discriminant validity. This study added a risk coping factor with two items. Scores range from 11 to 54, with higher scores indicating greater risk perception. Cronbach’s α coefficient for this study was 0.873.

The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale consists of 20 items.23 Some of the items were worded symptomatically positive rated on a 4–1 scale (a little of the time, some of the time, good part of the time and most of the time), and others (items 5, 9, 13, 17 and 19) were symptomatically negative rated on a 1–4 scale. The total score ranged from 25 to 100. The total score was determined as the original score multiplied by 1.25. Anxiety was classified as none (25–49 points), mild (50–59 points), moderate (60–69 points) and severe (70–100 points). The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.865.24

Acceptance Attitudes of Vaccination is a 22-item self-report scale developed by researchers and teams based on literature review, focus group discussion and expert consultation, with higher scores representing positive acceptance attitudes. Responses are provided on a 5-point scale (1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree). Items 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14 and 20 were scored in reverse. The overall score ranges from 22 to 110 points, the content validity of this scale ranged between 0.88 and 1.00, and the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.895.25

Data analysis

The scores for risk perception, anxiety and acceptance attitudes and the descriptive statistics were analysed in SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). T-tests and one-way analysis of variance were used to examine factors influencing acceptance attitudes. Multiple regression analysis identified the regression variables of acceptance attitudes. Independent variables with multiple categories were converted into dummy variables for binary assessment of their impact on the dependent variable (see online supplemental appendix 1). All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Discussion

The study examined acceptance attitudes towards vaccination, risk perception and anxiety, aiming to identify factors influencing vaccination acceptance and establish an influencing factor model. The findings revealed that both internal variables (such as gender, age, marital status, professional background and region) and external variables (including average family income, distance from home to vaccination site and family medical background) significantly influenced acceptance attitudes towards vaccination. Moreover, gender, region, distance from home to vaccination site, anxiety, family medical background, risk coping, emotional perception and psychological representation of unusual severity emerged as predictors of acceptance attitudes. Based on these results, a theoretical model of influencing factors for vaccine acceptance attitudes was developed. This model provides valuable insights into understanding and addressing vaccine hesitancy, contributing to the development of targeted interventions and public health strategies aimed at promoting vaccination acceptance during public health crises.

Overall, the findings of the study suggest that vaccination acceptance was above the moderate level. This positive trend underscores a favourable inclination towards vaccination within the studied population, indicating a strong willingness to engage in preventive measures against infectious diseases through vaccination. Specifically, the above-moderate acceptance attitudes among the Chinese population reflect a strong demand for vaccines and a widespread recognition of their crucial role in pandemic control efforts. As reported by Joshi et al, global vaccine acceptance underwent fluctuations over the course of the year, beginning at 86% in March 2020, declining to 54% in July 2020 and then rebounding to 72% by September 2020.12 Vaccine acceptance fluctuations suggest hesitancy, potentially posing risks in public health emergencies.

Fluctuations in vaccine acceptance over a short period may be due to changing perceptions of safety and efficacy, influenced by evolving information. Media coverage, government communication and individual experiences also play a role, reflecting the complex nature of vaccine acceptance. Studies indicate that distrust in vaccines, their manufacturers, institutions and governments, combined with individual beliefs, emotions and demographic factors such as age, gender, education and socioeconomic status, play a significant role in shaping behaviours and attitudes towards COVID-19 vaccination.27,30 Although this study demonstrates a higher-than-moderate level of vaccine acceptance, it is contingent on the severity of the public health event and the timing of the survey. During periods of heightened public health risk, individuals may prioritise concerns about the severity of the public health event over the safety of the vaccine itself. The timing of this survey coincided with a period when the epidemic had not yet been effectively controlled, thereby resulting in relatively higher vaccine acceptance among respondents. Hence, multiple studies suggest that vaccine acceptance or refusal is subject to diverse factors,1214,19 31 showing variability in response to external influences. This volatility might pose an underlying risk factor for significant public health events, demanding careful consideration.

Although participants in our study exhibited above-moderate acceptance towards vaccination, their risk perception scores were below the moderate level, indicating a disparity between acceptance attitudes and risk perception. This raises questions about the factors influencing individuals’ vaccination attitudes during public health crises. As demonstrated by the findings of Tlale et al, this study revealed a high acceptance rate for the COVID-19 vaccine and a low level of risk perception in Botswana.27 This calls for further research and interventions to understand and address the reasons behind the high acceptance rate and low-risk perception. Smith et al acknowledge the significance of health status in shaping attitudes towards vaccine acceptance.29 Participants’ risk perception significantly influenced their vaccination acceptance, with those perceiving a high or very high risk of infection being more inclined to promptly get vaccinated rather than delaying it. For instance, individuals with chronic diseases showed increased COVID-19 risk perceptions compared with healthy controls. While they expressed a willingness to be vaccinated for protection, concerns about vaccine safety also emerged, particularly among those with autoimmune diseases, who feared adverse reactions. In this study, risk coping and emotional perception significantly influenced acceptance attitudes towards vaccination in this study. Psychological representation of unusual severity acts as a psychological alert system for potential infectious diseases. Stronger psychological suggestions decrease the likelihood of vaccine rejection. Risk perception in the study is linked to the extent of health damage caused by the virus. Individuals who perceive themselves as healthy enough to withstand the virus may feel safer and less inclined to vaccinate. However, Martinelli and Veltri found that increasing levels of perceived risk and confidence might not be a sufficient strategy to increase vaccine acceptance rates.32 These findings stress the need for personalised vaccination strategies and targeted responses to manage vaccine hesitancy and address public health emergencies effectively.29

Anxiety emerged as a complex predictor of vaccine acceptance attitudes. While univariate analysis indicated that individuals without anxiety had higher acceptance scores, further regression analysis revealed that participants with moderate levels of anxiety were more likely to accept vaccines than those with no anxiety. This suggests a nuanced relationship between anxiety and health behaviour. Individuals experiencing moderate anxiety may perceive vaccines as a means of mitigating potential health threats, leading them to take protective actions.25 33 Conversely, those without anxiety may underestimate the risks of infectious diseases, resulting in lower motivation to get vaccinated. The emotional response to public health crises can thus influence vaccine acceptance, where moderate concern about personal or public health risks may encourage preventive behaviours. This highlights the need for carefully crafted public health messages that balance awareness without inducing excessive fear, which could otherwise lead to vaccine hesitancy.

The observed gender differences in vaccine uptake, with women more likely than men to receive the vaccine, are consistent with prior findings, such as those from Wong et al.34 This can be attributed to women’s generally higher health-seeking behaviour and risk awareness. However, contrasting evidence from Tlale et al highlights that gender-related patterns in vaccine acceptance are not universal.27 Such variations may result from differences in study populations, regional contexts or sociocultural factors influencing gender norms. Recognising and addressing these disparities is important for developing more targeted and equitable vaccination strategies, ensuring broader vaccine access across diverse groups.

As people get older, they are more likely to accept vaccines. Research indicates that individuals aged 60 and above face a higher risk of severe illness or death following infectious diseases.29 35 This could be due to age-related declines in health and immunity, along with the presence of chronic illnesses. Vaccinations were essential in preventing infectious diseases and related mortality in older adults.36 A meta-analysis confirmed that COVID-19 vaccines effectively reduce the risk of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection (OR=0.38) and related deaths among elderly individuals (OR=0.16). Additionally, vaccinated older adults exhibited significantly higher levels of antibody seroconversion (OR=24.42) and geometric mean titre (Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)=0.92) compared with unvaccinated individuals.35 It is worth noting that vaccine-preventable diseases impose a substantial burden on global health, resulting in enduring consequences for those affected, especially older adults who may face functional decline, disability and even mortality.36 It is crucial to underscore to the elderly the significant benefits of vaccination, which far outweigh any potential risks. Therefore, vaccination should be strongly advocated as the primary strategy for managing these diseases and mitigating associated deaths among older adults during major public health crises. This also suggests that age is part of risk perception and that risk perception promotes vaccination willingness. In the same way, participants who had been exposed to areas with the highest risk, had a medical background or family with a medical background had higher acceptance attitudes.

Married participants had higher acceptance attitudes due to family responsibilities and commitments. Measures should be taken according to the characteristics of the participants. Participants who lived in moderately sized cities or counties were more likely to be vaccinated than those who lived in large cities or rural areas.34 37 This is because large cities are the focus of major public health infectious diseases prevention and control with high population mobility and timely prevention and control measures, while rural areas are the areas with the least mobility of people, medium-sized cities are areas where population concentration and movement are relatively difficult to control but also areas where vaccination is easy to access. Participants with an average family income (CNY) of 5001–20 000 yuan had higher acceptance attitudes than those with less than 5000 yuan and more than 20 000 yuan, which is similar to living regions because people with the highest and lowest average family income tend to live in large cities and rural areas, and vaccination is free. Participants do not worry about the price of vaccines, so the availability of vaccines and whether they are necessary become the main reasons. This is confirmed by the following: participants who were more than 1 hour from home to the vaccination site had lower vaccination attitude scores than those who were less than 1 hour away.

In this study, participants with a family medical background exhibited significantly higher vaccine acceptance attitudes compared with those without such a background. This finding aligns with previous research suggesting that individuals with more knowledge or exposure to healthcare settings are often more inclined to trust medical interventions, including vaccinations.38 39 Having a family member with a medical background may increase access to reliable health information, enhance trust in medical recommendations and foster a deeper understanding of the benefits and safety of vaccines. Additionally, people with medical backgrounds are likely to have heightened awareness of public health risks, which may contribute to more proactive health behaviours, such as vaccine uptake. However, some studies have shown that individuals with a medical background also exhibit a certain degree of vaccine hesitancy. The reasons for their hesitation are largely similar to those of the general population, including the perception that vaccines are unnecessary, concerns about potential side effects and doubts about the effectiveness of vaccines.40 41 Therefore, healthcare literacy and familial influence play a critical role in shaping individuals’ attitudes toward vaccinations, highlighting the importance of targeted communication strategies to improve vaccine acceptance in populations without such backgrounds.

According to the results differences and regression analysis, the results are summarised as follows: the factors affecting the attitude towards vaccination are both internal and external reasons, and the internal reasons are the characteristics of the participants, such as gender, age, marital status, professional background and region. External reasons are as follows: average family income (CNY), distance from home to vaccination site and family medical background. Risk perception and anxiety are also internal attribute variables of individuals but are affected by the general demographic characteristics of individuals. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the authors performed the survey online via social networks to reach a wide research population to maintain social distancing. However, this kind of online self-answered method may target only certain sociodemographic groups. Second, the self-report questionnaires may not truly reflect the thoughts of participants due to flaws in this method of data collection. Further studies are needed to investigate the acceptance of vaccination in different periods of the pandemic and to take vaccine efficiency and safety into consideration after the vaccine is made available to the public. Finally, according to the research results, the authors have developed a theoretical model of factors affecting attitudes towards vaccine acceptance, but this model needs theoretical support and verification in subsequent studies.

Conclusions

This study explored the acceptance attitudes of vaccination, risk perception and anxiety, and the factors associated with acceptance attitudes of vaccination. The mean PRCPS score was below the moderate level, the mean acceptance attitudes were above the moderate level. Vaccine acceptance attitudes are influenced by a combination of internal and external factors. Internal factors include gender, age, marital status, professional background and region, while external factors encompass average family income, proximity to vaccination sites and family medical background. Additionally, risk perception and anxiety, which are also internal variables, play a significant role in shaping vaccine acceptance attitudes and are influenced by sociodemographic characteristics. The theoretical model developed from these results indicates that both internal and external factors can directly or indirectly affect individual risk perception and vaccine acceptance, underscoring the importance of these variables in shaping attitudes towards vaccination. For future research, it is recommended to further validate and refine this theoretical model. Longitudinal studies could be conducted to assess changes in these influencing factors over time or during different public health crises. Additionally, future research should consider incorporating other potential factors, such as the influence of social media, cultural differences and public trust in healthcare systems, to better understand the complexity of vaccine hesitancy and acceptance. Ultimately, validating the model with broader data will contribute to the development of more targeted interventions aimed at improving vaccination rates and overall public health outcomes.
