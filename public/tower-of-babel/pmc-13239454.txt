Working to enhance HIV care: integrating implementation science and improvement science for effective quality improvement – a scoping review
Authors: Thomas O’Grady, Michelle Pendill, Megan Urry, Tarik Itum, Riahana Rahman, Clemens Steinbock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveHealthcare systems struggle to balance the need for frontline problem-solving with the requirement for theory-driven strategies that ensure sustainable system-wide improvement. Implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement have emerged as complementary approaches to bridge gaps between evidence and practice, yet their integration remains limited. This scoping review examines how these disciplines intersect and can be applied to enhance HIV care delivery.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of peer-reviewed articles published between 2016 and 2025. From an initial identification of 244 articles, 18 core studies were selected for snowball sampling, resulting in a pool of 113. After screening for accessibility and relevance, 30 studies were included in the final review that explicitly addressed overlaps between implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement. Data were charted on study objectives, methodologies, frameworks and relevance to healthcare improvement, and synthesised to identify common themes, models and barriers to integration.ResultsThe integration of these three disciplines creates a robust framework for health system change: implementation science emphasises fidelity and evidence-based uptake, improvement science prioritises generalisable knowledge and quality improvement enables rapid, local problem-solving. Their combined application can enhance the scalability, sustainability and efficiency of interventions. Models such as the HIV/AIDS Bureau Implementation Science Model, Reach-Effectiveness-Adoption-Implementation-Maintenance and the Learning Evaluation framework illustrate practical integration, showing how iterative quality improvement efforts can inform broader implementation strategies. Barriers include siloed approaches, variable terminology and limited organisational capacity, highlighting the need for cross-disciplinary collaboration, training and infrastructure support.ConclusionIntegrating implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement provides a pathway to accelerate evidence translation, strengthen system-wide healthcare quality and improve patient-centred outcomes in HIV care. Synthesising shared principles and alignment opportunities offers actionable guidance for healthcare systems seeking to leverage the strengths of each discipline for more effective, scalable and sustainable care delivery.

Introduction

Healthcare systems globally continue to face increasing pressure to improve outcomes, reduce costs and deliver high-quality, evidence-based care.1 Implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement have emerged as key approaches aimed at closing the gaps between research and practice.

Despite major advances in HIV prevention and treatment, persistent gaps remain across the HIV care continuum, including delayed diagnosis, suboptimal linkage to care, inconsistent retention and inequities in viral suppression across populations and regions.2 Optimal HIV care is defined by sustained viral suppression, which improves individual health outcomes and prevents onward transmission. Although highly effective antiretroviral therapy and evidence-based strategies are widely available, they are not consistently implemented or sustained across settings. In the USA, structural barriers, fragmented service delivery and variability in implementation of evidence-based interventions continue to limit progress toward Ending the HIV Epidemic goals.3 These challenges underscore the need not only for effective interventions, but also for systematic approaches to translating evidence into routine practice and sustaining improvements in real-world HIV care settings.

While the results of this scoping review were initially focused on their application to HIV care, these approaches are relevant across healthcare, as improving care experiences and outcomes remain universally critical challenges.

Implementation science focuses on the systematic integration and sustainment of evidence-based practices in routine care,4 while improvement science investigates the mechanisms and contextual factors that influence the success of quality improvement efforts. Quality improvement, often driven by frontline teams, emphasises iterative, pragmatic efforts to address specific challenges in real-world settings.5

The intersection of improvement science, implementation science and quality improvement as it relates to enhancing HIV care delivery experiences and outcomes remains underexplored in the published literature. While literature exists that examines the individual contributions of each field, few explicitly discuss the potential collaboration or overlap between these fields. This gap in knowledge limits our understanding of how evidence-based, iterative improvements from implementation science and quality improvement can inform the adaptive testing processes of improvement science interventions, both within the context of HIV care and in other areas of healthcare.

Prior literature has described conceptual distinctions and areas of overlap between implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement, distinguishing them by their scale, methodological rigour and orientation toward generalisability versus local problem-solving.58 Several frameworks and commentaries have noted that quality improvement initiatives often generate rapid, context-specific gains driven by frontline teams, addressing immediate challenges in real-world settings.5 8 Implementation science, by contrast, focuses on theory-driven strategies to support the systematic adoption, spread and sustainment of evidence-based practices across organisations and systems.6 9 Improvement science bridges these approaches, seeking to understand how local improvements can generate generalisable knowledge and inform system-wide learning.5 7 Although implementation science and improvement science share a common goal of enhancing healthcare delivery and outcomes, integration between these fields remains limited. Real-world improvement practice can address care issues quickly and pragmatically, but these efforts often fail to produce generalisable knowledge,10 whereas implementation strategies may be theory-driven yet overlook the operational realities of frontline care.11 Quality improvement and improvement science highlight the need to improve efficiency, an aspect that is often lacking in other fields, such as implementation science.12 Integrated approaches are particularly underexplored in HIV care, where complex, multi-level challenges require both rapid-cycle improvements and theory-informed scaling strategies.13 Greater synergy between these fields could facilitate more efficient, sustainable and scalable improvements that are responsive to patient and system needs in real time.

Building on this literature, this scoping review synthesises existing frameworks and applied models that integrate implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement, with a specific focus on the application across the HIV care delivery system. By mapping shared principles, practical integration strategies and real-world applications, this review aims to clarify how these approaches can be combined to support scalable, sustainable and equitable improvements across the HIV care continuum. Specifically, it examines how implementation science can support the systematic spread and scaling up of evidence-based practices within improvement science and inform how quality improvement methods and practices can enhance the efficiency and practicality of implementation strategies. By identifying shared principles and opportunities for integration, this review also aims to strengthen the collective impact of these disciplines on healthcare improvement, with a focus on informing HIV care and treatment systems.

Methods

Step 1: study design and identifying the research question

This scoping review was conducted following the methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley14 and aligned with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist.15 Our review was guided by two primary inquiries: first, how implementation science supports the systematic scaling of evidence-based practices within improvement science; and second, how quality improvement methods can enhance the efficiency of implementation strategies. Together, these questions framed our analysis and synthesis of the available evidence at the intersection of the three fields.

Step 2: identifying relevant articles and screening

A scoping review was conducted of literature published in English using PubMed as the primary database. Articles published between 1 January 2016 and 1 December 2025 were included to capture contemporary approaches reflecting current implementation science and quality improvement practices. The final database search was conducted on 1 December 2025. The search focused on articles related to implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement. Various combinations of keywords related to these disciplines were used to inform the search strategy, and a comprehensive list of Medical Subject Headings terms is provided in table 1.

Operationalisation of the search terms

Database searching in PubMed identified 244 records. Following title and abstract screening, 18 ‘core articles’ were selected through consultation with three academic experts in implementation science, based on their recognised contributions to the field and relevance to the intersection of quality improvement, improvement science and HIV care. Core article selection was guided by predefined criteria: (1) explicit engagement with at least two of the three domains; (2) presentation of a conceptual framework, model or methodological approach; and (3) relevance to healthcare delivery contexts. Reference lists from these core articles were subsequently screened, yielding 113 additional records for retrieval. Of all articles sought for full-text review, 100 were fully accessible and met eligibility criteria, and were therefore included in the final synthesis. Articles were prioritised if they addressed multiple focus areas (implementation science, improvement science and/or quality improvement) or explicitly discussed their generalisability, and if they were published within the last 5–7 years to reflect contemporary implementation and improvement practices. Although improvement science was a focus of the review, studies were not required to be explicitly labelled as such, because relevant concepts and approaches are often embedded within quality improvement or implementation science studies. Older publications were included if they contributed foundational conceptual frameworks or applied models. Studies conducted in high-income countries with comparable healthcare and HIV service delivery infrastructures (eg, the USA, Canada, Sweden and Australia) were included to ensure relevance to similarly resourced and organised health systems. Approaches may differ in lower-resource or structurally distinct settings. For this review, ‘generalisable findings’ were defined as conclusions, frameworks or models that could be applied beyond a single clinic, patient population or context. Studies reporting site-specific interventions without broader applicability, or those conducted in narrowly defined experimental settings were excluded. This approach ensured that the findings could inform HIV care delivery across multiple healthcare settings and support the development of scalable, system-level improvements.

Although this review is intended to inform HIV care collaboratives, HIV-specific terms were intentionally not included in the search strategy. The purpose of this review was to synthesise generalisable implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement frameworks that support systematic spread and scale-up across healthcare settings. Limiting the search to HIV-focused literature would have excluded foundational and cross-cutting approaches developed in other clinical domains that are directly applicable to HIV care. HIV care is therefore treated as the primary application context for the synthesised findings rather than as a criterion for study inclusion.

Step 3: article selection

Of the 100 full-text articles that met initial eligibility criteria, 70 were excluded for the following reasons: failure to address the intersection of both implementation science and quality improvement (n=67), lack of practical application in either field (n=2) or focus on a specific disease without broader generalisability (n=1). This refinement process resulted in a final sample of 30 articles that explicitly addressed the intersectionality of implementation science and quality improvement. Step 4: data charting

Data were extracted using a structured charting tool created in Microsoft Excel 365.

The tool included prompts to guide the extraction of data relevant to the study objectives. Specifically, it captured study characteristics and purposes; definitions of implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement; the intersections between these fields and their application to HIV care; and their respective clinical and geographical contexts. This structured extraction process facilitated the identification of thematic patterns across the selected literature and informed the subsequent synthesis of findings. Four independent reviewers completed data extraction; discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Formal interrater reliability was not calculated due to the conceptual nature of the extraction, but the structured tool ensured consistency across reviewers.

Findings

This review examined the intersectionality of quality improvement, improvement science and implementation science to address fundamental questions about their definitions, applications and synergistic potential within healthcare improvement efforts. Although the frameworks of implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement can often be conflated or used interchangeably, each offers distinct contributions to advancing healthcare quality. Definitional clarity is essential for selecting appropriate strategies for service delivery, implementation and evaluation.16 While previous work has attempted to differentiate these three fields, their boundaries remain inconsistently defined. This review synthesises their intersections and highlights their major differences to promote greater conceptual and practical clarity in healthcare improvement.

The 30 studies included in this review were published between 2003 and 2025 and represented a diverse range of study designs, including conceptual papers, case studies, systematic reviews, observational studies, quasi-experimental trials and mixed-methods evaluations. Most studies originated from the USA, Australia and other high-income healthcare systems, and spanned multiple areas of care, including HIV/AIDS, oncology, perinatal health, mental health and general healthcare delivery. Many were conducted in applied healthcare settings such as ambulatory health clinics, academic medical centres and community-based organisations. 18 studies explicitly examined integration across at least two of the three domains, implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement, while the remaining studies provided foundational conceptual or methodological contributions within a single domain relevant to healthcare improvement and HIV service delivery. We have included an online supplemental table 1 that summarises the key characteristics of included studies, including discipline focus, study design, origin, care setting and primary frameworks or outcomes examined.

Implementation science

Implementation science is the study of methods and strategies that facilitate the uptake of evidence-based practices and research into routine use by practitioners and policymakers.17 18 It identifies ways in which healthcare practices can systematically integrate evidence-based strategies into healthcare delivery19 while addressing structural barriers by emphasising readiness, stakeholder buy-in and fidelity.13 20 Implementation science bridges the gap between theory and sustainable application of evidence-based practices within healthcare settings.21 22

Improvement science

Improvement science evaluates system-level efforts to improve the quality, value or safety of care.23 It adopts a pragmatic approach to understanding ‘what is done and why’ to cater to unique contexts24 and seeks to generate broadly applicable knowledge with generalisable findings that can be applied to clinical practice.25 26 By investigating the mechanisms of change behind specific interventions, improvement science leverages scientific knowledge to create continuous improvement at a systematic level.5 19

Quality improvement

Quality improvement promotes the enhancement of healthcare systems through data analysis and iterative change.27 By employing systematic changes, quality improvement seeks to improve the quality, safety and cost-effectiveness of healthcare systems. This approach relies on performance data to identify areas of improvement, implement targeted changes and achieve superior patient outcomes.16 28 29 Quality improvement methodologies, such as the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle, emphasise small-scale rapid changes,30 stakeholder engagement, continuous feedback loops4 5 and adaptation to local contexts.12 25

Key differences

As summarised in table 1, the three disciplines diverge in purpose, scope and methodological orientation. While implementation science uses theory-driven approaches to ensure the long-term fidelity of evidence-based practices, improvement science prioritises system-level generalisability and often employs experimental or quasi-experimental designs to test improvement strategy effectiveness across diverse contexts. In contrast, quality improvement is primarily focused locally and emphasises rapid-cycle, iterative change within specific organisational settings.

These distinctions extend to operational priorities: quality improvement and improvement science emphasise adaptability through practice-oriented tools like Six Sigma and Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles, while implementation science prioritises fidelity to the intervention design through collaborative research.27 31 32 As noted in the terminology row of table 2, each field uses distinct language to describe its mechanisms: implementation science refers to ‘strategies’, improvement science to ‘interventions’ and quality improvement to ‘processes’.23 Despite shared characteristics, implementation science focuses primarily on the mechanisms of integration, while improvement science evaluates whether improvement efforts are effective and generalisable.26

Intersectionality of implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement

Despite definitional differences, all three disciplines share common goals: improving health outcomes, optimising healthcare delivery systems and sustaining effective improvements.303234 Each emphasises patient-centred care, stakeholder engagement, use of data and the importance of multidisciplinary approaches.

Implementation science provides frameworks to spread and scale effective practices identified through improvement science, while also guiding real-time quality improvement efforts.20 30 32 35 Quality improvement serves as a testbed for implementation science interventions and improvement science principles, contributing real-world data on feasibility, acceptability and effectiveness across diverse contexts.13 19 Improvement science evaluates quality improvement interventions, determining whether they achieve intended outcomes and identifying mechanisms that explain their effectiveness.35

Taken together, these relationships suggest a complementary cycle in which quality improvement generates local, practice-based evidence, improvement science translates these insights into generalisable knowledge and implementation science ensures broad uptake and sustainability across healthcare systems. While prior literature has described each domain separately, this scoping review synthesises their intersections and highlights actionable models and mechanisms for integration in HIV care. By identifying synergies between quality improvement, improvement science and implementation science, this review provides guidance for practitioners and researchers seeking to design integrated, system-wide improvement initiatives.

Implications for healthcare

Despite conceptual alignment, several practical and structural barriers hinder integration. Rapid-cycle quality improvement projects, while flexible and responsive, may lack the methodological rigour or theoretical foundation characteristic of implementation science or improvement science.19 This gap can limit generalisability and hinder the systematic spread of successful interventions. Additionally, many organisations lack the internal capacity, including expertise and infrastructure, to conduct effective implementation or improvement science research.36 37

Addressing these challenges requires fostering cross-disciplinary collaboration and supporting capacity-building initiatives. Investments in training, methodological support and infrastructure can enhance the design, implementation and evaluation of integrated improvement initiatives.4 22

RE-AIM provides structured methods to accelerate implementation research and integrate implementation science with quality improvement, emphasising evaluation across diverse contexts that align with improvement science principles.34 Similarly, the HIV/AIDS Bureau Implementation Science Model pairs rapid implementation of proven HIV care strategies with continuous evaluation to generate insights that extend beyond local settings.39 The Learning Evaluation Model, by contrast, primarily emphasises rapid-cycle quality improvement and implementation science strategies to provide real-time feedback for local adaptations, drawing less explicitly on broader improvement science evaluation methods.38 By clarifying where elements of improvement science are incorporated, these models highlight opportunities to bridge local practice-based improvements with system-wide learning and evidence-informed decision-making.25 26

These models demonstrate the potential for implementation science and improvement science to strengthen quality improvement by embedding evidence-informed, scalable solutions tailored to local needs.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this review is its comprehensive and systematic approach, combining a structured PubMed search with expert-identified core studies and snowballing techniques. This process yielded 30 articles representing diverse study designs, healthcare settings and perspectives, ensuring that the review captured a broad range of relevant literature across implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement. By synthesising frameworks and models that are often siloed, the review offers a novel perspective on their intersections and highlights insights particularly relevant for improving HIV care delivery and outcomes. Several limitations warrant consideration. Terminology across the three fields is evolving, and inconsistencies in definitions may have led to the exclusion of relevant articles or variability in interpretation. As a scoping review, this study maps the landscape but does not assess study quality or effectiveness, limiting conclusions about the relative impact of different approaches. This study did not include grey literature, potentially excluding valuable insights from these sources. Finally, much of the literature focuses on high-income healthcare systems, which may limit generalisability to other global contexts.

Conclusion

This review highlights the complementary roles of implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement in strengthening healthcare delivery and outcomes that can be applied both within HIV care and more broadly. Quality improvement offers rapid, pragmatic solutions to frontline challenges, improvement science advances generalisable insights into effective improvement strategies, and implementation science provides frameworks to systematically spread and sustain evidence-based practices. Yet, these disciplines are too often siloed, limiting their collective impact.

Emerging models such as the HIV/AIDS Bureau Implementation Science Model, the Learning Evaluation framework, and RE-AIM illustrate how integration can enhance efficiency, scalability and sustainability. By combining the rigour of implementation science with the adaptability of quality improvement and the contextual insights of improvement science, healthcare systems can more effectively address structural barriers, optimise care delivery and support patient-centred outcomes.

Several included frameworks demonstrate how integrated application of implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement can strengthen HIV care delivery processes and support system-level learning. The Learning Evaluation Model emphasises mixed-methods assessment alongside rapid-cycle quality improvement, enabling real-time adaptation of interventions while generating insights applicable across settings.38 Similarly, RE-AIM provides a structured approach for evaluating reach, adoption, implementation and maintenance, facilitating broader dissemination of evidence-based practices while preserving flexibility for local contexts.34 The HIV/AIDS Bureau Implementation Science Model illustrates how implementation strategies, paired with continuous evaluation, can accelerate the uptake of proven HIV interventions while identifying barriers and facilitators to sustainment across diverse care settings. Collectively, these models show how integrating quality improvement with implementation and improvement science supports more effective care processes, informs system-level decision-making and builds capacity for scalable and sustainable HIV service delivery.

Greater collaboration across these fields is essential to accelerate the translation of evidence into practice. Future work should focus on developing shared evaluation strategies, fostering cross-disciplinary training and creating platforms for real-time learning across settings. Aligning implementation science, improvement science and quality improvement not only strengthens the methodology of healthcare delivery but also ensures that innovations are delivered rapidly, efficiently and at scale.
