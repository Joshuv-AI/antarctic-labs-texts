Active site engineering of recombinant human paraoxonase 1 for enhancing its catalytic efficiency against V-type nerve agent analog- Demeton S
Authors: Manik Goel, Pulkita Pandey, Subhashree Sahu, Rinkoo Devi Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman Paraoxonase 1 (HuPON1) is a calcium-dependent serum enzyme with broad substrate specificity and therapeutic potential for detoxifying the organophosphates, including nerve agents. The V-type nerve agents are the most toxic synthetic organophosphates, with high lethality and environmental persistence, necessitating the development of an efficient catalytic bioscavenger for their detoxification. However, HuPON1 exhibits promiscuous hydrolytic activity towards various organophosphates; its catalytic efficiency against V-type nerve agents and its analogs remains extremely low. In this study, a structure-guided active site engineering approach was used to enhance the catalytic efficiency of recombinant HuPON1 against Demeton S, a surrogate for the V-type nerve agent. The in silico analysis has identified key amino acid residues (H115, Q192, F222, and L240) for a mutagenesis study, and the residue F77 in the catalytic lid was selected to influence substrate accessibility and binding. Since the wild-type HuPON1 exhibits very low activity, the engineering approach was initiated using the active site H115W mutant, which showed ∼2.86-fold higher enzymatic activity against Demeton S, bringing its hydrolytic activity within a measurable range. The combinatorial mutations were constructed progressively, resulting in the generation of double, triple, quadruple, and quintuple mutants. These engineered mutants displayed a stepwise improvement in catalytic activity, with the quintuple mutant (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S and L240S) showing ∼10.6-fold higher hydrolytic activity and a ∼ 28.89-fold improvement in catalytic efficiency (kcat/km). Furthermore, the protein retained 58% residual acetylcholinesterase activity and remained structurally and functionally stable in human serum. This engineered HuPON1 mutant demonstrated enhanced hydrolysis of Demeton S, a surrogate V-type nerve agent, supporting its development as a broad-spectrum catalytic bioscavenger against various organophosphorus compounds.Keywords: Human paraoxonase 1, Protein engineering, Site-directed mutagenesis, Catalytic bioscavenger, V-type nerve agent

Introduction

Organophosphorus (OP) nerve agents, including VX and VR, are among the most lethal synthetic chemical warfare agents due to their high toxicity and environmental persistence.1, 2 These compounds exert their toxicity via irreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase, leading to accumulation of acetylcholine, causing cholinergic crisis and subsequent neuromuscular dysfunction.3, 4 Current chemotherapeutic treatments, including atropine, oximes and benzodiazepines, can improve the survivability following OP exposure, but often fail to prevent long-term neurological damage.5 These treatments provide limited protection and failed to neutralize the OP compounds. Therefore, the catalytic bioscavengers offer a promising solution by enzymatically hydrolyzing the OP nerve agents in circulation before they reach their physiological targets.6 OP hydrolyzing enzymes derived from both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms have emerged as promising biocatalytic agents capable of degrading OP compounds, representing potential prophylactic and post-exposure countermeasures.7, 8, 9, 10, 11

Human PON1 (HuPON1) is a calcium-dependent enzyme with broad substrate specificity, including lactones, aryl esters and certain OPs.12, 13 PON1 exhibits native lactonase activity but can also hydrolyze aryl esters and OPs, displaying significant catalytic promiscuity.14 The hydrolysis of OPs by HuPON1 has been shown to proceed via a general-base mechanism, where a Ca+2-activated water molecule initiates nucleophilic attack on the substrate, aided by the direct involvement of active-site residues.15, 16 Several studies have demonstrated that PON1 mutants can mitigate the in vivo toxicity of G-type nerve agents.17, 18, 19, 20, 21 However, its catalytic efficiency against toxic nerve agents, particularly V-type nerve agents, is under a non-detectable range or shows very low enzymatic activity. The mutagenesis and directed evolution approaches have demonstrated that PON1 variants can achieve measurable detoxification of G-type nerve agents, laying the foundation for engineering efforts against V-type nerve agents.19

The structural and mechanistic barriers to V-type nerve agent hydrolysis by HuPON1 remain a major challenge.22 The strong hydrophobicity and thiolate leaving group of these compounds prevent PON1 from binding and hydrolyzing them, necessitating specific modifications to enhance substrate accommodation and catalytic turnover. Computational modelling and rational design approaches can assist in identifying suitable mutations that improve function without compromising solubility and stability.23, 24, 25, 26 A similar strategy was previously used to enhance huSMP30's ability against γ-TBL and a surrogate V-type nerve agent.26, 27 Since the crystal structure of WT HuPON1 has not been solved, most structural insights were derived from the engineered PON1 variant G2E6, designed for high solubility and stability in the bacterial system.28 Despite the availability of chimeric PON1 variants, native HuPON1 remains an important engineering target because it exhibits intrinsic activity against V-type nerve agents and retains substrate specificity distinct from that of chimeric variants.29 Based on these structural insights, the 3D structure of HuPON1 was designed using homology modelling, and molecular docking identified the key active-site residues (H115, Q192, F222, and L240) for further engineering. Additionally, F77 was selected for the catalytic lid to improve substrate accommodation. The present study focuses on enhancing HuPON1's OP hydrolase activity through active-site engineering. Using an in silico approach, mutations were designed as double, triple, quadruple and quintuple mutants on the H115W backbone. The WT and mutant enzymes were expressed recombinantly, purified, and characterized through enzymatic studies. Comparative analysis of mutants with the WT HuPON1 provided critical insights into the structural-functional relationship and guided the identification of suitable mutations.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of recombinant HuPON1

The HuPON1 gene was cloned into the pET28a(+) vector as described in our previous article.12 Briefly, the amplified HuPON1 gene from the cDNA of the Huh7 cell line was cloned into the pET28a(+) vector with the EcoRI/XhoI restriction sites. The plasmid construct was transformed into BL21 (DE3) E. coli competent cells for protein expression. The positive transformant was cultured overnight in LB broth with an antibiotic at 37 °C. For secondary culture, 1% of the primary culture was inoculated in LB broth containing kanamycin and incubated at 37 °C until the O.D. reached 0.4–0.6. Then, the induction was performed with 0.1 mM IPTG and incubated overnight at 28 °C in a shaker incubator. The harvested cells were resuspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.5% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, 5 mM DTT, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mg/mL Lysozyme, and 1 mM CaCl2) and kept at room temperature on a rocker for 30–45 min. The cells were then sonicated using 30-s ON/OFF pulses at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 12000 RPM for 30 min. The protein pellet was resuspended in the lysis buffer without lysozyme. The supernatant and pellet were loaded onto a 12% SDS-PAGE gel to analyze the protein expression.

Since the protein was expressed in the pellet fraction, Inclusion body (IB) solubilization was used for further purification. The obtained pellet was resuspended in buffer I (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mg/mL Lysozyme and 1 mM CaCl2) after cell harvesting, followed by incubation on a rocker at room temperature for 40 min. After incubation, 1% Triton X-100 and 300 mM NaCl were added, and incubated for an additional 15 min. Lysis was performed by sonication, and the supernatant and pellet were obtained by centrifugation at 12000 RPM for 30 min. The obtained cell pellet was washed using buffer II (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM PMSF, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM CaCl2). This was followed by 4–5 additional washes without Triton X-100. The pellet was then solubilized using solubilization buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 6 M GdnHCl, and 1 mM CaCl2).

The protein was further purified using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography under denaturing conditions. The solubilized protein was allowed to bind to Ni-NTA agarose beads at 4 °C for 4–5 h, calibrated using the denaturing binding buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 6 M GdnHCl, and 500 mM NaCl). The unbound proteins were removed by washing the beads with a buffer containing 10 mM imidazole, and the bound protein was eluted with a buffer containing 250 mM imidazole. The eluate was collected into fractions and analysed by SDS-PAGE to assess purity and confirm the successful recovery of the purified protein.

Refolding of recombinant HuPON1

The purified protein underwent refolding using flash dilution and dialysis method as described in the previous article.12 The protein (0.1 mg/mL) was diluted in the refolding buffer (4 M Urea, 350 mM arginine HCl, 5% n-propanol, 4 mM DTT, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.1 mM Glutathione oxidized, and 1 mM Glutathione reduced). The proteins were incubated at 4 °C for 36 to 48 h. Then, the proteins were concentrated using Millipore Amicon centrifugal filters (10 kDa MW cutoff). The concentrated proteins were further dialyzed using the dialysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, and 1 mM CaCl2) to remove excess salts.

Functional characterization of purified recombinantly produced HuPON1

Protein concentration was determined by the Bradford assay, and comparative enzymatic assays were performed using equal amounts of total refolded protein. The paraoxonase, arylesterase, and lactonase activities of purified HuPON1 were studied using methyl paraoxon, phenylacetate, and γ-TBL (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as respective substrates. The purified protein (2 μM) and substrates (0.5 mM) were diluted in the activity buffer (50 mM Tris HCl pH 8.0 and 1 mM CaCl2). The assay was conducted in triplicate with the reaction volume adjusted to 200 μL. The substrates were added just before the readings were taken. For lactonase activity, DTNB (0.1 mM) was added as an indicator. The enzymatic activity was measured at regular 1-min intervals for 30 min at specific wavelengths (405 nm for paraoxonase activity, 320 nm for arylesterase activity and 412 nm for lactonase activity). The product formed was determined as μM/min and plotted in a bar graph.

Structural modelling and molecular docking analysis of HuPON1

The 3D structure of HuPON1 was designed in Discovery Studio 4.0, using Chi-PON1 (PDB ID 1V04) as the reference structure. All non-protein components, heteroatoms, and water molecules were removed prior to refinement. The resulting structure underwent energy minimization with the steepest descent algorithm. Among the minimized structures, the model with the most favorable energy parameters- the lowest total PDF energy and the most negative DOPE score was selected. The model was assessed using the Ramachandran plot and the main-chain RMSD relative to the template protein. The structures of selected substrates (Amiton and Demeton S) were available on the PubChem database. The structures were retrieved and prepared in Discovery Studio 4.0 prior to energy minimization with charge assignment to bring them into HuPON1's active site.

Molecular docking was performed using the CDOCKER algorithm in Discovery Studio. The dockings were performed with selected substrates using the default parameters. The spatial coordinates of the binding-site residues were derived from the Chi PON1-ligand complex, which served as a reference for active-site mapping. It allows the binding of substrate molecules at the protein's active site. Following docking, the spatial distances between the interacting active site residues and substrate molecules were analysed for each molecule. The larger distances indicate a more expanded active-site pocket, corresponding to weaker interactions and reduced binding affinity. The maximum number of poses and Cα RMSD were used as selection criteria to select the best pose.

Selection of targeted amino acid substitutions

The targeted amino acid residues identified through molecular docking and literature studies were subjected to mutational analysis. The stability of single amino acid substitutions was determined using the CUPSAT stability analysis tool.30 Subsequently, the 3D structures of stable mutants were generated and evaluated through molecular docking. The structural superimposition of WT and mutant proteins was performed to determine the RMSD. Mutants with more negative binding energies and improved interaction profiles were selected for in vitro creation.

In vitro construction of mutants

Based on the molecular docking results, the single mutation (H115W) was introduced into the HuPON1 gene using the HuPON1-pET28a(+) as the starting point. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using a PCR-based approach with Platinum Taq High Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Invitrogen). Each mutation was introduced using the specifically designed partially overlapping primers (Supplementary Table S1). Following amplification, the PCR product was digested with the restriction enzyme DpnI (New England Biolabs, US) to remove the template plasmid. The DpnI-digested product was transformed into E. coli XL10 competent cells. The double, triple, quadruple and quintuple mutants were created sequentially using the preceding mutant as the template. The designed mutants were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Enzymatic activity and kinetics study with Demeton S

The OP hydrolase activity was evaluated using Demeton S, a surrogate V-type nerve agent. The reaction was set up in 200 μL of the activity buffer containing 2 μM protein, 0.1 mM DTNB, and 2.5 μM substrate. The reaction was initiated by adding the protein, and the absorbance was recorded every minute at 412 nm for 30 min using the multimode plate reader (Synergy HT). The progress curve of the enzymatic activity of WT HuPON1 against Demeton S was monitored at 412 nm for 30 min using the DTNB assay. The assay detection limit was calculated as mean + 3*Standard deviation. Additional controls (no substrate, no enzyme, heat-inactivated HuPON1, and EDTA-treated HuPON1) were performed to validate the specificity of the DTNB assay. Enzyme activity was calculated from the rate of product formation. For kinetics analysis, the WT HuPON1, H115W, and the designed mutant, which showed improved catalytic activity, were tested under similar assay conditions using Demeton S concentrations ranging from 0.01 mM to 1.25 mM. Kinetic parameters (Vmax, km, and kcat) were obtained from a Lineweaver-Burk plot. To ensure accuracy, non-linear regression analysis was also performed, and the data were fitted to the Michaelis-Menten kinetics using the GraphPad Prism.

Assessment of Demeton S hydrolysis by AChE inhibition assay

The hydrolysis of Demeton S by WT, H115W, and quintuple mutant was assessed using an acetylcholinesterase (AChE) inhibition assay. Initially, the minimum concentration of Demeton S required to inhibit approximately 80–90% of AChE activity was determined. Various concentrations of Demeton S were incubated individually with 0.2 U of AChE (provided in Amplex® Red Acetylcholine/Acetylcholinesterase Assay Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, US) in PBS for 5–10 min. Following incubation, 0.5 mM acetylthiocholine (ATCh) and 0.3 mM DTNB, an indicator, were added, and enzymatic activity was determined at 412 nm. AChE activity in the absence of Demeton S was considered 100% for reference. Once the required Demeton S concentration for ∼80–90% AChE inhibition was established, the hydrolytic efficiency of the designed enzyme against Demeton S was evaluated under similar conditions.

Stability analysis of the designed HuPON1 mutant using the commercially available human serum

All experiments were performed using commercially available pooled human serum (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, USA). The lyophilized human serum was reconstituted according to the manufacturer's instructions, aliquoted, and used immediately or stored at −80 °C until further use. For stability analysis, the HuPON1 mutant (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S, and L240S) was added to the reconstituted human serum to achieve a final concentration of 5 μM. The mixture was then incubated at 25 °C for up to 48 h. Aliquots were withdrawn at defined time intervals (6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h), and enzyme stability was evaluated by measuring OP hydrolase activity. Briefly, 5 μL of each serum aliquot was diluted 50-fold in PBS, and Demeton S hydrolysis was monitored using 1 mM Demeton S as the substrate, with the activity measured as the rate of product formation in 20 min. For Western blot analysis, serum aliquots were diluted in PBS, mixed with 4× protein loading dye, and heated at 95 °C for 5–10 min. SDS-PAGE was run, and the gel was transferred to a PVDF membrane. After the transfer, the blot was incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary anti-6× His tag antibody (Thermo Scientific, US) at 1:1000. The membrane was washed three rounds with TBST and incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. The protein bands were visualised using enhanced chemiluminescence in a Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least 3 independent replicates, and results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was evaluated using a t-test; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 denote increasing levels of significance. Differences with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant relative to the control group.

Results

Recombinant production of purified and active HuPON1

The recombinant HuPON1 construct was previously generated in the pET28a(+) vector and verified by sequencing.12 After cloning, the recombinant HuPON1 was expressed in BL21 (DE3) E. coli cells. The expression pattern analysis reveals that the protein was expressed in the pellet fraction. A protein band was observed at ∼42 kDa in the pellet fraction, indicating the tendency of the protein to form IBs in the bacterial expression system (Fig. 1A). Due to its insoluble nature, purification was performed using the IB solubilization strategy. After IB solubilization, SDS-PAGE revealed a prominent band of HuPON1 at the expected molecular weight, accompanied by residual protein bands likely representing co-purifying host proteins that persisted through the washing and solubilization steps (Fig. 1B). The protein was further purified by Ni-NTA chromatography under denaturing conditions, enabling selective binding of His-tagged HuPON1 and efficient separation from contaminants. Ni–NTA chromatography yielded a prominent band corresponding to HuPON1 at 42 kDa in the elution fraction, with markedly reduced contaminating proteins (Fig. 1C). Elution fractions showed improved purity relative to the crude lysate, confirming successful affinity-based purification.

Molecular docking analysis of HuPON1 with OP compounds

The 3D structure of WT HuPON1 has not yet been resolved. However, the only resolved structure corresponds to a recombinant variant, Chi-PON1, which was engineered to enhance solubility in E. coli.28 To obtain a structural model of HuPON1, homology modelling was performed in Discovery Studio 4.0 using Chi PON1 as the template. The Ramachandran plot analysis revealed that the majority of amino acid residues were located within the favored and allowed regions, and the RMSD score indicates good structural stability of the modeled protein (Supplementary Fig. S1). The binding site coordinates for docking analysis were determined from the PON1-ligand complex structure (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Molecular docking analysis was carried out using Amiton and Demeton S (Supplementary Fig. S3). The docking results reveal that both Amiton and Demeton S occupy the same catalytic cavity of HuPON1 and share a broadly similar binding site (Fig. 2). In both complexes, the substrates are positioned adjacent to the catalytic residues (H115, H134, and H184) and stabilized by the extensive network of van der Waals interactions (Y71, L167, F222, L240, and I291). The stability of both complexes is further supported by the presence of comparable hydrogen bonds and additional hydrophobic interactions. In addition, π–alkyl and π–sulfur interactions with aromatic residues, particularly F222 and F292, further stabilize their binding. These findings suggest that HuPON1 recognizes both compounds through conserved structural and physicochemical features within its active site. The binding affinity of HuPON1 with methyl paraoxon is more negative than Amiton and Demeton S (Supplementary Table S2). Their interaction analysis reveals that although the V-type nerve agent occupies the same active site, its failure to undergo hydrolysis by HuPON1 is not attributed to its poor binding, but due to improper positioning of the reactive phosphorous centre and steric hindrance from bulky alkyl and thioester substituents.

Docking analysis of the WT HuPON1 against Demeton S revealed that the substrate occupies the active site pocket (4475.2 Å3) through a network of hydrogen-bonding, hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions. The docked substrate was predicted to bind with the binding energy of −38.01 kcal/mol. Y71 forms a conventional H-bond with the substrate, while N168 and Q192 form a carbon‑hydrogen bond. H115 and F222 exhibited π‑sulfur interaction with the sulfur moiety of Demeton S, whereas H134, H285, I291, F292 and L240 contributed to alkyl and π-alkyl interactions. Additional van der Waals interactions, including D269, T332, V346, N224, P189, L167, N166, and D183, stabilize the docked substrate within the binding site. The catalytic distance and other structural features of HuPON1-Demeton S complex are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. These results facilitated the identification of key mutational hotspots that are likely to influence the binding affinity and catalytic performance of HuPON1. The selected residues for mutational study include H115, Q192, F222, and L240, which are located in close proximity to the active site and participate in predicted non-covalent interactions that may directly contribute to substrate binding and orientation within the active-site pocket. Additionally, F77 was selected to modulate access and conformational flexibility through the catalytic lid, as it is positioned along the substrate access channel and may influence substrate entry into the active site (Supplementary Fig. S4). These residues were further subjected to mutational analysis to enable the selection of targeted mutations that can enhance the catalytic activity of HuPON1 against V-type nerve agent analog, Demeton S.

Rational designing of HuPON1 for the hydrolysis of V-type nerve agent

Previous attempts to enhance its activity against V-type nerve agents have been largely unsuccessful, as effective catalysis requires precise, coordinated modifications of multiple amino acid residues rather than single amino acid substitutions.18, 21, 31, 32, 33 Rather than pursuing extensive or random mutagenesis, amino acid residues were selected rationally and sequentially in this study, as coordinated modifications are more likely to alter substrate accommodation and enhance catalytic efficiency. Based on the literature, H115W is the only reported mutation predicted to enhance activity against VX; however, this finding was derived from computational studies, and activity was measured in a chimeric PON1 variant engineered for enhanced solubility.34 Consequently, the impact of the H115W mutation on WT HuPON1 has not been completely understood. Therefore, we first designed the H115W mutant using the WT HuPON1-pET28a(+) plasmid construct as the template. The mutant protein was expressed, purified and characterized in the same way as the WT protein. The enzymatic activity revealed that the H115W mutant exhibited measurable activity towards Demeton S, bringing it to detectable levels and demonstrating ∼2.86-fold higher activity than WT HuPON1 (Fig. 3A). Therefore, the H115W mutation served as a starting point for the development of further mutants. Subsequent mutations were guided by molecular docking and stability analyses of the selected amino acid residues (F77, Q192, F222, L240). The selected residues were analysed for stability in the presence of point mutations. The result indicated that several point mutations were structurally stable following targeted amino acid substitutions (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S4). The in silico stable mutants were then analysed by performing molecular docking and interaction analysis. Out of all the designed mutations, F77I, Q192N/K, F222S and L240S were selected for in vitro mutant creation. These mutations were chosen based on low binding affinity and enhanced interaction profiles as compared to the WT protein (Supplementary Table S5). The double mutations (H115W, Q192N/K and H115W, F77I) were designed to address the following considerations: Q192N/K may strengthen the polar and electrostatic interactions involved in substrate positioning, and F77I may improve substrate accommodation through steric modulation. Subsequently, two triple mutations (H115W, F77I, Q192N/K) were designed based on both selection criteria. To further improve its catalytic efficiency, F222S and L240S mutations were introduced into the triple mutant to design quadruple and quintuple mutations, optimizing the active site geometry suitable for the hydrolysis of V-type nerve agents. The selected mutations were introduced sequentially onto the H115W backbone via PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis (Supplementary Fig. S6 and S7), followed by verification using Sanger sequencing. Expression, purification, and functional characterization were then assessed by evaluating their activity with a surrogate V-type nerve agent, Demeton S (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Enzymatic hydrolysis of Demeton S

The hydrolytic activity of WT HuPON1 and the designed double, triple, and quintuple mutants was evaluated using the surrogate V-type nerve agent, Demeton S. WT and mutant HuPON1 proteins were expressed, purified and refolded using the identical experimental conditions. WT HuPON1 showed very low enzymatic activity against Demeton S, while the H115W mutation resulted in significant enhancement (∼2.86 fold) in hydrolytic activity. The progress curve for WT HuPON1 showed product formation, whereas all negative controls remained at the background level (Supplementary Fig. S9). The WT signal was above the detection limit, confirming the reliable detection of enzymatic activity (Supplementary Table S6). The enzymatic activity of all designed mutants against Demeton S was described in Supplementary Table S7. F77 and Q192 are positioned along the substrate access channel and are in close proximity to the bound substrate. The subsequent incorporation of Q192N and F77I has improved catalytic activity, likely by enhancing substrate positioning and enzyme-substrate interaction within the active site. In contrast, Q192K has not been able to enhance its activity, as the positively charged lysine at position 192 may alter the local electrostatic balance (Supplementary Fig. S10). The triple mutant (H115W, F77I and Q192N) has shown improved hydrolytic activity (∼4.82-fold) compared to WT HuPON1, however, no significant enhancement was observed relative to its corresponding double mutations. F77I and Q192N may modulate the steric environment at the substrate binding site by reducing steric constraints while maintaining favorable substrate accommodation (Supplementary Fig. 4). These structural changes may contribute to overlapping features, including improved substrate access, accommodation, and positioning, thereby enhancing catalytic activity. To further enhance the hydrolytic ability of the designed triple mutation, L240S and F222S were introduced, replacing bulky hydrophobic residues with serine, thereby increasing solvent accessibility (SASA) and reducing steric constraints within the active site (Supplementary Fig. S11). These structural changes may facilitate improved substrate positioning, thereby contributing to the improved enzymatic activity. The enzymatic activity has shown that the quintuple mutation (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S and L240S) has demonstrated the highest activity (∼10.59 folds) towards Demeton S, suggesting that increased active site polarity, flexibility and solvent accessibility likely contribute to improved catalytic turnover (Fig. 3A). The designed quintuple mutant showed a higher rate of product formation, followed by the H115W mutant and the WT HuPON1 (Supplementary Fig. S9). Therefore, a stepwise improvement in catalytic activity was observed with the subsequent addition of a rationally designed mutation.

For kinetics study, the WT HuPON1, H115W and quintuple mutant (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S and L240S) were selected. The kinetic parameters were determined using the Lineweaver-Burk plot (Supplementary Fig. S12). The mutant H115W shows ∼3.8-fold higher catalytic efficiency (kcat/km) than WT, and this enhancement is attributed to a lower km value (∼3.2-fold). The catalytic efficiency of the quintuple mutant has been improved by ∼28.89-fold and ∼ 7.62-fold compared to WT HuPON1 and H115W mutant, respectively (Table 2). Kinetic parameters obtained by non-linear regression were consistent with those from the Lineweaver-Burk plot, confirming the reliability of the kinetics data. The results from non-linear regression fits are shown in Supplementary Fig. S13. The overall efficiency was increased due to a higher binding affinity as reflected by a lower km value and a modest increase in catalytic turnover.

Comparative assessment of other enzymatic activities of HuPON1 and its mutants

HuPON1 is a highly promiscuous enzyme that exhibits diverse enzymatic activities, including lactonase, arylesterase, and paraoxonase activities.35 The effect of designed mutations on other enzymatic activities was also analysed to assess whether mutations selectively improve one function without compromising the enzyme's versatility. The fold changes in enzymatic activities of selected mutations are described in Supplementary Table S7. The H115W mutation resulted in a significant increase in paraoxonase activity (∼7.9-fold) towards methyl-paraoxon, while no significant effect was observed on the lactonase and arylesterase activity (Fig. 3B). This suggests that H115W plays an important role in organophosphate hydrolysis. The introduction of Q192N on the H115W backbone reduced both paraoxonase and arylesterase activities as compared to H115W alone; however, it did not affect the lactonase activity. In contrast, Q192K did not affect any of its three activities, but was similar to the WT protein. While the F77I mutation resulted in no improvement in lactonase and paraoxonase activity, it enhanced the arylesterase activity. This suggests that F77I resulted in less steric hindrance and can accommodate a bulkier substrate due to the smaller side chain of isoleucine. The triple mutation moderately enhanced the aryl esterase and paraoxonase activities. The introduction of L240S to the triple mutant (H115W, F77I and Q192N) has improved lactonase activity, suggesting the role of L240S in modulating the lactone-hydrolyzing ability of HuPON1. In contrast, the introduction of the F222S mutation has restored the paraoxonase activity. This suggests that the activity profile of HuPON1 can be modulated through targeted mutations (Fig. 3C and D), with the designed quintuple mutation (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S, and L240S) being able to hydrolyze Demeton S and methyl-paraoxon, implicating its application as a broad-spectrum OP hydrolyzing agent.

Structure-Function analysis of the designed quintuple mutant via docking studies

To further understand the underlying mechanism by which the quintuple mutation enhances its binding affinity against Demeton S, molecular docking was performed. The results suggest that these multi-site mutations have a high binding affinity (−92.975 kcal/mol), resulting from extensive cooperative interactions within the active site (Supplementary Table S3). The amino acid substitutions- H115W and F77I enhance aromatic and hydrophobic packing, while Q192N and L240S contribute additional stabilizing polar interactions. The F222S mutation modulates π‑sulfur interactions, optimizing substrate positioning. These mutations preserved the key catalytic contacts (Y71, H134, H184, V346) while expanding the interaction network through hydrophobic, π-mediated, and hydrogen-bond interactions, resulting in structural stabilization and maximal binding efficiency (Fig. 4A). The molecular overlay suggests that the quintuple mutations resulted in the substrate adopting a more extended, linear, and catalytically favorable conformation, thereby improving substrate accommodation within the active site channel (Fig. 4B). Reduced steric clashes and improved complementarity within the active-site pocket enable closer proximity to key catalytic residues. This optimized binding geometry likely enhances transition-state stabilization and results in improved enzymatic activity.

Acetylcholinesterase protection assay

To further characterize the in vitro efficacy of the designed quintuple mutant, the hydrolysis of Demeton S was evaluated using an acetylcholinesterase inhibition assay. The acetylcholinesterase activity in the presence of Demeton S reveals that Demeton S, at a concentration of 5 μM, inhibits 80–90% of the acetylcholinesterase (0.2 U) (Supplementary Fig. S14). The 5 μM concentration was used for further experimentation. The untreated sample was used as the control and was taken as 100% activity. The WT and H115W mutant were unable to enhance acetylcholinesterase activity against Demeton S and were found to be similar to the sample treated with Demeton S alone. The quintuple mutant design has shown a ∼ 58% enhancement in acetylcholinesterase activity (Fig. 5). Studies indicate that maintaining at least 30% of residual blood AChE activity is enough to counteract the toxic effects of OP compounds on AChE enzymes in various tissues, including the brain and diaphragm, thereby preventing fatal outcomes.36 The residual acetylcholinesterase activity after treatment with the quintuple mutant resulted in more than 50% activity and holds potential for use in the neutralization of V-type nerve agents.

Stability and functional analysis of purified HuPON1 mutant

The stability of purified quintuple HuPON1 mutant protein was evaluated in pooled human serum. The enzyme was exogenously introduced into the serum, and its stability was assessed by monitoring OP hydrolase activity over time and evaluating protein integrity through Western blot analysis. Western blot analysis further confirmed the absence of detectable degradation of the added enzyme for up to 48 h (Fig. 6A). The addition of a quintuple mutant protein resulted in a substantial increase in total OP hydrolase activity against Demeton S, with no significant reduction observed even after 48 h of incubation (Fig. 6B). The quintuple mutant maintained catalytic activity and structural stability in human serum, highlighting its potential suitability for in vivo and therapeutic applications against organophosphate toxicity.

Discussion

There is a growing demand for robust and scalable expression platforms that can produce therapeutically relevant human enzymes in sufficient quantities. For HuPON1, the bacterial expression system is particularly advantageous for screening engineered variants with enhanced catalytic properties against nerve agents, a prerequisite for therapeutic efficacy. Since the recombinant protein was expressed in a bacterial expression system, which may not fully reproduce the native folding environment and post-translational modifications, this platform provides a rapid and cost-effective means of producing and screening engineered mutants. Previous studies have highlighted the use of an engineered HuPON1 variant designed to be expressed in a bacterial expression system, which limits the ability to accurately assess the intrinsic characteristics of WT HuPON1.28 This limitation has been addressed in our current study, in which WT HuPON1 was successfully expressed, purified, and characterized, thereby, enabling direct measurement of its enzymatic activities. The HuPON1 is the most studied member of the paraoxonase family, which is named for its ability to hydrolyze paraoxon, an active metabolite of parathion.37, 38 The ability to hydrolyze organophosphate compounds make it distinct; however, the hydrolysis activity towards nerve agents is low, even in the non-detectable range or extremely low with V-type nerve agents. In this study, we have used a protein engineering approach, focusing on the active site and catalytic lid of HuPON1, to enhance its enzymatic activity against surrogate V-type nerve agent-Demeton S.

An enzyme-based detoxification strategy for organophosphate compounds provides a promising alternative to overcome the drawbacks of current treatment approaches. The HuPON1 has emerged as a suitable candidate for application as a catalytic bioscavenger. Molecular docking studies have revealed that V-type nerve agents or its analog bind to the same active site but with lower binding affinity than their native substrates. However, their hydrolysis remains insufficient due to the suboptimal orientation of the substrate within the catalytically active site. Although HuPON1 contains a predominantly hydrophobic active site, efficient hydrolysis requires a more polar catalytic environment and an open entry site to accommodate the substrate in proper orientation. The rational design approach facilitated the identification of suitable amino acid substitutions (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S, L240S), which were introduced to the HuPON1-pET28a(+) template in a progressive manner. The molecular docking successfully guided the selection of active-site mutations, it cannot fully predict the catalytic efficiency. The resulting quintuple mutant has been characterized in a manner similar to WT HuPON1 and has been found to have ∼10.59-fold higher catalytic activity than the WT protein. The intermediate mutants were also evaluated, revealing that some substitutions enhanced enzymatic activity, while others had minimal or no effect. This design approach, based on molecular docking analysis and the selection of active-site residues unlikely to disrupt native lactonase activity, provides the advantage that none of the introduced mutations has significantly compromised native lactonase activity, as all mutants showed no significant effect.

The quintuple mutant was further characterized for its kinetic parameters. Kinetic analysis revealed that the engineered variant (H115W, F77I, Q192N, F222S, and L240S) exhibited an approximately ∼10.59-fold enhancement in enzymatic activity and a ∼ 28.9-fold improved catalytic efficiency over the wild-type protein. The improved catalytic efficiency is primarily attributed to enhanced substrate binding, as reflected by the lower km value relative to the wild type. The increase in the kcat value of the designed mutant was minor, indicating a limited effect on catalytic turnover. Similar mutations, with a Q192K substitution, had a non-significant effect on enzymatic activity, suggesting that the substitution to a positively charged lysine decreased the positive effect of other mutations. This suggests that this Q192K substitution may introduce structural strain, modify the architecture of the active-site cavity, or disrupt critical interactions, thereby impairing catalytic function.

The stability and functional integrity of designed mutations are critical determinants for their therapeutic applicability. Therefore, the residual acetylcholinesterase activity was determined in the aqueous buffer to establish baseline performance. The quintuple mutant has shown 58% residual acetylcholinesterase activity in the presence of Demeton S, a V-type nerve agent analog. Subsequently, the structural integrity and functionality of this mutant were evaluated in human serum using western blotting and OP hydrolase activity assays. The results showed that the designed mutant remained stable and functionally active for 48 h, suggesting its therapeutic potential for hydrolyzing V-type nerve agents. Even the paraoxonase activity remained intact in the designed mutant, making it a potential broad-spectrum catalytic bioscavenger against toxic organophosphate compounds. Although the mutant enzyme exhibited promising activity, further investigations are required to fully assess its safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy before clinical translation. Future work will integrate molecular dynamics simulations or QM/MM studies, directed evolution, and an alternative expression system to further enhance the catalytic performance of the designed quintuple mutant. While in vivo studies would provide a more accurate assessment of therapeutic potential, challenges such as protein stability, delivery mechanisms, and immunogenic responses could be addressed through advanced protein engineering strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the designed quintuple HuPON1 mutant demonstrated a significant enhancement in catalytic efficiency towards Demeton-S and may serve as a promising candidate for detoxification of V-type nerve agent analog. These findings suggest that a rational protein engineering approach can effectively enhance enzymatic performance without compromising stability, provided that appropriate mutations are carefully selected. Future work will focus on expanding the mutational hotspots through random mutagenesis and combinatorial library approaches to further enhance substrate specificity and robustness under physiological conditions. Future in vivo studies are required to validate the therapeutic relevance of this approach for detoxifying V-type nerve agent analog. As this study was conducted using Demeton S, a surrogate for V-type nerve agent, future studies should evaluate the engineered enzyme against authentic V-series nerve agents under appropriate containment conditions to confirm its detoxification potential and therapeutic applicability.
