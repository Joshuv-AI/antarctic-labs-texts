Introduction

Pisolithus is an ectomycorrhizal fungus commonly associated with trees growing in loose, sandy soils, especially along coastal pine woodlands. These fungi can grow quite vigorously and even through surfaces covered with asphalt, the genus Pisolithus is from Greece, piso (pea) and lith (stone).1 These genera are found in a wide variety of environments with different temperature, nutrient and heavy metal levels, and have been reported in temperate, tropical and subtropical regions.2, 3, 4

Martin et al. (2002)5 recognize three definite evolutionary lineages in Pisolithus, comprising at least 11 species. More recent records indicate that the genus currently includes 20 recognized species (MycoBank 2022). Of these, Pisolithus albus is a widely distributed taxon, reported from several countries of the world such as Australia, China, India, Malta, Brazil, Morocco, Italy, New Zealand, New Caledonia, Senegal, Thailand, Spain, Tunisia, Pakistan and USA. However, this occurrence is new report of P. albus in Egypt. P. albus may be divided into four genetically distinct ecotypes.

The genetic differentiation and geographic distribution models may be associated with long-range dispersal events and may also have played a role in explaining the species remarkable mycorrhizal colonization of a variety of hosts.6, 7 Macrofungi are important nutritional resources and traditional remedies since ancient times, especially in Japan and China. In the past few decades, many bioactive metabolites have been extracted from basidiomycota that exhibit antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral activity.

The Pisolithus spp. are widespread around the world in temperate and tropical regions and form ectomycorrhizal associations with numerous woody plants, but there is little information on their antimicrobial activity. Their symbiotic association with host plants, genetic diversity, taxonomy, management practices, soil improvement and erosion control have been the topics of the previous studies.8, 9 The current study was undertaken to assess the antioxidant, antimicrobial and antitumor activities of Pisolithus extracts in order to evaluate the potential of using them as therapeutic candidates.

Materials and methods

Sample collections and morphological description

A sandy soil macro-fungus was wild located around the area of Ismailia Canal Zone, El Qulubia Governorate, Egypt. It was collected at various intervals throughout the period among September 2025 and November 2025. Collected samples were situated in sterile plastic bags and carried in the lab for further analysis and examination. The fungus was cleaned, and the specimens were harvested and the genus identified, comparing and contrasting the microscopic as well as the macroscopic observations made with the known species, and identifying the genus to be the same one as described by O'Reilly.1

Molecular identification and Phylogenetic tree analysis of selected isolate

Genomic DNA was isolated directly from fungal fruiting bodies utilizing the QIAampDNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Cat. No. 69104) due to the manufacturer's instructions. ITS1/ITS4 was the primer set used to amplify ITS region.10 The PCR products were initially purified on a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Valencia). Then, the cycle sequencing was carried out with the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Kit (Perkin-Elmer). These sequencing reactions were then further purified by using Centri-Sep spin columns. Lastly, sequencing was performed on an Applied Biosystems 3130 Genetic Analyzer (HITACHI, Japan). The initial sequence annotation was done by comparing the sequences to GenBank sequences using the NCBI BLAST® platform.11 Sequences were also compared using the Lasergene DNASTAR v12.1 software.12 Next, phylogenetic reconstruction was carried out in the software MEGA6 using the neighbor-joining method with the Utmostcombined likelihood model.13

Preparation of Pisolithus albus extracts

A sandy soil fungus Pisolithus albus was collected. The fresh fungus was cleaned and dried. The dried Pisolithus material was milled into a fine powder and kept in a dark, dry environment at room temperature until extraction. Separately, 40 g of the powder was mixed with 200 ml of each solvent (ethanol, methanol, acetone, ethyl acetate, and hexane) and stirred for 2 h at ambient temperature. After collecting the supernatants, the solvents were eliminated using a rotary evaporator, and the resulting extracts were preserved at 4 °C for subsequent analyses.

Antimicrobial activity of Pisolithus albus

The antibacterial activity of Pisolithus albus for ethanol, methanol, acetone, ethylacetate and hexane extracts were tested against five pathogenic bacteria strain. Gram positive bacterial strain (Staphylococcus aureus ATCC6538 and Bacillus subtilis ATCC6633, Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli Strain ATCC10536, Salmonella typhimurium stain ATCC 14028, Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain ATCC9027), and Candida albicans ATCC10231. The bacterial strains and yeast were obtained from microbiology Lab, Arab Company for Pharmaceuticals and Medicinal Plants (Mepaco), Sharkiya, Egypt. All tested strains were maintained on nutritive agar and stored at 4 °C.The antimicrobial potential of five solvent-derived extracts was evaluated utilizing the agar well diffusion assay by determining the inhibition zones (mm) against pathogenic microorganisms. Wells (10 mm in diameter) were cut using a sterile cork borer from agar media and 100 μL of each extract was added into the wells in inoculated plates. Bacterial and yeast isolates were grown at 37 °C for 24 h, while filamentous fungi were protected separately at 29 °C for a interval of 48 h, sterilized distilled water was used as negative control and gentamycin and Ketoconazole were used as positive standard. After incubation inhibition zone diameters were measured and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined. All the experiments were repeated three times, and all the measurements were recorded.14, 15

Total phenolic content (TPC) assay

Total phenolic content (TPC) was measured as described by Folin-Ciocalteu colorimetric method.16, 17 In brief, 0.05 ml of the ethanolic separate was mixed with 3 ml of 10% Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 0.8 ml of 7.5% sodium bicarbonate. After 30 min incubation at room temperature the absorbance was measured at 765 nm on a Milton Roy Spectronic 1201 spectrophotometer. The results were conveyed as micrograms of Gallic acid equivalents (μg GAE)/g of extract.

Total flavonoid content (TFC) assay

0.1 ml of extract, 3.9 ml of distilled water, and 0.3 ml of 5% sodium nitrite were added and the mixture was incubated for 5 min. Then 0.3 ml of 10% aluminum chloride was added and incubated for 6 min. After this, 2 ml of 1 mol L−1 sodium hydroxide and 2.4 ml of distilled water were added. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm against a non-reacted blank, with the help of a Milton Roy (Spectronic 1201) spectrophotometers. The concentration of quercetin equivalents (QE) was reported as μg QE g−1 extract.18

HPLC for determination of phenolic and flavonoids compound of ethanol extract

HPLC analysis was performed using an Agilent 1100 HPLC system fitted with dual LC pumps, UV–Vis detector and an Agilent C18 column (125 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). Agilent ChemStation software was used for collecting and handling chromatographic data. Phenolic acids were quantified with an isocratic mobile phase of 0.1% methanol–phosphoric acid (50:50, v/v) at 1.0 ml/min flow rate and at 280 nm. The flavonoids were separated under isocratic conditions with a methanol/water mixture (50/50, v/v) at pH 2.8 (adjusted with phosphoric acid) flowing at 1.0 ml/min.19

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity

A freshly prepared 0.004% (w/v) methanolic solution of 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) was kept in the dark at 10 °C. The test compound was also dissolved in methanol. An aliquot of 40 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 3 ml of the DPPH solution. Absorbance was immediately recorded using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Milton Roy, Spectronic 1201). The reduction in absorbance at 515 nm was monitored continuously at 1-min intervals until stabilization (16 min). Measurements were also performed for the DPPH control (without antioxidant) and for ascorbic acid as the reference standard. All analyses were performed in three independent replicates, and the average of the obtained values was considered for subsequent evaluation. The percentage of DPPH radical scavenging activity (PI) was calculated according to the following equation:

PI = [{(AC- AT)/ AC} x 100] (1).

Where AC and AT are the absorbance of the control and the absorbance of the sample mixed with DPPH after 16 min, respectively.20 Dose–response curves were generated and analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA, USA) to determine the IC₅₀ (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) – the concentration of an inhibitor should to inhibit DPPH radical scavenging by 50%.

Cytotoxicity and antitumor evaluation

Mammalian cell lines for Cytotoxicity assay: The human skin fibroblast cell line HSF-1 and the diploid lung fibroblast line WI-38 were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). The experimental assay was conducted at Al-Azhar University.

Cell line Propagation for Cytotoxicity assay: Cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 1% l-glutamine, HEPES buffer, and 50 μg/ml gentamycin. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C in a humidified environment encompassing 5% CO₂. The cultures were subcultured twice weekly to maintain optimal growth conditions.

Mammalian cell lines for antitumor assay: Human cancer cell lines HepG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma), MCF-7 (breast adenocarcinoma), and A549 (lung carcinoma) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD, USA). The experimental assay was accompanied at Al-Azhar University.

Cell line Propagation for antitumor assay: Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium enriched with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 50 μg/ml gentamicin. Incubation was carried out at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO₂. The cultures were routinely subcultured two to three times per week to maintain optimal growing conditions.

Cells were broadcast into Corning® 96-well tissue culture plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well in culture medium and incubated for 24 h. Afterwards, the fungal extract was added in triplicate wells to obtain eight different concentrations for each tested compound. For each plate, six wells containing medium only (vehicle control) were included. After an additional 24 h of incubation, cell viability was determined using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. In short, the culture medium was taken out and replaced with 100 μL of new phenol red free medium. Then, 10 μL of 12 mM MTT solution was added to each well (involving the controls). The plates were then left to incubate for 4 h at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO₂ and humidity. The 85 μL of culture medium was carefully removed from each well and 50 μL of DMSO was added. The mixture was then thoroughly homogenized using a pipette and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. After that, the absorbance at 590 nm was measured with the microplate reader (SunRise, TECAN Inc., USA) to find out the amount of viable cells.

The cell viability was calculated as a percentage operating the following equation: [(ODt / ODc) × 100%], where ODt is the mean optical density of the treated samples, and ODc is the mean optical density of the untreated control cells. Survival curve of the treated cell line was plotted after exposure to the cell line to the compound under test for varying concentrations of the compound and values of cell viability. The dose–response plots were drawn using GraphPad Prism software (version 6.0; San Diego, CA, USA) and the cytotoxic concentration (CC50) was calculated as the dose that killed 50% of cells.21, 22, 23

Neutron activation analysis (NAA)

The elemental compositions of crude fungal powder of two different isolates collected at different locations were obtained by neutron activation analysis (NAA). These samples were neutron irradiated and the induced gamma radiation captured by a high purity germanium (HPGe) detector. The obtained gamma spectra was then evaluated for the identification of elements and determination of the concentration of the elements. The method used was according to the analytical protocol provided by the Egypt Second Research Reactor (ETRR-2).24

Statistical analysis

All information were depicted as mean values ± standard deviation (mean ± S.D.), based on three independent triplicate measurements. Statistical significance between control and treated samples was assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Results were thought significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Morphological description of the fungus

Pisolithus albus (as depicted in fig. (1) is a fungus of Phylum: Basidiomycota- Class: Agaricomycetes - Order: Boletales Family: Sclerodermataceae. The fruiting body is generally ellipsoid to ovoid, mostly in the early stages. As it matures, sometimes a brief, simple stipe is developed. It consists of fertile spore-producing tissue (Fig. 1A–C). The basidiocarp is typically about 5–10 cm in diameter, but can sometimes be up to 20 cm along the largest dimension, often vertical. In the early stage, the internal part (peridium) is made up of many separate chambers, which look like compacted grains (pseudoperidioles) in which the spores are formed (Fig. 1D). The walls of these pseudoperidioles are thin and as the fruit matures the spores are released. Eventually the spore dissemination takes place when the outer layer of the peridium bursts and disperses. The spores are globose to subglobose, golden yellow and reddish-brown when mature. Generally, 7 to 11 μm in diameter, and covered in irregular triangular spines up to 2 μm tall (Fig. 1E).

Cytotoxicity evaluation of Pisolithus albus extract

Cytotoxicity activity on three cancer cell lines (HepG-2 human Hepatocellular cancer, MCF-7 human breast cancer, A-549human Lung cancer cells) and two normal human cell line (HSF-1human foreskin fibroblast skin cell and WI-38 diploid human lung fibroblasts were measured and shown in Table 6. CC50 values (presented as three readings) were detected against all cell line using MTT assay under the experimental conditions (24 h treatment). At a concentration of 250 μg/ml, the ethanol extract exhibited cytotoxicity against HepG-2 human hepatocellular carcinoma, MCF-7 breast cancer, and A-549 lung cancer cells, resulting in 88.93%, 79.29%, and 85.02% cell death, respectively. A clear dose-dependent increase in activity was observed with rising concentration. Cytotoxicity on normal cell line was 48.71 and 67.52 for HSF-human foreskin fibroblast skin cell and WI-38 diploid human lung fibroblasts. This study highlights the growing interest in identifying natural, bioactive compounds for cancer therapy. The ethanol extract of the naturally occurring fungus demonstrated strong anticancer effects across all tested cancer cell lines.

Discussion

Pisolithus albus is a species is described here for the new record in Egypt, GenBank accession number PX788599. This ectomycorrhizal fungus is usually found with trees in sandy well-drained soils, particularly in coastal pine ecosystems. The literature on this species is still limited, and most of the research has been devoted to the mycorrhizal associations with host plants, genetic diversity, taxonomic classification and capability of improving soil fertility and controlling soil erosion. The antioxidant and antimicrobial activity of bioactive components of Pisolithus species is still poorly explored. To investigate their potential as therapeutic candidates, it was decided to evaluate the antioxidant, antimicrobial, and antitumor behavior of fungal extracts in the present work. The antimicrobial activity of Pisolithus albus extracts in vitro was assessed using the agar well diffusion method against five different pathogenic bacteria species along with a yeast strain. The ethanol and acetone extracts of Pisolithus albus affected Salmonella typhimurium and Bacillus subtilus, followed by Ps. aeruginosa (22 mm), Bacillus subtilis (21 mm) and Staphylococcus aureus (20.5) by ethyl acetate extract, whereas, the E. coli low affected by all tested solvent extracts (15 mm). Regarding to antifungal activity, Ethanol extract affected Candida albicans with an inhibition zone (23–18.3 mm) with all tested extracts. The most efficient extract was ethanol extract with MIC 20 mg/ml against all tested pathogenic isolates. It was proven that extracts of ectomycorhazial fungus at a concentration of 10 mg/ml were capable of suppressing the growing of numerous pathogenic bacteria, including Salmonella typhi coli, Escherichia, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Candida albicans was more sensitive to all tested fungal extract at MIC 20 mg/ml.8 Also, The antimicrobial activity of various extracts of Pisolithus albus fruiting bodies were tested at 40 mg/ml against 30 MRSA and MSSA strains by Ameri et al.9, Their results demonstrated that chloroform extract had no inhibitive activity against any of the bacteria tested while the aqueous extract had weak activity with an inhibition zone of between 7.0 and 11.75 mm. However, the ethyl acetate extract exhibited the greatest antibacterial activity with inhibition zones ranging from 7.0 to 21.25 mm and an average of 14.54 ± 3.23 mm.

Later, Mohan et al.25 investigated the antagonistic effects of eight ectomycorrhizal fungi, including Pisolithus, in contradiction of nine different plant pathogenic fungi. All tested ECM fungi displayed inhibitory activity; notably, Pisolithus albus showed variable suppression levels, with inhibition percentages ranging from 12.22% to 47.78% depending on the target pathogen.

The main antioxidant compounds found in the extracts of the present investigation were total phenolic compounds and total flavonoids. The total phenolic content found was 116.6 mg GAE/g while the total flavonoid content of the ethanol extract was 165.34 mg quercetine/g. Flavonoids are well established in terms of their wide range of biochemical and pharmacological properties, involving antimicrobial, anti- carcinogenic, and antioxidant properties.26

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was further utilized to characterize the bioactive constituents, aiming to separate and identify the various individual compounds and/or compound mixtures present in the ethanol extract of Pisolithus albus. Fractions for Flavonoids showed six fractions, Apegenin and Querestin were detected at the highest concentration 16.54 and 13.95 μg/g, respectively. Also, regarding to Phenolic compounds, there were also six fraction, the highest was Gallic (16.75 μg/g) at retention time 9.8, then p-Coumaric 12.52μg/g at RT 6. Multiple studies have documented a variety of bioactive complexes that can be isolated from Pisolithus tinctorius.27, 28, 29 Dilsad et al.,26 found that the methanolic extracts of mushrooms showed the highest amount of total phenolics and flavonoids, and total antioxidant activity. Furthermore, a high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of flavonoids showed that the methanol extracts had relatively higher levels of rutin, luteolin, catechin, and quercetin.

The antioxidant activity of the ethanol extract was evaluated at different concentrations by DPPH assay. The results showed a definite dose-dependent relationship with the radical scavenging activity. The extract showed excellent DPPH inhibition, and was slightly lower than the standard compound (98.65%). The findings are in agreement with Sevindik, et al.,30 who found P. arrhizus have good antioxidant activity. Accordingly, it is suggested that the bioactive constituents responsible for the antioxidant activity of P. arhizus could be isolated and potentially utilized as natural antioxidant agents.

Cytotoxicity activity on three cancer cell lines (HepG-2 human Hepatocellular cancer, MCF-7 human breast cancer, A-549 human Lung cancer cells) and two normal human cell line (HSF-human foreskin fibroblast skin cell and WI-38 diploid human lung fibroblasts were measured, a concentration of 250 μg/ml, the ethanol extract induced marked cytotoxicity, leading to the death of 88.93% of HepG-2 carcinoma cells, 79.29% of MCF-7 human breast cancer cells, and 85.02% of A-549 human lung cancer cells. A dose-dependent response was observed, where the level of cell mortality increased progressively with rising extract concentrations. The cytotoxicity of normal cell line was 48.71 and 67.52 for HSF-human foreskin fibroblast skin cell and WI-38 diploid human lung fibroblasts, respectively. The demand for identifying effective natural compounds in cancer therapy has been increasingly emphasized. In the present work, the investigated naturally derived fungus exposed strong anticancer activity versus all tested cancer cell lines. Measuring the cytotoxic effects, the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was used in liver cells, and basidiomycetous mushrooms were reported to be with a remarkable anticancer potential.31 Alves et al.,28 compared extracts extracted from spores of Pisolithus tinctorius using dichloromethane–methanol (2:1) and EtOAc–MeOH (2:1), revealing that the dichloromethane–methanol (2:1) extract had the greatest cytotoxic effect against cancer cell lines. Dilsad et al.,26 have also demonstrated significant cytotoxicity of ethanol and methanol extracts of P. arrhizus in colon cancer cells in a concentration-dependent manner.

Heavy metals are very poisonous and can cause a progressive damaging effect over time to various organs. They are known to have a vascular toxicity linked to several pathological disorders, particularly due to their ability to increase oxidative stress, inflammation and coagulation activity. In this aspect, it was recommended that P. albus could be used as a significant source of antioxidant compounds. The elements that enter the human body are divided into two categories, essential and non-essential elements, depending on the physiology that they serve.32, 33 Essential elements incorporate N, K, P, Ca, Cl, Fe, Mn, Mg, Zn, Ni, Co, Cu, Na, Se, Cr and many others. These are also distinguished into macro- and microelements according to their occurrence in biological systems. Macro-elements—these are present in relatively large quantities (N, K, P, Mg, Ca, Na, Cl and Fe). They are essential for maintaining a number of important physiological processes such as bone health, dental health, blood health, protein synthesis, energy metabolism, cell growth, cardiovascular function, and nervous system function.34 In this present study, quantitative analysis of a total of 11 elements was carried out. Of these, Ca, K, Na and Fe were detected as macro-nutrients in both samples analyzed, while Co, Zn and Cr were classified as trace (micro) essential elements. Additionally Br, Sb, Sc and La were detected, but are believed to be non-essential for human health because they do not have physiological functions. The sodium level varied from 461 to 2250 mg/kg. According to the US NRC, the recommended daily dose (RDI) of Na is 1500 mg/day, and the tolerable upper intake level (UL) is 2300 mg/day. Potassium was the most abundant mineral with levels ranging from 16,200 to 31,800 mg/kg, and an RDI of about 4700 mg/day, but no UL has been established. Calcium content varied from 2480 to 5260 mg/kg; its RDI is 1300 mg/day and the UL is 2500 mg/day (US NRC). Concentrations of chromium were measured at 11.5–22.2 mg/kg and there is no RDI or UL. Iron range was 299–761 mg/kg; the RDI for men is 8 mg/day and for women 18 mg/day, and the UL is approximately 45 mg/day. The contents of cobalt ranged from 0.522 mg/kg to 1.41 mg/kg, the RDI is 0.12 mg/day, and the UL is 1–2 mg/day. The concentrations of zinc were in the range of 45.6–66.4 mg/kg, RDI-11 mg/day, UL-40 mg/day. Non-essential elements, such as Sc, Sb, Cs and La were also found at low levels throughout the samples, with bromine reaching 55.4–80.2 mg/kg. Other researchers had previously used atomic absorption spectrophotometry (AAS) to determine elemental composition of mushrooms, and it was observed that the heavy metal content of P. arhizus was generally safe.30 Similarly, Naveen and Raja36 determined the concentration of heavy metals (Ni, Pb, Cr, Cd) in AAS and it was found that the highest concentration was for chromium (13.25%) and followed by Ni (2.35%).

Conclusion

Fungi are well known for their medicinal properties, comprising their antioxidant, antibacterial, antifungal and anticancer effects, especially the macro-fungi that produce bioactive metabolites. From this point of view, Pisolithus albus is an interesting species for study, because of the number of its chemical and pharmacological properties that has not been well documented in studies of its fruiting bodies. The present morphological and molecular identification confirmed that Pisolithus albus was a new record Egypt and the collected specimens were identical with the data of GenBank accession number (PX788599). The antimicrobial properties of P. albus extracts in ethanol were found to be significant against pathogenic bacteria and yeast. Chromatographic methods based on HPLC were used to obtain the highest extraction efficiency of bioactive constituents. P. albus also showed high antioxidant properties and the compounds present in it might be isolated and used as natural antioxidant agents. Dose-dependent activity was seen in the cytotoxic assays against three cancer cell lines (HepG-2 hepatocellular carcinoma, MCF-7 breast cancer and A-549 lung cancer), as well as 2 normal human cell lines (HSF foreskin fibroblasts and WI-38 lung fibroblasts) with higher concentrations of extract yielding higher degrees of inhibition. The cytotoxicity values for normal cells were 48.71% and 67.52% for HSF and WI-38, respectively. The neutron activation analysis results for P. albus showed generally acceptable concentrations of heavy metals, with relatively high concentrations of iron. The overall results indicate that P. albus is a potential source of bioactive compounds. Future applications can be related to its metabolites, which can be used for the improvement of pharmaceutical agents with anticancer, antibacterial, antifungal and antioxidant properties, as a multifunctional natural drug candidate.
