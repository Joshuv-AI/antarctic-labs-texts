Ageing Between Two Cultures: How Older Indonesian Immigrants in Australia Negotiate Identity and Family Roles—A Qualitative Study
Authors: Kevin Sanly Putera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAustralia is expecting a growing ageing population from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds. This study explored how older Indonesian immigrants in Australia negotiate their multicultural identities and family roles, and how participants experienced these negotiations in relation to their psychological well‐being.MethodsIn‐depth semi‐structured qualitative interviews were conducted with 31 older Indonesian immigrants (aged 65 years and over) residing in Southeast Queensland, Australia. Participants were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling via Indonesian community networks. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke's inductive thematic analysis with adherence to COREQ guidelines.ResultsThree themes emerged: (1) navigating belonging between cultural contexts, describing participants' ongoing evaluation of Australia and Indonesia across affective, practical and identity dimensions; (2) actively exercising heritage multicultural identities, capturing how participants deliberately maintained, rediscovered and performed their heritage identities through personal agency, cultural anchors and diaspora community engagement; and (3) negotiating roles and authority within family, documenting caregiving, moral guidance and heritage cultural transmission as the roles through which elder identity was enacted and experienced as meaningful. Cultural identity was not a fixed attribute but a continuously negotiated practice, enacted through roles in family and diaspora contexts.ConclusionsOlder Indonesian immigrants actively negotiate their multicultural identities and family roles in ways associated with sustained psychological well‐being. Role performance sustains meaning, cultural continuity and social purpose in later life. Aged care and policy frameworks should attend to these role‐enabling dimensions as central to well‐being among older immigrants from CALD backgrounds.

Introduction

Population ageing among immigrants is accelerating globally. In Australia, older adults aged 65 years and over now constitute approximately 23% of the population, and around 37% of this cohort was born overseas [1]. Indonesians now represent the fourteenth‐largest migrant community in Australia [2], and the proportion aged 65 years and over is projected to nearly double over the next 30 years [3]. Despite this growth, older immigrants from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds remain underrepresented in both ageing research and aged care policy design.

Existing research documents that older immigrants prefer to age in place within their own homes and families, even when this produces significant caregiving challenges [4]. Language and cultural barriers have been consistently identified as impediments to appropriate care [5], but these barriers are rooted in deeper dynamics of how older immigrants enact their sense of self and social purpose. Acculturation research has documented that cultural identity orientation is associated with psychological well‐being (PWB) among immigrants [6]. Psychosocial well‐being refers to both feeling good and functioning effectively in life, operationalised through six dimensions [7]: autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life and self‐acceptance. However, acculturation frameworks have largely focused on attitudes and self‐identification, paying insufficient attention to the behavioural and relational mechanisms through which identity becomes experientially meaningful [6, 8].

The Indonesian case is strategically productive here. Indonesia is one of the world's most culturally diverse nations, with over 700 languages and 1300 ethnic groups, so Indonesians who migrate to Australia arrive not as culturally singular heritage bearers but as individuals whose identity structures were shaped through decades of navigating internal Indonesian diversity before migration [9]. This pre‐migration multicultural complexity is not well‐captured by bicultural frameworks that conceptualise acculturation primarily as a negotiation between two cultural poles [10].

Indonesian family life is organised around strong collectivist norms, in which filial obligation and elder deference remain central to how identity and family roles are understood [11, 12]. Older adults are typically expected to remain embedded within extended family structures, with intergenerational co‐residence and mutual obligation functioning as markers of a well‐ordered family rather than as a constraint [13]. These expectations, shaped further by religious and regional custom across Indonesia's ethnic groups, provide the cultural backdrop against which participants' negotiation of identity and family roles in Australia should be read.

A further dimension that existing frameworks have largely overlooked is the role of family roles in the identity–well‐being relationship. Role theory posits that roles are patterned behaviours linked to social positions and guided by shared expectations [14]. In family contexts, roles can be categorised as task‐based (instrumental activities such as housekeeping and financial management) or relationship‐based (such as giving advice and attending to younger generations' emotional well‐being) [15]. For older immigrants, performing culturally valued elder roles may provide purpose, social authority and cultural continuity, though the mechanisms through which this occurs remain underspecified in the literature.

This study explored how older Indonesian immigrants in Australia negotiate their multicultural identities and family roles, and what participants' accounts reveal about the conditions they associate with their psychological well‐being.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative design using in‐depth semi‐structured interviews. Thematic analysis was chosen for its suitability in identifying patterns of meaning across diverse participants while preserving interpretive depth. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines. Ethics approval was granted by the University of Queensland—Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval number: 2024/HE000777).

Researcher's Positionality

The researcher is Indonesian‐born who, at the time of data collection, had lived in Australia for under 2 years and was enrolled as a full‐time doctoral candidate. The shared cultural background facilitated linguistic access and contextual sensitivity.

The researcher had received training in qualitative methods and reflexivity as part of his doctoral coursework. Guided by a phenomenological approach [16], the researcher employed epoché (bracketing) and maintained a reflexivity journal to manage the influence of prior cultural assumptions on interpretation. Two supervisors reviewed coding outputs at two stages of analysis.

Participants and Recruitment

Eligible participants were Indonesian‐born individuals aged 65 years or older who had lived in Australia for at least 5 years and were not residing in residential care. Purposive and snowball sampling were used. Facebook recruitment yielded limited response due to trust concerns; faith‐based WhatsApp networks and peer referrals proved more effective. No prior relationship existed between the researcher and participants before study commencement.

Before each interview, participants received an information sheet outlining the researcher's institutional affiliation and the aims of the study. They signed a consent form in advance and were informed of their full right to withdraw at any time without questioning. Thirty‐two participants were recruited from Southeast Queensland between October and December 2024 (Table 1). One participant withdrew consent after the interview and is excluded from the study, resulting in the final amount of 31 participants. All participants migrated to Australia before the age of 65 years and aged in place within the country; none relocated to Australia after entering the threshold age of 65 years.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted face‐to‐face and one‐on‐one in participants' preferred location (home, shopping centre or café) and language: 24 in Bahasa Indonesia, four bilingually and three in English. The average interview duration was 57 min. Due to time constraints, two interviews had to be continued via phone calls.

An interview guide covered migration background, cultural identity, identity negotiation and family roles, sequenced from broader acculturation experience towards specific role enactments and refined through three preparatory discussions with non‐participant older Indonesians. Audio recordings were transcribed using an AI‐assisted transcription tool with full manual verification, producing verbatim transcripts not returned to participants for comment.

Data Analysis

The author conducted an inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's six‐phase framework [17]. Qualitative analysis software NVivo 15 was used for data management and coding. Two cycles of coding were conducted, with first‐cycle codes refined into subthemes and themes in the second cycle. Data saturation was assessed using a stopping‐criterion approach [18, 19]; no new themes emerged after the 27th interview, and four further interviews confirmed this, adding illustrative material only. A codebook with descriptions for all codes, subthemes and themes is available upon request. Participants were not asked to provide feedback on the final findings, consistent with the inductive approach adopted.

Frequency counts reported throughout indicate the relative prevalence of patterns within the sample and are not intended to support statistical generalisation, consistent with established guidance on the use of numbers in qualitative research [20].

Gender emerged as the most consistent differentiating variable across all three themes and is reported where the patterns were sufficiently developed for meaningful comparisons.

Theme 1: Navigating Belonging Between Cultural Contexts

This theme captured participants' ongoing, nonlinear negotiation of belonging across affective, practical and identity dimensions (198 coded references, all 31 participants).

Comparing Australia and Indonesia as Home

Twenty‐four participants described Australia through its systemic advantages: Medicare, civic order, financial security and environmental quality. Six participants of Chinese Indonesian heritage held the most unequivocal preference for Australia, grounded in direct experience of ethnic persecution:Riots happen since I was a kid… We were hunted… That's why I had to migrate. (F06, 69, female, 28 years in Australia)

Riots happen since I was a kid… We were hunted… That's why I had to migrate. (F06, 69, female, 28 years in Australia)

The pull towards Indonesia was almost exclusively relational: Six participants described longing for the communal solidarity and informal reciprocity of Indonesian social life that Australia could not replicate, whereas three had reached an irreversible sense of home in Australia despite retaining strong emotional attachment to Indonesian cultural practices.

Adjusting to Australian Life

Twenty‐seven participants described three persistent challenges. Sixteen reported limited English proficiency as a significant and in some cases ongoing barrier. Twenty described friction with Australian communication norms and institutional expectations. A third pattern, less commonly discussed, was the loss of domestic support systems: 10 participants, predominantly female, described the absence of household workers as a fundamental disruption to their domestic identity:If you have money, (ageing in) Jakarta is better. You don't need to do anything because the housemaids do it all. After migration, I need quite some time to adjust and manage all domestic tasks myself. (F02, 75, female, 27 years in Australia)

If you have money, (ageing in) Jakarta is better. You don't need to do anything because the housemaids do it all. After migration, I need quite some time to adjust and manage all domestic tasks myself. (F02, 75, female, 27 years in Australia)

In Indonesia, employing domestic workers signals economic standing and autonomy. In Australia, equivalent support is structured around assessed care need, reframing a marker of independence as institutional dependency. This finding connects interpretively to Ryff's autonomy dimension [7]: Participants described an unanticipated reduction in control over their domestic environment, which for female participants also involved resuming gendered household responsibilities [21] that domestic workers had previously managed. This loss of taken‐for‐granted support parallels findings that role and status disruption after migration can undermine older immigrants' sense of self‐directed functioning, independent of physical care needs [22].

Positioning Self Between Cultural Identities

All 31 participants addressed this subtheme. Fourteen maintained their Indonesian or ethnic heritage identity as a stable, continuing reference point regardless of years in Australia:I am still holding on to Indonesian culture, because I belong to that culture. I just say I'm Indonesian. I reside here, but I don't feel I'm Australian. (F04, 69, female, 27 years in Australia)

I am still holding on to Indonesian culture, because I belong to that culture. I just say I'm Indonesian. I reside here, but I don't feel I'm Australian. (F04, 69, female, 27 years in Australia)

Thirteen described multiple identities as additive rather than conflicting, and two primarily identified as Australian. Participants holding multiple identities were among the most actively engaged with heritage practices, indicating that identity complexity does not reduce heritage engagement.

Theme 2: Actively Exercising Heritage Multicultural Identities

This theme captured deliberate, agentic engagement with heritage identities (213 coded references, all 31 participants). A cross‐cutting observation was that Australia's perceived civic openness enabled heritage cultural expression, particularly for Chinese Indonesian participants whose practices had been constrained in Indonesia.

Shaping Identity Through Agency

Twenty‐one participants described motivated heritage engagement spanning a perceived‐obligation spectrum: 10 maintained practices as a non‐negotiable internalised commitment; nine acted from felt duty to represent or transmit culture; and 13 were driven by pride and personal fulfilment. Male participants were more prominent in pride‐driven engagement; female participants more often described obligation and felt responsibility, particularly towards intergenerational transmission. This division is consistent with gendered patterns of cultural identity work, in which men are more often socialised towards visible, pride‐oriented cultural display, whereas women are positioned as custodians responsible for continuity [23]:Australians praise our culture when they see it. I feel we must not lose it while living here. (M04, 72, male, 50 years in Australia)

Australians praise our culture when they see it. I feel we must not lose it while living here. (M04, 72, male, 50 years in Australia)

Preserving Heritage Through Cultural Anchors

Twenty participants used language, food, ritual, music, dress and material objects as identity anchors. A particularly significant finding was cultural rediscovery: seven participants, predominantly of Chinese Indonesian heritage, re‐engaged with practices suppressed or inaccessible in Indonesia, including Mandarin language learning and ancestral rituals, enabled by the social safety Australia provided from the ethnic pressures they had experienced before migration. Although the cultural rediscovery pattern was most pronounced among Chinese Indonesian participants, several participants from other ethnic backgrounds described a related process, for instance renewed engagement with Javanese or Balinese practices after migration, suggesting the pattern extended beyond a single ethnic group even as its specific content varied:I actually appreciate my Javanese background more after leaving Indonesia. (M04, 72, male, 50 years in Australia)

I actually appreciate my Javanese background more after leaving Indonesia. (M04, 72, male, 50 years in Australia)

For six further participants, primarily female, cultural practices served as emotional anchors against displacement, captured in the Indonesian phrase mengobati kangen (remedying longing). Participant F09 described sharing traditional Balinese songs with her husband to ease their shared homesickness; participant F06 kept childhood furniture because it connected her to where she came from. These accounts suggest a connection to Ryff's positive relations dimension [7], in that sustaining cultural practices maintained emotional continuity participants associated with feeling well.

Expressing Identity Through Diaspora Communities

All 31 participants described community engagement: 17 engaged broadly across diverse Indonesian networks, 13 maintained anchored membership within ethnic or faith‐based enclaves and two maintained identity independently. Communities served as spaces for cultural performance, social connectedness and surrogate family support. Shared Indonesian migrant identity often functioned as a stronger basis for participation than subcultural differences did as barriers: Several participants joined communities whose ethnic or religious background differed from their own, a form of intra‐Indonesian multicultural participation many had not practised in Indonesia:No matter how far the birds fly, they will come back home to their nests. (M03, 76, male, 12 years in Australia)

No matter how far the birds fly, they will come back home to their nests. (M03, 76, male, 12 years in Australia)

Negotiating Identity Through In‐Group Conflicts

Thirteen participants described tensions within Indonesian communities based on religion, ethnicity, class or intermarriage. These conflicts functioned not only as sources of stress but also as occasions for participants to clarify their own cultural orientation:Once I know an opinion leader… starting to deliberately divide the members based on ethnicities, I stopped going there. Just why? We have the same religion! (M13, 78, male, 36 years in Australia)

Once I know an opinion leader… starting to deliberately divide the members based on ethnicities, I stopped going there. Just why? We have the same religion! (M13, 78, male, 36 years in Australia)

Male participants contextualised tensions within organisational and ideological dynamics; female participants more often described interpersonal tensions arising through judgement or gender‐norm policing, becoming more selective in community participation as a result.

Theme 3: Negotiating Roles and Authority Within Family

This theme documented how participants performed and negotiated elder roles within families as the primary arena where identity negotiation became concretely enacted (189 coded references, 28 participants). Consistent with role theory [14], participants did not merely hold cultural identities; they performed them through specific, socially positioned roles. Although both task‐based and relationship‐based roles were present [15], participants across genders emphasised relationship‐based roles, with female participants more consistently represented in caregiving and cultural custodianship, and male participants more frequently describing guidance and transmission in philosophical and collective terms. This division reflects broader patterns of gendered caregiving in immigrant families, where women are more often positioned as kin‐keepers responsible for sustaining relationships and daily continuity, whereas men more often take on advisory or symbolic authority roles [23]. It also echoes documented patterns of gendered domestic and caregiving expectations within Indonesian households specifically [21].

Caregiving for Younger Generations

Twenty participants described caregiving for grandchildren, adult children or ageing parents. Eleven framed it as an inherent familial duty:The most important thing is we have to take care of our parents. My sister and I take turns. It is a responsibility. (F02, 75, female, 27 years in Australia)

The most important thing is we have to take care of our parents. My sister and I take turns. It is a responsibility. (F02, 75, female, 27 years in Australia)

Twelve described it as voluntary and emotionally fulfilling, providing purpose and structure in a life stage that Australian retirement norms did not always fill meaningfully:I want to share and help my child look after their grandchildren. It gives me purpose rather than being too empty. (M02, 71, male, 11 years in Australia)

I want to share and help my child look after their grandchildren. It gives me purpose rather than being too empty. (M02, 71, male, 11 years in Australia)

Across both framings, caregiving was associated with being needed and purposeful, connecting interpretively to Ryff's purpose in life dimension [7]. It also functioned as the relational foundation for the roles below: without the access it provided, elder authority to guide and transmit was harder to exercise in Australia, where intergenerational living is less structurally supported than in Indonesian collectivist norms [13].

Guiding Younger Generations With Wisdom

Fourteen participants described exercising elder authority through moral guidance, value transmission and advice about self‐sufficiency: setting limits against harmful behaviours, fostering independence and transmitting foundational values such as respect, diligence and religious conduct:The difficulty is that we are put in a situation with many choices. Even if we say, 'don't drink alcohol,' the environment outside makes it available. A child might obey me at home but not outside. (M01, 65, male, 18 years in Australia)

The difficulty is that we are put in a situation with many choices. Even if we say, 'don't drink alcohol,' the environment outside makes it available. A child might obey me at home but not outside. (M01, 65, male, 18 years in Australia)

Role conflict was evident throughout, arising from tension between Indonesian elder norms emphasising hierarchical deference and Australian family norms emphasising children's autonomy. Guidance about self‐sufficiency also operated bidirectionally: Although directed at younger generations, it simultaneously expressed participants' own hopes that they too would not become dependent and might be cared for by capable family members.

Transmitting Heritage Culture and Identity

Twenty‐six participants described heritage cultural transmission as among the most meaningful family roles they performed. Four distinct meanings emerged. Nine framed heritage as a cultural birthright, an immutable inheritance belonging to descendants regardless of where they were raised:…because it's their heritage. Even though they don't feel like they're Indonesian because they already grew up here. Because they got Indonesian blood in them. (F04, 69, female, 27 years in Australia)

…because it's their heritage. Even though they don't feel like they're Indonesian because they already grew up here. Because they got Indonesian blood in them. (F04, 69, female, 27 years in Australia)

Eighteen participants used heritage as a moral compass, transmitting filial piety, adab (refined propriety and social deference) and religious conduct as a protective ethical framework. Thirteen embedded culture in shared daily and ritual practice as an intergenerational bond:I don't just talk about it. If I talk about it but can't practice it, what's the point? I do it so the grandchildren understand naturally. (F09, 66, female, 25 years in Australia)

I don't just talk about it. If I talk about it but can't practice it, what's the point? I do it so the grandchildren understand naturally. (F09, 66, female, 25 years in Australia)

Twelve described a fading legacy framing: They acknowledged that full transmission across generations was unlikely while continuing the effort regardless:I think three or four generations from now, they will all be Aussie… If they are no longer going to Indonesia, then perhaps it is good. So, they can truly become part of this country. (M14, 71, male, 8 years in Australia)

I think three or four generations from now, they will all be Aussie… If they are no longer going to Indonesia, then perhaps it is good. So, they can truly become part of this country. (M14, 71, male, 8 years in Australia)

This pattern raises a question role theory does not fully address: Why do participants continue performing a role whose full achievement they do not expect? Their accounts suggest an answer rooted not in anticipated social reinforcement, which role theory assumes sustains performance [14], but in the constitutive relationship between the role and their identity as Indonesian elders; the performance itself was experienced as meaningful independent of its outcome.

Discussion

This study explored how older Indonesian immigrants in Australia negotiate their multicultural identities and family roles, and what participants described as the conditions sustaining their psychological well‐being. Three observations carry the main theoretical and practical weight.

Multiple Cultural Identities as Additive Rather Than Conflicting

A consistent finding across Themes 1 and 2 was that participants did not experience their multiple cultural identities as inherently conflicting. Most described them as additive, with accumulated cultural experiences expanding rather than fragmenting their sense of self. Recent scholarship has similarly argued for frameworks that account for pre‐migration complexity rather than treating migration as the starting point of cultural identity negotiation [24, 25].

Participants who had spent much of their lives navigating Indonesia's internal cultural diversity appeared accustomed to managing multiple affiliations before migration [26], and this capacity carried into Australia, where they incorporated elements of Australian culture while maintaining a strong attachment to Indonesian heritage practices. Bicultural frameworks may not fully capture such migrants; the findings indicate the value of supplementing rather than replacing these models when examining populations from highly diverse societies.

The cultural rediscovery finding further illustrates this complexity. Migration to Australia was associated with renewed access to cultural practices that had previously been constrained in Indonesia, most visibly among Chinese Indonesian participants through Mandarin language learning and ancestral traditions and among participants from other ethnic backgrounds through practices such as Javanese and Balinese traditions. This finding aligns with research on heritage revitalisation within diaspora settings [27, 28], while highlighting how migration may create opportunities for cultural recovery rather than cultural loss.

This pattern also connects to Ryff's positive relations dimension [7], in that renewed access to heritage practices gave participants opportunities to build and sustain culturally meaningful relationships, both within diaspora networks and across generations.

For aged care practice, these findings reinforce the importance of recognising diversity within CALD populations. National origin alone provides limited insight into how older immigrants understand, maintain or express their cultural identities.

Family Roles as a Plausible Pathway Between Identity and Well‐Being

The accounts documented in Theme 3 suggest that family role performance may function as a pathway through which cultural identity becomes experientially meaningful. These experiences of purpose, belonging and social significance align with Ryff's purpose in life and positive relations dimensions [7], extending into the discussion the interpretive connections drawn in the Results section.

This interpretation is consistent with existing literature demonstrating the importance of intergenerational roles for older immigrants' well‐being [22, 29, 30, 31]: Caregiving created opportunities for connection, guidance sustained elder authority and influence, and cultural transmission maintained continuity between generations.

A notable finding was the apparent interdependence of these roles: Caregiving often created the relational foundation through which guidance and cultural transmission became possible, with values and stories shared through routine interactions with younger family members.

The fading legacy pattern warrants particular attention. Several participants acknowledged that complete cultural transmission across generations was unlikely, yet continued to perform transmission roles regardless, suggesting the act of transmitting culture was itself an important expression of identity and elderhood, independent of its eventual success. Future research may further examine how role performance contributes to meaning‐making among older migrants in later life.

Implications for Aged Care Practice and Policy

The findings challenge approaches that position older immigrants primarily as recipients of culturally appropriate care [32, 33]. Participants consistently described themselves as active contributors to family and community life, with well‐being associated not only with receiving support but also with maintaining opportunities to provide care, guidance and cultural knowledge to others.

The domestic support finding has practical implications for assessment processes. Several participants described the loss of household support systems after migration as more than a practical adjustment. For them, this shift represented a change in how independence, competence and social status were experienced in later life. This shift speaks directly to Ryff's autonomy dimension [7], in that the loss of a taken‐for‐granted support structure altered participants' sense of independent functioning and self‐directed capability in daily life. Assessments that focus solely on physical or functional needs may overlook these broader psychosocial dimensions.

Participants also highlighted the importance of diaspora communities, which offered cultural participation, social connection and identity affirmation that family networks alone could not always provide. The findings support approaches that integrate family and community dimensions of care when supporting ageing in place among older immigrants [34].

Policy responses may include support for intergenerational cultural programs, investment in diaspora community infrastructure and cultural competency training that recognises diversity within CALD populations rather than assuming cultural homogeneity within national groups.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths include a relatively large qualitative sample, the use of participants' preferred languages and an in‐person interview format that facilitated detailed discussion of migration, identity and family experiences.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Participants were recruited exclusively from Southeast Queensland and were predominantly urban‐origin and relatively well‐educated, so findings may not transfer to older Indonesian immigrants with different migration histories or socioeconomic backgrounds. Length of residence in Australia also varied considerably across participants (Table 1), but the sample size did not allow systematic comparison of experiences by duration of residence, a direction future research with larger or duration‐stratified samples could pursue.

Bracketing is inherently harder to sustain when researcher and participants share deep cultural familiarity. Despite ongoing reflexivity and journaling [16], some interpretive assumptions rooted in the researcher's own Indonesian background may have shaped how accounts were understood and coded, consistent with documented challenges of insider positionality in qualitative research. At the same time, this shared heritage supported the rapport and linguistic depth that made detailed accounts of identity and family life possible, a trade‐off common to insider research on culturally specific populations [35].

The qualitative design captures participants' accounts at one point in time and cannot establish causal relationships between identity, family roles and psychological well‐being. Finally, only older immigrants' perspectives were examined. Future research incorporating adult children's and grandchildren's perspectives would provide a more comprehensive understanding of intergenerational role negotiation.

Conclusions

Older Indonesian immigrants in Australia actively negotiate their multicultural identities and family roles in ways they associate with psychological well‐being. Cultural identities were generally experienced as additive rather than conflicting, and family roles provided the everyday means of enacting and sustaining those identities.

Supporting older immigrants' well‐being therefore requires attention not only to care needs but also to opportunities for meaningful role performance within families and communities, a dimension of ageing well that remains under‐recognised for older adults from CALD backgrounds.
