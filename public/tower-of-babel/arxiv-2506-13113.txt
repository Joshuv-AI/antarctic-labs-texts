Dynamic Reinsurance Treaty Bidding via Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Stella C. Dong, James R. Finlay

Abstract
This paper develops a novel multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework for reinsurance treaty bidding, addressing long-standing inefficiencies in traditional broker-mediated placement processes. We pose the core research question: Can autonomous, learning-based bidding systems improve risk transfer efficiency and outperform conventional pricing approaches in reinsurance markets? In our model, each reinsurer is represented by an adaptive agent that iteratively refines its bidding strategy within a competitive, partially observable environment. The simulation explicitly incorporates institutional frictions including broker intermediation, incumbent advantages, last-look privileges, and asymmetric access to underwriting information. Empirical analysis demonstrates that MARL agents achieve up to 15 % 15\% higher underwriting profit, 20 % 20\% lower tail risk (CVaR), and over 25 % 25\% improvement in Sharpe ratios relative to actuarial and heuristic baselines. Sensitivity tests confirm robustness across hyperparameter settings, and stress testing reveals strong resilience under simulated catastrophe shocks and capital constraints. These findings suggest that MARL offers a viable path toward more transparent, adaptive, and risk-sensitive reinsurance markets. The proposed framework contributes to emerging literature at the intersection of algorithmic market design, strategic bidding, and AI-enabled financial decision-making.

Reinsurance Markets; Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning; Algorithmic Bidding; CVaR Optimization; Market Frictions; Risk Allocation; Strategic Learning

Introduction

Reinsurance markets play a critical role in the global risk transfer ecosystem by enabling insurance carriers to offload portions of their exposure to third-party capital providers. This process facilitates portfolio diversification, enhances capital efficiency, and stabilizes insurer solvency in the face of large-scale and low-frequency events such as natural disasters or systemic shocks
[
33
,
19
]
. Despite its importance, reinsurance treaty placement remains characterized by persistent inefficiencies: high transaction costs, limited price transparency, and strategic frictions stemming from broker intermediation
[
28
,
42
]
.

The prevailing placement model relies on bilateral negotiations facilitated by brokers who control the flow of information between insurers and reinsurers. Three institutional features reinforce inefficiencies in this arrangement. First,
broker intermediation
restricts reinsurers’ access to underlying underwriting data, impeding independent pricing and risk assessment. Second,
incumbent advantage
privileges reinsurers with prior participation in the treaty, often at the expense of competitive newcomers. Third,
last-look privilege
permits selected reinsurers to revise their bids after viewing competitors’ quotes. These market frictions distort efficient price discovery, inhibit dynamic learning, and raise barriers to entry.

The need for improved placement mechanisms is becoming increasingly urgent due to heightened systemic risk from climate volatility, pandemic-related losses, and macroeconomic uncertainty
[
66
,
48
,
58
]
. While workflow digitization platforms such as
eReinsure
have streamlined administrative aspects of treaty placement, they do not address the deeper problem of strategic inefficiency nor do they incorporate adaptive decision-making capabilities
[
9
]
.

In parallel domains such as financial trading and supply chain contracting, the application of reinforcement learning and autonomous agents has led to significant gains in efficiency and responsiveness under uncertainty
[
80
,
13
]
. Motivated by these advances, this paper investigates whether multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) can serve as a viable market design mechanism in reinsurance—specifically, whether it can improve the allocation of treaty capital relative to traditional broker-mediated processes.

We introduce a novel MARL-based framework for reinsurance treaty bidding
, in which reinsurers are modeled as autonomous agents that learn to optimize dynamic bidding strategies through repeated interactions in a simulated placement environment. Each agent jointly maximizes underwriting profit, tail risk performance (as measured by Conditional Value-at-Risk, CVaR
[
1
]
), and competitive positioning. The simulation incorporates key institutional features of the market, including sequential bid solicitations by insurers, asymmetric access to historical loss data, and insurer-specific utility functions that evaluate bids across multiple attributes such as pricing, capital relief, and counterparty diversification
[
87
,
36
]
.

This framework enables a comparative evaluation of MARL-based bidding against conventional benchmarks, including static actuarial pricing and rule-based heuristics. More broadly, it contributes to the design of algorithmically-mediated markets by examining how learning agents can navigate complex environments characterized by information frictions, competition, and risk constraints.

Summary of Contributions

•

We formulate reinsurance treaty placement as a sequential, strategic interaction among learning agents, grounded in a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework that reflects real-world market frictions and institutional constraints.

•

We also discuss adjacent applications in catastrophe bond markets, sovereign risk auctions, and infrastructure procurement.

Related Work

Despite substantial research in reinsurance modeling and reinforcement learning across financial domains, no prior study has applied dynamic, multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) to the treaty bidding problem. This section reviews foundational approaches from insurance economics, auction theory, and AI-driven market design—highlighting how our proposed framework addresses persistent limitations in adaptivity, institutional realism, and strategic competition.

2.1
Modeling Reinsurance Markets

Classical models of reinsurance treaty placement fall into two broad categories: game-theoretic formulations and portfolio optimization methods. Equilibrium models such as Nash or Bayesian-Nash games describe reinsurer behavior under idealized assumptions of complete information and rationality
[
33
,
19
]
. While analytically tractable, these models abstract away from critical institutional frictions such as broker intermediation, last-look bidding rights, and asymmetric access to treaty-specific information.

A separate literature frames reinsurance structuring as a single-period portfolio optimization problem. These models optimize risk-based objectives (e.g., VaR or CVaR) under regulatory and capital constraints from the insurer’s perspective
[
24
,
87
,
21
]
. However, they typically ignore the strategic behavior of reinsurers, and cannot capture iterative, competitive dynamics or adaptive learning over repeated treaty auctions.

Although digital platforms like
eReinsure
have improved document workflows and quote standardization, empirical studies report that reinsurance markets continue to lag behind other sectors in algorithmic adoption and AI integration
[
28
,
72
]
. Negotiation and pricing still largely depend on human brokers, making automation of strategic behavior a critical next step.

Synthesis and Research Gap

To summarize:

•

Prior models rely on static or equilibrium frameworks that do not accommodate real-world frictions such as broker influence, asymmetric information, and last-look advantages.

•

Most approaches treat insurers as optimizing agents but neglect strategic interaction among reinsurers bidding for treaty share.

•

No existing studies use MARL to simulate dynamic competition under institutional constraints in treaty reinsurance.

This paper addresses these gaps by introducing a MARL-based framework for treaty bidding. Reinsurance agents learn bidding policies through repeated interaction in a simulated environment that reflects key institutional realities—including incumbent favoritism, limited observability, and broker-mediated quoting. Our formulation enables a shift from static optimization to dynamic market design.

Problem Formulation

We model reinsurance treaty placement as a high-stakes, sequential decision-making process involving multiple autonomous reinsurers. To capture its decentralized, strategic, and partially observed nature, we formulate the problem as a
Partially Observable Markov Game
(POMG)
[
73
,
10
]
, a generalization of Markov decision processes to multi-agent settings.

Each agent represents a reinsurer learning a policy through repeated bidding episodes. The environment models an insurer issuing treaties, reinsurers submitting bids, and a centralized mechanism selecting winners. Unlike classical equilibrium models, this formulation supports adaptive learning, bounded rationality, and endogenous market feedback under realistic institutional frictions.

We provide an overview of this process in Figure
2
, which illustrates the bid generation, selection, and feedback cycle that drives learning across agents and time.

3.2
Agent Objectives

Each agent
i
seeks to learn a policy
\pi_{i}
that maximizes its long-term expected discounted reward:

\max_{\pi_{i}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\gamma^{t-1}R_{i}(t)\right],

(2)

where
\gamma\in[0,1]
is a temporal discount factor.

The reward function balances three institutional goals:

R_{i}(t)=\text{Profit}_{i}(t)-\lambda\cdot\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(X_{i}(t))+\gamma_{\text{eff}}\cdot\text{Efficiency}_{i}(t),

(3)

with:

•

•

\text{Profit}_{i}(t)
: Underwriting result (premium - claims - commissions).

•

\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}
: Tail risk of the portfolio (95% default).

•

\text{Efficiency}_{i}(t)
: Operational score.

Efficiency Metric:

\text{Efficiency}_{i}(t)=w_{1}\cdot\text{WinRate}_{i}+w_{2}\cdot\text{InvLatency}_{i}+w_{3}\cdot\text{CostScore}_{i},\quad\sum w_{j}=1.

Interpretation:
Agents are incentivized to bid quickly and competitively, simulating real-world underwriting discipline.

Loss Ratio (Monitoring Metric):

\text{LossRatio}_{i}=\frac{\text{Claims Paid}_{i}}{\text{Premium Earned}_{i}},

(4)

A solvency indicator widely used in insurance.

3.3
Learning Setup

To train reinsurance agents in a competitive, partially observable environment, we adopt the
Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution
(CTDE) framework
[
39
]
. This approach enables agents to access global state information during training—facilitating coordinated credit assignment and stable policy updates—while enforcing decentralized decision-making at execution time based solely on local observations. This design mirrors the informational asymmetries faced by real-world reinsurers.

3.3.1
Algorithms Compared.

We evaluate three state-of-the-art actor-critic algorithms for multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), all tailored to continuous action spaces and strategic bidding dynamics:

•

Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG)

[
60
]
: An extension of DDPG with decentralized actors and a centralized critic conditioned on joint actions. While effective in low-noise control tasks, MADDPG is often unstable in highly non-stationary environments such as competitive bidding.

•

Multi-Agent Twin Delayed DDPG (MATD3)

[
97
]
: Builds upon MADDPG with twin critics and policy smoothing to reduce Q-value overestimation. MATD3 improves robustness and sample efficiency, especially under stochastic rewards.

•

Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)

[
94
]
: Combines PPO’s clipped surrogate objective with centralized critics. MAPPO provides greater training stability, especially under adversarial interactions and partial observability. It has demonstrated strong empirical performance across standard MARL benchmarks.

As shown in Figure
3
, MAPPO consistently achieves faster convergence, more stable learning curves, and better generalization across random seeds compared to MADDPG and MATD3. Its clipped policy updates and advantage normalization mitigate instability from opponent-induced non-stationarity. Based on these results, we adopt MAPPO as the default algorithm for all subsequent experiments.

3.3.2
Environment Characteristics.

Our reinsurance bidding environment introduces domain-specific challenges that motivate algorithmic care:

•

High-dimensional input and action spaces:
Each agent encodes treaty metadata and internal portfolio context to generate continuous-valued bids, including premium rate, attachment, and limit.

•

Episodic interaction with structured resets:
Each episode simulates a full treaty placement cycle—starting from issuance and ending in reward allocation—consistent with real-world quarterly or annual reinsurance operations (see Figure
4
).

•

Strategic coupling:
Agent outcomes are interdependent due to overlapping exposures and competitive pricing. This creates a highly non-stationary learning environment where naïve policies quickly become obsolete.

These characteristics make reinsurance treaty placement a natural testbed for evaluating stability and coordination in competitive MARL systems.

System Design and Methodology

This section presents the architectural and methodological foundation of our multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework for modeling competitive treaty reinsurance bidding. We describe the simulation environment that captures key structural and stochastic features of real-world reinsurance markets. We then detail the learning setup, including algorithmic choices, training protocols, and environment dynamics. Baseline strategies—both actuarial and heuristic—are introduced to contextualize performance benchmarks. Finally, we outline a suite of stress-testing scenarios designed to assess robustness under economic shocks and adversarial conditions.

4.1
Simulation Environment

We construct a simulation environment to capture the structural complexity and stochastic variability of real-world reinsurance markets. Each episode begins with the issuance of a new treaty, characterized by a feature vector drawn from empirically calibrated distributions. These features include: exposure size, historical loss distribution, line of business, attachment point, coverage limit, and retention level. Table
1
summarizes the main components of treaty generation.

Loss outcomes are generated using a two-part probabilistic model. Attritional losses—representing frequent, low-severity claims—are sampled from a compound Poisson distribution based on calibrated frequency and severity parameters. Catastrophic losses—rare but high-impact events—are drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution (e.g., Pareto or Lognormal)
[
36
]
. This layered structure captures both volatility in expected losses and extreme tail exposure typical of commercial treaty portfolios.

The bid evaluation mechanism is governed by a stochastic utility function used by the insurer to rank submitted bids. Each bid is evaluated based on a joint consideration of price and coverage generosity. The insurer’s utility for bid
i
is defined as:

U_{i}=-\text{Premium}_{i}+\theta\cdot\text{CoverageQuality}_{i}+\epsilon_{i},

(5)

where
\theta
is a weighting parameter reflecting risk-adjusted preferences and
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
introduces selection noise. This formulation reflects real-world frictions such as broker discretion, asymmetric information, and placement uncertainty
[
33
]
.

The presence of stochastic utility rankings introduces strategic uncertainty for bidding agents. Agents must learn robust policies that balance exploration and exploitation in the face of variable market conditions and partial observability, aligning with the decision-making complexity faced by real reinsurers.

4.2
Agent Architecture

We model each reinsurer as an autonomous learning agent embedded in a decentralized, partially observable multi-agent environment. Each agent maintains two neural components:

•

A
policy network
(actor) that processes local treaty features and market observations to output a continuous bid vector—comprising premium rates, quota shares, and ceding commissions. This actor governs the agent’s decisions at deployment time.

•

A
value network
(critic) trained centrally with access to global state information to estimate expected returns. This critic is used exclusively during training to provide stable gradient feedback for actor updates.

We implement the
Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)
algorithm
[
94
]
as the core learning method. MAPPO extends PPO with centralized critics, clipped surrogate objectives, and advantage normalization—offering robust training dynamics in environments with adversarial coupling and partial observability. Empirical evaluation shows MAPPO outperforms alternative MARL algorithms such as MADDPG and MATD3 in convergence speed and reward stability (see Figure
3
).

Training follows a
Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution
(CTDE) paradigm
[
39
]
. During training, agents share a prioritized experience replay buffer
[
67
]
and update policies using global state trajectories. At execution, agents operate independently based on local observations—mirroring real-world informational asymmetries in treaty markets.

Agent rewards incorporate both profitability and downside risk. In particular, a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) penalty
[
1
,
85
]
discourages exposure to catastrophic tail losses, promoting risk-aware bidding strategies that satisfy solvency and regulatory constraints.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, agents iteratively receive treaty opportunities, submit bids, receive placement feedback, and update their policies through repeated simulation. This loop enables the emergence of adaptive, economically rational bidding behaviors in a dynamic and competitive reinsurance market.

4.3
Modeling Market Frictions

To better reflect institutional constraints and informational asymmetries characteristic of reinsurance markets, we explicitly incorporate three salient market frictions that shape bidding dynamics and outcomes. These frictions introduce deviations from idealized auction behavior and are grounded in both empirical market design and auction-theoretic foundations
[
64
,
55
]
.

Formal Setup.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\dots,N\}
denote the set of reinsurer agents bidding on a treaty
T
. Each agent
i\in\mathcal{A}
submits a bid
b_{i}=(r_{i},c_{i})
, where
r_{i}
is a quoted premium rate and
c_{i}
comprises additional coverage terms such as quota share, coverage limit, and attachment level. The insurer evaluates each bid using a stochastic utility function

U_{i}=u(b_{i};\theta_{T})+\epsilon_{i},

where
u(\cdot)
is a deterministic function that maps the bid and treaty attributes
\theta_{T}
into utility space, and
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
captures idiosyncratic heterogeneity in preferences
[
33
]
.

Under frictionless conditions, the treaty is awarded to the agent offering the highest utility:

i^{*}=\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}}U_{i}.

However, if agent
j\in\mathcal{A}
is the incumbent reinsurer, they are awarded the treaty even when slightly outbid, provided their utility is within a threshold buffer
\delta>0
of the top bid:

i^{*}=\begin{cases}j,&\text{if }j\text{ is incumbent and }U_{j}\geq(1-\delta)\cdot\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}}U_{i},\\
\arg\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}}U_{i},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

This mechanism formalizes
incumbent advantage
—a structural bias favoring previously selected reinsurers, justified by continuity of relationship, reduced onboarding cost, and trust in claim settlement.

Last-Look Mechanism.

If the incumbent is not initially selected via Equation (
6
), a
last-look
opportunity is provided. The incumbent is allowed to revise their bid,
b_{j}\rightarrow b_{j}^{\prime}
, after observing partial market outcomes. The updated bid is accepted if it exceeds the prior best offer in utility terms:

U_{j}^{\prime}=u(b_{j}^{\prime};\theta_{T})+\epsilon_{j}\geq\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{j\}}U_{i}.

(7)

This introduces a sequential, dynamic auction structure with asymmetric timing, in line with the literature on efficient dynamic auctions
[
2
,
64
]
.

Broker Visibility and Asymmetry.

Brokers facilitate the market by collecting bids from all agents and recommending placements to the insurer. Brokers have full visibility into all submitted bids
\{b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Reinsurers, in contrast, observe only their own bid and final outcome (
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
). This creates a
partially observable game with asymmetric information
, departing from classical Nash equilibrium settings. Strategic reasoning under such opacity leads to emergent behavior shaped by Bayesian updating and belief modeling across repeated episodes.

4.4
Baseline Models for Comparison

To assess the value and robustness of our proposed multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) framework, we benchmark its performance against three widely recognized baseline strategies. These baselines reflect common practices in actuarial pricing, naive heuristics, and single-agent learning, allowing us to disentangle the contributions of strategic adaptation, coordination, and environmental awareness.

•

Static Actuarial Pricing
: Each agent computes a bid based on the expected loss
\mathbb{E}[L]
plus a fixed risk loading
\lambda
, i.e.,
b_{i}=\mathbb{E}[L_{i}]\cdot(1+\lambda)
, where
\lambda
is calibrated from historical reinsurance margins. This method approximates standard actuarial pricing rules used in industry, grounded in underwriting discipline and loss ratio targeting
[
14
,
89
]
.

•

Random Bidding
: Bid vectors are drawn uniformly from the admissible action space
\mathcal{B}
without reference to treaty features or opponent actions. This naive strategy serves as a lower-bound baseline and exposes the performance degradation under uncoordinated, non-strategic behavior.

•

6.1
Managerial Implications

This study provides actionable insights for insurance executives, reinsurance underwriters, and strategic decision-makers evaluating digital transformation in treaty placement.

For insurers
, MARL-based bidding platforms enable real-time evaluation of competitive reinsurance offers, replacing slow, opaque processes. For instance, what traditionally takes 2–4 weeks via broker-mediated negotiations could be reduced to 2–3 days under algorithmic bidding—especially during peak renewal seasons or systemic crises like COVID-19
[
61
,
43
]
. Insurers gain deeper visibility into reinsurer risk appetites, pricing behaviors, and treaty differentiation, allowing them to better manage counterparty concentration and diversify ceded risk portfolios
[
19
,
92
]
.

For reinsurers
, the ability to adapt bids in response to real-time treaty data and competitive signals improves capital deployment and portfolio efficiency. For example, in a simulated capacity shock, agents dynamically prioritized lower-risk treaties and avoided overbidding on high-severity contracts, improving their expected return-to-risk ratios. These insights translate into more selective, data-driven underwriting workflows that outperform static actuarial pricing or fixed bidding heuristics
[
68
,
6
]
.

Implementation Challenges
: Despite promising benefits, practical deployment requires overcoming several organizational and technical hurdles:

•

Data integration
: Real-time treaty ingestion requires interoperability across insurer systems, contract repositories, and pricing models
[
35
]
.

•

Model governance
: Firms must establish safeguards for monitoring MARL behavior, detecting failure modes, and ensuring alignment with underwriting guidelines
[
76
]
.

•

Human-AI interface
: Underwriters and treaty managers will need tools that allow MARL agents to provide interpretable rationales for actions, rather than fully automating strategic judgment
[
29
]
.

Role of brokers
: Rather than eliminating intermediaries, MARL may shift broker roles toward higher-value advisory services—e.g., helping clients interpret agent signals, structure innovative treaties, or manage emerging risks. As with algorithmic trading in finance, domain expertise will still be essential in ensuring that algorithmic tools enhance rather than displace professional judgment
[

,

.

The systemic adoption of MARL-based platforms raises both opportunities and responsibilities for market governance.

Regulatory Challenges

6.3
Limitations and Future Directions

Several caveats should guide both the interpretation of results and future research:

•

Static Bidding Episodes
: The current framework models one-period treaty decisions. Real-world placements involve multi-year relationships, rolling renewal dynamics, and interdependencies with capital reserving.

•

Simplified Market Frictions
: We abstract from strategic interactions like multi-party negotiations, broker bundling, and information leakage—all common in practice.

•

Macroeconomic Exclusion
: External factors such as interest rate shifts, inflation, and global reinsurer solvency (e.g., from rating agency pressures) are not explicitly modeled, though they influence bid and capacity decisions in reality
[

•

Limited Strategic Behavior

Scalability and Explainability

Summary and Outlook

This study demonstrates the feasibility of deploying multi-agent reinforcement learning in a domain traditionally governed by expert judgment and static models. MARL agents not only outperform static competitors, but also exhibit resilience under stress and robustness across hyperparameter variations.

Concrete benefits include faster placement, lower bid volatility, and strategic reallocation of capital post-shock. Policy applications may include stress-testing tools, transparency reforms, or market conduct supervision frameworks.

Yet full implementation will require careful alignment of data systems, oversight structures, and human expertise. As the reinsurance industry increasingly digitizes, MARL offers a blueprint for how algorithmic systems and expert insight can co-evolve to meet 21st-century risk challenges.

7
Limitations and Future Research

While this study demonstrates the promise of multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) for reinsurance treaty bidding, several limitations constrain the current framework. These limitations are not only technically important but carry practical implications for real-world deployment. Addressing them provides a roadmap for future innovation.

•

Single-Period Modeling:
The current model treats each treaty placement as an isolated event. While simplifying analysis, this abstraction omits the strategic considerations insurers and reinsurers face over multi-year relationships, renewal cycles, and capital reserving commitments. Extending the framework to multi-period settings would capture dynamic contract management, reputation effects, and intertemporal tradeoffs
[
21
,
87
]
.

•

Simplified Market Frictions:
Although the environment includes frictions such as capacity limits and broker opacity, it omits features like syndication, exclusive deals, and regulatory capital charges. These omissions may limit the fidelity of the simulation. Future work could adopt agent-based simulations or structured game-theoretic models to represent market structure and intermediation dynamics more comprehensively
[
92
,
42
]
.

•

Macroeconomic Stationarity Assumption:
The current model assumes a stable macroeconomic regime. In reality, market cycles, inflation, and capital volatility significantly affect reinsurance pricing and appetite. Incorporating regime-switching models such as hidden Markov processes
[
44
,
45
]
would enable agents to adjust behavior based on macro-financial signals and latent economic states.

•

Limited Scalability:
While the framework demonstrates learning efficiency in moderate-scale markets, scaling to global reinsurance portfolios with multi-region networks will require improved MARL techniques. Potential solutions include distributed training architectures, curriculum learning, or enhanced credit assignment strategies to handle increased complexity
[
39
,
60
]
.

•

Lack of Strategic Adversarial Behavior:
Agents in this study compete competitively but do not exhibit adversarial or deceptive behavior, such as collusion or misinformation. Introducing adversarial learning or opponent modeling
[
75
]
would help evaluate the robustness of the MARL framework in adversarial market environments.

•

Behavioral Simplifications:
The agents assume rational, expected-utility-maximizing behavior. However, empirical research shows that real-world decision-makers often follow boundedly rational or heuristic-driven strategies. Embedding behavioral risk preferences, ambiguity aversion, or satisficing objectives
[
41
,
3
]
could lead to agents that better reflect realistic institutional decision-making.

•

Lack of Field Validation:
All results in this study are derived from simulated environments. While these simulations are grounded in realistic treaty features, field validation through pilot deployments with reinsurers, insurers, or brokers is essential to test adoption feasibility, compliance implications, and decision-support capabilities in live settings
[
27
]
.

Research Opportunities.
Addressing these limitations leads to several novel directions:

•

Multi-Year Treaty Simulation:
Enable agents to optimize long-term profitability and capital allocation across multi-period engagements.

•

Macroeconomic-Aware Bidding:
Incorporate inflation trends, capital cycles, and macro-regimes into agents’ learning objectives.

•

Strategic Broker Modeling:
Simulate broker incentives and intermediation strategies, especially under information asymmetry or anti-selection.

•

Behavioral Economics-Driven Agents:
Develop models that integrate non-linear utility functions, cognitive biases, or fairness constraints in alignment with emerging AI governance norms.

•

Human–AI Collaboration:
Design MARL tools not as replacements, but as advisory co-pilots supporting underwriters with interpretable outputs and override mechanisms.

•

Field Deployment and Evaluation:
Collaborate with industry partners to test MARL systems in operational or sandbox environments, allowing for iterative refinement and impact assessment.

Together, these enhancements offer a path toward building robust, ethical, and industry-ready AI systems for reinsurance treaty placement and beyond.

This study presents a novel framework for dynamic reinsurance treaty placement using multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL), addressing long-standing inefficiencies in broker-mediated, static bidding processes

Our core contributions are threefold. By reducing dependency on manual intermediation and enabling algorithmic bidding under uncertainty, MARL offers a powerful tool for modernizing risk transfer mechanisms.

Ultimately, this study offers a viable path toward a future where reinsurance markets are more transparent, adaptive, and resilient—powered by advanced multi-agent learning systems.
