Attitudes, perceptions, and UTAUT-based factors influencing the acceptance of medical artificial intelligence among Chinese oncology healthcare professionals: a national cross-sectional survey
Authors: Zhaoyu Wang, Qianqian Dai, Maoshu Yang, Shiwu Shi, Jiaojiao Liao, Zhaoji Li, Xiaoqiu Dai, Liyuan Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo conduct a nationwide survey among professionals working in oncology departments in China to investigate their attitudes, perceptions, and experiences regarding medical artificial intelligence (AI), and to explore and compare the factors influencing AI behavioral intention (BI; willingness to adopt AI) between physicians and nurses using the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT).Materials and MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted among professionals in oncology departments across China. Sociodemographic characteristics, awareness, perceptions, and user experiences related to medical AI were collected. The UTAUT was specifically applied to the third section of the survey to evaluate participants’ perceptions. Based on this framework, structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed to examine associations between BI and 6 latent constructs: performance expectancy (PE), effort expectancy (EE), social influence (SI), facilitating conditions (FC), perceived risk (PR), and trust (TR) based on the UTAUT. Subgroup SEM analyses were then conducted for physicians and nurses.ResultsSix hundred ten valid responses were included in the final analysis, consisting of 188 (30.8%) physicians and 422 (69.2%) nurses. Only 17.7% reported having both heard of and used medical AI, while 67.7% had heard of it but never used it. SEM results showed that TR (confidence in system reliability; β = .668, P < .001) had the strongest effect on BI, followed by effort EE (perceived ease of use; β = .155, P = .026). Subgroup analyses revealed that for physicians, both EE (β = .257, P = .038) and TR (β = .486, P < .001) significantly influenced BI, while among nurses, only TR (β = .729, P < .001) showed a significant effect.ConclusionsThere is a significant “awareness-usage gap” among Chinese oncology professionals, with high awareness but low practical integration of medical AI. This nationwide study indicates that TR and EE are associated with the intention to use AI. To bridge this gap, hospital management should implement differentiated training and local validation protocols that address the unique technical and ethical concerns of physicians and nurses, respectively.Keywords: medical artificial intelligence, oncology, UTAUT, behavioral intention, structural equation modeling

Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has undergone rapid development. With its powerful capabilities in data processing, pattern recognition, and predictive analytics—coupled with the increasing availability of large-scale, multimodal medical and health data—AI has been progressively integrated into healthcare.1–4 This integration is significantly enhancing the quality and efficiency of medical services while improving the experiences of both healthcare providers and patients.5–7 In particular, oncology—a field characterized by complex diagnostic and staging processes, challenging treatment and care pathways, and a strong dependence on multidisciplinary collaboration—has witnessed substantial transformation driven by AI. From pathological diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making to clinical management, medical AI is reshaping multiple aspects of oncological practice, demonstrating remarkable potential and a broad spectrum of clinical applications.8–12

Despite these advancements, the successful translation of medical AI into real-world clinical practice depends not only on technological breakthroughs but also, critically, on the acceptance and engagement of end users, such as physicians and nurses.13–15 Although the potential and future promise of medical AI are increasingly recognized by healthcare professionals, several concerns continue to hinder its widespread adoption in clinical settings. These concerns include the “black-box” nature of AI algorithms that limits clinical interpretability, algorithmic bias that may favor specific features or population groups, difficulties integrating AI tools into existing clinical workflows—which may inadvertently increase operational complexity—as well as unresolved issues around accountability for diagnostic errors and the potential erosion of humanistic care.13,16–19 Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of physicians’ and nurses’ perceptions and attitudes toward medical AI—both at a general level and across different medical disciplines—is essential. Equally important is the identification of key factors that influence their willingness to adopt and use AI in practice, as this understanding is critical for facilitating the effective and responsible implementation of AI technologies in clinical care.

At present, both domestic and international studies have conducted multi-dimensional investigations into healthcare professionals’ perceptions, attitudes, and experiences regarding medical AI across various specialties.20–23 Several of these studies have specifically focused on oncology professionals. For example, Hantel et al24 conducted a survey among US oncologists to explore the ethical implications of using AI in cancer care; Bourbonne et al25 investigated the perceptions of French radiation oncologists toward AI-based automatic segmentation technologies; and Sahin et al26 surveyed Turkish oncologists regarding their views, knowledge, and experiences related to AI applications. In China, Li et al27,28 carried out a cross-sectional study in 2023 involving 228 oncologists to assess their attitudes and perceptions of AI in healthcare. Their findings revealed that 74.13% of respondents believed AI was beneficial and innovative in medicine, while 52.19% expressed trust in AI. The study also identified key drivers of AI adoption. However, existing studies have primarily focused on physicians, with limited inclusion of nursing professionals, particularly within the oncology setting. As a result, current evidence fails to provide a comprehensive understanding of attitudes and perceptions toward medical AI across the entire oncology workforce. Moreover, few studies have directly compared the perspectives of physicians and nurses or grounded their analyses in comprehensive theoretical frameworks,29,30 such as the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT).31

The intention to use medical AI is shaped by multiple complex and interacting factors. Several theoretical frameworks have been developed to understand technology adoption behaviors, including the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT).32 Among these, UTAUT, developed by Venkatesh et al,31 has gained significant attention due to its robustness and ability to integrate elements from 8 prominent technology acceptance theories (including TAM and TPB). UTAUT provides a comprehensive explanation of user behavior toward emerging technologies, particularly in the context of information systems and technology acceptance.33 Its core presupposition is that an individual’s behavioral intention is primarily determined by 4 key constructs: performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions.31

The methodological synergy between UTAUT and structural equation modeling (SEM) offers a distinct analytical advantage. SEM is a powerful analytical tool that allows for the examination of complex relationships between latent constructs, which are not directly observable but are inferred through other measured variables.34–36 Unlike traditional regression models, which can oversimplify the relationships between constructs, SEM enables the simultaneous assessment of multiple causal paths and accounts for measurement errors, thereby increasing the accuracy and theoretical validity of the relationships being studied.34–36 Previous studies have successfully applied the UTAUT model in a variety of fields, including healthcare, where it has been extended and refined to capture the nuanced and multi-faceted nature of healthcare professionals’ technology adoption decisions.14,37,38 Researchers have integrated SEM with UTAUT to assess the complex interactions among different adoption factors and to quantify their influence on behavioral intentions, particularly in the context of adopting new technologies like artificial intelligence in medical settings.39–42 By incorporating latent variables and causal modeling, SEM offers a more rigorous and detailed understanding of the underlying psychological mechanisms behind technology adoption in healthcare.39–42

Given the critical role of oncology as a key application area for medical AI, this study aims to conduct a nationwide survey among clinical professionals working in oncology departments in China. Specifically, the study investigated their attitudes, perceptions, and experiences regarding medical AI. The UTAUT was used as the core theoretical framework for the perceptions section of the survey to enable a structured exploration and comparison of the factors influencing physicians’ and nurses’ behavioral intentions toward AI. The findings provide empirical evidence and strategic insights to support the effective integration and implementation of AI technologies into oncology clinical practice.

Methods

Study design and participant recruitment

This study conducted a nationwide online survey targeting clinical healthcare professionals working in oncology departments across China from February to March 2025. The questionnaire was distributed via Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), a widely recognized professional online survey platform in China. Participants were recruited using a nonrandom convenience sampling approach, and invitations were sent anonymously and voluntarily to members of the Oncology Society of the Chinese Medical Association and the Chinese Nursing Association. Prior to completing the questionnaire, all participants were required to provide electronic informed consent, ensuring that they fully understood the purpose and procedures of the study and were aware of their right to withdraw at any time. To maintain data quality, each participant was allowed to respond only once, and responses completed in under 120 seconds were considered invalid and excluded from the analysis. This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (Approval number: M20241008), and all procedures adhered to ethical standards involving human participants. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.43

Questionnaire items

The questionnaire consisted of 4 main sections: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, (2) awareness of medical AI, (3) perceptions of medical AI, and (4) user experiences with medical AI. The full questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary File 1.

The sociodemographic section collected data on sex, age, profession, highest educational attainment, involvement in clinical care, level of hospital, type of practice, professional title, and years of work experience. The section on medical AI awareness included the following items: whether respondents had heard of or used medical AI; their level of awareness; frequency of use; perceived attention to medical AI at their workplace; perceived future prospects of medical AI; and areas of interest in its application. Only respondents who reported having used medical AI (n = 108) were eligible to complete the fourth section on user experiences. The section on user experiences included items regarding the duration of AI use, satisfaction with AI tools, primary purposes for using medical AI, and suggestions for improvement to current AI systems.

The third section—perceptions of medical AI—constituted the core of the questionnaire. It was developed based on the UTAUT and included 7 dimensions: the 4 foundational constructs of performance expectancy (PE), effort expectancy (EE), social influence (SI), and facilitating conditions (FC), supplemented by 2 context-specific variables, perceived risk (PR) and trust (TR), as well as behavioral intention (BI). Each dimension consisted of 2 to 4 items, totaling 24 items. Responses were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree). In the present study, TR was operationalized as healthcare professionals’ overall confidence that medical AI can be used in a reliable, professionally acceptable, and appropriately governed manner in oncology practice. Specifically, the TR items captured confidence in AI’s potential to improve care quality, avoid undermining professional skills, protect patient privacy and data security, and operate within procedures such as informed consent that may enhance trustworthiness.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations. For the section on perceptions of medical AI, the variables were treated both as categorical and continuous. Given the non-normal distribution of these perception scores, we reported both means with standard deviations (SD) and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous data.

Group differences between physicians and nurses in terms of sociodemographic characteristics, awareness of medical AI, and user experiences were compared using independent-sample t-tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables.

SEM was performed using the lavaan package in R (version 4.4.1) to explore the associations between 6 latent constructs—PE, EE, SI, FC, PR, and TR—and the outcome construct, BI. A latent construct refers to an underlying concept that is not measured directly by a single question but is statistically inferred from multiple observed items. The SEM included both a measurement model and a structural model. In the measurement model, PE, EE, SI, FC, PR, TR, and BI were specified as latent variables measured by 2 to 4 observed items each. To ensure the reliability and validity of the measurement model, composite reliability (CR), convergent validity (based on standardized factor loadings [SFL], average variance extracted [AVE], and squared multiple correlations [SMC]), and discriminant validity were rigorously assessed prior to estimating the structural paths. The structural model was built based on the UTAUT, assuming that all 6 predictor constructs exert direct effects on BI. Parameter estimation was conducted using maximum likelihood estimation. To improve the robustness of standard errors and confidence intervals under the condition of non-normality, bootstrapping with 500 resamples was applied.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple goodness-of-fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). To assess the variation in influencing factors across different professional groups, we conducted subgroup SEM analyses separately for physicians and nurses. Furthermore, to ensure the robustness of our findings and address potential selection bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by restricting the SEM to participants who had never used medical AI (n = 502). All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 798 oncology healthcare professionals completed the online survey, with an average completion time of 4.81 minutes. After quality control screening for eligibility and response validity, 610 (76.4%) valid responses were included in the final analysis. The detailed participant selection process is presented in Figure S1. Among the respondents, 84.9% were female and 15.1% were male. The mean age was 36.11 years (SD, 8.70). In terms of professional roles, 30.8% were physicians and 69.2% were nurses. The predominance of female respondents largely reflected the professional composition of the sample, as nurses accounted for a substantial proportion of participants and the nursing subgroup was predominantly female. The largest proportion of respondents were from the Eastern region of China (45.4%). A majority (81.3%) worked in tertiary A-level hospitals. Regarding educational background, 59.7% held a bachelor’s degree, and 9.2% were engaged in traditional Chinese medicine or integrated Chinese and Western medicine practices. Awareness of respondents on medical AI

Among the 610 eligible respondents, only 17.7% reported having both heard of and used medical AI, while 67.7% had heard of it but never used it. Regarding their level of awareness, only 5.2% of respondents reported having a good or complete understanding of medical AI, and 6.6% reported using it regularly or very frequently. When asked about the level of attention paid to medical AI at their workplace, 9.0% of participants reported that their institution paid very high attention, and 24.9% reported high attention. In contrast, 3.4% of respondents stated that their workplace paid no attention at all. Despite this, the vast majority (82.6%) expressed optimism about the future application prospects of medical AI in healthcare. Detailed results are shown in Table 2.

Perceptions of respondents on medical AI

We developed a 24-item questionnaire grounded in the UTAUT to assess the perceptions of oncology healthcare professionals regarding medical AI. As shown in Figure 2, respondents generally held favorable views of medical AI across most dimensions. The PE dimension exhibited the highest average scores, with a median of 4.00 (IQR, 3.75-5.00) and a mean of 4.16 (SD, 0.76). EE also received relatively positive evaluations, with a median of 4.00 (IQR, 4.00-5.00) and a mean of 4.21 (SD, 0.73). SI and FC shared similar scoring patterns, both with medians of 3.67 and means of 3.60 (SD, 0.82) and 3.66 (SD, 0.81), respectively. In contrast, PR scored lower, with a median of 3.25 (IQR, 3.00-4.00) and a mean of 3.43 (SD, 0.79). TR in medical AI was relatively high, with a median of 4.00 (IQR, 3.25-4.00) and a mean of 3.83 (SD, 0.68), and BI to use AI also showed strong agreement, with a median of 4.00 (IQR, 3.50-4.25) and a mean of 3.97 (SD, 0.69).

To investigate the drivers of medical AI adoption, we employed SEM, which evaluates relationships between multiple latent predictors (PE, EE, SI, FC, PR, and TR) and the outcome construct (BI) simultaneously. Unlike traditional regression, SEM accounts for measurement error and provides a holistic view of complex structural paths by inferring theoretical constructs from observed survey indicators. To further examine the determinants of BI to use medical AI, we constructed a SEM incorporating 6 latent predictors. We assessed the reliability and validity of the measurement model using CR, convergent validity, and discriminant validity. Detailed results are presented in Tables S1 and S2. All constructs demonstrated strong internal consistency, with CR coefficients ranging from 0.915 (TR) to 0.972 (BI), exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70. The AVE values ranged from 0.729 to 0.907, indicating that each construct accounted for more than 50% of the variance of its indicators, which supports acceptable convergent validity. At the item level, all SFL were substantial, ranging from 0.766 (PR4) to 0.975 (BI2), and all were statistically significant with small standard errors. The SMC for individual items ranged from 0.587 to 0.951, further supporting indicator reliability. As shown in Table S2, The square roots of the AVE values (shown on the diagonal) were greater than the inter-construct correlations, confirming that each construct was empirically distinct from the others.

Overall, the model demonstrated a good fit to the data, with fit indices meeting commonly accepted thresholds: CFI = 0.960 (threshold >0.90), TLI = 0.952 (threshold >0.90), RMSEA = 0.070 (threshold < 0.08), and SRMR = 0.039 (threshold < 0.08), as shown in Table S3. The model explained 72.9% of the variance in BI (R2 = 0.729). The detailed SEM results are presented in Table S4 and the path diagram is shown in Figure 3. SEM results indicated that TR exerted a strong and statistically significant positive effect on BI (standardized path coefficient β = .668; 95% CI, 0.623-0.854; P < .001). EE also had a smaller but still significant positive effect (β = .155; 95% CI, 0.017-0.280; P = .026). The remaining latent variables did not exhibit statistically significant direct effects on BI.

In addition, we conducted subgroup SEM analyses separately for nurses and physicians, applying the same model specifications and fit criteria. Before interpreting subgroup-specific path estimates, measurement invariance across physicians and nurses was assessed using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis. Configural and metric invariance were supported, and scalar invariance was additionally supported based on changes in practical fit indices, whereas strict invariance was not supported (Table S5). These findings indicate that the latent constructs were measured comparably across physicians and nurses, providing support for subsequent subgroup SEM analyses. Both subgroup models demonstrated acceptable fit to the data. The measurement reliability, validity, and estimated coefficients are provided in Tables S6 to S11. Among physicians, the model accounted for 65.8% of the variance in BI (R2 = 0.658). Within this group, only EE (β = .257; 95% CI, -0.020-0.439; P = .038) and TR (β = .486; 95% CI, 0.344-0.931; P < .001) were significantly associated with BI. The path diagram for the physician group is shown in Figure S2. In contrast, the model for nurses explained 77.5% of the variance in BI (R2 = 0.775), and among all the latent variables, only TR demonstrated a statistically significant positive effect on BI (β = .765; 95% CI, 0.627-0.886; P < .001), as illustrated in Figure S3. To further address the reviewer’s concern regarding the high proportion of non-users (82.3%), a sensitivity analysis was performed on the AI-naïve subgroup. The results remained consistent with the primary analysis, confirming that the structural relationships—particularly the dominant influence of TR—were not skewed by the inclusion of experienced users (see Table S3 and Figure S4).

User experiences of respondents on medical AI

We also surveyed the user experience of the 108 respondents who reported having used medical AI. Among them, 47 individuals (43.5%) had used medical AI for more than 1 year, and 22 (20.4%) had used it for over 3 years. In terms of satisfaction, 68 respondents (63.0%) rated their experience as satisfactory or higher. When asked about the primary purposes for which they used medical AI, the most frequently cited use among physicians was diagnostic assistance (78.6%), while among nurses, the most common application was patient monitoring (77.3%) (see Figure 4. Regarding suggestions for improving satisfaction with medical AI, both physicians and nurses most frequently selected enhancing system stability and reliability, with 83.3% of physicians and 92.4% of nurses endorsing this need (see Figure 5).

Discussion

This study conducted a nationwide cross-sectional survey among oncology healthcare professionals in China to assess their awareness, perceptions, and experience with medical AI. Based on the UTAUT, a comprehensive framework for explaining technology adoption, we further constructed a SEM, a multivariate statistical technique used to estimate complex relationships between variables. This allowed us to explore key dimensions influencing their intention to use (behavioral intention, BI), which represents the likelihood of an individual to adopt and use AI technologies in clinical practice. Our findings indicate that while a majority of oncology professionals were aware of medical AI, only a small proportion reported a high level of understanding or actual use. Nevertheless, the overall attitude toward its future application prospects was optimistic. Physicians, compared with nurses, demonstrated higher levels of both awareness and use. They were more interested in AI applications in clinical decision-making (eg, diagnostic assistance and data management), whereas nurses focused more on patient monitoring and disease surveillance. SEM results revealed that effort expectancy (EE) and trust (TR) were significant predictors of BI among all respondents. In the physician group, both EE and TR remained significant, while in the nurse group, only TR was associated with BI. These findings offer valuable insight into the current landscape of AI adoption in oncology and support the development of targeted strategies, such as role-specific education and interventions, to enhance trust and ease of use.

Survey results revealed that while the vast majority of respondents were aware of medical AI (85.4%) and held an optimistic view regarding its future application prospects (82.6%), only a small proportion had actually used medical AI (17.7%), and even fewer reported frequent use (6.5%). These findings suggest a clear gap between awareness and practical implementation of medical AI among oncology healthcare professionals in China. This pattern is not unique to our study. In a systematic review and cross-sectional survey conducted by Chen et al44 on the acceptance of clinical AI among physicians and medical students, 5 out of 8 included studies reported awareness rates exceeding 65%, while actual usage ranged only between 10% and 30%. Their own survey similarly reported an AI usage rate of 20%. Aldhelaan and McNamara,45 in a study exploring perceptions of AI among UK general practitioners in primary care, found that only 10.0% of respondents had used AI in clinical practice, despite 53.45% believing that AI would be integrated into primary care within the next 5 years. This disconnect between high awareness and low adoption appears to be widespread across countries, specialties, and professional roles. One possible explanation lies in the rapid pace of AI advancement in recent years—particularly the global popularity of large language models like ChatGPT—which may have fueled heightened public awareness and enthusiasm for AI technologies.46 However, the limited deployment of clinically relevant AI systems, poor integration with hospital information infrastructures and clinical workflows,47–49 the lack of formal medical education on AI principles and applications,50,51 and shortage of specialty-specific AI training programs14,45 have likely contributed to insufficient in-depth understanding and limited practical use of medical AI among physicians.

Based on the SEM analysis based on the UTAUT, EE and TR emerged as significant positive predictors of BI to use medical AI among oncology healthcare professionals. EE reflects the perceived ease of learning and using a technology in the context of demanding clinical workflows. In the present study, TR was conceptualized as an overarching construct reflecting healthcare professionals’ overall confidence that medical AI can be used in a clinically reliable, professionally acceptable, and appropriately governed manner. TR, similarly, plays a critical role in AI acceptance. On one hand, medical AI often operates as a “black box,” lacking transparency and interpretability; thus, physicians need to trust that the system can effectively improve the quality and efficiency of medical services.17,52 On the other hand, the development and implementation of medical AI often rely on large volumes of patient data, and physicians must trust that these technologies safeguard data privacy and avoid ethical risks.52,53 These findings are consistent with existing research on AI acceptance in healthcare settings. In a review conducted by Lambert et al,14 which examined the acceptance of AI among medical professionals in hospitals, the authors synthesized factors influencing acceptance using the UTAUT and identified EE as 1 of the 4 key dimensions, with frequent emphasis on time and workload. Similarly, Su et al,54 in a study exploring factors influencing the adoption of artificial intelligence health assistants based on the UTAUT, identified performance expectancy (PE) (β = .693, P < .001), EE (β = 582, P < .01), and TR (β = .583, P < .01) as the most influential positive predictors of BI. In another study based on the UTAUT, Dingel et al37 found TR to be the strongest predictor of healthcare practitioners’ BI of AI-based clinical decision support systems (AI-CDSSs). Aldhelaan and McNamara45 also highlighted barriers to AI use, including “being unsure of current options” and “almost no guidance on this.” Together, these studies reinforce that across both the general population and clinical professionals, perceived trustworthiness and usability of AI systems remain central to their acceptance and adoption.

Further SEM analyses conducted separately for oncology physicians and nurses revealed potential variations in the determinants of BI to use medical AI, although the structural differences between the 2 groups did not reach statistical significance in formal group comparisons. For physicians, both EE and TR were significant predictors of BI, whereas among nurses, only TR demonstrated a significant association. These differences may be closely tied to their respective professional responsibilities. Oncology physicians are primarily involved in diagnosis and clinical decision-making, whereas nurses focus more on execution, monitoring, and patient care. This divergence in professional roles likely shapes their expectations of medical AI functionalities. Our findings corroborate this explanation. Physicians expressed the highest interest in AI applications for diagnostic assistance (91.0%) and medical data management (84.0%), whereas nurses were most interested in AI applications for patient monitoring (85.1%) and disease surveillance (72.3%). In terms of actual usage patterns, physicians most frequently used AI for diagnostic assistance (78.6%), while nurses primarily employed AI for patient monitoring (77.3%). The diagnostic and therapeutic responsibilities of oncology physicians often involve high cognitive workload and time-sensitive decision-making,55 which heightens their concern over whether medical AI integration improves or hinders clinical efficiency. Moreover, medical AI tools used in oncology typically require processing large-scale multimodal data and executing complex tasks such as disease classification, treatment planning, and prognosis prediction.8,56,57 The medical AI tools utilized by nurses tend to be more automated and workflow-integrated,58 reducing concerns around usability and shifting the emphasis toward system reliability and trustworthiness.

These findings underscore the need for profession-specific strategies in promoting medical AI adoption. For physicians, minimizing learning costs and ensuring seamless integration into existing clinical workflows may enhance BI. For nurses, emphasis should be placed on enhancing AI transparency, and conducting rigorous validation to build trust and confidence in medical AI.

Drawing on our empirical results, we outline 3 strategic initiatives for hospital leadership to streamline the clinical integration of medical AI. First, hospitals should develop role-specific AI training programs tailored to the distinct needs of different healthcare professionals to enhance AI adoption and effective use.14,59 For physicians, training should focus on enhancing technical proficiency that integrates with existing clinical workflows, such as the use of AI-assisted diagnostic tools, including radiologic image recognition systems.60 For nurses, training should emphasize AI literacy and ethical transparency, particularly in understanding how AI monitors patients, secures data, and ensures patient privacy to alleviate trust concerns.14 Second, given that trust is a pivotal determinant of adoption, hospitals should move beyond vendor promises by establishing internal clinical oversight committees.61 These committees, integrating expertise from frontline clinicians, IT specialists, and ethics officers, serve to verify the real-world safety and local utility of AI applications. Third, hospitals should designate “AI champions” within departments to lead peer-to-peer mentorships.14,62 These champions would conduct hands-on demonstrations of AI tools, helping colleagues overcome perceived complexity and foster a supportive environment for the 82.3% of professionals who currently lack usage experience.14,62,63 This mentorship approach may further promote AI integration into clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the participants were recruited using a nonrandom convenience sampling method. Although the survey covered most provinces across China, it was distributed online and relied on voluntary participation, which may have led to underrepresentation of certain subgroups. This recruitment strategy may also have introduced selection bias, as oncology professionals who were more digitally engaged, more interested in medical AI, or more connected to professional society activities may have been more likely to participate. This may partially explain discrepancies with findings from similar studies in other contexts. Second, due to the anonymous nature of the data collection and the constraints of our ethical approval, we were unable to conduct follow-up qualitative interviews to further explore the specific dimensions of trust. Future research should consider employing mixed-methods approach to provide deeper insights into the underlying reasons behind healthcare professionals’ trust in or skepticism toward medical AI. Additionally, while this study applied the UTAUT to identify key determinants of behavioral intention, it may not fully capture all relevant factors influencing medical AI adoption. Contextual variables such as local policy, cultural norms, and individual personality traits were not included. Other potentially important but unmeasured factors, such as digital literacy, prior institutional exposure to AI, local implementation climate, and broader institutional context, may also have influenced participants’ perceptions and behavioral intention. Future research should expand the framework to incorporate these broader influences, preferably using larger and more diverse samples. Finally, this study focused exclusively on oncology departments. Given that the needs, expectations, and applications of medical AI vary considerably across clinical specialties, the results may not be generalizable to other medical domains. In addition, because of the cross-sectional design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships or as definitive evidence for implementation effects. Further investigations are needed to examine the perceptions and adoption factors of medical AI in other specialties.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated attitudes, awareness, and user experiences related to medical AI among oncology healthcare professionals in China through a nationwide cross-sectional survey. Although only a limited proportion of oncology physicians and nurses reported having actually used or deeply understood medical AI, the vast majority expressed strong recognition of its future prospects. SEM based on the UTAUT showed that effort expectancy and trust were the constructs most strongly associated with behavioral intention to adopt medical AI, while among nurses, trust emerged as the sole significant associated factor. These findings suggest that different professional groups may have distinct concerns and priorities regarding medical AI adoption: specifically, optimizing clinical workflow integration for physicians and enhancing algorithmic transparency for nurses. Furthermore, hospital administrators should prioritize institutional validation mechanisms and peer-mentorship programs to mitigate perceived risks. Together, these evidence-based strategies may facilitate the safe, standardized, and widespread integration of AI into oncology practice.
