FPGA realization of fractional-order chaotic oscillators using ABM and EFORK methods
Authors: José Cruz Núñez Pérez, Ramon Ulises Almada Prieto, Opeyemi Micheal Afolabi, Francisco Javier Pérez Pinal, Miguel Angel Estudillo Valdez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the growing theoretical interest in fractional-order chaotic systems, there is a lack of systematic, hardware-oriented methodology for their real-time digital realization that preserves chaotic dynamical properties and optimize computational resource utilization, particularly in the context of FPGA implementations using different fractional-order numerical integration schemes. This work presents the development of two reconfigurable VHDL modules for the digital emulation of fractional-order chaotic oscillators based on the Adams–Bashforth–Moulton (ABM) and explicit fractional-order Runge–Kutta (EFORK) methods. Fractional-order implementations of the Lorenz, Rabinovich–Fabrikant, Li, Chen, and Yu–Wang oscillators are realized on an Artix-7 FPGA platform. To validate the proposed architectures, Matlab simulations of each oscillator using both numerical methods are first conducted, followed by a bifurcation analysis of the Lyapunov exponents (LEs) as a function of the fractional-order. It is shown that, in general, ABM yields positive LEs with larger magnitudes and over wider intervals than EFORK, except for the Chen oscillator. Finally, the logic resources required to emulate each oscillator on an FPGA device are analyzed, and the results show that Runge–Kutta systematically reduces LUT usage due to a more hardware-efficient computational structure, particularly by avoiding division operations, which are more resource-intensive in FPGA implementations. As a reference, Adams–Bashforth–Moulton implementation requires the fewest logic resources for the Lorenz system, with 47,780 LUTs, whereas for Runge–Kutta, Li system requires 38,860 LUTs. Overall, the results highlight a clear trade-off between dynamical complexity and resource efficiency in the digital emulations of fractional-order chaotic oscillators on FPGA devices.

Introduction

Fractional-order dynamical systems have emerged as one of the active research frontiers in nonlinear science and engineering over the past two decades. Unlike their integer-order counterparts, fractional-order systems possess memory and hereditary properties inherent in many real-world processes, which allows them to model complex phenomena with greater accuracy. Moreover, the study of fractional-order nonlinear dynamical systems has grown drastically due to their wide range applications in areas such as chemistry 1, control 2, electronics 3, medicine 4, and telecommunications 5, among others. Within this landscape, chaotic oscillators are particularly applicable nonlinear systems because they exhibit high sensitivity to perturbations and initial conditions, and although they are deterministic, they produce aperiodic trajectories over time 6,7 which makes them useful in several domains. The hardware realization of the fractional-order chaotic oscillators on Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) has gained considerable momentum, driven by the need for fast, reconfigurable, and energy-efficient implementations that are suitable for real-time applications8. FPGA offer the distinct advantages of parallel processing and low-latency computation that can optimize several iterative discretization schemes required by most fractional differential equations. This makes them an hardware resource that many researchers consider in the acceleration of computationally intensive numerical methods, particularly for real-time or high-performance applications involving fractional differential equations9.

However, a key challenge in this domain lies in the selection of appropriate numerical methods to discretize the continous fractional-order differential equations into hardware-amenable difference equations. This has allowed researchers to develop several methods applied to this problem such as Adams–Bashforth–Moulton (ABM)10, Grünwald–Letnikov (GL)11, and, more recently, fractional-order Runge–Kutta (FORK) schemes12. The ABM method is a well-established multi-step predictor-corrector approach that leverages Caputo fractional derivative formulation and it is known for its accuracy and stability in capturing long-memory dynamics. Although, ABM avoids the long-term memory accumulation problem by expanding the nonlinear terms of the system in Adomian polynomials which is parallelizable and well suited for FPGA pipelines, it also comes at the cost of significant memory overhead13. The comparative study in14, examined six fractional-order chaotic oscillators including the Lorenz and Chua systems, and demonstrated that GL and ABM algorithms provide feasible solutions for generating chaotic attractors with the ABM method generally exhibiting superior accuracy in capturing the fine structure of attractor topology. Furthermore, the authors show that qualitative differences in trajectory accuracy becomes more pronounced at lower fractional orders and step sizes, where the memory kernel exerts a stronger influence on the systems dynamics. These findings reinforce the status of ABM method as a reliable benchmark for fractional-order chaotic simulation, even if its direct hardware implementation present challenges.

In contrast, FORK methods are a more recently formalized class of methods derived from the classical Runge-Kutta techniques but extended to fractional order settings which offers a computationally lighter alternative with reduced memory requirements while preserving acceptable numerical fidelity. While the ABM and GL methods require accumulation of weighted sums over the entire solution history15, FORK method evaluates the system equations only at a small number of intermediate stages within each time step without requiring explicit storage of past states beyond the current steps. Nevertheless, there are open challenges in terms of numerical accuracy and hardware benchmarking protocols for fractional-order chaotic oscillators FPGA implementations. Also, most current FPGA implementations focus on commensurate systems leaving incommensurate implementations unexplored where different states of the oscillators have different derivative orders. Researchers have extensively documented the chaotic dynamics of well-known oscillator models extended to the fractional domain. Systems such as the Lorenz 16, Rössler17, Chua18, Chen19, Lü20, and Hyperjerk21 oscillators have been simulated as fractional-order systems using various numerical methods. In terms of stability, the fractional-order (FO) parameter significantly contributes to the dynamic richness of a chaotic oscillator. Its influence is typically quantified using Lyapunov exponent analysis, which serves as an objective measure of stability by evaluating the average rate at which small perturbations either grow or decay over time 22. Complementing this, bifurcation diagrams provide a visual representation of the system’s dynamics, illustrating qualitative changes in behavior as system parameters vary. These diagrams reveal transitions from periodic orbits to chaotic regimes, offering a comprehensive view of the structural evolution of the system’s dynamics 23.

At present, chaotic systems have applications in different fields, such as thermodynamics 24, neural networks 25, Earth system dynamics 26, among others. In particular, they have significant applications in telecommunications, where their complex dynamics are exploited to generate pseudo-random sequences aimed at protecting information 27. This in turn, motivates the need for the hardware implementations that emulate their behaviors. Of particular interest, chaotic oscillators have been exploited in chaos-based communication systems because of their inherent unpredictability and sensitivity to initial conditions that are of great importance in developing secure transmission schemes. These properties essentially makes them even more attractive for applications such as spread-spectrum systems, and random sequence generation. In this context, efficient hardware implementation becomes a critical requirement, especially for real-time and embedded applications. FPGA-based realizations enables the high-speed and parallel processing of chaos-based systems in practical communication scenarios, including deployment in edge devices and software-defined radio platforms.

Moreover, FO increases the complexity of chaotic waveforms and yields encryption sequences that are more difficult to break.

In particular, Adeyemi et. al.28 implemented a parameter switching scheme in VHDL to stabilize spherical FO systems, synchronized master–slave oscillators using a Hamiltonian approach, and demonstrated an application of secure image (RGB and grayscale) encryption/transmission on Artix-7 AC701 and Intel Cyclone V boards, also they reported correlation metrics and logic-resource usage, while all the discretizations of the FO system was carried out with GL method. For their part, Soriano–Sánchez et. al.29 synchronized complex networks of nine FO Liu oscillators coupled via the second state variable and showed an FPGA implementation on a Zynq-7000 board using fixed-point arithmetic and a LabVIEW–FPGA software/hardware chain. Meanwhile, Pano-Azucena described an end-to-end architecture to solve different families of FO chaotic oscillators via GL on a Cyclone IV, applying the short-memory principle implemented in rom/ram blocks, a control unit for hardware reuse, and the experimental observation of attractors through a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) DAS1612, reporting operating frequencies between 77.59 and 84.9 MHz 30.

More recently, several complementary research directions further motivate accurate and efficient hardware emulation of chaotic dynamics beyond purely numerical-method considerations. On the control side, Liu et. al.31 propose a noise-tolerant fuzzy-type zeroing neural network (NTFTZNN) with a fuzzy time-varying convergent parameter for robust synchronization of chaotic systems, where an FLS-generated parameter adaptively tunes the convergence rate according to the synchronization error; they provide predefined-time and noise-tolerance analysis and validate the approach through comparative synchronization experiments on chaotic systems of different dimensions. In a closely related work, Jin et. al.32 introduce a time-varying fuzzy-parameter ZNN (TVFP-ZNN) for chaotic synchronization under external noise, benchmarking it against multiple ZNN variants and additionally demonstrating practical feasibility by displaying the synchronization process on an FPGA-based oscilloscope setup. From the hardware-platform viewpoint, Huang et. al.33 present EdgeLLM, a highly efficient CPU–FPGA heterogeneous edge accelerator that combines mix-precision systolic processing elements with an end-to-end compilation flow, illustrating best practices for mapping compute-intensive workloads onto heterogeneous CPU–FPGA systems. Finally, recent advances also reveal that deploying synchronized chaotic cryptosystems in hardware introduces new security concerns: Almada-Prieto et. al.34 demonstrate a chaos-based hardware-Trojan covert-channel concept that exploits the synchronization phase of chaotic cryptosystems to leak secret information, underscoring that faithful FPGA realizations of chaotic oscillators are critical not only for functionality and performance, but also for evaluating security-relevant behaviors in edge-device deployments.

In this work, the FPGA realization of the ABM and explicit fractional-order Runge-Kutta (EFORK) numerical methods is presented through a modular and reconfigurable architecture that enables straightforward replacement of the governing equations and flexible tuning of oscillator parameters, facilitating reproducible and didactic experimentation. As a case study, the proposed approach is evaluated using five widely cited chaotic oscillators: Lorenz 35, Rabinovich–Fabrikant 36, Li 37, Chen 38, and Yu–Wang 39.

Additionally, all five oscillators are simulated in Matlab, where bifurcation diagrams and LE diagrams are obtained as the FO is varied. This analysis aims to identify fractional–order values that yield pronounced dynamical divergence while preserving stable chaotic behavior. Theoretical framework

This section presents a brief description of fractional calculus, together with the equations of the Lorenz, Rabinovich–Fabrikant, Li, Chen, and Yu–Wang oscillators. Afterwards, the description of ABM and EFORK methods used to solve FO chaotic systems are presented. Finally, LEs and Benettin–Wolf algorithm for computing LEs in FO systems are introduced.

Despite the availability of analytical methods for solving FO systems, many physical systems are so complex that it is very difficult or even impossible to obtain an analytical solution that describes their behavior. To overcome these limitations, numerical methods have emerged as an effective alternative for obtaining numerical approximations, since they rely on discrete computational algorithms that achieve results with a reasonable degree of accuracy for systems that do not admit an analytical solution. In what follows, the ABM and EFORK methods for numerically solving FO chaotic oscillators are presented.

Adams-Bashforth-Moulton method

The ABM predictor–corrector method employs successive predictor and corrector iterations to improve numerical accuracy while avoiding complex mathematical formulations, which facilitates its efficient implementation in software and hardware.

Lyapunov exponents

LE were developed to analyze the stability of deterministic systems of differential equations, focusing on the effect of small perturbations on the evolution of a system and determining whether, under such perturbations, the system remains stable. As mentioned above, chaotic systems are deterministic and exhibit high sensitivity to arbitrarily small changes in their initial conditions; this sensitivity can be characterized using LEs. LEs are widely used in dynamical-systems theory to distinguish among stable, periodic, and chaotic behaviors 45. Simulation of FO chaotic oscillators

In this section, we present Matlab simulations of the FO chaotic Lorenz, Rabinovich–Fabrikant, Li, Chen, and Yu–Wang oscillators. We solve each oscillator using both the ABM and EFORK methods. Figure 1 compares the simulations obtained with the two numerical methods. The selected fractional orders were initially guided by values reported in previous studies. Furthermore, for each oscillator, the same parameters were used in both the ABM and EFORK simulations to ensure a fair comparison between the two numerical methods. In terms of dynamic richness, the selection of the fractional order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q$$\end{document} for each oscillator was guided by a systematic criterion aimed at balancing irregularity and numerical stability. This selection was supported by bifurcation analysis and Lyapunov exponent evaluation, which allowed identifying regions where the systems exhibit sustained chaotic dynamics. In this way, the selected fractional orders are not arbitrary, but correspond to operating points that maintain the essential characteristics of fractional-order chaotic behavior.

VHDL implementation of numerical methods

The VHDL language operates on a bit-by-bit basis, which complicates the handling of large data volumes. Many libraries convert bit vectors to base-10 representations, but they are often intended only for simulation and are not synthesized on an FPGA; therefore, only IEEE.STD_LOGIC_1164 and IEEE.NUMERIC_STD were used, since they support basic arithmetic on bit arrays and generate synthesized code for reconfigurable FPGA modules.

FO chaotic oscillators require high numerical precision, but the word length on an FPGA is limited: too few bits lead to information loss and instability, whereas too many bits increase resource utilization. A 32-bit signed fixed-point format was adopted for the state variables, parameters, initial conditions, FO, and sampling time, achieving six decimal digits of precision; thus, 32-bit variables represent numbers from -214.7483648 to 214.7483647. This choice balances precision and resource usage and enables the FO chaotic systems to be solved using ABM and EFORK.

Due to the inherent sensitivity to initial conditions in chaotic systems, a point-by-point comparison between MATLAB simulations and FPGA-generated trajectories is not expected to yield identical results. Small numerical discrepancies arising from differences in floating-point precision, fixed-point arithmetic, and rounding schemes lead to exponential divergence over time, even when both implementations correctly reproduce the underlying system dynamics. Consequently, the validation methodology employed in this study relies on statistical consistency and the preservation of global dynamical behavior, rather than direct trajectory matching. Metrics, including normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), mean, standard deviation, and extreme values, are utilized to demonstrate that FPGA emulations retain the essential characteristics of the corresponding numerical simulations. The reduction in LUT utilization observed for the EFORK method can be primarily attributed to the absence of division operations, which are significantly more resource-intensive in FPGA implementations. Division units typically require iterative or pipelined architectures, leading to increased hardware utilization and latency. Although EFORK involves a slightly higher number of multiplications per state variable, these operations can be efficiently mapped onto dedicated DSP blocks available in modern FPGA devices. This efficient mapping reduces the demand on general logic resources, resulting in a lower overall LUT usage. From a design perspective, these results indicate that EFORK is better suited for resource-constrained implementations, where minimizing hardware cost is a priority. In contrast, the ABM method, while more demanding in terms of logic resources due to the inclusion of division operations, tends to preserve richer chaotic dynamics, as evidenced by the broader regions of positive Lyapunov exponents.

Discussion

By comparing the MATLAB simulations in Fig. 1 with the corresponding FPGA simulation in Fig. 9, together with the quantitative results summarized in Table 5, we observe that each emulated oscillator preserves its characteristic phase-space structure and reproduces the main statistical properties of the numerical trajectories. Although the FPGA implementation does not replicate the MATLAB trajectories exactly on a sample-by-sample basis, the statistical descriptors remain close in both cases. In particular, the absolute difference between the MATLAB and FPGA means represents, on average, only 0.33% of the signal range for ABM and 0.31% for EFORK. Likewise, the standard deviation differs on average by 3.28% for ABM and 2.46% for EFORK, whereas the minimum and maximum values differ by about 2.82% and 4.28% of the signal range for ABM, and 3.22% and 4.60% for EFORK. These results indicate that, despite local discrepancies, the FPGA realizations preserve the global dynamical behavior of the FO chaotic oscillators. This difference is expected because MATLAB computes the trajectories using double-precision floating-point arithmetic, whereas the FPGA implementation relies on a fixed-point architecture, which introduces quantization effects that accumulate over time. In addition, the implementations support both commensurate and non-commensurate FO operation, as illustrated by the Lorenz system: the commensurate-order results appear in Figures (a–b) and (a–b), whereas the non-commensurate-order results appear in Figures (c–d) and (c–d).

In Section Bifurcation of LEs, we compare the bifurcation diagrams with the corresponding LE spectra for each oscillator and observe that whenever the LE spectrum includes a positive component, the bifurcation diagram displays a chaotic regime; moreover, as the positive LE increases, the bifurcation outcomes become more dispersed, which indicates stronger divergence in the underlying dynamics. From the LE spectra, we also find method-dependent differences: when we solve the Lorenz oscillator with ABM, the positive LE attains larger values and remains positive over a wider range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q$$\end{document} than when we solve the system with EFORK, and we observe the same trend for the Rabinovich–Fabrikant and Yu–Wang oscillators; in contrast, for the Li oscillator, both methods yield the same positive LE magnitude and the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q$$\end{document} interval over which it remains positive. For the Chen oscillator, EFORK yields a larger positive LE than ABM, while both methods keep this exponent positive over nearly the same \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q$$\end{document} range. Overall, these results indicate that ABM tends to produce larger positive LEs than EFORK for most of the studied FO chaotic oscillators, implying stronger divergence and potentially increasing the complexity of chaos-based encryption keys.

Table 6 indicates that LUTs dominate the hardware cost of the FO chaotic oscillator implementations, and the Yu–Wang system reaches the highest utilization with 72,498 LUTs when we solve it with ABM and implement it on the Artix-7 AC701 board. Among the ABM-based designs, we implement the Lorenz oscillator with the lowest LUT count (47,780 LUTs), whereas among the EFORK-based designs, we implement the Li oscillator with the lowest LUT count (38,860 LUTs). Although the Li system performs fewer operations in its dynamics, it evolves in a four-state-variable space; moreover, our ABM architecture allocates eight variables per state dimension, whereas our EFORK architecture allocates seven variables per state dimension. Consequently, ABM incurs a higher logic cost per dimension, so the Li system can consume more LUTs than the Lorenz system on the FPGA despite its lower arithmetic workload. These results agree with the theoretical analysis presented in Table 4, since, although ABM requires one fewer multiplication per state dimension than EFORK, ABM also requires two divisions, whereas EFORK requires none. Division operations are considerably more demanding in terms of logical resources, which helps explain the higher LUT utilization observed for the ABM-based implementations. Finally, EFORK consistently reduces LUT utilization across all oscillators because it requires fewer computations and fewer state-related variables than ABM.

From an application perspective, these results are particularly relevant for chaos-based communication systems, where both dynamical complexity and hardware efficiency play a critical role. Systems that prioritize stronger chaotic behavior, such as those implemented with ABM, may provide enhanced security characteristics, while EFORK-based implementations offer a more suitable alternative for resource-constrained platforms requiring real-time processing. This trade-off is especially important in practical deployments such as embedded systems, edge computing devices, software-defined radios, and FPGA where hardware limitations directly impact system performance.

Conclusions

This work presents an FPGA implementation of reconfigurable modules that emulate FO oscillators solved using the ABM and EFORK methods. We use these modules to virtually emulate five FO chaotic oscillators: Lorenz, Rabinovich–Fabrikant, Li, Chen, and Yu–Wang. We first report Matlab simulations of each FO oscillator obtained with ABM and EFORK, and we analyze the bifurcation diagrams and LE spectra as the FO \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q$$\end{document} varies. Finally, we present the corresponding System Generator for DSP emulations and quantify the logic resources required to implement each oscillator under each numerical method.

From the FPGA simulation results, together with the quantitative comparison with the MATLAB simulations, we observe that each oscillator preserves its characteristic phase-space structure and the main statistical properties of the corresponding numerical trajectories under both commensurate and non-commensurate FO conditions. Although the FPGA implementation does not reproduce the MATLAB trajectories exactly on a sample-by-sample basis, the overall dynamical behavior remains consistent. Therefore, we conclude that the digital implementation of the FO chaotic oscillators is correct and that it provides a reliable hardware realization of their global dynamics.

On the other hand, based on the bifurcation diagrams and the LE spectra, we conclude that solving an FO chaotic oscillator with ABM generally yields a positive LE that matches or exceeds the value obtained with EFORK. This behavior indicates stronger dynamical divergence and makes ABM better suited for chaos-based encryption systems. This trend holds for all the systems considered, except for the Chen oscillator, for which EFORK yields a maximum positive LE that is 16.6% higher than the value obtained with ABM.

Likewise, by comparing the logic resources required to emulate the five chaotic systems on an FPGA device, we conclude that EFORK consistently requires fewer logic resources than ABM for the same FO chaotic oscillator. This result agrees with the theoretical complexity analysis, since, although EFORK requires one additional multiplication per state dimension, it avoids division operations and requires fewer state-related variables than ABM, which leads to a more hardware-efficient implementation.

Finally, ABM provides the most suitable option for generating complex dynamics without destabilizing the oscillator because it generally yields a larger positive LE; in contrast, when logic resource optimization is the primary goal, the appropriate choice is the EFORK-based module.
