Potential and pitfalls of personal reference intervals in general practice: A focus group and case vignette study among Dutch general practitioners
Authors: R A Krimpenfort, Y M Drewes, W P J den Elzen, R K E Poortvliet, B A de Boer, A M de Jong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersonalised medicine is increasingly used in clinical practice and tailors care to individual characteristics. A new application in laboratory diagnostics is personalised reference intervals (PeRIs). Unlike population-based reference intervals (PoRIs), PeRIs use previous results of the patient, providing ranges tailored to an individual’s own baseline. This may improve interpretation of laboratory results and possibly patient care.ObjectivesThis study aims to explore perspectives of general practitioners (GPs) on PeRIs, and maps the identified benefits, disadvantages, risks and prerequisites before implementation of PeRIs.MethodsFocus groups (n = 3; 16 GPs) and a vignette study with fictional cases (n = 98) were used to explore the opinion, interpretation and application of PeRIs among Dutch GPs.ResultsFocus groups and the survey identified benefits, including easier interpretation, efficiency and more meaningful follow-up and referrals, as well as risks such as alert fatigue, overreliance, and underdiagnosis. The survey showed that PeRIs may alter GP behaviour. When a haemoglobin result was presented with versus without a PeRI comment, a significant portion of GPs adjusted their clinical strategy in these fictional cases. Participants emphasised that PeRIs should support, not replace clinical judgement.ConclusionOur study reveals arguments both supporting and opposing PeRIs. Importantly, GPs perceived added value for diagnostic decision-making. We advise accompanying implementation of PeRI with education on responsibilities and underlying algorithms. In this way, PeRIs can contribute to behavioural change that may lead to better and more meaningful, personalised care.Keywords: Diagnostics, personalised, qualitative, reference intervals, GP

Introduction

Personalised medicine, driven by advances in genetic profiling, data analytics, and lifestyle assessment, is a trend that enables tailored treatments for each individual. A new application of personalised medicine in laboratory diagnostics involves personalised reference intervals (PeRIs): reference ranges for clinical biomarkers based on a person’s individual physiological baseline [1]. Reference intervals (RIs) accompany laboratory results and assist in the interpretation of test findings. Most traditional population-based reference intervals (PoRIs) reflect the lower and upper limit that include 95% of the healthy population. More recent strategies use big data from large laboratory datasets to extract healthy and non-healthy distribution ranges [2].

While effective in many contexts, these PoRI may not fully account for the physiological variability among individuals. Some tests show strong individuality, with less within-person than between-person variation, including creatinine and hemoglobin [3–5]. One limitation of PoRI, especially for patients with a personal baseline different from the PoRI, is that pathological chances may be detected later. In such cases, individualised reference intervals may better distinguish health from disease [1,6,7]. Practically, this implies that an individual’s result may fall within a PoRI but outside a patient’s baseline, or vice versa. A clear explanation of PeRIs, and schematic illustrations of scenarios where PeRIs can be valuable, are visualised in Box 1. PeRIs are calculated from previous steady-state results from the individual and estimates of analytical and biological variation. These variations can be sourced from population data (within-subject variation) or the individual’s own data (within-person variation) [1]. Previous studies have described PeRI calculation methods [8–14] and suitable laboratory parameters [4,15–23].

Concept of personalised reference intervals (PeRI).

For some laboratory parameters, such as haemoglobin or creatinine, the variation within an individual is considerably smaller than the variation between individuals. For these parameters, personalised reference intervals can be calculated from a patient’s historical test results. These may provide a more accurate representation of the patient’s baseline values. Schematic overview of situations where PeRI can be valuable to the interpretation of laboratory findings.

As healthcare increasingly shifts towards data-driven decision-making, integrating PeRIs into clinical workflows may improve diagnostic accuracy, treatment, and patient outcomes. Quite some studies already describe the the mathematical background of PeRIs [8–12], therefore the calculation methods of PeRI lie outside the scope of this study. However, the practical appreciation of PeRIs such as interpretation and clinical decision strategy by medical professionals has never been investigated. Before implementation of PeRI, the needs of caregivers should be mapped. This study aimed to explore the perspective of general practitioners (GPs) on PeRI and to map opportunities, pitfalls and prerequisites of PeRI-based comments accompanying laboratory results through focus groups. A case vignette study was used to supplement the findings of the focus groups and to investigate the effect of PeRI-based comments on GP decision-making.

Methods

Study design

This mixed-method study consisted of focus groups and a case vignette study among Dutch GPs. Focus groups were chosen to support interaction between GPs in order to elicit their perspectives on the value of PeRIs. Thematic content analysis was performed to explore GPs reasoning by interaction and discussion on clinical strategies. A questionnaire was developed to supplement findings of the focus groups and to investigate whether PeRIs could affect clinical decision-making. The survey consisted of three fictional case scenarios and general questions on PeRI.

Recruitment and selection of study subjects

Recruitment for both survey and focus groups took place through open invitations, news letters, personal invitation and email. One focus group included GPs responding -out of intrinsic interest- to open invitation. The other 2 focus groups were organised in the context of professional development. Here, GPs were rewarded with accreditation. Different settings were used to capture a range of experience levels, facilitating discussion of arguments against and in favour of PeRIs. A total of 16 participants ranged from residents to GPs with >30 years’ experience and included practice owners and locums (for overview of focus group characteristics, see Supplementary data 1).

The questionnaire was additionally shared on social media (LinkedIn). Therefore, the response rate is unknown. In total, 98 responses were included for analysis out of whom 24 GP residents and 74 fully trained GPs.

Qualitative methods

Focus groups: Interview guide and data collection

Three focus groups involving 16 GPs were held between November 2024 and January 2025. The focus groups were organised online using Microsoft Teams. After providing informed consent, participants were recorded and transcribed using Microsoft Teams. Automated transcripts were reviewed and corrected by one researcher (R.K.). Each focus group lasted approximately 90 min, followed by a 10 min de-briefing by the researchers.

The focus groups were moderated by one of the researchers (Y.D.) who is experienced in moderating qualitative research. A predefined topic guide addressed perceived benefits, drawbacks, and prerequisites of PeRI (Supplementary data 2). During each focus group, two or three additional researchers (R.K., A.J., W.E.) assisted and took fieldnotes.

Case vignette

A case vignette study was conducted to complement the focus group findings and to assess the impact of PeRI-based comments on GP decision-making. To avoid using sensitive information, realistic fictional cases were developed and reviewed by experienced clinical chemists and a GP. Additionally, the cases were reviewed by a GP for plausibility. As multiple studies suggest haemoglobin to be a suitable parameter for the use of PeRI [5,13,19,22,24], we chose to address haemoglobin with the PeRI comment. GPs indicated their management strategy based on the haemoglobin result, first shown with PoRI only. Next, the same cases were presented including comments about the PeRI. GPs then reassessed their strategy after receiving the PeRI comment. Participants were unaware of the focus on PeRIs until after responding to the cases without PeRI comments.

The three cases included one patient whose haemoglobin concentration deviated from the PeRI within the PoRI; here the comment offered an alert. The two other cases involved haemoglobin values outside the PoRI but within the patient’s PeRI, where the comment offered nuance. The cases were followed by general questions regarding PeRIs. For the exact content of the case vignette study, see Supplementary data 3. Responses were collected between April and August 2025.

Outcomes and analysis

Focus groups

Transcripts were read to familiarise with the data. R.K. performed an open coding without theoretical assumptions. The researchers who were present at the focus groups discussed and categorised the codes using their fieldnotes. New codes were added if necessary. This process continued until an analytical framework for axial coding was developed, which was applied to all transcripts. After coding, the data were sorted according to the framework, relations between categories were explored and themes were identified. Coding was performed in Microsoft Excel and Word.

Case vignette study

Survey responses were analysed for additional perspectives on PeRI to supplement results from focus groups. In post hoc subgroup analyses, Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare median differences in clinical decisions between residents and fully trained GPs in amount of adjustments upon PeRI comments, and their general opinions about PeRI. Additional participant characteristics were unavailable due to anonymity.

Results

In this mixed-method study we gained insight in GPs opinions about PeRIs through focus groups and a case vignette study.

Focus groups

Current interpretation of laboratory results and reference intervals

The GPs differed in their current interpretation of reference intervals. Some had developed intuition about the accuracy of different intervals. Most GPs recognised that PoRIs differ from clinical decision thresholds and target values. Most GPs take clinical context into consideration when interpreting laboratory data, such as medication or muscle mass for creatinine. It was recognised that this level of personalised understanding requires familiarity with the patient, and such contextual data are not available in laboratory systems. Experienced GPs often use historical data to estimate the patient’s PeRI. These considerations are more difficult when the patient is seen by a locum doctor or even impossible when historical data are unavailable.

GP4: I usually quickly scan the results, and I also use the patient as his own control

The interpretation of laboratory results is also influenced by the GP information system, and these systems vary widely. Some systems directly show PoRIs, others only use colour coding and require clicks to access details on the reference intervals provided. The same counts for review of previous results: some systems require extra clicks and extra windows, whereas others show them on the same screen.

The GPs remarked that explanatory comments are generally read carefully. The display of laboratory results from medical specialists or third diagnostic parties differs as well, depending on the privacy preferences of the patient, and settings of the GP information system. It is noteworthy that these differences also affect how laboratory information is used.

Perceived applicability of PeRIs in clinical practice

Which healthcare settings or professionals can benefit from PeRIs?

GPs believed PeRIs could support GPs, practice assistants, practice nurses, residents, and -particularly when familiarity with a patient’s medical history is limited- locums. PeRIs may also be useful for specialists monitoring stable outpatients but were considered less relevant in emergency settings focused on acute pathology. Benefits of PeRI include support with result interpretation, triage, and patient communication.

GP3: any setting is interesting as long as you have continuity of data

Which patient groups can benefit from PeRIs?

The participating GPs indicated that PeRIs can be particularly valuable for patients undergoing stable, long-term monitoring as part of their routine care. In these patients, PeRIs may help detect subtle deviations from stable patterns. This includes cardiovascular risk management or diabetes care, as well as chronic conditions like kidney disease, thyroid disorders, or patients using specific long-term medications. Also patients with hereditary or chronic conditions such as thalassaemia were suggested to benefit.

GPs considered PeRIs less suitable for patients who only visit healthcare services during more acute illness. For these patients, there is insufficient baseline data to establish meaningful PeRIs.

Lastly, healthy individuals were suggested to benefit from PeRIs. In the Netherlands this would currently only involve health-conscious individuals proactively requesting check-ups.

GP12: I can imagine that in the future, we won’t need population reference intervals anymore, and we only use personal reference intervals

Perceived impact of PeRIs on clinical practice

Impact on workflow efficiency and workload

Some of the GPs speculated that comments on PeRI may initially cost extra. For instance, where a PeRI challenges the initial interpretation, requiring re-evaluation. Other GPs however argued that such contradictions can serve as an early warning, helping to detect issues sooner and potentially save time or even prevent escalation in the long run.

Most GPs nevertheless expected PeRIs to save time. Firstly by providing immediate context, PeRIs reduce the need to review historical data. Secondly, particularly nuancing comments can save time for general practitioners as well as practice nurses and assistants, to proactively reassure a patient on the phone. Furthermore, if the patient him/herself can access these comments, PeRIs can help even prevent unnecessary phone calls and follow-up consultations. This may reduce workload for nurses and GPs.

Benefits and risks of PeRI for clinical interpretation

Comments on PeRI were perceived to offer both advantages and challenges for interpretation. On the one hand, PeRIs can make the interpretation of laboratory results easier and more intuitive.

GP1: the more personal, the better

Concerns were raised that the use of PeRIs might lead to unintended consequences. Some GPs noted that frequent PeRI notifications could induce overreliance, potentially making clinicians less critical in their evaluations. This is especially risky when PeRIs are unavailable for certain patients, or when the GP relies too heavily on PeRI-based comments without considering broader clinical contexts, such as acute events or recent interventions. This could contribute to undertreatment.

Another challenge is the ambiguity around the final responsibility for interpreting lab data. The use of PeRIs may shift part of the interpretive role to the laboratory, which does not have access to the full clinical background of the patient. GPs emphasised that clinical responsibility remains theirs.

Some GPs mentioned that PeRIs also have limitations in distinguishing between a healthy personal baseline and chronic illness. Hereby, the risk of undertreatment exists if abnormal values are mistakenly considered stable or acceptable. Furthermore, in cases where the PeRI range is narrower than the general reference interval, clinicians may act more defensively. This was speculated by the participants to lead to unnecessary diagnostics or overtreatment, although nuancing comments might prevent overdiagnostics.

Special attention is needed for patients under specialist care in hospitals or those in temporary conditions like pregnancy, where relevant lab values may not be fully visible in the GP’s information system. In such situations, a complete and updated patient history remains essential for accurate interpretation. Overall, PeRIs should complement, not replace, clinical judgement.

Despite these hesitations, GPs acknowledge that notifications on PeRIs can trigger GPs to reconsider their initial assessments, especially during quick reviews. This enhanced alertness may help prevent clinically relevant information from being overlooked and allows early intervention, potentially improving patient outcomes, according to the participants. In cases where PeRIs align with the GP’s original interpretation, the comment can serve as supportive confirmation. Additionally, PeRIs are particularly helpful for locum GPs, residents, and practice nurses or assistants who may not be familiar with a patient’s history.

GP3: I would appreciate it if the lab result would say: wake up, pal, don’t go home just yet, first look at the data

GP5 support: …And very patient-centered, so I think that also feels a bit safer than when you’re just kind of winging it like, ‘Well, I guess it’s probably okay.’

Impact of patient access to laboratory results and PeRI information

Since recently, patients have access to their own laboratory results in the Netherlands, which often leads to unnecessary concerns about minor deviations. As a consequence, the general practices are increasingly approached by phone for explanation, consults, or medication requests. All GPs acknowledged this considerably burdensome.

Only a specific group of patients is highly engaged in their own health and closely analyzes their own results. Some patients get unnecessarily concerned by results outside the PoRI. A nuancing comment on the PeRI (that a result fits a patient’s personal baseline) may provide a sense of reassurance. Eventually this can reduce the urgency to reach out. This is helpful for both the GP and the practice nurse, as these patients often urge follow-up or treatment. This may reduce workload. If a value falls outside the individual’s PeRI, it is entirely appropriate for the patient to get in contact, as closer attention is required; therefore, this is not considered problematic. A key condition for patient accessibility however, is that the comment must be understandable for the patient.

GP4: It’s also an ethical discussion: if information on personal reference intervals exists, it is only ethical to make it available for the patient him/herself: they are his/her own data.

Lastly, the parameters for which PeRIs are implemented should be considered based on biological variation and cost effectiveness. Case vignette results

Effect of PeRI comments on case management

In total, 296 case assessments were completed by 98 GPs. Participants were unaware that the questionnaire focused on PeRIs in advance. All cases included some patient history and laboratory results. All three cases were first presented without PeRI comments and subsequently with a PeRI comment on haemoglobin.

In the first case where GPs were alerted on a value outside the PeRI within the PoRI. 45 out of 97 GPs, 46.4% (95% CI: 36.8%–56.3%), adjusted their decision. 36 GPs decided on further examination or treatment without notification. Out of the 60 GPs who initially chose a more conservative strategy, 40 intensified their initial strategy upon the comment on the PeRI (Figure 1(A)).

Clinical decisions and adjustments made upon a notification on PeRIs for case 1, providing an alarming comment on the PeRI (A), and case 2 and 3, with a nuancing comment on the PeRI (B,C). (D) Overview of the total adjustments made. (E) Total adjustments made per GP.

In the second case, a partially overlapping PeRI nuanced a result outside the PoRI. 37 out of 98 GPs, 37.8% (95% CI: 28.8%–47.6%), adjusted their initial strategy. 28 GPs initially chose to further examine, to treat, or to refer to a specialist. This group represented the main target for a nuancing comment. 17 GPs in this group decided to adjust their decision down. Following the comment, 33 GPs adopted a less intensive strategy, resulting in 45 taking no further action (Figure 1(B)).

In the third case, there was no overlap of the PeRI and the PoRI. The fictional patient had known thalassaemia. The Hb value showed a decrease within the PeRI. 36 out of 98, 36.7% (95% CI: 27.9%–46.6%), adjusted their decision. Initial decisions varied widely from 26 deciding to take no action, but 32 of the GPs would initially consult or refer to a specialist. Following the PeRI comment, 19 GPs adopted a less intensive strategy to take no further action.

In sum, 40.3% of all clinical decisions were adjusted upon the notification (95% CI: 34.8% − 46.0%) (Figure 1(D)). Out of 98 participants, 24% of the GPs made no changes to their initial decisions, 76% made at least 1 adjustment upon a notification about the PeRI, 44% made 2 or 3 out of 3 adjustments (Figure 1(E)). Exploratory subgroup analysis showed no significant differences (Mann–Whitney U test, p = 0.076) between registered GPs (median: 1, IQR 0–2) and residents (median: 2, IQR 1–2) in the number of decisions adjusted upon PeRI comments. General opinions on PeRIs also did not differ between groups (Supplementary data 4).

Most GPs felt supported by the notifications, even when they did not adjust any of their initial decisions. The question ‘notifications about the PeRIs helped me interpreting the results’ was answered with 3.7 out of 5. This score increases to 4.2 out of 5 for unknown patients. Few GPs scored below 3. The question ‘comments on the PeRI saves time’ scores 3.8 out of 5, which was also explicitly commented by some GPs in the survey. Discussion

Main findings

The aim of this study was to map GPs perception of the opportunities, pitfalls, concerns and prerequisites on PeRIs. Our findings give insight into the perceived utility of PeRIs, the practical and ethical considerations that are essential for successful implementation but also demonstrate the potential influence that PeRIs may have on clinical decision-making.

GPs see PeRIs as valuable for interpreting lab results, especially in early disease detection, chronic condition monitoring, and treatment adjustments. While many GPs already estimate PeRIs themselves and may not change their decisions based on notifications, for others, comments on PeRIs may possibly influence clinical strategies, especially when they are alarming. Nuancing notifications (showing a continuum) are also generally perceived positively. Some concerns about PeRIs in general were raised by the participants. Firstly, practical limitations of PeRIs were addressed: PeRIs require multiple prior measurements to establish a personal baseline. Consequently, their clinical relevance may be limited to tests and patients that are monitored regularly over time. Secondly, notification fatigue may lead to missed critical information. Over-reliance on algorithms could result in underdiagnosis or undertreatment. Additionally, interpretation support by PeRIs could blur professional responsibilities.

The survey with clinical cases showed that GPs clinical decisions might be affected by alarming and nuancing comments on PeRIs. Interestingly, even when decisions were unchanged, GPs reported that PeRI comments often confirmed their interpretation. Adjustment rates did not differ significantly between residents and fully trained GPs, although the resident subgroup may have been underpowered. For implementation of PeRIs in general practice, we recommend development of communication and training protocols on rationale, underlying algorithms, limitations, application and clinical responsibilities associated with PeRIs, to minimise the risk of misinterpretation or reliability. Important methodological and mathematical questions remain, including algorithm selection, data requirements and handling of pathology.

Additionally, due to inter-laboratory variation, calculation of PeRIs should only be done using data from one laboratory, to exclude analytical differences. Further research should evaluate both methodological aspects and clinical impact.

Overall, participants agreed PeRIs are a tool designed to support, not replace, clinical judgement, as they do not incorporate individual patient context, history or personal background. As such, they should not be interpreted as complete or standalone clinical indicators.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of our study is the mixed-method design, combining qualitative and quantitative methods strengthened the depth of the findings. Including focus groups with and without accreditation reduced the likelihood of recruiting only participants with a specific interest in PeRIs. With a case vignette study administered without prior knowledge that the topic concerned PeRIs, we were able to capture a broad spectrum of perspectives. This design minimises bias from prior expectations and enhances the authenticity of responses, although selection bias towards GPs interested in laboratory diagnostics or reference intervals cannot be excluded. The study is based upon fictional cases, and as also full clinical context is lacking, the results of the study therefore reflects intended rather than actual clinical behaviour.

Interpretation of the study results in relation to existing literature

Physicians typically interpret laboratory results by contextualising longitudinal trends with a patient’s clinical history rather than reacting to single out-of-range results. However, electronic health records do not always facilitate efficient review of historical data [25]. Although there is no prior literature about GPs’ appreciation about PeRIs, some qualitative studies have been published about interpretative comments accompanying laboratory results. Qualitative research suggested that interpretative comments appended to laboratory reports can save time and help prevent misdiagnosis by clarifying the clinical meaning of results [26,27]. Moreover, several studies indicate that such comments can influence physicians’ clinical decision-making [28,29]. These studies raise similar arguments and behavioural consequences of interpretation support as compared to our results on comments on PeRI. As our study is descriptive in nature, controlled studies are needed in the future to establish causality between interpretational PeRI comments and clinical decision. Finally, Kopanitsa’s work on patient behaviour showed that interpretative comments were associated with more proactive patient engagement and higher follow-up rates [30]. Although future research is needed, it would be interesting to study the effect of comments on PeRIs on patient-on-patient control.

Implications for clinical practice

To our knowledge, this is the first study to map the GPs perspectives on PeRI, revealing interest for adoption, but also critical views on challenges and limitations and prerequisites of PeRIs. If these are properly addressed before implementation, PeRI could form an additional layer to support interpretation of laboratory results. Although this study focused on primary care, in the future, similar principles could be investigated for outpatient specialist care. The complexity of these settings may further increase the value of personalised interpretation.

PeRIs may also have future applications in preventive medicine. If sufficient longitudinal data become available, personal baselines in healthy individuals could enable earlier detection of physiological changes. Although this would require a significant paradigm change in The Netherlands as well as robust evidence for time and cost efficiency, it represents a compelling avenue for future research.

Conclusion

Based upon focus groups and a case vignette study among GPs, PeRI may represent a valuable enhancement to traditional laboratory reports, with the potential to improve patient care. Importantly, PeRIs are intended to complement, rather than replace, clinical judgement, as the diagnostic process goes beyond laboratory results alone. Therefore, successful implementation will require clear communication, education, and ongoing evaluation, ensuring that clinicians are adequately informed and supported. Future research is needed to investigate the actual clinical benefit of PeRI.
