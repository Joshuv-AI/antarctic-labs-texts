Novel TCOF1 Frameshift Variant and Phenotypic Heterogeneity in a Chinese Family With Treacher Collins Syndrome
Authors: Feiyang Fan, Ying Chen, Tianyu Zhang, Jing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTreacher Collins syndrome (TCS) is a congenital craniofacial disorder characterized by malar and mandibular hypoplasia, downward‐slanting palpebral fissures, and conductive hearing loss. Pathogenic variants in TCOF1 account for most cases, with POLR1D, POLR1C, and POLR1B also implicated.MethodsWhole‐exome sequencing was performed in a two‐generation Chinese family with TCS, followed by Sanger sequencing validation. Clinical features were systematically evaluated, and bioinformatic analyses combined with structural modeling were employed to assess the potential pathogenicity of the identified variant.ResultsIn this study, a novel heterozygous frameshift variant in TCOF1 (NM_001371623.1:c.1601_1602delCC, p.Pro534Leufs*15) was identified in the proband and his affected father. The proband presented classic TCS features including craniofacial skeletal hypoplasia, downward‐slanting palpebral fissures, and conductive hearing loss. He also carried a right‐sided preauricular fistula, a nonclassical feature of TCS. The same variant was detected in his affected father with a substantially milder phenotype, indicating marked intrafamilial phenotypic variability. Bioinformatic analysis and structural modeling predicted that this variant produces a severely truncated Treacle protein lacking key functional domains, which is predicted to disrupt nucleolar localization and ribosome biogenesis.ConclusionOur findings expand the variant spectrum of TCOF1, highlight phenotypic heterogeneity in TCS, and reinforce the critical role of molecular diagnosis in distinguishing TCS from phenotypically overlapping craniofacial syndromes.

Introduction

Treacher Collins syndrome (TCS, OMIM #154500), also known as mandibulofacial dysostosis type 1 (MFD1), is a rare congenital craniofacial disorder with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 50,000 live births (Posnick and Ruiz 2000). It arises from defective development of derivatives of the first and second branchial arches. Core clinical features include bilateral and often asymmetric craniofacial malformations such as downward slanting palpebral fissures, lower eyelid coloboma, zygomatic and mandibular hypoplasia, microtia or auditory canal atresia, and conductive hearing loss (Nassar et al. 2024). Additional features may include cleft palate, choanal atresia, and rare systemic involvement (Nassar et al. 2024). TCS exhibits wide phenotypic variability both within and across families (Chang and Steinbacher 2012). Clinical overlap with other craniofacial dysostoses, including Goldenhar syndrome, Nager syndrome, and Miller syndrome, often creates diagnostic uncertainty (Marszałek‐Kruk et al. 2021).

TCS is genetically heterogeneous with four subtypes caused by variants in TCOF1, POLR1D, POLR1C, and POLR1B (Marszałek‐Kruk et al. 2021). TCS1 caused by TCOF1 variants accounts for 86%–88% of cases and follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (Marszałek‐Kruk et al. 2021). Approximately 55%–61% of autosomal dominant TCS cases occur sporadically (Barbosa et al. 2024). TCOF1 encodes Treacle, a nucleolar phosphoprotein essential for ribosome biogenesis and neural crest cell (NCC) survival (Valdez et al. 2004). Treacle contains functional domains including nuclear export signals, nuclear localization signals (NLS), a central repeat domain for RNA polymerase I binding activity, and a C‐terminal region that interacts with UBF and Nopp140 (Winokur and Shiang 1998; So et al. 2004). These domains are required for pre‐rRNA transcription and proper nucleolar targeting (Lin and Yeh 2009). Most pathogenic TCOF1 variants are expected to cause loss‐of‐function, predominantly through the introduction of premature termination codons followed by nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay (NMD) or truncated proteins. Reduced Treacle impairs ribosome biogenesis, increases neuroepithelial apoptosis, and compromises NCC formation and proliferation, ultimately leading to craniofacial maldevelopment (Fan et al. 2019; Dixon et al. 2006). Other forms of TCOF1 loss of function, including aberrant mRNA splicing (Macaya et al. 2009), missense variant‐induced protein mislocalization (Chen et al. 2025), and large genomic deletions (Bowman et al. 2012), have been reported for other TCOF1 variants. For the c.1601_1602delCC variant identified in the present study, bioinformatic analysis predicts a frameshift and a premature termination codon, which may result in a truncated Treacle protein.

Molecular diagnosis is essential for accurate classification and clinical counseling given the phenotypic overlap and genetic complexity of TCS. Here we report a novel TCOF1 frameshift variant in a Chinese family with TCS and pronounced intrafamilial phenotypic divergence. The proband presented with a right‐sided preauricular fistula, a non‐classical feature of TCS (Marszałek‐Kruk et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024). We characterized the variant's molecular impact via bioinformatics and structural modeling and provided personalized clinical management and genetic counseling. This study broadens the known pathogenic variant spectrum of TCOF1 and highlights the value of whole exome sequencing (WES) in definitive diagnosis and differential clinical evaluation of TCS.

Materials and Methods

Study Subjects and Ethics Approval

The proband and his parents were recruited at the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye & ENT Hospital of Fudan University (approval number 2020069). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Multidisciplinary Clinical Evaluation

All participants underwent comprehensive clinical assessment by a multidisciplinary team including otolaryngologists, ophthalmologists, and radiologists. Clinical examinations evaluated craniofacial morphology, skeletal development, and systemic anomalies. Audiological evaluation included PTA and tympanometry. Ophthalmological assessment included refraction and slit‐lamp examination. High‐resolution temporal bone CT with multiplanar and 3D reconstruction was used to evaluate middle and inner ear structures, craniofacial bony development, and orbital morphology.

Genomic DNA Extraction

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Only high‐quality DNA samples were used for sequencing.

Whole‐Exome Sequencing

Exome capture was performed using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Sequencing was conducted on the SURFSeq5000 high‐throughput sequencing platform (GeneMind, Shenzhen, China) to generate 150‐bp paired‐end reads. Raw FASTQ files were subjected to quality control and preprocessing using fastp v0.23.2 to remove adapter sequences and low‐quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37/hg19 using bwa‐mem2 v2.2.1. The resulting BAM files were processed with samblaster v0.1.26 to mark PCR duplicates, followed by sorting, indexing, and basic alignment statistics using SAMtools v1.21. Base quality score recalibration and variant calling for single nucleotide variants and small insertions/deletions were performed using GATK (v4.6.1.0) HaplotypeCaller, followed by hard filtering based on quality‐by‐depth, strand bias, and other standard metrics. Target‐region coverage depth and sequencing uniformity were assessed using mosdepth v0.3.3. For the proband, father, and mother, the mean sequencing depths of the target regions were 219.12×, 203.34×, and 195.54×, respectively, with ≥ 10× coverage of 99.89%, 99.88%, and 99.69%, and ≥ 30× coverage of 99.79%, 99.75%, and 99.58%, respectively.

Copy number variation (CNV) detection was performed using CNVkit (v0.9.12). Raw coverage was computed separately for on‐target and off‐target regions from the processed BAM files and the exome capture BED file. GC‐content and length biases were corrected by referencing normal controls, and log2 copy‐number ratios were calculated. Segmentation was performed using the circular binary segmentation (CBS) algorithm, and CNV events were called based on log2 ratio thresholds (homozygous deletion ≤ −1.0, heterozygous deletion ≤ −0.4, amplification ≥ 0.3). Gene‐level annotations, segmentation files, and quality control metrics were generated for review.

Variants (SNVs, indels, and CNVs) were annotated using ANNOVAR and SnpEff (v5.0e) in conjunction with multiple databases, including population frequency databases (1000 Genomes Project, gnomAD v2.1.1, and ChinaMAP), in silico pathogenicity prediction tools (SIFT, PolyPhen‐2, MutationTaster, PROVEAN, REVEL, AlphaMissense, and CADD; all from dbNSFP v4.7a), evolutionary conservation scores (GERP++ and phyloP100way), clinical databases (ClinVar, HGMD, OMIM, and DECIPHER), and transcript reference sets (MANE and NCBI RefSeq). All databases were last accessed on September 30, 2024. Candidate pathogenic variants were prioritized based on inheritance mode, allele frequency (minor allele frequency < 0.01 in public databases), predicted functional impact, and phenotypic relevance, followed by clinical interpretation according to the 2015 ACMG/AMP guidelines (Richards et al. 2015).

Sanger Sequencing Validation

Sanger sequencing was used to validate the candidate TCOF1 variant and perform family segregation analysis. Specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) primers flanking the variant site were designed. The forward primer sequence was 5′‐TCCCCTCACTCACATTCTCC‐3′ and the reverse primer sequence was 5′‐GACAGAGTCCCAGTGTGAGGTT‐3′. The expected amplicon size was 368 bp. PCR amplification was performed in a 50 μL reaction volume containing 25 μL of 2× Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; cat. no. P222‐01), 2 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 4 μL of template DNA, and 17 μL of ddH2O. Cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 15 s, annealing at 60°C for 20 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 2 min. PCR products were verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis.

PCR products were purified using the FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China; cat. no. DC301‐01) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, the target band was excised from a 1.5% agarose gel and dissolved in Buffer GDP at 56°C for 10 min. The dissolved solution was transferred to an adsorption column and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 s. The column was then washed with Buffer GDP (static incubation for 1 min followed by centrifugation), followed by two washes with Buffer GW (containing anhydrous ethanol). DNA was eluted with 20 μL of Elution Buffer after 2 min incubation at room temperature, followed by centrifugation to collect the purified DNA. The purified products were subsequently sequenced on a capillary electrophoresis system. Sequencing data were analyzed and aligned to the TCOF1 reference sequence NM_001371623.1.

Bioinformatic and Structural Modeling Analysis

The Mutalyzer tool (v2.0) was used to predict amino acid changes and protein truncation. 3D structural models of wild‐type and truncated Treacle proteins were constructed using SWISS‐MODEL (accessed October 1, 2025). The selected template was the AlphaFold DB model of human Treacle, Q13428.1.A (gene: TCOF1; organism: Homo sapiens ), which was generated using AlphaFold v2 and annotated as a monomer without ligands. The sequence identity was 99.44%, and the GMQE score was 0.43.

Use of Artificial Intelligence Tools

No artificial intelligence (AI) or large language model (LLM) tools were used in the preparation, writing, or editing of this manuscript. All content was generated by the authors.

Results

Clinical Phenotype of the Proband

A 25‐year‐old Chinese male was referred for right‐sided hemifacial microsomia. He had normal growth and intellectual development. General physical examination showed normal height, limb development, and systemic health. Multidisciplinary assessment identified classic TCS features and additional nonclassical findings (Table 1). Ophthalmological examination showed bilateral downward‐slanting palpebral fissures, lateral lower eyelid coloboma, partial lower eyelash agenesis, and bilateral myopia. Otolaryngological evaluation revealed right external auditory canal stenosis, a right preauricular fistula, bilateral moderate conductive hearing loss, tinnitus, and impaired speech articulation. Skeletal examination demonstrated severe zygomatic hypoplasia, right orbital wall hypoplasia, malocclusion, and asymmetric craniofacial morphology (Figure 1a).

Family History and Phenotypic Variability

Family assessment confirmed autosomal dominant inheritance. The proband's 52‐year‐old father (I‐1) had a mild TCS phenotype with subtle downward‐slanting palpebral fissures and right‐sided hearing impairment. The 50‐year‐old mother (I‐2) was clinically unaffected with no craniofacial or auditory anomalies (Figure 1b).

The proband exhibited a severe phenotype while his father showed mild manifestations, consistent with well‐documented intrafamilial variability in TCS (Dixon et al. 2004).

Audiological Findings

Pure‐tone audiometry (PTA) confirmed bilateral moderate conductive hearing loss. Air‐conduction thresholds were 51 dB in the left ear and 58 dB in the right ear. Bone‐conduction thresholds were 25 dB bilaterally, yielding air‐bone gaps of 26 dB and 33 dB (Figure 2a). Tympanometry showed a type B pattern in the right ear consistent with middle ear effusion or structural anomaly, and a type As pattern in the left ear with reduced tympanic membrane compliance (Figure 2b).

Temporal Bone Computed Tomography Findings

High‐resolution temporal bone computed tomography (CT) with three‐dimensional (3D) reconstruction confirmed asymmetric middle ear and craniofacial dysplasia with intact inner ear structures (Figure 3). Middle ear anomalies included bilateral tympanic cavity hypoplasia, ossicular chain malformation with malleus‐incus fusion, inferior displacement of the right facial nerve canal and right oval window atresia. Craniofacial bony defects involved right inferolateral orbital wall and floor hypoplasia, mandibular and condylar hypoplasia consistent with the clinically observed right‐sided hemifacial microsomia, and asymmetric temporomandibular fossae with a widened right temporomandibular joint space, which likely accounts for the proband's malocclusion. Additional findings included nasal septum deviation and nasal cavity stenosis. External ear findings included right external auditory canal stenosis and tympanic membrane ossification. Surgical hearing rehabilitation was not recommended due to severe middle ear dysplasia, facial nerve displacement and oval window atresia (Jahrsdoerfer et al. 1992).

Genetic Identification of a Novel TCOF1 Frameshift Variant

WES identified a novel heterozygous 2‐bp deletion in TCOF1 (NM_001371623.1: c.1601_1602delCC) localized in exon 11. Sanger sequencing confirmed the variant in the proband and affected father and its absence in the unaffected mother, consistent with autosomal dominant transmission (Figure 4a). The identified variant was submitted to ClinVar (SUB16124305) and is currently under review.

Validation, localization, and predicted structural effects of the TCOF1 protein. (a) Sanger sequencing electropherograms confirm the heterozygous TCOF1 c.1601_1602delCC variant in the proband (II‐1) and his father (I‐1). The mother (I‐2) carries the wild‐type sequence. The deleted nucleotides are highlighted in a red box. (b) Schematic representation of the TCOF1 gene structure. The location of the identified variant within exon 11 is indicated. (c) Domain architecture of the Treacle protein. The variant introduces a 2‐bp frameshift deletion, generating a downstream frameshift that results in a premature stop codon (p.Pro534Leufs*15) and leads to the loss of the Pol I binding domain and C‐terminal nuclear localization signals. (d) Predicted three‐dimensional model of the truncated Treacle protein constructed with SWISS‐MODEL. The predicted model suggests the severely truncated structure of the altered protein.

The c.1601_1602delCC alteration causes a frameshift and premature translation termination (p.Pro534Leufs*15), leading to a premature termination codon at position 547 of the Treacle protein. The wild‐type Treacle protein contains 1489 amino acids, whereas the changed protein is truncated to 547 residues. The truncated protein loses 942 C‐terminal amino acids including the RNA polymerase I‐binding domain, C‐terminal NLSs, and UBF/Nopp140‐binding motifs (Figure 4c). 3D structural modeling predicted severe disruption of the truncated protein with complete loss of the C‐terminal functional region and altered global folding (Figure 4d). It should be noted that, given the intrinsically disordered nature of Treacle—particularly its central repeat domain—these structural predictions are subject to inherent uncertainty and should be interpreted with caution. According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) 2015 guidelines, the variant was classified as likely pathogenic based on PVS1, PM2, PP1, and PP4 criteria.

Discussion

This study identifies a novel heterozygous frameshift deletion in TCOF1 (c.1601_1602delCC; p.Pro534Leufs*15) in a Chinese TCS pedigree with striking intrafamilial phenotypic divergence. As the first specific pathogenic frameshift variant reported in exon 11 of TCOF1 associated with TCS, this finding expands the global variant spectrum of the gene. The proband's atypical preauricular fistula, a feature more commonly associated with Goldenhar syndrome (Martelli‐Júnior et al. 2010), underscores the diagnostic challenges caused by phenotypic overlap across craniofacial dysostoses.

TCOF1 contains 26 coding exons and two alternatively spliced exons (So et al. 2004). More than 300 pathogenic variants have been reported, most of which are small insertions or deletions leading to premature termination (Grzanka and Piekiełko‐Witkowska 2021). Variant hotspots in exons 10, 15, 16, 23, and 24 account for more than 50% of all cases (Splendore et al. 2002). The exon 11 variant identified here is a non‐hotspot but conforms to the canonical loss of function mechanism in TCS. Bioinformatic analysis and structural modeling predict that the variant produces a severely truncated Treacle protein lacking key domains required for RNA polymerase I interaction and nucleolar localization. Loss of these domains may lead to cytoplasmic mislocalization (Jiang et al. 2025), NMD, and subsequent haploinsufficiency (Chen et al. 2025). These defects may disrupt ribosome biogenesis and increase NCC apoptosis, resulting in the craniofacial phenotype. Co‐segregation of the variant with disease status in the family further supports its pathogenic role.

Marked intrafamilial phenotypic variability ranging from severe in the proband to mild in the father is a well recognized feature of TCS, with no consistent genotype–phenotype correlation (Nassar et al. 2024). Underlying mechanisms may include modifier genes, epigenetic regulation, environmental influences, and stochastic differences during NCC development (Sakai and Trainor 2016). The presence of a preauricular fistula in the proband, a nonclassical feature of TCS (Marszałek‐Kruk et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024) but common in Goldenhar syndrome (Martelli‐Júnior et al. 2010), highlights the importance of molecular testing to achieve a definitive diagnosis and avoid clinical misclassification.

Molecular genetic analysis in this family confirmed the diagnosis of TCS and directly guided personalized clinical management and genetic counseling. Hearing rehabilitation relied on bone conductive hearing devices because severe middle ear malformations precluded surgical reconstruction. Long term monitoring was recommended for external auditory canal cholesteatoma. Craniofacial symmetry may be improved by facial fat grafting for hemifacial microsomia, with ongoing surveillance for obstructive sleep apnea related to mandibular hypoplasia. Audiological assessment was advised for the affected father to optimize his hearing care. Given the autosomal dominant inheritance, the family received comprehensive genetic counseling including a 50% recurrence risk per pregnancy, options for preconception planning, prenatal diagnostic testing and preimplantation genetic diagnosis to reduce reproductive risk.

This study has some limitations. Functional experiments in vitro or in vivo were not performed to directly validate the pathogenic impact of the novel variant. In addition, limited clinical and imaging data from the affected father restricted detailed phenotypic comparison. Furthermore, as Treacle is an intrinsically disordered protein, the structural models presented should be regarded as qualitative in silico visualizations rather than experimentally validated three‐dimensional conformations and interpreted with caution. Future studies will include functional assays to characterize the molecular consequences of this variant and expand the Chinese TCS cohort to identify additional variants and explore potential genotype–phenotype relationships.

In summary, we report a novel pathogenic TCOF1 frameshift variant associated with TCS in a Chinese family. The proband presented a nonclassical preauricular fistula that expands the clinical spectrum of the disorder. Our findings highlight the genetic and phenotypic complexity of TCS and demonstrate the pivotal role of WES in achieving an accurate molecular diagnosis. These results provide valuable insights for clinical evaluation, genetic counseling, and multidisciplinary management of affected individuals and families.

Conclusions

Taken together, we identified a novel pathogenic heterozygous frameshift deletion in TCOF1 in a Chinese pedigree with TCS, thereby further enriching the variant spectrum of TCOF1 implicated in TCS. Our observations highlight the pronounced intrafamilial phenotypic heterogeneity in TCS, as well as the diagnostic dilemmas raised by atypical clinical features such as preauricular fistula and overlapping manifestations with other craniofacial dysostoses. This study reinforces that molecular genetic testing serves as an indispensable tool for accurate and definitive diagnosis of TCS and demonstrates how molecular findings can inform personalized clinical strategies, including tailored hearing rehabilitation, craniofacial reconstruction planning, and systematic genetic counseling for affected families. These results advance the current understanding of the genetic etiology and phenotypic spectrum of TCS in the Chinese population and provide valuable evidence for the precise diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and reproductive guidance of individuals with this condition.
