Enhancing cassava yield value and profitability through Tithonia diversifolia and cow dung applications in a tropical derived savanna Alfisol
Authors: Aruna Olasekan Adekiya, Opeyemi Abosede Faronbi, Seun Mary Adekiya, Adebisi Oluwaseun Oloniluyi, Tunde Ezekiel Lawal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Robust evidence linking organic amendments to cassava yield, starch content, and profitability on derived savanna sandy loam Alfisols remains limited. Therefore, field experiments were conducted in April 2021 and 2022 at the Teaching and Research Farm of Landmark University, Omu-Aran, Kwara State, Nigeria. This study was conducted to determine the effects of Tithonia diversifolia (Tithonia) and cow dung (CD) on soil physical and chemical properties, cassava performance, and economic returns. The experiment consisted of seven treatments: control, CD applied at the rate of 10 t ha-1 (CD 10), CD applied at the rate of 20 t ha-1 (CD 20), Tithonia applied at the rate of 10 t ha-1 (Tithonia 10), Tithonia applied at the rate of 20 t ha-1 (Tithonia 20), CD10 + Tithonia 10 and NPK 15-15-15 fertilizer applied at a rate of 400 kg ha-1 arranged in a randomized complete block design with three replications. Data on the initial soil properties (bulk density, particle size, pH, soil organic matter (SOM), N, P, K, Ca and Mg), along with the chemical properties of the soil on a per-plot basis at the end of the experiment, were collected. Additionally, data on soil bulk density, porosity and moisture content were collected during the vegetative growth of the cassava. Cassava plant height, number of leaves, leaf area, tuber yield and starch content determined. Economic evaluation was conducted using partial budget analysis to estimate the gross margin per hectare for each treatment. Data collected were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the General Linear Model procedure. Treatment means were separated using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at p < 0.05. Results revealed that organic amendments significantly improved bulk density, porosity, moisture content, soil organic matter, and nutrient status compared with the control and NPK fertilizer. The combined CD10 + Tithonia10 treatment produced the greatest improvements, reducing bulk density from 1.63 g cm⁻³ to 1.20 g cm⁻³ (36%), and increasing porosity from 38.5% to 54.9% (43%) and moisture content from 9.5% to 14.9% (57%) compared to the control. Average over two years, CD10 and Tithonia 10 recorded the highest values of SOM (3.19%), N (0.25%), P (20.9 mg kg⁻¹), K (0.61cmol kg⁻¹), Ca (5.67 cmol kg⁻¹), and Mg (0.91 cmol kg⁻¹), which were significantly (p < 0.05) greater than all other treatments. This improved soil environment promoted vigorous cassava growth, resulting in an increase in cassava tuber yield and starch content from 19.5 t ha⁻¹ and 21.1% in the control to 32.4 t ha⁻¹ (66%) and 48.9% (57%) respectively in the CD10 + Tithonia10 plots. In addition, CD10 + Tithonia10 produced the highest net return compared with other treatments, confirming its economic superiority. Among sole applications, Tithonia10 was the most cost-effective, delivering high yield gains at minimal input cost, making it an attractive option for resource-limited farmers. Although NPK fertilizer improved yield over the control, its high cost reduced its cost-benefit ratio (2.42) relative to organic treatments. These findings demonstrate that integrating low-cost organic amendments, particularly the combination of CD10 and Tithonia10, offers a sustainable pathway to improving cassava yield, starch quality, and farmer income while enhancing long-term soil fertility. For smallholder farmers, Tithonia10 alone represents a practical, low-cost strategy for yield improvement, whereas integrated organic applications are recommended where the goal is to maximize both economic returns and soil health.

Introduction

Cassava (Manihot esculenta Crantz) remains one of the most important staple and industrial crops in Nigeria and across much of sub-Saharan Africa. Nigeria is the largest global producer, with about 60.8 Mt in 20221, making cassava a cornerstone of national food security. It is consumed in diverse traditional forms such as garri, fufu (or akpu), lafun, abacha, cassava flour, and starch meals, providing a primary source of dietary carbohydrates for millions2. Beyond household consumption, cassava is increasingly used for industrial applications, including starch extraction, ethanol and biofuel production, and adhesives, thus adding substantial economic value. Cassava residues (peels, chips, leaves) also serve as livestock feed, contributing to integrated crop-livestock systems.

Despite its socio-economic importance, cassava productivity in Nigeria remains far below potential. The actual yield of cassava in Nigeria3 is between 11 and 15 t ha−1 relative to the potential yield4 of 40 t ha−1. This persistent yield gap is largely attributed to poor soil fertility, particularly on sandy loam Alfisols that dominate the derived savanna zone5. These soils are typically coarse-textured, low in organic matter, and prone to nutrient depletion under continuous cultivation, making them highly responsive to fertility management interventions. Although cassava is regarded as relatively tolerant of marginal soils6, sustained cultivation without adequate nutrient replenishment has led to declining yields, reduced starch content, and lower profitability—posing a serious threat to food security and farmer livelihoods.

Traditionally, farmers have relied on inorganic fertilizers to address soil fertility gaps. However, the escalating cost of these inputs has placed them beyond the reach of many smallholder farmers. Moreover, exclusive and long-term use of mineral fertilizers risks degrading soil structure, depleting organic matter, and suppressing biological activity7,8. This has renewed interest in affordable, ecologically sound soil fertility management strategies that use locally available resources.

Organic materials such as Tithonia diversifolia (Mexican sunflower) and CD have emerged as promising alternatives. Tithonia diversifolia is a fast-growing, nutrient-rich green biomass that decomposes rapidly and releases substantial amounts of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium9. CD, on the other hand, improves soil aggregation, enhances microbial activity, and contributes to gradual nutrient release10. Several studies have demonstrated their benefits in improving soil chemical properties and cassava yield11–13. For instance, Hafifah et al.14 observed that Tithonia application reduced bulk density by 27.47% and increased organic carbon, nitrogen, and available phosphorus levels, leading to higher cassava tuber yields.

However, most existing research has focused narrowly on yield responses and has been conducted predominantly in forest and humid zones11,12. There is limited research on derived savanna Alfisols—soils that present unique nutrient constraints and are often more degraded and less resilient to nutrient mining. Soil amendment responses are highly context-dependent, varying with ecology and soil type15, thus findings from humid zones may not directly apply to derived savanna systems.

This study is novel in three major ways. (1) It generates evidence specific to sandy loam Alfisols of Nigeria’s derived savanna zone, filling a gap where data remain scarce. (2) It goes beyond conventional yield assessment to include starch content, a critical determinant of cassava’s industrial and market value16. (3) It integrates a profitability analysis, comparing gross and net returns per hectare under Tithonia, CD, and their combinations—providing practical decision-making tools for smallholder farmers facing rising input costs and volatile farm-gate prices.

By combining agronomic, quality, and economic evaluations, this study provides a holistic understanding of how locally available organic amendments can sustainably close cassava yield gaps, improve starch content, and enhance farmer profitability. These insights will support region-specific fertilizer recommendations and promote adoption of low-cost, sustainable soil fertility strategies.

The objective of this study is therefore to evaluate the effects of Tithonia and CD, applied individually and in combination, on (i) the physical and chemical properties of a sandy loam Alfisol typical of Nigeria’s derived savanna zone, (ii) cassava growth, fresh tuber yield, and starch content, and (iii) the economic returns from cassava production. It is hypothesized that these organic inputs, either alone or in combination, will (1) significantly improve soil properties, (2) increase cassava growth, yield, and starch content, and (3) enhance gross and net farm profitability relative to an untreated control.

Pre-cropping soil and chemical analysis of manures used

Table 1 showed the result of the soil physical and chemical properties before cassava cropping in 2021 and 2022. The soils have a sandy loam texture (68.1% sand, 15.7% silt, 16.2% clay in 2021 and 68.3% sand, 15.6% silt, 16.1% clay in 2022) with low organic matter (2.41% in 2021 and 2.40% in 2022), nitrogen (0.18% and 0.16% in 2021 and 2022 respectively), phosphorus (8.9 mg/kg and 9.1 mg/kg in 2021 and 2022 respectively), potassium (0.13 cmol/kg in both 2021 and 2022), calcium, (1.71 cmol/kg and 1.74cmol/kg respectively in 2021 and 2022) and moderate in magnesium in both 2021 (0.44 cmol/kg) and 2022 (0.46 cmol/kg) according to Akinrinde and Obigbesan17. The soils were slightly acidic, pH (5.98 in 2021 and 5.91in 2022) is within an acceptable range3. The bulk density of the soils was moderately high. Table 2 presents the nutrient composition of different organic amendments. The chemical composition of Tithonia and CD differed significantly across most parameters (Table 2). Tithonia contained significantly lower organic carbon (OC) (38.6%) than CD (45.6%). Similarly, its nitrogen (N) content (2.81%) was greater than that of CD (1.96%), resulting in a significantly lower C: N ratio for Tithonia (16.23) compared with CD (19.69). Phosphorus (P) content was higher in CD (0.97%) than in Tithonia (0.72%). In contrast, potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and magnesium (Mg) contents were significantly greater in Tithonia (3.40%, 2.90%, and 0.77%, respectively) than in CD (2.40%, 1.60%, and 0.32%, respectively).

Effects of Tithonia and CD on soil physical properties of cassava

The application of Tithonia and CD significantly influenced soil physical properties in cassava plots across both years (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Bulk density (BD) (Fig. 1) was highest in the control and NPK fertilizer, while the lowest BD values were recorded in the 20 t ha⁻¹ CD, 20 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia, and the combined CD 10 + Tithonia 10 treatment in both 2021 and 2022. Porosity (Fig. 2) followed an opposite trend, being lowest in the control (39.2% and 37.8%) and NPK fertilizer (41.1% and 40.0%) and highest in Tithonia 20 (56.9% and 56.6%), CD 20 (55.5% and 54.7%), and the combined CD 10 + Tithonia 10 (55.1% and 54.7%). Similarly, soil moisture content (Fig. 3) was significantly (p < 0.05) greater in the organic amendments than in the control or NPK fertilizer treatments. The highest moisture contents were observed in CD 20 (14.8% and 14.9%), Tithonia 20 (14.6% and 14.9%), and CD 10 + Tithonia 10 (15.1% and 14.8%), while the lowest values occurred in the control (9.6% and 9.4%) and NPK fertilizer (9.4% and 9.5%).

In both years, the combined application of 10 t ha⁻¹ CD and 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia (CD10 + Tithonia 10) recorded the highest values of SOM (3.19–3.18%), N (0.26–0.24%), P (20.7–21.1 mg kg⁻¹), K (0.61–0.60 cmol kg⁻¹), Ca (5.68–5.66 cmol kg⁻¹), and Mg (0.92–0.90 cmol kg⁻¹), which were significantly (p < 0.05) greater than all other treatments. This was followed by sole application of 20 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia, which also produced significantly higher nutrient values than equivalent rates of cow dung. Among the sole amendments, 20 t ha⁻¹ CD ranked best in SOM (3.01–2.96%) and P (18.8–18.3 mg kg⁻¹), but had lower exchangeable K, Ca, and Mg compared to Tithonia treatments. Application of 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia resulted in higher exchangeable bases (K, Ca, Mg) than the same rate of cow dung, although it’s SOM and P contents were slightly lower than those of 20 t ha⁻¹ CD. NPK fertilizer improved some soil chemical properties compared with the control but was generally less effective than organic amendments, particularly for SOM and exchangeable bases. The control plots consistently recorded the lowest nutrient levels across all parameters in both years. Overall, the combined application of CD and Tithonia proved most effective in enhancing soil fertility, followed by higher sole application rates of Tithonia.

Effects of Tithonia and CD on growth, yield and starch content of cassava

Application of Tithonia, CD, and their combinations significantly improved cassava growth parameters, tuber yield, and starch content compared with the control in both 2021 and 2022 (Table 3). The combined application of 10 t ha⁻¹ CD and 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia (CD10 + Tithonia 10) produced the highest plant height (1.65–1.69 m), number of leaves (120–128), leaf area (0.37–0.38 m²), tuber yield (32.1–32.6 t ha⁻¹), and starch content (48.6–49.1%) in both years, significantly outperforming all other treatments. Among the sole amendments, 20 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia and 20 t ha⁻¹ CD produced comparable plant heights (1.51–1.56 m and 1.48–1.45 m, respectively) and number of leaves (100–102 and 99), tuber yields (28.1–28.4 t ha⁻¹ and 27.8–27.1 t) and starch content (33.6–34.6% and 32.1 and 32.3%).

Lower rates of either amendment (10 t ha⁻¹) or NPK fertilizer improved cassava performance over the control but were less effective than higher rates or the combined organic treatment. NPK fertilizer, while increasing plant growth relative to the control, produced yields (25.1–25.8 t ha⁻¹) and starch content (28.1–28.5%) similar to 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia or CD. The combined application of CD and Tithonia proved most effective in enhancing cassava growth, yield, and starch accumulation, followed by higher sole application rates of Tithonia. Compared with the control, CD10 t, CD 20 t, Tithonia 10 t, Tithonia 20 t and NPK fertilizer, the combined application of 10 t ha⁻¹ CD and 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia (CD10 + Tithonia 10) increased tuber yield of cassava by 66.32%, 25.63%, 15.74%, 21.16%, 14.39% and 27.11%, respectively. Similarly, compared with the control, CD 10 t, CD 20 t, Tithonia 10 t, Tithonia 20 t and NPK fertilizer, the combined application of 10 t ha⁻¹ CD and 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia (CD10 + Tithonia 10) increased the starch content of cassava tuber by 101.45%, 68.23%, 50.47%, 65.6%, 42.08% and 71.20%, respectively.

Effects of Tithonia and CD on economics of producing cassava

Economic analysis revealed that all amendments increased monetary gain, production value, and net returns over the control (Table 4). The control plots, with a yield value of ₦3,315,000 ha⁻¹, had no additional production gains. The combined application of 10 t ha⁻¹ CD and 10 t ha⁻¹ Tithonia (CD10 + Tithonia10) recorded the highest monetary gain (₦5,508,000 ha⁻¹), corresponding to a production increase of ₦2,193,000 ha⁻¹ (66.15%) over the control. After deducting the cost of purchase, cutting, and transportation (₦300,000 ha⁻¹), this treatment gave the highest net return (₦1,893,000 ha⁻¹) and a cost ratio of 6.31.

Discussion

Soil improvement mechanisms

The observed reductions in bulk density and increases in porosity and moisture content following the application of Tithonia and CD highlight the pivotal role of organic amendments in improving soil physical conditions. When incorporated into the soil, these amendments decompose to form humic substances that bind soil particles into stable aggregates, thereby reducing compaction and creating more continuous pore spaces18. The fibrous nature of Tithonia biomass physically loosens the soil matrix, while the fine texture of cow dung fills microvoids, enhancing both macro- and microporosity and promoting better water infiltration and retention19,20.

Higher application rates (20 t ha⁻¹) and the combined CD10 + Tithonia 10 treatment had the most pronounced effects on bulk density and porosity because they supplied more organic matter and nutrients that stimulated root proliferation. Increased root biomass supports aggregation, improves pore continuity, and enhances hydraulic conductivity21,22. This positive response between roots and soil structure leads to better aeration and moisture availability which are key factors for cassava, which requires well-drained yet moisture-conserving soils during the bulking phase.

By contrast, the control and NPK fertilizer treatments exhibited higher bulk density and lower porosity and moisture content, consistent with the fact that mineral fertilizers provide nutrients but do not add organic residues that promote aggregate formation23. These findings are corroborated by Acar et al24. and Agbede et al.25, who reported that organic amendments significantly lowered bulk density and increased water-holding capacity in cassava systems. The combined application of CD and Tithoniaagrees with Xing et al26, who noted that using organic inputs with different decomposition rates maximizes improvement of soil structure and hydraulic properties.

Complementary nutrient release

The significant improvements in soil organic matter (SOM), total N, available P, and exchangeable bases (K, Ca, Mg) observed in this study demonstrate the capacity of Tithonia biomass and cow dung to enhance soil fertility. Both amendments are nutrient-rich, but they differ in their decomposition dynamics, which when combined, create a complementary nutrient release pattern. Tithonia has a low C: N ratio and decomposes rapidly, releasing most of its nutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg) within the first week of incorporation27. This provides an early nutrient boost that supports vigorous initial crop growth. CD, by contrast, has a slower mineralization rate, with only a small fraction of its carbon mineralized over several weeks28,29. This slow decomposition helps maintain SOM levels and provides a sustained nutrient release throughout the growing season, thereby improving nutrient use efficiency and reducing leaching losses30.

The combined CD10 + Tithonia 10 treatment consistently recorded the highest values for SOM and nutrient availability, underscoring the importance of balanced nutrient release for maintaining soil fertility. The improved SOM enhanced cation exchange capacity (CEC), allowing the soil to retain more Ca, Mg, and K, thus reducing nutrient loss and ensuring steady nutrient supply to cassava31. Interestingly, Tithonia alone at 20 t ha⁻¹ was more effective at increasing exchangeable bases, likely due to its inherently high K, Ca, and Mg content. CD at the same rate slightly outperformed Tithonia in SOM and available P, which could be due to its higher total organic carbon and P content23. Organic matter also chelates Al and Fe, thereby reducing P fixation and enhancing its availability in acidic soils32.

NPK fertilizer, though it increased nutrient levels compared with the control, did not contribute to SOM buildup, CEC improvement, or soil biological activity. Over time, continuous use of mineral fertilizers alone has been shown to cause nutrient imbalances, soil acidification, and physical degradation24,26. This highlights the importance of integrating organic inputs for sustaining soil fertility in tropical systems.

Contributions to cassava yield and starch quality

The consistently superior performance of the combined CD10 + Tithonia 10 treatment (highest plant height, leaf number, leaf area, tuber yield, and starch content in both years; Table 3) is best explained by complementary decomposition dynamics. Tithonia biomass is nutrient-dense (particularly K and N) and decomposes rapidly after incorporation27, releasing mineral N, P and K early in the season when cassava requires these nutrients for canopy development and photosynthetic capacity. CD, in contrast, mineralizes more slowly28 and provides a more prolonged nutrient supply during tuber bulking. The temporal complementarity—fast early supply from Tithonia plus sustained release from CD—improves nutrient synchronization with cassava demand throughout the growth cycle, supporting both greater biomass accumulation and continued starch deposition during bulking28,30. The result is higher assimilate production and prolonged availability of K during the critical starch-synthesis window, which is consistent with the elevated starch percentages observed under CD10 + Tithonia 10. Again, organic amendments increase microbial biomass and enzyme activities (C-, N- and P-acquiring enzymes), accelerating the conversion of organic nutrients into plant-available forms and improving nutrient retention in the rhizosphere33. Mixtures of green manures and animal manures often produce larger increases in microbial biomass and hydrolytic enzyme activity than single amendments, thereby improving short-term mineral availability without the leaching losses commonly associated with soluble mineral fertilizers34. Enhanced microbial processing can also promote formation of labile organic pools that feed plant-available N and P during tuber bulking, aiding sustained starch accumulation. These biological effects likely contributed to the consistent yield and starch gains observed across both years.

Application of organic inputs increases soil organic matter, reduces bulk density and improves porosity and water-holding capacity which promotes deeper rooting, steadier water supply to roots and reduced drought stress during bulking11. Under improved moisture regimes and better root exploration, photosynthesis and assimilate partitioning to tubers are maintained, which supports higher starch concentration per unit tuber mass. In semi-variable seasonal environments like the derived savanna, improved soil water buffering by organic matter can raise crop WUE (yield per unit water) and stabilize yields from year to year—explaining the reproducible advantage of CD10 + Tithonia 10 in both 2021 and 2022. Furthermore, potassium plays a pivotal role in phloem loading, assimilate translocation and the enzymatic steps of starch biosynthesis; therefore, amendments that supply and retain K during tuber bulking are particularly effective at increasing starch content35. Tithonia’s relatively high K content provides an early K pulse, while CD’s slower release helps maintain K supply during bulking—together promoting both tuber bulking and high starch deposition.

Economic implications

The economic analysis confirmed that integrating Tithonia and CD not only improved soil fertility and yield but also enhanced profitability. CD10 + Tithonia 10 recorded the highest gross monetary gain (₦5,508,000 ha⁻¹) and net return (₦1,893,000 ha⁻¹), indicating that combining two organic sources at moderate rates provided the most favorable cost–benefit outcome. Economically, the CD10 + Tithonia 10 combination reduces reliance on purchased mineral NPK and achieves better “nutrient efficiency per naira” because (i) Tithonia is often low-cost or locally available biomass, (ii) the nutrient synchronization reduces early-season losses that would otherwise require repeated inputs, and (iii) CD contributes to soil organic matter that raises productivity in subsequent seasons.

Tithonia alone at 10 t ha⁻¹ recorded the highest cost–benefit ratio (10.9), indicating its superior economic efficiency per unit cost. This is largely due to its low procurement cost and high nutrient concentration, especially K, which is crucial for starch accumulation36. CD 20 also improved net returns but was less cost-effective (cost ratio 2.61) due to higher transport costs and bulk handling requirements. Nonetheless, its contribution to building SOM and improving soil health means that its benefits likely extend into subsequent cropping seasons, providing cumulative productivity gains. Although NPK increased yield value by 30.77% over the control, it had the lowest cost ratio (2.42), aligning with previous studies that found mineral fertilizers less profitable in root and tuber systems unless supplemented with organic inputs23.

Limitations of the study

This study was limited by the fact that the experiment was conducted on a single soil type (Alfisol) within one agroecological zone (derived savanna). Consequently, the results may not fully represent other soil types, climatic zones, or management systems without further validation. In addition, the study spanned only two cropping seasons (2021 and 2022); thus, the long-term effects of continuous Tithonia and cow dung application on soil fertility, nutrient cycling, and potential nutrient buildup or depletion were not assessed. Furthermore, the economic analysis was based on prevailing farm-gate prices during the study years, and fluctuations in cassava market prices, labour costs, and organic input prices may influence profitability under different economic conditions.

Conclusion

This study clearly demonstrated that organic amendments, particularly the combination of CD 10 and Tithonia 10 substantially improves cassava yield, starch quality, and profitability compared with sole, amendments, NPK fertilizer or no amendment. The combined treatment enhanced nutrient supply, soil fertility, and moisture retention, leading to consistently higher marketable yields and net returns across two seasons.

From a practical perspective, the results provide clear, farmer-friendly recommendations. For commercial or larger-scale producers aiming to maximize absolute yield and profit, the combined CD10 + Tithonia 10 treatment is the most effective strategy for boosting production while sustaining soil health. For smallholder or resource-limited farmers, applying 10 t ha⁻¹ of Tithonia alone is the most affordable and accessible option, as it requires minimal cash outlay, decomposes rapidly, and provides a quick nutrient boost.

Beyond immediate yield gains, both sole and combined organic applications build soil organic matter, improve water-holding capacity, and sustain soil fertility over time—reducing dependence on costly synthetic fertilizers. To accelerate adoption, extension services and policymakers should promote on-farm production and use of Tithonia biomass and low-cost manures, organize training on composting and residue management, and support community-level biomass collection schemes to reduce labor constraints. Such interventions could strengthen the resilience and profitability of cassava-based systems, improve rural livelihoods, and contribute to sustainable agricultural intensification.

Materials and methods

Site description

Field experiments were conducted in April 2021 and 2022 at the Teaching and Research Farm of Landmark University, Omu-Aran, Kwara State, Nigeria (latitude 8° 7’ 26.21388” N and longitude 5° 5’ 0.1788” E). The aim of the experiment was to determine the effects of Tithonia and CD on soil properties, cassava yield, starch content, and economic returns in a sandy loam Alfisol. The experimental locations experience a bimodal rainfall distribution with an average annual rainfall of approximately 1300 mm and a mean annual temperature of 32 °C. The sites lies within Nigeria’s derived savanna ecological zone. The soil type is classified as an Alfisol, specifically Oxic Haplustalf or Luvisol37. Before cultivation, the dominant vegetation at the sites consisted primarily of guinea grass (Panicum maximum).

Land preparation, plot layout, manure analysis and pre- and post- cropping soil sampling

The experimental site was manually cleared, and all weeds were removed. The soil was then tilled by ploughing and harrowing to a depth of 20 cm, after which ridges were constructed at 1-meter intervals. CD, sourced from the animal unit of Landmark University, was left to decompose and stabilize before being applied. Fresh Tithonia plant materials, consisting of green leaves and young stems, were gathered from surrounding vegetation. These were chopped into approximately 5 mm pieces prior to incorporation into the soil. Both cow dung and Tithonia biomass were applied at rates of 10 and 20 t ha− 1 and incorporated into the soil to a depth of around 10 cm using hand hoes. The control and NPK fertilizer plots underwent the same land preparation procedures as the organic amendment plots, except no green manure was added. All plots were allowed to settle for one week before planting commenced.

In 2021 and 2022, prior to the commencement of the experiment, five intact samples were randomly collected from five different locations at each site, at a depth of 0–15 cm, using a core steel sampler. These samples were utilized to assess the bulk density after being dried in an oven at 100 °C for 24 h. Additionally, soil samples from the topsoil layer (0–15 cm) were gathered from various random locations within the study areas and combined to create a composite sample. The soil, CD and Tithonia samples were air-dried, ground, and then passed through a 2-mm sieve. Their nutrient compositions were analyzed using recognized standard methods38. At the end of the experiment, additional samples were collected from each plot for chemical analysis, excluding soil texture analysis. The textural analysis of the soils was determined by the method of Gee and Or39. Soil organic carbon (OC) was determined by the procedure of Walkley and Black using the dichromate wet oxidation method40. Total nitrogen (N) was determined by the microKjeldahl digestion method41. Available phosphorus (P) was determined by Bray-1 extraction followed by molybdenum blue colorimetry42. Exchangeable potassium (K), calcium (Ca), and magnesium (Mg) were extracted using 1 M ammonium acetate43. K in the extract was read on a flame photometer, while Ca and Mg were read on an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer.

Eight weeks following the application of manure treatments, measurements of bulk density, total porosity and gravimetric water content were initiated across all plots and repeated at three-week intervals. Bulk density was assessed using the previously outlined method. Total porosity was calculated from the values of bulk density by using particle density of 2.65 g cm− 3. Soil moisture content was evaluated gravimetrically by drying samples in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h.

Treatments and experimental design

The experiment was a single-factor trial consisting of seven treatments: control, CD applied at 10 t ha⁻¹ (CD10), CD applied at 20 t ha⁻¹ (CD20), Tithonia applied at 10 t ha⁻¹ (Tithonia 10), Tithonia applied at 20 t ha⁻¹ (Tithonia 20), CD10 + Tithonia 10, and NPK 15-15-15 fertilizer. Treatments were arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Each plot measured 5 × 5 m, with 1 m spacing between plots and 2 m between blocks. The trials were conducted on nearby sites (20 m apart) in 2021 and 2022 during the same growing season (April–March) under similar land preparation, cassava variety (TMS 30572), and management practices. Rainfall and temperature were monitored and found to be within the normal range for the derived savanna zone, ensuring comparable growing conditions across years. The sites were relatively uniform in topography and vegetation, and composite soil samples taken prior to treatment application confirmed homogeneity in initial soil properties. The RCBD design and inclusion of block effects in the ANOVA model statistically controlled for spatial variability, ensuring that treatment effects reflected true responses to fertilizer and manure applications rather than field heterogeneity.

Agronomic practices

Cassava stems of the TMS 30,572 variety were sourced from the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA) in Ibadan, Nigeria. These stems were cut into 25 cm segments, each containing 4 to 7 nodes. Planting was carried out on the crest of ridges at a spacing of 1 × 1 m, with one cutting per hill, on 21 April of 2021 and 2022. This arrangement resulted in 25 plants per plot, translating to a planting density of 10,000 plants ha− 1. NPK fertilizer (15-15-15) was applied at a rate of 400 kg ha− 1 using the ring method, divided into two applications: the first at one month after planting (1 MAP), and the second at three months (3 MAP). Weed control was achieved through manual weeding conducted four times—at 3, 7, 12, and 17 weeks after planting (WAP).

Data collection

To evaluate growth and yield characteristics, ten cassava plants were randomly selected per plot. Measurements were taken six months after planting (MAP). Growth parameters assessed included plant height, number of leaves, and leaf area. Leaf area was estimated using a non-destructive approach as described by Phoncharoen et al.44. Plant height was measured using a graduated ruler, extending from the soil surface at the base of the plant to the tip of the tallest shoot (apical meristem). The number of leaves was determined by manually counting all fully expanded leaves from the base to the apex, including those on the main stem and branches.

At 12 MAP, cassava was harvested by cutting the stems approximately 20–30 cm above the ground using a machete. The soil around the base of each plant was loosened with a hoe, after which the tuberous roots were carefully pulled out by hand. Tubers were cleaned to remove soil and detached from the stalk.

For starch analysis, 100 g samples of peeled cassava tubers were crushed and filtered through a 15 μm mesh sieve with the aid of tap water. The starch-rich filtrate was left undisturbed for 6–12 h to allow sedimentation. After settling, excess water was carefully poured off, and the starch sediment was dried in an oven at 80 °C until a constant weight was achieved, following the procedure outlined by Agbede45.

Economic analysis

The economic evaluation was conducted using partial budget analysis to estimate the gross margin per hectare for each treatment. Cost components included the cost of procuring and transporting Tithonia diversifolia biomass, cost of cow dung, labour for cutting, transporting, and incorporating biomass, fertilizer cost (for the NPK treatment), and weeding and harvesting labour. Labour time for each field operation was carefully measured through direct field observation and manual time recording. For every activity—such as biomass collection, transportation, incorporation, weeding, and harvesting—two trained field assistants recorded the start and end times using stopwatches and field logs, along with the number of labourers involved. The total labour time (in hours) was converted to man-days using the local standard (1 man-day = 8 working hours). For example, if four labourers worked for 2 h on biomass incorporation, it was recorded as 1 man-day (4 × 2 ÷ 8).

Labour cost was then calculated by multiplying the total man-days per treatment by the prevailing local wage rate, which was ₦2,500 per man-day in 2021 and ₦3,000 per man-day in 2022, based on interviews with farm labourers and agricultural extension officers in Omu-Aran. This procedure ensured that labour cost estimates reflected realistic field conditions and captured the true differences in labour demand among treatments, such as the higher labour requirements for Tithonia application compared to NPK fertilizer.

Transportation costs for cow dung and Tithonia diversifolia were based on actual expenses incurred using a pickup truck (₦8,000 per 3-ton load in 2021 and ₦10,000 in 2022), while fertilizer costs (NPK 15-15-15) were taken from retail prices at local agro-input dealers. Gross revenue was computed using cassava farm-gate prices (₦45,000 t⁻¹ in 2021 and ₦50,000 t⁻¹ in 2022) obtained from market surveys and agricultural extension bulletins. Net revenue was determined by subtracting total variable costs from gross revenue, ensuring that the profitability outcomes reflect realistic and locally applicable economic conditions faced by smallholder farmers.

Data analysis

Data collected in 2021 and 2022 on soil physical and chemical properties, as well as cassava growth, yield, and starch content, were subjected to analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the General Linear Model procedure. Prior to ANOVA, data were examined for compliance with the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Where necessary, data were log- or square-root–transformed to meet ANOVA assumptions. Treatment means were separated using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at p≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM Corp., 2021 SPSS version 28.046.
