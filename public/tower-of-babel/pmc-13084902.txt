Lived experiences of individuals affected by HIV/AIDS: understanding the dynamics of stigma and discrimination within the Sunyani Municipality, Ghana
Authors: Vivian Annan, Edward Appiah Boateng, Selma Ofori, Emmanuel Keku, Benedicta Princess Dotse, Clement Naayaara Zuuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite progress in HIV treatment and prevention, stigma and discrimination remain significant barriers to the health and well-being of people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA), particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. In Ghana’s Sunyani Municipality, where HIV prevalence is notably high, PLWHA continue to face social, emotional and systemic challenges that undermine their quality of life and treatment adherence. This study explored the lived experiences of PLWHA in Sunyani Municipality, focusing on the drivers, manifestations and impacts of HIV-related stigma and discrimination.MethodsA qualitative descriptive phenomenological approach was employed, involving in-depth interviews with 15 purposively sampled participants living with HIV/AIDS from two public healthcare facilities. Data were analysed thematically using Braun and Clarke’s framework, with NVivo software supporting coding and theme development.ResultsSix main themes and multiple subthemes emerged: (1) drivers of stigma, referring to underlying beliefs and perceptions that give rise to HIV-related stigma, including lack of knowledge, fear of infection and social judgement; (2) sociocultural and structural facilitators of stigma, describing contextual conditions that enable and reinforce stigmatising attitudes and behaviours, such as prevailing sociocultural norms and prejudice; (3) direct experiences of maltreatment and rejection; (4) challenges faced by affected individuals, including fear of disclosure, emotional distress and economic hardship; (5) negative effects of stigma, such as treatment interruption, social isolation and thoughts of self-harm and (6) coping and mitigation strategies, including public education, spiritual support, family involvement and calls for legal and policy reforms. Comparisons with global studies revealed broadly similar patterns; however, the persistence of misinformation and the limited availability of integrated mental health support were particularly pronounced in the Ghanaian context.ConclusionsStigma and discrimination remain embedded in the social and healthcare environments of PLWHA in Sunyani, with serious implications for treatment adherence and mental well-being. Addressing these issues requires multifaceted interventions, including community education, healthcare provider training, psychosocial and spiritual support, and legal enforcement to create a more inclusive and supportive environment.Keywords: Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome, Depression, Anxiety, Attitude

Introduction

HIV/AIDS, a global public health crisis since the 1980s, has claimed millions of lives and continues to disproportionately impact low-income and middle-income countries. According to the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS,1 2 approximately 40 million people were living with HIV by the end of 2023, with 1.3 million new infections reported globally. Despite advancements in antiretroviral therapy (ART) and prevention strategies, stigma and discrimination hinder epidemic control.

Globally, stigma and discrimination against people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) remain significant barriers to healthcare access and quality of life.3 These social issues manifest in multiple forms, including internalised, anticipated, enacted and structural stigma.4 Misinformation and fear of contagion contribute to social exclusion, emotional distress and reluctance to seek healthcare services, hindering effective HIV/AIDS management and prevention.5 6 In Africa, the burden of HIV/AIDS is disproportionately high, with over two-thirds of global HIV cases occurring on the continent.7 8 The WHO identifies multiple structural and social factors contributing to HIV-related stigma, including limited access to healthcare, sociocultural practices, economic inequality and the marginalisation of people living with PLWHA; these dynamics have been empirically documented in studies conducted in Kenya and Nigeria.9,11 These challenges are compounded by myths and misconceptions about how HIV is transmitted, leading to unwarranted fear and discriminatory attitudes within communities and even among healthcare providers.

Within sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), stigma and discrimination remain key obstacles to the HIV care continuum; from testing and diagnosis to treatment adherence and viral suppression. A 2024 systematic review12 found that in countries such as South Africa and Uganda, stigma discourages individuals from disclosing their HIV status, initiating treatment and remaining in care. Internalised stigma, particularly, has been linked to depression, poor mental health outcomes and increased rates of ART default. These factors significantly undermine public health interventions aimed at achieving the UNAIDS ‘95-95-95’ targets by 2030.13

Ghana, like many other SSA countries, continues to grapple with the burden of HIV/AIDS and the challenges associated with stigma and discrimination.14 In 2023, the Ghana AIDS Commission highlighted that HIV-related stigma remains a significant barrier to early testing, consistent treatment and social integration of PLWHA.15 Despite nationwide educational campaigns and improved access to ART, many individuals with HIV continue to experience social judgement, family rejection and discriminatory treatment in healthcare settings.4 These experiences not only affect the physical well-being of PLWHA but also have severe psychological and socioeconomic implications.

The Bono Region, particularly the Sunyani Municipality, has been identified as having a comparatively high HIV prevalence in Ghana, with regional surveillance data estimating a prevalence of approximately 2.5% in 2023, the highest reported among the country’s regions.16 Emerging qualitative evidence suggests that PLWHA in Sunyani experience stigma and discrimination from family members, peers, community leaders and health professionals.16 These experiences are driven by inadequate knowledge of HIV transmission and entrenched cultural beliefs that associate HIV infection with moral failure or promiscuity. In this study, HIV-related stigma and discrimination are examined as a multidimensional lived phenomenon encompassing its drivers, facilitators, enacted experiences and perceived effects within familial, community and healthcare contexts. This study explores the lived experiences of PLWHA in the Sunyani Municipality and examines the sociocultural and structural factors underlying stigma, with the aim of informing targeted interventions and policy reform.

Methods

Study setting

This research was conducted in the Sunyani Municipality, located in the Bono Region of Ghana-an area that has consistently recorded some of the highest HIV prevalence rates in the country.16 The study analysed data from two major public healthcare facilities in the Bono Region: the Sunyani Regional Hospital and the Sunyani Municipal Hospital. The Sunyani Municipal Hospital, a secondary-level facility, provides general medical services and minor surgeries. The Sunyani Regional Hospital, a tertiary referral centre, offers specialised services and an HIV/AIDS clinic. These facilities were chosen due to their high HIV patient attendance and role in the region’s care continuum.

Study design

The study employed a qualitative approach within a descriptive phenomenological framework to explore HIV-related stigma and discrimination as a lived, multidimensional phenomenon among PLWHA. The phenomenon was examined through participants’ narratives of disclosure, social interactions, experiences of discrimination, emotional responses, coping strategies and perceived consequences. This approach enabled the capture of rich, first-hand accounts that reflect how stigma is perceived, experienced and managed in everyday life. Open-ended, participant-centred inquiry facilitated exploration of thoughts, emotions and social interactions shaped by HIV status, thereby providing an in-depth understanding of stigmatised experiences within their sociocultural context.17

Study population

Participants were purposively sampled18 to provide rich, experiential accounts of HIV-related stigma and discrimination. Eligible participants were adults aged 18-60 years, who had received an HIV diagnosis at least 1 year prior and were actively receiving care at ART clinics in the Sunyani Municipality. Individuals who were critically ill or unable to provide informed consent were excluded.

Prospective participants were identified with the assistance of clinic staff and approached privately by members of the research team during routine clinic visits. The study objectives, procedures, risks and benefits were explained, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged from successive interviews. A total of 15 participants were recruited. A small number of eligible individuals declined participation, mainly due to time constraints or personal reasons. No participants withdrew after providing informed consent.

Data collection

Data were collected through semistructured, in-depth face-to-face interviews conducted June–August 2021. Interviews were conducted by a qualified and trained researcher, held in private rooms within ART clinics, and lasted approximately 30–60 min. All interviews were carried out without the presence of third parties, including healthcare staff or family members, to maintain confidentiality and enable participants to freely express themselves and share their experiences. This ensured confidentiality and allowed participants to speak freely about their experiences.

An interview guide (online supplemental file 1) was used to explore participants’ experiences of HIV-related stigma and discrimination, including emotional responses to diagnosis, disclosure experiences, enacted and perceived stigma, coping strategies and recommendations for reducing stigma. Open-ended questions and probes were used to encourage detailed narratives and allow participants to express their experiences freely. All interviews were conducted in participants’ preferred languages, audio-recorded with consent, and supplemented with field notes documenting non-verbal cues and contextual observations. Interviews were conducted by a female qualitative researcher with training and experience in qualitative health research, who was fluent in local languages and familiar with the sociocultural context, thereby facilitating rapport and open communication.

Data analysis procedures

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and, where necessary, translated into English prior to analysis. Data were analysed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework.19 Using NVivo V.12 software, two members of the research team independently reviewed and applied inductive coding to the interview transcripts. Codes were compared and refined through iterative discussions and organised into defined themes. Themes and subthemes were developed based on patterns across participants’ accounts, ensuring close alignment between the data and the findings. Formal transcript-level member checking was not conducted; however, analytic rigour was enhanced through iterative team discussions, peer debriefing and systematic comparison of themes with verbatim participant quotations to ensure that findings accurately reflected participants’ accounts.

The interview guide (online supplemental file 1), completed Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research checklist (online supplemental file 2), and additional supporting quotations (online supplemental file 3) are provided as supplementary materials.

Reflexivity statement

The research team comprised professional healthcare staff, researchers and academic lecturers with experience in HIV care, public health and qualitative research. While this professional background informed the team’s interest in the topic, reflexivity was maintained through awareness of potential assumptions, use of a semistructured interview guide, reflexive note-taking and regular team discussions during data collection and analysis to minimise bias.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

This study included 15 participants from the Sunyani Municipality aged 26 years and older. The majority were female (n=13). Marital status was evenly split between single and married, with one divorced participant. Occupations varied, including farming, catering/trading and artisan work, while some participants were unemployed. Time since diagnosis ranged from one to over 10 years, and all participants were receiving ART (table 1).

MT1: drivers of stigma and discrimination

The study identified drivers of stigma and discrimination as central factors sustaining HIV/AIDS-related stigma in the Sunyani Municipality. Understanding these drivers is essential for developing context-specific interventions to mitigate stigma and discrimination. A widespread lack of accurate knowledge regarding HIV transmission emerged as a major contributor to stigma. Many participants described how misinformation among family and community members resulted in marginalisation. One participant stated, “My siblings were spreading rumours about my condition. They alleged that they went to a man of God who said I was going to infect them with the disease…” (FP04). Another participant recounted fears expressed by relatives, stating, “I will be infected if I sleep in the same room with her since the disease could be contagious” (FP09).

Participants also described family and community members having persistent fear of contracting HIV, even through casual contact, which led to distancing behaviours and social rejection. One participant explained, “Initially, we ate together, but after the discovery, he stopped eating with me… anything I touch or use, he makes sure he does not touch it, including the drinking cup, so I left his place” (FP08). Similarly, another participant reported, “She cannot live in the same house with us, says the sister-in-law, she could easily infect us” (FP01). Cultural and moral judgements further intensified stigma, with several participants reporting being labelled as promiscuous or personally responsible for their infection. One participant noted, “Most people think I got it through promiscuity… but people need to know that it is not only promiscuity because anyone can have it” (FP05). Another participant shared, “People that I confided in thought I had been sleeping around with other men… My husband was the only person I had been with” (FP03).

MT2: facilitators of stigma and discrimination

Facilitators of stigma and discrimination included sociocultural norms and prejudice, which indirectly reinforced stigmatising behaviours toward PLWHA. Participants described how cultural expectations influenced public attitudes towards HIV infection and contributed to social and economic consequences. For instance, one participant explained, “I am a caterer; the patronage was low when they heard rumours that I am HIV positive although nobody confronted me” (FP07). This illustrates how community perceptions and fear-based assumptions can lead to economic and social exclusion.

Participants also reported experiencing prejudice through negative stereotyping and moral judgement. One male participant stated, “Some friends around me think I am a womanizer, but I am not” (MP15). Similarly, a female participant described being shamed by a relative who said, “How could you have two kids without a husband, it could mean you are not faithful, that is why you got this condition” (FP10). Such prejudicial attitudes contributed to emotional distress and discouraged open communication about HIV status.

MT3: stigma and discrimination practices and experiences

Participants described direct experiences of stigma and discrimination that negatively affected their physical, psychological, social and spiritual well-being. Several participants reported experiencing inhumane treatment and emotional neglect, leading to feelings of worthlessness. One participant stated, “I would have died long ago if not for God. They could maltreat me to the extent that I wanted to kill myself. It was a friend who encouraged me” (FP04). Another participant expressed frustration regarding healthcare encounters, stating, “Is it a crime to suffer from this condition, even sometimes health care providers shout at you?” (FP11).

Rejection and emotional abandonment by family members were also frequently reported. One participant explained, “I could be sick, and nobody cares” (FP04). These experiences contributed to feelings of isolation and discouraged disclosure of HIV status and timely health-seeking behaviour.

MT4: challenges faced by PLWHA

Participants described multiple challenges associated with living with HIV/AIDS, including fear of disclosure, emotional distress and economic constraints. Many participants expressed reluctance to disclose their HIV status due to fear of stigma and discrimination. One participant stated, “I don’t disclose my HIV status to other people because I think when they get to know it, they will use it to insult me” (FP01). Another participant reported, “I only told my wife; none of my family members or friends is aware that I am HIV positive!” (MP06).

Participants also described emotional challenges such as shock, sadness, denial and suicidal thoughts following diagnosis. One participant stated, “I cried when I got to know that I am HIV positive… I even wanted to take poison and kill myself because the man kept it from me for long” (FP05). Another participant explained, “No, I don’t even believe I have the disease, I was emotionally disturbed” (FP13). Financial difficulties were also commonly reported, particularly transportation costs and the need to purchase additional medications. One participant noted, “Sometimes some people don’t have money for even transportation, what they will use to buy medications. Now my medication that I bought was approximately 50 Ghana cedis and it is not everyone that can afford” (FP03). Another participant stated, “Transportation fare to come for my medications sometimes becomes a challenge” (MP15).

MT5: perceived effects of stigma and discrimination

Participants described significant negative effects of stigma and discrimination on their mental health, treatment adherence and social relationships. Many participants reported withdrawing from social interactions to avoid judgement. One participant explained, “I am not staying there again. I have gone to get my place. Now I don’t care about what they’ll think or do…” (FP04). Another participant stated, “It has worried me because my lifestyle has changed… I think by isolating myself from friends, I would be comfortable” (FP05).

Stigma also influenced treatment behaviours, with some participants delaying or avoiding ART due to fear of being identified as HIV positive. One participant reported, “One day, one of my neighbours found out I was on the antiretroviral drugs… I nearly stopped taking the medication just to prove that I am not infected with HIV” (FP07). Another participant explained, “She will stop treatment if I don’t come for the drugs for her” (MP06). Some participants described severe emotional distress, including thoughts of self-harm. One participant stated, “I had decided to end my life. This is not how I was before the condition” (FP08), while another explained, “When I started taking the medication… they started distancing themselves from me. It was then that I had the thought of committing suicide” (FP09).

MT6: strategies for addressing stigma and discrimination

Participants recommended several strategies to address stigma and discrimination, including public education, social support, spiritual support and legal protection. Public education was widely described as essential for correcting misconceptions about HIV. One participant stated, “Broadcasting or public education needs to be done… because people still think the disease is deadly and they fear it” (FP03). Another participant suggested, “Maybe if we send the education to many places… they can come close to those with that disease because that is needed” (FP04).

Participants emphasised the importance of family support in promoting treatment adherence and emotional well-being. One participant stated, “My wife is very supportive… all through this period she has been encouraging me” (MP06), while another noted, “My son told me to keep taking the medication… he could even come for it for me” (FP05). Spiritual support was also identified as a coping mechanism, with participants describing encouragement through religious engagement. One participant stated, “I usually get encouraged whenever I go to church” (FP04), and another reported, “I believe my hope is heightened when I go to church” (MP06). Participants further recommended strengthening legal protections against stigma and discrimination. One participant stated, “So, the government should enact a law that will penalize those who do that. This will help reduce the stigma” (FP10), while another suggested, “Anti-stigma and discrimination campaigns should be launched again, and offenders should be punished” (MP06).

Discussion

The study examines PLWHA in Sunyani Municipality, revealing complex themes affecting daily experiences, care access and psychosocial well-being, with six main themes providing critical insights.

The predominance of female participants reflected the gender distribution of individuals accessing ART services during the recruitment period and willingness to participate. This pattern is consistent with HIV service utilisation trends in many sub-Saharan African settings, where women more frequently access testing and treatment services, particularly through antenatal and routine screening programmes.20

The study identified knowledge deficits, fear of transmission and social judgement as the principal drivers of HIV-related stigma among PLWHA in Sunyani Municipality. These findings are consistent with those reported across SSA. For instance, widespread misconceptions about HIV transmission in Ghana were observed, particularly the belief that the virus could be spread through casual contact.21 22 Similar trends were documented in Kenya and Ethiopia, where poor HIV literacy was strongly associated with discriminatory attitudes.23 Despite ongoing national awareness campaigns in Ghana, misinformation continues to fuel fear, delay testing, discourage disclosure and undermine adherence to ART, posing a significant challenge to achieving the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets.

Sociocultural norms and prejudices that associate HIV infection with immorality or promiscuity further facilitated stigma in this study. Comparable findings have been reported in Nigeria, Ethiopia and parts of Rwanda, where HIV is often perceived as a punishment for sexual deviance.24 25 However, unlike in Rwanda where strong community-led anti-stigma initiatives have begun to shift perceptions,26 such community-based behavioural interventions are limited in Ghana, thereby reinforcing entrenched prejudices. This underscores the need to contextualise HIV stigma not only as a medical issue but as a sociocultural challenge, requiring both clinical and anthropological responses.

Participants reported direct experiences of maltreatment and rejection, reflecting enacted stigma similar to findings from India and Brazil, where PLWHA frequently experience abuse, social isolation and inadequate treatment within health systems.3 11 In Ghana, such stigma has been linked to patient mistrust, delays in care and reduced ART adherence.15 27 In contrast, studies from South Africa noted a decrease in provider-based stigma following the implementation of national stigma-reduction guidelines and mandatory sensitisation training.28 This difference underscores the health value of policy enforcement and institutional accountability. In Ghana, while the National HIV/AIDS Strategic Plan acknowledges stigma, operationalising policies at the facility level remains weak. Strengthening this policy-practice link could reduce stigma within healthcare settings and improve retention in care.

The study further revealed that fear of disclosure, emotional distress and financial constraints remain significant challenges for PLWHA. These findings resonate with results from China29 and Zambia,30 where stigma-induced secrecy often leads to social withdrawal and psychological distress. Notably, economic challenges were particularly emphasised by participants in this study, despite ART being provided free of charge in Ghana. Costs related to transportation, diagnostic services and supplementary medications were described as prohibitive. This suggests that economic stigma, the compounded burden of living with HIV and economic vulnerability, may be more pronounced within Ghana’s decentralised health system. Such financial barriers have important public health implications, as they may contribute to treatment discontinuation and increased risk of viral rebound, further reinforcing community-level stigma.

Consistent with previous research, the findings demonstrate that stigma is associated with social isolation, treatment default and suicidal ideation. Systematic reviews continue to show that HIV-related stigma, including enacted, anticipated and internalised forms, is associated with adverse mental health and care engagement outcomes, and that community-based stigma-reduction interventions can be effective when contextually tailored.31 These outcomes align with findings demonstrating that the psychological burden of stigma transcends geography.2332,34 However, the rate of suicidal ideation appeared especially high especially in this study as compared with others elsewhere in the Western countries.35 36 One possible explanation is the absence of integrated mental health support within HIV care settings in countries like Ghana. Unlike in many Western contexts where psychosocial services are embedded within HIV clinics, Ghanaian ART centres rarely provide dedicated mental health support.37 38 This gap is critical, as unaddressed emotional distress may intensify stigma and negatively affect treatment outcomes.

Participants identified education, family support, spiritual coping and legal protection as key strategies for addressing stigma and discrimination. These recommendations align with best practices from stigma-reduction interventions implemented in Indonesia, South Africa and Malawi.39,41 Notably, spirituality emerged as a particularly important source of resilience in this study, with many participants describing religious engagement as a source of hope and emotional support. This contrasts with findings from Indonesia, where some faith-based communities have been reported as sources of stigma.42 In the Sunyani context, however, churches were often perceived as safe spaces, presenting an opportunity to engage faith-based institutions in stigma-reduction advocacy. Participants also strongly advocated for punitive measures against discrimination. While this aligns with UNAIDS global guidance, enforcement mechanisms remain weak in many parts of Ghana. In comparison, South Africa’s comprehensive anti-discrimination legal framework has strengthened protections for PLWHA.43 Strengthening legal literacy and accessible mechanisms for redress in Ghana may therefore empower PLWHA and contribute to stigma reduction.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative phenomenological study provides rich, context-specific insights into the lived experiences of PLWHA in Sunyani Municipality, a high HIV-burden locality in Ghana, where such lived experience data have been limited. However, the small, facility-based sample, predominance of female participants, reliance on self-reported data and the absence of an HIV stigma-specific analytical framework may limit transferability and differentiation of stigma domains. Future studies could build on these findings by applying stigma-specific frameworks using qualitative or mixed-methods designs across diverse care settings.

Conclusions

This study explores the lived experiences of PLWHA in Sunyani Municipality, Ghana, with stigma and discrimination, in which six main themes were identified. The findings highlight the pervasive nature of stigma, its impact on social isolation, emotional distress, treatment adherence and suicidal thoughts. Public education, anti-stigma training, healthcare services, secrecy, family support, legal reforms and spiritual and mental health assistance are some of the ways the study recommends lowering stigma and prejudice against PLWHA in order to enhance patient well-being.
