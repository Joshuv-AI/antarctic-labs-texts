Feedback That Clicks: Introductory Physics Students’ Valued Features in AI Feedback Generated From Self-Crafted and Engineered Prompts Preprint: APS/123-QED
Amogh Sirnoorkar, N. Sanjay Rebello

Abstract
Since the advent of GPT-3.5 in 2022, Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) has shown tremendous potential in STEM education, particularly in providing real-time, customized feedback to students in large-enrollment courses. A crucial skill that mediates effective use of AI is the systematic structuring of natural language instructions to AI models, commonly referred to as prompt engineering. This study has three objectives: (i) to investigate the sophistication of student-generated prompts when seeking feedback from AI on their arguments, (ii) to examine the features that students value in AI-generated feedback, and (iii) to analyze trends in student preferences for feedback generated from self-crafted prompts versus prompts incorporating prompt engineering techniques and principles of effective feedback. Results indicate that student-generated prompts typically reflect only a subset of foundational prompt engineering techniques. Despite this lack of sophistication, such as incomplete descriptions of task context, AI responses demonstrated contextual intuitiveness by accurately inferring context from the overall content of the prompt. We also identified 12 distinct features that students attribute the usefulness of AI-generated feedback, spanning four broader themes: Evaluation, Content, Presentation, and Depth. Finally, results show that students overwhelmingly prefer feedback generated from structured prompts, particularly those combining prompt engineering techniques with principles of effective feedback. Implications of these results such as integrating the principles of effective feedback in design and delivery of feedback through AI systems, and incorporating prompt engineering in introductory physics courses are discussed.

II
Background

II.1
Prompt engineering

Since the advent of GPT-3.5 in 2022, AI has garnered huge traction among students, teachers, administrators, and researchers alike. This rise stems from the ease of generating diverse and sophisticated multimedia content using simple, natural language instructions, referred to as ‘prompts’. The specificity and effectiveness of prompts strongly determine the quality of AI-generated output. Prompt engineering thus has caught huge traction, especially surrounding the use of AI in education settings. Prompt engineering is an empirical and emerging domain which deals with the ways of systematically structuring clear, concise, and effective prompts that enhance the quality of AI-generated content. Specifically, prompt engineering refers to the “
systematic design and optimization of input prompts to guide the responses of large language models, ensuring high levels of accuracy, relevance, coherence, and usability in the generated output
”
Chen et al. 2025
. With the growing capability and accessibility of emerging AI models, prompt engineering has been increasingly recognized as an essential competency for the future workforce, particularly in STEM.

Broadly, prompt engineering techniques can be categorized into two types: foundational and advanced. Foundational techniques emphasize clarity, structure, and organization of prompts to optimize outputs. Advanced techniques, on the other hand, involve more sophisticated and dynamic interactions with the responses (such as directing the model ‘to think step by step’ or pretraining it with disciplinary information) to yield precise, accurate, and high-quality outputs.

In this study, we primarily focus on the foundational techniques and suggest readers
Chen et al. 2025
for the details on advanced techniques. Below, we briefly outline the foundational techniques to better contextualize discussions in the later sections of this manuscript.

1.

Providing instructions
. This technique involves designing directives that guide the AI model toward the intended and relevant output. For example, consider the objective of understanding the significance of the resistance
R
of a substance with respect to temperature (
T
). A prompt such as ‘
Relation between
R
and
T
’ may yield broad and diverse results with no clear instructions in the statement. Additionally, since
R
and
T
could correspond to notations for numerous physical quantities (e.g., Molar gas constant or Time period respectively), the prompt may likely invoke diverse responses.

2.

Being clear and precise
. Crafting clear and precise instructions helps the model generate accurate and relevant output. In contrast, general instructions often lead to overly broad or ambiguous responses given the model’s vast training dataset. For example, if the objective is to gain a basic understanding of electrical resistance, the prompt “
Explain the meaning of
R
in science
” may yield broad or contextually irrelevant results.

3.

Role-based prompting
. The feature of assigning a role to the AI model to yield task-specific output. Within this prompting technique, two subtypes can be distinguished: static role prompting and dynamic role-play prompting. Static role prompting involves assigning a fixed role to the model to enhance contextual accuracy and task-specific performance. Expert Prompting is one such approach, where the AI is explicitly defined as a domain expert. For example, in the context of grading numerical physics problems, static role prompting (aligned with ExpertPrompting) might take the form: “
You are an expert physics instructor tasked with grading student responses…
”

Dynamic role-play prompting, in contrast, varies the assigned role to the AI model based on user interaction. Following up with the grading example above, role-play prompting could extend the static role by supplementing the instruction with: “
Now, expand on the mathematical principle and convey he geometric implications of the results
”. Such dynamic variation is likely to further enhance the accuracy and depth of the output.

4.

Use of delimiters
. When the task is complex and the prompt has multiple components, delimiters such as triple quotes or custom symbols (*) are used to separate the components. Clear demarcation between the prompt components helps AI generate more coherent and structured output.

5.

Trying several times
. Also referred to as resampling, this technique involves running the model multiple times and selecting the best candidate output, thereby increasing the likelihood of obtaining an optimal response. It is particularly beneficial given the nondeterministic nature of AI models in generating outputs.

6.

Zero-shot, One-shot, or Few-shot prompting
. The feature of providing none (Zero-shot), single (One-shot) or multiple examples (Few-shot prompting) to draw from and generate responses. In the context of zero-shot prompting, the model relies on its large training data for generating responses.

7.

Variations of model settings
. Varying the parameter settings of the AI model such as ‘Temperature’ can also influence the outputs. The Temperature parameter regulates the degree of randomness of the generated output. A lower temperature parameter can lead to higher deterministic outputs and vice-versa.

Among the seven techniques described above, we adopt five by omitting the fifth and the seventh from the list. Furthermore, we adopt zero-shot prompting (sixth in the list) by not embedding any examples in our prompt templates. Table

II.2
Principles of effective feedback

Feedback is any information provided by an agent (people or material artifacts) focused on aspects of one’s performance
Hattie and Timperley 2007
. As an integral component of both student learning and teaching practices, feedback serves to bridge the gap between current performance and desired goals. The effectiveness however, depends on the design, content, and structure of the feedback. Effective feedback (student-facing and in the context of formative assessment) entails the following three components
Hattie and Timperley 2007
;
Burke and Pieterick 2010
;
Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick 2006
:

1.

Desired performance (‘
Where is the learner going?
’)
: This feature involves explicitly and clearly specifying the goals associated with a given task or performance. Clear communication of goals helps students set appropriate self-directed objectives, thereby providing a strong impetus for learning. This aspect of feedback is often referred to as ‘Feed Up’. For example, in the context of physics problem-solving, this might correspond to clarifying the expected product (e.g., the correct answer) or the process (e.g., use of relevant mathematical formalisms).

2.

Current performance (‘
How is the learner going?
’)
: Clarifying the current state of performance, often in relation to the desired performance. Also referred to as ‘Feed Back’, it includes highlighting both strengths or well-executed parts of the task and gaps in student understanding through probing questions. Such a ‘praise and question’ approach can support students’ self-efficacy, enhance their motivation to engage with feedback, and enable them to develop as self-regulated learners.

3.

Concrete approaches to fill the gaps (‘Where to next?’)
: Prescriptions about ways to address gaps between current and desired performances. It may include cognitive elements such as task descriptions, specific areas for improvement, actionable suggestions, and, where possible, plans for implementing changes. This component is often referred to as ‘Feed Forward’.

III
Methods

III.1
Data and Context

Our data comes from introductory students’ responses to an extra-credit assignment at a Midwestern land-grant R1 university in the United States. The assignment was part of a large-enrollment calculus-based physics course for future engineers and was administered through Qualtrics
1
1

1

https://www.qualtrics.com/
during Spring 2025. The course follows the Matter and Interactions textbook by Chabay and Sherwood
Chabay and Sherwood 2015
, with a focus on the principles of energy, momentum, and angular momentum. It also includes assignments in which students constructed arguments using the ‘Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning (CER)’ structure
Toulmin 2003
;
McNeill and Krajcik 2008
.

The assignment focused on the variation of a satellite’s potential energy with respect to its distance from a planet’s center. Hypothetical data corresponding to the satellite’s altitude from the planet’s surface, its distance from the planet’s center, and associated potential energy were provided in a table (Fig
1
). The task consisted of three parts. The first part focused on determining the planet’s radius and magnitude of the acceleration due to gravity on its surface. Students were asked to submit their written solutions in PDF or image file formats. The task’s second part focused on interpreting the physical significance of the slope of a graph with gravitational potential energy plotted against the satellite’s distance from its center. Students were asked to communicate this significance through arguments structured in the form of Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning (CER).

The final part of the assignment required students to seek feedback from AI platforms of their choice on their arguments about the graph’s slope (second task). The task was intentionally designed to encourage students to include relevant details in their prompts rather than copying-and-pasting the problem statement. This allowed to gather data on how students constructed prompts which would further be analyzed through prompt engineering techniques. Students interacted with AI through three prompt types: (i) self-crafted prompts, (ii) a provided prompt incorporating foundational prompting techniques (Section
II.1
), and (iii) a provided prompt combining foundational prompting techniques with principles of effective feedback (Section
II.2
). The corresponding feedback from AI to these three prompts are henceforth referred to as ‘Feedback A’, ‘Feedback B’, and ‘Feedback C’. For the prompts that were provided, students were required to insert their arguments into highlighted areas. No specific AI platform was recommended, allowing students to turn to the platform of their choice and enabling more authentic observations of their interactions. The survey did not allow students to go back and modify their prompts or their responses at any point during the task.

Information regarding both their self-crafted and provided prompts along with corresponding responses (feedback statements) from AI were collected. In the final stage of the task, participants were asked to rank the three feedback types by perceived usefulness and provide detailed justifications. They were also asked to list up to three things (if any) they had learned from the activity and to reflect on its usefulness for their learning. Our analysis focuses primarily on this third part of the task i.e., students’ engagement with AI feedback, particularly on student-generated prompts, their rankings of feedback, and their justifications.

III.2
Analysis

Of the 2044 students enrolled in the course during the Spring 2025 semester, 1661 responded to the extra-credit activity (81% response rate). The data were downloaded into a spreadsheet, and incomplete responses were removed, resulting in 1235 qualified responses. To address the first research question, students’ self-crafted prompts were analyzed through the lens of foundational techniques, with a particular focus on the first four techniques (‘Providing instructions’, ‘Being clear and precise’, ‘Role-based prompting’ and ‘Use of delimiters’). The remaining three techniques: ‘trying several times’, ‘one-shot/few-shot prompting’, and ‘variation of model settings’ were not included either in the provided prompt design or in the analysis of student-generated prompts.

The first four techniques were coded dichotomously (‘y’ or ‘n’), with ‘y’ indicating presence and ‘n’ indicating absence of evidence of foundational techniques. Prompts that included image or PDF attachments (17.6%) were reviewed and coded separately. Evidence of the first technique, ‘providing instructions’ was coded as present if students’ prompts included expectations from the AI model, such as seeking feedback or evaluating their arguments. Any instruction or request in students’ prompts about their arguments was considered evidence for this technique. The second technique, ‘being clear and precise’ was coded into two subcategories: (i) explicit description of the task’s objective as identifying the slope of a graph of gravitational potential energy versus distance from the planet’s center, and (ii) explicit mention of the student’s argument or their final claim. Both subcategories were coded separately on a binary scale (‘y’ or ‘n’) and only if students evidenced both the components, the prompt was coded as evidencing the second technique. Evidence for the third technique, ‘role-based prompting’ was coded as present if the prompt assigned a role to the AI model or else was marked as absent. In a similar fashion, the evidence for the final technique, ‘use of delimiters’ was also coded. A total of 84 student prompts (around 7%) were excluded since they were either unanswered or sought feedback for earlier parts of the task.

For the second research question, the first author employed emergent coding approach
Creswell et al. 2007
to analyze students’ justifications for their preferred feedback types. Justifications that were left unanswered or were difficult to interpret were removed, leaving 1160 valid responses from the initial 1235 (75 disqualified). These responses were analyzed in two stages. In the first stage, emergent codes were identified by noting the features that students referred to while justifying their choice. A ‘code sheet’ was then created documenting each feature along with its corresponding meaning (Table
3
). In the second stage, each student justification was coded in binary (‘yes’ or ‘no’) based on the features identified in the first stage to capture their prevalence across the dataset. Two independent researchers reviewed the code-book and coded a random subset of 25 responses. Their coding was compared and discussed until full agreement was reached. No major additions or deletions were made to the original codes identified in the first stage after the discussion. For the third research question, basic spreadsheet functions such as ‘COUNTIF’ were used to identify trends in students’ rankings of the three feedback types and the associated feedback features identified earlier.

IV.1
Prompt sophistication

RQ1:
To what extent do students’ self-crafted prompts to AI evidence the features of foundational prompt engineering techniques?

As discussed in Section
III.1
, we evaluated students’ self-crafted prompts in terms of prompt-engineering techniques, focusing on ‘Providing clear instructions’, ‘Being clear and precise’, ‘Role-based prompting’, and ‘Using delimiters’. The distribution of these features is presented in Figures
2
and
3
, while Table
2
provides exemplar prompts evidencing each technique.

The results reveal three overarching trends: (i) none of the students’ prompts contained all four foundational techniques together, (ii) contemporary AI models often demonstrated sophistication in interpreting contexts when prompts were ambiguous, and (iii) despite this sophistication, AI responses at times did not provide appropriate feedback.

IV.1.1
Providing clear instructions

Overall, 90.2% responses evidenced the technique of providing clear instructions in their prompts by explicitly seeking feedback for their arguments. These instructions were framed as requests (“
Please review and provide feedback for the argument
”), commands (“
Give me feedback to this argument”)
, or questions (“
Can you let me know if it makes sense and if I explained the idea clearly?
”). The remaining 9.8% prompts provided only arguments (with no explicit instruction to provide feedback) and yet the model consistently responded with feedback. For instance, for one of the students’ prompt with no explicit instruction for feedback:

The slope of the gravitational potential energy U vs r graph is equal to the negative of the gravitational force acting on the satellite. From the equation U = -GMm/r the derivative is dU/dr = GMm/
r^{2}
. The slope of the U vs r graph tells us how fast potential energy changes with distance. The force is the negative rate of change of potential energy. The slope of this graph represents gravitational force.

The AI responded with feedback:

Your argument demonstrates a strong conceptual understanding of the relationship between gravitational potential energy and gravitational force. You correctly identified that the slope of the U vs r graph represents the derivative and you clearly linked it to the gravitational force.

IV.1.2
Being clear and precise

To capture the second technique, ‘Being clear and precise’, we analyzed students’ prompts across two components: (i) explicit description of the task’s context by specifying the slope of gravitational potential energy versus distance graph and (ii) explicit mention of arguments. If students evidenced both components, we coded the prompt as evidencing this technique. While 73% of students clearly described the graph’s context and 83.7% included their arguments, only 61.8% demonstrated both together. In contrast, 11.2% provided context without arguments, 21.9% included arguments with unclear context, and 3.3% provided neither. These trends are represented in Fig.
3
.

Given these variations, we further examined how AI responses varied when prompts lacked clear or complete context descriptions. Of the 293 such cases, AI correctly interpreted the context and generated feedback in 285 instances. In only 8 cases did AI request additional clarifications or recommend significant rewording of the prompt. For example, for one student’s prompt with incomplete context regarding the type of energy (potential vs. gravitational potential):

“
Provide me with feedback about my claim, evidence, and reasoning regarding the potential energy and distance relationship. Claim: As the distance increases, the potential energy increases. Evidence: There is a positive slope between the distance and potential energy. Reasoning: Therefore there is a positive and linear relationship between potential energy and distance. However, the magnitude of the potential energy decreases as distance increases.
”

AI responded by suggesting clarity in the argument:

“
Your claim, evidence, and reasoning contain important observations, but there are a couple of points that could be clarified for better consistency and accuracy. Here is some feedback:

Claim: The statement “As the distance increases, the potential energy increases” is straightforward, but it assumes a specific scenario. Clarify whether this refers to gravitational potential energy, elastic potential energy, or another type of potential energy, as the relationship can vary depending on the context.
”

Furthermore, though AI demonstrated impressive ‘contextual intuitiveness’ (i.e., the feature of appropriately capturing the right context despite contextually ambiguous prompts) in 97% of cases, these responses were not always fully appropriate or correct. For instance, in one case a student described the slope of the graph as representing the variation of a generic potential energy with distance, without specifying gravitational potential energy or reasoning about the slope as gravitational force. The AI’s response merely corrected the relationship between the quantities but did not prompt further clarification or address the incorrect physical interpretation of the slope:

Prompt:

“
I was given some data which I graphed and was asked to find the physical significance of the slope of potential energy vs distance graph. I graphed it and the data had a positive slope. I argued that the positive slope indicates that the gravitational potential energy decreases as the distance between the planet and satellite increases. What do you think of my argument?
”

AI response:

“
Correct understanding: gravitational potential energy changes with distance.

Correction needed: potential energy increases (not decreases) with distance, even though the values are negative—you’re moving toward zero.

Let me know if you want help connecting this to orbital motion or force interpretation!
”

IV.2
Valued features in AI-generated feedback

RQ2:
What features of AI-generated feedback do students perceive as useful when the feedback is based on (a) self-crafted prompts, (b) prompts incorporating foundational prompt-engineering techniques, and (c) prompts combining foundational prompt-engineering techniques with principles of effective feedback?

Based on the emergent coding of students’ justifications of their preferred feedback types (generated from the three different prompts), we identify the following four themes associated with students’ valued features in AI-generated feedback: (i) Evaluation, (ii) Content, (iii) Presentation, and (iv) Depth. IV.2.1
Evaluation (E)

The first predominant theme corresponds to features of feedback that critically evaluate the strengths and shortcomings of student solutions along with a clear description of ways for improvement to facilitate better performance on similar tasks in future. Three features are associated with this theme: (E1) Affirmation (acknowledgment of the solution’s correctness or strengths), (E2) Critique (pointing out errors or shortcomings of the solutions) and (E3) Guidance (actionable suggestions for improvements). A total of 65.2% of student descriptions evidenced at least one of these features in their justifications. The following is one of the quotes which evidences all three features:

I found Feedback B the most useful because it gave a clear evaluation of my argument and highlighted areas of improvement without being vague. It confirmed what I did well (identifying the slope as the negative of the force) and then pointed out small gaps, like not fully explaining the curvature of the graph and not connecting the shape to the inverse-square nature of gravity. This helped me reflect more deeply on physics, not just the formula. I also appreciated the suggestion to use real-world examples, which would make my explanation stronger and more relatable.

The student’s reference to feedback acknowledging what was done well, i.e., identifying the graph’s slope as the negative of the gravitational force between the planet and satellite, illustrates the ‘Affirmation’ feature. In addition, the feedback’s identification of minor gaps in the solution, along with recommendations to include real-world examples in the arguments, reflects the ‘Critique’ and ‘Guidance’ features, respectively.

IV.2.2
Content (C)

This theme reflects feedback features that predominantly focus on content knowledge. These include the elucidation of the ‘correct’ approach to solving the physics problem or the specification of the ‘correct answer’. The theme also encompasses the application of physics and mathematics principles, along with relevant equations. In addition, it involves explicit discussion of content knowledge embedded within the argumentation structure, i.e., claim, evidence, and reasoning. We identified three features associated with this theme: (C1) Correctness (focus on the correct answer), (C2) Conceptual (discussion of physics or mathematics principles), and (C3) Schema (integration of content knowledge within the argument structure). Overall, 51.8% of responses evidenced at least one of these features. The following quote illustrates all three features associated with the Content theme:

Feedback C was the most helpful because it provided mathematical explanations with clear equations illustrating any mistakes in my argument. Additionally, it explicitly highlighted the correct answer and allowed me to compare it to my answer, easily allowing me to understand any flaws. Lastly, it provided a list of tips to improve my argument, which was far more useful than the other two arguments, which only provided at most 1-2 areas for improvement.

The reference to ‘
explicitly highlighting the correct answer
’ evidences the Correctness feature. In addition, the inclusion of mathematical explanations with clear equations reflects the Conceptual feature, while the use of an enumerated list with recommendations to strengthen the argument illustrates the Schema feature.

IV.2.3
Presentation (P)

The third theme encompasses features related to the presentation of feedback that are independent of the task’s content. These include the clarity of the provided information, the external structure or formatting (e.g., use of an enumerated list), the feedback’s length or brevity, and its overall ease of comprehension. Four features capture this theme: (P1) Clarity, (P2) Structure, (P3) Conciseness, and (P4) Intelligibility. Approximately 45% of student justifications referenced at least one of these features. The following quote illustrates all four features associated with this theme:

I found Feedback C to be the most useful and my favorite among the three because it offered a clear, structured, and detailed evaluation of my argument. What stood out was how it explicitly separated the correct answer, my response, and any gaps between the two. This side-by-side comparison made it easy to understand not only what I did well but also what specific areas could be improved. Additionally, the three bulleted suggestions at the end were especially helpful because they were clear, actionable, and directly applicable to future problems. The word limit also made the feedback feel focused and concise without losing depth. [..]

The response’s repeated references to clarity of evaluation of the argument (first sentence) and actionable suggestions (fourth sentence) evidence the ‘Clarity’ feature. Along the same lines, the response’s multiple references to structured evaluation of the argument, i.e., ‘side-by-side’ comparison of what was expected and what was written along with bulleted suggestions reflect the ‘Structure’ feature of the feedback. The last sentence on the feedback being short yet specific, evidences the ‘Conciseness’ feature. Lastly, the feedback’s ease of comprehension, the strengths and weaknesses of the argument mark the final ‘Intelligibility’ feature.

IV.2.4
Depth (D)

The last theme corresponds to the extent of detailed discussion of the student’s solution within or beyond the purview of the task. This theme however does not correspond to the ‘quantity’ or ‘wordiness’ of the feedback but rather the quality of detailed discussion of the students’ solution. Two features are associated with this theme: (D1) Detail (detailed discussion of the solution) and (D2) Specificity (feedback’s primary focus on the solution with reference to the task with no generic discussions). 44.2% of students’ descriptions evidenced at least of these two features. An example highlighting the four features is provided below.

I think that [Feedback] B gave more direction in the best way to explain the prompt in a detailed manner. It gave specific answers as to what was missing from my response and how to improve it, where as the others were more generalized.

In the above quote, the first sentence referring to the feedback feature to explain the task in a detailed fashion evidences the ‘Detail’ feature. On the other hand, the next sentence referring to feedback’s specificity about the shortcomings and ways to improve the solution mark the second feature, i.e., ‘Specificity’.

Student preferences of AI-generated feedback across prompt types

IV.3.1
Feedback Ranking

RQ3:
What are the broad trends in students’ preferences for AI-generated feedback based on (a) their self-crafted prompts, (b) prompts entailing foundational prompt-engineering techniques, and (c) prompts combining the foundational prompt-engineering techniques with principles of effective feedback?

As noted in Section
III
, students used AI models of their choice to generate three distinct feedback statements through three different prompts: (i) self-crafted prompts (resultant feedback referred to as ‘Feedback A’), (ii) provided prompts that entailed features of foundational techniques of prompt engineering (Feedback B), and (iii) provided prompts that entailed the combination of foundational techniques and the principles of effective feedback (Feedback C). Students then ranked the three feedback statements generated by AI based on their perceived usefulness along with relevant justifications. Here, we discuss the trends in students’ ranking of the three feedback types along with the trends associated with students’ attribution of the feedback features (discussed in the previous subsection) across each feedback type.

Figure

highlights the trends in students’ preferences of the three feedback types. Overall, students preferred Feedback C (generated from the provided prompt having the features of both prompt engineering and effective feedback) as their top choice and rated Feedback A (generated from self-crafted prompts) as their least preferred one. Thus, an overwhelming majority of students preferred feedback generated from structured prompts (combined preferences across Feedback types B and C). These trends are detailed below.

Across 1235 responses, around 61.4% of students ranked Feedback C as the most useful, followed by B (29.7%) and A (8.9%). For their second choice, 61.7% chose Feedback B, followed by A (29.6%), and C (8.7%). Finally as their least preferred feedback, a major section of students ranked Feedback A (61.2%), then C (30.2%), and lastly B (8.6%). These results highlight three overarching trends about student preferences. Firstly, a substantial section (91.1%) of students preferred feedback generated using structured prompts (combined first choices across B and C). Within this preference, a majority of students preferred the feedback generated using the prompt with combined features of prompt engineering and effective feedback (C). Secondly, this top choice was either liked or disliked by students. This is evident from 61.7% indicating C as their top choice and 30.1% preferring it as their least one. Only 8.2% however highlighted C as their second choice. Lastly, students’ least preferred feedback corresponded to the one generated using their self-crafted prompts.

Within Evaluation, Critique and Guidance were the most valued features. Within Content, Conceptual and Schema were rated most highly. For Presentation, Conciseness was emphasized most, while for Depth, the Detail feature was most frequently valued. Notably, no single feature was consistently valued across all three feedback types.

V
Discussion

In the previous section, we presented results on the extent of sophistication evidenced in students’ prompts to AI when seeking feedback on their arguments about a physics task (
RQ1
). We also discussed the feedback features and associated themes that emerged in students’ justifications of their preferred feedback choices among three options (
RQ2
). The three feedback versions were generated using (i) self-crafted prompts (Feedback A), (ii) provided prompts incorporating foundational prompt-engineering techniques (Feedback B), and (iii) provided prompts combining foundational techniques with principles of effective feedback (Feedback C). Finally, we examined trends in students’ rankings of the three feedback types and the distribution of identified feedback features across them (
RQ3
). The results across the research questions can be summarized as follows:

1.

Student-generated prompts rarely incorporate foundational prompting strategies in combination, as outlined in the prompt engineering literature.

2.

The emerging large language models frequently demonstrate ‘contextual intuitiveness’, i.e., taking into account the missing information such as the task’s context from prompts. Despite this sophistication, the models may not always provide conceptually accurate feedback.

3.

We identified four themes capturing the features students attribute for the perceived usefulness of AI-feedback: Evaluation, Content, Presentation, and Depth. The specific features include:: (i) Affirmation, (ii) Critique, (iii) Guidance, (iv) Correctness, (v) Conceptual, (vi) Schema, (vii) Clarity, (viii) Structure, (ix) Conciseness, (x) Intelligibility, (xi) Detail, and (xii) Specificity.

4.

Those who preferred feedback generated from self-crafted prompts preferred conceptual-related information in feedback the most. Students who preferred feedback generated from prompt entailing prompt engineering techniques valued detailed discussion of conceptual-related information along with critical evaluation of their solutions. Students who preferred feedback generated from combining of prompt engineering techniques and principles of effective feedback valued the features of critical examination of their responses, actionable suggestions along with their communication in a structured way. There was no single feature that was consistently valued by students across the different preferred feedback types.

5.

An overwhelming fraction of students preferred feedback generated from structured prompts, particularly the one entailing the combination of prompt engineering techniques and the principles of effective feedback. Students least preferred the feedback generated using self-crafted prompts. For a given preferred feedback choice, there was no common second preferred candidate.

The above results highlight several overarching aspects pertinent to the use of AI in STEM education. Firstly, students seldom employed best practices together when prompting AI. Despite this lack of sophistication, evolving AI models demonstrated notable ‘contextual intuitiveness’ by rightly interpreting prompts based on overall content, even with ambiguous contexts. However, the need for well-structured prompts becomes evident in cases where AI responses to imperfect prompts were not well-positioned to facilitate students’ learning. While techniques such as the use of delimiters may be less critical given the sophistication of current AI models, our findings underscore the importance of helping students craft well-structured prompts to maximize the potential of AI for their own learning.

Secondly, students overwhelmingly preferred feedback generated from structured prompts, particularly those embedding principles of effective feedback. Conversely, they showed the least preference for feedback generated from self-crafted prompts. This reinforces not only the importance of prompt engineering but also the value of leveraging education research on effective feedback in designing and delivering AI-driven feedback systems.

Thirdly, students identified a spectrum of valued features in AI feedback, ranging from conceptual discussions to structured communication. Yet, no single feature was consistently valued across all feedback types. Instead, each preferred type reflected unique combinations: students favoring Feedback A valued content-focused features; those preferring B valued content in combination with evaluative features such as strengths, shortcomings, and actionable suggestions; while those preferring Feedback C emphasized evaluative features along with structured presentation. These observations suggest a complex preference structure among students which warrants further investigation.

These findings both complement and extend prior research on students’ use of AI in STEM contexts. While earlier studies have examined prompting strategies in domains such as embedded systems
Sawalha et al. 2024
, programming
Zhang et al. 2024
, and chemistry problem solving by pre-service teachers
Tassoti 2024
, our study extends this work into the domain of physics argumentation tasks. Sawalah
et al.

Sawalha et al. 2024
found that undergraduates primarily relied on three prompting strategies: directly copying-and-pasting questions, presenting a partially or completely phrased one-time question, and posing multiple questions. They also observed that 41% of participants (23 of 56) used role-based prompting, particularly when posing multiple questions. Somewhat along with same lines, Tassoti
Tassoti 2024
reported a predominant reliance of their participants on the ‘copy-and-paste’ approach when interacting with AI. Zhang
et al.

Zhang et al. 2024
observed programming students preferring feedback derived from prompts that included their code, with feedback features such as specificity, clarity, and corrective nature guiding their preferences.

Observations made in the current study align as well as differ from the above observations in several ways. Firstly, our task’s context involved students to make arguments about the physical significance of a graph and then seek feedback from AI. It naturally made the ‘copy-and-paste’ approach an inefficient way of prompting. As a result, our data did not reflect this practice to the extent observed in the above studies, suggesting a possible relationship between task objectives and prompting approaches. Secondly, unlike the results from Sawalah et al., only 6 out of 1235 students in our data employed role-based prompting. However, consistent with Zhang et al., our participants preferred feedback from structured prompts that specified their arguments, attributing its usefulness to features such as Specificity, Clarity, Critique, and Guidance.

Valeri
et al.

Valeri et al. 2025
similarly examined upper-secondary students’ prompting approaches to ChatGPT. They reported that 61% of participants employed prompting strategies such as specifying output structure (e.g., bulleted lists), requesting detail or concise responses though explicit descriptors (e.g., ‘brief’, ‘longer’, or ‘medium’), and providing specific contexts to optimize results. Our observations show partial overlap with these findings. While two-thirds of our students specified context, we observe requests for output format or explicit specification of detail levels. Interestingly, these aspects emerged in the valued feedback features students highlighted in their justifications.

On feedback features, our findings also resonate with contemporary research. Henderson
et al.

Henderson et al. 2025
qualitatively analyzed university students’ use of AI for soliciting feedback and identified valued features such as depth (nuanced vs. superficial), understandability, and contextualization (sensitivity to assignment requirements). A subset of our features—such as Specificity, Intelligibility, and Schema—align closely with these observations. Perhaps, the study from Prompiengchai
et. al

Prompiengchai et al. 2025
stands as the closest counterpart to our study in terms of integrating the principles of effective feedback into feedback from AI systems. The authors discuss the potential of leveraging prompting techniques such as Zero-shot and One-shot approaches in integrating the principles of effective feedback for students’ formative assessment responses. Our study complements their discussion by not only blending prompting techniques with feedback principles, but by also capturing students’ perspectives about its efficacy on feedback quality.

VI
Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work

There is a growing body of research on leveraging AI platforms to facilitate students’ customized learning in physics, particularly through real-time feedback. This study contributes to these efforts by unpacking the spectrum of features students value in AI-generated feedback and their associated themes. We explore students’ preferences across three types of feedback generated from distinct prompt types: self-crafted prompts, provided prompts incorporating foundational prompt-engineering features, and provided prompts combining prompt-engineering principles with those of effective feedback. Finally, we also analyze students’ AI interactions by examining their prompts through the lens of foundational prompt-engineering techniques. Finally, Detailed results are presented and discussed in Section
V
.

Each result presented in this study offers a unique contribution to contemporary research on the use of AI systems for physics teaching and learning. As noted in Section
I
, while numerous studies have examined the efficacy of AI models in solving physics assessments and grading student responses, research on AI-generated feedback remains comparatively sparse. Among the few studies that focus on feedback, most employ empirical approaches that provide AI-driven feedback with little to no grounding in the theoretical principles of effective feedback from the education research literature. Noting this trend, there have been calls for blending theory into practice in the context of design and delivery of feedback in education settings. In addition to incorporating the principles, the current study also highlights students’ clear preferences for feedback generated from prompts embedding these principles.

While prior research has considered students’ perceptions of linguistic quality or scientific accuracy in AI outputs
Dahlkemper et al. 2023
, few studies have examined the features students associate with the perceived usefulness of feedback in the context of physics problem solving. This study contributes to this literature by identifying twelve distinct features that students value in AI-generated feedback and grouping them into broad themes. Lastly, our study contributes to the literature by analyzing students’ prompts in terms of their sophistication through the lens of foundational prompting techniques.

The results reported in this study carry several implications for researchers. Firstly, students-attributed valued feedback features varied depending on their preferred feedback type, suggesting a potential complex structure guiding their choices. These results suggest the need for investigation focused on factors such as task’s context, perceived task’s difficulty, familiarity with AI models, or even attitudes toward AI use in learning environments on students’ choices. We also observed students’ prompting strategies often fall short of expectations from the prompt-engineering literature. Future research could examine how students’ diligence in crafting prompts depends on factors such as task type (well-structured vs ill-structured) or participation format (individual vs. collaborative). Furthermore, while AI models demonstrated ‘contextual intuitiveness’, equity concerns remain regarding the educational divide concerning access to sophisticated models.

For instructors, our finding that students preferred feedback generated from structured prompts underscores the importance of blending AI literacy with content knowledge in physics learning environments. Such efforts can better prepare students to effectively integrate emerging technologies like AI into their learning. Our results also highlight the need to explicitly embed principles of effective feedback when leveraging AI models for designing and delivering feedback.

This study has several limitations. First, by design, students were aware of the prompts used to generate the three types of feedback. This may have introduced a bias in their preferences with notions such as - more sophisticated prompts yield more sophisticated feedback. Second, the study involved students’ interactions with AI on a single physics task. Future research using a wider range of tasks – varying in type (multiple-choice, open-ended, conceptual, numerical, etc.) and quantity – could yield deeper insights into students’ prompting approaches and feedback preferences. Third, students were restricted to crafting a single prompt for seeking feedback. In practice, AI interactions often involve iterative exchanges to refine responses, which was not captured in this study.

Future work will explore addressing the above limitations along with unpacking the co-occurrence of feedback features across preferred feedback types. Examining student preferences in relation to the correctness of their solutions also remains an avenue. Finally, we aim to investigate students’ perceptions of the usefulness of integrating AI-related activities into their assignments for their learning experiences.
