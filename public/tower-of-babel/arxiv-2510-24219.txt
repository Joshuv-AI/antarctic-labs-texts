Denseness in total variation and the class of rational-infinitely divisible distributions
A. A. Khartov

Abstract
We study a new class of so-called rational-infinitely (or quasi-infinitely) divisible probability laws on the real line. The characteristic functions of these distributions are ratios of the characteristic functions of classical infinitely divisible laws and they admit Lévy–Khinchine type representations with “signed spectral measures”. This class is rather wide and it has a lot of nice properties. For instance, this class is dense in the family of all (univariate) probability laws with respect to weak convergence. In this paper, we consider the questions concerning a denseness of this class with respect to convergence in total variation. The problem is considered separately for different types of probability laws taking into account the supports of the distributions. A series of “positive” and “negative” results are obtained.

1
Introduction

Let us consider the family of all probability laws on the real line. One of the most important classes in this family is the class of infinitely divisible laws (see
[
6
]
). Their theory is deep and beautiful (the Lévy–Khinchine formula, subclasses of self-decomposable and stable laws, decomposition problems). Their significance for probability and stochastic processes is fundamental and great (limit theorems for independent random variables, the Lévy processes, stochastic calculus). The applications are rather extensive (physics, teletraffic models, insurance mathematics).

Let us recall the definition and the main properties of infinitely divisible laws. Let
F
be a (right-continuous) distribution function on the real line
\mathbb{R}
with the characteristic function

\displaystyle f(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{itx}\,dF(x),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that
F
(and the corresponding law) is called
infinitely divisible
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
(set of positive integers) there exists a distribution function
F_{1/n}
such that
F=(F_{1/n})^{*n}
, where “
*
” denotes a convolution operation, i.e.
F
is
n
-fold convolution power of
F_{1/n}
. So
f(t)=(f_{1/n}(t))^{n}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1/n}
is the characteristic function of
F_{1/n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It is well known that
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{N}
. The most important fundamental fact is that
F
is infinitely divisible if and only if
f
is represented by
the Lévy–Khinchine formula
:

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\biggl\{it\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(e^{itx}-1-it\sin(x)\bigr)\tfrac{1+x^{2}}{x^{2}}\,dG(x)\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1)

with some
shift parameter

\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and with a bounded non-decreasing
spectral function

G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is assumed to be right-continuous at every point of the real line with the condition
G(-\infty)=0
.
The spectral pair

(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
f
and hence by
F
. Using spectral pairs, it is possible to describe the various properties of
F
and characterize weak convergence of infinitely divisible laws (see
[
19
]
and
[
22
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{F}
be the family of all distribution functions on the real line. We will denote by
\boldsymbol{I}
the class of all infinitely divisible distribution functions. Note that the class
\boldsymbol{I}
is rather wide in
\boldsymbol{F}
and it contains important well known distribution functions. Nevertheless, a lot of probability laws are not infinitely divisible, for instance, non-degenerate distributions with bounded support (see
[
2
]
). In addition, sequences of distribution functions from
\boldsymbol{I}
can not approximate (say converge to) non-infinitely divisible distribution functions even in weak sense, because
\boldsymbol{I}
is closed under weak convergence (see
[
21
]
, Lemma 7.8).

There is a class of distributions functions, which is very similar to
\boldsymbol{I}
and it is additionally dense in
\boldsymbol{F}
with respect to weak convergence. Let us give precise definitions. We call a distribution function
F
(and the corresponding law)
rational-infinitely divisible
if there are some infinitely divisible distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
such that
F_{1}=F*F_{2}
. This equality is equivalent to
f(t)=f_{1}(t)/f_{2}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are characteristic functions of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. It is clear that here
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, because
f_{1}
does not vanish and
f_{2}
is bounded (both on
\mathbb{R}
). It is not difficult to see that
f
admits
the Lévy–Khinchine type representation
(
1
) with
\gamma=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{2}
and
G=G_{1}-G_{2}
, where
(\gamma_{1},G_{1})
and
(\gamma_{2},G_{2})
denote the spectral pairs of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, respectively. It is important to note that the function
G
has a bounded variation on
\mathbb{R}
and it is non-monototic in general. In addition,
G
is right-continuous at every point and
G(-\infty)=0
. The pair
(\gamma,G)
is uniquely determined by
F
as for the class
\boldsymbol{I}
(it was proved
[
6
]
, p. 80). Now we inversely suppose that
f
is represented by (
1
) with some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
G
satisfying the above-mentioned conditions. Following the terminology from
[
16
]
, a distribution function
F
is called
quasi-infinitely divisible
in this case. Then
F
will be rational-infinitely divisible by the Hahn–Jordan decomposition for the function
G
of bounded variation. It should be noted that, due to the Lévy–Khinchine type representations, class
\boldsymbol{Q}
finds interesting applications in various fields (see reviews and references in
[
5
]
,
[
10
]
, and
[
11
]
).

We will denote by
\boldsymbol{Q}
the class of all rational-(quasi-)infinitely divisible distribution functions. It is clear that
\boldsymbol{Q}\neq\boldsymbol{F}
, because
\boldsymbol{Q}
doesn’t contain distribution functions, whose characteristic function vanish at some points. However, according to the above, we have
\boldsymbol{I}\subset\boldsymbol{Q}
. There are particular examples of distribution functions from
\boldsymbol{Q}\setminus\boldsymbol{I}
, which appeared even in
[
6
]
(see p. 81–83) and
[
15
]
(see p. 165). Moreover,
\boldsymbol{Q}
is a very significant extension of
\boldsymbol{I}
that is partially confirmed by the facts below. Let us denote by
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
the set of all discrete distribution functions and let
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
be the subclass of its lattice representatives. The following very nice result was proved in
[
16
]
(Theorem 8.1 and Corollary 8.2).

Fact 1

Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
. Then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

In the paper
[
3
]
, this assertion was extended to an important class of mixture distributions. Here and below
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
denotes the set of all absolutely continuous distribution functions.

Fact 2

Suppose that
F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{a}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
F_{d}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
,
F_{a}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
, and
c_{d}\in(0,1]
. Let
f(t)=c_{d}f_{d}(t)+(1-c_{d})f_{a}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
f_{d}
and
f_{a}
are the characteristic functions of
F_{d}
and
F_{a}
, respectively. Then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
f_{d}(t)\neq 0
and
f(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

There are several recent results, which complement these propositions. For instance, Fact
1
was generalized in
[
1
]
and
[
8
]
. Since it will be used below, we recall it here.

Fact 3

Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
. Then
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f(t)|>0
.

The analog of Fact
2
for
F_{d}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
can be find in
[
4
]
. Mixtures with continuous singular parts were considered in
[
10
]
. There is a special approach in
[
9
]
, which is convenient for pure absolutely continuous distribution functions in some cases. Simpler conditions of belonging to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
and a lot of other important results and examples concerning this class are given in the above-mentioned paper
[
16
]
, which remains the most important reference on this topic.

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is dense in
\boldsymbol{F}
with respect to weak convergence, as was proved in
[
16
]
(see Theorem 4.1). In fact, the authors constructively showed even more, namely, that the subclass
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}:=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
is dense in
\boldsymbol{F}
in weak sense, i.e. for any
F\in\boldsymbol{F}
we can construct a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
(the authors provided a method for it), which weakly converges to
F
. We note, however, that this denseness property is lost if we turn to the multivariate analog of
\boldsymbol{Q}
(see
[
5
]
) as it follows from one assertion by Kutlu (see
[
13
]
).

From the above, we get at once the following denseness results with respect to (formally stronger) uniform convergence in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!c}
of all continuous distribution functions. Indeed, if
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
weakly converges to a function
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!c}
, then this sequence uniformly converges to
F
by the well known fact (see
[
19
]
, Theorem 1.11). Thus
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
and hence
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
are dense in
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!c}
in that sense. Next, let
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive reals such that
\sigma_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and let
\Phi_{\sigma_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote the Gaussian distribution functions with zero means and variances
\sigma_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively. Then the sequence
(F_{n}*\Phi_{\sigma_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
uniformly converges to
F
too, but here all
F_{n}*\Phi_{\sigma_{n}}
are absolutely continuous functions from
\boldsymbol{Q}
. It means that
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}:=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
is dense in
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!c}
with respect to uniform convergence. In addition, the class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{s}
of all continuous singular representatives of
\boldsymbol{Q}
is dense in
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!c}
in that sense. In order to show it, we take a continuous singular infinitely divisible distribution function
H_{s}
(it is known that such a function exists, see
[
23
]
) and define
H_{s,\sigma_{n}}(x):=H_{s}(x/\sigma_{n})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
(H_{s,\sigma_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to the degenerate distribution function (with unit jump at
x=0
), the sequence
(F_{n}*H_{s,\sigma_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly and hence uniformly convergence to continuous
F
. Here the functions
F_{n}*H_{s,\sigma_{n}}
belong to
\boldsymbol{Q}
, and they are continuous singular as convolutions of discrete
F_{n}
and continuous singular
H_{s,\sigma_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This yields the required assertion. By the way, it seems that these remarks have not been discussed before in papers.

It is natural and interesting to consider the questions concerning a denseness of the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
with respect to convergence in total variation on
\mathbb{R}
(for simplicity, we will say
denseness in variation
). However, we are not aware of any general results here and so we want to devote the article to these problems. Namely, we will invesigate an existence of such denseness property for the most important subclasses
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}
in the sets
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
, correspondingly. Moreover, we will obtain the answer about the denseness in variation of the whole class
\boldsymbol{Q}
in the family
\boldsymbol{F}
. The results will be rather non-trivial and even surprising.

There will be two groups of results. The first group consists of “positive” results, where the denseness property holds (see Section 3). The results for which it is not true (say “negative” results) are assigned to another group (see Section 4). All these results are obtained due to the tools from Section 2. Some additional notation will be introduced in these sections before formulating the results.

2
The main tools

Let us start with necessary notation. Following
[
15
]
, for any distribution function
F
we define the set of all points of increase of
F
(the support of the corresponding distribution)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(F):=\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F(x+\varepsilon)-F(x-\varepsilon)>0\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0\bigr\}.

and its boundaries

\displaystyle\mathrm{lext}(F):=\inf\mathcal{S}(F)\geqslant-\infty,\qquad\mathrm{rext}(F):=\sup\mathcal{S}(F)\leqslant\infty.

For bounded
\mathcal{S}(F)
we also define its center

\displaystyle\mathrm{cext}(F):=\dfrac{\mathrm{lext}(F)+\mathrm{rext}(F)}{2}.

As usual (see
[
17
]
, p. 30) we call a distribution
F

symmetric
if
F(x)=1-F(-x-0)
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, but
F
is said to be
shift-symmetric
if the distribution function
x\mapsto F(x+c)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, is symmetric for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. In that case,
c=\mathrm{cext}(F)
for bounded
\mathcal{S}(F)
.

We first formulate a useful lemma, which will be a key tool in the proofs of “positive” results. In fact, it is a modification of one assertion by Ibragimov from
[
15
]
(see Theorem 7.1.1.). For the convenience of the reader we fully prove this lemma here (besides, the proof in
[
15
]
contains some inaccuracies).

Lemma 1

Let
F_{0}
be a distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
with a bounded
\mathcal{S}(F_{0})
. Let
f_{0}
be the characteristic function of
F_{0}
. Let
\gamma_{0}
be an arbitrary real number such that
\gamma_{0}\neq\mathrm{cext}(F_{0})
if
F_{0}
is shift-symmetric.
Then for all
\delta\in(0,1)
except a countable set (it may be empty, finite or infinite) we have

\displaystyle\delta e^{it\gamma_{0}}+(1-\delta)f_{0}(t)\neq 0\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(2)

In particular, for any
\tau\in(0,1]
there exists
\delta\in(0,\tau)
such that (
2
) holds.

Proof of Lemma
1
.
Let us fix any
\gamma_{0}
as in the formulation and define
F_{1}(x):=F_{0}(x+\gamma_{0})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. The distribution function
F_{1}
has the characteristic function
f_{1}(t):=f_{0}(t)e^{-it\gamma_{0}}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The support
\mathcal{S}(F_{1})
is bounded and hence
f_{1}
is an entire characteristic function (see
[
15
]
p. 35), i.e. there is a function
\tilde{f}_{1}
, which is analytic in
\mathbb{C}
(set of complex numbers), such that
\tilde{f}_{1}(t)=f_{1}(t)
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that, due to the assumptions on
\gamma_{0}
, the distribution function
F_{1}
is non-symmetric for any case. Therefore the function
t\mapsto\mathrm{Im}f_{1}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is not an identical zero. Additionaly, observe that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}f_{1}(t)=\dfrac{f_{1}(t)-\overline{f_{1}(t)}}{2i}=\dfrac{f_{1}(t)-f_{1}(-t)}{2i}=\dfrac{\tilde{f}_{1}(t)-\tilde{f}_{1}(-t)}{2i},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

It is clear that the function
z\mapsto\tfrac{\tilde{f}_{1}(z)-\tilde{f}_{1}(-z)}{2i}
,
z\in\mathbb{C}
, is an analytic in
\mathbb{C}
and it is not an identical zero. Therefore it has only a countable set of zeroes in
\mathbb{C}
. Consequently,
\mathrm{Im}f_{1}(t)=0
only for
t
from some countable set
T\subset\mathbb{R}
(
T
may be empty, finite or infinite).

For every
\delta\in(0,1)
we define the following function

\displaystyle f_{\delta}(t):=\delta+(1-\delta)f_{1}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let us fix
\delta\in(0,1)
and observe that

\displaystyle f_{\delta}(t)=\delta+(1-\delta)\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t)+i(1-\delta)\mathrm{Im}f_{1}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore
f_{\delta}(t)=0
if and only if
\delta+(1-\delta)\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t)=0
and
\mathrm{Im}f_{1}(t)=0
. Hence
f_{\delta}(t)\neq 0
if
\mathrm{Im}f_{1}(t)\neq 0
, i.e.
t\notin T
. We now assume that
t^{\prime}\in T
. So we have
f_{\delta}(t^{\prime})=\delta+(1-\delta)\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime})
. If
\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime})\geqslant 0
, then
f_{\delta}(t^{\prime})\geqslant\delta>0
. If
\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime})<0
, then
f_{\delta}(t^{\prime})=\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime})+\delta(1-\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime}))=0
only in the case

\displaystyle\delta=\delta^{\prime}:=\dfrac{-\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime})}{1-\mathrm{Re}f_{1}(t^{\prime})}\in(0,1).

Let
D
denote the set of all values of such
\delta^{\prime}
for varying
t^{\prime}\in T
. Thus if
\delta\notin D
then
f_{\delta}(t)\neq 0
and hence
f_{\delta}(t)e^{it\gamma_{0}}=\delta e^{it\gamma_{0}}+(1-\delta)f_{0}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e. (
2
) is valid. Since
T
is countable,
D
is a countable set too (it may be empty, finite or infinite). In particular, for any
\tau\in(0,1]
the set
(0,\tau)\setminus D
is always uncountable and, in this set, there exists a
\delta
for which (
2
) holds as required.
\Box

The next lemmata are used in the proofs of “negative” results. The first is an interesting assertion proved by Kutlu in the short paper
[
13
]
(see Theorem 1).

Lemma 2

The function

\displaystyle\varphi(t_{1},t_{2}):=\dfrac{1}{3}\,e^{it_{1}}+\dfrac{1}{3}\,e^{it_{2}}+\dfrac{1}{3}\,e^{i(t_{1}+t_{2})},\quad t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

cannot be approximated arbitrarily well by zero-free continuous
functions from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{C}
with respect to uniform convergence on
[-\pi,\pi]^{2}
.

The next lemma is a recent non-trivial result from
[
11
]
(see Theorem 2). It complements Facts
1
–
3
from the introduction.

Lemma 3

Suppose that
F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{c}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
c_{d}\in[0,1]
,
F_{d}
and
F_{c}
are discrete and continuous distribution functions, respectively. Let
f_{d}
be the characteristic function of
F_{d}
. If
F\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and
c_{d}>0
, then
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d}(t)|>0
.

The proofs of these lemmata can be found in the given references.

3
The “positive” results

Recall that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
denotes the family of all absolutely continuous distribution functions and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}
denotes the class of rational-infinitely divisible representatives of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
. We additionally introduce their subclasses, which are corresponded to distributions with supports contained in a particular set. Namely, for any
S\subset\mathbb{R}
we define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S):=\bigl\{F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}:\mathcal{S}(F)\subset S\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}(S):=\bigl\{F\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}:\mathcal{S}(F)\subset S\bigr\}.

In particular,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(\mathbb{R})=\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}(\mathbb{R})=\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}
. It is also clear that
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}(S)=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
.

For any
S\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\tau>0
we need closed
\tau
-neighbourhoods of
S
. More precisely, we define

\displaystyle[S]_{\tau}^{+}:=\overline{S+(0,\tau)}\quad\text{and}\quad[S]_{\tau}^{-}=\overline{S+(-\tau,0)}.

(3)

Here
\overline{A}
means the closure of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}
, and the sums of sets are understood in the arithmetic sense, i.e.
A+B:=\{x+y:x\in A,y\in B\}
for any
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
.

In the proofs of the results, we will use the following indicator functions:

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{A}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&x\in A,\\
0,&x\notin A,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathds{1}_{a}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&x\leqslant a,\\
0,&x>a,\end{cases}

with
A\subset\mathbb{R}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, for any function
H
defined on
\mathbb{R}
we denote by
\|H\|
the total variation of
H
on
\mathbb{R}
.

Now we are ready to formulate and prove the first “positive” result.

Theorem 1

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
such that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)\neq\varnothing
.
Then for any
\tau>0
the classes
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{+})
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{-})
are dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
.

This theorem obviously implies the following important assertion.

Corollary 1

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}
is dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.
Let
F
be a distribution function from
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
with the density
p
. Let us take the density function
b_{q}(x):=C_{q}\cdot q^{x}(1-q)^{1-x}\mathbb{I}_{[0,1]}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, of the continuous Bernoulli distribution (see
[
12
]
), where
q\in(0,1)
is the main parameter and
C_{q}
is a constant such that
C_{q}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}q^{x}(1-q)^{1-x}\,dx=1
. Let
B_{q}
denote the corresponding distribution function. Note that we come to the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
if
q=\tfrac{1}{2}
. But we fix
q\neq\tfrac{1}{2}
and for any
\tau>0
we define transformed versions of
B_{q}
:

\displaystyle B_{q,\tau}^{+}(x):=B_{q}(x/\tau)\quad\text{and}\quad B_{q,\tau}^{-}(x):=B_{q}((x+1)/\tau),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

which have the densities

\displaystyle b_{q,\tau}^{+}(x):=\dfrac{b_{q}(x/\tau)}{\tau}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{q,\tau}^{-}(x):=\dfrac{b_{q}((x+1)/\tau)}{\tau},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

respectively. It is easily seen that these distributions weakly converge to the degenerate law as
\tau\to 0+
, i.e. we have

\displaystyle B^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(x)\to\mathds{1}_{0}(x)\quad\text{as}\quad\tau\to 0\!+\quad\text{for any}\quad x\neq 0.

(4)

We first show that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\tau\to 0+}\|F-F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau}\|=0.

(5)

For every
\tau>0
the distribution functions
F*B_{q,\tau}^{\pm}
have the densities

\displaystyle(p*b^{\pm}_{q,\tau})(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x-u)b^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

respectively, and hence we have the formula

\displaystyle\|F-F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau}\|=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|p(x)-(p*b^{\pm}_{q,\tau})(x)\bigr|\,dx.

We observe that

\displaystyle p(x)-(p*b^{\pm}_{q,\tau})(x)=p(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x-u)b^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)\,du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(p(x)-p(x-u)\bigr)b^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

and, consequently,

\displaystyle\bigl|p(x)-(p*b^{\pm}_{q,\tau})(x)\bigr|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|p(x)-p(x-u)\bigr|b^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)\,du,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus for any
\tau>0
we get

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau}\bigr\|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\biggl(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|p(x)-p(x-u)\bigr|b^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)\,du\biggr)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta(u)b^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)\,du=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta(u)\,dB^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u),

(6)

where

\displaystyle\Delta(u):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|p(x)-p(x-u)|\,dx,\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
p\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, it is known that
\Delta(u)\to 0
as
u\to 0
(see
[
18
]
, p. 518, and
[
20
]
, p. 182). So
\Delta
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
, because for any real
u
and
u_{0}

\displaystyle|\Delta(u)-\Delta(u_{0})|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl||p(x)-p(x-u)|-|p(x)-p(x-u_{0})|\bigr|\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|p(x-u_{0})-p(x-u)|\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|p(y)-p(y-(u-u_{0}))|\,dy=\Delta(u-u_{0}).

Since the function
p
is a density,
\Delta
is bounded on
\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\Delta(u)\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}(p(x)+p(x-u))\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x-u)\,dx=2,\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

According to these properties of
\Delta
and the convergence from (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\tau\to 0+}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta(u)\,dB^{\pm}_{q,\tau}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta(u)\,d\mathds{1}_{0}(u),

by the extended Helly’s Second Theorem (see
[
17
]
p. 47). The integral is equal to
\Delta(0)=0
in the right-hand side. Therefore (
5
) holds due to (
6
). Note that (
5
) may be proved by direct applying of Theorem 9.3.3 from
[
18
]
, but we preferred a more transparent proof.

We now fix
\tau>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. According to (
5
), there is
\tau_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\tau)
such that
\bigl\|F-F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|<\varepsilon
. Next, we choose
r_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx<\varepsilon.

(7)

Let us define
c_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx>1-\varepsilon>0
, and
p_{\varepsilon}(x):=\tfrac{1}{c_{\varepsilon}}\,p(x)\mathbb{I}_{[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It is clear that
p_{\varepsilon}
is a density of some distribution function on
\mathbb{R}
, say
F_{\varepsilon}
. Let
f_{\varepsilon}
denote its characteristic function. It is seen that

-r_{\varepsilon}\leqslant\mathrm{lext}(F_{\varepsilon})<\mathrm{rext}(F_{\varepsilon})\leqslant r_{\varepsilon}
. So
\mathrm{cext}(F_{\varepsilon})\in[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]
and we fix any real
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}(F)\cap[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]
, but
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\neq\mathrm{cext}(F_{\varepsilon})
. By Lemma
1
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that
\delta_{\varepsilon}e^{it\gamma_{\varepsilon}}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. The function
f_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t):=\delta_{\varepsilon}e^{it\gamma_{\varepsilon}}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is characteristic for the distribution function
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x):=\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}(x)+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which is a mixture of the absolutely continuous function
F_{\varepsilon}
and the degenerate component
\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}
. By the way, we note that
\mathcal{S}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ})\subset\mathcal{S}(F)\subset S
. According to Fact
2
(where we set
F_{d}:=\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}
,
F_{a}:=F_{\varepsilon}
, and
c_{d}:=\delta_{\varepsilon}
),
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
if and only if
e^{it\gamma_{\varepsilon}}\neq 0
and
f_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Since the last inequalities are true, it follows that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Next, in the paper
[
9
]
(see Example 3.3, p. 7), it was shown that
B_{q}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
(we fixed
q\neq\tfrac{1}{2}
). Consequently,
B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and hence
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
too, because the class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is closed under shifting, scaling and convolution (see introduction). The distribution functions
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}
are absolutely continuous as a result of convolution with absolutely continuous
B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}
. Thus we have
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}
. Next, we observe (using Theorem 3.2.1. from
[
15
]
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}})=\overline{\mathcal{S}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ})+\mathcal{S}(B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}})}\subset\overline{S+\mathcal{S}(B^{\pm}_{q,\tau})}=[S]_{\tau}^{\pm}

due to
\tau_{\varepsilon}<\tau
,
\mathcal{S}(B^{+}_{q,\tau})=[0,\tau]
,
\mathcal{S}(B^{-}_{q,\tau})=[-\tau,0]
, and according to the definitions from (
3
). So we conclude that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{\pm})
, respectively.

We now estimate the total variation of the difference
F
and
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}
. We first observe that

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|\leqslant\bigl\|F-F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|+\bigl\|F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|.

Here the first term of the sum is strictly less than
\varepsilon
(see above), and for the second term we have

\displaystyle\bigl\|F*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|=\bigl\|(F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ})*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|\leqslant\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\cdot\bigl\|B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|=\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|,

where we used well-known properties of the convolution and the total variation (see
[
7
]
, p. 165–166). We now consider
F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}=F-\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}
. It has the discrete part
\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}
(with the total variation
\delta_{\varepsilon}
) and the absolutely continuous part
F-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|=\|\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}\|+\|F-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|=\delta_{\varepsilon}+\|F-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|<\varepsilon+\delta_{\varepsilon}+\|F-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|.

(8)

Let us estimate the latter variation:

\displaystyle\|F-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|=\|F-c_{\varepsilon}F_{\varepsilon}-(1-c_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|\leqslant\|F-c_{\varepsilon}F_{\varepsilon}\|+|1-c_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}|\cdot\|F_{\varepsilon}\|,

where
\|F_{\varepsilon}\|=1
(because
F_{\varepsilon}
is a distribution function). Let us consider the function
F-c_{\varepsilon}F_{\varepsilon}
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle F(x)-c_{\varepsilon}F_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,x]}p(u)\,du-c_{\varepsilon}\int_{(-\infty,x]}\dfrac{1}{c_{\varepsilon}}\,p(u)\mathbb{I}_{[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,x]}\bigl(p(u)-p(u)\mathbb{I}_{[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}(u)\bigr)\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,x]}p(u)\mathbb{I}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}(u)\,du.

There is non-negative integrand in the last integral. Then, on account of (
7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|F-c_{\varepsilon}F_{\varepsilon}\|=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\mathbb{I}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx<\varepsilon.

(9)

So we have
\|F-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|<\varepsilon+|1-c_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}|
. Using this estimate in (
8
), we get

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|<2\varepsilon+\delta_{\varepsilon}+|1-c_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}|.

Recall that
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\varepsilon)
and
c_{\varepsilon}\in(1-\varepsilon,1]
(see above). So
1-c_{\varepsilon}\in[0,\varepsilon)
and hence
|1-c_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}|<\varepsilon
. Therefore

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\bigr\|<4\varepsilon,

where
\varepsilon
can be chosen arbitrarily small and
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}*B^{\pm}_{q,\tau_{\varepsilon}}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{\pm})
as we showed above.

Thus for any
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
we can find an element from
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{+})
or
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{-})
(with any
\tau>0
), which is arbitrarily close to
F
in variation. It means that the classes
\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}([S]_{\tau}^{\pm})
are dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
.
\Box

We now turn to the family
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
of all discrete distribution functions and to the class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
. Similarly to the case of absolutely continuous distribution functions, for any
S\subset\mathbb{R}
we introduce the following classes:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(S):=\bigl\{F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}:\mathcal{S}(F)\subset S\bigr\}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(S):=\bigl\{F\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}:\mathcal{S}(F)\subset S\bigr\}.

It is clear that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(\mathbb{R})=\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(\mathbb{R})=\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
, and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(S)=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(S)
. Next, it is important for us to divide
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}
into two subclasses
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
of all discrete lattice and non-lattice distribution functions, respectively (see
[
19
]
, p. 2). Similarly, we divide the class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
into
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}:=\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{nl}}:=\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}\cap\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
. In this notation, if
L
is a non-empty subset of a lattice
\{a+bk:\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with some
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b>0
, then
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\subset\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)\subset\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
.

The second “positive” result is obtained for the discrete lattice case.

Theorem 2

Suppose that
L\subset\{a+bk:\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with some
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b>0
, and
L\neq\varnothing
. Then
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)
is dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)
.

Hence we have the following obvious conclusion.

Corollary 2

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
is dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.
Let
F
be a distribution function from
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)
with characteristic function
f
. Then
f
is represented by the formula

\displaystyle f(t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}p_{k}e^{it(a+bk)},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

where each
p_{k}
denotes the probability of the point
a+bk
of the corresponding distribution. So
p_{k}\geqslant 0
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}p_{k}=1
, and
p_{k}=0
if
a+bk\notin L
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\setminus\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)
. Then
F
is non-degenerate, because all degenerate distribution functions belongs to
\boldsymbol{Q}
(see introduction). This means that
\mathcal{S}(F)
(and hence
L
) contains at least two distinct points
a+bk_{1}
and
a+bk_{2}
with integers
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
(we set
k_{1}<k_{2}
).

Let us fix arbitrary
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and choose integers
K_{1,\varepsilon}
and
K_{2,\varepsilon}
such that
a+bK_{1,\varepsilon}
and
a+bK_{2,\varepsilon}
belong to
\mathcal{S}(F)
,
K_{1,\varepsilon}\leqslant k_{1}<k_{2}\leqslant K_{2,\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle q_{1,\varepsilon}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\,k<K_{1,\varepsilon}}p_{k}<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{2,\varepsilon}:=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\,k>K_{2,\varepsilon}}p_{k}<\dfrac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(10)

Next, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}(t):=(p_{K_{1,\varepsilon}}+q_{1,\varepsilon})e^{a+bK_{1,\varepsilon}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}:\\
K_{1,\varepsilon}<k<K_{2,\varepsilon}\end{subarray}}p_{k}e^{it(a+bk)}+(p_{K_{2,\varepsilon}}+q_{2,\varepsilon})e^{a+bK_{2,\varepsilon}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

If the set
\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:K_{1,\varepsilon}<k<K_{2,\varepsilon}\}
is empty, then the corresponding sum is assumed to be zero. It is easily seen that
\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}
is the characteristic function of some probability distribution, whose function will be denoted by
\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}\bigr\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\,k<K_{1,\varepsilon}}|p_{k}-0|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{Z}:\\
K_{1,\varepsilon}<k<K_{2,\varepsilon}\end{subarray}}|p_{k}-p_{k}|+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\,k>K_{2,\varepsilon}}|p_{k}-0|

\displaystyle{}+|p_{K_{1,\varepsilon}}-(p_{K_{1,\varepsilon}}+q_{1,\varepsilon})|+|p_{K_{2,\varepsilon}}-(p_{K_{2,\varepsilon}}+q_{2,\varepsilon})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\,k<K_{1,\varepsilon}}p_{k}+\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}:\,k>K_{2,\varepsilon}}p_{k}+q_{1,\varepsilon}+q_{2,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(q_{1,\varepsilon}+q_{2,\varepsilon}).

Therefore
\|F-\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}\|<2\varepsilon
due to (
10
).
Next, by definitions of
K_{1,\varepsilon}
and
K_{2,\varepsilon}
, we have
\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})\subset\mathcal{S}(F)\subset L
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{lext}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})=a+bK_{1,\varepsilon}\leqslant a+bk_{1}<a+bk_{2}\leqslant a+bK_{2,\varepsilon}=\mathrm{rext}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}).

From this we conclude that
\mathrm{cext}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})
can not be equal to
\mathrm{lext}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})
or
\mathrm{rext}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})
. So we set
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\mathrm{lext}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})=a+bK_{1,\varepsilon}
, and, according to Lemma
1
, we choose
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t):=\delta_{\varepsilon}e^{it\gamma_{\varepsilon}}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}(t)\neq 0\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}
denote the distribution function corresponded to
\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}
, i.e.
\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x)=\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}(x)+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By Fact
1
, we know that
\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
. Moreover, since
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})
and
\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon})\subset L
, it is clear that
\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)
. Next,

\displaystyle\bigl\|\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}-\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\bigr\|=\bigl\|\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}\bigr\|=\bigl\|\delta_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}-\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}})\bigr\|\leqslant 2\delta_{\varepsilon}<2\varepsilon.

Therefore

\displaystyle\bigl\|F-\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\bigr\|\leqslant\bigl\|F-\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}\bigr\|+\bigl\|\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}-\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\bigr\|<4\varepsilon.

Thus for any
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)
we found
\widetilde{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)
, which is arbitrarily close to
F
in variation.
\Box

We supplement the previous theorems by the following “positive” result for the mixtures of discrete lattice and absolutely continuous distribution functions. We previously introduce the necessary notation. For any two classes
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!1}
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!2}
of distribution functions we denote by
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!1}\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!2}
the family of all distribution functions of the form
F(x)=cF_{1}(x)+(1-c)F_{2}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
c\in[0,1]
,
F_{1}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!1}
and
F_{2}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!2}
. For instance,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
denotes the class of all mixtures of discrete and absolutely continuous distribution functions. Next, we introduce the classes

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}:=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap(\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a})\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathrm{lat},a}^{\mathrm{mix}}:=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap(\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}).

Let
L
and
S
be subsets of
\mathbb{R}
such that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)
and
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
are non-empty. We define

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S):=\boldsymbol{Q}\cap(\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)).

It is important to understand that
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)\neq\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}(S)
, however, of course,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)\subset\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{Q}_{a}(S)\subset\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S).

(11)

If
L
is a non-empty subset of a lattice, then
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)\subset\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathrm{lat},a}^{\mathrm{mix}}
for any admissible
S
.

Theorem 3

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
such that
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)\neq\varnothing
. Let
L\subset\{a+bk:\,k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
with some
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b>0
, and
L\neq\varnothing
. The class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
is dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
.

From this we immediately get the following assertion.

Corollary 3

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\mathrm{lat},a}^{\mathrm{mix}}
is dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{lat}}\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.
Suppose that
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
and
F
has the characteristic function
f
. Then

\displaystyle F(x)=c_{d}F_{d}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{a}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where
c_{d}\in[0,1]
,
F_{d}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)
and
F_{a}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
. Let
f_{d}
and
f_{a}
be the characteristic functions of
F_{d}
and
F_{a}
, respectively. So we have

\displaystyle f(t)=c_{d}f_{d}(t)+(1-c_{d})f_{a}(t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

We note the case
c_{d}=1
is covered by Theorem
2
according to (
11
). So we next assume that
c_{d}\in[0,1)
.

1)
Suppose that
L
contains only one point
\gamma_{1}:=a+bk_{1}
with some
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. It means that
\mathcal{S}(F_{d})=\{\gamma_{1}\}
, i.e.
F_{d}=\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}
, and

\displaystyle F(x)=c_{d}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{a}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
p
be the density of
F_{a}
. For any
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
we define a new density function
p_{\varepsilon}
exactly as in the proof of Theorem
1
with the same
r_{\varepsilon}
and
c_{\varepsilon}
(see the definitions after (
7
)). Let
F_{a,\varepsilon}
and
f_{a,\varepsilon}
be the distribution function and the characteristic function corresponding to
p_{\varepsilon}
, respectively. Note that
\mathcal{S}(F_{a,\varepsilon})\subset\mathcal{S}(F_{a})\subset S
and
-r_{\varepsilon}\leqslant\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})<\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})\leqslant r_{\varepsilon}
. Next, we define the distribution function

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x):=c_{d}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{a,\varepsilon}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{S}(F_{\varepsilon})
is bounded. Let
f_{\varepsilon}
be the characteristic function of
F_{\varepsilon}
, i.e.
f_{\varepsilon}(t):=c_{d}e^{it\gamma_{1}}+(1-c_{d})f_{a,\varepsilon}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

1.a)
Assume that
\mathrm{cext}(F_{\varepsilon})\neq\gamma_{1}
. Then, according to Lemma
1
, there exists
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that
\delta_{\varepsilon}e^{it\gamma_{1}}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}(t)\neq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that
f_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t):=\delta_{\varepsilon}e^{it\gamma_{1}}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})f_{\varepsilon}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is the characteristic function for the distribution function
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x):=\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}(x)+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, which is rewritten in the form

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t)=(\delta_{\varepsilon}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})c_{d})\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}(x)+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})(1-c_{d})F_{a,\varepsilon}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(12)

It is seen that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
. By Fact
2
, where we set
F_{d}:=\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}
(i.e.
f_{d}(t)=e^{it\gamma_{1}}\neq 0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
), we have
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and, consequently,
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
. Let us estimate the variation of
F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}
. Observe that

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\leqslant\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|+\|F_{\varepsilon}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|.

(13)

For the first term of the right-hand side we have

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-c_{d})\|F_{a}-F_{a,\varepsilon}\|\leqslant\|F_{a}-F_{a,\varepsilon}\|

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|F_{a}-c_{\varepsilon}F_{a,\varepsilon}-(1-c_{\varepsilon})F_{a,\varepsilon}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|F_{a}-c_{\varepsilon}F_{a,\varepsilon}\|+(1-c_{\varepsilon})\|F_{a,\varepsilon}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|F_{a}-c_{\varepsilon}F_{a,\varepsilon}\|+1-c_{\varepsilon}.

Here
1-c_{\varepsilon}<\varepsilon
and
\|F_{a}-c_{\varepsilon}F_{a,\varepsilon}\|<\varepsilon
as in the proof of Theorem
1
(see formulas (
7
) and (
9
)). Therefore
\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|<2\varepsilon
. We next estimate the second term in the right-hand side of (
13
):

\displaystyle\|F_{\varepsilon}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|=\|F_{\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}-(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})F_{\varepsilon}\|=\delta_{\varepsilon}\|F_{\varepsilon}-\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}\|\leqslant 2\delta_{\varepsilon}<2\varepsilon.

(15)

Thus we obtain
\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\leqslant 4\varepsilon
.

1.b)
We now assume that
\mathrm{cext}(F_{\varepsilon})=\gamma_{1}
. Since
\mathcal{S}(F_{\varepsilon})=\{\gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{S}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
and
\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})<\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
, the inequalities
\gamma_{1}\leqslant\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
and
\gamma_{1}\geqslant\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
can not be valid. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})<\gamma_{1}<\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})

(16)

and hence
\gamma_{1}=\mathrm{cext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
. In order to apply Lemma
1
here, we will slightly change the function
F_{a,\varepsilon}
. Namely, we choose a real number
\hat{r}_{\varepsilon}
such that
\gamma_{1}<\hat{r}_{\varepsilon}<\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
and
\int_{[\hat{r}_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx<\varepsilon
. Here we define
\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{[-r_{\varepsilon},\hat{r}_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx
and
\hat{p}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\tfrac{1}{\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}}\,p(x)\mathbb{I}_{[-r_{\varepsilon},\hat{r}_{\varepsilon}]}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
, the constant
\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}
is (strictly) positive:

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{[-r_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx-\int_{[\hat{r}_{\varepsilon},r_{\varepsilon}]}p(x)\,dx>(1-\varepsilon)-\varepsilon=1-2\varepsilon>0.

(17)

So
\hat{p}_{\varepsilon}
is the density function of a probability law. We denote by

\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon}
and
\hat{f}_{a,\varepsilon}
the distribution function and the characteristic function corresponding to
\hat{p}_{\varepsilon}
, respectively. Observe that
\mathcal{S}(\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon})\subset\mathcal{S}(F_{a,\varepsilon})\subset\mathcal{S}(F_{a})\subset S
and
\mathrm{lext}(\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon})=\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
, however
\mathrm{rext}(\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon})\leqslant\hat{r}_{\varepsilon}<\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
. Next, we set

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}(x):=c_{d}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}(x)+(1-c_{d})\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Here
\mathcal{S}(\hat{F}_{\varepsilon})=\{\gamma_{1}\}\cup\mathcal{S}(\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon})
and, on account of (
16
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{cext}(\hat{F}_{\varepsilon})=\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigl(\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})+\max\bigl\{\mathrm{rext}(\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon}),\gamma_{1}\bigr\}\Bigr)<\dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{lext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})+\mathrm{rext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})\bigr)=\mathrm{cext}(F_{a,\varepsilon})=\gamma_{1}.

Thus
\mathrm{cext}(\hat{F}_{\varepsilon})\neq\gamma_{1}
and we come to the case
1.a)
. Similarly to that case, we define a new distribution function
\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x):=\delta_{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}(x)+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, whose characteristic function has no zeroes on the real line for some
\delta_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\varepsilon)
. So
\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
too. The estimates for the variation of
F-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}
are analogous:

\displaystyle\|F-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\leqslant\|F-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}\|+\|\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|,

where
\|F-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}\|\leqslant\|F_{a}-\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}\hat{F}_{a,\varepsilon}\|+1-\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}<4\varepsilon
(see (
9
), (
14
) and (
17
)), and
\|\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|=\delta_{\varepsilon}\|\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}-\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{1}}\|\leqslant 2\delta_{\varepsilon}<2\varepsilon
(see (
15
)). So we get
\|F-\hat{F}_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\leqslant 6\varepsilon
.

2)
We now turn to the case, when
L
contains at least two distinct points
a+bk_{1}
and
a+bk_{2}
with integers
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
(we set
k_{1}<k_{2}
). Since we have already handled the case of singleton set
\mathcal{S}(F_{d})
, we can think that
\mathcal{S}(F_{d})
contains these points too without loss of generality. According to Theorem
2
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
we can find
F_{d,\varepsilon}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}(L)
such that
\|F_{d}-F_{d,\varepsilon}\|<\varepsilon
. We choose such an
F_{d,\varepsilon}
with the bounded set
\mathcal{S}(F_{d,\varepsilon})
(see the proof of Theorem
2
). Let
f_{d,\varepsilon}
be the characteristic function of
F_{d,\varepsilon}
. Due to Fact
3
,
\mu_{d,\varepsilon}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d,\varepsilon}|>0
. Next, we define
F_{a,\varepsilon}
and
f_{a,\varepsilon}
as above, see
1)
. Recall that
\mathcal{S}(F_{a,\varepsilon})
is bounded and it is contained in
S
. We also have the estimate
\|F_{a}-F_{a,\varepsilon}\|<2\varepsilon
, see (
14
) in
1.a)
. Let us define the distribution function

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x):=c_{d}F_{d,\varepsilon}(x)+(1-c_{d})F_{a,\varepsilon}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(18)

with the corresponding characteristic function
f_{\varepsilon}
. It is clear that
\mathcal{S}(F_{\varepsilon})
is bounded. Since
L
contains at least two points, there is
\gamma_{2}\in L
such that
\gamma_{2}\neq\mathrm{cext}(F_{\varepsilon})
. By Lemma
1
, for any
\tau\in(0,1]
there exists
\delta\in(0,\tau)
such that

\displaystyle f^{\circ}_{\varepsilon}(t):=\delta e^{it\gamma_{2}}+(1-\delta)f_{\varepsilon}(t)\neq 0\quad\text{for any}\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(19)

For any fixed
\delta
the function
f^{\circ}_{\varepsilon}
is characteristic for the distribution function
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x):=\delta\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{2}}(x)+(1-\delta)F_{\varepsilon}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Using (
18
), we have

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{2}}(x)+(1-\delta)c_{d}F_{d,\varepsilon}(x)+(1-\delta)(1-c_{d})F_{a,\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\delta+c_{d}-\delta c_{d})F_{d,\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x)+(1-\delta-c_{d}+\delta c_{d})F_{a,\varepsilon}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

where

\displaystyle F_{d,\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x):=\dfrac{\delta\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{2}}(x)+(1-\delta)c_{d}F_{d,\varepsilon}(x)}{\delta+c_{d}-\delta c_{d}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

It is easily seen that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
. If
c_{d}=0
, i.e.
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}(x)=\delta\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{2}}(x)+(1-\delta)F_{a,\varepsilon}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then we choose
\tau:=\varepsilon
and so
\delta=\delta_{\varepsilon}<\varepsilon
. This case has already been covered in
1.a)
(see (
12
) with
c_{d}=0
). So we have
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
and
\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\leqslant 4\varepsilon
. Next, if
c_{d}>0
then we choose
\delta=\delta_{\varepsilon}<\tau:=\min\{\varepsilon,(1-\varepsilon)c_{d}\mu_{d,\varepsilon}\}
and consider the characteristic function of
F_{d,\varepsilon}^{\circ}
:

\displaystyle f_{d,\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t):=\dfrac{\delta_{\varepsilon}e^{it\gamma_{2}}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})c_{d}f_{d,\varepsilon}(t)}{\delta_{\varepsilon}+c_{d}-\delta_{\varepsilon}c_{d}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Observe that

\displaystyle|f_{d,\varepsilon}^{\circ}(t)|\geqslant\dfrac{(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})c_{d}|f_{d,\varepsilon}(t)|-\delta_{\varepsilon}}{\delta_{\varepsilon}+c_{d}-\delta_{\varepsilon}c_{d}}\geqslant\dfrac{(1-\varepsilon)c_{d}\mu_{d,\varepsilon}-\delta_{\varepsilon}}{\delta_{\varepsilon}+c_{d}-\delta_{\varepsilon}c_{d}}\geqslant\dfrac{\tau-\delta_{\varepsilon}}{\delta_{\varepsilon}+c_{d}-\delta_{\varepsilon}c_{d}}>0.

From this and (
19
) we conclude that
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
due to Fact
2
. Hence
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
. Next, we have the following estimates for the variations:

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|\leqslant\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|+\|F_{\varepsilon}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|,

where

\displaystyle\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|c_{d}(F_{d}-F_{d,\varepsilon})+(1-c_{d})(F_{a}-F_{a,\varepsilon})\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle c_{d}\|F_{d}-F_{d,\varepsilon}\|+(1-c_{d})\|F_{a}-F_{a,\varepsilon}\|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle c_{d}\varepsilon+(1-c_{d})2\varepsilon<2\varepsilon,

and
\|F_{\varepsilon}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|=\delta_{\varepsilon}\|F_{\varepsilon}-\mathds{1}_{\gamma_{2}}\|\leqslant 2\delta_{\varepsilon}<2\varepsilon
(see (
15
)). Therefore
\|F-F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\|<4\varepsilon
.

Thus for any
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
and for any case,
1)
or
2)
, we can find
F_{\varepsilon}^{\circ}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
, which is arbitrarily close to
F
in variation. So we come the required denseness of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d,a}^{\mathrm{mix}}(L,S)
in
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}(L)\oplus\boldsymbol{F}_{\!a}(S)
.
\Box

Thus, according to Theorems
1
–
3
, discrete lattice laws, absolutely continuous laws and their mixtures (i.e. the distributions, which are the most important for practice) can be approximated arbitararily well by rational-infinitely divisible distributions (of the same types, correspondingly) with respect to very strong distance in total variation, i.e. a distribution with one of these types is almost indistinguishable with some rational-infinitely law of this type.

4
The “negative” results

Here we follow the notation from the previous sections. We focus on the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
of discrete non-lattice distribution functions.

Theorem 4

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
is not dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
.

The following obvious corollary is opposite to Corollary
2
for the lattice case.

Corollary 4

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
is not dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.
Let
\alpha
be a positive irrational number and

\displaystyle F(x):=\dfrac{1}{3}\,\mathds{1}_{1}(x)+\dfrac{1}{3}\,\mathds{1}_{\alpha}(x)+\dfrac{1}{3}\,\mathds{1}_{1+\alpha}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

It is easy to see that
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
. We assume that there is a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
such that

\displaystyle\|F_{n}-F\|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(20)

We represent every
F_{n}
in the following form:

\displaystyle F_{n}(x)=p_{n,1}\mathds{1}_{1}(x)+p_{n,\alpha}\mathds{1}_{\alpha}(x)+p_{n,1+\alpha}\mathds{1}_{1+\alpha}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}}\mathds{1}_{u_{k}}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we set
p_{n,u_{k}}\geqslant 0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
p_{n,1}+p_{n,\alpha}+p_{n,1+\alpha}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}}=1
. Here, without loss of generality,
\mathcal{U}:=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k},\ldots\}
is an infinite countable set of distinct real numbers such that
\{1,\alpha,1+\alpha\}\cap\mathcal{U}=\varnothing
and the set
\{1,\alpha,1+\alpha\}\cup\mathcal{U}
contains all the sets
\mathcal{S}(F_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So we have

\displaystyle\|F_{n}-F\|=\bigl|p_{n,1}-1/3\bigr|+\bigl|\,p_{n,\alpha}-1/3\bigr|+\bigl|p_{n,1+\alpha}-1/3\bigr|+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(21)

Let
f_{n}
denote the characteristic function of
F_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle f_{n}(t)=p_{n,1}e^{it}+p_{n,\alpha}e^{it\alpha}+p_{n,1+\alpha}e^{it(1+\alpha)}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}}e^{itu_{k}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
F_{n}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
, we know that
\mu_{n}:=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)|>0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, by Fact
3
. Next, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we choose
K_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{k=K_{n}+1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}}<\mu_{n}/2
, and we set

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{n}(t):=p_{n,1}e^{it}+p_{n,\alpha}e^{it\alpha}+p_{n,1+\alpha}e^{it(1+\alpha)}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}e^{itu_{k}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Observe that

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\tilde{f}_{n}(t)|\geqslant\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)|-\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{n}(t)-\tilde{f}_{n}(t)|=\mu_{n}-\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\sum_{k=K_{n}+1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}}e^{itu_{k}}\biggr|

and hence we have

\displaystyle\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|\tilde{f}_{n}(t)|\geqslant\mu_{n}-\sum_{k=K_{n}+1}^{\infty}p_{n,u_{k}}>\mu_{n}-\dfrac{\mu_{n}}{2}=\dfrac{\mu_{n}}{2}>0.

(22)

We next select a basis of the system
\mathcal{U}_{0}:=\{1,\alpha,1+\alpha\}\cup\,\mathcal{U}
over
\mathbb{Q}
by the following procedure. We set
\beta_{1}:=1
and
\beta_{2}:=\alpha
. We next sequentially cross out all numbers
y
of
\mathcal{U}_{0}
satisfying the relation
r_{1}\beta_{1}+r_{2}\beta_{2}+r_{3}y=0
with any
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
and
r_{3}\neq 0
from
\mathbb{Q}
. Of course,
1
,
\alpha
and
1+\alpha
will be cross out. Let
\beta_{3}
be the first term of the sequence
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(
\mathcal{U}
with order), which was not crossed out. Next, we cross out from the set of the numbers, which have not been crossed out yet, all numbers
y
satisfying the relation
r_{1}\beta_{1}+r_{2}\beta_{2}+r_{3}\beta_{3}+r_{4}y=0
with any
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
,
r_{3}
and
r_{4}\neq 0
from
\mathbb{Q}
. We denote by
\beta_{4}
the first number in
(u_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, which was not crossed out. By continuing this process analogously, we get the collection
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, …,
\beta_{k}
,…, which may be finite (if all elements of
\mathcal{U}_{0}
will be crossed out at some step of the process) or infinite. By construction, it is seen that
\beta_{1}
,
\beta_{2}
, …are non-zero and they linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, i.e. if
r_{1}\beta_{1}+r_{2}\beta_{2}+\ldots+r_{m}\beta_{m}=0
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
r_{1}=r_{2}=\ldots=r_{m}=0
. In addition, every
u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}
is represented by the finite linear combination

\displaystyle u_{k}=a_{k,1}\beta_{1}+a_{k,2}\beta_{2}+\ldots+a_{k,m_{k}}\beta_{m_{k}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

with some
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{k,1},a_{k,2},\ldots,a_{k,m_{k}}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Of course, this representation is unique (if the order of summation and the summands with zero coefficients
a_{k,j}
are disregarded). Since zero values are admissible for
a_{k,j}
, we will assume that
m_{k}\geqslant 2
for convenience.

We now return to the functions
\tilde{f}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and find the minimal positive integer
\varkappa_{n}
such that
A_{k,j}^{n}:=\varkappa_{n}a_{k,j}\in\mathbb{Z}
for any
k=1,\ldots,K_{n}
and
j=1,\ldots,m_{k}
. We set
\bar{\beta}_{k}^{n}:=\beta_{k}/\varkappa_{n}
for every
k=1,\ldots,K_{n}
. Therefore we write

\displaystyle u_{k}=A_{k,1}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}+A_{k,2}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}+\ldots+A_{k,m_{k}}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{m_{k}}^{n},\quad k=1,\ldots,K_{n},

Thus we can represent
\tilde{f}_{n}
in the following form

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{n,1}\exp\bigl\{it\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}\bigr\}+p_{n,\alpha}\exp\bigl\{it\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}\bigr\}+p_{n,1+\alpha}\exp\bigl\{it\bigl(\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}+\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}\bigr)\bigr\}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}\exp\biggl\{it\sum_{j=1}^{m_{k}}A_{k,j}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{j}^{n}\biggr\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
M_{n}:=\max\{m_{k}:k=1,\ldots,K_{n}\}
. We introduce the function

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{M_{n}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{n,1}\exp\bigl\{it_{1}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}\bigr\}+p_{n,\alpha}\exp\bigl\{it_{2}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}\bigr\}+p_{n,1+\alpha}\exp\bigl\{i\bigl(t_{1}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}+t_{2}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}\bigr)\bigr\}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}\exp\biggl\{i\sum_{j=1}^{m_{k}}t_{j}A_{k,j}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{j}^{n}\biggr\},\quad t_{1},\ldots,t_{M_{n}}\in\mathbb{R},

for which the function
\tilde{f}_{n}
is diagonal, i.e.
\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(t,\ldots,t)=\tilde{f}_{n}(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
\tilde{\varphi}_{n}
is a continuous periodic function with periods
2\pi/\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}
, . . . ,
2\pi/\bar{\beta}_{M_{n}}^{n}
over variables

t_{1},\ldots,t_{M_{n}}
, respectively (recall that all
A_{k,j}^{n}
are integers). Since the reciprocals of the periods are linear independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, the set of values of
\tilde{f}_{n}
is dense in the set of values of
\tilde{\varphi}_{n}

(see
[
14
]
, Theorem 2.4.1., p. 116). Hence
|\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{M_{n}})|\geqslant\mu_{n}/2
for any
t_{1},\ldots,t_{M_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}
due to (
22
). Next, we consider the function

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{2,n}(t_{1},t_{2})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{n}(t_{1},t_{2}/\alpha,0,\ldots,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{n,1}\exp\bigl\{it_{1}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}\bigr\}+p_{n,\alpha}\exp\bigl\{it_{2}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}/\alpha\bigr\}+p_{n,1+\alpha}\exp\Bigl\{i\bigl(t_{1}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}+t_{2}\varkappa_{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}/\alpha\bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}\exp\Bigl\{i\bigl(t_{1}A_{k,1}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{1}^{n}+t_{2}A_{k,2}^{n}\bar{\beta}_{2}^{n}/\alpha\bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{n,1}e^{it_{1}}+p_{n,\alpha}e^{it_{2}}+p_{n,1+\alpha}e^{i(t_{1}+t_{2})}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}\exp\Bigl\{i\bigl(t_{1}a_{k,1}+t_{2}a_{k,2}\bigr)\Bigr\},\quad t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

By the above, in particular, we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{\varphi}_{2,n}(t_{1},t_{2})|\geqslant\mu_{n}/2>0\quad\text{for any}\quad t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

(23)

We now vary index
n
. Let us introduce the function

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}(t_{1},t_{2}):=\dfrac{1}{3}\,e^{it_{1}}+\dfrac{1}{3}\,e^{it_{2}}+\dfrac{1}{3}\,e^{i(t_{1}+t_{2})},\quad t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

and observe that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\tilde{\varphi}_{2,n}(t_{1},t_{2})-\varphi_{2}(t_{1},t_{2})\bigr|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\biggl|\bigl(p_{n,1}-1/3\bigr)e^{it_{1}}+\bigl(p_{n,\alpha}-1/3\bigr)e^{it_{2}}+\bigl(p_{n,1+\alpha}-1/3\bigr)e^{i(t_{1}+t_{2})}

\displaystyle{}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}\exp\Bigl\{i\bigl(t_{1}a_{k,1}+t_{2}a_{k,2}\bigr)\Bigr\}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\bigl|p_{n,1}-1/3\bigr|+\bigl|p_{n,\alpha}-1/3\bigr|+\bigl|p_{n,1+\alpha}-1/3\bigr|+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}p_{n,u_{k}}.

According to (
20
) and (
21
), we have
\Delta_{n}\leqslant\|F_{n}-F\|
and hence
\Delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Due to Lemma
2
, we conclude that
\tilde{\varphi}_{2,n}
have zeroes in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. However, it obviously contradicts to (
23
).

Thus it is impossible to select a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
, which converges to
F
in variation.
\Box

The next theorem is rather non-obvious corollary of the previous one. It is obtained due to the fact from Lemma
3
(see the recent results from
[
11
]
).

Theorem 5

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is not dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.
Suppose, contrary to our claim, that
\boldsymbol{Q}
is dense in variation in
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
. Then for any
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
there exists a sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
\boldsymbol{Q}
such that

\displaystyle\|F_{n}-F\|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(24)

Let us represent each
F_{n}
as the sum of its discrete and continuous parts:

\displaystyle F_{n}(x)=c_{d,n}F_{d,n}(x)+(1-c_{d,n})F_{c,n}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
F_{d,n}
is a discrete distribution function,
F_{c,n}
is a continuous one, and
c_{d,n}\in[0,1]
. Then

\displaystyle\|F_{n}-F\|=\|c_{d,n}F_{d,n}-F\|+\|(1-c_{d,n})F_{c,n}\|=\|c_{d,n}F_{d,n}-F\|+1-c_{d,n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

From (
24
) we have
\|c_{d,n}F_{d,n}-F\|\to 0
and
c_{d,n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. It implies that
\|F_{d,n}-F\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, it follows from the estimates:

\displaystyle\|F_{d,n}-F\|\leqslant\|(1-c_{d,n})F_{d,n}\|+\|c_{d,n}F_{d,n}-F\|=1-c_{d,n}+\|c_{d,n}F_{d,n}-F\|,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Next, we know that
F_{n}\in\boldsymbol{Q}
and
c_{d,n}>0
for all large enough
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, according to Lemma
3
, the characteristic function of each
F_{d,n}
, say
f_{d,n}
, is separated from zero, i.e.
\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|f_{d,n}(t)|>0
. By Fact
3
, it means that
F_{d,n}\in\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Thus for arbitrary
F\in\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
we can always find the sequence
(F_{d,n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
from
\boldsymbol{Q}_{d}
, which converges in variation to
F
. But it is not true by Theorem
4
. Hence the initial assumption is false, i.e.
\boldsymbol{Q}
is not dense in variation in
\boldsymbol{F}_{\!d}^{\mathrm{nl}}
.
\Box

Theorem
5
immediately implies the following important assertion, which we formulate as theorem.

Theorem 6

The class
\boldsymbol{Q}
is not dense in variation in the class
\boldsymbol{F}
.

Due to the results by Kutlu (see
[
13
]
), this theorem is also valid for the multivariate analog of
\boldsymbol{Q}
, because, for instance, the multivariate version of Corollary
2
is false. However, does Theorem
1
(or Corollary
1
) hold in the multivariate case? This is an interesting question.
