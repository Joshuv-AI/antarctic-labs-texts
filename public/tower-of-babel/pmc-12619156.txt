Molecular insights into prognostic model for meningiomas treated with stereotactic radiosurgery: negative impacts of 1q gain on tumor control and survival
Authors: Motoyuki Umekawa, Yuki Shinya, Yudai Hirano, Satoru Miyawaki, Hirotaka Hasegawa, Yu Sakai, Yu Teranishi, Shotaro Ogawa, Atsuto Katano, Daisuke Komura, Hiroto Katoh, Masako Ikemura, Hideaki Ono, Tetsuo Ushiku, Shumpei Ishikawa, Nobuhito Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular classifications enhance prognostic accuracy in meningiomas; however, their predictive significance following stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) remains unclear. This study aimed to assess whether molecular characteristics identified by whole-exome sequencing (WES), specifically driver mutations and copy number alterations (CNAs), are prognostic indicators of outcomes after SRS. This retrospective cohort study included 95 patients (median age 61; 66% female) with 97 surgically resected meningiomas treated with SRS between 1999 and 2023. Primary outcomes were progression-free survival (PFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). Tumors were molecularly classified using WES into Group A (NF2-wildtype), Group B (NF2 mutation/22q loss without high-risk CNAs), and Group C (high-risk CNAs including 1p loss, 1q gain, 6p/6q loss, 10p/10q loss, 14q loss, 18p/18q loss, and CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion). Group C exhibited significantly inferior PFS at 5 years (49.7%) compared with Groups A (88.5%, p < 0.001) and B (100%, p = 0.002). DSS at 10 years was also significantly reduced in Group C (60.4%) relative to Groups A and B (100%, p < 0.001 and p = 0.016, respectively). Within Group C, 1q gain correlated strongly with poorer outcomes, with significantly lower 5-year PFS (15.9% vs. 64.3%, p < 0.001) and DSS (51.3% vs. 90.4%, p < 0.001). Furthermore, even WHO grade 1 tumors with 1q gain demonstrated significantly worse outcomes (5-year PFS: 33.3% vs. 76.5%, p = 0.023; DSS: 44.4% vs. 89.3%, p = 0.011). Molecular classification utilizing WES-derived CNAs substantially improves prognostic prediction after SRS for meningiomas. Introduction

Meningiomas, the most common primary central nervous system tumors, may arise everywhere in the cranium, compressing and/or invading the brain and nerves [31]. Whilst typically benign, they may cause various symptoms depending on their location. Surgical resection is the standard treatment for symptomatic or larger meningiomas [12, 13]. For World Health Organization (WHO) grade 2 or 3 tumors, adjuvant conventional radiotherapy may be used for tumor control [13]. Additionally, favorable evidence of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) has been accumulated recently, in terms of tumor control and low complication rates [21, 38, 42]. Because SRS can deliver meticulous concentrated irradiation to the tumor margin in a less invasive manner, it may be an alternative to resection for small or asymptomatic lesions, patients with contraindications to general anesthesia for craniotomy, or those refusing surgery [38].

The most recent fifth edition of the WHO grading system introduced genetic factors into meningioma grading with detection of TERTp mutation or CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion definitively diagnosing grade 3 meningiomas [3, 26]. Although WHO grades can accurately predict prognosis, some grade 1 meningiomas exhibit aggressive behavior that differs significantly from that of “benign tumor.” In the context of heterogeneity beyond WHO grading, even after introduction of genetic factors, several studies have demonstrated comprehensive prognostic predictions by integrating genetic, chromosomal, epigenomic, and transcriptomic analyses.

Currently, large-scale multi-omics analyses predominantly utilize three major molecular classifications based on driver mutations and copy number alterations (CNAs): (1) NF2-wild type tumors with other driver mutations; (2) tumors harboring only NF2 mutations with low CNA burden and prominent immune cell infiltration; and (3) tumors harboring NF2 mutations accompanied by high CNA burden and strong proliferative capability [2, 5, 28, 30, 32]. These classifications are increasingly used to predict tumor progression; however, few studies have attempted to develop prediction models using molecular insights in SRS-treated meningiomas. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate SRS outcomes by integrating genetic mutation and CNA analyses in the tumor using comprehensive exome-panel sequences, and to establish a molecular prognostic model for meningiomas treated with SRS.

Methods

Study participants and tumors

One hundred and fifty-nine meningiomas in 146 patients who underwent surgical resection followed by SRS between June 1999 and August 2024 were identified retrospectively. SRS was performed for postoperative residual or regrowing tumors. Owing to the unavailability of frozen tissues, 62 tumors from 51 patients were excluded. Therefore, 97 meningiomas in 95 patients were included in the analysis with resection performed between June 1999 and August 2023 (Online Resource 1). Pathological diagnoses were based on the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Central Nervous System, 5th edition, incorporating driver mutation data [3]. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (approval numbers 2231 and G10028). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

SRS procedures and techniques

The procedures for single-fraction and hypofractionated SRS using a Leksell Gamma Knife (Elekta Instruments, Stockholm, Sweden) are described in our previous studies [17, 44]. After head fixation using a Leksell frame (Elekta Instruments) for single-fraction SRS, stereotactic imaging (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI] between January 1998 and January 2018, and cone-beam computed tomography [CT] thereafter) was performed to obtain precise three-dimensional coordinates. For hypofractionated SRS, a thermoplastic mask (Icon Mask Nanor; Elekta Instruments) and cushion (Icon Patient Cushion Klarity; Elekta Instruments) were used for head fixation, and stereotactic imaging was obtained using cone-beam CT in the same fashion as single-fraction SRS. Thin-slice MR images (gadolinium-contrasted T1-weighted and fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition) obtained the day before SRS were co-registered to define the tumor, surrounding tissues, and organs at risk. Radiosurgical treatment was planned and approved by dedicated neurosurgeons and radiation oncologists and all treatment planning was performed using Leksell GammaPlan® software (Elekta Instruments). For WHO grade 1 meningiomas, the tumor margin was basically circumscribed with a 50% isodose line with prescriptions of 14 Gy for single-fraction SRS and 28 Gy in five fractions for hypo-fractionated SRS. Prescription doses were increased based on WHO grade or Ki-67 labeling index (LI).

Follow-up and treatment outcomes

Clinical evaluation and MRI were performed every 3–6 months after SRS. Data on patient and tumor status, and SRS-related complications were prospectively collected in the Institutional Gamma Knife database. Two neurosurgeons and two neuroradiologists independently assessed radiological evidence of recurrence. Tumor progression was defined as a > 10% increase in volume inside the 50% isodose line or new progression outside the 50% isodose line, and shrinkage was defined as a 25% reduction in tumor volume [1, 14, 42, 43]. Disease-specific mortality was defined as death associated with the treated tumor, including death owing to uncontrollable tumor progression and death within 30 days of salvage surgery for tumor progression.

Genetic status and CNA analyses by whole-exome sequencing

Genomic analyses were conducted as described in our previous publications, with comprehensive methodology provided in the supplementary materials [15]. DNA was extracted from freshly frozen tumor samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands). DNA quality was evaluated using a spectrophotometer. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed using a Twist Comprehensive Exome Panel Kit (Twist Bioscience, San Francisco, CA). Samples were fragmented using an acoustic solubilizer (Covaris, Woburn, MA) and adapter sequences were added to both ends. Targeted exon regions were hybridized with biotin-labeled RNA probes and subsequently captured using streptavidin-linked magnetic beads. The enriched DNA was amplified using polymerase chain reaction to generate sequencing libraries. The DNA library was subjected to 150-base paired-end read sequencing using a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Following quality control and trimming via fastq with default parameters, the reads were aligned to the human genome reference hg38 using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner [24]. Tumor mutations were identified using Mutect2 (Parabricks v3.8.0; https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360037593851-Mutect2) in tumor-only mode [6]. To exclude non-pathogenic mutations, FilterMutectCalls (GATK 4.1.7.0; https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360036856831-FilterMutectCalls) was used with default values for mutations detected by Mutect2. CNAs were analyzed using Cnvkit, employing tumor samples and a reference composed of five pooled peripheral blood samples [40]. Arm-level somatic CNAs were defined as events covering over 80% of a chromosomal arm. Acrocentric chromosomes (13p, 14p, 15p, 21p, 22p) were excluded from the analysis. Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B was defined as a log2 copy ratio of less than − 1.1 across the CDKN2A/B locus and its flanking region.

1p loss, 1q gain, 6p/6q loss, 10p/10q loss, 14q loss, 18p/18q loss, and CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion were defined as high-risk CNAs based on previous publications [28, 30, 45]. Meningiomas were broadly classified into three groups based on molecular characteristics: Group A, NF2-wildtype including tumors harboring driver mutations, such as TRAF7, KLF4, AKT1, and POLR2A or those in which neither driver mutations nor high-risk CNAs were detected; Group B, NF2 mutations and/or CNA of 22q loss without high-risk CNAs; and Group C, the presence of high-risk CNAs with NF2 or without other known driver mutations (Online Resource 1).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), whereas categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages). Baseline characteristics of the patients were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate therapeutic effects, with outcomes including progression-free survival (PFS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) rates. To further analyze the association between molecular status and SRS outcomes, PFS and DSS rates were analyzed using the three molecular groups, and high-risk CNAs were used to analyze Group C patients. The PFS and DSS rates were compared between groups using the log-rank test, and multiple curves were compared using the log-rank test with Bonferroni correction. Continuous variables (age, maximum diameter, target volume, Ki-67 LI, and radiosurgical dose) were dichotomized using median values, and factors associated with tumor progression and disease-specific mortality were examined using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses. In the subgroup analysis, the same evaluations and calculations were performed for patients with WHO grade 1 meningiomas. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP® Pro 17.0.0 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Participant characteristics

Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age in the entire cohort was 61 years (IQR 50–71 years) with a female dominance (66%). The median follow-up after SRS was 50 months (IQR 22–117 months), and skull base meningiomas were more frequently treated than convexity, falx, or parasagittal meningiomas (77% vs. 23%). Only one patient with NF2-related schwannomatosis was included. Regarding WHO grades, 74 meningiomas were grade 1, 17 were grade 2 (including 15 atypical meningiomas), and six were grade 3 (all anaplastic), as confirmed by resection prior to SRS. Two patients with multiple meningiomas underwent resection and SRS for each tumor. The median Ki-67 LI was 4% (IQR 2–6%). Upfront adjuvant SRS was performed for residual tumors after resection in 52 lesions (54%), whereas salvage SRS was performed for progressive tumors after resection in 45 lesions (46%). Single-session SRS with a median prescription dose of 16 Gy was predominantly performed for 88 lesions (91%), and hypofractionated SRS (28 Gy/5-fraction) was performed for nine lesions (9%).

Genetic mutation and CNA profiles

In the entire cohort, the most common driver mutations were NF2 (29 tumors, 30%), followed by TRAF7 (15 tumors, 15%), AKT1 (11 tumors, 11%), POLR2A (eight tumors, 8%), and KLF4 and PIK3CA (three tumors each, 3%) (Fig. 1). CDKN2A/B homozygous deletions were detected in two tumors (2%) and no TERT promoter mutations were detected. Regarding CNAs, 22q loss was the most frequent (49 tumors [50%]), followed by 1p loss (35 tumors [36%]), 1q gain (14 [14%]), 6q loss (14 [14%]), 10q loss (12 [12%]), 14q loss (24 [25%]), and 18q loss (18 [19%]). Additionally, losses of 6p (five tumors), 10p (six tumors), and 18p (six tumors) were observed, but these were always accompanied by corresponding losses of 6q, 10q, and 18q within the same tumors.

Oncoplot for 97 meningiomas treated with surgical resection followed by stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS). Data are acquired from whole-exome sequencing of resected specimens before SRS. Molecular groups are defined using driver mutations and copy number alterations (CNAs) as follows: Group A, NF2-wildtype without high-risk CNAs; Group B, NF2 mutations and/or CNA of 22q loss without high-risk CNAs; and Group C, presence of high-risk CNAs with NF2 or without other known driver mutations

As a result, 97 tumors were categorized into three molecular groups: 36 tumors in Group A (37%), 13 tumors in Group B (13%), and 48 tumors in Group C (49%). One with TRAF7/AKT1 mutations and 22q loss was classified as A, and one with AKT1 mutations and 1p loss was classified as C. Comparing patient backgrounds among the groups (Table 1), Group C showed a significantly higher median age (68 years; A: 61, B: 51; p < 0.001), larger median tumor volume (11.0 mL; A: 3.7, B: 6.9; p = 0.010), and a higher rate of convexity/falx/parasagittal location (38%; A: 15%, B: 6%; p = 0.002). Additionally, Group C had a higher proportion of grade 2/3 tumors (44%; A: 8%, B: 8%; p = 0.010) and a higher median Ki-67 LI (5%; A: 3%, B: 2%; p < 0.001). Group C tumors were less frequently managed with upfront adjuvant SRS (42%; A: 54%, B: 69%; p = 0.041) and more frequently treated with salvage SRS after progression (58%).

Progression-free survival

Among the 97 tumors, 63 (65%) achieved tumor control at the last follow-up, including 32 with shrinkage (33%) and 31 with no change (32%). Thirty-four tumors (35%) progressed within a median period of 19 months (IQR 11–65 months) after SRS. The cumulative PFS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 89.5%, 74.9%, and 69.8%, respectively (Fig. 2A). For the three molecular groups, PFS rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were as follows: Group A (97.2%, 94.0%, and 88.5%, respectively), Group B (all 100%), and Group C (81.0%, 55.6%, and 49.7%, respectively) (Fig. 2B). No significant difference was observed between Group A and B (p = 0.821); however, Group C had a significantly higher recurrence rate compared with Groups A (p < 0.001) and B (p = 0.002). In the risk analysis of tumor progression after SRS, univariate analysis identified older age, convexity/falx/parasagittal tumor location, larger tumor size, increased prescription dose, higher WHO tumor grade, higher Ki-67 LI, and molecular Group C as potential risk factors (Table 2). Difference between upfront adjuvant and salvage SRS and timing of SRS after resection was not significant in tumor progression. Subsequent multivariable analysis revealed that Group C (odds ratio [OR] 3.99, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.25–12.78; p = 0.020), increased tumor volume (OR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.04; p = 0.004), and higher Ki-67 LI (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.00–1.10; p = 0.032) were significantly associated with tumor progression. When the analysis was limited to the 47 Group C tumors, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year PFS rates for tumors harboring 1q gain were 63.5%, 23.8%, and 15.9%, respectively, significantly lower than those for tumors without 1q gain (88.1%, 68.7%, and 64.6%, respectively; p < 0.001). Among the high-risk CNAs, 1q gain was the only alteration significantly associated with tumor progression (Fig. 3). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis in Group C tumors revealed that 1q gain (OR 3.60, 95% CI 1.47–8.83; p = 0.005) and increased tumor volume (OR 1.03 95% CI 1.01–1.05; p < 0.001) were associated with poorer tumor control while age, prescription dose, WHO grade, and Ki-67 LI were not (Table 3).

Disease-specific survival

Of the 95 patients, 79 (83%) were alive and 16 (17%) had died by the last follow-up. Fifteen patients died from disease-specific mortality within a median of 23 months (IQR 11–62 months), including 14 patients (15%) who died owing to uncontrollable meningioma progression, one patient (1%) who died from postoperative septic shock following salvage surgery for post-SRS progression, and one patient (1%) who died from an unrelated cause. The cumulative DSS at 3, 5, and 10 years were 89.4%, 85.8%, and 80.2%, respectively (Fig. 2C). Comparing the three molecular groups, the 3-, 5-, and 10-year DSS rates were all 100% for Group A and B, and PFS rates for Group C were 79.4%, 73.0%, and 60.4%, respectively (Fig. 2D). Group C had a significantly higher mortality rate compared with Group A (p < 0.001) and B (p = 0.016). In the risk analysis for disease-specific mortality following SRS, univariate analysis revealed that male sex, larger tumor size, increased prescription dose, higher WHO tumor grade, and higher Ki-67 LI were associated with disease-specific mortality (Online Resource 2). Difference between upfront adjuvant and salvage SRS and timing of SRS after resection was not significant in disease-specific death. ORs according to molecular groups were not adequately calculated because Group A/B patients did not experience disease-specific mortality. When the analysis was limited to the 45 Group C patients, the 3-, 5-, and 10-year DSS rates for tumors harboring a 1q gain were 51.3%, 42.7%, and 28.5%, respectively, significantly lower than those for tumors without a 1q gain (90.6%, 85.8%, and 73.6% respectively; p < 0.001). Among the high-risk CNAs, 1q gain was the only alteration significantly associated with disease-specific mortality (Fig. 4). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis in Group C tumors revealed that 1q gain (OR 19.48, 95% CI 2.92–129.93; p = 0.002) and increased tumor volume (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.02–1.07; p < 0.001) were associated with poorer survival, whereas age, sex, prescription dose, WHO grade, and Ki-67 LI were not (Online Resource 3).

Subgroup analysis for WHO grade 1 meningiomas

Among the 74 WHO grade 1 meningiomas, 33 were classified as Group A, 12 as Group B, and 29 as Group C (Online Resource 4). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Online Resource 5. At the last follow-up, 55 (74%) patients achieved tumor control, including 29 with shrinkage (39%) and 26 with no change (26%). Nineteen tumors (26%) progressed within a median period of 39 months (IQR 15–91 months) after SRS. The cumulative 1-, 3-, and 5-year PFS rates were 95.6%, 86.2%, and 84.0%, respectively (Online Resource 6). Comparing the three molecular groups, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year PFS rates were all 97.0% for Group A, all 100% for Group B, and 93.0%, 71.5%, and 67.0%, respectively for Group C (Online Resource 6). Group C had a significantly higher recurrence rate compared with Group A (p < 0.001) and B (p = 0.011). In the analysis of tumor progression following SRS, increased tumor volume (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06; p = 0.006), higher Ki-67 LI (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.17; p = 0.007), and Group C (OR 11.37, 95% CI 2.49–51.99; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with tumor progression (Online Resource 7). In this subgroup, higher prescription dose was significantly associated with favorable tumor control (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.57–0.99; p = 0.044).

Regarding mortality, the cumulative DSS rates at 3, 5, and 10 years were 95.4%, 93.1%, and 86.4%, respectively (Online Resource 6). Comparing the three molecular groups, the 3-, 5-, and 10-year DSS rates for Group A and B were all 100%, and 89.7%, 84.9%, and 67.6%, respectively for Group C (Online Resource 6). Group C had a significantly higher mortality rate than Group A (p = 0.002) and B (p = 0.031). In the risk analysis of disease-specific mortality following SRS, multivariable analysis identified no related factors, and ORs according to molecular groups were not adequately calculated because Group A/B patients did not experience disease-specific mortality (Online Resource 8).

For the 29 patients with grade 1 tumors in Group C, the 1-, 3-, and 5-year PFS rates for tumors harboring 1q gain were 83.3%, 33.3%, and 33.3%, respectively, significantly lower than those for tumors without 1q gain (95.7%, 82.4%, and 76.5%, respectively; p = 0.023) (Online Resource 9). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis in grade 1 and Group C tumors revealed that 1q gain (OR 11.11, 95% CI 1.91–64.62; p = 0.007) and increased tumor volume (OR 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.10; p = 0.001) were associated with poorer tumor control and higher prescription dose was significantly associated with favorable tumor control (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.36–0.80; p = 0.003) (Online Resource 10). Age and Ki-67 LI were not associated with tumor progression. The 3-, 5-, and 10-year DSS rates for tumors harboring 1q gain were 66.7%, 44.4%, and 44.4%, respectively, significantly lower than those for tumors without 1q gain (95.7%, 89.3%, and 74.4%, respectively; p = 0.011) (Online Resource 11). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis in Group C tumors revealed that 1q gain (OR 377.41, 95% CI 4.45–32,011.49; p = 0.009) and increased tumor volume (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.03–1.28; p = 0.028) were associated with poorer tumor control, whereas age, sex, prescription dose, and Ki-67 LI were not (Online Resource 12). Similar to the whole Group C cohort, 1q gain was the only alteration among the high-risk CNAs significantly associated with tumor progression and mortality in grade 1 meningiomas.

Discussion

This study is the first to utilize molecular analysis as a predictive model for SRS outcomes. By simplifying existing multi-omics classifications using WES-derived driver mutations and CNAs, we demonstrated high accuracy in predicting PFS and DSS after SRS. Many studies have investigated prognostic factors for meningiomas treated with SRS, including age, sex, tumor size, location, prior treatments, and WHO grade [20, 21, 37, 39], and Ki-67 LI has recently emerged as a robust predictor of post-SRS prognosis, even within identical WHO grades [41–43]. However, the molecular basis of such heterogeneity has not yet been incorporated into prognostic models for SRS outcomes. In fact, previous molecular investigations into SRS outcomes have been limited. Notably, Damen et al. reported that 1p36 loss identified by fluorescence in situ hybridization was associated with tumor progression in WHO grade I meningiomas treated with Gamma Knife radiosurgery [9]. Nonetheless, molecular-based outcome prediction has remained insufficient, despite the inclusion of genetic alterations in the WHO grading criteria.

Since the identification of non-NF2 driver mutations [7], molecular biological studies on meningiomas have expanded considerably, suggesting various tumorigenesis pathways. Subsequent studies evaluating CNAs, DNA methylation profiles, and RNA expression patterns have resulted in currently accepted multi-omics-based molecular classifications [2, 5, 28, 30, 36]. The three-group framework presented in our study represents a simplified molecular model that builds on existing multi-omics classifications using only WES-derived driver mutations and CNAs and reported its utility in prognosis for the postoperative clinical course of meningiomas [16]. Despite its simplicity, this framework effectively predicted tumor progression and prognosis after SRS, making it highly suitable for clinical application. Notably, although univariate analyses in our study confirmed previously reported recurrence-associated factors such as age, tumor location, and prescription dose [8, 22, 33, 34, 37], multivariable analyses notably identified molecular profiles and tumor size as primary independent predictors of recurrence after SRS. Ongoing efforts have aimed to inform treatment decisions based on postoperative molecular groupings. Moreover, several studies have started to integrate methylation clustering and CNA profiling or gene expression profiling for prognostication in radiotherapy, with an emerging focus on tailoring clinical decisions based on the extent of resection and molecular groups [4, 10, 27, 46]. Our study adds valuable information in the context of SRS-based treatment, presenting a clinically applicable simplification of existing integrated classifications using WES.

Our findings indicate that within the poor prognosis Group C, 1q gain further identifies tumors at significantly increased risk of progression and poor survival, a significance that is also present in histologically grade 1 tumors, emphasizing its potential as a clinically relevant biomarker. Regarding 1q gain, Gabeau-Lacet et al. initially linked 1q gain with shorter PFS in atypical meningiomas [11]. Jansen et al. subsequently validated these findings in an independent cohort, confirming the predictive value of 1q gain for reduced PFS [19]. After developing multi-omics models, Nassiri et al. further categorized meningiomas into four molecular groups based on CNAs and metabolic characteristics, identifying 1q gain as a characteristic CNAs of “the proliferative group”, and Choudhury et al. also showed that 1q amplifications of specific locus were enriched in “Hypermitotic meningiomas”, which showed the poorest prognosis [5, 30]. Youngblood et al. reported a synergistic elevation in recurrence risk associated with NF2 loss, high Ki-67 labeling, and 1q gain [47]. Although cIMPACT-NOW update 8 made limited mention of chromosome 1q gain [35], recent work by Landry et al., using a large multi-institutional cohort, confirmed 1q gain as a marker of poor prognosis, proposing its inclusion as a criterion for WHO grade 3 meningiomas [23]. Although few studies have discussed the association between tumors harboring 1q gain and radioresistance, their previous study also identified 1q gain as an adverse prognostic factor for both post-surgical PFS and post-radiotherapy PFS, suggesting that the present study likewise demonstrated a similar prognostic value of 1q gain from the perspective of SRS. The 1q region harbors several genes, including MDM4, KIF14, and PARP1, which are involved in DNA damage response, cell-cycle regulation, and chromatin remodeling [25, 29]. Dysregulation of these genes has been implicated in tumor progression and may modulate cellular tolerance to radiation-induced stress. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking 1q gain to radiosensitivity.

Our findings extend these observations by showing that such CNAs also correlate with outcomes in the specific setting of SRS. Given that tumor control after SRS can be affected by intrinsic radiosensitivity, the persistence of CNA-associated risk even in this treatment context suggests that these genomic alterations may not only reflect aggressive tumor biology but could also influence radiation response. However, because our study is limited to a cohort of patients treated with SRS, our results should be interpreted as identifying prognostic factors in the post-SRS setting rather than predictive biomarkers of SRS efficacy. To determine whether CNAs act as predictive markers, a more rigorous design would be required; specifically, comparing outcomes between CNA-positive and CNA-negative meningiomas in both SRS-treated and non-SRS-treated cohorts matched for clinical and pathological features. Such a design would allow evaluation of whether the relative benefit of SRS differs according to CNA status, thereby distinguishing prognostic from predictive effects on radiosensitivity. Propensity score–matched analyses or large multicenter studies incorporating both surgical and non-SRS cohorts would be particularly valuable to address this question. Our current findings represent an initial step, highlighting the potential importance of CNA profiling in risk stratification for meningiomas treated with SRS, but further work will be necessary to establish their predictive utility.

This study had a few limitations. First, the single-institution, retrospective design may introduce selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings. The potential bias should be acknowledged that this cohort includes patients previously treated with surgery and subsequently undergoing SRS; patients requiring combined modality treatment have been associated with poorer prognosis [18]. Second, the small sample size may reduce the statistical power of the findings. Third, the incomplete CNA evaluation, particularly the inability to detect loss of heterozygosity, could have influenced the results. Finally, since this simplified classification used information of driver mutations and CNAs, there was a lack of validation using methylation, transcriptomic, and proteomic data. Therefore, it does not directly correspond on a one-to-one basis with any of the existing multi-omics analytical frameworks [5, 28, 30, 32]. These factors highlight the need for further studies to validate this molecular framework and support its clinical application.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that molecular classification using driver mutations and CNAs from WES significantly improves the prediction of PFS and DSS after SRS for meningiomas. This simplified model reliably stratifies tumors by risk, even WHO grade 1, and highlights 1q gain as a key negative prognostic marker. These findings support the integration of molecular diagnostics into clinical practice, facilitating precise, personalized treatment. Future research should validate this simplified framework for broader clinical application.
