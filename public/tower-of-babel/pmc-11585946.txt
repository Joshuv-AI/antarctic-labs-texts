HIV and risk of hypertension: a two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ruo-Wei Zhu, Hui-Yi Guo, Lu-Na Niu, Mi Deng, Xue-Fei Li, Lu Jing
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePrevious studies have shown that human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection is associated with hypertension; however, the results of these studies are affected by a variety of confounding factors. There is no definite evidence to prove a causal relationship between these two factors. This study aimed to investigate the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension.MethodsA two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study was conducted using genome-wide association study (GWAS) statistics published online. The data were collected mainly from the OpenGWAS and FinnGen databases. The HIV database contained 357 HIV patients and 218,435 control patients; the hypertension database contained 54,358 patients and 408,652 control patients; and the blood pressure database contained 436,424 samples. Random effect inverse variance weighting (IVW) was used as the main analysis method, weighted median and Mr-Egger analysis methods were used to ensure the accuracy of the results, and Cochran’s Q test and Mr-Egger regression methods were used to detect heterogeneity and correct multiple horizontal effects. Finally, the leave-one-out method was used to analyse the reliability of the test results. In order to further verify the research results, different databases were used and the same statistical method was used for a replication analysis. In order to prevent false positive results caused by multiple tests, Bonferroni correction is used to correct the statistical results.ResultsAfter screening, a total of 9 SNPs (single-nucleotide polymorphisms) were selected as the instrumental variable (IV) used in this study. The IVW MR analysis results showed a causal relationship between HIV infection and the risk of hypertension (IVW: OR = 1.001, P = 0.03). When systolic blood pressure was the outcome, the IVW method results were positive (OR = 1.004, P = 0.01280), and when diastolic blood pressure was the outcome, the weighted median method results were positive (OR = 1.004, P = 0.04570). According to the sensitivity analysis, the results of this study were unlikely to be affected by heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy. The leave-one-out analysis showed that the results of this study did not change significantly with the elimination of a single SNP. In replication analysis, when diastolic blood pressure was taken as the outcome, the weighted median method was positive (OR = 1.042, P = 0.037). Sensitivity analysis shows that there is heterogeneity, but there is no horizontal pleiotropy. The leave-one-out analysis showed that the results of this study did not change significantly with the elimination of a single SNP.ConclusionAs the first exploratory study using MR method to study the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension and blood pressure, this study found that HIV infection may increase systolic and diastolic blood pressure and increase the risk of hypertension. PLWH, as a high-risk group of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, should prevent the occurrence of hypertension in order to further improve their quality of life. However, this study also has some limitations. The results of the relationship between HIV infection and hypertension and blood pressure may be affected by the lack of statistical efficacy. In order to further confirm this conclusion, more large-scale RCT or genetic studies should be carried out.Keywords: Hypertension, AIDS, Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, Human immunodeficiency virus, Mendelian randomization

Introduction

Hypertension is a cardiovascular syndrome characterized by elevated systemic arterial pressure, which is the main clinical manifestation. It is not only an important risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases such as coronary heart disease, cerebral hemorrhage, and stroke but also an important risk factor for diseases such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease. Hypertension remains a major secondary risk factor for global mortality, causing tens of millions of deaths annually [1]. A data integration analysis revealed that although the blood pressure of people in high-income countries has declined, the prevalence of hypertension continues to increase in low-income and middle-income countries, including China and India, the most populous countries [2]. According to a 2015 study, the awareness, treatment, and control rates in the Chinese hypertensive population were 46.9%, 40.7%, and 15.3%, respectively [3]. Compared with the results of a study conducted in 1991 and 2002, three data points showed larger increases [4, 5]. Both have greatly improved, but the control rate is still far lower than the global average of 23% for women and 18% for men in 2019 [6].

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) is an immunodeficiency disease caused mainly by T-cell immunodeficiency caused by infection with HIV. It was first diagnosed in the United States in 1981. Since its discovery, it has become an important issue affecting human health worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNAIDS, there are an estimated 39.0 million people living with HIV, 0.65 million people dying from HIV-related causes, and 1.5 million people newly infected with HIV by the end of 2022. There is insufficient evidence on the causal relationship between HIV infection and the risk of hypertension; a meta-analysis in 2017 revealed that the prevalence of hypertension in people living with HIV (PLWH) treated with antiretroviral therapy (ART) was greater [7]. A cross-sectional study of 11,101,581 PLWH in 2021 showed that HIV-infected people had a greater risk of hypertension than uninfected people; however, the results of the study showed strong heterogeneity, which may be due to large regional differences [8]. Some studies have also pointed out that the combination of ART is related to an increase in blood pressure, but there are also confounding factors for weight gain [9–11]. Although previous observational studies have shown a correlation between HIV infection and hypertension, this correlation is inevitably affected by various confounding factors. According to the principle of random distribution, genetic variation is not affected by other confounding factors; therefore, Mendelian randomization (MR) can effectively eliminate the influence of confounding factors and avoid bias, reverse causation, and other advantages [12–14]. Currently, MR is widely used to demonstrate causal relationships between other risk factors and hypertension [15–17].

In this study, we applied two-sample MR and summarized genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from the OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) [18] and the FinnGen project (https://www.finngen.fi/en) [19] to reveal the causal effect of HIV infection on hypertension.

Methods

Study design

Using GWAS summary data, this study was conducted using R language two-sample MR analysis to assess the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension, and a sensitivity analysis was conducted to verify the reliability of the results. Mendelian randomization uses SNP as an instrumental variable (IV), and the analysis must meet three assumptions: Assumption 1 is the correlation assumption, IV must be strongly correlated with exposure, Assumption 2 is the exclusive assumption, IV cannot be related to the outcome, Assumption 3 is the independence assumption, and IV has nothing to do with the other confounding factors affecting the outcome [20]. Data sources

All the data used in this MR study are publicly available. The exposure GWAS data for HIV infection were derived from the FinnGen project (finn-b-AB1_HIV) and consisted of 357 patients with HIV and 218,435 controls. The series of studies, launched in 2017, is based on public-private partnerships between Finnish universities, biological banks, hospital districts and a number of international pharmaceutical companies to promote research, implementation and economic development in the field of personalized health care. A total of 500,000 participants have been recruited. Published GWASs of hypertension and blood pressure from the OpenGWAS database (hypertension: ukb-b-12493, systolic blood pressure: ukb-b-20175, and diastolic blood pressure: ukb-b-7992) were used as data sources for outcome events. The above two aggregated data sets, characterized by essential hypertension, systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure, are derived from the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit in the UK Biobank, a large and detailed GWAS database that recruited more than 500,000 participants (aged 40–69) worldwide between 2006 and 2010. In addition, in order to further increase the reliability of the research results, replication analysis was carried out using the data sets from the International Hypertension Federation, characterized by systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure, with ID as ieu-b-38 and ieu-b-39 respectively. All the subjects were European, and people in other areas have not found enough databases to study. Additional information on the exposure and outcome datasets is presented in Table 1.

Selection of instrumental variables

Based on the above three assumptions, we first screened the SNPs strongly related to exposure at the genome-wide significance level (P < 5 × 10− 6), a clumping window of 10,000 kb, and a linkage disequilibrium level (r2 < 0.001). We tried to select suitable SNPs with P < 5 × 10− 8 as the standard, but there were not enough SNPs to conduct the MR analysis. The PhenoScape database was used to determine whether the selected instrumental variables were related to other known confounding factors of hypertension, and a heterogeneity test was performed to eliminate instrumental variables with heterogeneity. By calculating the F statistics, we can determine whether the selected IVs are strongly related to the exposure factors and eliminate IVs with F statistics less than 10 to reduce the bias caused by weak IVs [21]. Statistical analysis

Many methods, such as inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, and MrEgger, have been used to infer the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension, prioritizing the IVW method, weighted median, and Mr. Egger auxiliary support data results. The presence of a causal relationship was inferred from the P and OR values. If the P value is less than 0.05, there is a potential causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension, whereas if the OR is greater than 1, HIV infection will increase the risk of hypertension. When all IVs are valid, the IVW method can yield the most accurate results, and the weighted median can be robust to horizontal pleiotropic bias without the assumption of InSIDE, whereas Mr-Egger requires the assumption of InSIDE to obtain a relatively accurate estimate when the IVs are invalid. These three methods work together to ensure the accuracy of the results of this study [22, 23].

The heterogeneity of the IVW model was assessed using Cochran’s Q test. Cochran’s test (indicated heterogeneity P < 0.05). If heterogeneity was present, the Mr-Presso method was used to detect and eliminate SNPs with heterogeneity. The Mr-Egger regression method was used to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy. When P < 0.05, the data showed horizontal pleiotropy, and the conclusion was unreliable [24, 25]. Leave-one-out analysis was used to test whether a single SNP would have a significant impact on the results, further proving the reliability of the results. In order to prevent the false positive results caused by multiple tests, Bonferroni correction was used to correct the statistical results, Associations with P < 0.01 (0.05/5) were considered statistically significant, whereas associations with P > 0.01 and P < 0.05 were defined as suggestive associations.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0) with the TwoSampleMR package.

Causal effects of HIV infection on hypertension

The IVW results showed that the P value was less than 0.05 (P = 0.03043), and the OR value was greater than 1(OR = 1.001, 95% CI = 1.000-1.002). The weighted median and Mr Egger results had a P value greater than 0.05, but the OR value was the same as that of the IVW method, and the result was greater than 1(Weighted median: OR = 1.001, 95% CI = 0.999–1.002. Mr-Egger: OR = 1.001, 95% CI = 0.999–1.002). Cochran’s Q test revealed no heterogeneity among the IVs (P > 0.05). Using Mr Egger regression to test horizontal pleiotropy, the results showed P = 0.68716, and there was no horizontal pleiotropy. Leave-one-out analysis revealed that the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension was not affected by a single SNP.

Causal effects of HIV infection on blood pressure

Causal effects of HIV infection on systolic blood pressure

The results of the MR analysis with systolic blood pressure as the outcome were similar to those of the previous analysis. The results of the IVW method showed that the P value was less than 0.05 (P = 0.01280). Although the P value of the other two methods was greater than 0.05, the OR value was similar to that of the IVW method, both of which were greater than 1(Weighted median: OR = 1.002, 95% CI = 0.998–1.006. Mr-Egger: OR = 1.003, 95% CI = 0.997–1.008), and there was no heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy. When using supplementary data sets for replication analysis, the P values of the three methods are all greater than 0.05, and the OR values are all greater than 1. (IVW: OR = 1.099, 95% CI = 0.981–1.231. Weighted median: OR = 1.036, 95% CI = 0.967–1.109. Mr-Egger: OR = 1.074, 95% CI = 0.878–1.312). The results of sensitivity analysis show that although there is heterogeneity in the data, there is no horizontal multiplicity, and the results of leave one out analysis of the two data sets also show that causality does not affect with the elimination of either SNP.

Causal effects of HIV infection on diastolic blood pressure

The results of MR with diastolic blood pressure as the outcome showed that the P value of the weighted median method was less than 0.05 (P = 0.04570), while the P value of the other two methods was greater than 0.05; however, the OR values were similar, and both were greater than 1(IVW: OR = 1.003, 95% CI = 1.000-1.008. Mr-Egger: OR = 1.004, 95% CI = 0.998–1.012). Although the Cochran’s Q test showed heterogeneity, the MR-presso method was used to eliminate heterogeneity, which did not affect the reliability of the results (P = 0.0955). The results of replication analysis using supplementary data sets show that the P value of weighted median method is less than 0.05 (P = 0.037), and the OR values of the three methods are all greater than 1. (IVW: OR = 1.052, 95% CI = 0.987–1.122. Weighted median: OR = 1.042, 95% CI = 0.999–1.087. Mr-Egger: OR = 1.049, 95% CI = 0.936–1.176). The results of sensitivity analysis show that although there is heterogeneity in the data, there is no horizontal multiplicity, and the results of leave one out analysis of the two data sets also show that causality does not affect with the elimination of either SNP.

Discussion

Through MR analysis, the potential causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension and blood pressure was analyzed for the first time. Although the statistical results of each method did not exceed the P value threshold corrected by Bonferroni, it was still within the range of suggestive associations, and the direction of effect was consistent across MR methods, so the study suggested that HIV infection may increase the systolic and diastolic blood pressure of patients and increase the risk of hypertension. Heterogeneity test and sensitivity analysis showed that heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy did not affect the results. The results of remaining analysis showed that the causal relationship between HIV infection and blood pressure was not affected by any single SNP. Although the repetitive analysis results only show that there is a causal relationship between HIV infection and diastolic blood pressure increase, but considering that the SNP detected in the database is less than that in other databases, and the sensitivity analysis also shows some heterogeneity, the results may be biased. In addition, the results of this study also need to be carefully explained because of the small sample size of patients with HIV infection in the database, the statistical efficacy will be affected, but as an exploratory study, the purpose of this study is to find the potential causal relationship between exposure and outcome, and to provide direction for follow-up research.

With the continuous improvement of current strategies for the prevention and treatment of HIV infection and the widespread use of antiretroviral therapy (ART), preexposure prophylaxis (PrEP), and other methods, the prevalence of HIV has obviously decreased, and the survival rate of patients has greatly improved [26–30]. This progress has made the life expectancy of PLWH close to that of healthy people [31–33]. One study revealed that middle-aged HIV-positive patients were effectively inhibited after receiving free ART for one year, with an estimated average life expectancy of 80 years [34]. This change indicates that an increasing number of elderly people are affected by HIV [35]. Some studies have shown that by 2030, the overall proportion of PLWH aged over 50 years will increase from 28% in 2010 to 73% [36]. This also means that the probability of PLWH suffering from cardiovascular disease (CVD) and other geriatric diseases has increased. At present, research reports have pointed out that the risk of CVD in HIV-infected people suffering from CVD is 1.3-2 times greater than that in HIV-negative people [37, 38]. There is evidence that a large number of patients suffer from hypertension [7, 39]. Hypertension is an important risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and PLWH are a high-risk group for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Understanding the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension is helpful for preventing the occurrence of disease in advance and further improving the quality of life of this population.

However, the mechanisms underlying hypertension caused by HIV infection are not completely understood. After infection, blood pressure can be affected in many ways, such as by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), thus enhancing virus replication [40]. Activation of the RAAS plays an important role in increasing blood pressure. In addition, HIV infection changes the morphology, metabolism, and distribution of adipose tissue [41], which leads to metabolic syndrome in patients and increases the risk of hypertension. HIV infection can also activate the immune response, increase lymphocyte subsets and cytokines, cause vascular and endothelial remodelling, lead to increased blood pressure [42], and produce proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), thus inducing endothelial dysfunction and hypertension [43, 44]. Furthermore, HIV preferentially infects CD4 + T cells in gut-associated lymphoid tissues, which leads to mucosal destruction and the entry of microorganisms into the systemic circulation, and the heterotopia of lipopolysaccharide and soluble CD14 has been proven to be related to the occurrence of hypertension [45–48].

There is also a high prevalence of hypertension among PLWH treated with ART. In the HIV-HY study, the prevalence of prehypertension was 41.6%, of which only two-thirds were aware and one-third were control [49]. In a study of PLWH and metabolic syndrome patients, the prevalence of hypertension was 96% [50]. ART-mediated therapy can cause vascular endothelial damage. Moreover, although ART significantly inhibited viral replication, viral replication did not disappear completely. Long-term low-level replication of the virus leads to long-term microinflammation, a damaged immune system, and activated inflammatory pathway-induced oxidative stress, leading to endothelial dysfunction and induced hypertension [51, 52]. In addition, it has been reported that an increase in CD4 + cell counts is related to an increase in blood pressure [53]. In the general population, CD4 + cells are equally important in the pathophysiology of hypertension and can produce immunosuppressive cytokines to inhibit immune response activation. After HIV infection, the CD4 + cell count decreases, and a low CD4 + cell count is associated with a high risk of hypertension in PLWH [54–56]. After ART treatment, CD4 + T cells begin to proliferate [7], but some studies have speculated that increased CD4 + T cells still have functional defects and an insufficient ability to produce immunosuppressive factors, such as low levels of transforming growth factor (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), leading to abnormal blood pressure [53]. Evidence of decreased taste in patients with HIV infection has been reported [57], and salt sensitivity may be abnormal owing to HIV infection or ART treatment [58], thus affecting salt intake. A high-salt diet is also a risk factor for hypertension and cardiovascular diseases and indirectly increases the risk of hypertension. In addition, traditional antihypertensive drugs can also affect salt sensitivity [59, 60], such as the calcium channel blocker amlodipine, the angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor captopril and the angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB) candesartan, losartan and valsartan [61–64].

Our research has several advantages. In recent years, no related MR studies have explored the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension. We are the first to use the double sample MR method to confirm the potential causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension. Therefore, our study provides new information on the correlation between HIV and hypertension. HIV infection may increase systolic and diastolic blood pressure in patients and increase the risk of hypertension, and we summarize the possible underlying mechanisms. Secondly, there have been many observational studies on the relationship between HIV infection and hypertension. It is believed that there is a correlation between HIV infection and hypertension. However, the previous observational research is more affected by confounding factors and has a certain bias. Mendelian randomization research method can effectively avoid confounding factors and reverse causality.

However, this study also has some limitations. The GWAS population used in this study is European, and whether the results of other populations are consistent needs further study. In addition, affected by the small sample size of HIV patients in the database and other factors, the statistical efficacy of this study is insufficient, which will have some impact on the reliability of the results.

Conclusion

This is the first two sample MR study to explore the causal relationship between HIV infection and hypertension. Our MR analysis results show that HIV infection may increase systolic and diastolic blood pressure, increase the risk of hypertension, and the results are not affected by heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy. However, due to the sample size of HIV patients, the statistical efficacy of this study is insufficient, for a progress to confirm this conclusion, the follow-up can carry out larger-scale RCT research or genetic research in this direction.
