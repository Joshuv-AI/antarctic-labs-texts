Swimming exercise training (SET) and MitoQ supplementation can mitigate the atrophy and inflammation process in skeletal muscles of male Wistar rats with spinal cord injury (SCI): NF-κB/MuRF-1/proteasome pathway
Authors: Zahra Behroozi, Beydolah Shahouzehi, Daruosh Moflehi, Mahdiyeh Moradi, Fatemeh Rastegari Nasab Ravari, Mahdiye Lashkari-Zadeh, Maryam Hadadi, Mohammad Mojtaba Farazi, Soheil Aminizadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the present study, we investigated the effects of a rehabilitation period with MitoQ supplement and swimming exercise training (SET) on atrophy and the inflammation process in the skeletal muscles of male rats with spinal cord injury (SCI). In the present study, rats were assigned to five groups (n = 7): Sham, SCI, SCI + SET, SCI + MitoQ (250 μM), and SCI + SET + MitoQ. In the training groups, the rats underwent swimming training for 4 weeks, 5 days/week. Motor function was assessed via the Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) locomotor scale method. The diameter of the skeletal muscle cells, along with the expression of the genes (NF-κB, MuRF-1, proteasome, and myostatin) and proteins (NF-κB, proteasome, and myostatin) were measured using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, real-time PCR (2−ΔΔCT), and Western blot analysis, respectively. Rsults: After 4 weeks, the BBB score in the SCI + MitoQ and SCI + SET + MitoQ groups did not return to normal levels compared to the sham group. MitoQ helped improve the motor function score compared to the SCI group (p < 0.0001). In skeletal muscles, performing SET significantly decreased proteasome (p < 0.0001) and myostatin (p = 0.0003) proteins, as well as myostatin and NF-kB gene expression compared to the SCI group (p = 0.02). SET, MitoQ, and the combination of treatments helped improve motor function and reduce muscle atrophy. Although MitoQ's antioxidant effect on reducing atrophy was not as strong as the combined treatment, it still led to better improvements in motor function.Keywords: Swimming, Skeletal muscle, Spinal cord injuries, Atrophy

Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) often causes a variety of problems, including sensory, motor, and autonomic nerve impairments, and it is a significant health issue around the world [1]. The pathological process of SCI is complicated and can be divided into two parts. The first part is the initial injury, which is a direct damage to the spinal cord tissue leading to issues such as demyelination and cell death, including apoptosis. The second part, which involves many different body processes, includes local bleeding, ionic imbalance, free radical stress, and uncontrolled inflammation, which can lead to further cell damage [2], and the formation of inhibitory barriers that stop nerve signals. These processes can also induce nerve pain (NP) [3].

Damage to the neuromuscular system is a key feature of SCI [4], resulting in muscle atrophy, particularly in skeletal muscles. It leads to reduced muscle mass and strength, impeding motor function recovery [5]. After SCI, the muscle's cross-sectional area decreased by about 18 to 24% [6]. Even if the nerves and muscles reconnect, the damage can be permanent [7]. Therefore, maintaining the integrity of skeletal muscles is necessary for homeostasis and systemic metabolism. Research on the prevention and treatment of muscle atrophy after SCI is especially important for such patients [7]. Understanding how spinal cord injury affects the body at a molecular level is key to creating better treatment options.

Myostatin is a key part of the TGFβ family and a potent negative regulator of skeletal muscle mass [8]. Its expression is frequently upregulated in atrophic conditions following SCI, where the inhibition of the PI3k-AKT pathway may increase its activity [9]. While myostatin inhibition has shown promise in attenuating muscle loss in various disuse models, its efficacy in the context of complete denervation, such as in severe SCI, can be more limited. In such cases, the overwhelming activation of alternative proteolytic pathways (e.g., the ubiquitin-proteasome system) may dominate the atrophic process. Supporting this, in a recent study, pharmacologic myostatin inhibition failed to induce hypertrophy in denervated muscles of male mice, despite being effective in innervated controls [10,11].

Another molecular pathway involved in atrophy is the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which is the primary regulator of protein breakdown. This system provides a specific mechanism for the selective degradation of structural and regulatory proteins. This destructive system involves key enzymes, including the two inducible E3 ubiquitin ligases atrogin-1 and muscle RING finger protein 1 (MuRF1), which are major players in breaking down skeletal muscles during different types of muscle atrophy. The removal of these two factors from cells can lead to a decrease in muscle atrophy induced by the denervation and suspension of the lower limb [12]. Research shows that NF-κB activation can lead to the expression of ubiquitin proteasome system proteins, which leads to protein degradation [13]. NF-κB is one of the most important signaling pathways associated with muscle mass reduction in various pathological and physiological conditions. Its activity in skeletal muscles can lead to the destruction of specific muscle proteins, which ultimately leads to inflammation and fibrosis [14].

MitoQ is an oral derivative of the antioxidant ubiquinone. It has a specific part called triphenylphosphonium (TPP), a lipophilic cation. This part helps MitoQ move quickly into the membrane of mitochondria and build up inside them. In the mitochondrial matrix, MitoQ is continuously reduced to its active form by the respiratory chain, thereby protecting mitochondrial proteins and DNA against oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage [15]. Due to the characteristic of selective accumulation and continuous recycling in the mitochondria, the function of MitoQ has generated much interest; MitoQ has been shown in vitro and in vivo to have sound protective effects against many pathologies associated with oxidative stress, including ischemia-perfusion injury [16]. Importantly, clinical trials have shown that MitoQ has no severe side effects [17].

The effect of this specific mitochondrial antioxidant on various aspects of CNS and peripheral nervous system damage has also been investigated [18]. MitoQ plays a neuroprotective role in neurological disease, especially by reducing intracerebral bleeding [19] and improving neurological pain [20]. MitoQ can preserve spinal cord tissue after SCI by increasing angiogenesis, inhibiting inflammation and apoptosis, reducing oxidative stress, and improving mitochondrial function [21]. Studies have suggested that balancing mitochondrial function could be a potential treatment for oxidative disorders that occur following neuroinflammation after induction of SCI [22,23].

Research shows that exercise training, such as swimming in cold water, improves performance and moderates spinal pain after brachial plexus avulsion in rats [24]. The many beneficial effects of swimming training can also be due to the reduction of Caspase3 expression and the inhibition of apoptosis [25]. Exercise effectively reduces neuropathic pain by reducing inflammatory factors and improving GABA neurotransmitter levels [26].

Overall, research shows that antioxidant supplements change exercise adaptations, but whether MitoQ is effective on muscle atrophy caused by SCI has not yet been investigated. Therefore, in this research, for the first time, we will explored the role of MitoQ supplement alone and together with swimming exercise on muscle atrophy pathways and pathological indicators of skeletal muscle tissue after SCI.

Methods

Animals

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kerman University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.KMU.AEC.1402.020). All experiments were performed according to the guidelines and regulations for using experimental animals. The animal experiments reported follow the recommendations in the ARRIVE guideline. In this experimental study, 35 male Wistar rats (200 ± 10 g, 8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Breeding and Reproduction Center of Kerman University of Medical Sciences. During the entire research period, the animals were kept in cages under standard laboratory conditions, including food and water ad libitum, air temperature of 22 to 24 °C, relative humidity of 55 to 60 percent, and 12-h light/dark cycle. Behavioral tests were done from 10 a.m. until 3 p.m. to ensure homogeneous circadian rhythm.

SCI induction

The BBB test was carried out one week before the injury and then weekly for 4 weeks following the injury. The animals were anesthetized using intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Then, the skin of the back area was shaved and disinfected with betadine. After a midline incision, the skin and superficial muscles were retracted to perform a laminectomy at the T11-T12 level, exposing the spinal cord. A severe SCI was then induced by bilaterally applying an aneurysm clip (model RS6474; closing force: 35–45 g) for 120 s at the junction of the T11 and T12 vertebrae without damaging the dura mater. The muscles and skin were then separately sutured (0.3 suture thread) [27]. Tetracycline spray was used to prevent infection at the injury site, and 70% alcohol was sprayed in the animal cage every day until the wound healed. In addition, the urinary bladders of animals were manually emptied twice a day until they were able to control urination [28]. Before starting the protocol, the rats were randomly divided into two main groups: sham and spinal cord injury. The rats in the sham group only underwent laminectomy. However, in the spinal cord injury group, after confirming SCI with the BBB test, the rats were randomly divided into subgroups. Therefore, the animals were randomly divided into five groups (n = 7): 1- Sham (received only laminectomy), 2- SCI (laminectomy + spinal cord compression at T11-T12 using a clip), 3- MitoQ (animals with SCI that received MitoQ supplement), 4- SET (animals with SCI that received swimming exercise training) and 5- swimming and MitoQ group (SCI + SET + MitoQ) (Fig. 1.).

Time line of the research study.

Swimming exercise training protocol

Swimming training was performed in a cylindrical pool 200 cm in diameter, 60 cm in height, and 25 cm in water depth. During the familiarization phase, the rats were first supported by handlers to slowly become familiar with the aquatic environment. However, before the spinal cord injury occurred, the training groups became familiar with the pool environment at different depths. After familiarization with the water environment, the animals were subjected to swimming exercises for 4 weeks, five sessions per week, each session for 30 min in six 5-min stages (swimming training was done individually). The water temperature for swimming training was 31 ± 2 °C [29].

MitoQ supplementation protocol

During the present study, the animals received MitoQ supplement (MitoQ Company, New Zealand) for 4 weeks in the form of a solution in the drinking water with a dose of 250 μM [30]. The concentration of MitoQ was measured in the whole lysate tissues by HPLC-MS to assess the targeting of MitoQ in muscle tissue. All standards and reagents used were of analytical-grade purity (<95%) unless otherwise specified. MeOH and ACN (LC-MS grade) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), and formic acid (FA) 99% was acquired from Fluka (Buchs, Switzerland). Triphenylphosphine hydrochloride (ΤPP), ascorbic acid, and citric acid were acquired from Sigma Aldrich (Steinheim, German). A TSQ Quantum Access triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source was connected to an Accela UHPLC system (Thermo Electron Corporation, San Jose, CA, USA) for the determination of TPP and TPP-HT. An Atlantis T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 3 μm) and an Acquity BΕH column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) from Waters (Milford, MA, USA), equipped with a guard column, were evaluated for use in the chromatographic analysis, and the compounds’ ionization was performed in positive mode (+ESI). The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C throughout the analysis, and the injection volume was set at 10 μL. The mobile phase consisted of (A) MeOH and (B) 0.01% FA. The elution program was gradient, starting with 10% solvent A and remaining constant for 1 min. It was then gradually increased to 100% in 9 min and held constant for 3 min. The initial conditions were restored within 0.1 min, and the column was re-equilibrated for 2 min before the next injection. The flow rate was set to 0.1 mL min−1, and the total chromatographic time for each injection was 15 min. Thermo Fisher Scientific Xcalibur software, version 2.3 (Waltham, MA, USA), was used for instrument control and data acquisition [31]. For HPLC-MS calibration, we used the MitoQ internal standard (MRC Mitochondrial Biology Unit and Department of Medicine, University of Cambridge), and the tissue levels of MitoQ in the Sham and MitoQ groups were 0 and 4.23 ± 0.76 pmoles/100 mg protein, respectively [32].

Tissue extraction

After the 4-week implementation protocol of the study, the animals in the five groups were anesthetized using ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) [33]. Then, the medial head of the gastrocnemius muscle tissue of the animals (right leg) was extracted and stored at −70 °C for molecular assessment, and the skeletal muscle of left leg was extracted and preserved in formalin (10%) for histopathology. A CO2 fill rate of 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute was used for euthanizing the rats [33].

Real-time PCR

RNA extraction was performed according to the Synaclon kit (RNA extraction kit-EX6051) protocol. To evaluate gene expression, 50 μg of soleus skeletal muscle tissue was removed. The tissues were powdered by mortar. To remove the protein components, the resulting product was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and mixed with Buffer A at a ratio of 0.5 to 1 and shaken vigorously for 15 s. The product was centrifuged at 12000g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the mineral and water parts were separated. The part containing RNA was removed and mixed with the washing buffer at a ratio of 1 to 0.5 and then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The RNA-containing pellet was washed in ethanol and dissolved in 20 μL of RNAS-free water. RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and the ratio of 260 to 280 between 1.8 and 2 was used as the criterion for optimal purity. cDNA synthesis (cDNA synthesis kit, Parstous, A101162) was done using 1 μg of total RNA using random hexamer primers and M-MLV reverse transcriptase. Real-timePCR was performed using the 15 μL of Master Mix SYBR Green (Ampiqon, Denmark) and 100 ng of cDNA. 18S was used as a control gene. The real-time PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min (repetition of 40 cycles). The effectiveness of the research primers was validated prior to the main experiments. Briefly, for each gene, primer efficiency was determined using a standard curve generated from serial dilutions of cDNA, and specificity was confirmed by a single peak in the melting curve analysis. The efficiency for all primer sets was between 95% and 105%, with correlation coefficients (R2) > 0.98. The expression level of the target genes was then measured using the 2–ΔΔCT method (Table 1) [30].

Western blotting

The Western blot method was used to quantify proteasome, myostatin, and NF-kB protein levels. About 40 mg of each sample was used for Western blotting. Initially, skeletal muscle tissue was homogenized using cold RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, and protease inhibitor [AEBSF (4 mM), aprotinin (2 μg/mL), leupeptin (1 μg/mL), pepstatin A (1 μg/mL), and EDTA (5 mM)]) on ice. The homogenized tissue was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min. The Bradford method was used to measure total protein concentration in muscle supernatant samples. The supernatant was incubated with sample buffer (0.125 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 10% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.004% bromophenol blue) at 95 °C for 5 min. Then, the sample was loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and subjected to electrophoresis. Following electrophoresis, the separated proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane (200 mA for 1 h). Subsequently, the blocking process and antigen-antibody reaction were carried out using specific antibodies for the target proteins (NF-kB: Santa Cruz-8008, 1:1000; Proteasome: Santa Cruz-48367, 1:500; Myostatin: Santa Cruz-134345, 1:1000; and β-actin: Santa Cruz-517582, 1:300). Finally, the antibody-antigen complex was visualized on radiographic film using the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) system, followed by developing and proofing solutions. The band density was quantified using Lab Works software. In the final step, the PVDF membrane was incubated in stripping solution at 55 °C for 30 min, followed by washing with TBS and reblocking for β-actin detection [34].

Histopathological study

The skeletal muscles were removed, washed with saline, fixed with 10% buffered formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Slides were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined microscopically by two pathologists blinded to the animal groupings. Muscle fiber width was assessed in longitudinal sections. For each fiber, three measurements were taken along the long axis, and the mean value was calculated. A digital microscope (OBN 132T241) was used to measure the longitudinal sections of muscle fibers for assessing the atrophy in different groups (ten fibers per histopathologic slide) [35].

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SD. The data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad v.8.4.3., San Diego, LLC, USA). The normality of Data were analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA (for BBB scores) and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test were used to compare means among groups. If the data were nonparametric, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of SET and MitoQ on motor function (BBB score)

The Basso, Beattie, and Bresnahan (BBB) scale was used until 4 weeks after SCI. Significant differences in BBB scores between the SCI + MitoQ and SCI + SET + MitoQ groups (mean ± SD: 10 ± 2.96; p = 0.0002 and 10.41 ± 2.87; p = 0.0006, respectively) compared with the SCI group (5 ± 2.5) were observed from week 3 and persisted through week 4 (MitoQ: 11.083 ± 2.69; p = 0.001, SCI + SET + MitoQ 11.41 ± 1.96; p = 0.003 and SCI: 6.25 ± 1.76). After 4 weeks, the BBB score in the SCI + MitoQ and SCI + SET + MitoQ groups could not reach normal levels and there was significant different between the treated and sham groups in all weeks (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2).

Effects of SET and MitoQ on histopathology and muscle cell diameter

The muscle cell diameter in the SCI group decreased significantly over 4 weeks compared to the sham group (p < 0.001). However, the group that received the MitoQ supplement showed a significantly lower reduction in diameter than the SCI group (p < 0.001). Also, muscle fiber diameter in the combined group (SCI + ET + MitoQ) was significantly lower than in the SCI + MitoQ group (p < 0.001, F = 102.9) (Fig. 3).

Effects of SET and MitoQ on the atrophy signaling pathway

In the SCI + SET + MitoQ and SCI + MitoQ groups, the gene expression of MuRF-1 decreased significantly compared with the SCI group (p = 0.004, F = 5.384) (Fig. 4). The protein expression level of NF-κB significantly decreased in the SCI + SET + MitoQ group compared to the SCI group (p = 0.002, F = 12.49) (Fig. 4). In addition, the gene expression of NF-κB in the SCI + SET (p < 0.0001), SCI + MitoQ (p < 0.0001), and SCI + SET + MitoQ (p < 0.0001) groups significantly decreased compared to the SCI group. In the SCI group, protein and gene expression of proteasome and myostatin increased significantly compared to the sham group (p < 0.01). Swimming exercise training alone led to a significant decrease in the protein expression of proteasome (p = 0.003, F = 12.25), but MitoQ supplement alone did not affect this protein's expression (p = 0.41). Interestingly, the combination of swimming training and MitoQ supplement reduced the protein expression of proteasome (p = 0.001) (Fig. 5). Training, supplementation, and the combination of both reduced the amount of proteasome gene expression in the intervention groups significantly (p = 0.004, p = 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, respectively). The myostatin gene and protein expression levels in the training groups and MitoQ groups significantly decreased compared to the SCI group (p < 0.05, F = 24.03) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

SCI results in traumatic damage to spinal cord tracts, leading to disrupted motor output and profound skeletal muscle atrophy [36]. Our study investigated two therapeutic strategies—swimming exercise training (SET) and the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant MitoQ—to counteract this atrophy.

The key findings reveal a dissociation. Both interventions, alone and combined, improved locomotor function and attenuated molecular atrophy pathways compared to untreated SCI. Although the functional benefit was primarily driven by MitoQ, the most comprehensive suppression of muscle catabolic markers was achieved by the combined treatment. This suggests distinct yet complementary mechanisms of action.

At the molecular level, SCI triggered a coordinated upregulation of key effectors of muscle proteolysis, including NF-κB, MuRF-1, proteasome, and myostatin [6,12,14]. The significant reduction in these markers following SET underscores the potent anti-catabolic effect of physical activity. This likely involves exercise-induced suppression of inflammatory signaling (NF-κB) and its downstream ubiquitin-proteasome targets (MuRF-1/proteasome) [13,37], coupled with a downregulation of the potent growth inhibitor myostatin [8,10]. These adaptations collectively shift muscle homeostasis away from breakdown. Notably, MitoQ supplementation also attenuated several of these pathways, implicating mitochondrial oxidative stress as a significant driver of the atrophic signaling cascade after SCI [21,22].

This is particularly relevant given that maintaining motor function is a primary goal in rehabilitating SCI patients, and the downregulation of miR-124-3p and concurrent upregulation of BDNF suggest a potential regulatory role for miR-124-3p in modulating BDNF expression after SCI [38]. The neuroprotective effects of MitoQ, combined with the physiological benefits of exercise, highlight the potential for these interventions to promote recovery and enhance quality of life for individuals with SCI [39].The most intriguing finding is the dissociation between functional recovery and molecular improvements. MitoQ supplementation restored locomotor scores (BBB) to the same extent as the full combination therapy (SET + MitoQ), despite the latter causing a more robust decrease in proteasome and myostatin levels. This strongly suggests that the primary mechanism for improved motor function in our model was not the reversal of peripheral muscle atrophy per se, but rather MitoQ's well-established neuroprotective effects on the injured spinal cord [19,21]. By reducing central oxidative damage and inflammation, MitoQ may preserve white matter integrity and enhance residual neural transmission, thereby improving output to the muscles [21,39]. Furthermore, given the centrality of denervation in this model, the benefits may also involve protection of neural structures or modulation of the nerve-muscle interface, beyond direct effects on muscle catabolism. In contrast, SET's major contribution appears to be direct anti-atrophic action on the muscle itself, which, while significant at the molecular level, did not translate into increased functional gains in this paradigm. The combination therapy thus represents a multi-target strategy addressing both central (neuroprotection with MitoQ) and peripheral (anti-catabolic with SET) pathologies of SCI.

The histomorphometric analysis partially supports this mechanistic separation. While SET alone did not significantly restore muscle fiber diameter, MitoQ conferred a measurable protective effect against atrophy. This aligns with the theory that reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress is crucial for preserving muscle mass post-denervation [40,41]. The additive effect of combining SET with MitoQ on fiber size suggests that targeting both exercise-induced adaptation and mitochondrial health yields the optimal structural outcome.

The lack of significant improvement in the BBB score during the first post-injury week in our treated groups is an important observation. This likely reflects the formidable challenge of modulating the acute inflammatory cascade within the initial critical phase of SCI. During this period, rapid activation of inflammasome complexes (e.g., NLRP1, NLRP3) at the injury epicenter drives a potent release of IL-1β and IL-18, establishing a highly destructive local microenvironment that severely limits early neuroprotection and functional recovery [42,43]. The cited studies provide a crucial context: significant early functional recovery, as reported by Mohamadi et al. (2019), was achieved through the direct intrathecal delivery of mesenchymal stem cells, an intervention capable of potently and rapidly suppressing the NLRP1 inflammasome and its downstream cytokines at the injury site within 1 week [42]. Similarly, Khazaei et al. (2025) demonstrated that systemic anti-inflammatory treatment could reduce remote inflammatory consequences, though its primary functional assessment extended over a longer timeline [43]. In contrast, our interventions, systemic MitoQ supplementation and swimming exercise, likely do not exert a sufficiently rapid or direct inhibitory effect on this focal inflammasome-mediated burst. Their primary mechanisms, such as reducing mitochondrial oxidative stress and modulating chronic inflammatory tone, may require more time to manifest measurable functional benefits against the backdrop of acute pathophysiology. Therefore, the significant functional recovery observed from the third week onward in our study highlights the efficacy of our strategies in influencing the sub-acute and chronic phases of SCI recovery, rather than overriding the acute inflammasome-driven cascade. This distinction underscores a key principle in SCI therapeutic strategy: interventions targeting the immediate, intense focal inflammation may require direct or highly potent delivery to the injury site, whereas rehabilitative and mitochondrial-supportive approaches like ours may be pivotal for sustaining and enhancing recovery beyond the initial phase.

Our study has several limitations that can inform future research. The 4-week intervention period may not capture long-term adaptations. Furthermore, our molecular analysis was focused on the NF-κB/proteasome axis; while gene expression changes were assessed, the lack of protein-level data for key regulators such as AMPK and MuRF-1 limits a full understanding of their role in the observed effects. Mechanistically, while we propose distinct central vs. peripheral sites of action, this study did not include direct measures of spinal cord tissue preservation or motor neuron survival to confirm MitoQ's neuroprotective role. Moreover, the specific intracellular sensors linking mitochondrial ROS to the activation of atrophy pathways in denervated muscle remain to be fully elucidated. Another limitation of the present study was the longitudinal sections used to assess atrophy or hypertrophy. It would be better to use transverse sections to measure this factor. Future studies employing conditional knockout models, comprehensive proteomic analyses, or spinal cord-specific assessments are needed to definitively dissect these central and peripheral mechanisms.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that swimming exercise and MitoQ supplementation reduce the consequences of SCI through distinct and complementary primary mechanisms. MitoQ's strength lies in promoting functional recovery, likely via central neuroprotection, whereas SET excels at suppressing the molecular drivers of muscle catabolism. Their combination provides a comprehensive, multi-targeted approach, simultaneously supporting the neural output and the muscular effector organ. This strategy yields the most robust attenuation of molecular atrophy markers and structural benefits, though its functional outcome aligns with that achieved by MitoQ alone. This mechanistic insight refines our understanding of post-SCI rehabilitation and highlights the potential of integrated, multi-modal therapies.
