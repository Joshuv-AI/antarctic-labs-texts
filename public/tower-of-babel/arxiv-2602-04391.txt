Beyond Rejection Sampling: Trajectory Fusion for Scaling Mathematical Reasoning
Jie Deng, Hanshuang Tong, Jun Li, Shining Liang, Ning Wu, Hongzhi Li, Yutao Xie

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have made impressive strides in mathematical reasoning, often fine-tuned using rejection sampling, which retains only correct reasoning trajectories. While effective, this paradigm treats supervision as a binary filter that systematically excludes teacher-generated errors, leaving a gap in how reasoning failures are modeled during training. In this paper, we propose TrajFusion , a fine-tuning strategy that reframes rejection sampling as a structured supervision construction process. Specifically, TrajFusion forms fused trajectories that explicitly model trial-and-error reasoning by interleaving selected incorrect trajectories with reflection prompts and correct trajectories. The length of the fused sample is adaptively controlled based on the frequency and diversity of teacher errors, providing richer supervision for challenging problems while safely reducing to vanilla rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) when error signals are uninformative. TrajFusion requires no changes to the architecture or training objective. Extensive experiments across multiple math benchmarks demonstrate that TrajFusion consistently outperforms RFT, particularly on challenging and long-form reasoning problems.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have made significant progress in mathematical reasoning, facilitated by techniques such as chain-of-thought prompting
30
and supervised fine-tuning on high-quality solution trajectories
4
;
35
.
When combined with strong teacher models, these approaches enable LLMs to acquire increasingly sophisticated problem-solving behaviors and deliver exceptional accuracy across diverse mathematical benchmarks.

A widely adopted method for constructing supervised reasoning data is
rejection sampling

37
. In this approach, a teacher model generates multiple candidate trajectories for a given problem, retaining only those with correct final answers. Rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) then performs supervised training on this filtered set of correct trajectories. RFT is appealing due to its simplicity, requiring no human annotation, and its demonstrated ability to improve model performance across a wide range of mathematical tasks
36
;
29
;
31
. Conceptually, RFT treats supervision as a binary filtering process, where correctness alone determines whether a trajectory contributes to learning.

However, this paradigm has a fundamental limitation: it systematically discards all
teacher-generated incorrect trajectories, despite the fact that such solutions contain
valuable diagnostic information about reasoning failures. Incorrect trajectories reveal
common computational slips, missing assumptions, misapplied formulas, and logically
plausible yet invalid argument paths. These failure patterns are not noise; rather, they
provide informative supervision that can help models recognize and avoid common reasoning
errors, especially on long or ambiguous problems where distinguishing valid from flawed
reasoning is essential
1
;
21
. This leads to our central insight:

teacher-generated errors should not be discarded, but incorporated as
structured supervision.

Building on this insight, we propose
TrajFusion
, a simple yet effective fine-tuning framework that extends RFT by incorporating selected incorrect reasoning trajectories into the supervision signal.
For each problem, TrajFusion constructs a single training sample by interleaving a selected subset of incorrect trajectories with the correct one, linked by brief reflection prompts.
This approach explicitly simulates a trial-and-error reasoning process, exposing the model to both typical failure modes and their resolutions.
The selection in TrajFusion is fully adaptive. When a problem yields diverse or informative errors, more incorrect trajectories are incorporated to enrich supervision; when error signals are sparse or uninformative, TrajFusion provably degenerates to vanilla RFT.
In our method, error diversity is treated as an explicit control signal rather than an implicit assumption, which allows the method to behave robustly across problems of varying difficulty.
Importantly, TrajFusion requires no additional sampling budget, no auxiliary models or critique generators, no architectural modifications, and no external difficulty labels.

We evaluate TrajFusion on two base models, LLaMA3-8B and DeepSeekMath-7B, across six mathematical reasoning benchmarks covering both in-domain and out-of-domain settings. Under both low-data (15K) and full-data (100K) regimes, TrajFusion consistently outperforms RFT, with particularly large gains on challenging datasets such as MATH, TheoremQA, and OlympiadBench.
To assess its scalability, we further apply TrajFusion to long-form reasoning data distilled from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and fine-tune a 32K-context version of Qwen2.5-Math-7B. TrajFusion again surpasses RFT, achieving higher accuracy on competition-level benchmarks including AIME24, AIME25, and MATH-500.

Contributions.

•

We formalize a previously underexplored supervision gap in RFT: the systematic exclusion of teacher-generated incorrect trajectories despite their diagnostic value for modeling reasoning failures. This motivates treating such errors as valuable supervision, rather than noise.

•

We propose
TrajFusion
, a structured supervision framework that reframes RFT from a binary filtering mechanism into a supervision construction process, adaptively fusing incorrect and correct reasoning trajectories through lightweight reflections. An adaptive mechanism automatically adjusts the amount of fused content based on the diversity and informativeness of teacher errors, while preserving standard RFT behavior when errors provide limited value.

•

We demonstrate that TrajFusion consistently and scalably improves over RFT across multiple models, datasets, data regimes, and context lengths, with notable gains on challenging benchmarks and long-context mathematical reasoning.

Preliminaries

2.1
Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning

Rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT) constructs supervised data by sampling multiple
candidate trajectories for a problem
x
,

\mathcal{Y}(x)=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{K}\},\qquad y_{k}\sim T(\cdot\mid x),

and retaining only those with correct final answers,

\mathcal{Y}^{+}(x)=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}(x)\mid\operatorname{corr}(y)=1\}.

All incorrect trajectories,

\mathcal{Y}^{-}(x)=\mathcal{Y}(x)\setminus\mathcal{Y}^{+}(x),

are discarded. While this produces a clean training signal, it also removes the full
distribution of reasoning attempts. We analyze these sampling distributions to
understand what information RFT leaves unused.

2.2
Sampling Distributions

We analyze the sampling behavior of Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct
34
on the DeepMath dataset
10
by examining error rates and the diversity of incorrect answers across sampled trajectories. Our analysis focuses on problems with mixed outcomes, where both correct and incorrect trajectories are observed.

Figure
1
summarizes the sampling statistics.
The left panel shows the distribution of error rates across problems.
Approximately
40\%
of problems have near-zero error rates and are almost always solved
correctly, while about
18\%
are nearly always answered incorrectly.
Between these extremes lies a substantial set of problems with mixed outcomes, where the
model alternates between success and failure across attempts; these intermediate cases
are particularly informative for analyzing reasoning behavior under uncertainty.

The right panel characterizes failure structure by measuring the diversity of incorrect
final answers using Shannon entropy.
Error diversity increases with error rate (
\rho=0.55
,
p<10^{-10}
), indicating that
harder problems tend to induce a broader range of distinct failure modes rather than
repeated identical mistakes.

Beyond this overall trend, two characteristic patterns emerge. Some problems exhibit
low error rates but high diversity
among incorrect answers. In these cases, the model usually follows a reliable reasoning path, while rare failures arise from unstable or divergent intermediate steps. Other problems show
high error rates but low diversity
, where the model fails frequently yet converges to nearly the same incorrect answer each time. This behavior indicates a systematic misunderstanding or a strong inductive bias that repeatedly drives the model toward a specific incorrect solution.

Taken together, these observations demonstrate that the discarded set
\mathcal{Y}^{-}(x)
encodes rich structural information beyond a binary notion of correctness. Incorrect trajectories vary not only in frequency but also in qualitative structure, reflecting distinct modes of reasoning instability and persistent misconceptions.

Building on the observations in Section
2
, we introduce
TrajFusion
, a structured fine-tuning approach that integrates selected incorrect reasoning trajectories together with a correct one into a single fused training sample.
Instead of relying exclusively on the correct solution, TrajFusion leverages information from diverse failure modes to provide richer supervision, while remaining fully compatible with vanilla RFT pipelines.

3.1
Sampling and Scoring

For each problem
x
, we sample
K
reasoning trajectories

\mathcal{Y}(x)=\{y_{1},\dots,y_{K}\},\qquad y_{k}\sim T(\cdot\mid x),

and partition them into correct and incorrect subsets using an automatic verifier,
yielding
\mathcal{Y}^{+}(x)
and
\mathcal{Y}^{-}(x)
respectively.

To characterize the sampling outcomes at the problem level, we compute two statistics. The first is an
error-rate estimate

r(x)=\frac{|\mathcal{Y}^{-}(x)|}{K},

which measures how frequently the teacher model produces incorrect trajectories among the sample attempts for problem
x
.

The second statistic quantifies the diversity of incorrect outcomes:

u(x)=\left|\,\{\operatorname{Ans}(y):y\in\mathcal{Y}^{-}(x)\}\,\right|.

Here distinct final answers represent different error types, allowing us to distinguish
between problems where the model collapses to a single failure mode and those where it
explores multiple incorrect reasoning paths.

3.2
Difficulty- and Diversity-Aware Selection

TrajFusion selectively incorporates incorrect trajectories into training.
The selection is jointly guided by error frequency and diversity: incorrect trajectories are included only when they occur with sufficient frequency, and a larger number of incorrect trajectories are selected as failures become more diverse.

The number of retained incorrect trajectories is determined by

k(x)=\min\!\left(k_{\max},\ \left\lfloor\alpha\,r(x)\,u(x)\right\rfloor\right),

where
k_{\max}
is a fixed upper bound and
\alpha
controls the sensitivity to error diversity. This rule yields an adaptive behavior:

•

When no incorrect trajectory is observed,

r(x)=0
and thus
k(x)=0
, reducing TrajFusion to vanilla RFT without introducing any
additional error supervision.

•

When errors are present,
k(x)
increases with both the error rate
r(x)
and the answer diversity
u(x)
: homogeneous failures (small
u(x)
) result in fewer selected incorrect trajectories, while higher error rates and more diverse outcomes lead to richer supervision.

To obtain representative yet compact supervision, we organize incorrect trajectories
\mathcal{Y}^{-}(x)
by their final answers and prioritize error groups by frequency. Within each selected group, we favor shorter trajectories as representatives, encouraging concise supervision while preserving diversity across failure outcomes.

3.3
Fused Sample Construction

Given a set of selected incorrect trajectories
\{y_{e_{1}},\dots,y_{e_{k}}\}
and a correct trajectory
y^{\star}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}(x)
, TrajFusion constructs a single fused training sample

T(x)=[\,y_{e_{1}},\ \rho_{1},\ \dots,\ y_{e_{k}},\ \rho_{k},\ y^{\star}\,],

where each
\rho_{i}
is a fixed reflection prompt that explicitly marks the transition between successive attempts and provides contextual signals for correction.

The training of TrajFusion proceeds with standard supervised fine-tuning on the fused sequence sample. The model is optimized via next-token prediction using the negative log-likelihood objective

\mathcal{L}_{SFT}=-\sum_{t=1}^{|T(x)|}\log p_{\theta}(T_{t}\mid T_{<t}),

without any modification to the model architecture or training objective.
TrajFusion therefore operates as a supervision construction mechanism, that enriches the learning signal while maintaining compatibility with existing fine-tuning pipelines.

4.2
Main Results

Overall performance and data efficiency.

Table
1
summarizes the performance of TrajFusion across six
mathematical reasoning benchmarks. With only 15k training examples, TrajFusion already
achieves competitive results on both backbones. On DeepSeekMath-7B, it reaches 55.8% on
MATH and 48.8% on average, outperforming several data-construction baselines that use
substantially larger training sets (e.g., MathFusion at 60k and MMIQC at 2.3M). This
demonstrates that TrajFusion can extract meaningful supervisory signals even in low-data
regimes.

At 100k examples, TrajFusion achieves the highest average accuracy among all compared
training strategies: 41.3% on LLaMA3-8B and 52.3% on DeepSeekMath-7B. The method
continues to yield improvements as data scales, indicating that trajectory-level
supervision from both correct and selected incorrect attempts remains beneficial in
larger training regimes.

Effectiveness varies with reasoning complexity.

TrajFusion provides the largest gains on benchmarks characterized by longer or more
structured reasoning, such as MATH, OlympiadBench, and TheoremQA. These tasks naturally
produce more diverse incorrect attempts, aligning with the model’s strength in leveraging
trajectory-level variation. At 100k examples, TrajFusion improves over DARTMath by +4.8
points on MATH for LLaMA3-8B and +5.5 points for DeepSeekMath-7B, and achieves the highest
accuracy on TheoremQA across all methods.

On GSM8K, TrajFusion performs similarly to other approaches, with smaller gains than on
the more complex benchmarks. GSM8K problems involve short, well-structured reasoning
chains with limited variability, resulting in fewer opportunities for trajectory fusion
to provide additional benefit.

Comparison with vanilla RFT.

To isolate the contribution of integrating incorrect trajectories, the vanilla RFT
baseline uses the same sampled queries and the same correct trajectories as TrajFusion, but
discards all incorrect attempts. Across both model backbones and training sizes,
TrajFusion consistently outperforms vanilla RFT. For LLaMA3-8B, the improvements are
+6.1 points at 15k and approximately +3 points at 100k. For DeepSeekMath-7B, the gains
are +3.0 and +2.1 points respectively.

Gains are especially pronounced on structurally challenging benchmarks. On OlympiadBench,
TrajFusion improves LLaMA3-8B from 6.4% to 8.6%, and DeepSeekMath-7B from 19.6% to
23.1% at 15k. On TheoremQA, improvements of 5–6 points are observed on both backbones.
These results support the central hypothesis of TrajFusion: selectively incorporating
informative incorrect reasoning trajectories provides richer supervision than
correctness-only training, yielding consistent benefits in both low- and high-data
settings.

Related Work

5.1
Data Construction for Math Reasoning

Prior work improves mathematical reasoning primarily by constructing
high-quality solution data.
Most approaches focus on expanding the coverage and diversity of

correct
reasoning trajectories, for example by modifying problem
difficulty, reformulating questions, recombining solution components,
or sampling multiple valid solution paths
18
;
35
;
14
;
29
;
8
.
To ensure reliability, these pipelines typically apply strict correctness
checks or rejection sampling, retaining only fully correct generations
as supervision
38
;
37
;
32
;
8
;
31
.
As a result, incorrect or partially incorrect trajectories are systematically
excluded at the data level.

5.2
Negative Signals for Math Reasoning

Several approaches introduce negative signals to improve
mathematical reasoning. Preference-based and reinforcement learning methods, such as
DPO
24
, GRPO
27
, and their variants
19
;
17
;
13
,
leverage suboptimal or lower-reward samples to guide models away from
undesirable reasoning paths. NSR
40
shows that exclusively optimizing negative samples in reinforcement learning yields significant gains. NFT
2
incorporates negative signals by modifying the
supervised training loss. Self-correction approaches
1
;
21
introduce errors
through multi-turn data synthesis, exposing models to flawed trajectories and
their revisions. TrajFusion differs in that it operates within the RFT pipeline and directly
utilizes discarded samples. It does not require preference modeling or loss modification, enabling
single-stage learning from negative signals under standard next-token
prediction.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed TrajFusion, a lightweight fine-tuning method that leverages both correct and incorrect reasoning trajectories during rejection sampling fine-tuning. By structuring diverse failed completions and reflection prompts into adaptive fused trajectories, TrajFusion provides structured supervision by a compact trail-and-error reasoning path without requiring architectural changes or modifications to the training objective.
Experiments across diverse math benchmarks and models show consistent gains over vanilla rejection fine-tuning, especially on challenging and long-form regimes. TrajFusion offers a simple yet effective way to scale mathematical reasoning through supervision.

7
Limitations

TrajFusion leverages supervision signals from problems where model sampling yields both correct and incorrect trajectories, as such cases provide the most informative reasoning variations.
Consequently, the strength of the supervision signal depends on the presence
of sufficient outcome diversity during sampling, while questions that are consistently solved correctly or incorrectly offer limited contrastive information, in which case TrajFusion naturally reduces to vanilla rejection sampling fine-tuning behavior.

Appendix A
Experimental Details

A.1
Sampling Setup

We adopt a diverse sampling strategy to expose the model to both successful and unsuccessful reasoning paths.

Sampling Strategy.

For each input query, we generate 16 independent completions using nucleus sampling (top-
p=0.95
) with a temperature of 0.7. This configuration balances diversity and coherence, encouraging exploration of multiple reasoning trajectories without producing excessively random outputs.

Prompt Format.

All completions are generated using a fixed instruction-style prompt:

Please reason step by step and put your answer within \boxed{}.

This encourages models to follow a chain-of-thought reasoning process and produce a clearly marked final answer suitable for automatic extraction.

Output Filtering.

To ensure semantically valid supervision signals, we filter generated responses to remove malformed outputs.
Specifically, we discard completions that:

•

exhibit excessive repetition;

•

switch languages mid-response (e.g., mixing English and Chinese);

•

lack meaningful reasoning steps or a final boxed answer.

This step ensures that the training data remains pedagogically useful.

Answer Extraction and Verification.

We extract the final boxed or inline answer from each completion using simple pattern matching heuristics. To assess correctness, we apply the automatic math verifier from opencompass
5
, which executes symbolic evaluation of the extracted answer against the ground truth. Each completion is labeled as
correct
or
incorrect
based on exact match criteria.

Model-Specific Configurations.

Sampling is conducted using two teacher models:
Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct
(4K context) and
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B
(16K context). Each model uses its default maximum context length and the same sampling settings above. Queries for which no correct completions are found are excluded from training.

A.2
Trajectory Fusion

Construction.

For each query, we identify the set of incorrect responses generated during rejection sampling. These responses are grouped by their final extracted values, and we select the shortest representative from each group to prioritize concise reasoning paths with minimal redundancy.

Sequence Formatting.

A complete assistant reply is constructed by interleaving the selected incorrect responses with fixed reflective prompts, simulating a trial-and-error reasoning process. The sequence concludes with a correct solution followed by a positive feedback phrase and the final answer. The trajectory is encapsulated within a thinking block to provide structured supervision:

<think>

[Incorrect Response 1]

[Reflective Comment]

…

[Correct Response]

[Success Phrase]

</think>

The answer is \boxed{[Answer]}.

If no eligible incorrect completions are found, the sequence defaults to a standard format containing only the correct response within the
<think>
block, omitting the error and reflection components.

\frac{\sec x}{\sin x}-\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}

\sec x
and
\sin x

\cos x
and
\sin x

\sec x=\frac{1}{\cos x}\quad\text{and}\quad\sin x=\sin x.

Substituting these into the expression, we get:

\frac{\sec x}{\sin x}=\frac{\frac{1}{\cos x}}{\sin x}=\frac{1}{\cos x\sin x}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}.

So the expression becomes:

\frac{1}{\cos x\sin x}-\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}.

To combine these fractions, we need a common denominator. The common denominator for
\cos x\sin x
and
\cos x
is
\cos x\sin x

\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\frac{\sin x\sin x}{\cos x\sin x}=\frac{\sin^{2}x}{\cos x\sin x}.

Now we can combine the fractions:

\frac{1}{\cos x\sin x}-\frac{\sin^{2}x}{\cos x\sin x}=\frac{1-\sin^{2}x}{\cos x\sin x}.

We recognize that
1-\sin^{2}x
is a difference of squares, which can be factored as:

1-\sin^{2}x=(\cos x+\sin x)(\cos x-\sin x).

So the expression becomes:

\frac{(\cos x+\sin x)(\cos x-\sin x)}{\cos x\sin x}.

We can split this into two fractions:

\frac{\cos x+\sin x}{\cos x}\cdot\frac{\cos x-\sin x}{\sin x}.

This simplifies to:

\left(1+\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right)\left(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}-1\right).

We know that
\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}=\tan x
and
\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}=\cot x
, so the expression becomes:

(1+\tan x)(\cot x-1).

Using the identity
\cot x=\frac{1}{\tan x}
, we can rewrite the expression as:

(1+\tan x)\left(\frac{1}{\tan x}-1\right)=(1+\tan x)\cdot\frac{1-\tan x}{\tan x}=\frac{(1+\tan x)(1-\tan x)}{\tan x}=\frac{1-\tan^{2}x}{\tan x}.

We know that
1-\tan^{2}x=\sec^{2}x-\tan^{2}x=\sec^{2}x-\frac{\sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}=\frac{\cos^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}-\frac{\sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}=\frac{\cos^{2}x-\sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}=\frac{\cos 2x}{\cos^{2}x}

\frac{\frac{\cos 2x}{\cos^{2}x}}{\tan x}=\frac{\cos 2x}{\cos^{2}x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}=\frac{\cos 2x\cos x}{\cos^{2}x\sin x}=\frac{\cos 2x}{\cos x\sin x}=\frac{2\cos^{2}x-1}{\cos x\sin x}=\frac{2\cos x}{\sin x}-\frac{1}{\cos x\sin x}=2\cot x-\csc x.

Therefore, the simplified form of the expression is:

\boxed{2\cot x-\csc x}.
