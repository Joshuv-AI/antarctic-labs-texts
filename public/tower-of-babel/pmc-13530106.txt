Intratumoral but not circulating estradiol predicts postoperative survival in men with hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yaqi Wang, Nan Zhang, Hongye Zhao, Yewei Liu, Meng Hu, Xiaoming Wang, Panpan Ma, Shaohua Wang, Shuang Cao, Yanan Zhao, Chao Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSex disparity in HCC indicates vital estrogen signaling roles. The clinical significance of intratumoral and circulating estradiol (E2) remains unclear in male HCC. This study explored their correlations with clinicopathological features, postoperative survival and tumor growth.MethodsPaired tumor and adjacent liver tissues and preoperative serum were collected from 120 male HCC patients; serum from 120 healthy men served as controls. E2 levels were measured by iodine-125 radioimmunoassay, and estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) and estrogen receptor beta (ERβ) expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression, with sensitivity analyses based on cohort median, 12-month time-dependent ROC-derived cutoffs, and continuous-variable Cox models. HepG2 cells were treated with E2 and evaluated by CCK-8, colony formation, RT-qPCR, and western blotting. Mouse xenograft models were established with HepG2 cells pretreated with or without E2.ResultsIntratumoral E2 was significantly lower in HCC than in adjacent tissues, while serum E2 was higher in HCC patients than in healthy controls (both P < 0.001). ERα and ERβ expression was reduced in tumor tissues (both P < 0.01). Low intratumoral E2, but not serum E2, was associated with larger tumor size and poorer postoperative survival (HR = 1.722, 95% CI 1.145–2.592, P = 0.009). E2 inhibited HepG2 proliferation and reduced PCNA expression in vitro, whereas xenografts established from E2-pretreated HepG2 cells showed reduced growth.ConclusionLower intratumoral E2 levels were associated with larger tumor size and poorer postoperative survival in men with HCC. These findings support further evaluation of intratumoral E2 as a potential prognostic biomarker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s12672-026-05732-4.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant histological subtype of primary liver cancer and remains a major global health burden. In 2022, liver cancer accounted for approximately 866,136 new cases and 758,725 deaths worldwide, with a markedly higher burden in men than in women [1]. Curative-intent hepatectomy is a mainstay treatment for selected patients with HCC; however, postoperative recurrence remains frequent, with cumulative 5-year recurrence rates commonly reaching 60–70% after resection [2, 3]. Importantly, the postoperative course is highly heterogeneous, and patients with apparently similar clinicopathological features may experience markedly different recurrence and survival outcomes, underscoring the need for biologically informative prognostic markers. This complexity is also reflected in recent surgical outcome studies. For example, a retrospective study of patients with HCC undergoing hemihepatectomy showed that middle hepatic vein resection increased operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative liver enzyme levels, but did not significantly affect long-term overall survival or recurrence-free survival [4]. These findings suggest that postoperative prognosis after curative resection is shaped by a complex interaction among surgical factors, liver functional recovery, and tumor biology.

Conventional biomarkers such as alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), AFP-L3, and des-γ-carboxy prothrombin (DCP/PIVKA-II) are clinically informative but remain imperfect [5, 6]. AFP can be elevated in the setting of hepatic injury, inflammation, and regeneration, and its clinically used thresholds vary considerably across studies and practice settings, ranging from lower cutoffs such as 20–100 ng/mL to higher thresholds of 200 or 400 ng/mL [5, 7]. Likewise, DCP is susceptible to non-tumor-related perturbation, particularly in patients receiving vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin [8]. More broadly, even emerging blood-based biomarkers continue to face important challenges related to assay standardization, etiologic diversity, biological heterogeneity, and cross-cohort generalizability [6, 9].

The estrogen axis is of particular interest in HCC because sex disparity in incidence and prognosis strongly suggests a role for sex hormones in liver tumor biology [10]. Women with HCC have been reported to show better survival than men in several cohorts, and this advantage appears to be more evident in premenopausal or normally menstruating women, but attenuates after menopause, supporting a potential protective role of estrogenic signaling [11–13]. In parallel, clinical studies have linked tumor estrogen receptor status to patient outcome, while exogenous estrogen exposure has been associated with reduced mortality and longer survival in women with HCC [14–16]. Experimental studies further showed that 17β-estradiol (E2) can suppress HCC cell growth through estrogen receptor-related pathways, providing biological plausibility for its prognostic relevance [17].

However, the current literature remains incomplete and partly conflicting: while many studies support a tumor-suppressive role of estrogen signaling in HCC, others suggest that, in specific settings, locally increased aromatase-driven estrogen production may instead promote liver tumor growth [18, 19]. More broadly, recent evidence on tumor-associated regulatory molecules, such as ZG16B, highlights that local tumor microenvironmental factors can influence tumor progression and immune modulation. This evidence supports the tissue-based biomarkers may provide biological information that is not adequately captured by circulating measurements [20]. In this study, we quantified both intratumoral and serum E2 levels in male patients with HCC and analyzed their associations with clinicopathological characteristics and postoperative survival, with the aim of determining whether intratumoral E2 could serve as a biologically interpretable tissue-based biomarker that complements established postoperative markers by more directly reflecting the local estrogenic microenvironment than circulating measurements [6, 9]. This approach may help identify a subgroup of male patients with impaired local estrogen signaling and poorer outcomes, thereby refining postoperative risk stratification and providing a rationale for future biomarker-enriched mechanistic and therapeutic studies.

Materials and methods

Patients and sample collection

120 cases of both liver tumor tissues and adjacent tissues (which were over 5 cm from tumor edges) were collected from HCC patients who underwent surgery at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Province (Shijiazhuang, China), and all patients were male. The HCC diagnosis was confirmed by two pathologists. All samples were fixed for histochemistry staining and estradiol assay for further analysis. Then, we collected preoperative blood from surgical patients. At the same time, venous blood from 120 healthy male patients were collected as the control group. Further, venous blood samples were collected without anticoagulant, allowed to clot, and centrifuged to obtain serum for subsequent experiments.

Cell culture and treatment

The human liver cancer HepG2 cells (cat. no. HB-8065; Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in MEM medium containing 10% FBS and penicillin and streptomycin (100 U/mL and 100 mg/mL, respectively) in a humidified environment at 37℃ with 5% CO2. For E2 treatment, HepG2 cells were treated with DMSO-dissolved estradiol (1–150 nM) for 24, 48, and 72 h, when the cells grew to 70–80% confluent. It should be noted that the control group were treated with DMSO only. The treated cells were used for CCK-8 assay or other detection methods.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation was measured using the CCK-8 kit (GK10001, GlpBio, America) according to the manufacturers’ recommendations. Briefly, 1 × 104/ 100 µL cells were seeded into 96-well cell culture plates in triplicate, allowed to adhere for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with different final concentrations of E2 (1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 150 nM) dissolved in DMSO for 24, 48, 72 h. CCK-8 solution was diluted with serum-free MEM medium at a ratio of 1:10. At the indicated time, the cell culture medium was replaced with 100 µL of diluted CCK-8 solution. After 1 h cultured, the optical density (OD) readings were performed at 450 nm to determine the numbers of viable cells by a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). The experiment was repeated three times.

Colony formation assay

Single HepG2 cells were prepared by the trypsinization and were seeded into 6-well plates at 500 cells/well and cultured for 3 days, following by the E2 treatment (1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 150 nM) for another 3 days. Then, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (E607309, Sangon Biotech) for 15 min. The number of colonies was counted under microscope with the representative photograph shown. The experiment was replicated three times.

Animals and treatment

Mouse xenograft tumor model Male Balb/c nude male mice (5 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. and housed in a pathogen-free barrier facility with a 12/12 h day/night cycle and free access to chow and water. To test the inhibitory effects of E2 on tumor growth, mice were randomly distributed into two groups: HepG2 and HepG2 + E2 groups (n = 5/ group) and were injected subcutaneously with 5 × 106 HepG2 cells treated with or without E2 (30 nM) respectively. Tumor growth and changes in body weight were monitored. At 42 days post-inoculation, mice were humanely euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of a lethal overdose of sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg body weight). Death was confirmed by the absence of spontaneous respiration, cardiac arrest, and corneal reflex.The xenograft tumors were then harvested and weighed. The tumor volume was calculated as V = 0.5 × length × width2. The protocols using animals were approved by the ethical committee of Hebei University of Chinese Medicine and were conducted in Hebei University of Chinese Medicine. The animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Hebei Provincial Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (The First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei University of Chinese Medicine). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the approved protocol and applicable institutional guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HepG2 cells and the liver tissues of HCC patients using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was then synthesized using a SuperScript Reverse Transcription Kit (Invitrogen, USA) and used as a template for qPCR with SYBR Green fluorescence (Monad, Shanghai, China). Quantitative real time PCR was run on an ABI 7500 Fast system (Life Technologies). 18S was used as internal control and for normalization. Relative mRNA expression was determined using the 2-ΔΔ CT method [21]. Each experiment was conducted with three replicates. The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, and their sequences were: PCNA forward 5’-ACAGAGCATGGATTCGTCTCAC-3’ and reverse 5’-AGAAAACTTCACCCCGTCCTTT-3’; 18S forward 5’-CGCCGCTAGAGGTGAAATTC-3’ and Reverse: 5’-CCGGTCGGCATCGTTTATGG-3’.

Western blotting

HepG2 cells or were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Thermo Scientific, USA) with protease inhibitors. The tissue supernatants were collected (12,000 g; 30 min; 4 °C). The harvested cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors for more than 15 min on ice. The cell supernatants were collected (8,000 g, 10 min, 4 °C). The protein concentration of supernatant was determined using the modified Bradford method with bovine serum albumin as a standard. Proper exposure protein marker (G2086, Servicebio) and equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) were electrophoresed on 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Ireland). Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk and incubated with the following primary antibodies: 1:1000 rabbit anti-PCNA (Cell Signaling, Inc., catalog no. 13110) and then incubated in 1:1,000 mouse anti-β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., catalog no. sc-47778) horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Enhanced chemiluminescence was used for detection of the antibody-antigen complexes. Images were captured by FusionCapt Advance Fx5 software (Vilber Lourmat). The expressions of various protein were expressed in relative grayscale, with β-actin as the protein loading control.

Estradiol assay

According to the instructions of the Iodine Estradiol Radioimmunoassay Kit (DELTA Bio, China), 8 tubes were labeled as total T, NSB, S0, standard S1, standard S2, standard S3, standard S4, standard S5. Further, according to the kit instructions, we added dilution buffer, standard 10, 50, 100, 250, 750 pg / ml. Then, we mixed it well, and placed at room temperature (37 ℃) for 1.5 h. Then, we added the separating agent and mixed it well. Further, it was centrifuged at 3600 rpm for 3 min at 20 ℃. Then, the supernatant was discarded, and the activity intensity (cpm) of each tube was measured on a gamma counter. Then, we recorded the cpm reading as the ordinate, and the standard concentration as the abscissa to draw the standard curve. For tissue E2 measurement, 100 mg of wet tissue was homogenized in 300 μL of normal saline and centrifuged at 10,000×g. A 200-μL aliquot of the supernatant was used for the assay. The calibration range of the assay was 10–750 pg/mL, with an analytical sensitivity of < 5 pg/mL. The manufacturer-reported recovery was 94–109%, and the intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 10% and < 15%, respectively. Tissue E2 content was normalized to tissue wet weight using the following equation: tissue E2 (pg/mg) = supernatant E2 concentration (pg/mL) × total homogenate volume (0.300 mL) / tissue wet weight (mg). Serum E2 concentrations were expressed as pg/mL.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Sections were blocked with goat serum and incubated with estrogen receptor alpha (Erα) and beta (Erβ) antibody. After extensive washing, the sections were incubated with the biotinylated secondary antibody, followed by incubation with HRP-streptavidin D. Then, the signal was developed using a diaminobenzidine kit (Zhongshan Goldenbridge Bio- technology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Hematoxylin was used for nucleus counterstaining. Images were acquired using a Leica microscope (Leica DM6000B, LAS V.4.3, Switzerland). The number of positive cells was graded and scored by the number of positive chromogenic cells: 0–1% = 0, 1–10% = 1, 10–50% = 2, 50–80% = 3, and 80–100% = 4. The staining intensity was graded: 0 (negative), 1 (weakly positive), 2 (positive), and 3 (strongly positive). The immunohistochemistry score (IHS) was calculated using the following formula IHS = A × B, where A was the score for the percentage of stained cells, while B is the grade of staining intensity. The minimum score is 0, while the maximum score is 12.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the paired or unpaired t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, according to data type and distribution. Multiple-group comparisons in cell experiments were analyzed using analysis of variance followed by post hoc tests when applicable. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Survival was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and log-rank test. Prognostic factors were evaluated using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals reported. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. To assess the robustness of the findings to cutoff selection, sensitivity analyses were performed using cohort-median cutoffs and outcome-based thresholds derived from time-dependent receiver operating characteristic analyses. The 12-month overall survival horizon was used as the primary ROC analysis, whereas the 24- and 36-month estimates were considered exploratory. Intratumoral and serum E2 were also modeled as continuous variables and expressed per unit and per standard-deviation increase. The multivariable models adjusted for lymph node metastasis and tumor differentiation. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. Variables included in the previously specified multivariable models were intratumoral or serum E2, lymph node metastasis, and tumor differentiation.

Associations of intratumoral and serum estradiol levels with clinicopathological characteristics

Using the cohort median intratumoral E2 level of 9.832 pg/mg as the cutoff, patients were classified into high and low intratumoral E2 groups, with 60 patients in each group. As shown in Table 1, intratumoral estradiol level was not significantly associated with age, AFP level, ALT level, HBV infection status, satellite lesions, lymph node metastasis, or tumor differentiation (all P > 0.05). In contrast, tumor size was significantly associated with intratumoral estradiol level (P = 0.006). Analyses using the original reference-based cutoffs, namely the mean intratumoral E2 level in adjacent non-tumorous liver and the mean serum E2 level in healthy controls, are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. The association between lower intratumoral E2 and larger tumor size remained directionally consistent across the alternative cutoff definitions. Taken together, these results suggest that intratumoral, rather than circulating, estradiol level is more closely associated with tumor burden in HCC.

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of factors associated with survival outcome

To further evaluate prognostic factors associated with survival outcome in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed. The results showed that low intratumoral E2 was associated with a higher mortality risk in the univariable Cox model, although the association was of borderline statistical significance (HR = 1.486, 95% CI 0.999–2.212, P = 0.051; Table 3). After adjustment for lymph node metastasis and tumor differentiation, low intratumoral E2 was independently associated with an increased mortality risk (adjusted HR = 1.722, 95% CI 1.145–2.592, P = 0.009; Table 4). Serum E2 was not associated with mortality in the corresponding adjusted model (HR = 1.052, 95% CI 0.710–1.559, P = 0.800). The inverse association between intratumoral E2 and mortality was consistent across alternative analytical approaches. When modeled continuously, each 1-SD increase in intratumoral E2 was associated with a 29.9% reduction in adjusted mortality risk (HR = 0.701, 95% CI 0.578–0.850; P < 0.001). Similarly, low intratumoral E2 was associated with higher mortality using both the original reference-based cutoff (adjusted HR = 2.723, 95% CI 1.657–4.475; P = 0.001) and the 12-month ROC-derived Discussion

Estrogen has long been implicated in the marked sex disparity of HCC, yet its prognostic significance remains incompletely understood, particularly with respect to the distinct biological roles of local versus systemic hormone compartments. To our knowledge, this study is the first to integrate paired tumor tissue and preoperative serum samples from patients with HCC and to highlight that the biological and prognostic significance of E2 in HCC is compartment-dependent. This compartment-based perspective represents a key innovation of the present work, because it helps reconcile previous inconsistent views regarding the role of estrogen in liver cancer and provides a more biologically interpretable framework for prognostic evaluation. Our results showed that intratumoral E2 was decreased in HCC tissues, whereas serum E2 was increased, and that only low intratumoral E2, but not circulating E2, was associated with larger tumor size, poorer survival, and independent adverse prognosis.

Our observation that intratumoral E2 was lower in HCC tissue than in paired adjacent liver is directionally consistent with prior evidence that estrogen signaling is attenuated during hepatocarcinogenesis, including early studies showing reduced estrogen receptor content in cirrhosis/HCC and later work demonstrating ERβ downregulation in HCC tissue [17, 22, 23]. Mechanistically, estrogen signaling has been reported to restrain hepatocarcinogenesis by suppressing Kupffer cell-derived IL-6, dampening pro-tumor inflammatory signaling, and repressing MTA1-dependent proliferative and metastatic programs [24–27].

In contrast to the reduction in intratumoral E2, our finding that serum E2 was elevated in HCC is supported by several clinical studies showing that men with HCC, or with cirrhosis plus HCC, have higher circulating estradiol levels than healthy controls or cirrhotic controls [28–31]. At the same time, the literature is clearly not uniform, because Pignata et al. found no significant alteration in blood estradiol in male patients with viral cirrhosis and HCC, whereas Tanaka et al. reported that lower estrogens and a higher testosterone:estradiol ratio were associated with subsequent HCC development among cirrhotic men [32, 33]. A plausible explanation for these discrepant findings is that circulating E2 in chronic liver disease reflects systemic endocrine disequilibrium rather than tumor-restricted biology, because impaired hepatic metabolism and clearance of estrogens, altered steroid conversion, and extrahepatic or peritumoral aromatization can all increase serum estrogen concentrations independently of the intratumoral estrogenic state.

The association between lower intratumoral E2 and larger tumor size in our cohort is biologically coherent with prior evidence showing that loss of estrogen signaling accompanies a more aggressive HCC phenotype, whereas E2 directly suppresses proliferation and malignant behavior in hepatoma cells [17, 34, 35]. From a pathological–radiological perspective, larger tumors may also be more likely to interact with adjacent vascular structures and develop invasive pathological features. A recent retrospective study showed that preoperative compression of the hepatic or portal vein by HCC was an independent predictor of microvascular invasion and satellite nodules, supporting a link between radiological tumor–vessel interaction and pathological aggressiveness [36]. Although satellite lesions did not reach statistical significance in our cohort, possibly due to limited power after dichotomization, these findings provide a relevant framework for interpreting the association between low intratumoral E2, larger tumor size, and a potentially more aggressive tumor phenotype. By contrast, the lack of a significant relationship between serum E2 and tumor size in our study is also biologically plausible, because circulating estradiol is a composite readout of liver dysfunction, whole-body steroid metabolism, and background cirrhosis rather than a direct surrogate for the local tumor microenvironment [29, 37, 38]. This may also help explain why previous studies detected overall elevations in serum E2 in HCC but observed tumor-size-related changes more readily in hormone ratios such as testosterone-to-estradiol, rather than in E2 alone [30, 33]. Taken together, these findings suggest that intratumoral E2 is more closely linked to tumor biology and tumor burden than circulating E2 in HCC.

More broadly, our results may help explain why the previous literature has generated apparently contradictory views of E2 as either tumor-suppressive or tumor-promoting in HCC. When the focus is placed on intratumoral estrogen availability, preserved ERα/ERβ signaling, inflammatory restraint, and direct cellular responses, E2 is more likely to appear protective, because these pathways generally suppress HCC growth, invasion, and metastatic behavior [17, 24–26]. In contrast, when the biological context is dominated by aromatase-driven local estrogen formation, altered receptor isoforms, or cirrhosis-associated endocrine imbalance, estrogen may appear neutral or even tumor-promoting [18, 19, 39, 40]. Accordingly, our data do not necessarily refute earlier “pro-estrogen” or “anti-estrogen” models, but rather suggest that the biological meaning of E2 in HCC is compartment-dependent and should be interpreted according to where E2 is measured, how it is generated, and which receptor context is preserved within the tumor.

In our cohort, low intratumoral estradiol remained an independent adverse prognostic factor, and patients with high intratumoral estradiol had significantly longer postoperative survival than those with low intratumoral estradiol, supporting a close link between the local estrogenic milieu and HCC outcome. Although direct prognostic studies based specifically on intratumoral E2 concentration are still limited, our findings are concordant with earlier clinical evidence showing that preserved estrogen signaling within the tumor is associated with better survival in HCC [14, 15]. In particular, Villa et al. reported that estrogen receptor status in tumor tissue was the strongest prognostic factor for survival in inoperable HCC, indicating that maintenance of intratumoral estrogen responsiveness has major clinical relevance [14]. This concept was further supported by their later study proposing an estrogen-receptor-based classification for HCC, in which variant estrogen receptor transcripts were associated with worse prognosis, again suggesting that disruption of local estrogen signaling is linked to adverse outcome.

Our in vitro and in vivo results indicate that E2 can directly restrain the malignant phenotype of HCC cells, as evidenced by reduced HepG2 proliferation, impaired clonogenicity, decreased PCNA expression, and suppressed xenograft growth, thereby providing experimental support for the biological relevance of estrogen signaling in HCC progression. These findings suggest that restoration of estrogenic signaling, rather than simple measurement of circulating hormones alone, may represent a potentially actionable strategy in a biologically defined subset of HCC, which is consistent with prior studies showing that 17β-estradiol suppresses HCC growth and that estrogen receptor agonists can oppose oncogenic signaling in HepG2 cells [17, 41, 42]. These data also support the concept of biomarker-guided patient stratification, because patients with low intratumoral E2 and reduced tumor estrogen signaling may be the subgroup most likely to benefit from therapeutic approaches aimed at restoring or reactivating local estrogenic pathways.

This study has several limitations, including its single-center design and restriction to male patients, which may limit generalizability. In addition, the prognostic value and cutoff of intratumoral E2 were not externally validated and should be confirmed in independent multicenter cohorts. Another limitation is that ERα and ERβ expression was evaluated by IHC in only 10 paired tumor and adjacent tissue samples. Therefore, the receptor-expression results should be regarded as exploratory and supportive, and they cannot establish the generalizability of ERα/ERβ downregulation across the entire cohort. Larger studies with systematic receptor profiling are needed to validate the relationship between intratumoral E2 levels, estrogen receptor expression, and clinical outcome. At the same time, the xenograft experiment in our study was based on implantation of HepG2 cells pretreated with E2 in vitro, rather than direct E2 administration to tumor-bearing mice. Therefore, this pretreat-and-inject design may not fully recapitulate the genuine in vivo therapeutic effect of E2 or the local estrogenic microenvironment in established tumors. Direct E2 treatment or local modulation of estrogen signaling in tumor-bearing animals are needed in the future.

Future clinical study should focus on biomarker-enriched early-phase studies incorporating intratumoral E2 and ER profiling, pharmacodynamic monitoring, and careful safety evaluation, in order to determine whether modulation of local estrogen signaling can be integrated into postoperative risk-adapted management or combined therapeutic strategies for HCC. Given the context-dependent and potentially dual roles of estrogens in liver cancer, local or mechanism-based targeting of the estrogen signaling axis is likely to be more rational than nonselective endocrine therapy, and our data provide a rationale for pursuing such a precision-medicine direction in future HCC study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the compartment-dependent significance of E2 in male HCC and suggests that intratumoral, rather than circulating, E2 is more closely linked to tumor biology and prognosis. Low intratumoral E2 may identify a subgroup of patients with more aggressive disease and poorer postoperative outcome. Beyond its prognostic relevance, the observed antitumor effects of E2 provide a rationale for further mechanistic and clinical studies. Future work should validate intratumoral E2 as a tissue-based biomarker and explore its value in biomarker-guided therapeutic strategies for HCC.
