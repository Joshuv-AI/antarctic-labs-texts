The food additive 4-hexylresorcinol impairs intestinal stem cell function and exacerbates inflammatory bowel disease in Drosophila melanogaster via suppression of mapk signaling pathway
Authors: Xinxin Fan, Jinhua Yan, Yu Yuan, Yi Chen, Haiyang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global incidence of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is rising, an epidemiological trend strongly linked to the increasing consumption of ultra-processed foods. Although certain food additives are implicated in IBD pathogenesis, the direct impact of these additives on intestinal stem cells (ISCs), key effectors of epithelial homeostasis and repair, is poorly defined. Specifically, 4-hexylresorcinol (4-HR), a widely used phenolic preservative, has not been thoroughly investigated for its long-term impact on intestinal health, particularly during inflammation and repair. Using a Drosophila melanogaster model of bleomycin (BLM)-induced intestinal injury, we combined genetic analysis, immunofluorescence staining, and functional assays to evaluate the effects of 4-HR on tissue pathology, ISC proliferation, and evolutionarily conserved pro-repair signaling pathways. Our findings indicate that 4-HR significantly aggravates intestinal damage and impedes epithelial regeneration by suppressing the injury-induced proliferation of ISCs. Mechanistic studies revealed that 4-HR inhibits the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway, which is critical for initiating the reparative program following injury. Importantly, we extend these findings to a mammalian system, demonstrating that 4-HR similarly impairs intestinal repair and suppresses the conserved MAPK signaling axis in a mouse model of colitis. Collectively, our data elucidate a previously unrecognized mechanism whereby the food additive 4-HR aggravates intestinal damage and impedes epithelial regeneration by directly suppression MAPK-dependent, ISC-driven repair. This cross-species validation establishes the detrimental role of 4-HR and its core mechanism as conserved from invertebrates to mammals, not only establishing a novel link between dietary factors and epithelial repair dysregulation but also underscoring a significant dietary environmental contributor to IBD pathogenesis. Furthermore, this study establishes a foundation for future research into how dietary components influence stem-cell biology, underscoring the urgent need to re-evaluate the long-term safety of common food additives in vulnerable populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-026-06159-4.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic, progressive inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. Since the mid-twentieth century, its global incidence has risen markedly [23], a trend paralleled by the Westernization of diets and a concomitant increase in the consumption of processed foods and food additives [24]. As a key modifiable environmental factor, diet is consequently recognized as a pivotal dietary driver in the pathogenesis and potential management of IBD.

Ultra-processed foods are characterized by non-natural components, including artificial flavors, emulsifiers, thickeners, and preservatives. Accumulating evidence indicates a dose-dependent relationship between the consumption of such foods and an elevated risk of IBD [45]. For instance, a multinational prospective cohort study across 21 countries revealed that individuals consuming more than five servings of ultra-processed foods per day had a significantly increased risk of developing IBD (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.82) [3]. Food additives are implicated in IBD pathogenesis through mechanisms such as altering gut microbiota composition, inducing dysbiosis, impairing intestinal barrier integrity, and promoting chronic inflammatory and aberrant immune responses [53].

Although the influence of food additives on the gut microbiota and epithelial barrier has been extensively characterized, their direct impact on intestinal stem cells (ISCs) remains largely unknown. Resident at the crypt base, ISCs are the primary drivers of epithelial renewal and damage repair [6, 10]. Dysregulation of ISCs is strongly associated with IBD progression [2]. The self-renewal and differentiation of ISCs are governed by evolutionarily conserved signaling pathways, including the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (regulating proliferation), the JAK-STAT pathway (mediating inflammatory responses) [20], and the MAPK pathway (a key signaling pathway in cellular response to damage and repair) [21]. Disruption of these pathways may compromise epithelial integrity and contribute to IBD development [49].

4-Hexylresorcinol (4-HR), a phenolic compound with an amphipathic structure comprising a hydrophobic alkyl chain and a hydrophilic phenol group, demonstrates potent antioxidant and antimicrobial activities [18]. It is widely utilized as a preservative in food products and as an antimicrobial agent in cosmetics [26]. Although conventional toxicological evaluations indicate no significant acute toxicity or mutagenicity at standard doses, the long-term effects of 4-HR exposure on intestinal health—particularly within the context of chronic inflammatory conditions such as IBD—remain poorly characterized [37]. A particularly critical knowledge gap exists regarding whether 4-HR disrupts ISC self-renewal, differentiation, or niche function, thereby impairing mucosal repair mechanisms in IBD.

To address this question, we utilized Drosophila melanogaster as an experimental model. The Drosophila melanogaster offers distinct advantages for studying intestinal inflammation: its digestive system exhibits structural and functional conservation with mammals [14, 48],approximately 75% of human disease-associated genes possess functional orthologs in Drosophila [41],and key signaling pathways (e.g., JAK-STAT, Wnt, Notch, and MAPK) are evolutionarily conserved [12, 40, 42]. The Drosophila midgut, functionally analogous to the mammalian small intestine, consists of enterocytes (ECs), enteroendocrine cells (EEs), and adult ISCs, providing a robust in vivo system for investigating ISC biology [35, 38].

This study represents the first investigation into the role of 4-HR in exacerbating intestinal inflammation and its putative effects on ISCs. We demonstrated that 4-HR significantly aggravates bleomycin (BLM)-induced intestinal damage in Drosophila and suppresses ISC proliferation during the repair process. To substantiate the physiological relevance of these findings, we validated the core pathogenic mechanism in a mammalian system. Our experiments in a mouse model of colitis confirm that 4-HR similarly impairs intestinal repair and that its detrimental effect is mediated through the suppression of the conserved EGFR-MAPK signaling axis [7]. Mechanistically, 4-HR appears to selectively inhibit the activation of the MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase) signaling pathway, thereby disrupting epithelial repair processes. Our findings not only provide critical experimental evidence for reassessing the long-term intestinal safety of 4-HR across species but also yield novel insights into the mechanisms by which food additives may influence IBD pathogenesis—specifically through direct modulation of signaling pathways that regulate intestinal stem cell function. The demonstration of this conserved mechanism from invertebrates to mammals significantly strengthens the translational implications of our work.

Experimental models and study participant details

Drosophila stocks and husbandry

The following Drosophila strains were used in this study: esg-GFP/CyO and UAS-lacZ lines were kindly provided by Dr. Allan Spradling; esgts-GAL4 line was generously gifted from Dr. Benjamin Ohlstein; w1118 (BDSC #3605) and UAS-rl2A (BDSC # 36270) were obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center.

Drosophila melanogaster were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (25°C, 60% relative humidity, 12/12 h light/dark cycle) unless otherwise indicated. A standard cornmeal-agar diet was provided, prepared by dissolving 80 g sucrose, 50 g cornmeal, 20 g glucose, 18.75 g yeast, 5 g agar, and 30 mL propionic acid in 1 L distilled water. Experiments were conducted exclusively using mated adult females. To homogenize genetic backgrounds, all strains were backcrossed for 5–6 generations prior to analysis. For conditional gene expression assays, flies were initially reared at 18°C to ensure transgene silencing. Thermal induction was achieved by transferring animals to 29°C for specified durations; control cohorts were continuously cultured at 25°C. Midgut examinations were restricted to mated females. The esgts-GAL4 driver system was employed for temperature-sensitive modulation of gene expression, with transcriptional repression at 18°C and activation at 29°C.

Mice husbandry and procedures

All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Center of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Protocol 2021577A) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male mice from a C57BL/6 background (back-crossed for ten generations) were used throughout the study, with sex-matched and age matched controls employed for each experiment. Eight week old mice were housed under specific pathogen free conditions (23 ± 1 °C, 45–65% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) in groups of five and acclimatized for one week. Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 4 per group) and provided ad libitum access to normal drinking water (Control), water containing 2.0% (w/v) DSS, or DSS water supplemented with 1 μM 4 HR for 7 consecutive days. All mice were euthanized on day 7 for tissue collection. Detailed information regarding animal age and sample size for individual experiments is provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Methods details

Drosophila drug treatment

4-HR (4-Hexylresorcinol; Macklin, Shanghai, China, # H835898) was first dissolved in DMSO (Dimethyl Sulfoxide, BioFroxx, #1084ML100) and subsequently mixed with standard medium to generate food containing graded concentrations of the compound. Following eclosion, freshly hatched flies were collected and maintained on the 4-HR-supplemented diets under controlled conditions.

Bleomycin treatment

Bleomycin administration was conducted following established protocols (Du et al., 2020). Filter paper strips (3.5 × 5.5 cm) were saturated with 25 μg/mL bleomycin (Aladdin, B107423) prepared in 5% (wt/vol) sucrose solution. Following a 2-hour starvation period, adult flies were transferred to empty vials containing the drug-saturated strips for 24-hour exposure, after which they were returned to standard food for recovery.

Bromophenol blue treatment

The copper cell region (CCR) acidity in Drosophila midguts was assessed via bromophenol blue staining [31]. Standard food medium was perforated and supplemented with 200 μL of 2% bromophenol blue sodium (Sigma, #B5525) in 5% sucrose. After 2 h starvation, adult flies were exposed to BPB-medium for 24 h. Finally, the guts were dissected and promptly imaged. “Homeostasis” refers to CCR as yellow and “Perturbed” refers to CCR as blue.“Non­eating” means the flies did not eat, and the guts are not stained with bromophenol blue. Three independent replicates (n=30 flies/vial) were performed under standard conditions.

‘Smurf’ assay

The Smurf assay was performed to evaluate intestinal barrier function as previously described [43, 44]. Standard Drosophila medium was supplemented with 2.5% (wt/vol) brilliant blue dye (Aladdin, #B295002). Adult flies (n=30 per vial) were starved for 1-2 h prior to 12-24 h exposure to the dyed medium. Following dye exposure, flies were categorized based on staining patterns: those with extraintestinal dye diffusion were classified as Smurf(+), while flies with dye restricted to the digestive tract were designated as barrier-competent. Three independent replicates were conducted for each experimental condition, including 4-HR-treated and untreated controls.

Fly excretion measurement

The flies were subjected to a 2-hour fasting period prior to 24-hour administration of bromophenol blue-containing diet. Fecal deposits (identified as discrete blue markings) were collected from vial surfaces via chromatography paper transfer. Spot enumeration was conducted using a Leica M205 FA stereomicroscope (Leica, Weztlar, Germany), with each marking corresponding to a single defecation event.

Intestinal morphology assay

Adult Drosophila intestines were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes at room temperature, followed by three 5-minute washes with 0.1% PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100). After nuclear staining with DAPI, tissues were washed and mounted in 70% glycerol solution. Intestinal morphology was examined using a Leica M205 FA stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), with gut length quantification performed using ImageJ software (NIH). For murine studies, freshly isolated colons were longitudinally stretched on chilled PBS-moistened filter paper before macroscopic measurement.

Immunofluorescence and microscopy for drosophila midguts

Midguts from adult female Drosophila melanogaster were dissected in PBS and fixed for 30 min in 4% paraformaldehyde/n-hexane (1:1 v/v). Following two 5-min methanol washes, tissues were permeabilized with 0.1% PBST (3 × 10 min). For colon tissue of mice, the samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, then sectioned and baked at 65 °C for 30 min. A biotransparent agent was applied for 30 min. The sections were sequentially washed in ethanol gradients (anhydrous, 95%, 90%, 80%) for 2–5 min each, followed by a 5-min rinse in double-distilled water. Microwave antigen retrieval was done using citrate, and the sections were washed twice with PBS for 5 min each. Subsequently, the samples were blocked in 0.5% BSA for 30 min under 25 ℃ and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 0.1% PBST. Next day, the tissues were washed with 0.1% PBST as described before and incubated with solution containing secondary antibodies and 4’, 6-diamino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, guts were performed the same washing. For phalloidin staining, the samples were incubated in fluorescent probe solution (Invitrogen, #A30106) for 60 min at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C in 0.1% PBST, followed by PBST washes and 2-h incubation with secondary antibodies and DAPI (1:1000) at room temperature. Final washes were conducted as described above.

The sources and dilutions of the antibodies used are as follows: Chicken anti-GFP (1:1000; ab13970, Abcam), Rabbit anti-phospho-histone H3 (Ser10) (Millipore, 06-570), Mouse anti-Delta (1:50; C594, 9B, DSHB), Mouse anti-Armadillo (1: 100; AB_528089, Abcam), Rabbit anti-pERK(1: 100; 4370, Cell Signaling), Mouse anti-Ki67(1: 1000; GB121141-100, Servicebio). The secondary antibodies Goat anti-chicken Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China, #AB_2921070), Goat anti-mouse Alexa 568 (Invitrogen, #AB_144696), and Goat anti-rabbit Alexa 568 (Invitrogen, #AB_143157) were used at a 1:2000 dilution. The nuclei were stained using 1 µg/ml DAPI (Sigma, #D9542).

Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a Leica TCS-SP8 confocal microscope. Image assembly was performed using Leica Application Suite X (LAS X), Adobe Photoshop CC 2023, and Adobe Illustrator 2023.

Disease activity index (DAI) scoring

Colitis severity was assessed daily using the disease activity index (DAI), a composite measure that quantifies weight loss relative to baseline, stool consistency, and the presence of rectal bleeding, following the scoring system outlined by Kim et al. (2012) (Kim et al., 2012). DAI scores were recorded throughout the study to monitor the clinical course of colitis.

Histological scorin

Intestinal tissues were fixed in 10% formalin for 12–24 h, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. Sections were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and images were acquired using a Leica DM6 B optical microscope. Histopathological evaluation of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections from colon was carried out in a blinded manner by board-certified veterinary pathologists, according to the criteria previously established by Otsuka et al. (2010) [39].

RNA purification and RT-qPCR

Midguts from 100 wild-type female Drosophila were dissected and immediately placed in ice-cold DEPC-treated PBS. The tissues were enzymatically digested in 1 mg/mL elastase (Sigma, cat. no. E0258) dissolved in DEPC-PBS at 25°C, with gentle agitation at 15-min intervals. Following digestion, the samples were centrifuged at 600 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the pellets were resuspended in fresh ice-cold DEPC-PBS. The suspensions were sequentially filtered through 70-µm cell strainers and subjected to cell sorting using a FACS Aria II Sorter (BD Biosciences, USA). Three independent biological replicates were processed for each experiment. Total RNA was isolated from the sorted cells using the Cell Total RNA Isolation Kit (FOREGENE, RE-03111) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with oligo(dT) primers using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Vazyme, R312-01). The resulting cDNA was diluted 10-fold with nuclease-free water for subsequent real-time PCR analysis (Vazyme, Q711-02). Gene expression levels were quantified via the 2−ΔΔCT method, with RpL15 serving as the endogenous reference gene. The control group expression was arbitrarily set to 1 for comparative analysis. Primer sequences used for qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Protein extraction was performed on mouse colonic crypts, whole Drosophila intestine. Tissues were homogenized on ice in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (1%) and PMSF (0.1%). The homogenate was incubated on ice for 30 min and then centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were denatured at 100°C for 10 minutes in loading buffer, electrophoresed on a 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel, and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (1:2,000; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and quantified by densitometry with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health). The following primary antibodies were used in this study: Rabbit anti-phospho-Erk (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, 4370) and Mouse anti-β-actin (1:10000; Abcam, AC004 ) as a loading control.

Fluorescence intensity statistics

Confocal z-stack images were analyzed using ImageJ (NIH) to quantify fluorescence intensity in regions of interest (ROIs). Background-corrected integrated density values were calculated according to the equation: IDcorrected = IDROI - (IDbackground × (AROI/Abackground)), where ID represents integrated density and A denotes area.

BLI analysis

MAPK1 protein (His-tagged, Proteintech, Ag27156) was reconstituted in PBS to a final concentration of 50 mg·mL⁻¹. Prior to the binding assay, nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) biosensors were hydrated and equilibrated in PBS to establish a stable baseline. The MAPK1 protein was then immobilized onto the biosensors, which were subsequently transferred to a 96-well black, flat-bottom microplate (Greiner, 655209). For the binding measurements, a series of 4-HR solutions were prepared in PBS at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.6 µM, with a final assay volume of 300 µL per well. A PBS-only control was included in each assay for reference.

Results

Previous studies have established that ISCs in Drosophila are multipotent progenitors capable of two primary fate decisions: self-renewal to maintain the stem cell pool, or differentiation into ECs and EEs [16] (Fig. 1A). Under conditions of intestinal injury, both ISCs and their immediate progeny, enteroblasts (EBs), undergo marked proliferation to facilitate epithelial repair. To systematically evaluate the potential regulatory effects of the food additive 4-HR on ISCs during homeostasis, this study employed the esg-GFP/CyO reporter strain, which permits specific tracing of ISCs and EBs via GFP expression [28]. The experimental strategy combined immunofluorescence staining using an antibody against Delta (Dl) for precise ISC identification, and phospho-histone H3 (pH3) immunolabeling to quantitatively assess ISC proliferation [54], thereby aiming to determine whether 4-HR influences the balance between self-renewal and differentiation under homeostasis. For the experimental design, flies 5 days post-eclosion were subjected to dietary supplementation with 4-HR at three concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 mM) or maintained on a standard diet as an untreated control (Fig. 1B, C). Following 8 days of continuous exposure, comprehensive phenotypic analysis under homeostatic conditions demonstrated that the number of esg-GFP⁺ cells showed no statistically significant differences among the experimental groups (Fig. 1D). The population size of Dl-positive intestinal stem cells remained similarly unchanged (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, pH3-positive cell counts confirmed that treatment with various concentrations of 4-HR did not significantly alter the proliferative activity of intestinal stem cells (Fig. 1F). Under conditions of intestinal damage and stress, ISCs and their progeny, EBs, undergo rapid activation and substantial proliferation to facilitate epithelial repair and tissue regeneration [33]. Consistent with our laboratory’s established methodology, this study employed a bleomycin (BLM)-induced intestinal injury model in Drosophila [1], categorizing the repair process into four distinct phases: BLM-0D (homeostasis, pre-injury); BLM-1D (acute injury, post-24 h BLM); REC-1D (early repair, 24 h recovery); and REC-3D (late repair, 72 h recovery) [15]. Previous studies indicate that during intestinal repair, cells expressing esg-GFP, Dl, and pH3 increase markedly at the REC-1D stage, reflecting active ISC proliferation, and then return to homeostatic levels by REC-3D, signifying repair completion [8].

Although prior studies had established that the food additive 4-HR does not impair ISC function under homeostatic conditions, its potential role in modulating ISC proliferation during the early damage repair phase (REC-1D) remained unclear. To address this question, 5-day post-eclosion esg-GFP/CyO flies were subjected to 25 μg/mL BLM for 24 h to induce acute intestinal injury, followed by dietary intervention using standard feed supplemented with 0, 0.1, 1, or 10 mM 4-HR (Fig. 2A).The results were consistent with prior observations: BLM stimulation during REC-1D significantly activated ISC proliferation, as demonstrated by a significant increase in the number of cells expressing esg-GFP, Dl, and pH3 (Fig. 2A–D) [8]. However, 4-HR treatment markedly suppressed this BLM-induced proliferative response in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2A–D). In summary, this study provides novel evidence that 4-HR specifically inhibits the proliferative activity of ISCs during the early phase of intestinal repair (REC-1D). These findings offer new insights into the potential adverse effects of food additives on the cellular response to intestinal stress and repair mechanisms.

Previous studies have demonstrated that during the REC-3D stage, the number of proliferating cells gradually returns to homeostasis levels, indicating the completion of the repair process [8]. To clarify how 4-HR affects ISCs early in damage repair, we asked a key question. Does the reduction in ISC number at REC-1D occur because 4-HR speeds up repair, making ISC counts closer to homeostatic levels sooner? Or does it happen because 4-HR suppresses proliferation, blocking the repair process? To answer this, we set up an observation group at the late repair phase (REC-3D). We aimed to see if the 4-HR group fully repaired like the control group, or if it remained in a state of unfinished repair with ongoing proliferation.We used 5-day-old esg-GFP/CyO flies. Acute intestinal injury was induced with 25 μg/mL BLM for 24 h. Then, flies were moved to food containing 0.1, 1, or 10 mM 4-HR for a 3-day recovery (REC-3D phase).The results indicated that in the control group, ISCs dynamically returned to homeostasis by the REC-3D time point, with the counts of esg-GFP⁺, Dl⁺, and pH3⁺ cells restoring to baseline levels, suggesting successful repair (Fig. 3A-D). The intestine is a vital multifunctional organ that plays a central role in nutrient absorption, metabolic waste excretion, and immune defense, though its functional integrity is highly vulnerable under damage conditions [36]. Building on previous findings that 4-HR inhibits BLM-induced ISC proliferation and impedes repair progression, this study further investigated whether 4-HR modulates intestinal function during damage recovery. Based on the observed 4-HR-mediated delay in tissue repair, we focused on evaluating the impact of the optimal concentration (1 mM) of 4-HR on intestinal functional integrity.

Using Armadillo (Arm) protein immunofluorescence staining to assess epithelial morphology, we found that during the REC-3D, the control group without 4-HR exhibited a restoration of regular epithelial architecture and intact cellular junctions (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, the 1 mM 4-HR treatment group showed significant disruption of epithelial structure and loss of cellular connectivity (Fig. 4A, B). To further evaluate intestinal barrier function, we performed the “Smurf” assay—a method based on dye leakage to assess intestinal permeability [43]. The results revealed a low dye leakage rate in the BLM-only group, whereas the BLM + 4-HR co-treatment group showed significantly increased leakage (Fig. 4C, D), indicating that 4-HR exacerbates BLM-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction. Following the method established by Dubreuil [9], we assessed intestinal function by examining the acidic state of the copper cell region (CCR) in Drosophila. Under normal physiological conditions, intake of bromophenol blue leads to a yellow color in the CCR due to acid production and subsequent neutralization; however, structural or functional impairment of the CCR resulting in reduced acid content leads to a blue color [9]. This study found that 4-HR treatment significantly increased the proportion of blue CCR areas, indicating disruption of the acidic microenvironment (Fig. 4E, F). Additionally, morphological analysis showed that BLM treatment resulted in shortened intestinal length, and this phenotype was further aggravated by co-treatment with 4-HR (Fig. 4G). This study aimed to investigate the regulatory mechanism of 4-HR on the MAPK signaling pathway during intestinal damage repair. Previous studies have indicated that upon intestinal injury, ISCs in Drosophila activate the MAPK pathway to initiate proliferative responses and promote tissue regeneration [11]. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis revealed that following BLM-induced injury, the mRNA expression levels of EGFR (a key upstream receptor of the MAPK pathway) and Cyclin D1 (a key downstream cell-cycle regulator of the MAPK/ERK pathway) were both significantly upregulated compared to the control group [30]. In contrast, combined treatment with BLM and 4-HR markedly suppressed the transcription of both genes, showing a consistent regulatory effect on the MAPK pathway-related molecules (Fig. 6A,B). To validate the regulatory effect of 4-HR on the MAPK pathway at the protein level, we examined phosphorylated ERK (pERK)—a direct marker of MAPK signaling activation—by immunofluorescence in the Drosophila posterior midgut [52]. We found that the expression of pERK was significantly elevated in esg-GFP+ cells when the Drosophila intestine was damaged by BLM and 4-HR supplementation can suppress the activation of MAPK signaling (Fig. 6C, D). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that following BLM treatment, the 4-HR co-treatment group exhibited a significant reduction in p-ERK signal intensity (Fig. 6E, F). To further validate the role of MAPK/ERK signaling in 4-HR-mediated repair suppression, ERK (a key downstream effector of MAPK, rl2A) was specifically overexpressed in ISCs using the esg-GAL4 system. At 24 h post-injury (REC-1D stage), 4-HR treatment significantly reduced the number of esg-GFP⁺, Dl⁺, and pH3⁺ cells compared to the untreated group. Notably, overexpression of ERK substantially reversed the 4-HR-induced reduction in these cell populations (Fig. 6G–J). These results demonstrate that 4-HR inhibits the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway, thereby impairing the proliferative and reparative functions of ISCs. Discussion

This study elucidates the crucial role of the food additive 4-HR in intestinal homeostasis and injury repair. Under homeostatic conditions, 4-HR does not affect the function of ISCs; however, upon intestinal injury, 4-HR significantly inhibits the normal proliferation of ISCs, leading to delayed epithelial repair and ultimately exacerbating the pathological phenotype in IBD models. Our findings integrate into a growing body of evidence positioning dietary components as key environmental modulators of intestinal stem cell biology [23]. ISC function is governed by a complex molecular network, with the Wnt/β-catenin, JAK-STAT, and Notch pathways working in concert to balance self-renewal and differentiation. The MAPK pathway, particularly the Ras-Raf-MEK-ERK cascade (hereafter referred to as the ERK pathway), is a critical node within this network, uniquely responsible for transducing mitogenic and stress signals to initiate the proliferative response essential for repair [22, 51]. The disruption of this finely tuned network by external factors is increasingly implicated in IBD pathogenesis.

The MAPK signaling pathway plays a central role in intestinal injury repair [42], responding to external injury signals and rapidly being activated to drive ISCs from a quiescent to a proliferative state [32], which is a key step for the timely regeneration and repair of epithelial tissue. Based on this theoretical foundation, our results indicate that the inhibitory effect of 4-HR is achieved through specific targeting of this pathway. It is critical to clarify that the MAPK family comprises several distinct sub-pathways (e.g., ERK, JNK, p38. Our work specifically implicates the ERK sub-pathway. This pathway is evolutionarily conserved from Drosophila to mammals,the core kinase cascade components—Raf (D-Raf in Drosophila, MEK (Dsor1, and ERK (Rolled—are direct orthologs of mammalian RAF, MEK1/2, and ERK1/2, respectively [17]. The functional conservation of this pathway in mediating injury-induced ISC/intestinal crypt proliferation is well-established in both systems. Molecular docking and experimental validation demonstrate that 4-HR has high affinity for MAPK1 (ERK2), thereby selectively inhibiting its activation. Given the critical role of ERK signaling in driving ISCs to respond to injury, this mechanism plausibly explains why 4-HR is ineffective under homeostatic conditions but strongly impedes intestinal regeneration in the context of injury.

It is important to note that our findings are established within the Drosophila melanogaster model, which provides a foundational genetic system for this initial exploration. The promising insights gained here pave the way for essential future investigations in mammalian models to fully assess the relevance of 4-HR’s effects on intestinal repair. Furthermore, while our work identifies ERK2 as a critical target of 4-HR, the complete downstream signaling cascade and its potential effects on the broader intestinal cellular niche represent exciting avenues for further research.

Future studies should focus on: 1) examining the in vivo consequences of ERK pathway inhibition by 4-HR on the transcriptome and fate decisions of ISCs; and 2) investigating potential interactions between 4-HR and other common dietary additives that may synergistically disrupt intestinal repair.

This study has direct implications for the dietary management of IBD patients, recommending that they minimize the intake of processed foods containing 4-HR to avoid potential risks. From a translational medicine perspective, future research should be dedicated to: 1) investigating whether reactivating the ERK pathway through genetic or pharmacological means can reverse the effects of 4-HR; 2) screening for dietary or intervention strategies that can antagonize the effects of 4-HR, thereby providing new insights for improving IBD treatment. In conclusion, our study uncovers a specific mechanism by which a common food additive, 4-HR, exacerbates intestinal disease by interfering with a conserved pro-repair signaling axis in stem cells. It underscores the necessity of evaluating the long-term impact of food additives not just on tissue integrity, but specifically on the regenerative capacity of stem cell compartments, particularly in susceptible individuals.
