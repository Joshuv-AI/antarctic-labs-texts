Seismic performance assessment of irregular self-centering rocking dual-column piers
Authors: Negin G. Sabagh, Mahmoud R. Shiravand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Geometric irregularities caused by topographical variations often result in unequal pier heights in bridge systems.These height differences make it challenging to achieve consistent seismic performance. While self-centering (SC) rocking systems have shown strong potential for enhancing seismic resilience, their performance in irregular bridge configurations has not been thoroughly examined. This study aims to evaluate the seismic behavior of concrete bridges incorporating SC rocking dual-column piers with height irregularities. For this purpose, three types of dual-column piers with unequal heights were considered: conventional reinforced concrete (RC) piers and SC rocking piers with or without external energy dissipators. The SC rocking piers were examined under three tendon-stress-based design conditions: (a) prevention of simultaneous collapse, (b) equal drift demand, and (c) simultaneous uplift. First, nonlinear cyclic analyses were conducted to assess the behavior of the models. Subsequently, incremental dynamic analyses (IDA) were performed using selected earthquake records. Fragility curves were developed based on drift ratio and residual drift as the engineering demand parameters (EDPs). The results indicate that incorporating external dissipators in irregular SC dual-column piers substantially decreases both maximum drifts and residual drifts.

Introduction

Bridges are among the most important structures in engineering, and as crucial components of transportation infrastructure, they are constantly subjected to dynamic and seismic loads. Therefore, the seismic resistance of these structures to earthquakes and other loads caused by ground motions is of paramount importance. In this context, the use of earthquake-resistant systems capable of absorbing and dissipating seismic energy is considered one of the most effective approaches for the design and improvement of bridge structures. The conventional RC bridge piers are vulnerable to severe damage or collapse due to inelastic behavior and residual deformations, which not only compromise post-earthquake serviceability but also lead to high repair and reconstruction costs. These limitations highlight the need for innovative seismic systems capable of restoring structural integrity with minimal damage after seismic events.

One of the innovative systems for improving the seismic performance of bridges is the use of SC systems. These systems have proven to be an effective in reducing permanent or residual seismic deformations and enhancing structural safety due to their ability to return to their original position after a seismic load. Compared to conventional systems, SC systems show superior performance in mitigating damage and reducing repair costs after an earthquake.

However, the performance of SC systems depends on various factors, including pier height and structural design. Piers are the essential elements of a bridge’s substructure that are responsible for resisting lateral forces and absorbing seismic energy. During seismic events, piers are the first structural components to be subjected to seismic forces. Therefore, any failure in their design or performance can lead to severe damage and eventual collapse of the bridge1.

In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on SC systems. Mander et al.2 were the first to propose the prestressed SC rocking bridge piers and utilized unbonded-prestressing to improve their seismic behavior. Palermo et al.3 and Ou et al.4 suggested adding internal energy dissipating steel reinforcements to the bases of rocking bridge piers to improve their energy dissipation capacity. Their studies showed a significant increase in the lateral load-bearing capacity of the piers and their ability to absorb seismic energy.

However, the use of internal energy-dissipating reinforcements also led to increased residual displacements and posed challenges for post-earthquake replacement. In addition to their application in SC piers, post-tensioning tendons are extensively utilized in various structural connections5–7.

Marriott et al.8 proposed the use of replaceable external energy dissipating steel reinforcements as a solution for energy dissipation in bridge piers. Furthermore, other researchers introduced external energy-absorbing steel reinforcements9–11, external energy-dissipating aluminum bars12, and shape memory alloy bars13,14. These systems significantly improved the post-earthquake reparability of post-tensioned bridge piers.

Experimental investigations by Han et al.15 indicated that bridge piers equipped with external buckling-resistant steel plates exhibited significantly superior energy dissipation behavior compared to those utilizing external steel rods. In addition, researchers have applied steel jackets and fiber-reinforced polymer sheets to strengthen parts of bridge piers, with results indicating increased yield strength and reduced local deformations16–18. In this context, other researchers have recommended the use of high-strength or superelastic materials to control local deformations19–21. Due to the crushing of the base compression toe in post-tensioned reinforced concrete (PRC) rocking piers, experimental studies have investigated improvement strategies such as base confinement using steel tubes and the application of ultra-high-performance concrete. The results indicate that these enhancement techniques can significantly reduce damage in the base region compared to conventional PRC piers22.

Recent experimental studies have investigated precast post-tensioned segmental concrete-filled double-skin steel tubular bridge piers using shake table tests. Du et al.23 evaluated the seismic behavior of these systems under tri-directional far-field ground motions. In addition, dynamic responses with and without the vertical component of ground motion were compared, and the results indicated that vertical excitation has a significant influence on relative displacement and tendon force demand. Against this background, experimental and numerical studies have investigated the behavior of these piers under three-directional near-fault ground motions. A comparison of the results shows that the gravity-induced shear resistance of the connections in the near-fault records can be decreased by as much as 75%, while far-field ground motions result in an approximate reduction of 33%24.

Zhong et al.25 proposed a rocking mechanical hinge (RMH) for SC bridge piers. The proposed RMH can minimize local damage and improve the construction speed of bridge piers. Rasoulpour et al.26 proposed a dual seismic-resistant mechanism with SC cores for continuous-span concrete bridges that can be implemented without energy dissipation devices. In their proposed system, SC piers were added to a bridge with fixed piers as the core, working in parallel with the conventional system.

Moreover, other studies have explored the influence of soil–structure interaction on the seismic performance of SC piers, indicating that such systems contribute to a reduction in seismic-induced settlement27. In addition, a reliability analysis was conducted on SC post-tensioned piers, showing that the decrease in seismic reliability over time necessitates re-tensioning of the tendons before the end of their service life28. Damage assessment has been performed using energy-based indices, and backbone curve prediction for SC systems has also been developed29,30.

Other researchers have also conducted studies on the seismic resilience assessment of reinforced concrete arch bridge columns using seismic fragility curves, considering both short- and long-duration earthquakes, and the time-dependent seismic resilience of deteriorated highway bridges incorporating the effects of retrofitting31–33.

On the other hand, post-earthquake assessments of bridge damage have shown that the direction of structural movement and the damage pattern are influenced by parameters such as geometric configuration and structural regularity. In this regard, the use of SC piers with different heights requires more detailed investigation, as variations in pier height lead to differences in dynamic behavior and energy absorption capacity. One of the limitations of the AASHTO34 and Eurocode 835 standards is the prediction of failure mechanisms for irregular bridges with piers of unequal height and the coordination of the failure of these piers.

Despite the advantages of regularity criteria, studies on the seismic behavior of bridges with piers of different heights have shown that these criteria cannot guarantee simultaneous pier failure. Tamanani et al.36 evaluated these provisions for bridges with unequal pier heights and found that the requirements of Eurocode 835 cannot be fully validated. Following these procedures does not ensure simultaneous pier failure, especially when the height difference is significant. They also demonstrated that by adjusting the transverse reinforcement in each pier, simultaneous failure could be achieved.

Most previous studies focused on experiments and numerical analyses of single piers. In some cases, SC frames with two piers were considered, and experiments were conducted on large bridge with hybrid rocking-sliding piers37, however, in all these cases, the piers had identical heights.

Until now, no comprehensive study has investigated the seismic behavior of systems with SC dual-column piers of differing heights. Accordingly, this study focuses on evaluating the seismic behavior of concrete bridges with SC rocking dual-column piers of varying heights under seismic loading. For this purpose, three types of dual-column piers with unequal heights were considered: (1) conventional RC piers, (2) SC piers without external dampers, and (3) SC piers with external dampers. The SC dual-column piers, with or without external dampers, were examined at different heights under three tendon-stress-based conditions: (a) prevention of simultaneous collapse, (b) equal relative displacement, and (c) simultaneous uplift.

Case (a), in which the behavioral mechanism requiring flexibility of the SC dual-column piers is achieved through their oscillatory post-uplift behavior, is illustrated in Fig. 1. The SC pier behavior can be characterized by two distinct phases: (i) the pre-uplift phase (Fig. 1a), where the pier behaves as a fixed-base system, and (ii) the post-uplift phase (Fig. 1b), which begins once the applied lateral force exceeds the resisting moment capacity.

To maintain the required tendon tension in the SC piers, a specific prestress level must be applied. During these phases, the increasing prestress force may induce tendon yielding, which Dawood and Elgawady38 proposed could serve as a collapse performance limit for SC pier. Alternatively, a drift ratio of 4.5% has been adopted as a collapse criterion12,38.

In case (b), two piers with unequal heights are interconnected so that their tops experience equal displacements (Fig. 2). In this configuration, the drift demand of the shorter pier governs the system’s collapse mechanism. A reduced final drift ratio allows for a higher maximum prestressing stress, enabling the initial prestressing force in the shorter pier to be increased without causing tendon yielding before global system failure. Modeling and verification

Numerical modeling

To perform a nonlinear analysis, two-dimensional finite element (FE) models were developed utilizing the OpenSees platform. In this study, three different configurations of concrete dual-column piers, including both unequal and equal-height cases, were investigated under seismic loading. These configurations included RC piers, SC piers with energy dissipators, and SC piers without energy dissipators.

In the first model, piers with heights of 4 and 8 m were analyzed. Based on the pier heights and Eqs. (3) and (4) of the AASHTO code34, the piers had an effective stiffness ratio of the piers was equal to 0.125, indicating that the bridge exhibited a highly irregular configuration. In the second model, two piers with heights of 6 and 8 m were examined. According to the AASHTO34 code, the effective stiffness ratio was calculated as 0.42, which indicates a low level of irregularity in the bridge system. In the third model, two piers with equal heights of 8 m were evaluated to assess the seismic performance of a symmetric pier configuration under seismic loading. These analyses were conducted to investigate the influence of pier height variation and energy dissipation devices on the seismic performance on the design design of bridge systems. The modeling approach for each configuration is described in detail in the following sections.

In this modeling, the SC piers are assumed to behave elastically. To simulate the contact behavior between the components, the Zero-length elements are employed using the Elastic–No Tension (ENT) material model (Fig. 3a). The top nodes of the Zero-length elements are connected to other components via the RigidLink elements, while the lower nodes are fixed.

Fig. 3Stress-strain relationships of materials: (a) ENT material, (b) Concrete02 and (c) Steel02.

Stress-strain relationships of materials: (a) ENT material, (b) Concrete02 and (c) Steel02.

The post-tensioned (PT) tendons are modeled with the CorotTruss elements, which account for geometric nonlinearity associated with large displacements. The bottom node of the tendon element is fixed in all degrees of freedom. The tops of the piers are connected through a hinged connection using an ElasticBeamColumn element. A roller support is included to prevent horizontal sliding at the base. The detailed of modeling the SC piers is illustrated in Fig. 2a. A fiber section consisting of core concrete, cover concrete, and longitudinal is assigned to the pier elements. The material model Concrete02 is assigned for both the core and cover concrete (Fig. 3b), while the Steel02 material model is assigned for the longitudinal reinforcement (Fig. 3c).

In this study, SC bridge piers with different heights are assumed to have an identical circular cross-section with a diameter of 0.8 m and a tendon cross-sectional area of 0.00251 m². These piers are analyzed under the following conditions:

Condition (a):

Based on Eq. (6), the maximum initial stresses that prevent tendon yielding at a deformation ratio of 4.5% are 697 MPa and 1102 MPa for unequal-height piers with heights of 4 m and 8 m, respectively. For unequal-height piers with heights of 6 m and 8 m, the corresponding values are 967 MPa and 1102 MPa, respectively. Assuming a tendon yield stress of 1675 MPa, the prestress ratio (σ/σy) are 0.66 and 0.42 for the 4 m and 8 m piers, respectively, and 0.66 and 0.58 for the 6 m and 8 m piers, respectively.

Condition (b):

Since the piers are connected as shown in Fig. 2a, the tops of the piers experience identical displacements. However, due to their unequal heights, the deformation ratios of the piers differ, and the taller pier reaches the final deformation ratio earlier. Consequently, the prestress level of the shorter pier can be increased so that tendon yielding is prevented up to this deformation level.

Using Eq. (6), the prestress ratio of the shorter pier is adjusted and increased to prevent yielding before reaching the deformation ratio of the shorter pier. For unequal-height piers with heights of 4 m and 8 m, as well as 6 m and 8 m, the maximum initial stress of the shorter pier is 1102 MPa, corresponding to a prestress ratio of 0.66. Under this condition, the final deformation ratio is 2% for the 4 m pier and 3% for the 6 m pier.

Condition (c):

Considering the differences in uplift forces between the piers, the tendon properties can be adjusted to achieve simultaneous uplift of the piers. In this case, the overall assumptions remain similar to those of Condition (b). By modifying the tendon cross-sectional areas using Eq. (9), the tendon area of the 8 m pier in the 4–8 m configuration is set to 0.005026 m². For the 6–8 m configuration, the tendon cross-sectional areas are 0.00375 m² and 0.00143 m² for the 6 m and 8 m piers, respectively.

These piers were then remodeled to investigate the effect of external energy dissipators on the seismic behavior. In this stage, two external dampers were installed on each side of the pier (Fig. 2b). The external energy-dissipating bars were modeled using Truss elements. To simulate bolted connections incapable of resisting compressive forces, Zero-length elements with an ENT material model and very high stiffness were employed.

Nonlinear analyses and discussion of results

Nonlinear cyclic analysis

Three different configurations of concrete dual-column piers subjected to seismic loading are analyzed, including conventional RC piers, SC piers without energy dissipators, and SC piers with energy dissipators. In the first model, piers with heights of 4 m and 8 m were considered. In the second model, pier heights of 6 m and 8 m were analyzed, while in the third model, both piers had equal heights of 8 m. All piers have circular cross-sections with a diameter of 0.8 m. The compressive strength of concrete is assumed to be 30 MPa, while the yield strengths of the longitudinal and transverse reinforcement are 400 MPa and 200 MPa, respectively. For the SC piers, with and without energy dissipators, PT tendons with a yield stress of 1675 MPa and a cross-sectional area of 0.00251 m² are considered.

Cyclic analysis of RC dual-column piers

The cyclic analyses are performed using a displacement-based loading protocol. The piers were subjected to lateral displacements corresponding to drift ratios ranging from ± 0.5% to ± 4.5%. The RC model represents a conventional dual-column pier with a fixed-base connection. The cyclic responses of the piers are presented in Fig. 7a for the 4–8 m model, Fig. 7b for the 6–8 m model, and Fig. 7c for the model with equal pier heights of 8 m.

The corresponding quantitative performance metrics, including initial stiffness, secant stiffness, stiffness ratio, peak shear strength, and cumulative energy dissipation, are also reported in Table 1.

The presence of piers with unequal heights introduces geometric irregularity into the system, which affects the distribution of stiffness and seismic demand. This behavior is quantitatively reflected in Table 1, where the RC-4 m pier exhibits a significantly higher initial stiffness (9395 kN/m) compared to the RC-8 m pier (1544 kN/m), indicating a strong dependence of stiffness on pier height. Similarly, the peak shear forces of the RC-4 m and RC-8 m piers are 378 kN and 163 kN, respectively (Fig. 7a), confirming that shorter piers provide substantially higher lateral resistance.

In the model shown in Fig. 7b, similar trends are observed, where the shorter pier consistently shows higher stiffness and strength. For example, the RC-6 m pier exhibits a peak shear force of 234 kN compared to 158 kN for the RC-8 m pier, as shown in Table 2.

For equal pier heights (Fig. 7c; Table 3), both RC-8 m piers exhibit similar stiffness(1611 kN/m) and energy dissipation capacity, with a peak shear force of approximately 160 kN, indicating a uniform seismic response when geometric irregularity is eliminated.

Overall, RC piers show higher stiffness and strength in shorter columns, while taller piers exhibit improved deformation capacity.

Fragility analysis

In this study, IDA and fragility analysis were conducted from the FEMA P695 far-field records42. Seismic fragility represents the probability that a structure will reach a certain performance level for a given earthquake intensity. This probability depends on various engineering parameters, such as PGA, the drift ratio, and the material properties. In this study, the drift ratio was used as EDP, and the seismic analyses were performed in the transverse direction.

The records were scaled based on PGA, starting from 0.05 g and increasing incrementally to capture the full nonlinear response up to collapse. Based on the results from the previous section, the best performance was observed among the configurations of SC dual-column piers with and without energy dissipators in the condition (a). IDA and fragility curves were obtained for the performance level at a maximum drift of 4.5% and the collapse condition for these models and RC piers.

The results of the fragility analysis for the collapse performance level are presented for :

Configuration 1: Unequal-height dual-column piers (4 m and 8 m).

The fragility curves for the 4 m and 8 m piers of the first configuration are presented in Fig. 18a and b, respectively. The RC4-m demonstrates a damage probability of about 35% at a PGA of 0.5 g, with a monotonic increase towards 100% as the PGA intensifies. This specimen exhibits superior seismic fragility performance under low seismic demand compared to alternative models.

Fig. 18Drift-based fragility curves for the performance level at a maximum drift of 4.5% with pier height of : (a) 4 m, and (b) 8 m.

Drift-based fragility curves for the performance level at a maximum drift of 4.5% with pier height of : (a) 4 m, and (b) 8 m.

In contrast, the SC1- 4 m shows lower seismic resistance than the RC counterpart. At PGA = 0.5 g, the probability of damage increases to approximately 50%, progressing sharply to near 100% at PGA ~1 g. This rapid fragility growth signifies heightened vulnerability to moderate seismic excitations.

The SCED1- 4 m model demonstrates enhanced seismic resilience relative to the SC1 model. At PGA = 0.5 g, the damage probability is limited to approximately 25%, all considered models. The damage probability increases gradually and approaches 100% near PGA = 1.25 g, indicating that the SCED1 specimen exhibits an increased capacity to withstand escalating seismic intensities due to the improved energy dissipation provided by the external dampers.

For the 8 m piers, the SCED1-8 m model exhibits the most favorable seismic performance among the three configurations. While the SC1-8 m pier shows a higher probability of damage than the conventional RC pier, the inclusion of external energy dissipators in the SCED1-8 m model significantly reduces damage probability. At lower PGA levels, the differences among the models are relatively small.

configuration 2: Unequal-height dual-column piers (6 m and 8 m).

The fragility curves for the 6 m and 8 m piers are shown in Fig. 19a and b, respectively. At low PGA levels, the differences in damage probability among the RC, SC1, and SCED1 models are relatively minor for both pier heights. As the PGA increases, the SCED1 model consistently exhibits the lowest probability of damage, demonstrating enhanced seismic resilience. Since conventional drift-based fragility curves do not adequately capture the advantages of SC piers, an additional set of fragility curves was developed using residual drift as the demand parameter. According to Cao and Guo12, two residual drift limits of 1/100 and 1/300 are recommended to define the performance limits for “collapse prevention” and " emergent usage,” respectively. The " emergent usage " level corresponds to a condition in which the structure sustains visible damage but retains its essential function for emergency operations.

Dawood and ElGawady38 reported that the residual drift in SC piers is approximately 10% of the maximum drift experienced. This implies that to reach a residual drift of 1%, an SC pier must undergo a maximum drift of around 10%. Furthermore, since the collapse drift limit is typically assumed to be 4.5%, structural collapse is expected to occur before residual drifts reach the 1% level. Therefore, residual drift–based fragility curves were only developed for the 1/300 drift ratio limit, as illustrated in Fig. 21.

Fig. 21Fragility curves based on the residual drift with pier height of: (a) 4–8 m, (b) 6–8 m, and (c) 8–8 m.

Fragility curves based on the residual drift with pier height of: (a) 4–8 m, (b) 6–8 m, and (c) 8–8 m.

As illustrated by the fragility curves based on residual drift, SC piers- both without and with external energy dissipators- exhibit superior seismic performance compared to conventional RC piers. Across all PGA levels, these systems significantly reduce the probability of residual drift exceeding acceptable limits. The incorporation of SC mechanisms, particularly in models equipped with external energy dissipators, enhanced post-earthquake stability and minimizes the need for immediate repair. Overall, these findings highlight the effectiveness of SC piers to improve seismic resilience and maintain the post-event functionality of bridge structures.

Conclusions

SC systems are recognized as an effective solution to reduce earthquake damage and its consequences on structures. The main objective of this study was to determine the effects of pier height on the seismic response of bridges with SC dual-column piers and to identify the strengths and weaknesses of this structural system compared to conventional RC dual-column piers and SC dual-column piers of the same height. To this end, numerical models were developed using the OpenSees platform and performance parameters such as drift ratio, shear force, residual drift and PT ratio were evaluated.

Three types of piers with unequal heights were considered: conventional RC piers and SC piers with or without external energy dissipators. The SC piers with or without external energy dissipators were modeled and analyzed under three conditions based on maximum tendon stresses: (a) Prevention of simultaneous collapse, (b) Equal drift ratio, (c) Simultaneous uplift.

Nonlinear cyclic analyses were conducted to evaluate the behavior of the models. Subsequently, IDA was performed using selected earthquake records. Fragility curves were derived using drift ratio and residual ratio as the engineering demand parameters. The following results and findings were extracted from the analyses:

The extent of geometric irregularity caused by unequal pier heights, had a significant impact on the seismic performance of the bridge systems. In configurations with severe irregularity, the mismatch in stiffness and height led to unbalanced force distribution, resulting in higher shear demands on the shorter pier and larger drift demands on the taller pier. This imbalance reduced the overall energy dissipation efficiency and increased residual drifts, particularly in RC dual-column piers.
