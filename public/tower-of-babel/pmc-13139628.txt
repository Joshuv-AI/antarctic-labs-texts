Association Between Hydration Status, Nutrition, and Cognitive Performance in School‐Age Children in Kumasi, Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Nathaniel A. Commey, Janice O. Anum, Boniface Daayi, Naab Justine T‐Baliana Martha, Andrews Baah, Marina A. Tandoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdequate hydration is essential for maintaining good health and optimal cognition in school‐age children. Adequate water intake enhances metabolic and physiological processes, yet school‐age children are vulnerable to dehydration.AimThis cross‐sectional study investigated the associations between hydration status, nutritional anthropometry, and cognitive performance among 200 school‐age children (aged 6–13) in Kumasi, Ghana.MethodEthical approval, parental consent, and child assent were obtained. Hydration status was assessed via urine specific gravity (USG) and categorized as well‐hydrated (USG ≤ 1.010) or dehydrated (USG ≥ 1.011). Nutritional status was evaluated using BMI‐for‐Age Z‐score (BAZ), mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC), and height‐for‐age Z‐score (HAZ) based on WHO growth reference charts. Cognitive performance was measured using Raven's Colored Progressive Matrices. Linear and binary logistic regressions examined associations between hydration status, nutritional indicators and cognitive performance.ResultsThe sample comprised 37.5% males and 62.5% females. Mean Z‐scores for BAZ (−0.13) and HAZ (−0.11) indicated generally normal growth, though 17.5% of children were overweight/obese and 8.5% were stunted. Notably, 89% of participants were dehydrated, while 70% passed the cognitive test. Multilinear regression revealed an inverse association between BAZ and continuous USG (β = −0.273, p = 0.005) and a positive association between HAZ and USG (β = 0.189, p = 0.01). In binary logistic regression, only HAZ remained significantly associated with categorized hydration status (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.36–0.93, p = 0.02). No direct associations were found between hydration status and cognitive performance.ConclusionDehydration was highly prevalent among school‐age children in Kumasi and was significantly associated with height‐for‐age. These findings emphasize the need for integrated nutrition and hydration assessments in pediatric health programs.

Introduction

Hydration status among school‐age children is a crucial yet often overlooked aspect of their nutritional and cognitive well‐being [1, 2, 3, 4]. A review of 22 observational studies from 25 countries reported that approximately 60% of children did not meet their water intake guidelines [5]. Access and availability of safe drinking water are significant barriers to adequate hydration at school [6]. This raises concerns about their hydration status and overall health. The prevalence of dehydration among school‐age children, particularly in Sub‐Saharan Africa, is a pressing health concern that is closely linked with malnutrition and access to clean water. Several studies have shown that nutritional indicators such as stunting (measured by Height‐for‐age Z‐score [HAZ], mid‐upper arm circumference [MUAC], and the BMI‐for‐age Z‐score [BAZ]) are associated with hydration status and cognitive performance in school‐age children [4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. According to Kozioł‐Kozakowska et al. [10], children are susceptible to dehydration because they have higher body water content than relative body mass. Consequently, they lose water easily.

Adequate hydration is important in maintaining optimum physiological processes in children, particularly for cognitive development and function [13, 14]. Even mild dehydration has been associated with impaired critical cognitive processes such as attention, memory, and overall learning abilities [15]. An intervention study has highlighted that frequent hydration is linked to improved mental processing outcomes in school‐age children [16]. This further highlights the significance of adequate hydration in cognitive development.

Nutrition is closely linked to hydration status and cognitive performance. Studies have shown that higher BAZ and HAZ correlate with improved hydration status in children [17, 18]. These nutritional indicators impact hydration by affecting fluid balance, metabolic processes, and water retention capacity in children. Specifically, low HAZ, which indicates stunting is commonly associated with chronic malnutrition, infectious diseases, and adverse socioeconomic factors [19, 20]. Research shows that children who experience stunting at an early age in life are more likely to exhibit cognitive deficits in their school‐age years [21]. In Ghana, for instance, approximately 23% of children in a study were found to be stunted, with considerable regional variation [22].

Higher MUAC also correlates with improved hydration status [18]. MUAC measures total muscle and subcutaneous fat in the upper arm. It is a valuable tool for detecting acute malnutrition, especially in emergency contexts [23]. A study indicates that low MUAC and BAZ are associated with poor cognitive performance in children [24]. This is because low MUAC and BAZ signify inadequate nutrient intake and compromised overall health. The association between MUAC, BAZ, and cognitive performance suggests that nutrition and hydration interventions could alleviate cognitive deficits in school‐age children. In addition, while higher BAZ has been linked to better cognitive performance in pre‐school populations with variations across different age groups [25], a higher BAZ in aging preschoolers may also indicate an increase in fat mass compared to lean mass. Higher fat mass is generally associated with lower total body water relative to lean mass. This shift often leads to lower water retention, making obese children more susceptible to dehydration [26].

Water consumption habits influence hydration status [24]. In Ghana, access to good drinking water is uneven, with socioeconomic factors influencing household water availability. As a result, local water consumption patterns have been shown to affect overall dietary diversity, subsequently affecting children's growth and cognitive performance. For example, a study by Hansen et al. [27] found that households with better access to potable water reported lower instances of stunting and wasting among children. Parents' educational attainment and employment statuses also often correlate with better water management practices and nutritional choices. Consequently, socioeconomic status has been consistently reported to influence children's nutritional status and hydration [28, 29].

The present study explored the intricate relationship between hydration status, nutritional status, and cognitive performance among school‐age children in Kumasi, Ghana. This study is vital as it provides novel, context‐specific evidence from a low‐resource setting where such integrated data is currently scarce.

Methods

Study Design, Study Site, and Study Population

A correlational cross‐sectional study was conducted among randomly selected school‐age children from four schools in Oforikrom Municipality of Kumasi in Ghana. All four schools were public schools managed by the government of Ghana. Participants were included based on returned consent forms: all students within the age eligibility range received consent forms, and only those who returned written consent and provided assent were enrolled in the study. The final sample size of 200 participants reflects the number who provided consent. The study commenced from May to September 2024 in the Oforikrom Municipality, which is an urban enclave of the Kumasi Metropolitan Area. It has a population of about 214,000, and is characterized by parental occupations in trading, public services, and informal sector jobs. It is characterized by mixed socioeconomic conditions, where most households have access to pipe‐borne water (34%−95%), and a minority rely on boreholes and wells (13%–38%). Generally, access to drinking water stands at 65%–80%. The participating schools served predominantly urban households where local schools adhere to 8:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. day‐school schedules, frequently without on‐site water sources and limited midday hydration opportunities ([30, 31]; GSS, 2021).

Sample Size

The a priori calculated sample size was 259 however, a total of 200 school‐age children were randomly selected for this study. The study sample comprised 75 male and 125 female participants from Basic 4 to Basic 9 classes.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Both male and female school children aged 5–13 who were in school and actively participating in academic work were recruited to participate in the study. Children who were not within the age range or were in the age range but not actively participating in academic work were exempted from the study.

Data Collection

A structured questionnaire was used to collect information from participants. The questionnaire captured demographic characteristics (including age and sex), household and school drinking water sources, as well as water intake‐related behaviors such as frequency of water consumption during the school day. In addition, information on anthropometric assessment was recorded. The questionnaire was designed to provide contextual data for interpreting hydration status and related outcomes and was administered as part of the school‐based data collection process. Anthropometric measurements were conducted with participants wearing light clothing (school uniform) and without footwear, while height and MUAC were measured using standard procedures. All measurements were taken by trained study personnel, and privacy was maintained during data collection. MUAC was assessed by measuring the nondominant left mid‐upper arm of participants with the standard noninsertion Shorr MUAC tape. The participants' heights were measured using a stadiometer (Seca 213 mobile stadiometer, Germany) to the nearest 0.1 cm. Weight was also measured using a body weighing scale (Tanita, China, serial number: 851125) to the nearest 0.1 kg, with participants taking off their shoes before being weighed. BAZ and HAZ were computed and interpreted based on the WHO child growth standards for the determination of the nutritional status of participants [32].

Hydration Status Assessment

Urine samples were collected at school in the mid‐morning (approximately 9:00 a.m) following participants' arrival at school. A sterile urine container with a screw cap was given to each participant to provide about 5 mL of post‐arrival spot urine. The samples were stored at four (4) degrees Celsius in a refrigerator. Analysis was conducted on the urine samples within 24 h of sample collection at 20 degrees Celsius. Determination of urine specific gravity (USG) was done using the semi‐automated A. KRṺSS density meter (A. KRṺSS Optronic, Model DS7050, Serial No. 2770510113, Hamburg, Germany) to obtain the mean density of the urine sample. The mean density of the urine was divided by the standard density of water (1 g/cm3) to obtain the mean USG. Results were categorized according to manufacturer‐specified operational guidelines and dehydration threshold, and dichotomized as “Well Hydrated” or “Dehydrated” [33]. USG between 1.002 and 1.010 was categorized as “Well Hydrated”, and SG between 1.011 and above was classified as “Dehydrated” [34]. This threshold is lower than the refractometer standards of (greater than or equal to USG 1.020) due to measurement differences. The category for “Dehydrated” was a combination of “Mildly Dehydrated” (USG between 1.011 and 1.020), “Moderately Dehydrated” (USG between 1.021 and 1.030), and “Severely Dehydrated” (USG above 1.030) [33]. Although urine osmolarity is considered the gold standard for hydration assessment, its measurement was not feasible in this school‐based field setting due to logistical and resource constraints. USG has been widely used as a practical, non‐invasive proxy for hydration status in pediatric field studies and demonstrates moderate to strong correlation with urine osmolarity [35].

Cognitive Performance Assessment

The Raven's Colored Progressive Matrices (RCPM) for nonverbal reasoning ability was adapted and used to assess the cognitive ability of the participants. This assessment tool has previously been used in Ghanaian school‐aged children [36]. The test comprises 36 questions divided into three sets of 12, each increasing progressively in difficulty. Each question involves a geometric pattern or design with a missing piece, which participants must provide from the list of six options given. The first question of the test served as a demonstration for the participants to help them understand the concept of the test. A correct answer was awarded one point, and an incorrect answer was awarded no points. The raw scores were converted to percentages for analysis. Cognitive performance status was categorized into three levels: less than 50% indicated poor performance, between 50% and 69.99% reflected good performance, and scores of 70% and above showed excellent performance [37]. The test was administered individually, without a time limit, in the book format, according to Raven's procedure [38]. The cognitive performance was assessed on the same day as urine sample collection during school hours, following urine collection. The assessments were administered under supervised classroom conditions by trained study personnel.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the IBM Software Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27 (SPSS Inc., an IBM company, Chicago, Illinois, USA). The distribution of all continuous variables, including age, weight, height, USG, and anthropometric indices, was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables were considered normally distributed if the p‐value is > 0.05. All variables met this criterion; therefore, means and standard deviations were reported. Descriptive statistics were then calculated for sociodemographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, MUAC, USG, participants' weight and height, BAZ, HAZ, cognitive performance, and stunting. Cross‐tabulation tests were utilized to compare the frequencies of sociodemographic characteristics, participants' water (fluid) consumption habits, MUAC, BAZ, HAZ, cognitive performance, and hydration status of the participants. Hydration status was compared across age groups using Chi‐square analysis to explore potential age‐related differences. Furthermore, linear regression and binary logistic regression analysis were carried out to identify predictors of hydration status among the participants. Subgroup analyses were not performed due to small numbers within individual age groups. All statistical analyses were two‐tailed, with p‐values less than 0.05 and 0.001 deemed statistically significant.

Ethical Approval

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Committee on Human Research Publications & Ethics at the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (CHRPE), with reference number 241581174622. Also, permission was sought from the Headteachers of the four schools that made up the study site. Informed consent was also obtained from parents of participating students, while assent was obtained from participating students. This ensured the study was conducted with confidentiality, thus protecting the participants. The consent process covered explanations of the study's objectives and characteristics, potential benefits, and assurance to opt out of the study at any point.

Association Between Sociodemographic Characteristics and Hydration Status

Chi‐square analysis was conducted to assess the association between various sociodemographic characteristics and hydration status among participants. The results indicated that none of the sociodemographic variables showed a statistically significant association with hydration status. Hydration status did not significantly vary by age group (χ² = 0.869, p = 0.71), gender (χ² = 2.302, p = 0.16), or school attended (χ² = 0.612, p = 0.89). Similarly, no significant associations were found for ethnicity (χ² = 12.847, p = 0.46), community of residence (χ² = 31.883, p = 0.23), or religious affiliation (χ² = 0.043, p > 0.99). Guardians' occupation (χ² = 0.436, p = 0.80) also showed no significant relationship with hydration status.

Predictors of Hydration Status Among Study Participants (Linear Regression Analysis)

As shown in Table 6, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the extent to which MUAC, cognitive performance, BAZ, and HAZ predicted hydration status among participants. The overall model was statistically significant (R 2 = 0.62, F = 3.227, p = 0.01), indicating that the predictor variables explained some proportion of the variance in hydration status. Among the predictors, BAZ (β = −0.273, p = 0.005) was found to be a significant negative predictor of hydration status, suggesting that as BMI‐for‐Age increases, hydration status decreases. Conversely, height‐for‐age HAZ (β = 0.189, p = 0.01) was a significant positive predictor of hydration status, indicating that better linear growth is associated with improved hydration status. MUAC (β = 0.093, p = 0.33) and cognitive performance (β = 0.025, p = 0.72) did not significantly predict hydration status. These findings highlight the relevance of nutritional growth indicators, particularly BAZ and HAZ, in determining hydration status among the study population.

Predictors of Hydration Status Among Study Participants (Binary Logistic Regression Analysis)

Table 7 shows a binary logistic regression analysis to determine predictors of hydration status. HAZ was a significant predictor of hydration status (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.362–0.929, p = 0.02). BAZ (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.71–2.12, p = 0.4), MUAC (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.710–1.475, p = 0.90), and cognitive performance (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.100–2.913, p = 0.47) did not significantly predict hydration status of participants in this model. Other sociodemographic characteristics, water availability, and accessibility did not predict hydration status. The water drinking habits of participants also did not predict their hydration status.

Discussion

This study investigated the intricate associations between hydration status, nutritional indicators, and cognitive performance in school‐age children in Kumasi, Ghana. Participants' hydration status was categorized into “Well hydrated” and “Dehydrated.” As urine samples were collected in the mid‐morning after school arrival, the measured USG reflects early daytime hydration status rather than overnight urine concentration, and may be used as an estimate of hydration status within a school‐day context [39, 40]. Our findings indicated that most participants were dehydrated (89%), suggesting that poor hydration based on USG may be a widespread issue among school‐age children in Kumasi, Ghana (Table 3). The prevalence of dehydration in the present study exceeds prior reports of 33%−84% in school‐aged children, likely due to our sensitive USG cutoffs, the 9 AM sampling amid tropical conditions, and the Ghanaian‐focused cohort [4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. These factors highlight a heightened risk profile, underscoring the value of field‐adopted assessments in revealing urgent hydration gaps for intervention.

Contrary to several existing studies, our analysis indicated no significant association between age (χ² [df = 3, N = 200] = 0.87, p = 0.71) or gender (χ² [df = 1, N = 200] = 2.30, p = 0.16) (Table 5). This contrasted with a study in Belgium, which found that boys had an increased risk of low hydration levels compared to girls [41]. Several other studies have also reported similar trends of age‐related and gender‐based associations with hydration status in school‐age children [9, 10, 26, 41]. Other sociodemographic characteristics assessed in our study, including age and sex, did not show significant associations with hydration status. These discrepancies may be a result of the relatively homogeneous environment examined, where both boys and girls likely shared similar access to water and engaged in comparable school activities. Additionally, using USG to assess hydration patterns, while practical for assessment, can be influenced by fluid intake and timing of sample collection, and may not have captured chronic hydration patterns and overall hydration status, compared to plasma osmolarity, thereby failing to detect acute variations potentially linked by age and sex. The lack of significant associations between age, sex, and hydration status may also reflect the unique socio‐cultural context of urban Ghana, where gender norms and domestic burden of water‐fetching impose similar physical demands and heat exposure on both boys and girls. Consequently, these shared environmental and behavioral stressors likely override biological differences, resulting in the relative uniform hydration profile across school‐age children in the present study [42, 43]. These findings collectively suggest that the determinants of hydration status in school‐age children may be highly context‐specific and should be interpreted with local behavioral patterns and environmental access firmly in mind.

Investigating the association between nutritional factors and hydration status, our linear regression analysis identified BAZ (β = −0.273, p = 0.005) and HAZ (β = 0.189, p = 0.01) as significant predictors (Table 6). Specifically, a higher BAZ was inversely associated with hydration status, while a greater HAZ showed a positive association. These results suggest that long‐term nutritional and growth indicators may significantly influence hydration patterns in school‐age children. The findings align with research suggesting that undernutrition and stunted growth can impair fluid regulation and increase vulnerability to dehydration [8]. Conversely, MUAC (β = 0.093, p = 0.33) and cognitive performance (β = 0.025, p = 0.72) were not significantly associated with hydration in this linear model. The lack of association with cognitive scores in this model supports earlier findings suggesting that short‐term cognitive variations may not strongly reflect hydration status, particularly when confounding nutritional factors are present [44]. The strongest predictor was BAZ, which showed a significant negative association, followed by HAZ, which was positively associated with hydration. MUAC and cognitive performance were not significant predictors. This visualization emphasizes the relative strength of predictor variables in explaining hydration status among participants.

To further explore categorical risk factors, a complementary binary logistic regression analysis was conducted. Hydration status was dichotomized as “well hydrated” and “dehydrated.” Among all predictors, HAZ was significantly associated with hydration status (OR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.362–0.929, p = 0.02) (Table 7). This indicates that for every unit increase in HAZ, the odds of being well hydrated decreased by 42%, suggesting that children with lower HAZ were more likely to be dehydrated. This result highlights a negative impact on hydration status. The association we observed aligns with findings from other low and middle‐income countries (LMICs), which suggest that stunted children often experience altered physiological functions, including renal concentration and hormonal imbalances affecting thirst, as well as lower intake of water‐rich foods [6, 45]. These findings also agree with recent studies that link undernutrition and chronic malnutrition to impaired innate and adaptive immune responses [46, 47]. The same studies suggested that compromised immunity may lead to conditions such as environmental enteropathy, which affects nutrient absorption and hydration status. Children experiencing stunting may also face challenges such as limited access to clean water and lower dietary diversity, compounding the risk of poor hydration.

In contrast to the linear regression model, the logistic regression analysis revealed that BAZ was not a significant predictor of hydration status in the sample population (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.705–2.124, p = 0.47) (Table 7). This contrasts with several previous studies that suggest higher BAZ correlate with suboptimal hydration [9, 10, 26, 41]. Specifically, children with higher BAZ may have elevated metabolic demands and thermoregulatory challenges leading to increased insensible water loss through respiration and perspiration [26]. The discrepancy between our linear and logistic regression findings may be explained by the loss of statistical power and information inherent in dichotomizing continuous biological measures such as USG [48, 49, 50]. Linear regression effectively captures subtle variations in USG across the full USG distribution. This enables it to detect meaningful trends associated with BAZ. However, categorizing USG into a binary “well hydrated” and “dehydrated” status can obscure these associations, particularly for clusters of measures near the classification threshold [51, 52]. This highlights a critical limitation of dichotomizing continuous biological variables, suggesting that BAZ likely influences hydration status along a gradient rather than as a discrete state.

Similarly, MUAC was not a significant predictor of hydration in the logistic regression model (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 0.710–1.475, p = 0.90) (Table 7). This result is contrary to findings by Stahl et al. [18] and warrants further consideration, especially given the high prevalence of dehydration (89%) alongside a high proportion of children having MUAC values within the normal range (88.5%). MUAC is a widely accepted anthropometric indicator of chronic nutritional status and reflects long‐term muscle and fat reserves [53, 54]. It is, however, generally less sensitive to acute physiological changes such as fluid balance, which can fluctuate daily or hourly [55, 56]. In our study, hydration status was assessed by USG, a measure of acute hydration influenced by fluid intake, physical activity, and environmental factors. The differing temporal sensitivities of these markers may partly account for the lack of statistical correlation observed. Furthermore, the limited variance in MUAC values across the sample, where most children were adequately nourished, may have introduced statistical restrictions, thereby reducing the power to detect subtle associations with hydration status. Additionally, in the suburban Ghanaian sociodemographic context, children may have relatively consistent access to food, thereby maintaining stable nutritional status. At the same time, disparities in water intake, behavioral patterns, or school water availability may have significantly contributed to the widespread suboptimal hydration observed. These findings suggest that MUAC may not be a reliable standalone indicator of hydration status in relatively well‐nourished populations and underscore the necessity for multidimensional assessment tools to comprehensively understand the interplay between nutrition and hydration in school‐age populations.

Finally, the logistic regression analysis also indicated that cognitive performance was not a significant predictor of hydration status (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.100–2.913, p = 0.47) (Table 7). This result contrasts with several studies that suggest dehydration impairs attention, memory, and processing speed in school‐age children [2, 3, 4, 44]. However, it is crucial to note that many of these associations have been primarily demonstrated in controlled intervention studies focusing on acute water intake and task‐specific performances rather than cross‐sectional measurements of habitual hydration status [2, 3, 4, 44]. Moreover, while USG is valuable for indicating hydration status, its sensitivity might be insufficient to capture rapid, short‐term fluctuations that are often linked to immediate cognitive effects. Cognitive function itself is a multifactorial construct, influenced by a complex array of factors including overall nutrition, sleep quality, socioemotional stress, undiagnosed micronutrient deficiency, classroom environment, and socio‐demographic factors. Therefore, while hydration is undoubtedly vital for general health, its direct influence on cognitive function in this population might be more subtle or context‐dependent. Warranting further longitudinal studies to elucidate these relationships background dietary patterns, including diet composition, salt intake, and meal frequency, were not assessed in our study but could be driving individual variations in water needs, and partially explaining hydration status was not directly predicted by cognitive performance alone. Also, physical activity level, recent illness, chronic health conditions, and environmental heat exposure were not assessed and may have acted as unmeasured confounders influencing hydration status and cognitive performance outcomes in the present study.

Conclusion

This study offers novel insights into the complex interrelationships among hydration status, nutritional anthropometry, and cognitive performance in school‐age children in Kumasi, Ghana. We found that most participants (89%) were dehydrated. This finding underscores the challenges faced by school‐age children in maintaining optimal hydration. We also found that while continuous hydration status, assessed by USG, was inversely associated with BAZ and positively associated with HAZ, only HAZ remained a significant predictor of hydration status after USG was categorized. This observation highlights the loss of statistical power and information inherent in dichotomizing continuous variables, a crucial consideration for future research. Although direct associations between hydration status and cognitive performance were not established in this cohort, the significant relationships identified between anthropometric measures and hydration status underscore the importance of their integrated assessment. These findings enhance our understanding of child health in urban Sub‐Saharan Africa and suggest that strategies addressing nutritional well‐being may indirectly influence hydration. Future research should further explore the underlying mechanisms driving the observed anthropometric‐hydration associations and investigate the long‐term impact of hydration interventions on both nutritional status and cognitive performance in vulnerable populations.

Limitations

1.The primary limitation of this study is that hydration status was assessed at a single time point, which may not fully capture daily fluctuations or chronic hydration patterns. However, this approach is commonly used in field settings and provides valuable insight into hydration status and cognitive performance relationships.2.The cross‐sectional study design limits the ability of the study to infer causal relationships between the hydration status of participants and the predictor variables.3.Although efforts were made to control for major variables, unmeasured factors such as physical activity level, recent illness, chronic health conditions, or environmental heat exposure may have influenced hydration and cognitive outcomes.4.Additionally, the final sample size fell short of the calculated target, which may have reduced statistical power to detect certain associations and may have prevented subgroup analysis by age, which should be considered in future research.5.Participation may have been influenced by parental consent, potentially introducing bias towards families with higher educational and socioeconomic status.
