Kdf1 missense mutation caused enamel defects by disrupting cell adhesion and Hippo-YAP signaling in dental epithelium
Authors: Pei Li, Runling Zeng, Jingyan Xue, Yaling Song, Jie Wu, Binghui Zeng, Ling Lin, Dongsheng Yu, Wei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmelogenesis imperfecta is a hereditary enamel defect arising from dental epithelium dysfunction. Although keratinocyte differentiation factor 1 (KDF1) acts as an intracellular regulator in epithelial cells, the underlying disease mechanism of the patient-derived KDF1 missense mutation in amelogenesis remains unclear. Here, we show that a patient-derived KDF1 mutation (c.908 G > C, p.R303P) causes enamel defects by disrupting cell adhesion and Hippo-YAP signaling. Immunohistochemistry revealed strong KDF1 expression throughout dental epithelium development, particularly at the cell membrane. Kdf1 mutation knock-in heterozygotes and homozygotes displayed graded defective enamel with reduced thickness, inadequate mineralization and disorganized microstructure. This phenotype correlated with a gradual reduction in enamel matrix proteins and proteases across genotypes. Bulk RNA sequencing of ameloblasts suggested marked changes in adhesion-related genes and the Hippo-YAP pathway. We characterized cellular consequences of this variant using both LS8 and ALC cell lines, which appeared abnormalities including accelerated proliferation, undermined differentiation, weakened adhesion, and enhanced migration. In vivo and in vitro findings supported a model wherein the KDF1 mutation impaired intercellular and cell-matrix adhesion in ameloblasts. As a result, ameloblast differentiation was hampered through excessive nuclear yes-associated protein (YAP) accumulation and overactivation of downstream proliferative genes. Pharmacological blockade of YAP and TEA domain family member 1 (TEAD1) interaction rescued the mutant phenotypes. Taken together, Kdf1 mutation compromised murine amelogenesis through adhesion defects and subsequent Hippo-YAP dysregulation.Subject terms: Differentiation, Dental diseases, Genetics research

Introduction

Dental enamel, the hardest substance in the human body, develops its remarkable properties through a highly controlled formation process directed by dental epithelium.1,2 Mutations in the genes governing these cells may disrupt their differentiation and function, leading to amelogenesis imperfecta.3–5 The dental defects confer significant lifelong burdens on patients, such as tooth sensitivity, extreme susceptibility to decay, and degraded esthetics.6,7 Therefore, understanding the pathological mechanisms of amelogenesis imperfecta and developing effective therapeutic interventions are critically important.

KDF1 is primarily expressed in epithelial tissues and has been proved to influence skin barrier formation.8,9 As the tooth develops, it localizes from the dental epithelium to the subsequent ameloblast lineage.10 At the subcellular level, KDF1 distributes in the cytoplasm and is enriched at sites of cell-cell junctions,11 suggesting a potential role in intercellular communication and signal integration. Molecularly, KDF1 functions as a scaffold protein by relying on its central domain to mediate protein-protein interactions and recruit downstream effectors.11,12 This is exemplified by its characterized function in skin epidermis, where KDF1 stabilizes inhibitory kappa b kinase α (IKKα) through the recruitment of the deubiquitinase ubiquitin specific peptidase 7 (USP7), thereby promoting terminal differentiation.12,13 KDF1 is also known to influence other key signaling axes, such as the PI3K/AKT pathway,14 highlighting its role as a signaling node pivotal for maintaining epithelial homeostasis. Despite this progress, the core function of membrane-localized KDF1 in the developing tooth germ remains poorly understood.

During tooth development, the indispensability of KDF1 has been firmly established through both cellular knockout and animal model studies.10,14 These investigations have found that KDF1 is essential for proper tooth morphogenesis and pre-ameloblast differentiation. However, while these loss-of-function models confirmed the necessity of KDF1, the precise molecular pathogenesis triggered by the patient-derived p.R303P mutation has yet to be elucidated. Previous studies showed KDF1 p.Phe251Leu and p.His254Tyr mutants were gain-of-function variants that drove ectodermal dysplasia through aberrant IKKα stabilization,13 a mechanism contrasting directly with loss-of-function. Similarly, the KDF1 c.908 G > C mutation has been linked to an autosomal dominant form of non-syndromic tooth agenesis and amelogenesis imperfecta in a clinical pedigree.8 We recently demonstrated that adult Kdf1 knock-in mice partially recapitulated the human phenotype, exhibiting severe hypomineralization and ultrastructural disarray in the enamel.15 A crucial question is whether this patient-specific mutation exerts its effects through a loss-of-function or gain-of-function mechanism, thereby altering the KDF1 protein interaction network and ultimately disrupting the cellular processes that govern ameloblast differentiation. Thus, we continued using our established knock-in mouse model for systematic investigations.

Amelogenesis requires dental epithelial cells to differentiate into functional ameloblasts, a process induced by the dental mesenchyme.16 These specialized cells step-by-step assemble the enamel by secreting related proteins in a methodical manner.17 During the secretory stage, amelogenin (AMELX), ameloblastin (AMBN), and other matrix proteins together guide hydroxyapatite crystal growth and organization.18 In maturation stage, proteases, including matrix metalloproteinase 20 (MMP20) and kallikrein-related peptidase 4 (KLK4) precisely cleave these matrix proteins. This proteolytic processing is important for the enamel matrix to transform into the most highly mineralized vertebrate tissue.19,20 It is still unclear how the Kdf1 mutation affects the expression patterns of these crucial factors. Therefore, this study is also designed to explore how the Kdf1 mutation modulates the key processes and core proteins in amelogenesis.

Herein, we identified that the Kdf1 mutation impaired ameloblast lineage cells differentiation via disrupting cell-matrix and cell-cell junctions and accordingly inhibiting the Hippo-YAP pathway. This dysregulation changed the activity and nucleo-cytoplasmic localization of YAP, which unbalanced the proliferation and differentiation. Our findings revealed a novel pathogenic mechanism of KDF1 mutation and underscored the essential part for membrane-associated KDF1 protein in coordinating cell adhesion with Hippo-YAP signaling.

Results

KDF1 was primarily localized to the dental epithelium in the mouse tooth germ

We successfully genotyped a cohort of Kdf1 mutant mice, confirming the presence of wildtype (WT), heterozygous (Het), and homozygous (Homo) mutants (Fig. S1). At PN0.5 and PN60.5, compared with the wildtype mice, both heterozygous and homozygous mutant mice had slightly smaller body sizes and overall skeletons (Figs. S2, S3). Local magnification of the mandible showed that the incisor cusps had erupted beyond the alveolar bone in wildtype mice, whereas homozygotes failed cusp exposure at PN0.5 with full penetrance. We examined the expression pattern of KDF1 throughout enamel formation via tissue and cell immunofluorescence. KDF1 was consistently expressed in the epithelia of the cervical loop and ameloblasts across all stages and localized predominantly to the membrane and cytoplasm. The spatial distribution remained the same in heterozygous and homozygous Kdf1 mutants (Fig. 1a). In LS8 and ALC cell lines, KDF1 displayed clear co-localization with the plasma membrane and cytoplasmic distribution (Fig. 1b). Kdf1 transcript levels were comparable across the ameloblasts of wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous mice. Subcellular fractionation revealed that the mutation specifically impaired KDF1 localization to the cell membrane, without altering its cytoplasmic levels (Fig. S4).

Kdf1 mutation altered enamel thickness and mineralization in mice

Micro-CT reconstruction revealed no obvious cranial morphological differences across genotypes (Fig. S5). At PN21.5, Kdf1 mutants showed divergent cusp morphology (arrows) relative to wildtype (Fig. 2a). In terms of mandibular first molar (M1) dimensions, both buccolingual diameter (BLD) and mesiodistal diameter (MDD) of wildtype mice were significantly longer than those of homozygous mutant mice (Fig. 2b). Sagittal plane imaging of mandibular molars clearly delineated the enamel layer above the blue dotted line (Fig. 2d). Semi-quantitative analysis of enamel volume revealed statistically significant differences between wildtype mice and both heterozygous and homozygous mutants (Fig. 2c). Under identical threshold settings, enamel mineralization was less complete in Kdf1 mutants than in wildtype littermates (Fig. 2e). Cross-sectional images at the early maturation site (initial mineralization) and pre-eruptive site (late mineralization), demarcated by blue dashed lines, were analyzed for mineral density (Fig. 2f). Kdf1 heterozygotes and homozygotes had significantly lower enamel mineral density than wildtype at both stages (Fig. 2g). These results suggested that Kdf1 heterozygous and homozygous mutations impaired enamel mineralization. SEM showed abnormal enamel prism morphology and disorganization in Kdf1 mutants versus wildtype, with homozygotes displaying more severe defects (Fig. 2h). Within each experimental cohort, the key assessed phenotypes including abnormal crown morphology, reduced enamel thickness, and hypomineralization, showed complete penetrance in the corresponding mutant mice.

Kdf1 mutation affected dental epithelium functions during enamel formation

Masson’s trichrome staining was used to observe the morphology of M1 tooth germs at PN4.5, PN9.5, and PN14.5. Arrowheads marked the loose adhesion of ameloblasts to the enamel matrix in both heterozygous and homozygous mutants at PN4.5 (Fig. 3a). The thickness of enamel matrix at PN9.5 showed a significant decrease in both heterozygous and homozygous mutants relative to wildtype mice, with the most severe reduction occurring in homozygotes (Fig. 3d). By PN14.5, wildtype mice exhibited nearly entire enamel mineralization with minimal residual enamel matrix, whereas heterozygous mutants retained a small amount of matrix and homozygous mutants displayed substantial amounts (Fig. 3a). Transcript and protein levels of secretory-stage enamel matrix markers (AMELX and AMBN) and maturation-stage protease markers (MMP20 and KLK4) were notably downregulated in both heterozygous and homozygous mutants compared to wildtype mice, with the most severe reductions seen in homozygotes (Figs. 3b, e, S6, 7). We assessed inner enamel epithelium (IEE) proliferation via Ki67 staining of PN14.5 incisors, representing the progenitor cell layer of the ameloblasts. In WT, Ki67-positive cells were restricted to the cervical loop, with no signal in secretory or maturation ameloblasts. In Kdf1 mutants, Ki67-positive cells were significantly increased in the cervical loop and presented in secretory and maturation ameloblast layers, indicating dysregulated proliferation (Fig. 3c,f, S8).

Kdf1 mutation impaired cell adhesion molecules in the ameloblasts

Bulk RNA-seq analysis of ameloblasts from wildtype and homozygous mutants identified 135 up-regulated and 805 down-regulated genes in the homozygotes (Fig. 4a). GSEA showed marked down-regulation of the extracellular matrix structural constituent, hippo signaling, cell-cell adhesion mediated by cadherin and cell-matrix adhesion (Fig. 4b). KEGG enrichment analysis displayed pronounced differential enrichment in ECM-receptor interaction, hippo signaling pathway, and cell adhesion molecules, etc (Fig. 4c). A volcano plot further highlighted the significant down-regulation of adhesion molecules within these pathways (Fig. 4d). As a preliminary validation, both heterozygous and homozygous mutants exhibited a graded reduction in E-cadherin (CDH1) and integrin β4 (ITGB4) at both the transcriptional and protein levels within the ameloblasts at PN4.5 and PN9.5, with homozygotes showing the most severe decrease (Fig. S9).

Red boxes indicate pathways related to cell adhesion. d Volcano plot of DEGs with genes involved in the aforementioned pathways labeled. Thresholds were p < 0.05 and log2 (ratio) > 0.5

Kdf1 mutation led to the disruption of cell–cell and cell–matrix junctions

TEM of secretory-stage ameloblasts of the mandibular incisors presented a tightly connected adhesion belt in wildtype mice, approximately 30 nm in width. Homozygous mice, however, displayed enlarged intercellular spaces where the adhesion belt exceeded 60 nm in width and was abnormally shortened. Similarly, cell–matrix junctions appeared structurally intact and robust in wild-type ameloblasts. By contrast, the homozygous mice exhibited incomplete cell–matrix junctions accompanied by severe degradation of the extracellular matrix (Fig. 5a). Analogous to the TEM results, the immunofluorescence staining of mandibular M1 tooth germs at PN4.5 and PN9.5 showed continuous intercellular and cell–matrix connections, whereas these connections were disrupted in homozygous mutants, as indicated by arrowheads (Fig. 5b, S10). Colocalization analysis revealed that KDF1 was highly colocalized with CDH1 and ITGB4 (Figs. 5c, S10).

Kdf1 mutation changed the adhesion and migration functions of ameloblast cell lines

To determine how this mutation specifically affected ameloblasts, we assessed cellular functions concerning adhesion ability in LS8 and ALC cell lines following transfection with lentiviruses carrying mutant plasmids. Lentiviral vectors containing either empty vectors (NC), wild-type Kdf1 overexpression plasmid (OE-WT), or mutant Kdf1 overexpression plasmid (OE-MUT) were stably introduced into both cell lines, with transfection efficiencies exceeding 80% across all groups. KDF1 expression was substantially elevated at the transcriptional and protein levels in the OE-WT and OE-MUT groups compared to the NC group (Fig. S11). Scratch assays revealed that OE-MUT cells migrated faster than NC cells, while OE-WT cells showed the slowest wound closure among all groups (Fig. 6a). Similarly, transwell assays mirrored this trend, with OE-MUT cells migrating the most and OE-WT the least (Fig. 6b). Thus, the Kdf1 mutation may promote cell migration. In adhesion assays, the OE-WT group displayed a higher number of cells attached to the matrix compared to the NC group, while the OE-MUT group showed the fewest adhered cells (Fig. 6c), suggesting that mutant Kdf1 had a negative impact on adhesive ability.

Kdf1 mutation induced adhesive impairment in LS8 and ALC cell lines. a Scratch wound healing assays across NC, OE-WT, and OE-MUT groups with representative images and quantified closure rates at 12 h and 24 h. n = 3 per group. b Transwell migration assays showing stained transmigrated cells and quantifications. n = 3 per group. c Cell adhesion assays with representative images and quantification of adherent cells. n = 4 per group. ns represented P > 0.05, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.000 1

Kdf1 mutation promoted proliferation and suppressed differentiation of ameloblast cell lines

The cell cycle analysis revealed that, compared with the NC and OE-WT groups, the OE-MUT group had a lower proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase and a concomitantly higher proportion of cells in the S phase (Fig. 7a). EdU assays demonstrated a major increase in EdU-positive cells in the OE-MUT group relative to the NC group, and a marked decrease in the OE-WT group (Fig. S13), suggesting that mutant Kdf1 promoted cell proliferation. Both ALP staining and quantitative assays showed significantly diminished mineralization in the OE-MUT group compared to the NC group, whereas the OE-WT group exhibited enhanced mineralization. These findings were further supported by Alizarin red staining (ARS) results (Fig. 7b, S14), indicating that mutant Kdf1 impaired ameloblast differentiation.

Kdf1 mutation inhibited the Hippo-YAP pathway through disrupting adhesion molecules

In vivo, p-YAP was reduced in homozygous ameloblasts (Fig. 8a, b). In vitro, OE-MUT cells displayed a higher nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio of YAP versus NC, while OE-WT cells showed a lower ratio (Fig. S15). Similarly, nuclear fraction analysis displayed more nuclear YAP in OE-MUT cells, accompanied by reduced phosphorylation of MST1/2 and YAP in total lysates (Fig. S16). Co-IP revealed increased TEAD1–YAP binding and decreased TEAD1 and vestigial-like family member 4 (VGLL4) interaction in OE-MUT cells versus controls, consistent with the elevated transcription of YAP-TEAD1 target genes Cyr61, Ctgf, and Myc (Fig. 8c, d, Fig. 16). These results confirmed the downregulation of the Hippo-YAP pathway. To establish a direct functional link between adhesion disruption and Hippo-YAP dysregulation, we performed loss-of-function and rescue experiments (Fig. S17). Cdh1 or Itgb4 knockdown suppressed YAP phosphorylation in wild-type cells (Fig. S18), whereas their overexpression partially restored it in Kdf1 mutant cells (Figs. 8e–h, S19). Besides, wildtype KDF1 expression and YAP phosphorylation were positively correlated (Figs. 8i, j, S20). Thus, adhesion molecules link Kdf1 mutation to Hippo-YAP pathway inhibition.

Verteporfin partially reversed the proliferation and differentiation abnormalities caused by Kdf1 mutation

To assess rescue, we used the YAP-TEAD1 inhibitor verteporfin. Co-IP confirmed that verteporfin disrupted YAP-TEAD1 binding and enhanced VGLL4-TEAD1 interaction in OE-MUT cells (Fig. S21). Verteporfin did not alter YAP nuclear translocation in OE-MUT cells (Fig. S22), but slightly increased p-YAP in total lysates and markedly suppressed Cyr61, Ctgf, and Myc transcription (Fig. S23). Similarly, CCK-8 assays showed reduced proliferation in verteporfin-treated OE-MUT cells versus DMSO controls (Fig. 9a). Besides, verteporfin partially restored enamel matrix protein expression toward NC levels (Figs. 9b, S24), which was supported by ALP activity and ARS in vitro (Fig. S25). The rescue efficacy was further tested by culturing E16.5 mandibular first molars ex vivo. Verteporfin treatment yielded a tissue growth area intermediate to that of DMSO-treated controls, which was characterized by irregular enlargement of the enamel matrix (Fig. 9c). To verify that verteporfin can promote enamel matrix deposition, we conducted in vivo experiments. Verteporfin increased enamel volume in homozygous mutant mice compared to vehicle‑treated homozygous controls. (Fig. 9d), but mineral density remained comparable to homozygous controls and lower than wildtype (Fig. S26). These findings suggested that verteporfin alleviated Hippo pathway suppression caused by the Kdf1 mutation.

Discussion

Previous studies have established KDF1 as a regulator of epidermal development.11 It stabilized IKKα through USP7-mediated deubiquitination, promoting keratinocyte proliferation-to-differentiation transition.12 In contrast, KDF1 gain-of-function variants (e.g., p.Phe251Leu, p.His254Tyr) showed enhanced affinity for IKKα, causing excessive stabilization and contributing to hidradenitis suppurativa and ectodermal dysplasia.13 Our team first reported the KDF1 mutation (c.908 G > C, p.R303P) in non-syndromic tooth agenesis patients,8 and subsequently, several novel KDF1 mutation sites have been reported in affected families.9,21,22 Beyond tooth agenesis, these patients exhibited dental hard tissue abnormalities. We generated a Kdf1 mutation mouse model that exhibited enamel microstructural defects in adult mice, with some mice displaying missing teeth.15 This model precisely replicates the human pathogenic mutation at a conserved amino acid residue, and recapitulates the core enamel defects, establishing its relevance for dissecting the pathogenesis. Further analysis revealed that while KDF1 expression persists during ameloblast differentiation, the mutation impairs its membrane targeting, suggesting that membrane-associated KDF1 is indispensable for enamel development (Fig. 10).

A schematic diagram comparing the molecular mechanisms in wild-type versus Kdf1 mutant ameloblasts. In WT ameloblasts, active Hippo kinase phosphorylates YAP, promoting cytoplasmic retention and controlled degradation, with nuclear YAP tightly regulated by VGLL4 to balance TEAD1-mediated transcription. In contrast, Kdf1 mutation impairs ITGB4 and CDH1 complex formation and reduces Hippo kinase activity, enabling YAP nuclear translocation with diminished VGLL4 competition, thereby upregulating proliferative genes Cyr61, Ctgf, and Myc

Kdf1 mutants exhibited severe enamel defects, including disorganized prisms and reduced thickness, accompanied by insufficient matrix deposition and defective mineralization.23,24 Molecularly, the mutation downregulated enamel matrix proteins and proteases in a graded manner, with homozygotes showing the most pronounced reductions and indicating a gene dosage effect.25,26 These histopathological features link Kdf1 mutation to failure in enamel secretion and mineralization.27–29 The absence of incisor cusp exposure in the mutant newborns likely represents a delay in eruption. The precise drivers of tooth eruption remain unclear but may involve coordinated processes such as alveolar bone remodeling,30,31 periodontal ligament transformation,32 and proper matrix formation.33,34 We infer that the observed delay might be a direct, secondary consequence of the enamel formation deficits and epithelial dysfunction in our model.

Beyond its known intracellular roles,10 we explored the functions of membrane-associated KDF1. Ultrastructurally, wildtype ameloblasts exhibited well-defined, continuous adherens junctions (200–500 nm in length, 35–50 nm in width)35 and distinct cell-matrix junctions.36 In contrast, mutant tissues exhibited shortened and widened adherens junctions with deteriorated matrix adhesion. KDF1 colocalized with CDH1 and ITGB4 in vivo, but the mutant KDF1 may destabilize this structure, leading to reduced junction molecules and adhesion while enhancing migration. The opposing function was verified by the up-regulation of CDH1 and ITGB4 following wildtype Kdf1 overexpression(Figure S12). These findings align with reports linking aberrant adhesion protein expression to disorganized ameloblast structure.18,37

As a key signaling hub downstream of cadherins and integrins, the Hippo-YAP pathway is a pivotal regulator of tooth development and epithelial homeostasis.38–41 Nuclear YAP-TEAD1 complexes promote proliferation by activating target genes Ctgf, Cyr61 and Myc, which drive cell cycle progression through facilitating G1-S transition, enhancing growth factor responses, and activating S-phase genes, respectively.42,43 This process is antagonized by VGLL4, which competes with YAP/TAZ for binding to TEAD1 through shared interaction sites.44–46 Kdf1 mutation disrupted this pathway. In vitro immunofluorescence showed reduced p-YAP in mutant ameloblasts, with a western blot revealing excessive nuclear YAP accumulation. Co-IP in cell lysates demonstrated enhanced TEAD1-YAP binding alongside decreased TEAD1-VGLL4 interaction, and nuclear YAP activated pro-proliferative genes Ctgf, Cyr61 and Myc. These molecular changes aligned with observed proliferation acceleration via G1-phase shortening and early S-phase onset in transfected cell lines. In vivo Ki67 staining revealed increased proliferation in cervical loop progenitors of Kdf1 mutants, with proliferative cells emerging within normally non-proliferative ameloblasts. This failure to exit the cell cycle depletes the progenitor pool and disrupts differentiation,47,48 likely underlying impaired ameloblast differentiation and function. This observation highlights that a shared underlying mechanism can drive interconnected, stage-specific phenotypes across distinct developmental windows, thereby linking the early deficit in IEE differentiation to the subsequent defects in ameloblast function. Thus, a shared mechanism links early IEE deficits to subsequent functional defects across developmental windows, consistent with YAP-TEAD1 functions.43,49 Collectively, Kdf1 mutation disrupted adhesion and weakened cell cohesion, accounting for migratory defects and reduced Hippo pathway input. The upregulation of proliferative genes then drove a proliferation-differentiation imbalance, ultimately compromising enamel formation.

The Kdf1 mutation enhances proliferation via Hippo-YAP inhibition but reduces tooth size, underscoring that tooth size depends on coordinated epithelial-mesenchymal interactions, not merely proliferation.50,51 Disrupting this balance in Kdf1 mutants leads to dysregulated growth and reduced size, mirroring studies where forced YAP activity causes malformation without enlargement,52 and loss of Ctnna1 induces ectopic proliferation but smaller tooth germs.53 Besides, YAP knockout triggers premature differentiation.54 Thus, precise YAP regulation coordinates patterning and differentiation, not mere proliferation. Reduced body size in Kdf1 mutant mice is also inconsistent with enhanced YAP activity. This may result from dysregulated proliferation causing compensatory growth suppression,55 metabolic burden,56 and could preferentially affect other pathways critical for growth in other organs,57,58 warranting further investigation.

Verteporfin, an FDA-approved drug for macular degeneration, acts as an inhibitor of the YAP-TEAD1 complex by blocking their interaction.59 Accordingly, verteporfin partially reversed the abnormal gene expression induced by the Kdf1 mutation, enhanced the cellular differentiation in vitro, and improved enamel deposition in vivo, which implies that the pathogenicity of the mutation depends on nuclear YAP–TEAD1 complex formation.60 The rescue effect also argued against a major contribution from TEAD-independent YAP functions, such as interactions with SMAD or p53 family members,38,61 as no downstream gene overexpression was observed after verteporfin treatment. Besides, verteporfin treatment slightly increased p-YAP levels, suggesting a potential feedback mechanism linking nuclear YAP–TEAD1 disruption to upstream phosphorylation dynamics,38,39 which offers new insight into YAP pathway homeostasis. While early intervention during the secretory stage rescued enamel volume, it did not increase final mineral density, suggesting that verteporfin’s therapeutic effect did not extend to the mineralization phase. Future studies will explore the mechanisms underlying in vivo mineralization.

In conclusion, the mutant KDF1 protein may disrupt cell adhesion, triggering YAP overactivation and ultimately giving rise to differentiation-defective dental epithelia. We demonstrated that KDF1 functioned at the membrane to stabilize adhesion junctions, and its mutation compromised this function. Future studies should complete proliferation and differentiation datasets in both incisor and molar models, and the mechanisms underlying the reduced body and organ size in mutant mice also merit further investigation.
