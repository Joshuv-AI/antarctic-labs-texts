A Chickpea NCR Peptide Suppresses Postharvest Alternaria alternata Infection by Disrupting Fungal Membranes, Mitochondria and Respiration
Authors: Alon Shomron, Raviraj Kalunke, James Godwin, Dilip Shah, Noam Alkan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostharvest fungal diseases cause substantial losses in horticultural crops and are commonly managed using chemical fungicides, which raise concerns regarding resistance development and environmental impact. Plant‐derived antifungal peptides represent a promising alternative for sustainable disease control. Here, we evaluated the antifungal activity and mode of action of three nodule‐specific cysteine‐rich (NCR) peptides—NCR044, NCR192 and NCR13_PFV2—against major postharvest fungal pathogens. Among the peptides tested, NCR13_PFV2 exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity against Alternaria alternata , Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, Lasiodiplodia theobromae and Penicillium expansum, although activity against C. gloeosporioides was predominantly fungistatic rather than fungicidal. Mechanistic analyses using A. alternata as a model pathogen revealed that NCR13_PFV2 rapidly associates with fungal membranes, induces membrane permeabilization and exhibits selective binding to anionic phospholipids and phosphoinositides. Peptide treatment was associated with mitochondrial depolarization, elevated reactive oxygen species accumulation, reduced cellular respiration and disruption of vacuolar integrity. These intracellular effects were accompanied by loss of turgor pressure and hyphal collapse. Importantly, application of NCR13_PFV2 to wounded bell pepper fruit significantly reduced lesion development caused by A. alternata in a concentration‐dependent manner. Together, these findings demonstrate that NCR13_PFV2 suppresses postharvest fungal infection through a multi‐target mechanism that disrupts fungal cellular homeostasis, highlighting its potential as a peptide‐based alternative to synthetic fungicides.

Introduction

As the global population grows, so does food demand. Pathogenic fungi alone are responsible for a third of all wasted crops worldwide, where the source of contamination can be traced back to any stage of produce production, from the field to harvest, transport, storage, to retail and up to the consumer (Sutton 1990; Eckert and Eaks 1989). Such a potent problem of fungal decay has led to the use of chemical fungicides, which have become an indispensable source of global food security. However, consumers worldwide demand a longer shelf life of agricultural produce with reduced fungicides (Rydlo et al. 2006). Due to consumer demand and environmental concerns, many alternative approaches to chemical fungicides have been studied over the years (Samada and Tambunan 2020). One potential solution involves harnessing naturally occurring antifungal peptides and proteins produced by plants' immune systems, which evolved over eons alongside pathogenic fungal attacks. These peptides are largely noncytotoxic to plants and humans and therefore present a promising potential for the next generation of products used in decay control and extending shelf‐life (Holzknecht and Marx 2024).

Antimicrobial peptides/proteins (AMPs) are a major component of the innate immune response of all plants. Among AMPs, antifungal proteins/peptides (AFPs) have been recognized as an innovative measure to control fungal infection (Ul Haq et al. 2024; Lobo and Boto 2022; Tang et al. 2023; Rosa et al. 2022). These small peptides (< 100 amino acids) usually exhibit broad‐spectrum antifungal activity against several species of fungi, inhibiting basic fungal physiological processes which could include spore germination and mycelial growth, and causing both the spores and hyphae to become shrivelled, swollen or generally deformed (Thery et al. 2019). Depending upon the peptide and fungal pathogen, AFPs exhibit different, often multifunctional modes of action (MoAs) (Alunni and Gourion 2016; van der Weerden et al. 2013).

Legumes of the inverted repeat lacking clade synthesize small defensin‐like peptides called nodule‐specific cysteine‐rich (NCR) peptides, which are exclusively expressed in the root nodules and nowhere else in the plant (Mergaert et al. 2003). Several hundred NCRs have been discovered in different legumes; they are typically 30–50 amino acids in length and diverse in sequence, and their net charge can be anionic, neutral or cationic. They contain regularly spaced 4–6 cysteine residues within the coding sequence, forming 2–3 disulphide bridges. Some NCR peptides can generate disulphide pairing variants that differ in antimicrobial properties (Godwin et al. 2024; Shabab et al. 2016).

Several NCR peptides have been studied for their antimicrobial activity and MoA. Thus, 105 chemically synthesized NCR peptides differing in length, sequence and net charge from five different IRLC legumes were tested, and 50 of them were determined to have antibacterial activity that differed in potency and spectrum (Lima et al. 2022). In an earlier study, 19 cationic NCRs from Medicago truncatula were tested for their ability to inhibit the growth of a human fungal pathogen Candida albicans . Of these, nine with a net charge above +9 inhibited the growth of this pathogen at concentrations ranging from 1.5 to 10.5 μM (Órdögh et al. 2014). Previously, we reported that a M. truncatula 36‐amino acid NCR044 with two disulphide bonds and a unique structure exhibited potent antifungal activity at low micromolar concentrations against economically important plant fungal pathogens, including Botrytis cinerea. Topically sprayed NCR044 on the surface of the leaves of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) and Nicotiana benthamiana plants reduced Botrytis cinerea (grey mould) fungal disease symptoms (Velivelli et al. 2020). Chickpea (Cicer arietinum) NCR13 is a unique 32‐amino acid cationic peptide. When expressed in Pichia pastoris, it generated two disulphide cross‐linking variants, NCR13 Peptide Folding Variant1 (PFV1) and NCR13_PFV2, with an identical amino acid sequence, but exhibited profound differences in antifungal activity, with NCR13_PFV1 showing consistently higher potency, MoA and ability to control grey mould disease in pepper (Capsicum annuum) and tomato plants when used as fungicides (Godwin et al. 2024).

In recent years, the multi‐target MoA of NCR peptides against plant fungal pathogens has begun to be understood (Le et al. 2017; Polikanov et al. 2018). For example, NCR044 permeabilizes the plasma membrane, induces the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and travels to nucleoli in the germinated conidia (germlings) cells of B. cinerea (Velivelli et al. 2020). NCR13_PFV1 and NCR13_PFV2 also permeabilize the plasma membrane and travel to nucleoli in the germling cells of this pathogen. In addition, they bind to ribosomal RNA and inhibit protein synthesis in vitro. However, they greatly differ in their intracellular stability in fungal cells and their efficiency in executing these functions (Godwin et al. 2024).

The antifungal potential of NCR peptides against postharvest fungal pathogens of fruits remains largely unexplored. Here, we show that M. truncatula NCR044, NCR192 and chickpea NCR13_PFV2 exhibit significant differences in antifungal activity in vitro against four different postharvest fruit pathogens: Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, Lasiodiplodia theobromae, Penicillium expansum and Alternaria alternata . NCR13_PFV2 was most effective in inhibiting the hyphal growth of these fungi in vitro. It significantly reduced disease symptoms caused by A. alternata in wounded bell pepper fruits. Mechanistic studies provided insight into the multi‐faceted MoA of this peptide in this postharvest fruit pathogen. This study paves the way for subsequent research to optimize peptide sequences and configurations to enhance their antifungal efficacy, thereby reducing economic losses and minimizing reliance on synthetic chemical fungicides.

We hypothesized that NCR13_PFV2 suppresses postharvest fungal infection through a multi‐target mechanism involving plasma membrane destabilization, lipid‐specific interactions, and downstream disruption of mitochondrial function and cellular bioenergetics. To test this hypothesis, we compared the antifungal activity of multiple NCR peptides across economically relevant postharvest pathogens, resolved the temporal sequence of cellular events in A. alternata , and evaluated disease suppression in harvested fruit. By integrating functional assays with mechanistic imaging and lipid‐binding analyses, this study advances molecular understanding of how plant‐derived NCR peptides interfere with fungal pathogenesis during postharvest infection.

Results

NCR044, NCR192 and NCR13_PFV2 Inhibit Fungal Germination and Growth In Vitro

The effects of the three peptides (NCR044, NCR192 and NCR13_PFV2; Table S1) when combined at a concentration of 12 μM each were examined in vitro against four postharvest fungal pathogens (Table S2). The combined treatment was used as an initial screening approach to compare the overall antifungal potential of the three NCR peptides. The combined treatment inhibited 75%–100% growth of A. alternata , L. theobromae and P. expansum during the first 72 h post‐treatment (hpt). The combined peptide treatment was not designed to formally assess synergistic interactions, but rather to evaluate overall inhibitory capacity. Accordingly, all subsequent mechanistic and efficacy analyses were performed using individual peptides rather than peptide combinations. When tested individually, all three peptides inhibited fungal growth in 0.25% potato dextrose broth (PDB) in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 1). NCR13_PFV2 consistently exhibited greater inhibitory activity than NCR192, which was more potent than NCR044 (Figure 1, Figure S1). NCR044 did not inhibit P. expansum under the conditions tested.

Quantitative analysis of germination and germ‐tube elongation revealed that L. theobromae was the most sensitive species, followed by P. expansum , A. alternata and C. gloeosporioides (Figure 1). All NCRs, whether applied individually or as a peptide consortium, displayed fungistatic activity (temporary inhibition of fungal growth without irreversible loss of viability) against C. gloeosporioides, with mycelial growth resuming after an average inhibition period of approximately 19 h (Figure 1g). In contrast, appressoria formation in C. gloeosporioides was fully inhibited by NCR13_PFV2 and reduced by 60%–80% by NCR044 and NCR192 at 12 μM after 72 h (Figure S2). This pattern indicates a predominantly fungistatic response, in which peptide treatment delays fungal development but does not permanently eliminate fungal viability.

Characterization of the Antifungal Activity of NCR13_PFV2 Against A. alternata

Based on the results shown in Figure 1, NCR13_PFV2 was selected for further characterization against A. alternata . Conidia were treated with NCR13_PFV2 in 0.25% PDB for 16 h under continuous agitation, then transferred to glass slides for germination and analysis after an additional 24 h. Increasing peptide concentrations resulted in increased membrane permeabilization, reduced germ‐tube elongation and decreased germination rates (Figure S3). Under continuously agitated conditions, no germination of A. alternata was detected following NCR13_PFV2 treatment, whereas 64% germination was observed when conidia were treated on glass surfaces (Figure 1, Figure S3). To standardize experimental conditions and facilitate mechanistic analysis, conidia were allowed to germinate prior to peptide treatment in all subsequent experiments.

NCR13_PFV2 Antifungal Activity Exhibits Concentration and Exposure Time Dependency

The duration of NCR13_PFV2 exposure significantly affected spore germination and germ‐tube elongation. Under continuous exposure, concentrations of 0.375–0.75 μM did not suppress germination or mycelial growth, and germ tubes continued to elongate between 24 and 48 hpt. In contrast, concentrations ranging from 1.5 to 12 μM reduced mycelial expansion within 24 hpt (Figure S4A). At 12 μM, loss of internal cellular compartmentalization was observed within 60 min of treatment (Figure S4B,C).

At 24 μM, germination was strongly inhibited, and exposure‐time dependence was therefore assessed at this concentration. A 5‐min exposure to 24 μM NCR13_PFV2 reduced germination at 24 hpt from 96% in controls to 32%, whereas continuous exposure for 24 h reduced germination by 99% (Figure S4D).

These data demonstrate that both peptide concentration and exposure duration influence the extent of growth inhibition and cellular disruption. Based on these observations, peptide concentrations could be categorized as sub‐permeabilizing (< 0.75 μM), permeabilizing (≥ 0.75 μM) or collapse‐inducing (≥ 12 μM). Sub‐permeabilizing concentrations produced little or no detectable propidium iodide uptake, permeabilizing concentrations produced clear intracellular propidium iodide fluorescence indicating membrane disruption, and collapse‐inducing concentrations resulted in rapid hyphal collapse accompanied by intense propidium iodide staining.

Alternaria alternata Developmental Stages Display Differential Susceptibility to NCR13_PFV2

To assess stage‐dependent susceptibility, A. alternata conidia were incubated in 0.25% PDB and treated with NCR13_PFV2 (6 μM) either at inoculation (0 h) or after 8 h, following germ‐tube emergence. Peptide treatment resulted in greater inhibition of germlings compared to dormant conidia (Figure S5D,E).

Quantitative analysis of germ‐tube length showed that peptide application at 16 h post‐inoculation caused stronger suppression of hyphal elongation than treatments applied at 0 or 24 h (Figure S5A,C). Differences in inhibition magnitude were dependent on the developmental stage at which NCR13_PFV2 was applied.

Peptide Treatment Induces Fungal Permeabilization

Membrane permeabilization was assessed using propidium iodide, a cell‐impermeable fluorescent dye that stains nucleic acids following loss of membrane integrity (Park et al. 2000). In L. theobromae, fully mature, melanized conidia were not permeable to propidium iodide following peptide treatment, whereas immature, unmelanized conidia exhibited propidium iodide uptake (Figure S6).

In A. alternata , propidium iodide fluorescence was detected within 1 min of treatment with NCR13_PFV2 in both conidia and germlings (Figure 2a). Complete nuclear staining and cellular collapse were observed within 15 min of treatment (Figure 2c,d). At lower peptide concentrations, propidium iodide uptake was observed at concentrations as low as 0.75 μM but not at 0.375 μM (Figure 2g).

NCR13_PFV2 Does Not Induce Direct Fungal DNA Degradation

DAPI staining was used to assess nuclear and extranuclear DNA following NCR13_PFV2 treatment. A. alternata conidia were germinated in 0.25% PDB for 16 h prior to peptide exposure and subsequent DAPI staining. After 5 h of treatment, DAPI fluorescence was detected in extranuclear compartments in addition to nuclei (Figure S7).

DNA integrity was further examined by agarose gel electrophoresis of extracted DNA. No detectable evidence of large‐scale DNA fragmentation was observed following treatment with NCR13_PFV2 (Figure S7), indicating that the peptide did not produce detectable genomic DNA strand breaks under the conditions tested.

At 5 min post‐treatment (mpt), TAMRA‐labelled NCR13_PFV2 was detected at the cell periphery, colocalizing with the plasma membrane and adjacent to the cell wall (Figure 3a–d). By 45 mpt, localized regions of hyphae exhibited loss of turgor pressure and structural collapse, and the peptide signal was detected within the cytoplasm and along the plasma membrane (Figure 3e–h). In these regions, calcofluor white fluorescence was markedly reduced or absent (Figure 3e,f).

At 1.5 h post‐treatment, TAMRA‐labelled NCR13_PFV2 was detected within intact hyphal regions, where it accumulated in discrete intracellular structures (Figure 3i–m).

Lipid‐binding specificity of NCR13_PFV2 was examined using a PIP strip assay containing membrane‐spotted lipids at decreasing concentrations (Figure 4). NCR13_PFV2 bound strongly to phosphatidic acid (PA), with a detectable signal across the concentration range, but did not bind to phosphatidylinositol (PI) lacking phosphate substitutions on the inositol headgroup (Figure 4a). Binding assays using phosphorylated PI derivatives revealed interaction with mono‐, bis‐ and tris‐phosphorylated phosphoinositides, including PI(3)P, PI(4)P, PI(5)P, PI(3,4)P2, PI(3,5)P2, PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,4,5)P3 (Figure 4b). Binding intensity generally increased with lipid phosphorylation state, although this relationship was not strictly linear. In contrast, minimal or no binding was detected for several other membrane lipids, including phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylglycerol (PG), sphingomyelin (SM) and diacylglycerol (DAG).

NCR13_PFV2 Disrupts Mitochondrial Function, Elevates ROS Production and Impairs Respiration in A. alternata

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) was assessed using the fluorescent probe JC‐1. In untreated A. alternata germlings, JC‐1 fluorescence was predominantly red, indicating polarized mitochondrial membranes. Following treatment with 12 μM NCR13_PFV2, fluorescence shifted toward green monomers, consistent with mitochondrial depolarization (Figure 5a). Quantification of the red‐to‐green fluorescence ratio revealed a sustained decrease beginning within several min of peptide exposure (Figure 5b).

NCR13_PFV2 Impacts Fungal Cell Wall Chitin Integrity

Chitin, a major fungal cell wall polysaccharide, was visualized using calcofluor white following treatment of A. alternata hyphae with purified water or TAMRA‐labelled NCR13_PFV2. At early time points following peptide treatment, hyphae remained structurally intact and calcofluor white fluorescence was readily detected along the cell wall.

By 60 min post‐treatment, treated hyphae exhibited loss of turgor pressure and structural collapse, accompanied by a marked reduction or absence of calcofluor white fluorescence (Figure S8). No comparable changes were observed in water‐treated controls.

NCR13_PFV2 Suppresses A. alternata Lesion Development on Harvested Bell Pepper Fruit

To assess postharvest antifungal activity, wounded bell pepper fruits were inoculated with A. alternata and treated with NCR13_PFV2 at concentrations of 24 or 48 μM. These concentrations are consistent with the higher peptide doses typically required for in vivo efficacy relative to in vitro conditions. Lesion diameters were measured 7 days post‐inoculation. Peptide‐treated fruits exhibited a concentration‐dependent reduction in lesion size, with mean lesion diameters of 0.59 and 0.25 cm at 24 and 48 μM, respectively, compared to 1.07 cm in sterile water‐treated controls (Figure 7a). These differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Representative images showed reduced necrotic lesion development in peptide‐treated fruits relative to controls (Figure 7b).

Discussion

Using synthetic chemical fungicides is crucial for crop protection but has well‐documented environmental and human health drawbacks, and extensive reliance on single‐site MoAs has accelerated the emergence of resistant fungal populations (Holzknecht and Marx 2024; Ul Haq et al. 2024; Thery et al. 2019). Consequently, safe and sustainable alternatives to chemical fungicides are urgently needed. In this study, we explored the potential of small antifungal NCR peptides to control postharvest fungal pathogens of fruits. Although NCR peptides are natively expressed in legume root nodules during symbiosis, their biochemical properties can be decoupled from their native biological context and exploited as exogenously applied antifungal agents, analogous to the repurposing of plant defensins and other host‐derived AMPs for crop protection. Rather than focusing solely on disease suppression, this study aimed to resolve the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which NCR13_PFV2 interferes with fungal growth during postharvest infection, placing NCR‐mediated antifungal activity within a mechanistic framework relevant to molecular plant pathology.

We tested the antifungal activity of three different NCR peptides, NCR044, NCR192 and NCR13_PFV2, that differed in their sequence, number of disulphide bonds and net charge. Among these peptides, NCR13_PFV2 demonstrated the most potent antifungal activity against A. alternata , L. theobromae, P. expansum and C. gloeosporioides (Figure 1). NCR192 and NCR044 were less effective against these pathogens. These differences highlight the importance of peptide folding, charge distribution and sequence‐specific features in determining antifungal potency, consistent with previous observations that disulphide pairing variants of NCR peptides can exhibit distinct biological activities (Godwin et al. 2024; Shabab et al. 2016). When applied in combination, all three peptides exhibited strong growth inhibition of A. alternata , L. theobromae and P. expansum , whereas C. gloeosporioides displayed predominantly fungistatic responses (Figure 1, Table S2).

The multifaceted mechanisms of AFPs, including plant defensins, have been shown to promote synergistic activity when multiple targets are engaged simultaneously (Vriens et al. 2014). Such synergy arises from the capacity of different peptides, or different functional domains within a peptide, to interfere with distinct cellular processes. In the case of NCR peptides, reported targets include the plasma membrane, mitochondria, ribosomes and nucleoli, resulting in layered disruption of fungal cellular organization (Godwin et al. 2024; Velivelli et al. 2020). This multi‐target mode of action is expected to impose a higher barrier to resistance development compared to single‐site antifungal agents. Ungerminated and melanized conidia of L. theobromae exhibited greater resistance to peptide‐induced permeabilization than immature, unmelanized conidia (Figure S6). Similarly, A. alternata conidia were less susceptible to NCR13_PFV2 than actively growing germlings. This stage‐dependent susceptibility likely reflects differences in cell wall architecture and metabolic activity: germlings undergo rapid polarized growth and continuous cell wall remodelling, processes that may transiently expose membrane‐proximal regions, whereas mature conidia possess thicker and more structurally reinforced cell walls that confer increased resistance to chemical and environmental stressors (Chevalier et al. 2023).

The composition and organization of the fungal cell wall are key determinants of susceptibility to antifungal agents. Melanin and extracellular matrix components can act as physical and chemical barriers that restrict penetration of antimicrobial compounds (Nosanchuk and Casadevall 2003; van Duin et al. 2002). In melanized conidia, cross‐linked layers of chitin, glucans, and melanin form a dense extracellular matrix that enhances structural rigidity and limits access to the plasma membrane. The reduced permeabilization of melanized conidia observed in this study suggests that cell wall architecture substantially influences susceptibility to NCR13_PFV2, although whether melanin directly interferes with peptide binding or indirectly restricts access to membrane targets remains unresolved (Qin and Xia 2024).

Because NCR13_PFV2 exhibited the strongest and most consistent antifungal activity, it was selected for detailed mechanistic characterization. Although L. theobromae displayed the greatest overall sensitivity to all three NCR peptides based on inhibition of germination and germ tube development (Figure 1), A. alternata was chosen as the primary model system due to its reproducible growth, high spore yield, readily observable morphological responses and the availability of genetically tractable strains.

In contrast to L. theobromae, which typically acts as an endogenous pathogen colonizing internal vascular tissues (Galsurker et al. 2018), A. alternata is an exophytic pathogen that initiates infection from the fruit surface. This infection strategy makes A. alternata particularly suitable for mechanistic analysis of externally applied AMPs and their interactions with fungal cells during early stages of infection. In addition, the limited availability of environmentally sustainable control measures for A. alternata underscores the relevance of elucidating NCR peptide‐mediated antifungal mechanisms in this pathosystem.

Treatment of A. alternata germlings with NCR13_PFV2 resulted in membrane permeabilization at concentrations as low as 0.75 μM (Figure 2). Spatially resolved imaging revealed preferential permeabilization at distal hyphal regions near actively growing tips compared to regions proximal to the conidial head. Rather than introducing a distinct susceptibility mechanism, this spatial pattern is consistent with the heightened vulnerability of polarized growth zones, which undergo continuous cell wall remodelling and membrane expansion. Regions farther from the hyphal apex possess more mature and structurally reinforced cell walls, likely conferring increased resistance to peptide‐induced permeabilization. These observations further support the conclusion that NCR13_PFV2 preferentially compromises fungal cells at sites of active growth, contributing to the increased sensitivity of germlings relative to dormant conidia.

NCR13_PFV2 treatment increased membrane permeabilization in P. expansum and B. cinerea , similar to its effects on A. alternata (Figure 2, Figure S9). In A. alternata , a concentration of 0.75 μM was sufficient to induce membrane permeabilization but did not fully suppress mycelial growth, as conidial development into mature mycelium remained detectable even at higher concentrations such as 12 μM. These observations are consistent with previous studies showing that small, cationic, cysteine‐rich AFPs, including plant defensins and NCRs, can disrupt fungal membrane integrity and/or traverse the membrane to interact with intracellular targets (Vriens et al. 2014; Cools et al. 2017; Parisi et al. 2019; Guerra‐Garcia and Sankari 2025).

Consistent with this model, TAMRA‐labelled NCR13_PFV2 localized to fungal membranes adjacent to the cell wall (Figure 3), and the peptide exhibited preferential binding to phosphatidic acid and multiple phosphorylated phosphoinositides in vitro (Figure 4), recognizing that PIP strip assays provide a simplified representation of lipid‐binding preferences rather than native membrane organization. The strong interaction of NCR13_PFV2 with phosphatidic acid and phosphoinositides, particularly PtdIns(4,5)P2 and PtdIns(3,4,5)P3, suggests that membrane association is driven primarily by electrostatic interactions with negatively charged, signalling‐enriched lipid headgroups. Although binding generally increased with lipid phosphorylation state, deviations from strict linearity suggest that lipid accessibility and headgroup geometry also influence peptide–lipid interactions. These findings support a model in which NCR13_PFV2 preferentially associates with anionic membrane microdomains, facilitating membrane destabilization and subsequent intracellular effects that contribute to its multifaceted antifungal activity.

Exposure to NCR13_PFV2 resulted in a pronounced reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential, as indicated by a decreased JC‐1 red‐to‐green fluorescence ratio. This reduction was detectable within 30 min of treatment (Figure 5), consistent with rapid mitochondrial depolarization following peptide exposure. Whether ROS accumulation arises directly from mitochondrial depolarization or from earlier membrane‐associated stress responses remains unresolved and may involve overlapping mechanisms. These findings indicate that mitochondrial function is an early intracellular target affected during NCR13_PFV2‐mediated antifungal activity. Mitochondria are a major intracellular source of ROS, and sustained ROS accumulation can lead to oxidative damage of nucleic acids, proteins and lipids, ultimately compromising mitochondrial function and cell viability (Suski et al. 2012). In response to NCR13_PFV2 treatment, mitochondrial dysfunction was accompanied by a concentration‐dependent increase in ROS levels and a concomitant reduction in cellular respiration (Figures 5 and 6). The observed coupling between decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨ) and elevated ROS is consistent with established links between mitochondrial depolarization, impaired electron transport, and oxidative stress (Suski et al. 2012). These findings indicate that NCR13_PFV2‐induced mitochondrial perturbation is closely associated with oxidative imbalance and bioenergetic collapse.

Together, these observations support a model in which plasma membrane destabilization represents the initiating event of NCR13_PFV2 activity. Following membrane permeabilization, the peptide enters fungal cells and is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, increased ROS accumulation, and metabolic activity collapse, indicating that these intracellular effects arise as downstream consequences of membrane disruption. Initial membrane perturbation likely triggers a rapid cellular stress response associated with elevated ROS production, while the subsequent interaction of the highly cationic peptide with negatively charged mitochondrial membranes may further compromise mitochondrial integrity. This compounded stress is consistent with the observed increase in ROS levels and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. Overall, the data support a progressive interplay between plasma membrane disruption, oxidative imbalance and mitochondrial damage during NCR13_PFV2‐mediated antifungal activity. Although the present experiments do not distinguish whether mitochondrial dysfunction represents a primary intracellular target or a secondary consequence of membrane permeabilization, the rapid temporal sequence of events suggests that mitochondrial perturbation occurs shortly after peptide entry into fungal cells.

Assessment of fungal metabolic activity using the vacuole‐sensitive dye FUN‐1 revealed early disruption of vacuolar organization following NCR13_PFV2 treatment. In untreated cells, red‐fluorescent intravacuolar structures were readily detected, consistent with intact vacuolar function and active metabolism. In contrast, within 30 min of peptide exposure, vacuolar structures became disorganized and indistinguishable from the surrounding cytoplasm (Figure 6a, Figure S11). Quantitative analysis showed a rapid decrease in the FUN‐1 red‐to‐green fluorescence ratio within 5 min of treatment, reaching minimal levels by 60 min (Figure 6b, Figure S11). These results indicate that NCR13_PFV2 rapidly compromises vacuolar integrity and metabolic function, consistent with a loss of cellular homeostasis following peptide exposure.

At the macroscopic level, antifungal peptides have been reported to induce a range of morphological abnormalities in fungal cells, including swelling, distortion, and structural collapse of hyphae and spores. Consistent with these reports, NCR13_PFV2 treatment resulted in pronounced physical alterations of fungal structures, most notably loss of turgor pressure in the hyphae of A. alternata and C. gloeosporioides, which became evident approximately 60 min after treatment (Figure 6a,b; Figures S2 and S10). Similar morphological disruption has been described for other membrane‐active AFPs (Zhao et al. 2022). In addition, L. theobromae conidia exposed to NCR13_PFV2 exhibited clear structural damage, including warping, cracking, and rupture of the spore wall (Figure S10), further supporting a loss of cellular integrity following peptide treatment.

AMPs have been reported to target nucleic acids or interfere with translation by interacting with ribosomal components (Le et al. 2017; Park et al. 2000). Previous studies demonstrated that NCR044 localizes to fungal nucleoli, the site of ribosome biogenesis and that NCR247 interacts with ribosomal proteins in symbiotic Sinorhizobium meliloti (Farkas et al. 2014), highlighting the capacity of NCR peptides to engage intracellular targets beyond membranes. Whether the disulphide bonds of NCR13_PFV2 remain intact following entry into the reducing intracellular environment of fungal cells remains unknown and warrants future investigation. In this study, A. alternata germlings treated with NCR13_PFV2 exhibited dispersed DAPI fluorescence within hyphae 5 h after treatment, whereas control, NCR044‐ and NCR192‐treated samples retained normal nuclear staining patterns (Figure S5A). Importantly, agarose gel electrophoresis revealed no detectable genomic DNA fragmentation in any treatment at either time point (Figure S5B). These findings indicate that NCR13_PFV2 does not induce direct DNA cleavage. The altered DAPI staining pattern observed following NCR13_PFV2 treatment is therefore more consistent with loss of membrane integrity and redistribution of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, rather than classical programmed cell death involving genomic fragmentation. Together, these results support a model in which NCR13_PFV2 compromises fungal cellular integrity through a multi‐tiered mechanism without directly targeting genomic DNA.

AFPs that exhibit potent activity through diverse MoAs represent promising alternatives to conventional chemical fungicides. NCR13_PFV2 is particularly notable due to its capacity to disrupt plasma membrane integrity and induce downstream effects that include cell wall destabilization, mitochondrial dysfunction, vacuolar collapse and metabolic impairment. Many widely used chemical fungicides act by targeting a single cellular pathway, such as ergosterol biosynthesis in the case of azoles, rendering them vulnerable to the rapid emergence of resistant fungal populations (Fisher et al. 2018). While resistance development was not experimentally assessed in this study, the engagement of multiple cellular targets by NCR13_PFV2 is expected to impose a higher evolutionary barrier to resistance compared with single‐target antifungal agents, consistent with current models of AMP activity (Wang et al. 2021). Consequently, small NCR peptides with multifaceted MoAs represent attractive candidates for the development of sustainable, green‐chemistry‐based antifungal strategies.

Previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of NCR044 and NCR13_PFV2 folding variants (PFV1 and PFV2) as spray‐applied antifungal agents for controlling grey mould in tomato and pepper plants (Godwin et al. 2024; Velivelli et al. 2020). Building on this work, the present study evaluated NCR044, NCR192 and NCR13_PFV2 against multiple filamentous fungal pathogens, with a particular focus on elucidating the multi‐target antifungal activity of NCR13_PFV2. In addition to in vitro analyses, antifungal efficacy was assessed using a postharvest fruit infection assay, where treatment of wounded bell pepper fruit with NCR13_PFV2 resulted in a dose‐dependent reduction of A. alternata lesion development. Direct comparison of NCR13_PFV2 efficacy with commercial postharvest fungicides was intentionally beyond the scope of this mechanistic study, which was designed to resolve MoA features rather than perform product‐level benchmarking, but will be essential for future translational evaluation under commercial postharvest conditions.

These findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating the effectiveness of NCR13_PFV2 variants against B. cinerea on tomato and bell pepper leaves (Velivelli et al. 2020), as well as reduced grey mould symptoms following foliar application under greenhouse conditions (Godwin et al. 2024). Collectively, these observations support a model in which plasma membrane destabilization is closely coupled with intracellular effects, including mitochondrial perturbation and oxidative stress, which may proceed in overlapping or sequential phases depending on peptide concentration and exposure time.

Increasing emphasis has been placed on validating AFP activity under conditions that approximate commercial application environments. In this regard, the use of wounded fruit infection assays provides a relevant translational framework for assessing peptide efficacy beyond controlled in vitro systems, supporting the potential utility of NCR13_PFV2 in postharvest disease management.

While NCR peptides show strong antifungal potential, their practical deployment as plant protection products presents several challenges, particularly with respect to large‐scale production. Extraction from natural sources yields limited quantities, and chemical synthesis is generally cost‐effective only for relatively short peptides (Saubenova et al. 2024). NCR13_PFV2 can be produced recombinantly using a P. pastoris expression system in a bioreactor; however, expression results in two disulphide‐bonded folding variants, PFV1 and PFV2 (Godwin et al. 2024). Importantly, postharvest applications require comparatively small amounts of peptide, as treatments are applied collectively to harvested produce rather than repeatedly in the field, potentially mitigating production‐related constraints.

Nonetheless, additional studies will be required to fully define the translational potential of NCR13_PFV2. Evaluation of its performance at larger scales across diverse fruit and vegetable postharvest systems will be important for determining optimal application timing, dosage and delivery strategies. In parallel, systematic assessment of potential off‐target effects, including interactions with plant tissues and mammalian cell systems, will be necessary to establish safety profiles relevant to agricultural use. Such studies will be critical for advancing NCR13_PFV2 from experimental validation toward practical postharvest deployment.

In summary, this study elucidates the multifaceted antifungal mechanisms of NCR peptides and demonstrates their capacity to suppress fungal growth and disease development in postharvest produce. NCR13_PFV2 acts through a multi‐target MoA involving plasma membrane destabilization, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, vacuolar collapse and metabolic impairment, resulting in loss of fungal cellular homeostasis. These findings provide mechanistic insight into NCR peptide activity in postharvest fungal pathogens and support the further development of NCR13_PFV2 as a peptide‐based alternative to synthetic fungicides.

Experimental Procedures

AFPs

The properties of three peptides, NCR044, NCR192 and NCR13_PFV2 used in the present study are summarized in Table S1, and the predicted three‐dimensional structure of NCR13 is shown in Figure S12. All three peptides were expressed in P. pastoris and purified to homogeneity using ion exchange chromatography and HPLC using previously described protocols (Godwin et al. 2024; Velivelli et al. 2020).

Fungal Pathogens

The fungal pathogens C. gloeosporioides (Cg14), L. theobromae (Avo62), P. expansum (Pe21) and A. alternata (Aa15) were routinely grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Difco), incubated at 23°C. To produce conidia, C. gloeosporioides was grown in potato dextrose broth (PDB) (Difco) in an orbital‐shaker incubator at 28°C, and L. theobromae was grown in minimal medium with starch replacing glucose at 23°C. All growth media contained 0.25 g/L chloramphenicol. All fungal conidia were isolated by suspending mycelium in sterile water and filtering through autoclaved gauze. The concentration of conidia was determined using a haemocytometer and diluted to 105 conidia mL−1.

Growth Media Calibration

To calibrate growth media concentration for subsequent experiments, A. alternata , L. theobromae, C. gloeosporioides or P. expansum conidia (10 μL of 105 conidia mL−1) were placed in a 96‐well plate with various concentrations of PDB solutions (0.125%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 1%). Each solution was combined with a mixture of NCR044 + NCR192 + NCR13_PFV2, each at a concentration of 12 μM. The plate was incubated at 23°C, and optical density measurements were taken every hour for 66 h at 600 nm (OD600) based on Duanis‐Assaf et al. (2021). 0.25% PDB was selected as the concentration of choice (Table S2). Each treatment was made in triplicate. The OD values of three test wells of replicates were averaged together, and the background was corrected by subtracting the average OD of control wells with medium alone.

Fungal Growth Kinetics

Fungal growth kinetics were measured using a MicroAmp optical 96‐well reaction plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Applied Biosystems). A 10 μL aliquot of conidial suspension (105 conidia mL−1) of each fungus (C. gloeosporioides, L. theobromae, P. expansum or A. alternata ) was added to individual wells containing 140 μL of 0.25% PDB supplemented with either NCR044, NCR192 or NCR13_PFV2 at final concentrations of 12, 6 or 3 μM, or without peptide to serve as biological controls. Background wells (150 μL; no conidia) were prepared with 0.25% PDB supplemented with NCR044, NCR192 or NCR13_PFV2 at the same final concentrations. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. Plates were incubated at 23°C, and OD measurements were recorded every hour for 60 h at 600 nm (OD600) using a Synergy LX plate reader (BioTek) (Duanis‐Assaf et al. 2021). OD values from replicate wells were averaged and background‐corrected by subtracting the mean OD values of the corresponding background wells.

Assessment of Peptide Activity on Conidial Germination and Conidial Tube Length

For each fungus, conidial suspension of 105 conidia mL−1 was added to a glass slide, followed by the addition of a peptide (NCR044, NCR192 or NCR13_PFV2) at a final concentration of 12, 6 or 3 μM. For P. expansum conidia only, 0.25% PDB was added to elicit germination, as P. expansum spores germinate poorly in sterile water on glass. For control, sterile purified water (Synergy UV water purification systems, Merck Millipore) was added without the peptide. A photographic depiction of the NCRs' effect on A. alternata can be seen in Figure S1. The control and each peptide treatment were tested in triplicate. The slides were incubated in a humid chamber at 23°C for 16 h. Each slide was photographed and analysed under a microscope (IX83 inverted imaging system, Olympus). The lengths of conidial germ tubes and germination percentages were measured across four photographs for each droplet using Fiji (ImageJ) with the NeuronJ plug‐in. The percentage of appressoria formation was also assessed where relevant, such as in the case of C. gloeosporioides (Figure S2).

Calibration of the Antifungal Activity of Peptides

To assess the potency of peptide treatment on fungal growth kinetics, A. alternata was grown in a 96‐well plate and 10 μL of a 105 conidia mL−1 suspension was added to wells containing NCR13_PFV2 at final concentrations of 12, 6, 3, 1.5, 0.75 or 0.375 μM. The addition of NCR13_PFV2 was done at two time points, at time 0 or after 8 h. Control wells were filled with 140 μL of 0.25% PDB and 10 μL of conidial suspension (105 conidia mL−1) without supplementing peptides. 150 μL blank wells were made with 0.25% PDB supplemented with NCR13_PFV2 at concentrations of 0.375, 0.75, 1.5, 3, 6 or 12 μM.

To assess the potency of peptide treatment on germ tube elongation under a light microscope, aqueous solutions of 10 μL of 105 conidia mL−1 in 0.25% PDB were pipetted onto glass slides. NCR13_PFV2 was added at different time points (0, 16 or 24 h) at concentrations of 0.375, 0.75, 1.5, 3, 6 or 12 μM. Measurements were taken 16, 24 and 48 hpt.

To assess the potency of peptides as a function of exposure time, A. alternata conidia were tested in two experiments: one for short‐term and another for long‐term exposure to the NCR13_PFV2 peptide. In the long‐term exposure test, conidia were fully submerged in various concentrations of NCR13_PFV2 (0.375, 0.75, 1.5, 3, 6 or 12 μM) for 16 h on a rotary table at 23°C, followed by pipetting them onto glass slides and allowing them to germinate for 24 h. The slides were subsequently treated with propidium iodide, and were examined for germination, conidial tube length, and permeabilization under a fluorescent microscope (Figure S3). For the short exposure test, conidia submerged in 0.25% PDB were mixed in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes with NCR13_PFV2 (24 μM) for 0, 5, 15, 30 min or 24 h, followed by washing off the free peptide and allowing the spores to germinate for 24 h at 23°C in a peptide‐free growth medium. At 24 hpt, each treatment's conidial germination percentage was measured (Figure S4).

Fungal Nuclei Analysis

Alternaria alternata conidial suspension containing 105 conidia in 1 mL was added to 14 mL 0.25% PDB and germinated on an orbital shaker at 150 rpm for 16 h at 23°C. Mycelium was treated with NCR044, NCR192 or NCR13_PFV2, each at 12 μM for 5 h. Nuclei were stained with 10 μg/mL DAPI (4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole, dihydrochloride), incubated at 23°C for 10 min, and photographed using a fluorescent microscope equipped with a DAPI filter set (Figure S5A). In addition, DNA extractions (DNeasy Plant Pro Kit, Qiagen) of A. alternata were done following a 3 or 5‐h treatment with NCR044, NCR192 or NCR13_PFV2, each at 12 μM, to assess nuclear degradation (Figure S5B).

Cytoplasmic Membrane Permeabilization

The peptides' ability to permeate the fungal cell was evaluated using propidium iodide (Park et al. 2000). A 1 μg mL−1 solution of propidium iodide was added to A. alternata germinating on glass for 16 h, followed by incubation for 1 min in the dark. Fluorescence was evaluated using a fluorescent microscope at Ex/Em: 535/615 nm.

Alternaria alternata (105 conidia mL−1) were germinated in 0.25% PDB suspension for 16 h. 10 μL of these early germinated conidia, or ‘germlings’ were placed onto glass slides for further analysis. Several permeabilization tests were conducted. First, A. alternata germlings were treated with NCR13_PFV2 (24 μM) and immediately stained with propidium iodide, followed by time‐series photographs in a fluorescent confocal microscope (SP8 laser scanning microscope, Leica) for 20 min. Second, germlings were treated with NCR13_PFV2 (12 μM) or purified water (control) for 24 h, followed by staining with propidium iodide and photographed using fluorescent microscopy. Third, germlings were treated with either NCR13_PFV2 (0.375, 0.75, 1.5, 3, 6 or 12 μM) or purified water (control) for 24 h, followed by staining with propidium iodide, and the percentage of permeabilized germlings was tabulated by calculating the ratio of fluorescent germlings to non‐fluorescent germlings. Each treatment was made in triplicate, n = 400.

In addition, L. theobromae 105 conidia mL−1 at different stages of maturity were treated with NCR13_PFV2 (1.5 μM) for 1 h, washed to remove peptides, stained with propidium iodide, and photographed using a fluorescent confocal microscope (Figure S6). Furthermore, 10 μL of P. expansum or B. cinerea conidia at 105 mL−1 suspension was treated with NCR13_PFV2 (12 μM) at T = 0 for 1 h, washed to remove the peptide, stained with propidium iodide and photographed using a fluorescent confocal microscope (Figure S7).

Peptide Intracellular Localization

A chemically synthesized 5‐TAMRA‐labelled NCR13_PFV2 peptide (Biomatik, Ontario, Canada) was used to visualize the peptide's location in cells of A. alternata using fluorescent confocal microscopy (Biomatik, Ontario, Canada). 10 μL of 105 conidia mL−1 was pipetted onto glass slides with 0.25% PDB and germinated for 16 h in a humid chamber at 23°C. After 16 h, germlings were treated with either 5‐TAMRA‐labelled peptide or sterile water and stained with various intracellular organelle stains: for mitochondria (5,5,6,6′‐tetrachloro‐1,1′,3,3′‐tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide, JC‐1), cell wall chitin (calcofluor white), vacuoles (ViaVac Red/Green FUN1) (Hua et al. 2011) and nuclei (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Applied Biosystems) (Niu et al. 2020).

Lipid‐Binding Assay

Lipid‐binding specificity of NCR13_PFV2 was assessed using a PIP strip assay (P‐6001, Echelon Biosciences Inc.), in which defined lipid species were prespotted onto nitrocellulose membranes in a concentration gradient (100–1.56 pmol/spot). Two membranes were used: one containing a variety of general membrane lipids (e.g., PA, PC, PE, PS, DAG, SM) and one containing mono‐, bis‐ and tris‐phosphorylated phosphatidylinositol derivatives.

Membranes were blocked for 1 h at 23°C in TBS‐T buffer (20 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.5; 150 mM NaCl; 0.1% Tween 20) supplemented with 3% (w/v) fatty acid‐free bovine serum albumin (BSA). NCR13_PFV2 peptide (diluted in blocking buffer) was incubated with the membranes overnight at 4°C, following three washes in TBS‐T. Then, the membranes were incubated with a custom rabbit polyclonal anti‐NCR13_PFV2 antibody (1:5000), followed by horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit as secondary antibody (1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch). Signal was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Clarity Western ECL Substrate, Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.) and visualized using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad Laboratories Inc.). The procedure was adapted from the protocol described by Godwin et al. (2024) for analysing peptide–lipid interactions in Alternaria‐targeting NCR variants.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (ΔΨm) Analysis

Effects of NCR13_PFV2 on mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) were tested with JC‐1 mitochondrial stain. A. alternata conidia (1 mL of 105 conidia mL−1) were added to 14 mL 0.25% PDB and germinated on a tabletop orbital shaker at 150 rpm for 16 h at 23°C. Germlings of A. alternata were then pipetted into a 96‐well plate, and 1× working solution of JC‐1 was added and incubated for 30 min at 37°C, followed by treatment with either sterile water (control) or NCR13_PFV2 at 3, 6 or 12 μM. Fluorescence was evaluated every 30 min at 595 + 530 nm for 3 h. An increase in the 595/530 nm ratio indicated healthy polarized mitochondria, and a decrease in this ratio indicated depolarized mitochondria. Additionally, photos of germlings treated with either sterile water (control) or NCR13_PFV2 (24 μM) for 1 h and subsequently stained with JC‐1 were taken using a fluorescent microscope.

Vacuoles and Cell Wall Integrity Analysis

Alternaria alternata conidia (1 mL of 105 conidia mL−1) were added to 14 mL 0.25% PDB and germinated on a tabletop orbital shaker at 150 rpm for 16 h at 23°C, after which a 60‐min treatment was done with either purified sterile water (control) or TAMRA‐labelled NCR13_PFV2 (24 μM). Subsequently, fungal germlings were stained with the blue fluorescent chitin‐labelling dye calcofluor white (Hua et al. 2011) and photographed using a fluorescent microscope.

For vacuole integrity, 105 conidia mL−1 of A. alternata were pipetted into a 96‐well plate containing 0.25% PDB and treated at T = 0 with either sterile water (control) or NCR13_PFV2 (3, 6, or 12 μM), and membrane‐permeant FUN‐1 dye (ViaVac Red/Green FUN1). Fungal viability was determined by kinetic fluorescent readings of red (Ex/Em: 485/620 nm) vacuole structures and green (Ex/Em: 485/530 nm) cytoplasm in a microplate reader. Readings were taken every 5 min for 4 h. Red/green ratios were determined to monitor the viability of fungal cells. Over time, an increase in the red/green ratio indicated the metabolic activity required for forming the red fluorescence of healthy intravacuolar structures. Each treatment was made in triplicate.

ROS Generation and Cellular Respiration Analysis

Alternaria alternata conidia (1 mL of 105 conidia mL−1) were added to 14 mL 0.25% PDB and germinated on a tabletop orbital shaker at 150 rpm for 16 h at 23°C. After 16 h, A. alternata germlings were pipetted into a 96‐well plate containing 0.25% PDB and treated with either sterile water (control) or NCR13_PFV2 0.1875, 1.5, 3, 6 or 12 μM, and stained with the ROS indicator 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCF‐DA) (Leiter et al. 2005). Fluorescent readings were taken every 30 min for 16 h at Ex/Em: 480/510 nm. Each treatment was made in triplicate. Additionally, A. alternata conidia were grown for 24 h, and the mycelium was treated with purified sterile water (control) or 12 μM NCR13_PFV2 and stained with H2DCF‐DA immediately after the peptide addition. The A. alternata mycelium was photographed using a fluorescent microscope.

Cellular respiration was measured by pipetting 10 μL 105 conidia mL−1 suspension of A. alternata and 140 μL 0.25% PDB into a 96‐well plate, followed by treatment with NCR13_PFV2 at 1.5, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36 or 48 μM. At 24 hpt, redox‐indicator stain 7‐hydroxy‐3H‐phenoxazin‐3‐one 10‐oxide (resazurin) (Chadha and Kale 2015) was added and measured using a microplate reader Ex/Em: 550/590 nm. Each treatment was performed in triplicate.

Bell Pepper Lesion Development Assay

Yellow bell peppers of variety Shir were purchased at commercial maturity and surface‐disinfected by washing with distilled water, followed by a 70% ethanol spray, and allowed to air‐dry inside a laminar flow hood. The fruits were wounded six times along the equator using gentle abrasion with sterile carborundum powder to induce uniform micro‐injuries. Each spot was inoculated with 10 μL of an A. alternata conidial suspension (105 spores mL−1 in sterile water), followed by 10 μL of treatment solution containing either sterile water (control), 24 μM or 48 μM NCR13_PFV2. Each treatment was applied to two wounds per fruit, across 15 fruits.

Inoculated fruits were incubated for 7 days at 25°C and high relative humidity (RH > 95%) in perforated plastic containers lined with moist paper towels. Lesion diameters were measured, and representative images were taken 7 days post‐inoculation (DPI) to assess lesion diameter measurements visually.

Image Acquisition and Statistical Analysis

Image acquisition was done using an SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica) and an IX83 fluorescent inverted microscope (Olympus).

The experimental observations were statistically analysed using JMP v. 18.0 Pro (JMP Statistical Discovery). Tukey's multiple comparison test (p < 0.05) was employed to calculate the significance level between the means. Unless otherwise stated, all experiments represent at least three independent biological replicates with technical triplicates. Statistical differences between treatments were assessed using ANOVA followed by post hoc multiple comparison tests.

Figure S11: NCR13_PFV2 induces rapid disruption of vacuolar organization and metabolic activity in live Alternaria alternata germlings. Upon peptide exposure, red‐fluorescent intravacuolar structures progressively disappeared, accompanied by redistribution of fluorescence into the cytoplasm within 30–60 min, indicating loss of vacuolar organization and metabolic activity. The time‐lapse movie captures the dynamic collapse of vacuolar integrity and cellular homeostasis in response to NCR13_PFV2 treatment.

Figure S12: Three‐dimensional structure of the chickpea nodule‐specific cysteine‐rich peptide NCR13. The three‐dimensional structure of NCR13 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB; accession 7TH8) and visualized to illustrate the spatial organization of this cysteine‐rich peptide. NCR13 contains multiple cysteine residues predicted to form disulfide bonds that stabilize the peptide fold and contribute to the compact defensin‐like architecture characteristic of nodule‐specific cysteine‐rich (NCR) peptides. Because NCR13_PFV1 and NCR13_PFV2 share an identical amino acid sequence but differ in disulfide pairing patterns, this structure provides a structural reference framework for interpreting the antifungal activity of the NCR13_PFV2 folding variant examined in this study.
