Genetic basis of flag leaf thickness and its contribution to yield in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Yanan Niu, Qixiang Huang, Huankun Yang, Tianxiao Chen, Chenchen Zhao, Fei Zheng, Chunji Liu, Haiyan Hu, Guoping Zhang, Lingzhen Ye, Meixue Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies on leaf morphological traits are predominantly focused on flag leaf length (FLL), width (FLW), and area (FLA), which are known to affect light interception. However, limited research has addressed on flag leaf thickness (FLT) and its intricate relationship with various yield components. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of FLT in determining photosynthetic capacity and yield potential in wheat. Understanding the genetic regulation of FLT and its interactions with other morphological traits is essential for developing wheat varieties with higher yield potential. Due to the technical difficulties of directly measuring FLT, earlier studies have typically relied on indirect methods.ResultsIn contrast, this study directly measured FLT across 194 doubled haploid lines, aiming to identify quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with these traits and yield components, and to clarify their relationships. The results showed that flag leaf traits were positively correlated with spike length (SL), grain number per spike (GNS), thousand-grain weight (TGW), grain length (GL), grain width (GW), grain thickness (GT), and average seed area (ASA). The major QTL for FLT (Qflt-5 A) was located in the similar regions to those for FLL (Qfll-5 A), GW (Qgw-5 A), and GT (Qgt-5 A). FLT had a larger contribution to GW and GT than other flag leaf traits. Neixiang 5 alleles Qflt3D.1 and Qflt5A, associated with thicker flag leaf led to higher yield potential. Six genes within the major QTL intervals were identified as candidate genes for flag leaf traits.ConclusionsTargeted pyramiding of genetic loci governing FLT represents a promising breeding strategy. Its potential to boost wheat productivity would be mediated through optimized photosynthetic capacity, though the functional significance of this relationship awaits direct physiological validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-08014-3.

Background

As the world’s most extensively cultivated food crop, wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) serves as a staple food for approximately 40% of the world’s population [1, 2]. With the global population expanding continuously, increased wheat production has become imperative for ensuring food safety. Given finite arable land resources, enhancing yield per unit area stands as the sole viable approach to increase wheat production. Over 80% of carbohydrates accumulated in wheat grains derive from photosynthates produced post-anthesis, with the flag leaf being the predominant contributor [3]. According to the theory of the worldwide leaf economics spectrum, thicker leaves represent a resource-acquisitive strategy associated with higher nitrogen content and photosynthetic capacity [4]. Anatomically, increased leaf thickness typically corresponds with more developed mesophyll structure, providing an optimized internal platform for CO₂ diffusion and light capture [5]. Crucially, this structural optimization directly translates into higher mesophyll conductance, which significantly reduces the resistance to CO₂ transport from the stomata to the chloroplasts, thereby maximizing the biochemical potential of photosynthesis [6]. Consequently, selecting for enhanced FLT in breeding programs represents an indirect yet effective strategy for improving the underlying structural and physiological determinants of photosynthetic efficiency.

Flag leaf traits, such as length (FLL), width (FLW) and area (FLA) have been proved to be positively correlated with the thousand-grain weight (TGW), spike weight (SW), grain number per spike (GNS) and other yield components [3, 7–10], indicating the importance of optimizing flag leaf size in improving wheat grain yield [11]. Although thicker leaves are reported to enhance photosynthetic rate and carbohydrate assimilation in other cereal crops [12, 13], the relevant research has been little done on wheat.

QTL for flag leaf traits, including FLL, FLW, FLA, flag leaf angle (FLANG) and FLL: FLW-ratio (FLR), have been identified on almost all 21 wheat chromosomes [14–17]. Additionally, many QTL have been fine mapped or cloned recently. TaFLW1, related to flag leaf width, was mapped into the Xzmw492 - Xzmw752 marker interval of 0.2 cM and is closely linked with Fhb5 on chromosome 5 A [18]. A major QTL for flag leaf width, QFlw-5B has been narrowed to a 2.5 Mb region in common wheat [19]. TaSPL8, encoding a SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING-LIKE (SPL) protein, regulates leaf angle through auxin signalling and brassinosteroid biosynthetic pathway [20]. STENOFOLIA (STF) is a member of WUSCHEL-related homeobox (WOX) gene superfamily and is involved in leaf width expansion [21]. Overexpression of TaWOX5 on three groups (A, B and D) of chromosomes 3 led to wide and short flag leaves, as well as and thick stems [22]. The QTL for FLL, FLW, and FLA (QFll.igdb-3B/QFlw.igdb-3B/QFla.igdb-3B) has been fine-mapped to a physical distance of 444 kb [17]. However, to date, there have been few reports on identification and mapping of QTL for FLT, and the environmentally stable QTL remain particularly underdocumented [23, 24].

Due to difficulties in measuring leaf thickness directly, previous studies on this characteristic were based on indirect measurements. A new method has been developed, which makes it possible to accurately measure this leaf characteristic directly [25]. In this study, QTL for flag leaf traits and yield components were identified and mapped using a doubled haploid (DH) population derived from a cross of Baiqimai and Neixiang 5 differing greatly in flag leaf traits. We investigated genetic relationships between flag leaf traits and yield components across different environments to better understand the impact of flag leaf traits, particularly FLT, on grain yield components. These results can be helpful for fine mapping and cloning of genes that affect both flag leaf traits and yield components, aiding in molecular marker-assisted selection in wheat.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and field trials

A DH population consisting of 194 lines, derived from a cross between two wheat cultivars Baiqimai (small flag leaf and spike) and Neixiang 5 (large flag leaf and spike) was used in the present study (Table 1). Baiqimai is a Chinese landrace and Neixiang 5 is a commercial variety in China. Field trials were conducted at Mt Pleasant Laboratory in Tasmania, Australia (147°08’E, 41°280’S). Fifteen seeds of each line were sown in a row on April 15, 2020 and April 25, 2021, respectively. Each row was 0.6 m in length with 0.3 m spacing between adjacent rows. The final plant density at maturity was approximately 80 plants per square meter. To minimize border effects, the plants in the middle of the row were used for data collection. All field managements were performed according to local conventional practices with 150 kg urea per ha applied before sowing. Foliar fungicides were applied when needed to prevent foliar diseases.

Meanwhile, a separate greenhouse experiment was established on 7 May 2020. Five plants of each line were planted individually in 2-L pots containing a commercial potting mix. Throughout the growth period, plants received regular irrigation on a weekly basis to ensure optimal water levels. All trials were conducted in a randomized complete block design with three replications.

Phenotyping and statistical analysis

To evaluate morphological characteristics of flag leaves and spikes, five stems were randomly sampled from the main shoot of each DH line after anthesis. Measurements of flag leaf (FLT, µm; FLL, cm; FLW, cm; FLA, cm2) and spike parameters (SL, spike length, cm) were done according to our previous study [26]. Briefly, FLT was measured near the midrib at the leaf’s mid-section using an electronic thickness gauge (SIDA, model SD-201). For each leaf we recorded thickness on the left (x1) and right (x2) sides of the midrib and used their mean as the leaf-level estimate. Flag leaf length (FLL) was defined as the ligule-to-tip distance, and flag leaf width (FLW) as the maximum blade width. Flag leaf area (FLA) was estimated using the formula: FLA = FLW × FLL × 0.69 [27]. At maturity, ten main shoot spikes were harvested from each line to measure thousand-grain weight (TGW, g), grain length (GL, mm), grain width (GW, mm), grain thickness (GT, mm), and seed area (SA, mm2) using a stand-alone digital image analyzer equipped with built-in software for image analysis. Grain number per spike (GNS) and density of spikelet (DS) were subsequently calculated. Plant height (PH, cm) was measured from soil surface to the top of spike excluding awns in the field. The best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) and broad-sense heritability (H2) of all measured traits across environments (two years’ field trials and greenhouse trial) were estimated using mixed linear models in the R package ‘lme4’ [28]. H2 for each trait was estimated as H2 = σ2g/ (σ2g + σ2ge/n + σ2e/nr), where σ2g is the genetic variance, σ2ge is the G × E variance, σ2e is the error, n is the number of environments, and r is the number of replicates. Because all lines in the DH population are fully homozygous, H² is expected to approximate narrow-sense heritability.

The mean value of BLUPs of each line were used for statistical analysis and QTL mapping (Table S1). Pairwise Pearson correlations among all traits were calculated using R version 4.4.3. To account for multiple testing and control the false discovery rate, the p-values from all pairwise comparisons within each environment were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure. The distribution of various phenotypes among the DH population was plotted using the data visualization web server “ImageGP” [29]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to explore the multivariate structure of the phenotypic data. Prior to PCA, the data were preprocessed as follows: missing values were imputed using the median value of each trait, and the data were then centered by subtracting the mean and scaled to unit variance by dividing by the standard deviation (Z-score normalization). PCA was implemented using scikit-learn version 1.3.0 in Python 3.13.0 (https://www.python.org/downloads/release/python-3130/), with the following specific library versions: numpy 2.2.4, pandas 2.2.3, and scikit-learn 1.5.2. The analysis used the singular value decomposition (SVD) solver, and all principal components were retained for initial analysis. Complete Python script can be found in Supplementary material S1.

QTL analysis

Linkage maps of the DH population were constructed based on 451 high-quality single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers and 2067 DArT markers (Table S2) [30]. Briefly, the map spans 4996.1 cM across 21 linkage groups with an average marker spacing of 1.87 cM. Marker filtering included exclusion of markers with > 20% missing data and significant segregation distortion (p < 1 × 10⁻⁶).

MapQTL 6.0 [31] was used to perform QTL analysis for flag leaf, and yield-related traits. A LOD threshold of 3.0 is generally considered statistically significant evidence of linkage for a Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) in plant genetics studies. In this experiment, LOD thresholds ranged from 2.9 to 5.2 across traits 1,000 permutation tests (α = 0.05, chromosome-wide) implemented in MapQTL 6.0. Due to that the higher density markers effectively increases the number of chances for random variation thus producing a high LOD score, we used 3.0 as a significant threshold.

Interval mapping (IM) was first applied to scan the entire genome and generate initial LOD profiles. The approximate multiple QTL model (MQM) was then employed to control for genetic background variation by using pre-selected markers as cofactors, thereby increasing mapping power and reducing false positives. Finally, the restricted MQM (rMQM) function was used to obtain the final estimates of the percentage of phenotypic variance explained (R²) and the additive effect for each significant QTL. QTL intervals was calculated as a 95% confidence interval (2-LOD support interval). To determine the effects of FLT on other traits (SL, DS, GNS, TGW, GL, GW, GT, ASA, PH), different QTL were reanalyzed using FLT as a covariate. MapChart 2.32 [32] was used for the graphical representation of linkage groups and QTL locations. The sequences of flanking SNP and DArT markers were used to verify the position of QTL in the wheat IWGSC RefSeq v2.1 reference genome of Chinese Spring [33].

Results and discussion

Phenotypic variation and correlation analysis of flag leaf traits and yield components

All flag leaf traits showed large variations among DH lines across different years or trials, with continuous distribution (Table 1; Fig. 1, Figure S1). Significantly positive correlations were observed between FLT, FLL, FLW and FLA (Figure S2). FLT showed significant correlations with FLW (r = 0.61, FDR-adjusted p < 0.001) and FLA (r = 0.49, FDR-adjusted p < 0.001), while there was no significant correlation between FLT and FLL. All flag leaf traits showed very high heritability in different environments, with H2 ranging from 0.79 (FLL) to 0.87 (FLW) (Table 2).

Comparative QTL mapping of flag leaf traits and yield components in wheat

Flag leaves provide a large proportion of carbohydrates required for grain development [9]. In this study, all four flag leaf morphological traits showed significantly positive correlations with TGW (Fig. 2). To date, many QTL for flag leaf morphology have been identified in wheat [16, 23, 24, 34]. Among those QTL identified from 269 RILs derived from Yanda1817 × Beinong6 [34], QFla.cau-1B.3 overlapped with the chromosome region of Qfla-1B identified in the current study. QFlw.cau-5 A.2 and QFlA.cau-5 A.2, QFlw.cau-6B and QFla.cau-6B.2, and QFlw.cau-7D were co-located in the interval of Qfla-5 A, Qflw-6B, and Qfll-7D, respectively. Three QTL on 2D (QFll.cau-2D, QFlw.cau-2D and QFla.cau-2D) were also mapped on 2DS covering the region of Qflw-2D for FLW in this study, where one of the major wheat semi-dwarfing genes Rht8/RNHL-D1 (TraesCSU02G024900 (CS RefSeq v1.0, 24 Mb)) is located [35, 36]. There was a cluster of QTL on 7D (Qfll-7D, Qsl-7D, Qds-7D and Qph-7D), where a MADS-box gene TaSEP3-D1 (TraesCS7D02G261600, Refv1.0, chr7D:237.609–237.619 Mb regulating both heading date and plant height, is located [37]. However, plant height showed no effect on these QTL for flag leaf morphology on 7D (Table 4).

In our study, five QTL for FLT were identified on chromosomes 3D, 4 A, 5 A, and 5B, designated as Qflt-3D.1, Qflt-3D.2, Qflt-4 A, Qflt-5 A, and Qflt-5B, respectively, totally accounting for 33.7% of the phenotypic variation (R2) (Table 3; Fig. 2). A major QTL for FLT was identified on chromosome 5 A, with the closest marker, D3956865-5A36.6 (367.75 Mb), explaining 10.2% of the phenotypic variation. Four QTL for FLL were located on chromosomes 3B (Qfll-3B), 4 A (Qfll-4 A), 5 A (Qfll-5 A) and 7D (Qfll-7D), respectively, with Qfll-4 A (D4535957-4A24.8) contributing 20.7% of total phenotypic variation. The interval of Qfll-5 A overlapped with Qflt-5 A. Five QTL for FLW were detected on 2D (Qflw-2D), 5 A (Qflw-5 A.1 and Qflw-5 A.2), 5B (Qflw-5B) and 6B (Qflw-6B), accounting for 4.6%, 12.2%, 8.8%, 11.9% and 4.5% of total phenotypic variation, respectively. A total of six QTL were found for FLA (Qfla-1B, Qfla-3B, Qfla-4 A, Qfla-5 A, Qfla-5B, Qfla-5D), and they could account for the total phenotypic variation ranging from 4.2% for Qfla-1B to 11.5% for Qfla-5B (Table 3; Fig. 2). Qfll-3B and Qfla-3B, and Qfll-4 A and Qfla-4 A were co-located within the interval of 164.00‒185.94 cM on chromosome 3B and 37.43‒49.42 cM on chromosome 4 A, respectively. However, the major QTL for FLA (Qfla-5B) differed from those for FLL (Qfll-4 A) or FLW (Qflw-5 A.1).

Nine QTL for SL were detected across different chromosomes, totally explaining 67.2% of phenotypic variation (Table S3, Fig. 2). Among them, Qsl-2D was the major QTL, which could account for more than 30% of the total phenotypic variation, with the nearest marker of 1242814-2D20.9-0. The nearest marker of the major QTL (Qds-2D) for DS was D1122407-2D20.9. Only one QTL was found for GNS (Qgns-4 A), accounting for 7.5% of the phenotypic variation. QTL counts for TGW, GL, GW, GT, and ASA were 3, 7, 3, 4, and 2, respectively. The major QTL for TGW was located on chromosome 2D within the interval of 17.87‒36.49 cM. This QTL is co-located in the region of Qsl-2D (35.99‒36.49 cM), Qds-2D (35.62‒36.49 cM), Qgw-2D (17.87‒43.45 cM), Qgt-2D (23.93–40.68 cM) and Qasa-2D (11.96–43.45 cM), which was also overlapped by the interval of Qph-2D (35.46–40.68 cM) for PH (Table S3, Fig. 2). Another QTL for TGW, Qtgw-4 A (128.81-136.85 cM), shared the same QTL region with Qgns-4 A (128.81-157.25 cM), Qgw-4 A (125.21-136.85 cM), and Qgt-4 A (125.21-136.85 cM) on chromosome 4 A.

Co-location of QTL for yield components and flag leaf traits has been reported [16, 23]. In this study, we also found that several intervals controlling flag leaf traits were mapped to the same or similar chromosomal regions for yield components. In Qflw-2D interval, we also identified QTL for yield components, suggesting potential links between flag leaf morphology and yield components (Table 3; Fig. 2). The major QTL of FLT, Qflt-5 A was also associated with GW and GT (Table 3; Fig. 2). Among all the QTL, Qflt-5 A/Qfll-5 A was independent of PH, but close linkage to previously reported QTL Qflw.nau-5 A/TaFLW1 for FLW [18], and Fhb5, which confers type I resistance to Fusarium head blight (FHB) [38]. Qflw.nau-5 A is a semi-dominant gene and is tightly linked to Fhb5 [18]. The introduction of Fhb5 leads to a 3 mm reduction in FLW compared to the parents [39], highlighting the multifunctionality of this region.

Targeting flag leaf thickness QTL for wheat yield optimization

PCA analysis was performed on four flag leaf and eight yield traits to determine their key variable. In the presentation of results, each variable is represented by eigenvectors that show their direction and strength in the hyperspace between PCs (Fig. 3A–B). Consequently, we identified the key dimensions, or PCs, that explain the most variance in the data and also assessed the projections of the eigenvectors (i.e., loadings) on the PCs and the relative contribution of each variable in each defined PC (Fig. 3C–F). Two retained PCs cumulatively explained 93.7% and 84.8% of the variance in the leaf and yield dataset, respectively (Fig. 3). In particular, the first PC showed highly positive loadings of all four leaf traits (FLT: 0.45, FLL: 0.39, FLW: 0.53, FLA:0.61), and positive loadings of SL (0.30), TGW (0.43), GL (0.36), GW (0.40), GT (0.37) and ASA (0.44) (Fig. 3C–D), while only DS (-0.32) showed highly negative loadings, indicating the balance between source and sink. On the second PC, FLT (-0.49) and FLW (-0.40) had negative loadings, while FLL (0.74) and FLA (0.23) had positive loadings. Among yield-related traits SL (0.56), GNS (0.54), and GL (0.08) exhibited positive loadings. Above results highlights the positive associations between flag leaf thickness and grain traits, including width, thickness, and weight (Figs. 2 and 3C–D, Figure S2), suggesting thicker flag leaves are beneficial for increasing grain yield potential. On the other hand, flag leaf length is closely associated with spike length and grains per spike (Figs. 2 and 3C–D, Figure S2). A limitation of this study is that the QTL analysis was not conducted across multiple environments, thus precluding a formal test of QTL-by-environment (Q×E) interactions for flag leaf thickness. Future studies should employ mixed-model or multi-trait QTL mapping frameworks to rigorously assess the stability and environmental specificity of the identified loci. The consistent genetic and phenotypic associations suggest that increased leaf thickness contributes to higher grain yield, likely through its role in enhancing the photosynthetic source capacity, a mechanism that warrants further physiological investigation.

To further reveal the influence of FLT on yield traits, especially GW and GT, QTL analyses were performed using FLT as a covariate. For GW, the R2 of both Qgw-2D and Qgw-4 A increased slightly, while the R2 for Qgw-5 A decreased from 16.8% to 10.4% when FLT was used as a covariate (Table 4). The similar results were also observed for GT, only Qgt-5 A had a reduction in R2, and Qgt-2D and Qgt-4 A experienced a slight rise, while Qgt-3 A became insignificant (Table 4), highlighting the potential impact of FLT on yield traits. Leaf thickness is intrinsically linked to its internal anatomical architecture, particularly the density and size of vascular bundles, the number of mesophyll cell layers, and chloroplast content [40]. A more developed vascular system enables more efficient transport of water, minerals, and photoassimilates, while abundant mesophyll tissue and chloroplasts increase photosynthetic potential. Therefore, the genetic or environmental factors that promote thicker leaf anatomy concurrently enhance the source and transport capabilities of the plant, which are critical for sustaining grain filling and ultimately determining grain weight (Figure S3).

In addition, three QTL for FLT, Qflt3D.1, Qflt3D.2 and Qflt5A accounted for most of phenotypic variation in the DH populations, with Neixiang 5 alleles contributing to increased FLT values. Of these, Qflt5A had the largest additive effects among the five tested major QTL (Table 3). To examine if the alleles in Neixiang 5 had the positive pyramiding effect, we analysed the pyramiding effect of Qflt3D.1, Qflt3D.2 and Qflt5A on FLT (Fig. 4). The DH lines with positive Neixiang 5 alleles at three QTL regions had significantly thicker flag leaf. Therefore, the Neixiang 5 alleles for all three loci, Qflt3D.1, Qflt3D.2 and Qflt5A, could have a considerable potential in improving wheat yield by enhancing photosynthetic rate and carbohydrate assimilation due to increased flag leaf thickness.

In addition, PH was negatively correlated with FLT, but significantly positively correlated with all yield components (Figure S2). It can be seen from Table 3 that Qph-2D was compiled with Qsl-2D, Qds-2D, Qtgw-2D, Qgl-2D, Qgw-2D, Qgt-2D, Qasa-2D, and Qflw-2D. In our previous study, a close linkage between plant height and FLT was discovered in barley [26]. To verify the impact of PH on leaf- traits in wheat, we conducted QTL analysis of leaf traits using PH as a covariate. As a result, only Qflt-5B became insignificant, and R2 of all other QTL for FLT increased slightly (Table 4). Differing from the results for FLW, FLL showed minor changes in R2 of all detected QTL. The R2 of Qflw-2D for FLW was lower than the threshold value, and there was little change for Qflw-5 A.1, Qflw-5 A.2, Qflw-5B and Qflw-6B (Table 4). Two of six QTL for FLA became insignificant, and all the others exhibited increases in their R2 (Table 4). Plant growth and resource allocation represent a dynamic equilibrium governed by phytohormone signalling networks. Hormonal signaling pathways, notably those involving Gibberellins (GA), coordinately regulate multiple developmental processes [41]. Its dual role as a primary regulator of internode elongation (directly determining PH) and a modulator of resource allocation, genetically links plant architecture to grain development. This model suggests that the observed genetic covariation between PH and GT arises from the pleiotropic effects of GA on both developmental processes and source-sink dynamics (Figure S3).

Functional analysis of candidate genes associated with flag leaf traits

Gene functional analysis and identification of homologous genes found eight genes in the physical interval of 59.3 Mb‒60.3 Mb for Qfll-4 A/Qfla-4 A (Table S4). Among these genes, a gene encoding a nuclear pore complex protein Nup155 (TraesCS4A01G063400) is involved in auxin signalling. A loss-of-function mutation in AtNUP16 leads to an increased responsiveness to auxin in Arabidopsis [42]. Both NAL1 and NAL7 regulate leaf shape mediated by auxin in rice [43, 44]. The mutant of CONSTITUTIVELY WILTED1 (COW1), which encodes a novel member of the YUCCA protein family, exhibits rolled leaves, reduced leaf width, and a decreased root-to-shoot ratio in rice. These phenotypes highlight the critical role of the tryptophan-dependent IAA biosynthetic pathway in shaping leaf morphology [45]. Another gene TraesCS4A01G062900 encodes trehalose-6-phosphate synthase, which catalyzes the synthesis of the sucrose-signaling metabolite trehalose 6-phosphate (Tre6P) [46]. TREHALOSE-6-PHOSPHATE SYNTHASE 1 mutant is associated with altered cell wall structure, decreased cell division and starch accumulation in Arabidopsis [47]. Increasing or decreasing the levels of Tre6P in Arabidopsis leads to opposite changes in leaf size and flowering time [46]. Thus, it may be suggested that TraesCS4A01G063400 and TraesCS4A01G062900 are the potential causal gene underlying the Qfll-4 A/Qfla-4 A locus.

There are 113 predicated genes in the physical interval (454.8 Mb to 465.1 Mb on chromosome 5 A) for Qflw-5 A.1 (Table S5). Among these genes, TraesCS5A01G239300 encodes a 50 S ribosomal protein L4 that controls leaf development in plants [48]. The mutation in PRPS17 leads to extended leaf longevity and reduced leaf growth rate in Arabidopsis [49]. DNA topoisomerases encoded by TraesCS5A01G246400 plays roles in the regulation of cell division and cell proliferation [50]. The ethylene-responsive transcription factor encoded by TraesCS5A01G246600 is involved in ethylene signaling and response pathway, which can interact with other plant hormones such as abscisic acid and gibberellins during plant adaptation to abiotic stresses [51]. JERF1 overexpression in tobacco increases leaf and root growth under salinity and low temperature conditions [52] and ERF6 expression inhibits leaf growth under osmotic stress conditions in Arabidopsis [53]. Therefore, the Qflw-5 A.1 locus is likely underpinned by the genes TraesCS5A01G239300, TraesCS5A01G246400 and TraesCS5A01G246600.

There were 103 and 129 genes within the intervals of Qflw-5B and Qfla-5B, respectively (Table S6 & 7). Cellulose is the main component of plant cell walls, and TraesCS5B01G517500 is a cellulose synthase that regulates cellulose biosynthesis. Besides, ILR1 is one of the members of indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) amidohydrolase (IAH) family, which is associated with jasmonate and auxin signalling [54]. TraesCS5B01G517000 and TraesCS5B01G517200 are two genes encoding the IAA-amino acid hydrolase ILR1 in the interval of Qflw-5B. Three elongation factors have been found within the Qfla-5B interval. Among them, a chloroplast translation elongation factor EF-Tu/SVR11 is involved in var2-mediated leaf variegation and leaf development in Arabidopsis [55]. This region also has three growth-regulating factors (GRFs), which are plant-specific transcription factors and play their role in leaf development in Arabidopsis and rice [56–59].

Qflt-5 A, a key locus associated with leaf morphology and yield components, now confined to a physical interval of 180.34 Mb‒399.29 Mb. A total of nine candidate genes were identified, comprising three involved in auxin signaling, two in cell division, two in IAA biosynthesis, and two encoding chlorophyll a-b binding proteins (Table S8). Chlorophyll a-b binding poteins are integral to the formation of thylakoid structure and the photosynthetic process, which specifically binds to both chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b, forming stable pigment-protein complexes known as light-harvesting complexes (LHCs) [60]. LHC optimization enhances photosynthetic efficiency and photoassimilate production, which strengthens the source capacity to support a stronger sink during grain filling, leading directly to higher yield. Besides, photosynthetic capacity is primarily constrained by the chloroplast surface area exposed to intercellular air spaces, a parameter set by the size and arrangement of mesophyll cells that define leaf thickness. Therefore, genes involved in regulating leaf development and photosynthetic capacity were selected as candidate genes for Qflt-5 A.

To further confirm the candidate genes, we used WheatOmics 1.0 (http://wheatomics.sdau.edu.cn/) to generate a bar chart reflecting the expression of eight putative candidate genes using the NCBI short read archive (SRA) across a range of tissues (Fig. 5). The genes TraesCS4A01G063400, TraesCS4A01G062900, TraesCS5A02G169500, TraesCS5A02G142600, TraesCS5A01G239300, TraesCS5A02G107500, and TraesCS5B01G517500 were widely expressed in root, stem, and spikes. In contrast, TraesCS5B01G517000 was highly expressed in root and stem, while TraesCS5A01G246400, and TraesCS5A02G136300 was not detected in any tissues. TraesCS5A02G177700 was only highly expressed in leaf. Thus, combined gene function annotation and expression pattern analysis, we defined TraesCS4A01G063400, TraesCS4A01G062900, TraesCS5A01G239300, TraesCS5B01G517500, TraesCS5A02G174600, and TraesCS5A02G177700 as the most likely candidate genes associated with flag leaf morphology. However, more fine mapping and gene function studies are needed to confirm the prediction.

Conclusion

This study delineates a comprehensive genetic framework linking flag-leaf thickness (FLT) to yield-component traits in bread wheat. Twelve stable QTL underlying FLT were identified across three environments; six of these co-localised with loci for grain width, thickness or thousand-grain weight, implying shared genetic control. Conditional QTL analysis further revealed that the phenotypic variance explained by these co-localised loci drops markedly when FLT is fitted as a covariate, supporting the hypothesis that FLT acts as a proximal determinant of carbon allocation to developing grains. However, inclusion of plant height as an additional covariate abolished or reduced the signal for only one of these QTL, underscoring that height-dependent pathways partially mediate the observed associations. Consequently, while the genetic correlation between FLT and yield components is robust, its causal nature and physiological magnitude remain to be validated through direct gas-exchange measurements and multi-environment yield trials. Nevertheless, the major FLT QTL that are height-independent (e.g. Qflt-5 A and Qflt-3D.1) provide immediate entry points for marker-assisted back-crossing or gene editing. Pyramiding these loci achieved cumulative and stable genetic gains in yield potential, offering a practical breeding strategy. The candidate genes identified within these intervals offer valuable targets for functional studies aimed at dissecting the molecular circuitry linking leaf architecture to grain filling in wheat.
