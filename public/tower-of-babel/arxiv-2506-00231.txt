Extensions of Schrödinger operators that generate C 0 C_{0} contraction semigroups
Lawrence Frolov, [10pt] Department of Mathematics, Rutgers University (New Brunswick)

Abstract
Consider a non-relativistic quantum particle with wave function ψ \psi in a bounded C 2 C^{2} region Ω ⊂ ℝ n \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n} , and suppose detectors are placed along the boundary ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . Assume the detection process is irreversible, its mechanism is time independent and also hard, i.e., detections occur only along the boundary ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . Under these conditions Tumulka [ 31 ] argued that the dynamics of ψ \psi must be governed by a C 0 C_{0} contraction semigroup that weakly solves the Schrödinger equation and proposed modeling the detector by a time-independent local absorbing boundary condition at ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . In this paper, we apply the newly discovered theory of boundary quadruples [ 33 ] to parameterize all C 0 C_{0} contraction semigroups whose generators extend the Schrödinger Hamiltonian, and prove a variant of Tumulka’s claim: all such evolutions are generated by the placement of (potentially nonlocal) absorbing boundary conditions on ψ \psi along ∂ Ω \partial\Omega . We combine this result with the work of Werner [ 14 ] to show that each C 0 C_{0} contraction semigroup naturally admits a probability distribution for the time of detection along ∂ Ω \partial\Omega , and we prove for a wide class of absorbing boundary conditions that the probability of the particle being ever detected is equal to 1 1 .



1
Introduction and Statement of Main Results

1.1
Irreversible Hard Autonomous Detection

Suppose that a detecting surface, such as a scintillating screen, is placed along the boundary
\partial\Omega
of a bounded region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
in physical space, and suppose that a non-relativistic quantum particle is prepared at time
0
with wave function
\psi_{0}
whose support lies in
\Omega
. We would like to understand how placing this detector along the boundary
\partial\Omega
affects the dynamics of the wave function
\psi
in
\Omega
. We expect that as detections occur, the total probability that the particle remains undetected in
\Omega
decreases. As an idealization, we take the detector to be
hard
, so detections only occur along the boundary
\partial\Omega
. The detection process should also be
irreversible
, i.e. probability in the particle-detector state space should irreversibly transfer from the space of undetected states to the space of detected states. We additionally assume that the mechanism of detection is time-independent, it depends only on the initially prepared state of the detector. This paper aims to provide an explicit parameterization of all quantum-mechanical models which satisfy these assumptions.

Our work is primarily motivated by a recent paper of Tumulka
[
31
]
, in which he argued that the dynamics of such systems should be governed by a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup that weakly solves the Schrödinger equation. In summary, Tumulka analyzes the particle and detector together as a quantum system with wave function
\Psi_{t}
. The system evolves according to
i\partial_{t}\Psi=\hat{H}_{S}\Psi
in a Hilbert space of the form
\mathcal{H}_{P}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{F}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{F}
denotes the space of states in which a detector has fired while
\mathcal{H}_{P}=L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes\mathcal{H}_{D}
consists of states in which the particle resides in
\Omega
and the detectors are primed. The system is initially prepared as a pure product state
\Psi_{0}=\psi_{0}\otimes\phi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes\mathcal{H}_{D}
, with
\psi_{0}
and
\phi_{0}
of unit norm.

Since the detector is assumed to be
hard
, there should be no interaction between the quantum particle and the detector before a detection has taken place. The system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S}
must then split on
\mathcal{H}_{P}
as
\hat{H}_{S}\big|_{L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes\mathcal{H}_{D}}=\hat{H}\otimes\mathds{1}+\mathds{1}\otimes\hat{H}_{D}
, where
\mathds{1}
denotes the identity,
\hat{H}_{D}
the Hamiltonian of the detector and
\hat{H}
the non-relativistic Schrödinger Hamiltonian. In addition, the projection of
\Psi_{t}
to
\mathcal{H}_{P}
cannot become entangled throughout its evolution, as the particle should not become entangled with the detector before detection. This leads to two conditions on the dynamics of the particle detector system:

(C0)

\Psi_{t}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{P}}=\psi_{t}\otimes\phi_{t}
remains a pure product state, and the dynamics of the quantum particle in
\Omega
before detection is given by the wave function
\psi_{t}
.

(C1)

\psi_{t}
weakly satisfies a Schrödinger equation inside
\Omega
.

i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\hat{H}^{*}\psi\quad\text{in }\Omega

(1.1)

where, setting
\hbar=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}=1
,
\hat{H}^{*}
denotes the adjoint of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=-\Delta+V
defined on
D(\hat{H})=C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
V
a real valued potential depending on the experimental apparatus.

Tumulka models detection as an irreversible process that transports probability from
\mathcal{H}_{P}
to
\mathcal{H}_{F}
. Consequently, the dynamics of
\psi_{t}\otimes\phi_{t}
are norm non-increasing and autonomous, they are not affected by the dynamics in
\mathcal{H}_{F}
. After additionally assuming that the detection mechanism is autonomous; depending only on the initial detector state
\phi_{0}
; Tumulka arrives at three further conditions on the dynamics of
\psi_{t}
.

(C2)

The evolution maps
W_{t}:\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi_{t}
define a
C_{0}
semigroup on
L^{2}(\Omega)
:

(a)

The maps
W_{t}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
are linear for
t\geq 0
.

(b)

They are strongly continuous,
\lim_{t\to t_{0}}||W_{t}\psi-W_{t_{0}}\psi||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
for all
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
t_{0}\geq 0
.

(c)

They form a semigroup under composition,
W_{t}W_{s}=W_{t+s}
for
t,s\geq 0
, with
W_{0}=\mathds{1}
.

In this model
||\psi_{t}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
represents the probability that the particle has remained undetected in
\Omega
up to time
t
, so we expect the dynamics of
\psi_{t}
to be non-unitary. This returns the final condition.

(C3)

W_{t}
are contractions,
||W_{t}\psi||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq||\psi||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for all
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

The
C_{0}
contraction semigroup condition has historically been employed in modeling quantum particles undergoing irreversible interactions with their environment (see e.g.
[
12
,
13
]
). This condition allows for a simpler analysis of the system, but can lead to undesirable physical phenomena. For example, the minimal unitary dilation
\exp(-it\hat{H}_{S})
of any non-unitary
C_{0}
contraction semigroup
W_{t}
satisfies
\sigma(\hat{H}_{S})=\mathbb{R}
, so the particle detector system would have an energy unbounded from below
[
11
]
. But
C_{0}
contraction semigroups can be approximated by projections of unitary evolutions with semibounded Hamiltonian generators (
[
13
, Proposition 4.1.1]
), so they may still act as valid approximations of the true physical dynamics that is taking place.

Allcock
[
9
]
famously modeled
soft
detection with the non-unitary evolution operators
W_{t}=\exp(-it(-\Delta+iV)):L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
iV
is an imaginary potential. In this model, quantum particles are gradually detected upon entering the region where
iV
is supported. It is tempting to model hard detection along the boundary of a region
\Omega
using an imaginary potential supported in the complement
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
and taking a limit of the dynamics as
V\to\infty
. However, this limit famously returns norm-preserving dynamics in
\Omega
, so the particle is never detected with probability
1
.

Instead of modeling hard detectors as a naive limit of soft detectors, Tumulka pointed out that conditions (C1), (C2), and (C3) suggest that
\psi_{t}
should satisfy a boundary condition along
\partial\Omega
. He proposed that the interaction between the particle and the detector should be modeled by a local time-independent absorbing boundary condition, so that the dynamics of
\psi_{t}
in
\Omega
are governed by the initial-boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclr}i\partial_{t}\psi&=&(-\Delta+V)\psi&\text{in }\Omega\\
\psi&=&\psi_{0}&\text{at }t=0\\
\partial_{n}\psi&=&i\beta\psi&\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

Here,
\partial_{n}
denotes the outwards normal derivative of
\Omega
, and
\beta
is a function on
\partial\Omega
satisfying
\text{Re}(\beta)\geq 0
.

Tumulka’s absorbing boundary condition was originally motivated by the question of
detection time distributions
. Despite the widespread success of quantum mechanics in predicting detection distributions for particle
positions
, a theoretical description for the distribution of
times
at which the particle is detected on a screen is still an open question. Various competing proposals have been made for this distribution (see
[
20
]
for a wide review), but Tumulka’s model admits a natural Born rule. For initial wave functions satisfying
||\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
, the
absorbing boundary rule
states that over any time interval
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}
, the probability of detecting a quantum particle with wave function
\psi_{t}
satisfying equation
(
1.2
) is

\text{Prob}_{\psi_{0}}(t_{1}\leq t\leq t_{2})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\vec{n}\cdot\vec{j}_{\psi_{t}}~dx^{n-1}dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega}2\text{Re}(\beta)|\psi_{t}|^{2}~dx^{n-1}dt

(1.3)

where
dx^{n-1}
denotes the surface element on
\partial\Omega
,
\vec{n}
the outwards unit normal and
\vec{j}_{\psi_{t}}=2\text{Im}(\psi_{t}^{*}\vec{\nabla}\psi_{t})
is the probability current. In has been shown in
[
37
]
that for sufficiently regular
\beta
, the initial-boundary value problem (
1.2
) admits a unique global-in-time solution
\psi_{t}
for each
\psi_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
satisfying
\partial_{n}\psi_{0}\big|_{\partial\Omega}=i\beta\psi_{0}\big|_{\partial\Omega}
. It was also shown that the solution mappings
W_{t}:\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi_{t}
uniquely extend to a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and the detection time probability distributions are well-defined for any initial data
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We emphasize that this proposal provides a probability distribution for the times that the particle is first
detected
along
\partial\Omega
, not the arrival time of the particle in the absence of detectors.

This paper aims to establish a converse of the result in
[
37
]
. We shall prove that every
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
L^{2}(\Omega)
whose generator is extended by
-i\hat{H}^{*}
corresponds to the placement of a (potentially non-local) time-independent absorbing boundary condition on
\psi
along
\partial\Omega
. Such a result can already be found in the literature (see e.g.
[
19
,
28
]
), using the theory of boundary triples and linear relations (multivalued linear operators). The boundary triple framework goes back to the work of von Neumann
[
2
,
3
]
and later J.W. Calkin
[
4
]
on the parameterization of self-adjoint extensions of symmetric operators; we direct the reader to the text of V.I. and M.L. Gorbachuk
[
17
]
for a detailed history of the subject. Boundary triples and their more modern incarnations have proven very robust in parameterizing closed extensions of symmetric and skew-symmetric operators, and is notably applied in the study
of spectral properties for closed elliptic differential operators (see e.g.
[
21
,
25
]
). However, the theory of linear relations is not widely known in the mathematical physics community, so the parameterization results remain somewhat inaccessible. It is also challenging to derive a general formula for the detection time distribution in terms of these linear relations. Recently, Wegner
[
27
]

demonstrated that a large class of
C_{0}
contraction semigroups could be parameterized in the boundary triple framework without the use of linear relations. This result made the theory accessible to a wider audience, but the boundary triple framework was too narrow to cover all cases. His result was soon extended to all cases by Arendt et al.
[
33
]
through the newly discovered theory of boundary quadruples.

For a densely defined skew-symmetric operator
-i\hat{H}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a
boundary quadruple
for
-i\hat{H}
consists of two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
and two surjective linear mappings
G_{\pm}:D(-i\hat{H}^{*})\to\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
satisfying

\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}

(1.4)

for all
\psi,\phi\in D(-i\hat{H}^{*})
, and
(G_{+},G_{-}):D(-i\hat{H}^{*})\to\mathcal{H}_{+}\times\mathcal{H}_{-}
is surjective. When
\hat{H}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
is a symmetric differential operator on
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Omega)
, it is often possible to construct a boundary quadruple for
-i\hat{H}
where
G_{\pm}\psi\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
are functions of
\psi
or its derivatives restricted to
\partial\Omega
, in which case we call equation (
1.4
) an “abstract Green’s identity”. All densely defined skew-symmetric operators admit boundary quadruples, and any quadruple can be used to parameterize the set of
C_{0}
contraction semigroups whose generators are extended by
-i\hat{H}^{*}
.

Theorem
.

[
33
, Theorem 3.10]
Let
-i\hat{H}
be a densely defined skew-symmetric operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
(\mathcal{H}_{\pm},G_{\pm})
be a boundary quadruple for
-i\hat{H}
. Then the following are equivalent

(a)

W_{t}=\exp(tB)
is a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{H}
with generator
B\subset-i\hat{H}^{*}
.

(b)

There exists a linear contraction
\Phi:\mathcal{H_{-}}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
such that
B=-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
where

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi}):=\{\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*}):G_{+}\psi=\Phi G_{-}\psi\},\quad\hat{H}_{\Phi}\psi:=-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi.

(1.5)

If
\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})
is a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup with
\hat{H}_{\Phi}
of the form in equation (
1.5
), we call
G_{+}\psi=\Phi G_{-}\Psi
an “absorbing boundary condition” because the norm of
\psi_{t}=\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})\psi_{0}
is lost at rate

\frac{d}{dt}||\psi_{t}||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=||\Phi G_{-}\psi_{t}||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}-||G_{-}\psi_{t}||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}\leq 0.

(1.6)

Since
C_{0}
contraction semigroups preserve the domains of their generators;
\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi}):D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})\to D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})
; the theorem above formally states that for every linear contraction
\Phi:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
the initial-boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclr}i\partial_{t}\psi&=&\hat{H}^{*}\psi\\
\psi\big|_{t=0}&=&\psi_{0}\\
G_{+}\psi&=&\Phi G_{-}\psi\end{array}\right.

(1.7)

admits a unique global-in-time solution for each initial
\psi_{0}\in D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})
, and the solution mappings
W_{t}:\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi_{t}
extend to a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{H}
. It also states that the converse is true, any
C_{0}
contraction semigroup whose generator is a restriction of
-i\hat{H}^{*}
must be the solution mapping of an initial-boundary value problem of the form (
1.7
).

Dissipative Extensions of Skew-Symmetric Operators

In this section we review some applications of boundary quadruples presented in
[
33
]
, then present a proof of Theorem
1
. A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is taken to be a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
equipped with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
that is linear in the first slot and anti-linear in the second slot, such that
\mathcal{H}
is complete with respect to the norm
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{H}}:=\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}}
. An operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
is a linear mapping defined on a subspace
D(A)
, called its domain, which takes values in
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 2.1
.

(a)

An operator
B
is an
extension
of
A
, denoted
A\subset B
, if
D(A)\subset D(B)
and
B\psi=A\psi
for
\psi\in D(A)
.

(b)

An operator
A
is called
dissipative
if

\text{Re}\langle A\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 0,\quad\forall\psi\in D(A).

(2.1)

(c)

An operator
A
is called
skew-symmetric
if
\pm A
are dissipative.

(d)

An operator
A
is called

if
A\subset B
with
B
dissipative implies that
A=B
.

(e)

An operator
A
is called

if
A
is maximal dissipative and

. Equivalently,

(f)

Let

B
a dissipative operator such that
A_{0}\subset B
. Then
B\subset(-A_{0})^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in D(B)
, and
\psi\in D(A_{0})
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
t>0
, we may compute

0\geq\text{Re}\langle B(\psi+\lambda t\phi),\psi+\lambda t\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=t\text{Re}\langle B\psi,\lambda\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+t\text{Re}\langle\lambda B\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+t^{2}\text{Re}\langle\lambda B\phi,\lambda\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(2.4)

since
\text{Re}\langle B\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\text{Re}\langle A_{0}\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0
. Dividing by
t
and taking a limit as
t\to 0
returns

0\geq\text{Re}\langle\lambda^{*}A_{0}\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\text{Re}\langle\lambda B\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(2.5)

Now taking
\lambda=\pm 1
then
\lambda=\pm i
returns

\langle A_{0}\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,B\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0

(2.6)

as desired.
∎

Any dissipative extension of
A_{0}
must be given by some restriction of
(-A_{0})^{*}
, so we would like to identify which class of restrictions return
m
-dissipative operators. To do this we apply the theory of boundary quadruples. For convenience we henceforth denote
A:=(-A_{0})^{*}
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
boundary quadruple
for a densely defined skew-symmetric operator
A_{0}
consists of Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
and continuous, surjective linear maps
G_{\pm}:D(A)\to\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
satisfying

\langle A\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,A\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{\mathcal{H}_{-}}}

(2.7)

for all
\psi,\phi\in D(A)
, and

\ker G_{+}+\ker G_{-}=D(A),\text{ or equivalently }(G_{+},G_{-}):D(A)\to\mathcal{H}_{+}\times\mathcal{H}_{-}\text{ surjective}.

(2.8)

Boundary quadruples always exist (see
[
33
, Example 3.6 and Example 3.7]
), and all boundary quadruples are isometric (see
[
33
, Theorem 3.16]
), but they may not obviously correspond to the behavior of wave functions along the boundary of some region. The example below does.

Example 2.1
.

Consider
A_{0}=\partial_{x}
densely defined on
C_{c}^{\infty}((0,1))\subset L^{2}((0,1))
. This operator is skew symmetric, and its maximal extension is
A=\partial_{x}:H^{1}((0,1))\to L^{2}((0,1))
. Recall by Sobolev embedding theorems
[
22
]
that
H^{1}((0,1))\subset C([0,1])
, the space of continuous functions on
[0,1]
, so the values of
H^{1}
functions are well-defined at the endpoints. Integration by parts returns

\langle\partial_{x}\psi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}((0,1))}+\langle\psi,\partial_{x}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}((0,1))}=\psi(1)\phi^{*}(1)-\psi(0)\phi^{*}(0),\quad\forall\psi,\phi\in H^{1}([0,1]).

(2.9)

A boundary quadruple for
A
is thus given by
G_{\pm}:H^{1}((0,1))\to\mathbb{C}
with
G_{+}\psi:=\psi(1)
,
G_{-}\psi:=\psi(0)
, after checking that
(G_{+},G_{-}):H^{1}((0,1))\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}
surjective.

Given a fixed boundary quadruple
(\mathcal{H}_{\pm},{G}_{\pm})
for a densely defined skew symmetric operator
A_{0}
, we will now parameterize all of its
m
-dissipative extensions. It is useful to first prove a lemma relating the boundary quadruple to
\overline{A_{0}}
. Recall that any densely-defined skew-symmetric operator
A_{0}
is closeable, and its closure
\overline{A_{0}}=(A_{0}^{*})^{*}
is also skew-symmetric.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
33
, Proposition 3.9]

D(\overline{A_{0}})=\ker G_{+}\cap\ker G_{-}
and
\overline{A_{0}}\psi=A\psi
for all
\psi\in D(\overline{A_{0}})
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{A_{0}}
is a dissipative extension of
A_{0}
, Proposition
2.1
implies
\overline{A_{0}}
is extended by
A
.

For the first inclusion, let
\psi\in D(\overline{A_{0}})
. Then for all
\phi\in D(A)

\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}=\langle A\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,A\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0

(2.10)

From the surjectivity of
(G_{+},G_{-}):D(A)\to\mathcal{H}_{+}\times\mathcal{H}_{-}
, there exists
\phi\in D(A)
such that
G_{+}\phi=G_{+}\psi
and
G_{-}\phi=-G_{-}\psi
. Hence

||G_{+}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}^{2}+||G_{-}\psi||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}=0,

(2.11)

so
\psi\in\ker(G_{+})\cap\ker(G_{-})
.

For the other inclusion, let
\psi\in\ker(G_{+})\cap\ker(G_{-})
. Then

\langle A\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,A\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}=0

(2.12)

for all
\phi\in D(A)
. Hence

\langle A\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\langle\psi,A_{0}^{*}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(2.13)

for all
\phi\in D(A_{0}^{*})
, so
\psi\in D((A_{0}^{*})^{*})=D(\overline{A_{0}})
as desired.
∎

Like
\overline{A_{0}}
, the domain of any
m
-dissipative extension of
A_{0}
can be easily expressed in terms of the boundary quadruple. For
\Phi:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
a linear contraction, we define the operator
A_{\Phi}
on
\mathcal{H}
via

D(A_{\Phi}):=\{\psi\in D(A):\Phi G_{-}\psi=G_{+}\psi\},\quad A_{\Phi}\psi:=A\psi.

(2.14)

Clearly
A_{0}\subset A_{\Phi}\subset A
, and
A_{\Phi}
dissipative because for all
\psi\in D(A_{\Phi})

2\text{Re}\langle A_{\Phi}\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=||G_{+}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}^{2}-||G_{-}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}^{2}=||\Phi G_{-}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}^{2}-||G_{-}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}^{2}\leq 0.

(2.15)

We will now parameterize all
C_{0}
contraction semigroup generators that extend
A_{0}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
33
, Theorem 3.10]
Let
B
be an operator on
\mathcal{H}
such that
A_{0}\subset B
. Then the following are equivalent

(a)

B
is
m-
dissipative.

(b)

There exists a linear contraction
\Phi:\mathcal{H_{-}}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
such that
B=A_{\Phi}
.

Before proving this theorem, we first show that the operators
A_{\Phi}
are maximal in their own class.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
be contractions. If
A_{\Phi_{1}}\subset A_{\Phi_{2}}
then
\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{-}
. Then there exists
\psi\in D(A)
such that
G_{-}\psi=\xi
and
G_{+}\psi=\Phi_{1}\xi
. Thus
\psi\in D(A_{\Phi_{1}})
, and by our assumption
\psi\in D(A_{\Phi_{2}})
as well. The proof follows from

\Phi_{2}\xi=\Phi_{2}G_{-}\psi=G_{+}\psi=\Phi_{1}\xi.

(2.16)

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
B
be a dissipative extension of
A_{0}
. Then
B\subset A
, and

0\geq 2\text{Re}\langle B\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=||G_{+}\psi||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}-||G_{-}\psi||^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}\quad\forall\psi\in D(B).

(2.17)

So
||G_{+}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}\leq||G_{-}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}
for all
\psi\in D(B)
. Note that
G_{-}\psi
uniquely determines
G_{+}\psi
for
\psi\in D(B)
, since
G_{-}\psi_{1}=G_{-}\psi_{2}
implies
G_{+}\psi_{1}=G_{+}\psi_{2}
. Let us define the contraction
\Phi:G_{-}D(B)\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
via
\Phi G_{-}\psi:=G_{+}\psi
. We may extend this contraction to all of
\mathcal{H}_{-}
by first extending to
\overline{G_{-}D(B)}
by continuity, and then by
0
on
(\overline{G_{-}D(B)})^{\perp}
. Still denoting this extension by
\Phi
, it follows that
B\subset A_{\Phi}
. Hence
B
is
m
-dissipative only if
B=A_{\Phi}
, since
B
cannot admit any dissipative extensions.

To show each
A_{\Phi}
is
m-
dissipative one repeats the argument above with any dissipative extension
B
of
A_{\Phi}
, so there must exist some linear contraction
\Phi^{\prime}
such that
A_{\Phi}\subset B\subset A_{\Phi^{\prime}}
. By the previous lemma this implies
A_{\Phi}=B=A_{\Phi^{\prime}}
, so
A_{\Phi}
admits no dissipative extensions.
∎

When the boundary quadruple mappings
G_{\pm}
directly relate to the values of the wave function along the boundary
\partial\Omega
of some region
\Omega
, as was the case in Example
2.1
, the condition
G_{+}\psi=\Phi G_{-}\psi
becomes identifiable as an “absorbing boundary condition”. In combination with the theorem of Lumer-Phillips, Theorem
2.2
states that for every linear contraction
\Phi:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
, the initial-boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclr}\partial_{t}\psi&=&A\psi\\
\psi\big|_{t=0}&=&\psi_{0}\\
G_{+}\psi&=&\Phi G_{-}\psi\end{array}\right.

(2.18)

admits a unique global-in-time solution for each initial
\psi_{0}\in D(A_{\Phi})
, and the solution mappings
W_{t}:\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi_{t}
extend to a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{H}
. Theorem
2.2
states that a converse is also true, any
C_{0}
contraction semigroup whose generator is an extension of
A_{0}
(or equivalently, a restriction of
-A_{0}^{*}
) must be the solution mapping of an initial-boundary value problem of the form (
2.18
).

When the boundary condition is
strictly
absorbing;
||\Phi\xi||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}<||\xi||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}
for all
\xi\neq 0
in
\mathcal{H}_{-}
; one may expect
\exp(tA_{\Phi})\psi
to asymptotically vanish as
t\to\infty
. This is generally not true, but the Theorem below provides necessary and sufficient conditions when
A_{\Phi}
has compact resolvent. Note that since
A_{\Phi}
is
m
-dissipative its resolvent set
\rho(A_{\Phi})
is non-empty with
1\in\rho(A_{\Phi})
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
be a linear contraction satisfying
||\Phi\xi||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}<||\xi||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}
for all
0\neq\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{-}
, and suppose
(A_{\Phi}-\alpha)^{-1}
is a compact operator on
\mathcal{H}
for some
\alpha\in\rho(A_{\Phi})
. Then the following are equivalent

1.

\overline{A_{0}}
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis.

2.

||\exp(tA_{\Phi})\psi_{0}||_{\mathcal{H}}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
for each
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

The first direction is straightforward. Suppose
\overline{A_{0}}
admits an eigenvalue
i\lambda
on the imaginary axis, and let
0\neq\psi_{\lambda}\in D(\overline{A_{0}})
satisfy
\overline{A_{0}}\psi_{\lambda}=i\lambda\psi_{\lambda}
. Then
A_{\Phi}\psi_{\lambda}=i\lambda\psi_{\lambda}
, hence
\exp(tA_{\Phi})\psi_{\lambda}=e^{i\lambda t}\psi_{\lambda}
, which does not asymptotically vanish as
t\to\infty
.

To prove the other direction, we rely on a theorem of Arendt, Batty, Lyubich and Phong.

Theorem
.

[
15
,
16
]
Let
W_{t}=\exp(tB)
be a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
\mathcal{H}
such that
B
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis and
\sigma(B)\cap i\mathbb{R}
is countable. Then
||W_{t}\psi_{0}||\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
for each
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.

We aim to show that
A_{\Phi}
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis and
\sigma(A_{\Phi})\cap i\mathbb{R}
is countable. The second condition follows almost immediately from our assumption that
A_{\Phi}
has compact resolvent.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
B
is a densely defined operator with
(B-\lambda)^{-1}
compact for some
\lambda\in\rho(B)
. Then
\sigma(B)
consists only of countably many eigenvalues.

Proof.

When
\mathcal{H}
is finite dimensional this proof becomes trivial, so take
\dim(\mathcal{H})=\infty
. Since
(B-\lambda)^{-1}
is a compact operator on
\mathcal{H}
, the spectral theorem for compact operators
[
22
, Theorem 6.8]
states that
\sigma((B-\lambda)^{-1})
consists only of countably many eigenvalues and
0
. Take
0\neq\mu\in\rho((B-\lambda)^{-1})
. Then

\frac{1}{\mu}-(B-\lambda)=\frac{1}{\mu}(B-\lambda)\left((B-\lambda)^{-1}-\mu\right).

(2.19)

So
\frac{1}{\mu}\in\rho(B-\lambda)
with

\left(\frac{1}{\mu}-(B-\lambda)\right)^{-1}=\mu\left((B-\lambda)^{-1}-\mu\right)^{-1}(B-\lambda)^{-1}.

(2.20)

Hence for any
\nu\in\sigma(B-\lambda)
we have
\nu\neq 0
(since
B-\lambda
is invertible) and
\frac{1}{\nu}\in\sigma((B-\lambda)^{-1})
, in particular
\frac{1}{\nu}
must be an eigenvalue of
(B-\lambda)^{-1}
. But this implies
\nu
is an eigenvalue of
B-\lambda
, so the spectrum of
B-\lambda
only consists of countably many eigenvalues, and consequently
\sigma(B)
only consists of countably many eigenvalues.
∎

Returning to our proof of Theorem
1
, it is now sufficient to prove that
A_{\Phi}
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis whenever
\overline{A_{0}}
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. We proceed by contrapositive, suppose
A_{\Phi}
has an eigenvalue
i\lambda
along the imaginary axis, and let
\psi_{\lambda}\in D(A_{\Phi})
be a corresponding eigenvector. Then

||\Phi G_{-}\psi_{\lambda}||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}^{2}-||G_{-}\psi_{\lambda}||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}^{2}=2\text{Re}\langle A\psi_{\lambda},\psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=2\text{Re}\langle i\lambda\psi_{\lambda},\psi_{\lambda}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0.

(2.21)

Since
||\Phi G_{-}\psi_{\lambda}||_{\mathcal{H}_{+}}=||G_{-}\psi_{\lambda}||_{\mathcal{H}_{-}}
and
\Phi
is
strictly
contractive, we must have
G_{+}\psi_{\lambda}=\Phi G_{-}\psi_{\lambda}=G_{-}\psi_{\lambda}=0
. By Lemma
2.1
,
\psi_{\lambda}\in D(\overline{A_{0}})
and
\overline{A_{0}}\psi_{\lambda}=A_{\Phi}\psi_{\lambda}=i\lambda\psi_{\lambda}
, so
\overline{A_{0}}
must also have eigenvalues along the imaginary axis.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Theorem
1
is typically useful when
A_{0}
is a differential operator on a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega
with domain
D(A_{0})=C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
dense in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. In practice
D(A_{\Phi})
is often (but not always) a subset of
H^{s}(\Omega)
for some
s>0
, in which case
A_{\Phi}
has compact resolvent on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We conclude this section by stating the necessary and sufficient conditions on
\Phi
for
A_{\Phi}
to generate a strongly continuous unitary group.

Definition 2.4
.

A
C_{0}
contraction semigroup
U_{t}
is called a
\mathbf{C_{0}}

unitary semigroup
if
U_{t}
are invertible and norm preserving. Such semigroups can be extended to
t\in\mathbb{R}
by setting
U_{t}=U_{-t}^{*}
for
t<0
, and this extension is called a
\mathbf{C_{0}}

unitary group
.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
33
, Theorem 4.2]

Let
B
be an operator on
\mathcal{H}
such that
A_{0}\subset B
. Then the following are equivalent

(a)

B
generates a
C_{0}
unitary group
\exp(tB)
.

(b)

iB
is a densely defined self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

(c)

There exists a unitary operator
\Phi:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}

.

3
Schrödinger Operators on Bounded
C^{2}
Domains

In this section we parameterize all
m
-dissipative extensions of the densely defined skew-symmetric operator
-i\hat{H}_{0}=-i(-\Delta+V):C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is some bounded
C^{2}
region in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
V
a real valued and bounded potential. Parameterization results for second order elliptic differential operators can already be found in the literature(see e.g.
[
23
,
25
]
) and go back to the classical work of Birman
[
6
]
, Visik
[
5
]
, and Grubb
[
8
]
. However, we will find that taking the approach of Wegner
[
27
]
and Arendt et al.
[
33
]
simplifies the parameterization to a form that is well adapted to defining detection time distributions.

Before tackling the higher dimensional case, let us first review the parameterization result in a simple one dimensional setting.

3.1
One Dimensional Case

For simplicity we only consider the case of a bounded interval
\Omega=(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
. We define the
pre-minimal
operator
\hat{H}_{0}=-\partial_{x}^{2}+V:C_{c}^{\infty}((a,b))\to L^{2}((a,b))
, where
V\in L^{\infty}((a,b),\mathbb{R})
. The closure of the pre-minimal operator is called the
minimal
operator
\hat{H}
and has domain
D(\hat{H})=H^{2}_{0}((a,b))
, while the maximal operator
\hat{H}^{*}=(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)
has domain
D(\hat{H}^{*})=H^{2}((a,b))
. For
\psi\in H^{2}((a,b))
the Sobolev embedding theorems
[
22
]
guarantee
\psi
and
\partial_{x}\psi
admit continuous representatives in
C([a,b])
. We define the mappings
G_{\pm}:H^{2}((a,b))\to\mathbb{C}^{2}

G_{+}\psi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\psi(a)-i\partial_{x}\psi(a)\\
\psi(b)+i\partial_{x}\psi(b)\end{pmatrix},\quad G_{-}\psi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\psi(a)+i\partial_{x}\psi(a)\\
\psi(b)-i\partial_{x}\psi(b)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.1)

It is then easy to apply integration by parts and show

\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}((a,b))}+\langle\psi,-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}((a,b)}=\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}},\quad\forall\psi,\phi\in H^{2}((a,b)).

(3.2)

We now state the parameterization result for Schrödinger operators in one space dimension.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
33
, Theorem 6.5]
Let
(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
be a bounded interval, and let
\hat{H}:=(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)\big|_{H^{2}_{0}((a,b))}
with
V\in L^{\infty}((a,b),\mathbb{R})
. Then the maps
G_{\pm}:H^{2}((a,b))\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
define a boundary quadruple for
-i\hat{H}
. Consequently, a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup
W_{t}
on
L^{2}((a,b))
has its generator extended by
-i\hat{H}^{*}
if and only if there exists a linear contraction
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
such that
W_{t}=\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})
with

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi}):=\{\psi\in H^{2}((a,b)):G_{+}\psi=\Phi G_{-}\psi\},\quad\hat{H}_{\Phi}\psi:=(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)\psi.

(3.3)

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
, Theorem
2.2
, and equation (
3.2
), it suffices to show

\ker G_{-}+\ker G_{+}=H^{2}((a,b)).

(3.4)

Let
\psi\in H^{2}((a,b))
, and choose
\xi,\phi\in C^{2}((a,b))
such that

\phi(a)=-i\partial_{x}\psi(a),\quad\partial_{x}\phi(a)=i\psi(a),\quad\phi(b)=\partial_{x}\phi(b)=0.

(3.5)

\xi(b)=-i\partial_{x}\psi(b),\quad\partial_{x}\xi(b)=i\psi(b),\quad\xi(a)=\partial_{x}\xi(a)=0.

(3.6)

Then

\frac{1}{2}(\psi+\phi-\xi)\in\ker G_{+},\text{ and }\frac{1}{2}(\psi-\phi+\xi)\in\ker{G_{-}},

(3.7)

so the sum
\psi\in\ker G_{+}+\ker G_{-}
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

This simple boundary quadruple construction fails for Schrödinger operators in dimensions
n\geq 2
. This is because in higher space dimensions the domain of the maximal operator
D(\hat{H}^{*})
is not contained within any Sobolev space
H^{s}(\Omega)
for
s>0
. So, one cannot rely on the standard Sobelev embedding theorems to make sense of the values of
\psi
and its normal derivative along the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

The following proposition reviews some classical examples of boundary conditions and their associated extensions of
\hat{H}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Setting
\Phi_{N}=\mathds{1}
, the identity on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, returns the
Neumann
extension
\hat{H}_{N}
of
\hat{H}
, with domain

D(\hat{H}_{N})=\{\psi\in H^{2}((a,b)):\partial_{x}\psi(a)=\partial_{x}\psi(b)=0\}.

(3.8)

Setting
\Phi_{D}=-\mathds{1}
returns the
Dirichlet
extension
\hat{H}_{D}
of
\hat{H}
, with domain

D(\hat{H}_{D})=\{\psi\in H^{2}((a,b)):\psi(a)=\psi(b)=0\}.

(3.9)

Setting
\Phi_{P}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
returns the
periodic
extension
\hat{H}_{P}
of
\hat{H}
, with domain

D(\hat{H}_{P})=\{\psi\in H^{2}((a,b)):\psi(a)=\psi(b),~\partial_{x}\psi(a)=\partial_{x}\psi(b)\}.

(3.10)

Since these linear contractions are all unitary, we have that
\hat{H}_{N},\hat{H}_{D}
, and
\hat{H}_{P}
are all self-adjoint extensions of
\hat{H}
.

Remark 3.2
.

The evolutions generated by these self-adjoint operators preserve the total probability in
\Omega
and are thus not valid as theoretical models for quantum particles undergoing detection. The last example additionally gives rise to non-local dynamics, with probability allowed to exit through one end-point and be instantly transported to the other.

Proposition 3.2
.

\hat{H}_{\Phi}
generates local dynamics in
(a,b)
if and only if
\Phi
is of the form
\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{a}&0\\
0&\Phi_{b}\end{pmatrix}
with
|\Phi_{a}|,|\Phi_{b}|\leq 1
. If
\Phi_{a}\neq 1\neq\Phi_{b}
then
H_{\Phi}
is a
local Robin
extension

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})=\{\psi\in H^{2}((a,b)):-\partial_{x}\psi(a)=i\frac{1-\Phi_{a}}{1+\Phi_{a}}\psi(a),~\partial_{x}\psi(b)=i\frac{1-\Phi_{b}}{1+\Phi_{b}}\psi(b)\}.

(3.11)

If
|\Phi_{a}|
and
|\Phi_{b}|
are strictly less than
1
, then by Theorem
1
the evolution generated by
\hat{H}_{\Phi}
is asymptotically stable,
||\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}((a,b))}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
for each
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}((a,b))
.

3.2
Higher Dimensions

Fix
n\geq 2
and let
\Omega
be a bounded region of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{2}
boundary
\partial\Omega
. In this section we seek to describe all
m-
dissipative extensions of the Schrödinger operator
-i\hat{H}_{0}=-i(-\Delta+V):C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
, where
V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. We accomplish this by constructing a boundary quadruple for the maximal operator
-i\hat{H}_{0}^{*}
. This section will closely follow the boundary triple construction presented in chapter
9
of
[
28
]
.

We denote the Sobolev space of order
k\in\mathbb{R}
on
\Omega
by
H^{k}(\Omega)
, and the closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
in
H^{k}(\Omega)
is denoted
H_{0}^{k}(\Omega)
. Sobolev spaces on the boundary are denoted
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
. For
s>0
the dual space pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{H^{-s}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{s}(\partial\Omega)}
is anti-linear in the second slot and satisfies

\langle\xi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-s}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{s}(\partial\Omega)}=\begin{cases}\langle\xi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},\quad&\xi\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\\
\langle\xi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-t}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{t}(\partial\Omega)},\quad&\xi\in H^{-t}(\partial\Omega),~0\leq t\leq s\end{cases}

(3.12)

for
\chi\in H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
. Let
\iota_{\pm}:H^{\pm 1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
denote the isometric isomorphisms such that

\langle\xi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}=\langle\iota_{-}\xi,\iota_{+}\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(3.13)

Remark 3.3
.

On
\mathbb{R}^{n}
the isometries
\iota_{\pm}:H^{\pm 1/2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be represented in terms of the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
as
\iota_{\pm}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(1+|\cdot|^{2})^{\pm 1/4}\mathcal{F}
.

For
s\in[0,3/2]
the restrictions

\iota_{+}:H^{s+1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s}(\partial\Omega)

(3.14)

and

\iota_{-}:H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s+1/2}(\partial\Omega)

(3.15)

are isometric isomorphisms such that
\iota_{+}\iota_{-}\xi=0
for all
\xi\in H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
and
\iota_{-}\iota_{+}\chi=\chi
for all
\chi\in H^{s+1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.

Denoting the unit normal vector field pointing outwards of
\Omega
by
\partial_{n}
, we recall a classical result for the trace operator of
H^{2}(\Omega)
functions to the boundary.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
10
, Theorem 8.3]
For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded
C^{2}
domain, the trace map
\psi\mapsto\left(\psi\big|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{n}\psi\big|_{\partial\Omega}\right)
defined for
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\to H^{{3/2}}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{{1/2}}(\partial\Omega)
admits a continuous extension
\psi\mapsto\left(\tau_{D}\psi,\tau_{N}\psi\right)
that is surjective for
H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{{1/2}}(\partial\Omega)
and admits a continuous right inverse.

We call
\tau_{D}
and
\tau_{N}
the Dirichlet and Neumann operator respectively. These operators can be used to prove a Green’s identity for the Laplacian.

Lemma
.

(Green’s identity).
For
u,v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, one can integrate by parts to show

\langle i\Delta u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle u,i\Delta v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=i\left(\langle\tau_{N}u,\tau_{D}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle\tau_{D}u,\tau_{N}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right).

(3.16)

To construct a boundary quadruple for
-i\hat{H}_{0}^{*}
, we wish to extend these trace operators to
D(-i\hat{H}_{0}^{*})
and prove a similar Green’s identity.

First, let us recall that the
preminimal
operator for
-\Delta+V
is defined on
D(\hat{H}_{0})={C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. The closure of
\hat{H}_{0}
is the
minimal
operator
\hat{H}
, and has domain
D(\hat{H})=H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
. The
maximal
operator is the adjoint of the
minimal operator
, i.e
\hat{H}^{*}
. We must point out that
H^{2}(\Omega)\subset D(\hat{H}^{*})
but the two sets are not equal, so some care must be taken when defining
(\psi,\partial_{n}\psi)\big|_{\partial\Omega}
for
\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*})
.

In addition to the minimal and maximal operators, our boundary quadruple construction will frequently refer to two closed extensions of
\hat{H}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
C^{2}
bounded region, and let
\hat{H}:=(-\Delta+V)\big|_{H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}
with
V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Then the Dirichlet extension
\hat{H}_{D}
defined by

D(\hat{H}_{D}):=\{\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega):\tau_{D}\psi=0\},\quad\hat{H}_{D}\psi:=(-\Delta+V)\psi

(3.17)

is a closed symmetric operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Similarly, the Neumann extension
\hat{H}_{N}
defined by

D(\hat{H}_{N}):=\{\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega):\tau_{N}\psi=0\},\quad\hat{H}_{N}\psi:=(-\Delta+V)\psi.

(3.18)

is a closed symmetric operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

These operators are useful in allowing us to write the domain of the maximal operator as a direct sum of familiar spaces.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})
we have the direct sum decompositions

D(\hat{H}^{*})=\ker\tau_{D}\oplus\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)

(3.19)

H^{2}(\Omega)=\ker\tau_{D}\oplus\left\{\psi_{\lambda}\in H^{2}(\Omega):(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)\psi_{\lambda}=0\right\}.

(3.20)

Similarly for any
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{N})
we have

D(\hat{H}^{*})=\ker\tau_{N}\oplus\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)

(3.21)

H^{2}(\Omega)=\ker\tau_{N}\oplus\left\{\psi_{\lambda}\in H^{2}(\Omega):(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)\psi_{\lambda}=0\right\}.

(3.22)

Proof.

We prove this for
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})
, and claim an identical proof holds for
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{N})
. Let
\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*})
. By the invertibility of
(\lambda-\hat{H}_{D}):H^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique
\psi_{D}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
such that
(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)\psi_{D}=(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)\psi
. Since
\hat{H}_{D}\subset\hat{H}^{*}
, it follows that
\psi_{\lambda}:=\psi-\psi_{D}
satisfies
(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)\psi_{\lambda}=0
. Hence
\psi=\psi_{D}+\psi_{\lambda}\in D(\hat{H}_{D})\oplus\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
, and the proof follows from
D(\hat{H}_{D})=\ker\tau_{D}
.
∎

These decompositions allow us to extend the trace operators
\tau_{D}
,
\tau_{N}
to the domain of the maximal operator, and play a crucial role in writing down an “abstract Green’s identity” for
-i\hat{H}^{*}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

[
28
, Theorem 8.3.9]

The Dirichlet and Neumann trace operators
\tau_{D}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\tau_{N}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
admit continuous and surjective extensions

\tilde{\tau}_{D}:D(\hat{H}^{*})\to H^{{-1/2}}(\partial\Omega),\quad\tilde{\tau}_{N}:D(\hat{H}^{*})\to H^{{-3/2}}(\partial\Omega)

(3.23)

In addition,

\ker(\tilde{\tau}_{D})=\ker(\tau_{D})=D(\hat{H}_{D}),\quad\ker(\tilde{\tau}_{N})=\ker(\tau_{N})=D(\hat{H}_{N}).

(3.24)

The extended trace operators can be used to prove an extended Green’s identity for elements in
\hat{H}^{*}
.

Corollary 3.1
.

[
28
, Corollary 8.3.11]

The Green’s identity can be extended to

\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi,-i\hat{H}^{*}v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=i\left(\langle\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi,\tau_{D}v\rangle_{H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi,\tau_{N}v\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\right).

(3.25)

for
\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*})
,
v\in H^{2}(\Omega)
.

The formula above does not seem sufficient for our purposes, as we require a Green’s identity in the case that both
\psi
and
v
are in
D(\hat{H}^{*})
. Luckily, it is possible to extend this identity by decomposing the wave functions appropriately.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\eta\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, which exists since
\hat{H}_{D}-V
is a positive operator and
V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Also let
\iota_{\pm}:H^{\pm{1/2}}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
be the isomorphisms such that
\langle\xi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}=\langle\iota_{-}\xi,\iota_{+}\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
. Then, defining
G_{\pm}(\eta):D(-i\hat{H}^{*})\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
by

G_{+}\psi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi+i\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}\right)

(3.26)

G_{-}\psi:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi-i\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}\right)

(3.27)

where
\psi=\psi_{D}+\psi_{\eta}
is decomposed according to equation (
3.19
) returns a further extension of the Green’s identity for elements in
\psi,\phi\in D(\hat{H}^{*})

\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi,-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(3.28)

Proof.

Let
\psi,\phi\in D(\hat{H})
, and decompose
\psi=\psi_{D}+\psi_{\eta}
,
\phi=\phi_{D}+\phi_{\eta}
according to equation (
3.19
). First, since
\hat{H}_{D}
is self-adjoint

\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi_{D},\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi_{D},-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle-i\hat{H}_{D}\psi_{D},\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi_{D},-i\hat{H}_{D}\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

(3.29)

Similarly, since
\eta
is real we have

\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi_{\eta},\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi_{\eta},-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle-i\eta\psi_{\eta},\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi_{\eta},-i\eta\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

(3.30)

Hence

\begin{split}\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi,-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi_{\eta},\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi_{\eta},-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&+\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi_{D},\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi_{D},-i\hat{H}^{*}\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(3.31)

Since
\psi_{D},\phi_{D}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, we may apply the generalized Green’s identity
(\ref{Green's Identity Star})
to the two pairs of terms. Applying the identity along with
\tau_{D}\psi_{D}=\tau_{D}\phi_{D}=0
returns

\begin{split}\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}\psi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\psi,-&i\hat{H}^{*}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\\
&=i\left(\langle\tau_{N}\psi_{D},\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi_{\eta}\rangle_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi_{\eta},\tau_{N}\phi_{D}\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\right)\\
&=i\left(\langle\tau_{N}\psi_{D},\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi\rangle_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi,\tau_{N}\phi_{D}\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\right)\\
&=i\left(\langle\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D},\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi,\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right)\end{split}

(3.32)

It is then not difficult to compute

\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=i\left(\langle\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D},\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}-\langle\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi,\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\phi_{D}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right).

(3.33)

This concludes our proof of Proposition
3.3
. ∎

We now restate and prove the second main result of this paper.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain, and let
\hat{H}:=(-\Delta+V)\big|_{H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}
with
V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Then for any
\eta\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, the maps
G_{\pm}(\eta):D(\hat{H}^{*})\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
defined in Proposition
3.3
define a boundary quadruple for
-i\hat{H}
. Consequently, a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup
W_{t}
on
L^{2}(\Omega)
has its generator extended by
-i\hat{H}^{*}
if and only if there exists a linear contraction
\Phi:L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
such that
W_{t}=\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})
with

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi}):=\{\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*}):\Phi G_{-}\psi=G_{+}\psi\},\quad\hat{H}_{\Phi}\psi:=(-\Delta+V)\psi.

(3.34)

Proof.

Fix
\eta\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
. By Theorem
2.1
, Theorem
2.2
, and equation (
3.28
), it suffices to show

(G_{+},G_{-}):D(\hat{H}^{*})\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\times L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\text{ is surjective.}

(3.35)

To that end, let
\xi,\chi\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and consider
\iota_{-}^{-1}\chi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\iota_{+}^{-1}\xi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. From (
3.1
) we have that
\tau_{N}
is a surjective mapping from
D(\hat{H}_{D})
onto
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, hence there exists some
\phi_{D}\in D(\hat{H}_{D})
such that
\tau_{N}\phi_{D}=\iota_{+}^{-1}\xi
. Recall also that
\tilde{\tau}_{D}
is surjective from
D(\hat{H}^{*})
to
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and that
\ker\tilde{\tau}_{D}=\ker\tau_{D}=D(\hat{H}_{D})
. It follows from the direct sum decomposition (
3.19
) that the restriction
\tilde{\tau}_{D}:\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\eta)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is bijective, hence there exists
\phi_{\eta}\in\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\eta)
such that
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi_{\eta}=\iota_{-}^{-1}\chi
. It follows that
\phi:=\phi_{D}+\phi_{\eta}\in D(\hat{H}^{*})
satisfies

\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi=\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\phi_{\eta}=\iota_{-}\iota_{-}^{-1}\chi=\chi

(3.36)

\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\phi_{D}=\iota_{+}\iota_{+}^{-1}\xi=\xi.

(3.37)

It follows that

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}(G_{-}+G_{+})\\
\sqrt{2}i(G_{-}-G_{+})\end{pmatrix}:{D(\hat{H}^{*}})\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\times L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\text{ is surjective}.

(3.38)

One can then easily show that
(G_{+},G_{-})
is surjective as well.
∎

4
Examples of Boundary Conditions in Higher Dimensions

In this section we fix a boundary quadruple and construct linear contractions
\Phi
which correspond to commonly known boundary conditions for the Schrödinger operator. We will find it most convenient to fix
\eta\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
, which exists since
\hat{H}_{D}-V
and
\hat{H}_{N}-V
are positive operators and
V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. For a given contraction
\Phi
, the
m
-dissipative extension
-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
of
-i\hat{H}
is defined via

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi}):=\left\{\psi\in\hat{H}^{*}:G_{+}\psi=\Phi G_{-}\psi\right\}=\left\{\psi\in\hat{H}^{*}:i(\mathds{1}-\Phi)\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=(\mathds{1}+\Phi)\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}\right\}.

(4.1)

Example 4.1
.

Dirichlet
boundary conditions: Setting
\Phi=-\mathds{1}
returns

D(\hat{H}_{-\mathds{1}})=\left\{\psi\in\hat{H}^{*}:\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=0\right\}.

(4.2)

Since
-\mathds{1}
is unitary, it follows from Theorem
2.3
that
\hat{H}_{-\mathds{1}}
is a self-adjoint extension of
\hat{H}
. Also, from Lemma
3.4
we know
\ker(\tilde{\tau}_{D})=\ker(\tau_{D})=D(\hat{H}_{D})
, so
\hat{H}_{-\mathds{1}}=\hat{H}_{D}
.

Example 4.2
.

Krein type extension
: Setting
\Phi=\mathds{1}
returns

D(\hat{H}_{\mathds{1}})=\left\{\psi\in\hat{H}^{*}:\tau_{N}\psi_{D}=0\right\}.

(4.3)

\hat{H}_{\mathds{1}}
is also a self-adjoint extension of
\hat{H}
, but this is
not
the Neumann extension, as
\psi_{D}=(\hat{H}_{D}-\eta)^{-1}(\hat{H}^{*}-\eta)\psi\neq\psi
.

To recover the Neumann boundary condition we must construct a contraction
\Phi
so that
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi
and
\tau_{N}\psi_{D}
drop out of the boundary condition, and we are just left with
\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi=0
. To accomplish this we introduce the “Dirichlet-to-Neumann” map. This map relies on the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})
. Then for each
\xi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
, there exists a unique
\psi_{\lambda}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
such that
\tau_{D}\psi_{\lambda}=\xi
.

Proof.

From the surjectivity of
\tau_{D}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
, there exists a
\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
such that
{\tau}_{D}\psi=\xi
. Decomposing
\psi=\psi_{D}+\psi_{\lambda}
according to equation (
3.20
), we find
{\tau}_{D}\psi={\tau}_{D}\psi_{\lambda}=\xi
, proving existence. For uniqueness, we observe that if
\psi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
have the same trace, then
\psi_{\lambda}-\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}\in\ker\tau_{D}\cap\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)=\{0\}
since
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})
.
∎

Definition 4.1
.

For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, the “Dirichlet-to-Neumann” map is defined by

D(\lambda):H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),\quad D(\lambda)(\tau_{D}\psi_{\lambda}):=\tau_{N}\psi_{\lambda}.

(4.4)

Proposition 4.1
.

For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, the “Dirichlet-to-Neumann” map is a bounded linear operator
D(\lambda):H^{3/2}(\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\Omega)
and is symmetric on
L^{2}(\Omega)
. For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
this operator is a bijective linear map with bounded inverse.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})
and let
\chi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Choosing some
f_{\lambda}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
such that
\tau_{D}f_{\lambda}=\chi
. we may apply integration by parts to see

\begin{split}\langle D(\lambda)\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}&=\langle D(\lambda)\tau_{D}f_{\lambda},\tau_{D}f_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=\langle\tau_{N}f_{\lambda},\tau_{D}f_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=||\vec{\nabla}f_{\lambda}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle\Delta f_{\lambda},f_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=||\vec{\nabla}f_{\lambda}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle(V-\lambda)f_{\lambda},f_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.\end{split}

(4.5)

Hence
\langle D(\lambda)\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
\chi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
, i.e
D(\lambda)
is symmetric on
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
and thus closeable. Its closure maps
H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and is thus a bounded linear operator between these two spaces by the closed graph theorem.

When
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})
both decompositions (
3.20
), (
3.22
) hold and injectivity of
D(\lambda)
follows from
\ker\tau_{N}\cap\ker(H^{*}-\lambda)=\{0\}
. For surjectivity, recall from Lemma
3.1
that
\tau_{N}:H^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is surjective. Since
\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
can be decomposed into
\psi=\psi_{N}+\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}\in\ker(\tau_{N})\oplus(H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\ker(H^{*}-\lambda))
with
\tau_{N}\psi=\tau_{N}\psi_{\lambda}^{\prime}
, we have
\tau_{N}:\left(H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\ker(H^{*}-\lambda)\right)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
must also be surjective, hence
D(\lambda)
is surjective.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
. Then the operator
\Theta_{N}(\lambda):H^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, defined as
\Theta_{N}(\lambda):=\iota_{+}D(\lambda)\iota_{-}^{-1}
is a densely defined bijective symmetric operator in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, and is hence an unbounded self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
with
\pm i\in\rho(\Theta_{N})
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\langle\Theta_{N}(\lambda)\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
\chi\in H^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Let
\chi
be such an element, and let
\xi=\iota_{-}^{-1}\chi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Then

\langle\iota_{+}D(\lambda)\iota_{-}^{-1}\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\langle\iota_{+}D(\lambda)\chi,\iota_{-}\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\langle D(\lambda)\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

(4.6)

Hence
\langle\Theta_{N}\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\langle D(\lambda)\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
\chi\in H^{2}(\partial\Omega)
as desired.
∎

Example 4.3
.

Neumann
boundary condition: Set
\Theta_{N}=\Theta_{N}(\eta)
and
\Phi=(i+\Theta_{N})(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}
, which is well defined since
i\in\rho(\Theta_{N})
. It is easy to verify

(\mathds{1}+\Phi)=2i(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1},\quad(\mathds{1}-\Phi)=-2\Theta_{N}(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}.

(4.7)

so the domain of
\hat{H}_{\Phi}
is given by

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})=\left\{\psi\in\hat{H}^{*}:-\Theta_{N}(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}\right\}.

(4.8)

We mention that
\Phi
is the Caley transform of a self-adjoint operator, so
\Phi
is unitary and
\hat{H}_{\Phi}
is a self-adjoint extension of
\hat{H}
. For
\psi\in D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})
our boundary condition implies

\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=(i-\Theta_{N})(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=i(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi+(i-\Theta_{N})^{-1}\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}

(4.9)

hence
\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\in D(\Theta_{N})=H^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Consequently, the trace
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
which by Lemma
3.4
implies that
\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, so
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=\tau_{D}\psi
. Applying
(i-\Theta_{N})
to both sides of the boundary condition returns

\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}=-\Theta_{N}\iota_{-}\tau_{D}\psi=-\Theta_{N}\iota_{-}\tau_{D}\psi_{\eta}=-\iota_{+}D(\eta)\tau_{D}\psi_{\eta}=-\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{\eta}=\iota_{+}(\tau_{N}\psi_{D}-\tau_{N}\psi)

(4.10)

so
\tau_{N}\psi=0
, and we have
\hat{H}_{\Phi}=\hat{H}_{N}
as desired.

For the remainder of this section we will be primarily interested in extensions of
-i\hat{H}
that generate non-unitary dynamics. The extensions will have domains that are subsets of
H^{s}(\Omega)
for some
s>0
, so the following lemma will be useful in proofs concerning asymptotic stability.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded
C^{2}
domain, the minimal operator
\hat{H}=(-\Delta+V):H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
admits no eigenvalues.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction. Suppose that there exists a
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
and
\psi_{\lambda}\in H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
\hat{H}\psi_{\lambda}=\lambda\psi_{\lambda}
. Denote
V_{+}=V+||V||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, so
\psi_{\lambda}
is an eigenvector of
\left(-\Delta+V_{+}\right)
with eigenvalue
\lambda+||V||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. We may extend
\psi_{\lambda}
to a function
\overline{\psi}_{\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by setting
\overline{\psi}_{\lambda}=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Since this extension mapping
\psi\mapsto\overline{\psi}
is a continuous linear operator
[
22
]
from
H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows that
\overline{\psi}_{\lambda}
is an eigenvector of the operator
(-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+}):H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with eigenvalue
\lambda+||V||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. However, it can be shown that this operator has no eigenvalues. To see this we note that
(-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+})
is a positive operator on
L^{2}(\Omega)
so
\sigma(\Delta+\overline{V}_{+})\subset[0,\infty)
. By Rellich–Kondrachov
[
22
]

\mathds{1}_{\Omega}\circ(-\Delta+\mu)^{-1}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a compact operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
\mu\in\rho(-\Delta)
, so
-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+}
is a relatively compact perturbation of
-\Delta
. This implies that the essential spectrum of these operators are the same
[
18
, Theorem 8.4.3]
,
\sigma_{\text{ess}}(-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+})=\sigma_{\text{ess}}(-\Delta)=[0,\infty)
. Hence
\sigma(-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+})=\sigma_{\text{ess}}(-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+})
, contradicting our early statement that
\overline{\psi}_{\lambda}
is an eigenvector of
-\Delta+\overline{V}_{+}
.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

(regular
Robin
boundary condition) Let
\beta:H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
be a compact operator such that
\text{Re}\langle\beta\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\geq 0
for all
\chi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Then the operator defined via

D(-i\hat{H}_{\beta}):=\left\{\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega):\tau_{N}\psi=i\beta\tau_{D}\psi\right\},\quad-i\hat{H}_{\beta}:=-i\hat{H}^{*}\big|_{D(-i\hat{H}_{\beta})}

(4.11)

is an
m
-dissipative extension of
-i\hat{H}
. If the real part of
\beta
is strictly positive;
\text{Re}\langle\beta\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}>0
for all
0\neq\chi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
; then
||\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\beta})\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
for each
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

As before, our goal is to construct a contraction
\Phi
so that
\tau_{N}\psi_{D}
drops out of the boundary condition and we are left with a relation between
\tau_{D}\psi
and
\tau_{N}\psi
. Towards this goal we first prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
. Then the operator
\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda):=\Theta_{N}(\lambda)-i\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}:H^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is a densely defined operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, with
i\in\rho(\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda))
.

Proof.

Recall that
i-\Theta_{N}(\lambda):H^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is a bijective and bounded linear map, and is thus Fredholm with index
0
. Since
\beta
is compact and
\iota_{-}^{-1}:H^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\iota_{+}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
are isometries, it follows that
\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}:H^{2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
is compact. By the stability of Fredholm operators under compact perturbations
[
22
]
, we have that
i-\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda)=i-\Theta_{N}(\lambda)+i\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}
is also Fredholm of index
0
, so it suffices to prove that the map is injective. Let
\xi\in H^{2}(\partial\Omega)
with
\chi=\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Since
\Theta_{N}(\lambda)
is symmetric

\text{Im}\langle(i-\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\text{Im}\langle i\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\text{Re}\langle\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=||\xi||^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\text{Re}\langle\beta\chi,\chi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(4.12)

Hence

||\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\leq|\text{Im}\langle(i-\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}|\leq||(i-\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}||\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

(4.13)

which implies
||\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq||(i-\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
. It follows that
(i-\Theta_{\beta}(\lambda))
is an injective Fredholm operator of index
0
and is thus invertible.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Set
\Theta_{\beta}=\Theta_{\beta}(\eta)
and
\Phi=(i+\Theta_{\beta})(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1}
. It is easy to verify

(\mathds{1}+\Phi)=2i(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1},\quad(\mathds{1}-\Phi)=-2\Theta_{\beta}(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1}.

(4.14)

The domain of
\hat{H}_{\Phi}
is then given by

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})=\left\{\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*}):-\Theta_{\beta}(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{+}\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi_{D}\right\}.

(4.15)

To show
\hat{H}_{\Phi}
is a maximally dissipative extension of
\hat{H}
, we must prove
||(i+\Theta_{\beta})(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1}\chi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq||\chi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
. Setting
\xi=(i-\Theta_{\beta})^{-1}\chi
, it is equivalent to prove
||(i+\Theta_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq||(i-\Theta_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
for all
\xi\in H^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. This follows immediately from

||(i-\Theta_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}-||(i+\Theta_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}=4\text{Re}\langle\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi,\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=4\text{Re}\langle\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi,\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\geq 0.

(4.16)

Repeating the same steps as in the Neumann example, the boundary condition implies
\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\in D(\Theta_{\beta})=H^{2}(\partial\Omega)
for all
\psi\in D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})
, and in particular
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
which by Lemma
3.4
implies that
\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
, so
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=\tau_{D}\psi
. Applying
(i-\Theta_{\beta})
to both sides of the boundary condition returns

\begin{split}\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}&=-(\Theta_{N}-i\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1})\iota_{-}\tau_{D}\psi\\
&=(i\iota_{+}\beta\tau_{D}\psi-\Theta_{N}\iota_{-}\tau_{D}\psi_{\eta})\\
&=\iota_{+}(i\beta\tau_{D}\psi-D(\eta)\tau_{D}\psi_{\eta})\\
&=\iota_{+}(i\beta\tau_{D}\psi-\tau_{N}\psi+\tau_{N}\psi_{D})\end{split}

(4.17)

so
D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})=\left\{\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega):\tau_{N}\psi=i\beta\tau_{D}\psi\right\}
as desired.

Now, when the real part of
\beta
is
strictly
positive we have from equation (
4.16
) that
\Phi
is
strictly
contractive on
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The contraction semigroup generator
-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
has compact resolvent because for
\lambda\in\rho(-i\hat{H}_{\Phi})
the resolvent operator
(-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}-\lambda)^{-1}
maps
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
H^{2}(\Omega)
, which by Rellich-Kondrachov
[
22
]
embeds compactly into
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The generator
-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
meets the requirements of Theorem
1
, so
||\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
holds for all
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
if the operator
(-\Delta+V):H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
admits no real eigenvalues. This follows immediately from Lemma
4.3
, concluding our proof of Theorem
4.1
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Since the embeddings
H^{1/2+\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)\xhookrightarrow{}H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and
H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\xhookrightarrow{}H^{3/2-\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)
are compact
[
22
]
for any
\epsilon>0
, a sufficient condition for
\beta
to be compact is taking
\beta:H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2+\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)
or
\beta:H^{3/2-\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
bounded for some
\epsilon>0
. If
\beta
is multiplication by some function, it is sufficient to take
\beta\in H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
for any
s>\max\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{n-2}{2}\}

[
30
]
.

However, this construction does not allow
\beta
to be multiplication by some function of lower regularity, such as an
L^{\infty}
function. Our generalization to lower regularity
\beta
will be slightly more complicated, and we no longer expect
D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})
to reside within
H^{2}(\Omega)
since
\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi=i\beta\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\notin H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. We now restate and prove the third main result of this paper.

Theorem 3
.

(
Robin
boundary condition) Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain of dimension
n>1
, and let
\hat{H}:=(-\Delta+V)\big|_{C_{c}^{\infty}}(\Omega)
with
V\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Then for
\beta:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
a compact operator such that
\text{Re}\langle\beta\chi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\geq 0
for all
\chi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, the operator defined by

D(-i\hat{H}_{\beta}):=\left\{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap D(\hat{H}^{*}):\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi=i\beta\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\right\},\quad-i\hat{H}_{\beta}:=-i\hat{H}^{*}\big|_{D(-i\hat{H}_{\beta})}

(4.18)

is a maximally dissipative extension of
-i\hat{H}
. Consequently, for each
\psi_{0}\in D(-i\hat{H}_{\beta})
the initial-boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclr}i\partial_{t}\psi&=&\hat{H}\psi&\text{in }\Omega\\
\psi&=&\psi_{0}&\text{at }t=0\\
\partial_{n}\psi&=&i\beta\psi&\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.

(4.19)

admits a unique, global-in-time solution
\psi_{t}=\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\beta})\psi_{0}\in C^{1}([0,\infty),L^{2}(\Omega))
, and the solution mappings
W_{t}:\psi_{0}\mapsto\psi_{t}
extend continuously to a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on
L^{2}(\Omega)
. If the real part of
\beta
is strictly positive;
\text{Re}\langle\beta\chi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}>0
for all
0\neq\chi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
; then
||\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\beta})\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
for each
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Our first step will be to introduce the extended “Dirichlet-to-Neumann” maps.

Definition 4.2
.

For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, the extended “Dirichlet-to-Neumann” map is defined by

\tilde{D}(\lambda):H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega),\quad\tilde{D}(\lambda)(\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi):=\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi_{\lambda}

(4.20)

where here
\psi
has been decomposed into
\psi=\psi_{D}+\psi_{\lambda}\in\ker\tau_{D}\oplus\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
according to equation (
3.19
).

Proposition 4.2
.

For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, the extended “Dirichlet-to-Neumann” map
\tilde{D}(\lambda)
is equal to the adjoint of
D(\lambda):H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and is thus a bounded linear operator from
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
to
H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega)
. In addition, its restrictions
\tilde{D}(\lambda):H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s-1}(\partial\Omega)
are bounded linear operators for
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
, with
\tilde{D}(\lambda):H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
“symmetric” in the sense that
\langle\tilde{D}(\lambda)\zeta,\zeta\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\in\mathbb{R}
for all
\zeta\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
the operators
\tilde{D}(\lambda):H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s-1}(\partial\Omega)
are bijective linear maps with bounded inverse for
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
,
\xi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\chi\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
, and choose
f_{\lambda}\in\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
and
g_{\lambda}\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap\ker(H^{*}-\lambda)
such that
\tau_{D}f_{\lambda}=\xi
,
\tau_{D}g_{\lambda}=\chi
. Then by the extended Green’s identity (
3.25
) we have

\begin{split}\langle\tilde{D}(\lambda)\xi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}&=\langle\tilde{\tau}_{N}f_{\lambda},\tau_{D}g_{\lambda}\rangle_{H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=\langle\tilde{\tau}_{D}f_{\lambda},\tau_{N}g_{\lambda}\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=\langle\xi,D(\lambda)\chi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\end{split}

(4.21)

where in the second line we used
\langle-i\hat{H}^{*}f_{\lambda},g_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\langle f_{\lambda},-i\hat{H}^{*}g_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=0
. Hence
\tilde{D}(\lambda)
is equal to the Banach space adjoint of
D(\lambda)
, making it a bounded linear operator from
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
to
H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Since
\tilde{D}(\lambda)
also extends
D(\lambda)
, and
D(\lambda)
is bounded from
H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
to
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, interpolation (see e.g.
[
10
, Theorems 5.1 and 7.7]
) implies the restrictions

\tilde{D}(\lambda):H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s-1}(\partial\Omega)

(4.22)

are bounded for
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
.

To prove
\tilde{D}(\lambda):H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is “symmetric”, let
\zeta\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Choosing
h_{\lambda}\in\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
such that
\tilde{\tau}_{D}h_{\lambda}=\zeta
, we apply the generalized Green’s identity for elements of
D(\hat{H}^{*})
(see equation (
3.32
)) to find

\begin{split}\text{Im}\langle\tilde{D}(\lambda)\zeta,\zeta\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}&=\text{Im}\langle\tilde{\tau}_{N}h_{\lambda},\tilde{\tau}_{D}h_{\lambda}\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\\

(4.23)

as desired.

When
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
both decompositions (
3.19
), (
3.21
) hold, and
\tilde{D}(\lambda)
is bijective. Hence
\tilde{D}^{-1}(\lambda):H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is bounded and extends

\tilde{D}^{-1}(\lambda):H^{s-1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s}(\partial\Omega)

(4.24)

are bounded for
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
.
∎

Corollary 4.1
.

For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
, the operator
\Theta_{N}(\lambda)=\iota_{+}\tilde{D}(\lambda)\iota_{-}^{-1}:H^{1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
is “symmetric” in the sense that

for all

. For
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
this operator is also bijective.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\rho(\hat{H}_{N})\cap\mathbb{R}
. Then the operator
(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\lambda)):=(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{N}(\lambda)+i\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}):H^{1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
is a bijective linear operator with bounded inverse.

Proof.

Recall that
\tilde{\Theta}_{N}(\lambda):H^{1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
is a bijective and bounded linear map, and is thus Fredholm with index
0
. Since
\beta
is compact and
\iota_{-}^{-1}:H^{1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
\iota_{+}:H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
are isometries, it follows that
\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}:H^{1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
is compact. By the compact embedding of Sobolev spaces, multiplication by
i
is also a compact operator from
H^{1}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
. So stability of Fredholm operators under compact perturbations
[
22
]
informs us that
i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\lambda)=i-\tilde{\Theta}_{N}(\lambda)+i\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}
is also Fredholm of index
0
, and it once again suffices to prove that the map is injective. Let
\xi\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with
\chi=\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. Since
\tilde{\Theta}_{N}(\lambda)
is symmetric

\text{Im}\langle(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi,\xi\rangle_{H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1}(\partial\Omega)}=||\xi||^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\text{Re}\langle\beta\chi,\chi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(4.25)

Hence

||\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\leq|\text{Im}\langle(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi,\xi\rangle_{H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1}(\partial\Omega)}|

(4.26)

which implies
\xi=0
if
(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\lambda))\xi=0
. It follows that
(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\lambda))
is an injective Fredholm operator of index
0
and is thus invertible.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Set
\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}=\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(\eta)
and
\Phi=(i+\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\big|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
. Since
(i+\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})
maps its domain into
H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)
, it is not immediately clear that
\Phi
maps into
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Before we continue, we will prove that
||(i+\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\chi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq||\chi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
for all
\chi\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. Setting
\xi=(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\chi\in H^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, it is equivalent to prove
||(i+\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq||(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
. This follows immediately from

\begin{split}||(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}-||(i+\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})\xi||_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}&=4\text{Im}\langle\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}\xi,\xi\rangle_{H^{-1}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1}(\partial\Omega)}\\
&=4\text{Re}\langle\beta\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi,\iota_{-}^{-1}\xi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}\geq 0.\end{split}

(4.27)

\Phi
is therefore contractive on
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
, and the associated extension
-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
is maximally dissipative with domain

D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})=\left\{\psi\in D(\hat{H}^{*}):-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta}(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{+}\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi_{D}\right\}.

(4.28)

For
\psi\in D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})
our boundary condition implies

\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi=i(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi+(i-\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})^{-1}\iota_{+}\tau_{N}\psi_{D}

(4.29)

so
\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\in D(\tilde{\Theta}_{\beta})=H^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, and in particular
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
. To show that
\psi
must reside in
H^{1}(\Omega)
we rely on the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\rho(\hat{H}_{D})\cap\mathbb{R}
and
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
. For each
\xi\in H^{s}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique
\psi_{\lambda}\in H^{s+1/2}(\Omega)
to the boundary value problem

\begin{cases}(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)\psi=0\\
\psi\big|_{\partial\Omega}=\xi\end{cases}

(4.30)

The solution mappings
\xi\to\gamma(\lambda)\xi:=\psi_{\lambda}
are continuous from
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s+1/2}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Existence and uniqueness of a solution to (
4.30
) is easily derived from equation (
3.20
) for the case
s=\frac{3}{2}
and (
3.19
) for
s=-\frac{1}{2}
. We will first show that
\gamma(\lambda)
is equal to the adjoint of
-\tau_{N}(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, making it a bounded operator from
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
. To see this, let
\xi\in H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and
\phi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then
\gamma(\lambda)\xi\in\ker(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)
and we have

\begin{split}\langle\gamma(\lambda)\xi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}&=\langle\gamma(\lambda)\xi,(\hat{H}^{*}-\lambda)(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=\langle\gamma(\lambda)\xi,\hat{H}^{*}(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle\lambda\gamma(\lambda)\xi,(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=\langle\gamma(\lambda)\xi,\hat{H}^{*}(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\langle\hat{H}^{*}\gamma(\lambda)\xi,(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
&=-\langle\xi,\tau_{N}(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi\rangle_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\times H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)},\end{split}

(4.31)

where in the last step we applied the abstract Green’s formula (
3.25
) along with
\tilde{\tau}_{D}\gamma(\lambda)\xi=\xi
and
\tau_{D}(\hat{H}_{D}-\lambda)^{-1}\phi=0
. We have now that
\gamma(\lambda)
is a bounded operator from
H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
that also maps
H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{2}(\Omega)
. The closed graph theorem then implies that the restriction
\gamma(\lambda):H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{2}(\Omega)
is also a bounded operator, and interpolation (see e.g.
[
10
, Theorems 5.1 and 7.7]
) returns that the restrictions
\gamma(\lambda):H^{s}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{s+1/2}(\Omega)
are bounded for
s\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
.
∎

Applying
(i-\Theta_{\beta})
to both sides of the boundary condition in (
4.28
) returns

\begin{split}\iota_{+}\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi_{D}&=-(\tilde{\Theta}_{N}-i\iota_{+}\beta\iota_{-}^{-1})\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\\
&=(i\iota_{+}\beta\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi-\tilde{\Theta}_{N}\iota_{-}\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi_{\eta})\\
&=\iota_{+}(i\beta\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi-\tilde{D}(\eta)\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi_{\eta})\\
&=\iota_{+}(i\beta\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi-\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi+\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi_{D})\end{split}

(4.32)

so
D(\hat{H}_{\Phi})=\left\{\psi\in H^{1}(\Omega)\cap D(\hat{H}^{*}):\tilde{\tau}_{N}\psi=i\beta\tilde{\tau}_{D}\psi\right\}
as desired.

When the real part of
\beta
is
strictly
positive we repeat the last steps in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.
\Phi
is
strictly
contractive on
L^{2}(\Omega)
by equation (
4.27
), and
-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
has compact resolvent because for
\lambda\in\rho(-i\hat{H}_{\Phi})
the resolvent operator
(-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}-\lambda)^{-1}
maps
L^{2}(\Omega)
to
H^{1}(\Omega)
, which by Rellich-Kondrachov
[
22
]
embeds compactly into
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The generator
-i\hat{H}_{\Phi}
meets the requirements of Theorem
1
, so
||\exp(-it\hat{H}_{\Phi})\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0
holds for all
\psi_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
if the operator
(-\Delta+V):H^{2}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
admits no real eigenvalues. This follows immediately from Lemma
4.3
, and we conclude our proof of Theorem
3
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Since the embeddings
H^{-1/2+\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)\xhookrightarrow{}H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\xhookrightarrow{}H^{1/2-\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)
are compact
[
22
]
for any
\epsilon>0
, a sufficient condition for
\beta
to be compact is taking
\beta:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2+\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)
or
\beta:H^{1/2-\epsilon}(\partial\Omega)\to H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)
bounded for some

. If

is multiplication by some function, it is sufficient to take
\beta\in H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
for any
s>\frac{n-2}{2}
[
30

, or

.

5
Detection Time Distributions

We now wrap this paper up with a short discussion on detection time distributions. Following Werner
[
14
]
, we consider a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup
W_{t}
with densely defined generator
B
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and point out that the mapping

\psi\to-\frac{d}{dt}||W_{t}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\bigg|_{t=0}

(5.1)

defines a positive semidefinite quadratic form on
D(B)
. This quadratic form induces a non-negative Hermitian form, and if
D(B)
is Hausdorff with respect to the topology induced by this form then one can construct a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
by taking its Hausdorff-completion. Let
j:D(B)\to\mathcal{K}
denote the canonical embedding, so the inner product on
\mathcal{K}
is defined on the dense set
j(D(B))\subset\mathcal{K}
by

\langle j\psi,j\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}:=\langle-B\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,-B\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(5.2)

Any Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
with a map
j:D(B)\to\mathcal{K}
satisfying equation (
5.2
) is called an
exit space
. For each
\psi\in D(B)
we consider the function
J\psi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathcal{K}
defined by
(J\psi)(t):=j(W_{t}\psi)
. Then

\int_{0}^{\infty}||J\psi||_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}(t)dt=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}||W_{t}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}dt=||\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\lim_{t\to\infty}||W_{t}\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq||\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(5.3)

where these limits exist by contractivity of
W_{t}
. This bound shows that
J
extends from
D(B)
to a contraction
J:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{K})\cong L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes\mathcal{K}
.

For quantum particles undergoing irreversible hard detection, the quantity
||J\psi||_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}
measures the rate at which probability flows from the space of states where the detector is primed
\mathcal{H}_{p}
to the space of states where the detector has fired
\mathcal{H}_{F}
. It therefore acts as a probability density for the quantum particle’s time of detection along
\partial\Omega
. By definition, this probability density is independent of the choice of exit space, and one can prove that exit spaces always exist whenever the generator
B
is an extension of some densely-defined skew-symmetric operator.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
A_{0}
be a densely defined skew-symmetric operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
(\mathcal{H}_{\pm},G_{\pm})
be a boundary quadruple for
-A_{0}^{*}
. Then for each maximally dissipative extension
B
of
A_{0}
, there is a mapping
j_{B}:D(B)\to H_{-}
such that
(H_{-},j_{B})
forms an exit space for
B
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, there exists a linear contraction
\Phi:\mathcal{H}_{-}\to\mathcal{H}_{+}
such that
D(B)=\{\psi\in D(-A_{0}^{*}):G_{+}\psi=\Phi G_{-}\psi\}
. Let
\Phi^{*}:H_{+}\to H_{-}
denote the Hilbert space adjoint of
\Phi
. Then for all
\psi,\phi\in D(B)

\begin{split}\langle-B\psi,\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}+\langle\psi,-B\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}&=\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{H_{-}}-\langle G_{+}\psi,G_{+}\phi\rangle_{H_{+}}\\
&=\langle G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{H_{-}}-\langle\Phi G_{-}\psi,\Phi G_{-}\phi\rangle_{H_{+}}\\
&=\langle(1-\Phi^{*}\Phi)G_{-}\psi,G_{-}\phi\rangle_{H_{-}}\\
&=\langle\sqrt{1-\Phi^{*}\Phi}G_{-}\psi,\sqrt{1-\Phi^{*}\Phi}G_{-}\phi\rangle_{H_{-}}\end{split}

(5.4)

where the square root of the bounded positive operator
1-\Phi^{*}\Phi
on
\mathcal{H}_{-}
is defined using the spectral theorem (or by power series). It follows that
\sqrt{1-\Phi^{*}\Phi}G_{-}:D(B)\to H_{-}
is an exit space map for
B
.
∎

Introducing an exit space for
W_{t}
allows us to write down a Born rule for the probability of detecting the particle over any time interval.

Proposal

(Werner Detection Time Distribution)
.

A quantum particle prepared at time
0
with initial wave function
\psi_{0}
of unit norm admits a probability distribution of detection time given by

\text{Prob}_{\psi_{0}}(t_{1}\leq t\leq t_{2})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}||J\psi_{0}||_{\mathcal{K}}^{2}(t)~dt=||W_{t_{1}}\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-||W_{t_{2}}\psi_{0}||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

(5.5)

for any
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}
, where
W_{t}
is the
C_{0}
contraction semigroup mapping
\psi_{0}
to
\psi_{t}
and
\mathcal{K}
is any exit space for
W_{t}
.

One can also show that the space
J(\mathcal{H})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\otimes\mathcal{K}
admits a natural time operator
T:=t\otimes\mathds{1}
which satisfies an uncertainty relation with the evolution generator
B
. This was originally proven in
[
24
]
, although the proof we present more closely follows
[
32
]
.

Theorem
.

[
24
, Section A.3]

[
32
, Theorem 2]

(Energy - Detection Time Uncertainty Principle) Let
W_{t}=\exp(tB)
be a
C_{0}
contraction semigroup on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
(\mathcal{K},j)
be an exit space for
W_{t}
. For
\psi\in D(B)\cap\ker(j)
with unit norm, set
p=||\psi||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\lim_{t\to\infty}||W_{t}\psi||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
, i.e. the probability that the particle prepared in state
\psi
is ever detected. Then the energy variance
\sigma^{2}_{iB,\psi}

\sigma^{2}_{iB,\psi}:=||iB\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}-\langle\psi,iB\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

(5.6)

along with the conditional time variance
\sigma^{2}_{T,\psi}

\sigma^{2}_{T,\psi}:=||t\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{K})}^{2}-\langle\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}},t\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{K})}^{2}

(5.7)

satisfy the inequality

\sigma_{iB,\psi}\sigma_{T,\psi}\geq\frac{\sqrt{p}}{2}.

(5.8)

Proof.

Consider the shift operator
\tilde{S}_{t}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})
via
\tilde{S}_{t}\Psi[s]=\Psi[t+s]
.
\tilde{S}_{t}
is a
C_{0}
unitary group with self-adjoint infinitesimal generator
D(\tilde{H})=H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})
,
\tilde{H}=i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
. Multiplication by
t
also defines a self-adjoint operator
\tilde{T}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})
, and it is a classical result of Kennard
[
1
]
that
\tilde{T}
and
\tilde{H}
satisfy the uncertainty principle

\sigma_{\tilde{T},\phi}\sigma_{\tilde{H},\phi}\geq\frac{1}{2}

(5.9)

for any
\phi
with
||\phi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})}^{2}=1
.

If we regard
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{K})
as a subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})
(extending functions by
0
for
t<0
), then for
\psi\in D(B)
and
\phi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})

\begin{split}\langle iJB\psi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})}-\langle J\psi,\tilde{H}\phi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})}=&\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle ij(W_{t}B\psi),\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}-\langle j(W_{t}\psi),\tilde{H}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}dt\\
=&\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}j(W_{t}\psi),\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}-\langle j(W_{t}\psi),i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}dt\\
=i&\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}j(W_{t}\psi),\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}+\langle j(W_{t}\psi),\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}dt\\
=i&\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\langle j(W_{t}\psi),\phi\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}\right)dt=-i\langle j(\psi),\phi(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{K}}.\end{split}

(5.10)

Hence for
\psi\in D(B)\cap\ker(j)
we have
J\psi\in D(\tilde{H}^{*})=D(\tilde{H})
with
\tilde{H}J\psi=iJB\psi
.

Recall
||J\psi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{K})}=\sqrt{p}
, so
\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}
is a unit vector. Applying the Kennard relation with
\phi=\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}
returns

\begin{split}\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{T},\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}}}&\leq\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{H},\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}}\\
&=\frac{1}{p}||\tilde{H}J\psi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})}^{2}-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\langle J\psi,\tilde{H}J\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})}^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{p}||\tilde{H}J\psi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{K})}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{p}||iJB\psi||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{K})}^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{p}||iB\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.\end{split}

(5.11)

The variance of a symmetric operator is invariant under addition of real constants, and since
\psi\in\ker(j)
we have
\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{H},\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}}=\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{H}+E_{0}\mathds{1},\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}}
for any
E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Replacing
\tilde{H}
with
\tilde{H}+E_{0}\mathds{1}
in equation (
5.11
) returns

\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{T},\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}}}\leq\frac{1}{p}||(iB+E_{0}\mathds{1})\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(5.12)

We recall an equivalent definition of variance for symmetric operators

\sigma^{2}_{iB,\psi}=\inf_{E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}||(iB+E_{0}\mathds{1})\psi||_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(5.13)

Taking an infimum of equation (
5.12
) over
E_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
returns

\frac{1}{4\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{T},\frac{J\psi}{\sqrt{p}}}}\leq\frac{1}{p}\sigma^{2}_{iB,\psi}.

(5.14)

which is a slight re-arrangement of the desired inequality (
5.8
).
∎

6
Summary and Outlook

We have shown that wave functions of non-relativistic quantum particles undergoing irreversible hard autonomous detection along the boundary of some bounded
C^{2}
region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
must evolve according to the Schrödinger equation while satisfying a time-independent absorbing boundary condition along
\partial\Omega
. Every such detector model admits a natural Born rule for the distribution of times when the particle is detected along
\partial\Omega
, and for a wide class of models, we have shown that the probability of the particle never being detected is
0
.

There are many directions in which we can extend these results. The modern theory of boundary triples for Schrödinger operators
[
25
,
26
]
can be used to extend the theorems in this paper to the case of unbounded Lipschitz domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A boundary triple construction for the Dirac Hamiltonian is essentially complete
[
29
,
35
]
and will provide us with an extension of these theorems to spin
\frac{1}{2}
relativistic quantum particles undergoing irreversible hard autonomous detection. We also plan to generalize this result to cases where the mechanism of detection is not constant over time. In such cases the dynamics of the wave function are not autonomous, so one would have to generalize the result of Wegner
[
27
]
and Arendt et al.
[
33
]
to the setting where
W_{t}
does not form a semi-group under composition.

More pressing is an experiment that can determine the validity of the absorbing boundary model. One such experiment has already been proposed in
[
34
]
using an unconventional double slit configuration. However, for this model to be falsifiable, we must demonstrate that the detection time distribution continuously depends on the boundary condition. This will be investigated in an upcoming paper
[
36
]
.
