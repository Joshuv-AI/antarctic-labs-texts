Mechanistic insights into how water-network disorder determines the vitrification concentration in cryopreservation solutions
Authors: Euihyun Lee, Nora Gaby-Biegel, Rebecca D Sandlin, Carlos R Baiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCryopreservation enables long-term storage of biological materials using cryoprotective agents (CPAs) to suppress ice crystal formation and prevent cell damage. However, despite considerable efforts, a molecular-level understanding of how CPAs prevent ice-crystal formation has not yet been established, particularly one that generalizes across different CPAs. In this study, we correlate molecular factors derived from low-temperature molecular dynamics simulations with measured Cv (minimum concentration required for vitrification) across several CPAs. Specifically, tetrahedral order parameter analyses show that CPAs disrupt the natural tetrahedral structure of water to different degrees, and this disruption correlates strongly with Cv. Additionally, we show that clustering predicts Cv, relating CPA-induced changes in the H-bond network to vitrification ability. We establish a molecular connection between CPA-driven disruption of the water H-bond network and vitrification capability. We use this to predict the CPA efficiency of new compounds. The approach provides a route toward the first-principles design of effective CPA compositions.

INTRODUCTION

Low-temperature cryopreservation stops metabolic and biochemical processes, thereby preserving cell viability over an extended period of time. Cryopreservation requires the use of cryoprotective agents (CPAs), which are mixtures of polar organic compounds that inhibit ice nucleation and growth in order to promote vitrification (amorphous, glass-like state) and prevent mechanical damage caused by ice crystal formation.1–9 However, while CPAs prevent ice-crystal formation, CPAs themselves exhibit a concentration-dependent toxicity.10,11 Thus, balancing the high concentration required to vitrify water while controlling toxicity has been the most important consideration in CPA formulations.

CPAs encompass a wide range of compounds from small polar molecules to macromolecules. However, the most widely used are dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethylene glycol (EG), and glycerol (hereby referred to as “standard CPAs” to distinguish them from other agents).12 Although their basic properties and applications are well established, the precise mechanisms by which they inhibit ice crystal formation and preserve biological materials remain underexplored.13 Elucidating these mechanisms is the first step toward the effective formulation of CPA mixtures for low-temperature storage.

Each CPA has a specific vitrification concentration (Cv) that is the minimum concentration at which the mixture vitrifies at a specified cooling rate. Often, Cv is measured by rapidly freezing a macroscopic sample and observing the scattering that arises from ice crystals (see Figures 1A and 1B). A transparent sample indicates that the solution is vitrified. Measuring CV is the first important step in determining the potential efficacy of a compound, since a higher Cv means that more of the compound is required to vitrify the solution. Because organic compounds’ CPAs are toxic to cells, a high concentration of CPAs can damage the cells. Thus, minimizing CPA use is required for maintaining cell viability.11,14,15

The properties of cryoprotective agents have been characterized using a range of experimental and computational methods, including FTIR and Raman spectroscopy, differential scanning calorimetry, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and quantum-mechanical calculations; however, to our knowledge, there has not been a systematic study that directly correlates molecular properties with measured Cv (a bulk property).13,16–24 In this study, we measured Cv and performed MD simulations for a set of CPAs (Figure 1C) to find a molecular descriptor of Cv. The set included the following. (1) Three commonly used CPAs (DMSO, ethylene glycol, and glycerol) find a basic mechanism for how CPAs vitrify the solution. (2) Two non-vitrifying CPAs (NV-CPAs), formamide (FA), and urea, as no Cv exists for these compounds.25 These compounds are used in cryopreservation solutions, which are cocktails of compounds. Hence, these two compounds can be considered CPAs but do not vitrify on their own, only in combination with other compounds. These were included to compare their ability to disrupt the water H-bonding structure with respect to vitrifying CPAs. (3) Four additional previously unreported “candidate” CPA compounds (hexane-n-ols) were included to verify our theory and to test its validity in new CPA systems. These compounds range in the number and position of the hydroxyl groups. In general, given the range in polarity and the number and position of H-bond donors/acceptor groups (O or OH), this set of compounds serves as a basis to explore the structural and dynamic effects of CPAs on water’s H-bond network and how molecular environments correlate with bulk Cv. Polyols such as glycerol, 1,2-propanediol, and 2,3-butanediol are commonly used as CPAs, even though they have a different number of hydroxyl groups and backbone length.

Low-temperature MD simulations are a powerful way to investigate the water H-bond networks in mixed-solvent systems.13,16 The correlations between computed molecular-level properties and experimental observables can offer a mechanistic understanding of the vitrification. Here, we describe water’s H-bonding structure using the local tetrahedral order parameter as a descriptor and the more global analysis of water cluster size distributions based on the H-bond connectivity. From a molecular perspective, since ice requires a long-range tetrahedral arrangement of molecules, first, the hypothesis that CPAs’ lower tetrahedrality, introducing disorder, is a reasonable starting point to understand how these compounds prevent ice crystal formation. Second, the question of what is the minimum size of the water cluster that can form ice remains under debate,26–28 but the general hypothesis is that CPAs create clusters of water molecules too small to nucleate ice; thus, we perform a cluster size analysis based on the H-bond connectivity to obtain some insight into how these molecular-level properties correlate with measured Cvs.

The experimental Cv of each CPA under rapid cooling rates (~105 K/min) was measured using transparent, glass capillaries plunged into liquid nitrogen.29,30 After reaching equilibrium, visual inspection confirms crystallization or vitrification (Figure 1A). For each compound, the concentration was increased in 0.25 M increments until vitrification was confirmed. Effect of CPAs on the water H-bond network

To compare the effects of CPAs on water H-bonding structure, we performed MD simulations of the same solutions and analyzed the configurations using two metrics. First, the tetrahedral order parameter (q) is a useful metric to describe the local structure of particles in the liquid phase. In general, for a given water molecule, the parameter can be thought of as a projection of the four nearest polar neighbors onto a perfect tetrahedron (Equation 1; Figure 2A).31–33 In Equation 1, ψjk represents the angle between the central water molecule’s oxygen atom and the four nearest possible oxygen atoms (Figure 2A).

In ice, where the structure between the water molecules is tetrahedral, the value of q approaches 1. In contrast, for ideal-gas-like random structures, the average value 〈q〉 is 0. In liquid water, the tetrahedral-like geometry of water (H-atoms and lone pairs) is reflected in the H-bond structures. However, the greater degrees of freedom compared to a solid ice structure result in broad distributions of the order parameter q. In solutions, the CPA disrupts the H-bond networks. Standard CPAs (e.g., EG, glycerol, and DMSO) can be classified into two different types depending on the molecular structure. (1) EG and glycerol: these CPAs include H-bond donor/acceptor through their hydroxyl groups. These compounds can be incorporated within the H-bonding network of water (Figure 2B). (2) DMSO is amphiphilic: the O atom of DMSO supports H-bonding with water molecules, but its hydrophobic methyl groups disrupt the H-bond networks, resulting in a distorted intermolecular tetrahedral structure (Figures 2C and S1).

The tetrahedral order parameter distributions for CPAs at three concentrations are shown in Figure 2. In this analysis, we only consider water; including all oxygen atoms in the system yields comparable results (see Figure S2). Firstly, the q distribution of the liquid water shows a bimodal character consistent with the literature interpretation of high-density and low-density water (Figure 2D).32 In cases of CPA solutions at 198 K, while the tetrahedral parameter distributions for CPA systems exhibit smaller q values and broader distributions than those for supercooled water at 198 K, the CPA systems have a narrower distribution at high q than that for water at room temperature, indicating a change in the ice-like structure among the water molecules. At 10 mol %, most CPA solutions maintain an ice-like structure, while at 30 mol %, they exhibit a more liquid-like structure, despite the low temperatures, with a distinct second peak appearing around q = 0.4. This clearly shows that increasing CPA concentration significantly disrupts the H-bond structure between water molecules.

FA and urea, referred to as NV-CPAs, do not vitrify. In Figure 2, NV-CPAs show increased populations at high q compared to CPAs. The average tetrahedral order parameters decrease with increasing CPA/NV-CPA concentration (Figure S2A), indicating that molecules disrupt the water H-bond network. In Figure 2E, NV-CPAs have higher average order parameters than CPAs. This indicates that NV-CPAs disrupt the water H-bonding structure to a lesser extent than CPAs. Indeed, NV-CPAs replace water molecules, but behave like water, stabilizing and preserving the water H-bonding structure.

It is worth noting that the H-bond structure in DMSO (Cv = 5.0 M) appears more disrupted when all O and N atoms are used (Figure S2B) to calculate the order parameter,13 compared to when only water molecules are used (Figure 2). This discrepancy can be explained by microheterogeneous phase separation in DMSO solutions due to DMSO’s molecular structure (Figures 2B and 2C),34,35 leading to water-rich regions that are relatively unaffected by DMSO. This sensitivity to amphiphilic CPAs that can preserve locally ice-like water while still disrupting global connectivity motivates the use of complementary local, tetrahedral parameters, and network-level metrics (cluster size, see next paragraph). Other CPAs studied here are not known to phase separate.

The water cluster size analysis reports on water H-bond network connectivity, disorder, clustering, and heterogeneity.36–40 Previous studies,13,34,36 showed that the water cluster is an important factor underlying DMSO’s vitrification behavior, which disorders the water H-bond structure (supporting smaller water clusters). As an extension, this approach was used to analyze the water H-bond structure across all systems. The H-bond connectivity matrix is a sparse matrix where each row and column represents an H-bond donor or acceptor. Non-zero off-diagonal elements represent an H-bond between two groups. Conceptually, the connectivity matrix represents an instantaneous snapshot of the interactions present. In pure crystalline ice Ih, each water molecule is H-bonded to four neighbors. In this case, a stable H-bond network leads to long-range structure throughout the system, and the entire system can be considered part of a single cluster. Here, to belong to the same cluster, the water molecule should be connected to other water molecules in the cluster (Figure 3A). Thus, in ice, the cluster size is larger than that of liquid water, which has a weaker and less stable H-bond network. Similarly, CPAs further disrupt the H-bond network as there is an imbalance between donors and acceptors, and hydrophobic groups restrict the configurational space of the surrounding waters.41

In this study, the water cluster sizes were calculated using the participation ratio (PR)39 model (Equation 2). The PR represents the average size of the water cluster in systems and can be obtained from the sum of the eigenvector elements (U) corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the H-bond connectivity matrix.

In Figure 3B, the water cluster sizes are shown along with the average molar concentrations in MD, which were calculated from the simulation box size and the number of CPA molecules. In general, as concentration increases, the water cluster size decreases, given that both CPAs and NV-CPAs disrupt the water-water H-bond network by replacing water as a partner and introducing an imbalance between donors and acceptors. For these reasons, the water cluster size decreases, and the extent of the decrease is dependent on the type of CPA. NV-CPAs have larger water cluster sizes than CPAs, indicating that NV-CPAs preserve the water H-bond network. This follows a consistent trend compared to the tetrahedral order parameter, as described above.

To further explore this trend, we included an exponential fit as an interpolation curve to represent the observed water cluster size with concentration (Figure 3B). The curve shows that the water cluster size in CPA systems is well correlated with the ranking of these CPAs based on Cv. This analysis shows that NV-CPAs preserve the H-bonding structure and support large cluster sizes. In contrast, standard CPAs disrupt the extended H-bond network of liquid water, creating smaller H-bond clusters. This prevents ice-crystal formation and leads to vitrification. More importantly, since water cluster size reports on the ability of CPAs to disrupt the H-bond network, we can use this metric as a quantitative measure of the CPA’s effects, and as a determinant of CPA efficacy (i.e., concentration required to vitrify). In other words, by correlating molecular properties such as cluster size with Cv, we predict experimental Cv of candidate CPAs based on simulations alone.

The above analysis provides a measure of average cluster size, but the distribution of cluster sizes can provide additional information. It is therefore useful to understand the extent to which CPAs induce heterogeneity and disorder within the system. For example, increased disorder may be represented as a broader distribution of cluster sizes. We turn to a community detection algorithm using the Louvain method.42–46 This algorithm separates “groups” (or water clusters) within the H-bond network based on the density of connections (Figure S4). NV-CPA solutions contain large water clusters, which are slightly smaller than those of supercooled water, indicating that NV-CPA does not affect the H-bond network. On the other hand, CPAs have smaller water clusters than supercooled water and NV-CPA systems. This analysis reveals that the water clustering is also correlated with the measured Cv of CPAs. Interestingly, the population of small water clusters in the DMSO solution is smaller than that in other solutions (Figure S4B), which is a result of the phase separation induced by DMSO as discussed above.

Cv prediction for CPA candidates

So far, analyses of the tetrahedral order parameter and water cluster size have demonstrated a strong correlation between the water H-bond network and the measured Cv for commonly used CPA, implying that a higher concentration is necessary to disrupt the water H-bond network. In other words, the extent to which CPA perturbs the water H-bond network can be regarded as an intrinsic property of the CPA.

To validate the mechanism identified for standard CPAs, the same H-bond analyses can be used to predict Cv and assess the efficacy of CPA “candidates” that have not been previously reported. These are small molecules that preserve a similar hydrocarbon backbone as glycerol or EG but range in the number and position of the hydroxyl groups (Figure 1).

Similar to standard CPAs and NV-CPAs, both the order parameter and the water cluster size are correlated with the trend of measured Cv (Table 1; Figures 4 and S5) in the candidate CPA solutions, suggesting a generalization of our theory to these new compounds. Detailed tetrahedral order parameter distributions of CPAs can be found in Figure S5. The water cluster size analysis also shows a similar trend; NV-CPAs exhibit substantially larger water clusters than CPAs, indicating that they preserve the water H-bond network more effectively (Figure 4B). Among CPAs, the relative cluster sizes follow the same order as the measured Cv values. In other words, CPAs with lower Cv more effectively disrupt the water H-bond network, resulting in smaller water cluster sizes than those with higher Cv CPAs.

Interestingly, the water cluster size distributions shown in Figure S6A are broader for the candidate CPA series than for structurally simple CPAs, such as EG, DMSO, and glycerol. This may be an effect of the molecular structure (i.e., chain length and the number of OH groups). For instance, in the case of 1,2,6-HX, its effect on the local water H-bonding structure is increased as a result of the three hydroxyl groups, inducing a more disordered H-bond structure and smaller water cluster fragments than 2,5-HX, which has a lower Cv (2.25 M) than 1,2,6-HX (Cv: 2.75 M).

Quantitative correlations between Cv measurements and simulations

For all CPAs in the mixture, we observed that measured Cv is determined by H-bond structure, as computed by the q parameter and network analysis. Since ice crystal formation involves the rearrangement of the water H-bond network, we can further posit that Cv is reached when the H-bond network is sufficiently disrupted. Assuming that clustering is an important factor in preventing ice crystal formation, we can ask the question: “at which concentration does the cluster reach a certain size?” For this, we use predicted concentrations at the water cluster sizes of 50 and 100. These are arbitrary values for illustration here (Figure S7). Though the cluster size may depend on the water model used, here we used TIP4P-Ew, which has shown good performance for supercooled water systems47,48; cluster sizes between 30 and 120 water molecules produce very similar coefficients of determination with respect to experiment. To predict the needed concentration to reach a given cluster size, we use the previously computed concentration-cluster interpolation curves across all compounds (Figures 3B and 4B). Interestingly, the CPA ranking correlates well with the ordering of experimentally measured Cv values. For instance, by arbitrarily setting the water cluster size to 50 and 100 water molecules, we extracted the corresponding concentrations from MD simulations and compared these with the experimentally measured Cv (Figure 5B). The coefficient of determination (r2) was 0.84 at a cluster size of 50 and 0.79 at a cluster size of 100, indicating a strong correlation. The predicted concentrations needed to reach these cluster sizes agree with measured Cv, pointing to two important conclusions: (1) clustering is indeed one of the molecular mechanisms that leads to vitrification, and (2) most importantly, from a practical perspective, this method can be used to predict CPA performance based on simulations alone.

Furthermore, the average tetrahedral order parameter also describes the experimentally measured Cv (Figure 5A). This suggests that Cv can also be predicted from the tetrahedral order parameter, emphasizing the significant influence of the H-bond structure on the vitrification properties of water. Indeed, the experimental vitrification concentration typically falls within the 10–20 mol % range, indicating that the MD simulation effectively captures this behavior. On the other hand, for the 30 mol % solutions, where the H-bond structure is already significantly disrupted, the correlation between the order parameter and Cv may be less reliable.

In a complementary analysis, we also construct an “ideal” NV-CPA, which is water itself. This is done by randomly selecting water molecules in the MD analysis and assigning them as “CPA” within the algorithm. This excludes from being counted in the H-bond analysis those waters that generate “holes” but otherwise preserve the geometries, since those waters are part of the simulation. We observe that, in this case, both the tetrahedral order parameter and cluster size follow the same trends as “NV-CPAs” but with even less disruption (Figure S8). Since obviously water is the ultimate NV-CPA, this analysis sets the upper limit for how little a compound can affect water. These findings emphasize the importance of the water H-bond network as a molecular descriptor of Cv.

DISCUSSION

In this work, we investigated the molecular-level mechanism by which various CPAs influence the water H-bonding structure using all-atom MD simulations. More importantly, we demonstrate that molecular properties correlate with measured vitrification concentrations (Cv) for nine compounds, including seven CPAs and two NV-CPAs, as well as an “ideal NV-CPA” (i.e., water).

Through analyses of the tetrahedral order parameter and water cluster size, we provided a comprehensive structural perspective on how CPAs disrupt the water H-bond structure. This disordered network is essential for vitrification, as ice-crystallization is prevented. Our simulations and experiments revealed that the tetrahedral order parameter decreases with increasing CPA concentration, reflecting a breakdown of the tetrahedral water structure. This structural change strongly correlates with experimental Cv values, especially at 10 and 20 mol % concentrations (r2 = 0.78 and 0.71, respectively), suggesting that the vitrification involves H-bond heterogeneity for water.

Likewise, water cluster size analyses provided insights into the relationship between the water H-bonding structure and vitrification. Cluster sizes decreased as CPA concentration increased, demonstrating the ability of CPAs to fragment the water H-bond network. Notably, an exponential fitting of cluster size data at multiple concentrations revealed a strong correlation (r2 ~ 0.8) with experimentally measured vitrification concentration, emphasizing that a critical disruption of water connectivity is required for successful vitrification.

Overall, our findings reveal the role of the water H-bond structure in determining the vitrification capability of CPAs and offer predictive molecular metrics, such as the tetrahedral order parameter and the water cluster size, which can serve to computationally determine CPA performance. These findings are consistent with recently reported studies that describe the molecular-level mechanism of the solute-induced vitrification.49–51 To our knowledge, for the first time, we established a first-principles molecular mechanism for vitrification: disruption of H-bond networks. More practically, these molecular descriptors serve as useful tools for prospective in silico screening of next-generation cryoprotectants. Furthermore, the molecular descriptors may provide structured metrics that can directly be applied in data-driven frameworks. Warren et al.52 reported that MD simulations were used to calculate hydration descriptors, which, when incorporated into machine-learning models, significantly improved the prediction of small-molecule ice recrystallization inhibitors. Similarly, we believe our approach, based on CPA structure and mechanism, is ideal for data-driven models that help high-throughput screening in CPA candidate discovery.53 We also believe that our approach can be further extended for studies such as multicomponent CPA mixture systems and macromolecular systems. In crowded environments, the H-bond network and dynamics of water near biomolecules are altered by crowding and confinement54–58; also, interaction/exclusion between biomolecules and CPAs may affect the water H-bond network structure and dynamics within the biomolecules. Future studies are needed to begin to address these complexities.

METHODS

Experiment (Cv)

Apparent vitrification was assessed by loading each CPA solution into 0.3 × 3.0 × 50 nm3 rectangular glass capillaries (VitroCom catalog# 3530–50) via capillary action. The capillaries were then sealed using a tube sealer (Carolina Capillary), where the air gap inside was kept to a minimum. Each capillary was then rapidly plunged into liquid nitrogen and allowed to equilibrate. The capillaries were handled using plastic forceps. Upon equilibration, the capillary was carefully moved into nitrogen vapors, barely above the surface of the liquid nitrogen. A bright lamp was then used to visually assess whether ice crystallization was apparent (Figure 1A). Initially, each CPA was tested at a concentration of 5 M in deionized water. If ice crystallized, the CPA was retested at a 0.5 M higher concentration. If the CPA solution appeared transparent, it was considered to be vitrified (Figure 1B) and retested at a 0.5 M lower concentration. This process was repeated until the apparent vitrification concentration was narrowed to within the 0.5 M range. This was followed by further testing to narrow the apparent vitrification concentration to a 0.25 M resolution.

MD simulations

Simulations were carried out using the GROMACS2023.4 simulation package59–61 and the CHARMM36 general force field62–65 with the TIP4P-Ew water model, which shows a good performance for supercooled water system.47,48 To compare the effect of different CPAs on the water H-bonding structure, three classified systems (pure water and CPA systems) were constructed as follows: (1) pure water at 198 and 298 K and (2) CPAs and NV-CPAs shown in Figure 1. Both CPA and NV-CPA solutions include concentrations of 10, 20, and 30 mol % (111, 250, and 429 solutes in 1,000 water molecules, respectively).

Initial configurations were built using the Packmol package.66 After the energy minimization of the initial configurations, a 2-ns NVT equilibration and a 5-ns NPT equilibration were performed at 298 K. Next, simulated annealing was carried out from 298 to 198 K over 10 ns, followed by 5 ns NVT equilibration and 50 ns NPT equilibration using the V-rescale thermostat67 and the C-rescale barostat68 at 198 K. Simulations were carried out with a time step of 1 fs. Following equilibration, 20 ns NPT production simulations were carried out using the same thermostat/barostat combination and the SHAKE algorithm69 at 198 K. The trajectories were saved every 10 ps for the water H-bonding structure analysis.
