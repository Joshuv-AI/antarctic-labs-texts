A variance reduced framework for (non)smooth nonconvex–nonconcave stochastic minimax problems with extended Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz property
Muhammad Khan, Yangyang Xu

Abstract
In this paper, we study stochastic constrained minimax optimization problems with nonconvex–nonconcave structure, a central problem in modern machine learning, for which reliable and efficient algorithms remain largely unexplored due to its inherent challenges. Prior approaches for nonconvex minimax optimization often require (strong) concavity on the maximization part, or certain restrictive geometric assumptions on the joint objective to have guaranteed convergence. In contrast, our method only assumes weak convexity in the primal variable and the extended Kurdyka–Łojasiewicz (KŁ) property, with exponent θ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \theta\in[0,1] , in the dual variable, significantly broadening the class of tractable problems. To this end, we propose a variance reduced algorithm that provably handles this general setting and achieves an ε \varepsilon -stationary solution with state-of-the-art sample complexity: in the smooth finite-sum setting, the sample complexity is 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ ε − max ⁡ { 4 ​ θ , 2 } ) \mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon^{-\max\{4\theta,2\}}\right) , where N N is the number of total samples, and in the online smooth setting, it is 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε − max ⁡ { 6 ​ θ , 3 } ) \mathcal{O}\Big(\varepsilon^{-\max\{6\theta,3\}}\Big) . For the structured nonsmooth problem, the sample complexity is 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ max ⁡ { ε − 3 , ε − 5 ​ θ , ε − 11 ​ θ − 3 2 ​ θ } ) \mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\,\max\Big\{\varepsilon^{-3},\varepsilon^{-5\theta},\varepsilon^{-\frac{11\theta-3}{2\theta}}\Big\}\right) and 𝒪 ⁡ ( max ⁡ { ε − 4 , ε − 15 ​ θ − 1 2 , ε − 31 ​ θ − 9 4 ​ θ } ) \mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\varepsilon^{-4},\varepsilon^{-\frac{15\theta-1}{2}},\varepsilon^{-\frac{31\theta-9}{4\theta}}\right\}\right) respectively for the two settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first unified framework that jointly accommodates weak convexity, the extended KŁ property, and variance-reduced stochastic updates, making it highly suitable for large-scale applications.

Practical challenges in minimax optimization have sparked extensive research across a wide spectrum of problem settings, from convex–concave to fully nonconvex–nonconcave regimes. Early works concentrated on convex–concave problems, leading to the development of optimal primal–dual methods
(
4
;
8
)
. Subsequent work addressed nonconvex–concave settings using gradient descent–ascent (GDA) based algorithms and near-optimal methods
(
17
;
16
)
, while weakly convex–weakly concave problems were explored in
(
19
)
. More recent advances have introduced adaptive and universal algorithms under Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PŁ) and smoothness assumptions
(
29
;
9
)
, as well as universal GDA-type algorithms that provide convergence guarantees across multiple problem structures
(
32
;
33
)
, and rates for nonsmooth nonconvex–nonconcave problems leveraging primal–dual balance and KŁ properties
(
15
)
.

A key limitation of much of the existing literature is the focus on deterministic settings or unconstrained smooth cases. While some methods have been extended to stochastic problems, they often require large batch sizes for each update
(
17
;
18
)
and thus high computational costs, limiting their practicality. This motivates the incorporation of variance reduction techniques, such as SVRG
(
12
)
and SPIDER
(
6
)
, which have demonstrated effectiveness in classical minimization tasks, into the more challenging minimax setting. Recent efforts in this direction aim to bridge the gap between theoretical efficiency and practical scalability.
For instance,

3
analyzed the SPIDER-based algorithm under the PŁ condition and demonstrated improved convergence rates, while
10
proposed a shuffling gradient descent-ascent method with variance reduction for nonconvex-strongly concave stochastic problems. More recently,
11
introduced single loop algorithms, incorporating variance reduction, tailored to the finite-sum regime and general nonconvex-concave stochastic minimax problems.

Despite these advances, a common limitation is the reliance on strong assumptions, such as unconstrained smoothness, and (strong) concavity, or the PŁ condition on the maximization part, which restricts their applicability to general nonconvex–nonconcave minimax problems. Addressing this limitation remains a major open challenge in the design of scalable and reliable minimax optimization algorithms.

1.2
Contributions

We summarize our key contributions as follows.

First, in contrast to prior methods, our approach addresses the fully nonconvex-nonconcave minimax problem under significantly
weaker
and more realistic assumptions in both the finite-sum and stochastic online regimes. Specifically, we assume the extended KŁ property in
y
in the sense that the exponent parameter
\theta
can be any value on
[0,1]
; see Assumption
2.2
. This property allows for significantly broader applications as compared to the PŁ condition assumed in
(
3
)
, which only applies to unconstrained smooth cases with
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
. Though
33
have also studied minimax problems with a classical KŁ property in
y
, their algorithm relies on deterministic gradients and thus will be inefficient on solving a finite-sum structured or stochastic online minimax problem. In addition, their analysis does not cover the case of
\theta=1
that is implied by the restricted (a.k.a. star-) concavity (see Remark
2.3
) and is strictly weaker than the concavity condition assumed in
(
11
)
.

Second, designed for both the stochastic
online
and
finite-sum
settings, our algorithm employs a framework integrated with SPIDER-type variance reduction, enabling
guaranteed convergence while significantly reducing computational as well as the sample complexity. In the smooth finite-sum setting, the sample complexity of our algorithm is

\mathcal{O}\big(\sqrt{N}\,\varepsilon^{-\max\{4\theta,2\}}\big)
to produce an
\varepsilon
-stationary solution in expectation. Compared to the deterministic method in
(
33
)
applied to the finite-sum case, our result achieves a factor
\sqrt{N}
improvement. In the online smooth setting, we obtain a sample complexity of

\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon^{-\max\{6\theta,3\}}\big)
, which is new.

Third, by using the Moreau-envelope smoothing technique, we further extend our method and analysis to a structured nonsmooth nonconvex-nonconcave minimax problem, for which
the sample complexity results of our method are

\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{N}\,\max\Big\{\varepsilon^{-3},\varepsilon^{-5\theta},\varepsilon^{-\frac{11\theta-3}{2\theta}}\Big\}\right)

and

\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\varepsilon^{-4},\varepsilon^{-\frac{15\theta-1}{2}},\varepsilon^{-\frac{31\theta-9}{4\theta}}\right\}\right)

respectively for the finite-sum and the online
settings. Both results are new.

To the best of our knowledge, our method is the first unified framework that jointly accommodates weak convexity, the extended KŁ property, and variance-reduced stochastic updates for nonconvex–nonconcave minimax optimization.

3.2.1
Smoothing by Moreau Envelope and Key Properties

The potential nonsmoothness of
h
implies that

F(x,y)
is typically nonsmooth in
x
, although it remains smooth in
y
. Hence, Algorithm
1
cannot be directly applied. To address the challenge caused by the nonsmoothness, we propose to apply the Moreau envelope smoothing technique to each component of

, yielding a smooth approximation. The resulting smoothed objective function is given by

\displaystyle F^{\lambda}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[f^{\lambda}(x,y;\boldsymbol{\xi})\right]

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathbb{P}}\left[\varphi\big(h^{\lambda}(c(x;\boldsymbol{\xi})),y;\boldsymbol{\xi}\big)\right].

(13)

Here,

is the component-wise Moreau envelope of

, defined as

h^{\lambda}(w):=\Big(h^{\lambda}_{1}(w),\dots,h^{\lambda}_{d_{h}}(w)\Big),\quad\forall w\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}},

where

h^{\lambda}_{j}(w):=\min_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}}\left\{h_{j}(q)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|w-q\|^{2}\right\},\;j=1,\dots,d_{h}.

Remark 3.3
.

Since
{\mathcal{X}}
and

are both compact, there must exist a constant

such that (

Moreover, denote

This smoothing step enables us to apply Algorithm
1
developed for smooth problems while carefully controlling the approximation error introduced by the Moreau envelope.

However, before applying Algorithm
1
and deriving iteration and sample complexity guarantees in the nonsmooth regime, we need to establish several key properties of the smoothed objective

that are required for the algorithm’s convergence analysis.

Details on showing these properties are given in the appendix. Here we simply state what properties

has. We begin by establishing the Lipschitz continuity of

Having established the necessary properties, we are consequently in a position to apply Algorithm

\varepsilon

{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{N}\varepsilon^{-5})

{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-8})

(

)
if

under a weaker condition. Moreover, in the online setting, when
\theta\in[0,\frac{3}{5}]

{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})
, which matches the
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-4})
complexity in
(
18
)
for the smooth nonconvex-strongly-concave case and strictly better than
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-6})

(

)
for the nonsmooth nonconvex-strongly-concave case. When

4
Concluding Remarks

We have presented a variance-reduced framework for solving fully nonconvex-nonconcave stochastic minimax optimization problems under weak convexity and the extended KŁ property — assumptions which are substantially weaker than those commonly adopted in literature. By integrating SPIDER-type variance reduction, our method achieves guaranteed convergence with improved iteration and sample complexity in both the
online
and
finite-sum
settings. By Moreau-envelope smoothing, we are able to further extend our framework by applying it to a structured nonsmooth problem and establish rigorous complexity results.

We observe that, in the nonsmooth case, the sample complexity becomes
{\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon^{-7})
in the extreme case of
\theta=1
, which is implied by a restricted concavity condition in the dual variable. Although this result is new, we conjecture that this complexity can be further reduced, which is worth exploring in future work.

when

Moreover, we have for any

Appendix C
Motivating Applications - Formulations

In this section, we present a detailed description of two classes of problems whose cost function can be formulated in accordance with (
8
).

i.

\phi
-Divergence DRO

As discussed earlier, the objective of distributionally robust modeling is to ensure that the learned models remain reliable not only under adversarial perturbations but also in the presence of distributional shifts. In contrast to traditional empirical risk minimization, which optimizes performance under the empirical data distribution, distributionally robust optimization seeks to minimize the worst-case loss over an uncertainty set of probability distributions.

14
study this DRO problem and consider a slight generalization of
\phi
-divergence–based DRO. Specifically, let
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a convex function satisfying
\phi(1)=0
, and define the
\phi
-divergence between distributions
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathbb{P}
as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\phi}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P}):=\int\phi\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\right)d\mathbb{P},

(120)

where
\mathbb{Q}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. Then, for a convex function
\psi
with
\psi(1)=0
, a constraint radius
\rho\geq 0
, penalty parameter
\lambda\geq 0
, and
\ell(\theta;\cdot)
denoting the loss function parameterized by
\theta
, the objective considered in
(
14
)

takes the form:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{\mathbb{Q}:D_{\phi}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\leq\rho}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathbb{Q}}[\ell(\theta;\boldsymbol{\xi})]-\lambda D_{\psi}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})\right\}.

(121)

We consider the case when
\mathbb{P}
is the empirical distribution induced by
N
i.i.d samples with support
\Xi=\left\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}\right\}
. Since
\mathbb{Q}
is absolutely continuous wrt
\mathbb{P}
, it follows that
\mathbb{Q}
assigns probability mass only to points in
\Xi
. By the Radon–Nikodym theorem, for each
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\Xi
, we then have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})=\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\implies\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})=\frac{\mathbb{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})}{\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})}.

(122)

Without loss of generality, denote
\mathbb{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})=q_{i}
with
q\in\Delta_{N}
, the
N
-probability simplex. Then,
utilizing (
120
) and (
122
) yields

D_{\phi}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi\left(\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})\right)\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi\left(\frac{\mathbb{Q}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})}{\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})}\right)\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi\left(\frac{q_{i}}{\frac{1}{N}}\right)\frac{1}{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi(Nq_{i}).

Similarly, we get

D_{\psi}(\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{P})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi(Nq_{i}).

Now, assuming each data sample
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
is a pair
(x_{i},y_{i})
of features and targets, denoting the predictor function parameterized by
\theta
as
f_{\theta}
and letting
e_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}}
denote the
i^{th}
standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the DRO problem in (
121
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{q\in\Delta_{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi\left(Nq_{i}\right)\leq N\rho}\;\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Nq_{i}\ell\left(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),y_{i}\right)-\lambda\psi(Nq_{i}).

(123)

Under the choices of

\displaystyle c\left(\theta;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)=\left(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),y_{i}\right),\;h(v_{1},v_{2})=\ell(v_{1},v_{2}),\;\text{and }\varphi\left(w_{1},w_{2};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)=N\left(e_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}}^{\top}w_{2}\right)w_{1}-\lambda\psi\left(N\left(e_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}}^{\top}w_{2}\right)\right),

it then follows that the cost function in (
123
) exhibits the structure in (
8
).

ii.

Group–DRO

In many real-world applications, data points naturally belong to disjoint groups or subpopulations, and models trained via standard empirical risk minimization often underperform on minority or underrepresented groups. Group distributionally robust optimization (Group–DRO) addresses this by explicitly optimizing the worst-case performance across groups, rather than the average loss. This approach is particularly useful in domains where performance differences across groups can have undesired consequences; in healthcare where it can help address subgroup performance disparities
(
23
)
, in recommendation systems to improve worst-case user experience
(
28
)
, and in Natural Language Processing (NLP) to train models that are robust to topic shifts
(
21
)
.

Formally, let the dataset be partitioned into
M
groups with distributions
\{\mathbb{P}_{g}\}_{g=1}^{M}
, and let
f_{\theta}
denote a predictor function parameterized by
\theta
. Following
(
27
)
, we have the Group–DRO problem objective as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\max_{q\in\Delta_{M}}\;\sum_{g=1}^{M}q_{g}\;\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathbb{P}_{g}}\left[\ell(f_{\theta}(x),y)\right],

where
\Delta_{M}
is the
M
-dimensional probability simplex, and
q
assigns weights to the groups. We note that:

\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{M}q_{g}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathbb{P}_{g}}\left[\ell\left(f_{\theta}(x),y\right)\right]=\sum_{g=1}^{M}q_{g}\;\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{G}_{g}\rvert}\sum_{i=1}^{\lvert\mathcal{G}_{g}\rvert}\ell\left(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),y_{i}\right)\overset{(i)}{=}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{Nq_{g_{i}}}{\lvert\mathcal{G}_{g_{i}}\rvert}\ell\left(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),y_{i}\right),

where
(i)
follows from the fact that the groups are disjoint and
collectively cover all data points, and
g_{i}
denotes the index of the
group to which the data point
(x_{i},y_{i})
belongs. Let
e_{g_{i}}
denote the
g_{i}^{\text{th}}
standard basis vector in

\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Then under the choices of

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\left\{\lvert\mathcal{G}_{j}\rvert\right\}_{j=1}^{M},\;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i}),\;c\left(\theta;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)=\left(f_{\theta}(x_{i}),y_{i}\right),\;h(v_{1},v_{2})=\ell(v_{1},v_{2}),\;\varphi\left(w_{1},w_{2};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\right)=\frac{N\left(e_{g_{i}}^{\top}w_{2}\right)w_{1}}{e_{g_{i}}^{\top}\mathcal{G}},

it then follows that the cost function exhibits the structure in (
8
).

Appendix D
KŁ example

In this section we present an explicit example of a smooth but nonconcave function that satisfies the KŁ property.
Consider the function
g:[-2,2]\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

g(y)=\begin{cases}2e^{y+1}-1,&-2\leq y\leq-1,\\[6.0pt]
-y^{2}+2,&-1<y\leq 1,\\[6.0pt]
2e^{-y+1}-1,&1<y\leq 2.\end{cases}

(124)

As can be observed, the function
g
is continuously differentiable on the compact set
[-2,2]
. Moreover, despite being nonconcave, it attains its unique global maximum at
y^{\star}=0
.

Figure
1
illustrates the shape of the function.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of the nonconcave function defined in (
124
).

Proposition D.1
.

The function
g(y)
defined in (
124
) satisfies the KŁ property with
\theta=\frac{1}{2}
and
\mu=\frac{1}{10}
, i.e., for all
y\in[-2,2]
,

\mathrm{dist}\left(0,-g^{\prime}(y)+{\mathcal{N}}_{[-2,2]}(y)\right)\geq\mu\left(\max_{y^{{}^{\prime}}\in[-2,2]}g(y^{\prime})-g(y)\right)^{\theta}.

(125)

Proof.

First, we note that
\max_{y^{\prime}\in[-2,2]}g(y^{\prime})=2
. Additionally, we have

{\mathcal{N}}_{[-2,2]}(y)=\begin{cases}(-\infty,\,0],&y=-2,\\[6.0pt]
0,&y\in(-2,2),\\[6.0pt]
[0,\,\infty),&y=2,\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g^{\prime}(y)=\begin{cases}2e^{y+1},&-2\leq y\leq-1,\\[6.0pt]
-2y,&-1<y\leq 1,\\[6.0pt]
-2e^{-(y-1)},&1<y\leq 2.\end{cases}

Since
g^{\prime}(-2)>0
, we have

\mathrm{dist}\left(0,-g^{\prime}(-2)+{\mathcal{N}}_{[-2,2]}(-2)\right)=\mathrm{dist}\Big(0,\left(-\infty,-g^{\prime}(-2)\right]\Big)=\lvert g^{\prime}(-2)\rvert=2e^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{10}\left(3-2e^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus (
125
) is satisfied for
y=-2
.
Similarly, since
g^{\prime}(2)<0
, we have

\mathrm{dist}\left(0,-g^{\prime}(2)+{\mathcal{N}}_{[-2,2]}(2)\right)=\mathrm{dist}\Big(0,\left[-g^{\prime}(2),\infty\right)\Big)=\lvert g^{\prime}(2)\rvert=2e^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{10}\left(3-2e^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence, (
125
) is also satisfied at
y=2
.

We now discuss the scenario when
y\in(-2,2)
. Here, we have

\mathrm{dist}\left(0,-g^{\prime}(y)+{\mathcal{N}}_{[-2,2]}(y)\right)=\lvert g^{\prime}(y)\rvert.

Thus, it suffices to verify

\displaystyle\lvert g^{\prime}(y)\rvert\geq\frac{1}{10}\left(2-g(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\;\forall y\in\left(-2,2\right).

(126)

For the interval
y\in(-2,-1]
, we have
g^{\prime}(y)=2e^{y+1}
and
2-g(y)=3-2e^{y+1}
. Then

\inf_{y\in(-2,-1]}\,\lvert g^{\prime}(y)\rvert=2e^{-1}\approx 0.37,\qquad\sup_{y\in(-2,-1]}\frac{1}{10}\left(3-2e^{y+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{10}\left(3-2e^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx 0.15.

Thus, (
126
) is satisfied on this interval.

For the interval
y\in(-1,1]
, we have
g^{\prime}(y)=-2y
and
2-g(y)=y^{2}=\lvert y\rvert^{2}
. Then (
126
) follows from
2\lvert y\rvert\geq\frac{1}{10}\lvert y\rvert
.

For the interval
y\in(1,2)
, we have
g^{\prime}(y)=-2e^{-(y-1)}
and
2-g(y)=3-2e^{-(y-1)}
. Then

\inf_{y\in(1,2)}\,\lvert g^{\prime}(y)\rvert=2e^{-1}\approx 0.37,\qquad\sup_{y\in(1,2)}\frac{1}{10}\left(3-2e^{-(y-1)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{10}\left(3-2e^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\approx 0.15.

Hence, (
126
) is satisfied on this interval as well. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎
