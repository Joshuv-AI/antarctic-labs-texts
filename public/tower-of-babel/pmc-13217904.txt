The trade-off between coverage and benefit: spatiotemporal variation of China’s health insurance and its influencing factors
Authors: Zhaoxi Wang, Yuyuan Zhang, Mi Xu, Shaoguo Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAchieving universal health coverage has become a global priority, yet internal regional inequalities remain a major challenge within health systems worldwide. China has achieved universal health insurance coverage; however, substantial inequalities persist across provinces, shaped by decentralized welfare governance and uneven socioeconomic development. Existing research has primarily examined urban–rural and population-based inequities, but comprehensive national-level evidence on regional disparities remains limited, particularly since UHC was achieved. This study investigates the spatiotemporal variation of China’s health insurance development and identifies the structural factors driving inter-regional differences.MethodsMacro-panel data were collected from official statistical yearbooks. The entropy weight method was used to construct development indices, and the Dagum-Gini coefficient decomposition method was applied to analyze sources of regional disparities. Ward hierarchical clustering was used to identify provincial distribution patterns of coverage and benefit. Fixed-effects ordinary least squares models were employed to examine influencing factors.ResultsDespite overall developmental progress, substantial and widening inter-regional disparities persist, primarily driven by divergence between eastern and central/western provinces. Most provinces achieved full or broad coverage with moderate benefit, whereas economically developed eastern provinces showed high benefit but lower coverage, indicating a coverage–benefit trade-off. Under the hukou-based eligibility system, population inflow significantly reduced local coverage rates. Benefit expenditure was positively correlated with fiscal capacity and ageing-related risk structure, while inter-provincial competition significantly shaped benefit generosity, particularly in developed regions.ConclusionsRegional disparities in China’s health insurance system remain substantial. Rather than a deliberate strategic trade-off, these disparities reflect a structural polarization driven by decentralized governance. Population mobility strongly affects coverage, while benefit levels are determined by fiscal capacity, demographic risk structure, and governance competition effects. Policy efforts should focus on eliminating hukou-based restrictions, enhancing portability for migrant populations, strengthening equalization transfers, and developing nationally standardized benefit packages to promote equitable progress toward UHC.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14540-z.

Introduction

Universal health coverage (UHC) aims to ensure that everyone can access quality health services without facing financial hardship. However, inequalities continue to be a fundamental challenge for UHC, especially in low-income countries [1]. International evidence demonstrates that meeting national-level UHC targets does not necessarily translate into equity within countries, as substantial subnational geographic disparities persist across health systems. For example, in the United States, although the Affordable Care Act (ACA) led to overall improvements in insurance coverage, significant disparities persisted across racial and ethnic groups and between Medicaid expansion and non-expansion states [2]. In the United Kingdom, regional variation in National Health Service productivity ranged from 5% above to 6% below the national average [3]. Japan achieved UHC in 1961; however, substantial disparities remain in co-payment rates and more than a threefold difference exists in the proportion of income paid as insurance premiums across plans [4]. Similar challenges have been reported in Myanmar, Iraq, and Ethiopia, where wide regional gaps in service coverage and financial protection continue to hinder progress toward UHC by 2030 [5–7]. Together, these cases indicate that internal regional inequalities constitute a common challenge for both high-income and low- and middle-income countries, underscoring the need to understand the political, fiscal and institutional determinants of UHC equity. Characterized by substantial socioeconomic inequalities across its provinces, China, faces significant challenges regarding spatial disparities in the financial burden of healthcare [8]. This may be linked to unequal levels of financial protection, underscoring the urgent need to examine regional disparities in health insurance protection.

As the world’s largest developing nation, China achieved universal health insurance coverage by establishing three key social insurance systems: Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI) in 1998, New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NRCMS) in 2003, and Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URBMI) in 2007. In 2016, the URBMI and NRCMS merged into a single system known as the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI). Since then, China’s health system rests on two main pillars: UEBMI (for employees) and URRBMI (for non-employed residents). By the end of 2023, China’s health insurance system covered 95% of the population, reaching 1.33 billion people, making it the world’s largest health insurance network. However, even with universal health insurance coverage, there are still large variations in financial risk protection across subgroups [9]. Existing research on inequality in China’s health insurance system has predominantly examined urban–rural differences and population-based inequities, such as the effects of urban–rural integration on access to care and health outcomes [10–12]. However, research on regional disparities remains comparatively underdeveloped.

Governance systems in China, which combine political centralization with economic decentralization, reflect inter-governmental relations characterized by fragmented authority and Chinese-style federalism [13–16]. The decentralized nature of China’s welfare system allows local governments to make autonomous decisions in social welfare policies, influenced by factors such as local finances, central government transfers, promotion incentives, and neighboring regional policies within a unified central planning framework [17–21]. This has resulted in a fragmented social welfare system, a phenomenon known as welfare regionalism [22, 23]. When national health policy reforms involve multiple interventions, local authorities often have the discretion to selectively adopt policy packages and prioritize certain interventions in their implementation agendas. Consequently, local policy discretion creates diverse policy mixes across regions [22, 23]. Besides the decentralized governance characteristics, China, with its vast territory and large population, experiences unbalanced economic development. This leads to disparities in medical resources and health protection levels across various regions. Although China has substantially reduced out-of-pocket expenditure and alleviated the financial burden of medical treatment in recent years, spatial differences and inequities remain prominent [24].

Recent research has begun to explore regional disparities in health protection, identifying substantial provincial differences in health insurance expansion, catastrophic health expenditure, medical impoverishment, out-of-pocket burden and benefit equity [24–28]. However, most studies rely on single outcome indicators, focus on specific provinces rather than national patterns, or examine the coverage expansion period prior to universalization, all of which limit understanding of the overall structure and determinants of inter-provincial disparities since UHC was achieved.

To address this gap, this study examines the spatiotemporal distribution of China’s health insurance system after achieving universal coverage and investigates the factors driving subnational disparities to support the development of an equitable and efficient health insurance system. We draw on the World Health Organization’s UHC framework, including the breadth, depth, and height of coverage [29], and follow Huang’s theoretical model of subnational variation in Chinese social health insurance governance [27]. Accordingly, we use insurance coverage rate and benefit expenditure as core indicators to analyze the spatial structure and evolution of provincial disparities. Specifically, coverage aligns with the breadth of the UHC framework (the proportion of the population insured), while benefit expenditure reflects the depth and height of financial protection (the extent to which out-of-pocket costs are covered).

Regarding influencing factors, regional disparities in public health services are closely linked to differences in economic and fiscal capacity [8, 30]. Although narrowing regional economic disparities may reduce inequality in insurance benefits, economic divergence has increased since the system’s establishment. In addition, local policy adjustments permitted by the central government have sustained variations in health insurance [31]. Moreover, demographic risk factors—such as population aging, child dependency and population mobility—shape regional variation in insurance expansion and benefit levels [27]. These factors may jointly influence health insurance coverage and benefits. Therefore, this study uses fiscal expenditure, population mobility, and both old-age and child dependency ratios as independent variables. In addition, China’s system of official incentives influences regional policy dynamics; competitive pressure among local health departments may encourage benefit expansion [32]. To capture potential spatial spillover effects and inter-provincial policy diffusion, we incorporated health insurance indicators from neighboring provinces as independent variables, building on the methodological approach by Guo et al [17]. The neighbors of a province were defined as provinces in the same region sharing common borders with the province [17]. Refer to it, this study incorporates the influence of neighboring provinces by constructing spatial neighboring variables based on geographic proximity and fiscal transfer classification.

This study utilizes provincial-level panel data from 30 provinces in mainland China spanning from 2012 to 2022. Tibet was excluded due to data unavailability and the structural incompatibility of its free healthcare system for agricultural and pastoral populations compared to the standard URRBMI scheme. This study applies the entropy weight method (EWM), Dagum Gini coefficient, cluster analysis, and fixed-effects ordinary least squares regression to analyze the spatiotemporal variation and determinants of regional disparities in health insurance. The possible contributions of this paper are as follows: (1) Applying the WHO UHC framework to providing a national-level assessment of provincial disparities in coverage and benefit after the achievement of UHC; (2) exploring the determinants of regional disparities under decentralized governance; and (3) offering policy insights to strengthen regional equity, particularly for resource-constrained developing countries seeking to reduce internal imbalances while advancing UHC.

Methods

Data sources

The data used in this study were obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook, China Health Statistical Yearbook, China Financial Statistical Yearbook, and China Population and Employment Statistical Yearbook. The analysis sample comprised macro-level data from 30 provinces in mainland China (Tibet was excluded as explained before) from 2012 to 2022. The observation period begins in 2012, as China’s national health insurance coverage consistently exceeded 95% after 2011. This threshold shift provides the prerequisite for transitioning the analytical focus from overall coverage expansion to the underlying structural disparities across regions.

Statistical analysis

First, the entropy weight method (EWM) was employed to construct a composite evaluation index, quantifying the developmental dynamics of China’s health insurance from 2012 to 2022. Next, the inequity of the development was calculated using the Dagum-Gini coefficient decomposition method. Subsequently, we chose two main factors, coverage rate and per-capita expenditure, to conduct a cluster analysis to group the characteristics of regional variation in China’s health insurance. Finally, an ordinary least squares (OLS) fixed-effects model was used to analyze the key factors influencing the distribution of health insurance in terms of coverage and expenditure.

Entropy weight method

To objectively evaluate regional disparities in China’s basic health insurance, we applied the Entropy Weight Method (EWM), which quantifies the degree of variation across indicators to determine their relative importance. Unlike subjective weighting, EWM assigns higher weights to indicators with greater informational differentiation, thereby enhancing the accuracy of composite evaluations [33].

Following data normalization for comparability, entropy and redundancy values were calculated to derive indicator weights. These weights were then used to construct a composite index capturing three core dimensions—coverage rates, benefit expenditure, and risk funding—reflecting the World Health Organization’s emphasis on universal health coverage and system sustainability. While coverage and benefit reflect the breadth, depth, and height of coverage, we also include risk to demonstrate the sustainability of the system. Without sustainability, UHC cannot be maintained to protect the population. The index system for China’s basic health insurance was presented in Table 1.

By applying EWM to data from 2012 to 2022, we assessed how each dimension contributed to regional disparities in China’s health insurance system, providing a clearer empirical picture of interregional differences in coverage, expenditure, and risk management. The full computational steps and equations are presented in the Supplementary Material.

Cluster analysis

We performed a cluster analysis of health insurance across 30 provinces (autonomous regions and municipalities, excluding Tibet). We grouped these provinces based on their coverage and expenditure to assess regional differences. We use a multi-year average to smooth short-term annual fluctuations in fund expenditure and to capture more stable provincial distribution patterns of coverage and benefit. The steps were as follows. First, we calculated the five-year average by dividing the sum of the values from 2018 to 2022 by five. Second, for the per capita expenditure indicator, we took the logarithm and multiplied it by 100 to reduce skewness. Third, we applied the Ward linkage method of hierarchical clustering to coverage rates and adjusted per capita expenditure, the core variables in the analysis. The number of clusters was determined by maximizing Je(2)/Je(1) and minimizing the pseudo-T-squared values. Based on this method, we identified the optimal grouping for analysis.

Measures

This study used the health insurance coverage rate (coverage) and per capita expenditure (benefit) as the primary dependent variables to examine regional differences in health insurance. Coverage rates were defined as the proportion of individuals enrolled in local health insurance (including urban employee health insurance and urban and rural resident health insurance) relative to the total resident population. Expenditure was measured by per-capita expenditure on local urban and rural resident health insurance and was calculated as the ratio of total expenditure to the number of residents enrolled in urban and rural resident health insurance.

Social risks and governance factors were the independent variables used in this study. Socio-demographic structure—serving as a proxy for healthcare demand and system sustainability—was operationalized through the population’s age structure and mobility. Governance factors were defined within China’s decentralized welfare regime, including resource effects and inter-governmental competition. Regional per-capita fiscal revenue was used as a measure of the resource effect, whereas the coverage rate and expenditure level of neighboring provinces serve as indicators of inter-provincial effects.

Considering regional imbalances in health resources, such as hospitals and health technicians [35–37] and the significant correlation between health insurance demand and the supply of health resources, we included health technician density (per 1,000 people) and the proportion of tertiary hospitals as control variables.

Thus, we used coverage rate and per capita health insurance expenditure as dependent variables, and old-age dependency ratio (above 65 years old) and child dependency ratios (0–14 years old), population mobility, per capita fiscal revenue, coverage rates and per capita expenditures in neighboring provinces as independent variables. We controlled for the number of health technicians per 1,000 people and the proportion of tertiary hospitals. The data sources and variable designs are listed in Table 2.

Results

Spatiotemporal variation

Figure 1 presents the spatiotemporal variation in the comprehensive development index of basic health insurance across Chinese provinces from 2012 to 2022 (the specific results can be seen from Table S1), highlighting regional disparities and dynamic trends. From a national perspective, the development index of basic health insurance showed an overall fluctuating upward trend, increasing from 0.2091 in 2012 to 0.3419 in 2022.

Spatiotemporal variation in comprehensive development index of basic health insurance across China Provinces (2012–2022)

However, regional disparities were prominent. The Eastern region consistently outperformed the other regions, with Beijing’s index rising from 0.2016 in 2012 to 0.5915 in 2022, while Shanghai followed a similar trajectory (0.2032–0.5804). In contrast, the Central and Western regions experienced slower growth, with provinces such as Henan and Shanxi reaching only moderate development (0.3060 and 0.3103, respectively, in 2022). Although the Western region started with a lower baseline, its growth accelerated, partially narrowing the gap to the east. Notably, Xinjiang’s index increased from 0.1863 in 2012 to 0.3522 in 2022, and Qinghai’s from 0.2257 to 0.4231.

We then used the cluster analysis to explore the category of regional variation. Table 3 presents the results of the clustering analysis, where the provinces were categorized into six groups based on coverage rate and per capita expenditure. The final grouping and naming were determined based on research convenience and practical considerations. Among the six categories, the first included Beijing and Shanghai, where coverage rates were relatively low, fluctuating around 90% or below (91.63%, 77.90%), whereas per capita expenditure led the nation, ranking in the top two. The second category included Zhejiang, Jiangsu, and Tianjin, which were further divided into two clusters (Clusters 2 and 3). These provinces share a common feature: their per-capita expenditures far exceed the national average (833), ranking among the top five, while their coverage rates, except for Jiangsu, were below 90% (Tianjin, 83.73%; Zhejiang, 85.68%), which was significantly lower than the national average of 95%. Thus, these three provinces were grouped together with Beijing and Shanghai as regions with low coverage and high benefit. By contrast, Guizhou, Jilin, and Gansu (Cluster 5) had the lowest per capita expenditures but high coverage rates. Notably, provinces like Jilin and Guizhou achieved or even exceeded 100% nominal coverage, yet clustered into the ‘high coverage, low generosity’ typology. Similarly, provinces such as Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hunan, Guangxi, Chongqing, Sichuan, and Shaanxi (Cluster 6) also exhibited coverage rates near 100%, ranking among the top, but their per-capita expenditures fell within the middle range (ranked 15–26 overall), representing regions with high coverage and medium benefit. Cluster 4 includes provinces such as Hebei, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Heilongjiang, Fujian, Shandong, Hubei, where both coverage rates (almost from 90% to 95%) and expenditure levels (770–850 yuan/person) are generally in the medium rank. Based on these findings, four cluster categories were established. Groups with an average coverage rate exceeding 95% were labeled as full coverage, those between 90% and 95% as wide coverage, and those below 90% as low coverage. Expenditure levels were classified as low, medium, or high, according to their relative rankings.

Overall, most provinces fall into the full or wide coverage categories with medium benefit, while economically developed eastern provinces, such as Shanghai and Beijing, are characterized by high benefit but low coverage. To further illustrate the substantive meaning of the clustering results, we conducted a comparative review of health insurance policies in four representative cities corresponding to different cluster types: Beijing (low coverage, high benefit), Guiyang city in Guizhou Province (full coverage, low benefit), Xi’an city in Shaanxi Province (full coverage, medium benefit), and Wuhan city in Hubei Province (wide coverage, medium benefit). Regarding eligibility rules, Beijing restricts participation primarily to residents with local household registration or urban employees with stable employment, whereas the other three cities adopt resident-based eligibility criteria that include permanent residents. In terms of fiscal subsidies, Beijing provides higher government contributions to the urban–rural resident insurance scheme, with per capita subsidies for elderly residents exceeding 4,000 RMB, while the other cities largely follow the nationally mandated minimum standard of less than 700 RMB. Substantial differences are also observed in benefit design. For outpatient services, Beijing sets an annual reimbursement ceiling of 4,500 RMB, compared with only 200–400 RMB in the other cities. With respect to inpatient coverage under catastrophic health insurance, Beijing imposes no upper reimbursement limit, Xi’an applies no explicit ceiling, Wuhan sets a ceiling of 300,000 RMB, and Guiyang limits coverage to 200,000 RMB.

This geographic pattern and policy design reflects a trade-off between coverage and benefit in regional variations in China’s health insurance system.

Equity

To further examine regional disparities, the Dagum-Gini coefficient method was applied to decompose inequity into intra-regional and inter-regional components. According to the classification standards of the National Bureau of Statistics in 2011, China’s economic regions are divided into four major regions: the eastern, central, western, and northeastern regions. The eastern region includes Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanghai, Jiangsu,Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong, and Hainan.The central region includes Shanxi, Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei, and Hunan.The western region includes Inner Mongolia, Guangxi,Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia and Xinjiang. The northeast region includes Liaoning, Jilin and Heilongjiang. The results are presented in Fig. 2 and Table S2.

Figure 2A shows the Gini coefficient trend for health insurance development in China from 2012 to 2022. During the observation period, the overall Gini coefficient remained stable between 0.07 and 0.09, with two turning points occurring in 2015 and 2018. After the merger of urban and rural resident health insurance in 2015, the Gini coefficient increased. The level of health insurance development in some economically developed regions improved after the merger, whereas the increase in less economically developed regions was relatively small. In comparison, the Gini coefficient decreased after the establishment of the National Healthcare Security Administration in 2018.

Figure 2B shows the trend in the Gini coefficient in the four regions from 2012 to 2022. Decomposition of the Dagum-Gini coefficient highlights a diverging spatial trajectory. While intra-regional inequalities within the Central, Western, and Northeastern regions gradually converged, the Eastern region experienced a steady widening of internal disparities, indicating increasing welfare stratification even among developed provinces. Specifically, the Gini coefficients in the Central, Western, and Northeastern regions decreased from 0.04, 0.09, and 0.07 in 2012 to 0.02, 0.03, and 0.02 in 2022. In comparison, the Gini coefficient in the eastern region increased from 0.09 in 2012 to 0.13 in 2022. This trend indicates that disparities in the eastern region increased, whereas disparities within other regions narrowed further. Additionally, by 2022, the degree of within-region disparity in the eastern region is the largest, followed by the Western, Central, and Northeastern regions.

The trends in inter-regional disparities are shown in Fig. 2C. Two trends were observed in the regional disparities. The differences between the eastern region and the other three regions fluctuated upwards, whereas the disparities within the other regions fluctuated and narrowed. In 2022, the disparity between the Eastern and Northeastern regions was the largest (0.12), followed by the disparities between the Eastern and Central regions (0.08), and between the Eastern and Western regions (0.06). Disparities between the other regions ranged from 0.03 to 0.05, with the smallest difference between the Central and Northeastern regions (0.03).

Figure 2D illustrates the changing contribution rates of each decomposition term to the overall Gini coefficient from 2012 to 2022. The contribution rate of intra-regional differences remained stable at approximately 24%-28%. Trans-variation intensity decreased from 35.23% in 2012 to 17.21% in 2022, with the highest peak of 54.17% occurring in 2016. However, the contribution rate of inter-regional differences showed the opposite trend, increasing from 37.54% in 2012 to 58.47% in 2022, with the lowest point at 17.50% in 2016. From 2012 to 2016, the trans-variation intensity was the main source of overall disparities, followed by intra-regional-and inter-regional differences. However, with the increasing contribution of inter-regional differences, inter-regional differences have become the main source of overall spatial disparities since 2017.

Overall, China’s regional health insurance disparities are currently primarily driven by between-region differences, particularly the disparity between the eastern region and other areas.

Factors affecting health insurance coverage and benefit

After running the Hausman test, fixed-effect OLS regression models were used to examine the determinants of coverage and benefit. At the same time, to facilitate comparison of effect sizes across variables, we additionally report standardized coefficients (beta coefficients). All continuous variables, including the lagged dependent variable, were standardized within provinces by demeaning and dividing by their within-group standard deviation. The results (Table 4) indicate that population mobility is the main predictor (-0.705, P < 0.001). Regions with higher population inflows tend to have lower coverage, whereas outflow regions exhibit higher rates of coverage. Overall, in areas with population inflow, the insurance participation rate calculated based on the resident population is much lower than that of the hukou-registered population. Conversely, in areas with population outflow, the insurance participation rate calculated based on the resident population is much higher than that of the hukou-registered population (Table S3 and Table S4). For instance, in 2022, Shanghai’s coverage rate was 80.39% for the resident population and 132.30% for the hukou-registered population, while Guizhou reported higher coverage for residents (109.47%) than for those with local hukou (91.14%). This finding underscores why coverage rates were chosen as the primary indicator: although the national rate has exceeded 95%, results reveal uneven coverage across regions with varying labor mobility, driven by hukou-based (household registration system) eligibility restrictions.

Fiscal resources (-7.752, P < 0.01) also negatively impacted coverage, suggesting that wealthier regions allocate funds to enhance benefit expenditure rather than expanding coverage. However, older population dependency ratios (-0.480, P < 0.1) indicated that demographic aging may also influence coverage decisions. As for the standardized coefficients, elderly dependency ratio and population movement contribute most to the coverage rates (Table 5).

Factors influencing health insurance benefit

Regarding to the benefit expenditure, this is driven by a combination of demographic, fiscal, and competitive factors. Within the risk effect, the older population’s dependency ratio is positively correlated with expenditure (17.95, P < 0.01), showing robust results, which also contribute most among the standardized coefficients. If risk represents demand for the health insurance system, then fiscal resources represent its supply capacity. Higher local per capita fiscal revenue correlated with greater expenditure (194.0, P < 0.1), implying that fiscal resources influence benefit levels through supply incentives and capacity. Given the relationship between fiscal revenue and economic development, both economic growth and fiscal capacity have emerged as the direct influences on regional health insurance outcomes. In addition, fiscal transfers significantly shaped local policy decisions. In the context of hierarchical financing, the central and western regions received higher central transfer payments, with the central government contributing more than half of the fiscal subsidies (Table S5 and Table S6). These transfers were allocated directly to each province’s risk funding based on the number of insured participants. Moreover, these regions had a higher proportion of citizens receiving health insurance and were more reliant on fiscal subsidies, resulting in lower or moderate expenditure levels.

To examine potential spatial interdependence among provinces, we further estimated a fixed-effects spatial autoregressive (SAR) model based on a contiguity weight matrix. Discussion

This study investigated regional disparities in China’s health insurance system within the framework of universal coverage and decentralized governance, identifying inequality patterns and their underlying mechanisms across provinces. The findings contribute to the global discussion on equity in universal health coverage by demonstrating that regional inequalities persist even after near-universal enrollment was achieved, a trend also observed in many high-income and low- and middle-income health systems [2, 3, 5–7]. China’s experience illustrates that achieving extensive coverage does not automatically translate into equal financial protection or access to essential care, reinforcing the view that internal disparities represent one of the most significant barriers to UHC equity.

First, China’s basic health insurance showed steady growth, though uneven development. Results indicate overall improvement from 2012 to 2022, yet disparities persist across regions, with the eastern provinces consistently outperforming the central and western regions. The Dagum Gini decomposition indicates that the dominant source of inequity stems from interregional variation. This pattern echoes research showing that provincial disparities in financial protection and access to health coverage remain a major constraint to UHC progress in China, with developed regions enjoying greater protection than less developed ones [24–26, 28], and aligns with evidence from other high- or low-income countries where internal regional disparities continue despite national progress in coverage [2, 3, 5–7]. These findings illustrate that while national policies have improved overall health insurance standards, local economic conditions, fiscal capacities, and the policy autonomy mean that national-level expansion alone is insufficient. Targeted governance reforms are required to reduce territorial inequities in resource allocation and service protection.

Second, regional variations in China’s health insurance system reflect a trade-off between insurance coverage and benefit generosity. Consistent with the WHO’s three-dimensional UHC framework, coverage reflects the breadth of population protected, while benefit expenditure indicates depth and height of financial protection. Previous studies have also highlighted that developing countries should lower their health insurance premiums to cover broader medical services [38]. By reducing these premiums, most households in rural and urban areas become more willing to enroll in health insurance schemes. Our findings extend the combination of breadth, depth and height of financial protection in China. Within China’s decentralized fiscal and welfare system, this trade-off is further shaped by regional resource constraints and institutional governance arrangements. Within China’s decentralized welfare framework, local governments face the administrative performance requirements that emphasize health insurance enrollment targets based on hukou-registered populations. Constrained by limited fiscal capacities, less-developed regions tend to suppress benefit levels in order to meet enrollment policy targets under budgetary pressure. As a result, they are structurally incentivized to adopt a “high-coverage, low-generosity” compliance strategy. While the economically developed regions, supported by stronger fiscal resources, tend to prioritize higher benefit levels and reimbursement rates. At the same time, hukou-based eligibility restrictions limit the inclusion of migrant populations in local insurance schemes, thereby containing coverage obligations. This allows affluent regions to concentrate public resources on registered residents while using generous benefit packages to attract high-skilled and younger workers, who contribute to the sustainability of local insurance funds. Together, these different policy responses generate persistent regional patterns of “low coverage–high generosity” in developed regions, “high coverage–low generosity” in less-developed areas, and full or wide coverage with moderate generosity in most other regions.

Third, our results further demonstrate that population mobility and hukou segmentation are the critical determinants of insurance coverage differences. It could be hypothesized that the lower coverage in affluent eastern provinces is partly driven by high-income urban residents strategically opting out of the public scheme in favor of private commercial health insurance. However, this alternative explanation is structurally and economically constrained. First, high-income urban residents are predominantly formal sector employees. Under China’s Social Insurance Law, participation in the urban employee health insurance is legally mandatory for formal employees and is enforced via payroll tax deductions, which precludes strategic non-participation. Second, for those with the flexibility to opt out (e.g., wealthy freelancers or business owners), substituting basic insurance entirely with commercial insurance is economically irrational and contrary to basic choice behavior under uncertainty. In China’s multi-pillar healthcare system, commercial health insurance acts strictly as a supplementary tier. Most commercial policies impose severe financial penalties—such as drastically reducing the reimbursement rate (e.g., from 100% to 60%) or enforcing high deductibles—if medical claims are not first processed through the basic social health insurance. Such rules that commercial insurance is built on social insurance effectively prevents the widespread substitution of the public scheme. Therefore, the “low coverage” phenomenon in eastern provinces cannot be meaningfully attributed to wealthy locals abandoning the public system. Instead, the different coverage levels among regions are fundamentally driven by population flow and enrollment eligibility under the decentralized system. The coverage rate at the national level is over 95%, almost all the population participate in the social health insurance. However, regions with high inflows of migrants exhibit significantly lower resident coverage rates compared to hukou-registered populations, whereas outflow regions show the reverse pattern, confirming the exclusionary effect of population movement. This misalignment between entitlement and actual risk exposure creates structural access inequities for migrant workers, despite national universal coverage. Similar patterns have been observed in the United States, where racial disparities persisted under the ACA despite overall coverage improvements [2]. Such findings reinforce that universal eligibility does not guarantee universal access, a core theme in social determinants of health frameworks. The exclusionary effect of hukou-based regulation functions as a structural determinant of inequality and represents a major barrier to equitable financial protection for vulnerable groups. This dynamic can be described as regional protectionism, in which well-off localities erect administrative barriers to prevent welfare migration from other regions [23]. In other words, the territorially managed health insurance system fails to address the needs arising from transregional population mobility, resulting in a significant gap in healthcare protection for migrant populations. This spatial mismatch not only generates substantial gaps in financial protection but profoundly undermines the fairness of health service utilization for migrant workers. From the perspective of equal opportunity, tethering fundamental health entitlements to legacy hukou statuses institutionalizes health inequities, relegating the migrant population to systemic vulnerability despite the nominal achievement of national universal coverage.

Fourth, fiscal capacity, demographic structure, and intergovernmental competition jointly shape regional inequity in benefit expenditure. As the proportion of older people increases, so does the overall incidence of illness, which leads to increased medical demand and associated costs. Given that health insurance policy design inherently favors the older population, these dynamics lead to increased expenditure on health insurance benefits. Thus, the health insurance system has adapted to the social risk structure shaped by the demographic distribution of the older population. Population inflows largely consist of young laborers, moderating the overall population structure, resulting in a younger demographic and reduced demand for health insurance spending. Local government spending on social policy programs—such as education, healthcare, and social welfare—is strongly correlated with fiscal capacity [17, 19, 22]. Specifically, in the health insurance sector, government funding is a major source of urban and rural resident health insurance schemes, with fiscal subsidies accounting for more than 60% of total financing in recent years. Greater fiscal capacity enables wealthier provinces to invest more aggressively in benefit enhancement, whereas poorer regions operate within financing constraints [39, 40]. Similar to previous studies, central transfer payments influence local decision-making but do not fully correct structural disparities. For example, greater dependency on central fiscal transfers to finance the Dibao program in a province was negatively correlated with the local Dibao threshold [17, 20].

In addition, policy competition and welfare tournaments driven by local government performance incentives contribute to spatial differentiation in benefit generosity [40]. Lower-level governments under the same superior authority tend to view each other as potential or real rivals competing for scarce political and economic opportunities [41]. The “promotion tournament 2.0” framework suggests that the cadre assessment system subsequently made healthcare a “hard target,” and thus the competition for office has compelled local cadres to implement health-care expansion. Besides, this model also confirms the pivotal role of technical bureaucrats by emphasizing the knowledge–power coalition between these political actors [32]. In the specific domain of health insurance integration, local adoption of both within-program integration and urban–rural integration are driven by top-down pressure for policy compliance [18]. In addition, the case of health care reform in Sanming City also illustrates the local policy entrepreneurs can play a pivotal role in policy changes [42]. Specifically, we find that while coverage, as a binding indicator, is relatively insulated from competitive pressures, expenditure exhibits strong upward competition. Once coverage rates stabilize, benefit generosity exhibits a stark polarization between affluent and less-developed regions, thereby exacerbating structural imbalances in health insurance development. Although fiscal constraints limit expenditure in poorer areas, competitive dynamics drive benefit expansion in wealthier regions. No local government wishes to be regarded as a welfare laggard because a lower standard in the social safety net may yield undesired consequences in terms of political image and perhaps societal reputation [17]. This pattern reflects a dual-tier welfare tournament mechanism: while less-developed regions aim to avoid ranking last in social welfare, wealthier regions compete to lead and actively enhance benefits to attract skilled labor and maintain social stability. As a result, although all regions have incentives to improve health insurance benefits, their ability to do so varies significantly. Under the institutional constraint that welfare benefits are difficult to retrench once expanded, benefit levels tend to increase more rapidly in developed regions, thereby widening interregional disparities [40]. However, welfare tournaments may also generate negative consequences, including inefficient spending races, rising fiscal pressure, and benefit expansion that deviates from cost-effective priorities. Without stronger institutional coordination and fiscal equalization mechanisms, such competitive dynamics risk reinforcing interregional divergence and undermining the long-term sustainability and equity of the health insurance system. These findings underscore the importance of balancing regional policies to prevent excessive disparities in benefit expenditure. Finally, it is worth noting that policy interventions such as China’s health poverty alleviation programs have partially mitigated disparities in less-developed western regions by improving financial protection for vulnerable populations [43]. These areas show a steady upward trend, which contributes to relatively lower within-region inequality, while the overall disparity pattern remains dominated by between-region differences, particularly between eastern and other regions.

These results resonate with comparative governance research showing that decentralization often increases territorial health inequalities, leading several European countries to pursue recentralization to improve equity [44]. In China, a similar governance transition has been observed since the establishment of the National Healthcare Security Administration in 2018. Health insurance governance has gradually shifted from fragmented decentralization toward moderate recentralization, with policies aimed at reducing regional disparities, including the introduction of standardized benefit packages and the expansion of trans-provincial immediate reimbursement mechanisms [45]. Under this framework, improving equity in health insurance requires addressing structural drivers of inequality rather than relying solely on enrollment expansion. Furthermore, a one-size-fits-all approach is insufficient for addressing the challenges of achieving UHC in China [26]. On the one hand, removing hukou-based eligibility restrictions and adopting resident-based coverage indicators would be a critical step toward equity. Under a recentralized framework, central authorities could incorporate resident population coverage rates-rather than hukou-based enrollment-into intergovernmental performance evaluation and fiscal transfer formulas. Such a design would reduce local incentives to exclude migrant populations and better align coverage expansion with the realities of population mobility. On the other hand, developing a nationally unified benefits catalog and reimbursement framework may limit excessive welfare competition and moderate interprovincial inequities. International experience also indicates that resource allocation modalities must be designed to ensure that financing is channeled to where it is needed the most, and not to create barriers to effective risk pooling and cross-subsidization [46]. Prioritizing financial risk protection for vulnerable, disadvantaged populations or low-performing regions is also recommended by studies of developed countries pursuing UHC [5, 7]. Consequently, we recommend that priority central governments prioritize fiscal transfers to compensate less-developed regions for their heightened demographic health risks and limited fiscal capacity. At the same time, for wealthier provinces, further benefit expansion should be conditional on ensuring inclusive coverage of resident populations, preventing benefit-driven competition that exacerbates regional inequality.

This study is limited by provincial-level. Although some municipal statistics are publicly available, fragmented reporting standards and incomplete scheme-specific data currently limit systematic subnational analysis. As reporting systems improve, future research should prioritize intra-provincial inequality. Additionally, the “per capita benefit expenditure” is a proxy for generosity, other detailed benefit design features such as deductibles and co-payment rates are not captured. Future research should integrate service utilization and health outcome variables to strengthen the evaluation of equity beyond financial indicators.

Conclusion

While China has achieved the major milestone of universal health insurance enrollment, this study reveals persistent structural disparities that undermine regional equity, reflecting a coverage–benefit trade-off. The regional disparities are driven by risk factors and decentralized welfare governance, including fiscal effects, local competition, and the hukou system. Therefore, removing administrative barriers and establishing nationwide enrollment procedures and uniform benefit standards could mitigate inequalities in health insurance coverage and benefit expenditure. Based on these findings, this study offers insights for developing countries aiming to identify key determinants of regional disparities and reduce interregional imbalances in the pursuit of universal health insurance coverage.
