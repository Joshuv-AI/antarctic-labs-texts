MLOps with Microservices:A Case Study on the Maritime Domain
Renato Cordeiro Ferreira, Rowanne Trapmann, Willem-Jan van den Heuvel

Abstract
This case study describes challenges and lessons learned on building Ocean Guard : a Machine Learning–Enabled System (MLES) for anomaly detection in the maritime domain. First, the paper presents the system’s specification, and architecture. Ocean Guard was designed with a microservices’ architecture to enable multiple teams to work on the project in parallel. Then, the paper discusses how the developers adapted contract-based design to MLOps for achieving that goal. As a MLES, Ocean Guard employs code, model, and data contracts to establish guidelines between its services. This case study hopes to inspire software engineers, machine learning engineers, and data scientists to leverage similar approaches for their systems.

Introduction

Maritime transportation is the cornerstone of global trade and commerce,
facilitating the movement of goods and commodities across vast distances.
The maritime domain represents one of the most dynamic and complex operational
environments in global logistics and transportation. It is characterized by
continuous movement across international boundaries, varying regulatory
frameworks, and vulnerability to unpredictable weather conditions. As such,
maritime operations generate a large amount of heterogeneous data from multiple
sources.

The digital transformation of the maritime industry has accelerated in recent
years, driven by economic pressures, environmental regulations, and safety
concerns
[
3
]
. According to the International
Maritime Organization (IMO), ships now function as floating data centers,
equipped with hundreds of sensors. Improving maritime navigation and
communication by facilitating the implementation of safe data sharing between
vessels and between vessels and shore-based systems through automation
[
1
]
. This complex web of information flows across
organizational boundaries between shipping companies, port authorities, maritime
safety agencies, and commercial partners
[
8
]
.

This rich data ecosystem presents opportunities and challenges, including the
potential for predictive analytics, anomaly detection, and route optimization
using Machine Learning (ML)
[
2
]
.
However, extracting actionable insights requires systems capable of ingesting,
processing, and analyzing different data streams at scale while maintaining
operational reliability in an environment where connectivity and conditions
rapidly change
[
17
]
.
Machine Learning Operations (MLOps) refers to a collection of best practices,
sets of concepts, and development culture that aims to achieve that goal
[
10
]
.

Since 2015, the microservices architectural style have emerged as a promising
approach to address challenges of modularity, scalability, and resilience by
decomposing complex systems into smaller, independently deployable services
with well-defined interfaces
[
14
,
16
]
.
This approach can be particularly suited to the maritime domain’s nature, where
specialized components can be designed to handle different maritime data sources
and analytical needs.

The novel field of Software Engineering for Artificial Intelligence (SE4AI)
studies the adaptation of Software Engineering techniques for the creation of
ML-Enabled Systems (MLES)
[
9
]
. Since 2022, different
reference architectures have been proposed for them
[
11
,
6
,
10
]
. Fundamentally, they describe these systems
as containing multiple components, which can be divided across different
services.

This case study describes the development of
Ocean Guard
: a ML-Enabled System (MLES) for
anomaly detection in the maritime domain.

2
System Specification

This section describes the specification of the
Ocean Guard
tool, which was defined
from conversations with stakeholders at the beginning of the
MARIT-D
project.
The specification is divided into two parts:
functional
and

non-functional
requirements
[
15
]
.

2.1
Functional Requirements

Functional Requirements
, document the functionalities that directly
affect the user of a system
[
15
]
.
There are two actors related to the
Ocean Guard
tool. The following descriptions
detail the main functionalities associated with them.

An
Investigator
is a human user who uses the tool to obtain
clues about anomalous behavior at sea. They must be able to:

I1.

see geolocations of marine objects in a map,

I2.

filter geolocations by area of interest, date and time,

I3.

discern different types of marine objects
(vessels, structures, or unidentified),

I4.

retrieve geolocations gathered from different data sources,

I5.

check details (metadata) associated with a given marine object,

I6.

highlight the trajectory of a marine object,

I7.

see anomalies identified by the tool in a map,

I8.

filter anomalies by area of interest, date and time, and

I9.

inspect why an anomaly was considered so by the tool.

The
Anomaly Detector
is a component of the tool responsible for detecting
anomalous behavior of marine objects. It must be able to:

A1.

detect anomalies related to a marine object,

A2.

list anomalies by area of interest, date and time, and

A3.

explain why an anomaly is considered so.

2.2
Non-Functional Requirements

Non-Functional Requirements
, also known as

Architectural Requirements
, describe a set of
nonstructural attributes that are important for the success of a
system
[
15
]
.
There are four main non-functional requirements proposed for the
Ocean Guard
tool.
The following descriptions detail the main characteristics the system
should have to attend its users.

Compliance.

The
Ocean Guard
tool should be compliant with the latest European laws, including the
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), and the Artificial Intelligence Act
(AI Act).

Explainability.

The
Ocean Guard
tool should be able to explain why it considers a marine object to be
anomalous. For algorithm-based anomaly detection, this means clarifying which
decisions were taken until the anomaly was identified. For AI-based anomaly
detection, this means using interpretable models (such as tree-based models) or
implementing black-box explainability techniques (such as SHAP or LIME).

Resiliency.

The
Ocean Guard
tool should be able to handle the expected amount of data.
This is important because the system may process high-volume and high-velocity
data sources, such as those providing tracking data.

Compatibility.

The
Ocean Guard
tool should be able to interoperate with other existing tools, extending
the
Investigator
’s current existing capabilities. As such, the tool
should follow EU standards that enable quick integration with other ecosystems.

Data Collection.

To fulfill the requirements described in
section

2
, the

Ocean Guard
tool required comprehensive data acquisition capabilities. The Data
Acquisition subsystem collects data from multiple heterogeneous sources:

3rd party Providers (1)
,
Physical Sensors (2)
,
and
Data Crawlers (3)
. The data is stored in one of the two
data stores: the
Label Store (A)
for known data about
anomalies – which may come from the
3rd Party Providers (1)

or the
Web Application (1)
– and the
Raw
Data Store (B)
for unprocessed data.

Continuous Training.

The next step in the implementation was to enable continuous training.
Four components were developed for this purpose:
a
Synthetic Data Generation Pipeline (I)
;
a
Data Augmentation Pipeline (II)
;
a
Rule-Based Training Pipeline (III)
; and
a
ML-Based Training Pipeline (IV)
.
The creation of these pipelines is an opportunity to introduce two critical
model management tools:
the
Metadata Store (F)
and
the
Model Registry (G)
,
which together facilitate model lineage tracking and versioning.

Serving.

The
Batch Prediction Pipeline (VIII)
facilitates efficient
processing of large-scale offline predictions, while results are stored in a
dedicated
Prediction Store (H)
. Complementing this offline
processing capability, the
API Prediction Service (8)
is the
main component of the system, providing low-latency inference capabilities to
support real-time application requirements.

Monitoring.

The
Ocean Guard
tool requires a
Governance Application (7)
to enable
model monitoring and analysis of system usage. This application interfaces
with a dedicated
Telemetry Store (I)
that will store data
about the use of the system while it runs.

Development.

Currently, software engineers, data scientists, and machine learning engineers
can access the
Ocean Guard
source code maintained in the centralized

Code Repository (C)
. Developers can easily modify the
source code by employing one of the pre-configured tools:

Integrated Development Environments (IDEs) (4)

for structured software engineering tasks,
and
Notebooks (5)
for interactive prototyping and analysis.
This approach accommodates the diverse workflows and preferences of a
multidisciplinary team.

Crontinuous Delivery.

Following modern software engineering practices, a
Continuous
Integration (CI) Pipeline (v)
is used to build and test code. After that, dedicated

Continuous Delivery (CD) Pipelines (VI)
deploy traditional
software-based components, whereas specialized
Continuous
Delivery of Machine Learning (CD4ML) Pipelines (VII)
do the same for ML-based
components. These pipelines interact with a centralized

Artifact Registry (D)
that maintains versioned software
components, containerized applications and serialized models, ensuring
reproducibility and traceability throughout the development cycle.

API Architecture

This section dives deeper into the architecture of the
Ocean Guard
API, which
corresponds to the
API Prediction Service (8)
in the
architecture presented in
section

3
.
The
Ocean Guard
API is the center of the ML-Enabled System, since it applies the
rule-based and machine-learning based anomaly detection models over
the data received by the system.

Figure 2:

Component Architecture of the
Ocean Guard
API.

It follows the hexagonal architecture
[
12
]
.
The dark blue hexagon represents the
Business Logic
,
where Domain Driven Design patterns such as

Entities
,
Value Objects
and
Services

[
4
]
are implemented.
The light blue hexagon represents the
Dependencies
,
where the
Ports
and
Adapters
pattern is implemented
as an Anti-Corruption Layer against external dependencies
[
12
]
.
Rectangles illustrate the external dependencies themselves, with
an orange rectangle representing a
Reading Dependency
and
and yellow rectangle representing an
Output Dependency
.
Lastly, circles represent the different
Ports
and

Adapters
themselves, one per dependency.
Solid arrows, going from a port to an adapter, represent the inside-out
dependency flow, going from the
Core
to
Ports
then

Adapters
.

Figure

2
represents the key components of the API
according to a
hexagonal architecture
, also known as
onion
or

clean architecture

[
12
]
.
This architectural pattern is designed to enhance flexibility and
maintainability of a system by ensuring that its core business logic remains
independent of external technologies.

According to the
hexagonal architecture
pattern, the system can be
divided into three key elements:
core
,
ports
and
adapters
.
The system follows a dependency rule: As one moves inward towards the core, the
level of abstraction and the scope of policy increases. Specifically, the deeper
layers of the architecture encapsulate higher-level policies and overarching
business rules, while the outer layers handle more concrete implementation
details. The core, being the innermost layer, represents the most general and
highest level of abstraction
[
12
]
.

Core.

Responsible for implementing the business logic. The
Ocean Guard
API follows the
patterns of Domain Driven Design (DDD)
[
4
]
:

entities
and
value objects
represent the key objects related
to the maritime domain. The
services
, also known as
use cases

[
12
]
, orchestrate the lifecycle of these
objects.

Ports.

Responsible for establishing the contract and managing the communication between
the core and the adapters. Within the
Ocean Guard
API, key components such as database
repositories, dependency injection, security mechanisms, and web routers are
defined as
ports
, facilitating the necessary integrations and ensuring
that external concerns do not directly impact the core business logic.

Adapters.

Responsible for communication with external dependencies, making key details
of the business logic. Adapters are categorized based on their dependencies:
reading and output. Within the
Ocean Guard
API,
reading
adapters include
the model adapter, third-party API adapter, storage adapter, database adapter,
and configuration adapter, whereas
output
adapters include the web
adapter and the cache adapter.

Team Configuration and Development Workflow

As a ML-enabled system, developing the
Ocean Guard
tool requires multiple specialists,
including software engineers, data scientists, and machine learning engineers

.

Table 1:

Ocean Guard

The

The project workflow is deliberately structured to minimize interdependency

Research Team.

It consists of Master students completing their theses. This team primarily
focuses on exploring state-of-the-art techniques in the maritime domain.
Although primarily research-oriented, their work directly contributes data
products to the API, particularly to the API Model Adapter.

Innovation Team.

It consists of technical trainees focused on the exploration and implementation
of state-of-the-practice techniques. This team employs rapid prototyping
methodologies within constrained timelines to deliver demonstrable improvements
to the API Model Adapter.

Core Development Team.

It consists of the authors of this paper focused on the fundamental aspects of
the project infrastructure, including back-end development and infrastructure
management. This team focuses on the API, databases, and model repository, with
particular emphasis on refining the central API that serves as the integration
point for all microservices.

UI Development.

It consist of hired web developers specializing in user interface design and
front-end development. This team focuses primarily on the WebApp, which will be
used by the end users for maritime tracking activities.

As the
Research
and
Innovation
teams consist of students and
trainees, they operate under specific academic deadlines. These external
constraints have been factored into the development process, further reinforcing
the decision to implement a parallel work approach.

6
Contract-based development

As an ML-enabled system with a microservice architecture, the
Ocean Guard
tool was a
complex system, as discussed in
section

3
. A recommended
modeling technique for systems with such an architecture is
Design by
Contract

[
7
]
: an approach that establishes clear
interfaces and agreements between software components or teams, defining how
they interact without requiring knowledge of each other’s internal
implementation
[
7
]
.

In the development of the
Ocean Guard
tool, contract-based development was
systematically implemented across the boundaries of interaction of
the four teams introduced at
section

5
.
These contracts served as formal specifications defining interaction patterns
without constraining the internal implementations
[
13
]
.

6.1
Code Contracts

For this project,
Code Contracts
document the expected behavior between
two services – owned by different teams – which interacting synchronously or
asynchronously via the network, usually under the HTTP protocol. As such, these
specifications include endpoints, request parameters, and expected response.

There are two main
code contracts
in the
Ocean Guard
tool:

•

Between
Data Crawlers (3)
and external data sources.
Creating these contracts provides the
Core Development
team
with an anti-corruption layer
[
4
]

against the external data sources. This allowing them to create tests
that verify the expected behavior of 3rd party APIs, which may change
without notice.

•

Between the
API Prediction Service (8)
and the

Web Application (8)
.
Creating this contract allows the
UI Development
team
to mock the responses of the
API Prediction Service (8)
.
This allows them to develop the front-end in parallel with the
back-end developed by the
Core Development
team.

6.2
Data Contracts

For this project,
Data Contracts
document the expected formats in a
data storage whose data is produced and consumed by services or pipelines
owned by different teams. As such, these specifications include types, formats,
distributions (when applicable or known) of data, besides the protocol for
reading and writing the data itself.

There are three main
data contracts
in the
Ocean Guard
tool:

•

At the
Label Store (A)
,
between the

3rd Party Applications (1)
and

Web Application (9)

that produce them, and the

Notebooks (5)
,

Synthetic Data Generation Pipeline (I)
and

Data Augmentation Pipeline (II)

that consume them.
These contracts ensure the
Research
and
Innovation
teams
know what to expect to process labels.

•

At the
Raw Data Store (B)
,
between the

3rd Party Applications (1)
,

Physical Sensors (2)
and

Data Crawlers (3)

that produce them, and the

Notebooks (5)
,

Synthetic Data Generation Pipeline (I)
,

Data Augmentation Pipeline (II)
and

Batch Prediction Pipeline (VIII)

that consume them.
These contracts ensure the
Research
and
Innovation
teams
know what to expect to process raw data, as well as what their models
might use during prediction.

•

At the
Data Store (E)
,
between the

Synthetic Data Generation Pipeline (I)
and

Data Augmentation Pipeline (II)

that produce them, and the

Rule-Based Training Pipeline (III)
and

ML-Based Training Pipeline (IV)
pipelines
that consume them.
These contracts ensure the
Research
and
Innovation
teams
know how to consume the post-processed raw data and labels created
by each other.

6.3
Model Contracts

For this project,
Model Contracts
document the expected input and output
of a rule-based or ML-based model, as well as the format on which a trained
model should be stored. As such, these specifications include data types,
file formats, and data scientist–defined hyperparameters.

There is one main
model contract
in the
Ocean Guard
tool,
at the
Model Store (B)
,
between the

Rule-Based Training Pipeline (III)
and

ML-Based Training Pipeline (IV)
pipelines
that create them, and both the

Batch Prediction Pipeline (VIII)
and

API Prediction Service (9)

that use them.
These contracts ensure the
Research
and
Innovation
teams
know what to create for the
Core Development
team to use in the

Ocean Guard
tool back-end.

7
Challenges

In the experience of the authors, there were three main challenges for the
development of the
Ocean Guard
tool:
coupling
,
alignment
, and

communication
.

Coupling.

As an ML-enabled system, the
Ocean Guard
tool was intrinsically complex, as
discussed in
section

3
. Any modification to one of its
components – either on code, model, or data – can quickly cascaded through
the system. Therefore, mitigating the drawbacks of coupling was an important
architectural challenge, particularly since services were being implemented
concurrently during development.

Alignment.

As an ML-enabled system, the
Ocean Guard
tool required specialized developers, as
discussed in
section

5
. Four teams worked simultaneously on
different components, integrated via the
Ocean Guard
API. Therefore, keeping all team
members oriented towards the same goal was an important organizational
challenge, particularly due to the time-consuming nature of coordination
activities.

Communication.

As an ML-enabled system with a microservices architecture, the
Ocean Guard
tool was
a complex system, as discussed in
section

3
. Therefore,
communicating the evolution of the
Ocean Guard
tool to internal and external
stakeholders – with diverse technical backgrounds – was an important project
management challenge, particularly since there were frequent feedback sessions
due to its agile development process.

8
Lessons Learned

The challenges described in
section

7
led to two lessons learned,
related to applying techniques from the microservices literature that can be
suitable for ML-enabled system development:
contract-based design
and

ubiquitous language
.

Contract-Based Design.

As previously mentioned in
section

6
, the authors
employed
design by contract

[
7
]
during the
development of the
Ocean Guard
tool, a recommended modeling technique for systems with
a microservices architecture
[
16
]
. This
approach facilitated the definition of clear and precise specifications between
components, setting expectations for interfaces and behaviors.

By applying design by contract through the development process, it tackled two
challenges above:
coupling
, by reducing potential integration issues and
improving system cohesion, and
alignment
, making it easier for the team
to discuss how to integrate the components of the
Ocean Guard
tool.

Ubiquitous Language.

As previously mentioned in
section

3
, the authors employed

Domain-Driven Design (DDD)

[
4
]
during
the development of the
Ocean Guard
tool, a recommended modeling technique for systems
with a microservices architecture
[
14
]
. This
approach facilitated the creation of a shared vocabulary between developers and
stakeholders, grounding discussions about the evolution of the system.

By refining a ubiquitous language through the development process, it tackled
two challenges above:
communication
, by deepening stakeholders’ and
developers’ understanding of the system and improving delivery feedback, and

alignment
, making it easier for the team to propose how to evolve the
components of the
Ocean Guard
tool.

9
Conclusions

As the digital transformation of the maritime industry has accelerated, ships
now function as floating data centers equipped with hundreds of sensors.

To extract actionable insights from this vast amount of data, this paper
describes the
Ocean Guard
tool: a Machine Learning-Enabled System (MLES) for anomaly
detection in the maritime domain. Its microservices architecture enables
multiple specialized teams to work on the project in parallel, facilitated by a
contract-based design approach.

Developing the
Ocean Guard
tool presented three major challenges:

•

coupling
,
related to cascading changes throughout the system;

•

alignment
,
related to coordinating four specialized teams working concurrently); and

•

communication
,
related to explaining the evolving system to stakeholders with diverse
technical backgrounds.

To address them, the authors applied two techniques from the microservices
literature during development:

•

contract-based design
,
which established clear specifications between components and reduced
integration issues; and

•

ubiquitous language
,
which created a shared vocabulary among all participants via the
application of Domain Driven Design (DDD).

Contract-based design
effectively mitigated
coupling
between
components and improved
alignment
among the team, whereas the

ubiquitous language
enhanced
communication
with stakeholders and
further strengthened
alignment
among the team.
Therefore, this case study in the maritime domain illustrates how established
software engineering practices can be successfully adapted to complex Machine
Learning-Enabled Systems.

Future work will focus on continuing the development of the
Ocean Guard
tool to ensure
all functional and non-functional requirements are met. The development process
will remain guided by the established contract-based approach and shared
ubiquitous language. Moreover, the developers will continue to explore and
implement both state-of-the-art and state-of-the-practice techniques with the

Research
and
Innovation
teams. These parallel exploration streams will enable the
Ocean Guard
tool to evolve with the latest advancements in maritime anomaly detection.
