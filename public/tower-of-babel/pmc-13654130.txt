Left ventricular function and volumes from cardiac magnetic resonance and gated [13N]-ammonia positron emission tomography myocardial perfusion imaging in patients with ischemia with no obstructive coronary arteries: a prospective head-to-head comparison using hybrid positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance
Authors: Runze Wen, Qiang Xie, Ming Ni, Bo Pan, Xingxing Zhu, Xueer Meng, Dan Li, Xuemei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectrocardiogram (ECG)-gated 13N-ammonia positron emission tomography (PET) myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) has been widely validated for the assessment of left ventricular (LV) volumes and function. This study aimed to compare the accuracy of LV volumes and functional parameters derived from 13N-ammonia PET with those obtained by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), using CMR as the reference standard.MethodsA total of 75 consecutive patients with known or suspected ischemia with no obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) who simultaneously underwent ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET and CMR using a hybrid PET/magnetic resonance (MR) scanner were enrolled in this prospective study. Left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and regional wall motion score (RWMS) were derived from both imaging modalities and compared using correlation and Bland-Altman (BA) analyses. Additionally, inter- and intraobserver variability in LV volumetric parameters and LVEF were evaluated for both imaging modalities.ResultsThe correlation between PET and CMR was strong for LV volumes (r>0.83) and moderate for LVEF (r=0.65). The moderate LVEF correlation may be partially explained by the narrow range of LVEF values across the study cohort. Moreover, the agreement between 13N-ammonia PET and CMR for LVEF was good, with minimal bias (−0.57%); however, 13N-ammonia PET systematically underestimated LV volumes, with negative biases for LVEDV (−7.76 mL), LVESV (−3.28 mL), and LVSV (−5.33 mL). Similar, strong correlations and good agreement were observed in patients with reduced myocardial flow reserve (MFR). For both PET and CMR, inter- and intraobserver agreement for LV volumes and LVEF was high and comparable between the two modalities. In addition, RWMSs derived from ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET and CMR also exhibited good agreement (kappa =0.84±0.02).ConclusionsLVEF and RWMS can be reliably and accurately quantified by ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET, although LV volumes are systematically underestimated compared with those quantified by CMR. ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET may serve as a complementary modality for the assessment of LV systolic function; however, CMR remains the benchmark for LV volumetric measurement and myocardial tissue assessment.Keywords: Positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance (PET/MR), ischemia and no obstructive coronary arteries, left ventricle volume, left ventricle wall motion

Introduction

Accurate, precise, and reproducible quantification of left ventricular (LV) function and volumes is critical in the clinical management and risk stratification of cardiac diseases, as well as in the detection of potential cardiotoxicity related to pharmacotherapy or radiotherapy (1,2). In clinical practice, echocardiography is the most widely used modality for the evaluation of these parameters, while cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is considered the reference standard due to its excellent ability to distinguish myocardial tissue from the blood pool and lung parenchyma, as well as its high reproducibility for LV volume quantification (3). Nuclear cardiology also offers several validated approaches for the assessment of cardiac that perform comparably to CMR. Electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) is widely used for the evaluation of LV function and myocardial perfusion (4,5). Cardiac positron emission tomography (PET) with various radiotracers is well validated for the non-invasive evaluation of absolute myocardial perfusion, glucose metabolism, and myocardial viability (6). Notably, precise quantitative data on myocardial blood flow (MBF) and myocardial flow reserve (MFR) can be acquired with 13N-ammonia PET, and these indices have been well established as critical diagnostic and prognostic markers in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) (7). Compared with SPECT, 13N-ammonia PET myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) provides higher spatial resolution and an improved signal-to-noise ratio with lower radiation exposure and may thus be well suited for the quantification of LV volumes and functional parameters (8).

Further, distinct clinical phenotypes are associated with different LV remodeling patterns, highlighting the clinical importance of such classification. For example, left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) is the key metric used to distinguish between concentric and eccentric LV remodeling patterns, while left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) or left ventricular systolic function is considered a strong independent predictor of cardiac mortality (9,10). The distinction between heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is considered clinically critical, as well-established, evidence-based therapies are available to reduce morbidity and mortality in patients with HFrEF. Conversely, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and finerenone are currently the only approved disease-modifying therapies available for HFpEF (11,12). Notably, myocardial ischemia with no obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) is increasingly regarded as a major contributor to long-term adverse cardiovascular events such as HFpEF (13). In patients with INOCA, coronary microvascular ischemia has been linked to endothelial dysfunction and subsequent diastolic dysfunction, potentially contributing to the development of HFpEF (14). Women with INOCA have been reported to have an elevated risk of major adverse cardiovascular events, most commonly HFpEF (14). Age-adjusted LVEF has been shown to be significantly lower in patients with INOCA and HFpEF than in reference controls, further emphasizing the high clinical value of precise, accurate, and reproducible quantification of LVEF and LV volumes for cardiovascular risk stratification (15).

To date, most head-to-head comparisons of ECG-gated PET and CMR for LV functional assessment using hybrid PET/magnetic resonance (MR) systems have been conducted in broad cohorts of patients with suspected or confirmed obstructive CAD. In contrast, research on patients with INOCA remains limited, despite the established prognostic importance of LV function and remodeling in this population, which is at increased risk of HFpEF. Additionally, evaluating reproducibility presents inherent challenges, as temporal changes in LV function may introduce confounding when imaging examinations are performed sequentially (16). Simultaneous image acquisition using a hybrid PET/MR scanner minimizes temporal variations in measurements and allows variability related to readers or analytical software to be assessed. This study aimed to conduct a head-to-head comparison of LV volumes and functional parameters and to explore differences in the reproducibility of these parameters derived from ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET and CMR acquired simultaneously using a hybrid PET/MR scanner in patients with INOCA. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0773/rc).

Methods

Study design and population

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (No. 2024KYER-232), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Consecutive eligible patients with INOCA, aged 18 to 75 years, who underwent rest/stress 13N-ammonia PET/MR scans at The First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China between August 2024 and March 2026 were prospectively enrolled in the study. Of the 84 potentially eligible patients, nine were excluded from the study, including three with claustrophobia, two with a history of contrast allergy, one with a metallic implant, and three with insufficient image quality due to severe arrhythmias or poor ECG gating. The final study cohort comprised 75 prospectively recruited patients. No gadolinium-based contrast agent was used in this study; however, a history of contrast allergy was retained as an exclusion criterion in accordance with institutional safety screening protocols for all hybrid PET/MR examinations. Flow diagram of the study cohort. INOCA, ischemia with no obstructive coronary arteries; MR, magnetic resonance; PET, positron emission tomography.

Patients who met the consensus diagnostic criteria for INOCA were enrolled in this study. The criteria included clinical evidence or suspicion of myocardial ischemia in the absence of obstructive epicardial coronary artery stenosis, as confirmed by prior coronary angiography (CAG) or coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA). Known or suspected ischemia was operationally defined as the presence of typical or atypical angina, exertional dyspnea, or abnormal findings on prior non-invasive stress tests, including stress electrocardiography, stress echocardiography, and myocardial perfusion scintigraphy.

Of the entire cohort, 42 patients (56.0%) underwent CAG and 33 (44.0%) underwent CCTA for anatomical assessment of the coronary arteries. All coronary imaging studies were performed within 3 months before the PET/MR scan, with a median interval of 28 days (interquartile range, 14–45 days). Invasive coronary function testing was not routinely performed in this study population. Only six patients (8.0%) underwent additional invasive physiological assessment at the time of CAG, and none showed hemodynamically significant epicardial stenosis. All included patients underwent 13N-ammonia PET/MR for further evaluation of coronary microvascular function following CAG or CCTA.

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (I) non-obstructive CAD, defined as no significant stenosis or <50% diameter stenosis on CAG or CCTA; (II) clinical suspicion or objective evidence of myocardial ischemia as defined above; and (III) availability of high-quality, interpretable PET/MR data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) obstructive CAD, defined as luminal stenosis ≥50% on CAG or CCTA; (II) a history of percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting; (III) structural heart disease, including clinically significant myocarditis, primary or secondary cardiomyopathy, and moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease; (IV) a diagnosis of malignant neoplasm; (V) an inability to provide written informed consent; (VI) acute coronary syndrome [as defined by the American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology guidelines (17)], including myocardial infarction (MI) within 3 months before study enrollment, or any history of MI or other major adverse cardiac events within 6 months before study enrollment; (7) contraindications to CMR (including implanted metallic devices or claustrophobia) or contraindications to regadenoson (including severe asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bradyarrhythmias, or systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg); and/or (8) severe arrhythmias or poor ECG gating quality that precluded accurate quantification of LV volumes and function.

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), body surface area, blood pressure, heart rate (HR), and cardiac risk factors such as smoking history, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, family history of CAD, and concomitant medication use.

Image acquisition

As illustrated in the schematic of the imaging protocol (Figure 2), all participants underwent ECG-gated PET/MR imaging. All imaging data were acquired simultaneously using a Biograph mMR PET/MR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Patients were instructed to refrain from caffeine-containing products for at least 24 hours, to fast for a minimum of 6 hours, and to withhold beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and nitrates for at least 24 hours before the imaging examination.

13N-ammonia PET/MR acquisition protocol. MR, magnetic resonance; MRAC, magnetic resonance attenuation correction; PET, positron emission tomography.

PET acquisition

All participants underwent both rest and regadenoson-induced stress hyperemia MPI, with an interval of approximately 60 minutes between the two acquisitions. Rest PET scans were acquired initially, followed by stress PET scans during regadenoson-induced vasodilatory stress. For rest PET imaging, approximately 370 MBq (10 mCi) of 13N-ammonia was administered via intravenous (IV) injection, followed by a 5-mL normal saline flush. A 10-minute three-dimensional list-mode dynamic scan was then initiated. Stress PET scans were acquired using the same parameters as those described above for rest perfusion imaging, except that regadenoson was administered 20–30 seconds before the IV injection of 13N-ammonia (370 MBq). A single IV bolus of regadenoson (400 µg) was delivered over <10 s via an antecubital venous cannula, followed immediately by a 5-mL saline flush.

PET image reconstruction was performed from list-mode PET datasets using a three-dimensional attenuation-weighted ordered-subset expectation maximization iterative algorithm with six iterations and 21 subsets, a 4-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian smoothing filter, an image matrix size of 172×172, an in-plane voxel size of 2.0 mm × 2.0 mm, and an axial slice thickness of 2.0 mm (18). For attenuation correction of the PET datasets, an attenuation map was derived from a two-point Dixon sequence acquired at the initiation of PET data acquisition during an end-expiratory breath-hold (19). The list-mode datasets were subsequently resampled to generate both ECG-gated images (eight bins per cardiac cycle) and non-gated dynamic images (19 frames over 10 minutes: 10 s ×12, 30 s ×2, 60 s ×4, and 3 min ×1).

CMR acquisition

Cardiac cine images were acquired using an ECG-gated balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence during an end-expiratory breath-hold. Full LV coverage was achieved with a stack of contiguous short-axis slices with no interslice gap, extending from the mitral valve annulus to the apical tip, along with standard two-chamber and four-chamber long-axis views (20). The imaging parameters were as follows: slice thickness, 8 mm; repetition time, 2.8 ms; echo time, 1.5 ms; flip angle, 67º; field of view, 256 mm × 256 mm; matrix size, 192×109; reconstructed cardiac phases, 25 per R-R interval; and mean temporal resolution, approximately 45 ms.

Imaging analysis

PET imaging analysis

All LV volumes and functional parameters were derived exclusively from the resting ECG-gated PET dataset, which was reconstructed into eight bins per cardiac cycle from the list-mode raw data. PET imaging datasets were analyzed using commercially available software (Syngo MI Cardiology, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Left ventricular end-systolic volume (LVESV), LVEDV, and LVEF were automatically derived at end-diastole and end-systole using a validated model-based algorithm. Left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV) was calculated using the following formula: LVSV = LVEDV − LVESV. Valve plane localization and delineation of the endocardial and epicardial borders were performed automatically by the software. Manual adjustment of the automated contours and valve plane position was performed only when the initial automated delineation was deemed anatomically inaccurate. Such adjustment was required in approximately 20% of all cases, most commonly for refinement of the basal valve plane and apical border. These two anatomical regions contribute disproportionately to total ventricular volume, and even limited manual correction may introduce measurable inter- and intraobserver variability. All adjustments were restricted to minor repositioning of boundaries, and no extensive contour redrawing was performed.

Myocardial perfusion defect scores, including the summed stress score (SSS), summed rest score (SRS), and summed difference score (SDS), were automatically quantified according to the AHA 17-segment standard model for all rest and stress studies (21). The SDS was calculated by subtracting the SRS from the SSS. Perfusion findings were classified as normal (SSS ≤3) or abnormal (SSS >3) (22).

Rest and stress MBF values were computed for each coronary vascular territory by averaging the perfusion values across the corresponding myocardial segments. MFR was calculated as the ratio of stress MBF to rest MBF. For 13N-ammonia PET imaging, an MFR <2.0 was classified as abnormal based on a previously validated cutoff value for the diagnosis of hemodynamically significant coronary microvascular disease (CMVD) (7).

Resting regional wall motion score (RWMS) was assessed across 16 LV myocardial segments, with the apical cap excluded. A standard four-point scale was used, on which 0 represents normokinesia, 1 represents hypokinesia, 2 represents akinesia, and 3 represents dyskinesia. All abnormal scores were reviewed by two blinded readers by consensus to exclude false-positive findings due to motion artifacts or suboptimal cavity delineation.

To evaluate the interobserver variability of the method, all PET image analyses were conducted independently and in a blinded manner by two board-certified, experienced nuclear medicine physicians. To evaluate intraobserver variability, one physician repeated the blinded analysis of all imaging datasets. To minimize recognition bias, a 4–8 weeks time interval was maintained between the first and second intraobserver analyses.

CMR imaging analysis

All acquired CMR imaging datasets were fully de-identified and subsequently analyzed using dedicated cardiac post-processing software (CVI42 version 5.11.1, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Alberta, Canada). Endocardial and epicardial contours were automatically delineated in short-axis views at both the end-diastolic and end-systolic phases of the cardiac cycle, with manual correction in cases of suboptimal anatomic delineation. Manual adjustment was needed in approximately 15% of datasets, primarily for clarification of trabecular boundaries at the apical and basal segments. LVEDV, LVESV, LVSV, and LVEF were subsequently computed. Trabeculae and papillary muscles were incorporated in the LV blood pool volume. CMR datasets were independently evaluated by two experienced radiologists with specialized training in CMR, who were blinded to all PET imaging findings.

Resting regional wall motion analysis was performed using the same 16-segment model and apical cap exclusion as that used for PET to ensure direct comparability between modalities. The four-point scoring system described for ECG-gated PET was applied by consensus of twoexperienced radiologists, with all abnormal segments jointly adjudicated to exclude artifacts from respiratory motion or endocardial border misplacement.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages, and means ± standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables. Correlations between LVEF and LV volumes measured by PET and CMR were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and linear regression analysis. LV volumes and LVEF obtained from 13N-ammonia PET and CMR were compared using paired Student’s t-tests after assessing the normality of the data distribution. Agreement was assessed using Bland-Altman (BA) analysis with calculation of the mean bias and 95% Bland-Altman limits of agreement (BA-LA), as well as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Inter- and intraobserver variability were assessed using a two-way random-effects, average-measures ICC for agreement and BA analysis. Cohen’s kappa statistic was used to assess the agreement between 13N-ammonia PET and CMR for regional wall motion analysis. For subgroup analysis, patients were stratified into two groups based on normal (non-CMVD group) or abnormal global MFR (CMVD group). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

LV volumes and ejection fraction

ECG-gated PET analysis was time-efficient, requiring no more than a few minutes per patient. Among all patients with INOCA, the mean LVEF measured by 13N-ammonia PET (60.56%±7.39%) was lower than that obtained by CMR (61.13%±7.18%), but the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.420). A moderate correlation between the two imaging modalities was observed (r=0.65; P<0.0001), with a minimal negative bias (−0.57%) and a narrow BA-LA (−12.51% to 11.37%). Additionally, the mean LVEDV measured by 13N-ammonia PET (115.21±22.41 mL) was significantly lower than that measured by CMR (122.96±23.36 mL, P<0.0001). Similarly, the mean LVESV measured by 13N-ammonia PET (45.29±12.60 mL) was significantly lower than that measured by CMR (48.56±13.78 mL, P<0.0001) (Figure 4). Excellent correlations were observed between the two imaging modalities for LVEDV (r=0.95, P<0.0001) and LVESV (r=0.89, P<0.0001); however, 13N-ammonia PET underestimated LV volumes compared with CMR, yielding wider BA-LAs for LVEDV (−22.16 to 6.65 mL, mean bias of −7.76 mL) and LVESV (−15.40 to 8.85 mL, mean bias of −3.28 mL). Inter-modality ICCs derived from a two-way random-effects absolute agreement model further supported these findings, demonstrating good to excellent agreement across all LV volumes and functional parameters (Table S1).

Regional wall motion analysis

In total, 1,200 LV regional wall motion segments were evaluated. With CMR, 854 (71.2%) segments were classified as normokinetic, 202 (16.8%) as hypokinetic, 132 (11.0%) as akinetic, and 12 (1.0%) as dyskinetic; with 13N-ammonia PET, 849 (70.8%) segments were classified as normokinetic, 210 (17.5%) as hypokinetic, 128 (10.7%) as akinetic, and 13 (1.1%) as dyskinetic (Table 4). All wall motion assessments were derived exclusively from resting image acquisitions. The majority of abnormal wall motion segments were located in territories showing reduced MFR and mild resting perfusion defects, suggesting subclinical segmental systolic impairment secondary to coronary microvascular dysfunction. Given the exclusion of prior MI and structural heart disease from the study cohort, scar-mediated wall motion abnormalities were considered unlikely. As no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was included in this study protocol, definitive assessment of focal myocardial fibrosis was not performed. Overall agreement was 92.5% (1,110/1,200), with a kappa value of 0.84±0.02. Correlation analysis of RWMSs further revealed a statistically significant correlation (r=0.89, P<0.001).

Discussion

In this prospective single-center study, good agreement was observed between 13N-ammonia PET and CMR for the assessment of LV volumes, LVEF, and regional RWMSs in patients with INOCA. ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET enabled quantitative assessment of LV volumes and LVEF, with overall acceptable agreement with the CMR reference standard, despite systematic underestimation of volumes and relatively wide limits of agreement for ejection fraction. Similar results were observed in the subgroup analyses. RWMSs derived from gated 13N-ammonia PET and CMR also showed good agreement. The present study supports the use of ECG-gated 13N-ammonia PET as a reliable imaging modality for the quantification of LVEF and abnormal RWMS, both of which have been validated as critical prognostic markers for clinical management and therapeutic decision-making in clinical practice (23,24). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate LV volumes, LVEF, and RWMS in patients with INOCA using a hybrid PET/MR device, with CMR serving as the reference standard. This study extends previous findings from general CAD populations to a clinically important and growing patient group with specific risk stratification needs.

Clinically, these findings are directly relevant to patients with INOCA who are at elevated risk of HFpEF and long-term adverse events. The good agreement for LVEF and RWMS supports the use of gated 13N-ammonia PET as a reliable one-stop assessment of both coronary microvascular function and LV contractility, which is particularly valuable for patients with contraindications to CMR. The systematic underestimation of absolute LV volumes by PET carries important clinical implications. Absolute volume thresholds established with CMR as the reference standard cannot be directly applied to PET measurements, as such practice would lead to misclassification of ventricular remodeling patterns. Nevertheless, the strong linear correlation and consistent magnitude of bias across the cohort indicate that serial PET measurements may remain useful for monitoring longitudinal changes in ventricular dimensions during clinical follow-up. For clinical scenarios in which precise absolute volume values are required to guide threshold-based decisions, CMR remains the preferred reference standard. These data support the integration of gated functional analysis into routine PET perfusion workflows for INOCA, thereby streamlining diagnostic evaluation.

Evaluation of LV volumes and LVEF using hybrid PET/MR has been shown to have excellent reproducibility in several studies, most of which included broad cohorts of patients with suspected or known CAD. Maurer et al. (15) documented strong correlations between LV volumes and LVEF measured by 13N-ammonia PET and CMR in 51 patients. Minimal bias was observed for LVEF and global LV function, as well as for LVESV and LVEDV; however, the authors identified marked underestimation of LV volumes derived from gated 13N-ammonia PET, which is consistent with our findings. Notably, bSSFP cine sequences are regarded as the optimal technique for quantitative assessment of cardiac function. The ability of these sequences to generate well-defined endocardial boundaries is attributable to their excellent intrinsic blood-pool-to-myocardium contrast and high signal-to-noise ratio. Given that these sequences are susceptible to artifacts from respiratory motion and steady-state magnetization disruption, image acquisition is typically carried out during end-expiratory breath-hold. However, Kiko et al. (3) used free-breathing cine acquisition, administered a lower dose of radiotracer, and obtained ECG-gated PET data with 16 bins instead of eight bins. Additionally, a previous study (25) revealed that the correlation between 15O-water PET and CMR was robust for LV volumes but relatively modest for LVEF (r=0.52), which is consistent with our results. This observation is unsurprising given the narrow distribution of LVEF values. The present study exclusively included patients with suspected INOCA and no history of MI, and was thus restricted to patients with preserved LV systolic function.

Agreement between 13N-ammonia PET and CMR for LV volumes and LVEF was high; however, inter‑modality differences in the measured values were observed in the BA plots. The significant underestimation of LV volumes derived from gated 13N-ammonia PET was consistent with previous studies that conducted head‑to‑head comparisons between gated fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) PET and CMR (26,27). Conversely, Schaefer et al. (28) reported a non-signiﬁcant trend toward the overestimation of LVESV and the underestimation of LVEDV in 30 patients when 18F-FDG PET was directly compared with CMR. Riemer et al. (27) reported a similar trend in 38 patients diagnosed with chronic CAD in their comparison of gated 18F-FDG PET and CMR. Based on these previous findings, 18F-FDG PET showed substantially less underestimation of LV volumes or no underestimation at all. One potential explanation for this finding is the substantially higher spatial resolution of 18F compared with 13N, which may enable more accurate delineation of LV endocardial and epicardial borders (29).

Several technical differences in image acquisition and processing may account for the observed systematic volume underestimation and varying agreement across parameters. Spatial resolution and voxel size differed substantially between the two modalities. CMR was acquired with an in-plane resolution of approximately 1.3 mm × 2.3 mm and a slice thickness of 8 mm, while reconstructed PET images had an in-plane voxel size of 2.0 mm × 2.0 mm and a system spatial resolution of approximately 4 mm after smoothing. The lower effective resolution of PET may have resulted in partial volume effects at the blood-myocardium interface, which may have blurred the endocardial borders, leading to systematic volume underestimation. Temporal resolution and gating design also contributed to inter-modality differences. Twenty-five cardiac phases were reconstructed for CMR cine imaging with a mean temporal resolution of 45 ms, whereas gated PET data were sorted into only eight bins per cardiac cycle. The lower number of gating frames may have reduced the precision of end-diastolic and end-systolic phase identification, which may partly explain the wider limits of agreement for LVEF.

In addition, the respiratory conditions during acquisition were not identical. CMR cine sequences were obtained during end-expiratory breath-hold to minimize cardiac motion, while PET data were collected during free breathing over 10 minutes. Residual respiratory motion during PET acquisition may have affected border delineation and volumetric quantification. Finally, the segmentation algorithms followed distinct principles. CMR analysis relied on edge detection with high blood-myocardium contrast, and papillary muscles were classified as part of the blood pool. Gated PET algorithms used a model-based approach incorporating tracer uptake distribution, and papillary muscles were often included in the myocardial wall. These inherent differences in segmentation approaches may have led to systematic discrepancies in measured cavity volumes.

In addition, the number of cardiac gating bins differed between the two imaging modalities. This difference may have affected imaging quality and the accuracy of myocardial border delineation. A reduction in the number of cardiac gating bins increases uncertainty in the identification of end-diastole and end-systole phases, potentially impairing the accuracy of volume measurements at these cardiac phases. Kumita et al. (30) demonstrated that a reduction in gating frame rate from 32 frames to 16 and eight frames resulted in lower LVEDV, higher LVESV, and significantly reduced LVEF. The accuracy of LVEF quantification may be further influenced by patient and respiratory motion; however, proper patient instructions may help mitigate these effects.

The PET analysis method employed in the present study was fully automated and could be incorporated as a supplementary module into routine quantification of MBF and MFR, with only a minimal increase in post-processing time. Gated data processing required no more than a few minutes, a duration that is comparable to or shorter than that of conventional CMR post-processing. This approach could thus be readily integrated into the clinical workflow of 13N-ammonia PET assessments. Moreover, hybrid PET/MR may minimize measurement bias related to HR and blood pressure, as hemodynamic measurements from PET and CMR are acquired simultaneously under the same conditions. These advantages may account for our finding of no inter-modality differences in RWMS within individual patients. Consistent with our findings, Kiko et al. (3) reported a similar kappa coefficient (0.82±0.02) for RWMS assessments between gated 13N-ammonia PET and CMR, using a four-point grading scale in a comparable study population.

Beyond volumetric and regional wall motion analysis, more refined quantification of myocardial deformation can be achieved from gated PET datasets via feature tracking techniques. Two recent studies conducted on the same hybrid PET/MR platform have validated PET-derived LV and right ventricular strain measurements against CMR reference standards, demonstrating good correlation and acceptable agreement for global longitudinal and circumferential strain (31,32). These methodologically comparable studies show that simultaneous PET/MR acquisition can support comprehensive multi-dimensional assessment of cardiac function in a single-scan protocol. The volumetric and wall motion findings presented in this study complement these advanced strain analyses by establishing the reliability of basic functional metrics in patients with INOCA, and provide a methodological basis for future exploration of myocardial deformation in this population.

Combined CCTA with stress computed tomography myocardial perfusion provides an additional non-invasive approach for integrated anatomical and functional coronary assessment. Recent studies have demonstrated the diagnostic and risk-stratification value of this combined protocol across a range of clinical settings, including the functional evaluation of myocardial bridges and the comprehensive assessment of moderate CAD in real-world practice (33,34). Despite these advances, the diagnostic performance of computed tomography myocardial perfusion for CMVD is less extensively validated than that of PET. Further, absolute MBF quantification with this technique has not been as thoroughly established in patients with INOCA.

In clinical practice, MPI using SPECT and PET provides critical diagnostic and prognostic information for patients with CAD (35,36). Changes in LV volumes and function following vasodilator or exercise stress, such as transient ischemic dilatation (TID), are widely recognized as specific indicators of severe diffuse CAD, and also serve as independent predictors of adverse cardiovascular events (37). Gated 13N-ammonia PET has the advantage of simultaneously integrating quantitative myocardial perfusion and contractile function assessment, without the need for additional scan time or radiotracer administration. Accordingly, reliable assessment of cardiac function using gated 13N-ammonia PET, without the need for additional imaging modalities, will significantly reduce the examination time and associated costs of revascularization examination. Compared with PET, CMR represents the current reference standard for the assessment of LV function and serves as the reference method for the quantification of ventricular geometry. In routine clinical practice, CMR also enables comprehensive assessment of myocardial viability via LGE imaging, although this sequence was not part of the present study protocol (38). However, CMR does not currently enable reliable and accurate quantification of absolute MBF and is also unsuitable for some patients with implanted electrical devices.

Several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, this single-center study included a modest sample size and may thus be statistically underpowered. Second, manual adjustments were required for both 13N-ammonia PET and CMR when automated LV border detection was suboptimal. These operator-dependent adjustments may have influenced the assessment of LV volumes and functional parameters. Third, gated PET imaging was acquired using only eight bins; thus, the potential effect of 16-bin acquisition on quantitative accuracy was not evaluated. Notably, the present study supports the clinical utility of 13N-ammonia PET for the evaluation of LV volumes and function, extending its well-established role in the quantification of myocardial ischemia and MBF. However, the study population was limited to patients with suspected or known INOCA and preserved LVEF. Thus, further studies are necessary in other patient groups, including those with reduced systolic function. Moreover, the quantification of LV volumes and LVEF was performed exclusively at rest. Evaluation of LVEDV and LVEF during pharmacological or exercise stress may provide novel insights into TID or LV dysfunction, both of which represent established indicators of advanced cardiac pathology. However, stress imaging is associated with technical difficulties related to increased atrioventricular plane displacement and patient motion artifacts. Therefore, associations between gated PET and CMR measurements under stress conditions remain unknown. Further evaluation is needed to assess the utility of this method during stress imaging. Finally, multiple factors constrain the generalizability of the study findings. The results may not be directly extrapolated to patients with dilated ventricles, severe LV dysfunction, or clinically significant arrhythmias, as these conditions were not represented in the study cohort. In addition, the conclusions are limited to 13N-ammonia PET processed using the described reconstruction protocol and analysis software, and cannot be directly extended to other radiotracers or software platforms.

Conclusions

In patients with INOCA, gated 13N-ammonia PET demonstrated favorable agreement with CMR across a wide range of LV volumes, LVEF, and RWMS, despite small systematic differences between the modalities. Gated 13N-ammonia PET may thus provide precise and clinically relevant information for the reliable and reproducible evaluation of LV volumes and systolic function in patients with INOCA.
