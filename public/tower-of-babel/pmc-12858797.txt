Artificial intelligence technology for the ethical issues research from a Marxist perspective under deep learning
Authors: Enrui Zhang, Dan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgainst the backdrop of artificial intelligence (AI) deep learning–driven recommendation systems increasingly shaping consumer decision-making, this study examines how algorithms may inadvertently reinforce consumer alienation and inequity from a Marxist critical perspective. To systematically uncover the mechanisms underlying the divergence between user preference representation and situational demand, this study integrates graph neural networks (GNNs) with a context-adaptive Transformer architecture, constructing a multidimensional ethical analysis framework using the MovieLens and LDOS-CoMoDa datasets. Using the million-scale user–movie interaction data from MovieLens, GNNs model the complex user–item relational network, capturing group preference biases inherent in collaborative filtering (CF). Simultaneously, leveraging the twelve fine-grained situational dimensions unique to the LDOS-CoMoDa dataset, a context-aware Transformer model analyzes how dynamic situational factors modulate consumer decision-making. By quantifying the mismatch between “exchange value” (predicted ratings) and “use value” (actual situational needs) in recommendation outputs, a Consumer Alienation Index (CAI) is constructed, and adversarial fairness constraints are applied to optimize model parameters. Experimental results show that the proposed model, through the integration of GNNs and context-aware Transformer, reduces CAI by 29.9%. Additional experiments indicate that neglecting “peer” contextual information leads to a 22.3% mismatch in recommendation value, while the CAI variance for low-activity user groups decreases from 0.041 to 0.012. These findings highlight the critical role of situational embedding and fairness constraints in mitigating algorithmic bias. Deep learning–based recommendation systems must incorporate contextual value embeddings and algorithmic constraints grounded in Marxist ethical principles. Doing so can break the cycle of “false demand” reproduction and provide theoretical and methodological support for developing trustworthy AI that fosters holistic human development.Keywords: Marxist ethics, AI recommendation systems, Consumer alienation, Context-aware modeling, Algorithmic fairnessSubject terms: Mathematics and computing; Science, technology and society

Introduction

Research background and motivations

With the rapid advancement of digital technologies, artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning are increasingly permeating every aspect of human society. Recommendation systems have become indispensable tools for information acquisition and consumer decision-making. In particular, on social media platforms, e-commerce websites, and streaming services, deep collaborative filtering (CF)–based recommendation algorithms are reshaping user behavior and consumption patterns at an unprecedented pace1,2. Beneath this technological logic lies an implicit value orientation: by analyzing historical data, predicting user behavior, and pushing content or products, these systems guide users toward certain “highly relevant” or “highly rated” choices3,4. In this process, consumer preferences are no longer independently generated; instead, they are continuously reinforced, replicated, and even distorted under algorithmic control, gradually diverging from the users’ authentic situational needs5,6. This phenomenon exemplifies the Marxist critique of consumer alienation, in which individuals progressively lose their intrinsic understanding of personal needs in consumption, becoming passive subjects shaped by the intertwined logics of technology and capital7.

More critically, this study argues that such alienation does not simply remain at the level of a basic opposition between “exchange value” and “use value,” but further manifests as what Lukács describes as “reification.” Within the digital consumption cycle of “demand–production–consumption,” algorithms solidify the user’s rich and dynamic subjectivity into static, computable “preference labels.” User data thus becomes an externalized force that exists independently of the user and, in turn, exerts control over them. By continually reinforcing “popular preferences,” the algorithm imposes ideological influence and replaces authentic needs with singular, capital-driven “false needs.” To break this ideological closed loop, this study introduces a key evaluative metric—the Consumer Alienation Index (CAI). The CAI is designed to quantify the divergence between the algorithm’s predicted “exchange value” and the user’s context-based perception of “use value.” It functions as an assessment tool and a critical bridge linking Marxist ethical critique with deep learning algorithm optimization.

Consumer alienation manifests not only in the mismatch between user preferences and actual needs but also in the erosion of fairness within technological systems. Deep recommendation models tend to amplify “popular preferences,” resulting in highly concentrated resource allocation and limiting the visibility and responsiveness to marginalized groups or non-mainstream demands8. From a Marxist perspective, such preference mechanisms reinforce existing power structures, orienting recommendation systems toward an “average rational consumer” while neglecting human complexity and social dimensions9,10. Particularly in the context of increasingly digitalized and platform-mediated consumption, recommendation systems influence not only individual choices but also broader patterns of cultural reproduction11. Without ethical reflection, these effects risk systematically undermining human freedom, dignity, and developmental rights.

Given the techno-value conflicts inherent in recommendation systems’ influence over consumption preferences and daily decision-making, a systematic analysis of the ethical issues underlying deep learning algorithms is necessary from a Marxist critical perspective. This study does not focus on the efficiency of the technology itself. Instead, it examines how its application may inadvertently reproduce social inequities, manipulate cognition, and mislead users regarding their actual needs. In this context, the study integrates graph neural networks (GNNs) with a context-adaptive Transformer to identify and quantify the mechanisms that generate consumer alienation and inequity. The study further aims to construct ethical constraint models, ensuring that recommendation systems maintain performance while aligning with human-centered, holistic development–oriented values.

Research objectives

This study aims to investigate, from a Marxist ethical perspective, the underlying issues of consumer alienation and fairness deficiencies embedded in deep-learning-based recommender systems. By integrating GNNs with a context-adaptive Transformer model, the study seeks to systematically represent the misalignment between algorithmically inferred user preferences and users’ genuine contextual needs. Specifically, the focus extends beyond optimizing recommendation performance to examining the technological role in shaping social relations and individual consciousness. Through constructing and refining the CAI, this study endeavors to establish an AI recommendation paradigm that balances technical rationality with ethical values. In doing so, the study aims to open a critical fissure within the reifying logic of algorithmic systems and restore users’ contextualized and situated subjective needs.

Literature review

Ethical research on AI has been steadily advancing. Amid the reshaping of social structures and individual behaviors by AI deep learning systems, Peters and Besley12 introduced the concept of “post-digital critical philosophy.” They argued that, in an era of pervasive digital technology, philosophy had to refocus on human subjectivity and value judgment. Technology should not have been treated as a neutral tool; its embedded ideological structures had to be critically examined12. This perspective provides a critical framework for AI ethics research. From a Marxist philosophical standpoint, Lan and Shu13 explored breakthroughs in the philosophy of mind within AI conceptualization. They emphasized that technological rationality should serve human emancipation rather than exacerbate alienation. Their analysis highlighted the need to restore essential human agency in AI development13.

Efe14 focused on the digital divide and labor exploitation driven by AI, noting that technological acceleration had not promoted equitable sharing but had further excluded marginalized groups from algorithmic systems. He revealed the latent mechanisms of class reproduction embedded in AI from a social-structural perspective14. Paci15 emphasized the “hidden domains” in AI production, arguing that mainstream ethical studies often overlooked the real labor relations and technological governance logic behind algorithms. He advocated expanding ethical perspectives to encompass AI’s economic foundations and ideological attributes15.

Fan16 suggested that incorporating Marxist network management theory into bioethics education could foster a collectivist, historical-materialist approach to technology governance16. Ricaurte17, adopting a “majority world” perspective, criticized current AI ethical standards as serving minority power structures dominated by global capital, while neglecting the discursive agency of the Global South. He argued that ethical standards themselves constituted a form of technological power17. Santoni de Sio et al.18 noted that AI was reshaping labor freedom, justice, and capital relations, and proposed that ethical frameworks should consider social justice and structural inequality18.

Hou19 proposed three ethical constraints for AI development—value, emancipation, and responsibility—arguing that technology could not evolve independently of societal objectives and had to be closely aligned with human freedom and collective well-being19. Muldoon and Wu20, in their critique of AI within the “colonial power matrix,” highlighted that AI design and deployment were strongly influenced by colonial histories and global inequality structures, extending AI ethics into the realm of structural power critique20. Maassen et al.21, through surveys of German physicians, found that user trust and expectations in medical AI were constrained by the technology’s limited understanding of context, revealing the algorithmic obscuration of complex human needs21. Schlagwein and Willcocks22 raised ethical concerns regarding the use of generative AI in research, noting potential knowledge-alienation effects caused by automation and knowledge restructuring22.

Although prior research has established a solid theoretical foundation and elevated AI ethics from technical regulation to philosophical critique and socio-political analysis, two major gaps remain. (1) There is a lack of empirical studies that embed ethical critique directly into the architecture of deep learning models. Most ethical discussions remain at the normative level and have not been translated into concrete algorithmic constraints. (2) Existing fairness metrics primarily emphasize statistical equality (e.g., demographic parity) but fail to integrate technical modeling with Marxist value analysis. Consequently, current approaches lack an operational framework capable of quantifying “alienation,” a deeper socio-psychological phenomenon. Recent scholarship has begun addressing the divide between Marxist structural critique and the cognitive dimensions of AI. While traditional Marxist analyses focus largely on economic alienation, the algorithmic era introduces a new form of “epistemic displacement.” As Shin23 argues in Minds, Machines, and Misinformation: Decoding Bias, Algorithms, and Trust23, algorithmic systems generate not only distributive inequalities but also profound epistemic distortions. By curating users’ information environments, these systems shape the very standards by which credibility and value are judged, effectively “colonizing” users’ cognition through algorithmic epistemology. This process creates a form of cognitive alienation, wherein users surrender interpretive autonomy to machine logic. Shin’s notion of “cognitive transparency” suggests that fairness metrics must move beyond statistical parity to uncover these concealed interpretive layers. Building on this perspective, the present study extends Marxist critique from the economic misalignment of “exchange value” to the epistemic misalignment of user needs. The goal is to restore users’ interpretive agency through context-aware constraints. Guided by Marxist ethical principles, this study develops a technical–value alignment model that integrates GNNs with a context-aware Transformer architecture. Unlike previous studies, the core contribution of this work lies not only in empirically examining consumer alienation and fairness deficits in recommender systems but also in introducing the CAI as both an evaluation metric and an optimization objective. CAI transforms Marxist qualitative critiques—particularly the masking of “real needs”—into a computable quantitative loss function. Combined with adversarial constraint mechanisms, it aims to mitigate cognitive alienation at the algorithmic level and restore users’ interpretive autonomy. This approach offers a novel pathway for aligning deep learning–based recommender systems with ethical values grounded in Marxist theory.

The operationalization of Marx’s theory of alienation was reflected in the construction of the CAI model, which was theoretically grounded in Marx’s distinction between “use value” (the utility derived from concrete consumption contexts) and “exchange value” (the market-oriented score predicted by the system). This qualitative critique was translated into the following quantitative indicators: (1) Representation of exchange value: the baseline prediction score denoted the system-imposed “mass taste” or “exchange value,” typically shaped by collaborative-filtering biases that favored high-traffic items over personally relevant ones. (2) Representation of use value: the context-aware score served as a proxy for “use value,” capturing the user’s genuine needs under specific contextual factors (e.g., emotion, companion). (3) Quantification of alienation: the deviation value mathematically captured the extent to which algorithmic recommendations (exchange value) suppressed the user’s contextual reality (use value). To ensure robustness, the weights in Eq. (12) were not arbitrarily assigned; instead, they were dynamically derived from the attention scores generated by the context-aware Transformer. This design ensured that context dimensions strongly influencing a user’s decision contributed more substantially to the alienation index, aligning the metric with the user’s cognitive focus rather than imposing static theoretical assumptions.

This study further expanded the notion of algorithmic fairness beyond technical metrics to encompass Rawls’s theory of justice, particularly the difference principle. On recommendation platforms, highly active users typically enjoyed a data advantage that yielded more accurate service, thereby reinforcing a “rich-get-richer” Matthew effect. The fairness constraint aimed to maximize the welfare of the least advantaged groups (low-activity users or users with marginalized interests), consistent with Rawls’s ethical requirement that social inequalities should benefit the least favored members of society. Additionally, the study acknowledged the epistemological limits of defining “true needs” purely through algorithmic means. Although context-aware data (e.g., emotion, companion) approximated users’ use value more closely than rating-only data, they still functioned as digitized proxies of lived experience. Thus, the CAI did not claim to capture users’ true needs in an ontological sense; rather, it served as a critical reference system that quantified the divergence between algorithmically produced “system rationality” and the user’s “lifeworld.”

Experimental design and performance evaluation

Datasets collection

This study employed the MovieLens-32 M dataset, which records 32,000,204 five-star rating interactions and 2,000,072 free-text tag applications from 200,948 anonymous users for 87,585 movies, spanning January 9, 1995, to October 12, 2023. All users were randomly selected and met a minimum activity threshold of rating at least 20 movies. The dataset does not include any demographic attributes; each user is represented by a unique identifier, ensuring strict privacy protection and anonymity. The LDOS-CoMoDa dataset contains 2,296 user–movie rating records, covering 30 fields that integrate user attributes, contextual dimensions, and movie features. This dataset is suitable for context-aware recommendation and ethical modeling research.

Although MovieLens and LDOS-CoMoDa are standard benchmark datasets, this study selected them based on their unique capacity to serve as proxies for socio-ethical structures. In particular, LDOS-CoMoDa provides twelve fine-grained contextual dimensions (e.g., “end of workday,” “emotional state,” “social companions”). From a Marxist perspective, these contextual features represent the material conditions of users’ existence (“use value”), which are often stripped away by standard CF algorithms that treat users as abstract data points.

To address potential sampling bias and missing data, a stratified sampling strategy based on user activity was applied. Missing contextual values in the sparse interaction matrix were handled using learnable “unknown” embeddings rather than zero-filling, preventing the model from misinterpreting absent social contexts as a lack of user preference. Users were explicitly categorized into “low-activity” (marginalized) and “high-activity” (dominant) groups according to interaction frequency (< 50 interactions vs. >200 interactions), enabling an empirical evaluation of the algorithm’s fairness in resource allocation.

Experimental environment and parameters setting

All experiments were conducted on Ubuntu 22.04, leveraging an NVIDIA A100 GPU and Intel Xeon Platinum CPU to construct a high-performance computing platform. The models were implemented using Python and the PyTorch deep learning framework. GNNs were developed with the DGL graph computation library, while the context-aware Transformer utilized the HuggingFace Transformers toolkit. The system is CUDA-compatible and enabled mixed-precision acceleration. Key hardware and software configurations are summarized in Table 1.

Key hyperparameters.

To ensure reproducibility and clarify model capacity, the GNN encoder employed a 2-layer GCN with a hidden dimension of 128. LeakyReLU activation functions (negative slope = 0.2) were used between layers to prevent the dying ReLU problem during the propagation of sparse user signals. The context-aware Transformer module consisted of two stacked encoder layers with a 4-head self-attention mechanism. Each contextual feature had an embedding dimension of 32, and these embeddings were concatenated to form the input sequence.

Feature Fusion: Outputs from the GNN (collaborative signal) and the Transformer (contextual signal) were integrated using a gated fusion mechanism rather than simple concatenation. This allowed the model to dynamically weigh the relative importance of long-term preferences versus immediate contextual needs.

Training Setup: The model was trained for 100 epochs using the Adam optimizer, with a weight decay of 1e-5. An early stopping mechanism with a patience of 10 epochs was applied, monitoring the RMSE on the validation set. To ensure gradient stability for the fairness constraint loss, a batch size of 256 was used.

Statistical Robustness: All quantitative metrics in this study were computed as the average over 10 independent runs to ensure statistical reliability and reproducibility. Key performance indicators (RMSE, MAE, and CAI) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (± standard deviation). Additionally, to verify whether the proposed GNN-Transformer model outperformed baseline models (DeepCF, GNN-CF) with statistical significance, a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test was conducted. Results indicated that, at a 0.05 significance level (p < 0.05), improvements in prediction accuracy and reductions in the alienation index were statistically significant, ruling out the possibility of random fluctuations.

Performance evaluation

Figure 3 presents the performance of baseline recommendation models and comparisons of popularity bias. On the MovieLens-32 M dataset, the proposed GNNs–Transformer model achieved the lowest RMSE (0.863) and MAE (0.661). It also outperformed baseline models on ranking metrics, attaining a Recall@10 of 0.165 and an NDCG@10 of 0.259. The most notable improvement was observed in exposure concentration: in the traditional DeepCF model, the top 10 popular items accounted for 38.7% of exposure, whereas the proposed model reduced this concentration to 24.1%. These results demonstrate that integrating the GNNs with a context-aware Transformer effectively mitigates the model’s overemphasis on popular items, alleviating the Matthew effect in resource allocation.

Ablation experiments are presented in Fig. 6. When the CAI constraint was removed from the full model (i.e., setting λ₁ = 0), although RMSE increased only slightly (0.863 → 0.868), the CAI value deteriorated sharply, rising from 0.871 to 0.997. This indicates that maintaining recommendation accuracy alone does not automatically alleviate user alienation; an explicit ethical regularization term is necessary to correct the algorithm’s preference for “exchange value.” Similarly, removing the fairness constraint (λ₂ = 0) caused the inter-group fairness variance F to double (0.012 → 0.021). This controlled ablation experiment confirms that the observed improvements in ethical performance—both CAI reduction and fairness enhancement—cannot be attributed solely to the feature extraction capabilities of the GNN or Transformer modules. Even the GNN-Trans model without constraints exhibits higher CAI than the full model. Instead, the gains are a direct causal result of the adversarial loss functions explicitly designed according to Marxist principles.

To investigate the intrinsic trade-off between recommendation accuracy and ethical constraints, a Pareto frontier analysis was conducted by varying the regularization coefficient λ for multi-objective optimization. The results are summarized in Table 5. As shown, increasing the ethical constraint weight λ clearly demonstrates a trade-off between performance and ethical compliance. When λ = 0, no additional ethical constraints are applied; the model achieves its lowest RMSE of 0.863, but the CAI is as high as 0.997, and the inter-group fairness variance F is 0.045. This indicates that, although the system is accurate in prediction, it exhibits a high risk of consumer alienation. As λ gradually increases to 0.5, RMSE rises only slightly to 0.871, remaining within an acceptable error range, while CAI decreases significantly to 0.871, and the fairness variance F is substantially reduced to 0.012. This demonstrates that around this threshold, the model reaches a Pareto-optimal state, achieving maximum ethical benefit with minimal sacrifice of recommendation accuracy. However, further increasing λ to 1.0 and 2.0 leads to continued reductions in CAI (0.815 and 0.764, respectively) but results in a sharp deterioration in RMSE (0.894 and 0.932), indicating that excessive ethical weighting can seriously undermine the system’s core predictive utility. Therefore, selecting λ = 0.5 represents a statistically reasonable balance point.

However, by Round 10, DeepCF’s Gini coefficient surged to 0.71, an increase of 69.0%, reflecting the “rich-get-richer” feedback loop inherent in traditional algorithms, concentrating recommendation resources on a few popular items. In contrast, the proposed GNN-Trans model maintained a relatively stable distribution, with the Gini coefficient increasing only slightly to 0.43 (+ 13.1%) after ten rounds. More importantly, the long-tail item exposure experienced a drastic drop under DeepCF, from 18.5% to 6.4% (− 65.4%), indicating that numerous non-popular items—potentially aligned with users’ real situational needs—were systematically “hidden.” This model, by contrast, maintained 20.1% long-tail exposure at Round 10, a decline of only 9.9%, demonstrating the CAI constraint’s persistent corrective effect. These results strongly indicate that integrating Marxist-inspired ethical constraints not only improves single-shot prediction fairness but also disrupts the algorithmic reproduction of “false demand” in long-term dynamics, preserving diversity and inclusivity in the recommendation ecosystem.

Discussion

The findings of this study both contrast with and complement current research in AI ethics. Zhang et al. (2024) emphasized that deep learning applications in ideological and political education must prioritize human development needs32. This study empirically validated this claim by quantifying outcomes using the CAI. Similarly, Burriss et al. (2024) introduced the concept of “everyday AI ethics literacy”33, which translated abstract ethical concerns into practical algorithmic modules. In particular, the adversarial fairness constraint implemented in this study effectively mitigated the occlusion of low-activity user groups, bridging the gap between critical theory and applied technology. Lin et al. (2024) highlighted the importance of incorporating emotional contexts in educational AI to support students’ creativity34. The present study extends this insight, demonstrating that cross-domain AI systems also require dynamic perception of users’ psychological states. Unlike Lin, who offered primarily high-level recommendations, the context–value alignment model presented here embeds emotional factors directly into the algorithm, achieving a technical-level integration of ethical principles with engineering practice. Taken together, these results suggest that AI ethics research can move beyond the superficial notion of “technological neutrality” and implement Marxist value-oriented regulatory mechanisms within the core of algorithmic design, ensuring that ethical considerations are operationalized rather than purely theoretical.

Although this study primarily quantifies consumer-side alienation, this phenomenon is deeply rooted in Marxist theory, particularly in labor alienation and commodity fetishism. Under digital capitalism, each user click and interaction constitutes a form of “unpaid digital labor,” producing data as a core commodity. Recommendation algorithms, as the crystallization of “dead labor,” in turn govern the users who perform “living labor.” This exemplifies Marx’s concept of reification: social relationships (recommendations among people) are obscured beneath relations between things (data and algorithms).

The CAI index proposed in this study reveals the consequences of this fetishistic mechanism: algorithms assign commodities a false “exchange value” (predicted high score), thereby masking their genuine social use value. Consequently, the adversarial fairness constraints introduced here serve not merely as a technical correction, but as an internal attempt to “de-fetishize” the algorithm, seeking to restore users’ subjectivity in specific life contexts beyond the surface of data. Future work should extend this analysis to the production side, exploring how algorithmic governance might resist the exploitation inherent in digital labor.

Conclusion

Research contributions

Drawing on Marxist alienation theory, this study reveals how AI recommendation systems, through the reinforcement of algorithmic preferences, can exacerbate both consumer alienation and group-level inequities. The proposed architecture, which integrates GNNs with a context-adaptive Transformer, effectively captures users’ dynamic usage-value needs, overcoming the traditional CF focus on exchange value alone. By operationalizing the CAI and incorporating adversarial fairness constraints, the system mitigates the occlusion of marginalized groups caused by popularity bias. This approach demonstrates how recommendation algorithms can be aligned more closely with comprehensive human development, translating ethical principles into actionable technological design.

Future work and research limitations

This study has three main limitations. First, contextual modeling relies on structured, labeled data, which limits adaptability to complex social interactions in open or uncontrolled environments. Second, cross-cultural ethical standards are not yet integrated into the algorithmic design, reducing the model’s applicability in Global South contexts. Third, the consumer alienation metric has not yet been linked to labor-alienation analysis, leaving a gap in the broader socio-economic interpretation. Future research should address these limitations by developing a multimodal, context-aware framework that incorporates Marxist geographic perspectives to expand contextual dimensions. Researchers should also establish differentiated academic-ethical systems that integrate postcolonial critique into algorithmic governance. Finally, exploring the interaction between recommendation systems and labor alienation will help achieve a complete, closed-loop integration of technological critique and theory, providing both practical and theoretical guidance for ethically grounded AI systems.
