Generalization Error in Quantum Machine Learning in the Presence of Sampling Noise
Fangjun Hu, Xun Gao

Abstract
Tackling output sampling noise due to finite shots of quantum measurement is an unavoidable challenge when extracting information in machine learning with physical systems. A technique called Eigentask Learning was developed recently as a framework for learning with infinite input training data in the presence of output sampling noise. In the work of Eigentask Learning, numerical evidence was presented that extracting low-noise contributions of features can practically improve performance for machine learning tasks, displaying robustness to overfitting and increasing generalization accuracy. However, it remains unsolved to quantitatively characterize generalization errors in situations where the training dataset is finite, while output sampling noise still exists. In this study, we use methodologies from statistical mechanics to calculate the training and generalization errors of a generic quantum machine learning system when the input training dataset and output measurement sampling shots are both finite. Our analytical findings, supported by numerical validation, offer solid justification that Eigentask Learning provides optimal learning in the sense of minimizing generalization errors.

Introduction

Exploring quantum machine learning (QML) schemes that leverage underlying quantum systems for processing classical data has been studied in various applications
[

,

,
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
One particular way of applying quantum systems to supervised machine learning algorithms is called parametric quantum circuits (PQC)
[

,

,

,

,

,

,
13
,
14
,

,

,

]
, where the quantum circuits depend not only on the task inputs but also some internal parameters.

Making predictions using PQCs requires accessing information from these circuits. To achieve this, one can measure all qubits in the PQC under computational bases and get the probabilities of obtaining classical bitstrings.
These probabilities, as functions of inputs, are called
features
in QML. Then, we can use the linear combination of these features through a set of
output weights
to make predictions for any given input. In generic PQC schemes, both the internal parameters and output weights need to be trained.

However, optimizing the internal parameters inside PQC is usually difficult and costly.
It is a challenge being widely investigated
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. To circumvent this difficulty, QML schemes without optimizing PQC internal parameters, including quantum kernel methods
[
20
]
, quantum extreme learning machine
[
21
]
, and quantum reservoir computing (QRC)
[
22
,
4
]
, are proposed.
Among these schemes, QRC is a QML scheme that maps the classical input data to a high-dimensional vector in the space of features, and only the output weights are optimized, leaving all randomly initialized internal parameters untrained.

It can be implemented in NISQ devices on many tasks more easily
[
4
,
23
]
. Therefore, we will restrict our discussion to QRC schemes in this article.

Even in an ideal quantum computer, we emphasize that QML contains two main types of stochasticities.
The first one is the randomness of input samples appearing in any machine learning algorithms with both classical and quantum systems.
Specifically, in practice, the learning process in any supervised QML algorithms must be carried out on a finite training input dataset, which can be considered drawn from some prior distribution.
The evaluation of the trained weights and parameters based on this finite training input dataset is directly related to two important quantities in classical machine learning: training and generalization errors
[
24
]
.
The second stochasticity is called quantum sampling noise (QSN)
[
25
]
– the statistical fluctuations during repeated measurement
shots
of quantum systems – on the output side.
In contrast to classical machine learning, QML suffers from this inherent QSN which places a strong constraint on practical optimization and performance
[
26
,
27
]
.

A recently developed theoretical framework
[
25
]
, Eigentask Analysis, presents a canonical representation of the quantum reservoir computing schemes and rigorously quantifies the impact of QSN.

The goal of Eigentask Analysis is to find functions restricted as specific linear combinations of original features so that these eigentasks are minimally affected by QSN. Those newly constructed functions are referred as
Eigentasks
in Ref.
[
25
]
.
Here, restricting eigentasks to be the linear combination of original features does not change the essence of quantum reservoir computing. This is because it is equivalent to training output weights either using the combination of all original features or using the combination of all eigentasks.

Thereafter, Ref.
[
25
]
provided numerical evidence that maximal testing accuracy can be achieved by truncating specific high-noise eigentasks. This technique was referred as Eigentask Learning. The motivation of Eigentask Learning is that eigentasks with high noise are irrelevant or even harmful for learning.
However, a more comprehensive theoretical understanding of such a phenomenon was unclear before
[
25
,
28
]
, and we now solve this problem in this paper.

Various mathematical techniques derived from statistical mechanics have found applications in computing generalization error in classical machine learning
[
29
,
30
,
24
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. This connection stems from the observation that the equilibrium distribution of a stochastic gradient descent training process corresponds to the Gibbs distribution in thermodynamics
[
30
]
. And the corresponding free energy plays a role of generalization error after training.
Therefore, these previous approaches analogize trained input data and training weights as quenched disorder (the coupling strength) and spin configuration, respectively, in a spin-glass system.
This analogy enables a quantitative analysis of the performance of QML systems when encountering new, unseen data after being trained on finite datasets.

In this article, we generalize this methodology to QRC, where both input data and output samples are finite. These two randomnesses constitute the newly introduced analog of quenched disorder.
Specifically, this paper formally defines training and generalization errors as functions of input dataset size
N
and the shot number
S
in QRC. We then solve them rigorously by using replica methods in spin-glass theory.
Based on these results, we formally justify that a well-defined truncation of eigentasks minimizes the generalization error in QRC, the phenomenon previously lacking a theoretical explanation in Ref.
[
25
]
. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first to analyze training and generalization while accounting for both input and output sampling noise in QML.

This article is organized as follows. Sec.
II
provides a theoretical setup of utilizing quantum systems for learning, with a brief review of the concepts of eigentask. Sec.
III
formally introduce the definitions of training and generalization errors. Those two errors evaluated from the numerical simulation are compared to the theoretical result derived by the replica trick. The close alignment observed in this comparison shows the validity of the analytical methods. Sec.
IV
applies the results established in Sec.
III
to prove the optimal order-truncation of eigentasks, laying a rigorous foundation of Eigentask Learning.

II
Eigentask Analysis for Learning with Quantum Systems

The underlying part of any QML architecture is a quantum system with an associated Hilbert space dimension
K
. For an
L
-qubit quantum system, the dimension of the quantum system is
K=2^{L}
. This quantum system encodes classical input data into the quantum system and outputs features through measurement.

To be more specific, we first let the
input dataset

\{\bm{u}^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}\}_{n\in[N]}
be
N
i.i.d.
D
-dimensional classical random variables which are drawn from a certain distribution
p(\bm{u})
. It naturally induces an expectation over the input
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f]=\int f(\bm{u})p(\bm{u})\differential\bm{u}
for any function
f
. Being initialized to
\hat{\rho}_{0}
, the quantum system encodes any input
\bm{u}
into a quantum circuit
\hat{U}(\bm{u})
, and then the initial state evolves to a new quantum state
\hat{\rho}(\bm{u})=\hat{U}(\bm{u})\hat{\rho}_{0}\hat{U}(\bm{u})^{\dagger}
. To make information accessible, we assign a set of positive operator-valued measure (POVM) elements
\{\hat{M}_{k}\}_{k\in[K]}
. One commonly chosen measurement in qubit system is through the projective basis
\hat{M}_{k}=\ket{k}\bra{k},

where
k
is in binary representation.
The direct readout feature functions are the quantum probabilities of obtaining bitstring
k
, conditioned on the input
\bm{u}
:
x_{k}(\bm{u})=\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{M}_{k}\hat{\rho}(\bm{u}))=\mathrm{Pr}[k|\bm{u}]
. We denote the collection of all features
\bm{x}(\bm{u})=\{x_{k}(\bm{u})\}_{k\in[K]}
which also has a dimension
K
.

In practice, the conditional probability above is usually inaccessible due to the finite number of quantum measurement repetitions or shots, denoted as
S
. For the input
\bm{u}
, in the
s
-th shot the quantum measurement only produces one outcome
k^{(s)}(\bm{u})
.

It is convenient to define
output sample sequence
(of a single
\bm{u}
)
\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})=\{k^{(s)}(\bm{u})\}_{s\in[S]}
and the
overall dataset

\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{u}^{(n)},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}^{(n)}))\}_{n\in[N]}.

(1)

From the perspective of statistical mechanics, similar to the case of spin-glass, we will see in Appendix
B.3
the overall dataset
\mathcal{D}
also serves as a quenched disorder in the (quantum) machine learning system.
The emprical
noisy readout features

\bm{X}(\bm{u})=\{X_{k}(\bm{u})\}_{k\in[K]}
counts the occurrence of each
k

X_{k}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\delta(k^{(s)}(\bm{u}),k)=x_{k}(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\zeta_{k}(\bm{u}),

(2)

where
\delta
is the indicator function, and
\bm{\zeta}
is a zero-mean random variable, with covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{u}):=\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[\bm{\zeta}]=\mathrm{diag}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}\bm{x}^{T}
. Here we use
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}
and
\mathrm{Cov}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}
as the conditional expectation and covariance over output samples
\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})
, conditioned on the input value
\bm{u}
.
The term
\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\zeta_{k}(\bm{u})
in Eq. (
2
) is called
quantum sampling noise
(QSN).

Theory of Eigentask Analysis
[
25
]
shows that there exists a unique
S
-independent tuple
\{\beta_{k}^{2},\bm{r}^{(k)}\}
which simultaneously diagonalizes both new signals
y^{(k)}=\sum_{j}r_{j}^{(k)}x_{j}
and new noises
\xi^{(k)}=\sum_{j}r_{j}^{(k)}\zeta_{j}
, such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[y^{(k)}y^{(k^{\prime})}]=\delta_{kk^{\prime}},\\
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[\xi^{(k)}\xi^{(k^{\prime})}]]=\beta_{k}^{2}\delta_{kk^{\prime}}.\end{array}\right.

(3)

Here, the tuple
\{\beta_{k}^{2},\bm{r}^{(k)}\}
can be uniquely solved via the generalized eigenproblem

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}\bm{r}^{(k)}=\beta^{2}_{k}\mathbf{G}\bm{r}^{(k)},

(4)

where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\bm{x}\bm{x}^{T}]
is the
Gram matrix
and
\mathbf{V}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbf{\Sigma}]
is the
mean noise matrix
. The newly constructed signals
\{y^{(k)}\}
are called
eigentasks
, and
\{\beta^{2}_{k}\}
are their associated eigen-noise-to-signal-ratios (eigen-NSRs). We use a non-decreasing order
0=\beta^{2}_{1}\leq\beta^{2}_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\beta^{2}_{K}<\infty
to label those eigentasks and eigen-NSRs. We will show in the next section that the training and generalization errors can be fully captured merely in terms of
N,S
and
\{\beta_{k}^{2}\}
, under the eigentask representation.

We also emphasize that, in Ref.
[
25
]
, it is shown that even if these
\{\beta_{k}^{2},\bm{r}^{(k)}\}
are defined through noiseless features
\bm{x}(\bm{u})
, they can be accurately estimated from noisy features
\bm{X}(\bm{u})
through a certain empirical correction (see Appendix D in Ref.
[
25
]
).

III
Training and Generalization Error

The basic task of quantum reservoir computing is using the linear combination of features
\bm{X}(\bm{u})
to fit some given (non-random) target function
f^{\star}(\bm{u})
. We let
f^{\star}
have a decomposition

\displaystyle f^{\star}=\bm{c}\cdot\bm{x}+f_{\bot},

(5)

where
\int f_{\bot}(\bm{u})x_{k}(\bm{u})\differential\bm{u}=0
for all
k
and this
f_{\bot}
captures the upper limit of the prediction performance of the quantum system.

It is also convenient to decompose
f^{\star}
under the basis of eigentasks

\displaystyle f^{\star}=\bm{a}\cdot\bm{y}+f_{\bot}.

(6)

The transform between
\bm{a}
and
\bm{c}
are through the
\{\bm{r}^{(k)}\}
coefficients computed in Eq. (
4
). Such transform exists in any generic quantum reservoir without particular symmetry due to the linear independence of
\{\bm{r}^{(k)}\}
(see Appendix C5 in Ref.
[
25
]
).

Without loss of generality, we assume
f^{\star}
is normalized
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{\star 2}]=\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{c}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]=1
. Conditioned on overall dataset (or quenched disorder)
\mathcal{D}
, the training process is to minimize the loss function
H(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{n}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}^{(n)}))+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}\|^{2}
over all possible
output weights

\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, where single-input loss function is
\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}))=(\bm{w}\cdot\bm{X}(\bm{u})-f^{\star}(\bm{u}))^{2}
. We emphasize that
\mathcal{L}
is also stochastic due to the finiteness of
S
. The term
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\bm{w}\|^{2}
with small parameter
\lambda
is added as a regularizer to ensure the uniqueness of optimized
\bm{w}^{\ast}

[
24
]
. The training procedure finally yields unique
\mathcal{D}
-dependent
trained weights

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{\ast}=\underset{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}{\mathrm{argmin}}~H(\bm{w}).

(7)

Ideally, if
N,S
tends to infinite,
\bm{w}^{\ast}
tends to
\bm{c}
for small enough regularizer
\lambda
.
The training error function is directly defined as the loss by setting output weights be the trained weights:

\epsilon_{t}(\bm{w}^{\ast})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w}^{\ast},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}^{(n)})).

(8)

The definition of the generalization error function of weights
\bm{w}
is a bit more involved. We define it as
\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}))]]
. A detailed calculation (see Appendix
A
) gives the explicit form of
\epsilon_{g}

\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w})=\bm{w}^{T}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{w}-2\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{w}+1.

(9)

Generalization error
\epsilon_{g}
evaluates how well the quantum learning system performs for unseen data using the output weights
\bm{w}
. It can be justified in the following sense. Suppose that quantum system sees a new testing dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})},\mathcal{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))\}_{n^{\prime}\in[N^{\prime}]}
, which is independent from
\mathcal{D}
, but still obeys
\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}\sim p(\bm{v})
. The testing error
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}}(\bm{w}\cdot\bm{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})-f^{\star}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))^{2}
can be shown to converge to the deterministic quantity in Eq. (
9
) when
N^{\prime}\to\infty
(see Appendix
A
).

However, quantities
\epsilon_{t}(\bm{w}^{\ast})
and
\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w}^{\ast})
are both
\mathcal{D}
-dependent and therefore random. To unambiguously quantify the training and generalization errors, we need to take the ensemble average over all possible
\mathcal{D}
. It leads to the central concepts of this work, the
average
training and generalization errors, namely

E_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\epsilon_{t}(\bm{w}^{\ast})],\quad E_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w}^{\ast})].

(10)

; the y-axis of this plot is log-scaled. For each value of

,
R

and

. The empirical training and generalization errors of each dataset are dots in orange. The mean values of empirical errors (red dots), and the theoretical error values computed from Eq. (

1-C[f^{\star}]
, the large
N

By using the replica trick, we can show that (see Appendix
D
and
E
) the average training and generalization errors
under eigentask representation
are

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}E_{g},

(11)

\displaystyle E_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\!\!\left(\!\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]+\!\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}\!\!\left(\!\frac{\left(\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}}\!\right)\!\!\right)\!\!,

(12)

where there are two new quantities we need to specify:

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\lambda+\kappa\sum_{k>1}\frac{1+\beta_{k}^{2}/S}{N(1+\beta_{k}^{2}/S)+\kappa},

(13)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>1}\frac{N(1+\beta_{k}^{2}/S)^{2}}{(N(1+\beta_{k}^{2}/S)+\kappa)^{2}}.

(14)

The value of
\kappa
must be solved self-consistently from Eq. (
13
). It is usually called Signal Capture Threshold (SCT)
[
34
]
in classical machine learning theory.

There are three comments that can justify the correctness of the formula Eq. (
11
-
12
). First of all, by taking the noiseless limit
S\to\infty
, we get
E_{g}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]+\sum_{k>1}a_{k}^{2}(\frac{\kappa}{\kappa+N})^{2})
, which reproduces results of generalization error in classical kernel regression with unit and flat Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) spectrum
[
24
,
31
]
. Secondly, when the regularization parameter
\lambda
is small enough, in the limit of large training dataset
N\to\infty
, the quantum system sees enough data so that it learns sufficiently, and there should be no difference between training and generalization errors:

\lim_{N\to\infty}E_{t}(\lambda=0)=\lim_{N\to\infty}E_{g}(\lambda=0)=1-C[f^{\star}],

(15)

where
C[f^{\star}]=\sum_{k}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{1+\beta_{k}^{2}/S}
is called the
functional capacity
of
f^{\star}
, defined in Ref.
[
25
]
. Finally, also for
\lambda\to 0
limit, we can show that
\kappa
is zero if
N
is larger than
K-1
, while
\kappa
is non-zero if
N
is smaller than
K-1
(see Appendix
E
).
It hints a phase transition at
N=K-1
.

When
N<K-1
, the training error
E_{t}
is shown to be zero for
\lambda\to 0
. This is because the quantum systems do not see enough data and overfit the data point by interpolation. As a result, the generalization error
E_{g}
maintains a relatively high value and reaches its maximum at
N=K-1
. On the other hand, when
N>K-1
, the quantum system can no longer interpolate; therefore,
E_{t}
starts increasing while
E_{g}
decreases.

In practice,
K=2^{L}
is exponentially large and is typically larger than
N
. As a result, if one does not properly drop some features, then this quantum reservoir computing algorithm is always in the interpolation phase
N<K-1
. This observation means a certain dropping of features is crucial for learning, which will be discussed in Sec.
IV
.

Fig.
1
shows the training error
E_{t}
, and the logarithm of deviation between generalization error
E_{g}
and its large
N
limit
1-C[f^{\star}]
, when fitting target function
f^{\star}(u)=\mathrm{Sgn}(u)
, where 1D input
u\in[-1,1]
. Here,
\mathrm{Sgn}(u)=-1
for
u<0
and
\mathrm{Sgn}(u)=1
for
u\geq 0
. The underlying quantum system is a
6
-qubit quantum Ising model with a random drive. More concretely, the Hamiltonian is
\hat{H}(u)=\hat{H}_{0}+u\cdot\hat{H}_{1}
, where
\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\langle l,l^{\prime}\rangle}J\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{l^{\prime}}^{z}+\sum_{l}(h_{l}^{x}\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{x}+h_{l}^{z}\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{z})
and
\hat{H}_{1}=\sum_{l}h_{l}^{I}\hat{\sigma}_{l}^{x}
, where all
h_{l}^{x/z/I}
are randomly drawn through uniform distribution of certain intervals. The encoding is
\hat{\rho}(u)=e^{-i\tau\hat{H}(u)}\hat{\rho}_{0}e^{i\tau\hat{H}(u)}
. Both empirical average training and generalization errors and their theoretical error values computed from Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
12
) are shown in Fig.
1
. These data match well, especially for the practical large
N
limit. This is because in the calculation of replica methods, one needs to take the saddle point approximation and assume
N
to be large (see Appendix
C.1
and
C.2
). Therefore, the larger
N
is, the more accurate the replica theory produces.

We find that our numerical is consistent with the theoretical prediction of phase transition at
N=K-1
.
When
N<K-1
, the quantum reservoir interpolates so that
E_{t}
is nearly zero but
E_{g}
is high.
Then, when
N>K-1
, training errors start to become non-zero since perfect interpolation is no longer possible for
N>K-1
. At the same time, generalization errors
E_{g}
also decrease until both
E_{t}
and
E_{g}
converge to their common limit
1-C[f^{\star}]
. Such phenomenon in generalization errors is called
double-descent
, it was also observed in many classical machine learning systems
[
24
,
35
]
.

IV
Eigentask Learning

As we discussed in Sec.
III
, the feature number
K
in QML is usually larger than the input training dataset size
N
, which corresponds to the interpolation phase. Therefore, truncating the number of used features for training and testing is necessary. Ref.
[
25
]
proposed a learning scheme called Eigentask Learning. It keeps the leading
K_{L}
eigentasks
\{y^{(k)}\}_{k\in[K_{L}]}
with lowest-noise to do the training and testing. This section will follow this methodology and determine its optimal truncation.

First of all, we take the limitation
\lambda\rightarrow 0
in Eq. (
12
) and fix the training dataset size
N
.
Then for any
K_{L}-1<N
, by solving self-consistent equation of
\kappa
, we have
\kappa=0
and
\gamma=(K_{L}-1)/N
(see Appendix
E
). Then, the average generalization error in this case is

\displaystyle E_{g}=~

\displaystyle\frac{N}{N-K_{L}-1}\Bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{K_{L}}a_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}/S}{1+\beta_{k}^{2}/S}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=K_{L}+1}^{K}a_{k}^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]\Bigg).

(16)

Now we can explain the efficacy of Eigentask Learning, by using Eq. (
16
).

. This again shows the higher accuracy of replica theory for relatively large

.
We also solved Eq. (

) and get the theoretical optimal truncation index
K_{L}^{\ast}(S)=15
for
N=10^{2}
and
K_{L}^{\ast}(S)=18
for
N=10^{3}
. This is also consistent with the
K_{L}
, which corresponds to the minimal values of numerical generalization errors.

By increasing the number
K_{L}
, the prefactor in Eq. (
16
)
N/\left(N-K_{L}-1\right)
increases as well. It means this prefactor contributes to the generalization error as a characterization of overfitting.
On the other hand, when including more eigentasks by increasing
K_{L}
, adding
y^{(k)}
into the set of used eigentasks causes a change from
a_{k}^{2}
to
a_{k}^{2}\times\beta_{k}^{2}/(S+\beta_{k}^{2})
(namely, it causes from the first line to the second line in Eq. (
16
)).
Therefore, the term in parenthesis of Eq. (
16
) decreases as
K_{L}
increases.
The different trends of the prefactor and the term in parenthesis of Eq. (
16
) as we increase
K_{L}
indicates an optimal
K_{L}
which minimize
E_{g}
.

To be more specific, if we evaluate the finite difference
\Delta E_{g}(K_{L})=E_{g}(K_{L}+1)-E_{g}(K_{L})
, we will obtain

\Delta E_{g}(K_{L})\approx\frac{1-C[f^{\star}]}{N(1-\gamma)^{2}}-\frac{c_{K_{L}}^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\left(1+\beta_{K_{L}}^{2}/S\right)}.

(17)

The optimal value
K_{L}^{\ast}
for
K_{L}
should make
\Delta E_{g}(K_{L})
vanish. Such
K_{L}^{\ast}
must be task-dependent. Namely, it depends on
\bm{a}
. However, the weights
\bm{a}
are unknown in most practical settings. One way to estimate the
optimal truncation index

K_{L}^{\ast}
is letting
a^{2}_{k}\approx\frac{1}{K},\forall k\in[K]
and then solving
\Delta E_{g}(K_{L})=0
. This approximation comes from the assumption that no eigentask
y^{(k)}
should have a larger contribution than other eigentasks for a generic task
f^{\star}
. It gives equation of
K_{L}^{\ast}

\frac{\beta_{K_{L}^{\ast}}^{2}}{S}=\frac{N-K_{L}^{\ast}}{K-C_{T}(S)},

(18)

where Resolvable Expressive Capacity
C_{T}(S)=\sum_{k}1/(1+\beta^{2}_{k}/S)\leq K
quantifies the effective number of resolvable functions generated by quantum systems
[
25
]
.
We remark here that as function of
K_{L}^{\ast}
,
\beta_{K_{L}^{\ast}}^{2}/S
is increasing while
(N-K_{L}^{\ast})/(K-C_{T}(S))
is decreasing. The intersection of these two curves yields the solution of the optimal truncation index. We also let
K_{L}^{\ast}=\min\{K,N\}
if the above equation has no solution. Non-negativity of
\beta_{K_{L}^{\ast}}^{2}/S>0
also ensures that the solution
K_{L}^{\ast}\leq N
.

In Fig.
2
, we present
E_{g}-(1-C[f^{\star}])
, the deviation between the average generalization error
E_{g}
and its large-
N
limit
1-C[f^{\star}]
, as a function of
K_{L}
. Examples are shown for two different input data sizes
N=10^{2}
and
N=10^{3}
.
The analytical and numerical generalization error not only matches well at small
K_{L}
for the relatively small input size
N=10^{2}
, but also fits all
K_{L}
perfectly for the relatively large input size
N=10^{3}
, this again indicates that replica theory presented in Sec.
III
works better for large dataset regime.
Also, by solving the equation of optimal truncation index Eq. (
18
), it gives a theoretical estimation
K_{L}^{\ast}(S)=15
and
K_{L}^{\ast}(S)=18
, for
N=10^{2}
and
10^{3}
respectively. They also match the
K_{L}
, which minimizes the numerical generalization errors.
We emphasize here that by taking a look at the generalization errors in the left panel of Fig.
2
, we find applying Eigentask Learning order-truncation at
K_{L}=15
is
O(10^{2})
times better than keeping all features (namely
K_{L}=64
). It presents that Eigentask Learning should be an essential procedure, especially when there is no sufficient data for learning in the small
N
regime.

V
Discussion

In this article, we formally study the training error and generalization error – as a function of training dataset size
N
and shot number
S
in those QML schemes that use parametric quantum circuits without training the internal parameters. We use the replica method as the approximation trick to evaluate the training and generalization errors. The eventual expressions are in terms of the eigen-NSR, the important concept in eigentask analysis. The result can be immediately employed to provide a rigorous explanation of the efficacy of Eigentask Learning.

However, several directions remain to be explored in the theory of Eigentask Learning. First of all, given the prior distribution
p(\bm{u})
and encoding circuit
U(\bm{u})
, the distribution of spectrum
\{\beta^{2}_{k}\}
is still not clear in general. This could cause difficulty in determining how many low-noise eigentask can be constructed from the quantum reservoir.

Secondly, both the Eigentask Analysis and replica methods in Ref.
[
24
]
only consider the first- and second-order cumulants of input data (see also the Gaussian approximation in Appendix
B.4
). Understanding the generalization error regarding higher-order correlation of data needs to be explored, and a new scope of feature representation beyond the Eigentask basis may be provided.

Third, the analysis in this paper works when accurate values of
\bm{r}^{(k)}
in the combination coefficients in eigentasks are known. In practice, those coefficients are unknown, and it remains a challenge to compute them as accurately as possible, especially those eigentasks with indices lower than threshold
K_{L}^{\ast}(S)
.
Ref.
[
25
]
provides a numerical method of estimating
\bm{r}^{(k)}
from noisy experimental data by using singular value decomposition. The replica trick in this work is not easy to be generalized to compute numerical deviation of
\bm{r}^{(k)}
because it is hard to use the weights expectations
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{k}^{\ast}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{j}^{\ast}w_{k}^{\ast}]
to express estimator of
\bm{r}^{(k)}
. Moreover, the numerical fluctuation of estimating
\bm{r}^{(k)}
should also depend on the concrete methods used, case by case. But intuitively, those eigentasks with indices lower than threshold
K_{L}^{\ast}(S)
are easier to obtain accurately since they are the features that are more noise-resilient.

This presented work establishes a theoretical framework for leveraging fewer output features to fit a specified target function accurately. This advancement holds significant promise across various applications and has the potential to enhance the resource efficiency of numerous QML protocols
[
21
,
22
,
4
,
20
]
.

Appendices

Appendix A
Definition of Generalization Error Function

In this section, we are going to give an detailed explanation about why Eq. (
9
) is a valid definition of generalization error function for given weights
\bm{w}
. It will be helpful to first recall the loss function of any
input
\bm{u}
and weights
\bm{w}
is

\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}))=\left(\bm{w}\cdot\left(\bm{x}(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u})\right)-\bm{c}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\right)^{2}.

(19)

The following conditional expectation of loss function over the output samples
\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})
(conditioned on the input
\bm{u}
) will be used frequently later

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}))]=(\bm{w}-\bm{c})^{T}\bm{x}(\bm{u})\bm{x}(\bm{u})^{T}(\bm{w}-\bm{c})+\frac{1}{S}\bm{w}^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{u})\bm{w}+f^{2}_{\bot}(\bm{u})-2f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\bm{x}(\bm{u})^{T}(\bm{w}-\bm{c}).

(20)

As what we pointed in Sec.
III
of main text, generalization error
\epsilon_{g}
can be understood in the scenario when quantum system sees a new, unseen testing dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\{(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})},\mathcal{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))\}_{n^{\prime}\in[N^{\prime}]}
. The testing error, which evaluates the performance of a
fixed

\bm{w}
, should be
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}(\bm{w}\cdot\bm{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})-f^{\star}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))^{2}
. Or more explicitly,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\left(\bm{w}\cdot\left(\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\bm{\zeta}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})\right)-(\bm{c}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})+f_{\bot}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))\right)^{2}

(21)

Now we show that
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})
in Eq. (
21
) should be close to
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(u))]]
with arbitrarily high probability when
N^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty
. This can be justified by two steps of argument.

The first step is using the so-called
concentration principle
. If
n_{1}^{\prime}\neq n_{2}^{\prime}
, then
u^{(n^{\prime}_{1})}
and
u^{(n^{\prime}_{2})}
are independent. Central limit theorem ensures that
\epsilon_{\rm{ts}}[\bm{w}^{\ast}]
should be close to its expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})]=\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})}\left[\left(\bm{w}\cdot\left(\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\bm{\zeta}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})\right)-(\bm{c}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})+f_{\bot}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})}))\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N^{\prime}}(\bm{w}-\bm{c})^{T}\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})^{T}(\bm{w}-\bm{c})+\frac{1}{S}\bm{w}^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})\bm{w}+f^{2}_{\bot}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})-2f_{\bot}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})^{T}(\bm{w}-\bm{c})

(22)

with arbitrarily high probability when
N^{\prime}\to\infty
, where the second line comes from Eq. (
20
). This can shown formally by Hoeffding’s inequality. Since both
\bm{w}
and
f^{\star}
are fixed, and the noisy features
\bm{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})=\bm{x}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\bm{\zeta}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})\in[0,1]
. We just let
|\bm{w}\cdot\bm{X}(\bm{u})-f^{\star}(\bm{u}))^{2}|<B
for all
\bm{u}
, where
B
is a positive constant. Then Hoeffding’s inequality states for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathrm{Pr}\big[\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})-\mathbb{E}[|\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})]|\leq\varepsilon\big]\leq 2\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{N^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2}}{2B^{2}}\right),

(23)

which proves that when
N^{\prime}\to\infty
, testing error
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})
will converge to its expectation
\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})]
with arbitrarily high probability, even features
\bm{X}(\bm{v}^{(n^{\prime})})
are influenced by sampling noise.

The second step is taking
integration by Monte Carlo
: it ensures the summation Eq. (
22
) and the following integral

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}\left[(\bm{w}-\bm{c})^{T}\bm{x}(\bm{u})\bm{x}(\bm{u})^{T}(\bm{w}-\bm{c})+\frac{1}{S}\bm{w}^{T}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{u})\bm{w}+f^{2}_{\bot}(\bm{u})-2f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\bm{x}(\bm{u})^{T}(\bm{w}-\bm{c})\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{T}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{w}-2\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{w}+1.

(24)

should be close with arbitrarily high probability when
N^{\prime}\to\infty
, where the last equality employs the definition of
\mathbf{G}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\bm{x}(\bm{u})\bm{x}(\bm{u})^{T}]
, the definition of
\mathbf{V}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbf{\Sigma}(\bm{u})]
, the orthogonality of
\int f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\bm{x}(\bm{u})\differential\bm{u}=0
, and the normalization condition
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{\star 2}]=\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{c}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]=1
.

Therefore, we verify that when taking the large testing dataset size limitation
N^{\prime}\to\infty
, the testing error should converge to a deterministic value

\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w})=\lim_{N^{\prime}\rightarrow\infty}\epsilon_{\mathrm{ts}}(\bm{w})=\bm{w}^{T}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{w}-2\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{w}+1

(25)

with probability
1
. We use this deterministic quantity as the definition of generalization function in Eq. (
9
).

Replica Theory for Solving Training and Generalization Errors

From now on, the remaining parts of Appendices is using replica theory to prove average training and generalization errors in Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
12
). Roughly speaking, replica tricks is a tool of calculating a physical quantity in one system, by taking
m
-replica duplication and then extrapolating to
m=0
.

Appendix
B
introduces the techniques of replica methods on analyzing the training and generalization errors. Then main result of this section is the replicated partition function
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[Z^{m}]
in Eq. (
56
). However, for solving the complicated Eq. (
56
), we need to introduce the replica-symmetry ansatz and saddle point approximation, which are delivered in Appendix
C
. It reduces the computation of Eq. (
56
) to
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z]
in Eq. (
78
). Eventually, we reach the final expression of average training and generalization errors under basis of original features and eigentasks, in Appendix
D
and
E
respectively. The are exactly the Eq. (
11
) and Eq. (
12
) presented in the main text.

B.1
Introducing Gibbs distribution

Recall that the average training and generalization errors are
E_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\epsilon_{t}(\bm{w}^{\ast})]
and
E_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w}^{\ast})]
, where

\bm{w}^{\ast}=\underset{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}{\mathrm{argmin}}H(\bm{w})=\underset{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}(\bm{w},\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}^{(n)}))+\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{w}\|^{2}.

(26)

In principle, the optimized weights
\bm{w}^{\ast}
has a closed algebraic form by solving Eq. (
26
). However, it is still hard to compute the quenched disorder ensemble average over such random
\bm{w}^{\ast}
, which is complicatedly
\mathcal{D}
-dependent. One solution is introducing the Gibbs distribution

p_{G}(\bm{w},\beta)=\frac{e^{-\beta H(\bm{w})}}{Z[\beta]},

(27)

where the partition function
Z[\beta]=\int\differential\bm{w}e^{-\beta H(\bm{w})}
, and then take
\beta\to\infty
limit:

p_{G}(\bm{w},\beta\rightarrow\infty)=\delta(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{\ast}).

(28)

This trick comes from the observation that the stationary distribution of a standard stochastic gradient descent is Gibbs distribution, whose low temperature limit corresponds to the stochasticity-vanishing situation
[
30
]
. Then, for any function
f
we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{w}^{\ast})]=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\int\differential\bm{w}p_{G}(\bm{w},\beta\rightarrow\infty)f(\bm{w})\right].

(29)

B.2
Adding source terms

By observing average generalization error
E_{g}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\epsilon_{g}(\bm{w}^{\ast})]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\bm{w}^{\ast T}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{w}^{\ast}-2\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\bm{w}^{\ast}\right]+1
, we find that as long as all terms like
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{k}^{\ast}]
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{j}^{\ast}w_{k}^{\ast}]
are known, the average generalization error can be computed by taking the linear combination

E_{g}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}\left(\mathbf{G}_{jk}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}_{jk}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{j}^{\ast}w_{k}^{\ast}]-2\sum_{j,k=1}^{K}c_{j}\mathbf{G}_{jk}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{k}^{\ast}]+1.

(30)

Just as what is usually done in quantum field theory and statistical mechanics, such first and second order moment can be obtained by adding source terms in partition function. Taking derivative near the zero-source yield those moments. Hence, it suggests us to add the source term in a newly defined partition function:

Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]=\int\differential\bm{w}e^{-\beta H(\bm{w})+\beta\bm{\xi}^{T}\bm{w}+\frac{1}{2}\beta\bm{\eta}^{T}\bm{w}\bm{w}^{T}\bm{\eta}}.

(31)

such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{k}^{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]]|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}},

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[w_{j}^{\ast}w_{k}^{\ast}]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\left.\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{j}\partial\eta_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]]\right|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}}.

(33)

Furthermore, we can verify that the new partition function
Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]
can also generate the average training error Eq. (
8
):

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\left((\bm{w}^{\ast}-\bm{c})\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u}^{(n)})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\bm{w}^{\ast}\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u}^{(n)})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u}^{(n)})\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{2\lambda}{N}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[H(\bm{w}^{\ast})]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|^{2}]\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda}{N}\left(-\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}}]-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|^{2}]\right).

(34)

B.3
Replica trick

Replica trick is a commonly used trick for dealing with quantity involving logarithm, in both high energy theory and machine learning theory. The mathematical intuition is the limitation identity

\ln Z=\lim_{m\rightarrow 0}\frac{Z^{m}-1}{m}.

(35)

By taking expectation over
\mathcal{D}
on both sides of Eq. (
35
), we can rewrite the averaged free energy into a limitation form
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z]=\lim_{m\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[Z^{m}]-1}{m}
.

Suppose a system with
m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
replicas of the original quantum systems, which have
m
different weights
\{\bm{w}^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in[m]}
but share the same quenched disorder
\mathcal{D}
:

\displaystyle Z^{m}[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]

\displaystyle=\int\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{m}\differential\bm{w}^{\mu}\right\}e^{-\beta\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\left(H(\bm{w}^{\mu})+\beta\bm{\xi}^{T}\bm{w}^{\mu}+\frac{\beta}{2}\bm{\eta}^{T}\bm{w}^{\mu}\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{\eta}\right)}

\displaystyle=\int\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{m}\differential\bm{w}^{\mu}\right\}e^{-\beta\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{w}^{\mu}-\bm{\xi}^{T}\bm{w}^{\mu}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\eta}^{T}\bm{w}^{\mu}\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{\eta}\right)}

\displaystyle\hskip 75.0001pt\times e^{-\beta\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\left((\bm{w}^{\mu}-\bm{c})\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u}^{(n)})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u}^{(n)})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u}^{(n)})\right)^{2}}.

(36)

By taking the transformation
\bm{w}^{\mu}\rightarrow\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c}
and define
\bm{W}^{\star}=(\bm{c}-\bm{\xi})-(\bm{\eta}^{T}\bm{c})\bm{\eta}
, one get

\displaystyle Z^{m}[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{m\beta}{2}(\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{\xi})^{T}\bm{c}}\int\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{m}\differential\bm{w}^{\mu}\right\}e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\bm{w}^{\mu T}(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})\bm{w}^{\mu}-\beta\bm{W}^{\star T}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\bm{w}^{\mu}}

\displaystyle\hskip 135.0002pt\times e^{-\frac{\beta}{2\lambda}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\left(\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u}^{(n)})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u}^{(n)})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u}^{(n)})\right)^{2}}.

(37)

The first term
e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\bm{w}^{\mu T}(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})\bm{w}^{\mu}-\beta\bm{W}^{\star T}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\bm{w}^{\mu}}
is independent of the quenched disorder
\mathcal{D}
. The quenched disorder ensemble average of the second term can be simplified into form of exponential due to the fact that
\mathcal{I}=\{\bm{u}^{(n)}\}
are i.i.d., and
\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}^{(n_{1})})
and
\mathcal{X}(\bm{u}^{(n_{2})})
should be independent if
n_{1}\neq n_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta}{2\lambda}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\sum^{N}_{n=1}\left(\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u}^{(n)})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u}^{(n)})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u}^{(n)})\right)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[e^{-\frac{\beta}{2\lambda}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\left(\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\right)^{2}}\right]^{N}

(38)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\!\left[e^{-\frac{\beta}{2\lambda}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\left(\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\right)^{2}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}\!\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}\!\!\left[e^{-\frac{\beta}{2\lambda}\sum^{m}_{\mu=1}\left(\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u})\right)^{2}}\right]\right].

(39)

There are two difficult integrals in Eq. (
37
). The first one is the integral with respect to quenched disorder in Eq. (
39
); the second one is the integral with respect to the replica weights
\left\{\prod_{\mu=1}^{m}\differential\bm{w}^{\mu}\right\}
in Eq. (
37
). We need to tackle them separately.

B.4
Integrate with respect to quenched disorder

The expectation in Eq. (
39
) is still general and does not has a closed form. By looking at the quadratic term at the exponent in Eq. (
37
), we adopt the Gaussian approximation used in Ref.
[
31
]
, and define
m
random variables
q^{\mu}=\bm{w}^{\mu}\cdot\bm{x}(\bm{u})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})\cdot\bm{\zeta}(\bm{u})-f_{\bot}(\bm{u})
, whose mean
\bm{R}
and
covariance
\bm{Q}
are

\displaystyle R^{\mu}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[q^{\mu}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{x}]+\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})^{T}\bm{\zeta}]]-\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f_{\bot}]=\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{d},

(40)

\displaystyle Q^{\mu\nu}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[q^{\mu}q^{\nu}]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[q^{\mu}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[q^{\nu}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{x}\bm{x}^{T}\bm{w}^{\nu}]+\frac{1}{S}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})^{T}\bm{\zeta}\bm{\zeta}^{T}(\bm{w}^{\nu}+\bm{c})]]+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f_{\bot}^{2}]-\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{w}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}^{\mu T}\mathbf{G}\bm{w}^{\nu}+\frac{1}{S}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{c})^{T}\mathbf{V}(\bm{w}^{\nu}+\bm{c})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f_{\bot}^{2}]-\bm{w}^{\mu T}\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{w}^{\nu}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}^{\mu T}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}-\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}\right)\bm{w}^{\nu}+\frac{1}{S}(\bm{w}^{\mu}+\bm{w}^{\nu})^{T}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}+\frac{1}{S}\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}],

(41)

where we use
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{X}(\bm{u})}[\bm{\zeta}]=0
,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f_{\bot}]=\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[x_{k}f_{\bot}]=0
and define
d_{k}=\int\differential up(u)x_{k}(u)
. Notice that the integrated second-order-moment matrix
\mathbf{D}:=\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{G}
studied in Ref.
[
25
]
is a diagonal matrix with exactly
\mathbf{D}_{kk}=d_{k}
for all
k
. Also, for convenience, we introduce two new matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}-\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}.

(42)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{S}\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}].

(43)

We now introduce a Gaussian approximation
\bm{q}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{R},\bm{Q})
here. It assumes
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is approximately a Gaussian random variable with mean
\bm{R}
and covariance
\bm{Q}
, which are computed above. The discussion of the validity of the Gaussian approximation can be found in Ref.
[
24
]
.

Appendix C
Replica Symmetry Solutions

C.1
Replica symmetry ansatz

Now we have already transformed the calculation of
\mathcal{D}
-averaged log-partition function
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]]
into a minimization form of
\mathcal{S}[\bm{Q},\hat{\bm{Q}},\bm{R},\hat{\bm{R}}]
. However, free energy function
\mathcal{S}[\bm{Q},\hat{\bm{Q}},\bm{R},\hat{\bm{R}}]
has
m^{2}+3m
degrees of freedom. A reasonable way to deal with it is assuming all its saddle points satisfy a certain symmetry, this is exactly the well-known
replica symmetry ansatz
(RSA):

\displaystyle Q^{\mu\mu}=q_{0},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\mu\mu}=\hat{q}_{0},

\displaystyle\quad R^{\mu}=r,

\displaystyle Q^{\mu\neq\nu}=q_{1},\quad

\displaystyle\hat{Q}^{\mu\neq\nu}=\hat{q}_{1},

\displaystyle\quad\hat{R}^{\mu}=\hat{r}.

(61)

Thus, the parameters in
\mathcal{G}_{0}
are simplified to

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{\oplus}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{\mu=1}^{m}\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N}{\beta S}(2\hat{q}_{0}+(m-1)\hat{q}_{1})\mathbf{V}\bm{c},

(62)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{0}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{X}^{\mu\mu}=(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})-\frac{2N\hat{q}_{0}}{\beta}\mathbf{C},

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{X}^{\mu\neq\nu}=-\frac{N\hat{q}_{1}}{\beta}\mathbf{C}.

(64)

Furthermore, by truncating the second and higher order terms of
m
in these listed quantities:
The second and higher order of the listed quantities are truncated.
we use Eq. (
60
) and get the
replica-symmetric free energy
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[Q,\hat{Q},R,\hat{R}]=~

\displaystyle r\hat{r}+(q_{0}-\tilde{\sigma}^{2})\hat{q}_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(q_{1}-\tilde{\sigma}^{2})\hat{q}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\lambda}(q_{0}-q_{1})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta(q_{1}+r^{2})}{\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1})}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2N}\left(\ln\det(\bm{\Delta})-\frac{N\hat{q}_{1}}{\beta}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})+\beta(\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{\xi})^{T}\bm{c}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\beta}{2N}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right)^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right),

(65)

where we recall
\bm{\Delta}=(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta}\mathbf{C}
and
\bm{W}^{\star}=(\bm{c}-\bm{\xi})-(\bm{\eta}^{T}\bm{c})\bm{\eta}
.

C.2
Saddle point equations

RSA reduce an
(m^{2}+3m)
-dimensional minimization problem into a
6
-dimensional minimization problem. These six saddle point equations are given by setting derivative
\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_{0}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_{1}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{q}_{0}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{q}_{1}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial r}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{r}}=0
be zero. Among those six saddle point equations, four equations are the same as the saddle point equations in classical generalization error problem
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_{1}}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hat{q}_{1}=\frac{\beta^{2}(q_{1}+r^{2})}{(\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1}))^{2}},

(66)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_{0}}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hat{q}_{0}=\frac{\hat{q}_{1}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta}{\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1})},

(67)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{q}_{0}}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow q_{0}=q_{1}+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}),

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial r}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\hskip 3.99994pt\hat{r}=-\frac{\beta r}{\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1})}.

(69)

The remaining two saddle points equation
\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{q}_{1}}=\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{r}}=0
are however quite different. They contain the
contributions from quantum sampling noise

\mathbf{V}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{q}_{1}}=0,\Rightarrow~

\displaystyle q_{1}=\frac{N\hat{q}_{1}}{\beta^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})

\displaystyle\hskip 18.00005pt+\left(\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right)^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.00005pt-\frac{2}{S}\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{V}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right)+\tilde{\sigma}^{2},

(70)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial\hat{r}}=0,\Rightarrow~

\displaystyle r=-\bm{d}^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}-\frac{N\hat{r}}{\beta}\bm{d}-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right).

(71)

Two commonly appearing terms introduce
\kappa
, the
Signal Capture Threshold
(SCT)

\displaystyle\kappa:=~

\displaystyle\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1})=\lambda+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}),

(72)

\displaystyle\frac{2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1}}{\beta}=~

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1})}=-\frac{1}{\kappa}.

(73)

Plugging into
\bm{\Delta}=(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})-\frac{N(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta}\mathbf{C}
yields
\bm{\Delta}=(\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})+\frac{N}{\kappa}\mathbf{C}
. Thus, we get implicit equation of
\kappa
.

\kappa=\lambda+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})=\lambda+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{C}\bm{}\left((\mathbf{I}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})+\frac{N}{\kappa}\mathbf{C}\right)^{-1}\right).

(74)

The value of
r
can be solved by Eq.(
69
) and Eq. (
71
), (notice
\frac{\hat{r}}{\beta}=-\frac{r}{\lambda+\beta(q_{0}-q_{1})}=-\frac{r}{\kappa}=\frac{r(2\hat{q}_{0}-\hat{q}_{1})}{\beta}
)

r=-\bm{d}^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}+\frac{Nr}{\kappa}\bm{d}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right)\Rightarrow r=-\frac{\bm{d}^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\bm{W}^{\star}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\right)}{1+\frac{N}{\kappa}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{d}},\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\hat{r}=-\frac{\beta r}{\kappa}.

(75)

By looking at the expression of
r
, we define for convenience that

\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}=\bm{W}^{\star}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}.

(76)

Solving all remaining four saddle point equations yields

\displaystyle q_{1}=\frac{\frac{Nr^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})+\left(\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}+\frac{Nr}{\kappa}\bm{d}\right)^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}+\frac{Nr}{\kappa}\bm{d}\right)-\frac{2}{S}\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{V}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\left(\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}+\frac{Nr}{\kappa}\bm{d}\right)}{1-\frac{N}{\kappa^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})},

\displaystyle q_{0}=q_{1}+\frac{\kappa-\lambda}{\beta},\quad\hat{q}_{1}=\frac{\beta^{2}(q_{1}+r^{2})}{\kappa^{2}},\quad\hat{q}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{q}_{1}-\frac{\beta}{\kappa}\right).

(77)

Finally, by combining solutions to
q_{0},q_{1},\hat{q}_{0},\hat{q}_{1},r,\hat{r}
, and using the
Sherman–Morrison formula

\left(\bm{\Delta}+\frac{N}{\kappa}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}\right)^{-1}=\bm{\Delta}^{-1}-\frac{N}{\kappa}\frac{\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}}{1+\frac{N}{\kappa}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\bm{d}}
, the replica-symmetry free energy is

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]]=\frac{N}{2}\left[\frac{\kappa-\lambda}{\kappa}-\ln\left(\frac{\kappa}{\lambda}\right)-\frac{\beta\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\kappa}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\ln\det(\bm{\Delta})+\frac{\beta}{2}[\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star T}\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}-(\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{\xi})^{T}\bm{c}]

(78)

where we recall
\bm{\Delta}=\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa}\mathbf{C}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T}
,
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}-\bm{d}\bm{d}^{T}
,
\bm{W}^{\star}=(\bm{c}-\bm{\xi})-(\bm{\eta}^{T}\bm{c})\bm{\eta}
,
\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}=\bm{W}^{\star}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}
, and finally
\kappa
is solved self-consistently through
\kappa=\lambda+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})
. We also newly define the matrix

\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}=\bm{\Delta}+\frac{N}{\kappa}\bm{d}^{T}\bm{d}=\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T},

(79)

Remark
. The correctness of Eq. (
78
) can be checked by taking

. Under this limit, Eq. (
76
) becomes
\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star}=\bm{W}^{\star}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}\rightarrow\bm{W}^{\star}
and Eq. (

\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}=\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T}\rightarrow\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa}\mathbf{G}-\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T}

[

]

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{j}\partial\eta_{k}}(\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star T}\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\widetilde{\bm{W}}^{\star})\right|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}}=~

\displaystyle-2\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})+\frac{N}{\kappa^{2}S}\mathbf{V}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\kappa\right)\bm{c}

\displaystyle+\frac{N}{\kappa^{2}}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\kappa\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}

\displaystyle+\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c},

(89)

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{j}\partial\eta_{k}}\left(-\frac{N\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\kappa}-(\bm{W}^{\star}-\bm{\xi})^{T}\bm{c}\right)\right|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}}=~

\displaystyle\frac{N\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\kappa+\bm{c}^{T}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})\bm{c}.

(90)

We now plug Eq. (
81
) to Eq. (
78
), and collect terms involving
\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\kappa
and
\partial_{j}\partial_{k}(\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{T})
separately. It gives the second order moment

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{w}^{\ast}\bm{w}^{\ast T}]=~

\displaystyle\frac{N(\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})}{\kappa^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{N(\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})}{\kappa^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(2\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\bm{c}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{w}^{\ast}]\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\bm{w}^{\ast T}].

(91)

D.3
Training and generalization errors

We first notice that

-2\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]]|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}}=\frac{N\tilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\kappa}-\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}+\bm{c}^{T}\bm{c}.

(92)

For simplicity, we abbreviate
\delta=\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})
. Then the
\mathcal{D}
-average of optimized weights norm-sqaure is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|^{2}]=~

\displaystyle\frac{N\delta}{1-\gamma}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\left(\frac{\delta\kappa}{1-\gamma}-2\right)\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}

\displaystyle-\frac{2N}{\kappa}\left(\frac{\delta\kappa}{1-\gamma}-1\right)\left(2\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\bm{c}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{\delta\kappa}{1-\gamma}+1\right)\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-2}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}+\bm{c}^{T}\bm{c}.

(93)

By using the relation
\delta\kappa+(\gamma-1)=-\frac{\lambda}{\kappa}

[
31
]
, we obtain the explicit expression of average training error

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\lambda}{N}\left(-\lim_{\beta\rightarrow\infty}\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\langle\ln Z[\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta},\beta]\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\right|_{\bm{\xi},\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\|\bm{w}^{\ast}\|^{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\kappa^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}-2\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\bm{c}\right).

(94)

Eventually, computation of average generalization error is more direct:

\displaystyle E_{g}=~

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\langle\bm{w}^{\ast}\bm{w}^{\ast T}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\right)-2\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\langle\bm{w}^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+1

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\left(\frac{N(\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})}{\kappa^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}+\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\left(\frac{N(\bm{\Delta}^{-1}\mathbf{C}\bm{\Delta}^{-1})}{\kappa^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(2\bm{c}^{T}\left(\mathbf{I}+\frac{N}{\kappa S}\mathbf{V}\right)\widetilde{\bm{\Delta}}^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\bm{c}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\left(\frac{\kappa}{N}\mathbf{I}+\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\right)\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}\bm{c}\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\left(\frac{\kappa}{N}\mathbf{I}+\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\right)\right)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle-2\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{G}\left(\frac{\kappa}{N}\mathbf{I}+\left(\mathbf{G}+\frac{\mathbf{V}}{S}\right)\right)^{-1}\mathbf{G}\bm{c}+1,

(95)

where
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{S}\bm{c}^{T}\mathbf{V}\bm{c}+\mathbf{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]=\bm{c}^{T}\left(-\mathbf{G}+\frac{1}{S}\mathbf{V}\right)\bm{c}+1
.

Appendix E
Transform to Eigentask Basis

Recall in the main text, we decompose the target function
f^{\star}
under basis of both original features Eq. (
5
) and eigentasks Eq. (
6
).

\displaystyle f^{\star}=\bm{c}\cdot\bm{x}+f_{\bot}=\bm{a}\cdot\bm{y}+f_{\bot},

(96)

Also, under eigentask basis, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{I},\quad\mathbf{V}=\mathrm{diag}(\{\beta_{k}^{2}\}),\quad\bm{d}=(1,0,\cdots,0)^{T}.

(97)

Then the average training and generalization errors are

\displaystyle E_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]+\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}}\right)\right)+\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{1+\frac{\beta^{2}_{k}}{S}}{\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}}-\frac{2}{1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}}\right)+1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]+\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}}\right)\right),

(98)

\displaystyle E_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\kappa^{2}(1-\gamma)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}}[f^{2}_{\bot}]+\sum_{k}a_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}+\frac{\kappa}{N}\right)^{2}}\right)\right)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}E_{g}.

(99)

where

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle=\lambda+\kappa\sum_{k>1}\frac{1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)+\kappa},

(100)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\sum_{k>1}\frac{N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)^{2}}{\left(N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)+\kappa\right)^{2}}.

(101)

One important scenario is the small regularization limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
. For sufficiently large data set
N\rightarrow\infty
, it is easy to verify that both Eq. (
98
) and Eq. (
99
) converge to

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}E_{t}(\lambda=0)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}E_{g}(\lambda=0)=1-\sum_{k}\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}.

(102)

We also notice that Eq. (
100
) should have completely different behaviour for
N>K-1
and
N\leq K-1
:

•

If
N>K-1
: Suppose the equation of
\kappa
has a positive solution, then

1=\sum_{k>0}\frac{1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)+\kappa}<\sum_{k>0}\frac{1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}}{N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)}=\frac{K-1}{N}<1,

(103)

which results in a contradiction. It means that
\kappa=0
for
N<K-1
. Similarly,

\gamma=\sum_{k>0}\frac{N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)^{2}}{\left(N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)+\kappa\right)^{2}}=\sum_{k>0}\frac{N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)^{2}}{\left(N\left(1+\frac{\beta_{k}^{2}}{S}\right)\right)^{2}}=\frac{K-1}{N}.

(104)

•

If
N\leq K-1
: Now
\kappa
has non-zero solution
\kappa=\kappa(N)>0
, and
\gamma=\gamma(N)<\frac{K-1}{N}
.

The non-analytical behaviour explains the phase transition phenomenon in Fig.
1
of the main text.
