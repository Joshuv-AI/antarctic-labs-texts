Medium- and Large-sized Mammals on the Plateau: An Evolutionary Crucible for Zoonotic Pathogens — Ecological Drivers, Adaptive Evolution, and One Health Control
Authors: Sha Tan, Jishan Wang, Afito Luciano, Jiani Li, Yuxin Huo, Yuting Ma, Jie Yang, Jiahao Guo, Bing Zhang, Xia He, Mingxu Zhao, Fanming Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlateau regions have emerged as pivotal epicenters of diverse zoonoses because of their distinctive ecological conditions and rich biodiversity. Against the backdrop of intensifying climate change, escalating interactions between wildlife, livestock, and humans, and expanding human activities, these regions now face formidable challenges. To assess public health threats within plateau ecosystems and establish targeted prevention frameworks, this review systematically synthesizes the prevalence and potential cross-species transmission risks of zoonotic pathogens-spanning parasites, viruses, and bacteria-carried by large- and medium-sized wild mammals across China’s four major plateaus (Tibetan Plateau, Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau, Loess Plateau, and Inner Mongolia Plateau). Critical issues, including ecological fragility, complexity of pathogen transmission networks, and delayed responsiveness of control measures, are comprehensively analyzed. Future strategies must embrace the One Health concept to construct a multidimensional, coordinated prevention system. By integrating pathogen surveillance, ecological regulation, and technological innovation, a refined zoonosis control framework anchored in safeguarding plateau biosafety and public health can be systematically advanced.Keywords: Zoonotic diseases, Wildlife, Plateau regions, Pathogen transmission, One health

Introduction

Spanning temperate to subtropical climate zones, ecosystems across China’s major plateau regions, shaped by geographic isolation, harsh environmental traits, and critical ecological functions, constitute fragile yet irreplaceable natural systems. The high-altitude environment-characterized by frigid temperatures, hypoxia, and intense ultraviolet radiation, and an average elevation of ≥ 1000 m (exceeding 4000 m for the Tibetan Plateau)-has driven the evolution of unique adaptive mechanisms in endemic species, such as the Tibetan antelope (Pantholops hodgsonii), wild yak (Bos mutus), and Tibetan wild ass (Equus kiang) [1]. These large- and medium-sized wild mammals serve as keystone species in plateau ecosystems, playing pivotal roles in trophic cascades and biodiversity maintenance [2]. Concurrently, they act as potential natural reservoirs for a diverse range of zoonotic pathogens, like Blastocystis spp., Echinococcus spp., and hepatitis E virus (HEV) [3]. Through migratory behavior, niche overlap, and predator-prey interactions, these species shape the formation of intricate transmission networks [4].

With the increasing development of tourism, the expansion of grazing, the “natural interface” between human-animal-environment is now under the risk of disruption. In recent years, systematic investigations have been conducted on zoonotic pathogens carried by endemic plateau species, focusing on (1) the epidemiological distribution patterns of parasites and bacteria; (2) the molecular evolutionary mechanisms deciphered through whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analyses; and (3) the ecological drivers shaping pathogen transmission networks [5, 6]. Driven by the deepening “One Health” concept, current research hotspots have transitioned from single-pathogen descriptions to interdisciplinary integration.

Research on zoonotic diseases in large- and medium-sized wild mammals in plateau regions constitutes a critical nexus for the integrated development of ecological health, the livestock economy, and public health. Given the intricacy and dynamics of pathogen transmission networks, coupled with the deepening interdisciplinary research guided by the One Health concept, prioritizing these mammals is not merely a requirement for biodiversity conservation in plateau ecosystems but, more crucially, an imperative strategic entry point for preventing emerging infectious diseases and safeguarding regional public health alongside sustainable livestock industry development [7].

This review was conducted through a systematic literature search of Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The search covered publications from 2019 to 2025, using combinations of the following keywords: “plateau, mammals”, “Qinghai-Tibet Plateau”, “wild animals”, “zoonotic pathogens”, “parasites”, “bacteria”, “viruses”, “spillover”, and “One Health”. Only peer-reviewed articles in English or Chinese were included. Studies were screened based on their relevance to large and medium-sized mammals and zoonotic pathogens in China’s plateau regions. Reviews, surveillance reports, and molecular epidemiological studies were prioritized. Duplicate records and studies lacking key epidemiological or molecular evidence were excluded.

Ecological Drivers of Spillover at the Human-Wildlife Interface

The inherent fragility of plateau ecosystems renders them highly sensitive to natural and social changes [8]. In recent years, the intensity and frequency of human activities in these areas have significantly increased, further exacerbating ecological pressures. Tourism development, infrastructure expansion, and agricultural/pastoral intensification increase the contact frequency between humans and wild mammals [9]. These factors collectively pose multifaceted threats to regional ecological security and public health (Fig. 1).

Among the factors, global warming brings the significant impact on the Plateau regions. Over the past 40 years, the Tibetan Plateau has warmed at nearly double the global rate, accelerating glacial retreat and permafrost degradation, significantly altering microbial habitats and distributions [10]. Critically, thawing permafrost may release long-term, ancient pathogens, raising epidemic threats. It also disrupts soil microbial communities, altering pathogen survival and transmission, endangering ecosystems and global biosecurity [11].

Moreover, plateau-dwelling large- and medium-sized wild mammals exhibit unique ecological patterns, with their biodiversity supporting diverse hosts and vectors for zoonotic disease transmission. Yet it is critical to clarify that this rich biodiversity per se does not inherently elevate zoonotic spillover risk; on the contrary, intact ecosystems with high biodiversity exhibit a pronounced dilution effect, where diverse host communities reduce contact rates between highly competent pathogen hosts and limit pathogen spread [12]. The elevated zoonotic risk in plateau regions arises not from biodiversity itself, but from the early-stage loss of ecological integrity triggered by the extreme fragility of these ecosystems: mild anthropogenic disturbances and rapid climate warming disrupt the original composition and structure of plateau biotic communities, suppress the natural dilution effect, and ultimately transform the rich wildlife pathogen pool into actual spillover risk [13]. Across major plateaus (e.g., the Tibetan Plateau and Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau), the mammals exhibit extreme environmental adaptability, including seasonal altitudinal migration in some species. For example, the long-distance migration of P. hodgsonii during mass calving is essentially a strategy to avoid gastrointestinal parasite infection [14]. Plateau ecosystems exhibit a trophic cascade structure anchored by large herbivores and regulated by apex predators. Predator-prey interactions (e.g., Snow leopard (Panthera uncia)-bharal (Pseudois nayaur)) can accelerate pathogen dispersal through food chains [15]. Significant overlap in grazing resources between wild herbivores and livestock facilitates cross-species pathogen transmission via shared habitats or water sources [16].

The high-altitude-adapted pathogens demonstrate enhanced cross-species transmission capabilities, driven by genetic plasticity, recombination events, and host-adaptive evolution. And the plateau’s socioeconomic constraints and traditional pastoral practices have contributed to the persistent burden of zoonoses [17]. Pathogens and distribution of large- and medium-sized wild mammals

To systematically understand the responses of zoonotic pathogens to ecological changes, we have constructed a conceptual framework based on the most sensitive component of their transmission cycle to key ecological drivers. This framework categorizes pathogens into four dominant response patterns which focus on primary intervention targets, thereby guiding control practices with greater precision. These categories are not mutually exclusive: the transmission of many pathogens is synergistically influenced by multiple drivers, reflecting the complexity of ecosystems within the One Health framework.

Climate-Sensitive Pathogens

This group includes pathogens whose life cycles involve a stage that is directly exposed to the external environment (e.g., water, soil, or air). The survival, development, and transmission efficiency of these infectious agents are strongly dependent on temperature and humidity [18]. As a result, this category of disease-causing organisms is predominantly driven by climate change, particularly global warming and shifts in precipitation patterns.

Protozoa

The protozoan genera Blastocystis, Entamoeba, Cryptosporidium, and Toxoplasma primarily colonize the intestinal tract or intracellular spaces of vertebrates. The former three are mainly associated with enteric manifestations, whereas Toxoplasma gondii, the principal zoonotic species within the genus, is capable of causing systemic infections. All four groups share a core fecal-oral transmission route through oocyst- or cyst-contaminated water, food, or direct contact, while T. gondii additionally exhibits trans-placental transmission via tachyzoites. Consequently, their transmission cycle critically depends on the environmental persistence of their infectious stages (oocysts or cysts). Normally, warmer or more humid climates are more favorable to parasites [19–21].

Blastocystis has at least 35 proposed subtypes (STs). Among these, ST1-ST9 are known to infect humans [22]. Among plateau wildlife in China, P. hodgsonii has a 4.8% infection rate, carrying subtypes ST10, ST13, and ST14. Notably, genetic recombination events occur between ST13 (the dominant subtype in P. hodgsonii) and human isolates [23]. In contrast, wild rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) present a high infection rate of 80.1%, with the ST1, ST2, ST3, and ST39 subtypes detected, with ST39 being a novel plateau-endemic subtype reported for the first time [24].

Entamoeba histolytica, classified as a Category B priority pathogen by biodefense agencies, is the only known pathogen causing amebiasis and causes approximately 100,000 human deaths annually worldwide [22]. Other Entamoeba species with potential pathogenicity, such as Entamoeba moshkovskii and Entamoeba nuttalli, have been shown to be pathogenic in mice. In China, provinces including Sichuan and Guangxi, Entamoeba infection rates in M. mulatta reach 69.2%, with significantly higher prevalence in low-altitude (80.2%) versus high-altitude (60.6%) populations (p < 0.001). This correlation is potentially linked to climatic humidity [25].

Among 627 P. hodgsonii fecal samples from Tibet, Cryptosporidium xiaoi and Cryptosporidium ubiquitum were detected at 3.03% prevalence [26]. In 2024, Gao et al. [27] employed nested PCR and genomic sequencing to determine Cryptosporidium infection rate of 14.17% in Mongolian gazelles (Procapra gutturosa), identifying a novel host-adapted genotype. This contributes to our understanding of the genetic diversity and adaptation of Cryptosporidium.

T. gondii is an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite with a broad host range and global distribution. On the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau, domestic yaks (Bos grunniens), the dominant livestock species, graze extensively and often share pasture and water sources with sympatric wildlife, including the plateau pika (Ochotona curzoniae) and Qinghai vole (Microtus fuscus), both of which have been reported to be infected with T. gondii. Serological surveys in yaks on the plateau have reported a seroprevalence of 9.38% (261/2784) based on ELISA detection [21].

Nematodes

Nematodes are unsegmented helminths widely distributed in soil, animal, freshwater, and brackish water. High-altitude pastoral regions serve as core endemic foci because low temperatures delay eggs degradation. Zhou et al. [28] first isolated Trichuris spp. from wild P. nayaur in China’s Helan Mountains via a combined approach that involved morphological characteristics (rectal morphology and egg dimensions) and molecular markers (ITS1 and 18 S rRNA genes), highlighting the necessity of molecular markers for Trichuris classification and the limitations of traditional morphology in distinguishing closely related species. In 2025, Zhu et al. [29] isolated Quilonia sp. larvae from the feces of wild Asian elephants (Elephas maximus) in Yunnan, China, providing the first documentation of morphological photomicrographs and ITS gene data.

Nematodes parasitizing wildlife in plateau regions exhibit significant diversity, including species such as Uncinaria stenocephala, Toxascaris spp., and Crenosoma vulpis. U. stenocephala can infect humans through skin contact or the ingestion of infective larvae, leading to cutaneous larva migrans. Additionally, coinfections are common in wildlife and may exacerbate host pathological damage [30].

Fasciola Hepatica

F. hepatica is endemic in Tibetan Plateau yaks (34.1–72.0% prevalence) and sheep (12.1–61.6%), with geographic variation. F. hepatica infection causes hepatic damage and emaciation in livestock. The adult flukes parasitize bile ducts, releasing eggs through feces, while requiring freshwater snails as intermediate hosts to complete their lifecycle. Humans acquire infection through ingestion of metacercariae-contaminated aquatic plants or untreated water. Infection risk is correlated with local environmental factors: lake-dense regions and abundant rainfall promote transmission [31].

Salmonella

Salmonella is widely distributed in Tibetan pigs across Tibet, with Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella London being the predominant serovars. Whole-genome sequencing revealed that Tibetan strains form a distinct evolutionary cluster (Cluster A), carrying unique antimicrobial resistance genes (e.g., blaTEM-55 and rmtB) and the virulence factor adeG. Crucially, 44.6% of Cluster A strains were isolated from human infections, with Tibetan pig isolates showing tight genetic linkages (≤ 10 core-genome SNPs on average) to human-derived strains within Cluster A. Moreover, the enhanced biofilm formation observed in Tibetan strains at low temperatures may substantially increase their risk of cold-chain transmission through meat trade networks [32, 33].

Vector-Dependent Pathogens

The transmission cycle for this class of infections is entirely dependent on arthropod vectors. The geographical distribution and spillover risk of these diseases are therefore directly dictated by the range and abundance of their vectors, which are in turn shaped by climate change (which determines vector survival and activity ranges) and land-use change (which modifies vector habitats).

Tick-Borne Pathogens

Babesiosis is a vector-borne disease of significant public health concern. Humans can acquire zoonotic Babesia infections through tick bites or blood transfusions. Surviving animals become persistent carriers, sustaining pathogen transmission, while wildlife serves as potential natural reservoirs that exacerbate transmission [34]. In China, multiple Babesia species (e.g., Babesia bovis, Babesia bigemina and Babesia venatorum) infect B. mutus and B. grunniens. The seroprevalence in Tibetan yaks was 27.2%, which was significantly greater than that reported in Qinghai, Sichuan, and Gansu Provinces. This diversity likely stems from conducive ecological conditions, including multi-species tick vectors (e.g., Rhipicephalus and Hyalomma), abundant bovid hosts, and inadequate tick control [35, 36].

Spotted fever group (SFG) rickettsioses are emerging global diseases. The infection rate of Rickettsia raoultii in B. grunniens on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau is 5.9% (25/425), and Haemaphysalis qinghaiensis parasitic on the body surface of B. grunniens might be the reservoir of R. raoultii [37]. Anaplasma spp. are important zoonotic tick-borne pathogens. Wild assess (Equus asinus) show strikingly high Anaplasma IgG seropositivity (82.5%), and stray dogs (Canis sp.) demonstrate 30.1% IgM positivity [38]. Anaplasma infection rates are significantly higher in wild Tianshan red deer (Cervus elaphus songaricus) than in their captive counterparts (73.1% vs. 4.2%) [39].

A novel strain of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV), YNDQL-415G, was isolated from the liver of Chinese serow (Capricornis milneedwardsii) in the Deqin region of Yunnan. Genomic analysis revealed intercontinental reassortment, as the S segment presented 87.03% nucleotide homology with African genotype I strains. CCHFV, which causes severe hemorrhagic fever and death in humans, is a highly pathogenic tick-borne virus [40].

Climate change may drive ticks to expand their distribution to higher altitudes, further extending the transmission range of tick-borne pathogens. These pathogens not only threaten public health but also cause ruminant anemia and productivity losses, directly undermining pastoral economies in plateau regions [41].

Arthropoda

Hippoboscid flies (Diptera: Hippoboscidae), comprising more than 200 known species globally, are obligate blood-sucking ectoparasites of animals and accidental parasites of humans. Mammalian-associated species (e.g., deer ked (Lipoptena cervi) and sheep ked (Melophagus ovinus)) primarily target cervids and sheep, occasionally infesting humans and other livestock [42, 43]. In Xinjiang’s Aksu region, M. ovinus has a high positivity rate (80.1%) for Bartonella spp., predominantly Bartonella melophagi, with 99.5% genetic homology with North American strains. Furthermore, the sheep louse (Linognathus ovillus), a co-parasite on sheep, was also found to carry B. melophagi (showing 99.2% homology with the strains from M. ovinus) [44].

Scabies, caused by the mite Sarcopetes scabiei, is a globally endemic condition that poses emerging threats to wildlife. In the Qinling Mountains, scabies has led to substantial caused significant mortality in C. milneedwardsii and goral (Naemorhedus goral) individuals [45]. Molecular analysis of the ITS2 and COI sequences suggested that global dissemination is linked to the international livestock trade, with certain haplotypes (e.g., COI-C26) detected in humans, domestic dogs, and wild boars, underscoring the need for enhanced surveillance to mitigate cross-border transmission [46].

Contact-Driven Pathogens

This mode of transmission is characterized by pathogens that require high host density and frequent, close contact to sustain themselves. The primary driver for the emergence of these pathogens is land-use change (urbanization and tourism development) and the expansion of livestock industry, which increases the frequency and intensity of interactions at the human-wildlife-livestock interface, creating ideal conditions for spillover. An effective control strategy centers on implementing host monitoring, standardizing livestock industry practices, and promoting public health awareness [47].

Echinococcus spp

Echinococcosis transmits via eggs in definitive host (dogs/foxes) feces, infecting diverse intermediate hosts (human/livestock/wildlife). Pathogen transmission between definitive and intermediate hosts depends on complex zoonotic cycles. In western pastoral regions of China, the disease significantly hinders economic development in affected communities [47].

Echinococcus granulosus, the primary etiological agent of cystic echinococcosis (CE), develops slowly growing cysts in the liver, lungs, and brain of intermediate hosts. After infection, significant alterations in the gut microbiota occur in intermediate hosts, such as Tibetan sheep (Ovis aries), potentially modulating host immunity and parasite colonization [48]. Mitochondrial genome sequencing effectively distinguished G1/G3 from G6/G7 genotypes, revealing G1 genotype dominance across seven Tibetan prefectures and providing critical molecular foundations for regional control strategies [49].

Echinococcus multilocularis causes alveolar echinococcosis (AE), termed “worm cancer”, owing to its invasive growth pattern. AE lesions are predominantly localized in the liver, with 10-year mortality rates reaching 90% in untreated patients [16]. High-risk areas in China include the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, western Sichuan, and northern Xinjiang, which exhibit significant spatial overlap with the distributions of wild foxes (Vulpes spp.) and stray dogs [47, 50]. The 4.03% infection rate of E. multilocularis in Qinghai’s wild foxes (Tibetan fox (Vulpes ferrilata), Red fox (Vulpes vulpes), and Corsac fox (Vulpes corsac)) confirms their crucial role as reservoir hosts in natural foci [51].

Echinococcus shiquicus, first reported in the Qilian region (Qinghai-Sichuan border), shows high prevalence in Qinghai wild foxes. Although human pathogenicity remains unconfirmed, the discovery of E. shiquicus extends the genetic diversity of Echinococcus, while its biological traits and public health implications require further investigation [51].

Rabies virus (RABV)

RABV, a negative-sense RNA virus of the genus Lyssavirus, causes rabies, a fatal neurological disease in humans and other mammals. The virus is transmitted mainly through bites or scratches via saliva from infected animals and is maintained in a wide range of mammalian host species. As a neglected zoonotic disease, rabies continues to impose a substantial global burden, with approximately 59,000 human deaths reported each year [52]. The prevalence rate of rabies virus (RABV) in V. vulpes populations across Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia is 7.8%. Its nucleoprotein gene sequence shows 99.3% genetic homology with local livestock isolates, confirming the pivotal reservoir role of foxes in RABV maintenance [53].

Staphylococcus Aureus and MRSA

On the Chinese plateau, the yak is the dominant livestock animal, domesticated from the B. mutus. S. aureus isolated from yaks in Ganzi, Sichuan, included 11 sequence types (STs), with 6 newly reported ones (from ST6262 to ST6267). Seven MRSA isolates were detected, all of which were ST59 and belonged to CC59, the dominant lineage of community-acquired MRSA in China. This confirms the human origin of yak-derived MRSA. These MRSA strains also carried the human immune evasion cluster (IEC), retaining human-infective potential [54].

Mycobacterium Tuberculosis Complex (MTC)

MTC is the primary pathogen causing tuberculosis (TB), and M. tuberculosis infection has impacted elephant populations, particularly E. maximus. In Chitwan National Park, Nepal, the elephant-derived M. tuberculosis strains DG (Lineage 1.2.2.2) and PK (Lineage 2.2) are each closely related evolutionarily to human isolates of the same lineages in the region, suggesting bidirectional transmission between humans and elephants. High-density human-elephant contact, particularly during tourism and patrolling in protected areas, exacerbates cross-species transmission risks [55].

Brucella

Globally, Brucella is endemic in multiple mammals and infects humans through direct contact with sick animals or their products. Brucella antibodies or strains have been detected in wild red deer (Cervus elaphus), reindeer (Rangifer tarandus), and B. mutus distributed in Qinghai and Tibet [56]. Notably, Brucella abortus biovar 4 (strain XZ19-1) was first identified in Tibetan yaks. Phylogenetic analysis confirmed its divergence from historical Chinese isolates, indicating that it may represent a unique plateau-endemic lineage [57].

Host-Generalist Pathogens

Agents within this category pose a distinct threat due to their broad host range, ability to persist in the environment, and diversity in transmission routes. They can flexibly capitalize on opportunities created by various drivers to maintain transmission in new geographical areas, at novel human-wildlife-livestock interfaces, and within new host communities.

Hepatitis E Virus (HEV)

As a significant pathogen within the Hepeviridae family, HEV has notable epidemiological implications because of its classification system and host diversity. The genus Paslahepevirus, under Orthohepevirinae, encompasses eight genotypes (HEV-1 to HEV-8), demonstrating a host specificity gradient: HEV-1/2 are strictly restricted to humans and nonhuman primates. HEV-3/4 has pronounced zoonotic potential and can infect mammals, including Tibetan pigs (Sus scrofa domesticus) and cervids (Cervidae). HEV-5/6: Host-specific viruses are currently detected exclusively in wild boar (Sus scrofa) populations. HEV-7/8: Emerging genotypes that can not only infect camelids (Camelidae) but also have experimentally confirmed ability to infect non-human primates [58–60]. This genotype-host correspondence is particularly prominent in plateau ecosystems, where active viral circulation has been documented in yaks and wild boars. Notably, farmed wild boars present a significantly greater seroprevalence (76.2%) than wild populations do (42.1%), with cocirculation of multiple subtypes. This indicates that artificial rearing environments may accelerate viral genetic divergence by increasing host density and contact frequency [59].

Porcine Circovirus (PCV)

PCV is the smallest known non-enveloped animal virus. PCV1-3 strains circulate in Chinese wild boars, with PCV2 DNA detected in 58.3% of tissue samples and PCV2b/PCV2d as the dominant genotypes [61]. Zhu et al. [62] reported PCV-like virus P1 (10.48%) and PCV2 (4.76%) in Tibetan yaks, with 5.71% coinfection rates. Yak-originated P1 and PCV2 strains harbor distinct evolutionary signatures, which are postulated to have arisen from extreme plateau conditions.

Feline Panleukopenia Virus (FPV) and Canine Parvovirus (CPV)

Represented by FPV and CPV, the species carnivore protoparvovirus 1 is widely distributed worldwide through continuous circulation in companion animals. Subsequently, both viruses have engaged in host-to-host transmission to other wild Carnivora (e.g., giant pandas (Ailuropoda melanoleuca)). An FPV strain (pFPV-sc) was isolated from captive giant pandas that had exhibited fatal symptoms. Notably, pFPV-sc can also infected human (HeLa, HEK-293T), porcine, and other mammalian cell lines, demonstrating broad cross-species cellular tropism [63].

Antelope Torovirus (AToV) and Aichivirus D (AiV-D)

A novel AToV detected in P. hodgsonii feces underwent recombination within the HE gene region with bovine-derived strains (bovine torovirus B150 and B155) from the Netherlands. This indicates that AToV has undergone cross-species transmission, and further relevant research is needed [4]. Concurrently, a novel AiV-D isolated from diarrheic yaks demonstrated fecal-oral transmission in animal models. AiV-D is a newly emerging Kobuvirus, members of which cause diarrheal diseases in humans and animals worldwide. Histopathology confirmed that AiV-D induced intestinal epithelial apoptosis, indicating significant pathogenic potential [64].

Enterocytozoon Bieneusi

E. bieneusi, the most prevalent pathogenic microsporidian in humans, exhibits high genetic diversity with over 500 genotypes, many of which are zoonotic and circulate in a wide range of animal hosts [65]. On the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, B. grunniens presents high infection rates, reaching 23.8% in Sichuan’s Ganzi region [66]. The overall prevalence among livestock and wildlife near Qinghai Lake is 2.77%. Towards Integrated Solutions under a One Health Framework

Significant knowledge gaps persist in current research: zoonotic studies on plateau large- and medium-sized wildlife are systematically insufficient, due to poor research infrastructure (from extreme geography and harsh field conditions), plus socioeconomic factors like slow traditional pastoralism transition and inefficient grassroots animal disease surveillance [68]. For example, endangered plateau species, such as P. uncia, inhabit remote, high-altitude areas with sparse populations and monitoring difficulties, resulting in chronically scarce pathogen data, which highlights the ecological uniqueness of wildlife disease dynamics in the highlands [69]. While captive data serve as “indirect evidence” of ecological parameters, they require validation through field-based empirical studies. This research lag is particularly acute in critical scientific domains such as pathogen transmission dynamics and host adaptive evolution, directly impeding evidence-based prevention strategies [70]. A growing concern is that accelerated globalization is reshaping disease transmission patterns, as expanded transportation networks and the livestock trade, facilitate the dissemination of cross-regional pathogens [71]. Overall, plateau regions face four core challenges: deficient baseline pathogen data, increasingly complex transmission networks, fragile biosecurity barriers, and climate-amplified ecological risks.

To address the challenges mentioned above, current research frameworks have achieved breakthroughs through multidimensional technological pathways: (1) Drones with high-definition cameras and infrared sensors enable large-scale, continuous monitoring of high-altitude, complex terrain; real-time data transmitted to intelligent centers supports dynamic tracking of wildlife populations/habitats, aiding wilderness zoonotic transmission model construction [1]. (2) Deployment of high-throughput laboratory platforms, field-based rapid diagnostics, and AI-powered monitoring technologies to establish baseline pathogen databases, providing scientific foundations for prevention [72]. (3) The impacts of climate change and human activities on wildlife habitats are assessed through modeling, and targeted conservation measures are formulated accordingly [73]. (4) Optimize livestock management protocols, build community-participatory prevention/control networks, and formulate precision deworming plans; reduce direct livestock-wildlife contact via herder behavioral interventions, and promote safe drinking-water projects and standardized food hygiene handling [71, 74]. (5) The establishment of interdisciplinary coordination mechanisms integrates ecology, human medicine, veterinary science, and public health resources, enabling early identification of potential zoonotic spillover risks, designing ecoadaptive intervention nodes [75] (Figure 3).

Limitations

Current evidence limits robust cross-plateau comparisons among China’s four major plateau systems. Most available studies are based on cross-sectional surveys reporting pathogen detection or infection prevalence at a single time point, whereas long-term longitudinal monitoring datasets (e.g., ≥ 5 years) remain scarce. This limits inference on temporal dynamics, reservoir maintenance, and the impacts of climate warming and anthropogenic activities on pathogen transmission. Furthermore, the roles of rodents, bats, and domestic animals as bridge hosts remain underrepresented in the current synthesis. Finally, the proposed prevention and control strategies require further field-based validation to confirm their effectiveness and feasibility in plateau socio-ecological contexts.

Conclusion

This review systematically synthesizes the diversity of pathogens carried by large- to medium-sized mammals distributed on plateau regions of China, and their different ecological response patterns and transmission risks. We propose enhancing the ecological adaptability and social sustainability of prevention strategies by bridging technological innovation with traditional ecological knowledge [7, 76]. This approach aims to establish a multidimensional “One Health” collaborative prevention framework. This paradigm not only provides a scientific pathway for synergistic “ecological-economic-health” governance in plateau regions but also contributes to a China-developed model of “risk assessment-targeted intervention-sustainable development” for infectious disease control in ecologically sensitive areas worldwide, highlighting the interdependence of maintaining ecological barriers and enhancing public health resilience.
