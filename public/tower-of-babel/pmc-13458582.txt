A secure encryption-steganography method for public images using symmetric keys without prior synchronization
Authors: Yosef Golovachev, Ephraim Ashush, Benjamin Milgrom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Secure data transmission within digital images remains a critical challenge due to vulnerabilities to key interception and steganalysis attacks. Traditional steganographic schemes often require shared keys, pre-trained models, or prior coordination, which limits their practical deployment in open environments without prior synchronization or shared secrets. This paper introduces a symmetric dual-key encryption-steganography hybrid, inspired by one-time pad (OTP) principles, that enables secure image-based communication without any key exchange or prior shared knowledge. The method achieves high secrecy and imperceptibility, embedding hidden data without introducing visible distortions or statistical artifacts. The approach is lightweight, general, and does not depend on training or image-specific assumptions. Experimental validation on 100 natural images demonstrates strong resilience to advanced steganalysis, high visual quality (SSIM > 0.97, PSNR > 40 dB), and secure hidden data transmission. These results highlight the method’s practical value as a robust and transparent solution for sensitive image-based communication, without the limitations of prior coordination or machine learning infrastructure.

Introduction

Secure communication is critical when transmitting sensitive information over public channels. Cryptography ensures confidentiality by transforming readable data (plaintext) into unreadable ciphertext using encryption algorithms. However, encrypted communication alone may attract attention or raise suspicion. In contrast, steganography conceals the very existence of information by embedding it within seemingly innocent digital media such as images or audio files1. Combining both approaches strengthens security: not only is the content encrypted, but its presence is also hidden.

A central challenge in steganography lies in balancing security, imperceptibility, and robustness against detection. Basic techniques like Least Significant Bit (LSB) embedding2 are computationally efficient but highly vulnerable to statistical detection methods such as histogram analysis and chi-square tests. More advanced frequency-domain techniques, such as the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) and Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)3, improve resilience against pixel-level analysis, yet still leave structural traces exploitable by modern steganalysis tools. The rise of machine-learning-based steganalysis4–7 has further intensified the need for methods that remain statistically undetectable under increasingly sophisticated analysis.

Recent years have witnessed the rapid evolution of image steganography, particularly through the use of deep learning. Approaches such as HiDDeN and GAN-based methods enable joint optimization of embedding and extraction, achieving high embedding capacity, imperceptibility, and robustness against compression and noise8,9. Hybrid models combining frequency-domain embeddings (e.g., DCT, DWT) with adaptive region selection via convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated improved resistance against advanced steganalysis tools10,11. Reinforcement learning techniques have also been proposed to dynamically select embedding locations12. In parallel, research on encryption-steganography integration has shown that chaotic encryption or symmetric schemes can enhance robustness; however, such systems often require key exchange protocols, which introduce vulnerabilities during transmission13,14. Despite these advances, current methods face significant limitations in balancing imperceptibility, robustness, computational efficiency, and the elimination of explicit key exchange.

Recent studies, such as Deb et al. (2019) and Deb et al. (2024), demonstrate that Linear Feedback Shift Register (LFSR)-based encryption schemes achieve strong security through pseudo-random keystream generation, chaotic scrambling, and advanced diffusion mechanisms, often combined with secure key exchange protocols such as Elliptic Curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH)15,16.

In contrast, the proposed method follows a fundamentally different paradigm. Rather than relying on synchronized keystream generation or shared keys, it enables independent key generation by both sender and receiver without any key exchange or prior coordination. Security is achieved through a structured sequence of encryption and embedding operations, rather than reproducible pseudo-random processes.

Materials and methods

Image encryption and steganographic embedding

This study introduces a dual-stage encryption-steganography method for securely embedding encrypted data within digital images while maintaining imperceptibility. The method builds upon fundamental principles of cryptography and steganography21–23. Classical cryptographic techniques such as the OTP provide theoretical perfect secrecy, but are limited in practice due to the requirement for secure key distribution24,25. To address this challenge, the proposed method introduces a symmetric dual-key mechanism, where both the sender and receiver independently generate random encryption keys without requiring any key exchange. At every stage of the embedding process, at least one encryption key is present in the image, ensuring that the data is never exposed in an unencrypted or partially encrypted state. This design significantly enhances operational security and protects against interception during transmission.

To further reduce detectability and visual artifacts, the method operates in the YCbCr color space instead of RGB. By applying encryption and embedding operations to the chrominance channels (Cb and Cr)—which are less perceptible to the human visual system than the luminance channel (Y)—the method minimizes visible distortions while improving statistical resilience to detection techniques26.

Alternative encryption scheme with reduced communication steps

Although the primary method effectively avoids key sharing, it necessitates multiple communication exchanges. To explore whether communication rounds could be reduced, an alternative method was developed and evaluated.

While the dual-key encryption-steganography method presented in Sect. 2.1 offers robust protection and avoids key exchange, its implementation requires multiple communication exchanges. These repeated transmissions may increase the risk of exposure, particularly if different versions of the same image are intercepted during transit. To address this, an alternative scheme was evaluated, designed to reduce the number of transmissions while preserving the principle that the data is always encrypted.

In this method, the receiver first encrypts the image using a symmetric key (Key A) and sends the encrypted image to the sender. The sender then embeds the hidden information directly into this encrypted image. Since embedding occurs on an already-encrypted image, the data is never exposed in plaintext or semi-encrypted form.

To improve resilience against detection, the sender distributes the hidden data across all three RGB channels, inserting misleading (decoy) data into two of the channels. The correct data is placed according to a predetermined channel sequence, derived from a one-way hash function (e.g., SHA-512). This sequence is shared in advance between sender and receiver.

Upon receiving the image, the receiver removes the encryption (Key A) and extracts the data from the correct channels based on the agreed-upon sequence. Although this method reduces the communication to two transmission steps, it requires pre-shared knowledge between sender and receiver, such as the channel sequence or the hash protocol used to derive it. This dependency on prior coordination reintroduces the very type of shared secret the original method avoids, undermining the primary benefit of eliminating key exchange.

A further simplified variant, which omits encryption entirely and relies solely on the channel sequence for data embedding, was also tested. However, this version exposes roughly one-third of the embedded data to direct analysis, making it insecure and impractical.

Summary

While the alternative scheme reduces the number of transmission steps, it requires prior shared knowledge (e.g., a predefined channel sequence or hash function parameters) between the sender and receiver. This requirement reintroduces a fundamental limitation of classical encryption systems. In contrast, the main method proposed in this study does not rely on any pre-shared secret, thereby offering a secure and autonomous encryption-steganography approach that eliminates key exchange and prior coordination. This distinction highlights the novelty and practical advantage of the proposed method.

Implementation and dataset

The proposed encryption-steganography hybrid was implemented and evaluated using MATLAB R2023a (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA). The implementation leveraged several specialized toolboxes, including the Image Processing Toolbox, Signal Processing Toolbox, Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox, and Wavelet Toolbox. Cryptographic hashing and key generation were handled via standard Java security libraries (java.security, javax.crypto, and javax.crypto.spec). Using this environment, The algorithm was rigorously tested on a dataset of over 100 natural images. The image used for demonstration in this manuscript was sourced from FreeArtBackgrounds.com, while the other publicly available images were collected from the Pixabay repository (pixabay.com) under a free-use license. The remaining images in the dataset were privately captured by the authors. These images were chosen to represent a wide range of textures, color distributions, and structural patterns to rigorously assess the robustness and generalizability of the method under realistic image conditions28. We confirm that all images were used with full consent and do not contain identifiable personal information.

Results analysis

Quantitative analysis

The performance of the proposed encryption-steganography method was evaluated using ten statistical metrics and three perceptual/information-based metrics: Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), and Fisher Information. Each metric was computed for five image versions, as defined in Sect. 2.6.

Figure 6 presents the average ratio between each processed image and its original counterpart for ratio-based statistical metrics, including LSB Ratio, Chi-Square, Entropy, Pixel Correlation, and Fisher Information. Most values remained close to 1, indicating high statistical similarity and minimal distortion. Slight deviations were observed in the Chi-Square values for the red channel and in several SPAM-derived features, especially in the “img coded” version where data embedding occurred.

Figure 7 shows the average absolute differences in metrics related to variability and frequency content, such as DCT and DWT coefficients. The image containing embedded data (“img coded”) exhibited the highest deviations, particularly in DCT-based features, while the singly encrypted versions (“img enc A” and “img enc B”) showed minimal changes.

Figure 8 includes two types of results. First, it displays the perceptual similarity metrics — PSNR and SSIM — which confirm that all image versions preserved high visual quality, with SSIM values consistently above 0.97 and PSNR exceeding 40 dB. Second, the figure reports the percentage of changed pixels between each image version and the original. This measure is not an evaluation metric per se but serves to verify that meaningful modifications were indeed applied to the image. Discussion

Building on the quantitative comparisons presented earlier, the following section interprets the findings in terms of security and visual fidelity.

Effectiveness of the primary encryption method

The primary encryption-steganography method exhibited strong statistical stealth across nearly all evaluation metrics. As shown in Figs. 6, 7 and 8, the encrypted images maintained LSB ratios, entropy levels, DCT/DWT coefficients, and high-order statistical features (SPAM and Rich Models) that closely matched those of the original images. These results indicate that the embedding process introduced minimal detectable alterations, even under targeted steganalysis.

Chi-Square deviations in the red channel were moderate but remained within acceptable bounds, suggesting no systematic statistical artifacts. Adjacent pixel correlation decreased slightly, consistent with increased randomness while preserving the image’s structural integrity.

Importantly, the method demonstrated robustness across frequency and spatial domains simultaneously—a combination that many classical steganography methods fail to achieve. This dual resilience enhances the method’s resistance to both conventional and modern detection techniques.

Finally, the continuous encryption throughout the embedding process ensured that no plaintext image ever existed during transmission. This design—based on independently generated symmetric keys (discussed in Sect. 2.1)—eliminates the need for prior key exchange and forms the cryptographic foundation of the method’s security.

The experimental results confirm that the proposed encryption-steganography hybrid effectively conceals encrypted data while minimizing detectability. This section interprets the findings, compares the two encryption approaches, and discusses their practical and theoretical security implications.

Effectiveness of the second method

As shown in Figs. 9, 10 and 11, this method achieved superior PSNR and SSIM scores compared to the original approach, indicating closer perceptual resemblance to the original image. The final embedded image exhibited smoother behavior in frequency-domain metrics (DCT and DWT), suggesting better alignment with natural image statistics.

However, this perceptual advantage came at the cost of increased statistical detectability. Greater deviations were observed in several SPAM features and in the Chi-Square test—particularly in the red channel—raising the likelihood of detection by advanced steganalysis techniques. These results point to reduced statistical consistency, especially in higher-order and sensitive color-channel metrics.

Another limitation of the second method lies in its reliance on pre-shared knowledge between the sender and receiver. While this approach avoids transmitting an explicit key, it assumes a secure prior agreement on the embedding structure or key sequence. As such, it partially reintroduces the key exchange vulnerability that the original method sought to eliminate.

Comparative evaluation and security implications

The comparison between the two encryption-steganography methods highlights a fundamental trade-off between statistical security and perceptual quality. The original method demonstrated superior resilience to statistical steganalysis across most metrics, including Chi-Square, SPAM, and Rich Models. Its design ensures that no shared secrets or explicit key exchanges are required, as both parties independently generate and remove their own keys. This feature eliminates a common vulnerability in traditional cryptographic systems.

In contrast, the second method prioritizes visual fidelity and requires fewer transmission steps. It achieved higher PSNR and SSIM scores, indicating minimal perceptual distortion, and produced smoother behavior in DCT and DWT-based analyses. However, this simplicity comes with two drawbacks: increased detectability under certain statistical metrics, and reliance on pre-shared knowledge between sender and receiver. This reintroduces a partial dependency on a shared secret or synchronization mechanism, which may weaken its security in some contexts.

In practical applications, such as messaging platforms or social media, the transmission of multiple images within a single communication session is common. However, the use of multiple versions of the same image may introduce a potential vulnerability to differential analysis, where an attacker could exploit differences between versions to infer embedded information. In such environments, the transmission overhead of the proposed method is not necessarily prohibitive. Moreover, repeated use of the protocol does not inherently compromise security, as the embedded data remains encrypted and each transmission incorporates a newly generated random key.

Therefore, while the second method offers efficiency and strong visual quality, the original method remains preferable in scenarios where statistical invisibility and independence from shared secrets are essential.

Summary

This section compared two encryption-steganography approaches, each offering distinct advantages. The original method provides strong statistical invisibility and avoids the need for shared secrets, making it suitable for secure applications where detection risk must be minimized. In contrast, the second method achieves higher visual quality and lower transmission overhead but depends on pre-shared knowledge and shows greater vulnerability to certain steganalysis techniques.

These findings underscore a fundamental trade-off between statistical robustness and operational efficiency. The choice between the two methods should be guided by the specific requirements of the communication scenario—whether the priority is undetectable transmission and cryptographic independence, or reduced complexity and visual transparency.

Future research should explore adaptive techniques capable of optimizing both security and efficiency, potentially leveraging dynamic embedding strategies, key coordination frameworks, and robustness against emerging steganalysis methods such as deep learning-based detectors.

Conclusions

This study proposed a secure encryption-steganography method for concealing encrypted information within public images while minimizing detectability. The method integrates dual-key symmetric encryption—eliminating the need for key exchange—with embedding in the YCbCr color space, taking advantage of reduced visual sensitivity to chrominance variations. This strategy minimizes visible distortion and enhances resistance to statistical steganalysis.

Experimental results on 100 natural images confirmed that the primary method preserves both visual and statistical properties while resisting conventional steganalysis tools such as histogram, correlation, entropy, and Chi-Square tests. Unlike traditional LSB techniques, the proposed approach maintains imperceptibility without compromising cryptographic strength.

To explore communication efficiency, an alternative method was tested with fewer transmission steps. While it improved perceptual quality (higher PSNR and SSIM), it introduced greater statistical deviations—particularly in SPAM and Chi-Square metrics—highlighting a trade-off between visual fidelity and statistical stealth. Moreover, this alternative requires pre-shared knowledge, reintroducing a vulnerability that the primary method avoids.

Despite the primary method’s robustness, limitations remain. Its reliance on multiple transmissions may expose it to differential analysis, although such patterns are unlikely to raise suspicion in high-traffic environments like messaging platforms. Furthermore, the method is not robust to compression or format conversion, due to the minimal and randomized nature of the embedded modifications. Advanced detection techniques, including deep-learning-based steganalysis, may still pose a challenge.

Future work should aim to develop adaptive embedding strategies, enhance resistance to compression, and expand the method to other media types such as video and audio. Incorporating adversarial learning or generative techniques may further strengthen the method’s stealth and resilience.

In summary, this research presents a practical framework for secure, image-based communication that balances encryption strength with imperceptibility, offering an effective solution for sensitive data transmission in real-world scenarios.
