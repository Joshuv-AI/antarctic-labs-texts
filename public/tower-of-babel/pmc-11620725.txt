Correcting Severe Relapsed Hallux Varus Using Hexapod Circular External Fixator
Authors: Sherif Galal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Congenital hallux varus is an unusual condition in which the big toe is medially angulated at the metatarsophalangeal joint. Different surgical methods have been described to correct it; however, there is always a risk of recurrence. The multiplanar nature of the deformity and the disrupted anatomy from a previous surgery make recurrent cases difficult to treat. A 16-year-old adolescent boy presented with a recurrent hallux varus deformity after a previous surgical correction. This case report describes its successful treatment using a hexapod circular external fixator as a salvage procedure.

Congenital hallux varus is an uncommon condition that is defined as medial angulation of the great toe at the metatarsophalangeal joint. The toe deformity can range from only a few degrees to as severe as 90°.1 Various surgical techniques (that includes soft-tissue and/or bony procedure) have been described.2 A thickened medial capsule, medial inclination of the first metatarsocuneiform joint line, the first metatarsal longitudinal epiphyseal bracket, short first metatarsals, and an imbalance between the adductor hallucis and abductor hallucis muscles are all risk factors of recurrence.3 Recurrent cases are hard to treat given the three-dimensional nature of the deformity, namely supination, medial deviation, and clawing of the great toe, as well as scars or secondary bony deformation from a previous surgery.4 This case report describes how a hexapod circular external fixator was used to successfully treat a recurrent congenital hallux varus with a hypertrophic medial scar as a salvage procedure.

Case Report

Patient Information

The patient is a 16-year-old adolescent boy who presented with a recurrent right hallux varus deformity. His surgical history included an open surgery to correct the deformity at the age of 1 year through soft-tissue release only (Figure 1) through a medial approach. He presented to our service with deformity and an inability to wear regular shoes.

Radiographs of original hallux varus deformity during childhood.

Clinical Findings

On examination, the right big toe showed marked medial deviation (Figure 2A), supination, and plantar flexion (clawing) deformity. It also had a medial hypertrophic scar (Figure 2B) from a previous surgery. The first metatarsophalangeal joint range of motion was 20° dorsiflexion and 20° plantar flexion without pain when moving or walking.

Images showing the (A) clinical frontal view and (B) clinical side view of preoperative deformity.

Diagnostic Assessment

Standing foot plain radiographs (Figure 3, A and B) showed maintained joint space, no osteophytes with medial subluxation, and the first metatarsal was short with a reduced head size and reduced articular surface size. The patient was interested in a salvage solution rather than joint fusion, given the contracted medial scar, his tendency for scar contracture, short metatarsal, and magnitude of the deformity. After counseling, the patient agreed to use a hexapod circular external fixator to correct the deformity.

A, Preoperative anteroposterior plain radiograph; (B) preoperative lateral plain radiograph.

Intervention

A “butt-frame” configuration hexapod circular external fixator was used with one full ring over the forefoot and a “U-plate full ring” block was mounted to the hindfoot and tibia. One (size 3 mm) Schanz pin was inserted in the first metatarsal head parallel to the first metatarsal distal articular surface; then through a small dorsal stab wound, the osteotomy site was marked (a distal subcapital osteotomy, made parallel to the pin) and then predrilled using a 2-mm Kirschner wire and finally completed with a small osteotome. The pin was fixed to the forefoot ring so that it is mounted orthogonally to the big toe in the frontal plane (Figure 4). A 2-mm Kirschner wire was used to stabilize the interphalangeal joint and metatarsophalangeal joint of the big toe and was fixed to the ring so that the ring is orthogonal to the big toe in the sagittal plane. The proximal ring was fixed to the calcaneus using two olive wires and to the tibia with two olive wires. The distal ring was the reference ring, and mounting parameters were taken. The rings were then connected using six struts. On the fifth postoperative day, distraction was initiated at a rate of 0.5 mm/day. The osteotomy site was lengthened by 10 mm and extended to help correct the big toe clawing, and medial translation was done to create a “reverse-Chevron” effect at the osteotomy site (Figure 5).

Follow-up

Two adjustment schedules were needed to achieve the desired correction (Figure 6) over the course of 1 month. The frame was maintained for another month for osteotomy consolidation, after which the frame was removed, and a short leg cast was applied for another month. Protected weight bearing (using two crutches) was allowed in the cast and then advanced to weight bearing as tolerated in a controlled ankle motion walker boot for another month after cast removal (Figure 7, A and B). Range-of-motion exercises for the first metatarsophalangeal joint were started once the cast was removed. Four months after the surgery, the patient was able to wear regular shoes and resume daily activities (Figure 8, A and B). The last follow-up was 1 year after the surgery.

Discussion

Recurrent hallux varus is a difficult condition to treat because of the disrupted anatomy from a previous surgery. The deformity is three-dimensional and may be very severe, with a need to lengthen a short first metatarsal.3 Fusion surgery of the first metatarsophalangeal joint has been suggested to treat these severe conditions, but it becomes challenging when there is an associated soft-tissue envelope deficiency or contractures or scar formation tendency, which poses a recurrence risk.4

Hexapod circular external fixator has been used to correct complex foot deformities5 because of its versatility and its ability to gradually lengthen bone and soft tissues. For good preoperative planning, it is important to understand the three-dimensional nature of hallux varus deformity.2 It is also very important that the surgeon is familiar with the use of the hexapod fixator. It is crucial to properly counsel patients about the slow process of using hexapods and the slower recovery compared with joint fusion surgery,6 emphasizing the importance of patient compliance. If properly executed, deformity correction using a hexapod circular fixator may offer an alternative to fusion for young patients seeking joint preservation surgery. It is crucial to carefully select patients and educate them about the potential risks of acute correction, which could lead to neurovascular compromise; gradual correction could be a safer option for these patients.

Conclusion

To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first report of using the hexapod circular external fixator to correct severe congenital hallux varus associated with soft-tissue contracture. The case suggests that in the setting of soft-tissue contracture and severe three-dimensional deformity with shortening, the hexapod external fixator may be a useful tool for correction as a salvage procedure instead of joint fusion, especially for young active patients.
