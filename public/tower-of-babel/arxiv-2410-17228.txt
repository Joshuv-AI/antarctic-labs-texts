Classical patterns in Mallows permutations
Victor Dubach

Abstract
We study classical pattern counts in Mallows random permutations with parameters ( n , q n ) (n,q_{n}) , as n → ∞ n\to\infty . We focus on three different regimes for the parameter q = q n q=q_{n} . When n 3 / 2 ​ ( 1 − q ) → 0 n^{3/2}(1-q)\to 0 , we use coupling techniques to prove that pattern counts in Mallows random permutations satisfy a central limit theorem with the same asymptotic mean and variance as in uniformly random permutations. When q → 1 q\to 1 and n ⁡ ( 1 − q ) → ∞ n(1-q)\to\infty , we use results on the displacements of permutation points to find the order of magnitude of pattern counts. When q ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) q\in(0,1) is fixed, we use the regenerative property of the Mallows distribution to compare pattern counts with certain U U -statistics, and establish central limit theorems. We also construct a specific Mallows process, that is a coupling of Mallows distributions with q q ranging from 0 0 to 1 1 , for which the process of pattern counts satisfies a functional central limit theorem.

Background

1.1
Mallows random permutations

Fix a real parameter
q\geq 0
and a positive integer
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We say that a random permutation
\tau_{n}
follows the
Mallows distribution
with parameter
q
on the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, denoted
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
, if:

\text{for all $\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}$},\quad\mathbf{P}\left(\tau_{n}=\sigma\right)=\frac{q^{{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma\right)}}{Z_{n,q}}

where
{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma\right):=\left|\left\{(i,j)\in[n]^{2}\,:\,i<j\,,\,\sigma(i)>\sigma(j)\right\}\right|
is the number of
inversions
of
\sigma
, and
Z_{n,q}:=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}
is a normalizing constant.
The parameter
q
serves as a
“bias”
on the number of inversions of
\tau_{n}
.
The number of inversions of
\tau_{n}
grows stochastically as
q
grows:
if
q=0
then
\tau_{n}=\mathrm{id}_{n}:=1\,2\dots n
almost surely, if
q=1
then
\tau_{n}\sim{\rm Unif}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)
is uniformly random, and if
q\to\infty
then
\tau_{n}\to\mathrm{id}_{n}^{\mathrm{rev}}:=n\dots 2\,1
in probability.

The Mallows distribution was introduced in
[
Mal57
]
, motivated by ranking problems in statistics.
There actually exists a wide range of Mallows-type models
[
Mal57
,
FV86
,
Muk16
,
Zho21
]
that use various other statistics instead of the number of inversions.
The present model is related to several topics, such as
q
-exchangeability
[
GO10
,
GO12
]
, the Bruhat decomposition
[
DS22
]
, and the asymmetric simple exclusion process
[
BN22
,
BB24
]
.
A number of properties of Mallows random permutations have been studied extensively by many authors:
this includes mixing times of related Markov chains
[
DR00
,
BBHM05
]
, scaling limits
[
Sta09
,
SW18
]
, longest monotone subsequences
[
MS13
,
BP15
,
BB17
]
, the degree process
[
BM17
]
, the cycle structure
[
GP18
,
HMV23
]
, the secreatary problem
[
Jon20
,
Pin22b
]
, binary search trees
[
ABC21
,
Cor23
]
, logical limit laws
[
MSV23
]
, and more.
When studying the asymptotics of such properties as
n\to\infty
, interesting phase transition phenomena often appear when
q
is allowed to depend on
n
.

A common question when studying random permutations is that of pattern occurrences and avoidance:
given a fixed permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
and a random permutation
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, what is the distribution of the number of occurrences of
\pi
as a (classical, consecutive, or vincular) pattern in
\tau
, and what is the probability that
\tau
avoids it?
This question has been partially addressed for Mallows random permutations.
Central limit theorems have been established for the number of inversions

DS22
]

[

He22

[

]
.
Growth rates for the probability of avoiding a consecutive pattern
[
CDE18
]

]

[
BT24
]

1.2
Previous results on pattern counts

Let
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a permutation and
I=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\}\subseteq[n]
be a subset of
r
indices.
The
pattern
induced by
\tau
on
I
, denoted
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(I,\tau\right)
, is the permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
defined by:

\text{for all }j,k\in[r],\quad\sigma(j)<\sigma(k)\Longleftrightarrow\tau\left(i_{j}\right)<\tau\left(i_{k}\right).

Now fix
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
A
classical occurrence
of
\pi
as a pattern in
\tau
is a subset
I\subseteq[n]
such that
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(I,\tau\right)=\pi
.
For instance, a classical occurrence of the pattern
\mathrm{id}_{r}:=1\,2\dots r
is an increasing subsequence of length
r
, and a classical occurrence of the pattern
2\,1
is an inversion.
The number of classical occurrences of
\pi
as a pattern in
\tau
is then:

{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau\right):=\left|\left\{I\subseteq[n]\,:\,{\mathrm{pat}}\left(I,\tau\right)=\pi\right\}\right|.

In this paper we are only interested in these classical (not consecutive, nor vincular) pattern occurrences, so we will simply call them pattern occurrences.
They have notable applications to independence testing
[
Hoe94
]
and parameter testing
[
HKMS11
]
.

The question tackled in this paper is that of the asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
as
n\to\infty
, when
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
is fixed and
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q_{n}\right)
.
In this framework, we can assume w.l.o.g. that
0<q_{n}\leq 1
.
Indeed, the reversed permutation
\tau_{n}^{\mathrm{rev}}:=\tau_{n}(n)\dots\tau_{n}(2)\,\tau_{n}(1)
follows the
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q_{n}^{-1}\right)
distribution, and
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)={\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi^{\mathrm{rev}},\tau_{n}^{\mathrm{rev}}\right)
for all patterns
\pi
.
Before stating our results, let us review the state of the art on this question.

1.2.1
The uniform case

Since
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,1\right)={\rm Unif}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)
, it is instructive to consider the simpler case of uniformly random permutations before studying general Mallows permutations.
In this case, the asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
are well known:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
JNZ15
]
)
.

Fix
r\geq 2
and
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
For each
n
, let
\tau_{n}\sim{\rm Unif}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)
.
Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)-\frac{1}{r!}\binom{n}{r}}{n^{r-1/2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\pi}^{2}\right),

for some explicit
\nu_{\pi}>0
, where
{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\pi}^{2}\right)
is the centered normal distribution with variance
\nu_{\pi}^{2}
.
This also holds with convergence of all moments, and jointly for any finite family of patterns (with explicit covariances).

Many more results, such as concentration inequalities and bounds on the speed of convergence, are available in the uniform case.
However, the focus of this paper is on the first- and second-order asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
.

1.2.2
First-order convergence through permuton theory

The first-order asymptotics of pattern counts are closely related to the theory of
permutons
, which are certain probability measures on
[0,1]^{2}
that serve as scaling limits for large permutations
[
HKM+13
,
GGKK15
]
.
Indeed, for each integer
n
, let
\tau_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a random permutation.
Then
\tau_{n}
converges in probability as
n\to\infty
to a limit permuton
\mu
if and only if for all
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
, the random variables
\binom{n}{r}^{-1}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
converge in probability as
n\to\infty
to the quantity

{\mathrm{dens}}\left(\pi,\mu\right):=\int_{\left([0,1]^{2}\right)^{r}}\sum_{\sigma,\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}}\mathbf{1}_{x_{\sigma(1)}<\dots<x_{\sigma(r)}}\mathbf{1}_{y_{\rho(1)}<\dots<y_{\rho(r)}}\mathbf{1}_{\rho^{-1}\circ\sigma=\pi}\,\mu(dx_{1},dy_{1})\dots\mu(dx_{r},dy_{r}),

see
[
HKM+13
]
for details.
In general, it may happen that
{\mathrm{dens}}\left(\pi,\mu\right)=0
; the correct order of magnitude of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
should then be determined by different methods.

The permuton limits of Mallows random permutations are well understood.
Suppose that
n(1-q_{n})\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\beta
for some
\beta\in[0,\infty]
.
Then:

•

If
\beta<\infty
then
\tau_{n}
converges in probability to an explicit deterministic permuton
\mu_{\beta}

[
Sta09
]
with a positive density.
Thus

\binom{n}{r}^{-1}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{dens}}\left(\pi,\mu_{\beta}\right)>0

(1)

in probability, for all patterns
\pi
.
If
\beta=0
then
\mu_{\beta}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
and
{\mathrm{dens}}\left(\pi,\mu_{\beta}\right)=\frac{1}{r!}
:
in this regime, the first-order asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
are the same as in the uniform case.

•

If
\beta=\infty
, the permuton limit of
\tau_{n}
can be found with
[
BP15
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Indeed, for any
\epsilon>0
:

\text{for all }i\in[n],\quad\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\tau_{n}(i)-i\right|\geq\epsilon n\right)\leq 2q_{n}^{\epsilon n}

where
q_{n}^{\epsilon n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, thus
\frac{1}{n}\left|\left\{i\in[n]:\left|\tau_{n}(i)-i\right|\geq\epsilon n\right\}\right|
converges in probability to
0
.
As a consequence,
\tau_{n}
converges in probability to the diagonal permuton
\mu_{\Delta}
, which puts uniform mass on the diagonal of the unit square.
Therefore:

\binom{n}{r}^{-1}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\underset{}{\longrightarrow}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{ if }\pi\neq\mathrm{id}_{r};\\
1&\text{ if }\pi=\mathrm{id}_{r};\end{array}\right.

(2)

in probability, as
n\to\infty
.
When
\pi\neq\mathrm{id}_{r}
, this does not provide the correct order of magnitude for
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
.

1.2.3
Asymtotic normality of inversions

To our knowledge, the only pattern for which more is known is the inversion pattern
\pi=2\,1
.
First-order asymptotics for the number of inversions, in many regimes of
q=q_{n}
, are given in
[
Pin22a
]
.
As remarked in
[
DR00
]
, a central limit theorem can also be proved; we state it in a restricted case for simplicity, and provide a proof in
Appendix
for completeness.

Theorem 1.2
.

For each
n
, let
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q_{n}\right)
.
Suppose that
n(1-q_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Then

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]=\tfrac{1}{4}n^{2}-\tfrac{1}{36}(1-q_{n})n^{3}+\underset{}{o}\left(n^{3/2}+(1-q_{n})n^{3}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]=\tfrac{1}{36}n^{3}+\underset{}{o}\left(n^{3}\right).

Furthermore, we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]}{\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]^{1/2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right).

Note that when
q_{n}=1
, we retrieve the famous central limit theorem for the number of inversions in a uniformly random permutation:

\frac{{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)-\frac{1}{4}n^{2}}{\frac{1}{6}n^{3/2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,1\right).

Overview of our results

The aim of this paper is to broaden the asymptotic study of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
for
\pi\neq 2\,1
and
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
, in three different regimes of
q=q_{n}
.
Our results are summarized in the following sections:
we present in
Section

2.2
a central limit theorem when
n^{3/2}(1-q)\to 0
, in
Section

2.3
first-order asymptotics when
q\to 1
and

, and in

joint central limit theorems when

does not depend on

.

2.1
Notation

Let us introduce some notation beforehand.
Throughout this paper, the parameter
q\in(0,1)
may depend on
n
(this is the case in
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
), but we often write
q
instead of
q_{n}
for simplicity.

We let
\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
be the set of nonnegative integers,
\mathbb{N}^{*}:=\{1,2,3,\dots\}
be the set of positive integers,
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the symmetric group on
[n]
.
If
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the size of
\sigma
is the integer
n
and denoted by
\left|\sigma\right|
.
We let
\mathfrak{S}:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the set of all finite permutations, and
\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
be the symmetric group on
\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

All permutations
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}
in this paper are written in one-line notation
\sigma=\sigma(1)\,\sigma(2)\dots\sigma(\ell)
.
The
direct sum
of
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}
and
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}
is the permutation
\sigma\oplus\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{\ell+m}
such that

\sigma\oplus\rho=\sigma(1)\;\sigma(2)\,\dots\,\sigma(\ell)\;\rho(1)\!+\!\ell\;\rho(2)\!+\!\ell\,\dots\,\rho(m)\!+\!\ell.

Note that the
\oplus
operator on
\mathfrak{S}
is associative.
When a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}
can not be written as the direct sum of two non-empty permutations, we say that it is
indecomposable
, or that it is a
block
.
For example, the only indecomposable permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{3}
are
2\,3\,1
,
3\,1\,2
, and
3\,2\,1
.
Conversely,
\sigma
is decomposable if there exists
k\in[n-1]
such that
\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\}=[k]
.
It is a classical fact that any permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}
can be uniquely decomposed as a sum of blocks
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\sigma_{d}
,
and we call the integer
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
the
number of blocks
of
\sigma
.

If
(E_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of events, we say that
E_{n}
happens with high probability (w.h.p.) when
\mathbf{P}\left(E_{n}\right)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.
If
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are sequences of positive random variables, we write
X_{n}=\underset{}{\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{P}}}\left(Y_{n}\right)
when:

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{P}\left(X_{n}\geq C\,Y_{n}\right)\underset{C\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

or equivalently when
\left(X_{n}/Y_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight.
Also,
X_{n}=\underset{}{o_{\mathbf{P}}}\left(Y_{n}\right)
means that
X_{n}/Y_{n}\to 0
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

In this paper, we use the notation
x_{n}=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(y_{n}\right)
when there exist constants
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that:

\text{for all }n\geq n_{0},\quad\alpha\,y_{n}\leq x_{n}\leq\beta\,(\log n)^{\gamma}\,y_{n}.

Note that this slightly differs from the usual
{\widetilde{\Theta}}
notation: here, there is no logarithmic factor in the lower bound.
Next, if
X_{n}
,
Y_{n}
are real r.v.’s, we write
X_{n}=\widetilde{\Theta}_{\mathbf{P}}\left(Y_{n}\right)
when there exist constants
\beta,\gamma>0
such that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist
\alpha_{\epsilon}>0
and
n_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that:

\text{for all }n\geq n_{\epsilon},\quad\mathbf{P}\left(\alpha_{\epsilon}\,Y_{n}\leq X_{n}\leq\beta\,(\log n)^{\gamma}\,Y_{n}\right)\geq 1-\epsilon.

Informally, this means that
X_{n}
has the same asymptotic order as
Y_{n}
in probability, but the upper bound suffers a logarithmic factor.

2.2
Stability close to the uniform regime

One might expect the
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
distribution to have similar properties to
{\rm Unif}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)
when
q
is close enough to
1
.
For instance, by
Theorem

1.2
, the convergence (
3
) holds for
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
if
n^{3/2}(1-q)\to 0
(otherwise
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]
has an additional term above
n^{3/2}
).
The same stability phenomenon occurs for all patterns, as can be deduced from a coupling lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
q\in(0,1)
such that
n(1-q)\leq 1
.
We can couple
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
with
u_{n}\sim{\rm Unif}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)
in such a way that there exists
\mathcal{H}_{n}\subset[n]
satisfying
{\mathrm{pat}}\left([n]\setminus\mathcal{H}_{n},\tau_{n}\right)={\mathrm{pat}}\left([n]\setminus\mathcal{H}_{n},u_{n}\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{H}_{n}\right]\leq\frac{3}{2}n^{2}(1-q)
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Fix
r\geq 2
and
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
.
For each
n
let
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
, where
n^{3/2}(1-q)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Then we have the following convergence in distribution:

\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)-\frac{1}{r!}\binom{n}{r}}{n^{r-1/2}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,\nu_{\pi}^{2}\right),

for the same
\nu_{\pi}>0
as in
Theorem

1.1
.
This also holds jointly for any finite family of patterns, with the same covariances as in
Theorem

1.1
.

This is proved in
Section

4
.
There are several ways to prove
Lemma

2.1
;
for example, we could use a point process approach and
[
MS13
, Lemma 4.2]
.
Here, however, we rather opt for a coupling via inversion sequences, as it is more in line with the rest of the paper and relies on different ideas.
We believe that our approach is interesting on its own, and that appropriate variants of it could be used to study other statistics of Mallows permutations.

It starts from the construction of Mallows permutations using independent truncated geometric variables with parameters
(k,q)
(see
Section

3
).

If
n^{2}(1-q)\to 0
, then it is possible to couple the
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k,q\right)
variables with
{\rm Unif}\left([k]\right)
ones so that they are all equal with high probability (see the computation in
[
MSV23
, Lemmas 7.2 and 7.3]
, and
[
BB17
, Remark 2]
).
In this regime, the permutation
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
can be replaced by a uniformly random permutation
u_{n}\sim{\rm Unif}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)
with high probability, and the convergence of
Corollary

2.2
is straightforward.

In our regime, that is when
n^{3/2}(1-q)\to 0
, such a strong coupling is no longer available.
A naive idea would be to couple
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k,q\right)
with
{\rm Unif}\left([k]\right)
so that as many variables as possible are equal, but this would not translate well into pattern occurrences.
The coupling of
Lemma

2.1
is more subtle:
when some
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k,q\right)
variable does not equal the corresponding
{\rm Unif}\left([k]\right)
variable, the subsequent
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k^{\prime},q\right)
variables should rather try to be equal to an appropriate
“local shift”
of the corresponding
{\rm Unif}\left([k^{\prime}]\right)
variables.
This shift is chosen so that
\tau_{n}
and
u_{n}
have a large pattern in common, with high probability.
See
Section

4
for more details.

2.3
First-order asymptotics in the transition regime

Now suppose that
q\to 1
and
n(1-q)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
In this regime, (
2
) only tells us that
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)=\underset{}{o_{\mathbf{P}}}\left(n^{r}\right)
for
\pi\neq\mathrm{id}_{r}
.
The following theorem describes the correct first-order asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
, up to logarithmic factors.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
q=q_{n}\in(0,1)
such that
q\to 1
and
n(1-q)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
Fix
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
, written as a sum of blocks
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}
.
Then, if
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
:

{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)=\widetilde{\Theta}_{\mathbf{P}}\left(\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right).

Moreover, for all
p>0
:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)^{p}\right]=\widetilde{\Theta}\left(\left(\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right)^{p}\right).

In particular if
q=1-cn^{-x}
for some
c>0
and
x\in(0,1)
, then
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
has order
n^{xr+(1-x)d}
.
This shows how the first-order asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
interpolate between
n^{d}
for fixed
q
(see
Section

2.4
) and
n^{r}
for bounded
n(1-q)
(recall
Equation

1
).
For
\pi=2\,1
(then
r=2
and
d=1
), this also coincides with
[
Pin22a
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Theorem

The idea behind the proof is that, while

is in general indecomposable if

This allows us to approximate

by a sum of subpermutations

The upper and lower bounds are then proved separately:
the former hinges on a trivial upper bound for the number of pattern occurrences, while the latter makes use of (

{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n_{k},q\right)

2.4
Joint and process convergence in the regenerative regime

In this section,
q\in(0,1)
does not depend on

This regime is called

This property comes from the embedding of finite Mallows permutations into the

This permutation, introduced in

2.5
Open questions

There is still much work to be done in the study of classical pattern occurrences in Mallows random permutations.
Below, we list a few questions for further research on this topic.

Question 1
.

In the
“thermodynamic”
regime
n(1-q)\to\beta\in(0,\infty)
, the Mallows distribution has a non-trivial permuton limit.

Equation

1
shows a phase transition for the first-order asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
, but what are the asymptotic fluctuations?

Question 2
.

In
Theorem

2.3
we gave a rough estimate for the first-order asymptotics of
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
, but what are the next terms in its expansion?
Does a central limit theorem hold under appropriate assumptions?

Question 3
.

Can we find an explicit formula for
e_{\pi,q}
, for general patterns
\pi
?

Question 4
.

Theorem

2.7
uses a Mallows process
(\tau_{n,t})_{t\in[0,1)}
whose definition is partly motivated by
Theorems

7.2
and
7.3
.
It would be interesting to relate it to the processes studied in
[
Cor22
]
; it is clearly smooth, but is it regular?
If not, could we prove an analog of
Theorem

2.7
for regular Mallows processes?

Question 5
.

In
Theorem

2.7
and
Proposition

2.8
, we proved a finite-dimensional convergence on
[0,1)
towards a Gaussian process
X_{\pi,\cdot}
which is continuous on the open interval
(0,1)
.
Does
X_{\pi,\cdot}
admit a continuous version on the semi-closed interval

, and does the convergence hold in the Skorohod space?
Note that when the limit process is continuous, this corresponds to uniform convergence on all compact subsets of
[0,1)
.

3
Preliminary results on inversion counts

In this section we collect a few classical facts about the left- and right-inversion counts of permutations, as these are essential for the study of Mallows distributions.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
We define its left- and right-inversion counts as

\ell_{i}(\sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in[n]\,:\,j<i,\,\sigma(j)>\sigma(i)\right\}\right|\quad;\quad r_{i}(\sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in[n]\,:\,j>i,\,\sigma(j)<\sigma(i)\right\}\right|

for
i\in[n]
.
Clearly,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i}(\sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}(\sigma)={\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma\right)
.
The sequence
\left(\ell_{i}(\sigma)\right)_{1\leq i\leq n}
is usually called the
inversion sequence
of
\sigma
; we avoid using this name here, since the sequence
\left(r_{i}(\sigma)\right)_{1\leq i\leq n}
is just as important for our study.

The maps

\varphi_{n}:\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mapsto\left(\ell_{i}(\sigma)\right)_{1\leq i\leq n}\quad;\quad\psi_{n}:\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mapsto\left(r_{i}(\sigma)\right)_{1\leq i\leq n}

are both one-to-one, and their ranges are given by:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(\ell_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
(r_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be sequences of nonnegative integers. Then:

(\ell_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\varphi_{n}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)\,\Leftrightarrow\,\forall i,\,\ell_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,i\!-\!1\}\quad;\quad(r_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\psi_{n}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{n}\right)\,\Leftrightarrow\,\forall i,\,r_{i}\in\{0,1,\dots,n\!-\!i\}.

The respective preimages of
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
can be constructed from left to right by two insertion procedures:

•

If
(\ell_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}=\varphi_{n}(\sigma)
, we can recover
\sigma
by successively constructing
\sigma^{(k)}={\mathrm{pat}}\left([k],\sigma\right)
for
k=1,2,\dots,n
.
First, set
\sigma^{(1)}=1
.
Then, if
\sigma^{(k)}\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
is constructed for some
k\in[n\!-\!1]
, define
\sigma^{(k+1)}\in\mathfrak{S}_{k+1}
by setting
\sigma^{(k+1)}(k+1)=k+1-\ell_{k+1}
and
\sigma^{(k+1)}(i)=\sigma^{(k)}(i)+\mathbf{1}_{\sigma^{(k)}(i)\geq k+1-\ell_{k+1}}
for each
i\in[k]
.
It can be checked that for each
k
,
\sigma^{(k)}
is the unique permutation with left-inversion counts
(\ell_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
, and in particular
\sigma^{(n)}=\sigma
.

•

If
(r_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}=\psi_{n}(\sigma)
, we can recover
\sigma
by successively constructing
\sigma(k)
for
k=1,2,\dots,n
.
First, set
\sigma(1)=r_{1}\!+\!1
.
Then, if
\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)
are constructed for some
k\in[n-1]
, define
\sigma(k+1)
as the
(r_{k+1}\!+\!1)
st lowest number in the set
[n]\setminus\left\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\right\}
.

Note that we could opt for right-to-left constructions by exchanging the two algorithms, since the sequence
\varphi_{n}\left(\sigma^{\mathrm{rev}}\right)
is obtained by reversing the sequence
\psi_{n}(\sigma)
.

These notions can be extended to infinite permutations, i.e. bijections of
\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We define the left- and right-inversion counts of
\Sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
as

\ell_{i}(\Sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\,:\,j<i,\,\Sigma(j)>\Sigma(i)\right\}\right|\quad;\quad r_{i}(\Sigma)=\left|\left\{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\,:\,j>i,\,\Sigma(j)<\Sigma(i)\right\}\right|

for
i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
The maps

\varphi_{\infty}:\Sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}\mapsto\left(\ell_{i}(\Sigma)\right)_{i\geq 1}\quad;\quad\psi_{\infty}:\Sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}\mapsto\left(r_{i}(\Sigma)\right)_{i\geq 1}

are again one-to-one, but their ranges are rather different from the finite case.
Let us focus on
\psi_{\infty}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(r_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of nonnegative integers. Then:

(r_{i})_{i\geq 1}\in\psi_{\infty}\left(\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}\right)\,\Longleftrightarrow\,r_{i}=0\text{ for infinitely many $i$'s}.

The map
\psi_{\infty}
can be inverted by an infinite analog of the previous left-to-right insertion procedure, namely:
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\Sigma(k)
is the
\left(r_{k}(\Sigma)\!+\!1\right)
st lowest number in the set
\mathbb{N}^{*}\setminus\left\{\Sigma(1),\dots,\Sigma(k\!-\!1)\right\}
.
This procedure is the only one we will use in this paper;
the infinite case (on
\mathbb{N}^{*}
) introduces an asymmetry between
\varphi_{\infty}
and
\psi_{\infty}
, and the range of the former is less explicit (see
[
AK24
, Remark 3.10]
for a related discussion).

Left- and right-inversion counts are fundamental tools in the study of Mallows random permutations.
Indeed, for
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
q\in[0,1)
, define the truncated geometric distribution
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k,1\!-\!q\right)
on
[k]
by giving mass
\frac{(1-q)q^{j-1}}{1-q^{k}}
to each integer
j\in[k]
.
Equivalently, the
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k,1\!-\!q\right)
distribution is the
{\rm Geom}\left(1\!-\!q\right)
distribution conditioned to the set
[k]
.
Then:

Lemma 3.3

(
[
Mal57
]
)
.

Let
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
.
The random variables
L_{i}:=\ell_{i}(\tau_{n})+1
are independent for
i\in[n]
and have distribution
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(i,1\!-\!q\right)
.
Similarly, the random variables
R_{i}:=r_{i}(\tau_{n})+1
are independent for
i\in[n]
and have distribution
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(n-i+1,1\!-\!q\right)
.

As is usual in probability theory, describing a random variable as the image of independent r.v.’s through a simple function proves to be very useful.
The infinite Mallows distribution is defined in an analogous way; see
Section

6.1
.

We end this section with a simple but handy description of decomposability via right-inversion counts.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
with right-inversion counts
(r_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and let
M\in[n]
.
We can write
\sigma=\rho\oplus\sigma^{\prime}
for some
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{M}
and
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n-M}
if and only if:

\text{for all }i\in[M],\quad r_{i}\leq M-i.

(8)

Proof.

First suppose that
\sigma=\rho\oplus\sigma^{\prime}
.
By definition of the direct sum, no couple
(i,j)
with
i\leq M
and
j>M
can create an inversion for
\sigma
.
Thus
r_{i}(\sigma)=r_{i}(\rho)
for all
i\in[M]
, and
Equation

8
follows.

Reciprocally, suppose that
(
8
)
holds.
By
Lemma

3.1
, there exists
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{M}
such that
r_{i}(\rho)=r_{i}(\sigma)
for all
i\in[M]
.
Recall that the insertion procedure constructs successively
\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(n)
using
r_{1},\dots,r_{n}
.
The first
M
steps of the procedure are the same for
\sigma
as they are for
\rho
, thus
\sigma(i)=\rho(i)
for all
i\in[M]
as desired.
∎

In the same way, an analogous lemma for the infinite case readily follows:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
with right-inversion counts
(r_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.
We can write
\Sigma=\rho_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\rho_{k}\oplus\dots
for some infinite sequence of finite permutations
\rho_{k}\in\mathfrak{S}_{M_{k}}
,
k\geq 1
, if and only if
(r_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is an infinite concatenation of sequences satisfying (
8
), that is:

\text{for all }k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\text{ and }i\in[M_{k}],\quad r_{M_{1}+\dots+M_{k\!-\!1}+i}\leq M_{k}-i.

4
The almost uniform regime: coupling and stability

This section is devoted to the proofs of
Lemma

2.1
and
Corollary

2.2
.
We start with two useful lemmas.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
0\leq q\leq 1
and
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
such that
m(1-q)\leq 1
.
Let
X_{m}\sim{\rm TruncGeom}\left(m,1-q\right)
and
Y_{m}\sim{\rm Unif}\left([m]\right)
.
Then for any
j\in[m]
:

\left|\big.\mathbf{P}\left(X_{m}=j\right)-\mathbf{P}\left(Y_{m}=j\right)\right|=\left|\mathbf{P}\left(X_{m}=j\right)-\frac{1}{m}\right|\leq 3(1-q).

Proof.

This can be found in the proof of
[
MSV23
, Lemma 7.2]
, but we include the computation here for completeness.
Recall that for any
x\in[0,1]
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\left|(1-x)^{j}-1\right|\leq jx
and
\left|(1-x)^{j}-(1-jx)\right|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}x^{2}
.
Therefore we can write
q^{j}=1+\underset{}{\mathcal{O}_{1}}\left(j(1-q)\right)
and
q^{j}=1-j(1-q)+\underset{}{\mathcal{O}_{1/2}}\left(j^{2}(1-q)^{2}\right)
where the notation
\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(t)
means that
\left|\mathcal{O}_{\delta}(t)\right|\leq\delta t
.
Hence, for all
j\in[m]
:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{P}\left(X_{m}=j\right)-\mathbf{P}\left(Y_{m}=j\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{(1-q)q^{j-1}}{1-q^{m}}-\frac{1}{m}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\left|m(1-q)q^{j-1}-(1-q^{m})\right|}{m(1-q^{m})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left|m(1\!-\!q)\left(1+\underset{}{\mathcal{O}_{1}}\left(j(1\!-\!q)\right)\right)-m(1\!-\!q)+\underset{}{\mathcal{O}_{1/2}}\left(m^{2}(1\!-\!q)^{2}\right)\right|}{m\left(m(1\!-\!q)-\tfrac{1}{2}m^{2}(1\!-\!q)^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\underset{}{\mathcal{O}_{3/2}}\left(m^{2}(1-q)^{2}\right)}{m^{2}(1-q)-\tfrac{1}{2}m^{3}(1-q)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tfrac{3}{2}(1-q)}{1-\tfrac{1}{2}m(1-q)}

\displaystyle\leq 3(1-q).\qed

In the following lemma, we write
\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)
for the total variation distance, and
\phi^{*}\mathcal{L}
for the pushforward of a probability distribution
\mathcal{L}
by a measurable function
\phi
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
be probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}
, and
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{N}
be measurable functions.
Let
\epsilon>0
and suppose that
\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\phi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{1},\phi_{2}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{2}\right)\leq\epsilon
.
Then there exists a coupling
(X_{1},X_{2})
of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(\phi_{1}(X_{1})\neq\phi_{2}(X_{2})\right)\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

It is well-known that there exists a coupling
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
of
\phi_{1}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\phi_{2}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{2}
such that

\mathbf{P}\left(Y_{1}\neq Y_{2}\right)\leq\epsilon.

Let
i\in\{1,2\}
.
For each
y
in the support of
\phi_{i}^{*}\mathcal{L}_{i}
, define
\mathcal{L}_{i,y}
as the probability distribution induced by
\mathcal{L}_{i}
on the fiber
\phi_{i}^{-1}(\{y\})
.
Then let
X_{i}
have distribution
\mathcal{L}_{i,Y_{i}}
, conditionally given
Y_{i}
.
It is readily verified that
X_{i}
is a.s. well defined, and that its law is
\mathcal{L}_{i}
.
Furthermore
\phi_{i}(X_{i})=Y_{i}
a.s., whence the desired inequality.
∎

.

L_{k}

.

First, set
L_{1}=U_{1}=1
a.s. and
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\emptyset
.
Then, assume that we have constructed

and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
as desired, for some

If
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}

(this definition is motivated later in the proof).
The function

is nondecreasing and onto

In particular,
\sum_{j=1+H_{k}}^{k+1}\left(\left|\phi_{\sigma}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|\!-\!1\right)=H_{k}
.
Below, for notational convenience, we allow ourselves to write
L_{k+1}
and
U_{k+1}
for generic variables with laws
{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k\!+\!1,1\!-\!q_{n}\right)
and
{\rm Unif}\left([k\!+\!1]\right)

Henceforth, conditionally given
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and using

4.1
on the fourth line:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d_{TV}}\left(\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}\left(L_{k+1}\right),\phi_{u_{n}^{k}}\left(U_{k+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1+H_{k}}^{k+1}\left|\mathbf{P}\left(\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}\left(L_{k+1}\right)=j\right)-\mathbf{P}\left(\phi_{u_{n}^{k}}\left(U_{k+1}\right)=j\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1+H_{k}}^{k+1}\left|\mathbf{P}\left(L_{k+1}\in\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right)-\frac{\left|\phi_{u_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|}{k+1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1+H_{k}}^{k+1}\left(\left|\mathbf{P}\left(L_{k+1}\in\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right)-\frac{\left|\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|}{k+1}\right|+\frac{\left|\left|\phi_{u_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|\!-\!\left|\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|\right|}{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1+H_{k}}^{k+1}\left(3(1-q)\left|\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|+\frac{\left|\phi_{u_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|\!-\!1+\left|\phi_{\tau_{n}^{k}}^{-1}(\{j\})\right|\!-\!1}{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(3(1-q)\left(k+1\right)+\frac{2H_{k}}{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{3}{2}(k+1)(1-q)+\frac{H_{k}}{k+1}.

Thus we can apply

4.2
:
there exists a coupling of
L_{k+1}
and
U_{k+1}

\mathcal{F}_{k}

(9)

Now, note that the last values of

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathrm{pat}}\left([k\!+\!1]\setminus\mathcal{H}_{k},\tau_{n}^{k+1}\right)(-1)=\tau_{n}^{k+1}(k\!+\!1)-\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{n}^{k}(h)<\tau_{n}^{k+1}(k+1)};\\

Recalling that

Our proof requires good control over the displacements of points in random Mallows permutations, specifically:

Theorem 5.1

(
[

]
)
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
and
\tau\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
.
Then for all
i\in[n]
and
t>0
:

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\tau(i)-i\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2q^{t}.

Proof of

2.3
.

We prove separately an upper and a lower bound on
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
, with similar techniques.
The intervals used in the proof are illustrated in

.

Upper bound:

Consider an arbitrary sequence
(\lambda_{n})
such that
\lambda_{n}-\log n\to+\infty
as
n\to\infty
, and define the event
E:=\left\{\forall i\in[n],\quad\left|\tau_{n}(i)-i\right|<\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\right\}
.
Then by
Theorem

5.1
along with a union bound:

1-\mathbf{P}\left(E\right)=\mathbf{P}\left(\exists i\in[n],\quad\left|\tau_{n}(i)-i\right|\geq\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\right)\leq 2n\,e^{\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\log q}\leq 2n\,e^{-\lambda_{n}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

(11)

This controls the distance of all points
(i,\tau_{n}(i))
to the diagonal, and will allow us to restrict the patterns of
\tau_{n}
in a sequence of overlapping intervals.
First set
m:=\lceil\frac{n(1-q)}{\lambda_{n}}\rceil
, and
I_{k}:=\left[1+\lfloor\frac{(k-1)\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\rfloor\,,\,\lfloor\frac{k\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\rfloor\wedge n\right]
for
1\leq k\leq m
.
These disjoint intervals cover
[n]
.

Now, let
\pi_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{r_{j}}
be a block of
\pi
and let
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r_{j}}\leq n
be such that
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r_{j}}\},\tau_{n}\right)=\pi_{j}
.
For each
\ell\in[r_{j}]
, define
k_{\ell}\in[m]
by

.
Then we claim that under
E
,
\left|k_{\ell+1}-k_{\ell}\right|\leq 2
for all
\ell\in[r_{j}\!-\!1]
.
Indeed, suppose that
k_{\ell_{0}+1}\geq k_{\ell_{0}}+3
for some
\ell_{0}\in[r_{j}\!-\!1]
.
Then for any
\ell\leq{\ell_{0}}
and
{\ell^{\prime}}\geq{\ell_{0}+1}

\tau_{n}(i_{\ell})<i_{\ell}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\leq i_{\ell_{0}}+\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\leq\frac{(k_{\ell_{0}}+1)\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\leq\frac{(k_{\ell_{0}+1}-2)\lambda_{n}}{1-q}<i_{\ell_{0}+1}-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\leq i_{\ell^{\prime}}-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{1-q}<\tau_{n}(i_{\ell^{\prime}}).

This contradicts the indecomposability of
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r_{j}}\},\tau_{n}\right)=\pi_{j}
, and proves the claim.

This leads us to define
J_{k}:=I_{k}\cup I_{k+1}\cup\dots\cup I_{k+2(r-1)}
for each
k\in[m-2(r\!-\!1)]
.
By the previous claim, if
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r_{j}}\},\tau_{n}\right)=\pi_{j}
, then there exists
k\in[m-2(r\!-\!1)]
such that
i_{1},\dots,i_{r_{j}}\in J_{k}
.
Subsequently, if
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\},\tau_{n}\right)=\pi
, then there exist
k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{d}
such that
i_{r_{1}+\dots+r_{j-1}+1},\dots,i_{r_{1}+\dots+r_{j}}\in J_{k_{j}}
for all
j\in[d]
.
This implies the following inequality under

:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{d}\leq m-2(r-1)}\,\prod_{1\leq j\leq d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},{\mathrm{pat}}\left(J_{k_{j}},\tau_{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq\dots\leq k_{d}\leq m-2(r-1)}\,\prod_{1\leq j\leq d}\left|J_{k_{j}}\right|^{\left|\pi_{j}\right|}

\displaystyle\leq m^{d}\left(\frac{2r\lambda_{n}}{1-q}\right)^{r}

\displaystyle\leq(2r)^{r}(\lambda_{n})^{r-d}\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}.

(12)

To deduce the probability upper bound, set
\lambda_{n}:=2\log n
, then
\beta:=(4r)^{r}
and
\gamma:=r-d
:

\mathbf{P}\left({\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\leq\beta\,(\log n)^{\gamma}\,\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right)\geq\mathbf{P}\left(E\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1

by (
11
).
To obtain a moment upper bound, we can set
\lambda_{n}:=(rp\!+\!1)\log n
for any desired

By (

) and (

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)^{p}\right]\leq(2r)^{rp}(\lambda_{n})^{rp}\left(\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right)^{p}+n^{rp}\left(\big.1-\mathbf{P}\left(E\right)\right)\\
\leq(2(rp\!+\!1)r)^{rp}(\log n)^{rp}\left(\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right)^{p}+2n^{rp+1}e^{-(rp+1)\log n}.

The desired upper bound readily follows.

Lower bound:

This time, the idea is to consider a disjoint sequence of boxes that contain a good proportion of permutation points.
The box sizes shall be tuned so that the induced sub-permutations are random Mallows permutations in the thermodynamic regime, and we can apply (

).

by

We could apply (
1
) to these induced permutations; however, some occurrences of

’s in

in

.
We solve this by cropping the

For each

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}I_{k}^{(1)}:=\left\{i\in I_{k}:\,\tau_{n}(i)\leq\frac{3(k\!-\!1)\lambda-\lambda}{1-q}\right\};\\

with the notation

.
The reason we introduced
I_{k}^{(2)}
is that if
i\in I_{k}^{(2)}
and
i^{\prime}\in I_{k^{\prime}}^{(2)}
for
k<k^{\prime}
, then
\tau_{n}(i)<\tau_{n}(i^{\prime})
. Therefore:

{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\geq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq m}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},{\mathrm{pat}}\left(I_{k_{j}}^{(2)},\tau_{n}\right)\right).

(13)

On the other hand, the definition of
J_{k}^{(2)}
is motivated by the fact that the random permutation
\tau_{n,k}^{(2)}:={\mathrm{pat}}\left(J_{k}^{(2)},\tau_{n}\right)
is
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\frac{1}{3}n_{k}\!+\!\mathcal{O}(1),q\right)
distributed.
Indeed, it can be written as
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(\left[1\!+\!\lceil\frac{1}{3}n_{k}\rceil,\lfloor\frac{2}{3}n_{k}\rfloor\right],\tau_{n,k}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
, which follows the claimed distribution by
[
BP15
, Corollaries 2.3 and 2.7]
.
We shall now prove that for many
k
’s,
J_{k}^{(2)}\subseteq I_{k}^{(2)}
and
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},\tau_{n,k}\right)
has order
n_{k}^{\left|\pi_{j}\right|}
.

For each
k\in[m]
and
i\in I_{k}
:

\mathbf{P}\left(i\notin I_{k}^{(2)}\right)\leq\mathbf{P}\left(\left|\tau_{n}(i)-i\right|\geq\frac{\lambda}{1-q}\right)\leq 2e^{-\lambda}

by
Theorem

5.1
.
Hence
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|I_{k}^{(1)}\cup I_{k}^{(3)}\right|\right]\leq 2n_{k}e^{-\lambda}
, and by Markov’s inequality:

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|I_{k}^{(1)}\cup I_{k}^{(3)}\right|\geq\frac{1}{3}n_{k}\right)\leq 6e^{-\lambda}.

Thus
H_{1}:=\left\{k\in[m]:\left|I_{k}^{(1)}\cup I_{k}^{(3)}\right|\geq\frac{1}{3}n_{k}\right\}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{1}\right|\right]\leq 6me^{-\lambda}
, and:

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|H_{1}\right|\geq\frac{1}{4}m\right)\leq 24e^{-\lambda}.

(14)

Let
k\in[m]
and suppose that
k\notin H_{1}
.
Then
\left|I_{k}^{(1)}\right|<\frac{1}{3}n_{k}
.
Since
\left|J_{k}^{(1)}\right|\geq\frac{1}{3}n_{k}
, and by construction, we deduce that
I_{k}^{(1)}\subset J_{k}^{(1)}
.
Likewise, it holds that
I_{k}^{(3)}\subset J_{k}^{(3)}
.
Subsequently
J_{k}^{(2)}\subset I_{k}^{(2)}
, and (
13
) implies:

{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\geq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq m}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},\tau_{n,k_{j}}^{(2)}\right)\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{1}}.

(15)

Write
r_{j}:=\left|\pi_{j}\right|
for
j\in[d]
.
Recall that
\tau_{n,k}^{(2)}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\frac{1}{3}n_{k}+\mathcal{O}(1),q\right)
, where
\frac{1}{3}n_{k}(1-q)\to\frac{1}{3}\lambda
as
n\to\infty
.
Then by (
1
), uniformly in
j\in[d]
and
k\in[m]
:

\mathbf{P}\left({\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},\tau_{n,k}^{(2)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{dens}}\left(\pi_{j},\mu_{\lambda/3}\right)\frac{n_{k}^{r_{j}}}{3^{r_{j}}r_{j}!}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Set
c_{j,\lambda}:=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{dens}}\left(\pi_{j},\mu_{\lambda/3}\right)}{3^{r_{j}}r_{j}!}
and
H_{2}:=\left\{k\in[m]:\,\exists j\in[d],\,{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},\tau_{n,k}^{(2)}\right)\leq c_{j,\lambda}n_{k}^{r_{j}}\right\}
.
Then by Markov’s inequality:

\mathbf{P}\left(\left|H_{2}\right|\geq\frac{1}{4}m\right)\leq\frac{4}{m}\sum_{1\leq k\leq m}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}\mathbf{P}\left({\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},\tau_{n,k}^{(2)}\right)\leq c_{j,\lambda}n_{k}^{r_{j}}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

(16)

Now (
15
) yields:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq m}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},\tau_{n,k_{j}}^{(2)}\right)\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq m}\prod_{j=1}^{d}c_{j,\lambda}n_{k_{j}}^{r_{j}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq m}\prod_{j=1}^{d}c_{j,\lambda}\left(\frac{\lambda}{1-q}-2\right)^{r_{j}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{k_{j}\notin H_{2}}

\displaystyle\geq c_{\lambda}(1-q)^{-r}\left(m-\left|H_{1}\right|-\left|H_{2}\right|\right)^{d}

for some
c_{\lambda}>0
.
Therefore, using (
14
) and (
16
) and setting
c_{\lambda}^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{2}c_{\lambda}/(6\lambda)^{d}
:

\mathbf{P}\left({\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\geq c_{\lambda}^{\prime}\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right)\geq\mathbf{P}\left(\left|H_{1}\right|+\left|H_{2}\right|<\frac{1}{2}m\right)\geq 1-24e^{-\lambda}-o(1)

Since
24e^{-\lambda}
goes to
0
as
\lambda\to\infty
, this concludes the proof of the probability lower bound.
The moment lower bound is then straightforward:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)^{p}\right]\geq\left(1-24e^{-\lambda}-o(1)\right)\left(c_{\lambda}^{\prime}\frac{n^{d}}{(1-q)^{r-d}}\right)^{p},

for any fixed
\lambda>0
such that
24e^{-\lambda}<1
.
∎

Remark 1
.

In the lower bound of the previous proof, we could replace the sub-permutations
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(J_{k}^{(2)},\tau_{n}\right)
by
{\mathrm{pat}}\left(I_{k}^{(2)},\tau_{n}\right)
, which follow the Mallows distribution by
[
GP18
, Lemma 3.15]
.
In this case, instead of proving that many
J_{k}^{(2)}
’s are in

6
The regenerative regime

6.1
Infinite Mallows permutations

The authors of
[
GO10
]
defined an infinite version of the Mallows distribution, as a random element of
\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
.
In this section we recall their construction, and present a few key properties.

Fix
q\in[0,1)
, and let
\left(R_{i}\right)_{i\geq 1}
be a sequence of i.i.d.
{\rm Geom}\left(1\!-\!q\right)
r.v.’s.
The sequence
(R_{i}-1)_{i\geq 1}
almost surely satisfies the condition of
Lemma

3.2
, and we say that the random permutation
\Sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
with right-inversion counts
(R_{i}-1)_{i\geq 1}
follows the
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},q\right)
distribution.
An important property of the
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},q\right)
measure is that it can be seen as the projective limit of
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
measures, in the following sense
[
GO10
, Proposition A.6]
:

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\Sigma\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},q\right)
then for any
n\geq 1
, the finite sub-permutation
\tau_{n}:={\mathrm{pat}}\left([n],\Sigma\right)
follows the
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
distribution.

Lemma

6.1
allows us to define
\tau_{n}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,q\right)
as a sub-permutation of
\Sigma\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},q\right)
, and to study its asymptotic properties as
n\to\infty
for fixed
q\in[0,1)
.
We call this regime
“regenerative”
because of the following property
[
PT19
, Corollary 5.1]
(see also the discussion at the beginning of
[
BB17
, Section 3]
):

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\Sigma\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},q\right)
.
Then we can write
\Sigma=B_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus B_{k}\oplus\dots
where
(B_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random blocks.
Conditionally given its size
|B_{1}|
, the permutation
B_{1}
is a random
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(|B_{1}|,q\right)
permutation conditioned to be indecomposable.

With the notation of the above lemma, we say that
B_{1}
is a
Mallows block with parameter
q
, and we denote its distribution by
{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
.
In
[
He22
]
, the random permutations
(B_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are called the
excursions
of
\Sigma
.
The law of
\left|B_{1}\right|
is described in
[
PT19
, Corollary 5.1 (iv)]
and rather intricate, but according to
[
BB17
, Proposition 4.6]
it admits some exponential moments, and thus finite moments of all orders (see also
[
He22
, Proposition 3.4]
).

As a consequence of
Lemma

6.2
, the sub-permutations
\tau_{n}={\mathrm{pat}}\left([n],\Sigma\right)
are in a sense
“almost regenerative”
.
Indeed, let
K_{n}:=\max\left\{k\geq 0:\left|B_{1}\right|+\dots+\left|B_{k}\right|\leq n\right\}
.
Then
\tau_{n}
can be written as

\tau_{n}=B_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus B_{K_{n}}\oplus\sigma_{n}

(17)

for some pattern
\sigma_{n}
of
B_{K_{n}+1}
.
Since the random variable
\left|B_{1}\right|
is
L^{p}
for all
p>0
, it is well-known that
K_{n}
satisfies a law of large numbers and a central limit theorem (see e.g.
[
Jan18
, Theorems 3.7, 3.8, 3.16]
):

Proposition 6.3
.

We have the following convergence as
n\to\infty
:

\frac{K_{n}}{n}\,\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\,\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{1}\right|\right]}\qquad;\qquad\frac{K_{n}-n/\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{1}\right|\right]}{n^{1/2}}\,\overset{\text{law}}{\longrightarrow}\,{\cal N}\left(0,\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\left|B_{1}\right|\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{1}\right|\right]^{3}}\right),

where
B\sim{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
.
Moreover, this holds with convergence of all moments.

6.2
Pattern-joint CLT

This section is devoted to the proofs of
Theorem

2.4
,
Proposition

2.5
, and
Theorem

2.6
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.4
.

We will use
Equation

17
, and the same techniques as in
[
Jan23
]
.
Throughout the proof,
B
denotes a generic
{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
random variable.

First, fix a pattern
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}\in\mathfrak{S}
.
Observe that any occurrence of a block
\pi_{j}
in
\tau_{n}
is wholly contained in a single term of the sum
\tau_{n}=B_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus B_{K_{n}}\oplus\sigma_{n}
.
Define
{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
as the number of occurences of
\pi
in
\tau_{n}
where all
\pi_{j}
’s are contained in distinct
B_{k}
’s, that is:

{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right):=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq K_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{k_{j}}\right).

(18)

It will be sufficient to prove the desired result for
{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
, after showing that it approximates
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
well.
For any occurrence of
\pi
in
\tau_{n}
which does not contribute to
{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
, either some
\pi_{i}
appears in the same
B_{k}
as
\pi_{i+1}
, or
\pi_{d}
appears in
B_{K_{n}+1}
(recall that
\sigma_{n}
is a sub-permutation of
B_{K_{n}+1}
).
The first type of contribution is bounded by

\displaystyle{\rm Occ}^{-}_{1}\,\leq\,\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\,\sum_{1\leq k_{j}\leq K_{n},\,j\neq i}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{k_{i+1}}\right)\prod_{j\neq i}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{k_{j}}\right)\,\leq\,\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\,\sum_{1\leq k_{j}\leq n,\,j\neq i}\,\prod_{j\neq i}\left|B_{k_{j}}\right|^{\left|\pi\right|},

while the second one is bounded by

{\rm Occ}^{-}_{2}\,\leq\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left({\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{\ell}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d},B_{K_{n}+1}\right)\sum_{1\leq k_{j}\leq K_{n},\,j<\ell}\,\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{k_{j}}\right)\right)\\
\leq d\,\left|B_{K_{n}+1}\right|^{\left|\pi\right|}\sum_{1\leq k_{j}\leq n,\,j<d}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d-1}\left|B_{k_{j}}\right|^{\left|\pi\right|}.

Recall that
(\left|B_{k}\right|)_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with
finite moments of all orders
[
BB17
, Proposition 4.6]
.
Moreover,
\left|B_{K_{n}+1}\right|
is asymptotically distributed as the size-bias distribution of
\left|B\right|
(see e.g.
[
He22
, Lemma 3.6]
and the discussion above), thus its moments are all finite and bounded over
n
.
Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\!-\!{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[{\rm Occ}^{-}_{1}+{\rm Occ}^{-}_{2}\right]\leq\underset{}{\mathcal{O}}\left(n^{d-1}\right),

and more generally, for any
p>0
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\!-\!{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\right|^{p}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left({\rm Occ}^{-}_{1}+{\rm Occ}^{-}_{2}\right)^{p}\right]\leq\underset{}{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(n^{d-1}\right)^{p}\right).

Thus it suffices to prove
Theorem

2.4
with
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
replaced by
{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)
.

Now, fix
d\geq 1
and a real vector
\Lambda=\left(\lambda_{\pi}\right)_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}
with finite support.
Using (
18
), we can write

\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq K_{n}}\,\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{k_{j}}\right)\\
=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq K_{n}}f_{\Lambda}\left(B_{k_{1}},\dots,B_{k_{d}}\right)

One may thus recognize an asymmetric
U
-statistic stopped at some random stopping time, as studied in
[
Jan18
]
.
Using
[
Jan18
, Theorems 3.9 and 3.11]
(beware the notation), we deduce that

\frac{1}{n^{d}}\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)\,\underset{}{\longrightarrow}\,\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]}

almost surely, which concludes the proof of (
5
), and

\frac{\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n}\right)-\binom{n}{d}\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}e_{\pi,q}}{n^{d-1/2}}\,\underset{}{\longrightarrow}\,{\cal N}\left(0,\gamma_{\Lambda}^{2}\right)

(19)

in distribution, for some
\gamma_{\Lambda}\geq 0
.
This also holds with convergence of all moments by
[
Jan18
, Theorem 3.17]
.
To describe the asymptotic variance
\gamma_{\Lambda}^{2}
, we first need to describe the (non-centered) univariate projections:

f_{\Lambda,j}(B):=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{\Lambda}\left(B_{1},\dots,B_{d}\right)\,\big|\,B_{j}=B\right]=\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)\right]}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B\right)\right],

(20)

for
j\in[d]
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,B\right)\right]>0
for all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}
and
q\in(0,1)
, since the support of
B
is the set of indecomposable permutations, and any given pattern is contained in at least one indecomposable permutation.
Using
[
Jan18
, Equation (3.28)]
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{\Lambda}^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]^{1-2d}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\binom{i\!+\!j\!-\!2}{i\!-\!1}\binom{2d\!-\!i\!-\!j}{d\!-\!i}\frac{1}{(2d-1)!}\mathrm{Cov}\left[f_{\Lambda,j}(B),f_{\Lambda,i}(B)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{2\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]^{-2d}}{(d-1)!d!}\left(\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B\right)\right]\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\left|B\right|,f_{\Lambda,i}(B)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]^{-2d-1}}{(d-1)!^{2}}\left(\sum_{\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}}\lambda_{\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B\right)\right]\right)^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[\left|B\right|\right].

(21)

Substituting in (
20
) and developing, we can write
\gamma_{\Lambda}^{2}=\Lambda^{\rm T}\Gamma^{(d)}\Lambda
where, for all patterns
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}
and
\rho=\rho_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\rho_{d}
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pi,\rho}^{(d)}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]e_{\pi,q}e_{\rho,q}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2d-1)!}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\binom{i\!+\!j\!-\!2}{i\!-\!1}\binom{2d\!-\!i\!-\!j}{d\!-\!i}\frac{\mathrm{Cov}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right),{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho_{i},B\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho_{i},B\right)\right]}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(d-1)!d!}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Cov}\left[\left|B\right|,{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho_{i},B\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho_{i},B\right)\right]}+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}\left[\left|B\right|,{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B\right)\right]}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(d-1)!^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[\left|B\right|\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]^{2}}\Bigg).

(22)

Equation

6
then follows from
Equation

19
along with the Cramér–Wold theorem.

Finally, to prove convergence of all joint moments in (
6
), by uniform integrability it suffices to argue that all joint moments are bounded as
n\to\infty
.
This simply follows from Hölder’s inequality, and the fact that (
19
) holds with convergence of all moments.
∎

Remark 2
.

For
\pi=2\,1
, it is known that
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]\sim\frac{q}{1-q}n
as
n\to\infty

[
BT24
, Proposition 29]
.
This shows the equality

e_{2\,1,q}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]}=\frac{q}{1-q}\,,

for which we do not have a direct proof.
Since
e_{\pi,q}=\prod_{j}e_{\pi_{j},q}
, this also implies explicit formulas for patterns whose blocks are only points or inversions, e.g.
e_{1\,3\,2,q}=e_{2\,1\,3,q}=\frac{q}{1-q}
.
For other patterns
\pi
, computing
e_{\pi,q}
seems difficult.

Before computing the ranks of specific sub-matrices of
\Gamma^{(d)}
, we state and prove a simple lemma which will be useful in the proof of
Proposition

2.5
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 2
.
Consider the linear system

\forall j\in[d],\quad\sum_{i<j}\lambda_{i,j}+\sum_{k>j}\lambda_{j,k}=0

(E)

in the real variables
\left(\lambda_{i,k}\right)_{1\leq i<k\leq d}
.

If
d=2
then this system has rank
1
, and if
d\geq 3
then it has rank
d
.

Proof.

The linear system (
E
) has
d
equations and
\binom{d}{2}
unknowns, thus we aim to prove that it has full rank.
If
d=2
, this is immediate.
If
d\geq 3
, it suffices to find an invertible sub-system of size
d\times d
.
The system (
E
) restricted to the variables
\lambda_{1,2},\lambda_{1,3},\dots,\lambda_{1,d}
and
\lambda_{d-1,d}
rewrites as:

\sum_{k>1}\lambda_{1,k}=0\;;\quad\lambda_{1,2}=0\;;\quad\lambda_{1,3}=0\;;\;\dots\;;\;\lambda_{1,d-2}=0\;;\quad\lambda_{1,d-1}+\lambda_{d-1,d}=0\;;\quad\lambda_{1,d}+\lambda_{d-1,d}=0,

which is easily seen to be invertible.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

2.5
.

First, fix a pattern
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}
.
As in the proof of
Theorem

2.4
, we can use the results of
[
Jan18
]
.
In this case, the real vector is set to
\Lambda:=\left(\mathbf{1}_{\rho=\pi}\right)_{\rho=\rho_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\rho_{d}}
and the uncentered univariate projections of (
20
) are given by
f_{\Lambda,j}(B)=\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)\right]}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B\right)\right]
for
j\in[d]
.
Therefore by
[
Jan18
, Equation (3.29)]
,
\Gamma_{\pi,\pi}^{(d)}=0
if and only if for all
j\in[d]
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B\right)\right]}-1\right)\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B\right)\right]=\left(\big.\left|B\right|-\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B\right|\right]\right)\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B\right)\right]

almost surely.

Patterns in the continuous-time infinite Mallows process

7.1
The continuous-time infinite Mallows process

In order to define a continuous-time coupling of
\Sigma_{t}\sim{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},t\right)
for
t\in[0,1)
, we use continuous-time geometric processes along with
Lemma

3.2
.
In
[
AK24
, Section 3.2]
this is done for the
bi-infinite
Mallows distribution (on
\mathbb{Z}
), which is trickier to construct than the
“unilateral”
version (on
\mathbb{N}^{*}
) considered here.

Say that a stochastic process
X:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
is a
birth process
if it is càdlàg, time-inhomogeneous Markov, and admits
infinitesimal birth rates

p(j,t)\geq 0
such that:

\text{for all }t\geq 0\text{ and }j\in\mathbb{N},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t+h}=j\,\big|\,X_{t}=j\right)=1-hp(j,t)+o(h);\\
\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t+h}=j\!+\!1\,\big|\,X_{t}=j\right)=hp(j,t)+o(h);\end{array}\right.\text{ as }h\to 0.

(28)

The law of
X
is then uniquely determined by these birth rates along with the law of
X_{0}
.
Conversely, these birth rates are uniquely determined by the functions
t\mapsto\mathbf{P}\left(X_{t}=j\right)
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
This follows from the Chapman–Kolmogorov forward equation (see e.g. the proof of
[
AK24
, Proposition 3.5]
).

Lemma 7.1

(
[
AK24
]
)
.

Let
R:[0,1)\to\mathbb{N}^{*}
be the birth process with
R_{0}=1
and birth rates
p(j,t)=\frac{j}{1-t}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
t\in[0,1)
.
Then
R
is non-explosive (i.e. it is a.s. well-defined on
[0,1)
, and has finitely many jumps on any proper subinterval), and has marginal distributions
R_{t}\sim{\rm Geom}\left(1\!-\!t\right)
for all
t\in[0,1)
.

Conversely, if
R:[0,1)\to\mathbb{N}^{*}
is a birth process with marginal distributions
R_{t}\sim{\rm Geom}\left(1\!-\!t\right)
for all
t\in[0,1)
, then it has birth rates
p(j,t)=\frac{j}{1-t}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
t\in[0,1)
.

The process

of
Lemma

7.1
shall be refered to as the
geometric birth process
.
We can now state an infinite analog of
[
Cor22
, Theorem 1.1]
.
Note that the authors of
[

]
and
[

]
chose to work with

-inversion counts, but for the purpose of this paper,

-inversion counts seem better adapted.
In particular, we do not know if an analog of

Theorem 7.2
.

There exists a unique time-inhomogeneous Markov process
\Sigma:[0,1)\to\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
such that:

1.

for all
t\in[0,1)
,
\Sigma_{t}
has marginal distribution
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*},t\right)
;

2.

the right-inversion counts

,
i\geq 1
are independent birth processes.

Furthermore, we can write
r_{i}(\Sigma_{t})=R_{i,t}\!-\!1
for all
i\geq 1
and
t\in[0,1)
, where
\left(R_{1,t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)},\left(R_{2,t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)},\dots
are i.i.d. geometric birth processes as defined in
Lemma

7.1
.

We call the process
\Sigma=\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
of
Theorem

, and write
\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*}\right)

Proof.

Let
\left(R_{i,\cdot}\right)_{i\geq 1}
be i.i.d. geometric birth process.
By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, for any fixed
t\in[0,1)
, almost surely
R_{i,t}=1
for infinitely many

Since the processes
\left(R_{i,t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}

.
Therefore by

3.2
, almost surely, we can define
\Sigma_{t}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\infty}
with right-inversion counts

for all

.

For each

by definition.
Since there is a one-to-one correspondence
\psi_{\infty}
between infinite permutations and admissible sequences of right-inversion counts, the Markov property of
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
follows from that of the independent processes
\left(R_{i,t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
,
i\geq 1
.
Hence this proves the existence of continuous-time infinite Mallows processes.

Now, let
\Sigma^{\prime}
be a time-inhomogeneous Markov process of infinite permutations, satisfying Properties

and

Then for any fixed
t\in[0,1)

\left(R_{i,t}^{\prime}\right)_{i\geq 1}:=\left(r_{i}(\Sigma_{t}^{\prime})+1\right)_{i\geq 1}

Thus by Property

, the processes
\left(R_{i,t}^{\prime}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}

Using the one-to-one correspondence

.

When studying the infinite Mallows permutation

For any fixed

Note that

\Sigma_{q}=B^{q}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus B^{q}_{k}\oplus\dots
where
B^{q}_{1},\dots,B^{q}_{k},\dots
are i.i.d. random blocks with
{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
distribution.
By
Lemma

3.5
, since the right-inversion counts of
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
are non-decreasing as
t
goes from
0
to
q
, we can write
\Sigma_{t}=B^{q}_{1,t}\oplus\dots\oplus B^{q}_{k,t}\oplus\dots
for all
t\in[0,q]
, where
B^{q}_{k,t}
has the same size as
B^{q}_{k}
for all
k\geq 1
(but might not be indecomposable).

For all
i\geq 1
and
t\in[0,q]
, let
R_{i,t}:=r_{i}\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)+1
.
By
Theorem

7.2
, the processes
\left(R_{i,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
are i.i.d. geometric birth processes.
Write
M_{k}:=\left|B^{q}_{k,t}\right|
for all
k\geq 1
.
An important observation is that the cumulative sums
M_{1}+\dots+M_{k}
are stopping times for the i.i.d. sequence of processes
\left(R_{i,\cdot}\right)_{i\geq 1}
.
Indeed,
M_{1}+\dots+M_{k}
is the first index
m
for which the sequence
R_{1,q},\dots,R_{m,q}
defines a sum of
k
blocks.
Furthermore, the sequence
\left(R_{i,\cdot}\right)_{i\leq M_{1}}
is independent from the sequence
\left(R_{i,\cdot}\right)_{i>M_{1}}
, and the latter is distributed like
\left(R_{i,\cdot}\right)_{i\geq 1}
.
As a consequence, the permutation processes
\left(B^{q}_{k,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
,
k\geq 1
are i.i.d., and it remains to check that they are distributed like
q
-block processes.

Conditionally given
\Sigma_{q}
, the processes
\left(R_{i,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
,
i\geq 1
, are independent geometric birth processes conditioned on their end-values
R_{i,q}=r_{i}\left(\Sigma_{q}\right)+1
.
Thus
\left(R_{i,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
,
i\in[M_{1}]
, are independent geometric birth processes conditioned to satisfy
R_{i,q}=r_{i}\left(B^{q}_{1}\right)+1
.
Hence the permutation process
\left(B^{q}_{1,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
, which has right-inversion counts
r_{i}\left(B^{q}_{1,t}\right)=R_{i,t}-1
for
i\in[M_{1}]
, is indeed a
q
-block process.
∎

7.2
Time-joint CLT and limit Gaussian process

We construct a continuous-time coupling of finite Mallows permutations as follows:
let
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
be a continuous-time infinite Mallows process as defined in
Theorem

7.2
, then let
\tau_{n,t}:={\mathrm{pat}}\left([n],\Sigma_{t}\right)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
t\in[0,1)
.

For any fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the process
\left(\tau_{n,t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)}
has
{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(n,t\right)
marginals by
Lemma

6.1
, and its right-inversion counts have unitary jumps.
Using the terminology of
[
Cor22
]
, it is therefore a smooth Mallows process.
However it is not clear whether its right-inversion counts are independent and Markov, i.e. whether
\left(\tau_{n,t}\right)_{t\in[0,1)]}
is a regular Markov process (with respect to its
right
-inversions rather than its
left
-inversions), or even
“close”
to being one.

Proof of
Theorem

2.7
.

Arbitrarily fix
q\in(0,1)
.
By
Theorem

7.3
, we can write
\left(\Sigma_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
as a sum of i.i.d.
q
-block processes:

\Sigma_{t}=B^{q}_{1,t}\oplus\dots\oplus B^{q}_{k,t}\oplus\dots

for all
t\in[0,q]
.
Therefore

\tau_{n,t}=B^{q}_{1,t}\oplus\dots\oplus B^{q}_{K_{n},t}\oplus\sigma_{n,t}

for all
t\in[0,q]
, where
K_{n}:=\max\left\{k\geq 0:\left|B^{q}_{1}\right|+\dots+\left|B^{q}_{k}\right|\leq n\right\}
and
\sigma_{n,t}
is a pattern of
B^{q}_{K_{n}+1,t}
.
Note that
K_{n}
depends on
q
, but not on
t
.
As in the proof of
Theorem

2.4
, we can define

{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t}\right):=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq K_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{k_{j},t}\right),

and for any
p>0
we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t}\right)-{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t}\right)\right|^{p}\right]\leq\underset{}{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(n^{d-1}\right)^{p}\right)

(29)

for all
t\in[0,q]
, where the constant inside the
\mathcal{O}
depends on
q
but not on
t
.
Now, fix
0\leq t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{m}\leq q
and a real vector
\Lambda=\left(\lambda_{\ell}\right)_{1\leq\ell\leq m}
.
We can write

\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}{\mathrm{Occ^{inj}}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t_{\ell}}\right)=\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<\dots<k_{d}\leq K_{n}}f_{\Lambda}\left(B^{q}_{k_{1},\cdot},\dots,B^{q}_{k_{d},\cdot}\right)

(30)

where

f_{\Lambda}\left(B^{q}_{k_{1},\cdot},\dots,B^{q}_{k_{d},\cdot}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{k_{j},t_{\ell}}\right).

One may thus see (
30
) as an asymmetric
U
-statistic with the underlying i.i.d. random variables
B^{q}_{k,\cdot}=\left(B^{q}_{k,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
for
k\geq 1
, and stopped at the random time
K_{n}
.
Using
[
Jan18
, Theorem 3.11]
and (
29
), we deduce that

\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t_{\ell}}\right)-\binom{n}{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t_{\ell}}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}}{n^{d-1/2}}\underset{}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}\right)

(31)

in distribution, for some
\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}\geq 0
, where
\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}\sim\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
.
When applied with
m=1
, this yields

\binom{n}{d}^{-1}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t}\right)\,\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}

in probability (and almost surely by
[
Jan18
, Theorem 3.9]
), for all
0\leq t\leq q<1
.
Using
Theorem

2.4
or the fact that
q\in[t,1)
is arbitrary, we deduce that

\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{t}_{t}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{t}\right|\right]}=e_{\pi,t}

(32)

for all
0\leq t\leq q<1
, which is a useful identity in itself.
Thus (
31
) becomes

\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t_{\ell}}\right)-\binom{n}{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}e_{\pi,t_{\ell}}}{n^{d-1/2}}\underset{}{\longrightarrow}{\cal N}\left(0,\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}\right).

(33)

The asymptotic variance
\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}
is expressed in terms of the (non-centered) univariate projections:

f_{\Lambda,j}\left((B^{q}_{t})_{t\in[0,q]}\right):=\mathbb{E}\left[f_{\Lambda}\left(B^{q}_{1,\cdot},\dots,B^{q}_{d,\cdot}\right)\,\Big|\,B^{q}_{j,\cdot}=B^{q}_{\cdot}\right]=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t_{\ell}}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t_{\ell}}\right)\right]}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B^{q}_{t_{\ell}}\right)\right],

for
j\in[d]
, where we set
\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{0}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{0}\right)\right]}:=1
for all
\rho\notin\{\mathrm{id}_{r},r\geq 1\}
by convention.
Note that
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]>0
for all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}
and
0<t\leq q<1
, since the support of
B^{q}_{t}
contains the set of indecomposable permutations.
Also,
\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\mathrm{id}_{r},B^{q}_{0}\right)\right]>0
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
since the support of
B^{q}_{0}
is the set of identity permutations.
Then by
[
Jan18
, Equation (3.28)]
:

\displaystyle\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]^{1-2d}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\binom{i\!+\!j\!-\!2}{i\!-\!1}\binom{2d\!-\!i\!-\!j}{d\!-\!i}\frac{1}{(2d-1)!}\mathrm{Cov}\left[f_{\Lambda,j}\left((B^{q}_{t})_{t\in[0,q]}\right),f_{\Lambda,i}\left((B^{q}_{t})_{t\in[0,q]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{2\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]^{-2d}}{(d-1)!d!}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B^{q}_{t_{\ell}}\right)\right]\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|,f_{\Lambda,i}\left((B^{q}_{t})_{t\in[0,q]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]^{-2d-1}}{(d-1)!^{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{\ell}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{k},B^{q}_{t_{\ell}}\right)\right]\right)^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right],

where
B_{\cdot}^{q}=\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}\sim\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
.
As in the proof of
Theorem

2.4
, we can write
\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}=\Lambda^{\rm T}H_{\pi}\Lambda
where for all
0\leq s,t\leq q
:

\displaystyle H_{\pi}(s,t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]e_{\pi,s}e_{\pi,t}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2d\!-\!1)!}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\binom{i\!+\!j\!-\!2}{i\!-\!1}\binom{2d\!-\!i\!-\!j}{d\!-\!i}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]},\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B^{q}_{s}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B^{q}_{s}\right)\right]}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(d\!-\!1)!d!}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathrm{Cov}\left[\frac{\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]},\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B^{q}_{s}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B^{q}_{s}\right)\right]}+\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B^{q}_{t}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(d\!-\!1)!^{2}}\mathrm{Var}\left[\frac{\left|B^{q}\right|}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}\right]\Bigg),

(34)

where
\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{0}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{0}\right)\right]}:=1
for all
\rho\notin\{\mathrm{id}_{r},r\geq 1\}
by convention.
The identity
\Lambda^{\rm T}H_{\pi}\Lambda=\eta_{\Lambda}^{2}\geq 0
for all vectors
\Lambda
shows that
H_{\pi}
is positive semidefinite, thus there exists a centered Gaussian process
\left(X_{\pi,t}\right)_{t\in[0,q]}
with covariance function
H_{\pi}
.
Since (
31
) holds for all
\Lambda=(\lambda_{\ell})_{1\leq\ell\leq m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the Cramér–Wold theorem implies that the random vector

\left(\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,t_{\ell}}\right)-\binom{n}{d}e_{\pi,t_{\ell}}}{n^{d-1/2}}\right)_{1\leq\ell\leq m}

converges in distribution, as
n\to\infty
, to a Gaussian vector with covariance matrix
\left(H_{\pi}(t_{\ell},t_{\ell^{\prime}})\right)_{1\leq\ell,\ell^{\prime}\leq m}
.
Since this holds for any
0\leq t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{m}\leq q<1
, this proves the finite-dimensional convergence in distribution of
\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,\tau_{n,\cdot}\right)-\binom{n}{d}e_{\pi,\cdot}}{n^{d-1/2}}
to
X_{\pi}
, and also that
H_{\pi}
does not depend on
q
(hence
X_{\pi,\cdot}
can be extended to
[0,1)
).
Convergence of joint moments holds as usual, by
[
Jan18
, Theorem 3.17]
, (
29
) and Hölder’s inequality.
∎

7.3
Continuity of the limit Gaussian process

The goal of this section is to prove
Proposition

2.8
, using in particular the Kolmogorov continuity theorem.
This requires moment estimates on
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi,B_{s}^{q}\right)
for all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}
and
0<s\leq t\leq q<1
, which we obtain by comparison with the number of jumps in a continuous-time infinite Mallows process.

In what follows, we write
\mu^{*k}
for the convolution of a measure
\mu
with itself
k
times. If
\mu
is a probability distribution, then
\mu^{*k}
is the distribution of the sum of
k
i.i.d. random variables with law
\mu
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
s\in[0,1)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Let
\left(R_{t}\right)_{t\in[s,1)}
be a birth process started at
R_{s}=k
, with birth rates
p(j,t)=\frac{j}{1-t}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
t\in[s,1)
.
Its marginal distributions are given by
R_{t}\sim{\rm Geom}\left(\frac{1-t}{1-s}\right)^{*k}
for all
t\in[s,1)
.

Proof.

For all
t\in[s,1)
, write
\mu_{k,s,t}
for the law of
R_{t}
, that is
\mu_{k,s,t}(j):=\mathbf{P}\left(\left.R_{t}=j\,\right|\,R_{s}=k\right)
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Clearly,
\mu_{k,s,t}(j)=0
whenever
j<k
, and
\mu_{k,s,s}=\delta_{k}
.
Moreover, the Chapman–Kolmogorov forward equations (see e.g. the proof of
[
AK24
, Proposition 3.5]
) yield:

\partial_{t}\mu_{k,s,t}(j)=\mu_{k,s,t}(j-1)\frac{j-1}{1-t}-\mu_{k,s,t}(j)\frac{j}{1-t}

for every
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
t\in[s,1)
.
One can check that the (only) solution of this system is given by

\mu_{k,s,t}(j)=\binom{j-1}{k-1}\left(\frac{t-s}{1-s}\right)^{j-k}\left(\frac{1-t}{1-s}\right)^{k},

for all
j\geq k
, which is indeed the
{\rm Geom}\left(\frac{1-t}{1-s}\right)^{*k}
distribution.
∎

Remark 3
.

The expression
\mu^{*k}
of the distribution of
R_{t}
in
Lemma

7.4
can be related to a
“branching property”
; it is a simple consequence of the fact that the birth rates are of the form
p(j,t)=j\cdot\tilde{p}(t)
.

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
\left(R_{t}\right)_{t\in[s,1)}
be a birth process started at
R_{0}=1
, with birth rates
p(j,t)=\frac{j}{1-t}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
t\in[0,1)
.
For all
0\leq s\leq t<1
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t}-R_{s}\right]=\frac{t-s}{(1-s)(1-t)}\quad;\quad\mathrm{Var}\left[R_{t}-R_{s}\right]=\frac{(t-s)(1+st-2s)}{(1-s)^{2}(1-t)^{2}}.

Proof.

Recall that
R_{t}\sim{\rm Geom}\left(1-t\right)
for all
t\in[0,1)
by
Lemma

7.1
, therefore:

\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t}-R_{s}\right]=\frac{1}{1-t}-\frac{1}{1-s}=\frac{t-s}{(1-s)(1-t)}.

Furthermore, by
Lemma

7.4
, the conditional law of
R_{t}
given
R_{s}
is
{\rm Geom}\left(\frac{1-t}{1-s}\right)^{*R_{s}}
.
Hence, by the law of total variance:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[R_{t}-R_{s}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[R_{t}-R_{s}\,\big|\,R_{s}\right]\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Var}\left[R_{t}-R_{s}\,\big|\,R_{s}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\left[R_{s}\frac{1-s}{1-t}-R_{s}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[R_{s}\frac{(t-s)(1-s)}{(1-t)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{(1-s)^{2}}\left(\frac{t-s}{1-t}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{1-s}\frac{(t-s)(1-s)}{(1-t)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(t-s)(1+st-2s)}{(1-s)^{2}(1-t)^{2}},

as announced.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
.
There exists a constant
c=c_{q}>0
such that for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq q
:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]\leq c(t-s)\quad;\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right)^{2}\right]\leq c(t-s)\,,

where
B_{\cdot}^{q}\sim\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma_{\cdot}\sim\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{Mallows}}\left(\mathbb{N}^{*}\right)
, and let
\left(R_{i,\cdot}\right)_{i\geq 1}
be its sequence of right-inversion counts.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, define:

J_{n,s,t}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(R_{i,t}-R_{i,s}\right)={\mathrm{inv}}\left(\big.{\mathrm{pat}}\left([n],\Sigma_{t}\right)\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\big.{\mathrm{pat}}\left([n],\Sigma_{s}\right)\right).

On the one hand, since this is a sum of i.i.d. random variables, we can use
Corollary

7.5
to compute its moments:

\mathbb{E}\left[J_{n,s,t}\right]=\frac{n(t-s)}{(1-s)(1-t)}\leq\frac{n(t-s)}{(1-q)^{2}}\quad;\quad\mathrm{Var}\left[J_{n,s,t}\right]=\frac{n(t-s)(1+st-2s)}{(1-s)^{2}(1-t)^{2}}\leq\frac{2n(t-s)}{(1-q)^{4}}.

(35)

On the other hand, recall that
\Sigma_{\cdot}=B_{1,\cdot}^{q}\oplus\dots\oplus B_{k,\cdot}^{q}\oplus\dots
by
Theorem

7.3
, where
\left(B_{k,\cdot}^{q}\right)_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
q
-block processes.
Let
K_{n}:=\max\left\{k\geq 0\,:\,\left|B_{1,\cdot}^{q}\right|+\dots+\left|B_{k,\cdot}^{q}\right|\leq n\right\}
and

J_{n,s,t}^{\prime}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}\left({\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{k,t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{k,s}^{q}\right)\right).

Then
J_{n,s,t}=J_{n,s,t}^{\prime}+\sum_{i=\left|B_{1,\cdot}^{q}\right|+\dots+\left|B_{K_{n},\cdot}^{q}\right|+1}^{n}\left(R_{i,t}-R_{i,s}\right)
, and thus

\left|J_{n,s,t}-J_{n,s,t}^{\prime}\right|\leq{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{K_{n}+1,t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{K_{n}+1,s}^{q}\right)\leq\left|B_{K_{n}+1,\cdot}^{q}\right|^{2},

(36)

which is bounded over
n
in
L^{p}
for all
p>0
, as in the proof of
Theorem

2.4
.
Since
J_{n,s,t}^{\prime}
is a
U
-statistic stopped at a renewal time, we can apply
[
Jan18
, Theorem 3.17]
:

\mathbb{E}\left[J_{n,s,t}^{\prime}\right]\sim n\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]}

(37)

as
n\to\infty
, where
B_{\cdot}^{q}\sim\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
, and

\mathrm{Var}\left[J_{n,s,t}^{\prime}\right]\sim n\Bigg(\frac{\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]}-2\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]\mathrm{Cov}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|,{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]^{2}}\\
+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]^{3}}\Bigg).

(38)

From
Equations

35
,
36
and
37
, we deduce that:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|\right]\frac{t-s}{(1-s)(1-t)}\leq c_{q}(t-s)

for some constant
c_{q}>0
.
Subsequently, the last term in (
38
) is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)(t-s)^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)(t-s)
.
Then, using
{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\leq\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|^{2}
, the second term in (
38
) is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(1)(t-s)
.
Therefore, by identifying (
38
) with (
35
) modulo (
36
), we obtain:

\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]\leq c_{q}^{\prime}(t-s)

for some constant
c_{q}^{\prime}>0
.
Finally, we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B_{s}^{q}\right)\right)^{2}\right]\leq c_{q}^{\prime}(t-s)+c_{q}^{2}(t-s)^{2}\leq\left(c_{q}^{\prime}+c_{q}^{2}\right)(t-s)

to conclude the proof.
∎

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that for all
i\in[n]
,
r_{i}(\sigma)\leq r_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})
.
Then, for all
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
:

\left|{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)-{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,\sigma^{\prime}\right)\right|\leq 2\left({\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma\right)\right)n^{r-1}.

Proof.

By induction, it suffices to prove the inequality when
{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma^{\prime}\right)={\mathrm{inv}}\left(\sigma\right)+1
.
In that case, there exists
i_{0}
such that
r_{i_{0}}(\sigma^{\prime})=r_{i_{0}}(\sigma)+1
and
r_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})=r_{i}(\sigma)
for all
i\neq i_{0}
.
It is easily checked that
\sigma^{\prime}
is then obtained from
\sigma
by swapping
\sigma(i_{0})
with
\sigma(j_{0})
, where
\sigma(j_{0})=\min\left\{\sigma(j):j>i_{0},\sigma(j)>\sigma(i_{0})\right\}
.
Consequently:

\left|{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)-{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,\sigma^{\prime}\right)\right|\leq\left|\left\{I\in\binom{[n]}{r}\,:\,I\cap\{i_{0},j_{0}\}\neq\emptyset\right\}\right|\leq 2n^{r-1}

as announced.
∎

Corollary 7.8
.

For all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}
,
q\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists
c>0
such that:

\text{for all }0\leq s\leq t\leq q,\quad\left|e_{\pi,t}-e_{\pi,s}\right|\leq c(t-s)^{\gamma}.

Proof.

We can assume w.l.o.g. that
\gamma\geq 1/2
.
Recall that

e_{\pi,t}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}

for all
t\in[0,q]
by
Equation

32
.
Therefore it suffices to prove that for all
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}
, the function
t\in[0,q]\mapsto\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]
is
\gamma
-Hölder-continuous.
Using
Lemma

7.7
and Hölder’s inequality, we can write for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq q
:

\left|\big.\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{s}\right)\right]\right|\leq\mathbb{E}\left[2\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{s}\right)\right)\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|^{\left|\rho\right|-1}\right]\\
\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{s}\right)\right)^{1/\gamma}\right]^{\gamma}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|^{(\left|\rho\right|-1)/(1-\gamma)}\right]^{1-\gamma}.

Then, using the inequality
\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{s}\right)\right)^{1/\gamma}\leq\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{s}\right)\right)^{2}
,
Lemma

7.6
and the fact that
\left|B^{q}\right|
has finite moments of all orders, we deduce that:

\left|\big.\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{t}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B^{q}_{s}\right)\right]\right|\leq c(t-s)^{\gamma}

for some
c>0
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 7.9
.

For all
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}
,
0<\epsilon<q<1
, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists
c>0
such that:

\text{for all }\epsilon\leq s,t,u\leq q,\quad\left|H_{\pi}(t,u)-H_{\pi}(s,u)\right|\leq c\left|t-s\right|^{\gamma},

where
H_{\pi}
is defined by (
34
).

Proof.

We can assume w.l.o.g. that
\gamma\geq 1/2
.
Let
B_{\cdot}^{q}\sim\overset{\longrightarrow}{\mathrm{MBlock}}\left(q\right)
.
Write
\pi=\pi_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\pi_{d}
as a sum of blocks, and set
\pi_{0}:=1
.
Then for each
0\leq i,j\leq d
, define

\text{for all }0\leq s,t<1,\quad f_{i,j}(s,t):=\mathrm{Cov}\left[\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]},\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}\right]

where
\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B_{0}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\rho,B_{0}^{q}\right)\right]}:=1
for all
\rho\notin\{\mathrm{id}_{r},r\geq 1\}
.
Since
{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{0},B\right)=\left|B\right|
,
34
rewrites as:

\text{for all }0\leq s,t<1,\quad H_{\pi}(s,t)=\sum_{0\leq i,j\leq d}c_{i,j,\pi,q}\,e_{\pi,s}\,e_{\pi,t}\,f_{i,j}(s,t)

(39)

for some constants
c_{i,j,\pi,q}>0
.
Therefore it suffices to prove that for each fixed
0\leq i,j\leq d
, there exists
c>0
such that:

\text{for all }\epsilon\leq s,t,u\leq q,\quad\left|\big.e_{\pi,u}e_{\pi,t}f_{i,j}(t,u)-e_{\pi,u}e_{\pi,s}f_{i,j}(s,u)\right|\leq c\left|t-s\right|^{\gamma}.

Since
\left|B^{q}\right|
has finite moments of all orders, we can bound:

\left|\big.e_{\pi,u}e_{\pi,t}f_{i,j}(t,u)-e_{\pi,u}e_{\pi,s}f_{i,j}(s,u)\right|\leq\left|\big.e_{\pi,u}\left(\big.e_{\pi,t}-e_{\pi,s}\right)f_{i,j}(t,u)\right|+\left|\big.e_{\pi,u}e_{\pi,s}\left(\big.f_{i,j}(t,u)-f_{i,j}(s,u)\right)\right|\\
\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\left|\big.e_{\pi,t}-e_{\pi,s}\right|+\mathcal{O}(1)\left|\big.e_{\pi_{j},u}e_{\pi_{i},s}\left(\big.f_{i,j}(t,u)-f_{i,j}(s,u)\right)\right|

(40)

where the constants inside the
\mathcal{O}(1)
’s only depend on
\pi
and
q
.
The first term of (
40
) is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)\left|t-s\right|^{\gamma}
by
Corollary

7.8
.
Then, let
\alpha,\bar{\alpha}\in(1,\infty)
be arbirtrary Hölder conjugates.
Recall that
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{j}
are indecomposable, and use (
32
) to write:

\displaystyle\left|\big.e_{\pi_{j},u}e_{\pi_{i},s}\left(\big.f_{i,j}(t,u)-f_{i,j}(s,u)\right)\right|

\displaystyle=e_{\pi_{j},u}e_{\pi_{i},s}\left|\mathrm{Cov}\left[\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{u}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{u}^{q}\right)\right]},\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\pi_{j},u}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{u}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{u}^{q}\right)\right]}-1\right|\cdot e_{\pi_{i},s}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{j},B_{u}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}-e_{\pi_{j},u}\right|^{\bar{\alpha}}\right]^{1/\bar{\alpha}}\cdot e_{\pi_{i},s}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}.

(41)

The first term of (
41
) is a
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
For the second term, by Minkowski’s inequality we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}.

First use (
32
) to write:

e_{\pi_{i},s}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}\leq\mathcal{O}(1)e_{\pi_{i},s}\left|\big.\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|\\
\leq\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]}\left|\big.\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)\right]\right|

which is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)\left|t-s\right|^{\gamma}
for
\epsilon\leq s\leq t\leq q
by
Corollary

7.8
, where the constant inside
\mathcal{O}(1)
only depends on
\pi
,
\gamma
,
\epsilon
and
q
(this is the only step at which
\epsilon
is relevant).
Then let
\beta,\bar{\beta}\in(1,\infty)
be arbitrary Hölder conjugates, and use
Lemma

7.7
to write:

\displaystyle e_{\pi_{i},s}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}-\frac{{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)}{\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right]}\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B^{q}\right|\right]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\big.{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{t}^{q}\right)-{\mathrm{Occ}}\left(\pi_{i},B_{s}^{q}\right)\right|^{\alpha}\right]^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{s}\right)\right)^{\alpha}\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|^{\alpha(\left|\pi_{i}\right|-1)}\right]^{1/\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\big.{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{t}\right)-{\mathrm{inv}}\left(B^{q}_{s}\right)\right)^{\alpha\beta}\right]^{1/(\alpha\beta)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|B_{\cdot}^{q}\right|^{\alpha\bar{\beta}(\left|\pi_{i}\right|-1)}\right]^{1/(\alpha\bar{\beta})}.

If we choose
\alpha,\beta
to satisfy
\alpha\beta=1/\gamma
, then this is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)\left|t-s\right|^{\gamma}
by
Lemma

7.6
.
We deduce that (
41
) is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1)\left|t-s\right|^{\gamma}
, where the constant inside
\mathcal{O}(1)
only depends on

,
\gamma
,

and

, therefore such a bound holds for (

By (

The proof of
Proposition

2.8
now follows readily.

Proof of
Proposition

2.8
.

The first item is just
Corollary

7.8
.

For the second item, we study the moments of
X_{\pi,t}-X_{\pi,s}
for fixed
0<\epsilon<q<1
and all
\epsilon\leq s\leq t\leq q
.
The random variable
X_{\pi,t}-X_{\pi,s}
follows the
{\cal N}\left(0,\big.H_{\pi}(t,t)+H_{\pi}(s,s)-2H_{\pi}(s,t)\right)
distribution, thus for all
p>0
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{\pi,t}-X_{\pi,s}\right|^{p}\right]=c_{p}\left(\big.H_{\pi}(t,t)+H_{\pi}(s,s)-2H_{\pi}(s,t)\right)^{p/2}\\
\leq 2^{p}c_{p}\left(\left|\big.H_{\pi}(t,t)-H_{\pi}(s,t)\right|^{p/2}+\left|\big.H_{\pi}(s,t)-H_{\pi}(s,s)\right|^{p/2}\right),

where
c_{p}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|Y\right|^{p}\right]
for
Y\sim{\cal N}\left(0,1\right)
.
Let
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
Using
Corollary

7.9
, we can write

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{\pi,t}-X_{\pi,s}\right|^{p}\right]\leq c^{\prime}(t-s)^{\gamma p/2}

for some
c^{\prime}>0
and all
\epsilon\leq s\leq t\leq q
.
Choosing
p
such that
\gamma p/2>1
allows us to apply the Kolmogorov continuity theorem (see e.g.
[
Kal06
, Theorem 4.23]
):
there exists a version of
\left(X_{\pi,t}\right)_{t\in[\epsilon,q]}
that is locally
\tilde{\gamma}
-Hölder-continuous, for every
\tilde{\gamma}<\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma p-1}{p}
.
Since this holds for any
0<\epsilon<q<1
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and large enough
p
, this concludes the proof.
∎

Appendix: the number of inversions

In this appendix, we provide a proof of
Theorem

1.2
.
Note that the same techniques can be used to study the asymptotics of
{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)
for different regimes of
q=q_{n}
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

Using
Lemma

3.3
, we can write

{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}-n

(42)

where
L_{k}\sim{\rm TruncGeom}\left(k,q\right)
are independent random variables.
According to
[
DR00
, Property 4]
, we have:

\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n+1}\right)\right]=\frac{nq}{1\!-\!q}-\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}\frac{jq^{j}}{1\!-\!q^{j}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n+1}\right)\right]=\frac{nq}{(1\!-\!q)^{2}}-\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}\frac{j^{2}q^{j}}{(1\!-\!q^{j})^{2}}.

The rest of the proof is standard computation.
Since
n(1\!-\!q)\to 0
, we can write uniformly in
j\in[n+1]
:

\displaystyle q^{j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[j\log\left(1-(1\!-\!q)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[j\left(-(1\!-\!q)-\tfrac{1}{2}(1\!-\!q)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}(1\!-\!q)^{3}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-j(1\!-\!q)-\tfrac{1}{2}j(1\!-\!q)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}j(1\!-\!q)^{3}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))\right]

\displaystyle=1-j(1\!-\!q)-\tfrac{1}{2}j(1\!-\!q)^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}j(1\!-\!q)^{3}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(j(1\!-\!q)+\tfrac{1}{2}j(1\!-\!q)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}j(1\!-\!q)^{3}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\tfrac{1}{6}\left(j(1\!-\!q)+\tfrac{1}{2}j(1\!-\!q)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{3}j(1\!-\!q)^{3}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))\right)^{3}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))

\displaystyle=1-j(1\!-\!q)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)+(1\!-\!q)^{3}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1)).

Then:

\displaystyle\frac{jq^{j}}{1\!-\!q^{j}}

\displaystyle=j\left(1-j(1\!-\!q)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(j(1\!-\!q)-(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)-(1\!-\!q)^{3}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=j\left(1-j(1\!-\!q)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot j^{-1}(1\!-\!q)^{-1}\cdot\left(1-(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left((1\!-\!q)^{-1}-j+(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(1+(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle=\left((1\!-\!q)^{-1}-j+(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(1+(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{12}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{12}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle=(1\!-\!q)^{-1}+\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{12}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{12}\right)(1\!+\!o(1)).

We can deduce the desired estimate for the mean:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{1\!-\!q}-n-\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}(1\!-\!q)^{-1}+\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{12}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{12}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{4}n^{2}-\tfrac{1}{36}(1\!-\!q)n^{3}+\underset{}{o}\left(n^{3/2}\vee(1\!-\!q)n^{3}\right).

Now, let us turn to the variance.
We have, uniformly in
j\in[n+1]
:

\displaystyle\frac{j^{2}q^{j}}{(1\!-\!q^{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=j^{2}\left(1-j(1\!-\!q)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(j(1\!-\!q)-(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)-(1\!-\!q)^{3}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{3}+\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{3}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle=j^{2}\left(1-j(1\!-\!q)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot j^{-2}(1\!-\!q)^{-2}\cdot\left(1-(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)-(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)^{-2}

\displaystyle=\left((1\!-\!q)^{-2}-j(1\!-\!q)^{-1}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(1+2(1\!-\!q)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)+2(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(-\tfrac{1}{6}j^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))+3(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\cdot(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle=\left((1\!-\!q)^{-2}-j(1\!-\!q)^{-1}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\left(1+(1\!-\!q)\left(j-1\right)+(1\!-\!q)^{2}\left(\tfrac{5}{12}j^{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}j+\tfrac{1}{12}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))\right)

\displaystyle=(1\!-\!q)^{-2}-(1\!-\!q)^{-1}+\left(-\tfrac{1}{12}j^{2}+\tfrac{1}{12}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))+\underset{}{\mathcal{O}}\left((1\!-\!q)j^{3}\right),

therefore:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{(1\!-\!q)^{2}}-\frac{n}{(1\!-\!q)}-\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}(1\!-\!q)^{-2}-(1\!-\!q)^{-1}+\left(-\tfrac{1}{12}j^{2}+\tfrac{1}{12}\right)(1\!+\!o(1))+\underset{}{\mathcal{O}}\left((1\!-\!q)j^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{36}n^{3}+\underset{}{o}\left(n^{3}\right).

It remains to deduce the announced asymptotic normality;
it suffices to prove it for
\sum_{k=1}^{n}L_{k}
.
Since this is a sum of independent variables, it suffices to check Lyapunov’s condition.
For any
\delta>0
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}\left[{\mathrm{inv}}\left(\tau_{n}\right)\right]^{1+\delta/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|L_{k}-\mathbb{E}L_{k}\right|^{2+\delta}\right]\leq\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{n^{3+3\delta/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{2+\delta}\leq\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{n^{3+3\delta/2}}\,n^{3+\delta}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

We can thus conclude with Lyapunov’s CLT.
∎
