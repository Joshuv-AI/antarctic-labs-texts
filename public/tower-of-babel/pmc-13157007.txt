Expected level of target cues determines prospective memory strategic processing: The effect of task switching
Authors: Wanqi Wang, Yaru Sun, Dongqing Li, Ning Chen, Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTarget expectancy and task switching are pivotal in shaping prospective memory (PM). In two dual-task experiments (event-based, N = 71; time-based, N = 68), participants performed an N-back task while maintaining PM intentions under varied expectancy and task structures (fixed vs. switching). Event-based prospective memory (EBPM) targets were specific radicals; time-based prospective memory (TBPM) targets required responding at 2/3-min intervals with clock access and color cues. Results showed that higher expectancy increased intention accessibility, speeding PM responses and reducing background task costs across PM types. However, switching moderated these benefits: in EBPM, switching impaired accuracy and weakened expectancy effects, reflecting increased retrospective load. Conversely, in our TBPM paradigm, switching was associated with higher accuracy—consistent with task-boundary “refresh” cues (a pattern that may depend on temporal-schedule features). These findings illustrate how internal expectations and task complexity jointly modulate PM architecture, providing new empirical support for the dynamic multiprocess framework.

Introduction

Prospective memory (PM) is memory for activities to be performed in the future, which is categorized as either event- or time-based.1 Event-based prospective memory (EBPM) refers to remembering to perform an action when a specific condition is met (e.g., “remember to post a letter upon encountering a mailbox on the street”), whereas time-based prospective memory (TBPM) involves remembering to execute an action at a specific future time (e.g., “attend the meeting in the conference room at 3 p.m. tomorrow”). Crucially, successful EBPM relies on external cues to trigger retrieval (as in the “mailbox” cue in the example), while TBPM depends more on self-initiated internal activation. Consequently, the latter generally demands greater cognitive resources.2

Whether event-based or time-based, successful PM execution relies on accurately identifying target cues (i.e., cues triggering PM execution). Thus, researchers have extensively studied the mechanisms by which target cues influence PM execution. These studies focus on the effect of target cue characteristics, such as focality and salience3,4 and the influence of cue source, complexity, and learning on cue processing.5,6 In addition to objective characteristics, the role of subjective expectations in PM is investigated as well. The most common approach in this line of research is manipulating the expectations of participants about cue timing or location. Cook et al.7 found that participants expecting a target cue to appear in the third phase of an experiment exhibited higher PM performance when the cue appeared as expected. Matching expected cues with their actual timing or context improves PM performance across various types—event-based, time-based,8 and mixed9 as well as age groups10 by modulating processing strategies.

Beyond expectations about temporal and situational aspects, subjective expectations regarding the likelihood of target cue appearance may also influence PM processing. Recent studies, employing a prospective person memory task involving searches for missing persons, have investigated how expectations about the probability of encountering the target (i.e., the missing person) affect search performance (i.e., responding upon target detection). These studies demonstrate that under certain conditions, higher expected probabilities correlate with increases in both hit rates and false alarm rates. However, this line of research11,12 has not yet deeply investigated this pattern of results from a processing strategy perspective.

To systematically examine these underlying strategies, PM is typically studied using a controlled dual-task paradigm. In this framework, the PM task is embedded within an ongoing activity (hereafter referred to as the background task), enabling researchers to manipulate task demands while assessing both prospective-memory performance and background-task costs. One effective way to vary these demands is through task switching. Task switching refers to the ability to rapidly shift between tasks in changing environments, and it is a fundamental aspect of cognitive flexibility. It has been proposed that successful task switching relies on a “gating” mechanism within working memory, which regulates transitions between distinct task sets.13 Researchers have extended the task-switching paradigm to investigate PM mechanisms. First, when detecting the target cue of the prospective task during the current task, requiring participants to interrupt the current task and switch to executing the PM task exemplifies the switching between these two tasks (i.e., the PM task and the current task).14 Second, each task within the PM dual-task paradigm can appear in the form of task switching. For instance, some researchers designed the current task as a switching task between lexical decision and initial letter judgment, finding that switching within the background task did not significantly affect PM performance.15 However, prior research has primarily manipulated switching demands within the background task, whereas no study to date has explicitly manipulated switching demands within the PM task itself (i.e., requiring participants to alternate between different PM intentions; prospective-task switching). Crucially, prospective-task switching is qualitatively different from the more commonly studied switching within the background task. Whereas background task switching primarily increases general control demands, switching between PM intentions requires participants to maintain multiple intention-cue mappings, select the currently relevant mapping at each task boundary, and inhibit competing intentions. This manipulation is therefore expected to disproportionately tax retrospective selection/discrimination processes (i.e., identifying “which intention applies now” and retrieving the appropriate response rule), even when the ongoing task remains constant. As a result, prospective-task switching provides a principled way to test whether probability expectancy benefits are robust or whether they are constrained by elevated intention-selection demands, especially for PM accuracy.

The executive process of PM comprises two core components: the prospective component and the retrospective component. The prospective component refers to activating the intended action at an appropriate time, primarily involving attentional processing; the retrospective component refers to the memory of the content of the execution intention, involving a recall process.1 Critically, subjective expectancy regarding the “probability” of target cue occurrence—which modulates the activation level of the target cue—primarily engages the prospective component. Conversely, task switching (particularly when involving PM tasks) alters the retrieval content of the PM intention, thereby affecting the retrospective component. Thus, embedding both task switching demands and subjective probability expectancy within the PM dual-task paradigm can better explore the mechanisms and characteristics of PM processing from the perspective of its two components.7,14 Furthermore, PM is intrinsically linked to daily functioning.16 In daily life, there are also scenarios where PM task switching combines with probability expectations. For instance, the prospective intention“bring soccer shoes for a football match if it is sunny tomorrow, or bring an instrument for band rehearsal if it rains”, and the expectation of rain probability also influences plan execution to some extent. This intention incorporates switching between two distinct tasks (attending a match vs. participating in rehearsal), while subjective expectancy about rain probability may modulate the execution of this plan to some extent.

The dynamic multiprocess framework (DMF) proposes that PM performance can be supported by multiple routes (e.g., spontaneous retrieval versus strategic monitoring), and that the balance between these routes depends on task context and available cues. However, an important empirical gap remains: it is still unclear how subjective probability expectancy (a top-down factor that may elevate intention accessibility) interacts with prospective-task switching (a structural constraint that increases intention-selection and retrospective demands). In other words, even if higher expectancy generally promotes readiness, does this benefit persist when the intention set must be repeatedly updated and competing intentions must be resolved at task boundaries? By testing probability expectancy under fixed versus prospective-task switching structures and by comparing EBPM and TBPM within the same dual-task framework, the present study aims to specify boundary conditions for expectancy effects and to clarify whether these constraints differ across PM types. Based on this, the present study uses the PM dual-task paradigm,1 with an N-back background task to test how probability expectancy translates into PM benefits (speed, accuracy, and costs) under low versus high intention-selection demands (fixed vs. prospective-task switching), and whether these constraints differ for EBPM (experiment 1) and TBPM (experiment 2). The study aims to investigate the impact mechanism of expectation levels on PM performance in fixed and switching tasks. According to the previous argument that probability expectations of PM targets primarily affect the prospective component of PM, and the finding that high-probability expectations enhance PM performance.12 Hypothesis 1 of this study is, for both event-based and TBPM, high-probability expectations of target cues lead to higher activation of prospective intentions, resulting in better PM performance than low-probability expectations. Additionally, as previously analyzed, task switching in PM primarily acts on the retrospective component rather than the prospective component. Switching tasks in PM involve more complex retrospective components, which negatively impact EBPM.17 Thus, hypothesis 2 is, for EBPM, fixed prospective tasks yield better performance than switching prospective tasks. For TBPM, cues from different prospective task switches can serve as task-boundary signals for the task, periodically reorienting attention and refreshing the time-monitoring goal. Therefore, hypothesis 3 is proposed: relative to EBPM, TBPM will show a smaller switching cost. We note that any switching-related facilitation, if observed, may depend on the temporal structure and boundary frequency of the TBPM task.

Interim discussion

Experiment 1 investigated the effects of target cue expectation and prospective task switching on EBPM. Results revealed that task type significantly influenced PM performance: compared with fixed-task conditions, switching-task conditions produced lower PM accuracy and longer PM reaction times. This finding aligns with Bisiacchi et al.,14 who demonstrated that switching tasks diminishes PM performance. However, task type did not significantly affect background-task performance (either accuracy or reaction time). This pattern suggests that switching increased PM specific processing demands, such as maintaining, selecting, and retrieving the appropriate PM rule under changing requirements, which may have impaired PM performance.19 These results supported hypothesis 2.

The overall anticipation of target cues did not significantly affect PM accuracy, but a significant interaction was observed between prospective task type and cue expectation levels. Cue expectations significantly improved PM accuracy specifically in the fixed-task condition. Furthermore, cue expectations shortened background-task reaction times in both fixed and switching conditions, suggesting a general facilitation in processing speed associated with expectancy. At the same time, because task type did not affect background-task accuracy or RT, the detrimental effect of switching in experiment 1 appears to be selective to PM performance rather than reflected in an additional background task cost (within the sensitivity of our background-task measures). In the switching condition, EBPM accuracy remained low and near floor across expectation groups, which may have limited the observable benefit of cue expectation. These findings suggest that task characteristics and cue expectations jointly influence PM processing, and they partially supported hypothesis 1.

Interim discussion

The results of experiment 2 showed that expectations facilitated TBPM, consistent with previous research20 and supported hypothesis 1. This improvement likely reflects that expectancy promotes more proactive time monitoring and more efficient use of available time-related information (e.g., strategically relying on the color cue when present), rather than continuous stimulus-driven target search. Contrary to experiment 1, switching tasks outperformed fixed tasks in TBPM, and the background tasks were completed more fluently. We propose that it reflects differences in monitoring requirements and the resulting background task interference patterns between time and EBPM.21 While EBPM requires continuous, stimulus-driven monitoring that competes directly with background task performance, TBPM relies on periodic, self-initiated time-estimation and clock-checking. In our study, the switching structure may have provided natural task boundaries that helped participants refresh the intention and recalibrate time monitoring. Importantly, the block-start numbers served only as block-level interval-rule indicators (2 vs. 3 min) and did not signal the exact moment for the PM response; participants still had to monitor elapsed time and respond at the appropriate 2- or 3-min mark. This task-boundary scaffolding may explain why TBPM performance was maintained or even improved under switching demands in experiment 2, whereas a cost was observed in experiment 1. These results supported hypothesis 3. We therefore treat the switching-related facilitation in TBPM as a boundary-condition finding rather than a general property of time-based PM. Because the fixed and switching blocks differed in temporal schedule (e.g., continuous 3-min pacing versus alternating 2- and 3-min interval rules, which also increases boundary salience), schedule-specific contributions cannot be ruled out.

Experiment 2 also revealed an interaction between prospective task type and cue expectation. In switching tasks, both expectancy groups showed lower TBPM accuracy than the no-expectation group, whereas in fixed tasks the opposite pattern emerged. This finding, together with the higher clock-checking frequency in the no-expectation group under switching, suggests that cue expectation may influence TBPM by shifting the balance between self-initiated periodic monitoring (indexed by clock checking) and reliance on expected external cues (e.g., the color cue), with the preferred strategy differing between fixed and switching tasks.

Discussion

Role of target cue probability expectations in facilitating event− and time-based prospective memory processing

From the background task performance in experiment 1’s EBPM, the no-expectancy, low-expectancy, and high-expectancy groups showed a decreasing trend in performance. Additionally, reaction times for both background and prospective tasks decreased with increasing expectancy, but only high expectancy led to a decrease in background task accuracy. These results suggest that expectations of target probability highlight the execution intentions (i.e., the prospective component) of event-based prospective tasks, allocating more cognitive resources to prospective tasks, which enhances the fluency of both prospective and background tasks but impairs background task performance. This indicates that cognitive resources are distributed and transferred between prospective and background tasks according to specific strategies, reflecting the effect of prospective interference. Consistent with our study, Pinto et al.22 manipulated the specificity of target expectations in EBPM and found that specific cues (e.g., “a target will appear after a square”) induced clearer intentions than indirect cues (e.g., “a target will appear after some shape”), promoting greater cognitive resource transfer from background to prospective tasks and leading to higher background task error rates. This aligns with the prospective interference effect, where maintaining prospective intentions affects reaction times and accuracy of background tasks.23

Previous research has shown that clock-checking in TBPM relies on an internal strategy to minimize checking frequency while approaching the target time.24 In experiment 2, the no-expectancy, low-expectancy, and high-expectancy groups showed a sequential decrease in clock-checking frequency. This is because expectations of TBPM targets, presented as external cues, serve as “aids” to the internal clock-checking strategy under high-expectancy conditions, enabling task completion with fewer checks. The cognitive resource balance between expectancy facilitation and clock-checking thus prevents background task performance from being affected.

In summary, target cues and probability expectations enable participants to establish more explicit intentions to perform PM tasks,25 primarily acting on the prospective component of PM. Thus, in both event and TBPM, expectations about the probability of target cues maintain the intention to execute the prospective task at a higher level of activation, making it more readily retrievable. This enhanced activation facilitates the automatic processing of PM.7 This finding consistent with the perspective of the DMF of PM,26 which proposes that automatic and strategic processes in PM dynamically adapt to task demands. Our findings move beyond a simple verification of the DMF by demonstrating that target probability acts as a strategic modulator of cognitive resource allocation. Rather than a static reaction to cues, the cognitive system pre-emptively reshapes the attentional landscape based on subjective expectancy. This proactive adjustment highlights a “dynamic optimization” process within the DMF, where internal states can override the default processing of ongoing activities to prioritize prospective intentions.

Effect of task switching on event- and time-based prospective memory processing

Experiment 1 indicated that regardless of target cue probability expectations, EBPM performance was significantly lower in switching than fixed task conditions. The characteristics of the two tasks reveal that switching task conditions require participants to maintain more complex prospective task content. That is, PM has a higher retrospective component load in the switching condition. Participants must determine the odd or even nature of block numbers, whether a two-character word contains the target radical, and whether the position of the target character corresponds to the odd or even nature of the block number, among other demands. This increased retrospective component load contributes to PM failure.19 While this result aligns with the established resource-competition accounts of PM, it serves as a crucial theoretical benchmark. It demonstrates that our switching manipulation effectively induces a typical cognitive cost, thereby highlighting the unique and counter-intuitive facilitation effect observed in our time-based experiment.

On the surface, for TBPM, the switching task (pressing a specific key every 2 or 3 min in response to presented numbers 2 or 3) appeared to impose a higher retrospective memory load compared to the fixed task (pressing a key every 3 min). However, an unexpected finding in experiment 2 was that, contrary to EBPM, TBPM accuracy was higher under switching task conditions than under fixed task. On the one hand, according to existing research, while the fixed task instruction of “pressing a specific key every 3 min” seems simple, it actually requires self-generation and accurate execution of temporal cues (e.g., at 3, 6, and 9 min), a process that consumes considerable cognitive resources.27 On the other hand, TBPM in fixed tasks may be easier during encoding (pressing the “0” key at fixed time intervals) but is more challenging during retrieval. This difficulty is attributable to the sole reliance of retrieval on the self-initiated internal cue of time monitoring. In switching tasks, task boundaries—marked by block-level numbers indicating the interval rule (2 vs. 3 min)—may have provided structural scaffolding for TBPM by prompting intention refreshing and recalibration of time monitoring. Importantly, these numbers served strictly as rule indicators rather than response signals and were not temporally aligned with the target moments. Notably, the TBPM pattern contrasts with experiment 1 (EBPM), in which the same switching structure reduced PM accuracy, suggesting that task boundaries may differentially affect stimulus-driven cue processing versus periodic time-monitoring demands.28 Nevertheless, we treat the switching-related facilitation in TBPM as a boundary-condition finding, because the fixed and switching blocks differed in temporal schedule (continuous 3-min pacing versus alternating 2- and 3-min rules, which also increases boundary salience). Future studies should target schedules across conditions and manipulate boundary salience independently of interval structure to assess generalizability.

Effect of task switching on the role of target cue probability expectations in prospective memory processing

Expectations regarding target cue probability can effectively facilitate event-based and time-based PM, yet the introduction of task switching in this study modulated this relationship. In experiment 1, target cue probability expectations under switching task conditions had no significant effect on the accuracy of EBPM. This is mainly because switching tasks led to high cognitive resource demand and more strategic processing, reducing the cognitive resources occupied by the prospective component.29 This reduction in cognitive capacity diminishes the role of probability expectations as a prompt for intentions and their facilitation of the prospective component.

The influence of task switching on the role of target cue probability expectations in TBPM is even more pronounced. In experiment 2 under switching task conditions, both groups with probability expectations exhibited lower TBPM performance, compared with the no-expectation group. When completing TBPM tasks with switching conditions, the high cognitive demands impede accurate monitoring of time-based target cues. Participants in the expectation groups relied more on expected cues to ensure the completion of both prospective and background tasks. This is corroborated by the finding that the expectation group checked the clock less frequently. However, a mismatch between expected and target cue probabilities was introduced in the experimental design. This discrepancy may explain the lower TBPM performance in the expectation groups under switching task conditions because of “misleading” expectations. This interpretation is supported by comparative studies of PM with and without target cue expectations. Researchers found that during the completion of PM tasks, the presence of expected target cues encourages participants to rely on these prompts. This reliance leads to monitoring of prospective intentions only when prompts appear because of an “economizing” principle. By contrast, internal monitoring becomes continuous when no expected target cues are present, and participants need to complete prospective tasks spontaneously.30 This finding also supports the perspective of the DMF.

The observed interaction reveals the boundary conditions of strategic processing. Specifically, the “misleading” effect of expectancy in TBPM under switching conditions underscores a critical “economizing” principle: participants may over-rely on internal expectations to the point of neglecting necessary monitoring when the environment becomes volatile. This reveals the maladaptive side of the DMF’s flexibility—the transition from continuous monitoring to expectancy-driven monitoring is a “double-edged sword” that can impair performance if internal strategies mismatch external realities. In summary, the presence of expectations requires participants to expend more cognitive resources to complete both prospective and current tasks under certain conditions than under no-expectation conditions.10 Thus, the effect of task switching on the role of target cue probability expectations in PM depends on the specific cognitive demands inherent to various types of PM tasks, such as event- and time-based tasks.

Overall, the present findings indicate that expectancy maintains prospective intentions at a heightened level of activation in both EBPM and TBPM, thereby facilitating PM processing. Importantly, this benefit was differentially constrained by task structure across PM types. In EBPM, switching weakened the expectancy effect, likely because it increased retrospective demands. In contrast, in the present TBPM paradigm, switching was associated with higher accuracy, possibly because task boundaries supported periodic monitoring. This facilitation, however, should be interpreted as a boundary-condition finding that may depend on specific temporal-schedule features. Taken together, these results refine the DMF by clarifying how internal expectancy and external task structure jointly shape PM processing under dynamic multitasking demands.

Limitations of the study

This study introduced novel manipulations of target probability (for event-based PM) and cue probability (for time-based PM), alongside task-switching variables. Our results demonstrate how expectation levels and task switching independently and jointly influence PM by modulating cognitive resources, providing empirical support for interpreting PM mechanisms through the lens of limited cognitive capacity theory. Notably, these experimental manipulations mirror common real-world PM scenarios—such as estimating the probability of encountering a colleague to return a book, or determining whether an afternoon meeting depends on morning negotiations—thereby offering practical insights into everyday complex PM.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, although time-based and event-based PM differ substantially in their underlying processing mechanisms,31 we interpreted both types of results within a unified limited-resource framework. While this provides a cohesive theoretical perspective, it lacks a more granular, targeted analysis of how these factors specifically affect the distinct characteristics of each PM system.

Second, the near-floor performance observed in event-based PM under switching conditions in experiment 1 suggests that the task difficulty may have been excessively high, potentially masking more nuanced effects.

Furthermore, in experiment 1, the fixed PM blocks always preceded the switching blocks (task-type order was not counterbalanced). Although the overall pattern of results is not readily explained by a pure time-on-task account, order effects remain a limitation. Future research should employ counterbalanced or interleaved designs and incorporate planned time-course analyses (e.g., block-half comparisons) to more rigorously isolate the independent effects of task structure from temporal variables.

Method details

Materials

Three hundred sixty neutral, low-frequency, two-character words (average frequency, 2.75 occurrences per 10,000 words) were selected from the Modern Chinese Common Word Frequency Dictionary. These words included 120 nouns, 120 verbs, and 120 adjectives, matched for stroke count. Each word appeared four times in the experiment.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. All analyses were conducted separately for Experiment 1 and Experiment 2. The exact sample sizes (n represents the number of human participants) and group distributions are detailed in the experimental model and study participant details section and relevant figure legends.

For both experiments, PM accuracy, PM reaction time, and background-task metrics were analyzed using mixed-design analyses of variance (ANOVAs), with expectancy group as a between-subjects factor and task type (fixed vs. switching) as a within-subjects factor. In Experiment 2, clock-checking frequency was analyzed using chi-square tests. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where sphericity assumptions were violated. Significant main effects or interactions were followed by simple-effects analyses or post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni corrections.

PM accuracy in Experiment 1 was calculated as the proportion of correct responses to PM target cues out of the total number of target presentations. In Experiment 2, TBPM scoring followed the time-window criteria described in the method details section; specifically, responses occurring within 20 s before to 5 s after the target time were coded as correct. Unless otherwise noted, data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Detailed statistical information, including the statistical tests used, exact sample sizes, test statistics (F, t, χ2), exact or thresholded p values, and effect sizes (ηp2, where applicable), is reported in the results section and corresponding figure legends.

Design

Experiment 1

The experiment adopted a 3 (probability expectation of PM target cues: no-expectation, low-expectation, high-expectation) × 2 (type of PM task: fixed task, switching task) mixed factorial design. The probability expectation of PM target cues was manipulated between participants. Accuracy and reaction times for the background and PM tasks served as dependent variables.

Experiment 2

The experiment employed a 3 (probability expectation of target cues: no-expectation, low-expectation, high-expectation) × 2 (type of PM task: fixed task, switching task) mixed factorial design. The expectation of target cues was a between-subjects variable. Dependent variables were accuracy, reaction times, and clock-viewing frequency for current and prospective tasks. TBPM performance was scored using criteria adapted from Huang et al.,32 who classified a PM response as successful if it occurred within a limited time window around the target time (±20 s), and treated responses outside this window (early/late) as failures. In the present cued time-based PM task, we therefore adopted an asymmetric scoring window to separate anticipatory time-based responding from delayed post-cue responding: a response was coded as correct if participants pressed the “0” key within 20 s before the scheduled target time (capturing timely, actively monitored intention retrieval) and within 5 s after cue onset (ensuring the response reflected an immediate cue-triggered execution rather than a delayed response after the cue period). Responses occurring earlier than −20 s or later than +5 s were scored as incorrect.

Procedure

Experiment 1

The experiment employed a dual-task paradigm for assessing PM. The background task was an N-back word type judgment task in which four two-character words were presented sequentially. The first three words consisted of one noun, one verb, and one adjective. Participants were required to judge which of the first three words matched the grammatical category of the fourth word by pressing the corresponding number key.

In the fixed task condition, the prospective task was as follows: if one character of the fourth two-character word contained the radical “氵”, participants were to skip the word type judgment and press the “0” key. To illustrate, four representative word examples demonstrate the radical “氵” distribution: 漂亮 (The first character“漂”contains “氵”); 检测 (The second character“测”contains “氵”); 活泼 (Both character“活” and “泼”contains“氵”); 壮丽 (Neither character contains“氵”). In the switching task condition, the target cue for the PM task was determined by the parity (the fact of being odd or even) of the numerical sequence presented at the beginning of each block. If the number was odd, participants responded when the first character of the fourth two-character word contained the radical “氵”(e.g., “漂亮”;漂 contains“氵”); If the number was even, the PM target was defined by the second character containing “氵”(e.g., “大海”;海 contains “氵”).33

The expected level of target cues was manipulated through the assumed probability of their occurrence. During the encoding phase of PM, participants were presented with 10 two-character words that met the criteria for target cues (having the radical“氵”) and were instructed to learn them for 2 min. They were then informed that 20% (2 words for the low-expectation group) or 80% (8 words for the high-expectation group) of these words would appear as target cues. The no-expectation group received no information about the probability of target cue occurrence. Five of the words that appeared during the learning stage appeared in actual experiments.

Based on the ratio of PM targets to non-targets in previous studies,23 the experiment comprised 36 blocks with 10 trials per block, totaling 360 trials. The blocks were equally divided between fixed PM tasks and switching PM tasks.20 Each block was separated by a 1,000-ms inter-block interval. Participants first completed 18 blocks of fixed PM tasks, followed by instructions for the switching task, and subsequently performed 18 blocks of switching tasks. Five PM target words each appeared once in both task types, resulting in a total of 10 target word occurrences. The total duration of the experiment was approximately 45 min (see Figure 1).

Experiment 2

In accordance with the objectives of the current study, the “time windows” procedure was adapted from Cook et al.7 The fixed task for TBPM required participants to press the “0” key every 3 min during the background task. In the switching task condition, the PM task changed based on a number presented at the start of each block. For instance, if the number was “2”, the prospective task was to press the “0” key every 2 min from the first trial of that block. If no number appeared at the start of the next block, the previous task continued. When the number “3” appeared, the prospective task switched to pressing the “0” key every 3 min. This pattern continued. It is important to note that these numbers served only as block-level rule indicators (2-min vs. 3-min interval) presented at block onset and were not temporally contingent with the target moments. Participants still had to self-initiate time monitoring (via clock-checking) and respond only when the elapsed time reached the correct 2- or 3-min mark.

Participants in the low-expectation group were informed that when the two-character words of the background task turned red, it indicated that 15 s remained before the time-based prospective task. However, these cues were infrequent, appearing at only 20% of the target time points and random positions. In the high-expectation group, participants were informed that these cues would occur more frequently, appearing at 80% of the target time points. By contrast, the no-expectation group received no information about the timing of the PM task. Moreover, cues appeared 4 times in both the high-expectation and low-expectation groups at identical positions (two trials for fixed and switching tasks each). Participants could press the spacebar at any time to check the elapsed time, ensuring accurate execution of PM tasks.

The experiment consisted of 36 blocks (total = 360 trials), evenly distributed with fixed and switching PM tasks. Adjacent blocks were separated by a 1000 ms interval. The balance of prospective task types and N-back task loads was identical to that in Experiment 1. As the formal experiment was programmatically controlled to maintain a fixed duration, all participants experienced an identical number of temporal targets (13 trials in total). Targets appeared 6 times in the fixed-task condition and 7 times in the switching-task condition, the latter incorporating 120-s intertarget intervals. The total experimental duration was approximately 45 min (see Figure 3).
