Genome-wide identification and expression analysis of the class III peroxidase gene family in Medicago sativa
Authors: Jiahua Wu, Hong Cheng, Ting Liu, Ziyi Hao, Yuwen Ma, Ali Raza, Peizhi Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClass III peroxidases, a class of antioxidant enzymes that regulate plant growth and development, contribute significantly to abiotic stress tolerance. However, comprehensive studies of the Class III PRX gene family in alfalfa (Medicago sativa) remain limited. Here, we identified and characterized the PRX gene family in alfalfa using bioinformatics approaches based on the Medicago sativa L. cv. ‘Zhongmu No.1’ reference genome. In total, 79 MsPRX genes were identified, which were unevenly distributed across the eight chromosomes. Furthermore, these genes were classified into 13 subgroups, and members of each subgroup exhibited conserved gene structures and motif distributions. Moreover, promoter cis-element analysis indicated the presence of numerous hormone-responsive and abiotic stress-responsive elements in promoter regions of the MsPRXs. Additionally, expression analysis revealed tissue-specific patterns, with higher expression levels in aboveground tissues than in belowground. Several MsPRX genes were strongly induced by low temperature, high temperature, drought, salt, and ABA treatments, suggesting their critical roles in abiotic stress regulation. This study systematically examines the identification, classification, evolution, and functional roles of PRXs in alfalfa. Consequently, these findings provide valuable insights into the evolutionary history and molecular mechanisms of PRXs, and foundations for future functional studies and genetic improvement of alfalfa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07325-9.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Expression profiles, PRX genes, Medicago sativa L.

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is a pioneer in the forage industry, renowned for its high protein content, rich nutritional value, and exceptional environmental adaptability [1–3]. It also plays an important role in livestock farming as well as in soil and water conservation [4]. Its ability to prevent wind erosion, stabilize sand, reduce soil and water loss, improve soil structure, and enhance soil water utilization efficiency has significant implications for maintaining ecological balance and promoting the healthy ecosystems functioning [5]. Abiotic stress is a major factor influencing plant growth, development, and yield [6–8]. Frequent extreme weather events significantly limit the yield and quality of alfalfa [9]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular response mechanisms of alfalfa to multiple stresses, particularly to high- and low-temperature stress, is crucial for developing stress-resistant cultivars and enhancing alfalfa productivity.

Peroxidases (PRXs) are a widely distributed class of enzymes that play pivotal roles in various biological processes, including innate immunity, plant-microbe interactions, oxidative stress responses, fungal lignin degradation, and cell wall metabolism [10–12]. By catalyzing redox reactions, PRXs help eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in living organisms [13]. In higher plants, PRXs are classified into four major types: catalases, Class I peroxidases, Class III PRXs, and glutathione PRXs [14]. Among them, Class III PRXs (PRXs, EC 1.11.1.7) are plant-specific secretory enzymes that play essential roles in plant growth, development, and stress responses. They are generally categorized into subfamilies based on substrate specificity and functional roles, including those involved in lignin biosynthesis and ROS scavenging [15–17]. Class III PRXs play critical roles in plant biology, including cell wall modification, wound healing, disease defense, and regulating plant hormone signaling pathways [11, 18–22]. These enzymes are crucial for plant responses to abiotic stresses [8, 23]. Several studies have identified Class III PRX gene families in various plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa), maize (Zea mays), and soybean (Glycine max) [23–26]. For instance, the peroxidases AtPRX-2, AtPRX-25, and AtPRX-71 effectively oxidize lignin precursors, thereby promoting lignification in A. thaliana stems [27]. Under nickel (Ni) stress, the expression of the Prx1C gene is significantly upregulated in alfalfa, enhancing the plant’s antioxidant capacity [28]. Additionally, the promoter motif of MsPRX1A binds the transcription factor MsMYBH [29]. This interaction promotes the expression of MsMYBH, ultimately improving drought tolerance in alfalfa. In Nicotiana, overexpression of AtPRX-64 enhances aluminum stress tolerance, while overexpression of 2-Cys PRX improves salt tolerance [30, 31]. Additionally, TaPRX-2 A has been shown to regulate ROS and salt stress-related genes in Triticum aestivum, enhancing salt tolerance [32]. Overexpression of AtPRX-22, AtPRX-39, and AtPRX-69 enhances cold tolerance in A. thaliana, suggesting that Class III PRXs are key enzymes in the plant’s response to abiotic stress [33]. Despite significant advances in identifying and characterizing Class III PRX gene families in many plants, studies on their role in alfalfa remain limited. With the completion and publication of the alfalfa genome, several gene families related to growth and development, such as transcription factors (TFs) [34], the most prominent family of transcription factors in plants (WRKY) [35], and the GASA gene family associated with abiotic stress [36], have been studied. However, the Class III PRX gene family in alfalfa has not yet been reported.

In this study, we systematically identified the MsPRX gene family in alfalfa and analyzed its expression responses across different tissues and under various stress conditions. We comprehensively analyzed the chromosomal localization, phylogenetic relationships, gene structure, and cis-acting elements of the MsPRX gene family. Additionally, we employed real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) to investigate the expression changes of MsPRX genes under low-temperature (4 ± 1 °C, 40% RH) and high-temperature (38 ± 5 °C, 40% RH) stresses. These findings provide insights into the characteristics of the MsPRX gene family in alfalfa, including its sequence features, evolutionary relationships, and expression patterns under extreme temperature stress. The analysis of transcriptional changes of MsPRX genes in response to temperature stress lays a foundation for further exploring the potential roles of peroxidases in alfalfa’s adaptation to extreme temperature environments.

Methods

Identification of MsPRXs in the alfalfa genome

The protein sequences of Class Ⅲ PRX gene family from A. thaliana were downloaded from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org) [37]. In addition, the genome data of Medicago sativa L. cv. ‘Zhongmu No.1’ was obtained from the figshare database (genome fasta sequence and annotation file. figshare. Dataset. 10.6084/m9.figshare.12327602.v3). Subsequently using the A. thaliana PRXⅢ gene family protein sequences as the query template, a bidirectional BLAST search against the M. sativa genome protein sequences was conducted with the “blast several sequences to a big database” function in TBtools software [38], employing an E-value cutoff of 10−5 to identify potential candidate members. The identified sequences were further verified and refined using the SMART 9.0 (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de), Pfam 36.0 (http://pfam.xfam.org/), and NCBI-CDD v3.21 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/cdd.shtml) databases with default parameters, to confirm the presence of conserved PRX domains. Moreover, the molecular weight (Mw), isoelectric point (pI), and average hydrophobicity of the alfalfa PRX proteins were predicted using TBtools [38], and subcellular localization was predicted using the WolF PSORT tool (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp).

Construction of phylogenetic tree

The PRX protein sequences of maize from Ensembl Genomes (https://plants.ensembl.org/Zea_mays/Info/Index/), and those of rice from the NCBI database (https://www.doubao.com/chat/?channel=baidu_pz&source=db_baidu_pz_01&keywordid=weizhi7). Subsequently, sequence alignment was performed using MUSCLE in MEGA11 software [39]. Following this, a Neighbor-Joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed with A. thaliana PRX sequences and the identified alfalfa PRX sequences. Additionally, a multi-species phylogenetic tree encompassing alfalfa, A. thaliana, maize and rice, was generated. To evaluate the reliability of the tree, Bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates was conducted, and the Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT) model was selected as the substitution model for both trees.

Analysis of conserved motifs, gene structure, conserved domains, and cis-acting elements

MEME v5.5.8 web tool (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) was used for conserved motif analysis, with the number of motifs set to 10. Following this, gene structure information for the PRX genes was extracted from the alfalfa genome annotation files, and both protein motifs and gene structures were visualized using TBtools. The 2000 bp upstream promoter regions of the alfalfa PRX genes were extracted using TBtools and then submitted to the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html) to predict potential cis-acting elements (set at a minimum motif occurrence of 3). Finally, the results were visualized using TBtools.

Chromosomal localization and synteny analysis of the MsPRX gene family

The chromosomal localization information of the MsPRX genes was obtained from the alfalfa genome annotation files and visualized using TBtools software. Intra-species synteny analysis of the MsPRX gene sequences was performed using TBtools. Additionally, genome data for A. thaliana (GCF_000001735.4) and rice (GCF_034140825.1) were retrieved from the NCBI database, and inter-species synteny analysis of the MsPRX gene sequences with those of Arabidopsis and rice was conducted using TBtools.

Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) network analysis of the MsPRX gene family

The identified MsPRX gene family sequences were submitted to the STRING v12.0 database (https://string-db.org/) for protein-protein interaction (PPI) network prediction, with A. thaliana as a reference. This analysis provided insights into the functional interactions and relationships among the MsPRX genes.

Expression pattern of MsPRX genes

Transcriptome data for alfalfa under cold stress, along with data from five alfalfa tissues (leaves, roots, nodules, post-elongating stems, elongating stems), were downloaded from the MODMS database (https://modms.lzu.edu.cn/alfalfa/rnaSeq/rnaSeqPage) [40]. Additionally, transcriptome data from three RNA-Seq projects under drought, salt, and ABA treatments (SRR7160313-SRR7160357) were included [41, 42]. The data were normalized using the FPKM method, subsequently subjected to normalization transformation using TBtools software, and visualized as a heatmap via the HeatMap tool. Significantly differentially expressed genes were defined as those with a q-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1.

GO enrichment and weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the MODMS database to retrieve GO annotations for the MsPRX gene family. This analysis, along with subsequent procedures, was conducted using R software (version 3.5.1). Specifically, the GO enrichment utilized the clusterProfiler (version 4.6.0) and GO.db (version 3.16.0) packages under their default parameters. Additionally, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) with the WGCNA package (version 1.71) to conduct association analysis of the MsPRX gene family under different stress conditions. Key parameters were configured as follows: a module merging cut height of 0.1, a minimum module size of 15 genes, and a correlation heatmap threshold of 0.6 for inter-sample relationships; all other parameters were default settings. For each hub gene, the top 10 co-expressed genes were selected to construct the co-expression network.

Growth conditions and stress treatments of alfalfa

Medicago sativa L. cv. ‘Zhongmu No.1’ was used as the experimental material, with Hoagland solution serving as the nutrient medium. Prior to stress treatment, seedlings were cultured in a growth chamber simulating field conditions, with a diurnal temperature cycle of 25 °C/15°C (day/night), a photoperiod of 16 h/8 h (light/dark), a light intensity of 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, and relative humidity maintained at 65%. When the seedlings reached 10 days of growth, they were subjected to low-temperature stress (4 °C), with samples collected at 0, 2, 6, 24, and 48 h under a 16 h/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod. Similarly, high-temperature treatment (40 °C/30°C day/night) was applied to 10-day-old seedlings, with samples collected at the same time points (0, 2, 6, 24, and 48 h).

Plants were sourced as follows: Alfalfa varieties used in this study were commercial cultivars. Alfalfa seeds were obtained from the College of Grassland Agriculture, Northwest A&F University. Permission was granted to use these seeds for scientific research. The collection and use of plant materials in this study complied with the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

RT-qPCR analysis of MsPRX gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from young leaves of alfalfa using the Eastep Total RNA Extraction Kit (Catalog No.: LS1040, Promega Biotech, Shanghai, China). cDNA synthesis was performed using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Catalog No.: R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The CDS sequences of the target genes were retrieved from the MODMS database, and specific primers for quantitative PCR were designed (primer sequences are listed in Table S1). Real-time quantitative PCR was conducted using the LightCycler 480 II system (Roche, Switzerland) with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Catalog No.: Q711-02, Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Detailed procedures are outlined in Tables S2 and Table S3. Each reaction included three technical replicates and three biological replicates. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻△△CT method [43], and correlation analysis with transcriptomic data was performed to validate the reliability of the RNA-seq results.

Results

Identification and annotation of the MsPRX gene family in alfalfa

In this study, 79 Class III PRX gene family members possessing conserved domains were identified from the genome of Medicago sativa ‘Zhongmu No.1’. Based on sequence similarity, these proteins were classified into 13 distinct groups (I-XIII). All 79 MsPRX proteins were distributed across these 13 groups, with members within the same group exhibiting close phylogenetic relationships, high sequence conservation, and likely functional similarity. Group V was the largest, comprising 32 MsPRX and 21 AtPRX proteins. The smallest groups, IV and IX, each contained only 2 MsPRX and 1 AtPRX protein. Other subgroups showed variation in member composition: Group I included 8 MsPRX and 3 AtPRX proteins, while Group XII contained 6 MsPRX and 7 AtPRX proteins, suggesting divergent evolutionary trajectories. Notably, both MsPRX and AtPRX proteins were present in all subgroups. Additionally, we supplemented the phylogenetic tree with alfalfa, A. thaliana, maize and rice (Figure S1). In this tree, the distribution patterns of most A. thaliana and alfalfa proteins are largely consistent with those in Fig. 1. However, the supplementary tree reveals an additional clade primarily composed of rice and maize proteins. Notably, three alfalfa proteins (MsPRX43, MsPRX65, and MsPRX71) cluster within this subgroup. Analysis indicates that the MsPRX proteins are intermingled with PRX proteins from other species across the tree. This pattern suggests that MsPRX proteins share common ancestors with PRX proteins from other plants, yet have undergone independent evolutionary changes along distinct lineages. Furthermore, each subfamily is subdivided into dicot- and monocot-specific branches, indicating significant structural and functional divergence in PRX genes between dicotyledonous and monocotyledonous plants. The physicochemical properties analysis (Table S4) of the MsPRX proteins revealed that lengths ranging from 183 amino acids (MsPRX9) to 530 amino acids (MsPRX59), corresponding to molecular weights between 19.41 kDa (MsPRX9) and 58.29 kDa (MsPRX59). The isoelectric points (pI) ranged from 4.44 (MsPRX26) to 9.61 (MsPRX29), with an average of 7.15 indicating that most MsPRX proteins are weakly alkaline. The instability index ranged from 23.66 to 50.04, suggesting that most of these proteins are stable. The aliphatic index values spanned 68.59 to 104.53, of which 87.65% exceeding 80, suggesting that most of these proteins are stable. The Grand Average of Hydropathy (GRAVY) values ranged from − 0.484 (MsPRX54) to 0.285 (MsPRX9). Notably, 87.65% MsPRX proteins exhibited GRAVY values < 0, indicating that they are predominantly hydrophilic. Subcellular localization prediction revealed diverse patterns, with most MsPRX proteins localized to the chloroplast, followed by the cytoskeleton, extracellular matrix, and nucleus.

Chromosomal distribution and duplication events of MsPRX genes

To provide a comprehensive overview of the gene characteristics, detailed chromosomal localization of the MsPRX genes was shown in Fig. 2. The 79 MsPRX genes were unevenly distributed across the eight chromosomes of alfalfa. Most of the genes were predominantly located on chromosomes 2, 4, 1, and 8, while chromosome 7 contained only three genes (MsPRX47, MsPRX23, and MsPRX70). Additionally, multiple tandem duplications were observed within the MsPRX gene family. A large tandem duplication gene cluster was identified on chromosome 2, which includes the genes MsPRX22, MsPRX8, MsPRX27, MsPRX7, MsPRX24, MsPRX69, MsPRX68, MsPRX25, and MsPRX26.

Chromosomal localization of the MsPRX gene family in alfalfa

To further investigate the evolutionary features of the MsPRX gene family, we conducted interspecies synteny analysis between alfalfa, A. thaliana, and rice. The analysis (Fig. 3) showed that 24 (30.38%) and 9 (11.39%) of the 79 genes had synteny with A. thaliana and rice genes, respectively, indicating the expansion of the PRX gene family in alfalfa. To explore the mechanism underlying the expansion, we performed a molecular mechanism study. Nine tandemly duplicated genes were identified (Fig. 4), suggesting that tandem duplication probably played a significant role in expanding the MsPRX gene family. However, no segmental duplication events were observed. Comprehensive phylogenetic analysis indicates that fewer homologous gene pairs exist between monocots and dicots, suggesting that these gene pairs likely formed after the divergence of monocots and dicots. Furthermore, the homologous gene pairs between alfalfa, rice, and A. thaliana may have existed prior to the divergence of their common ancestor.

Analysis of conserved motifs and gene structure in the MsPRX gene family

To better understand the evolutionary conservation of the MsPRX gene family, we analyzed their conserved motifs and gene structures. Ten conserved motifs (M1-M10) were identified by MEME (Fig. 5b). Among these motifs, Motif 1, Motif 2, Motif 3, Motif 4, Motif 5, Motif 7, Motif 8, Motif 9, and Motif 10 are distributed in most of the MsPRX genes, while only Motif 6 is less distributed. Notably, Motif 1 was presented in all MsPRX genes, highlighting its high degree of conservation and its potential role in the functional activities of the MsPRX family. The number of motifs varied from 5 to 10 among 79 MsPRX genes. Furthermore, the motif composition of MsPRX46 was highly unique, featuring two tandem repeats of Motifs 1, 3, 5, 6, and 10, suggesting a sequence duplication event within these motifs. Conserved domain analysis of the 79 MsPRX genes was conducted using NCBI-CDD and visualized (Fig. 5c). Six conserved domains were identified: secretory peroxidase, DUF4283 superfamily, zf-CCHC-4 superfamily, plant peroxidase-like superfamily, PLN00062 superfamily, RNaseH-like superfamily, PLN03030 superfamily, ATG8, and SGNH hydrolase superfamily. Most of the PRX genes contained the secretory peroxidase domain. The results demonstrated that the gene structure of MsPRX was relatively complex. In addition to containing six conserved structural domains and multiple functional motifs, the intron number exhibited significant variability, with the number of introns in different members distributed dynamically between 0 and 7 (Fig. 5d). Notably, four MsPRX genes (MsPRX3-1, MsPRX3-2, MsPRX3-3 and MsPRX65) were intronless, with three belonging to subfamily X and one to subfamily XI. Additionally, one gene (MsPRX37) belonging to subfamily V contained seven introns.

Phylogenetic relationship and gene structure analysis of MsPRX genes. a Phylogenetic tree of 79 MsPRX based on proteins sequences. b Conserved motif composition of MsPRX genes. c Conserved sequences of MsPRX. d Gene structure of MsPRX

To investigate the potential functions of PRX genes in alfalfa, cis-acting elements in the PRX gene promoters were predicted using the PlantCARE database (Fig. 6). A total of 29 cis-acting elements related to biosynthesis, stress responses, and growth and development were identified, including elements associated with environmental stress responses, plant hormone regulation, light signaling, biological rhythms, development, metabolic regulation, defense and stress responses, specific protein-binding sites, conserved DNA motifs, and other regulatory roles. These findings suggested that the MsPRX genes played significant roles in the growth, development, and abiotic stress responses of alfalfa. The distribution of cis-acting elements varied across the genes, with most MsPRX genes predicted to have between 7 and 12 cis-acting elements. Notably, MsPRX26 contained 12 cis-acting elements each, the highest number among all MsPRX genes. The most frequently identified cis-acting elements were predominantly associated with light responsiveness (including conserved DNA modules and regulatory elements) and an anaerobic induction-specific regulatory element. Interestingly, three of these elements were closely linked to plant photosynthesis. Among these, one part was a light-responsive element, and another part was a conserved DNA module involved in light responsiveness, both of which were components of light-responsive elements. The former directly participated in the perception and transduction of light signals, while the latter belonged to a conserved DNA module. Cis-acting regulatory element involved in light responsiveness is a light-responsive cis-acting regulatory element involved in the plant’s response to light signals. This variability in the distribution of cis-acting elements suggested that MsPRX genes might have performed distinct and specialized functions, contributing to their roles in plant growth, development, and stress adaptation.

Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) analysis of the MsPRX gene family

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks of the alfalfa PRX proteins were predicted to explore potential interactions within MsPRX proteins (Fig. 7). The analysis revealed a robust interaction network involving 34 MsPRX proteins, including MsPRX15, MsPRX79, MsPRX21, MsPRX74, MsPRX60, MsPRX37, MsPRX27, MsPRX71, MsPRX23, MsPRX77, MsPRX19, MsPRX70, MsPRX10, MsPRX40, MsPRX61, MsPRX41, MsPRX76, MsPRX42, MsPRX3-3, MsPRX28, MsPRX51, MsPRX63, MsPRX30, MsPRX68, MsPRX65, MsPRX66, MsPRX6, MsPRX78, MsPRX29, MsPRX47, MsPRX67, MsPRX48, and MsPRX72. The analysis also identified potential interactions between PRX family proteins and key proteins, including UGT72E2, UGT72E1, UGT72E3, MEE23, and T17H7.1. Among these, UGT72E1/2/3 belonged to the UDP-glycosyltransferase family [44]. They mediate the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites and catalyze the glycosylation of monolignols, where lignin serves as a key factor in the response to high temperature [45, 46]. MEE23 is associated with plant reproductive development [47], while T17H7.1 is involved in plant defense responses and signal transduction. Thus, peroxidase family proteins interact with UGTs, MEE23, and T17H7.1, collectively maintaining plant metabolic homeostasis and signaling pathways.

Expression analysis of the MsPRX gene family in different tissues under abiotic stresses

To investigate the tissue-specific expression patterns of MsPRX genes, a heatmap was generated using RNA-seq data from alfalfa leaves, roots, post-elongating stems, nodules, and elongating stems in the MODMS database (Fig. 8a). The results revealed significant differential expression of MsPRX genes across various tissues. MsPRX55, 14, 21, 48, 5, 64, 44, 77 and 79 showed significantly higher expression in leaves compared to other tissues. MsPRX12, 15, 7, 56 and 32 were highly expressed in nodules. MsPRX28, 72, 37 and 47 exhibited significantly higher expression in roots, while MsPRX4, 52, 20, 57, 22, 30, 41, 58, 69, 17, 25, 34, 50, 10, 38, 26, 70, 74, 27, 42, 65, 73, 40, 53, 33, 39, 24 and 68 were predominantly expressed in elongating stems. In contrast, MsPRX46, 75, 51, 60, 66, 67, 49, 13, 71, 35, 19 and 59 showed higher expression in post-elongating stems compared to other tissues (Fig. 8a). Furthermore, most MsPRX genes exhibited higher expression in aboveground tissues compared to underground tissues, suggesting distinct regulatory roles for these genes during alfalfa growth and development.

Expression profiles are represented by the colors green, white, and red: red indicates up-regulation (darker = higher expression), green indicates down-regulation (darker = lower expression), and white indicates unchanged expression relative to the reference group

To further investigate the response of MsPRX genes to abiotic stresses, their expression profiles under cold, drought, ABA, and salt stress conditions were analyzed based on transcriptomic data from publicly available databases (Fig. 8b-e). Most MsPRX genes exhibited significant changes in expression under these stresses. Under cold stress conditions (Fig. 8b), 27 MsPRX genes were up-regulated with prolonged stress duration, while only 10 were down-regulated. Under drought stress (Fig. 8c), only 7 MsPRX genes exhibited up-regulation over time, whereas 35 were down-regulated. Following ABA treatment (Fig. 8d), 33 MsPRX genes showed down-regulation with increasing exposure time, compared to 17 that were up-regulated. Under salt stress (Fig. 8e), 10 MsPRX genes were up-regulated and 25 were down-regulated as stress duration increased. These results indicated that the expression of MsPRX family genes varied differently to abiotic stresses. Specifically, MsPRX10 exhibited an upregulation response to cold, ABA, and salt stresses, while MsPRX51 showed an upregulation response to drought, ABA, and salt stresses. MsPRX57/18/13/42 were upregulated under both cold and ABA stresses, while MsPRX18/6/70 were upregulated under ABA and salt stresses. MsPRX24 showed an upregulation response to drought and ABA, and MsPRX73 was upregulated under both drought and salt stresses. These findings suggested the different roles and potential functions of MsPRX genes in response to abiotic stresses.

GO enrichment and weighted gene co-expression analysis of the MsPRX gene family

GO enrichment analysis of the MsPRX gene family (Fig. 9a) revealed that in the Biological Process category, genes were primarily enriched in fundamental biological processes, including biological process (100%), cellular process (93.62%), and response to stimulus (87.23%). Significant enrichment was also observed in response to chemical (80.85%) and metabolic process (78.72%), indicating a close association of PRX genes with basic plant physiology and environmental response processes. Furthermore, 29.79% of the genes were associated with salt stress and response to osmotic stress, respectively, suggesting their crucial roles in plant stress adaptation. Within the Cellular Component category, genes were predominantly enriched in functions related to cellular structures, including cellular component (100%), external encapsulating structure (86.05%), and cell wall (86.05%). It suggested that PRX genes are primarily localized in regions such as the cell wall and cell membrane, playing a significant role in maintaining cellular architecture. To explore the gene expression associations of MsPRX gene family members under different stress conditions, we conducted WGCNA analysis. According to the gene expression patterns, alfalfa genes affected by different stresses were divided into 5 modules (Fig. 9b). Module-trait association analysis showed that the brown module was extremely significantly strongly positively correlated with cold stress (r = 0.96, with an extremely small P-value). It was worth noting that the top four hub genes with the highest connectivity in the brown module are MsPRX24, MsPRX55, MsPRX63, and MsPRX32 in sequence. Structural and functional analyses revealed (Fig. 5) that MsPRX24, MsPRX32, and MsPRX55 all possess complete secretory peroxidase domains (cd00693), classifying them as classical peroxidases. In contrast, MsPRX63 belonged to the PLN03030 superfamily with a C-terminally truncated structure, indicating its atypical membership. GO annotations further demonstrated that MsPRX24, and MsPRX55 exhibit peroxidase activity in molecular function and primarily participate in metabolic processes. Notably, MsPRX24 displays broad subcellular localization spanning the extracellular region, intracellular compartments, and various organelles, whereas MsPRX32 lacks functional annotations.

RT-qPCR analysis of the MsPRX gene family

To further confirm the expression patterns of MsPRX genes under cold and heat stress, five MsPRX family genes (MsPRX4/29/32/48/51) were selected for RT-qPCR validation (Fig. 10). MsPRX4, MsPRX29, and MsPRX48 exhibited specific responsiveness to cold stress, with MsPRX4 and MsPRX29 demonstrating significant up-regulation under cold conditions, while MsPRX48 showed exclusive down-regulation to cold stress. MsPRX32 and MsPRX51, both non-responsive to cold, were included in the validation cohort. MsPRX51 functioned as a broad-spectrum responsive factor uniquely up-regulated under drought, ABA, and salt stresses, whereas MsPRX32 displayed an antagonistic regulatory pattern characterized by drought induction but ABA suppression. Furthermore, MsPRX32 was identified as a hub gene in WGCNA analysis. Collectively, these five genes represented unique expression profile. Under cold stress (Fig. 10a), the overall expression trend of these five genes were consistent with the transcriptomic data. However, some discrepancies were observed between the RT-qPCR results and the transcriptomic data (Fig. 8). For example, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that MsPRX32 expression was significantly downregulated at 2 h (P < 0.05), subsequently increased, peaked at 24 h, and then declined. In contrast, transcriptomic data indicated that MsPRX32 expression peaked at 2 h, followed by a decrease and a slight recovery at 48 h. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in the sample sources used for the two methodologies. Moreover, MsPRX4 and MsPRX29 exhibited similar expression patterns under cold stress, characterized by a significant increase at 2 h (P < 0.05), a subsequent decrease, and a peak at 48 h. Similarly, MsPRX32 and MsPRX51 showed comparable trends, with low initial expression levels, peaking at 24 h, followed by a decline. Conversely, MsPRX48 displayed a distinct expression pattern, showing significantly higher expression at 0 h compared to all other time points (P < 0.05), followed by a decrease and a slight recovery at 48 h. Under heat stress (Fig. 10b), both MsPRX4 and MsPRX29 peaked at 2 h, with expression significantly higher than at other time points (P < 0.05), subsequently decreased, and remained at low levels. MsPRX32 and MsPRX51 both exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease, but their peak times differed, MsPRX32 peaked at 6 h, while MsPRX51 peaked at 24 h. MsPRX48 again demonstrated a unique expression pattern, increasing significantly to peak at 6 h, decreasing thereafter, and rising again at 48 h. Validation by RT-qPCR (Fig. 10a) revealed that under cold stress conditions, the expression trends of these five genes were consistent with the transcriptomic analysis results. Furthermore, under heat stress, the regulatory patterns of these five genes differed from those observed under cold stress (Fig. 10b). A possible speculation for the inconsistency between the heat and cold data could be that different stress-sensing mechanisms are activated under heat and cold conditions.

Expression analysis of MsPRX genes in alfalfa under cold and heat stress. a Cold stress; b Heat stress. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences (P < 0.05)

Discussion

Numerous genes involved in abiotic stress responses in plants have been identified in alfalfa [48–50]. Among these, Class III peroxidases (PRXs) have emerged as pivotal enzymes in plant stress tolerance [13]. Studies have reported extensive PRX gene families, showing varied numbers across different species. 79 MsPRX genes were identified in the genome of the autotetraploid alfalfa (Fig. 1). This number is lower than the 124 PRX genes found in the allotetraploid soybean, the 138 in the diploid rice, and the 102 in the autotetraploid potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), but higher than the 73 in the diploid model plant A. thaliana [24–26, 51]. Furthermore, the reference genome of alfalfa used here is 816 Mb in size, larger than those of A. thaliana (125 Mb) and rice (385.7 Mb), but smaller than maize (2.3 Gb) and soybean (978.4 Mb). Thus, our comparison indicates a weak correlation between PRX gene family size and overall genome size. The diversity in PRX gene numbers across these species likely results from specific duplication and loss events during evolution. Moreover, our findings align with previous research suggesting that the expansion of gene families is primarily driven by lineage-specific expansion, rather than being solely dependent on changes in overall genome size [52, 53]. The cross-species clustering pattern revealed deep differentiation of class III PRX genes in dicotyledonous plants, with tandem duplication events (Figs. 2, 3 and 4) identified as the primary driver for the expansion of the MsPRX family. Notably, while tandem duplication dominates PRX expansion in sugarcane (Saccharum spontaneum), foxtail millet (Setaria italica) and grapevine (Vitis vinifera L.), alfalfa lacks segmental duplication events, contrasting sharply with the expansion mechanisms observed in potato (Solanum tuberosum L.), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) and soybean [26, 51, 54, 55]. This mechanistic diversity highlights the species-specific evolutionary strategies of class III PRX gene families. Class III peroxidases are widely distributed across various organelles. Our subcellular localization predictions indicate that they are predominantly found in chloroplasts (Table S4), as reported in Litchi (Litchi chinensis Sonn.), Olea europaea and sugarcane [24, 56, 57], suggesting evolutionary conservation of PRX functionality in photosynthetic tissues. Phylogenetic analysis plays a pivotal role in elucidating the evolutionary history of gene families, identifying orthologous genes, and inferring functional divergence [58]. In our study, the 79 MsPRX proteins were classified into 13 subgroups (Fig. 1), each containing homologous proteins from alfalfa (MsPRX) and A. thaliana (AtPRX) and suggesting their potential origin from a common ancestral gene.

Variations in conserved motifs and gene structures reflect functional diversification and evolutionary trajectories within the PRX family [59]. The unique dual Motif 1/3/5/6/10s configuration in MsPRX46 (Fig. 5b) suggests domain recombination as a mechanism for functional versatility, paralleling the multifunctional stress-response mechanisms reported in soybean GmPRX16, Ginseng PgHDZ27 and Chinese Rose RcWRKY65 [60–62]. Among the 79 MsPRX genes, 69 retained the canonical secretory peroxidase domain, while the remaining 10 members exhibited structural divergence: six harbored SGNH hydrolase superfamily domains, and four contained PLN03030 superfamily domains (Fig. 2c). This domain substitution phenomenon implies adaptive functional specialization during PRX evolution. Consistent with findings in rice [25] and maize [23], genes within the same phylogenetic clade displayed high similarity in gene structure and motif composition, underscoring the evolutionary conservation of PRX genes alongside adaptive structural modifications. Gene structure analysis identified intronless variants in three X subgroup members (MsPRX3-1/3 − 2/3–3) and the XI subgroup gene MsPRX65 (Fig. 5d), a phenomenon also observed in the maize ZmPRX family [23], suggesting that intron loss may enhance environmental adaptability through transcriptional plasticity. Promoter cis-element analysis showed enrichment of light-responsive element, ABA-responsive element (ABRE) and anaerobic-inducible elements (ARE) (Fig. 6), which is similar to the cis-regulatory pattern of the sugarcane ShPRX gene [57], Kandelia obovata COPT [63] and Pumpkin (Cucurbita moschata) CmoHSP70 [64]. Remarkably, MsPRX26 harbored 12 cis-elements, including four stress-responsive types, a combined pattern similar to the abiotic stress regulatory network in watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) ClPRX [65]. This similarity suggests that PRX genes in dicots employ a “cis-element code” strategy to regulate responses to complex environmental stresses. Based on observed differences in response to extreme climate stress responses, we hypothesize that functional heterogeneity among MsPRX genes may arise from variations in composition of cis-element within their promoter regions. The variation in cis-element architecture is likely the cause of distinct expression patterns of MsPRX genes under extreme climatic conditions, highlighting their regulatory significance in environmental adaptation.

PRX genes exhibit diverse spatiotemporal expression patterns, a key evolutionary feature enabling them to function across tissues during various stages of plant growth. In A. thaliana, under abiotic stress, AtPRX genes are primarily expressed in leaves, stems, and flowers, with AtPRX62 and AtPRX69 promoting root hair elongation under cold stress [66, 67]. In maize, ZmPRX is expressed in roots, stems, and leaves under stress conditions [23]. Similarly, most CsPRX genes in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) are expressed across tissues, with CsPRX17 mediating responses to abiotic stresses [68]. Most MsPRX genes were highly expressed in stems, with significantly higher expression in aboveground tissues compared to underground tissues (Fig. 8a). This tissue specificity may reflect structural adaptations in perennial stems for sustained stress tolerance. Furthermore, GO analysis revealed that the functions of the PRX gene family were primarily enriched in fundamental physiological processes and environmental stress responses in plants (Fig. 9a). As regulatory factor in plant growth and development, the PRX gene family responds to adverse environmental conditions across species [69, 70]. For example, in Triticum aestivum, overexpression of TaPRX-2 A reduces ROS accumulation and improves salt tolerance by enhancing osmotic regulation [71]. In Nicotiana, overexpression of CrPRX increases H2O2 levels and improves drought stress tolerance [72], while GsPRX9 overexpression in soybean enhances antioxidant responses, reducing ROS accumulation and enhancing salt tolerance [73]. This study revealed that MsPRX genes played critical roles in responses to abiotic stresses and hormone signaling. Transcriptomic analyses under cold, salt, drought, and ABA stresses demonstrated that most MsPRX genes responded to these conditions, exhibiting diverse response mechanisms (Fig. 8b-e). Notably, the three stress conditions inducing consistent up-regulation of MsPRX10 differed from those causing consistent up-regulation of MsPRX51. The promoter region of MsPRX10 contains multiple cis-acting elements associated with light response, low-temperature response, and ABA response. These elements may interact with transcription factors to regulate MsPRX10 expression under environmental stress. The promoter region of MsPRX51 contains auxin-responsive elements and MYB binding sites, indicating that its expression may be under dual regulation by hormone signaling and stress response pathways. Simultaneously, WGCNA analysis identified MsPRX24, MsPRX55, MsPRX63, and MsPRX32 as hub genes within the MsPRX gene family, playing important roles in environmental stress responses (Fig. 9b). The promoter analysis revealed distinct cis-regulatory elements for each gene: MsPRX24 contains defense/stress response, salicylic acid response, and ABA response elements that likely mediate its upregulation under drought and ABA stresses; MsPRX32 harbors similar defense/stress, salicylic acid, and ABA response elements potentially facilitating drought/ABA-inducible expression; MsPRX55 possesses low-temperature and ABA response elements implicating its role in cold/ABA stress responses; while MsPRX63 comprises low-temperature, ABA, and MeJA response elements that may regulate its expression during cold and ABA stresses. Functional divergence among MsPRX paralogs is thus likely driven by promoter cis-element composition and structural motifs that dictate substrate specificity and stress responsiveness [74]. Future studies could validate these predictions through mutagenesis of key motifs or yeast one-hybrid assays to confirm transcription factor binding.

In summary, this study offers valuable insights into the genomic structure and evolution of the alfalfa MsPRX gene family, providing a solid foundation for future functional genomics research and molecular breeding. Our findings highlight the potential of genomics in agricultural biotechnology, particularly for enhancing crop yield, quality, and stress tolerance.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified 79 MsPRX genes in alfalfa, which had undergone gene tandem duplication events. These genes were classified into 13 subfamilies, with genes in the same subfamily exhibiting similar gene structures and conserved domains. Notably, Motif 1 was universally conserved across all MsPRX proteins, suggesting its critical role in maintaining core peroxidase functionality. Subcellular localization predictions indicated that 39 MsPRX proteins are predominantly localized to chloroplasts, implicating their potential involvement in photoresponsive or redox homeostasis pathways.

The expression patterns of MsPRX genes across various tissues and their responses to abiotic stresses revealed that most MsPRX genes are essential for the development of aboveground tissues, whereas MsPRX10, MsPRX51, MsPRX24, MsPRX55, MsPRX63 and MsPRX32 play a pivotal role in environmental stress responses. Our findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the characteristics of the MsPRX gene family and underscore the potential functional significance of MsPRX in alfalfa development and stress adaptation. This study provides a solid foundation for future functional characterization of MsPRX genes under extreme climatic conditions. The findings support applications in stress-tolerance engineering—including CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockout/activation—and development of molecular markers (e.g., SNP/SSR markers linked to stress-resilient traits), thereby accelerating translational research for crop stress-resistance breeding.
