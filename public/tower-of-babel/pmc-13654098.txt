Serial changes in right ventricular function after mitral valve surgery: retrospective analysis of a prospective cohort
Authors: Xiaoxuan Lin, Xueyuan Lin, Ying Guo, Xiaofang Zhong, Miaoru Yu, Shuyu Luo, Yuanyuan Sheng, Qian Liu, Guijuan Peng, Yingqi Zheng, Yuxiang Huang, Xiaohua Liu, Yingying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the clinical relevance of right ventricular (RV) function assessed on echocardiography, its predictive utility for patients receiving mitral valve surgery remains uncertain. The primary objectives of this study were to characterize RV function before and after valvular surgery and to establish the diagnostic value of this assessment.MethodsEchocardiograms were collected prospectively from patients undergoing mitral valve surgery between 2020 and 2023. Subsequently, a retrospective analysis of preoperative and postoperative echocardiographic parameters was performed to evaluate RV function, including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), tricuspid annular systolic velocity (S’TDI), RV fractional area change (FAC), RV free wall longitudinal strain (RVFWLS), RV global longitudinal strain (RVGLS), and three-dimensional RV ejection fraction (3D RVEF). The study included 115 patients with mitral valve surgery. Serial echocardiographic evaluations were performed at four time points: preoperatively, 1 week postoperatively, 6 months postoperatively, and 1 year postoperatively. Serial changes in echocardiographic parameters (TAPSE, FAC, S’TDI, RVFWLS, and 3D‑RVEF) were analyzed via repeated‑measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and DeLong tests were used to evaluate and compare diagnostic performance. Reproducibility for RVFWLS, RVGLS, and 3D‑RVEF was assessed according to intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Bland-Altman plots in a random subset of 20 observations.ResultsThe principal findings were as follows: (I) RV systolic function demonstrated a statistically significant reduction postoperatively as compared to the preoperative baseline level (P<0.0001). (II) One year after operation, TAPSE and S’TDI remained significantly lower than their corresponding preoperative values, with mean changes of −0.34 mm [95% confidence interval (CI): −0.55 to −0.14; P=0.0003] and 1.48 cm/s (95% CI: 0.45 to 2.52; P=0.0024), respectively. In contrast, RVFWLS and 3D RVEF recovered to levels comparable to those at baseline, with mean changes of 0.29% (95% CI: −2.19% to 2.77%; P>0.05) and −3.04% (95% CI: −9.98% to 3.91%; P>0.05), respectively. (III) According to the DeLong test, RVFWLS demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy for RV systolic dysfunction (area under the curve =0.771), significantly outperforming conventional parameters (FAC, TAPSE, and S’TDI; all P values <0.001). (IV) RVFWLS exhibited stronger diagnostic agreement with the reference standard (3D RVEF) in identifying impaired RV systolic function, as indicated by the reclassification rate (31%), as compared to conventional parameters (57–64%).ConclusionsRVFWLS may serve as a novel indicator for assessing RV systolic function both preoperatively and postoperatively in patients with mitral regurgitation (MR).

Introduction

Right ventricular (RV) function is a critical prognostic determinant in patients undergoing valvular surgery. Impaired preoperative RV function is associated with a higher risk of postoperative mortality, and its impact may extend well beyond the early perioperative period. Indeed, reduced RV systolic function can persist for months to years (1) and thus may serve as an independent predictor of long-term survival (2,3). Research in this area has consistently identified an RVEF below 20% as an independent predictor of mortality and rehospitalization risk among patients with heart failure (4,5). Moreover, impaired RV function is particularly prevalent in patients undergoing cardiac surgery and, in severe cases, is associated with significantly higher mortality rates within 6 months of diagnosis (exceeding 50%) (4). RV failure is also prevalent in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery, although long-term follow-up suggests that a degree of functional recovery occurs in these patients (6). There is a substantial amount of evidence indicating that RV remodeling occurs in patients with mitral valve disease (7,8), including mitral regurgitation (MR) (9,10) and mitral stenosis (8). Donia et al. (9) demonstrated that even in patients with mitral valve prolapse without significant valvular regurgitation, early RV remodeling is present, manifesting as increased right atrial (RA) volume and tricuspid annular diameter.

The assessment of RV systolic function is critical to the management of various cardiovascular diseases (11). Conventional echocardiographic parameters, including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), tricuspid annular systolic velocity (S’TDI), and RV fractional area change (FAC), are consistently recommended as standard metrics. These measures have been formally incorporated into the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines for evaluating RV systolic function (11,12).

However, conventional parameters offer only an incomplete assessment of RV function, being limited in their ability to capture the intricate, three-dimensional (3D) mechanics of the RV. Therefore, dependence on these conventional parameters may result in an inadequate characterization of RV dysfunction severity and its value in clinical prognosis (13,14).

Two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography (2D-STE) enables the quantitative assessment of myocardial function. It is angle- independent, unaffected by cardiac translation motion, and less influenced by loading conditions, allowing direct evaluation of myocardial contractility. The myocardial fibers of the RV wall are predominantly oriented longitudinally, with approximately 80% of its contractile function dependent on the longitudinal motion of the RV wall. Due to this histological characteristic of the RV wall, 2D-STE parameters are considered suitable for evaluating the intrinsic contractility of the RV (15,16). Studies have demonstrated that RV free wall longitudinal strain (RVFWLS) is a robust predictor of adverse cardiovascular events (17-19).

In this study, we used 2D-STE to assess changes in RV function before and after surgery in patients with MR by measuring RV longitudinal strain parameters, with the aim to evaluate postoperative RV functional recovery. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0172/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Shenzhen People’s Hospital (No. 2020-028-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the non-interventional nature of the study, the retrospective design, and the use of anonymized data. A retrospective analysis of prospectively collected echocardiographic images from patients undergoing mitral valve surgery at Shenzhen People’s Hospital was conducted. Images were obtained at the following time points: reoperation (1 day before surgery), early postoperation (within 5 days after 1-week postoperation), at 6 months postoperation (within 1 month after 6-month postoperation), and at 1-year postoperation (within 2 months after 1-year postoperation). Data collection spanned from September 2020 to December 2023 (Figure 1).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) RV dysfunction or an inability to assess RV function; (II) concomitant congenital heart disease (e.g., atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, and endocardial cushion defect); (III) symptomatic coronary artery disease or documented ventricular wall motion abnormalities; (IV) concomitant cardiomyopathy (e.g., dilated cardiomyopathy and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy); (V) hepatic or renal insufficiency or the administration of chemotherapy for malignancy, hematologic disorders, or other diseases; (VI) intake of medications with established cardiotoxicity during the preoperative period or postoperative follow-up period; (VII) reoperation (i.e., the study surgery would be a repeat procedure); (VIII) requirement of repeat cardiac surgery during the postoperative follow-up period; (IX) death occurring intraoperatively or within 1 week after the operation; (X) loss to follow-up or a failure to adhere to the specified follow-up schedule; (XI) significant pulmonary hypertension [defined as systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) ≥50 mmHg on echocardiography], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (confirmed by spirometry), or obstructive sleep apnea as documented by formal sleep study (these condition were excluded to minimize confounding effects and to isolate the impact of left-sided valvular pathology on RV function); and (XII) poor image quality or the inability to obtain standard echocardiographic views.

Echocardiographic parameters

All transthoracic preoperative and postoperative echocardiograms were performed with the commercially available EPIQ 7C ultrasound system (Philips) equipped with S5-1 and X5-1 transducers.

Following a standard protocol, 2D loops were acquired from the parasternal, apical, and subxiphoid views (Figure S1). The digitally archived raw datasets subsequently underwent offline analysis via QLAB workstation software packages (Philips). All echocardiographic assessments, including image review and parameter measurement, were performed offline by a single, formally trained reader. All patients underwent a comprehensive echocardiographic study that included acquisition of all standard views. The RA area was measured by direct planimetry in the apical four-chamber view, with the tracing performed at end-systole. RVFAC was calculated as follows: RVFAC = 100 × (end-diastolic area − end-systolic area)/end diastolic area. This measurement involved tracing the endocardial border from an RV-focused view in both diastolic and systolic phases. RVFAC <35% was considered to be the threshold for abnormality. TAPSE was defined as the longitudinal displacement between end-diastole and peak systole, with the M-mode cursor positioned at the tricuspid annulus for measurement. A value below 17 mm was considered abnormal. Similarly, S’TDI was measured via pulsed-wave tissue Doppler, with the sample volume being that of the basal RV free wall and the cutoff value for abnormality being <10 cm/s. Tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity was used to estimate the maximal systolic difference between RV and RA pressures. RA pressure was estimated from the inferior vena cava diameter and its change in diameter with respiration, with the cutoff value for abnormality being an RA pressure >8 mmHg. RV systolic pressure (RVSP) was estimated by adding the RA pressure to the RVSP and RA systolic pressure difference. sPAP was derived from the RVSP in cases where no outflow gradient was detected. An sPAP value of 36 mmHg or higher was taken to indicate pulmonary hypertension.

Supplementing the conventional exam, we obtained electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated full-volume 3D image datasets, which were reconstructed from four cardiac cycles with specific optimization for cardiac chambers. Specifically, the 3D datasets from the left heart were analyzed offline with 3D Quantification Advanced (Philips) semiautomated software packages in the QLAB workstation (Philips). The protocol for echocardiographic measurements and the corresponding reference cutoffs were based on the established recommendations by the ASE (12).

Follow-up protocol

Serial echocardiographic assessments were conducted on all patients at predefined time points: preoperation (baseline), early postoperation (1 week), and follow-up at 6 months and 1 year after surgery. At each visit, conventional RV functional parameters (including TAPSE, FAC, and S’TDI) and 2D-STE-derived strain indices, including RV global longitudinal strain (RVGLS) and RVFWLS were measured according to standardized protocols. All examinations were performed by experienced sonographers under the same ultrasound system to ensure consistency. The serial measurements allowed for longitudinal evaluation of RV function recovery and changes over time (Figure S2).

STE analysis

Images from the apical four-chamber view focused on the RV, including three complete cardiac cycles, were analyzed with QLAB software for STE analysis. The RV endocardial border was manually traced starting from the lateral tricuspid annulus. The software automatically generated a region of interest (ROI) encompassing both the RV free wall and the interventricular septum. The width of the ROI was adjusted to 5 mm. The RV myocardium was automatically divided into six segments: the basal, mid, and apical segments of both the free wall and the interventricular septum.

RVGLS was derived as the average of the peak systolic longitudinal strain values obtained from all six segments of the RV, encompassing both the free wall and the septum. RVFWLS was defined as the average peak systolic longitudinal strain across the three segments of the free wall only. Both RVGLS and RVFWLS measurements are expressed as negative percentage values, indicative of systolic myocardial shortening. In the statistical analysis, the absolute values of the strain measurements were used to simplify interpretation (20).

3D echocardiographic measurement of RV volumes

Dedicated apical four-chamber 3D datasets focused on the RV were analyzed using QLAB 3D Advanced software. ECG tracing was used to determine cardiac cycle timing, which included measurements of the following: RV end-diastolic volume (RV EDV), for which the frame corresponding to the peak of the R-wave was selected as end-diastole, representing the maximum RV volume; and RV end-systolic volume (RV ESV), for which the frame corresponding to the end of the T wave was selected as end-systole, representing the minimum RV volume. Based on the manual identification of these key frames, the software automatically calculated RV ejection fraction (RVEF), a volumetric parameter, as follows: RVEF = [(RV EDV − RV ESV)/RV EDV] × 100%.

All echocardiographic measurements were performed by a single reader who was formally trained in echocardiography. The reader was blinded to clinical data but was not fully blinded to the study time point, as the presence of surgical changes (e.g., prosthetic valve and annuloplasty ring) was evident on the postoperative images. Intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility for RVFWLS, RVGLS, and 3D RVEF were assessed through echocardiographic observations from a random subset of 20 patients. The same reader repeated the measurements 4 weeks after the initial analysis for intraobserver variability, and a second independent reader performed the measurements for interobserver variability. Both readers were blinded to each other’s results. Reproducibility was evaluated according to intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) with 95% CIs, and Bland-Altman plots were constructed to assess agreement.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism v. 9.5.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. After the normal distribution of variables was verified by Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests, the clinical and echocardiographic characteristics were compared with the unpaired Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Multiple-group comparisons (>2) were performed via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Welch ANOVA and via the chi-squared or the Fisher exact text, as appropriate. To compare RV function parameters across preoperative, postoperative, 6-month follow-up, and 1-year follow-up time points, a repeated‑measures ANOVA was performed. All probability values were two-sided, and a value of ≤0.05 was considered significant. Intraobserver and interobserver variability for RVFWLS, RVGLS and 3D‑RVEF were assessed in a random subset of 20 echocardiographic observations according to ICCs and 95% CIs. The same reader repeated the measurements after the initial analysis for intraobserver variability, and a second independent reader performed the measurements for interobserver variability. Bland-Altman plots were constructed to visualize agreement.

To compare the diagnostic performance, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was analyzed via the DeLong test with MedCalc v. 22.018 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). To visualize the extent of patient reclassification due to diagnostic disagreement between conventional parameters, RVFWLS, and 3D RVEF, flow diagrams were constructed with SankeyMATIC (https://sankeymatic.com). The cutoff value for RVFWLS was determined via ROC curve analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics and follow-up of patients

This study prospectively collected data from patients undergoing surgery between September 2020 and December 2023. Two patients died during the initial postoperative hospital stay, and six patients failed to adhere to the scheduled follow-up appointments. During follow-up, two patients died at the 6-month follow-up time point. One patient discontinued follow-up due to initiating chemotherapy treatment. Consequently, the final preoperative cohort comprised 115 patients. The distribution of patients in terms of follow-up data collected at different time points was as follows: postoperation, 115 patients; 6-month follow-up, 63 patients; and 1-year follow-up, 50 patients. The predominant etiology among enrolled patients was mitral valve disease. Mitral valve prolapse was the most common diagnosis (55 patients, 47.0%), followed by rheumatic valvular heart disease (22 patients, 19.1%). Five patients (4.3%) had a preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50%. Preoperative echocardiography identified pericardial effusion in five patients (4.3%).

Correlation between RV STE parameters and 3D RVEF

RVEF measured by 3D echocardiography demonstrated weak but statistically significant correlations with both 2D strain parameters; that is, with RVGLS (r=0.1662; P<0.0001) and RVFWLS (r=0.1588; P<0.0001) (Figure S3).

Reproducibility of STE and 3D measurements

Intraobserver and interobserver reproducibility for RVFWLS and 3D RVEF were assessed in a random subset of 20 echocardiographic observations via ICC calculation and Bland-Altman analysis. The ICC values for the intra- and interobserver agreement of RVFWLS were 0.96 (95% CI: 0.91–0.98) and 0.95 (95% CI: 0.88–0.98), respectively; for 3D RVEF, the corresponding ICCs were 0.97 (95% CI: 0.94–0.99) and 0.98 (95% CI: 0.84–0.99) (Table S1 and Figure S4).

Comparison of RV function between the preoperative and postoperative time points

TAPSE

In the analysis of TAPSE, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene test: F=46.70: P<0.0001). Therefore, Welch ANOVA was employed for group comparisons. Postoperative RV function, as assessed by TAPSE, demonstrated significant changes over time. Postoperation RV function was significantly lower than the preoperation level (P<0.0001). RV function at 6-month and 1-year follow-up gradually improved compared to the 1-week time point. At 1-year follow-up, RV function recovered but remained significantly lower than preoperative levels (P=0.0062) (Figure 2A).

S’TDI

In the analysis of S’TDI, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene test: F=27.68; P<0.0001). Therefore, Welch ANOVA was employed for group comparisons. Postoperative S’TDI was significantly reduced compared to the preoperative level (P<0.0001). Gradual functional recovery was observed at the 6-month and 1-year postoperative follow-up, but S’TDI still remained significantly lower than preoperative baseline value (P=0.0062) (Figure 2B).

FAC

In the analysis of FAC, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene test: F=7.348; P<0.0001). Consequently, Welch ANOVA was applied for group comparisons. Analysis revealed no significant differences in FAC between preoperative levels and any postoperative time point (P>0.05, Figure 2C).

RVFWLS

In the analysis of RVFWLS, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene test: F=15.14; P<0.0001). Consequently, Welch ANOVA was applied for group comparisons. Postoperative RVFWLS was significantly reduced compared to preoperative levels (P=0.0238). At 1-year follow-up, RVFWLS recovered to the preoperative level (P>0.05, Figure 3A).

RVGLS

In the analysis of RVGLS, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene test: F=19.12; P<0.0001). Consequently, Welch ANOVA was applied for group comparisons. Preoperative RVGLS in patients with left-sided valvular heart disease was significantly lower than that in the control group (P<0.0001). At 1-week postoperation, RVGLS decreased slightly compared with the preoperative level and then gradually recovered at the 6-month and 1-year follow-ups. RVGLS was significantly higher at 1-year postoperation, compared to 1-week postoperation (P=0.0047); however, it remained significantly lower than that of the control group (P<0.0001, Figure 3B).

3D RVEF

In the analysis of 3D RVEF, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated (Levene test: F=39.63; P<0.0001). Consequently, repeated Welch ANOVA was applied for group comparisons. Postoperation 3D RVEF was significantly lower than the preoperative level (P<0.0001). At 6-month follow-up, 3D RVEF recovered significantly compared with the postoperative level (P<0.0001) and continued to improve to the 1-year follow-up, reaching a level significantly higher than that at 6 months (P<0.05). By 1-year postoperation, 3D RVEF was approaching its preoperative level (P>0.05, Figure 3C).

Accuracy of STE in evaluating RV function

RVFWLS exhibited the highest discriminatory power for RV systolic dysfunction (AUC =0.771; 95% CI: 0.719–0.817; P<0.001), exceeding that of RVGLS (AUC =0.767; 95% CI: 0.715–0.813; P<0.001), FAC (AUC =0.606; 95% CI: 0.549–0.662; P<0.001), TAPSE (AUC =575; 95% CI: 0.517–0.631; P<0.001), and S’TDI (AUC =0.539; 95% CI: 0.481–0.596; P<0.001). According to the DeLong test, RVFWLS had a significantly higher accuracy than did the other parameters (RVFWLS vs. TAPSE: P<0.001; RVFWLS vs. S’TDI: P<0.001; RVFWLS vs. FAC: P<0.001; RVFWLS vs. RVGLS: P=0.0203) (Figure 4).

Diagnostic reclassification in all cohorts based on the discrepancy between 3D RVEF and conventional parameters

This study analyzed 331 echocardiographic observations, assessed both preoperatively, postoperatively, and at follow-up. Of these, 230 (69%) had normal RV function as assessed by 3D RVEF. Moreover, a substantial proportion of echocardiographic observations classified as normal by each conventional parameter were classified as impaired function according to 3D RVEF. Specifically, among the 194 echocardiographic observations with normal TAPSE, 21% had reduced RVEF. Meanwhile, of the 193 echocardiographic observations with normal S’TDI, 27% were identified with dysfunction by 3D RVEF. Notably, a similar reclassification rate (27%) was observed among echocardiographic observations with normal FAC. Finally, RVFWLS had the highest reclassification rate at 31%, with 216 echocardiographic observations classified as normal (Figure 5, Tables 2,3).

Among the 331 echocardiographic observations, 101 (30.5%) were considered to indicate impaired RV systolic function according to the 3D RVEF criteria, representing a significant diagnostic discrepancy with conventional parameters. The number of cases identified with impaired RV dysfunction and the portion of these classified as normal by 3D RVEF was, respectively, 194 and 63% for TAPSE, 138 and 64% for S’TDI, 74 and 57% for FAC, and 155 and 31% for RVFWLS.

Reclassification according to subgroups

The following subgroups were defined: preoperative (n=115), postoperative (n=115), 6-month follow-up (n=63), and 1-year follow-up (n=50). The 1-year follow-up subgroup had the highest reclassification rate (95%), followed by the postoperative subgroup (81%). When TAPSE was used to assess RV function preoperatively, it demonstrated nominal agreement with 3D RVEF, but 25% of cases were reclassified as impaired RV systolic function, and 41% were reclassified as normal RV function. RVFWLS exhibited consistently lower reclassification rates compared to the conventional parameters across all subgroups (Figure S5).

Discussion

This study revealed significant inconsistencies across TAPSE, S’TDI, and RVFWLS in detecting RV systolic dysfunction during preoperative and postoperative assessments of MR. The principal findings were as follows: (I) RV systolic function was significantly reduced postoperatively compared to the preoperative baseline (P<0.0001); (II) gradual recovery of function occurred during follow-up, with RV systolic function returning to preoperative baseline levels by 1 year of follow-up; (III) RVFWLS exhibited stronger diagnostic agreement with the reference standard (3D RVEF) in identifying impaired RV systolic function (reclassification rate of 31%), as compared with conventional parameters (reclassification rate 57–64%).

In our study, 31% of patients with MR were considered to have RV dysfunction. In patients with chronic MR, elevated left atrial pressure obstructs pulmonary venous return, increasing pulmonary capillary pressure and consequently elevating pulmonary arterial pressure. Prolonged pulmonary hypertension induces pulmonary vascular remodeling and increased pulmonary vascular resistance, leading to irreversible pulmonary hypertension. This ultimately results in varying degrees of RV systolic dysfunction. RV dilation and increased wall stress or tension raise myocardial oxygen consumption while reducing coronary perfusion gradients, causing myocardial ischemia and progressive RV failure (7). Impaired RV systolic function may indicate severe valvular disease or comorbid pulmonary or coronary pathology (21). Previous studies reported that RVEF ≤20% strongly correlated with adverse outcomes including mortality, and independently predicts rehospitalization (4,5,22). Thus, assessing RV dysfunction in MR holds significant clinical importance.

Although conventional echocardiographic parameters are well established and guideline-endorsed for assessing RV systolic function, their reliance on one- or two-dimensional measurements constitutes an inherent limitation. This stems from an inability to fully capture the RV’s complex contractile patterns, which may reduce these measurements' accuracy in characterizing the full spectrum of dysfunction and in predicting associated adverse clinical outcomes. Consequently, as demonstrated in this study, conventional parameters exhibit a high probability of misclassification when identifying patients with impaired RV systolic function. We observed a significant discrepancy in the classification of RV systolic dysfunction, with high reclassification rates, reaching 63% for TAPSE, 64% for S’TDI, and 57% for FAC, relative to the 3D-derived standard. The diagnostic concordance between RVFWLS and 3D RVEF supports strain imaging as a complementary tool for RV systolic function assessment in patients with MR.

TAPSE and S’TDI are widely used in clinical practice due to their operational simplicity and good reproducibility. However, TAPSE and S’TDI have inherent limitations, including angle dependency, the single-plane nature of M-mode imaging, and extrapolation from a single segment in reflecting global RV performance. These characteristics render M-mode and color Doppler insensitive in detecting RV systolic dysfunction. FAC requires accurate tracing of the RV endocardium at end-systole and end-diastole. Due to the complex 3D structure of the right ventricle, FAC is a simple method for assessing global RV radial and longitudinal function. However, the RV apex has an abundance of trabeculations, demanding a high-quality image.

STE has become an essential tool for assessing ventricular function and left ventricular dysfunction. It provides more sensitive and objective metrics than does LVEF (23,24). For example, left ventricular longitudinal strain (LVGLS) was demonstrated to be a predictor of a composite endpoint (all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, heart transplant outcomes, and rehospitalization) (25-28). As RV function continues to receive heightened clinical attention, accurate assessment of RV performance has become critically important. It has been confirmed that STE provides a feasible method for evaluating RV function. Moreover, RVFWLS constitutes a strong and independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular events in the population with chronic heart failure (29). These findings underscore the value of STE in cardiac disease management, and it can be a more comprehensive assessment tool for clinicians to optimize patient management and prognosis. Our findings suggest that RVFWLS may serve as a sensitive exploratory marker of early RV dysfunction and longitudinal functional recovery. However, given the absence of clinical outcome assessment in this study, RVFWLS should be considered an adjunctive imaging tool rather than a validated prognostic indicator at this stage. Future prospective studies with objective clinical endpoints are warranted to establish its clinical utility.

The RV wall is mainly composed of deep muscle layers, constituting approximately 75% of the wall thickness. The fibers of the superficial layer are arranged more or less circumferentially in a direction that is parallel to the atrioventricular groove (30). This layer contributes approximately 80% of the total contractile force for RV systole (31). The predominance of longitudinally oriented myocardial fibers in the RV enables its longitudinal strain to serve as a measure of intrinsic systolic function, through which active contractility can be differentiated from passive tethering effects (15). RV longitudinal strain is the predominant strain metric used in clinical practice, offering several key advantages over other methods: it provides a quantitative assessment with lower operator dependency, it does not require perfect endocardial border tracing (unlike FAC), and it is both angle-independent and less susceptible to preload variations. These attributes may contribute to its superior diagnostic performance, as evidenced by Prihadi et al. (32,33), who reported that RVFWLS is associated with a higher prevalence of RV dysfunction and possesses stronger prognostic value than do conventional parameters. Furthermore, in a study of 208 patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, FAC identified RV dysfunction in 28% of cases, whereas RV systolic longitudinal strain (RVLS) detected RV systolic dysfunction in 75% of these patients (34). Consequently, as a more comprehensive metric for assessing RV function, RVFWLS may provide superior diagnostic information—particularly for evaluating perioperative RV function in patients with MR. The differential recovery pattern may involve a persistent reduction of annular-based indices (TAPSE and S’TDI) and recovery of deformation-based indices (RVFWLS), which suggest that postoperative TAPSE and S’TDI reduction may be influenced by surgical factors affecting annular motion rather than reflecting true RV systolic impairment.

In our study, impaired RV systolic function was found in patients with MR, both before and after surgery. Previous investigations have documented a similar phenomenon in which patients exhibit reduced RV systolic function following mitral valve repair. Moreover, one study assessed RV systolic function at 1-year follow-up and found that function had recovered gradually, albeit without full restoration to preoperative baseline levels (19), findings in alignment with our observations. In our study, patients exhibited a significant reduction in RV systolic function postoperatively, with gradual recovery emerging after 6 months of follow-up. Multiple mechanisms may contribute to early postoperative RV dysfunction. First, ischemia-reperfusion injury following cardiopulmonary circulation operates at lower chamber pressures; consequently, right coronary perfusion occurs throughout the cardiac cycle, prolonging ischemic exposure and potentially reperfusion damage (31,35). Second, the pericardium normally provides a passive mechanical constraint that limits acute cardiac enlargement, particularly for the thinner-walled RV. Pericardiotomy removes this constraint, allowing the RV to dilate more readily in response to postoperative volume shifts. Excessive dilation increases wall tension and reduces myocardial strain efficiency, thereby impairing RV longitudinal strain and systolic function (7,19). Third, the right and left ventricles, being interdependent, are mechanically coupled via the interventricular septum, shared myocardial fibers, and the pericardial space. Postoperatively, increased RV pressure or volume can shift the septum leftward, impairing left ventricular filling and systemic output. Conversely, left ventricular dysfunction can affect RV performance via septal displacement (36). These bidirectional interactions create a feedback loop that may amplify early RV dysfunction. Future studies integrating invasive hemodynamic measurements with serial imaging (e.g., pressure-volume loops and 3D echocardiography) are needed to quantify these interventricular interactions and to determine whether therapeutic strategies targeting one ventricle benefit the other.

Systemic inflammatory response, pulmonary reperfusion syndrome, intraoperative positive-pressure ventilation, and post-cardiopulmonary bypass pulmonary vasoconstriction collectively elevate pulmonary arterial pressure. These perioperative fluctuations in pulmonary pressure increase afterload on the preoperatively decompensated RV, potentially precipitating overt RV failure (21).

The current guidelines provide no explicit recommendations for synthesizing disparate RV systolic function parameters into unified clinical decision-making frameworks. Within this context, this study demonstrated that RVFWLS may serve as a potential adjunctive parameter for assessing early postoperative RV dysfunction, but its clinical utility should be validated in future studies that examine its effect on patient‑centered outcomes.

Study limitations

This study primarily evaluated RV function in patients with MR before and after surgery using conventional and 2D strain echocardiography. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the echocardiographic data were prospectively collected according to a predefined protocol, the analysis was conducted retrospectively after completion of patient enrollment and follow‑up. Therefore, this study can most accurately be described as a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data. Moreover, the follow‑up duration was relatively short (1 year), and whether the observed changes in RV function persist beyond this period remains to be determined. We will continue to improve follow‑up completeness and expand the dataset in future work. Second, we employed a single‑center design with a relatively small sample size, which may carry uncertain selection bias. Subsequent multicenter research is needed to validate our conclusions. Additionally, the attrition rate in this study was high, which was a consequence of the center’s referral status, with a large proportion of patients residing outside the local region. Nonetheless, the loss to follow-up was shown to be random. Third, we used 3D RVEF as the comparative standard for defining RV systolic dysfunction in accordance with ASE guideline-recommended cutoffs. However, 3D echocardiography was not validated against cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) in our cohort. Although previous studies have demonstrated good agreement between 3D echocardiography and CMR for RV volumetric assessment, the lack of paired CMR data in our study should be considered in the interpretation of the diagnostic performance of the parameters under investigation. Fourth, this study focused on diagnostic performance and longitudinal changes of echocardiographic parameters, rather than on clinical outcomes such as mortality, rehospitalization, or functional status. Consequently, the clinical implications of the observed reclassification rates and parameter-specific recovery patterns remain to be determined in future outcome-based studies. Therefore, future multicenter studies with larger and more stable populations are needed.

Conclusions

In patients with MR, RV dysfunction is already present preoperatively and 1 week postoperatively. During the postoperative period, RV function gradually recovers and returns to preoperative levels by 1 year after surgery yet remains significantly below normal levels. Our findings indicate that RVFWLS is associated with RV systolic function in patients with MR before and after surgery. However, given the absence of clinical outcome data in this study, RVFWLS should be regarded as an exploratory measure at the present stage. Nevertheless, its correlation with 3D RVEF and its ability to track longitudinal function changes suggest that it may hold promise as a sensitive marker of early RV dysfunction. Future studies with clinical endpoints validation are needed to establish its clinical utility.
