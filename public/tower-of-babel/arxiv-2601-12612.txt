What Trace Powers Reveal About Log-Determinants: Closed-Form Estimators, Certificates, and Failure Modes

Abstract

Standard scalable methods use many matrix–vector products and polynomial approximations to tr ⁡ ( log ⁡ A ) \operatorname{tr}(\log A) . We study a different information model: access to a few trace powers p k = tr ⁡ ( A k ) p_{k}=\operatorname{tr}(A^{k}) , natural when matrix powers are computed or repeated squaring is feasible. Classical moment-based approximations Taylor-expand log ⁡ ( λ ) \log(\lambda) around the arithmetic mean. This requires | λ − AM | < AM |\lambda-\operatorname{AM}|<\operatorname{AM} and diverges when κ > 4 \kappa>4 . We work instead with the moment-generating function M ⁡ ( t ) = 𝔼 ⁡ [ X t ] M(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{t}] for normalized eigenvalues X = λ / AM X=\lambda/\operatorname{AM} . Since M ′ ​ ( 0 ) = 𝔼 ⁡ [ log ⁡ X ] M^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\log X] , the log-determinant becomes log det ( A ) = n ( log AM + M ′ ( 0 ) ) \log\det(A)=n(\log\operatorname{AM}+M^{\prime}(0)) —the problem reduces to estimating a derivative at t = 0 t=0 . Trace powers give M ⁡ ( k ) M(k) at positive integers, but interpolating M ⁡ ( t ) M(t) directly is ill-conditioned due to exponential growth. The transform K ⁡ ( t ) = log ⁡ M ⁡ ( t ) K(t)=\log M(t) compresses this range. Normalization by AM \operatorname{AM} ensures K ⁡ ( 0 ) = K ⁡ ( 1 ) = 0 K(0)=K(1)=0 . However, this local interpolation cannot capture arbitrary spectral features. Introduction

Estimating
\log\det(A)
for large symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices is central to Gaussian process inference
(

)
, Bayesian model comparison
(

;

)
, and uncertainty quantification
(

;
Stuart 2010
)
. Standard methods combine stochastic trace estimation
(
Hutchinson 1990
;
Avron & Toledo 2011
)
with polynomial
(

)
or Lanczos-based
(
Ubaru et al. 2017
;
Saibaba et al. 2017
)
approximations of

. All require repeated matrix-vector products.

We study a different information model with access to a small number of
trace powers
.
Given only

p_{k}=\operatorname{tr}(A^{k}),\qquad k=1,2,\dots,m,

(1)

or unbiased estimates thereof, what can we infer about
\log\det(A)
? Such data arise naturally in two settings. First, workflows that form matrix powers can obtain additional traces cheaply: given

A^{q}

\operatorname{tr}(A^{p+q})
from the diagonal of

time
(
Higham 2008
)
. Second, low-precision hardware (tensor cores, TPUs) makes repeated squaring
A\to A^{2}\to A^{4}\to\cdots
attractive
(
Haidar et al. 2018
)
. This motivates our question:

What can (and cannot) be inferred about
\log\det(A)
from finitely many positive trace powers?

1.1
The Information Gap

The log-determinant
\log\det(A)=\sum_{i}\log\lambda_{i}
depends critically on small eigenvalues: if
\lambda_{\min}\to 0
, the sum diverges to
-\infty
. Trace powers
\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}
suppress them: as
\lambda_{\min}\to 0
, its contribution vanishes extremely fast (as
\lambda_{\min}^{k}\to 0
for
k\geq 1
). This creates an
information gap
: our data systematically suppress exactly the eigenvalues on which
\log\det(A)
depends most. The classical volatility-drag formula cannot bridge this gap—it is the Taylor expansion whose
\kappa>4
divergence we noted above.

How accurately can we estimate the geometric mean
\operatorname{GM}=(\det A)^{1/n}
from trace powers? We prove that no finite-trace estimator can uniformly approximate
\operatorname{GM}
over all SPD spectra; the achievable accuracy depends on the unknown spectrum itself. This impossibility result shifts the goal:
when can we trust an estimator?
Without a universal method, we need to know when estimators succeed, when they fail, and how to detect failure from the data.

1.2
Our Approach: Exponent-Space Interpolation

Rather than fight the information gap in
\lambda
-space, we change coordinates and treat
t
as a continuous exponent. Let
M(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]
for an eigenvalue random variable
X
. With
X=\lambda/\operatorname{AM}
, we have

\log\det(A)=n\bigl(\log(\operatorname{AM})+M^{\prime}(0)\bigr).

(2)

Therefore, given
M(k)
for
k=0,\dots,m
, we seek to estimate
M^{\prime}(0)
from integer samples
M(k)
.
However, directly interpolating
M(t)
is ill-conditioned:
M(k)
spans orders of magnitude as
k
grows, and further we show that in presence of noisy trace powers (floating point and Hutchinson), numerical differentiation amplifies impact of noise on
M^{\prime}(0)
.

To address the numerical instability, we show that any analytic transform
f(M(t))
can be used for estimating
M^{\prime}(0)
(Theorem
3.1
); and the choice of
f
can alleviate the instability and noise sensitivity, and depending on the eigenspectra and noise model, the conditioning of the problem. In particular, under multiplicative noise,
\log
is the unique variance-stabilizing transform (up to affine scaling), so we work with
K(t)=\log M(t)
. Since
M(0)=1
, we have
K^{\prime}(0)=M^{\prime}(0)
.
Trace powers give
K(k)
at positive integers; the problem becomes:
estimate
K^{\prime}(0)
from
K(0),K(1),\ldots,K(m)
.

Why exponent space works.

Trace powers give samples at integer
t
, and
K(t)=\log M(t)
is analytic near
t=0
, so
K^{\prime}(0)
is determined by nearby values of
K
. This turns estimation into a numerical differentiation problem in exponent space rather than a divergent Taylor expansion in
\lambda
-space. Differentiating the degree-
m
Lagrange interpolant at
t=0
yields simple closed-form weights, producing the distinct family of
k_{0:m}
estimators (Section
4
).

1.3
Contributions

We provide a theoretical and practical framework for
estimating the log-determinant of a
positive-definite matrix using only traces of its
powers. While trace-based estimators can be
computationally attractive, they are not universally
reliable. We therefore ask: under what spectral
conditions can we accurately reconstruct the
log-determinant from a finite number of trace
powers? When will such estimators fail
catastrophically? How can we detect failure from the data? We address these challenges with
six key contributions.

1.

How to estimate log-determinant from trace powers?

We provide a family of estimators (Section
4
) with closed-form weights
L^{\prime}_{j}(0)=(-1)^{j-1}\binom{m}{j}/j
from Lagrange interpolation (Theorem
4.1
).

2.

When is estimation exact?

For lognormal spectra, where
K(t)=\mu t+\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2
is quadratic (Theorem
5.2
).

3.

When must estimation fail?

No universal estimator exists: moments
\operatorname{tr}(A^{j})
stabilize while

(Theorem
6.1
).

4.

Can we predict failure?

Yes: Taylor radius
R=\pi/\log\kappa
determines breakdown (Proposition
6.3
);
m>R
guarantees divergence.

5.

Can we certify quality?

Rigorous bounds
L_{k}(r)\leq\operatorname{GM}\leq U_{k}
from traces alone via
(k+1)
-atom optimization, with complexity independent of matrix dimension (Theorems
7.12
,
7.5
).

6.

How does noise affect accuracy?

Variance grows as
\alpha_{m}\sim 2^{m}/m^{5/4}
(Theorem
8.2
, Proposition
8.6
); use
m=4
for Hutchinson noise.

2
Background and Related Work

Estimating log-determinants from trace powers connects classical moment theory, polynomial interpolation, and matrix computation.
We survey these connections before developing our framework in Section
3
.

2.1
Problem Setup and Notation

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be symmetric positive definite.
Its eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
have condition number
\kappa=\lambda_{n}/\lambda_{1}
.

We have the first
m
trace powers:

p_{k}=\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{k},\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,m,

and we want to estimate
\log\det(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\lambda_{i}
.

Split the log-determinant into two parts.
Define the arithmetic and geometric means of the eigenvalues:

\displaystyle\operatorname{AM}

\displaystyle=p_{1}/n,

\displaystyle\operatorname{GM}

\displaystyle=(\det A)^{1/n}.

Then

\log\det(A)=n\log\operatorname{AM}+n\log(\operatorname{GM}/\operatorname{AM}).

(3)

The first term follows directly from
p_{1}
.
The second term measures spectral deviation from uniformity.

2.2
Elementary Symmetric Polynomials

The GM/AM ratio in (
3
) involves a product of normalized eigenvalues. Elementary symmetric polynomials provide the classical machinery for relating power sums to such products.

Normalize the eigenvalues as
x_{i}=\lambda_{i}/\operatorname{AM}
and define power sums
q_{j}=\sum_{i}x_{i}^{j}
.
Newton’s identities relate these to elementary symmetric polynomials
e_{k}
.
For instance,
e_{1}=n
and
e_{2}=(n^{2}-q_{2})/2
.
Continuing,
e_{3}=(e_{2}n-e_{1}q_{2}+q_{3})/3
and
e_{4}=(e_{3}n-e_{2}q_{2}+e_{1}q_{3}-q_{4})/4
.
The normalized means
E_{k}=e_{k}/\binom{n}{k}
appear in the bounds below.

Three-variable example.

For positive numbers
a,b,c
, the elementary symmetric polynomials are
e_{1}=a+b+c
,
e_{2}=ab+bc+ca
, and
e_{3}=abc
.
Newton’s identities give
e_{1}=q_{1}
,
e_{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}(q_{1}^{2}-q_{2})
,
e_{3}=\tfrac{1}{3}(e_{2}q_{1}-e_{1}q_{2}+q_{3})
.
Thus
(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3})
determines
(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
and hence
abc
.

Large-
n
problem.

Computing
\det(A)=e_{n}
via Newton’s identities requires all
n
power sums.
With only
m\ll n
traces, exact computation is impossible.
What can we infer about
e_{n}
(or
\log e_{n}
) from just
(q_{1},\ldots,q_{m})
?
Our exponent-space framework addresses this by estimating the limit of these identities as
n\to\infty
without computing
e_{n}
explicitly.

2.3
Classical Moment Problems

Our problem can be mapped to the classical moment problem by treating the eigenvalues as a discrete probability distribution. In this view, the trace powers
p_{k}=\sum\lambda_{i}^{k}
correspond to the
k
-th moments
\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]
, and the log-determinant corresponds to
\mathbb{E}[\log X]
.

Classical results establish that a finite set of moments does not uniquely determine the underlying distribution
(
Shohat & Tamarkin 1943
;
Akhiezer 1965
)
. Consequently, many spectra can share the first
m
traces yet yield different log-determinants. This theoretical ambiguity underpins our impossibility result in Section
6
, which proves no estimator is uniform over all spectra, and motivates the moment-constrained bounds derived in Section
7
.

2.4
Classical Bounds on Geometric Means

Given only finitely many moments, what can we guarantee about the geometric mean?
Three classical inequalities provide progressively tighter bounds.

•

Maclaurin’s inequality.

The AM–GM inequality generalizes to
(
Hardy et al. 1952
)
:

E_{1}\geq E_{2}^{1/2}\geq E_{3}^{1/3}\geq\cdots\geq E_{n}^{1/n}=\operatorname{GM}.

(4)

Thus
\operatorname{GM}\leq E_{k}^{1/k}
using only
k
power sums.

•

Newton’s inequalities.

The sequence
(E_{k})
is log-concave
(
Hardy et al. 1952
;
Macdonald 1995
)
:

E_{k}^{2}\geq E_{k-1}E_{k+1},\qquad k=1,\ldots,n-1.

(5)

Consequently, the slopes
s_{k}=\log E_{k}-\log E_{k-1}
are nonincreasing.

•

Tight mean-variance bound.

Among spectra with fixed
(M_{1},M_{2})
,
Rodin 2017
proved the geometric mean is maximized by a two-point distribution:

\operatorname{GM}\leq(1-d)^{(n-1)/n}(1+(n-1)d)^{1/n},\quad d=\sqrt{(M_{2}-1)/(n-1)}.

(6)

This bound is sharp: equality holds for two-point spectra.

2.5
Why Direct Moment Expansions Fail

Rather than bounding the geometric mean, one might try to estimate it directly via Taylor expansion.
The volatility-drag formula expands
\log(\lambda)
around
\operatorname{AM}

(
Latané 1959
;
Kelly 1956
)
:

\mathbb{E}[\log X]\approx-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\operatorname{AM}^{2}}+\frac{\mu_{3}}{3\operatorname{AM}^{3}}-\frac{\mu_{4}}{4\operatorname{AM}^{4}}+\cdots

(7)

with
\mu_{k}=\mathbb{E}[(\lambda-\operatorname{AM})^{k}]
.
Our
k_{0:2}
estimator (Section
4
) recovers the leading term; higher orders give corrections.

For normalized eigenvalues with mean 1, this series converges only when
\lambda\in(0,2)
.
When
\kappa>4
, some eigenvalues approach 0, and convergence becomes impractically slow.
Higher-moment extensions
(
Jean 1983
)
do not fix this.
Figure
1
shows the degradation for ill-conditioned spectra.

2.6

2.7
Information-Theoretic Motivation

Beyond matrix computation, log-determinants arise naturally in information theory and portfolio optimization.
The target
\mathbb{E}[\log\lambda]=\log(\operatorname{GM})
is the optimal growth rate for long-term wealth
(
Kelly 1956
)
.

Breiman 1961
proved that maximizing
\mathbb{E}[\log(\text{wealth})]
asymptotically beats any other strategy.
In matrix terms,
\log\det(A)=n\cdot\mathbb{E}[\log\lambda]
represents the total growth potential of eigenvalues as multiplicative factors.

3
Cumulant Interpolation Framework

Section
2.5
showed that
\lambda
-space expansions
diverge for
\kappa>4
; adding more terms cannot rescue the
volatility-drag approach. The challenge is to use trace powers without falling into the same trap: trace powers
p_{k}=\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}
are dominated by large eigenvalues as
k
grows, yet
\log\det A=\sum_{i}\log\lambda_{i}
depends critically on the smallest eigenvalues. Any approach that treats traces at face value inherits this imbalance.

We work instead in exponent space: treat
t
as a continuous exponent in

M(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]
and estimate
M^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\log X]
by interpolating from
integer samples. Because
M(t)
spans orders of magnitude, we transform to

K(t)=\log M(t)
, which compresses the range and stabilizes interpolation.
This section develops the framework and formalizes the transform choice.
We first show that the choice of analytic transform is first-order equivalent: since all transforms share the same derivative at zero up to scaling, they all target the same log-sum quantity.

Theorem 3.1

(Analytic transform reduction)
.

Assume
X>0
,
M(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]
is analytic near
t=0
(true for finite SPD spectra),
and
f
is analytic on a neighborhood containing
\{M(0),M(1),\ldots,M(m)\}

with
f^{\prime}(1)\neq 0
. Define
G(t)=f(M(t))
. Then
G
is analytic near
t=0
and

G^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(1)\mathbb{E}[\log X].

Consequently
\log\det A=n\bigl(\log\operatorname{AM}+G^{\prime}(0)/f^{\prime}(1)\bigr)
.

Proof.

Since
M(0)=1
and
M^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\log X]
, the chain rule gives
G^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(1)M^{\prime}(0)
.
∎

All such transforms are equivalent to first order at
t=0
, so the practical choice
is about interpolation conditioning from integer samples. Any analytic
f
yields
the same first-order target, but interpolation error and noise amplification depend
on the choice of
f
.

Why log?

The Box–Cox family
f_{\alpha}(z)=(z^{\alpha}-1)/\alpha
interpolates between
identity (
\alpha=1
) and log (
\alpha\to 0
). Appendix
A.3

and Table
8
show that while the optimal
\alpha
varies with
spectrum,
\alpha=0
is consistently competitive, so we adopt it as our default.
The log transform is also the unique
variance-stabilizing choice under multiplicative noise (up to affine scaling) and
compresses the exponential growth of
M(t)
.

3.1
Cumulant Generating Function

With the log transform chosen, define the cumulant generating function

K(t)=\log M(t).

(8)

This compresses the exponential scale of
M(t)
and yields additive trace ratios. For
computation we rescale eigenvalues by their arithmetic mean:
x_{i}=\lambda_{i}/\operatorname{AM}
,
and let
X
be uniform on
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Then

M(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{t},\qquad M(1)=1,

(9)

so
K(0)=K(1)=0
.
For integer
k\geq 1
, write
M_{k}:=M(t)\big|_{t=k}
.
Differentiate: since
\frac{d}{dt}X^{t}\big|_{t=0}=\log X
,

K^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\log X]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_{i}=\log\frac{\operatorname{GM}}{\operatorname{AM}},

(10)

which is exactly the target. Thus

\log\det A=n\bigl(\log\operatorname{AM}+K^{\prime}(0)\bigr)
.
Traces give
K
at integers
k\geq 2
:

M_{k}=\frac{n^{k-1}p_{k}}{p_{1}^{k}},\qquad K(k)=\log\left(\frac{n^{k-1}p_{k}}{p_{1}^{k}}\right).

(11)

We denote
\beta=M_{2}
,
\gamma=M_{3}
,
\delta=M_{4}
, so
K(2)=\log\beta
, etc.
Jensen’s inequality gives
\beta\geq 1
(equality only when all eigenvalues equal), so
K(2)\geq 0
. With
K(0)=K(1)=0
and
K^{\prime}(0)<0
, the curve rises from zero; interpolation must capture this shape.

3.2
Interpolation Problem

From traces
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}
we obtain
K(2),\ldots,K(m)
plus anchors
K(0)=K(1)=0
—a total of
m+1
values at consecutive integers. Polynomial interpolation is natural: it is the unique lowest-degree fit, and differentiating polynomials is trivial. The task: fit degree-
m
polynomial
P(t)
through these points, read off
P^{\prime}(0)
.

3.3
Lagrange Form

Lagrange’s form expresses
P(t)
as a weighted sum of data values—ideal for computing
P^{\prime}(0)
:

P_{m}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}K(j)L_{j}(t),\qquad L_{j}(t)=\prod_{k\neq j}\frac{t-k}{j-k}.

(12)

Differentiate at zero:
P^{\prime}_{m}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}K(j)L^{\prime}_{j}(0)
. Since
K(0)=K(1)=0
, only nodes
j\geq 2
contribute:

\boxed{k_{0:m}:\quad\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}=\sum_{j=2}^{m}L^{\prime}_{j}(0)\cdot K(j)}

(13)

Section
4
derives closed-form weights
L^{\prime}_{j}(0)
; Appendix
A.2
gives the standard interpolation error bound.

Summary.

The CGF
K(t)=\log\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]
converts trace data to cumulants, and
K^{\prime}(0)
equals the target
\log(\operatorname{GM}/\operatorname{AM})
. Lagrange interpolation through
K(0),K(1),\ldots,K(m)
yields the
k_{0:m}
estimator: a linear combination of log-trace ratios with closed-form weights.

Proposition 4.3

(Weight magnitude growth)
.

5
When Is the
k_{0:m}
Estimator Exact?

With the
k_{0:m}
weights in hand, we now ask: when is the estimator exact? The answer identifies the best-case spectra and reveals the boundary beyond which all trace-based methods must approximate.

5.1
Polynomial Exactness

Proposition 5.1

(Polynomial exactness)
.

If
K(t)
is a polynomial of degree at most
m
, then
k_{0:m}
is exact:
\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}=K^{\prime}(0)
.

Proof.

Lagrange interpolation on
m+1
nodes reproduces polynomials of degree
\leq m
exactly. Differentiate at
t=0
to recover
K^{\prime}(0)
.
∎

Which eigenvalue distributions make
K(t)
polynomial? Only one non-trivial family: lognormal.

Theorem 5.2

(Lognormal exactness)
.

Let
\log X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. Then
K(t)=\mu t+\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2
is quadratic, and
k_{0:m}
is exact for all
m\geq 2
.

Proof.

For
Y=\log X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, the moment-generating function is
\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]=e^{\mu t+\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2}
. Since
X=e^{Y}
, we have
\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]
, so
K(t)=\mu t+\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2
. A quadratic is reproduced exactly by any interpolant of degree
\geq 2
.
∎

Remark 5.3

(Uniqueness)
.

Why is lognormal special? If
X
is lognormal, then
Y=\log X
is Gaussian and its cumulant generating function is
K_{Y}(t)=\mu t+\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2
. Gaussian distributions are uniquely characterized by having all cumulants of order
\geq 3
vanish, so the CGF is exactly quadratic. This quadratic form is inherited by
K(t)=\log\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]=\log\mathbb{E}[e^{tY}]
. Among non-degenerate distributions, only lognormal yields quadratic
K(t)
.

Closed form for lognormal spectra.

If
K(t)
is quadratic, skip
k_{0:m}
. From
K(t)=\mu t+\sigma^{2}t^{2}/2
, we get
K^{\prime}(0)=\mu
. Normalization
\mathbb{E}[X]=1
gives
K(1)=0
, so
\mu=-\sigma^{2}/2
and
K(2)=\sigma^{2}
. Hence
\operatorname{GM}/\operatorname{AM}=e^{-\sigma^{2}/2}=1/\sqrt{e^{K(2)}}
, yielding:

\widehat{\det(A)^{1/n}}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(A)^{2}}{n\sqrt{n\cdot\operatorname{tr}(A^{2})}}.

(16)

This needs only two traces, half the data of
k_{0:4}
.

Remark 5.4

(Connection to Latané)
.

Equation (
16
) is the exact version of Latané’s volatility-drag approximation
(
Latané 1959
)
. Latané used Taylor expansion; ours is exact for any lognormal spectrum.

Finite-

plug-in behavior.

Theorem
5.2
describes the population. Model eigenvalues as i.i.d. lognormal draws. The empirical traces converge to population moments as
n\to\infty
; continuity of the estimator (Lemma

Lemma 5.5

(Continuity of plug-in estimator)
.

Fix

and

(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})\mapsto n^{-H_{m}}\,p_{1}^{\,m}\prod_{k=2}^{m}p_{k}^{\,w_{k}},\qquad w_{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\binom{m}{k},\quad H_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{j},

Proof.

Each power
p_{k}\mapsto p_{k}^{\,w_{k}}
is continuous on

; multiplying by the constant

∎

Remark 5.6

(Practical guidance)
.

Lognormal marks the exact/approximate boundary. If log-eigenvalues look roughly Gaussian, formula (

is approximate; the exactness analysis shows why.

Lognormal spectra thus mark the exactness boundary. Beyond this case,

approximates. But how badly can it approximate? The next section shows that errors can be arbitrarily severe: no trace-based method using finitely many moments can succeed uniformly over all spectra.

6
Why No Estimator Can Succeed Universally

Section
5
identified when
k_{0:m}
is exact—a best-case scenario.
But most spectra are not lognormal. How badly can the estimator fail? If errors were
always modest, practitioners could accept
k_{0:m}
as “good enough.” If errors can
be arbitrarily large, we need safeguards. We now prove that no finite-moment estimator
can be uniformly accurate: the information gap is a fundamental barrier.

6.1
Setup

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be SPD with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
and mean
\operatorname{AM}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
. Define
x_{i}=\lambda_{i}/\operatorname{AM}
and draw
X
uniformly from
\{x_{i}\}
. Then
M(t)=\mathbb{E}[X^{t}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{t}
and
K(t)=\log M(t)
(the cumulant generating function from Section
3.1
). The target is
K^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\log X]=\log(\operatorname{GM}/\operatorname{AM})
, giving
\log\det A=n(\log\operatorname{AM}+K^{\prime}(0))
.
We observe
K(t)
only at
m
positive nodes
T=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}\subset(0,\infty)
.

6.2
Two Obstructions

Positive moments shrink small eigenvalues:
x^{t}\to 0
as
x\to 0
for
t>0
. So
M(t)
can stay finite while the smallest eigenvalue vanishes. Yet
K^{\prime}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\log X]
diverges like
\log(\min_{i}x_{i})
. This mismatch yields two distinct barriers:

•

Obstruction 1: Non-identifiability.
Different spectra can share identical moments yet differ in
K^{\prime}(0)
. Theorem
6.1
(a) constructs explicit examples; this is an instance of the classical moment problem.

•

Obstruction 2: Saturation.
Even continuous estimators must fail: moments can converge to finite limits while
K^{\prime}(0)\to-\infty
. Theorem
6.1
(b) proves this for any continuous estimator.

Can any finite set of moments determine
K^{\prime}(0)
universally? The answer is no:

Theorem 6.1

(Impossibility)
.

Fix any finite
T=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\}\subset(0,\infty)
.

(a) Non-identifiability.

There exist spectra
\mu,\nu
with
K_{\mu}(t_{j})=K_{\nu}(t_{j})
for all
j
but
K^{\prime}_{\mu}(0)\neq K^{\prime}_{\nu}(0)
. Hence no function of
(K(t_{1}),\ldots,K(t_{m}))
can always return
K^{\prime}(0)
. (This is an instance of the classical moment problem; see
Shohat & Tamarkin 1943
and
Akhiezer 1965
.)

(b) Saturation.

For any continuous estimator
\widehat{K^{\prime}_{T}}=f(K(t_{1}),\ldots,K(t_{m}))
, there exists a sequence
A_{\kappa}
with

\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}K_{A_{\kappa}}(t_{j})\text{ finite for each }j,\quad\text{but}\quad\lim_{\kappa\to\infty}K^{\prime}_{A_{\kappa}}(0)=-\infty.

So
\widehat{K^{\prime}_{T}}
stays bounded while the truth diverges.

Proof.

(a)
Pick
m+3
distinct points
x_{0},\ldots,x_{m+2}>0
. The constraint matrix

V=\begin{bmatrix}1&\cdots&1\\
x_{0}&\cdots&x_{m+2}\\
x_{0}^{t_{1}}&\cdots&x_{m+2}^{t_{1}}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
x_{0}^{t_{m}}&\cdots&x_{m+2}^{t_{m}}\end{bmatrix}

has a nonzero null vector
v
. For small
\varepsilon>0
, set
w^{(\pm)}=w^{(0)}\pm\varepsilon v
where
w^{(0)}>0
satisfies the mean-one constraint. The measures
\mu=\sum_{i}w_{i}^{(+)}\delta_{x_{i}}
and
\nu=\sum_{i}w_{i}^{(-)}\delta_{x_{i}}
share all moments in
T
, yet
K^{\prime}_{\mu}(0)-K^{\prime}_{\nu}(0)=2\varepsilon\sum_{i}v_{i}\log x_{i}\neq 0
generically.

(b)
Take
A_{\kappa}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\kappa)
. Then

M_{\kappa}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(2/(1+\kappa))^{t}+(2\kappa/(1+\kappa))^{t}\bigr]\to 2^{t-1},

so
K_{\kappa}(t)\to(t-1)\log 2
. But
K^{\prime}_{\kappa}(0)=\log\frac{2\sqrt{\kappa}}{1+\kappa}\to-\infty
. A continuous
f
keeps
\widehat{K^{\prime}_{T}}
finite, so the error grows without bound.
∎

Example 6.2

(Nodes
\{2,4\}
)
.

With support
\{0.1,0.5,1,2,10\}
, two weight vectors give identical
\mathbb{E}[X]=1
,
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]
,
\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]
, but
\mathbb{E}[\log X]
differs by 5%.

6.3
Taylor Radius

The saturation obstruction can be sharpened by locating singularities of
K(t)
.
Two-point spectra provide the sharpest bounds on this radius, making them the critical worst-case examples.

Proposition 6.3

(Taylor radius for two-point spectra)
.

Let
X
take values
x_{1},x_{2}
with weights
(1-p),p
and ratio
\kappa=x_{2}/x_{1}>1
. The Taylor series of
K(t)
about

has radius

\boxed{R=\frac{1}{\log\kappa}\sqrt{\log^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{1-p}{p}\bigr)+\pi^{2}}.}

For equal weights (
p=1/2
):
R=\pi/\log\kappa
.

Proof.

Singularities of
K(t)=\log M(t)
occur at zeros of
M(t)=(1-p)x_{1}^{t}+px_{2}^{t}
. Setting
(1-p)+p\kappa^{t}=0
gives
t_{k}=[\log((1-p)/p)+i(2k+1)\pi]/\log\kappa
. The radius equals the distance to the nearest zero.
∎

Geometrically,
R
marks the convergence boundary: polynomial interpolation at nodes
t>R
extrapolates beyond the analytic domain, causing divergence.

Corollary 6.4
.

For equal-weight two-point spectra with
\kappa>e^{\pi/2}\approx 4.81
, we have
R<2
, so the Taylor series diverges at
t=2
.

6.4
Practical Lesson

No estimator works for every spectrum. Ask instead: which spectra are safe?
Smooth
spectra (geometric, uniform) have large Taylor radius and permit accurate interpolation.
Outlier-dominated
spectra (two-point, bimodal) have small Taylor radius and cause failure (Figure
3
). These impossibility results are not curiosities—they imply that any point estimator needs backup. Section
7
provides such certificates: bounds that remain valid even when point estimates fail, with interval width signaling reliability.

7
Guaranteed Bounds

The impossibility results of Section
6
are sobering: no
point estimator can uniformly succeed across all spectra. But impossibility
results describe worst cases; they do not preclude useful guarantees. This raises
a practical question:
given that point estimates may fail, what can we still
certify?

The answer is
bounds
. Unlike point estimates, which may be arbitrarily wrong
for adversarial spectra, bounds provide deterministic guarantees: the true geometric
mean lies within
[L_{k}(r),U_{k}]
for
any
spectrum consistent with observed
moments.

Classical bounds exist—Maclaurin’s inequality, Newton’s inequalities, and Rodin’s two-point bound (Section
2.4
)—but they either require structure we lack or leave room for improvement.
Our contribution is twofold: (i)
new bounds
that exploit log-concavity (last-slope) and moment constraints (feasible-set optimization), and (ii) a
measure-theoretic framework
revealing that optimal bounds are achieved by
(k+1)
-atom distributions.
This second insight is the key: it reduces bound computation from an
n
-dimensional search over eigenvalues to a fixed
(2k+2)
-dimensional optimization—certification cost is independent of matrix size.

Narrow intervals pin down the answer; wide intervals diagnose unreliability.
The decision rule is simple: clip the estimate to
[L,U]
if outside, and always
report both the estimate and interval (Section
7.8
).

7.1
Last-Slope Bound

Maclaurin’s inequality (
4
) gives
\operatorname{GM}\leq E_{m}^{1/m}
.
We improve this by exploiting log-concavity of the
E_{j}
sequence,
guaranteed by Newton’s inequalities (
5
), which imply
E_{j-1}E_{j+1}\leq E_{j}^{2}
.

Define
g(j)=\log E_{j}
and slopes
s_{j}=g(j)-g(j-1)
.
Log-concavity implies
s_{j}
is nonincreasing in
j
.

Theorem 7.1

(Last-slope bound)
.

For
m<n
:

\boxed{U_{\mathrm{LS},m}=\left[E_{m}\left(\frac{E_{m}}{E_{m-1}}\right)^{n-m}\right]^{1/n}}

(17)

Proof.

Since
s_{j}\leq s_{m}
for
j\geq m
:

g(n)=g(m)+\sum_{j=m+1}^{n}s_{j}\leq g(m)+(n-m)s_{m}
.
Exponentiating:
E_{n}\leq E_{m}(E_{m}/E_{m-1})^{n-m}
.
Taking the
n
-th root and using
\operatorname{GM}(x)=E_{n}^{1/n}
yields the bound.
∎

Remark 7.2

(Dominance over Maclaurin)
.

The last-slope bound
U_{\mathrm{LS},m}
is strictly tighter than Maclaurin’s
E_{m}^{1/m}
whenever

E_{m}/E_{m-1}<E_{m}^{1/m}
, which holds for most spectra. The improvement comes from
extrapolating the log-concave decay rather than truncating at
E_{m}
.
However, the optimization-based bounds
U_{k}
(Section
7.3
) are typically tighter still; last-slope’s value is computational simplicity (closed-form, no optimization).

Combined closed-form bound.

Taking the minimum of all available bounds (Maclaurin (
4
),
Rodin (
6
), and last-slope):

\boxed{U_{\mathrm{closed}}=\min\left\{U_{\mathrm{Rodin}},\;E_{4}^{1/4},\;U_{\mathrm{LS},4}\right\}}

(18)

7.3
Compactness and Finite Support

One might attempt to find extremal distributions by optimizing over all
n
eigenvalues—a problem with
O(n)
variables.
However, since we only constrain
k
moments, the problem allows for a massive complexity reduction.
We show that optimal measures have at most
k+1
atoms, collapsing the search space from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2(k+1)}
(atom locations and weights).
For example, if
n=10^{6}
and
k=4
, the optimization involves just 10 variables rather than
10^{6}
.

Theorem 7.5

(Compactness and finite support)
.

Assume
M_{k}<\infty
for some
k\geq 1
.

1.

The set
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is convex and compact in the weak topology.

2.

Every extreme point of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is a discrete measure with

at most
k+1
atoms
.

3.

Consequently,
U_{k}
and
L_{k}(r)
admit optimizers supported on at most

k+1
points.

Proof.

(i) Tightness:
For any
T>0
, Markov’s inequality gives

\mu([T,\infty))\leq M_{k}/T^{k}

uniformly over
\mu\in\mathcal{F}_{k}
. Hence
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is tight.

Closedness:
If
\mu_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
weakly and
\sup_{n}\int x^{k}\,d\mu_{n}<\infty
,
then by uniform integrability,
\int x^{j}\,d\mu_{n}\to\int x^{j}\,d\mu
for
j\leq k
.
By Prokhorov’s theorem, tight
+
closed implies compact.

(ii) Extreme points:
We have
k+1
linear constraints:
\int 1\,d\mu=1

(normalization) and
\int x^{j}\,d\mu=M_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,k
. By Richter’s
theorem on moment spaces
(
Richter 1957
)
, extreme points have at most
k+1
atoms.

(iii) Existence:
The objective
\mu\mapsto\int\log x\,d\mu
is linear.
Existence follows from compactness; for
L_{k}(r)
with
r>0
,
\log x
is bounded
below on
[r,\infty)
.
∎

Corollary 7.6

(Monotonicity)
.

U_{k+1}\leq U_{k}
and
L_{k+1}(r)\geq L_{k}(r)
.
Additional constraints shrink feasible sets.

Remark 7.7

(Computational complexity)
.

The
(k+1)
-atom structure implies
O(k^{3})
complexity for solving the bounds optimization (Definition
7.4
), independent of matrix dimension
n
.
This arises from measure-theoretic compactness: the space of distributions matching
k
moments is weak-* compact, and extreme points are finitely supported.
Thus certification cost scales with the number of available traces, not the matrix size.

7.4
Relaxation Validity

Theorem 7.8

(Relaxation provides valid bounds)
.

For any
n
and any feasible spectrum

1.

\operatorname{GM}(x)\leq U_{n,k}\leq U_{k}
(upper bound valid)

2.

(lower bound valid)

Proof.

The empirical measure

and satisfies

\exp(\int\log x\,d\mu_{x})=(\prod_{t=1}^{n}x_{t})^{1/n}=\operatorname{GM}(x)
.
Taking supremum over
\mathcal{F}_{k}

\operatorname{GM}(x)\leq U_{k}
. The inequalities

U_{n,k}\leq U_{k}

∎

Corollary 7.9

(Quantization gap under bounded support)
.

If the optimizer of

has support in
[r,R]

8
Sensitivity to Noisy Trace Inputs

The bounds of Section
7
guarantee intervals containing the true
geometric mean regardless of spectrum. But those bounds assume exact trace powers

p_{k}=\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})
—an idealization. In practice, traces are estimated
stochastically via Hutchinson’s method or computed in low-precision arithmetic.

Challenge.
How does trace noise propagate through the
k_{0:m}
estimators? The Lagrange
weights
w_{k}=(-1)^{k-1}\binom{m}{k}/k
grow rapidly with
m
—does this cause
catastrophic noise amplification? If so, the interpolation order that is optimal
under exact traces may be the worst choice under noise. We derive closed-form variance expressions and show that noise amplification grows
as
2^{m}/m^{5/4}
. For typical Hutchinson noise levels (
\eta\approx 1
–
5\%
),

m=4
balances bias and variance; higher orders are counterproductive.

8.1
Noise Model

We model noisy trace observations as multiplicative perturbations:

\hat{p}_{k}=p_{k}(1+\varepsilon_{k}),\quad k=1,\ldots,m,

(24)

where
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}
are independent, zero-mean random variables with
variance
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{k})=\eta^{2}
. The relative noise level
\eta>0

is user-controlled. As a heuristic, Hutchinson’s estimator with
n_{v}
random
vectors yields
\eta\approx\sqrt{2/n_{v}}
in well-behaved cases. For
finite-precision arithmetic,
\eta
reflects the relative rounding error.

Remark 8.1

(Choice of multiplicative model)
.

A multiplicative model fits because trace magnitudes vary widely—
p_{k}
grows
as
\lambda_{\max}^{k}
—so additive noise with fixed variance would overwhelm
small traces or vanish for large ones. The i.i.d. assumption simplifies the
analysis; in practice, Hutchinson variance for
\operatorname{tr}(A^{k})
can depend on
k

and matrix structure. See
Roosta-Khorasani & Ascher 2015
for detailed sample complexity bounds.

8.2
Noise Propagation to
K(k)

Cumulant values are
K(k)=\log(n^{k-1}p_{k}/p_{1}^{k})
. With noisy traces:

\hat{K}(k)=\log\left(\frac{n^{k-1}\hat{p}_{k}}{\hat{p}_{1}^{k}}\right)=\log\left(\frac{n^{k-1}p_{k}(1+\varepsilon_{k})}{p_{1}^{k}(1+\varepsilon_{1})^{k}}\right).

(25)

Separating true values from noise:

\hat{K}(k)=K(k)+\log(1+\varepsilon_{k})-k\log(1+\varepsilon_{1}).

(26)

For small noise (
|\varepsilon_{k}|<1
almost surely, or
\eta
small enough
that
\Pr[\varepsilon_{k}<-1]
is negligible), Taylor expansion gives

\log(1+\varepsilon_{k})=\varepsilon_{k}-\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{k}^{2}+O(\varepsilon_{k}^{3})
. Keeping only first-order terms:

\hat{K}(k)=K(k)+\varepsilon_{k}-k\varepsilon_{1}+O(\eta^{2}).

(27)

Thus noise in
\hat{K}(k)
depends on both
\varepsilon_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{1}

(with coefficient
k
), reflecting normalization by
p_{1}^{k}
.

8.3
Variance of
\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}

The
k_{0:m}
estimator (Theorem
4.1
) is

\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}\hat{K}(k),\quad w_{k}=L^{\prime}_{k}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{k}\binom{m}{k}.

(28)

Substituting (
27
):

\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}K(k)+\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{1}\sum_{k=2}^{m}k\,w_{k}+O(\eta^{2}).

(29)

The first sum is the noise-free estimate with bias
b_{m}=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}K(k)-K^{\prime}(0)
.
The noise-dependent error is

\Delta=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{1}\sum_{k=2}^{m}k\,w_{k}.

(30)

Lagrange differentiation is exact on
\{0,1,\ldots,m\}
, so
\sum_{k=1}^{m}k\,w_{k}=1

(the derivative of
f(x)=x
at
x=0
). This identity holds because the estimator must exactly recover linear functions. Since
w_{1}=m
, we have

\sum_{k=2}^{m}k\,w_{k}=1-m
(see Appendix
A.8
for details).
Thus:

\Delta=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}\varepsilon_{k}+(m-1)\varepsilon_{1}.

(31)

Theorem 8.2

(Variance of
\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}
under noisy traces)
.

Under noise model (
24
) with i.i.d. zero-mean
\varepsilon_{k}

of variance
\eta^{2}
:

\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}\bigr)=\eta^{2}\alpha_{m}^{2}+O(\eta^{4}),

(32)

where the
noise amplification factor
is

\boxed{\alpha_{m}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}^{2}+(m-1)^{2}}.}

(33)

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}]=0
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\Delta]=0
. By independence:

\operatorname{Var}(\Delta)=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}^{2}\eta^{2}+(m-1)^{2}\eta^{2}=\eta^{2}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}^{2}+(m-1)^{2}\right].

(34)

The
O(\eta^{4})
term comes from higher-order terms in the Taylor expansion.
∎

Remark 8.3

(Heteroskedastic and correlated noise)
.

If
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{k})=\eta_{k}^{2}
varies with
k
or noise is correlated with
covariance matrix
\Sigma
, then
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta)=w^{\top}\Sigma w
where

w=(w_{2},\ldots,w_{m},m-1)^{\top}
. The exponential growth of
\lVert w\rVert_{2}
dominates,
so the key point remains: high
m
amplifies noise.

8.4
Noise-Induced Bias from Log Nonlinearity

First-order analysis shows
\mathbb{E}[\Delta]=0
, so noise introduces no bias at
order
\eta
. But logarithm nonlinearity creates systematic bias at order
\eta^{2}
.

Keeping the next Taylor term
\log(1+x)=x-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{2}+O(x^{3})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{K}(k)-K(k)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\varepsilon_{k})]-k\,\mathbb{E}[\log(1+\varepsilon_{1})]

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{k}^{2}]+\tfrac{k}{2}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1}^{2}]+O(\eta^{3})=\tfrac{k-1}{2}\eta^{2}+O(\eta^{3}).

(35)

Proposition 8.4

(Noise-induced bias)
.

The additional bias due purely to multiplicative noise is

b_{\mathrm{noise}}(m,\eta)=\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}(1-H_{m})+O(\eta^{3}),

(36)

where
H_{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}1/j
is the
m
-th harmonic number.

Since
H_{m}\approx\ln m+\gamma
for large
m
, the noise-induced bias

b_{\mathrm{noise}}\approx-\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\ln m
grows only logarithmically.
For typical noise levels (
\eta\lesssim 5\%
) and moderate
m
, this is much
smaller than interpolation bias
b_{m}
and variance
\alpha_{m}\eta
, so it can
usually be neglected.

8.5
RMSE Decomposition

We measure accuracy via root mean squared error:

\mathrm{RMSE}\bigl(\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}\bigr)=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}-K^{\prime}(0))^{2}\bigr]}.

Corollary 8.5

(Bias–variance decomposition)
.

The RMSE satisfies

\mathrm{RMSE}^{2}\bigl(\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}\bigr)\approx\bigl(b_{m}+b_{\mathrm{noise}}(m,\eta)\bigr)^{2}+\alpha_{m}^{2}\eta^{2}.

(37)

Ignoring the smaller
O(\eta^{2}\log m)
noise-bias term:

\boxed{\mathrm{RMSE}\bigl(\widehat{K^{\prime}(0)}\bigr)\approx\sqrt{b_{m}^{2}+\alpha_{m}^{2}\eta^{2}}\sim\max\{|b_{m}|,\alpha_{m}\eta\}}

(38)

(within a factor
\sqrt{2}
).

This decomposition reveals two regimes:

1.

Bias-dominated
(
\alpha_{m}\eta\ll|b_{m}|
):
RMSE
\approx|b_{m}|
, independent of noise. Increasing
m
reduces error.

2.

Noise-dominated
(
\alpha_{m}\eta\gg|b_{m}|
):
RMSE
\approx\alpha_{m}\eta
, growing linearly with
\eta
. Increasing
m

increases
error because
\alpha_{m}
grows exponentially.

The
crossover noise level
where bias and variance balance is
\eta_{*}=|b_{m}|/\alpha_{m}
; for
\eta>\eta_{*}
, reducing
m
may improve accuracy despite coarser polynomial approximation. Figure
4
visualizes this trade-off: RMSE plateaus at
|b_{m}|
until noise exceeds
\eta_{*}
, then grows linearly.

8.6
Asymptotic Growth of the Weight Norm

The noise amplification factor
\alpha_{m}
is dominated by

\lVert w\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum w_{k}^{2}}
for
m\geq 4
(see Figure

c).

Proposition 8.6

(
\ell_{2}
norm of Lagrange derivative weights)
.

Let
w_{k}=(-1)^{k-1}\binom{m}{k}/k
for
k=2,\ldots,m
. Then

\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}^{2}=\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cdot\frac{4^{m}}{m^{5/2}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

(39)

as
m\to\infty
, and therefore

\boxed{\lVert w\rVert_{2}=\frac{2}{\pi^{1/4}}\cdot\frac{2^{m}}{m^{5/4}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).}

(40)

Proof sketch.

The sum
\sum w_{k}^{2}=\sum\binom{m}{k}^{2}/k^{2}
is dominated by
k\approx m/2
.

Step 1
(Gaussian approximation):
\binom{m}{m/2}^{2}\sim 4^{m}/(\pi m)

by De Moivre–Laplace.

Step 2
(Convert to integral): The effective width is
\sim\sqrt{m}
,
and
1/k^{2}|_{k=m/2}=4/m^{2}
.

Step 3
(Evaluate): Combining yields

\sum w_{k}^{2}\sim(4^{m}/m)\cdot(4/m^{2})\cdot m^{1/2}=4^{m}/m^{5/2}
.
See Appendix
A.8
for full details and Figure
12
for empirical validation.

8.8
Practical Guidance

The optimal interpolation order balances bias and variance:

m^{*}=\argmin_{m}\sqrt{b_{m}^{2}+\alpha_{m}^{2}\eta^{2}}.

(41)

•

(

or
m=6

for minimal bias.

•

(

.

for moderate

,

•

Very noisy traces
(
\eta>10\%

m=3
or even
m=2

to avoid catastrophic noise amplification, accepting larger interpolation bias.

Remark 8.7

(Adaptive order selection)
.

In practice, estimate

from trace variance and

from spectrum shape
(if known), then solve (

) numerically. Or compute

for

and select the value with minimal apparent variance via bootstrap.

balances bias and variance for typical Hutchinson

validates this prediction on realistic

as noise

Sections

estimators on realistic spectra? Which interpolation order

Experimental questions.
(1) Which interpolation orders

For

or better. The bounds flag every failure, and the Taylor radius

•

Uniform
on
[1,\kappa]

Summary of Findings

These experiments yield practical guidelines:

1.

Order selection.
For
\kappa\in[50,200]
, use
m\in\{4,5,6\}
. Keep
m<R(\kappa)
to avoid divergence.

2.

Accuracy expectations.
On geometric spectra,
k_{0:4}
yields about
6\%
relative error in
K^{\prime}(0)
at
\kappa=100
; uniform and clustered are higher (around
20\%
), and lognormal is higher still. On pathological spectra (two-point, bimodal), estimators fail but bounds are exact.

3.

Bounds as diagnostics.
The optimization-based bounds
U_{k}
and
L_{k}
tighten monotonically with
k
. The last-slope bound is never tightest. When estimates fall outside

4.

Noisy traces.
For Hutchinson noise (
\eta\approx 1
–

), use
m=4
to balance interpolation bias against noise amplification.

10
Conclusion

Trace-based log-determinant estimation has three practical outcomes: (1) for smooth spectra with moderate
\kappa
, the
k_{0:m}
estimators achieve single- to low-double-digit percent error; (2) for pathological spectra,
all
finite-trace methods fail—but bounds
[L,U]
detect this; (3) the actionable rule is simple:
clip estimates to
[L,U]
and always report both
.

•

What works.

The
k_{0:m}
estimators with
m\in\{4,5,6\}
achieve single- to low-double-digit percent accuracy in
K^{\prime}(0)
on geometric and uniform spectra with moderate condition numbers (
\kappa\lesssim 200
). The closed-form weights (Theorem
4.1
) make implementation straightforward in
O(m)
operations, independent of matrix dimension
n
.

•

What does not work.

The impossibility theorem identifies two fundamental barriers—non-identifiability and saturation—that are information-theoretic, not algorithmic. Two-point and bimodal spectra demonstrate 100%+ errors that
only the bounds detect
.

•

Bounds as guardrails.

Our bounds provide deterministic intervals
[L,U]
with
O(k)
complexity independent of matrix dimension. Estimates inside this interval are typically accurate; clip estimates outside to the nearest bound. For two-point and bimodal spectra, the bounds are exact, while uncorrected estimates err by 100%+.

•

Practical recommendations.

For moderate condition numbers with high-precision traces, use
k_{0:5}
or
k_{0:6}
. For stochastic trace estimates (e.g., Hutchinson with
\eta\approx 1\text{--}5\%
noise), prefer
k_{0:4}
: higher orders suffer from noise amplification
\alpha_{m}\sim 2^{m}/m^{5/4}
(Section
8
). Always compute all three upper bounds and take
U=\min
. The rule is simple: clip estimates outside
[L,U]
and always report both the estimate and the interval. For matrices with large
\kappa
or suspected outliers, use stochastic Lanczos quadrature instead.

Future Work

•

Stochastic trace estimation.

This paper uses exact traces to isolate interpolation bias. Combining
k_{0:m}
with Hutchinson-style trace approximations introduces additional variance. The optimal order balances bias against noise via
m^{*}=\argmin_{m}\sqrt{b_{m}^{2}+\alpha_{m}^{2}\eta^{2}}
; characterizing this tradeoff precisely remains open.

•

Adaptive order selection.

The optimal interpolation order
m^{*}
depends on the unknown condition number
\kappa
. Can the sequence
K(2),K(3),\ldots
diagnose spectral structure and suggest when to stop?

•

Integration with Krylov methods.

Lanczos iterations produce trace estimates as byproducts. Can our bounds provide online certificates during SLQ iterations? This could enable early stopping when the interval
[L_{k}(r),U_{k}]
is sufficiently tight.

•

Lower bounds without spectral floor.

The lower bound
L_{k}(r)
requires
r\leq\lambda_{\min}/\operatorname{AM}
. Can we derive meaningful lower bounds from moments alone, without such a floor?

•

Complex Box-Cox transforms (exploratory).

The Box-Cox family
f_{\alpha}(z)=(z^{\alpha}-1)/\alpha
admits complex
\alpha=a+ib
.
Appendix
A.4
shows that oscillatory damping via
\cos(b\ln M_{k})

can dramatically stabilize interpolation for pathological spectra: for two-point spectra,

oracle-tuned
complex
\alpha
reduces error from 500%+ to under 1%.
However, optimal
(a,b)
depends on the unknown spectrum and cannot be selected from
traces alone—this is an oracle analysis, not a practical estimator.
Developing a data-driven selection heuristic is an open problem; a coefficient-of-variation
diagnostic shows promise (Appendix
A.4
).

A.1
Proof of Lagrange Weight Formula

We prove Theorem
4.1
: for nodes
\{0,1,\ldots,m\}
,

L^{\prime}_{j}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{j}\binom{m}{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,m.

Proof.

The Lagrange basis polynomial for node
j
is:

L_{j}(t)=\prod_{k=0,k\neq j}^{m}\frac{t-k}{j-k}.

Step 1: Denominator.

D_{j}:=\prod_{k=0,k\neq j}^{m}(j-k)=\prod_{k=0}^{j-1}(j-k)\cdot\prod_{k=j+1}^{m}(j-k).

The first product is
j!
. The second is:

\prod_{k=j+1}^{m}(j-k)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{m-j}(-\ell)=(-1)^{m-j}(m-j)!.

Thus
D_{j}=j!\cdot(-1)^{m-j}(m-j)!
.

Step 2: Express
L_{j}(t)
via
\omega(t)=\prod_{k=0}^{m}(t-k)
.

L_{j}(t)=\frac{\omega(t)}{(t-j)D_{j}}.

Step 3: Derivative at zero.

Since
\omega(0)=0
(the product includes
t-0=t
), we cannot directly apply L’Hôpital. Instead, factor out:

\omega(t)=t\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{m}(t-k)=:t\cdot\tilde{\omega}(t).

Then for
j\geq 1
:

L_{j}(t)=\frac{t\cdot\tilde{\omega}(t)}{(t-j)D_{j}}.

At
t=0
:

L_{j}(0)=\frac{0\cdot\tilde{\omega}(0)}{(0-j)D_{j}}=0.

For the derivative:

L^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{t\cdot\tilde{\omega}(t)}{(t-j)D_{j}}\right].

Using quotient rule:

L^{\prime}_{j}(t)=\frac{(\tilde{\omega}(t)+t\tilde{\omega}^{\prime}(t))(t-j)-t\tilde{\omega}(t)}{(t-j)^{2}D_{j}}.

At
t=0
:

L^{\prime}_{j}(0)=\frac{\tilde{\omega}(0)\cdot(-j)-0}{j^{2}D_{j}}=\frac{-j\cdot\tilde{\omega}(0)}{j^{2}D_{j}}=\frac{-\tilde{\omega}(0)}{j\cdot D_{j}}.

Step 4: Evaluate
\tilde{\omega}(0)
.

\tilde{\omega}(0)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(0-k)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}(-k)=(-1)^{m}m!.

Step 5: Combine.

L^{\prime}_{j}(0)=\frac{-(-1)^{m}m!}{j\cdot j!\cdot(-1)^{m-j}(m-j)!}=\frac{(-1)^{m+1}m!}{j\cdot j!\cdot(-1)^{m-j}(m-j)!}.

Simplify the signs:
(-1)^{m+1}\cdot(-1)^{-(m-j)}=(-1)^{m+1-m+j}=(-1)^{j+1}=(-1)^{j-1}\cdot(-1)^{2}=(-1)^{j-1}
.

Thus:

L^{\prime}_{j}(0)=\frac{(-1)^{j-1}m!}{j\cdot j!(m-j)!}=\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{j}\cdot\frac{m!}{j!(m-j)!}=\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{j}\binom{m}{j}.

∎

Case
j=0
: integral evaluation.

The Lagrange basis polynomials satisfy the partition-of-unity property

\sum_{j=0}^{m}L_{j}(t)=1
. Differentiating gives
\sum_{j=0}^{m}L^{\prime}_{j}(t)=0
,
so at
t=0
:

L^{\prime}_{0}(0)=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}L^{\prime}_{j}(0)=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{j}\binom{m}{j}.

We evaluate this sum via the integral identity

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1-(1-t)^{m}}{t}\,dt=H_{m}.

Proof of identity:
Substitute
u=1-t
to get

\int_{0}^{1}(1-u^{m})/(1-u)\,du=\int_{0}^{1}(1+u+\cdots+u^{m-1})\,du=H_{m}
.
Alternatively, expand
(1-t)^{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}\binom{m}{k}t^{k}
, so

\frac{1-(1-t)^{m}}{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k-1}\binom{m}{k}t^{k-1}.

Integrating term-by-term from
0
to
1
yields

\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k-1}\binom{m}{k}/k=H_{m}
.
Therefore
L^{\prime}_{0}(0)=-H_{m}
.

A.2
Interpolation Error Bound

The standard Lagrange error bound gives

K(t)-P_{m}(t)=\frac{K^{(m+1)}(\xi)}{(m+1)!}\prod_{j=0}^{m}(t-j)

(42)

for some
\xi
in the convex hull of the nodes. Differentiating at
t=0
:

K^{\prime}(0)-P^{\prime}_{m}(0)=\frac{K^{(m+1)}(\xi)}{(m+1)!}\cdot(-1)^{m}m!+\text{correction}.

(43)

The sign of the leading term alternates with
m
, but the full error depends on the unknown derivative
K^{(m+1)}(\xi)
. We make no universal sign claim.

A.4
Complex Box–Cox Transforms

The Box–Cox family extends naturally to complex exponents
\alpha=a+ib
:

f_{\alpha}(z)=\frac{z^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha}=\frac{z^{a}e^{ib\ln z}-1}{a+ib}.

For
z>0
, this is well-defined and
f^{\prime}_{\alpha}(1)=1
for all
\alpha\neq 0
.
Taking the real part yields a real-valued transform:

g(t)=\Re\!\left[\frac{M(t)^{\alpha}-1}{\alpha}\right]=\frac{M(t)^{a}\cos(b\ln M(t))-1}{|a|^{2}+b^{2}}\cdot a+\frac{M(t)^{a}\sin(b\ln M(t))}{|a|^{2}+b^{2}}\cdot b.

Why complex
\alpha
helps.

For spectra where

grows rapidly with
k
(e.g., two-point), the log-moments

span a large range, causing polynomial interpolation to become
ill-conditioned. The oscillatory factor
\cos(b\ln M(k))
damps this growth:
instead of
K(k)
growing from 0 to 11 (for two-point with
\kappa=100
), the transform
g(k)
oscillates in
[-1,1]
.

A.6
Newton Identities for Normalized Power Sums

For normalized eigenvalues
x_{i}=\lambda_{i}/\operatorname{AM}
with
\operatorname{AM}=p_{1}/n
, define the power sums:

q_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{k}.

Expressing
q_{k}
in terms of traces:

q_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{k}}{\operatorname{AM}^{k}}=\frac{p_{k}}{\operatorname{AM}^{k}}=\frac{p_{k}}{(p_{1}/n)^{k}}=\frac{n^{k}p_{k}}{p_{1}^{k}}.

The relationship with the normalized moment
M_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}x_{i}^{k}
is
q_{k}=n\cdot M_{k}
, giving
M_{k}=n^{k-1}p_{k}/p_{1}^{k}
. Note that
q_{1}=\sum_{i}x_{i}=n
by the normalization convention.

Newton’s identities for
(e_{1},e_{2},\ldots)
in terms of
(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots)
:
e_{1}=n
,
2e_{2}=n^{2}-q_{2}
,
3e_{3}=e_{2}n-e_{1}q_{2}+q_{3}
, and
4e_{4}=e_{3}n-e_{2}q_{2}+e_{1}q_{3}-q_{4}
.

A.8
Proof of Asymptotic Weight Norm Growth

We prove Proposition
8.6
: for
w_{k}=(-1)^{k-1}\binom{m}{k}/k
,

\lVert w\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}^{2}}=\frac{2}{\pi^{1/4}}\cdot\frac{2^{m}}{m^{5/4}}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).

Proof.

We compute
S:=\sum_{k=2}^{m}w_{k}^{2}=\sum_{k=2}^{m}\binom{m}{k}^{2}/k^{2}
using
a saddle-point approximation. The dominant contribution comes from
k\approx m/2

where the binomial coefficient is maximized.

Step 1: Gaussian approximation of the binomial.

By the local central limit theorem (De Moivre–Laplace), for
k
near
m/2
:

\binom{m}{k}=\frac{2^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi m/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{2(k-m/2)^{2}}{m}\right)\bigl(1+O(1/\sqrt{m})\bigr).

(44)

This approximation is valid uniformly for
|k-m/2|=O(\sqrt{m})
.

Step 2: Approximate the sum by an integral.

Squaring the Gaussian approximation:

\binom{m}{k}^{2}\approx\frac{4^{m}}{\pi m/2}\exp\left(-\frac{4(k-m/2)^{2}}{m}\right).

(45)

Replace the sum by an integral (justified since the summand is smooth on scale

\sqrt{m}
):

S\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{4^{m}}{\pi m/2}\cdot\frac{1}{k^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{4(k-m/2)^{2}}{m}\right)dk.

(46)

The bounds are extended to
\pm\infty
because the Gaussian tails decay
exponentially fast beyond the sum’s natural range.

Step 3: Change of variables.

Let
t=(k-m/2)/(\sqrt{m}/2)
, so
k=m/2+t\sqrt{m}/2
and
dk=(\sqrt{m}/2)\,dt
.
The Gaussian becomes
e^{-t^{2}}
:

S\approx\frac{4^{m}}{\pi m/2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t^{2}}}{(m/2+t\sqrt{m}/2)^{2}}dt.

(47)

Step 4: Expand the denominator.

For the integral,
|t|=O(1)
contributes the dominant mass. Writing

k^{2}=\left(\frac{m}{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+\frac{t}{\sqrt{m}}\right)^{2}\approx\frac{m^{2}}{4}\left(1+\frac{2t}{\sqrt{m}}\right),

(48)

to leading order
1/k^{2}\approx 4/m^{2}
. Substituting:

S\approx\frac{4^{m}}{\pi m/2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{m^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}dt=\frac{4^{m}}{\pi m/2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\cdot\frac{4}{m^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\pi}.

(49)

Step 5: Simplify.

S=\frac{4^{m}\cdot\sqrt{m}\cdot 4\cdot\sqrt{\pi}}{\pi m/2\cdot 2\cdot m^{2}}=\frac{4\cdot 4^{m}}{\sqrt{\pi}\cdot m^{5/2}}.

(50)

Taking the square root:
\lVert w\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{S}=\frac{2}{\pi^{1/4}}\cdot\frac{2^{m}}{m^{5/4}}
.
∎

Remark A.2

(Interpretation of the exponent)
.

The
m^{-5/2}
exponent in
\sum w_{k}^{2}
arises from three contributions:

•

m^{-2}
: the
1/k^{2}
factor evaluated at
k\sim m/2
;

•

m^{-1}
: the squared-binomial peak height (
\sim 4^{m}/(\pi m)
);

•

m^{+1/2}
: the effective summation width (
\sim\sqrt{m}
).

Together:
\sum w_{k}^{2}\sim 4^{m}\cdot m^{-2}\cdot m^{-1}\cdot m^{1/2}=4^{m}/m^{5/2}
.
Taking the square root gives
\lVert w\rVert_{2}\sim 2^{m}/m^{5/4}
.

A.9
Stochastic Trace Estimation and Polynomial Approximations

This appendix reviews stochastic trace estimation methods and polynomial/rational approximations of
\log(\cdot)
, which form the foundation of many scalable log-determinant methods.

Stochastic Trace Estimation.

Hutchinson’s estimator
(
Hutchinson 1990
)
approximates
\operatorname{tr}(B)
for an implicit matrix
B
using random vectors
z
with
\mathbb{E}[zz^{\top}]=I
:

\operatorname{tr}(B)\approx\frac{1}{s}\sum_{i=1}^{s}z_{i}^{\top}Bz_{i}.

(51)

Rademacher vectors (
z_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}^{n}
uniformly) achieve minimum variance among unbiased estimators
(
Avron & Toledo 2011
)
.
Roosta-Khorasani & Ascher 2015
improved the sample complexity bounds, and
Meyer et al. 2021
introduced Hutch++, achieving optimal
O(\sqrt{\lVert B\rVert_{F}/\epsilon})
sample complexity via low-rank deflation. Probing methods
(
Bekas et al. 2007
)
estimate matrix diagonals via structured probe vectors, enabling efficient trace computation when the matrix has known sparsity patterns.

For log-determinants, the identity
\log\det(A)=\operatorname{tr}(\log A)
reduces the problem to estimating
\operatorname{tr}(\log A)
. However, this reduction requires approximating the matrix logarithm—a nontrivial task since
\log(\lambda)
is unbounded near zero, making accurate approximation difficult for ill-conditioned matrices
(
Higham 2008
)
.

Polynomial and Rational Approximations.

Two major approaches approximate
\log(\lambda)
on
[\lambda_{\min},\lambda_{\max}]
:

Chebyshev methods.

Han et al. 2015
combine Hutchinson’s estimator with Chebyshev polynomial approximation of
\log(x)
, achieving
O(n)
complexity per sample. The polynomial degree scales as
O(\sqrt{\kappa}\log(1/\epsilon))
to achieve accuracy
\epsilon
, where
\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}
. This approach is effective for moderate condition numbers but requires many terms when
\kappa
is large.

Lanczos quadrature.

Ubaru et al. 2017
use Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (SLQ), interpreting
\operatorname{tr}(f(A))
as a Riemann-Stieltjes integral and applying Gaussian quadrature rules derived from the Lanczos process. This method adapts to the spectral distribution and often requires fewer iterations than Chebyshev methods for the same accuracy.
Saibaba et al. 2017
provide rigorous non-asymptotic error bounds for randomized log-determinant estimators based on subspace iteration.

Both approaches require either: (i) matrix-vector products with
A
or shifted solves
(A-\sigma I)^{-1}v
, or (ii) polynomial/rational approximations of
\log(\cdot)
that converge slowly for large
\kappa
. For background on Krylov methods, see
Saad 2003
; for polynomial approximation theory, see
Trefethen 2013
.

A.10
Computing
k
-Trace Bounds

This appendix details the optimization problem for computing the bounds
U_{k}
and
L_{k}(r)
from Section
7
, explains why
U_{3}\approx U_{4}
for most spectra, and analyzes computational complexity.

The Optimization Problem.

Theorem
7.5
establishes that
U_{k}
is achieved by a discrete measure with at most
k+1
atoms. This reduces the infinite-dimensional optimization over probability measures to a finite nonlinear program.

Definition A.3

(Upper bound optimization)
.

Given normalized moments
M_{1}=1,M_{2},\ldots,M_{k}
:

U_{k}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\Delta^{k},\,x\in(0,\infty)^{k+1}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}w_{i}\log x_{i}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}w_{i}x_{i}^{j}=M_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,k

(52)

where
\Delta^{k}=\{w:w_{i}\geq 0,\sum_{i}w_{i}=1\}
is the probability simplex.

The lower bound problem has the same structure but minimizes the objective and constrains
x_{i}\geq r
:

L_{k}(r)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\Delta^{k},\,x\in[r,\infty)^{k+1}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}w_{i}\log x_{i}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}w_{i}x_{i}^{j}=M_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,k

(53)

Solution strategy.

Sequential quadratic programming (SLSQP) with multistart handles the nonconvexity. Initialize with diverse starting points: uniform weights on linearly-spaced atoms, and random draws from Dirichlet-distributed weights with log-normal atoms. Five restarts typically suffice for
k\leq 8
.

Complexity Analysis.

The key insight is that computational cost depends only on
k
, not on the matrix dimension
n
.

Proposition A.4

(Complexity of
k
-trace bounds)
.

Computing
U_{k}
or
L_{k}(r)
requires
O(k)
variables (weights and atoms),
O(k)
constraints (normalization and
k
moment equations), and
O(k)
work per iteration. Total cost is
O(k^{2}T)
with
T<1000
iterations. For fixed
k
, complexity is
O(1)
in
n
.

This
O(1)
-in-
n
complexity arises because moments
M_{j}
are precomputed scalars. Once
p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}
are available (via trace estimation or direct computation), the bound optimization does not reference the matrix again.
