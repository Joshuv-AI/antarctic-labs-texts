Two types of q q -Gaussian distributions used to study the diffusion in a finite region


Abstract
In this work, we explore both the ordinary q q -Gaussian distribution and a new one defined here, determining both their mean and variance, and we use them to construct solutions of the q q -deformed diffusion differential equation. This approach allows us to realize that the standard deviation of the distribution must be a function of time. In one case, we derive a linear Fokker-Planck equation within a finite region, revealing a new form of both the position- and time-dependent diffusion coefficient and the corresponding continuity equation. It is noteworthy that, in both cases, the conventional result is obtained when q q tends to zero. Furthermore, we derive the deformed diffusion-decay equation in a finite region, also determining the position- and time-dependent decay coefficient. A discrete version of this diffusion-decay equation is addressed, in which the discrete times have a uniform interval, while for the discrete positions the interval is not uniform.

Two types of
q
-Gaussian distributions

As already mentioned, starting from the definition of
q
-exponential given in the equation (
1
), we will now consider two types of

-Gaussian distributions.
The first of them, which we will call
q
-Gaussian Type 1 distribution, has already been previously introduced:

P_{q}(x,a)=\sqrt{a}\,A_{q}\ e_{q}(-ax^{2})=\sqrt{a}\,A_{q}\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left(1-a\,({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})\,x^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}},&\ \mbox{if}~~1\geq a\,({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})\,x^{2},\\[6.45831pt]
\ \ \ \ \ 0,&\ \mbox{if}~~1<a\,({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})\,x^{2},\end{array}\right.\quad a>0.

(2)

From the previous expression it is easily deduced that if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>0
, then the distribution
P_{q}(x,a)
has compact support, which is the closed interval
|x|\leq 1/\sqrt{a({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}
, while if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}\leq 0
, then the distribution
P_{q}(x,a)
makes sense for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and the support is not compact.
The constant
A_{q}
in (
2
) is obtained by normalizing the function, that is, by imposing
\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{q}(x,a)\,dx=1
, and it is given by

A_{q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\dfrac{\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}\ \Gamma\left(\frac{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}{2({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\ \Gamma\left(\frac{1+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}\right)},&{\text{ for }}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>0,\\[15.0694pt]
\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}},&{\text{ for }}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}=0,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{\sqrt{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}|}\ \Gamma\left(\frac{1}{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}|}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\ \Gamma\left(\frac{2-|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}|}{2|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}|}\right)},&{\text{ for }}~-2<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}<0,\end{array}\right.

(3)

Therefore, for the distribution
P_{q}(x,a)
to be normalizable, the parameter

must be constrained by the condition
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>-2
.
It can also be verified that in the limit
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}\to 0
we have

\lim_{q\to 0}P_{q}(x,a)=\sqrt{\frac{a}{\pi}}\ e^{-ax^{2}}.

(4)

As already said, this distribution has been considered previously in the literature

B_{q}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\dfrac{\sqrt{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}\ \Gamma\left(\frac{2a+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}{2({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\ \Gamma\left(\frac{a+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}\right)},&{\text{ for }}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>0,\\[15.0694pt]

~~\infty,~~~~{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}\leq-\frac{2a}{3}.\end{cases}

3
Diffusion process in finite region

The standard diffusion equation in one spatial dimension is given by

\partial_{t}\varrho(x,t)=D\,\partial_{x}^{2}\varrho(x,t),

(11)

where
\varrho(x,t)
is the density of the diffusing material at location
x
and time
t
, and
D
is the diffusion coefficient, which depends on both the diffusing material and the substrate in which it diffuses and which we assume to be constant. A typical and simple initial condition used to solve (
11
) is

\varrho(x,0)=\delta(x),

(12)

where it has been established that the total mass of the substance released per unit cross-sectional area is unity. If we consider an infinite domain
(-\infty,\infty)
, the solution of (
11
) turns out to be

\varrho(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\,D\,t}}\ e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4\,D\,t}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\ e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\,\sigma^{2}}}.

(13)

Note that the solution found in (
13
) is a Gaussian distribution whose mean is zero and whose variance is
\sigma^{2}=\langle x^{2}\rangle=2\,D\,t
.
In the result (
13
) the conservation of the total amount of the substance that diffuses has already been taken into account, that is,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varrho(x,t)\,dx=1.

(14)

The density
\varrho(x,t)
has the following limits

\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}\varrho(x,t)=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\varrho(x,t)=0.

(15)

In the following subsections we will discuss the solution of the diffusion equation (
11
) in a finite region
-L\leq x\leq L
, based on the
q
-Gaussian distributions with finite support that were previously introduced.

3.1
Diffusion in a finite region based on Type 1
q
-Gaussian with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>0

If we want to find a solution to the diffusion equation in a finite interval, an interesting idea is to use
q
-Gaussians with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>0
which, as we have seen in the previous section, have a compact support and, therefore, automatically satisfy homogeneous boundary conditions, both at the ends and on the outside of the aforementioned compact where the solution is not zero.
Specifically, if we look for the concentration as a Type 1
q
-Gaussian distribution with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}>0
, we can use the fact that, as already seen, for this
q
-Gaussian the support is given by
L=1/\sqrt{a({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})q}
, therefore we can take this value as a starting point to solve the problem.
Let us now assume that
\varrho_{q}(x,t)
is the
q
-deformed density of the diffusing material at location
x
and time
t
, the variance of which depends on the variables
x
,
t
and the parameter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}
. Within the support we can assume the following ansatz based on (
13
) and (
2
):

\displaystyle\varrho_{q}(x,t)\!\!

\displaystyle\!=\!

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{K_{q}/\sqrt{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}}{\sigma_{q}(x,t)}e_{q}\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{(2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}))\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle\!=\!

\displaystyle\!\!\frac{K_{q}/\sqrt{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}}{\sigma_{q}(x,t)}\left[1-\frac{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})x^{2}}{(2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}))\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)}\right]^{\frac{1}{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}},

where
\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)
is a standard deviation
\langle x^{2}\rangle
for this
q
-deformed density (
16
), that obviously will depend on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}
,
x
and
t
, and the range of
x
is

-\sigma_{q}(x,t)\sqrt{\frac{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}}\leq x\leq\sigma_{q}(x,t)\sqrt{\frac{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}}.

(17)

In a finite region
-L\leq x\leq L
we can not demand
\langle x^{2}\rangle=2Dt
because we would obtain the undesirable result that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\langle x^{2}\rangle=\infty
. Instead we can assume that

\langle x^{2}\rangle=\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)=\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}(1-e^{-2({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})Dt}),

(18)

which reduces to
\langle x^{2}\rangle=2Dt
in the limit
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}\rightarrow 0
, as it should be. For Eq. (
18
) we have the limit

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\langle x^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}},

(19)

which is finite unless
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}=0
. Therefore, we can say that the diffusion characterized by the concentration (
16
) has a moving boundary because
\sigma_{q}(x,t)
is time-dependent in Eq. (
18
). In the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
, the domain of
x
is time idependent:

-\frac{\sqrt{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}\leq x\leq\frac{\sqrt{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}.

(20)

If we consider the diffusion in
|x|\leq L
, we should demand

\frac{\sqrt{2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}}\leq L,

(21)

which gives the lower bound for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}
,

({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})\geq\frac{3+\sqrt{9+8L^{2}}}{2L^{2}},

(22)

Differentiating the concentration with respect to
t
and
x
leads to

\frac{\partial\varrho_{q}(x,t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{D(1-({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t))}{\sigma^{2}}\left[1-\frac{2x^{2}}{(2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}))\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})x^{2}}{(2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}))\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)}}\right)\right]\!\!\varrho_{q}(x,t),

(23)

and differentiating the equation (
16
) with respect to
t
and
x
we arrive at

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\varrho_{q}(x,t)=-\frac{2x}{(2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}))\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)}\left(\frac{1}{1-\frac{({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})x^{2}}{(2+3({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q}))\sigma^{2}_{q}(x,t)}}\right)\varrho_{q}(x,t),

(24)

where we have used the following equation obtained from (
18
)

\sigma_{q}(x,t)\frac{d\sigma_{q}(x,t)}{dt}=D(1-({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})\sigma^{2}).

(25)

Therefore, we have a linear Fokker-Planck equation in a finite region:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\varrho_{q}(x,t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(D_{q}(x,t)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\varrho_{q}(x,t)\right),

(26)

where the position- and time-dependent diffusion coefficient is

3.2.1
Meaning of the deformed diffusion-decay equation in a discrete version

Equation (
39
) is different from the ordinary diffusion-decay equation because the ordinary derivative with respect to
x
is replaced with the deformed derivative
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
.
Now we will look for the physical meaning of the deformed diffusion-decay equation.
The second term on right hand side of Eq. (
39
) represents the decay with position- and time-dependent decay coefficient, while the first term differs from the ordinary diffusion term due to the presence of the deformed derivative.

Now let us consider the discrete version of Eq. (
39
) as

\Delta_{\tau}\varrho_{q}(x_{n},t_{m})=D\Delta_{a}^{2}\varrho_{q}(x_{n},t_{m})+\mu(x_{n},t_{m})\varrho_{q}(x_{n},t_{m}),

(42)

where time difference operator and position difference operator are given by

\Delta_{\tau}F(x_{n},t_{m})=\frac{F(x_{n},t_{m+1})-F(x_{n},t_{m})}{\tau},

(43)

\Delta_{a}F(x_{n},t_{m})=\frac{F(x_{n+1},t_{m})-F(x_{n},t_{m})}{a}.

(44)

If we consider the uniform time lattice obeying
t_{n+1}-t_{n}=\tau
and take the limit
\tau\to 0
, then the time difference operator reduces to the ordinary time derivative.
But this is not the case for the part corresponding to the spatial coordinate. The deformed derivative (
41
) implies that the position space must be discrete with a non-uniform interval. Here we assume that the discrete positions obey

x_{n+1}\ominus x_{n}=a,

(45)

where
\ominus
is a deformed subtraction.
The deformed derivative
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
given in (
41
) is not invariant under the ordinary translation
x\rightarrow x+a
due to the presence of the factor
(1-({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})x^{2})
.
Therefore, we will look for a
q
-deformed sum such that the
q
-deformed derivative (
41
) is invariant under the
q
-deformed sum. This can be achieved with the help of the pseudo-calculus
[
54
]
(or
f
-deformation
[
55
]
). Now let us consider
q
-deformed addition and
q
-deformed subtraction as

a\oplus b=\frac{a+b}{1+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})ab},\qquad a\ominus b=\frac{a-b}{1-({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})ab}.

(46)

Then, we have

x_{n+1}=\frac{x_{n}+a}{1+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})ax_{n}}.

(47)

The action of the position
q
-difference operator on a function
F(x_{n})
is

\Delta_{a}F(x_{n})=\frac{1}{a}\left(F\left(\frac{x_{n}+a}{1+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})ax_{n}}\right)-F(x_{n})\right).

(48)

Now let us consider the case where
x_{n}
corresponds to
x
in the limit
a\rightarrow 0
. Then we set

F\left(\frac{x+a}{1+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})ax}\right)=b_{0}+b_{1}a+{\cal O}(a^{2}),

(49)

where

b_{0}=F(x),

(50)

and

b_{1}=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial a}F\left(\frac{x+a}{1+({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})ax}\right)\right|_{a=0}=(1-({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1-q})x^{2})\partial_{x}F(x).

(51)

Therefore, the difference operator (
44
) reduces to the deformed derivative in the continuum limit. Thus, Eq. (
39
) is the continuum version of the diffusion-decay equation where the discrete times have a uniform interval, while the discrete positions have a non-uniform interval defined in (
45
).

4
Concluding remarks

In this article, we consider the
q
-Gaussian distributions of Type 1 and Type 2, obtained from two different definitions arising from the deformed
q
-exponential and the fact that
e_{q}(-ax^{2})\neq(e_{q}(-x^{2}))^{a}
.

We applied the Type 1
q
-Gaussian distribution to find solutions to the diffusion equation and obtained the range of the standard deviation in this case.
Next we consider a special form for the
q
-deformed standard deviation, which reduces to the ordinary case when a long time is allowed to pass, since the deformation parameter depends on time.
For the Type 1
q
-Gaussian distribution, we obtained the linear Fokker-Plank equation from the diffusion equation and showed that in this case the diffusion coefficient depends on both position and time.
By obtaining the continuity equation, it was shown both that the flow depends on the
q
-deformed diffusion coefficient, as well as the flow’s compliance with Fick’s law.
We repeat the calculation for the case in which at
t=0
the substance is released at a point other than zero.

We then applied the Type 2
q
-Gaussian distribution to find solutions to the diffusion equation, and the deformed diffusion-decay equation in a finite region was obtained.
The position- and time-dependent decay coefficient was found, which goes to zero as the deformation parameter approaches zero.
The deformed diffusion-decay equation thus obtained is different from the ordinary diffusion-decay equation because the ordinary derivative with respect to
x
is replaced by the deformed derivative
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
.
To search for the physical meaning of this deformed diffusion-decay equation, a discrete version of it
in which the discrete times have a uniform interval, while the discrete positions have a non-uniform interval is addressed.

Because the Type 2
q
-Gaussian distribution is related to the
q
-translation invariant derivative
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
, we can construct the
q
-translation invariant quantum mechanics with a help of
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
. In such a theory, Type 2
q
-Gaussian distribution can replace the ordinary Gaussian distribution in formulating the wave packet theory corresponding to the
q
-translation invariant quantum mechanics with spatial derivative
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
. Besides, Type 2
q
-Gaussian distribution can be used in the wave function for the ground state in the
q
-translation invariant quantum mechanics with spatial derivative
{\cal D}_{x}^{q}
.

We believe that the results found in this work are interesting and open the door to the use of this type of
q
-Gaussian functions for the analysis of some of the multiple physical phenomena that have been described in the introductory section and about which we are already working.
