Spicy food intake and dietary factors shape the gut microbiome and metabolism of mucin and short-chain fatty acids in healthy adults
Authors: Uigi Min, Jihyun Kim, Jinwook Kim, Hyunmi Jin, Hyunji Oh, Soyeon Ahn, Hyeonseok Shin, Wonhee Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhether spicy food intake independently modulates mucin metabolism and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production or depends on co-ingested factors such as alcohol remains poorly understood. Herein, shotgun metagenomics characterized gut microbial composition, functional pathways, and their relationship with spicy food intake, alcohol consumption, and intestinal fatty acid–binding protein (I-FABP) and liver fatty acid–binding protein (L-FABP) levels in 229 healthy Korean adults. Alcohol intake was positively correlated with urinary I-FABP levels indicating mild epithelial stress, whereas spicy food intake was not associated with either FABP biomarker. Consumption of highly spicy foods resulted in increased abundance of SCFA-producing and mucin-metabolizing taxa, along with mucin degradation and SCFA production. Individuals with high alcohol intake showed stronger enrichment of mucin-degrading taxa with reduced SCFA flux and increased abundance of Proteobacteria and Fusobacteria. The cross-classified dietary groups exhibited distinct mucin and SCFA activity patterns. The Drink-High–Spicy-High (DHSH) group displayed elevated mucin turnover and SCFA production with dysbiosis. These findings suggest that spicy food may modulate mucus layer metabolism in a context-dependent manner, whereas alcohol more consistently perturbs mucin–SCFA networks and epithelial integrity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53556-7.Keywords: Gut microbiome, Mucin metabolism, Spicy food intake, Alcohol consumption, Shotgun metagenomics, Cross-sectional studySubject terms: Biochemistry, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

The mucus layer constitutes the primary interface between the host gut microbiota and epithelium, performing multiple physiological functions, including providing a habitat for microorganisms, regulating the exchange of metabolic products, and coordinating immune responses1,2. This layer critically determines the stability of the gut ecosystem, and structural or chemical perturbations of the mucus layer are early indicators of increased intestinal permeability, chronic inflammation, and metabolic disorders2. Therefore, understanding the factors that regulate mucus layer homeostasis is essential for characterizing gut health.

Spicy food may modulate the mucosal layer composition and microbial function. It typically contains various bioactive compounds, most notably capsaicin, allyl isothiocyanate, piperine, and gingerol, which can regulate mucosal ecosystems, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) biosynthesis, and host energy metabolism3–6. While epidemiological studies have reported associations between habitual spicy food consumption and reduced risk of all-cause mortality and metabolic diseases, some literature reports contradictory findings7–10.

One critical consideration when interpreting these results is that spicy foods are rarely consumed in isolation. In practice, spicy foods are frequently co-ingested with other dietary factors, such as high-fat foods, refined carbohydrates, or alcohol. These factors may interfere with the inherent benefits of consuming spicy food. Among these cofactors, alcohol is particularly notable because of its well-established role in inducing epithelial cytotoxicity, inhibiting mucosal secretion, and disrupting the gut microbial composition11,12. Furthermore, spicy foods comprise a wide variety of dietary products. In Korea, spicy cuisine includes kimchi, spicy stews, and instant noodles13,14. These foods contain diverse nutritional components, including fermented vegetables, high dietary fiber, varying fat content, and refined carbohydrates, all of which can independently alter gut microbiota15,16. In addition to dietary factors, individual host characteristics, including sex, age, and body mass index (BMI), influence the intestinal environment and must be considered when evaluating the health effects of foods17. Therefore, determining the specific impact of spicy foods on the gut microbiome by evaluating the contribution of co-ingested factors in observational clinical studies is required.

While specific dietary factors can induce distinct regulatory functions at the metabolic pathway level, even within identical microbial profiles, previous research has been largely limited to analyzing microbial community structures using 16S rRNA gene sequencing18. However, this is insufficient for evaluating the practical implications of ecological interactions because it struggles to capture functional shifts directly related to intestinal barrier homeostasis, such as mucus layer-associated gene clusters or SCFA synthesis pathways. In contrast, shotgun metagenomic analysis enables the direct profiling of the microbial genome. Thus, it serves as an appropriate tool to elucidate the specific effects of dietary factors on microbial metabolic networks at the functional level19,20.

In this study, we performed shotgun metagenomic analysis of 229 healthy Korean adults to investigate the functional features of their gut microbiota and mucus layer-related metabolic pathways, and how they’re affected by spicy food and alcohol intake. By examining these relationships at both structural and functional levels, this study provides insights into how dietary habits may impact gut barrier homeostasis.

Results

Sample collection and baseline characteristics

In total, 240 healthy adults were recruited for this study. However, 11 participants either withdrew their consent or were lost to follow-up, resulting in a cohort of 229 participants. All participants completed a structured questionnaire on dietary and lifestyle habits and provided fecal, urine, and oral epithelial samples. Urine samples were analyzed to quantify inflammation- and epithelial injury-related biomarkers, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP), and liver fatty acid-binding protein (L-FABP). Oral samples were analyzed to measure TRPV1 expression, and fecal samples were subjected to shotgun metagenomic sequencing (Fig. 1A). Demographic and lifestyle characteristics of the participants are summarized in Supplementary Information.

Study design and metadata correlation. (A) Overview of the study design. (B) Correlation plot showing Spearman’s correlation coefficients between dietary factors, metadata, and intestinal epithelial injury biomarkers (I-FABP, L-FABP).

The study group comprised 87 males (38%) and 142 females (62%). The mean age was 38.3 ± 11.1 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 22.9 ± 3.4 kg/m2, which is within the normal weight range. The average frequency of spicy food consumption was 1.9 ± 0.7 points, corresponding to approximately “2–4 times per month.” Consumption frequencies of high-fat foods (1.9 ± 0.8), instant foods (1.7 ± 0.8), and fast foods (1.4 ± 0.7) fell within the “rarely to <1 time per week” range, indicating generally healthy dietary habits. Vegetable preference relative to meat was moderate (4.2 ± 2.0 on a 1–10 scale), suggesting a tendency toward vegetable-based diets. Vitamin (1.3 ± 0.5) and probiotic intake scores (1.6 ± 0.5) indicated infrequent use. Alcohol drinking frequency (1.7 ± 1.0) and drinking volume per occasion (1.6 ± 1.0) corresponded to “2–4 times per month, 1–3 drinks per occasion.” Smoking scores were low (0.2 ± 0.5), reflecting the predominantly non-smoking population.

Correlations between biomarker levels and metadata

Spicy food stimulates gastrointestinal physiological responses and mucus secretion, providing both beneficial and adverse health effects9,10,21, whereas alcohol consumption disrupts the intestinal barrier, inducing epithelial injury and suppressing mucus secretion11,12. Considering these prior findings, we evaluated the associations between these two dietary factors and markers of epithelial injury and composition of the gut microbiota.

First, Spearman’s correlation was conducted to assess the relationships between survey-based demographic, dietary, and lifestyle variables and various biomarkers: I-FABP and L-FABP, reflecting intestinal barrier damage; inflammatory markers (TNF-α and IL-6); and the sensory receptor for pungency (TRPV1). In the biomarker assessment, TNF-α, IL-6, and TRPV1 concentrations were at low levels and were therefore excluded from subsequent analyses (see Supplementary Information).

Spearman’s correlation analysis of demographic variables and epithelial injury markers showed that I-FABP exhibited weak positive correlations with age (ρ = 0.22), BMI (ρ = 0.19), and stool frequency (ρ = 0.24) and a negative correlation with sex (ρ = –0.27) (Fig. 1B). L-FABP levels were positively correlated with I-FABP (ρ = 0.31), suggesting that the two markers induce similar epithelial injury responses. Examination of correlations between dietary factors and biomarkers revealed that alcohol intake (g/day) was positively correlated with I-FABP (ρ = 0.26) but not correlated with L-FABP (ρ = 0.05). I-FABP (ρ = 0.01) and L-FABP (ρ = 0.05) were weakly associated with spicy foods and other dietary variables.

These patterns suggest that alcohol consumption is a dietary factor that exerts a direct and sensitive effect on intestinal epithelial injury. Accordingly, the interpretation of gut microbial composition and functional pathway changes requires analytical strategies that incorporate alcohol intake as a principal independent variable, while simultaneously adjusting for sex, age, BMI, and stool frequency as confounders. This approach enables a clearer separation of the effects of spicy food and alcohol consumption and provides a more accurate framework for explaining the physiological variations reflected by epithelial injury markers.

In addition, alcohol intake exhibited a correlation (although weak) with high-fat (ρ = 0.20) and instant food intake (ρ = 0.22), indicating that it may be part of a broader cluster of co-occurring dietary behaviors. Lifestyle variables such as smoking, physical activity, and sleep quality were not correlated with I-FABP or L-FABP levels.

Microbiome composition and diversity

Gut microbiome profiling yielded 1,527 species detected across all samples (see Supplementary Information). Consistent with previous reports, Bacillota and Bacteroidota were identified as the two most dominant phyla within the gut microbiota of the study participants22. When evaluating the extent to which environmental and dietary factors contributed to taxonomic variation, sex, stool frequency, and age showed the highest explanatory power, followed by BMI, I-FABP levels, smoking, and alcohol consumption (Fig. 2A). In contrast, other factors, including meat–vegetable preference, vitamin intake, and sleep quality, explained less than 1% of the variation in microbial community composition.

Group classification by spicy food intake and comparison of gut microbiome profiles

To determine changes in gut microbial composition following spicy food consumption, participants were divided into high-intake (SH; n = 40) or low-intake (SL; n = 189) groups based on consumption frequency. The SH group showed significantly higher spicy food intake scores than the SL group (p < 0.001), whereas fast-food consumption scores were significantly lower (p = 0.003). There were no differences between the groups in terms of demographic or lifestyle variables, including age, BMI, stool frequency, or alcohol consumption or other health-related factors, such as the use of vitamins, probiotics, or antibiotics (see Supplementary Information). Shotgun metagenome-based diversity analyses showed no distinct separation in alpha diversity, and Bray–Curtis distance–based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) displayed no clear separation in taxonomic composition between the groups; this showed no significant differentiation according to the permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, p = 0.09; Supplementary Information).

Species-level differential abundances were assessed using MaAsLin2 with a linear mixed-effects model, which identified 21 species with significant group differences (Fig. 2C). The SH group showed significantly higher abundances of Dialister invisus, Blautia_A caecimuris, Limisoma sp000437795, Ruminococcus_E sp003526955, Dorea_A longicatena_B, Faecalimonas phoceensis, Blautia hansenii, Anaerotignum sp001304995, Blautia sp001304935, Amedibacillus dolichus, Eubacterium_R sp000436835, Blautia_A sp900549015, Phocaeicola sp900542985, Butyribacter sp001916135, Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum, CAG-83 sp000435975, and Fusobacterium_A mortiferum. Among these taxa, species of the genera Blautia, Limisoma, Ruminococcus, Butyribacter, and Butyricicoccus are associated with SCFA production and mucus layer metabolism4,23–26, suggesting that spicy food intake may contribute positively to gut metabolic homeostasis. However, species of Fusobacterium have been linked to intestinal inflammation27 and genera such as Dialister and Dorea have been shown to have context-dependent associations with health in metagenomic studies28–32. In contrast, the SL group showed significantly higher abundances of Dialister hominis, Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus, UMGS856 sp900546265, and Gordonibacter pamelaeae. Lawsonibacter is a known butyrate producer33, and Gordonibacter is associated with ellagic acid metabolism34.

Group classification by alcohol consumption and comparison of gut microbiome profiles

Next, participants were classified into a high-consumption group (DH; n = 109) and a low-consumption group (DL; n = 120) according to their daily alcohol intake (g/day). The DH group showed markedly higher alcohol intake than the DL group (p < 0.001), and the proportion of smokers was also significantly higher (p < 0.001). Among the dietary variables, spicy (p = 0.034), high-fat (p = 0.014), and instant (p = 0.003) food consumption was higher in the DH group. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, BMI, stool frequency, Bristol stool score, abdominal pain, sleep quality, or other demographic and lifestyle variables (see Supplementary Information). Shotgun metagenome-based diversity analyses showed no distinct separation in alpha or beta diversity between the groups (PERMANOVA, p = 0.241; Supplementary Information).

MaAsLin2 analysis of species-level differences identified 18 microbial taxa that were significantly associated with alcohol consumption (Fig. 2D). In the DH group, Faecalimonas umbilicata, Amedibacillus dolichus, Lawsonibacter sp900545895, Evetepia excrementipullorum, Weissella koreensis, Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum, Fimisoma sp000435715, and Frisingicoccus caecimuris were significantly enriched. Species belonging to Lawsonibacter and Butyricicoccus are butyrate producers33,35, and species of Weissella are lactic acid bacteria originating from kimchi36. The presence of certain beneficial taxa in the DH group may reflect ecological selection under alcohol-induced stress conditions, wherein species capable of surviving or adapting to such environments persist or exhibit an increase in relative abundance37,38. The following species were relatively abundant: CAG-83 sp900548615, CAG-95 sp900066375, CAG-103 sp900543625, CAG-170 sp900553545, Blautia_A sp900066355, Alistipes_A ihumii, Ventricola sp900542395, Limivivens sp900543575, Lawsonibacter sp014287875, and Gemmiger sp900539695. Notably, Alistipes and Blautia are SCFA-producing and mucin-degrading, and they support the maintenance of mucosal homeostasis39. The distinction in I-FABP levels between the spicy food and alcohol intake groups further supports the notion that dietary factors may differentially influence intestinal epithelial injury (Fig. 2B). I-FABP and L-FABP levels did not differ significantly between the SH and SL groups. In contrast, I-FABP levels were significantly higher in the DH group than in the DL group (p = 0.004).

Comparison of epithelial injury markers and gut microbiome profiles across combined spicy food–alcohol intake groups

As spicy foods and alcohol are often co-ingested, it is necessary to evaluate their combined effects by examining interaction groups15,16. Based on the dietary questionnaire responses regarding the frequency of spicy food consumption and daily alcohol intake (g/day), the participants were classified into four groups: DLSL (Drink-Low-Spicy-Low; n = 101), DHSL (Drink-High-Spicy-Low; n = 88), DLSH (Drink-Low-Spicy-High; n = 19), and DHSH (Drink-High-Spicy-High; n = 21). Among the demographic and lifestyle characteristics, the sex distribution differed significantly across the groups (p = 0.001), whereas age, BMI, stool frequency, and Bristol Stool Scale scores did not show group-level differences (see Supplementary Information). Other lifestyle variables, including smoking status, physical activity, sleep quality, and hygiene awareness, did not differ significantly between the groups. Comparison of epithelial injury biomarker levels showed that I-FABP concentrations in the DHSL group were significantly higher than those in the DLSL group (p = 0.0271) (Fig. 3C). Analysis of shotgun metagenomic data revealed no distinct differences in the gut microbiome diversity among the four groups. Neither the Shannon index nor the PERMANOVA-based beta-diversity analysis indicated significant differences in taxonomic composition (PERMANOVA, p = 0.218; Supplementary Information).

Next, we examined the differences in the species-level abundances associated with the four groups using MaAsLin2. A total of 53 microbial species were significantly associated with at least one group (Fig. 3A). The DHSH group displayed an overall enrichment of Bacillota taxa, most notably multiple Blautia species, including Blautia_A sp900549015, Blautia hansenii, Blautia sp900541955, and Blautia sp001304935. Additional enriched taxa included Roseburia sp003470905, Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum, Agathobaculum sp900544475, Dialister invisus, and Faecalimonas phoceensis. In the DLSH group, a diverse set of Bacillota species, particularly members of the Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae families, such as RuminiclostridiumE siraeum, Anaerobutyricum soehngenii, Butyribacter intestini, Coprococcus eutactus_A, Coprococcus_A catus, Blautia stercoris, Dorea_A longicatena_B, and Lachnoclostridium_B sp900066555. Limisoma sp000437795 and Turicibacter sp001543345 were distinctly enriched, representing only the Muribaculaceae and Turicibacteraceae taxa observed among the groups. The DHSL group was characterized by the enrichment of Frisingicoccus caecimuris, Clostridium_Q sp000435655, and Angelakisella massiliensis, but, unlike other groups, did not show an increased abundance of Blautia species. The DLSL group showed higher levels of typical commensals, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, BlautiaA luti, Dysosmobacter sp900550685, and Lawsonibacter asaccharolyticus. The 53 associated species spanned multiple families within Bacillota and, to a lesser extent, Bacteroidota, demonstrating phylogenetically diverse responses to spicy food and alcohol intake. Blautia, Coprococcus, and Limisoma were consistently more abundant in the DHSH and DLSH groups (Fig. 3B). Additionally, we incorporated dietary variables associated with caloric intake into the linear regression model using MaAsLin2 to assess whether these associations were confounded by total energy intake. These variables included fast food, instant food, and fat consumption frequency, as well as preferences for vegetables over meat. The effect estimates from this extended model are highly correlated with those from the base model (r = 0.87; Supplementary Information). This consistency suggests that the observed associations remain unchanged after further adjustment.

Global metabolic pathways based on functional gene profiles

To explore the global metabolic potential of the gut microbiome, unstratified KEGG and MetaCyc pathway abundances were computed using HUMAnN 3.0, followed by MaAsLin2 analysis (Fig. 4). In total, 38 major pathways exhibited differential distributions among the four groups. Based on the MetaCyc ontology, these pathways were categorized as amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, cofactors, nucleotides, aromatic compounds, secondary metabolites, and energy metabolism.

Significantly enriched functional metabolic pathways of the gut microbiota. The bar plot shows 38 MetaCyc pathways that differ significantly among the groups. The x-axis represents -log10(P-value) derived from the MaAsLin2 model, and the color of each bar indicates the group with the corresponding enrichment.

The DHSH group was characterized by carbohydrate metabolic pathways, such as hexitol fermentation to lactate, formate, ethanol, and acetate (P461-PWY) and acetyl-CoA fermentation to butanoate II (PWY-5676), along with amino acid metabolic pathways, including l-isoleucine biosynthesis III (PWY-5103) and the super-pathway of l-serine and glycine biosynthesis I (SER-GLYSYN-PWY). The formaldehyde assimilation III (P185-PWY) pathway was also enriched in this group.

The DLSH group showed prominent enrichment of amino acid metabolism pathways such as L-methionine biosynthesis I (HOMOSER-METSYN-PWY) and L-alanine fermentation to propanoate and acetate (PROPFERM-PWY), and exhibited a broader diversity of pathways related to lipid, energy, and cofactor metabolism than the other groups. Notably, aromatic compound degradation pathways, including catechol degradation I (meta-cleavage pathway) (PWY-5415) and 4-hydroxyphenylacetate degradation (3-HYDROXYPHENYLACETATE-DEGRADATION-PWY), were detected.

The DHSL group displayed relatively few distinct features, with l-proline biosynthesis II (from arginine) (PWY-4981) emerging as the dominant amino acid pathway. The DLSL group showed a higher abundance of cofactor-related pathways, such as coenzyme A biosynthesis I (COA-PWY) and super-pathway of thiamine diphosphate biosynthesis I (THISYN-PWY), as well as nucleotide metabolism pathways, including inosine-5'-phosphate biosynthesis I (PWY-6123).

Comparison of SCFA-targeted gene family distributions among groups

To assess the activation status of SCFA-producing pathways, species-level stratified KEGG ortholog (KO) profiles were compared between the groups (Fig. 5A). SCFA-related genes were analyzed based on a previously established list of SCFA-producing-associated KOs40. Group-wise comparisons revealed that the DHSH group exhibited the highest overall abundance of gene families associated with acetate, propionate, and butyrate production (K00925, K01738, K16363, K01646, K19697, K01034, K01443, and K02535). The major contributing species were Anaerostipes hadrus, Blautia hansenii, Escherichia coli, and Fusobacterium mortiferum. Similarly, the DLSH group showed an elevated abundance of acetate-, propionate-, and butyrate-producing gene families (K01026, K00929, and K00138, respectively), with Blautia obeum, Blautia producta, and Klebsiella pneumoniae identified as key contributors. In contrast, the DLSL group displayed a high abundance of the acetate-producing KO K16363, associated with Bacteroides nordii, whereas the propionate- and butyrate-producing pathways were comparatively reduced. The DHSL group exhibited only a limited number of SCFA production-related genes, suggesting relatively weak activation of these metabolic pathways.

Comparison of mucin-degrading gene family distributions among groups

To investigate mucin degradation-related gene families, both KO and Glycoside Hydrolase (GH) family annotations were analyzed based on the glycan utilization gene set proposed by Labarthe et al. (2023)41. Distinct combinations of mucin-derived glycan degradation and downstream metabolic pathways were observed with respect to food and alcohol consumption (Fig. 4B). The DHSH group was enriched in gene families related to starch degradation (GH13), cellulose/β-glucan degradation (GH5), sulfate reduction (K00394, K00395, and K00956), and galactose metabolism (K00965 and K00849). Moreover, the characteristic enrichment of acetyl-CoA-derived SCFA-producing flux genes (K00625, K00925, K00656, K01034, and K00626) was observed, along with glycolytic enzymes (K01689, K01810, and K00927) and C1 metabolism–associated enzymes (K01491 and K00297). The major associated species were Anaerostipes hadrus and Fusobacterium mortiferum. Numerous glycoside hydrolase families were enriched in the DLSH group, including those involved in xylan degradation (GH10, GH120, GH30, and GH39), cellulose degradation (GH16 and GH26), and broad glycan hydrolysis (GH3), indicating a strong capacity for complex polysaccharide degradation. Additional enrichment of genes in the galactose-containing glycan degradation (GH35) and β-galactosidase/β-mannosidase families (GH2) was observed. Metabolic pathways associated with fructose, mannose, fucose (K01628, K01813, and K01818); sulfate assimilation (K00957); and butyrate production (K00929) were enriched. The dominant contributing species was Blautia producta, with additional contributions from Coprococcus eutactus and Fusobacterium mortiferum. The DHSL group exhibited characteristic enrichment in glycolytic pathways (K00134 and K00927), acetate formation (K00925), and sulfate assimilation (K00957). Within glycoside hydrolases, α-fucosidase families (GH29, GH95), broad glycosidase activity (GH3), and glucuronoxylan xylanohydrolase (GH30) were predominant. Furthermore, genes associated with the uronate pathway (xylulokinase and K00854) and C1 metabolism (methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase and K01491) were detected and mainly attributed to Parabacteroides distasonis. In contrast, the DLSL group showed enrichment of glycolytic enzymes such as enolase (K01689) and pyruvate kinase (K01810) related to Bacteroides nordii, but lacked mucin- or diet-derived glycan–degrading enzymes and downstream SCFA-producing genes.

Network analysis

Integrated network analysis revealed that host-related factors (alcohol, spiciness, I-FABP, and L-FABP) exhibited multiple correlations with key microbial species and metabolic gene clusters (Fig. 6). The consumption of spicy food was positively correlated with the abundance of Blautia producta and Fusobacterium mortiferum, which in turn was associated with genes related to SCFA synthesis (K00925, K00929, K01026, and K01034). The abundance of these species also positively correlated with mucin-degrading genes (K00965 and K00626), clustered with Blautia obeum, Coprococcus eutactus, Anaerostipes hadrus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli.

Conversely, alcohol consumption was positively correlated with the abundance of Parabacteroides distasonis and Bacteroides nordii and was associated with mucin degradation–related KOs (K00854 and K00134) and GH families (GH2, GH3, GH5, GH30, and GH95). These taxa formed a cluster with Blautia hansenii but showed relatively weak associations with SCFA-related gene clusters. At the metadata level, I-FABP positively correlated with L-FABP and alcohol intake, but not with spicy food intake. Spicy food intake was positively correlated with alcohol consumption, and these two factors were jointly associated with the abundance of Fusobacterium mortiferum.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the differential influences of spicy food and alcohol consumption on intestinal epithelial injury, dietary and lifestyle factors, and gut microbial composition in healthy adults. Among the epithelial injury markers, I-FABP significantly correlated with age, BMI, stool frequency, and sex, indicating that epithelial damage signals are strongly influenced by demographic and physiological factors (Fig. 1B). In contrast, the frequency of spicy food consumption was not associated with either I-FABP or L-FABP, and no association was observed between FABP-based injury markers and spicy sensitivity or pain response. These findings suggest that typical levels of spicy food consumption in healthy adults do not induce noticeable structural intestinal damage.

Interestingly, alcohol intake showed a clear positive correlation with I-FABP levels, which is consistent with previous reports linking alcohol to epithelial cytotoxicity, reduced mucus secretion, and weakened barrier function42,43. In contrast, L-FABP levels did not correlate with alcohol consumption. This suggests that alcohol consumption directly triggers the release of I-FABP, which is exclusively expressed in small intestinal enterocytes, thereby providing a more specific indication of alcohol-induced enterocyte disruption. As L-FABP is expressed across multiple organs, its association with alcohol may be less readily captured in a cross-sectional setting, potentially limiting its sensitivity to alcohol-specific epithelial disturbances11,44.

Urinary inflammatory cytokines and oral epithelial TRPV1 expression were assessed to characterize systemic inflammation and receptor expression45–47, and both were detected at low concentrations across samples (see Supplementary Information), which is consistent with the findings of previous reports that healthy adults have low basal cytokine levels and limited detectability46,48. This finding reinforces the idea that I-FABP was a relatively stable epithelial injury marker under the conditions of the present study.

In the shotgun metagenomic analyses, distinct species-level differences were observed when participants were grouped separately by spicy food or alcohol intake (Fig. 2A–B). In the high-spicy-food group (SH), increases were observed in Blautia, Limisoma, Ruminococcus, Butyribacter, and Butyricicoccus, which are taxa associated with SCFA production and mucin metabolism4,23–26. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that spicy food intake may selectively influence mucus layer-associated metabolic pathways, although the specific contribution of capsaicin to carrier food nutrients (e.g., dietary fiber in kimchi and fat in spicy stews) could not be determined from the study data4,13,15. Conversely, the increases in Fusobacterium mortiferum, Dialister, and species of Dorea, taxa with context-dependent or bidirectional health associations27–32, indicate that spicy food may act as a complex stimulus capable of eliciting both beneficial and adverse signals, which is in line with the findings of previous research9,10,21.

The microbial composition changes observed in the DH group differed markedly. Although some taxa typically considered beneficial, including Butyricicoccus, Lawsonibacter, and Weissella, were enriched33,35,36, this was in contrast with the findings in the DL group, which showed a greater abundance of SCFA-producing and mucin-degrading taxa, such as Alistipes and Blautia39. Notably, the levels of the epithelial injury marker I-FABP did not differ between the spicy food intake groups but were significantly elevated in the high alcohol intake group (p = 0.004), supporting that alcohol exerts a more direct effect on epithelial injury than spicy food. Thus, the presence of beneficial taxa in the DH group may reflect ecological persistence under alcohol-derived stress rather than improved gut function37,38. Taken together, our results indicate that spicy food intake induces variable and mixed shifts within metabolic and mucus-related microbial networks, whereas alcohol intake produces distinct alterations, particularly in the pathways linked to mucin metabolism. These findings suggest that spicy foods and alcohol influence the gut ecosystem via mechanistically divergent routes.

Analyses of the four combined spicy food–alcohol intake level groups (DLSL, DLSH, DHSL, and DHSH) revealed that the overall microbial diversity did not differ significantly across groups, and Bray–Curtis PCoA analyses showed no distinct clustering (see Supplementary Information). Nonetheless, functional pathways related to mucin metabolism and SCFA production were clearly differentiated according to spicy food intake, suggesting that although the global taxonomy remains stable, metabolic network configurations diverge more strongly when spicy food and alcohol are co-ingested (Fig. 3). The high-spicy food intake groups (DHSH and DLSH) were enriched in the genera Blautia, Roseburia, Coprococcus, and Limisoma, which are known to support combined fiber–mucin utilization and SCFA production4,23–25. Their presence aligns with the previously defined fiber–mucin coupling metabotypes 1–2, which represent homeostatic states sustaining both mucin turnover and epithelial energy supply41. This interpretation was reinforced by functional metagenomic analyses, which showed the differential activation of mucin-degrading pathways and SCFA-related gene families in the DHSH and DLSH groups (Fig. 4 and 5). These results support the hypothesis that spicy foods exert compensatory or modulatory effects on gut metabolic networks that are perturbed by alcohol exposure. Given that mucin turnover relies critically on cross-feeding among SCFA-producing bacteria49, the observed patterns suggest that spicy food intake, regardless of alcohol consumption, modulates the abundance of genes related to butyrate production, including buk (K00929) and bup (K01034) (Fig. 5B). In particular, the Blautia producta-associated butyrate kinase gene buk (K00929) was enriched in the DLSH group, whereas the Fusobacterium mortiferum-associated butyrate phosphotransferase gene bup (K01034) was enriched in the DHSH group. In the DHSH group, the enhanced carbohydrate metabolism (PWY-5676 and P461-PWY), sulfate reduction (K00394, K00395, and K00956), and glycosidase pathways (GH13–GH5 families), along with β-galactosidase (K01491) and galactokinase (K00965), suggest an active mucin degradation–SCFA production flux. Blautia hansenii and Anaerostipes hadrus appeared to sustain acetate–propionate flux (Fig. 5A–B), indicating active turnover of sulfo-mucin-derived sulfate, mucin glycan degradation, and SCFA production41. In contrast, the DHSL group was characterized by enrichment of Clostridium_Q species, which are H₂S producers, whereas the DHSH group contained H₂S consumers and SCFA producers, such as Anaerostipes hadrus (Fig. 3A, Fig. 5B). This implies a disrupted cross-feeding flux in DHSL, marked by H₂S accumulation and a reduction in H₂S-consuming taxa (Faecalibacterium, Roseburia, Anaerostipes), a condition linked to proinflammatory metabolic processes50. Network analysis consistently showed that alcohol-associated taxa, such as Parabacteroides and Bacteroides, formed mucin-degrading dominant networks with weak connectivity to the mucin–SCFA module (Fig. 6). These contrasting network structures (Fig. 6) indicate that spicy food may support mucin homeostasis via mucin–SCFA coupling, whereas alcohol promotes mucin degradation under reduced SCFA flux through cross-feeding loss and H₂S activation12,37,51. Collectively, these findings imply that the maintenance of mucin homeostasis by spicy food may serve as a determinant of resilience or disruption of gut metabolic health in the presence of alcohol exposure.

Nevertheless, the enrichment of Proteobacteria and Fusobacteria in the SCFA- and mucin-targeted gene families in the DHSH and DLSH groups (Fig. 5) warrants cautious interpretation. Fusobacterium mortiferum, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae are dysbiosis-associated taxa that can induce epithelial injury via endotoxin production and are linked to metabolic and inflammatory disorders27,52,53. Certain genes have been suggested to contribute indirectly to LPS biosynthesis, such as galK (K00849) and galT (K00965) 54,55, and incomplete reduction of H₂S-generating pathways (K00394, K00395, K00956, and K00957) by consumers in the human digestive tract may represent an additional risk factor56,57. Notably, Proteobacteria are key constituents of mucin profile 3, reflecting disrupted mucin homeostasis41.

In the DHSH group, the mucin- and SCFA-producing pathways were upregulated (Fig. 5B). Although an appropriate level of mucin turnover can strengthen the protective barrier, excessive degradation induces inflammatory injury, indicating the need for interpretation based on prior reviews49. The amino acid metabolic pathways enriched in the DLSH group, including methionine, arginine, ornithine, and alanine metabolism (Fig. 4), may represent terminal metabolic routes associated with mucin degradation41. However, hyperactivation of these pathways has also been linked to disturbances in host amino acid homeostasis, excessive production of H₂S and polyamines, and dysregulation of the serotonin–glucose axis, all of which may contribute to gut and metabolic diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease and insulin resistance58,59. Furthermore, acetate-generating and stratified Klebsiella pneumoniae, a species associated with the risk of fatty liver, may reflect a metabolically inflammatory transitional state60,61. These findings are consistent with previous reports that high concentrations of capsaicin can negatively affect the gut environment62; however, because this study did not include systematic quantification of capsaicin content in spicy foods, additional evidence is required. Nevertheless, given that the study population comprised healthy adults, epithelial injury markers showed alcohol-specific patterns, and inflammatory markers were detected at low levels, the spicy food intake observed here was likely within a range capable of maintaining mucin homeostasis. However, spicy food consumption may exert bidirectional modulation on mucus metabolism and microbial ecology, acting in either protective or detrimental directions depending on the consumption intensity.

This study has several limitations. The participants were healthy adults without clinical disease; thus, I-FABP fluctuations should be interpreted as physiological variations rather than acute inflammatory responses. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and classification based on consumption frequency rather than on quantitative intake limits the estimation of actual exposure. Additionally, because this was a remote study, blood and tissue analyses were not performed, and the mechanistic interpretations of mucin homeostasis relied on information from the literature rather than direct measurements. Differences in group sample sizes may also introduce statistical constraints; however, permutation-based tests and false discovery rate (FDR) corrections were applied to minimize potential bias.

A primary interpretive constraint is that spicy food consumption may result in heterogeneous outcomes in an observational study design. Korean spicy cuisine includes items with markedly different nutritional profiles, such as kimchi, spicy stews, and instant noodles. The dietary fiber, fat, and carbohydrate contents of these items can independently induce changes in the gut microbial function13,15,16. Consequently, the SCFA-producing taxa enriched in the SH group may partially reflect the nutritional components of the carrier foods rather than the spicy food compounds. Similarly, the co-occurrence of alcohol and spicy food intake in the DHSH group reflects the correlated dietary behaviors captured in the frequency questionnaire data and should not be interpreted as a nutrient-level interaction.

Sensitivity analyses with additional adjustments for the frequency of high-fat, instant, and fast food consumption, as well as preferences for vegetables over meat (used as surrogate markers for dietary caloric load and SCFA production), yielded effect estimates comparable to those of the primary model (see Supplementary Information). These findings indicated that the observed associations remained robust after accounting for energy-related factors and indirect indicators of SCFA production, such as preferences for complex carbohydrates and dietary fiber-rich foods. Nevertheless, future studies should incorporate detailed information on food ingredients and nutritional composition, including total dietary fiber and fat energy ratios, to determine the effects of spiciness.

Further, methodological considerations were applied to the analytical approach. The duration of habitual spicy food consumption and specific pungent compounds involved were not differentiated. Compounds such as capsaicin, allyl isothiocyanate, piperine, and gingerol possess distinct physicochemical properties and microbial targets that may yield divergent outcomes, even within the same intake level group5,6. Furthermore, taxonomic profiling was performed at the species level using MetaPhlAn4, which provides reliable species-level resolution but is not designed for strain-level inferences. Therefore, no strain-level conclusions could be drawn from these data. Although this study adopted read-based metagenomic profiling, assembly-based approaches such as metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) or gene-centric analyses can provide a deeper genomic resolution and should be integrated into future investigations63. Additionally, longitudinal designs and integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses using blood or tissue samples are required to confirm whether the observed microbial functional shifts translate into measurable epithelial damage signals or mucin regulatory responses, while accounting for individual variation and reproducibility.

Conclusions

In this study, we analyzed the effects of alcohol and spicy food consumption on intestinal epithelial injury, gut microbial composition, and metabolic pathways in healthy adults. Alcohol intake was associated with elevated I-FABP levels, reflecting microepithelial injury, whereas spicy food intake promoted mucin metabolism and SCFA-linked compensatory pathways, supporting mucus layer stability. However, enriched amino acid metabolism in the DLSH group represents a terminal mucin degradation process that may predispose individuals to metabolic inflammation. In addition, the concurrent enrichment of Proteobacteria and Fusobacteria in the DHSH group suggested a potential risk of dysbiosis through mucin degradation and endotoxin activation. Together, these findings suggest that spicy food exerts bidirectional regulation on gut mucus metabolism, maintaining moderate levels of mucin homeostasis but potentially aggravating mucus degradation and metabolic imbalance when combined with excessive alcohol consumption. Therefore, the intensity of spicy food intake and the level of alcohol consumption should be considered key determinants of mucin homeostasis and gut metabolic health.

Methods

Study population and design

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted between 2024 and 2025 and included 240 healthy Korean adults aged 19 years or older, recruited from Seoul and the surrounding metropolitan area. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy (KoNIBP) (IRB No. P01-202411-02-004) and registered with the Clinical Research Information Service of Korea (trial number KCT0010676). All participants were provided with a detailed explanation of the study objectives and procedures, and provided written informed consent prior to participation. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Healthy adults were specifically recruited to exclude potential disturbances in the gut microbiome caused by diseases or medications and to clearly evaluate functional variations associated with dietary factors in a physiologically stable population. The exclusion criteria included a history of clinically diagnosed disease, current medication use, and no prior consumption of Buldak hot chicken-flavored ramen (Samyang Foods Inc., Seoul, Korea), as this product was used as a benchmark for perception of spiciness. Eligibility screening was performed using self-report questionnaires and verification. To minimize sample degradation, recruitment was limited to participants residing within a 2 hour driving distance of the study site by driving.

Questionnaire assessment

To control for individual factors that could influence the gut microbiome composition, participants completed a structured questionnaire comprising 24 items grouped into three categories: demographic information (six items), health information (five items), and dietary lifestyle factors (13 items). The questionnaire developed for this study was partly based on published survey instruments used in previous microbiome and dietary studies involving the Korean population 14,17.

Demographic information included sex (male or female), age group (20s–60s and older), height (cm), weight (kg), and contact information. Health information encompassed disease diagnosis and medication status within the past three years (including gastrointestinal, hepatic, endocrine, cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and neurological disorders) using a four-tier scale (no diagnosis, past diagnosis, under treatment, and currently medicated). Participants were also asked about recent antibiotic use (within the past month) and intake of dietary supplements, such as vitamins or probiotics. Stool-related questions included bowel movement frequency (≤2 times per week to >1 time per day), stool form (Bristol Stool Scale, types 1–7), and the presence or absence of recurrent abdominal pain.

Dietary and lifestyle items encompassed spicy food preference (10-point extended Likert scale), frequency of spicy food consumption (from never to ≥5 times/week), intensity of post-consumption abdominal discomfort (visual analog scale, 0–10), onset of abdominal pain after consumption (from none to >1 d), and pain duration (from none to ≥2–3 days). Additional items assessed dietary tendencies such as meat/vegetable preference (1–10, lower scores indicating meat preference), consumption frequencies of high-fat, instant, or fast foods (from never to ≥5 times/week), alcohol consumption (from never to ≥5 times/week; drinking amount: 1 to ≥7 glasses per occasion), smoking (none to ≥1 pack/day), physical activity (0 to ≥5 times/week), and self-perceived health, mood, sleep quality, lifestyle regularity, and hygiene (5-point Likert scales).

To standardize the perception of spiciness across participants, Buldak hot chicken flavor ramen (Samyang Foods Inc., Seoul, Korea), a product with documented capsaicin content, was explicitly referenced in all related questionnaire items. This product was selected for two primary reasons: (1) its well-characterized and documented capsaicinoid content provides a consistent pungency anchor, and (2) it is one of the most widely consumed spicy instant food products in Korea, ensuring broad familiarity within the target population. In accordance with IRB recommendations, individuals with no prior consumption experience of this product were excluded.

Alcohol intake assessment

Daily alcohol intake (g/day) was calculated based on self-reported drinking frequency and the average amount of alcohol consumed per occasion obtained from the questionnaire. The calculations were standardized using soju, the most commonly consumed alcoholic beverage in Korea (16% alcohol by volume). The amount of pure ethanol contained in one glass sample (50 mL) was calculated as 50 mL × 0.16 × 0.789 g/mL [ethanol density], equivalent to 6.3 g of alcohol. Accordingly, the daily alcohol intake of each participant was calculated to be 6.3 g × (the average number of glasses consumed per day).

Drinking frequency reported on a weekly or monthly basis was converted to daily frequency by dividing by seven or 30 and multiplying by the average number of drinks per occasion to yield g/day. For nondrinkers, pseudo-counts were applied to prevent distortion by zero values during statistical analysis. This calculation method was adapted from the standardized procedures used in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES)64.

Group classification

For spicy food consumption, participants whose intake frequency exceeded the median value (once per week) were classified into the high-intake group (SH), and those at or below the median were assigned to the low-intake group (SL). Alcohol intake groups were defined using the same approach: Participants whose estimated alcohol consumption exceeded the median value (2.91 g/day) were placed into the high-intake group (DH), and those at or below the median as the low-intake group (DL).

The classification of spicy food and alcohol intake groups was based on the quartile distribution of each survey variable, with the median used as the cutoff. Participants whose values exceeded the median were placed into the high group, and those with corresponding values at or below the median were placed in the low group. Accordingly, participants were assigned to one of the following four groups: DLSL (Drink-Low–Spicy-Low), DHSL (Drink-High–Spicy-Low), DLSH (Drink-Low–Spicy-High), and DHSH (Drink-High–Spicy-High).

Sample collection and storage

The participants self-collected fecal (approximately 3 g), urine (approximately 25 mL), and oral epithelial cell (approximately 0.1 mL) samples using sterile collection kits provided by the study team. Sampling was performed at home or in the workplace, and specimens were immediately transported via rapid local delivery to the research facility. All samples were stored at −80°C until analysis to minimize degradation.

DNA extraction and shotgun metagenomic sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA quality and concentration were verified on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with 150-bp paired-end reads before shotgun metagenomic sequencing. The quality of the raw sequencing reads was initially assessed using FastQC to ensure overall read quality before downstream processing.

All steps for metagenome sequencing and preprocessing followed the bioBakery workflow (https://github.com/biobakery/biobakery/wiki/biobakery_workflows). Subsequent preprocessing was performed using KneadData (https://github.com/biobakery/kneaddata), which consisted of three main steps: (1) Quality trimming was performed using Trimmomatic, applying a sliding window approach to remove reads when four consecutive bases had Phred scores below 20. Reads shorter than 60 bp were excluded from the analysis. (2) Adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic. (3) Host-derived and common sequencing components were decontaminated using Bowtie2 by aligning reads against the human reference genome (hg38).

Taxonomic and functional profiling

Microbial taxonomic and functional profiles were generated using MetaPhlAn 4 (v4.1.1) and HUMAnN 3.6, respectively. Functional annotations included MetaCyc pathways and KOs. For taxonomic analysis, the species-level genome bin (SGBs)63 output by MetaPhlAn 4 was converted into a GTDB-based taxonomy using the sgb_to_gtdb_profile.py script provided by the MetaPhlAn 4 suite. Downstream analysis retained only taxa or functional features (MetaCyc pathway or KO) present at a minimum relative abundance of 0.005% in all samples. Alpha diversity was calculated using the Shannon index and phyloseq R package. For beta diversity, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated using the phyloseq R package, based on relative abundance data. PCoA was performed using the Bray–Curtis distance matrix. To test for class-level differences in community composition, PERMANOVA was conducted using 999 permutations, with sample class (DLSL, DLSH, DHSL, and DHSH) as the main factor, and BMI, sex, and age as covariates.

Linear mixed model analysis

To identify microbial taxa and functional features associated with metadata, linear mixed models were fitted using the MaAsLin2 R package. The fixed effects included the sample class, BMI, sex, and age. Only taxa and functional features (pathways or gene families) with a prevalence of at least 5% across all the samples were included. P-values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, implemented in MaAsLin2.

Microbial network and correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlations (for metadata) and Pearson’s correlations (for continuous microbial profiles) were computed to explore the associations between microbial species, functional features, and metadata. Microbial interaction networks were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.3).

Measurement of urinary biomarkers

Urinary samples were analyzed to quantify intestinal epithelial injury marker levels, including intestinal I-FABP and L-FABP, as well as inflammatory markers interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). Intestinal epithelial injury markers were measured using the Human I-FABP and L-FABP ELISA Kits (R&D Systems, MN, USA), and inflammatory markers were quantified using the Human IL-6 and TNF-α ELISA Kits (R&D Systems, MN, USA).

Urine samples were thawed gradually at 4°C overnight prior to analysis, and a single freeze–thaw cycle was performed to ensure consistency across all measurements. Before quantification, samples were centrifuged at 500 g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected to remove debris. All measurements and quantifications were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Analysis of TRPV1 expression in oral epithelial cells

TRPV1 expression in oral epithelial cells was measured by collecting tongue epithelial samples and quantifying TRPV1 and GAPDH levels using the Human TRPV1 ELISA Kit (Antibodies.com, Cambridge, UK) and the Human GAPDH ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA). The relative TRPV1 expression was calculated as the TRPV1-to-GAPDH ratio for intergroup comparisons.

The collected epithelial cells were dissolved in 1 mL of cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) containing protease inhibitors. Cell disruption and protein extraction were performed using a bead-beating grinder (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA) at 4 m/s for 20 s and repeated twice using Lysing Matrix E tubes. The samples were centrifuged at 500 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris, and all procedures were conducted on ice. TRPV1 and GAPDH levels were quantified using ELISA, according to the manufacturer’s protocols, and TRPV1 values were normalized to GAPDH levels to account for sample variability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (The R Project for Statistical Computing; v4.4.2; https://www.r-project.org) using RStudio (v2024.12.1.563). For multiple comparisons involving microbiome and functional gene analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Following the analytical threshold applied by Huang et al. (2024)65, an adjusted FDR < 0.25 was considered statistically significant for exploratory multi-omics association analyses.
