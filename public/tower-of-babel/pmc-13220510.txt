First dynamic endoscopic diagnosis of blind ileal stump intussusception after Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion
Authors: Iacopo Verbo, Mattia Angelo Bez, Danilo Di Giorgio, Alessandro Verbo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntussusception of the blind ileal stump is an extremely rare late complication of Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion. Diagnosis is challenging because symptoms may be intermittent and radiological imaging frequently inconclusive.Case presentationA 58-year-old woman presented several years after undergoing Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion with recurrent abdominal pain and vomiting. Repeated imaging showed no obstruction. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed intussusception of an elongated blind ileal stump prolapsing into the efferent limb. Surgical exploration confirmed a 12-cm elongated stump acting as the lead point. Case report

A 58-year-old woman presented with intermittent episodes of abdominal pain and vomiting several years after undergoing Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion. On examination, her abdomen was distended but soft, and laboratory findings were normal. Initial diagnostic studies, including abdominal X-ray and computed tomography, showed no evidence of bowel obstruction or a transition point. Despite these negative findings, her symptoms persisted.

Because of the episodic nature of the pain, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed. Endoscopic evaluation revealed a markedly elongated blind ileal stump that intermittently telescoped into the efferent limb, temporarily narrowing the lumen and reproducing the patient’s symptoms. The findings were consistent with blind ileal stump intussusception. Other potential causes, such as internal hernia, anastomotic stenosis, and adhesive small-bowel obstruction, were therefore considered less likely.

The original Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion had been performed via open approach several years earlier. Given the prior laparotomy and the anticipated presence of adhesions, an open exploratory procedure was selected to ensure safe evaluation of bowel viability and anastomotic configuration (Fig. 1).

Intraoperatively, a 12-cm elongated blind ileal stump was identified prolapsing into the efferent limb. The elongated stump was resected using linear staplers. Given the distortion and redundancy at the anastomotic level caused by the excessive stump length, the gastroileostomy was revised to restore appropriate anatomical alignment and eliminate the lead point.

At 12-month follow-up, she remained completely asymptomatic, with full resolution of her preoperative episodes of abdominal pain and vomiting (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Blind ileal stump intussusception after classical Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion is an exceptionally rare complication, with only isolated cases reported in the literature [1–8]. In previously published reports, diagnosis relied almost exclusively on computed tomography (CT), which typically demonstrated indirect or subtle signs of small-bowel telescoping [1–8]. To date, no case has described a dynamic, real-time endoscopic diagnosis in this specific anatomical setting. CT may be inconclusive in patients with intermittent or self-limiting symptoms, potentially leading to repeated hospital admissions and delayed definitive management [1–8].

In contrast to prior reports, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy in our case proved to be the decisive diagnostic tool. While CT remains essential to exclude other causes of obstruction, endoscopy enabled direct real-time visualization of the prolapse of the blind ileal stump into the efferent limb, correlating precisely with the patient’s episodic obstructive symptoms. Although dynamic endoscopic visualization of intussusception has been described in other bariatric contexts, particularly after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, this appears to be the first reported dynamic endoscopic diagnosis of blind ileal stump intussusception in the setting of classical Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion [1–8]. This distinction refines the novelty of our report while preserving its clinical relevance (Fig. 3).

A key predisposing factor identified both in the literature and in our case is the excessive length of the blind ileal stump. In the original description of the Scopinaro procedure, the blind stump is typically short, measuring approximately 2–4 cm [9]. In subsequent reports of intussusception following biliopancreatic diversion, an elongated stump or ileal segment has been implicated as the lead point for telescoping [1–6, 8]. In our patient, the stump measured 12 cm and clearly functioned as the lead point. Excessive stump length therefore represents a modifiable technical risk factor that should be minimized during the primary operation.

Importantly, the length and mobility of the blind ileal stump should not be regarded as static parameters. Several time-dependent mechanisms related to metabolic surgery may contribute to progressive changes in bowel behavior and help explain delayed presentation. Substantial postoperative weight loss is associated with progressive reduction of mesenteric fat, which may alter mesenteric tension and decrease bowel fixation, thereby increasing small-bowel mobility over time [10]. Increased mobility may allow an elongated blind stump to act as a lead point even if it was not initially considered excessive.

Additionally, hormonally mediated intestinal adaptation following biliopancreatic diversion may further contribute to this process. Elevated levels of trophic mediators such as glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) have been shown to promote mucosal hypertrophy and crypt cell proliferation after bariatric surgery, supporting a role in intestinal structural remodeling and functional adaptation [11]. These adaptive changes may progressively increase the effective bulk and motility of the blind stump, predisposing to intussusception months or years after the initial procedure.

The interplay between postoperative weight loss, mesenteric fat reduction, and hormonally driven intestinal adaptation provides a plausible pathophysiological framework for delayed symptom onset. Within this dynamic context, an elongated blind ileal stump should be considered not merely a technical remnant but a structure whose functional behavior may evolve over time. These considerations reinforce the importance of minimizing stump length during the primary operation and maintaining long-term clinical vigilance in symptomatic patients.

Across all reported cases, including ours, surgical management has consisted of resection of the lead point with or without revision of the anastomosis, resulting in favorable outcomes and prevention of recurrence [1–8]. This consistent therapeutic approach supports stump resection with anastomotic revision as the standard treatment once the diagnosis is established.

From a diagnostic standpoint, CT remains the initial investigation to exclude internal hernia and fixed mechanical obstruction in patients presenting with intermittent abdominal pain after Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion [1–8]. However, when imaging is negative or equivocal, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy may serve as a valuable complementary tool. Its ability to provide real-time visualization allows detection of dynamic and transient phenomena that may not be captured by static radiologic studies.

In summary, this case reinforces several key principles: Blind ileal stump intussusception, although rare, should be considered in patients with recurrent abdominal symptoms after Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion [1–8]; Excessive stump length is a modifiable technical risk factor that should be avoided at the time of the primary procedure [6–9, 8]; Endoscopy may provide direct and definitive diagnosis when imaging is inconclusive; Surgical resection of the elongated stump with anastomotic revision offers definitive treatment with excellent outcomes.

This report highlights the diagnostic value of dynamic endoscopic evaluation in a rare anatomical configuration and supports a more integrated role for endoscopy in selected patients with intermittent symptoms and nondiagnostic imaging after classical Scopinaro biliopancreatic diversion. However, as this is a single case report, the generalizability of these findings remains limited.
