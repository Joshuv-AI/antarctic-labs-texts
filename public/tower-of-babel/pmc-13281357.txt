Background

Celiac trunk thrombosis is an extremely rare but serious vascular emergency, accounting for less than 1% of all mesenteric vascular occlusions [1, 2]. Because the celiac trunk supplies the liver, spleen, stomach, and proximal duodenum, its occlusion can lead to multi-organ ischemia if left unrecognized [2]. This type of rare vascular event is particularly noteworthy in patients with conditions like ankylosing spondylitis (AS), a chronic autoimmune arthropathy affecting the axial skeleton [3]. Ankylosing spondylitis is associated with increased cardiovascular and thromboembolic risk due to systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and procoagulant changes [3]. Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors like adalimumab are well-established treatments for ankylosing spondylitis, and while they improve joint control and can prevent cardiovascular events, rare thrombotic events have been reported with their use [4]. The co-occurrence of celiac trunk thrombosis and ankylosing spondylitis under adalimumab has not been described in the literature. This highlights the importance of considering rare vascular complications in patients with autoimmune diseases receiving TNF inhibitors.

Case presentation

A 45‑year‑old male presented to the emergency department with acute epigastric pain starting during the night. The pain was constant, dull, and supraumbilical. He had multiple episodes of bilious vomiting but denied fever or urinary symptoms. Initial labs showed leukocytosis 15 × 103/μL and elevated C‑reactive protein (CRP) 20 mg/L, leading the referring physician to suspect appendicitis. His rheumatologic history included a misdiagnosis of rheumatoid arthritis, later reclassified in 2018 as human leukocyte antigen (HLA)‑B27–positive ankylosing spondylitis after bilateral sacroiliac ankylosis was confirmed on imaging. He underwent a biological aortic valve replacement in 2018 for severe insufficiency secondary to nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis, with normal coronaries documented. Anticoagulation management following valve replacement consisted of 6 months of warfarin therapy followed by lifelong aspirin therapy. No further anticoagulation work-up was done. Follow‑up echocardiography in 2020 showed left ventricular hypertrophy with preserved ejection fraction (67%) and normal prosthetic valve function. Current medications included aspirin and adalimumab (every four weeks subcutaneous injection; last dose one week prior). His AS was in clinical remission. He had no allergies, previous abdominal surgery, or thromboembolic history. He smoked about 10 cigarettes per day and consumed alcohol socially. On exam, vitals were stable. A systolic murmur was audible over the aortic valve area. Abdominal exam revealed diffuse tenderness, most prominent in the epigastrium, with mild guarding but no rebound or rigidity. Bowel sounds were preserved. Labs at presentation showed white blood cell count 16.2 × 103/µL and CRP 30 mg/L. ECG demonstrated sinus rhythm with left axis deviation, 1st‑degree AV block, and nonspecific repolarization changes. Contrast‑enhanced computed tomography (CT) with angiography. (Fig. 1a and b) revealed a 1.5 cm eccentric thrombus at the celiac trunk trifurcation, with subtotal occlusion of the splenic and common hepatic artery origins and severe stenosis of the left gastric artery. Multiple peripheral splenic infarcts were identified. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) showed increased metabolic activity in the artery wall of the truncus coeliacus (Fig. 1c and d) suggestive of active inflammatory changes.

a) Axial contrast-enhanced CT image showing an eccentric thrombus (arrow) at the celiac trunk trifurcation. b) Coronal reconstruction demonstrating subtotal occlusion of the splenic artery origin and consequent splenic infarctions (arrowheads). c) Fused PET-CT image revealing FDG uptake (arrow) in the wall of the celiac trunk, suggestive of active inflammatory change. d) Maximum intensity projection (MIP) PET image confirming localized inflammatory activity (arrow) at the site of thrombosis

The patient was managed conservatively with therapeutic anticoagulation (low‑molecular weight heparin transitioned to oral anticoagulation), supportive fluids, and analgesia. He subsequently developed hypertensive urgency requiring medical therapy with intravenous urapidil. This was interpreted as multifactorial, likely triggered by a pain-induced catecholamine surge combined with systemic inflammation and sympathetic activation. Renal artery involvement as reason for the hypertensive crisis could be excluded via CT. He recovered gradually and was discharged on long‑term anticoagulation with subcutaneous enoxaparin 80 mg twice per day.

Discussion

This case presents a rare manifestation of celiac trunk thrombosis in a patient with ankylosing spondylitis, highlighting several important clinical and pathophysiological considerations. The family physician’s initial misinterpretation of symptoms led to an incorrect suspicion of appendicitis, illustrating the diagnostic challenges inherent in this rare condition.

The association between inflammatory arthropathies and increased thrombotic risk is well-established in the literature [5, 6]. Chronic inflammation characteristic of ankylosing spondylitis leads to endothelial dysfunction, altered coagulation cascades, and increased platelet reactivity [3, 6]. In our case, PET-CT imaging demonstrated active inflammation of the celiac trunk, suggesting that vascular wall inflammation may have contributed to local thrombus formation.

The role of TNF-α inhibitors in cardiovascular and thrombotic events remains an area of ongoing debate. Recent studies have shown conflicting evidence regarding the cardiovascular effects of these agents. While some analyses suggest a potential association with elevated risk of specific cardiovascular events, particularly with early initiation [4], others demonstrate potential cardioprotective benefits through reduced systemic inflammation [7].

A recent pharmacovigilance analysis of the FAERS database (2004–2024) revealed that adalimumab was associated with the highest number of thrombotic adverse events among TNF-α blockers analyzed, with postpartum thrombosis displaying the strongest signal and deep vein thrombosis being most frequently reported [4]. Notably, celiac trunk thrombosis was not specifically reported in this dataset, underscoring the rarity of our case. The mechanisms underlying TNF-α blocker-associated thrombosis may include the formation of anti-adalimumab antibodies, which are thought to promote a hypercoagulable state [4]. In our patient, the disease was in clinical remission at presentation, and therefore anti-adalimumab antibody testing was not performed as it was not clinically indicated. Given the patient’s stable disease state, it remains unclear whether adalimumab, the underlying inflammatory disease, or other factors contributed to the thrombotic event.

From a clinical perspective, celiac trunk thrombosis typically presents with acute onset of severe epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and systemic signs of inflammation—all features observed in our patient [1, 2]. The nonspecific nature of this presentation can lead to diagnostic delays and potentially catastrophic outcomes, including bowel necrosis, multiorgan failure, and death [1, 2]. Splenic infarction, as occurred in this case, is a relatively uncommon diagnosis, with only 10% of cases diagnosed ante-mortem [1]. The main causes include thromboembolism, acute infection, and hematological diseases [8]. Early recognition through appropriate imaging, particularly contrast-enhanced CT angiography, is crucial for optimal patient outcomes.

Management of celiac trunk thrombosis depends on the extent of occlusion, presence of collateral circulation, and clinical stability [8]. Treatment options range from conservative management with anticoagulation and supportive care to interventional procedures, including thrombolysis, angioplasty, and surgical revascularization. In this case, the patient’s relatively stable clinical condition and presence of adequate collateral circulation supported the feasibility of conservative management [8].

The development of hypertensive urgency secondary to splenic infarction in this patient represents an uncommon complication of mesenteric vascular occlusion, and while the exact mechanism remains speculative, several factors may have contributed. The severe visceral pain could have activated segmental spinal reflexes through physiological neuronal connections, causing sympathetic nervous system activation with increased peripheral resistances, heart rate, and stroke volume [9], potentially triggering a catecholamine surge and leading to vasoconstriction. Additionally, systemic inflammatory mediators released during tissue ischemia might have contributed to endothelial dysfunction and blood pressure elevation [10]. No evidence of renal artery compromise was seen on imaging. However, it is important to acknowledge that the precise causality cannot be definitively established, and multiple interacting mechanisms likely played a role in precipitating this hypertensive emergency.

This case underscores the importance of maintaining high clinical suspicion for atypical presentations of vascular emergencies in patients with inflammatory conditions [2]. The combination of chronic inflammation, immunosuppressive therapy, and potentially prothrombotic states creates a unique risk profile that requires vigilant clinical assessment.

Conclusions

Celiac trunk thrombosis, though rare, should be considered in patients with inflammatory arthropathies presenting with acute abdominal pain. CT angiography is the diagnostic modality of choice. Conservative anticoagulation may be suitable in hemodynamically stable patients with adequate collateralization. Clinicians should remain aware of thrombotic complications in ankylosing spondylitis, particularly in patients receiving immunomodulatory therapy.
