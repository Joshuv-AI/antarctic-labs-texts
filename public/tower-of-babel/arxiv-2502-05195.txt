Using large language models for solving thermodynamic problems
Rébecca Loubet, Pascal Zittlau, Luisa Vollmer, Marco Hoffmann, Sophie Fellenz, Fabian Jirasek, Heike Leitte, Hans Hasse

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have made significant progress in reasoning, demonstrating their capability to generate human-like responses. This study analyzes the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs in the domain of thermodynamics. A benchmark of 22 thermodynamic problems to evaluate LLMs is presented that contains both simple and advanced problems. Five different LLMs are assessed: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o from OpenAI, Llama 3.1 from Meta, and le Chat from MistralAI. The answers of these LLMs were evaluated by trained human experts, following a methodology akin to the grading of academic exam responses. The scores and the consistency of the answers are discussed, together with the analytical skills of the LLMs. Both strengths and weaknesses of the LLMs become evident. They generally yield good results for the simple problems, but also limitations become clear: The LLMs do not provide consistent results, they often fail to fully comprehend the context and make wrong assumptions. Given the complexity and domain-specific nature of the problems, the statistical language modeling approach of the LLMs struggles with the accurate interpretation and the required reasoning. The present results highlight the need for more systematic integration of thermodynamic knowledge with LLMs, for example, by using knowledge-based methods.

Introduction

Thermodynamics is an essential part of both science and engineering, and it is key to understanding energy systems and mitigating climate change. It involves some of the most fundamental principles of physics, such as the first and second laws of thermodynamics, and its applications span almost every field of scientific inquiry. However, while the theoretical foundation of thermodynamics is well-established, applying this knowledge in practice presents a significant challenge. Solving practical thermodynamic problems often involves not only interpreting complex information but also understanding how to adapt and apply these theoretical physical principles to specific scenarios. Currently, most of this work has to be done by human experts, eventually assisted by computational tools, which are, for the most part, designed to solve domain-specific problems. For example, all process simulation tools used in the chemical industry contain hard-coded thermodynamic knowledge. Given the importance of thermodynamics, it is not surprising that its knowledge has also been included in ontologies that were developed for a variety of purposes

[

. This knowledge-based system selects the equations that describe a given problem from the ontology and then automatically and accurately solves them. KnowTD currently encompasses only a small part of the thermodynamic knowledge, but it is extendable and demonstrates the feasibility of knowledge-based approaches for flexibly solving thermodynamic problems. Another knowledge-based system is WolframAlpha, a computational engine based on the language Mathematica
[
10
]

that uses symbolic computation and real-time data processing for problem solving and can handle natural language input. It includes some thermodynamic knowledge but can handle only thermodynamic problems from narrow, well-defined classes. Its strength lies in solving the pertinent equations rather than in finding them.

A different approach to solving thermodynamic problems has become practically feasible only very recently: Thermodynamic problems can be given as prompts to large language models (LLMs) in the hope of obtaining a correct answer. This approach is explored in the present work.

LLMs are sophisticated computational models designed to process and generate human-like text
[
11
]
. They operate by converting text into smaller units called tokens, which are numerical representations of words, subwords, or characters. The models are based on a transformer architecture, which uses self-attention mechanisms to capture relationships and dependencies between tokens across an input sequence
[
12
]
. Through training on large datasets, these models learn to predict the likelihood of the next token given the context, enabling them to perform various tasks such as text generation, summarization, and question answering. Their performance is driven by two primary factors: the size of the training corpus, which influences the diversity of linguistic patterns they can learn, and the number of model parameters, which determine their capacity to encode and process complex relationships.

Our study complements and extends this in different ways: We focus on a single field, thermodynamics, which is studied in a systematic way. Within this field, we carry out tests with two problem sets of varying complexity and with different LLMs and compare the results. Furthermore, our problems were designed especially for this study, aiming to ensure clarity, consistency, and variety. The problems resemble textbook problems and are given entirely in text form. They are rigorously defined to ensure that each problem has a single, well-determined solution. We provide full access to the problems, solutions, and scoring system used in the Supporting Information, so that they can be reused in future studies, including, eventually, the training of LLMs. Without employing fine-tuning, we conducted preliminary tests using single-shot queries to identify the most effective prompting strategy. This process involved evaluating various general instructions and ways of presenting the problems. The optimal prompting strategy, determined through these tests, was consistently applied for the studies on which we report here. All prompts utilized in this research are disclosed herein. To assess the variance of the answers, each problem was given to each studied LLM three times in independent queries. The answers were then graded by trained domain experts based on a standard solution. Interestingly, problems from thermodynamics have been used very recently as test cases to study whether LLMs can be applied to assist in grading exams
[
21

The research questions we address in our work are:

1.

How good are LLMs at solving thermodynamic problems?

2.

How does the performance vary with the level of complexity of the problems?

3.

How do different state-of-the-art LLMs perform on the same test sets?

4.

What changes occur when going from one to the next version of the LLM?

5.

What is the variance in the answers when the same prompt is repeated?

6.

What types of errors occur?

The results of our study contribute to the important ongoing discussions of the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs within the scientific and engineering domains. Furthermore, we compare the problem-solving performance of LLMs with that of knowledge-based systems, specifically KnowTD
[

The prompts were generated consistently for all LLMs. The idea behind prompting was to think of the LLM as a student in an exam: A single-shot query approach was employed, where no feedback, intermediate guidance, or refinement was provided. Additionally, the LLMs were not fine-tuned prior to the problem-solving tasks. Each prompt was introduced with the following header:

"You are a student in a thermodynamics class. You are given problems and must find the solution. The answer should be formatted in the same way as the input to be readable in a standard text editor; do NOT use LaTeX encoding. Write formulas like this: p_1*v_1 = R*T_1. Write units like this: J/kg. Use a code generator."

Since le Chat and Llama 3.1 had the tendency to give long and verbose answers, the prompt was modified for these LLMs by adding:

"Please answer in a concise, scientific form."

Since the responses of LLMs to a given prompt are stochastic, i.e., the same prompt can yield different answers when submitted multiple times, each problem was presented three times to each of the studied LLMs. These repetitions were not part of the same dialogue and can, therefore, be considered to be independent; the LLMs did not retain information or learn from one repetition to the next. This approach represents a balance between the desire to increase the number of trials to enhance statistical reliability and the practical constraints of manually evaluating and correcting each response.

We have accessed the different models via APIs, except for Llama 3.1, which was downloaded and run locally on a GPU. Therefore, we were able to work with the largest available models at the time of the study, with the exception of Llama 3.1: While the largest model has
405\cdot 10^{9}
parameters, our work was conducted with the
70\cdot 10^{9}
parameter-model.

Several parameters control how LLMs generate their responses. In the following, we introduce the key parameters and report the values used for each LLM, which are the default values in all cases. The
temperature
parameter regulates the creativity and variability of the model’s outputs. Lower
temperature
values (i.e., below 1.0) lead to more deterministic and repetitive responses, while higher values (i.e., above 1.0) encourage greater creativity and randomness. For the models in this study, the
temperature
was set to 1.0 for GPT models, 0.8 for Llama 3.1, and 0.7 for le Chat.
Another key parameter is the
top_p
value, which influences the diversity and quality of the model’s responses by limiting the selection of tokens to those within a specified cumulative probability mass. A higher
top_p
value (e.g., 1.0) allows a broader range of tokens, increasing diversity, while lower values narrow the selection. For this study,
top_p
was set to 1.0 for both the GPT models and le Chat, and 0.9 for Llama 3.1.
The
presence_penalty
parameter encourages or discourages the introduction of new topics or tokens. Positive values penalize the introduction of new tokens based on their prior occurrence in the text, while negative values favor staying on previously discussed topics. The default setting for
presence_penalty
was 0 for all five LLMs.

Benchmark Problems and Evaluation

3.1
Overview

In this study, two sets of thermodynamic benchmark problems were used, which we will refer to as simple problems and advanced problems, respectively. Both sets include problems resembling those typically encountered in an introductory university course in engineering thermodynamics. Solving these problems requires the combination of different elements of thermodynamics. For a trained human, the simple problems are relatively straightforward – much easier than what we would require in an introductory course exam. The advanced problems approach the exam level but do not reach it.

All problems are reported in electronic form online

The problems were designed to be fully specified by text and have unique solutions. All tasks require the calculation of numerical values of variables. The set of simple problems used here is identical to the set of problems that we have recently used as examples to illustrate the application of KnowTD

3.2
Simple Problems

The 13 problems in this set are problems in which closed systems that contain an ideal gas undergo a single change of state from one state of equilibrium to another. The considered changes of state include different types of processes, including:

1.

Isothermal, isobaric, isochoric, polytropic

2.

Adiabatic, non-adiabatic

3.

Reversible, irreversible.

Heat and/or work can be supplied to the system. The problems in the set cover the feasible combinations of the main features, which makes this set different from many other test sets used to evaluate LLMs, in which the problems are basically drawn stochastically from a large pool. If all problems from the present set of simple problems can be solved, we may assume that any other well-posed problem from the considered class of problems can also be solved. Problem 1

This set contains nine problems that cover a wider range of thermodynamic knowledge than the first set. It includes processes with closed and open systems, multi-step processes (including cyclic processes), processes with liquids and gases, and processes with coupled systems. 3.4
Evaluation

The responses of the LLMs were evaluated by trained experts as if the LLMs had been given the problems in an academic exam. Since the task was always to calculate numerical values of certain variables, an option for the evaluation would have been to check whether or not this value was correct. While this has the advantage of being unambiguous (if numerical issues are left apart), it is not fair because a simple miscalculation could lead to a loss of all points despite a completely correct solution path. Furthermore, considering only the numerical correctness would introduce a bias in favor of LLMs that have the capability to use Python scripts for calculations, which only two out of the five studied LLMs can. Finally, in some cases, even a correct numerical value can be found for wrong reasons. Hence, we have decided to use the more tedious method to evaluate the correctness of the solution path, where partially correct solutions also increase the score. As a rule, in our evaluation, 0.5 points were awarded for each correctly executed step towards the final solution. We acknowledge that the scores obtained this way are not unambiguous. To reduce the variability, we have set up a master solution for each problem with suggestions for intermediate scores. However, this cannot entirely solve the issue of ambiguity, as there are usually many ways to obtain a certain solution. Comparing independent evaluations of the same answer by different experienced reviewers, we estimate the uncertainty of the scores to be typically +/- 0.5 points. A technical problem with this evaluation is that it can only be carried out by experienced personnel, that it requires cross-checking of the evaluation by different reviewers for non-trivial cases, and that it is time-consuming, which limits the scope of any empirical study like the one we have conducted.

As an example of our evaluation process, we provide in the Supporting Information a graded response for the two problems discussed in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
. These responses are accompanied by colored annotations, highlighting the awarded points (in green) and the identified errors (in red).

4.2
Error Types

To better understand the nature of the errors of the LLMs, we have classified them into six categories:

1.

Wrong assumptions
: The LLM makes an invalid assumption that is not aligned with the problem.

2.

Numerical errors
: The LLM makes mistakes in arithmetic operations, resulting in incorrect numerical answers.

3.

Inconsistent signs
: The LLM incorrectly applies positive and negative signs in calculations, leading to errors such as incorrect directions of heat flows or forces.

4.

Wrong equations
: The LLM selects or derives incorrect equations to solve the given problem, leading to false results.

5.

Context confusion
: The LLM fails to capture or retain the necessary context, leading to incorrect solution paths or a complete loss of direction.

6.

Verbose aberration
: The LLM gives verbose answers with excessive unnecessary details and thereby loses clarity and focus, ending up in being unable to produce any meaningful solution.

The errors were classified by the experts who corrected the answers. An overview of the classification of the errors observed in the benchmark tests is presented in Table
3
for the simple problems and in Table
4
for the advanced problems. The number count in these tables was determined as follows: For a problem with a maximum score of
N_{\text{max}}
points, if an LLM obtained
N
points, the difference
N_{\text{max}}-N
was divided into increments of 0.5 points. Each increment is referred to as a penalty, which was then assigned to one of the error classes listed above. In case of conflicts in the error class assignment, the class that was deemed to be dominant was chosen, or, when a given error led to more than one penalty, the penalties were split up between the pertinent classes. There is obviously ambiguity in this process and the numerical results should not be over-interpreted, but they still give an impression of how often a given type of error occurred.

"However, this result is incorrect because it doesn’t take into account the fact that the piston is compressed by an external force. In particular, GPT-4 and GPT-4o demonstrate this critical approach to their own responses. While they may initially start with incorrect assumptions, they frequently correct themselves to find the right solution path. The GPT-4o model, in particular, tends to stop when it reaches a point where it can no longer solve the problem rather than proceeding with incorrect assumptions. 5
Knowledge Based Systems for Thermodynamic Problem Solving

While LLMs have demonstrated significant potential for solving problems in diverse domains, the quality of their responses varies strongly, and they struggle in domains that require rigorous application of formal knowledge, such as thermodynamics.
The integration of LLMs and knowledge-based systems is a promising way to address these problems by exploiting the strengths of both approaches. LLMs excel at processing and interpreting large amounts of unstructured data, while knowledge-based systems offer structured, rule-driven approaches grounded in expert understanding. Combining the flexibility and adaptability of LLMs with the precision of knowledge-based methods is therefore highly attractive for solving complex scientific and engineering problems, ensuring both scientific rigor and interpretability in the solutions provided.

Before we discuss ways to intertwine both approaches in the field of thermodynamics, we first provide a brief overview of KnowTD, a knowledge-based system recently developed for solving thermodynamic problems, which will serve as our example.
KnowTD
[
9
]
is a specialized knowledge representation system designed to address thermodynamic problem-solving. It is built around an ontology that encodes thermodynamic theories, material properties, and process-related knowledge. This ontology is paired with a reasoner that utilizes the structured knowledge to define, set up, and solve user-specified problems. The ontology was meticulously curated by domain experts to ensure that it contains accurate and validated thermodynamic knowledge. It organizes thermodynamic theory into concepts, attributes, variables, and equations, along with their interconnections and dependencies. Additionally, it contains information on how these elements can be combined to represent given processes.

Currently, only simple problems can be solved by KnowTD, however, KnowTD is a modular system that can be expanded to include additional knowledge in the future. The reasoner in KnowTD first helps the user enter known and desired information, using the ontology to supplement the problem with related facts. It then constructs a knowledge graph containing all relevant variables and equations applicable to the specified problem and analyzes this graph, tracing a path from the given inputs to the target variables. This approach not only provides a numerical solution but also generates a transparent step-by-step solution path –- a guarantee for correctness.

A key feature of KnowTD is its ability to generate traceable and explainable solutions, making it valuable for both problem solving and educational purposes. The knowledge represented in the current version of KnowTD enables solving all problems from the simple test set presented in Section
3.2
. However, in its current form, KnowTD cannot solve the advanced problems from the second set presented in Section
3.3
– simply because they are outside the scope of the thermodynamic knowledge that is currently implemented in KnowTD.

The following section briefly discusses key research questions regarding the hybridization of LLMs and knowledge-based systems, using KnowTD as an example of the latter. There are two primary approaches to hybridizing these systems: First, using LLMs to enhance the performance of the knowledge-based system, and second, leveraging the knowledge-based system to improve the performance of the LLM.

Using LLMs to enhance the performance of KnowTD

The input to KnowTD is a problem posed in natural language, which must be translated into a machine-readable format by the user through a guided dialogue with the system. This task primarily requires text comprehension skills and basic thermodynamic knowledge to interpret the text. LLMs could facilitate this process by acting as an intermediary, converting the problem text into the structured input needed by KnowTD, with the ontology serving as the target "language." The problem text typically refers to real-world items and processes, while the ontology includes the theoretical elements necessary to construct the corresponding mathematical model. Each of the theoretical elements of the ontology may correspond to a wide range of items in the real world. For example, a closed system may represent a tank filled with biogas, liquid in a syringe, or gas in the cylinder of a compressor. LLMs could assist or even replace users in mapping these real-world items to the corresponding theoretical elements within the ontology.

Building on this idea, LLMs could also be used to translate the output of KnowTD into natural language. The results in Section
4.2
show that, although LLMs may introduce errors in their reasoning, they are still capable of providing clear and coherent textual explanations of their answers. By translating the graph-based solution path generated by KnowTD into natural language, LLMs could enhance the usability of KnowTD, making it more accessible and intuitive for users.

LLMs might also play a role in the reasoning process of KnowTD. Based on the ontology, KnowTD provides a set of valid equations that describe the problem. However, this set generally contains many more equations than needed to solve the problem. It is a difficult problem to reduce this large, over-defined set of nonlinear equations to a set of equations that enable the solution of the problem without containing redundant equations –- not to mention finding the set of equations that enables the most efficient solution. The pertinent equations and their associated variables can be represented as a graph. Currently, single-equation traversal is used in the reasoner of KnowTD to find a way through this graph that connects the given variables and the target values that are to be calculated
[
9
]
. This approach limits the scope of problems that can currently be solved. The results of the advanced benchmark set show that some of the LLMs are still able to handle this kind of complex calculations. Therefore, LLMs might be able to support KnowTD in this step of the reasoning process.

Using KnowTD to enhance the performance of LLMs

There are two primary approaches for using knowledge-based systems to enhance the performance of LLMs: (i) They could be used in the training or fine-tuning of the LLM, or (ii) they could be used for advanced prompt
engineering and for the verification of answers of a given LLM.

In the first approach (i), the ontology of KnowTD could be used for training or fine-tuning an LLM. Recent research demonstrates that the vocabulary, relationships, and reasoning strategies embedded in ontologies can be integrated into LLMs to enhance their performance
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
. However, further investigation is needed to understand how ontology-based fine-tuning can improve the analytical reasoning required for complex problem-solving tasks. Also, knowledge-based problem solvers such as KnowTD could be exploited to generate a vast amount of sample exercises and verified solutions, which can be provided as training data for the LLMs. However, while such measures may enhance their performance, they do not fundamentally alter the inherently statistical nature of these models.

Following the second approach (ii), knowledge-based systems could enhance language models by helping to identify, correct, or even prevent the errors discussed in Section
4.2
.
Errors such as inconsistent signs or the use of incorrect equations can be detected by cross-referencing the language model’s output with the validated equations stored in the KnowTD ontology.
Verification could be performed at varying levels. At a basic level, the ontology can be used to confirm whether or not the equations applied by the LLM exist within the knowledge base. At a more advanced level, the ontology can also be queried to check the context of the equations and whether they are allowed to be used in the scenario at hand. If problems are identified, the LLM could be prompted to revise its response, to specify where the error occurred and eventually to also specify additional information on the type and context of the error. In prompt engineering, KnowTD could, for instance, be used to provide background information and applicable equations.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have investigated the capabilities of current LLMs for solving thermodynamic problems. Two sets of benchmark problems were used, a simple and an advanced one. The benchmark problems were designed for the present study and are fully disclosed, as well as detailed information on the assessment of two of the answers of the LLMs, which was carried out by trained experts in the same way as the answers of students would have been evaluated. The variability of the answers of the LLMs – evaluated by running each problem three independent times – was found to be considerable. The standard deviation of the scores obtained in the repetitions was of the order of 1/3 to 1/2 of the mean score, underlining the statistical nature of the responses of the LLMs. The errors that occurred are of different nature, ranging from insufficient understanding of the problem over wrong assumptions to numerical problems. Not only the number of errors but also their nature varied strongly, depending on the type of problem (simple / advanced) and on the model. As expected, there was a clear deterioration going from the simple to the advanced problems in all cases. Interestingly, among the LLMs that were applied to the advanced problems (GPT-4, GPT-4o, Llama 3.1, le Chat), the differences between the scores were reduced compared to the simple problems. Not a single LLM clearly stands out, as measured by the scores: In all cases, the differences between the mean scores of the models were smaller than the standard deviation of the repetitions. Some of the observed differences in performance may result from random variability and not necessarily indicate differences in capability. However, a clear improvement was observed going from GPT-3.5, which was tested only on the simple problems, to the more recent versions of GPT models. The development of LLMs is rapid, new versions can perform very differently from older ones.

The LLMs rarely completely failed, even on the advanced problems, and for some of the simple problems, some of the LLMs consistently gave correct answers, which is remarkable. These results suggest that thermodynamic knowledge was included in the training data of all the studied LLMs. However, overall, the results highlight a critical limitation: The lack of analytical skills significantly restricts the use of LLMs for solving complex problems in science and engineering, a verification of their results is always necessary. The benchmark problems we have provided enable tracking future developments of LLMs and also devising and evaluating advanced prompting strategies. We question whether relying on general-purpose LLMs alone will be sufficient for reliably solving problems from science and engineering. The option of providing the LLMs with additional input, e.g., text from teaching materials, and evaluating their performance on the same benchmark problems is interesting to explore. Another solution could be to combine the LLMs with knowledge-based systems to take advantage of the strengths of both approaches. We have recently developed a prototype of such a knowledge-based system for thermodynamics
[
9
]
and discuss options for the hybridization of that system with LLMs. We view LLMs and knowledge-based systems as complementary partners rather than competitors.
