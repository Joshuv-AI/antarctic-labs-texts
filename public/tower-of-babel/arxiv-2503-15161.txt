UltraFlwr - An Efficient Federated Medical and Surgical Object Detection Framework
Yang Li †, Soumya Snigdha Kundu †, Maxence Boels, Toktam Mahmoodi, Sebastien Ourselin, Tom Vercauteren, Prokar Dasgupta, Jonathan Shapey, Alejandro Granados

Abstract
Object detection shows promise for medical and surgical applications such as cell counting and tool tracking. However, its faces multiple real-world edge deployment challenges including limited high-quality annotated data, data sharing restrictions, and computational constraints. In this work, we introduce UltraFlwr , a framework for federated medical and surgical object detection. By leveraging Federated Learning (FL), UltraFlwr enables decentralized model training across multiple sites without sharing raw data. To further enhance UltraFlwr’s efficiency, we propose YOLO-PA , a set of novel Partial Aggregation (PA) strategies specifically designed for YOLO models in FL. YOLO-PA significantly reduces communication overhead by up to 83% per round while maintaining performance comparable to Full Aggregation (FA) strategies. Our extensive experiments on BCCD and m2cai16-tool-locations datasets demonstrate that YOLO-PA not only provides better client models compared to client-wise centralized training and FA strategies, but also facilitates efficient training and deployment across resource-constrained edge devices. Further, we also establish one of the first benchmarks in federated medical and surgical object detection. This paper advances the feasibility of training and deploying detection models on the edge, making federated object detection more practical for time-critical and resource-constrained medical and surgical applications. UltraFlwr is publicly available at https://github.com/KCL-BMEIS/UltraFlwr .

While object detection models hold great promise, they face the general challenges of medical image analysis, which are the scarcity of high-quality annotated data, heterogeneous data sources, regulatory restrictions for medical and surgical data sharing, and computational constraints.
High-performing models require extensive datasets that cover diverse feature spaces to ensure generalization. Collaborative learning approaches, such as Federated Learning (FL), offer effective solutions to these challenges by enabling the training of models in a decentralized fashion

Classification tasks often dominate the FL research landscape in comparison to object detection. We hypothesize that this is mainly owed to state-of-the-art object detection models

Additionally, there is significantly more community support and established tools for building and deploying classification models in FL, further contributing to the focus on classification in FL research. Lastly, images in classification datasets only have one class label per image, whereas in detection datasets, each image can have multiple instances of different classes, making stratified partitioning more challenging.

In parallel, despite the growing attention to FL in medical image analysis, its application in surgical data science is still nascent. Existing research has primarily addressed tasks such as surgical instrument segmentation

Real-time object detection, especially during surgery, demands low-latency processing to ensure timely and accurate decision-making in critical scenarios.

Edge computing has emerged as a vital paradigm to meet these requirements, offering localized processing capabilities that make it particularly well-suited for both medical and surgical applications

Methods

2.1
YOLO Object Detection Model

Figure 1:
Overview of Flower and Ultralytics YOLO Integration.

At its core, the YOLO architecture consists of three fundamental components (Figure
1
). First, the backbone serves as the primary
feature extractor, utilizing convolutional neural networks to transform raw image data into multi-scale feature maps.
Second, the neck component acts as an intermediate processing stage, employing specialized layers to aggregate
and enhance feature representations across different scales. Third, the head component functions as the prediction
mechanism, generating the final outputs for object localization and classification based on the refined feature maps.

The base model utilized for all experiments is the YOLOv11n. We opted for YOLOv11 as it is the latest and best-performing YOLO model (as of date). Moreover, the YOLOv11n variant is aligned with our goals toward edge deployment since it is the smallest model in the YOLO family.

We hypothesized that selectively updating weights for specific components would be sufficient, given that YOLOv11n models undergo extensive pre-training. Hence, we opted to create our PA strategy based on selecting which YOLO components would participate in the FL process.
We investigated two distinct PA strategies specific to YOLO models. In our first approach, we performed aggregation on individual YOLO components, where we exclusively updated either the Backbone (FedBackbone), Neck (FedNeck), or Head (FedHead). Our second approach explored dual-component aggregation, where we combined updates for any two components simultaneously. We termed these strategies FedBackboneHead (Backbone and Head aggregation), FedBackboneNeck (Backbone and Neck aggregation), and FedNeckHead (Neck and Head aggregation).

These two sets of strategies are readily compatible with existing aggregation strategies, such as the widely used FedAvg
[
17
]
and FedMedian
[
25
]
. FedAvg aggregates model weights across clients by taking their arithmetic mean, while FedMedian takes the element-wise median of the weights. We focus our experiments on them where we add the terms as suffixes, i.e. FedHeadAvg or FedBackboneNeckMedian.
Our theoretical analysis demonstrates significant parameter reduction when operating with YOLO-PA.
For maximum overhead reduction, working solely with the head component achieves a reduction of 82.27% in total parameters (Table
1
). The overall parameters reduce further when larger variants of YOLO are opted.

2.3
UltraFlwr Framework

To set up the federated environment, we adapted the Flower framework
[
4
]
. Our proposed framework, UltraFlwr combines the Ultralytics and Flower framework, as depicted in Figure
1
. Ultralytics provides intuitive, high-level Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) for training computationally optimized YOLO models, which are available in multiple pre-trained scales. The Flower framework complements this setup by standardizing FL research while maintaining intuitive usability.

3
Experimental Design

Datasets

UltraFlwr is evaluated on two key datasets. BCCD
[
22
]
, a three-class dataset (white blood cells, red blood cells, and Platelets) of blood cell photos. There are 4888 labels across 3 classes (and 0 null examples). The other dataset is m2cai
[
11
]
, which contains spatial tool annotations for 2,532 frames across the first 10 videos in the m2cai16-tool dataset. The dataset consists of 3,141 annotations of 7 surgical instrument classes, with an average of 1.2 labels per frame and 7 instrument classes per video. Each dataset is randomly shuffled and split into equally sized partitions for the clients.

Training

For federated training, we use a batch size of 8 and conduct 20 rounds of training, where one round of training consists of each client training for 20 epochs, and the weights are aggregated to the server model. The YOLOv11n model is configured with the default hyperparameters provided by Ultralytics. UltraFlwr is designed to support any number of clients. We chose to evaluate our framework using 3 clients, each assigned a distinct data partition (
\mathcal{C}_{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\mathcal{C}_{3}
) derived from a global dataset
\mathcal{S}
.
Correspondingly, 3 local models (
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{3}
) are trained independently and subsequently aggregated into a global server model
\theta
.

To compare with non-federated training and showcase the importance of having access to model weights trained across other clients, we also conduct experiments using normal, non-federated training (
normal train
) on client data partitions, where locally trained models are denoted as
{\phi}_{1}
,
{\phi}_{2}
and
{\phi}_{3}
. Here, the models are trained solely on the client partition data with no weights from models trained on other clients. We also do normal training on the global dataset, yielding a model
{\phi}
. For consistency, we train each model for up to 400 epochs with an early stopping criterion set at 100 epochs, using a batch size of 8.

Ablation Studies

To ensure a robust ablation study, we systematically compare all possible combinations of YOLO-PA under both FedAvg and FedMedian schemes.
Additionally, to test the scalability and convergence efficiency of YOLO-PA, we set up separate experiments, increasing the number of clients to 15. We specifically investigate the best two YOLO-PA strategies’ model convergence during training compared to FedAvg and FedMedian.

Inference

For FA, we conduct inference based on the server model (
\theta^{FA}
) and client models (
\theta^{FA}_{1}
,
\theta^{FA}_{2}
and
\theta^{FA}_{3}
) on client partitioned test sets. We do not maintain a global test set which is common across clients as it is assumed this scenario is infeasible. For PA, we only report the inference of client models (
\theta^{PA}_{1}
,
\theta^{PA}_{2}
and
\theta^{PA}_{3}
) on client partitioned test sets. This is because the server model needs fine-tuning in order to make a reasonable inference, as component(s) in the server model remain frozen in the PA setting. Like PA, we perform similar inference for the models (
{\phi}
,
{\phi}_{1}
,
{\phi}_{2}
and
{\phi}_{3}
) based on normal training.

We evaluate object detection performance
1
using the widely accepted mean Average Precision (mAP) metric.
We define mAP formally as:

mAP=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}AP_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\int_{0}^{1}p_{i}(r)\,dr,

(1)

where
p_{i}(r)
denotes the precision as a function of recall for the
i

M
is the total number of categories. All scores are categorized under mAP50.

5
Discussion

Ultraflwr is a pilot framework that offers real-time federated object detection. Furthermore, as we built Ultraflwr to be scalable, it was a priority to reduce communication overheads.

Introducing YOLO-PA was a natural next step, especially with the experimental results highlighting comparable perform to full aggregration strategies.
Interestingly, more communication-efficient strategies, such as FedHeadMedian, do not necessarily underperform compared to less communication-efficient approaches like FedBackboneMedian. The results further highlight a key advantage of federated learning: harnessing diverse, otherwise inaccessible datasets to enhance model performance beyond the constraints of local training.

Most mainstream object detection architectures adopt a structured design comprising a backbone, neck, and head
[
6
]
. UltraFlwr and YOLO-PA can be seamlessly integrated into any of these architectures. This flexibility has paved the way for us to establish one of the first benchmarks in federated medical and surgical object detection.
Factoring in our results across multiple aggregation strategies, the proposal of PA strategies to reduce overheads, and building a simple-to-use framework, future research can easily extend UltraFlwr to develop federated object detection models. An initial direction could be either to enhance YOLO-PA with improved strategies or explore the performance of alternative detection architectures in a federated setting.

While this work highlights multiple important advancements, it also poses limitations. First, the lack of asynchronous FL support in Flower constrained our ability to simulate intermittent client participation or simulate a larger number of clients. Future integration of asynchronous capabilities could address this gap. Second, scalability testing was not performed on edge devices due to limited number of available edge devices. While we tested on platforms like Raspberry Pi and Nvidia Jetson, evaluating large-scale edge-device deployments in diverse environments remains an open challenge. Lastly, we plan to make Ultraflwr completely privacy preserving by integrating differential privacy into the pipeline in the future
[
16
]
.

6
Conclusion

This work represents one of the first steps toward communication efficient real-world deployment of federated medical and surgical object detection on edge devices.
Our results demonstrate the potential of FL and PA for decentralized, collaborative training in medical and surgical applications.
Additionally, we introduce the first publicly available benchmark for federated object detection, providing a foundation for standardized evaluation and future advancements.
