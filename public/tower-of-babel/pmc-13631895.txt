Dietary halloysite supplementation in Orvia ducks: effects on production performance, meat quality, blood biochemistry, muscle histological and molecular traits
Authors: Sebastian Wlaźlak, Aleksandra Dunisławska, Elżbieta Pietrzak, Olha Yatsenko, Anna Tuśnio, Marcin Barszcz, Ewa Święch, Kamil Gawin, Adrianna Konopka, Marek Adamski, Jakub Biesek, Mirosław Banaszak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The study evaluated the effects of dietary halloysite supplementation on growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, blood biochemical parameters, intestinal microbiota, muscle histology and selected gene expression in Orvia ducks, with consideration of sex-dependent responses. A total of 200 one-day-old Orvia ducks were allocated to control and halloysite-supplemented groups, with males and females analyzed separately. Birds in the experimental groups received diets supplemented with 1% halloysite throughout a 42-day rearing period. Growth performance, carcass composition, physicochemical and chemical traits of pectoral and leg muscles, post-mortem blood biochemical parameters, bacterial populations in intestinal segments, histological traits of the pectoral muscle and relative expression of selected metabolic genes were assessed. Halloysite negatively affected body weight and body weight gain during the first rearing period, but final body weight and total body weight gain were not significantly changed. Feed conversion improved during the later growth phase. Halloysite modified selected meat quality and chemical composition traits, including protein, intramuscular fat, water content, redness and water-holding capacity. Treated birds showed higher total protein and lower uric acid and urea concentrations, while liver enzymes and systemic lipid profile remained unchanged. No statistically significant differences were found in the analyzed bacterial populations or muscle histological traits. Gene expression changes were mainly limited to FST and CEBPB, treatment- and sex-associated variation in the transcript abundance of genes related to muscle growth and metabolic regulation. Dietary supplementation with 1% halloysite induced selective metabolic and molecular responses without impairing final growth performance or muscle microstructure. The observed sex-dependent effects indicate that sex should be considered when assessing halloysite supplementation in broiler duck production.

Introduction

Broiler duck production requires nutritional strategies that maintain high growth performance, efficient feed utilization, high slaughter yield, and appropriate meat quality. This is particularly important in fast-growing commercial lines, where a short rearing period, intensive growth, and high feed intake may affect not only body weight and feed conversion ratio, but also carcass composition, metabolism, and the properties of muscle tissue (Zeng et al., 2015; Kokoszyński et al., 2019; Ao and Kim, 2020).

Natural mineral additives, including aluminosilicates, have attracted interest in poultry nutrition due to their sorption properties, water-binding capacity, and potential interactions with selected compounds in the gastrointestinal tract. These minerals are used, among others, as technological feed components and adsorbents of undesirable substances; however, their effects may extend beyond the simple function of a physical carrier or feed additive (Saleh et al., 2024; Burton et al., 2025). They may influence the gastrointestinal environment, nutrient availability, digestive processes, and selected physiological indicators in birds. Studies on broiler chickens have shown that dietary supplementation with zeolite or halloysite may affect growth performance, carcass traits, meat quality, and jejunal structure, indicating that aluminosilicates should not be regarded as completely biologically inert feed additives (Biesek et al., 2021a; Reszka et al., 2026).

One such additive is halloysite, a natural clay mineral characterized by a specific tubular structure, well-developed specific surface area, and high adsorptive properties. These characteristics may be relevant in animal nutrition (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2021). Different doses of zeolite (1, 1.5 and 2%) in broiler chicken feed have been shown to positively affect body weight. It may effectively reduce Salmonella bacteria in the intestines (Al-Nasser et al., 2011). Nikolakakis et al. (2013) also confirmed that chickens achieved higher body weight gains and a lower feed conversion ratio (FCR). Zeolite contributes also to better digestibility of selected nutrients and more intensive secretion of digestive enzymes (Wawrzyniak et al., 2017; Pavlak et al., 2023). Chung and Choi (2019) observed no significant changes in the growth performance of ducks fed a 2% addition of bentonite and illite. According to Biesek et al. (2021b), a 4% zeolite in the feed reduced body weight and increased FCR in six-week-old broiler ducks. On the other hand, previous studies have shown that a lower level of zeolite (1%) may be recommended due to the higher weight of pectoral muscles and higher protein content in the leg muscles of male Pekin ducks (Wlaźlak et al., 2024).

The inclusion level of halloysite was selected based on previous research on aluminosilicates in poultry nutrition. In ducks, Biesek et al. (2021b) evaluated a relatively high inclusion level of 4% zeolite in Orvia and Cherry Valley ducks and reported reduced body weight gain and impaired feed conversion, concluding that further studies should investigate lower inclusion levels. Therefore, a substantially lower level of 1% was selected in the present study. This level was also supported by previous studies using 1% halloysite in poultry diets, including Cherry Valley ducks (Wlaźlak et al., 2025) and Ross 308 broiler chickens (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2023). Given the limited information on the dose-response relationship of halloysite in ducks, the present study was intended to further evaluate the biological effects of this inclusion level and provide a basis for future studies using a wider range of doses.

The evaluation of the effects of halloysite in duck nutrition should include not only growth performance and carcass traits, but also metabolic indicators and muscle tissue characteristics. Halloysite, as a natural aluminosilicate with sorptive properties, may affect the gastrointestinal environment, nutrient availability, and metabolic processes, potentially reflecting these changes in the blood biochemical profile (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2021, 2023). Parameters such as total protein, uric acid, urea, lipids, minerals, and enzyme activities may indicate whether the applied additive influences protein and energy metabolism, internal organ function, and the overall homeostasis of birds (Zálešáková et al., 2025).

The histological structure of the pectoral muscle and its gene expression provide important support for conventional muscle quality assessment, as they allow a better explanation of the biological basis of changes observed in physicochemical traits and the chemical composition of muscle tissue. Muscle fiber characteristics, such as cross-sectional area, diameter, and density, are associated with muscle growth and may influence pH, color, water-holding capacity, and the structure of poultry meat. At the same time, gene expression in the pectoral muscle reflects the activity of pathways related to myogenesis, energy metabolism, protein and lipid turnover, and extracellular matrix remodeling, all of which determine muscle development and meat quality. Changes in dietary supplementation may modify gene expression in the pectoral muscle and affect pathways associated with muscle development and meat quality (Petracci et al., 2013; Ismail and Joo, 2017; Liu et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2021). Therefore, histological assessment and gene expression analysis in the pectoral muscle may help determine whether halloysite supplementation induces tissue-level and molecular responses in broiler ducks.

Despite the available literature on the use of aluminosilicates in poultry nutrition, knowledge regarding the effects of halloysite in broiler ducks, particularly Orvia line, remains limited. Moreover, there is a lack of information from studies in which growth performance, carcass traits, quality and chemical composition of pectoral and leg muscles, blood biochemical parameters, histological characteristics, relative abundance of indicator intestinal bacteria and relative gene expression in the pectoral muscle were evaluated simultaneously. Such a comprehensive analytical approach makes it possible to determine whether the response of birds to halloysite supplementation is general in nature or limited only to selected parameters. Additionally, considering sex and the interaction between sex and supplementation enables a more precise assessment of biological variability in the response of ducks to this feed additive. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of dietary supplementation with 1% halloysite on growth performance, carcass characteristics, physicochemical parameters and chemical composition of pectoral and leg muscles, as well as blood biochemical parameters, histological traits, and relative gene expression in the pectoral muscle of broiler ducks, with consideration of sex and the interaction between these two factors.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted in accordance with applicable animal welfare regulations, including Directive 2010/63/EU of 22 September 2010 and the Polish Act of 15 January 2015 on the protection of animals used for scientific or educational purposes. The research involved only routine husbandry and breeding practices performed under standard agricultural and zootechnical conditions. Therefore, no procedures as defined by these regulations were carried out. Animals were not subjected to interventions causing pain, suffering, distress, or lasting harm. No experimental procedures were conducted on live animals, and biological material was collected post mortem according to the Council Regulation (EC) No 1099/2009 of 24 September 2009 on the protection of animals at the time of killing. The number and form of the kept birds are controlled and reported to the Animal Welfare Team of the Faculty of Animal Breeding and Biology in annual reports.

Animals and experimental design

A total of 200 one-day-old Orvia commercial hybrid ducklings obtained from a commercial waterfowl hatchery in Poland (ANAS, Ruchenna, Poland) were used in the experiment. On day 1, the mean body weight of the birds was 54.37 ± 6.027 g. The flock comprised 100 females and 100 males, which were allocated to two equal experimental groups with sex deviation: control males (C1), control females (C2), halloysite-supplemented males (H1), and halloysite-supplemented females (H2). Each group consisted of five replicate pens, with 10 ducks per pen. The birds were housed in pens with an area of 2 m², and stocking density did not exceed 17 kg/m². Chopped wheat straw was used as bedding. Each pen was equipped with two nipple drinkers. On the first day of rearing, the temperature in the duck house was maintained at 28°C and was gradually reduced to 18°C by the fourth week of life. An additional heat source, a heating lamp, maintained a local temperature of 30°C. Relative humidity was kept at 60–70%, and air velocity did not exceed 0.2–0.3 m/s.

The rearing period lasted 42 days and was divided into two feeding phases: a starter phase from day 1 to day 28 and a grower phase from day 29 to day 42. Birds in all groups were fed commercial diets formulated for broiler ducks, balanced according to poultry nutrition recommendations (Smulikowska and Rutkowski, 2018). Ducks in the experimental groups received the same basal diets supplemented with 1% halloysite throughout the entire rearing period. The starter and grower diets were thoroughly mixed with halloysite to ensure its uniform distribution. Halloysite was obtained from a mine in southern Poland (Krotoszyce, Poland). The halloysite used in the study had a powdered structure, a specific surface area ranging from 65 to 86 m²/g and a bulk density of 0.70–0.85 g/cm³. Its chemical composition was as follows: aluminum, 18.76%; silicon, 16.62%; oxygen, 45.15%; iron, 14.20%; titanium, 1.96%; calcium, 0.74%; potassium, 0.68%; sodium, 0.54%; other, 1.35%.

The commercial starter diet contained maize, wheat, soybean extraction meal, wheat bran, sunflower extraction meal, hulled sunflower seeds, barley, rapeseed extraction meal, wheat gluten feed, calcium carbonate, animal fat, monocalcium phosphate, vegetable oil and fat from raw sunflower, sodium chloride and sodium sulphate. According to the manufacturer’s declaration, its nutrient and vitamin composition was as follows: crude protein, 19.5%; ether extract, 3.9%; crude fibre, 4.2%; lysine, 0.93%; methionine, 0.42%; threonine, 0.72%; calcium, 0.85%; phosphorus, 0.69%; sodium, 0.17%; vitamin A, 10,000 IU; vitamin D₃, 3,000 IU; and vitamin E, 25 IU. The commercial grower diet contained maize, wheat, wheat bran, soybean extraction meal, sunflower extraction meal from dehulled sunflower seeds, triticale, rapeseed extraction meal, animal fat, calcium carbonate, monocalcium phosphate, sodium chloride and calcium bicarbonate. According to the manufacturer’s declaration, its nutrient and vitamin composition was as follows: crude protein, 17.1%; ether extract, 3.7%; crude fibre, 4.5%; lysine, 0.87%; methionine, 0.37%; threonine, 0.61%; calcium, 0.81%; phosphorus, 0.66%; sodium, 0.16%; vitamin A, 10,000 IU; vitamin D₃, 3,000 IU; and vitamin E, 25 IU. Both diets were supplied by De Heus (Łęczyca, Poland). Representative samples of the commercial diets from each feeding period and experimental group were collected for laboratory analyses. Approximately 500 g of feed was taken per sample and placed in five resealable plastic bags. Laboratory analyses were performed according to the methods specified by the Polish Committee for Standardization (pkn.pl). The contents of dry matter (DM), crude ash (CA), crude protein (CP), crude fibre (CF), ether extract (crude fat, EE), neutral detergent fibre (NDF), acid detergent fibre (ADF) and acid detergent lignin (ADL) were determined. Gross energy (GE) was determined using the KL-21 PLUS isoparabolic calorimeter (Precyzja-Bit PPHU LLC, Bydgoszcz, Poland) (Zaremba et al., 2024). Each sample was analyzed in 10 replicates. Growth performance and meat quality

Viability and feed intake (FI) were recorded daily during rearing. The body weight of birds was measured on days 1, 28, and 42. Viability (%), body weight gain (BWG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR), were calculated from the obtained data with formulas:BWG=finalbodyweight(g)−initialbodyweight(g); FCR=feedintake(g)bodyweightgain(g).

On the 42nd day of rearing, 10 birds from each group with a body weight close to the group average were selected. The birds were stunned and then slaughtered by severing the spinal cord between the first cervical vertebra and the occipital condyle (EUR-Lex, 2019a, 2019b). After bleeding, the carcasses were scalded by immersion in water at 65°C for 10 s and plucked in a mechanical plucker (Soda Plus, Krępice, Poland). The carcasses were dipped twice in melted food-grade wax (Polwax, Jasło, Poland) for 3 s and then cooled in water for easy feather removal. The carcasses were eviscerated, and the gizzard, heart, and liver were separated. The carcasses and offal were stored under refrigerated conditions (4°C) for 24 h until further analysis. Carcasses and offal were weighed (Radwag, Radom, Poland) to calculate the slaughter yield and slaughter yield with offal, using the following formula: carcassweight(g)livebodyweight(g)×100.

Dissection was performed by separating the neck, pectoral muscles, leg muscles (deboned), skin from the carcass, abdominal fat, wings, and carcass remains. All elements were weighed (Radwag, Radom, Poland), and their weight was calculated per 100 g of carcass. The pH of the pectoral muscles was measured using an electronic pH meter (Elmetron, Zabrze, Poland). A dagger electrode was used to measure the pH of the meat, which was inserted into the right pectoral muscle. The result was read after the pH value had stabilized. The color of the pectoral and leg muscles was assessed using a colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan) from the inside. Before starting the measurements, the device was calibrated using a white calibration plate. The meat color results were presented on a three-level scale: L* - lightness, a* - redness, and b* - yellowness. For the analysis of the drip loss, one pectoral muscle was weighed (Radwag, Radom, Poland). It was placed in a 150 × 200 mm string bag with a cut at the bottom and then in a larger 200 × 300 mm bag. The samples were stored at 4°C for 24 h. After this time, the drip loss was weighed again and calculated using the following formula: 100−(W2W1)x100, where W2 – pectoral muscle weight after 24 h, W1 – initial pectoral muscle weight. The pectoral and leg muscles were ground in a meat grinder (Hendi, Poznań, Poland). Six meat samples weighing 0.295-0.305 g from each group were weighed (Radwag, Radom, Poland) for water holding capacity (WHC) analyses. They were placed between two pieces of Whatman filter paper and loaded with a 2 kg weight for 5 min. After this time, the samples were weighed again, and WHC was calculated using the following formula: 100−(W2W1)x100, where M2 – sample weight after 5 min, W1 – initial sample weight. Drip loss and WHC were expressed as water loss from the meat. From the ground meat from each group, six collective samples of pectoral and leg muscles weighing approximately 500 g were weighed (Radwag, Radom, Poland) for chemical composition analysis. The content of protein, collagen, intramuscular fat (IMF), and water was assessed using the FoodScan apparatus (FOSS, Hillerød, Denmark) using the near-infrared transmission (NIT) spectrophotometry method. The methods of meat quality were described previously by Zaremba et al. (2024).

The data were analyzed using Statistica software (TIBCO Software Inc.). Mean values, standard error of the mean (SEM), and checks for sample homogeneity and normal distribution were calculated. A two-way analysis of variance was performed to evaluate the data, and Tukey’s test was used to identify statistically significant differences between the groups at a significance level of P < 0.05 for two independent factors – halloysite supplementation and sex of ducks. For the production results, 10 replicates were included per treatment group. The carcass characteristics and qualitative physicochemical properties were evaluated using 10 replicates per group. For interaction of two factors, two-way ANOVA was analyzed, with P < 0.05.

Post-mortem blood biochemical parameters

All biochemical parameters of blood plasma were determined spectrophotometrically on a MAXMAT PL multidisciplinary diagnostic platform (Erba Diagnostics France SARL, Montpellier, France) using diagnostic kits from ELITech Clinical Systems SAS (Sees, France).

Relative abundance of intestinal bacteria

Intestinal content samples collected separately from the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum of ducks were transferred into sterile tubes, placed on dry ice immediately after collection, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Bacterial DNA was extracted using the GeneMATRIX Stool DNA Purification Kit (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and DNA quality and concentration were assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis and spectrophotometry (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). The relative abundance of selected bacterial taxa, including Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., and Escherichia coli, was determined by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) using a LightCycler 480 II instrument (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Each reaction contained SG onTaq qPCR Master Mix (2x) (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland), 1 μM of each primer specific for the bacterial 16S rRNA gene, and 20 ng of template DNA, and all reactions were performed in two technical replicates. Primer sequences are listed in Table 2. PCR efficiency for each primer pair was determined using LightCycler 480 II software based on a five-point serial dilution of bacterial DNA. Histological parameters of pectoral muscle

Pectoral muscle samples were taken and fixed in Bouin's fixative, dehydrated in graded ethanol concentrations, cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin blocks. Then, the samples were sectioned at 6 µm sections using a rotary microtome HM355S (Thermo Shandon Limited, Runcorn, UK) with a section transfer system on a wet tray and Cool-Cut system, and stained with Masson's trichrome. The cross-sectional area of muscle fibres was measured using an Olympus BX51 microscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and (Fiji Is Just) ImageJ (ver. 1.54g, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Relative gene expression in pectoral muscle

At the end of rearing, eight birds were randomly selected from both the control and experimental groups. Pectoral muscle was collected post mortem and immediately immersed in a nucleic acid stabilization buffer (fixRNA, EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). Samples were transported under controlled conditions and stored at −80°C until further laboratory analyses. Total RNA was isolated from approximately 150 mg of muscle tissue. Samples were homogenized in 1 ml of RNA Extracol reagent (EURx, Gdańsk, Poland). Subsequently, 0.2 ml of chloroform per 1 ml of reagent was added, followed by thorough homogenization using a TissueRuptor (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA purification was performed using the GeneMATRIX Universal RNA Purification Kit (EURx) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, USA). Gene expression levels were quantified by real-time PCR following reverse transcription of RNA from individual samples. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the smART First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (EURx). Each qPCR reaction mixture contained SG onTaq qPCR Master Mix (2 ×) (EURx), gene-specific primers at a final concentration of 1 µM each (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and 70 ng of cDNA template. Amplification was performed using a LightCycler 480 II (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 58°C for 15 s, and extension at 72°C for 45 s. A melting curve analysis was conducted at the end of each run to confirm amplification specificity. Relative expression levels were analyzed for a selected panel of genes: FST, PDK4, CEBPB, PHKB, PRKAG3, ACSL1, and ABHD5. These genes were selected based on their involvement in metabolic regulation and energy homeostasis. Gene expression was normalized using the geometric mean of the Ct values of the reference genes GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) and ACTB (Beta-actin). Detailed information on the analyzed genes is provided in Table 3. The female control group (C2) served as a common calibrator, assigned a relative expression value of 1. This common calibrator was used solely for the graphical representation of relative changes in expression levels (fold changes) across all experimental groups; it did not influence the statistical analyses, which were performed using individual, normalized ΔCt values. Prior to statistical analysis, individual normalized ΔCt values were screened for extreme observations using a predefined conservative IQR-based algorithm applied identically to all genes and experimental groups. Potential outliers were initially identified outside the interval from Q1 − 1.5 × IQR to Q3 + 1.5 × IQR. No observations were excluded when IQR < 0.01. When more than 20% of observations were initially flagged, the threshold was expanded to 2.5 × IQR; if more than 20% of observations remained flagged, no observations were excluded. A flagged observation was excluded only if its deviation from the median exceeded 300%. The effects of sex, halloysite supplementation, and their interaction were assessed using two-way ANOVA on individual normalized ΔCt values. Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was subsequently used for pairwise comparisons among the four sex-by-treatment groups. The Tukey-adjusted pairwise p-values were additionally adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Results

Growth performance and meat quality

On day 28, a significant interaction between halloysite supplementation and sex was observed for BW (P = 0.019), indicating that the response to halloysite supplementation differed between males and females. A similar interaction was observed for BWG during days 1–28 (P = 0.019). The main effect of halloysite supplementation was significant for both BW and BWG during this period (P = 0.008), with lower values observed in halloysite-supplemented ducks. During days 29–42, a significant halloysite × sex interaction was observed for BWG (P = 0.036), indicating a sex-dependent response during the second rearing period. For FCR during days 29–42, a significant interaction between halloysite supplementation and sex was also observed (P = 0.001). Discussion

Halloysite adversely affected BW, BWG, and FCR of ducks during the first rearing period (P < 0.05). However, considering the entire rearing period, no significant differences between groups were found (P > 0.05). According to Karamanlis et al. (2008), the addition of clinoptilolite at a level of 2% to the feed increased BWG of Cobb 500 broiler chickens compared with the control group. Basha et al., [2016] obtained similar results using 15 g/kg of zeolite for broiler chickens. Similar results were reported by other authors for minerals such as bentonite (Ghazalah et al., 2021), halloysite (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2021), or fermented clay minerals (Joo et al., 2007). Elsherbeni et al. (2024) also confirmed the beneficial effect of zeolite in the nutrition of Silver Montazah laying hens through improvements in final BW, egg number and egg weight, laying rate, and FCR. These changes may result from the specific properties of natural minerals, which promote better growth parameters. In the study by Wlaźlak et al. (2025), Cherry Valley broiler ducks fed a diet supplemented with 1% halloysite had significantly lower BW and BWG both between days 1 and 28 of rearing and throughout the entire rearing period. Those authors pointed to the potential possibility that halloysite supplementation may slow the passage of intestinal contents and increase their viscosity, which could have had a key effect on the adverse changes in growth performance. The ducks were kept under similar conditions and received the same additive, which may indicate an important influence of genotype on the observed changes in productive parameters compared with our study. Moreover, young birds showed higher sensitivity to the applied additive, whereas in the later rearing period they were able to achieve a BW similar to that of birds in the control groups. Such discrepancies may be related to genotype, age, basal diet composition, nutrient density, or differences in the physiological response of birds to aluminosilicates. For this reason, the genotype-dependent explanation proposed previously should be regarded as a plausible interpretation rather than a definitive explanation. The possibility that a minor nutrient dilution effect contributed to the early response has therefore also been acknowledged.

In our study, the use of halloysite resulted in significantly lower a* value of leg muscles. Myoglobin content, as well as the rate of lipid oxidation processes, plays a key role in shaping poultry meat color (Shabani et al., 2016). In the study by Cheng et al. (2016), the a* value of pectoral muscles decreased with increasing levels of palygorskite in the diet of chickens. As indicated by those authors, these results may suggest a particular affinity of minerals for pigments, which would correspond with the results of the present study. Furthermore, the leg muscles of ducks were characterized by significantly higher WHC. Similar results were obtained in previous studies in which broiler ducks were fed a diet supplemented with zeolite (Wlaźlak et al., 2024). According to Abdelrahman et al. (2023), different doses of zeolite in feed (5, 10, 15, and 20 g/kg) for Ross 308 broiler chickens did not affect the final WHC of muscles. The value of this parameter is determined by interactions between water and protein in the meat. An important role is also attributed to dissolved structural proteins and myofibrils (Puolanne, 2017). WHC is mainly responsible for meat juiciness, which is important from the point of view of meat quality for the consumer and further processing (Barbut et al., 2024).

In our study, dietary halloysite decreased protein content in pectoral muscles and increased IMF content. In leg muscles, lower IMF content was found compared with the control group. Effect of halloysite was not uniform across muscle groups and may reflect tissue-specific nutrient partitioning rather than a generalized stimulation of lipid deposition. In contrast to the present study, Banaszak et al. (2021) found that the use of a mixture of zeolite and halloysite in litter and feed, at a ratio of 75:25, had a positive effect on protein content in the pectoral and leg muscles of Ross 308 chickens. In another report, the beneficial effect of halloysite added to feed (5 g/kg) and peat litter (0.5 kg/m²) for chickens was confirmed (Banaszak et al., 2022). On the other hand, 4% zeolite in feed negatively affected protein content in duck muscles after 6 weeks of rearing (Biesek et al., 2021a). A similar relationship between higher IMF and lower protein concentration has been reported in duck pectoral muscle, including in ducks receiving 1% halloysite, where the mineral increased IMF and reduced protein content (Wlaźlak et al., 2025). Moreover, the negative relationship between protein and IMF observed in duck breast muscle in that study supports the possibility that changes in the relative deposition of these components contribute to the observed chemical profile. Previous work in ducks has demonstrated that changes in management conditions can alter IMF deposition and lipid-metabolism-related gene expression differently in breast and thigh muscles, indicating that lipid deposition is tissue-specific rather than a uniform whole-body response (Guo et al., 2020). Sex-related differences in body composition and muscle metabolism may arise from differences in endocrine regulation as well as sex-specific cellular mechanisms. In birds, sex differences can result not only from circulating gonadal hormones but also from sex-chromosome-linked regulation of gene expression, which may contribute to differences in tissue metabolism between males and females (Arnold and Itoh, 2011). Therefore, the different magnitude of the halloysite response between male and female ducks may reflect differences in nutrient partitioning and metabolic sensitivity between the sexes rather than a direct effect of halloysite on muscle structure. In contrast to the changes observed in selected meat quality and chemical composition traits, halloysite supplementation did not affect the histological structure of the pectoral muscle. The absence of significant differences in cross-sectional area, Feret diameter, minimum diameter, and fiber density indicates that 1% halloysite did not induce detectable muscle fiber hypertrophy, atrophy, or structural remodeling. Therefore, the observed changes in protein, intramuscular fat, water content, and water-holding capacity are more likely related to metabolic or compositional modifications rather than to changes in muscle fiber structure (Petracci and Cavani, 2012; Ismail and Joo, 2017). This finding suggests that halloysite modified selected quality traits without impairing the microstructural integrity of the pectoral muscle.

Total protein concentration was significantly higher in halloysite-treated birds, which may indicate improved protein metabolism or may suggest enhanced nutrient utilization (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2019, 2021). This finding is consistent with the improved feed efficiency observed in the later growth phase, suggesting a more effective use of dietary protein (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2021; Wlaźlak et al., 2025). This effect may serve as an indirect indicator of improved metabolic efficiency. Uric acid levels were significantly affected by both halloysite supplementation and sex. Lower uric acid concentrations in treated groups may reflect improved nitrogen utilization and reduced protein catabolism, further supporting the hypothesis of enhanced metabolic efficiency (Eleroğlu et al., 2011; Rezende et al., 2017). Urea concentration was also significantly reduced in halloysite-treated birds, which aligns with the observed decrease in uric acid and suggests a shift toward more efficient protein retention rather than excretion (Rezende et al., 2017). However, these biochemical changes should be interpreted with caution, as they were not accompanied by significant differences in final body weight or total body weight gain. Therefore, the higher total protein concentration and lower uric acid and urea levels may indicate a selective metabolic adjustment rather than a general improvement in growth performance. This suggests that halloysite may influence nitrogen metabolism without necessarily translating these changes into final growth advantages. Lipase activity showed a significant interaction between treatment and sex, indicating that the effect of halloysite on lipid digestion may differ between males and females (Ghalehkandi et al., 2011; Ahmad et al., 2023). This may partially explain the sex-dependent differences observed in growth performance and gene expression (Wlaźlak et al., 2025). A significant interaction effect was also observed for calcium levels, suggesting that mineral metabolism may be influenced by both treatment and sex (Eleroğlu et al., 2011; Nadziakiewicz et al., 2023). Although the biological relevance of this change is less clear, it may be associated with differences in absorption or metabolic demand (Eleroğlu et al., 2011). The lack of significant changes in key liver enzymes indicates that halloysite supplementation did not adversely affect hepatic function (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2023). Similarly, stable levels of cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, and LDL suggest that lipid metabolism remained largely unaffected at the systemic level (Nadziakiewicz et al., 2023; Ahmad et al., 2023). The lack of significant changes in hepatic enzymes and systemic lipid indicators suggests that 1% halloysite did not exert adverse effects on liver function or whole-body lipid metabolism under the conditions of the present study. This suggests that the applied supplementation level did not induce detectable adverse systemic metabolic effects under the conditions of the present study.

The analysis of intestinal microbiota revealed segment-specific variability in bacterial populations across the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecum. In the duodenum, bacterial counts were generally comparable between groups, with the exception of a statistically significant difference in Bifidobacterium spp. abundance between male control and male halloysite groups (P = 0.046). This isolated finding may reflect a localized, sex-specific response; however, it does not support a general treatment-related effect on the analyzed bacterial taxa. In the jejunum and ileum, moderate variability in bacterial abundance was observed between groups, but none of the differences reached statistical significance (P > 0.05), and no consistent pattern was detected across taxa. The cecum exhibited greater variability in microbial populations compared to the other intestinal segments; however, although differences between control and halloysite-treated groups appeared more pronounced, particularly in terms of data dispersion, these differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The cecum is the primary site of microbial fermentation in poultry (Pan and Yu, 2014), and whether halloysite influences microbial activity in this segment remains to be determined in future studies with broader microbiome profiling and functional measurements, such as short-chain fatty acid analysis (Ali et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022). The absence of consistent statistically significant differences in the analyzed bacterial taxa indicates that halloysite did not exert a detectable effect on the intestinal microbiota under the conditions of this study. Overall, the microbiological findings do not support a general or consistent effect of halloysite on the relative abundance of the analyzed bacterial groups, and any potential influence on the gut ecosystem remains to be investigated.

The analyzed genes (FST, PDK4, CEBPB, PHKB, PRKAG3, ACSL1, and ABHD5) constitute an interconnected regulatory network governing key physiological processes in poultry, including energy metabolism, lipid turnover, and muscle development (Dunislawska et al., 2020; Mohammadabadi et al., 2021; Poompramun et al., 2021). Genes such as PDK4, PRKAG3, and PHKB are primarily involved in the regulation of glucose utilization and glycogen metabolism, whereas ACSL1, ABHD5, and CEBPB contribute to lipid metabolism and fat deposition (Sibut et al., 2011; Nematbakhsh et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2019). In turn, FST, CEBPB, and PRKAG3 play important roles in muscle growth and the determination of muscle-related traits (Sibut et al., 2011; Mohammadabadi et al., 2021). Collectively, these genes influence economically important production parameters, including muscle development, meat quality, fat deposition patterns, feed efficiency, and overall metabolic performance (Dunislawska et al., 2020; Poompramun et al., 2021). Their coordinated activity reflects the complex biological mechanisms underlying growth and nutrient utilization in poultry (Mohammadabadi et al., 2021; Poompramun et al., 2021).

The biochemical profile supports the hypothesis that halloysite supplementation primarily affects metabolic efficiency rather than inducing systemic physiological stress. The reduction in nitrogen waste products (uric acid and urea) corresponds well with the observed improvement in feed conversion ratio during the later grower phase, suggesting enhanced nutrient utilization (Rezende et al., 2017; Nadziakiewicz et al., 2021). These systemic changes are consistent with the gene expression results, where modulation of regulatory genes such as FST and CEBPB indicates altered energy allocation and growth regulation rather than disruption of core metabolic pathways (Mohammadabadi et al., 2021; Nematbakhsh et al., 2021). However, the present data do not establish that halloysite directly affects myostatin‑related signaling, C/EBPβ‑dependent transcription, or energy‑allocation pathways. The relatively stable expression of lipid metabolism genes (ACSL1, ABHD5) is in agreement with the unchanged blood lipid profile, reinforcing the conclusion that lipid turnover was not markedly affected (Liu et al., 2019; Nematbakhsh et al., 2021; Nadziakiewicz et al., 2023). The observed decrease in PDK4 expression, together with improved nitrogen utilization, may suggest a shift toward more efficient energy metabolism, potentially favoring carbohydrate utilization and protein retention (Sibut et al., 2011; Poompramun et al., 2021). Overall, the data indicate that halloysite supplementation induces an initial metabolic adjustment phase, followed by improved efficiency of nutrient utilization without compromising animal health. The presence of sex-dependent responses across multiple parameters highlights the importance of considering sex as a key factor in evaluating dietary interventions. The lack of significant changes in muscle fiber morphology suggests that halloysite supplementation did not induce structural alterations in skeletal muscle tissue (Petracci et al., 2013; Ismail and Joo, 2017). This finding is consistent with the absence of differences in final BW and total BWG, indicating that overall muscle development was not impaired.

The stable histological profile also supports the biochemical findings, which showed no evidence of systemic metabolic disruption or muscle damage (e.g., unchanged CK and LDH activities). These results suggest that muscle tissue integrity and development were maintained regardless of dietary treatment (Petracci et al., 2013; Ismail and Joo, 2017). The histological data confirm that halloysite supplementation does not adversely affect muscle microstructure and support the conclusion that its primary effects are related to metabolic regulation and nutrient utilization rather than morphological changes in muscle tissue.

In conclusion, dietary supplementation with 1% halloysite in Orvia ducks induced selective metabolic and molecular responses that were partly sex-dependent. Although halloysite negatively affected body weight gain during the early growth phase, final BW and total BWG were not impaired, while FCR improved during the later rearing period. The biochemical profile, characterized by higher total protein and lower uric acid and urea concentrations, suggests more efficient protein and nitrogen metabolism without signs of hepatic dysfunction or systemic lipid metabolism disturbances. Halloysite also modified selected meat quality traits and muscle chemical composition, particularly intramuscular fat, water content, protein content, redness, and water-holding capacity, indicating an effect on muscle composition rather than carcass yield. With the exception of a single significant difference in Bifidobacterium spp. abundance in the male duodenum (P = 0.046), no consistent statistically significant differences were detected in the analyzed intestinal bacterial populations, and no general microbial effect of halloysite was demonstrated. Histological analysis showed no adverse effect on pectoral muscle microstructure, whereas differences in FST and CEBPB transcript abundance indicate that dietary halloysite supplementation was associated with molecular variation in genes related to muscle growth and metabolic regulation. Overall, halloysite supplementation may support nutrient utilization and metabolic efficiency without compromising final growth performance or muscle integrity; however, the sex-dependent responses observed in several traits highlight the need to consider sex when evaluating the nutritional value and practical application of halloysite in broiler duck production.
