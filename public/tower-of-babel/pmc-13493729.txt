Quantitative accuracy of 177Lu SPECT/CT imaging using ring-shaped CZT versus dual-head NaI systems
Authors: Rachele Danieli, Clémentine Marin, Anna-Lena Theisen, Johannes Tran-Gia, Carlos Artigas, Bruno Vanderlinden, Nick Reynaert, Patrick Flamen, Hugo Levillain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRing-shaped systems based on CZT are promising for quantitative imaging, but their accuracy may be affected by septal penetration of high-energy photons—given the fixed collimator—and by limitations in scatter correction due to low-energy tailing. This study compares the quantitative performance of a ring-shaped CZT system with that of a conventional dual-head NaI SPECT/CT system (StarGuide™ and NM/CT 870 DR, both GE HealthCare) using the 208 keV photopeak of 177Lu.MethodsA NEMA phantom with 3D-printed anthropomorphic inserts, including kidneys and lumbar vertebrae, as well as standard spherical inserts, was filled with activity concentrations mimicking the distribution observed in patients 1 day post-injection of 7.4 GBq of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T. The phantom was scanned repeatedly on both systems (scan duration/bed position: 7 min for StarGuide™ and 20 min for NM/CT 870 DR). Quantitative accuracy and reproducibility were evaluated using the mean error and the coefficient of variation (CV) over repeated measurements, respectively. Additionally, a group of patients treated with [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T underwent sequential SPECT/CT on both systems to confirm the phantom-based results.ResultsThe total activity measured within the phantom deviated from the expected value by means of 13% for the StarGuide™ and 0% for the NM/CT 870 DR. After partial-volume-effect correction based on recovery coefficients, the activity concentration in kidney inserts was measured with ~ 5% accuracy on both systems. For the StarGuide™, mean errors in tumour inserts (volumes: 2.6–28 ml) ranged from − 8% to 3%, whereas a consistent underestimation by an average of − 11% was observed for the NM/CT 870 DR. The StarGuide™ significantly overestimated the background activity concentration (59%), while the NM/CT 870 DR showed a smaller error (− 8%). Reproducibility was lower for the StarGuide™, with maximum CV values of 7%, compared with 3% for the NM/CT 870 DR. Clinical findings were consistent with phantom results.ConclusionsThe StarGuide™ provided slightly more accurate activity quantification in regions of high relative uptake, such as tumours and kidneys, compared with the NM/CT 870 DR. However, quantification in regions of low relative uptake and total activity estimation were less accurate, likely due to septal penetration. The lower reproducibility suggests that longer scan durations may be considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00892-2.Keywords: Lu-177, Quantitative SPECT/CT, CZT, 3D printing

Background

Image-based dosimetry after radiopharmaceutical therapy with lutetium-177 (177Lu) relies on accurate quantitative SPECT/CT imaging. Quantitative 177Lu SPECT/CT imaging with dual-head NaI SPECT/CT systems is well established, with a number of original research papers and international guidelines that are readily accessible to assist physicists in defining clinical protocols [1, 2].

Recently, ring-shaped SPECT/CT systems based on cadmium–zinc–telluride (CZT)—such as the VERITON-CT series (200, 300 and 400—Spectrum Dynamics Medical, Caesarea, Israel) and the StarGuide™ (GE HealthCare, Haifa, Israel)—have become available in hospitals as alternatives to traditional dual-head NaI systems. These systems are recognised not only for the superior energy resolution typical of CZT detectors, but also for their potential to improve the spatial resolution and the enhanced sensitivity resulting from the non-standard system architecture [3]. These advantages make them attractive for a wide range of diagnostic and theranostic imaging applications [4–7].

Some groups have already investigated the suitability of these systems for quantitative SPECT/CT imaging [8–11]. However, the available literature remains limited, and further evidence is needed, particularly given that this technology is continuously evolving.

Two characteristics specific to ring-shaped CZT systems may compromise their quantitative accuracy: (1) the penetration of high-energy photons through the fixed-collimator septa and (2) the tail near the low energy region of the photopeak [12].

In a previous study, we showed that septal penetration has a non-negligible impact on the performance of StarGuide™ when using the 208 keV photopeak of 177Lu [13]. Although using the 113 keV photopeak would reduce the impact, we decided to focus exclusively on the 208 keV photopeak in this study, as we observed a significant instability in the image calibration factor (ICF [14]) when using the 113 keV photopeak [15]. This instability was most likely caused by a suboptimal scatter correction algorithm applied to the 113 keV photopeak, which, unlike the correction for the 208 keV photopeak, did not account for the low-energy tailing in the energy spectrum. Similar observations—namely, septal penetration and inadequate scatter correction for the 208 and 113 keV photopeaks, respectively—were reported more recently by other groups [12, 16].

Building on our previous study, this study aims to compare the quantitative accuracy of StarGuide™ with that of a conventional dual-head NaI-based SPECT/CT system (NM/CT 870 DR, GE Healthcare) equipped with a medium-energy collimator, where septal penetration is expected to be minimal, when using the 208 keV photopeak of 177Lu. The comparison was first performed using a patient-specific 3D-printed phantom, and the results were then verified in a small cohort of patients receiving [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T therapy.

Methods

Initially, a series of 177Lu-filled phantom measurements was performed to define a clinical quantification protocol for both systems, including a reconstruction protocol, an ICF, and a recovery coefficient (RC) curve.

The quantification accuracy was then assessed using a NEMA IEC phantom equipped with anthropomorphic 3D-printed inserts (kidneys and lumbar vertebrae). The same phantom was used to investigate the accuracy of various SPECT-based tumour segmentation techniques.

To verify the generalizability of observed findings to the clinical situation, the quantification protocol was subsequently applied to SPECT images of 11 patients treated with [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T, allowing for the determination of activity concentrations within kidneys, target tumours and remainder of the body.

All phantom and patient acquisitions were performed sequentially on a StarGuide™ and a NM/CT 870 DR system with 5/8″ crystal equipped with a medium-energy general purpose parallel-hole collimator. The results obtained from the two systems were compared.

Activities were determined using a radionuclide calibrator (RNC) equipped with a VIK-202 ionization chamber (Comecer) and the dial factor provided by the vendor, and were corrected for the residual activity. EDTA was added to the stock solutions and background compartments to ensure homogeneity and prevent sticking of the activity to the walls.

NEMA_anthro phantom

The NEMA_anthro phantom was designed to simulate a typical radiopharmaceutical activity distribution in the torso of a patient suffering from prostate cancer, one day post the injection of 7.4 GBq of [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T.

The phantom included the following 3D-printed inserts (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1): two single-compartment lumbar vertebrae (L1 and L2, volumes 27.8 and 26.6 ml, respectively), one two-compartment lumbar vertebra (L3I—12.3 ml; L3II—16.3 ml), and two single-compartment kidneys (right—174.2 ml; left—173.6 ml). The geometry of these inserts was based on the ICRP 145 computational model [17]. More details about the design and fabrication are given in the Supplementary Material.

Axial (left), sagittal (centre) and coronal (right) views of a fused SPECT/CT scan of the NEMA_Anthro phantom. The large VOI is shown in purple; the right and left kidney VOIs are in blue and green, respectively; L1 and L3II are in magenta and red; S1 and S2 are in yellow and light blue; and the background VOI, with a volume of approximately 630 ml, is in orange

The L1 and L3II inserts were used to simulate bone metastases, while L2 and L3I represented healthy spongiosa. Additional nodal or visceral tumours were simulated using the 22 mm diameter (S1) and 17 mm diameter (S2) spheres of the NEMA IEC phantom. These were attached to the NEMA top plate at locations maximising the distance from other radioactive compartments: S1 was positioned next to the left transverse process of L3, while S2 was placed adjacent to L3I (see Fig. 2).

An activity concentration of 2.9 MBq/ml was used to fill all inserts simulating tumours (Table 1). Both kidneys were filled with approximately one-third of that concentration. A concentration of around 14 kBq/ml was used for both the background compartment and the healthy spongiosa, resulting in a very high contrast against the kidneys (70:1).

Acquisition setup

Acquisitions on the StarGuide™ were performed in continuous sweep mode with 2° steps. The minimal overlap was set to 9.84 mm. Data were acquired in a 112 × 16 matrix, with the photopeak and scatter windows centred at 208.0 keV (± 6%) and 185.0 keV (± 5%), respectively, as suggested by the manufacturer. After the SPECT, low-dose CT scans (120 kVp, 512 × 512 matrix, 0.98 × 0.98 × 2.50 or 0.98 × 0.98 × 2.46 mm resolution) were acquired for attenuation correction.

All phantom acquisitions consisted of a single bed position (with a 27.5 cm longitudinal coverage), except for the NEMA_anthro phantom, for which two bed positions were used.

Scan durations varied between the phantoms. Prior to the reconstruction, projections corresponding to a scan duration of 7 min were created by using the lister tool of the SmartConsole, unless the physical scan duration was already set to 7 min. A single set of 7-min projections was generated from each acquisition, except for the NEMA_noBG phantom, for which 8 different sets of projections were obtained from the original 60-min acquisition.

Acquisitions on the NM/CT 870 DR were performed in non-circular step-and-shoot mode with 30 frames of 40 s per head. Data were acquired in a 128 × 128 matrix, with the emission and scatter energy windows centred at 208 keV ± 10% and 178 keV ± 5%, respectively. After the SPECT, low-dose CT scans (120 kVp, 512 × 512 matrix, 0.98 × 0.98 × 4.41 or 0.98 × 0.98 × 5.00 mm resolution) were acquired for attenuation correction.

Reconstruction setup

Data acquired on the StarGuide™ were reconstructed using the camera manufacturer's software (SmartConsole version 1.0, GE Healthcare). The reconstruction protocol followed the manufacturer's recommendations for the 208 keV photopeak of 177Lu. It comprised a block sequential regularized expectation maximization (BSREM) algorithm (Q.Clear, GE Healthcare) utilising relative differences for maximum regularization. The algorithm incorporated CT-based attenuation correction, CZT-specific scatter correction, resolution recovery and septal penetration modelling. The number of iterations recommended by the manufacturer (20) was increased from 5 to 40 to find an optimal balance between sphere-to-background ratio and noise. All other reconstruction parameters were set according to the manufacturer: 10 subsets, gamma 1, beta 0.08, by Sens, Clarity 3D Power 0.01, and a voxel size of 2.46 mm.

SPECT data acquired on the NM/CT 870 DR were reconstructed with an ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) reconstruction algorithm, including resolution recovery, CT-based attenuation correction, and a dual-energy-window scatter correction (SmartConsole version 1.0, GE Healthcare). The number of iterations was varied between 2 and 10, while the number of subsets was fixed to 10. No post- reconstruction filtering was applied. A voxel size of 4.42 mm was used.

Optimisation of the reconstruction protocol

To find an optimal reconstruction protocol for each system, the NEMA_BG phantom reconstructions with increasing number of iterations were analysed as illustrated in Fig. 1 by manually delineating spheres in the phantom on the CT images using MIM Software (version 7.3.1). Nine spheres with a diameter of 35 mm were drawn at different locations within the uniform background region of the phantom to create a single, large background volume of interest (VOI) [10].

Spherical VOI (left, different colors) and background VOI (centre, light red) delineated on the NEMA_BG phantom, and large VOI (right, green) delineated on the Cylinder phantom

The mean counts in each delineated sphere were divided by the mean counts in the background VOI (i.e., the light red VOI in Fig. 1) to calculate the sphere-to-background ratio. Noise was quantified as the coefficient of variation (CV) of counts in the background VOI, calculated as the ratio between the standard deviation (SD) and the mean of the counts. The reconstruction maximising the signal-to-background ratio while keeping the noise as low as possible was considered as optimal.

All the analyses below were performed on images reconstructed with the resulting optimal number of iterations.

Calibration

To compute the ICF (cps/MBq), a large VOI was drawn around the Cylinder phantom by expanding the CT-based contour by 2 cm (Fig. 1). The ICF was then computed by dividing the total number of counts inside this VOI by the scan duration and by the RNC-based activity inside the phantom decay-corrected to the scan time [14].

Recovery coefficients

The spheres of the NEMA_noBG phantom were delineated on the SPECT images using a region growing tool based on their nominal volumes. In this phantom, the configuration of the spheres differed from that of NEMA_BG shown in Fig. 1, with the red and blue spheres inverted, and the lung insert missing. For each sphere, RC was computed by dividing the SPECT-based by the decay-corrected RNC-based activity concentration.

An RC curve was obtained by fitting a two-parameter logistic function , where V is the sphere volume, to the measured RCs using a non-linear least-squares algorithm (Python 3.10). The RC for the smallest sphere (13 mm diameter) was excluded from the fit. For the StarGuideTM, where eight SPECT images corresponding to a scan duration of 7 min were created, the mean RC was calculated for each sphere.

The first two are gradient-based segmentation methods; the third is a threshold-based method, in which the maximum voxel value is replaced by the peak value, defined as the highest possible mean value within a spherical 1-ml VOI placed inside the tumour insert [19]. The PERCIST SUV Peak tool in MIM was used to determine this peak value. To establish the optimal threshold, threshold values yielding the nominal volumes of the five largest spheres in the NEMA_noBG phantom were averaged. This resulted in optimal threshold values of 32% for the StarGuide™ and 36% for the NM/CT 870 DR (data not shown).

Accuracy and precision of the volume and activity concentration (before and after PVC) estimates were determined for both systems. For PVC, RCs corresponding to the segmented insert volumes were used.

The method with the highest accuracy (i.e., the lower mean error) was used for segmenting tumours in the patient study.

Patient study

Our patient study involved eleven patients treated with [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-I&T (mean(± SD) injected activity: 7558 (± 267) MBq). Following the first therapeutic administration in nine patients and the second administration in two patients, sequential SPECT/CT imaging was performed using the StarGuide™ (time after the injection: 18.7(± 1.0) hours) and the NM/CT 870 DR (18.6 (± 1.3) hours) systems. Depending on patient size, 3–4 bed positions were acquired on the StarGuide™ in order to cover the salivary glands, the abdomen and the pelvis. In contrast, only a single bed position was acquired on the NM/CT 870 DR to minimize the additional burden on patients. The field of view was chosen to include as many target tumours as possible and, if feasible, the kidneys. For the first two patients scanned on the StarGuide™, a scan duration of 10 min per bed position was used. For subsequent patients, the scan duration was reduced to 7 min per bed position. On the NM/CT 870 DR, a scan duration of 20 min was used. All other acquisition and reconstruction parameters were left unchanged from the phantom experiments.

All patients included in this study provided informed consent. The study protocol, in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the local ethics committee (CE3644).

If included in the field of view of the NM/CT 870 DR, kidneys were delineated on the low dose CT using the Contour ProtégéAI workflow in MIM, and then visually verified.

Isolated tumours within the SPECT field of view on both systems, with a margin of five axial slices from the upper and lower edges, and with clearly identifiable uptake and boundaries at visual inspection, were selected [20]. Selected tumours were segmented using the methods considered most accurate based on the phantom study.

The activity concentration in the remainder of the body was sampled with two large VOIs placed in the gluteal region [21].

The mean activity concentration inside kidneys and tumours was partial volume corrected by applying the RC corresponding to the delineated volume. As for the inserts of the NEMA_anthro phantom, these RCs were derived from the RC curve generated from the NEMA_woBG phantom sphere measurements.

The NaI system exhibited a higher sphere-to-background ratio than the CZT system, with maximum values of 8.3 and 7.1, respectively, for the largest sphere, compared with a theoretical value of 9.8 (see Table 1).

For the StarGuide™, increasing the iterations from 20 to 30 improved the sphere-to-background ratio by 1% for the three largest spheres and up to 5% for the three smallest, with only a modest noise increase (4%); therefore, 20 iterations were considered a good trade-off between image quality and reconstruction time.

For the NM/CT 870 DR, 6 iterations were optimal, as increasing to 8 iterations produced only modest gains in sphere-to-background ratio (1% for the three largest spheres and up to 9% for the smallest) but substantially increased noise (22%).

All further analysis in this study were performed on images reconstructed with 20 iterations and 10 subsets for the StarGuide™, and 6 iterations and 10 subsets for the NM/CT 870 DR.

Before PVC, comparable underestimations were observed with both systems, with results being highly dependent on the insert volume. Performing the PVC removed the volume dependence and improved the accuracy, which was higher for the StarGuide™ (mean errors within ± 10%) than for the NM/CT 870 DR (mean errors up to − 13%). The CV increased as the insert volume decreased, reaching a maximum value of 7% for the StarGuide™ and 3% for the NM/CT 870 DR for the smallest insert.

Mean errors in the total activity within the NEMA_anthro phantom (approximately 550 MBq at the time of the acquisitions) were 13% and 0% (data not shown) for the StarGuide™ and the NM/CT 870 DR, respectively, with no variability across the three repeated measurements for either system (CV ~ 0%). The StarGuide™ significantly and systematically overestimated activity concentration in the background, with a mean error of 59% (7 kB/ml in absolute value), while for the NM/CT 870 DR a mean error of − 8% was measured (Table 2).

Tumour segmentation method

Mean errors in the volume and activity concentration within the tumour inserts of the NEMA_anthro phantom when using the PET Edge+, PET Edge, and % peak threshold methods are presented in Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 3.

Mean errors in the estimation of volume (left column) and activity concentration before (central column) and after PVC (right column) within the tumour inserts of the NEMA_anthro phantom segmented with PET Edge + (top row), PET Edge (middle row) and % peak threshold (bottom row). Thresholds of 32% and 36% of the peak value were used for the StarGuide™ and NM/CT 870 DR, respectively. Error bars represent the CV, and shaded areas represent ± 10% mean error

The % peak threshold provided the most accurate and precise volume estimates for both systems, with maximum mean errors (CVs) of 18% (12%) for the StarGuide™ and 23% (6%) for the NM/CT 870 DR, observed for the smallest insert (S2). For the same insert, PET Edge + and PET Edge produced larger negative mean errors (− 46% and − 60% for the StarGuide™; − 40% and − 58% for the NM/CT 870 DR). In addition, reproducibility was generally poorer for these methods. This was particularly evident for the S1 insert segmented with PET Edge+, with CVs of 41% and 35% for the StarGuide™ and NM/CT 870 DR, respectively.

Prior to PVC, both systems consistently underestimated the activity concentration to a similar extent, regardless of the segmentation method used. After PVC, mean errors obtained using the % peak threshold ranged between − 9% and + 7% for the StarGuide™, and between − 20 and − 4% for the NM/CT 870 DR (Supplementary Table 3). Measurements for the smallest insert were associated to the largest variability, with CVs of 14% and 7% for the StarGuide™ and the NM/CT 870 DR, respectively. As with volume, PET Edge+ and PET Edge produced larger and less reproducible errors.

Based on these results, the % peak threshold method was selected for segmenting tumours in the patient study.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the quantitative accuracy of the CZT-based StarGuide™ with that of a NaI-based NM/CT 870 DR SPECT/CT system for 177Lu applications, with a focus on PSMA-targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy. To this end, we designed and 3D-printed a series of anthropomorphic inserts, including fillable kidneys and vertebrae, compatible with a NEMA IEC phantom. We also made the corresponding 3D-printing files available online. After filling the inserts with 177Lu to mimic a PSMA patient, the phantom (NEMA_anthro) was imaged on both systems, and their quantification accuracy was compared. To verify the generalisability of the observed findings to the clinical situation, our observations were validated in a small cohort of PSMA patients, also scanned on both systems.

Mean errors in total activity within the NEMA_anthro phantom (13% and 0% for StarGuide™ and NM/CT 870 DR, respectively) indicated a higher accuracy for the NaI system. The NM/CT 870 DR outperformed the StarGuide™ also with respect to the quantification of the activity concentration in the background VOI, for which a mean error of 59% (CV = 2%) was registered for the StarGuide™, compared to − 8% (2%) for the NM/CT 870 DR (Table 2). This appears to contradict the results obtained using the Deluxe phantom (see Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 1), for which StarGuide™ is expected to produce an error of approximately − 3% at a similar activity concentration (12 kBq/ml). However, while the Deluxe phantom had a uniform activity distribution—the same as the Cylinder phantom used to calibrate the system—the NEMA_anthro phantom contained regions of higher activity (i.e., tumours and kidney inserts) near the background VOI. As a result, septal penetration from these nearby structures contributed more strongly than in a uniform scenario, explaining the observed overestimation of the activity concentration. From a clinical perspective, this indicates that quantifying activity in low-uptake regions, such as bone marrow, using StarGuide™ with the 208 keV photopeak is challenging when structures of higher activity are nearby, and that results strongly depend on the surrounding activity distribution.

An additional observation supports the hypothesis that septal penetration is responsible for this overestimation: when a smaller background VOI was placed along the z-axis in alignment with the tumour or kidney inserts, the overestimation increased by more than 200%, while it decreased to a minimum of 9% when the VOI was misaligned (see Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 4). This behaviour is consistent with the shape of StarGuide™ detectors, which causes septal penetration to be more pronounced along the z-axis, as can be seen in Fig. 8 of our previous article [13].

Hoog et al. reported a 15% overestimation for the background region of their customised phantom, acquired on a StarGuide™ with a similar protocol. This result, which remained unexplained by the authors, may also be attributed to septal penetration, with the lower error in their study likely reflecting the lower contrast between hot inserts and background (10:1 vs. 70:1 in this study) [9]. The same study also reported a 15% overestimation for the 113 keV image. While this could suggest a factor affecting both peaks, we believe the 113 keV overestimation is related to other physical effects.

Activity quantification within the kidney and tumour inserts was more accurate overall using the StarGuide™. Before PVC, both systems underestimated activity concentrations by similar amounts (Fig. 6). After PVC, the StarGuide™ showed better recovery of activity, with mean errors of 5% for kidneys and ranging from − 8 to 3% for tumours (Table 2). In contrast, the NM/CT 870 DR showed mean errors of − 4% and − 7% for the right and left kidneys, respectively, and − 11% on average for tumours, with minimal variation among inserts, indicating a systematic under-correction of partial volume effects. However, StarGuide™ estimations for the two smallest inserts (5.6 and 2.6 ml) were associated with a lower precision.

To better reflect clinical reality, the quantification accuracy for the tumour inserts was also assessed using segmentation without prior knowledge of the structures’ nominal volumes. The PET Edge + and PET Edge tools provided by MIM software proved not to be suitable for tumour segmentation on SPECT images. On the contrary, the % peak threshold performed acceptably, with mean errors on the volume estimation down to − 12% for volumes larger than approximately 5 ml (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 3). Activity concentration accuracy was similar to that obtained with the region growing segmentation, with mean errors after PVC between − 9 and 7% for the StarGuide™ and ranging from − 20 to − 4% for the NM/CT 870 DR. Precision was lower than when using the region-growing segmentation and remained higher for the NM/CT 870 DR. However, results for the StarGuide™ may still be clinically acceptable, depending on the application. The increased uncertainty suggests that, despite the higher sensitivity of CZT detectors, a 7-min acquisition on StarGuide™ may still be inferior to a 20-min acquisition on a conventional NaI SPECT/CT system in terms of precision. Longer scan times could improve precision; however, given the shorter longitudinal coverage of the StarGuide™ (27.5 cm vs. 40 cm), its advantage for quantitative imaging after 177Lu radiopharmaceutical therapy with longer scan times may be limited.

The patient study confirmed that our phantom experiment was representative of the clinical reality, with comparable activity concentrations in tumours and kidneys, as well as comparable percentage differences between the two systems (Fig. 8). In addition, our measured activity concentrations were consistent with previously published values. The mean percentage injected activity measured in the kidneys using our two systems (mean (SD): 2.5% (0.3%) at about 19 h post-injection) was comparable with that reported by Hermann et al. for [177Lu]Lu-PSMA-617 therapy (2.4% (0.8%) at 23 h post-injection) [23].

According to the Bland–Altman analysis in Fig. 8, a moderate systematic difference between the estimations from the two systems existed only for the activity concentration in kidneys and tumours before and after PVC, respectively. This indicates that, on average, the two systems produced similar results. Even if biases were small, however, LoAs were generally quite wide, indicating poor agreement, with wider LoAs for tumours than for kidneys. Surprisingly, this was also observed for kidney volumes measured on the CT using the Contour ProtégéAI workflow of MIM software, where differences up to 27% were observed, likely caused by respiratory artefacts. LoAs for kidney activity concentrations were narrower than for kidney volumes, and after PVC, activity concentrations from the two systems were concordant within approximately ± 20%. For tumours, LoAs for the activity concentration after PVC ranged from − 30 to 48%, indicating a weak agreement overall, partly due to the higher uncertainty in measuring activity concentration in tumours (Supplementary Table 3).

The activity concentration used to fill the background compartment of NEMA_anthro phantom was significantly higher than that measured in patients in the gluteal region. However, percentage differences remained comparable (Supplementary Fig. 2). A mean bias of almost 90% was observed, confirming that StarGuide™ systematically and significantly overestimates the activity concentration in regions of low relative uptake when using the 208 keV photopeak.

Prior to the NEMA_anthro phantom and patient acquisitions, a series of phantoms was imaged to define a clinical protocol for quantitative 177Lu SPECT/CT for each system. For StarGuide™, our analysis showed that 20 iterations, as recommended by the manufacturer, were a good compromise between signal-to-background ratio and noise (Fig. 3). With this reconstruction, we found an ICF of 94.6 cps/MBq, confirming our previous finding [13]. For the NM/CT 870 DR, 6 iterations were found to be optimal, corresponding to an ICF of 15.4 cps/MBq. This value is approximately 17% lower than that reported by Salvadori et al. (18.26 ± 0.20 cps/MBq) using a very similar protocol. This difference may be due to the lack of traceability of the activity measurements in both studies, as well as to the definition of the VOI used to calculate the ICF (external vs. internal VOI).

ICFs for the two systems proved to be sufficiently stable over a wide range of activity (Fig. 4). However, StarGuide™ tended to underestimate the activity at lower activities, with differences up to − 12% at 13 MBq. When extending the scan duration to 15 min, differences substantially reduced, suggesting that 7 min might not be enough at low count rates. For the NM/CT 870 DR, on the other side, the problem rises at high activities, possibly due to saturation effects, as also observed by Salvadori et al. [24]. However, in practical applications, activities lower than 2 GBq are typically measured in a single bed position, resulting in a maximum underestimation of − 5%, which is acceptable.

As far as we are aware, our group and the Swedish group [12] are the only ones to have previously reported numerical RC values for the 208 keV 177Lu photopeak imaged with a StarGuide™. In our previous publication, RCs were obtained using a NEMA phantom with sphere diameters ranging from 10 to 37 mm [13]. Stenvall et al., on the other hand, used a NEMA phantom, in which the 10 mm sphere was replaced by a 60 mm sphere, as was done in the present study. In both cases, no activity was injected into the background compartment. These two sets of RCs, together with the RCs obtained in the present study, are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4—left panel. Despite the different sphere configuration and positioning—which were recently proven to be factors significantly impacting the recovery [25]—as well as the different reconstruction protocols and segmentation methods, the agreement between the different RCs seems excellent, except for the smallest sphere (13 mm diameter). This result is promising in view of the definition of harmonization programmes such as the EARL accreditation for CZT-based SPECT/CT systems (https://earl.eanm.org/177lu-spect-ct/).

Compared to the StarGuide™, the NM/CT 870 DR demonstrated higher sphere-to-background ratios and RCs. It could be argued that this difference is related to the different reconstruction algorithm—BSREM versus OSEM–, but as can be seen in Supplementary Figs. 2 and 1 from our previous paper, BSREM and OSEM seem to behave similarly for similar noise levels on the StarGuide™ [13]. This is further supported by the agreement between our RCs and those published by Stenvall et al., which were obtained with an OSEM reconstruction with 30 iterations and 10 subsets [12]. RCs lower than for NaI systems were also previously observed for the Veriton 200 series [10, 26].

Stenvall et al. also reported RCs for a Discovery 670 with 5/8’’ crystal and medium-energy collimators, showing excellent agreement with the RCs for the NM/CT 870 DR in the present study, with differences within ± 10% for all the spheres. The agreement seems lower when compared to RCs obtained with a NEMA phantom without the 60 mm sphere. Supplementary Fig. 4—right panel compares the RCs from the present study and the study by Stenvall et al., as well as the RCs obtained by our group without the 60 mm sphere. The latter were obtained using the same NEMA phantom as in our previous publication. Further investigations are required to confirm this hypothesis, but the StarGuide™ appears more stable across different NEMA phantom configurations compared to the conventional NaI system.

During the writing of this study, a new version of the StarGuide™ software (SmartConsole version 1.1) became available, whose main novelty for 177Lu imaging is the introduction of a CZT-specific scatter correction for the 113 keV photopeak [27]. For the 208 keV photopeak, minor modifications such as slightly different recommended energy windows and reconstruction parameters, and adjusted parameters for the scatter correction were introduced. However, the manufacturer assured that the quantification for the 208 keV remains consistent to the previous version. In particular, nothing has changed regarding the previously mentioned correction of septal penetration. For these reasons, we believe the results presented here are still of interest for the community. In the future, now that a dedicated scatter correction is available, we plan to explore the potential of utilising the 113 keV photopeak, which may be better suited for quantitative 177Lu imaging with StarGuide™ due to its lower sensitivity to septal penetration.

In addition to this software update, GE HealthCare recently presented a prototype of StarGuide™ detectors with two energy-adapted collimators, allowing collimator selection based on the isotope used. This new technology could effectively address septal penetration.

This study has also some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the experiments were designed specifically in the context of 177Lu-PSMA imaging and may not fully represent other scenarios, such as peptide receptor radionuclide therapy, where the lesion-to-background contrast is often lower (e.g., liver lesions) than in PSMA imaging [28]. Second, only a single phantom configuration was used, which may limit the generalisability of the phantom-based findings. Finally, SPECT/CT was performed at a single time point, which prevented the calculation and comparison of absorbed doses as recently done by Zaragori et al. [26].

Conclusions

In regions of high relative uptake, such as kidneys and tumours, StarGuide™ provided slightly more accurate activity estimates overall than the NM/CT 870 DR. However, uncertainties for tumours were higher, suggesting that longer scan durations may be needed. Conversely, in regions of low relative uptake and in total activity quantification, the NM/CT 870 DR outperformed the StarGuide™, most likely because of the penetration of high energy photons through the StarGuide™ collimator septa. All measurements in the present study were performed using the 208 keV photopeak of 177Lu. Using the 113 keV photopeak, which is less affected by septal penetration and is now supported by a recent software upgrade providing a CZT-specific scatter correction, may improve these findings, but this was beyond the scope of the present study. These phantom-based findings were found to be representative of the clinical situation, which greatly facilitates future testing of new acquisition and reconstruction techniques. We have therefore made the files required for 3D printing available online.
