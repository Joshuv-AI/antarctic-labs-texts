Enforcing Convergence in Sensitivity-based Distributed Programming via Transformed Primal-Dual Updates Thanks: This work is funded by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG, German Research Foundation) under project no. 464391622.
Maximilian Pierer von Esch, Andreas Völz, Knut Graichen

Abstract
Sensitivity-based distributed programming (SBDP) is an algorithm for solving large-scale, nonlinear programs over graph-structured networks. However, its convergence depends on the coupling strength and structure between subsystems. To address this limitation, we develop an algorithmic variant: SBDP+. The proposed method utilizes first-order sensitivities and primal decomposition to formulate low-dimensional, decoupled subproblems, which are solved in parallel with neighbor-to-neighbor communication. SBDP+ differs from SBDP by enforcing convergence for all coupling structures through a carefully designed primal-dual update. It retains a low communication effort and handles couplings in the objective and constraints. We establish sufficient conditions for local convergence in the non-convex case. The effectiveness of the method is shown by solving various distributed optimization problems, including statistical learning, with a comparison to state-of-the-art algorithms.

Introduction

Large-scale nonlinear optimization problems arise in a wide range of applications, including statistical learning with high-dimensional data or features
[
3
]
, economical optimization problems
[
30
]
, electrical power systems
[
17
]
, and distributed nonlinear predictive control (DMPC)
[
4
]
. These problems often feature separable objectives and structured couplings, which distributed optimization techniques can exploit by decomposing the global problem into smaller, parallelizable subproblems. This enables local computation with limited communication, offering significant advantages over centralized methods in terms of scalability, flexibility, and robustness.

Therefore, a wide range of distributed algorithms have been developed. Popular approaches for constrained, convex problems include distributed projected gradient descent
[
31
]
, dual decomposition
[
9
]
, distributed non-smooth Newton methods
[
12
]
or the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM)
[
3
]
. However, a large share of relevant applications requires to solve non-convex problems for which the algorithms mentioned above might diverge or only find a suboptimal solution in terms of a non-vanishing duality gap.

Consequently, research has shifted towards distributed non-convex optimization techniques. A straightforward approach is to distribute the internal operations of standard nonlinear programming methods such as sequential quadratic programming (SQP), interior point methods (IPM) or augmented Lagrangian schemes. Examples include using ADMM to solve the quadratic programs (QP) arising in SQP
[
26
]
, distributing the Newton step in IPMs via ADMM
[
8
]
or exploiting the locally convex structure of augmented Lagrangian functions to apply dual decomposition
[
1
]
or distributed proximal minimization techniques
[
13
]
. The augmented Lagrangian alternating direction inexact Newton (ALADIN) method aims at combining SQP and ADMM and is a well-established approach for non-convex problems
[
14
]
, but relies on a central coordination step which may jeopardize scalability. This issue can be addressed with a bi-level scheme by distributing the coordination step using methods such as ADMM
[
7
]
.

These algorithms often rely on a generalized consensus formulation
[
3
]
, where additional optimization variables are introduced for shared components for which consistency is enforced via consensus constraints. Then, the dual formulation of the problem is used to solve the problem in distributed fashion. In contrast, primal decomposition methods iterate directly on the shared variables, avoiding the need for strong duality and resulting in smaller subproblem dimensions. Next to Jacobi iterations
[
5
]
and forward-backward splitting
[
27
]
, sensitivity-based distributed programming (SBDP) has recently become a promising alternative. Prior work demonstrates its effectiveness in linear-quadratic settings
[
23
,
25
,
24
]
, for optimal control
[
20
]
, general NLPs
[
22
]
or real-time DMPC
[
21
]
. However, existing convergence results for SBDP depend on the coupling structure and require a generalized diagonal dominance (GDD) condition
[
23
,
25
,
22
]
or a sufficiently short prediction horizon
[
20
,
22
]
.

This motivates the development of SBDP+, an algorithmic variant of SBDP to solve general, non-convex NLPs over graph-structured networks in a distributed fashion via neighbor-to-neighbor communication. Hereby, non-convexity is understood in the same sense as in e.g.
[
14
,
7
,
26
]
, that is, we assume the existence of regular local minima, while allowing the objective and constraint functions to be non-convex, and provide local convergence guarantees to these minima.
Building upon the original idea of SBDP, the method uses first-order sensitivities to capture the interaction between subsystems and applies primal decomposition to construct lower-dimensional, decoupled NLPs. We formulate the local NLPs in terms of a search direction which, together with the local Lagrange multipliers, is used to update the original primal and dual variables. In this sense, we first decompose and then optimize with classical NLP algorithms. This is in contrast to first applying NLP solvers and then decomposing their internal operations. We argue that this reduces the time-expensive communication by limiting it to the algorithm’s upper layer and allows for greater flexibility in choosing suitable NLP solvers. Furthermore, we rigorously investigate the convergence properties of SBDP+ and show linear convergence under appropriate assumptions. We compare the scheme to state-of-the-art algorithms for (non-)convex optimization such as ADMM
[
3
]
, ALADIN
[
14
]
and bi-level ALADIN
[
7
]
.

3
Algorithmic development

This section contains the algorithmic development of the proposed sensitivity-based approach
for solving general NLPs of the form (
1
) in a distributed
manner. The main idea relies on augmenting the individual cost functions (
1a
) of each agent with the first-order sensitivity information of its neighbors, thereby explicitly taking the effect of the coupled variables into account. The couplings in the cost function (
1a
) and constraints (
1b
) – (
1c
) are resolved via a primal decomposition approach.
First, we address the construction of modified, local NLPs to compute the search directions, then we discuss the update of primal and dual variables via the computed search direction, before the algorithm is presented.

3.1
Modified local NLPs

Each agent
i\in\mathcal{V}
constructs a decoupled, local NLP in terms of a local search direction
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
which is subsequently solved at the agent level in each iteration

of the proposed SBDP+ method

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}\quad

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})

(4a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s.\!t.}\quad

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\quad|\,\,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}

(4b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})\leq\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\quad|\,\,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}

(4c)

with the modified local cost functions

(5)

and local equality and inequality constraints

\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}):=\boldsymbol{g}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q}+\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}^{q}),\quad\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}):=\boldsymbol{h}_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q}+\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}^{q})

(6)

\boldsymbol{s}_{i}
and implicitly on the value of
\boldsymbol{p}

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{g_{i}}}

) and (

\boldsymbol{y}_{i}:=[\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}

3.2
Relation to SBDP and limitation of standard update

If, after solving (
4
), the Newton-like update
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q}+\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{q}
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{q}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}^{q}
is applied, the recently proposed SBDP method
[
22
,
23
]
is recovered. However, the convergence of this scheme depends on the coupling strength between subsystems as shown in the following example.

Example 1
.

Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x_{1},\,x_{2}}\quad

\displaystyle 0.5x_{1}^{2}+0.5x_{2}^{2}

(9a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s.\!t.}\quad

\displaystyle x_{1}+ax_{2}=0

(9b)

which is of the form (
1
) with
f_{i}(x_{i},x_{j})=0.5x_{i}^{2}
,
i\in\{1,\,2\}
,
g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}+ax_{2}
, and coupling parameter
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Explicitly solving the problems (
4
) with
\rho=0
and applying the Newton-like update, leads to the following recursion

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{q+1}\\
x_{2}^{q+1}\\
\lambda_{1}^{q+1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-a&0\\
0&0&-a\\
0&a&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}^{q}\\
x_{2}^{q}\\
\lambda_{1}^{q}\end{bmatrix}

(10)

which has the eigenvalues
\zeta_{1}=0
,
\zeta_{2/3}=\pm ja
and is therefore asymptotically stable if and only if
|a|<1
. Furthermore, the simple modification of the update rule to
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q}+\alpha\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{q}
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{q}+\alpha(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{q}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{q})
, and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{q}+\alpha(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}^{q}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{q})
with step size
\alpha>0
does also not guarantee convergence in general. For instance, adding the constraint
g_{2}(x_{2},x_{1})=x_{1}+x_{2}=0
to (
9
), one finds that for
a=4
the eigenvalues of the modified recursion (
10
) are
\zeta_{1/2}=1+\alpha
,
\zeta_{3/4}=1-3\alpha
. Thus, no choice of
\alpha
enforces convergence in this case.

3.3
Primal and dual update

Example
1
illustrates the need for a more sophisticated update scheme that ensures convergence for arbitrary coupling structures.
Therefore, once the search direction
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}^{q}
and the local Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{q}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}^{q}
have been computed by solving (
4
) in some iteration
q
, each agent
i\in\mathcal{V}
updates its primal and dual variables in a carefully constructed manner. Let
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}:=[\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
be the corresponding local elements of
\boldsymbol{p}
which are held by every
i\in\mathcal{V}
, then each agent updates its primal and dual variables as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{q+1}=\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{q}+\alpha\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{q})(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{q}-\boldsymbol{d}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{q}))

(11)

with the step size
\alpha\in(0,1)
, offset
\boldsymbol{d}_{i}(\boldsymbol{p}_{i}):=[\boldsymbol{0}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i}^{\top}]^{\top}
and the matrix-valued functions
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}:\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}\times p_{i}}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}^{2}\bar{L}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})&\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})&\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})\\
-\beta\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\bar{g}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})&\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
-\beta\boldsymbol{K}_{i}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})&\boldsymbol{0}&-\beta\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})\end{bmatrix}

(12)

for each
i\in\mathcal{V}
. In (
12
),
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
describes an additional step size for the dual updates,
\boldsymbol{K}_{i}:=\diag([\kappa_{1,i},\dots,\kappa_{n_{hi},i}])
is a diagonal matrix consisting of each
\kappa_{k,i}
in
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{[1,n_{hi}]}
, while
\boldsymbol{\bar{H}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})=\diag(\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i}))
is a diagonal matrix of all local inequalities. The matrix
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
defines a structured linear transformation that scales and rotates the primal-dual update. The upper left block of
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
incorporates local curvature information, scaling the primal step according to
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}_{i}\boldsymbol{s}_{i}}^{2}\bar{L}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
, while the constraint Jacobian matrices in the first row couple the primal and dual update. The terms on the lower left act as a dual regularization. Since
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\cdot)
depends only on local and neighboring variables, it is amenable to a neighbor-to-neighbor communication network.
The choice of
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\cdot)
also becomes more clear in the proof of Theorem
13
, where it alters the linearized algorithm iterations to ensure convergence.

3.4
Distributed optimization algorithm

The decoupling of the central NLP (
1
) into the local NLPs (
4
) is exploited by solving the individual problems in parallel at the agent level, see Algorithm
1
which is denoted as SBDP+. This requires a bi-directional, neighbor-to-neighbor communication network for which we assume the same graph structure
\mathcal{G}
as in the coupling structure of the central NLP (
1
).

Algorithm 1

SBDP+ for each agent
i\in\mathcal{V}

0:

Initialize
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{0}
; Choose step sizes
\alpha,\,\beta>0
, penalty parameter
\rho\geq 0
, and tolerance
\epsilon>0
; send
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{0}
to

; set
q\leftarrow 0

1:

Compute
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}L_{i}^{q}
via (
8
) for all
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}

2:

Send
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}L_{i}^{q}
to the corresponding neighbor
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}

3:

Compute the solution
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{q}
by solving the decoupled NLP (
4
) to local optimality

4:

Assemble
\boldsymbol{P}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{q})
and compute
\boldsymbol{p}_{i}^{q+1}
via the update rule (
11
)

5:

Send
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q+1}
to neighbors
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}

6:

Stop if a suitable convergence criterion, e.g.,
\|\boldsymbol{s}^{q}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon

with

.

In Step 1, each agent computes the partial derivatives
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}L_{i}^{q}
which are shared with neighboring agents in Step 2 to enable the evaluation of the local cost function (

). Subsequently, each agent independently and in parallel solves the local NLP (

) in Step 3, after which the primal and dual variables are updated via (

). Then, the new decision variable
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{q+1}
is communicated to the neighbors in Step 5. A practical tuning guideline for the step sizes

A possible stopping criterion is

\|\boldsymbol{s}^{q}\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon

with tolerance
\epsilon>0
. Since
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
is chosen, it can be evaluated in a distributed fashion and only convergence flags must be transmitted. However, other stopping criteria such as first order optimality or a fixed number of iterations are possible. Algorithm

only involves local computations and requires two neighbor-to-neighbor communication steps in which a maximum number of
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}2n_{i}|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
floats needs to be sent system-wide per SBDP+ iteration. The framework assumes access to a reliable low-level solver for the local NLPs (
4
). Thus, throughout this paper, we suppose these problems are solved to (local) optimality, although in practice the solution accuracy may affect convergence. The next two remarks discuss interesting structural special cases of NLP (
1
) which lead to a reduced computational and communication effort at the agent level, respectively.

Remark 2
.

A beneficial structural simplification of the local problems (
4
) arises if the cost functions are quadratic/linear in
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, the constraint functions are linear in
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, while the nonlinearity only appears via the coupled variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}}
. Then, the NLPs (
4
) reduce to QPs, indicating that if the nonlinearity in the central NLP (
1
) is encapsulated within the coupling functions, the sensitivity-based decomposition significantly reduces the computational burden at the agent level as only QPs need to be solved.

Remark 3
.

If the central NLP (
1
) is in so called neighbor-affine form, as discussed in
[
22
]
, then the agents are able to compute the gradients (
8
) locally. This property can be leveraged to reduce the number of communication steps by sending the Lagrange multipliers together with the decision variables in Step 5 of Algorithm
1
, computing
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}L_{j}
instead of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}L_{i}
in Step 1 and skipping Step 2. This leads to only one independent communication step in which a total number of
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}}(n_{i}+n_{gi}+n_{hi})|\mathcal{N}_{i}|
floats need to be exchanged.

4
Algorithmic Analysis

The analysis of Algorithm

1
. The last section contains a practical tuning guideline for the involved algorithmic parameters.

4.2
Local convergence

The subsequent convergence analysis of Algorithm
1
will revolve around showing that the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{p}^{q}\}
generated by repeatedly solving the KKT systems (
15

11
) or equivalently (
19
) converges towards a (local) central solution
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
of the KKT system (

To associate the limit of this sequence with the KKT point of (
1
), it is necessary to show that
\boldsymbol{y}^{q}=\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}^{q})=[\boldsymbol{0}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{q}}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{q}}^{\top}]^{\top}
implies that
\boldsymbol{p}^{q}
is also a KKT point of the central NLP (
1
). This relation is established in the next lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Let Assumptions
4
,
5
,
7
, and
8
hold. If
\boldsymbol{y}^{q}=\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}^{q})
, then
\boldsymbol{p}^{q}
is a locally unique KKT point
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
of NLP (
1
). Conversely, if
\boldsymbol{p}^{q}
is a KKT point
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
of NLP (
1
), then
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})=\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})
is a locally unique solution of all the KKT systems (
15
).

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.

Lemma
10
is important from two perspectives. First, it establishes the optimality of the distributed solution
\boldsymbol{p}^{q}
when the algorithm comes to a halt, that is when
\boldsymbol{y}^{q}-\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}^{q})=\boldsymbol{0}
in (

\boldsymbol{p}^{q}
satisfies the central optimality conditions. Second, it shows that
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})=\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})=[\boldsymbol{0}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}}^{\top},{\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{*}}^{\top}]^{\top}
is a local solution of the NLPs (
4
) such that we can employ the basic sensitivity theorem
[

to analyze the behavior of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{p})
for
\boldsymbol{p}
sufficiently close to

which is adapted for the present case and summarized in Lemma

\Gamma_{i}^{\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{I}}(\cdot)
be set-valued mappings that assign the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})
or
\mathcal{I}_{i}^{q}(\boldsymbol{s}_{i})
their global index in

.

Under Assumptions Assumptions

,
5

7

and the convergence rate, provided that the step size

and

as defined in (

Corollary 14

Suppose that all the requirements of Theorem

hold and that the constraints are decoupled, i.e.,

See Appendix

.

This simplification according to Corollary

is significant from two perspectives. First, it reduces the computational complexity, as the Hessian and Jacobian matrices in (

in (

4.3
A practical parameter tuning guideline

So far, three algorithmic parameters have been introduced: The primal step size
\alpha
, the dual step size
\beta
, and the penalty parameter
\rho
. These parameters need to be tuned to achieve satisfactory performance of Algorithm
1
. Fortunately, Theorem
13
together with the underlying assumptions implicitly provides tuning guidelines for good local performance. However, these depend on the central KKT point
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
which is not known beforehand such that we formulate the conditions in terms of some point
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})
. A natural choice is the initial guess
\boldsymbol{p}^{0}
or alternatively a small set of randomly sampled points. This reformulation is justified due to the continuity of all involved functions in
\mathcal{B}_{r}(\boldsymbol{p}^{*})
. The following list summarizes the steps.

1)

Choose the smallest possible penalty
\rho\geq 0
such that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}^{2}L_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathcal{N}_{i}})+\rho\boldsymbol{I}\succ\boldsymbol{0}
for all
i\in\mathcal{V}
.

2)

Perform Step 4 of Algorithm
1
and collect all local quantities. Choose the dual step size
\beta>0
as

\beta(\boldsymbol{p})=\frac{\underline{\lambda}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}L(\boldsymbol{\bar{x}},\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\kappa}))}{\bar{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{J}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\bar{K}}\boldsymbol{J}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}))}

(26)

with
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}=\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{s}
,
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x}):=[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x}),\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{x})]^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{\bar{K}}=\diag([\mathds{1}^{\top},\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\top}])
where
\mathds{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{g}}
is a unity vector.

3)

Choose
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that
\max_{i}\{|1-\alpha\lambda_{i}|\}<1
for all eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}_{[0,p]}
of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

Although these steps do not offer convergence guarantees, they provide a principled initial estimate for the parameters, upon which further refinement can be based. Importantly, when the central NLP (
1
) is an equality-constrained QP, these guidelines are independent of
\boldsymbol{p}
and hold globally.

Remark 15
.

The motivation for the choice of
\beta
in 2) stems from the quadratic eigenvalue problem (
54
) and aims at keeping the eigenvalues of its quadratic pencil reasonably well clustered. This improves the conditioning of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{p})
, allowing for larger
\alpha
. The choice of
\alpha
in 3) is motivated by enforcing the Schur stability of
\boldsymbol{I}-\alpha\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{p})
, cf. (
21
). Although a distributed eigenvalue evaluation of the steps 2) and 3) is generally possible
[
19
]
, and thus an adaptation of
\alpha
and
\beta
during the iterations, this aspect is not considered further.

5
Algorithmic extensions

Up to this point, the formal convergence guarantee for Algorithm
1
depends on the uniform positive definiteness of the Hessian of the central Lagrangian (
2
) at the central KKT point
\boldsymbol{p}^{*}
, see Assumption
5
. If this condition is not met, Algorithm
1
might not converge as shown in the next example.

Example 16
.

Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x_{1},\,x_{2}}\quad

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{2}

(27a)

\displaystyle\operatorname{s.\!t.}\quad

\displaystyle x_{1}-x_{2}=0

(27b)

which is of the form (
1
) with
f_{i}(x_{i},x_{j})=0.5x_{i}x_{j}
,
i\in\{1,\,2\},\,j\neq i
and
g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=x_{1}-x_{2}
. Problem (
27
) has a unique solution at
x_{1}^{*}=x_{2}^{*}=\lambda_{1}^{*}=0
which satisfies the LICQ and SOSC but not

The following assumption formalizes the SOSC

6.2
Comparison with ADMM and ALADIN

We compare the proposed SBDP+ method with ADMM
[
3
]
, SBDP
[

]
, ALADIN
[

and the bi-level D-ALADIN variant

, where the coordination QP is solved via ADMM. A brief characterization of each algorithm is provided, with further details available in the respective references.

All duality-based comparison methods reformulate the centralized NLP (
1

While all approaches require solving small-scale constrained NLPs at the agent level and are thus comparable in terms of local computation effort, SBDP+ and SBDP avoid the need for local copies, leading to smaller or equal-sized local decision vectors.
SBDP+, SBDP, ADMM, and bi-level ALADIN only require local computations and neighbor-to-neighbor communication, whereas ALADIN requires solving a centralized equality constrained QP and thus communication with a central entity, hence it is not fully distributed. In general, the communication effort for SBDP+, SBDP, ADMM and bi-level ALADIN scales linearly with the number of primal-dual variables, while it scales quadratically for ALADIN. If NLP (
1
) is in neighbor-affine form, see
[
22
]
, SBDP+ and SBDP require only one independent communication step compared to at least two for all other algorithms. Finally, local convergence properties of ALADIN
[
14
, Sec. 7]
and bi-level ALADIN
[
7
, Thm. 1]
are established under similar assumptions as here, while ADMM is only guaranteed to converge for certain non-convex problems
[
13
]
.

7
Conclusion

This paper introduces SBDP+, a framework for distributed optimization of non-convex, large-scale NLPs. It is characterized by decomposing the central NLP into small-scale NLPs at the agent level which are solved with neighbor-to-neighbor communication. Thus, SBDP+ pursues the approach of first decomposing and then optimizing. The coordination between agents takes place via sensitivities, which can be computed in a distributed fashion. The scheme is provably linearly convergent under standard regularity assumptions. Notably, SBDP+ extends the original SBDP method by ensuring convergence for general coupling structures, not just loosely coupled NLPs.

Furthermore, we suggest an extension to deal with problems for which the Lagrangian is locally convex only on the constraints by incorporating constraint information of neighbors.
The theoretical findings are validated in simulations, which also show that SBDP+ offers competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art solvers. Future work investigates the applicability of SBDP to convex optimization with non-differentiable objectives, different machine learning problems and explores the impact of inexact local NLP minimizations.

with

which implies R-linear convergence
[
18
]
.
\proofbox
