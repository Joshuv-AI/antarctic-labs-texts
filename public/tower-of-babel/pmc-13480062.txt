Antegrade submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap for the repair of maxillary alveolar process defects combined with personalized 3D-printed PEEK material for restoration of mandibular inferior border defects
Authors: Yi Ren, Haoyue Xu, Baoxing Pang, Liyan Liu, Minghao Li, Tong Pan, Yaling Wang, Lingxue Bu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the advantages, limitations, and potential complications associated with the antegrade submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap (ASAOF) for reconstructing maxillary alveolar process defects, in combination with personalized 3D-printed polyetheretherketone (PEEK) material for repairing bone defects at the inferior border of the mandible.MethodA retrospective analysis was performed on eight patients who underwent ASAOF for the repair of maxillary soft and hard tissue defects between August 2022 and November 2024 at a single institution. Clinical data, including medical history, defect site, osteomyocutaneous flap survival, operative duration, length of hospitalization, and postoperative recovery of oral function, were evaluated.ResultsEight patients underwent ASAOF reconstruction for maxillary alveolar defects. The mean operative duration was 335.6 min (range: 300–440 min), whilst the average hospitalization period was 13.4 days (range: 8–17 days). No recurrence, necrosis of the osteomyocutaneous flap, rejection or exposure of the PEEK material, or complications such as lower lip numbness and facial paralysis were observed. Two patients underwent maxillary implant placement combined with fixed denture restoration, with satisfactory aesthetic and functional recovery.ConclusionThe ASAOF is a reliable technique for repairing maxillary alveolar process defects. The personalized 3D-printed PEEK material exhibits no rejection and effectively repairs bone defects at the inferior margin of the mandible.

Introduction

Maxillary bone defects commonly result from tumor resection, trauma, or osteonecrosis of the jaw and can severely impair mastication, swallowing, speech, and facial aesthetics [1]. Functional and morphological restoration remains challenging, with the primary goal being successful maxillary reconstruction and restoration of occlusal relationships [2].

Multiple approaches have been developed for repairing maxillary defects, with prosthetics being the primary approach in the past [3]. However, concerns such as oronasal reflux during postoperative feeding, difficulty maintaining hygiene in the defect area, and the need for adjustments due to changes in local tissue morphology persist [4]. At present, transplantation of various autografts is considered the preferred method for reconstructing oral and maxillofacial soft and hard tissue defects [5]. Recent advances in microsurgical techniques have enabled the use of numerous free autologous bone grafting techniques (e.g., peroneal myocutaneous flap, iliac myocutaneous flap, and radial forearm osteocutaneous flap) for maxillary defect reconstruction [6]. However, these methods require the graft to be molded into the desired shape during surgery, although the intrinsic morphology of the donor tissue constrains complete restoration of the natural maxillary curvature. Postoperatively, these methods may also lead to delayed healing at the flap donor site, tendon adhesion, thick scarring, and functional impairment. Additionally, remote transplantation introduces an additional surgical field and vascular anastomosis, thereby increasing the risk of vascular compromise, prolonged operative duration, excessive bleeding, and delayed wound healing [7]. Earlier studies have described the use of reverse facial artery-submental artery mandibular osteomuscular flaps for maxillary defect repair [8]. However, previous reports on the use of these flaps for maxillary repair have often described the osteotomy descriptively, without indication of a universally adopted standardized protocol [8].

Thus, this study introduced and evaluated the application of the ASAOF in the repair of maxillary alveolar process defects. Personalized 3D-printed PEEK material was used to repair the bone defect at the inferior border of the mandible during the same operative session. A postoperative interval of 3–6 months has been regarded as feasible for subsequent maxillary dental implant placement. A total of 8 patients have been treated in recent years in our department, achieving favorable functional and aesthetic results through this technology, as outlined below.

Patients and methods

A retrospective analysis was conducted in eight patients who underwent maxillary lesion resection and repair with ASAOF at our hospital between August 2022 and November 2024. The eight patients exhibited maxillary defects resulting from surgical resection. According to the British James Brown classification system [9], one case demonstrated a Class 2b defect, whereas the remaining seven cases demonstrated Class 2a defects. The clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes of these eight patients were comprehensively evaluated.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao University Hospital. Given that this retrospective study involved anonymized patient data, written informed consent was not required.

Design and preparation of ASAOF

The ASAOF is an axial flap with a consistent blood supply from the facial artery and its submental artery branches [10]. Anatomical studies have established that the submental artery consistently originates from the facial artery and is accompanied by one to two constant veins [11]. Along its course, glandular, fasciocutaneous, muscular, and mandibular periosteal branches are distributed. The muscular and mandibular periosteal branches supply the anterior belly of the digastric muscle and the mandibular periosteum, respectively [12]. One to two periosteal branches on each side supply the periosteum of the mandible, with extensive anastomoses between their proximal branches, passing through the deep surface of the anterior belly of the digastric muscle [13].

CBCT is primarily used to acquire high-resolution images of the maxillofacial bony structures, including the maxillary alveolar process, the lower border of the mandible, and the mandibular canal. In contrast, conventional CT is used to assess soft tissue invasion and cervical lymph node status, which are essential for tumor assessment and tumor safety [14]. Therefore, preoperative combined CBCT and CT examinations are used for the comprehensive evaluation of patients undergoing maxillary lesion resection and simultaneous defect reconstruction (Fig. 1). In the reconstructed three-dimensional images, the position and orientation of the infraorbital foramen and mandibular neural canal were precisely identified. The safe boundary for malignant tumors is 1.5–2 cm outside the tumor, while for benign tumors, the entire tumor is removed from the normal bone within 0.5 cm outside the lesion boundary. An osteotomy was performed along the inferior border of the mandible to establish a saw path while preserving the mandibular nerve canal. Mimics software was utilized to delineate the complete resection boundaries of maxillary lesions and the required excision limits along the lower edge of the mandible (Fig. 2), followed by preparation of a mandibular osteotomy guide plate. The guide plate design incorporated a pre-set 1-mm saw path. Additionally, 3D-printing technology was applied to produce personalized PEEK materials for repairing defects along the inferior margin of the mandible [15].

The ASAOF design involved preparation of the vascular pedicle on the ipsilateral side, with Meilan markings used to outline the skin flap. The upper boundary of the skin flap was positioned approximately 1 cm from the lower edge of the mandible, whereas the lower boundary did not extend beyond the level of the hyoid bone, and the lateral boundaries typically remained within the region of the mandibular angle. Flap sizes were determined based on the extent of the soft tissue defect, with the length-to-width ratio not exceeding 3:1. Concurrently, the skin flap design could be integrated with the incision planned for neck lymphadenectomy (Fig. 3a).

The patient was placed under general anesthesia with nasotracheal intubation. The maxillary lesion was initially excised through a mandibular midline incision, following the preoperative plan (Figs. 3b, e). The patient was positioned supine with moderate extension of the head and neck. Next, an incision was made along the superior boundary of the skin flap, extending to the level of the platysma muscle. A T-shaped incision was made when neck lymphadenectomy was required. Subsequently, the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve is preserved approximately 1.5 cm below the inferior border of the mandible, and the submental artery and its accompanying vein are carefully dissected and isolated. The submental artery and accompanying vein are then traced anteriorly along the surface of the mylohyoid muscle, and branches traversing the submandibular gland and mylohyoid muscle, as well as the anterior jugular vein beneath the skin flap, are sequentially ligated until the anterior belly of the digastric muscle is reached. Posterior dissection is continued to the proximal segment of the facial artery, and the accompanying vein is isolated. The facial artery and vein superior to the inferior border of the mandible are ligated. Concurrently, at least one major venous drainage pathway, via either the internal or external jugular venous system, is preserved to ensure adequate venous outflow. After confirming vascular integrity, an incision was made along the lower boundary of the skin flap, and the anterior belly of the digastric muscle was detached from its insertion on the hyoid bone. The attachment of the anterior belly of the digastric muscle to the chin was preserved, and the periosteum was safeguarded from disruption. Dissection proceeded superiorly along the periosteum to the level of the mental foramen, preserving the mental nerve and the planned osteotomy boundaries. Then, the osteotomy guide plate was positioned and fixed on the lower edge of the mandible. A saw was then applied to excise the lower edge of the mandible attached to the anterior belly of the digastric muscle following the guide plate’s trajectory, while maximizing preservation of the periosteum (Figs. 3c, e). Subsequently, the prepared ASAOF was transferred to the maxillary defect site via a tunnel created in the parapharyngeal space medial to the mandible, ensuring complete mobilization of the flap pedicle without tension. The two ends of the grafted bone were fixed to the maxillary alveolar process and zygomatic bone using a four-hole microtitanium plate with bridging screws. Because the available mandibular bone height below the inferior alveolar nerve canal was insufficient to reconstruct an extensive maxillary bone defect, reconstruction was limited to the alveolar process defect. Accordingly, the preoperatively designed PEEK implant was fixed to the residual maxillary defect using small titanium plates and screws. The skin flap was then sutured to close the soft tissue defect. During the procedure, small titanium plates and screws were used to secure the PEEK material to the inferior border defect of the mandible, thereby restoring the mandibular contour (Figs. 3d, f). Bone healing was examined 3 to 6 months postoperatively, following which dental implant placement was performed.

Results

This study included eight patients (3 males, 5 females) with a mean age of 53.0 ± 14.7 years (range, 29 to 71 years), all of whom met the surgical indications. Postoperative pathological examination revealed two cases of ameloblastoma, one case of jaw cyst, one case of ossifying fibroma, and four cases of squamous cell carcinoma (2 cases CN0PN0, 2 cases CN0PN1). The maximum extent of the maxillary defect ranged from 13 to 28 mm, and the minimum ranged from 14 to 17 mm. The average operative time was 335.6 ± 51.8 min (range: 300–440 min), and the average hospitalization duration was 13.4 ± 2.9 days (range: 8–17 days). For the 8 patients, the minimum postoperative mouth opening was 3.5 cm, and the maximum was 4.6 cm. Patients were followed up for 7 to 27 months postoperatively. Clinical examination and postoperative three-dimensional CT scans of the mandible revealed no recurrence. Likewise, no dehiscence, necrosis, or infection of the osteomyocutaneous flap was observed, nor were any instances of PEEK material rejection, exposure, loosening, or displacement (Fig. 4). Moreover, no redness or swelling at the donor site, nor numbness of the lower lip, infraorbital region, or facial paralysis was detected. Two patients (25%) completed maxillary implant denture repair, with successful restoration of the occlusal relationship. As anticipated, the maxillofacial contour closely approximated the original appearance, the oral and nasal cavities were well sealed, and oral functions, including mouth opening, tongue movement, speech, mastication, and swallowing, recovered satisfactorily (Figs. 5). One patient (12.5%) developed a transient oronasal fistula postoperatively, which resolved successfully after dressing changes. In six cases (75%), the keratinized layer of the oral flap exhibited significant thinning, accompanied by hair loss and erythematous color changes, indicating progression toward mucosalization (Table 1).

Discussion

The submental island flap (SMIF) was first introduced by Martin in 1993 as a reliable approach for repairing oral and maxillofacial defects in eight cases [16]. Over the past three decades, this technique has undergone continuous refinement. Stern described a retrograde submental island flap supplied by the distal end of the artery, which effectively addresses long-distance defects in the oral and maxillofacial regions, including the jaw, forehead, and temporal areas [17]. Chen, Usman Khan, and colleagues reported the use of the SMIF, incorporating part of the mandible or its lower edge, retrogradely to repair maxillofacial bone defects, as well as the use of osteomyocutaneous flaps combined with dental implants to functionally repair maxillary defects, all yielding positive outcomes [18]. According to the Brown classification, existing reports on the use of the submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap to repair maxillary defects predominantly involve Class 2a defects, demonstrating that the flap, incorporating the lower edge of the unilateral mandible, is both safe and reliable. Based on the aforementioned findings, we hypothesize that the submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap can be further optimized and expanded for the repair and reconstruction of maxillary Class 2a-2b defects.

Based on prior experience with the submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap, it was integrated with current advanced CAD/CAM technology, 3D printing, and PEEK material to reconstruct maxillary defects in eight patients. Compared to existing repair methods, the antegrade submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap (ASAOF), combined with personalized 3D-printed polyetheretherketone (PEEK) material, offers the following advantages: (1) Eliminating the need for vascular anastomosis, resulting in shorter operation time and enhanced safety; (2) Aesthetic and well-concealed donor site incision; (3) Preservation of the natural curvature of the mandible, allowing seamless alignment with the contour of the maxillary bone and eliminating the need for sectional repair of the maxillary defect; (4) Avoidance of a second surgical area outside the oral and maxillofacial region, resulting in a lower incidence of donor site complications; (5) The elastic modulus of personalized 3D-printed PEEK material closely approximates that of the mandible, enabling perfect repair of defects along the lower edge of the mandible; (6) Capability to repair maxillary Class 2a-2b defects.

Notably, free flap surgery is associated with several inherent perioperative challenges, including complex procedures, extended surgical times, postoperative vascular compromise, flap infection and necrosis, and neck hematomas, all of which contribute to prolonged hospitalization [19]. Besides, postoperative complications such as delayed wound healing, pain, swelling, and restricted mobility may occur at the donor site, requiring a prolonged recovery period. In contrast, the average operative time for the ASAOF used herein was 335.6 min, with an average hospitalization duration of 13.4 days, and no complications at the flap or donor site were noted. Firstly, given that the ASAOF is an axial flap consistently supplied by the submental branch of the facial artery, vascular anastomosis is unnecessary, thereby significantly reducing operative time [20]. Secondly, the use of preoperative computer-assisted planning and osteotomy guides assists in minimizing damage to critical anatomical structures, such as the inferior alveolar nerve, thus avoiding complications such as lower lip numbness [21]. The natural curvature of the mandible seamlessly aligns with the maxillary contour, eliminating the need for manual shaping, thereby optimizing surgical accuracy and further reducing operative time. Furthermore, the ASAOF repair technique does not require the creation of an additional surgical site outside the oral and maxillofacial region, thus preventing complications at the donor site [22]. Lastly, patients avoid prolonged limb rehabilitation, which significantly reduces both physical discomfort and psychological distress. In addition, for patients requiring extensive maxillary resection, the Weber–Ferguson incision has traditionally been employed. This incision originates at the midline of the upper lip and extends superiorly along the philtrum, columella, and lateral nasal ala, continuing along the lateral aspect of the nose to approximately 5 mm below the medial canthus. However, in the present study, a midline lower lip incision was adopted because of several advantages. (1) The midline lower lip incision is continuous with the superior margin of the flap design incision, thereby concentrating the surgical incision on the mandible and neck and avoiding additional incisions on the maxilla; (2) This incision approach allows complete exposure of the maxillary lesion, thereby facilitating complete lesion resection; (3) Because ASAOF preparation requires partial resection of the inferior border of the mandible, this incision design provides full exposure of one side of the mandible, thereby facilitating mandibular bone harvesting; (4) Compared with the traditional incision, the midline lower lip incision contributes to improved postoperative symmetry of the maxillofacial region.

Of note, one patient in this study presented with a maxillary ameloblastoma spanning teeth 11 to 27, and the lesion measured approximately 5.85 × 3.39 × 2.83 cm. The preoperative maxillary osteotomy was designed horizontally from the distal aspect of tooth 12 to the distal aspect of tooth 27. Vertically, the design extended approximately 5 mm above the superior margin of the lesion, avoiding involvement of the orbital floor. This is consistent with a maxillary Class 2b defect. To achieve optimal repair, resection of the lower edge of the mandible was planned from the distal aspect of tooth 42 to the distal aspect of tooth 37. Noteworthily, the surgical intervention yielded favorable results. Anatomical studies have concluded that the periosteal branches of the mandible originate directly from the submental artery or the digastric branch, with extensive anastomoses present between the proximal ends of the periosteal branches on both sides [23]. The anterior belly of the digastric muscle is attached to the mandible at an approximately 30° angle, thereby increasing the attachment area to the periosteum. Besides, branches from the inferior alveolar artery, sublingual artery, and submental artery (mandibular periosteal branch) form a complex microvascular network within the mandibular bone, connected via the mandibular accessory foramen, thus ensuring adequate blood supply to the bone marrow cavity. In all eight cases, the resected lower edge of the mandible contained the bone marrow cavity, with continuous perioperative bleeding from the bone marrow cavity confirming its vascularization. Furthermore, Lin et al. documented significant variability in the venous drainage of the SMIF, with 73% draining through the internal jugular vein and 27% through the external jugular vein. Both ASAOF and SMIF share a common arteriovenous blood supply system [24]. The proper preservation of accompanying veins, as well as at least one venous system, is crucial for the survival of the osteomyocutaneous flap during surgery, particularly by avoiding simultaneous ligation of the retromandibular and common facial veins. Therefore, the present study suggests that, based on the mandible’s anatomical features and its adequate blood supply, the ASAOF, including the anterior belly of the digastric muscle, when designed and executed by experienced surgeons, can preserve all periosteal perforators, thereby ensuring the survival of a larger portion of the mandibular lower edge.

Furthermore, to address repairing distant defects and extending the vascular pedicle, Chen et al. employed a retrograde submental artery osteomyocutaneous flap, ligating and transecting the facial artery at the proximal end of the submental artery and reversing the osteomyocutaneous flap along the lateral aspect of the mandible to the maxillary defect [25]. Previous studies have pointed out that the retrograde submental island flap relies on retrograde blood flow through anastomoses between the facial artery and other vessels, ensuring a reliable blood supply and stable arterial pressure. Herein, all eight patients underwent antegrade ASAOF, which was elevated and positioned through the medial parapharyngeal tunnel of the mandible. The vascular pedicles were tension-free, and primary healing was successfully achieved in all cases. We speculate that antegrade ASAOF follows the natural anatomical course of the submental artery, offering a more reliable blood supply and arterial pressure. The medial parapharyngeal tunnel of the mandible is larger, allowing accommodation of a larger bone and muscle flap. Furthermore, the vascular pedicle is naturally protected by the lateral mandible. Taken together, these factors can enhance the survival rate of the transplanted bone and muscle flap.

The use of SMIF for repairing tissue defects after oral cancer resection remains controversial, with some authors asserting that it is only suitable for CN0 patients. However, Wang’s retrospective study demonstrated that careful neck dissection and appropriate postoperative adjuvant therapy did not increase the risk of local or regional tumor recurrence in patients with pN+ T1-2 OSCC [26]. Furthermore, a recent retrospective study by Ruenmarkkaew et al. further supports this concept [27]. Herein, four patients with squamous cell carcinoma were included, all of whom were CN0 on preoperative examination. A meticulous functional neck dissection was performed intraoperatively. Postoperative pathological examination revealed that two of the patients were pN1 stage. Both patients received adjuvant radiotherapy postoperatively, with a total dose of 6000 cGy, divided into 30 fractions (200 cGy per fraction). The target volume included the primary tumor bed and the ipsilateral cervical lymphatic drainage area (zones I-III). Importantly, no recurrence was observed in any of the four patients during follow-up, and no abnormalities were detected in the bone and muscle flaps. We posit that ASAOF application should adhere to the principles of tumor safety. ASAOF is recommended for CN0 patients, whilst suspicious lymph nodes in zone I should be biopsied intraoperatively. Conversely, it should be avoided in cases with nodal positivity with capsular breach. To limit the risk of local recurrence, postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy is recommended for patients with pN+ pathology.

After reconstruction of the maxillary bone defect using ASAOF, addressing the donor site bone defect is equally critical. Chen et al. employed MEDPOR surgical implants to repair defects along the lower edge of the mandible. However, the elastic modulus of the material was insufficient to provide effective support, limiting its role to a filler for the defect. With ongoing advancements in 3D printing technology and materials, PEEK has emerged as a promising option for repairing irregular defects, such as those of the skull and spine [28]. Indeed, personalized 3D-printed PEEK materials offer a novel solution for reconstructing facial curved bones, providing several advantages: (1) Excellent biocompatibility; (2) Lightweight and high mechanical strength; (3) An elastic modulus closely approximating that of cortical bone, allowing effective stress distribution and reducing bone resorption; (4) Stable performance; (5) Customizability to restore craniomaxillofacial bone contours; (6) Satisfactory imaging compatibility without artifact interference, facilitating postoperative CT evaluation. In this study, the use of PEEK to repair defects at the lower edge of the mandible resulted in satisfactory outcomes, with no complications such as implant breakage, rejection, or infection at the donor site, and primary healing was achieved. However, the indications for PEEK use must be strictly defined. Although PEEK materials exhibit high strength, they lack the osteoinductive and osteogenic properties essential for bone grafts, and their inherent bone conduction capabilities are limited. Consequently, the use of PEEK materials in the maxillofacial region remains restricted to areas that do not bear occlusal forces, such as the periorbital region, zygomaticomaxillary area, and lower edge of the mandible. The potential for PEEK materials to replace autologous bone grafts for defect repair and withstand occlusal forces warrants further investigation.

Repairing maxillary defects necessitates not only the reconstruction of maxillary contour but also the restoration of occlusal relationships. Implant-based fixed dentures offer patients a stable, aesthetically pleasing, comfortable, and cost-effective restorative solution. To ensure adequate stability and support for the implant, the minimum length of the implant post is typically no less than 5 mm. It is worthwhile emphasizing that previous studies have demonstrated that the average distance between the lower edge of the mandibular nerve canal and the lower edge of the mandible is 8.92 ± 1.99 mm, with the largest distance located at the mandibular second premolar and the smallest at the distal root of the mandibular first molar. This finding provides anatomical support for ASAOF preparation. However, due to the presence of individual variations, computer-aided technology was preoperatively applied for personalized surgical design. The average mandibular resection height in eight patients was 8.46 mm (range: 7.60–10.70 mm), which not only maintained a safety margin of at least 1 mm from the lower edge of the mandibular nerve canal but also ensured that the residual mandibular height was no less than 5 mm, thereby providing adequate support for the implant and ensuring both surgical success and optimal bone height for post-surgical restorative rehabilitation. However, dental implants are not recommended for patients requiring postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy due to the elevated risk of radionecrosis in the transplanted bone.

Although ASAOF was successfully applied for maxillary bone defect reconstruction in this study, several limitations remain. First, the sample size was limited, the extent of resection involving the inferior border of the mandible and the contralateral side was not fully defined, and the effects of mandibular resection on alveolar bone remodeling, periodontal ligaments, and occlusal relationships remain unclear. Furthermore, the current follow-up duration is insufficient to adequately assess the long-term success rate and potential late complications associated with PEEK implantation. To address these limitations, future studies should expand the sample size and extend the follow-up period to more comprehensively characterize long-term clinical efficacy.

Conclusion

This article presents a method that integrates ASAOF for reconstructing maxillary alveolar process defects and 3D-printed PEEK material to simultaneously repair mandibular inferior bone defects. This approach can effectively restore facial contour and occlusal relationships. PEEK material offers outstanding biocompatibility, allows for customization, and effectively repairs mandibular defects. The precise preoperative digital design, coupled with the use of an osteotomy guide, effectively preserves the mandibular nerve canal and mental foramen. Overall, this approach provides a novel option for the reconstruction of maxillary type 2a–2b alveolar process defects.
