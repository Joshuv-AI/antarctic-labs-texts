Protein corona modulates extracellular-vesicle recognition at glycan-based nanobiointerfaces
Authors: Tomas Bertok, Eduard Jane, Veronika Solovicova, Veronika Pinkova Gajdosova, Andrea Furdova, Matej Micusik, Monika Sramkova, Katarina Kozics, Jan Tkac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise surface engineering is essential for robust characterization of extracellular vesicles and their interactions with biologically relevant interfaces. In this study, Au substrates were modified with C6 self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) bearing different terminal groups after multiple cleaning protocols to optimize monolayer compactness and interfacial quality. Surface area and functionality were further enhanced by gold nanoparticle (AuNP) deposition and secondary SAM formation, enabling layer-by-layer assembly of glycan-based polysaccharide nanostructures. Interface fabrication and growth were monitored by electrochemical methods and atomic force microscopy (AFM). Extracellular vesicles (EVs) from MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells and benign RWPE-1 (control) cells were affinity isolated using magnetic microparticles, chromatographically preconcentrated, and subjected to protein-corona removal prior to analysis. The impact of the protein corona on antibody-mediated recognition was evaluated by ELISA and revealed markedly improved accessibility of EV surface markers following corona removal. Interactions of EVs with extracellular-matrix-mimicking glycan interfaces were investigated using surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Sensorgrams were analyzed using a physics-informed neural-network-assisted model to extract dissociation constants while reducing the influence of bulk refractive-index contributions typical of vesicle samples. The developed nanobiointerfaces enabled sensitive characterization of EV binding behavior and revealed distinct interaction phenotypes of malignant-derived and non-malignant-derived EVs toward glycan-based surfaces. These findings demonstrate that protein-corona composition and glycan-mediated interactions significantly influence EV recognition and highlight the potential of glycan-based nanobiointerfaces as tools for studying extracellular-vesicle biology, which may contribute to future liquid-biopsy development.

Why it matters

Extracellular vesicles are emerging as promising tools for liquid biopsy, but their interactions with biological environments remain insufficiently understood. This study demonstrates how protein-corona formation and extracellular-matrix-inspired glycan interfaces influence vesicle recognition and binding behavior. By combining rationally designed nanobiointerfaces with surface plasmon resonance and physics-informed neural-network analysis, this work provides a framework for studying extracellular-vesicle interactions that may facilitate the development of more robust biosensing platforms and liquid-biopsy technologies.

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-enclosed particles naturally released by cells that cannot replicate. Among them, exosomes represent a subtype of endosomal origin, formed as intraluminal vesicles within multivesicular bodies that fuse with the plasma membrane and are subsequently released from the cell.1,2 Small EVs analyzed in this study fall predominantly within the 30- to 150-nm size range. They were first described in the 1980s in a maturing reticulocyte system as vesicles that carried away obsolete, waste proteins.3 Over time, they have emerged as key mediators of intercellular communication by transporting lipids, proteins, nucleic acids (mRNA, microRNAs [miRNAs], long non-coding RNAs [lncRNAs], and, in some cases, DNA fragments), and metabolites.4 In the context of tumor biology, malignant-derived EVs carry oncogenic cargoes and modulate the tumor microenvironment, promoting angiogenesis, immune suppression, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, pre-metastatic niche formation, and therapy resistance.5,6,7,8,9 Because the molecular cargo (proteins, RNAs, and lipids) reflects the phenotype of the parent cells, EVs represent valuable sources of potential biomarkers. For example, subtype-specific proteomic and miRNA profiles have been demonstrated in breast malignant-derived EVs, supporting their application in molecular stratification and liquid biopsy.1,4,10,11 Beyond internal cargo, a key consideration is the “protein corona.”12,13,14,15 Once EVs are released into the bloodstream, they become coated with plasma proteins, changing their surface identity, receptor engagement, and uptake dynamics. While much work has focused on nanoparticle coronas, similar principles apply to membrane vesicles, altering EV distribution and internalization pathways (e.g., endocytosis, phagocytosis, membrane fusion), thus influencing both biology and biomarker availability (e.g., in the case of membrane-associated ligands). Interactions between EVs and the ECM are increasingly recognized: EV surface integrins, proteases (such as matrix metalloproteinases [MMPs]), and glycoconjugates bind ECM components, facilitating EV adherence, local matrix remodeling, and tumor cell migration or establishment of a supportive niche, thereby modulating the mechanical properties and stiffness of the tumor microenvironment.16,17,18,19,20 Since the ECM is both a barrier and a conduit for metastasis, EV-ECM interplay may represent a potential therapeutic axis. However, whether malignant-derived EVs exhibit enhanced affinity toward isolated ECM glycans remains unclear and may depend on surface accessibility of glycan-binding motifs rather than their biological abundance. Together, the biogenesis, uptake, biomarker potential, and microenvironment crosstalk of EVs make them central players in tumor progression as well as appropriate targets for diagnostics and therapy.21

Liquid biopsy refers to the analysis of malignant-derived materials (circulating tumor DNA [ctDNA], circulating tumor cells [CTCs], and EVs, including small EVs) in body fluids (blood, urine, saliva) as non-invasive or minimally invasive alternatives to tissue biopsies.22,23 Because EVs are secreted abundantly and constantly present in body fluids, they are promising sources of biomarkers for early detection, monitoring of disease progression, intra-tumor heterogeneity, and therapy response. In breast cancer (BCa), multiple studies and meta-analyses highlight promising metrics: a recent systematic review of 31 articles with 3,778 breast cancer patients vs. 2,722 controls found a pooled sensitivity of 0.89, specificity of 0.87, and area under the curve from ROC (AUC from receiver operating characteristic curve) of 0.94 for overall exosome biomarkers.24 For example, exosomal miR-21, miR-155, and miR-1246 have been repeatedly identified as elevated in BCa. In a meta-analysis, overexpression of miR-1246 and miR-155 was associated with a worse progression-free survival rate, and panels of biomarkers improved overall survival prediction.25,26,27 Another review notes that exosomes more reliably reflect the tumor biological fingerprint than ctDNA alone because of the protective lipid bilayer, richer content (glycoproteins, lipids, RNAs), and ability to provide insights into active intercellular signaling. However, translation to clinical practice encounters a number of barriers: heterogeneity of EV populations, lack of standardized isolation protocols (reproducibility issues), variability of marker panels, and variable EV quality together with the cost and time burdens of current methods.28,29,30,31,32 Importantly for BCa, EV-based liquid biopsy may help detect minimal residual disease, monitor therapy resistance, and stratify patients according to EV cargo profiles.33 Proteomic studies comparing whole-cell vs. EV profiles show that EVs bear subtype-specific signatures (invasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, drug resistance) and, hence, may assist in subtype stratification and personalized medicine.

Given the promising biomarker potential of EVs, the analytical challenge becomes how to detect, quantify, and characterize them rapidly, in a clinically relevant format and with sufficient analytical parameters. This is where biosensing technologies, especially those leveraging nanomaterials and nanocomposites, come to the fore. Modern biosensors incorporate Au/Ag nanoparticles, graphene, graphene-oxide, MXenes, quantum dots, plasmonic nanostructures, nanozymes, and field-effect transistor (FET) platforms to achieve extremely low limits of detection, small sample volumes, multiplexing, and rapid readout.34,35,36,37 For example, a recent review of nanotechnology-enabled biosensors for exosomal RNA/protein detection grouped sensors according to modality: fluorescence, colorimetric, electrical/electrochemical, plasmonics, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), photothermal- and ICP-mass spectrometry (inductively coupled plasma MS)-based systems, highlighting that many of these sensors achieved limits of detection orders of magnitude better than commonly used ELISA as a conventional method.34,38 While many of these technologies focus on quantification of exosome abundance or detection of individual protein biomarkers such as CD9, CD63, and CD81, considerably less attention has been devoted to characterizing functional interactions of exosomes with ECM components. Such interactions are highly relevant to tumor progression, tissue invasion, and cellular communication. In this context, glycan-based sensing interfaces may provide complementary information beyond conventional biomarker quantification by probing the interaction phenotype of EVs. The present study therefore combines EV characterization with investigation of biologically relevant interactions occurring at extracellular-matrix-mimicking glycan interfaces. In summary, this study presents a workflow in which affinity-enriched EVs are analyzed using glycan-based nanobiointerfaces to investigate how protein corona and extracellular-matrix-derived glycans influence their recognition and binding behavior.39 Such functional characterization may complement conventional molecular-profiling approaches and contribute to the future development of EV-based liquid-biopsy technologies by enabling not only rapid quantification of EVs but also analysis of their affinity toward ECM polysaccharide components, such as heparan sulfate.40 This convergence of EV biology, liquid biopsy, and nanomaterial-driven sensing holds great potential for early-stage cancer detection, real-time monitoring, companion diagnostics, and personalized oncology.41,42 In addition to the above, this work also focuses on the effect of the protein corona on the recognition of surface ligands, important, inter alia, for the EV uptake.43,44 Finally, this work validates the applicability of artificial neural networks to the extraction of dissociation constants from SPR sensorgrams that are poorly resolvable by standard commercial analysis tools. According to the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2023) recommendations, the term “exosome” should be reserved for vesicles whose endosomal biogenesis has been experimentally demonstrated. Since the present study did not directly verify the endosomal origin of the isolated vesicles, the more general term “EVs” is used throughout the manuscript, while the designation “small EVs” is employed where reference to the isolated size fraction is appropriate. The term “exosome” is retained only when referring to commercial reagents or previously published literature using this terminology.45

Materials and methods

Reagents and instruments

All basic inorganic and organic chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Merck, USA (unless indicated otherwise). Electrochemical supplies, including polycrystalline Au, counter Pt, and reference Ag/AgCl/3M KCl electrodes, were obtained from BASi Analytical, USA. SPR accessories and buffers, including sensor chips (bare Au from SIA kit and CM5 chips), were purchased from Cytiva, USA. Ultrapure water (0.055 μS) was used in all solutions in the study. After preparation, the solutions were filtered using a sterile 0.22-μm filter. Ethanol used in this study was of UV-visible (UV-vis) quality.

All electrochemical procedures were run on a potentiostat/galvanostat Autolab PGSTAT 128 N (Ecochemie, Utrecht, the Netherlands) in a cell with Ag/AgCl/sat. KCl reference and counter Pt electrodes. The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurement was run at 50 different frequencies (from 0.1 Hz up to 100 kHz) under Nova Software 1.10. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) signals were recorded using a Thermo Scientific Nexsa G2 Surface Analysis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) equipped with a micro-focused, monochromatic Al Ka X-ray source (1,486.68 eV). An X-ray beam of size 400 mm was used. The spectra were acquired in the constant analyzer energy mode with pass energy of 200 eV for the survey. Narrow regions were collected using the pass energy of 50 eV. Charge compensation was achieved with the system dual-beam flood gun. Thermo Scientific Avantage software, version 6.10.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for digital acquisition and data processing. Spectral calibration was determined by using the automated calibration routine and the internal Au, Ag, and Cu standards supplied with the NEXSA G2 system. The surface compositions (in atom %) were determined by considering the integrated peak areas of the detected atoms and the respective sensitivity factors. The fractional concentration of a particular element A was computed using the following equation:%A=IA/sA∑(In/sn)×100%,where In and sn are the integrated peak areas and the Scofield sensitivity factors corrected for the analyzer transmission, respectively. PeakForce tapping-mode atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Scan Asyst, Bruker, USA) in air was carried out on a Bioscope Catalyst instrument and Olympus IX71 microscope in conjunction with NanoScope 8.15 software and scanned using a SCANASYST-AIR silicon tip on a nitride lever (Bruker, USA, with f0 = 50-90 kHz and k = 0.4 N m−1), sharpened for a tip radius of 2 nm. Samples containing EVs were analyzed using a NanoSight NS300 Instrument (NanoSight, UK). Software settings for all samples were as follows: detection threshold, 5; blue 488-nm laser beam was applied to the loaded sample; video was recorded five times, for 60 s at a frame rate of 25 frames s−1. The video was analyzed using NTA v.3.4 software. In this study, common CM5 (i.e., carboxymethyl dextran-modified SPR chip) and bare Au SPR chips modified using 5 mM alkanethiol ethanolic solutions by overnight incubation at ambient temperature in dark were used. All surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments were performed using a Biacore X100 instrument (GE Healthcare, Tiefenbach, Austria). All SPR experiments were performed using independently prepared sensor surfaces. Unless stated otherwise, measurements were carried out in duplicate to verify experimental reproducibility. Representative sensorgrams are presented throughout the manuscript because duplicate measurements showed only negligible variation in both response profiles and extracted kinetic parameters.

Human cell lines

Human breast adenocarcinoma MDA-MB-231 cells (ATCC HTB-26) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; 4.5 g L−1 glucose) supplemented with 10% exosome-depleted fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The cells were maintained in medium supplemented with exosome-depleted FBS throughout cultivation and were not transferred to serum-free medium prior to conditioned medium collection. Conditioned medium was collected after 72 h of conditioning and immediately processed for EV isolation. All media and cell culture supplements were purchased from Gibco BRL (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Paisley, UK). The non-malignant RWPE-1 cell line was used as a source of EVs representing a non-malignant epithelial phenotype. It was not intended to serve as a tissue-matched control for the MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell line. Rather than comparing breast cancer with normal breast epithelium, the present study focused on evaluating differences in the recognition and binding behavior of EVs originating from malignant and non-malignant cellular phenotypes using glycan-based nanobiointerfaces.

EV processing

PBS buffer, pH 7.4 without calcium magnesium phenol red by Gibco and PBST (phosphate-buffered saline 10 × with 0.5% TWEEN20) by Gibco were used as dilution and washing buffers in EV size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) experiments. For ELISA plate (Nunc MaxiSorp, Thermo Fisher Scientific), antibody coating carbonate-bicarbonate coating buffer (0.795 g L−1 Na2CO3 and 1.465 g L−1 NaHCO3, both from Sigma-Merck) in PBS was used. Carbo-Free blocking solution (Vectorlabs, USA) was used as a blocking solution in ELISA. Antibodies were selected based on SPR and ELISA experiments (data not shown). Anti-CD63 (Abcam, UK; ab1318) and anti-CD81 (Abcam, UK; ab79559) were used as captures. As a detection antibody in direct ELISA, biotinylated anti-CD81 was used. Biotinylation was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol for biotinylation kit from Abcam (ab201795). For affinity-based isolation of small EVs from cell culture media, MagCapture Exosome Isolation Kit PS Ver. 2 (FUJIFILM Biosciences) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Tangential flow filtration (TFF) concentrator from HansaBioMed Life Sciences and SEC columns (PURE-EVs) were used for the purification of EVs. NTA (NanoSight NS300 instrument, Malvern, UK) was performed to determine the size distribution and concentration of the isolated EV preparations. NTA measurements were used solely for physical characterization of the particle population and were not intended to identify the detected nanoparticles as EVs. Their identity as EVs was inferred from the affinity-based isolation strategy together with the detection of characteristic EV surface markers. Throughout this manuscript, the isolated vesicles are referred to as “small EVs” in accordance with the MISEV2023 recommendations. The term “exosome” is retained only when referring to commercially available isolation reagents or previously published literature using that terminology.

DFT and adsorption kinetics modeling

The dipole moments of aminohexanethiol, hexanethiol, and hydroxyhexanethiol were calculated using ORCA 6.1.0 software. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirmed that the employed thiols exhibit different molecular dipole moments, consistent with the differences in surface packing discussed below (for details, see supplemental information).

To isolate non-Langmuir (1:1 binding) artifacts from the sensor response, we implemented a residual-learning model (physics-informed neural network [PINN]) in which the measured signal is decomposed into an ideal Langmuir kinetic component and a data-driven artifact term. The ideal binding response was computed using the standard Langmuir model parameterized by (ka, kd, Rmax). A one-dimensional convolutional neural network (CNN) was trained to estimate only the deviation from this ideal behavior, i.e., the artifact residual. Thus, the observed signal R(t) was expressed asR(t)=RLangmuir(ka,kd,Rmax,t)+ACNN(t),where ACNN(t) represents the learned artifact component. The CNN consisted of stacked Conv1D layers with ReLU (rectified linear unit) activations followed by a linear output layer to ensure unrestricted residual prediction. During training, the loss function minimized the error between the reconstructed signal (Langmuir + CNN residual) and the measured data while applying a penalty on long-timescale components. This constraint forced the network to capture only non-kinetic distortions and prevented it from absorbing true kinetic behavior. In addition, a sigmoidal coefficient, α, was added to the loss function to provide a warm-up at the start of neural-network training.Loss=MSE+α(ACNNpenalty+mean(ACNN)),where MSE is a mean square error. This formulation ensured that the physical binding kinetics remained intact while artifacts were flexibly captured by the network.46,47,48,49

Results and discussion

Au surface cleaning procedure

Before constructing glycan-based nanobiointerfaces, it was necessary to establish a reproducible and defect-free Au surface suitable for the formation of highly compact self-assembled monolayers (SAMs). Since the quality of the underlying Au substrate critically determines subsequent nanoparticle immobilization, glycan presentation, and ultimately the reproducibility of SPR measurements, we first optimized the Au pretreatment procedure. The following experiments therefore established the optimal pretreatment protocol used throughout the remainder of this study. The geometric area of the Au electrodes (AuEs) with a diameter of 1.6 mm is 2.01 mm2. Standard cleaning includes (obligatory) electrochemical cleaning (i.e., polishing and stripping procedures) and, optionally, alumina polishing and cleaning with a hot piranha solution. If alumina polishing is omitted, the RCT parameter (charge transfer resistance) obtained from EIS measurements (fitted using the Randles equivalent circuit) increases from 81.5 to 504 Ω, indicating substantial surface contamination. When the electrochemically active surface area is determined using the Randles-Sevcik equation, suboptimal results are obtained, amounting to approximately 4.5% of the geometric area in both cases. To obtain more reliable values, the real Au surface area was calculated from the reduction peak of Au oxides.50,51 Thus, alumina polishing contributes not only to cleaning but also to an increase in the effective surface area of the Au electrode. Treatment with hot piranha solution resulted in (1) an increase in the RCT parameter of approximately 285%; (2) the appearance of nanostructures with a size of approximately 50 nm (detected by AFM; Figure 1a), which, however, do not exhibit a catalytic effect toward the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) according to Tafel analysis (−40.2 mV·dec−1 vs. −55.9 mV·dec−1 for Au vs. Au + piranha; Figure S1); and (3) no significant change in surface wettability, which is 66° for clean Au, 46° for Au covered with 6-mercaptohexanol (6-MH; polar surface), and 68° for Au + piranha. Moreover, when the electrode is stored in a clean, dust-free environment, it remains stable for at least 10 days after cleaning without any change in RCT. However, a negative effect of the piranha treatment was observed during the polishing procedure, where a second reduction peak may appear. The reduction of Au oxides occurs at a potential of 0.96 V (vs. Ag/AgCl/sat. KCl, with a maximum at 1.15 V), while a second, well-defined peak with a maximum at approximately 0.75 V emerges and increases with each subsequent scan (Figure 1b). XPS measurements revealed a higher oxygen content on the surface (O1s): 8.3% for Au and 14.4% for Au + piranha. However, this increase is largely related to organic oxygen on the surface deriving from adventitious carbon (Figure S2; Table S1). Au4f signals showed no oxidation and, in both cases, the Au is in a metallic state, where the Au4f7/2 signal is at ca. 84.0 eV with the typical spin orbital splitting of 3.7 eV (Figure S2).52 If a SAM layer is formed on a surface where the second reduction peak is present, the RCT parameter is approximately 45% lower than for a surface without the second reduction peak. This leads to the hypothesis that domains are present on the surface that hinder SAM formation and contribute to defects in the layer.50,53 Formation of compact and defect-free SAMs critically depends on the cleanliness and pretreatment of the underlying Au surface, which directly affects electron transfer properties and reproducibility of biosensing interfaces.54 To verify this hypothesis, non-faradaic EIS was employed. For Au, a linear Nyquist plot (RQ fit) is obtained after SAM formation, whereas, for Au + piranha, a semicircle appears (R[QR] fit), indicating the presence of defects in the SAM layer through which current can flow (leakage paths). These defects can be caused, for example, by the adsorption of impurities or ions (shown as a semicircle with lower resistance compared to large high values of several MΩ to TΩ basically heading toward infinity; Figure S3). The RCT values after SAM formation are the same, despite different starting RCTs for both surfaces, leading to an increase in RCT of 563% and 308% for AuE with single and two reduction peaks, respectively. From the perspective of SAM formation, the densest monolayer was obtained using hexane-6-thiol (up to 88% of theoretical coverage, ∼4 molecules per nm2), with the packing density decreasing upon the introduction of functional groups at the surface due to electrostatic repulsion. ORCA 6.1 software was used to model the dipole moments of the employed thiols in vacuum (and upon solvation in ethanol): 6-MH = 2.32 D (3.42 D), 6-aminohexanethiol (6-AH) = 1.83 D (2.77 D), and hexane-6-thiol = 1.84 D (3.03 D). Impedimetric characterization

It should be noted that electrochemical measurements and SPR experiments were performed on different Au substrates. The electrochemical platform was primarily used as a rapid optimization tool for evaluation of cleaning procedures, SAM quality, and surface modification strategies, whereas the final analytical measurements were performed on commercial SPR sensor chips. Although minor differences in roughness and geometry between these platforms cannot be excluded, both systems employed identical surface chemistry, including thiol-based SAMs, gold nanoparticle (AuNP) deposition, and polysaccharide immobilization. Therefore, the electrochemical results were used mainly for comparative optimization of the interface architecture, while the ultimate validation of the sensing strategy was conducted using SPR. EIS has been used to study the nanobiointerface for EV sensing using polysaccharide-lectin binding as a model system, since exosomes internalization was shown to rely on cell surface heparan sulfate proteoglycans,40,43 possibly also significantly affected by protein corona formed in the complex environment of ECM.20,44 For different immobilization strategies of polysaccharides, two distinct SAM layers were tested: 6-MH and 6-AH for Schiff base formation using the reducing end of a polysaccharide chain (0.5 mg mL−1 of the ligand; incubation for 2 h at ambient temperature, then at 5°C overnight, followed by washing with 100 mM carbonate-bicarbonate buffer pH 9.5 at 5°C; addition of 5 mg mL−1 of NaBH4 or NaCNBH3 in 100 mM carbonate-bicarbonate buffer pH 9.5 at 5°C, V = 50 μL, and subsequent incubation for 30 min). For a 6-AH SAM layer (6-AH), initial RCT could not be estimated due to strong electrostatic attraction with hexacyanoferrate (5 mM [Fe(CN)6]3− and 5 mM [Fe(CN)6]4−) mediator. After modification with carboxymethyl dextran (CMD), RCT of 51.3 ± 8.5 Ω was obtained. For the 6-MH SAM layer, the RCT is 854 ± 214 Ω (consistent with the formation of a more extended polymer brush rather than a compact monolayer). This value increased to 1700 ± 504 Ω after CMD immobilization (increase by ∼99%; this could not be calculated for 6-AH because of unavailability of the RCT value for the bare 6-AH SAM layer). Finally, the response toward lectin concanavalin A (Con A, mannose specific but also capable of CMD binding; 1 mg mL−1) was measured.56 The increase in RCT (%) was estimated for 6-AH and 6-MH as 231 ± 37.9 and 73.5 ± 9.43, respectively—significantly higher for 6-AH/CMD. Thiol surface density for 6-AH was estimated as 4.28 ± 0.74 (relatively close to the theoretical value). The significant difference in RCT parameter could be attributed to the availability of the hydroxyl groups for lectin biorecognition and their amount on the surface, since the composition and stock solution concentration were the same for both 6-MH and 6-AH. Based on these results, 6-MH was chosen to provide a more compact and accessible layered glycan interface for further experiments. To increase the surface area of the biosensor, we applied an underlying SAM layer (SAM1 composed of 6-AH) for a subsequent immobilization of AuNPs as a scaffold for the second SAM (SAM2 composed of 6-MH) layer formation. Although EIS showed no difference between Au-SAM(6-AH) and Au-SAM(6-AH)-AuNPs(40 nm), a clear difference was observed for electrochemical (active) area determination (i.e.,2.47 vs. 3.35 mm2, respectively; ∼36% increase). Another difference is the formation of the second SAM layer (6-MH), which yielded RCT of only 71.9 Ω (in comparison with 854Ω ± 214 Ω for bare Au-SAM(6-MH)). These results suggest that the Au surface created by AuNPs becomes the new electrode surface conductively connected to the bare Au electrode (basically enhancing the electron tunneling as opposed to the SAM(OH)-modified Au interface, notably much thinner). However, the second SAM layer is far less dense and, hence, offers much lower initial RCT. Still, the occurrence of a Nyquist semicircle indicates the formation of the second layer. Prior to the AuNP size optimization for SPR chip preparation, the use of 40-nm AuNPs for Con A biorecognition was evaluated using EIS to observe a potentially positive future effect.

EV characterization using NTA

For the primary selection of cancer cell lines, the breast cancer cell lines Hs578T (HTB-126), BT747 (HTB-20), and MDA-MB-231 were selected as positive controls, while RWPE-1 was used as the control cell line. Following the isolation procedure detailed above, only the MDA-MB-231-derived samples passed the quality control based on NTA (Figure S4). The hydrodynamic diameter of MDA-MB-231-derived EVs was 141 ± 1.3 nm, which was smaller than that of RWPE-1-derived EVs (155 ± 1.2 nm). NTA confirmed the presence of nanoparticles with a size distribution consistent with small EV preparations. Their identification as EVs was based on the combined isolation strategy and marker analysis rather than on NTA measurements alone.

Detaching the protein corona from EVs

After EV isolation from cell culture media using the MagCapture Exosome Isolation Kit PS Ver. 2 (FUJIFILM Biosciences), the next step in the process of EV characterization would be the use of TFF (TFF-Easy) for EV pre-concentration. The TFF-Easy filtration device contains polysulfone hollow fibers with a pore size of 5 nm; this facilitates the pre-concentration of EVs and the removal of small proteins (usually below 100 kDa) and molecules from the diluted matrices. The preconcentrated EVs are recovered from the filtration unit using a syringe. Following the manufacturer’s instructions and protocol, we processed 20 mL of the sample using the TFF-Easy concentrator, resulting in 2 mL of concentrated sample (10×), which was subsequently transferred onto SEC columns for EV purification. SEC is widely recognized as one of the most efficient techniques for isolating and purifying EVs from various matrices. In this study, we combined this method with the high specificity of magnetic bead-based affinity (antibody-based) isolation to afford highly reliable and robust data of malignant-derived EV characterization. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, the SEC columns were preconditioned before each sample, and the EV isolation was performed using the EV Isolation Fast Protocol. The resulting 2 mL of the EV-containing fraction (SEC1) was divided into equal portions. One portion of 1 mL of EVs, to which PBS was added, was used as a control. To the second portion of 1 mL of EVs, 1 mL of 3 M NaCl was added, resulting in a final concentration of 1.5 M in the microcentrifuge tube, as published previously20,57 to observe the effect of the NaCl on the presence/absence of protein corona. A sample where EVs were treated with NaCl was incubated for 5 min. The samples thus prepared were subsequently transferred onto the SEC column again. After collecting the EV-enriched fractions from PBS control (SEC2) and NaCl-treated EVs (SEC3), a direct ELISA assay was performed. ELISA was already optimized for determination of the key parameters: sample volume and dilution, blocking reagent, choice of enzyme label and its dilution, and incubation time with the substrate until the reaction was terminated. Each capture antibody (Ab, 100 μL/well with concentration of 4 μg mL−1) was added to a microtiter ELISA plate, placed on a shaker for 30 min, and then incubated at 4°C overnight. Subsequently, 300 μL of Carbo-Free blocking solution was added to block the plate surface and incubated with gentle shaking at 37°C for 1 h . After blocking, the wells were washed three times with PBST. The EVs isolated by SEC were diluted 10×, and 50 μL of sample was added per well and incubated at 37°C overnight. PBS buffer served as a control. The next day, the wells were washed three times with 150 μL of PBST, and 100 μL of 1-μg mL−1 biotinylated anti-CD81 in PBST was added to each well and incubated with gentle shaking at 37°C for 1 h, followed again by three washes with PBST. Poly-HRP enzyme (horseradish peroxidase, diluted 1:5,000 in PBST) was then added, incubated with gentle shaking at 37°C for 1 h, and followed by three washes with PBST. To generate the signal, 1-Step Ultra TMB-ELISA solution was added and allowed to act for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 0.1 M sulfuric acid. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a multi-mode reader (Synergy HT BioTek, VT, USA).

SPR measurements

A layer-by-layer strategy for polysaccharide immobilization was employed, consisting of SAM1 and SAM2 separated by a monolayer of AuNPs, which were subsequently functionalized with carboxymethyl dextran (CMD) or, at a later stage, heparan sulfate (HS). The role of the OH-terminated SAM2 (6-MH) layer was investigated by deliberately omitting this layer from the surface architecture, thereby enabling direct attachment of CMD to the AuNPs. The inclusion of SAM2 significantly enhanced polysaccharide immobilization in comparison with bare AuNPs; this was attributed to intermolecular hydrogen bonding, resulting in an approximately 12-fold increase in binding. Furthermore, when Con A (c = 1 mg mL−1) was employed as a specific analyte, an approximately 26-fold higher EIS response was observed for CMD immobilized via the SAM2 layer.

Nevertheless, EIS exhibits limited applicability to quantitative analysis in this context and was, therefore, primarily used for stepwise surface optimization prior to implementation in more reproducible and robust analytical SPR platforms (as an orthogonal characterization technique to monitor individual stages of interface construction). To quantitatively assess the Con A–CMD interaction, several surface architectures were evaluated: (1) a commercially available CMD-modified CM5 Cytiva chip (3D), (2) Au/SAM(6-MH) (2D), (3) Au/SAM(6-MH)/CMD (2D), and (4) Au/SAM1/AuNPs (40 nm)/SAM2/CMD. The dissociation constants (KD) determined from single-cycle kinetics (SCK) experiments were 1.87 × 10−8 M, 4.08 × 10−9 M, 5.85 × 10−9 M, and 3.12 × 10−10 M, respectively, demonstrating that the nanostructured 3D biointerface provided the highest biorecognition performance—also with respect to the Rmax parameter, which was at least ∼5-fold higher in the case of the 3D-nanostructured interface than all the rest, i.e., 1,000 RU compared to 150–200 RU.

The final step in the optimization of the nanostructured biointerface focused on the effect of AuNP size. Figure 2 presents SCK measurements for the 3D nanointerface incorporating AuNPs with diameters of 10, 15, and 40 nm together with AFM images of the AuNP-modified surface (surface density ≈ 4 particles per 500 nm2). Notably, the most favorable kinetic parameters were obtained for 15-nm AuNPs in comparison with those with diameters of 10 and 40 nm. The data obtained needed fitting using a heterogeneous ligand model, as ligand orientation plays a critical role in CMD-mediated interactions. Accordingly, two dissociation constants (KD) corresponding to two distinct Rmax values were obtained. In this context, a higher Rmax, associated with a specific KD, indicates a more favorable biorecognition process, reflected by a higher signal amplitude. Interestingly, the 15-nm AuNPs did not produce an intermediate response between the 10- and 40-nm nanoparticles, indicating that the sensing behavior is not governed solely by nanoparticle size or total surface area. We hypothesize that the nanoparticle diameter influences the spatial organization, orientation, and steric accessibility of immobilized glycan chains. Since lectins recognize only surface-accessible glycan epitopes, differences in glycan presentation may significantly affect binding efficiency. This observation further suggests that nanobiointerface optimization should be tailored to individual glycan-binding partners rather than assuming a universally optimal nanoparticle size. In the subsequent step, the 3D nanostructured surface was functionalized with HS instead of CMD. Fibronectin was employed as a specific analyte, as it acts as a critical mediator of the cell-matrix assembly, tissue fibrosis, and tumor progression. Fibronectin is a high-molecular-mass glycoprotein (272 kDa; UniProt: P02751) containing two heparin-binding domains and exhibiting affinity toward collagen, actin, and fibrin. On this premise, malignant-derived EVs are expected to exhibit a higher binding affinity toward HS (i.e., HS-modified interfaces) than to non-malignant controls. This assumption was based on previous reports describing the involvement of HS proteoglycans in EV uptake and trafficking. Nevertheless, interactions between EVs and HS are governed by multiple physicochemical factors, including membrane composition, glycosylation patterns, protein-corona formation, steric accessibility of binding motifs, and vesicle surface organization. Therefore, the measured SPR response should not be interpreted as a direct measure of EV abundance, but rather as an integrated reflection of the accessibility and presentation of HS-interacting motifs on the vesicle surface.

To benchmark fibronectin binding to HS, the lectin Con A was used as a reference analyte on a previously optimized SPR chip surface, namely SAM1/15-nm AuNPs/SAM2/HS. Notably, upon refitting the data using a heterogeneous ligand model, fibronectin exhibited higher Rmax values but lower KD values than Con A. Specifically, for fibronectin, KD1 = 4.232 × 10−11 M (Rmax1 = 1,719) and KD2 = 3.275 × 10−11 M (Rmax2 = 992.5) were obtained, whereas, for Con A, KD1 = 1.061 × 10−9 M (Rmax1 = 284.4) and KD2 = 3.577 × 10−13 M (Rmax2 = 936.5), respectively. The presence of two KD values in SPR measurements reflects two distinct ligand geometries on the sensor surface with regard to their accessibility. The observed higher Rmax accompanied by a simultaneous lower KD of fibronectin than Con A can be attributed to differences in binding stoichiometry, multivalency, and surface accessibility rather than to intrinsic affinity. A higher Rmax often indicates a greater effective binding capacity of the surface, which may arise from multivalent interactions.

We further investigated the effect of the protective PB-HAT medium described in the literature (100 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, supplemented with 20 mM HEPES, 25 mM trehalose, and 0.2% albumin). NTA was employed to determine the concentration of MDA-MB-231-derived EVs (2.32 × 108 ± 1.32 × 107 particles mL−1) and to assess the changes following buffer exchange to PB-HAT (2.22 × 108 ± 8.05 × 105 particles mL−1), which were not statistically significant. PB-HAT alone produced a low apparent particle count by NTA of 9.60 × 106 ± 8.05 × 105 particles mL−1, which is approximately one order of magnitude lower and within the statistical variability relative to EV-containing samples.

Interestingly, PB-HAT generated a relatively high SPR response, exhibiting significant interaction with the sensor chip surface in comparison with MDA-MB-231-derived EVs dispersed in PB-HAT. These observations suggest that the formation of a protein corona may influence not only EV stability but also their interaction with the surrounding environment (in vitro and in vivo) and the sensing interface.58 EVs dispersed in PB-HAT were, accordingly, diluted to the same concentration as EVs devoid of a protein corona (in PB); i.e., 2 × 108 particles mL−1. During SCK measurements, EVs in PB-HAT exhibited a slightly higher Rmax than corona-free EVs; however, the latter generated an SPR response comparable to that of 25-μg mL−1 bovine serum albumin (BSA). The mean hydrodynamic diameters of both EV populations did not differ significantly; nevertheless, corona-free EVs presented as larger aggregates with diameters in the hundreds of nanometers, indicating reduced colloidal stability. One possible explanation for the increased aggregation tendency observed in corona-free EV preparations is a change in their surface physicochemical properties following corona removal, including alterations in surface charge. Although this hypothesis is consistent with the observed behavior, zeta-potential measurements were not performed in the present study and, therefore, the underlying mechanism could not be verified experimentally. Future studies integrating glycan-based interaction analyses with zeta-potential measurements may provide further insight into how protein-corona composition influences EV surface properties, colloidal stability, and recognition at glycan-based nanobiointerfaces. From these observations, several implications may be considered. The EV protein corona may either suppress or enhance specific EV recognition and, therefore, needs to be carefully evaluated with regard to the intended analytical application. Our observations suggest that corona-associated proteins (attached to EVs in various biological fluids) may influence interactions of EVs with biological interfaces.

To examine the latter hypothesis, the ELISA assay described above was employed to compare EVs dispersed in PB-HAT, corona-free EVs, PB-HAT alone, and PBS as a blank. Anti-CD63 and anti-CD81 antibodies were used as capture antibodies. PB-HAT alone produced slightly higher responses than the PBS blank, most likely due to non-specific adsorption or interaction of albumin present in the buffer. The low particle count detected in PB-HAT by NTA cannot be attributed specifically to EV contamination, as NTA does not distinguish EVs from protein aggregates or other non-vesicular particles. Notably, while both antibodies yielded clearly higher signals for corona-free EVs prepared by the chromatographic methods described above, anti-CD81 provided a substantially greater discrimination between the two differently processed EV samples. Specifically, the response of PB-HAT-dispersed EVs toward anti-CD81 was comparable to the blank level, whereas this did not pertain for anti-CD63, for which the response of PB-HAT-dispersed EVs remained higher than that of PB-HAT alone (note that using anti-CD63 as the capture antibody and anti-CD81 as the detection antibody yields mixed signals). The observed improvement of CD63/CD81 recognition following chromatographic removal of the protein corona highlights an important analytical and biological aspect of EV characterization. These results indicate that the corona does not merely represent an external contamination layer but actively modulates accessibility of membrane-associated biomarkers. Consequently, protein-corona formation may substantially affect both immunochemical detection and biological interactions of EVs within the extracellular environment. This observation has direct implications for liquid-biopsy applications, where differences in sample preparation may significantly influence analytical performance and biomarker detectability in future liquid-biopsy workflows. Ultimately, the insights regarding the potentially adverse effects of the protein corona were incorporated into the final SCK protocol employing the 3D nanostructured chip, in which EVs derived from both cell lines were deprived of their protein corona prior to analysis. RWPE-1-derived EVs produced a significantly higher signal (Rmax); however, due to a pronounced bulk contribution, reliable fitting of the corresponding sensorgrams proved to be challenging, which represents a common limitation in EV analysis. It should be noted that the composition of the protein corona may depend on the surrounding biological environment, including the composition of the culture medium or biofluid. The corona-removal procedure employed in this study was intended to minimize these environmental influences and improve the accessibility of surface glycans and membrane proteins for subsequent interaction studies. Although ELISA measurements demonstrated substantially improved accessibility of surface markers following corona removal, the present methodology does not allow quantitative verification of complete corona removal. Interestingly, contrary to our initial expectation, RWPE-1-derived EVs generated substantially higher SPR responses than MDA-MB-231-derived EVs. While this observation may appear counterintuitive considering the reported role of HS in tumor progression and EV uptake, it is important to recognize that SPR measures the overall interaction phenotype of the vesicle population rather than a single receptor-ligand interaction. The observed behavior may originate from differences in membrane glycosylation, steric shielding of HS-binding domains, protein composition, surface charge distribution, or accessibility of membrane-associated ligands. In particular, malignant-derived EVs frequently exhibit altered glycosylation patterns and increased presentation of heavily glycosylated membrane proteins, which may partially mask HS-binding domains despite their biological relevance. However, the molecular determinants underlying this observation remain unresolved and warrant dedicated glycomic and proteomic investigation in the future (e.g., lectin-based glycoprofiling or charge measurements). Consequently, the present results should be interpreted as evidence of distinct surface phenotypes between malignant and non-malignant EVs rather than a simple difference in binding affinity toward HS. An additional limitation should be considered when comparing EV populations normalized according to NTA. NTA provides particle concentration and size distribution but does not provide information regarding membrane protein abundance, glycosylation status, density of accessible ligands, or biochemical composition of individual vesicles. Therefore, equal particle concentrations do not necessarily correspond to equal numbers of accessible HS-binding sites. The observed differences in SPR response may thus reflect variations in EV surface composition and ligand accessibility rather than differences in particle concentration alone. From the linear fit of the calibration curves for both RWPE-1 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines, the limit of detection (LOD) on the 3D nanostructured biochip was calculated based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 3. The resulting LODs were 1.8 × 105 particles mL−1 for RWPE-1-derived EVs and 2.9 × 107 particles mL−1 for MDA-MB-231-derived EVs. As noted above, at higher EV concentrations, the SPR sensorgrams exhibit a pronounced bulk effect, which impedes reliable fitting using conventional models. In the present study, SCK sensorgrams were modeled using a PINN approach, which identified a residual signal component that was not captured by the Langmuir model. The resulting association (ka) and dissociation (kd) rate constants, alongside Rmax, were subsequently used to calculate the equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) as their ratio kd/ka. KD for MDA- and RWPE-derived EVs were estimated to be approximately 1.16 × 10−8 M and 8.41 × 10−8 M for the 3D nanobiointerface based on HS. The reported KD values should be interpreted as apparent interaction parameters derived from particle concentrations rather than absolute thermodynamic dissociation constants. The equilibrium dissociation constants were determined using a PINN model, and Figures 3c and 3d show the experimental sensorgrams, the corresponding fits, and the extracted artifact components for both cases. In SPR measurements of EVs from different cell lines, a lower Rmax despite an extremely low KD can result from the effective density and orientation of functional binding sites on the EV surface. Steric hindrance, restricted epitope accessibility, and differences in molecular mass or multivalency of surface proteins can further limit the maximum signal. Mass transport limitations and surface heterogeneity of extravesicular interactome may also modulate Rmax, decoupling the observed binding capacity from intrinsic affinity.60,61 The purpose of the PINN-assisted analysis was not to replace conventional kinetic modeling but to facilitate extraction of kinetic parameters from sensorgrams affected by substantial bulk refractive-index contributions, a phenomenon frequently encountered during EV measurements. Under such conditions, standard fitting procedures often produce unstable or physically unrealistic parameter estimates. The residual-learning framework therefore acts as an auxiliary correction tool that separates slowly varying non-kinetic artifacts from the kinetic component of the response while preserving the underlying Langmuir binding model.

Conclusions

The results highlight the critical importance of Au surface cleanliness and high-quality SAM layer preparation for achieving reliable and reproducible biosensor performance. Surface pretreatment has a direct impact on probe-immobilization efficiency and binding readouts. The incorporation of AuNPs increases the effective chip surface area and simultaneously alters steric constraints at the interface, thereby influencing both immobilization density and biorecognition behavior. In the context of extracellular-vesicle sensing, it should be noted that EVs carry surface proteins involved in extracellular matrix interactions, which are linked to their role in metastatic niche formation, although, in this study, we focused on interactions with ECM-mimicking glycan interfaces, not the matrix as a whole. Finally, future diagnostic interpretations must account for the formation of a protein corona, as it can substantially modify antigen-antibody binding characteristics and thus affect analytical accuracy. It should be noted that interactions with different components of the ECM may result in distinct outcomes, primarily governed by charge density and its spatial distribution within polysaccharide molecules, as exemplified by the difference in the degree of sulfation between heparin and HS.

In this work, we introduced a PINN workflow to extract dissociation constants from SPR sensorgrams of EVs measured on an AuNP-modified gold chip functionalized with HS as the biorecognition element. While SPR has already been explored for breast-cancer-EV sensing, and plasmonic profiling of EV-heparin/HS-related interactions has been reported in other disease contexts, we did not identify any prior studies that combine (1) PINN-based, physics-constrained kinetic extraction; (2) a heparan-sulfate interface; and (3) AuNP-enhanced SPR readout to differentiate breast-malignant-derived EVs from benign controls. EV SPR measurements are particularly susceptible to bulk refractive-index contributions, non-specific adsorption, and slow baseline distortions originating from the complex composition and colloidal nature of vesicle samples. These effects may obscure binding-related responses and complicate extraction of physically meaningful kinetic parameters. Therefore, a PINN was used to isolate non-kinetic signal components while constraining the fit through a mechanistic Langmuir binding model. This hybrid approach preserves interpretability of the kinetic analysis while improving robustness against artifacts frequently encountered in EV measurements.62 Beyond optimization of the sensing interface itself, the present study highlights two biologically relevant observations. First, the protein corona substantially influences accessibility of membrane biomarkers and consequently affects analytical detectability of EVs. Second, glycan-based interfaces provide information regarding the interaction phenotype of EVs that is complementary to conventional protein-marker analysis. Such interaction-based profiling may represent an additional layer of information for future liquid-biopsy applications and characterization of malignant-derived EVs. Collectively, this integrated strategy expands the toolbox for label-free EV profiling and supports heparan-sulfate-guided interaction fingerprints as a promising axis for translational SPR-based liquid-biopsy development rather than just focusing exclusively on EV enumeration. This work establishes glycan-based nanobiointerfaces as a versatile platform for investigating functional EV interactions beyond conventional molecular profiling.
