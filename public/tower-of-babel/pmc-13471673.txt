Analysis of factors influencing hospitalization costs for ureteral surgery based on a DRG profit-and-loss perspective: a multicenter study
Authors: Huiyue Liu, Dong Xia, Zhenju Yin, Haijiao Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiagnosis-related groups (DRGs) are a key instrument in health insurance payment reform. However, for DRG groups with substantial heterogeneity in disease severity and surgical complexity, uniform payment standards may lead to a mismatch between reimbursement and actual costs. The ureteral surgery group (LC19) under the China Healthcare Security Diagnosis-Related Groups (CHS-DRG) system shows marked variation in resource utilization and is therefore at high risk of systematic financial loss. This study aimed to identify factors influencing hospitalization costs in the LC19 group from a DRG profit–loss perspective, providing evidence to support the optimization of payment mechanisms.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted using data from two tertiary general hospitals in Hunan Province, China. A total of 2,568 inpatient cases classified under the CHS-DRG LC19 group between July 2023 and December 2024 were included. Total hospitalization cost was set as the dependent variable. Independent variables covered patient demographics, disease and treatment characteristics, surgical characteristics, and inpatient management factors. Background

Diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) are a case classification and payment system based on healthcare resource consumption and have been widely applied in hospital performance evaluation and health insurance reimbursement [1–9]. Previous studies have consistently shown that DRG-based payment can, at an aggregate level, curb the growth of inpatient expenditures and improve healthcare efficiency [10–12]. In China, with the ongoing implementation of DRG payment reform, the rationality of grouping rules and the scientific design of payment standards have become increasingly important determinants of hospital behavior and healthcare quality [13–15]. However, in DRG groups with substantial intra-group heterogeneity, particularly in surgical settings, grouping schemes that do not adequately account for disease severity, surgical complexity, and major complications may result in systematic discrepancies between reimbursement and actual costs [16–19]. Such mismatches can create structural financial risks and reduce hospitals’ willingness to admit and treat complex or high-risk patients [20–22].

The ureteral surgery group (CHS-DRG code LC19) represents a typical surgical DRG within the urological system, encompassing procedures ranging from routine ureteral stone surgery to complex radical ureteral operations, with marked variation in clinical pathways and resource utilization. Since the formal implementation of DRG-based payment in Hunan Province in July 2023, this group has incurred persistent financial losses across the study hospitals, with an average monthly loss rate exceeding 50%. Under the current CHS-DRG 2.0 grouping framework, LC19 is primarily classified based on the principal diagnosis and principal procedure. In contrast, other factors that substantially influence resource consumption, including surgical level, types of complications, and bilateral procedures, are not explicitly incorporated into the grouping criteria. As a result, hospitalization costs within the LC19 group exhibit considerable variability, and payment accuracy remains limited [23–25].

Against this background, the present study analyzed front pages of medical records and medical insurance settlement data for patients classified under the LC19 group to systematically identify key clinical and managerial factors associated with total hospitalization costs [26, 27]. By integrating cost analysis with DRG profit–loss outcomes, this study aims to provide empirical evidence to inform the refinement of grouping pathways and payment mechanisms for LC19 and other surgical DRGs with similar characteristics [19, 28–30].

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study adopted a retrospective cross-sectional design. Data were obtained from two routinely used administrative and clinical data sources in Chinese hospitals: the Front Page of the Medical Record (FPMR) and the medical insurance settlement list [26, 31].

The FPMR, which has been uniformly implemented in Hunan Province since 2012, is a structured summary of the inpatient care process. It records key information, including patient demographics, dates of admission and discharge, with diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) and surgical procedures coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, Clinical Modification, 9th Revision (ICD-9-CM-3), respectively. To support health insurance payment reform, a standardized medical insurance settlement list system has been established in China. Based on the FPMR, the settlement list serves as the official dataset for reimbursement claims. It provides detailed information on total hospitalization costs and their components, including comprehensive medical service costs, medication costs, medical consumable costs, surgical costs, laboratory test costs, and diagnostic examination costs [32–35].

Data were pooled from two representative tertiary general hospitals within Hunan Province (located in Changsha and Chenzhou) to capture a broader regional perspective on the LC19 group’s financial performance. While institutional variations in clinical pathways may exist, the decision to analyze a pooled dataset was driven by the observation that both major centers experienced consistent and significant financial deficits under the CHS-DRG system. By aggregating these data, the study aims to identify systemic high-loss factors inherent to the ureteral surgery group across the regional healthcare tier, rather than factors limited to a single institution’s management protocols. The study population consisted of inpatient cases discharged between July 1, 2023, and December 31, 2024. Cases were included if the principal diagnosis and principal procedure met the CHS-DRG LC19 grouping criteria. Records with incomplete or missing information in the FPMR or insurance settlement list were excluded. A total of 2,568 eligible inpatient cases were included in the final analysis.

The DRG payment standard utilized in this analysis refers to the final settlement amount determined by the regional health insurance authority for each LC19 case. These data consist of simulated settlement figures from hospital medical insurance departments and reflect real-world reimbursement outcomes. The payment standard is calculated by multiplying the group’s assigned weight under the CHS-DRG 1.0 framework by the regional base rate, and further incorporating provincial and municipal adjustment coefficients (such as specific hospital-grade coefficients). These figures represent the actual financial ceiling for reimbursement per case during the study period. Consequently, the analyzed profit-and-loss outcomes represent the actual financial impact and real-world balance between official payments and the participating tertiary centers’ resource consumption.

Variables and outcome measures

The primary outcome was total hospitalization cost per admission, obtained from the medical insurance settlement list. This measure comprised all cost components incurred during hospitalization, including comprehensive medical service costs, medication costs, medical consumable costs, surgical costs, laboratory test costs, and diagnostic examination costs.

Potential influencing factors were categorized into four domains based on clinical relevance and inpatient management characteristics: (1) patient demographics, including age and sex; (2) disease and treatment characteristics, including the presence of major complications or comorbidities (MCC), complications or comorbidities (CC), and pyonephrosis; (3) surgical characteristics, including whether the principal procedure was coded as ICD-9-CM-3 56.41 (ureterectomy), whether the surgery was classified as level IV, whether bilateral surgery was performed, and operative duration; and (4) inpatient management characteristics, including length of stay and whether the case was managed as day surgery. The disease lists used to define MCC and CC were derived from the urinary system–specific definitions in the CHS-DRG classification system [25, 30]. Detailed definitions and categorizations of variables are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

For DRG profit–loss analysis, a case was defined as generating a surplus when the actual hospitalization cost was lower than the corresponding DRG payment standard, and as a deficit when the actual cost exceeded the payment standard.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0. Because hospitalization costs were not normally distributed, continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges, and group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test.

Variables that were statistically significant in univariate analyses (P < 0.05) were included in a multivariate linear regression model to identify independent factors associated with hospitalization costs. Although the raw cost data exhibited a right-skewed distribution, a linear model was employed due to the large sample size (n = 2,568) and the clinical interpretability of the unstandardized coefficients (β). Post-estimation diagnostics, including P-P plots and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests, confirmed that the regression residuals were approximately normally distributed. Based on the standardized regression coefficients (βt) and clinical relevance, differences in DRG profit–loss status and cost structure indicators were further compared across key influencing factors. Statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 2,568 inpatient cases classified under the CHS-DRG LC19 group were included in this study. Male patients accounted for 61.80% of the cohort, while females accounted for 38.20%. Patients aged 45 to 65 years constituted the largest proportion (54.67%), followed by those aged ≥ 65 years (24.26%) and those aged < 45 years (21.07%). Regarding disease and treatment characteristics, 7.67% of patients had major MCC, 25.16% had CC, and 44.28% were complicated by pyonephrosis. With respect to surgical characteristics, 2.45% of cases had a principal procedure coded as ICD-9-CM-3 56.41 (ureterectomy), 4.95% underwent level IV surgery, 8.96% received bilateral surgery, and 10.51% had an operative duration of ≥ 2 h. Regarding inpatient management characteristics, 14.99% of cases were managed as day surgery, and 33.68% had a length of stay ≥ 7 days. Factors associated with hospitalization costs

The results of the univariate analyses are presented in Table 1. Overall, multiple factors across demographic characteristics, disease and treatment characteristics, surgical characteristics, and inpatient management characteristics were significantly associated with total hospitalization costs, including age group, presence of MCC or CC, pyonephrosis, surgical level, bilateral surgery, operative duration, length of stay, and day surgery status.

Variables showing statistical significance in the univariate analyses were entered into a multivariate linear regression model, with total hospitalization cost as the dependent variable. As shown in Table 2, age 45 to 65 years, presence of MCC, presence of CC, pyonephrosis, a principal procedure coded as 56.41, day surgery, level IV surgery, bilateral surgery, operative duration ≥ 2 h, and length of stay ≥ 7 days were independently associated with hospitalization costs (P < 0.05). The adjusted coefficient of determination (R²) of the model was 0.3916, indicating that these variables jointly explained 39.16% of the variance in hospitalization costs. Collinearity diagnostics showed that all variance inflation factors (VIFs) were below 10, suggesting no significant multicollinearity.

DRG profit–loss analysis across key influencing factors

Factors associated with DRG payment profit–loss status were further analyzed. The distribution of all examined variables differed significantly between the DRG profit and loss groups (P < 0.001), as shown in Table 3. The analysis revealed that cases involving level IV surgery or a principal procedure coded as 56.41 (ureterectomy) had the highest rates of DRG payment deficit, reaching 94.49% and 92.06%, respectively. In addition, higher proportions of DRG deficits were observed among cases with a length of stay ≥ 7 days, MCC, bilateral surgery, operative duration ≥ 2 h, pyonephrosis, and CC, with loss rates ranging from 61.61% to 80.00%. In contrast, the highest proportion of DRG payment surplus was observed in the day surgery group (98.18%). Cases with a length of stay of < 7 days and those without pyonephrosis were also more likely to generate DRG payment surplus, with surplus proportions of 61.36% and 58.77%, respectively.

With respect to the inpatient management mode, cases undergoing day surgery exhibited a distinct cost structure. Discussion

This study demonstrated, through univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses, that disease severity and surgical complexity were key drivers of increased hospitalization costs. Specifically, pyonephrosis (βt = 0.31), level IV surgery (βt = 0.21), and bilateral surgery (βt = 0.11) were all significantly and positively associated with total hospitalization costs. Further DRG profit–loss analysis revealed that cases with complex clinical characteristics were at markedly higher risk of payment deficits. The deficit rates reached 94.49% for cases undergoing level IV surgery and 92.06% for those with a principal procedure coded as 56.41 (ureterectomy). In addition, cases with major MCC, bilateral surgery, and pyonephrosis also showed high loss rates of 70.05%, 69.13%, and 66.84%, respectively. These findings indicate that the current LC19 grouping scheme, which is primarily based on the principal diagnosis and principal procedure, fails to adequately capture and compensate for the additional resource consumption attributable to surgical complexity and specific complications, resulting in systematic financial losses for cases with high technical complexity [19, 29, 32, 36].

This observation is consistent with prior DRG-related studies, which suggest that excessive within-group heterogeneity in resource utilization undermines the effectiveness of grouping and weakens the fairness and incentive functions of payment systems [20–22]. Some scholars have emphasized the need to introduce key nodes, such as surgical level and complication profiles, to enhance between-group discrimination. In contrast, others have proposed establishing MCC/CC reference tables for surgical groups to more precisely quantify the impact of complications on resource use [25, 32]. In line with these findings, and supported by the results of the present study, it may be appropriate to consider incorporating selected variables validated herein, such as MCC/CC status, surgical level, and bilateral surgery, into the grouping logic of LC19 and similar surgical DRGs, or to use them as criteria for defining refined subgroups, to reflect better actual clinical resource utilization [23, 24].

The present study showed that cases characterized by intensive resource utilization not only incurred higher hospitalization costs but also exhibited a concentrated risk of DRG payment losses. This highlights a critical tension within the current payment framework: hospitals that admit a higher proportion of complex and severe cases bear greater financial pressure under uniform payment standards, which may reduce their willingness to treat such patients. Similar findings have been reported in studies of coronary intervention procedures (FM19 group), where indicators of disease complexity, such as the number of treated vessels and stents, were identified as core risk factors for overspending [37].

To mitigate this tension and safeguard the healthcare system’s capacity to manage complex and critically ill patients, more flexible and differentiated payment mechanisms are required. Given that hospital level and functional positioning significantly influence hospitalization costs for comparable cases, it is recommended to explore tiered payment standards linked to the case mix index (CMI), hospital function, and case complexity. Providing appropriate compensation to hospitals with higher CMIs and greater proportions of complex cases may promote differentiated and sustainable development across healthcare institutions [17, 26, 38].

However, these findings and the resulting policy proposals should be interpreted with caution. As this study is based on data from two tertiary hospitals in Hunan Province, the results may not be directly generalizable to other regions or lower-level hospitals where medical insurance policies, pricing structures, and disease distributions differ. Therefore, our recommendations for grouping optimization and tiered payment mechanisms should be viewed as directional guidance. In practice, these strategies should be calibrated and validated against local clinical and economic realities to ensure their effectiveness and equity across different regional healthcare systems.

Moreover, consistent evidence from the present analyses indicates that day surgery is an effective approach to optimizing the cost structure and improving operational efficiency. In the cost determinant analysis, day surgery was the only factor negatively associated with hospitalization costs (βt = − 0.09). Cost structure analysis further demonstrated that, compared with non–day surgery cases, median laboratory and diagnostic examination costs in day surgery cases decreased by more than 90%. In contrast, costs for comprehensive medical services, medications, and surgical procedures were also significantly reduced.

These findings suggest that shortening hospital stays through process reengineering can substantially reduce non-core medical resource consumption. However, it is important to clarify that these results represent a significant association rather than a direct causal effect. The observed financial advantages are subject to potential selection bias, as patients admitted for day surgery are typically pre-screened for lower clinical complexity and greater physiological resilience, thereby reducing resource consumption compared with traditional inpatient cohorts. Despite this, the combined implementation of day surgery and DRG-based payment remains a strategic tool for improved service efficiency and more rational resource allocation. Therefore, it is recommended that hospitals, following rigorous safety assessments, actively incorporate eligible cases—such as unilateral ureteral surgeries without complications—into day surgery pathways. This approach may improve operational efficiency and reduce the institutional average length of stay, facilitating a balance between healthcare quality and cost control [39, 40]. Health insurance authorities may also consider incentive policies that allow hospitals to retain reasonable surpluses from day surgery cases to foster an endogenous transition toward higher-quality, more efficient, and more intensive healthcare delivery.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it was based on a relatively large sample drawn from two tertiary comprehensive hospitals, and the study period coincided with the early phase of DRG-based actual payment implementation, thereby enhancing its policy relevance. Second, by integrating front-page medical record data with health insurance settlement data, the study simultaneously evaluated clinical characteristics, hospitalization costs, and DRG profit–loss status within a unified analytical framework. The selected variables were clinically meaningful and managerially interpretable, and the multivariate regression model demonstrated acceptable explanatory power and stability.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is a retrospective cross-sectional analysis limited to an 18-month window (July 2023 to December 2024). While this timeframe aligns with the stable implementation of the NHSA DRG Version 1.0 in China—ensuring consistency in payment standards—it does not allow for a long-term observation of cost evolution or shifts in resource utilization as policies continue to mature. Future studies should employ longitudinal designs to track how costs and clinical behaviors adapt to subsequent DRG version updates (e.g., Version 2.0).

Second, while the data were collected from two distinct tertiary hospitals to increase sample robustness, the results were presented as a pooled dataset without a comparative analysis between the institutions. We acknowledge that variations in internal clinical pathways and regional supply costs may exist. However, the significant financial losses observed across both major centers suggest a systemic challenge within the LC19 group in the Hunan region. Future research involving a broader range of hospital types across provinces would help further validate these findings.

Third, while our multivariable model explained approximately 39.16% of the variance in hospitalization costs—a result comparable to similar studies in the DRG field—it is important to acknowledge that a significant portion of the variance remains unexplained. This is likely due to the inherent complexity of medical costs and the limitations of using administrative data from medical record home pages. Omitted variables, such as surgeon experience, intraoperative nuances not captured by ICD codes, and specific hospital-level management differences, could not be quantified in this study. Future research that uses more granular clinical data and integrates with electronic health record (EHR) systems may further improve model completeness.

Fourth, while the study utilizes actual settlement data, the influence of medical coding practices cannot be entirely ruled out. The accuracy of DRG grouping depends heavily on the precision of primary and secondary diagnosis coding, as well as procedural classification. Although the participating hospitals adhere to national coding standards, subtle variations in coding habits or documentation completeness could affect the case-mix index (CMI) and observed financial losses. Future research should integrate medical record quality audits further to isolate the impact of coding accuracy on DRG performance.

Finally, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the fact that data were sourced exclusively from two tertiary general hospitals in Hunan Province. In the Chinese healthcare system, tertiary (Grade 3 A) hospitals typically function as regional medical hubs that admit a higher proportion of patients with complex comorbidities, recurrent disease, or post-surgical complications.

Consequently, the hospitalization costs and DRG profit–loss patterns observed here may not be directly representative of lower-level hospitals. Secondary and primary healthcare institutions often manage more routine cases within the LC19 group—such as simple ureteral calculi—which involve lower resource consumption and potentially more stable financial outcomes under uniform payment standards. Future research should include a broader sample across multiple hospital tiers to quantify the variations in DRG performance between referral centers and community-based facilities.

Conclusions

In the CHS-DRG LC19 group, hospitalization costs and DRG profit–loss outcomes are primarily determined by disease severity, surgical complexity, and inpatient management patterns. Optimizing DRG grouping rules, exploring tiered or dynamic payment mechanisms, and promoting efficient care pathways, such as day surgery, may improve the precision of DRG-based payments, reduce structural financial losses for healthcare institutions, and ensure appropriate access to care for complex surgical cases.
