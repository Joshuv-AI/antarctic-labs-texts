Stratospheric impacts on Eurasian soil moisture on subseasonal timescales
Authors: Ying Dai, Peter Hitchcock, Flavio Lehner, Suvarna Fadnavis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSudden stratospheric warming (SSW) events and soil moisture are both key sources of subseasonal-to-seasonal predictability, raising the possibility that SSWs could enhance hydroclimate predictability through soil moisture. Yet this connection remains largely unexplored. Here we show that SSWs induce a robust dipolar soil moisture response across Eurasia, with wetter conditions over the Iberian Peninsula and pronounced drying from the Iranian Plateau to northern India during the two months following SSWs. These responses occur in both surface and root-zone soil moisture and are consistently captured by reanalysis, land surface model simulations, and satellite observations. Enhanced precipitation drives the wet anomalies over Iberia, whereas anomalous near-surface warming drives drying from the Iranian Plateau to northern India. Our findings identify a previously underrecognized pathway linking stratospheric variability to subseasonal Eurasian hydroclimate predictability.Subject terms: Atmospheric dynamics, Hydrology

Introduction

Subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) prediction bridges the gap between weather forecasts and seasonal outlooks and relies heavily on slowly varying components of the climate system1,2. Two key sources of S2S predictability are soil moisture and sudden stratospheric warmings (SSWs)3. Soil moisture influences surface temperature and precipitation through land–atmosphere coupling and can retain memory over a long time, sometimes weeks to months4–6, while SSWs disrupt the polar vortex and induce “forecasts of opportunities”: the periods following SSWs are marked by a persistent negative phase of the Northern Annular Mode, which influences surface weather patterns for several weeks7–9. Although both processes are recognized sources of S2S predictability, their potential interaction has received little attention.

One plausible interaction involves the impact of SSWs on soil moisture. Over the past two decades, SSW events have been robustly linked to an increased likelihood and severity of cold air outbreaks in Eurasia10–14 and to precipitation extremes over southwestern Europe15–18. In addition, SSWs are associated with widespread surface warming over midlatitude Asia, a response that has received less attention but is robustly evident in both reanalyses and Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 simulations (for example, Fig. S11 in ref. 19). Soil moisture responds sensitively to these atmospheric anomalies, with precipitation directly modulating soil wetness and temperature influencing evapotranspiration. Consequently, SSWs are expected to affect soil moisture over Eurasia, where both precipitation and temperature exhibit strong responses to stratospheric variability. Such an SSW-soil moisture interaction, if confirmed, could both enhance SSW-driven S2S predictability through land–atmosphere feedbacks and extend forecast lead times by combining soil memory with the “forecasts of opportunities” induced by SSWs.

Here, we examine whether SSWs influence Eurasian soil moisture and thereby provide an additional pathway for extended-range predictability. The analysis proceeds in three steps (Results). First, we document a robust dipolar soil moisture response to SSWs, characterized by anomalously wet conditions over the Iberian Peninsula and pronounced drying over Iran-Pak-NI (the region extending from the Iranian Plateau, Pakistan, to North India). Second, we identify the key meteorological drivers of this dipolar pattern, focusing on precipitation and temperature anomalies associated with SSWs. Third, we assess the persistence of soil moisture anomalies, considering both the role of preferential sampling of the background soil moisture states during SSWs and the intrinsic memory of the land surface. The main findings and their implications are summarized in the Discussion. A description of the data and methods is in “Methods”.

Results

A robust dipolar soil moisture response to SSWs

Using composites of historical SSW events, we find that the soil moisture response to SSWs exhibits a broader Eurasian-scale pattern. During the two months following SSW onset, significant soil moisture anomalies are widespread across the continent, characterized by generally wetter conditions over Europe and drier conditions over Asia (Figs. 1a, d, and S1a, d). Despite this broad spatial footprint, the Iberian Peninsula and Iran-Pak-NI exhibit the strongest and most spatially coherent soil moisture anomalies across datasets. In this study, the acronym “Iran-Pak-NI” refers to the region extending from the Iranian Plateau, Pakistan, to North India. We therefore focus on these two regions in the following analyses. This is because the Iberian Peninsula has long been recognized as a traditional hot spot of SSW surface impacts and serves as a useful benchmark region for evaluating hydroclimatic responses to SSWs. In contrast, Iran-Pak-NI represents a relatively underappreciated region in the SSW literature despite exhibiting a comparably strong soil moisture response. By examining both regions, we contrast a well-established center of SSW influence with a potentially emerging hot spot, thereby providing insight into both known and previously underexplored land–surface responses to SSWs.

The wet-Iberian/dry-Iran-Pak-NI dipolar soil moisture anomalies are evident in both surface soil moisture (upper 10 cm) (Fig. 1b, c) and root-zone soil moisture (upper 100 cm) (Fig. 1e, f). Most anomalies become statistically significant after SSW onset, while pre-onset anomalies are generally weak and insignificant (Fig. 1b, c, e). An exception is the negative root-zone soil moisture anomalies over Iran-Pak-NI, which persist throughout days [−60, 60] around SSWs (Fig. 1f, green curve). The pre-onset component of this signal cannot be attributed to SSWs; we examine this component in more detail later.

The robustness of the diagnosed dipolar soil moisture response is evaluated using two alternative datasets: GLDAS and AMSR (Figs. S1 and S2). The GLDAS composites are based on the same 44 SSW events during 1948–2014 as used for ERA5, whereas the AMSR composites are based on 12 SSW events during 2002–2022, representing a different subset of events. Despite differences in datasets, spatial resolution, and sample periods, all composites consistently show wetter soil conditions over Iberia and drier conditions over Iran-Pak-NI following SSWs. These results demonstrate that the dipolar soil moisture response to SSWs is robust and largely insensitive to dataset choice, analysis period, or SSW sample selection. Although the present study focuses on the Eurasian land regions where the responses are particularly pronounced and societally relevant, the influence of SSWs on soil moisture is not confined to these areas. Significant signals also emerge over other regions, including North America and North Africa. For completeness, Fig. S3 presents the full Northern Hemisphere soil moisture response to SSWs, providing a broader perspective on the spatial extent of stratospheric influences on land–surface hydrology.

Role of precipitation and temperature anomalies

We next investigate the roles of precipitation and temperature anomalies in shaping soil moisture responses to SSWs. This analysis is motivated by two considerations. First, SSWs exert a robust influence on Eurasian precipitation and temperature, characterized by enhanced precipitation over the Iberian Peninsula and reduced precipitation accompanied by anomalous warming over Iran-Pak-NI (Fig. S4). Second, soil moisture responds sensitively to these atmospheric anomalies: precipitation directly alters soil wetness, while temperature modulates evapotranspiration. Accordingly, for each SSW event, we examine soil moisture anomalies as a function of the associated precipitation and temperature anomalies, analyzing surface soil moisture (SSM) and root-zone soil moisture (RSM) separately (Fig. 2).

Each dot represents a historical SSW event; the pentagram shows the composite mean of all SSWs. Anomalies are averaged over days [1, 60] after each SSW. Dashed gray lines indicate zero. Numbers along the axes show correlations between ΔSSM and each variable, and the upper-right number shows the correlation between ΔPRCP and ΔT2m; only correlations significant at p < 0.10 (Student’s t test) are shown. c, d Same as a, b, but showing root-zone soil moisture anomalies (ΔRSM).

Over Iberia, SSM is strongly positively correlated with precipitation (r = 0.76) and shows no significant correlation with temperature (Fig. 2a). Stratifying SSWs by precipitation anomalies further indicates that wet soil responses occur primarily during enhanced precipitation. Repeating the analysis for RSM yields the same conclusion (Fig. 2c), confirming that precipitation anomalies are the primary driver of soil wetting over the Iberian Peninsula following SSWs. This precipitation increase arises from the circulation response to SSWs: they shift the North Atlantic storm track equatorward, direct more Atlantic cyclones toward Iberia, and thereby increase rainfall and soil moisture.

In Iran-Pak-NI, precipitation and temperature anomalies are significantly negatively correlated (r = − 0.45; Fig. 2b), meaning that reduced precipitation and elevated temperatures frequently co-occur in this region. SSM exhibits a strong positive correlation with precipitation (r = 0.72) and a strong negative correlation with temperature (r = − 0.73). Consistent with these relationships, dry SSM anomalies occur predominantly during negative precipitation anomalies and positive temperature anomalies, indicating that both factors contribute to surface soil drying (Fig. 2b). For the root zone, RSM is significantly negatively correlated with temperature (r = − 0.46) but shows no significant correlation with precipitation (Fig. 2d). Stratification by temperature anomalies further reveals that dry RSM responses occur primarily during anomalous warming, indicating that elevated temperatures are the primary driver of root-zone drying. These reduced precipitation and anomalous warming anomalies reflect the large-scale circulation response to SSWs, which generate a deep positive geopotential height anomaly throughout much of the troposphere over Iran-Pak-NI (Fig. S5). This vertically coherent structure promotes anomalous subsidence, increases near-surface temperatures, suppresses precipitation, and ultimately leads to drier soils. These features represent a typical hot-dry compound extreme pathway, which may be further amplified through land–atmosphere feedback processes.

In summary, during the 2 months following SSWs, anomalous precipitation primarily drives wetter soils in Iberia, whereas anomalous warming is the main contributor to drier soils in Iran-Pak-NI. Reduced precipitation also contributes to surface soil drying in Iran-Pak-NI, though its impact on root-zone moisture is comparatively minor.

Persistence of root-zone soil moisture anomalies in Iran-Pak-NI

In Iran-Pak-NI, negative root-zone soil moisture (RSM) anomalies persist throughout days [−60, 60] relative to SSW onset (Fig. 1f, green curve). When the analysis window is extended to 180 days prior to SSW onset, these negative anomalies remain strong and statistically significant (not shown). This persistence indicates that the dry signal precedes SSW events and therefore cannot be attributed solely to SSW-related processes.

To understand the origin of this pre-onset dry signal, we examine year-by-year Iran-Pak-NI RSM anomalies and their correspondence with SSW occurrence (Fig. 3a, c). Iran-Pak-NI RSM exhibits pronounced decadal variability and strong temporal persistence, effectively retaining memory of past dry or wet states on near-decadal timescales (Fig. 3a). A notable wet regime occurs during the 1990s (Fig. 3a, c), when annual-mean RSM anomalies remain close to one standard deviation above normal (Fig. 3c, black curve). This period is also characterized by a pronounced hiatus in SSW activity (Fig. 3c, black stars). The relative absence of SSWs during this near-decadal wet regime, together with their more frequent occurrence during dry regimes, indicates a statistical co-occurrence between SSW events and pre-existing dry conditions in Iran-Pak-NI. This result should not be interpreted as evidence of any causal relationship between the two, but rather as an observed association whose physical basis remains an open question. This statistical association is further supported by the non-SSW composites, which mostly remain positive (green shading in Fig. 1f). Because non-SSW winters (that is, winters without SSWs) largely coincide with wet regimes in Iran-Pak-NI, the complementary SSW winters must predominantly correspond to dry regimes, given that the long-term mean anomaly is close to zero. In this context, the apparent dry anomalies preceding SSW onset are better understood as reflecting the background hydroclimatic state, rather than indicating a causal influence of SSWs themselves.

This interpretation raises a further question: do the dry anomalies observed after SSW onset merely reflect the background dry state, or do SSWs actively contribute to these post-onset dry signals? To address this, we remove the background state by subtracting the annual-mean RSM anomaly from each day of the corresponding year and repeat the composite analysis. The resulting composites suggest that SSWs are associated with additional post-onset drying, which acts to reinforce pre-existing dry conditions in Iran-Pak-NI (Fig. 1f, blue curve). In ERA5, the peak magnitude of this SSW-induced additional drying is approximately half that of the pre-existing dry anomaly (Fig. 1f), whereas in GLDAS it is nearly comparable in magnitude to the background dry conditions (Fig. S1f). These relative magnitudes indicate that SSW-induced drying represents a substantial contribution to the reinforcement of pre-existing dry conditions in Iran-Pak-NI.

Finally, this near-decadal variability in RSM appears to be specific to Iran-Pak-NI. In contrast, RSM over Iberia exhibits pronounced S2S variability and substantially shorter-term persistence (Fig. 3b, d). RSM from GLDAS reveals similar results (Fig. S6), evidencing the robustness of these results. In summary, dry anomalies in Iran-Pak-NI preceding SSWs primarily reflect the background dry state, which is subsequently reinforced by additional drying associated with SSWs after their onset.

Discussion

Using multiple soil moisture datasets (ERA5, GLDAS, and AMSR), we identify a robust dipolar soil moisture response to SSWs during the two months following their onset, characterized by wetter conditions over the Iberian Peninsula and drier conditions over Iran-Pak-NI (the region extending from the Iranian Plateau, Pakistan, to North India). The consistency of this pattern across datasets and SSW samples demonstrates that it is not sensitive to dataset choice or analysis period.

The underlying drivers of these responses differ regionally. Over Iberia, enhanced precipitation following SSWs is the primary contributor to soil wetting in both the surface and root zone. Over Iran-Pak-NI, anomalous warming dominates soil drying, particularly in the root zone, while reduced precipitation contributes mainly to surface soil moisture deficits. These contrasting mechanisms highlight regionally distinct pathways linking stratospheric variability to land surface hydroclimate.

A key result is the strong persistence of root-zone soil moisture anomalies in Iran-Pak-NI. Negative anomalies extend well before SSW onset, reflecting the near-decadal variability of regional soil moisture and indicating that pre-onset drying largely represents the background hydroclimatic state rather than a direct SSW influence. After removing this background state, SSWs are nevertheless associated with additional post-onset drying that reinforces existing dry conditions. In contrast, Iberian soil moisture exhibits much weaker long-term persistence and stronger S2S variability.

These contrasting behaviors can be understood in the context of their distinct hydroclimate regimes. Iran-Pak-NI represents a typical dryland, moisture-limited system, whereas the Iberian Peninsula is characterized by a humid, precipitation-driven Mediterranean climate under strong Atlantic storm-track influence. Consistent with these differences, dryland regions, such as Iran-Pak-NI, tend to exhibit longer soil moisture memory due to infrequent precipitation and slow soil moisture recharge, allowing anomalies to persist over extended periods. In contrast, humid regions like the Iberian Peninsula generally show shorter soil moisture memory, as frequent precipitation events rapidly reset soil moisture conditions20. This fundamental difference in land–atmosphere coupling provides a physical explanation for the much stronger persistence of soil moisture anomalies over Iran-Pak-NI compared to the Iberian Peninsula.

These findings have potentially important implications for S2S predictability. In the Iran-Pak-NI region, the combination of persistent background aridity and SSW-induced reductions in precipitation and soil moisture may increase the likelihood of drought development and hot-dry compound events, suggesting a pathway through which stratospheric variability can modulate hydroclimatic extremes on S2S timescales. Moreover, the long memory of root-zone soil moisture implies that land–atmosphere coupling can amplify and prolong stratospheric signals, extending their influence beyond the intrinsic persistence of the atmosphere.

These results are particularly relevant for the Iran-Pak-NI region, which is among the world’s most drought-prone and densely populated regions and has experienced increasingly frequent and severe droughts in recent decades. Water shortages in this region have far-reaching consequences for agriculture, food security, ecosystems, economic development, and human livelihoods. Our findings demonstrate that SSWs constitute an important source of hydroclimatic variability in the region by systematically reducing precipitation and soil moisture through a physically identifiable mechanism. By establishing this previously underappreciated link between stratospheric variability and regional drought risk, our study highlights the potential value of SSW information for improving S2S prediction of hydroclimatic conditions. Incorporating stratospheric precursors into forecasting systems could therefore enhance preparedness and risk management for water resources, agriculture, and other climate-sensitive sectors across this highly vulnerable region. More broadly, the pronounced and robust hydroclimatic responses identified here suggest that the Iran-Pak-NI region should be recognized as an important new hot spot of SSW surface impacts. Given both its strong sensitivity to stratospheric forcing and its high societal vulnerability to drought, this region warrants greater attention in future studies of stratosphere–troposphere coupling, land–surface feedbacks, and climate-risk prediction.

Future work should assess the robustness of these land–atmosphere coupling pathways in S2S prediction models, including whether models realistically capture the persistence of root-zone soil moisture and its interaction with SSW-associated atmospheric anomalies. In addition, the alignment between dry conditions in Iran-Pak-NI and SSWs warrants further investigation. Specifically, it remains an open question whether anomalously dry land states in Iran-Pak-NI can actively contribute to tropospheric circulation anomalies that favor the occurrence of SSWs, implying a potential two-way coupling between land–surface conditions and stratospheric variability rather than a purely downward influence.

Although this study does not directly assess the predictability of SSW-related soil moisture anomalies, previous evidence suggests that such predictability may exist in climate prediction systems through their ability to capture the key hydroclimatic pathways. For instance, S2S forecast experiments from the Stratospheric Nudging and Predictable Surface Impacts (SNAPSI) project have been shown to successfully reproduce the precipitation signal associated with the 2018 SSW event over the Iberian Peninsula when the stratospheric circulation is properly represented18, suggesting potential predictability of the associated wet soil moisture anomalies in this precipitation-driven region. In addition, climate model evaluations indicate that CMIP6 models are generally able to capture the warm surface air temperature response over Iran-Pak-NI following SSW events19, implying potential predictability of the associated dry soil moisture anomalies through temperature-driven drying mechanisms. Taken together, these results suggest that SSW-related soil moisture anomalies may be predictable in climate prediction systems, albeit through different dominant pathways in humid versus dryland regimes.

In future work, we will further investigate soil moisture predictability associated with SSW events using dedicated subseasonal forecast datasets from the SNAPSI project21 and the CESM2-S2S hindcast ensemble22, which together provide a complementary framework for systematically evaluating forecast skill and the role of stratosphere–troposphere coupling in driving surface hydroclimate predictability.

Methods

Datasets

We analyze daily ERA5 reanalysis fields, including wind, precipitation, surface air temperature, surface soil moisture, and root-zone soil moisture23. ERA5 provides dynamically consistent land–atmosphere fields from a coupled numerical weather prediction and land–surface modeling system, although soil moisture is not directly constrained by soil moisture observations. The ERA5 data are provided at 1.0° × 1.0° latitude-longitude resolution and span 1948–2022. We clarify that the use of ERA5 data up to 2022 is intended to ensure consistency with the AMSR-E/AMSR2 dataset introduced below, which is also available only through 2022. This choice guarantees that both datasets are based on the same temporal coverage and thus the same set of SSW events, allowing for a fair and directly comparable composite analysis across datasets. In addition, although ERA5 is originally provided at a higher native resolution, we deliberately regridded the data to 1.0° × 1.0° resolution during the download stage. This was done for practical reasons, including downloading efficiency and storage considerations.

To assess the robustness of the diagnosed soil moisture signals, we also use surface and root-zone soil moisture from NASA Global Land Data Assimilation System version 2.0 (GLDAS-2.0), which employs the Catchment Land Surface Model (version 3.6) driven by observation-based meteorological forcing from ref. 24, without atmospheric coupling25. GLDAS-2.0 is available at 0.25° × 0.25° resolution and covers 1948–2014. Note that soil moisture in both ERA5 and GLDAS-2.0 is model-derived and lacks direct observational constraints. In GLDAS-2.0, root-zone soil moisture is defined as the soil moisture integrated over the 0–100 cm layer beneath the surface. To maintain consistency across datasets, we apply the same depth definition to ERA5 by computing root-zone soil moisture over the 0–100 cm soil layer.

To complement these model-based products, we incorporate an observation-based surface soil moisture dataset derived from the Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer for Earth Observing System and Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer 2 (AMSR-E/AMSR2) satellite brightness temperatures26. This product provides an observationally independent estimate of near-surface soil moisture, has a spatial resolution of approximately 36 km, and spans 2002–2022, but is limited to the surface layer. Together, these datasets provide complementary constraints on soil moisture variability associated with SSWs. In light of the limited and heterogeneous nature of soil moisture observations, synthesizing multiple datasets to constrain variability has become a commonly accepted and widely applied strategy in the literature [e.g.,27]. In particular, soil moisture estimates in arid regions are subject to uncertainty, as there are limited in situ measurements and heterogeneous soil properties can lead to large variability in soil moisture in response to precipitation events. Model products, such as GLDAS, generally compare well with in situ measurements in arid regions where they are available, with a reasonable representation of seasonality but a slight tendency to overestimate absolute soil moisture (e.g., Australia and the US28). Together with the documented agreement between model- and satellite-based products in our region of interest (Fig. S2), we think the datasets are fit for purpose.

Daily anomalies are computed by removing the seasonal cycle for each calendar day. The seasonal cycle is defined as the mean plus the first three Fourier harmonics of the daily climatology, following previous studies29–31, which helps filter out high-frequency sampling variability. Since the analysis is mostly based on regional means of soil moisture, the impact of differing native spatial resolutions among datasets is negligible, as area-averaged soil moisture computed from native grids is effectively equivalent to that obtained after area-conservative regridding to a common 1° grid.

Sudden stratospheric warmings

Sudden stratospheric warming events (SSWs) are identified following8 as events during which the zonal-mean zonal wind at 60°N and 10 hPa reverses from westerly to easterly. The onset date (day 0) is defined as the first day on which the daily-mean zonal-mean zonal wind becomes easterly. Only events occurring during the extended winter season (November–March; NDJFM) are considered. A list of SSW events identified from ERA5 is provided in Table S1 and forms the basis of the composite analysis.

Statistical significance assessment

Statistical significance of the SSW composite-mean maps (e.g., Fig. 1a, d) is assessed using 1000 bootstrap composites generated by resampling, with replacement, 44 SSWs from the events used in the composite analysis. Anomalies in the SSW-composite-mean map are considered statistically significant at the p < 0.10 level (two-sided test) if the 5th–95th percentile range derived from the randomly generated composites does not encompass zero.

Statistical significance of the SSW composite-mean time series (e.g., Fig. 1b, c, e, f) is assessed using 1000 sets of non-SSW events. Each set shares the same central dates as the observed SSWs but is randomly sampled from extended winters (NDJFM) without an SSW, thereby accounting for the seasonal cycle. Anomalies in the SSW-composite-mean time series are considered statistically significant at the p < 0.10 level (two-sided test) if they fall outside the 5th–95th percentile range (shading in Fig. 1b, c, e, f) derived from the non-SSW spread, defined as the distribution of the 1000 non-SSW composites.
