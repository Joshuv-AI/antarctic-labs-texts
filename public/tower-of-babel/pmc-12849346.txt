Barriers to timely and adequate antenatal care: a systematic review of socioeconomic, cultural, psychosocial, and health-system factors across high and low resource settings
Authors: Syeda Tabeena Ali, Sameera Ali Rizvi, Mah Talat, Simra Abuzar, Mahnoor Azhar, Marwa Rehman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSignificant disparities remain in prenatal care utilization across countries, the specific socioeconomic and systemic factors contributing to these gaps have not been consistently synthesized. This review evaluates the gap by comparing barriers to timely and adequate prenatal care between high and low resource settings by focusing on the factors affecting prenatal service utilization through socioeconomic status and geography cultural barriers and system-related factors.MethodologyThe systematic review explored databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect. The recent studies from 2014 up to 2024 underwent evaluation based on Boolean operators combined with indexed keywords. Eligible studies included quantitative study designs examining barriers to prenatal care among women of reproductive age. Screening was conducted by two reviewers independently by following PRISMA guidelines. A total of 20 studies were included as per the eligibility criteria. Due to heterogeneity in study design, a structured narrative review synthesis was conducted.ResultsThe main barriers identified were Socioeconomic limitations because of low income and insufficient insurance coverage and the challenges of distance to healthcare facilities and inadequate transportation combined with cultural barriers such as traditional gender roles and stigmatization and traditional beliefs. The barriers identified in high-income settings were that immigrant and refugee women faced challenges due to their status uncertainties, difficulties with language and unfamiliarity with healthcare systems. Depression along with abuse and multiple caregiving duties emerged as a major psychological factor that prevented women from accessing healthcare. System-related factors like Long waiting times and inadequate medical personnel along with insufficient respect from healthcare providers were the major identified factors.ConclusionThe utilization of prenatal care is influenced by a multifaceted interplay of the individual, cultural, socioeconomic, and systems factors. It is important to strengthen these community-based measures, including financial and structural assistance, to enhance equitable, timely, and sufficient prenatal care across diverse settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08565-1.Keywords: Prenatal care, Socioeconomic barriers, Strengthening community outreach

Introduction

Maternal and child health depends significantly on prenatal care because this intervention protects both maternal health and fetal development. Prevention strategies and medical examinations for diagnosis and treatment are meant to keep close watch on pregnancy and identify problems to help improve fetal health [1]. Many women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) encounter so many obstacles that prenatal care is often not used to its full advantage. Many things block women from getting prenatal care, including financial trouble, the location of services, problems with the healthcare system and various cultural and personal factors [2, 3].

Some women do not get early prenatal care because they cannot afford the high costs of medical checkups or transportation. Even with lower rates, many cannot afford healthcare when they depend on assistance from public programs [4]. There are serious geographical issues in rural and remote areas, which stem from how far away they are and the inadequate infrastructure [5]. Physician shortages in the healthcare system cause difficulties and long waiting periods, which influence patients to skip or postpone their usual healthcare visits [6]. The choices women make about their health can be affected by the values in their culture, social norms and how educated they are, often making them choose traditional medicine instead of seeing a doctor [7].

Rising stress, mental health problems and stressful living can lead to difficulties in getting medical care. Those who go through domestic violence, as well as being socially isolated, generally do not have the help they need, which makes it hard to interact with health services [8]. Many struggling issues in high-income countries, such as language issues, difficulty obtaining citizenship and unfamiliarity with healthcare, are experienced by refugees and immigrants [9].

Limited prenatal care exposure results in severe health consequences for both mother and baby which lead to more complications and premature delivery and underweight newborns and newborn deaths. Community outreach programs must be strengthened because they play an essential role in closing these gaps in high- and low-resource settings. Community health workers, together with mobile clinics or peer educators through outreach programs, can bring underserved women into healthcare services while using culturally appropriate support and trust. While such problems have been described in country-specific studies, there has not been a recent systematic review comparing problems with prenatal care access across resource settings. Such a larger-scale comparative approach may guide worldwide, context-sensitive interventions to improve maternal health.

Hence, the objective of this systematic review was to explore and synthesize the socio-economic and contextual determinants for poor uptake of timely and comprehensive antenatal care for both high- and low-resource settings, thereby informing equitable and focused interventions.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted by following the PRISMA guideline 2020. The review was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251065584. The full protocol is accessible at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251065584.

Search strategy

The literature was searched in five databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and ScienceDirect. The search was limited to the years January 2014–March 2024, with search syntax terms, Boolean operators and indexed keywords such as “barriers to prenatal care,” “prenatal care utilization,” “maternal healthcare access,” and “socioeconomic factors prenatal care”. The detailed search strings for all databases are provided in Appendix 1.

Eligibility criteria

The PICO criteria were developed to set inclusion criteria. Studies were eligible if they were about women in the reproductive age group, reported quantitative data on barriers to utilization of timely or adequate antenatal care. They were peer-reviewed studies written in English that used cross-sectional, cohort, or case-control study designs with statistical analysis. We excluded articles that were reviews or opinions, conference abstracts and those not focused on barriers to antenatal care.

Screening and data extraction

Two reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of identified studies independently. If they were considered potentially suitable, full texts were retrieved. Differences in opinion during screening and selection were resolved by discussion or by consulting with a third reviewer. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s κ. Significant agreement was observed for both phases: title/abstract screening κ = 0.81 and full-text screening κ = 0.87, indicating high reliability between reviewers. Differences were fixed through discussion and, consultation with a third reviewer.

Two reviewers also independently extracted the data using a pre-tested data extraction form. Data extracted included author characteristics, year of publication, location of the study, study design, characteristics of the sample, statistical methods, and identified barriers to prenatal care. Data were next divided into categories based on the eligibility criteria.

Quantitative meta-analysis could not have been done because of the high heterogeneity in the study designs, outcome definitions and population characteristics. Rather, a systematic narrative synthesis process was adopted, which is based on the methodological framework of Popay et al. [10]. The strategy included the formulation of a preliminary descriptive synthesis, relational scrutiny within and between studies, and evaluation of how well the evidence can be meant to produce contextually sensible findings. The comparative analyses were given in high and low resource settings (Table 2).

Operational definitions

In this systematic review, the term ‘timely antenatal care (ANC)’ is defined as the beginning of prenatal services within ≤ 12 weeks’ gestation, according to early-booking criteria. Similarly, the operational definition of adequate ANC is based on a two-tier operational framework: the first standard is the use of 8 contacts, and the second acceptable option is the ≥ 4 ANC visits, adding the WHO recommendation as well. To standardize the comparative synthesis of heterogeneous definitions using the different original definitions of a particular concept, a mapping exercise (Supplementary Table 3 Mapping of Operational Definitions of ANC Across Included Studies) was performed to align the original definition of each included study regarding the concept of timely or adequate.

Quality appraisal and risk of bias

We utilized the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tools specific to study design utilized to appraise the quality of evidence and risk of bias [30]. Two reviewers independently appraised each study. The items of assessment were elements associated with sample selection, confounders, outcome measures, and the analysis method used. Differences were adjudicated through consensus. A summary of the findings of this assessment is shown in Supplementary Table 4 (Risk of Bias Assessment and Certainty of Evidence of Included Quantitative Studies). Quality of evidence We assessed all included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists that were relevant to the study design (cross-sectional, cohort or case–control studies). Two reviewers evaluated the quality of respective studies based on the clear objectives, appropriate design, sample, justified, valid measures, adequate analysis, bias considered and overall quality.

Differences were determined by discussion and by an expert review. The complete quality of every article was categorized as low, high, and moderate. The summary of the quality appraisal is presented in Supplementary Table 4 (Risk of Bias Assessment and Certainty of Evidence of Included Quantitative Studies).

Since the number of included studies per outcome was small < 10, it was not statistically appropriate to run funnel plots or regression tests by Egger. Thus, a narrative approach evaluation of the publication bias was conducted with the possibility that the studies that found significant results with regard to socioeconomic barriers to prenatal care faced the higher probability of being published compared to the studies with no results or negative. This possibility of bias was considered during the interpretation of findings.

Results

The reviewed study collection included 20 research articles conducted across high and lower-middle income nations including research conducted in USA, Canada, France, Spain, Nigeria, Kenya, Ethiopia, India, Rwanda, Ghana, and Pakistan. Most researchers applied cross-sectional methods yet the studies also included cohort and retrospective cohort approaches and case-control designs. The studies included from 43 to 180,000 female participants [16, 17, 27]. The characteristics of the included studies are presented in Table 1.

Summary of studies on socioeconomic barriers to prenatal care services

The main results from each study are summarized in Table 1. Socioeconomic limitations such as insufficient income and uninsured status and service costs barred people from prenatal care in both economically privileged and less fortunate areas [16, 17, 20]. Studies from Ethiopia and Nigeria together with Pakistan consistently mentioned geographical barriers that included remote residence and poor access to health services and bad transport systems [19, 24, 26]. The studies from Ghana and Kenya showed cultural and attitudinal factors had significant influence on prenatal care utilization mainly through male consent requirements and antenatal misconceptions and fears of witchcraft [25, 27].

Foreign migrant women faced difficulties to early prenatal care due to their immigrant status and language barriers and lacking legal paperwork in high-income nations of France, Canada and the USA [14, 19, 21]. The percentage of inadequate prenatal visits among non-native women exceeded native-born women by a minimum factor of two according to research from both France and Canada [14, 19]. Multiple studies detected psychological obstacles which include depression along with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) along with a history of abuse and school-work tensions primarily affecting adolescent or undocumented women in both the USA and Spain [21, 22].

Various health systems experienced delays in appointments and unresponsive providers together with disrespectful maternity care and unsupportive provider attitudes as barriers to care [19, 25, 27]. COVID-19 brought extra delays together with service disruptions to healthcare services in Kenya [27]. Studies based in the United States examined racial and ethnic disparities by showing white women had fewer structural and interpersonal barriers than Black and Latinx women during their prenatal care period [21, 22].

The included studies were found to have a great deal of heterogeneity, which was mainly due to the difference in study designs and other factors such as population characteristics and the contextual settings. Differences were detected in the definitions and measurements of adequate prenatal care, modes of data collection, and indicators of outcomes. In high-income countries, studies frequently targeted migrant or minority women who were vulnerable in socioeconomic terms, whereas studies in low and middle income countries considered more general structural issues, including poverty, inadequate infrastructure, and shortages in the workforce in healthcare. Geographic disparity, urban versus rural population, and time variations, such as studies being done in the course of the COVID-19 pandemic or after it, also added to the heterogeneity. Although these contextual and methodological variations existed, the general pattern always proved that the socioeconomic disparities and the constraints of the health system are the primary determinants affecting the use of prenatal care in any setting.

Quality evaluation and certainty of evidence

The appraisal of 20 studies was done with Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists and was chosen based on the type of study design (cross-sectional, cohort, or case control). The quality appraisal was done by two independent reviewers in areas such as clear objectives, proper design, justified sampling, valid measures, sufficient analysis and consideration of bias. Any difference was solved by discussion and professional contribution.

Among the 20 studies, ten were rated as high quality, seven as moderate quality and four as low quality. Good quality research was usually clear in terms of objectives, sample designs, and statistical tests. By contrast, moderate and low-quality studies were constrained by small sample sizes, incomplete reporting of bias or lack of methodology. A full quantitative summary of risk-of-bias ratings for all included studies is presented in Supplementary Table 4 (Risk of Bias Assessment and Certainty of Evidence of Included Quantitative Studies). This table includes domain-level scoring, overall study quality classification, and GRADE certainty levels.

To identify the degree of confidence (confidence) in the overall body of evidence, the JBI quality assessments were projected onto the GRADE framework and assessed the evidence across four domains:

Methodological restrictions: Informed by JBI appraisal outcomes.

Relevance: The level of compatibility of study contexts with the question under review.

Coherence: The similarity of the results between countries and resource settings.

Adequacy of data: The breadth and quantity of studies used in each of the theme.

According to such evaluations, the confidence in the evidence was moderate in general, as most of the studies had a sound methodology and revealed consistent results in different settings, and only slight differences in reporting quality were found between LMIC-based studies.

Narrative comparative analysis

Altogether, it was synthesized that although both high-income countries (HICs) and low and middle income countries (LMICs) have barriers to prenatal care, their size, nature, and causes are significantly different. In HICs, issues related to access are mostly structural and systematic, including the absence of insurance and provider bias and cultural illiteracy among migrant groups, and instances of inequities in otherwise resource-rich health systems. On the contrary, LMICs have fundamental access and availability issues, such as poverty, lack of infrastructure, and incompetent human resources, which aggravate delays and restrict continuity of care. In LMICs, gender norms and sociocultural constraints are more evident, and women tend to lack their freedom in seeking care due to the barrier of spousal approval and traditional values. On the other hand, psychosocial determinants like stress, depression and migration-related trauma come out as the leading obstacles in HICs. The COVID-19 pandemic increased the existing inequalities in both contexts, but the impact was more severe and acute in LMICs because the health systems are less resilient. Taken together, the results highlight the fact that, although both settings encounter the categories of barriers, they can be directed differently, i.e. systemic disparities in HICs and structural inadequacies in LMICs, which require context-specific responses.

Discussion

The review examines various elements and local factors which obstruct prenatal care access between high-income nations and lower- and middle-income countries. Multiple studies confirmed that socioeconomic status creates obstacles to timely and sufficient prenatal care because low income and insurance-lessness, combined with financial struggles, prevent patients from obtaining care [11, 15, 19]. Urban and rural areas presented these barriers, but low-resource countries like Ethiopia, Nigeria, and Pakistan showed the greatest effect on prenatal care utilization [14–16].

The implementation of healthcare services faced considerable geographical obstacles alongside poor infrastructure in countries with low and middle incomes. Studies showed that inadequate distance to healthcare facilities and non-existent transportation links, and being a resident of rural areas, often make it difficult for patients to receive care [27, 29]. Research investigations from Kenya and Ethiopia established that rural women failed to start antenatal care early or achieve the necessary appointment count because of existing structural obstacles in their paths [14, 27].

Access to antenatal care became more challenging because of cultural and gender-related social restrictions in various African and South Asian regions. Women avoided seeking antenatal care because they needed permission from their husbands or family and faced discrimination about pregnancy disclosure, combined with wrong beliefs regarding care [16, 31, 32]. Female decision-making power, in addition to traditional beliefs, both impaired service utilization according to research conducted in Ghana and Pakistan [16, 31].

Immigrant and refugee women residing in high-income countries struggle with specific obstacles that include their legal documentation status as well as language problems and unfamiliarity with local healthcare systems [13, 23]. The status of being a migrant produced a consistent pattern of substandard care delivery in Canada, France and the United States, while undocumented women encountered the highest risks for adverse outcomes [13, 21]. The results demonstrate how important it is to develop care approaches which are culturally sensitive and linguistically appropriate and legally accessible when working with diverse populations.

Among the studied groups, psychosocial stressors proved significant mostly among adolescent females and victims of abuse and those with mental health diagnoses. Research has shown that depression combined with PTSD, together with school or work demands, leads to inconsistent prenatal care attendance, particularly within high-income countries, including the USA and Spain [21, 32].

Health system-level issues formed the core issues that affected every region across the study. Women mentioned inadequate healthcare provider attitudes, along with prolonged waiting times and insufficient healthcare personnel and insufficient respectful maternity care as leading factors for skipping or ending prenatal appointments [20, 32]. The COVID-19 pandemic made existing barriers worse because it decreased the availability of healthcare services, particularly in Kenya and similar under-resourced areas [18, 27]. A facility-based cross-sectional study revealed that women who had delivered during the pandemic were more likely to delay their first ANC checkup by 72% than during the pre-pandemic period, and approximately half the women stated that COVID-19 had a direct impact on them and their capacity to visit care [33].

The United States displayed clear racial and ethnic disparities because Black and Latinx women faced additional structural and interpersonal barriers than white women [22]. The existing discrepancies demand swift measures which purpose intended to achieve healthcare equity and fight against racism while remodeling the healthcare framework [28]. A wide variety of research that uses the cross-sectional design, however, restricts causal analysis; nevertheless, creates a strong base to study different ways that prenatal care use is influenced worldwide. The interventions need adaptation based on economic boundaries, together with sociocultural and systemic factors which determine women’s healthcare experiences. We evaluated methodological weaknesses as well as bias risks of the included research through structured critical appraisal instruments. Due to the high risk of bias identification, we approached such studies with caution, while their analysis did not affect this review’s main findings.

Study limitations

This review has several limitations, including variability in study designs and definitions of timely or adequate prenatal care, which limited comparability and precluded meta-analysis. Most included studies were cross-sectional, restricting causal inference. Country classification followed World Bank 2024 categories, though intra-country disparities may persist. Furthermore, the lack of funding source disclosure and unreported conflicts of interest in several studies may have limited transparency and introduced potential reporting bias. The review included only studies published in English due to resource constraints and a lack of validated translators. This may underestimate evidence from non-English LMIC studies., potentially excluding relevant studies in other languages.

Implications for policy, practice, and future research

Improving ANC utilization in LMICs requires targeted policies that reduce financial, structural, and gender-related barriers. Health systems should strengthen community outreach, ensure respectful and accessible ANC services, and integrate flexible, patient-centered delivery models. Future research must standardize ANC outcome definitions and evaluate context-specific interventions. Focus on underserved and high-risk groups remains essential to achieving equitable maternal health outcomes.

Conclusion

The utilization of prenatal care is influenced by a multifaceted interplay of the individual, cultural, socioeconomic, and systems factors. In developed nations, the barriers are usually associated with immigration processes, language difficulties, and social stresses, whereas the low- and middle-income countries experience challenges in inadequate infrastructures and workforce shortages, as well as financial constraints. To deal with these inequities, integrated context-sensitive strategies should be provided, which enhance the community outreach and engagement. Trust, cultural relevance and referral to antenatal care can be improved by the involvement of community health workers, mobile clinics, peer educators and groups of local women. It is important to strengthen these community-based measures, including financial and structural assistance, to enhance equitable, timely, and sufficient prenatal care across diverse settings.
