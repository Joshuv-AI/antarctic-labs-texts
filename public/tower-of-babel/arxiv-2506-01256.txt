Confidence intervals for forced alignment boundaries using model ensembles


Abstract
Forced alignment is a common tool to align audio with orthographic and phonetic transcriptions. Most forced alignment tools provide only a single estimate of a boundary. The present project introduces a method of deriving confidence intervals for these boundaries using a neural network ensemble technique. Ten different segment classifier neural networks were previously trained, and the alignment process is repeated with each model. The alignment ensemble is then used to place the boundary at the median of the boundaries in the ensemble, and 97.85% confidence intervals are constructed using order statistics. On the Buckeye and TIMIT corpora, the ensemble boundaries show a slight improvement over using just a single model. The confidence intervals are incorporated into Praat TextGrids using a point tier, and they are also output as a table for researchers to analyze separately as diagnostics or to incorporate uncertainty into their analyses.

1
Introduction

Forced alignment is a tool used to annotate where words and/or segments occur in an audio signal. Forced aligners take in an audio file, an orthographic transcription, and a so-called “grapheme to phoneme” dictionary, which are used to produce a TextGrid with segment and word boundaries usable in Praat
(
Boersma & Weenink 2024a
)
. Such tools are still widely used across research in phonetics and speech science
(
Mahr et al. 2021a
;
Yuan et al. 2023a
)
and were historically part of how automatic speech recognition systems were developed with hidden Markov models. These systems only provide a point estimate of the boundaries between segments and do not, traditionally, quantify the uncertainty around the boundary placement. The present paper introduces a method for deriving confidence intervals on forced alignment boundaries by using ensemble methods. A representational format for the confidence intervals using TextGrids in Praat
(
Boersma & Weenink 2024a
)
is also proposed. Finally, a method for comparing alignments with mismatched numbers of segment boundaries using dynamic time warping is evaluated, with a potential use case for comparisons between “ground-truth” transcriptions based on manual annotation and transcriptions derived from orthography.

Ensemble methods—such as model averaging—are not unheard of in machine learning
(
Breiman 1996a
;
Carney et al. 1999a
)
. They are prominent enough that competition-winning models have used such techniques to improve their performance
(
Koren 2009a
;
Krizhevsky et al. 2012a
)
, and they are basic enough to be covered in textbooks
(
Goodfellow et al. 2016a
)
. Despite their potential to offer uncertainty estimates of the boundaries, ensemble methods do not yet seem to have been used in creating end-user forced alignment systems.

A system that produces estimates of uncertainty in forced alignment models has a variety of potential benefits. The first of these is that it allows for more gradient representation of where it would be reasonable to place a boundary. Of course, confidence intervals should not be interpreted as theoretically-derived reasonable ranges for boundary placement. Second, ambiguous boundaries should exhibit greater uncertainty due to uncertainty in the acoustic models, offering a better reflection of the phonetic gradience between segment categories than would a single discrete boundary. Third, having confidence intervals would aid researchers in determining where the alignment model has made outlying predictions and caused significant errors in alignment.

2
The nature of forced alignment boundaries

Before deriving confidence intervals, I believe it is important to lay out a solid interpretation of what a forced alignment boundary is. In doing so, the nature of the uncertainty in the boundaries can be made more explicit, which may help avoid misinterpretations over what a confidence interval on a boundary represents. The nature of these boundaries is not straightforward to reason about since the time points for segment boundaries do not come directly from an acoustic model that outputs conditional probabilities of segment classes given some acoustic input. Rather, the time points come from subsequent processing of the model output.

For forced alignment systems that make use of boundaries from pre-aligned data
(
Gorman et al. 2011a
;
Kelley et al. 2024a
;
McAuliffe et al. 2017a
;
Kisler et al. 2012a
;
Yuan & Liberman 2008a
)
, an assumption is made that these are “true” boundaries, and the boundaries in the data used for training are taken as such. This assumption is made whether the boundaries are used for training or just comparison during evaluation. Yet, this assumption is clearly wrong since there is no “true” point of demarcation between two segments, trivially so because time is uncountably infinite and a range of segmentations is acceptable, and more meaningfully so because some segment combinations like approximant-vowel, vowel-vowel, and vowel-approximant sequences inherently have no clear, inarguable moment of demarcation. Nevertheless, this assumption is required in a utilitarian and not realist sense to get off the ground, much in the same way that linear regression assumes linearity.

With the assumption of true boundaries in mind, for most forced alignment systems, the acoustic model is approximating a function producing a time-varying conditional distribution is learned that outputs
P(\psi_{i}=\kappa|x_{i})
over all
\kappa\in k
, with
k
as the set of segment classes the acoustic model has. Additionally,
\psi
is the correct segment category and
i
represents a discrete time step on the interval
[1,\,n]
(with
n
as the total number of time steps in an audio file). In prose, the function outputs a distribution of probability over all segment categories in the system, which are specified by the user during training. Note that, for recurrent neural networks, the way that
P
is evaluated takes into account the sequential context of the moment where each individual
x
occurs.

When
P
is evaluated over all
n
time steps in an audio file, a matrix of probabilities is produced. The logs of the probabilities in this matrix are used in the alignment process to find a path that yields the maximal cumulative probability of labels that yields the specified label sequence
l
. That is, through dynamic programming, the alignment algorithm
(
Kelley et al. 2024a
, such as the Viterbi algorithm or the modification in)
yields the solution
c
defined as

c=\mathop{\arg\max}_{s\in S}\sum_{\sigma_{i}\in s}\log(P(\psi=\sigma_{i}|x_{i}))\,,

(1)

where
S
is the set of all sequences
s
that map onto the length-
m
label sequence
l
. That is, each
s
will collapse into
l
(the correct transcription) when duplicates are removed.

As a brief example, consider an audio file with five time-steps needing aligned labels, meaning that
n=5
and all
s\in S
are also therefore of length 5. Assume the correct transcription is [las] for Spanish
las
‘the.
fem.pl
’, implying that
m=3
. There are several sequences of segments that would yield [las] after removing duplicates. These include {laaas}, {llaas}, and {lasss}. Each of those examples would be one instance of
s\in S
, and each symbol in each sequence would be a
\sigma_{i}\in s
. For instance, for {laaas},
\sigma_{1}=\text{l},\,\sigma_{2}=\text{a},\,\sigma_{3}=\text{a},\,\sigma_{4}=\text{a},\,\sigma_{5}=\text{s}
. If the sequence with the highest probability were {laaas}, we would have that
c=\{\text{laaas}\}
.

In sum,
c
is the sequence of segment labels that collapses to the correct transcription with the highest overall probability given the acoustics is produced The solution
c
is not necessarily unique, of course, and it is possible that there is more than one
s
in
S
that maximizes the probability.

Working from the sequence in
c
, a vector of time points
t
of length
m
is derived, where each
\tau\in t
represents the latest moment for which

P(c^{\prime}*\lambda_{j})>P(c^{\prime}*\lambda_{j+1})

(2)

is true, constrained by
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\ldots<\tau_{m}
. In this example,
*
is a concatenation operator,
c^{\prime}
is the subsequence of
c
before adding the next symbol
\sigma
to the sequence, and
i<n
. Note that each
\lambda_{i}
is an element in
l
, the correct transcription (or sequence of collapsed labels). Here, there is effectively a decision for whether to continue assigning the current segment symbol
\lambda_{j}
or to proceed to the next one
\lambda_{j}+1
. Returning to the example of Spanish [las], it would be like choosing whether to continue saying the acoustics belong to [l] or proceed to say they acoustics now belong to [a].

The last symbol at time point
n
is a special case that is required to ensure that
\lambda_{n}
occurs at least momentarily in the output. These statements are merely a symbolic reformulation of how the backtracing algorithm derives
c
from the dynamic programming matrix.

Ultimately, each time point
\tau
in
t
of a boundary between segments is a quantity that is being estimated using the conditional probabilities a system outputs. Because of the assumption of true boundaries, there is an implicit assumption that the boundaries in the training set occur at the same moments of probability difference. Were this not the case, the boundaries would make little sense in the first place.

To summarize, the boundaries in a forced alignment system represent the latest moment in time where one segment symbol was more likely given the acoustics than the following symbol. Note that this is distinct from other possible semgentation schemes, like choosing the first moment when the following segment is more probable than the current one, which may result in premature boundaries since segment probabilities are not guaranteed to rise and fall smoothly throughout a particular utterance.

2.1
Deriving confidence intervals

A neural network learns an approximation of
P
,
\hat{P}
. Each time point derived from the alignment process is than an approximation
\hat{\tau}
for each
\tau\in t
. When several neural network approximations of
P
are trained, multiple estimates are calculated for each
\tau
. Taken together, this ensemble can be treated as a sample of measurements of the “correct” time point
\tau
, and a confidence interval can be derived. In forced alignment, outliers are not uncommon, so a robust statistic like the median of the sample is more useful than the mean. As such, the boundary the ensemble produces should be placed at the median of
\hat{t}
. From order statistics, using the 2nd and 9th ordered values (that is, the 2nd lowest and 9th highest values) as the extremes of the interval will produce an approximately 97.85% confidence interval. Note that this confidence interval is not necessarily symmetric, unlike more familiar confidence intervals calculated based on the mean and standard error (which are more susceptible to outliers).

Additionally, little is known about how best to train the approximation
\hat{P}
since reasonable segment probabilities are difficult to derive
a priori
. In practice, one-hot vectors being used for training, a choice which does not align with phonetic theory
(
Kelley et al. 2024a
)
. For this reason, one should note that the confidence intervals are based on approximations of distributions that are already less-than-ideal.

It is also important to keep in mind that these confidence intervals are estimates of uncertainty in the central tendency of the approximations of
\hat{P}
. That is, they represent how much the models in the ensemble vary in their estimate of the boundary. Care should be taken so as to not interpret this interval as a “reasonable range” for where a boundary could be, nor as some kind of theory-derived range where a segment boundary can be placed. The intervals are simply a representation of how certain or uncertain the model ensemble is in where the boundary goes. Subsequent analysis may find that there are useful links between the uncertainty and more theory-driven ranges for boundaries, but this assumption should not be made
a priori
.

3
Empirical analysis

To test both changes in boundary placement and how well the uncertainty correlates with boundary error, an ensemble system was created using pre-trained models created while creating the Mason-Alberta Phonetic Segmenter
(
Kelley et al. 2024a
, MAPS,)
. There were 10 models, and they were previously trained on a mix of data from the TIMIT
(
Garofolo et al. 1993a
)
and Buckeye
(
Pitt et al. 2007a
)
speech corpora of American English, which have time-aligned phonetic transcriptions. The splits for training, validation, and testing follow the descriptions in
Kelley et al. 2024a
. The hidden layers in the neural networks were 3 layers of long short-term memory (LSTM) layers with 128 units in each layer. The input was 13 mel frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) with the zeroth coefficient replaced by log energy and with delta and delta-delta features. The output was a set of American English segment categories. More details are in
Kelley et al. 2024a

3.1
Methods

Two types of alignments were made. The first was set up to use the transcriptions given in the annotations for TIMIT and Buckeye, with the label foldings used in
Kelley et al. 2024a
. This process was performed by creating a dictionary following the format of the Carnegie Mellon University (CMU) Pronunciation Dictionary
(
Lenzo 2013a
)
, where the headwords were the filenames and the pronunciations were the segment strings in the files. The text annotations for the files were then just the filenames. The error was calculated as both mean and median absolute error.

The second type of alignment was using dictionary lookup of an extended version of the CMU Pronunciation Dictionary. As a trained phonetician, I transcribed and added any words that were missing from the 0.7a version of the dictionary. The pronunciation of unfamiliar proper nouns was verified against what the speakers said in the recordings. The boundary errors were then determined by applying dynamic time warping to the end times of all boundaries between segments in the reference segmentation and the system’s output. Dynamic time warping is a necessary process since the dictionary forms do not always match what the speaker says. For this reason, the dynamic time warping index of error is pessimistic of how well the aligner would do in practice. The dynamic time warping cost was divided by the number of segments
k
in the alignment system’s output as a way of normalizing the error and producing an average of the error for each boundary. Then, this average number was added
k
times into the vector containing all of the determined segment errors. This allows for the error metrics to be an average over all segments in the data set, rather than a grand mean of the average boundary error in each file.

As previously described, the boundaries for the alignment were placed at the median of the ensemble’s output, and the confidence intervals were constructed using the 2nd and 9th ordered values. If there was only one segment in the transcription for a file, it was excluded from evaluation since the onset and offset should be the start and end of the file, which does not depend on the acoustic model.

3.1.1
Representing confidence intervals in Praat

The TextGrid format for speech annotation in Praat currently allows for two different types of tiers: interval tiers and point tiers. An interval tier is made up of a series of intervals that have a defined beginning and end point. Within a given interval tier, intervals cannot overlap. A point tier is made up of a series of instantaneous points that have a single temporal moment of time. Both intervals and points can have a text annotation given to them.

A single interval tier is incapable of containing both confidence intervals and segment labels since the confidence intervals must, by definition, overlap the segment boundaries. Similarly, a single interval tier for just the confidence intervals is inadequate since two adjacent sounds may have their confidence intervals overlap if the duration of one or both is short or if there is a great deal of uncertainty in the estimate. It is, in theory, possible to have a sufficient number of interval tiers that represent alternating intervals. However, it is difficult to determine in advance exactly how many of these alternating tiers would be needed.

What I propose instead is to use point tiers to indicate the low and high edges of the confidence interval. Since points have only an instantaneous temporal extent, they are very unlikely to overlap with each other, save for problems of numerical precision or a highly improbable scenario where the confidence intervals of two separate sounds happen to have the exact same moment as extrema. These points can be labeled to help indicate which sound they are paired with.

3.2
Results

The boundary error results from the ensemble alignment are given in Table
1
. The values are generally a slight improvement over what
Kelley et al. 2024a
reported. This improvement is a benefit of using the median of the ensemble since the median is robust to outliers. As a category, forced aligners can have extreme outliers from cascading errors based on maladaptive behaviors of the acoustic models, and the median ameliorates that problem to some degree. Note also that the alignment using dictionary-based transcriptions of words fared worse than using the phonetic transcriptions in the corpora. This was an expected result since speech is such a variable signal, and deviations from dictionary forms are extremely well attested in phonetics
(
Ernestus & Warner 2011a
;
Lindblom 1990a
;
Warner 2019a
)
.

The use of dynamic time warping to assess boundary error also generally shows greater degrees of error. This would be expected using dictionary-based transcriptions since the number of boundaries is not guaranteed to be the same between the system output and the reference alignment. Yet, this pattern is also present when comparing the results of using dynamic time warping to the manual assessment for the manual transcriptions, when, in theory, these values should match. At least in part, this discrepant result has to do with certain annotations in the Buckeye corpus that were ignored during the more manual evaluation that were not ignored in the evaluation with dynamic time warping with minimal pre-processing. These tags indicated events like the interviewer speaking or the speaker laughing. The dynamic time warping technique was intended to be used here with little manipulation of the annotations, so the effect of these labels is not unexpected. More careful evaluation of the labels would allow for this effect to be mitigated, though it does require more time from the user and high familiarity with the data set.

Regarding the size of the confidence intervals, they are somewhat sensitive to outliers in the data since it only takes three poor alignments across the ensemble to include an outlier as part of the confidence interval. This behavior can be seen in the difference between the mean and median width of the confidence intervals presented in Table
2
. The median width is noticeably smaller than the frame advance size of 10 ms, suggesting that the ensemble outputs were within one frame of each other more often than not. Regardless, it is important to keep in mind that the confidence interval relates to the uncertainty in the ensemble’s output. This uncertainty may not necessarily relate to the quality of the boundary placement itself.

4
Discussion

The use of the ensemble of networks is an improvement over using a single network. This is in line with neural network theory and previous findings
(
Breiman 1996a
;
Goodfellow et al. 2016a
)
. However, the gained resilience to outlying boundaries should help researchers get more consistent segmentation. Of course, this resilience is intertwined with the quality of the acoustic model and the transcription. Using the median of the ensemble time estimates does not categorically prevent cascading errors from happening, nor does it completely prevent a poor alignment. Regardless, it should still yield improved boundary placements over the singleton model.

The confidence intervals derived from the ensemble are a useful tool for phonetic research. The width of the confidence interval can be a useful heuristic for researchers to detect errorful alignments for manual correction. Moreover, it may be possible to use the uncertainty in some of the estimates to score how well a transcription matches the acoustics, with the intuition that a good transcription should have less uncertainty in the segmentation than a poor transcription. Of course, the cumulative probabilities between different transcriptions can also be compared for this purpose.

One drawback of using the ensemble approach is that the alignment must be performed several times—in this case, ten. For the present case, it produces approximately a tenfold increase in the time it takes to perform the alignment. While multiprocessing could, in theory, alleviate this problem since the ensemble alignment is easy to parallelize, having the requisite hardware—whether locally or in the cloud–to do this may be too costly to be viable for many researchers in speech science. Future work should explore speedups to increase the feasibility of calculating confidence intervals. With that being said, the alignment process happens faster than real-time, so it is not as though modest data sets are impractical to align with this technique.

Future research should explore other techniques by which estimates of uncertainty for alignments could be derived. One such example might be using Bayesian neural networks, which can yield estimates of uncertainty through credible intervals. Although, how the uncertainty in the network’s predictions plays into temporal aspects of the segmentation is not as straightforward. It would also behoove the research community to have a segmentation format that more naturally allows for confidence intervals (or similar), whether through an extension to Praat TextGrids or a new format altogether.

The dynamic time warping approach to comparing boundary error between sets of time points of different lengths seems to be viable, though its results are clearly pessimistic in comparison to careful analysis where “ground truth” transcriptions match the model output. Such instances of mismatch occur as when taking an off-the-shelf model that cannot use the manual transcriptions from data sets like Buckeye and TIMIT. It may also prove useful for evaluating pipelines that use automatic speech recognition systems to create orthographic transcriptions before forced alignment, which would then still be passed through to some form of grapheme-to-phoneme model. The technique is, ultimately, a method for deriving an upper bound on the boundary error without needing the model output to match the “ground truth” transcription.

5
Conclusion

Speech segmentation is a naturally uncertain process. Neither previous forced alignment systems nor current annotation conventions have represented uncertainty in their outputs. The ensemble modification to MAPS and transcription scheme allow researchers to estimate and make use of confidence intervals on the boundaries. Having intervals of any kind associated with segment boundaries also represents a step toward more gradient or overlapping segmentation schema, which would be more in line with representational frameworks where overlap is permitted in articulatory
(
Browman & Goldstein 1992a
)
or acoustic
(
Kelley 2024a
)
dimensions.
