Increased Plasma β‐Secretase 1 Activity Correlates With Neurodegeneration in Cerebral Small Vessel Disease
Authors: Ya Su, Qiong Wang, Feng Gao, Jiajie Xu, Jiayu Fu, Yunqing Ying, Yiwei Xia, Xiaoxiao Wang, Qiang Dong, Yong Shen, Xin Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background β‐secretase 1 (BACE1) plays a key role in amyloidogenic pathway and is considered a new mechanism for cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD). We explore the potential role of plasma BACE1 in CSVD and the pathological process it may be involved in.Methods and ResultsWe enrolled 163 participants with CSVD (114 cerebral amyloid angiopathy and 49 hypertensive hemorrhage), and 96 cognitively unimpaired elders and 40 participants with Alzheimer's disease as controls. We measured BACE1 activity using a synthetic fluorescence substrate enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. We used regression models to investigate associations between BACE1 and imaging and blood markers as well as clinical outcomes in CSVD. Plasma BACE1 activity was significantly higher in CSVD (median 862.0 relative fluorescence units [RFU], interquartile range 700.6–1032.9) compared with elder controls (522.5 RFU, 438.3–662.1, P<0.001) but lower than Alzheimer's disease (964.0 RFU, 838.4–1225.0, P=0.032). For CSVD, there was an association between plasma and cerebrospinal fluid BACE1 activity (β=0.50, P=0.030). Plasma BACE1 activity was negatively associated with hippocampal volume (coefficient −2.40, P<0.001), and positively associated with blood neurofilament light chain (coefficient 0.08, P<0.001) and total tau (coefficient 0.15, P=0.006) but not with specific cerebrovascular imaging markers. During 2‐year follow‐up, BACE1 activity was independently related to dementia conversion (odds ratio, 17.72; P=0.001) but not stroke in CSVD.ConclusionsPlasma BACE1 activity is moderately increased and associated with neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment risk in CSVD. It indicates that BACE1 is a promising biomarker especially for CSVD‐related neurodegeneration.

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a key pathological condition that mainly affects small vessels of the brain and frequently coexists with neurodegenerative disease, leading to stroke and vascular dementia in the elderly. 1 The 2 predominant subtypes are hypertensive arteriopathy (HA) and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA). Arteriosclerosis in HA is strongly associated with deep intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), lacunar stroke and vascular dementia, whereas CAA is pathologically characterized by the deposition of β‐amyloid in cortical small arteries that commonly relates to lobar ICH with high recurrent risks. Further, CAA shares the pathogenic pathways of β‐amyloid generation and clearance but differs in brain injury and clinical manifestations with Alzheimer's disease (AD). 2 , 3 Despite of distinct pathological basis, both types regard endothelial dysfunction, blood–brain barrier breakdown and secondary neurodegeneration as common features in the brain. 4 Detecting and tracking these neuropathological changes in vivo is critical for the better understanding of CSVD progression. Currently more focus is given on neuroimaging features of CSVD including cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), white matter hyperintensity (WMH), lacune and so on, however, these widely used magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) markers essentially represent late and irreversible structural lesions rather than disease pathophysiology. 5

β‐site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1, also known as β‐secretase 1) is responsible for the first and rate‐limiting step in the amyloidogenic pathway and also relates to regulation of synaptic remodeling, which plays a key role in AD dementia. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 More recently, we and others have found that BACE1 was expressed by cerebral vascular cells in both HA and CAA, and provoked blood–brain barrier leakage, microbleeds, small vessel degeneration and neuronal dysfunction via amyloid‐independent and dependent pathways. 10 , 11 , 12 Thus, BACE1 is thought to be a relevant pathophysiological biomarker that may directly relate to the disease pathogenesis of CSVD.

Circulating BACE1 provides valid associations with core AD biomarkers in cerebrospinal fluids (CSF). 8 Elevated plasma BACE1 concentrations and activities significantly correlated with brain β‐amyloid accumulation and neurodegeneration and were predictive biomarkers for AD as well as relevant cognitive decline progression. 8 , 13 , 14 Increased plasma BACE1 activities also contributed to cognitive impairment deterioration in patients with diabetes. 15 A better understanding of the overall impact of BACE1 on CSVD seems important for optimally elucidating the clinical values of BACE1 as a blood biomarker.

We hypothesized that plasma BACE1 levels differed in cases with CSVD and could aid in the severity evaluation and outcome prediction of cerebrovascular damage and neurodegeneration in CSVD. For this purpose, in the present prospective bicenter CSVD cohort of patients with hypertensive ICH, CAA‐ICH, and CAA‐cognitive decline (CD), we compared plasma levels of BACE1 activity in cases with CSVD and elder controls with CSVD risks as well as cases with AD. Then we cross‐sectionally explored the associations between BACE1 activity in plasma and that in CSF and multimodel imaging and blood markers related to cerebrovascular damage and neurodegeneration. Finally, we performed a longitudinal study to examine whether BACE1 would predict new cerebrovascular events and cognitive performances over 2 years.

METHODS

Study Population

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. We prospectively collected cases with spontaneous ICH or cognitive decline suspected of CSVD (≥55 years) who were admitted at 2 independent medical centers: Huashan Hospital Fudan University (Shanghai, China) from January 2018 to June 2021 and at First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (Hefei, China) from January 2019 to June 2020. We also recruited cognitively unimpaired elders with CSVD risks from participants in Shanghai Aging Study 16 and cases with AD from CANDI (China Aging and Neurodegenerative Initiative) 17 as controls during the same period.

Cases with CAA were pathologically or clinically diagnosed with probable CAA according to the modified Boston criteria (version 1.5) and were then classified to CAA‐ICH and CAA‐CD based on clinical manifestations and MRI scan. 18 Hypertensive ICH was confirmed with a diagnosis of hypertension and ICH in the basal ganglia, thalamus, ventricles, vermis cerebelli, or brainstem. We excluded cases with ICH with a secondary cause such as cerebrovascular structural abnormalities, coagulation or systemic disorders, or hemorrhagic tumor. Given the undetermined pathology of mixed ICH/microbleeds, none of the cases with CAA had microbleeds or ICH in deep regions, and cases with hypertensive ICH with lobar microbleeds or ICH were also excluded. All participants survived at least 3 months after disease onset and completed blood sample collection, MR images, neuropsychological evaluation, and follow‐up information. Because BACE1 activities might be influenced by inflammatory response, 19 we also excluded patients in critically ill intensive care status or with infections or abnormal liver and kidney function.

For controls, cognitively unimpaired elders with CSVD risks were diagnosed with no complaints of stroke, CD, or other neurological diseases, and SVD score ≥1 on an ordinal scale (0–4) expressed by the cooccurrence of WMH, lacune, CMBs, and enlarged perivascular space. 20 The diagnosis of AD was based on the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association criteria (2011). 21 Cases with AD and >2 CMBs, cortical superficial siderosis, or lobar ICH were excluded to avoid coexistence with CAA. Also, the diagnosis of CAA‐CD instead of AD was made collaboratively by 2 experienced neurologists in stroke and neurodegenerative diseases.

The current study was approved by ethics committees of Huashan Hospital Fudan University, Shanghai, China (KY2015‐263) and First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei, China (2019KY‐26). The written informed consent was provided by the patient or a legally responsible relative.

Events

All cases with CSVD were followed up by phone calls at least every 3 months for new events and were arranged a face‐to‐face interview every year for clinical and cognitive information. Neuropsychological assessments included the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly, which systematically evaluated daily intellectual abilities of the patient over the past 10 years by requesting relatives 22 at enrollment and a standardized neuropsychological assessment for global cognition, episodic memory, attention, processing speed, language, executive and visuospatial function annually after recruitment.

For clinical outcomes, key end points included new‐onset stroke, dementia conversion, modified Rankin score >2 or death, analyzed as a composite outcome and also as individual outcomes. New‐onset stroke was defined as a new event of acute neurological deficits confirmed by corresponding lesion on computed tomography (hemorrhagic stroke) or on diffusion‐weighted imaging (ischemic stroke). Dementia conversion was defined as new‐onset dementia during follow‐up in patients without preexisting dementia at baseline. The diagnoses of preexisting dementia and cognitive impairment were made using the short Chinese version of the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly score ≥64 and between 53 and 63 separately, and new‐onset dementia during follow‐up was made based on the clinical assessment according to diagnostic criteria of National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer's Association 2011 for all‐cause dementia. 21 The tests included the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly and Mini‐Mental state examination (MMSE).

MRI Protocols and Imaging Analysis

Cases with CSVD and AD were scanned on a Siemens Magnetom Verio 3.0‐T MRI system and elder controls on a GE Discovery MR750 3.0‐T scanner separately on the same day of blood sample collection. Images included T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, T2 fluid attenuated inversion recovery and susceptibility‐weighted imaging. Two experienced raters blinded to the clinical information independently evaluated SVD imaging markers including CMBs count, cortical superficial siderosis extent, enlarged perivascular space, WMH Fazekas score, lacunes count, total SVD score, and medial temporal lobe atrophy (MTA) score according to the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging and validated scales as previously described (Data S1). 5 , 23 , 24

In cases with CSVD who were willing and able to undertake a detailed 3‐dimensional MRI scan, sequences included T1 MPRAGE (repetition time/echo time 1780/2.9 milliseconds, in‐plane matrix 512×512, slice thickness 1.0 mm) and T2 fluid attenuated inversion recovery (repetition time/echo time 9000/99 ms, in‐plane matrix 512×512, slice thickness 2.0 mm) for hippocampal volumes and WMH volumes. All quantitative calculations were from the lesion‐free hemisphere in cases with ICH.

For brain volumes, the hippocampus segmentation was performed using a novel algorithm that was more accurate compared with FreeSurfer v6.0. 25 The Hippodeep 26 based on 3‐dimensional deep convolutional neural network model was trained on a wide and variable set coming from multiple cohorts including synthetic data, which was robust on both anatomical variability and MRI sequence variability.

WMH lesions were semiautomatically segmented by voxel ranges using the ITK‐SNAP software (University of Pennsylvania, Philadelphia, PA; www.itksnap.org). 27 All WMH measurements were manually inspected to ensure their accuracy and were performed by manual interventions. WMH volume percentage was calculated after adjusted by the whole brain volume.

Blood Samples and Biomarkers Measurements

Fasting blood samples were drawn from each participant and collected in tubes with EDTA, after at least 3 months since event onset for cases with CSVD and on the same day of MRI scan for cases with AD and elder controls. For cases with CSVD who were suspected of AD, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or needed to exclude other neurological diseases such as inflammation and infection, CSF samples were taken by lumbar puncture with patients' consent. After centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes, plasma and CSF were kept at −80 °C until measurement. All measures were performed blinded to the clinical data, and all samples were duplicated for detection and intra‐ and interassay concentration variabilities were<15%.

We performed plasma and CSF BACE1 activity assays blindly as previously described. 15 Synthetic Application SpaceWarp peptide substrates containing BACE1 cleavage sites [MAC‐Ser‐Glu‐Ile‐Asp‐Leu‐Met‐Val‐Leu‐Asp‐Arg‐Lys (Dnp)‐Arg‐Arg‐NH2] were dissolved in 2 mmol/L stock solution with dimethylsulfoxide. We monitored a 100 μL reaction system (EX/EM, 320 nm/405 nm) containing plasma and substrates in reaction buffer (50 mmol/L sodium acetate, PH 4.0) using relative fluorescence units (RFU) (Synergy H1, Biotek).

NfL (serum neurofilament light chain) and plasma total tau were measured in cases with CSVD with 3‐dimensional MRI. We used the SIMOA NF‐light V2 Advantage (Cat No: 104073) and Neurology 3‐Plex A (Cat No: 101995) Kit respectively, per manufacturer instructions (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, US) on a HD‐1 platform at GBIO (Hangzhou, China) with dilution at 1:4 ratio.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics included counts with percentages [n (%)] and mean±SD for normally distributed continuous variables or median with interquartile range for nonnormally distributed continuous variables. We compared clinical characteristics and distribution of plasma BACE1 activities among elder controls with CSVD risks, cases with AD, and cases with CSVD, as well as 3 clinical subtypes of CSVD using 1‐way ANOVA (with Bonferroni as post hoc test for pairwise comparison) or Mann–Whitney test for continuous variables and χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Associations of plasma and CSF BACE1 activities in CSVD were calculated with linear regression adjusted for age and sex.

We log‐transformed BACE1 activity levels to obtain a normal distribution for further analyses in cases with CSVD and AD. First, we used univariable and multivariable generalized linear models to estimate the cross‐sectional associations between plasma BACE1 activities and each imaging and biomarker of cerebrovascular damage or neurodegeneration (age, hypertension and clinical subtypes as covariables) separately. We used variance inflation factor to indicate collinearity among multiple covariates. If collinearity was found (variance inflation factor >3), principal component analysis was used to extract the main components. Linearity was assessed by plotting the residuals versus the fitted values. Heteroscedasticity was accounted for by Breusch–Pagan test.

Second, for clinical outcomes, we used univariable and multivariable Logistic regression models with forward stepwise selection to calculate odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI, and confirmed whether plasma BACE1 activity could serve as an independent predictor for clinical outcomes in all cases with nondemented CSVD and subtypes. We identified the potential valuables associated with new‐onset stroke, dementia conversion, and composite outcomes (P<0.1) and variables with known potential clinical significance. Candidate variables were age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, preexisting cognitive impairment, CSVD subtypes, the occurrence of new stroke before new‐onset dementia, CMB count, cortical superficial siderosis score, WMH Fazekas score, SVD imaging score, MTA score, and plasma BACE1 activity. We also performed logistic regression models adjusted by clinical features (Model 1), clinical and imaging features (Model 2), and enrollment centers to further confirm the associations between BACE1 activity and clinical outcomes in CSVD and subtypes.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, Ltd, College Station, TX). A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered as significant, and a Bonferroni‐corrected P value of 0.007 was used for associations between BACE1 activity and 7 imaging and biomarkers.

Cases with CSVD showed higher percentage of hypertension and antiplatelet drug use, lower MMSE score, and higher total SVD score and MTA score than elder controls and higher percentage of hypertension and antiplatelet drug use, higher MMSE score, and total SVD score than cases with AD. Plasma BACE1 Activity Is Moderately Elevated in CSVD

For cases with ICH, the median (interquartile range) time from stroke onset to blood collection was 3.5 (3.0–4.0) months. Cases with CSVD showed significantly higher plasma BACE1 activities compared with elder controls (median [interquartile range], 862.0 [700.6–1032.9] versus 522.5 [438.3–662.1] RFU, P<0.001) but lower BACE1 activities compared with cases with AD (964.0 [838.4–1225.0] RFU, P=0.032). There were no differences of BACE1 activities among 3 CSVD subtypes (for CAA‐ICH, 828.8 [698.0–1075.6] RFU, for CAA‐CD, 861.0 [630.7–1002.3] RFU, for hypertensive ICH, 892.3 [780.3–1103.4] RFU, P=0.12) (Figure 1), nor in cases with CSVD with and without hypertension or diabetes or in different age and sex groups (Figure S2). We did not find an association between BACE1 activity and MMSE or Montreal Cognitive Assessment score nor differences of BACE1 activity between cases with demented and nondemented CSVD after adjusted by age and MTA score (1004.7 [863.0–1313.8] versus 842.6 [692.5–1025.4] RFU, P=0.08).

Associations Between BACE1 Activity and Cerebrovascular Damage in CSVD

In all cases with CSVD, plasma BACE1 activity was not related to either specific imaging marker of cerebrovascular damage, including CMB count (coefficient, −0.0005 [95% CI, −0.001 to 0.0005], P=0.32), WMH Fazekas score (coefficient, 0.01 [95% CI, −0.03 to 0.04], P=0.61), or WMH volume percentage (coefficient, 0.005 [95% CI, −0.02 to 0.03], P=0.63), cSS extent (coefficient, 0.07 [95% CI, −0.01 to 0.15], P=0.08), or total SVD score (coefficient, −0.03 [95% CI, −0.08 to 0.02], P=0.23) after adjustment by age, hypertension, and clinical subtypes. Similar results were found in 3 clinical subtypes separately (Tables S2 through S4).

Associations Between BACE1 Activity and Neurodegeneration in CSVD

We selected cortical atrophy (MTA score, as well as hippocampal volume for cases with 3‐dimensional MRI) and blood NfL and total tau as markers of neurodegeneration. In all cases with CSVD, significant multivariable‐adjusted correlations were found between higher plasma BACE1 activities and greater MTA scores (coefficient, 0.13 [95% CI, 0.07–0.18], P<0.001), decreased hippocampal volumes (coefficient, −2.40 [95% CI −3.53 to −1.26], P<0.001), and increased serum NfL (coefficient, 0.08 [95% CI, 0.04–0.11], P<0.001) and plasma total tau (coefficient, 0.15 [95% CI, 0.04–0.26], P=0.006) (Figure 3). In subgroup analyses, BACE1 activity was related to cortical atrophy in both CAA and HA and associated with blood NfL and total tau in the subgroup with CAA (Tables S2 through S4). Similar results were not found in 40 cases with AD (Table S5).

BACE1 Activity and 2‐Year Clinical Outcomes in CSVD

Last, we explored the predictive values of plasma BACE1 activity for new stroke, cognitive deterioration, dependence, and death in cases with CSVD. In total, 146 cases with nondemented CSVD at baseline were included and the composite outcome occurred in 44 cases at 24 months, including 17 cases of new stroke (1 ischemic stroke, 16 hemorrhagic stroke), 33 cases of dementia conversion, 7 cases with modified Rankin Scale score >2, and 3 cases of death (Table S6). Associations between candidate variables and either clinical outcome in univariable analyses were presented in Table S7. Multivariable analyses showed that plasma BACE1 activity was the independent predictor for both the composite outcome (OR, 5.40 [95% CI, 1.37–21.28], P=0.016) and dementia conversion (OR, 17.72 [95% CI, 3.30–95.13], P=0.001) but not new‐onset stroke, dependence, or death during 2‐year follow‐up, along with other risk factors including preexisting cognitive impairment and MTA score (Table 2).

In the group with CAA, the composite outcomes, hemorrhagic stroke and dementia conversion, occurred in 35.0% (23/60 with CAA‐ICH and 12/30 with CAA‐CD), 13% (11/60 with CAA‐ICH and 2/40 with CAA‐CD), and 27% (15/60 with CAA‐ICH and 12/40 with CAA‐CD) cases during 2‐year follow‐up. Baseline BACE1 activity along with preexisting cognitive impairment status was also the predictor for composite outcomes (OR, 8.81 [95% CI, 1.95–39.78], P=0.005) and dementia conversion (OR, 28.39 [95% CI, 4.56–176.85], P<0.001) but not stroke, dependence, or death (Table S8). Cases with hypertensive ICH had relatively lower incidence of composite outcomes (19.6%, 9/46), stroke (8.7%, 4/46), and dementia conversion (13.0%, 6/46) compared with cases with CAA. Multivariable analyses did not show any associations of BACE1 activity with either clinical outcome.

Similar results of plasma BACE1 activity and clinical outcomes were found with multivariable regression models adjusted by clinical features, clinical and imaging features, or enrollment centers (Tables S9 and S10).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we found that plasma BACE1 activity in CSVD was significantly increased compared with that in elder controls with CSVD risks but lower than that in people with AD, with no differences among CAA and HA subtypes. Further, higher plasma BACE1 activities were cross‐sectionally associated with CSF BACE1 activity and neurodegeneration in CSVD, including neuroimaging markers of cortical atrophy and core validated biomarkers of NfL and total tau, rather than corresponding measures of cerebrovascular damage. Last, BACE1 activities longitudinally predicted dementia conversion instead of vascular events in cases with CSVD during 2‐year follow‐up. To our knowledge, this may be the first study to indicate a potential role of plasma BACE1 activity in relation to neurodegeneration in CSVD.

Here, we reported a Chinese bicenter CSVD cohort with longitudinal follow‐up. First, the incidence of 2‐year ICH recurrence was nearly 3 times higher in CAA‐ICH than hypertensive ICH, similar to that reported in previous literature. 28 , 29 Second, the incidence rate of 2‐year dementia conversion was 25.0% in the group with CAA‐ICH and 13.0% in the group with hypertensive ICH, which was comparable with the 2‐year dementia incidence of nearly 30% in lobar ICH and >10% in nonlobar ICH from a prospective cohort study in France 30 and with the dementia incidence of 25.8% in patients without dementia with CAA‐ICH during a median follow‐up time of 2.5 years in the United States. 31 Further, we reported 30% of cases with CAA‐CD converted to dementia during 2 years. One cohort study enrolled 158 cases with nondemented non‐ICH CAA, and the cumulative dementia incidence at 1 and 5 years were 14% and 73% separately and that at 2 years was >20%. 24 Another study included 226 cases with CAA (15.9% with previous ICH) from memory clinic, the predicted MMSE score dropped from approximately 24 to 18 during 3‐year follow‐up. 32 Cases with CAA‐CD in our study showed relatively higher lobar CMB burden than previously reported (median number 26.0 versus 5.0), which might contribute to the rate of cognitive decline.

CSVD is a major contributor to cerebrovascular dysfunction and stroke and also disturbs structural and functional network connectivity and relates to neurodegeneration, thus leading to cognitive decline, gait impairment, and mood disturbance. 1 Previous cellular and animal studies have confirmed that BACE1 plays a key role in the amyloidogenic pathway and synaptic plasticity in the brain and acts as a new mechanism for CSVD pathogenesis. 7 , 12 Here, we examined whether plasma BACE1 activity levels differed in the 2 cases with most prevalent sporadic CSVD, HA and CAA. Cases with CSVD showed moderately elevated BACE1 activities, which were higher than those in sex‐ and age‐matched elder controls with total SVD imaging score ≥1 but lower than cases with AD. During the past 10 years, several in vivo studies showed good diagnostic and predictive performance of blood BACE1 concentration and activity in AD. 8 , 14 Some studies also reported an elevation of plasma BACE1 activity in other neurological diseases including schizophrenia, 33 emergence delirium, 34 or diabetes with cognitive impairment. 15 Further, a significant correlation between plasma and CSF BACE1 activity was found in chronic CSVD, which had not been reported in AD or other neurological diseases. One possibility was that CAA exhibited more severe blood–brain barrier disruption than AD, mainly because 1 study showed that plasma amyloid better identified CSF amyloid in cases with high blood–brain barrier permeability. 35 Our finding further expanded the utility of plasma BACE1 activity as a disease biomarker to cerebrovascular disease.

Despite of distinct pathogenesis of HA (arteriosclerosis in vascular risk factors‐related forms) and CAA (vascular amyloid‐related forms), no differences of BACE1 activities among the 2 clinical subtypes of CSVD were found. This might indicate less impact of brain amyloidosis but more secondary vascular and neuronal injury on plasma BACE1 activity in CSVD. One previous study demonstrated a positive correlation between BACE1 concentration and global amyloid‐PET uptake in cognitively normal individuals at risk for AD. 14 The variability could probably be due to different disease conditions and physiological processes of BACE1 concentration and activity. The BACE1 concentration was supposed to reflect gene expression whereas activity was more regulated by posttranslational modification. 7

Similarly, there were no differences of BACE1 activities between subgroups of distinct clinical manifestations (CSVD‐related ICH and cognitive decline) nor vascular risk factors (with and without hypertension or diabetes). Also, we did not find an association between plasma BACE1 activity and CSVD‐specific imaging markers nor vascular clinical outcomes in CSVD. One possibility was that BACE1 accelerated endothelial dysfunction and increased blood–brain barrier permeability rather than directly led to small vessel rupture and ICH in the brain, as no clinically hemorrhagic event was observed in aged mice with endothelial‐specific BACE1 overexpression. 12 Further, total plasma BACE1 activities might reflect more of the neuron‐derived BACE1 because its protein level was reported much higher in neurons than in endothelial cells. 7 It would be better in future to differentiate neuronal and endothelial BACE1 in the blood for explicating its role in the pathogenesis and prognosis prediction of CSVD.

Our major finding was a significant correlation between plasma BACE1 activity and neurodegeneration in CSVD. First, we found a significant correlation between BACE1 activity and multimodal markers of axonal damage, including structural indicators of brain atrophy and validated biomarkers of NfL and total tau. Further, the baseline BACE1 activity was an independent predictor for dementia conversion in CSVD especially for CAA after adjusted by the known confounding factors. However, BACE1 activity was not related to cognitive outcomes in HA. Because the relatively fewer end point events occurred in the subgroup with HA, a larger cohort with extended follow‐up would be needed. In addition, we included only cases with hemorrhagic HA, and future studies should enroll cases with ischemia like lacunar stroke to fully examine the role of BACE1 activity in HA.

Solid evidence suggested that BACE1‐induced microtubule disruption in presynaptic dystrophic neurites impaired axonal transport and integrity through amyloid‐dependent pathways in AD brains. 36 Furthermore, endothelial BACE1 elevation accelerated caveolin‐1‐induced nitric oxide synthase inactivation and then provoked tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic degeneration, and neuronal loss through amyloid‐independent pathways in endothelial‐specific BACE1 transgenic mice. 12 We first proposed that plasma BACE1 might be more closely linked to brain atrophy than hemorrhagic or amyloid pathology in CSVD. Our study provided evidence for peripheral BACE1 as a potential predictive biomarker for CSVD‐related neurodegeneration.

Our study has several limitations. First, the hospital‐based recruitment from a specific geographical area of Asian ancestry may limit the generalizability of our results. Second, the data of apolipoprotein E genotype should be collected in our future study for analysis, because some studies found a correlation whereas others show no effect for BACE1. 14 , 37 Third, given the nature of cross‐sectional correlation analysis of our present study, we cannot infer a causative relationship between BACE1 activity and neurodegeneration. Future longitudinal studies are required to elucidate how plasma BACE1 activities changed over time in relation to the neurodegenerative progression in CSVD. Fourth, because of the high co‐occurrence of CAA with AD, we could not fully exclude overlaps of AD pathology on BACE1 activity in CAA. However, we also found the elevated BACE1 activity correlated with neurodegeneration in HA subtype and differences of BACE1 activity between CAA and AD. Last, CSF samples were obtained from cases with CSVD requiring exclusion of other neurological diseases rather than through random sampling, which might introduce selection bias.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, plasma BACE1 activities are moderately elevated in CSVD and are associated with neurodegeneration other than cerebrovascular damage. Baseline BACE1 activity is also an independent predictor for dementia conversion in CSVD. Our findings imply that BACE1 may represent a potential pathophysiologically relevant biomarker for CSVD‐related neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.
