Using Donabedian’s model to explore healthcare providers’ perspectives on early recognition and initial management of paediatric acute kidney injury in rural Tanzania: a qualitative case study protocol
Authors: Dedius E Peter, Dickson A Mkoka, Pius Temba, Walter C Millanzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute kidney injury is a global health concern with significant implications for morbidity and mortality when it comes to children. High morbidity and mortality result from consequences such as metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, fluid overload and cardiovascular issues. Good clinical outcomes among children with acute kidney injury can be achieved through early recognition and initiation of management to prevent disease progression. However, evidence on early recognition and management of acute kidney injury in children is lacking, especially in low-resource settings such as Tanzania. This study explores healthcare providers’ experiences with early recognition and management of acute kidney injury among children attending primary healthcare facilities.Methods and analysisThis exploratory qualitative case study aims to capture contextual experiences using semi-structured interviews to gain thematic insights. The estimated sample size for this study is 20 informants and will be selected purposively. Data will be collected through individual interviews using an interview guide with predetermined questions followed by probing questions. Each interview session will last for about 30–60 min. Data analysis will begin during the data collection period using the six-phase thematic analysis approach by Braun and Clarke.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been provided by the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Senate of Research and Ethical Committees with reference number DA.282/298/01.C/2630. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments, all respondents will provide verbal and written informed consent and will be free to participate voluntarily with no incentives. The findings will be shared with participating healthcare facilities, district and regional health authorities and relevant stakeholders through presentations and summary reports. Findings will also be presented at national and international scientific conferences and submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) remains a devastating complication in paediatric health worldwide, linked to significant short and long-term morbidity, including a rapid decline in kidney function.1 AKI is a subset of acute kidney disease characterised by an abrupt and rapid decline in kidney function sufficient to cause an increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or by ≥50% within 7 days, or reduced urine output of <0.5 mL/kg/hour for at least 6 hours.2 According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, AKI severity is staged based on changes in serum creatinine and urine output. Stage 1 involves an increase in serum creatinine to 1.5–1.9 times baseline or an increase of ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6–12 hours. Stage 2 is defined by an increase in serum creatinine to 2.0–2.9 times baseline or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥12 hours. Stage 3 involves an increase in serum creatinine to three times baseline, serum creatinine ≥4.0 mg/dL, initiation of renal replacement therapy or urine output <0.3 mL/kg/hour for ≥24 hours or anuria for ≥12 hours.2

In children, as individuals from birth to <18 years of age,3 AKI is associated with high rates of illness, extended hospital stays and substantially increased mortality, especially in resource-limited settings. The burden of AKI is disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where it is mostly community acquired and often caused by preventable conditions common in sub-Saharan Africa, such as severe dehydration, severe gastroenteritis, sepsis and the use of nephrotoxic drugs.4 Globally, the pooled in-hospital death rate for children with AKI is about 18.3%, with mortality rates rising sharply as severity increases.5

In sub-Saharan Africa, outcomes are often worse, with mortality rates documented as high as 58% in severe cases.4 The situation in Tanzania exemplifies this heavy regional burden, with studies from Tanzanian tertiary care centres showing an alarmingly high prevalence of AKI among hospitalised children, ranging from 31.5% to 57.9%.6 Importantly, in-hospital mortality among these paediatric patients with AKI is extraordinarily high, with rates ranging between 46% and 70.6% in critically ill neonates.7 These findings suggest substantial delays in recognition and management despite evidence that AKI is frequently preventable and reversible when identified early.

Most of these children first arrive at primary health facilities (PHFs), emphasising the need for effective early intervention at the community level.8 PHFs serve as entry points to the healthcare system and provide preventive services, initial diagnostic assessment, referral services and limited specialised care, particularly in LMICs.9 These essential healthcare services are delivered at the community level through dispensaries, health centres and district hospitals by trained healthcare providers (HCPs).10 However, in rural Tanzania, these facilities often face major structural challenges, including shortages of healthcare personnel, limited diagnostic capacity, inadequate laboratory services and insufficient medical supplies, all of which hinder timely AKI recognition and management.

To evaluate these systemic failures, this study uses the framework developed by Avedis Donabedian, a physician and founder of the study of quality in healthcare.11 Donabedian’s Structure-Process-Outcome Model posits that the conditions in which care is provided (Structure) influence the actual activities of healthcare (Process), which in turn dictate the health status of the patient (Outcome). This model has been selected because it provides a validated, multidimensional lens to move beyond documenting poor outcomes and instead pinpoint whether failures arise from resource scarcity or clinical decision-making. However, the application of this model has inherent limitations, which, while it excels at identifying organisational gaps, may overlook the complex, non-linear relationship between social determinants of health and patient behaviour. To mitigate this and ensure methodological rigour, the research team includes a senior health systems researcher to ensure that the framework is applied with both theoretical depth and clinical relevance.

In African countries, including Tanzania, the structure of the PHFs is characterised by a healthcare worker-to-patient ratio significantly below the WHO recommendation of 439 per 100,000, with some regions facing staff shortages of over 50%.12 This leads to overburdened HCPs and reliance on less specialised personnel for complex paediatric cases. Nevertheless, early diagnosis of AKI relies on simple, essential tests such as serum creatinine measurement.13 Yet, data suggest a critical deficit as few as one in four PHFs possess the appropriate staffing level for qualified health laboratory personnel, with the situation being far worse in rural public facilities.14

Consequently, providers at the primary level must rely on subjective clinical signs such as dehydration, oedema, fatigue or reduced urine output, which are non-specific and often indicate advanced, harder-to-reverse AKI.15 The poor structural environment, moreover, inevitably affects the process of care as early management of AKI, dictated by guidelines such as KDIGO, requires vigilant fluid management and the prompt cessation of nephrotoxic drugs.16 In the context of limited diagnostics and high patient load, providers face immense pressure in their clinical decision-making.17 While studies have quantified the negative outcomes of delayed care, the Donabedian’s model can be used to elicit crucial insights for the evidence gap through understanding the provider’s experience, their challenges, knowledge gaps and the specific systemic failures that lead to a breakdown in the initial recognition and subsequent management of paediatric AKI before the child reaches a tertiary centre.1

While the high burden and poor outcomes of paediatric AKI in Tanzanian tertiary hospitals are well-documented, a significant research gap exists regarding the frontline realities of early recognition and initial management at the PHF level.16 Moreover, there is a critical lack of qualitative, in-depth data on HCPs’ perspectives, their knowledge, attitudes, perceived structural barriers and the clinical decision-making process that dictates care quality in this crucial initial stage. Existing studies focus largely on epidemiological outcomes, while none have comprehensively applied Donabedian’s model to understand how the specific structural constraints of Tanzanian PHFs influence the process of early AKI care as experienced by the providers themselves.6 18 19

This study addresses this fundamental evidence gap by employing Donabedian’s model to provide a comprehensive and systemic evaluation by focusing on healthcare perspectives, and will specifically explore how the documented structural deficits in Tanzanian PHFs translate into barriers in the process of early AKI care. The study will explore the experiences of providers, the specific structure and process gaps impeding the early recognition and initial management of paediatric AKI in Tanzanian PHFs, generating actionable evidence for targeted quality improvement and policy formulation.

By applying this model, this study may identify precisely where the breakdown in the system occurs, be it in the available resources or providers’ application of knowledge. Findings of this study will provide crucial, context-specific evidence to inform targeted quality improvement interventions, policy adjustments and educational strategies to improve this life-saving process at the point of first contact. Operational definitions

Early recognition

Refers to the timely identification of AKI through clinical assessment and diagnostics to prevent progression. Indicators include serum creatinine rise ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours or ≥50% within 7 days; urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥6 hours; physical signs such as oliguria and oedema; risk factors like dehydration, nephrotoxins and infections like malaria.20

Initial management

Refers to immediate actions to stabilise and address reversible causes postrecognition. Indicators include treating underlying causes, such as rehydration for hypovolaemia and antibiotics for sepsis; monitoring fluid or electrolyte balance; referral for Renal Replacement Therapy if unresponsive hyperkalaemia or fluid overload and multidisciplinary collaboration.21

Methods and analysis

Study design

This will be an exploratory qualitative case study employing a semi-structured in-depth interview to explore the HCPs’ experiences in early recognition and initial management of paediatric AKI in PHFs. This design is chosen for its strength in providing an insight, contextualised understanding of complex clinical practices and decision-making processes in AKI paediatric care.22 23

Study setting

This study will be conducted at Manyoni District Hospital, a public facility under the Local Government Authority in Singida Region, Tanzania, which provides essential paediatric services as part of its general healthcare services delivery. Serving as a referral hub for lower PHFs, it provides paediatric services including outpatient or inpatient care, integrated management of childhood illnesses and nutritional support.

Common paediatric conditions in the district include malaria, respiratory infections, diarrhoea and malnutrition, which are linked with AKI. Children who require further specialised management beyond the scope of the district hospital are usually referred to higher-level facilities. These include the Singida regional referral hospital, St. Gaspar Referral Hospital and Benjamini Mkapa Hospital in Dodoma, all of which have specialised paediatric and super-specialised services in Tanzania.

Study population

The study population at Manyoni District Hospital includes three main categories of HCPs, which are nurses, clinicians and doctors. The nursing staffing comprises a mix of registered nurses, nurse midwives and enrolled nurses. These professionals have completed formal training, typically holding diplomas or degrees in nursing. They are skilled in comprehensive patient care, including administering medications, performing medical procedures, monitoring vital signs and providing health education to patients and families. In a district hospital setting, they often lead shifts and coordinate care in high-volume wards, forming the backbone of patient care at Manyoni District Hospital. They are essential for managing conditions like AKI in children, often referred from nearby facilities.

Clinicians refer to mid-level practitioners, such as clinical officers and assistant medical officers, who bridge the gap between nurses and doctors in resource-constrained rural settings. They are trained to handle a broad range of clinical duties, especially in primary and emergency care, and are key in units dealing with paediatric AKI and other referrals at Manyoni District Hospital. Moreover, doctors provide higher-level medical oversight, particularly in specialised wards like medical, paediatric and surgical units, where children with AKI are investigated and managed before referral. They are fewer in number compared with nurses and clinicians, reflecting national trends where rural hospitals have limited access to physicians, with most concentrated in urban centres like Dar es Salaam.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Inclusion criteria

Licensed HCPs who are currently working in medical wards, paediatric wards and surgical wards where children are cared for. Indicators include having >6 months of experience in clinical practice in paediatric-relevant units. Also, HCPs will include trained nurses, clinicians and doctors. Indicators include professional qualifications such as a degree, a diploma and a certificate, and their roles include assessment or management practice. Moreover, those who will complete both verbal and written informed consent will be included.

Exclusion criteria

Those who will be unfit to impair their responses will be excluded. Also, those who will be on annual leave will be excluded.

Sample size and selection

The study informants to be recruited will be licensed nurses, clinicians and doctors. Triangulation across professions will ensure comprehensive perspectives of paediatric AKI care. To ensure varied perspectives on managing AKI in children, informant-rich cases will be recruited based on diversity in gender, profession and experience. The estimated sample size for this study is 20 informants and will be selected purposively.24 However, the information saturation will determine the actual sample size of the study,25 whereby recruitment will cease when no new patterns or insightful information will emerge from additional interviews. This will be observed through constant comparison of the latest data with previous transcribed data from interviews and field notes.

Data collection and procedure

The planned start and end dates for this study are between February 2025 and September 2026. Data collection will be conducted at Manyoni District Hospital, a public district-level facility in Singida Region, Tanzania. Eligible nurses, clinicians and doctors will be identified and will be given full information about the purpose of the study. Moreover, those who will agree to participate in the study will be contacted through mobile phone to schedule a suitable date and time for an interview. Interviews will be conducted during non-clinical hours and at convenient times to informants, with consent forms signed and audio recording consent sought.

A semi-structured interview guide will cover demographic information and open-ended questions on experiences regarding practices, challenges and strategies (online supplemental file 1). Probes or prompts will be employed to extract more information and depth. The interviews will be conducted in a safe private room and a digital audio recorder will be used. Field notes will be taken to capture non-verbal responses and emerging issues. The interview will be conducted in a well-ventilated, separate room to ensure confidentiality, and each interview session will last for about 30–60 min. The researcher will use the initial interviews, observations and field notes to shape the subsequent interviews to ensure the richness of the collected data. All interviews will be conducted in the Swahili language, which is convenient and the national language of the participants in Tanzania.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis with an inductive approach will be employed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the experience of HCPs in the early recognition and initial management of AKI in children. The analysis process will follow the six phases of thematic analysis by Braun and Clarke.26 The first step will involve familiarising oneself with the data by transcribing it verbatim, comparing transcripts with audio files and repeating the reading of the transcripts. The second step will involve systematically manually coding interesting features across the dataset and collating relevant data.

Furthermore, the third step will be to examine patterns and group them according to similarities. The fourth step will be to review themes and generate thematic maps. The fifth step will be to define and name themes and ongoing analysis to refine the specifics of each theme, and the overall story the analysis tells will be considered. The iterative process involving a researcher and the research team, input for rigour as qualitative experts, will be employed. The identified themes will guide the report writing and the selection of vivid extract examples, final analysis and relating the study to the research question and literature.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the study’s findings are trustworthy, the four principles of Guba and Lincoln, credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability, will be employed.27 Credibility will be enhanced through a well-prepared semi-structured interview guide, and physical and virtual meetings with the research team. In addition, probe questions, iterative questioning and paraphrasing of responses will be employed to obtain detailed information. Initial findings will be shared with informants during hospital visits to ensure their perspectives are considered and captured. Data will be transcribed verbatim from recorded files for analysis.

Moreover, dependability will be achieved through meticulous documentation and purposeful selection of informants. Interviews will be triangulated through observation and field notes, and the moderation of the interviews. Also, transcripts, field notes and audio recordings will be used to document the data collection process. Confirmability will be achieved through monitoring the recorded data process, triangulating data and member-checking. Also, quotes from participants will support the study findings. Transferability will be achieved through providing a detailed description of the study setting and informants’ characteristics, enabling comparisons with similar situations in Tanzania and other countries.

Discussion

The proposed study is designed to address a critical gap in the paediatric nephrology literature, particularly within sub-Saharan Africa. While the high prevalence and devastating mortality rates of AKI in Tanzanian tertiary hospitals are well-documented, the literature is conspicuously silent on the preceding failures of the primary health system, the critical point of first contact.28 The study’s primary significance lies in its dual focus on provider perspectives and the application of a rigorous system-level framework (Donabedian’s model). It anticipates moving beyond mere descriptions of high mortality to establish a clear, evidence-based link between structural deficiencies and the resulting suboptimal process of care. This systemic approach will offer a comprehensive diagnosis of the barriers to quality care for paediatric AKI in Tanzania.

The core anticipated contribution of this proposal lies in its potential to stratify the causes of poor outcomes using the Structure-Process-Outcome model. Based on the existing literature indicating acute shortages of qualified laboratory personnel and limited diagnostic availability in rural Tanzanian PHFs, it is anticipated that the findings of this study will confirm significant structural deficits. Specifically, it is expected that providers will highlight the absence of reliable, accessible point-of-care creatinine testing and challenges with accurate urine output monitoring. Quantifying these infrastructural limitations through provider experiences will underscore the necessity for policy changes, such as the strategic investment in decentralised diagnostics and strengthening the laboratory and care chain in PHFs.

The qualitative data on provider perspectives aim to highlight key failures in the clinical process, including gaps in knowledge about the specific, context-appropriate criteria for diagnosing early AKI. However, this study is expected to reveal challenges that HCPs face in delivering adequate fluid resuscitation or in identifying and stopping presumptive nephrotoxic agents, often due to limited guidelines or pressure from high patient volume. It may also uncover referral delays within the system from PHFs to secondary care, caused by a lack of transportation, funding or clear, standardised referral protocols. These findings will directly guide the development and implementation of targeted training and ongoing professional development programmes.

The use of a qualitative case study approach is intentionally chosen to capture the nuanced realities of care delivery. By focusing on the experiences of clinical officers, nurses and medical doctors who are the actual frontline personnel, the study ensures that data are contextually rich and highly relevant to the Tanzanian environment. This methodology reduces the factors that influence the quality of care in resource-limited settings. Additionally, the focus on PHFs addresses the common selection bias in AKI research, which often centres on tertiary centres and overlooks the critical stage where preventive and early supportive care can have the greatest impact on reducing the need for costly and usually unavailable renal replacement therapy.

Therefore, this protocol is a vital first step towards improving paediatric AKI care in Tanzania. By rigorously applying Donabedian’s model to capture the provider perspective, the study aims to produce actionable data essential for saving the lives of vulnerable children. The next research phase will concentrate on developing and testing quality improvement interventions directly based on these foundational findings.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been approved by the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences Senate of Research and Ethical Committees with reference number DA.282/298/01. C/2630. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments, all respondents will provide verbal and written informed consent and will be free to participate voluntarily with no incentives. They will be oriented on the study’s aim, interview procedures, benefits and risks. Participants will be given a special code instead of their names and audio-recorded files will be securely stored on a password-protected computer. A notebook containing observations and field notes will be kept securely to prevent unauthorised access to the study’s data.

The findings will be shared with participating healthcare facilities, district and regional health authorities and relevant stakeholders through presentations and summary reports. Findings will also be presented at national and international scientific conferences and submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals.
