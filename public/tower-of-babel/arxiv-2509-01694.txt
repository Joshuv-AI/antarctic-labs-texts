Abstract
. We propose a framework for resource provisioning with QoS guarantees in shared infrastructure networks. Our novel framework provides tunable probabilistic service guarantees for throughput and delay. Key to our approach is a Modified Dirft-plus-Penalty (MDP) policy that ensures long-term stability while capturing short-term probabilistic service guarantees using linearized upper-confidence bounds. We characterize the feasible region of service guarantees and show that our MDP procedure achieves mean rate stability and an optimality gap that vanishes with the frame size over which service guarantees are provided. Finally, empirical simulations validate our theory and demonstrate the favorable performance of our algorithm in handling QoS in multi-infrastructure networks.

1.
Introduction

Next generation (NextG) networks
(
Zeb et al. 2023
;
Nguyen and Modiano 2023
;
Nguyen et al. 2022
;
Nguyen and Modiano 2025
)
are emerging due to the rapid advancements of 5G/6G infrastructures
and in response to the stringent Quality-of-Service (QoS) requirements of modern data-intensive applications.
A key attribute of NextG networks is the architectural design that permits seamless integration of heterogeneous Infrastructure Providers (InP) to offer over-the-top (OTT) services to end clients
(
Liu and Modiano 2022
)
(See Figure
1
). Such utilization of underlying shared infrastructure not only enhances overall network capacity, but also facilitates robust service provisioning.
These advances in network capabilities call for the development of efficient infrastructure sharing mechanisms that can meet emerging service requirements.

In this paper, we focus on designing efficient algorithms for QoS-based service provision in shared multi-infrastructure networks.
Efficient service provision is essential for maintaining large-scale multi-infrastructure network operation in order to maintain utilization at the InPs, while meeting clients’ service requirements
(
Abdel-Malek et al. 2022
)
. From the system perspective, the implementation of a network orchestrator to facilitate the coordination of infrastructure sharing has been the de facto practice in NextG network deployment
(
Zeb et al. 2023
)
. Therefore, the design of infrastructure sharing for networked systems has garnered significant research interest, encompassing both generalized resource allocation
and specific contexts such as spectrum sharing
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
and network slicing
(
Leconte et al. 2018
)
.
Most of the previous work on QoS modeling focused on capturing simple requirements such as throughput
(
Neely et al. 2008
)
or energy level
(
Huang and Neely 2013
)
, which can be cast as long-term linear constraints. Unfortunately, more sophisticated requirements such as delay and reliability involve non-linear probabilistic constraints that are difficult to model and provision. While there has been a rich literature on modeling real-world QoS requirements (see
(
Hou et al. 2009
)

and the references therein), modeling realistic QoS requirements may result in non-linear problems whose solutions are often NP-hard
(
Rajkumar et al. 1998
)
and where relaxation techniques result in drastic sub-optimality
(
Xin and Xiang 2009
)
.
Moreover, there has been very limited work on QoS-based OTT service provision for multi-infrastructure networks, mostly focusing on empirical evaluation
(
Samdanis et al. 2013
)
.

Figure 1
.
Infrastructure sharing in NextG networks.

To exemplify a realistic shared multi-infrastructure network, consider a setting of two 5G cellular InPs and five clients running various applications, including video streaming, environmental monitoring and data backup, as in Figure
1
.
The InPs dynamically schedule links to serve the clients every 1 ms, which is the supported switching frequency of 5G cellular
(
Pedersen et al. 2016
)
.
Operating at a slower time-scale, the network orchestrator gathers the clients’ job requests and computes the scheduling decisions for the InPs to serve the clients every time-frame.
A common approach in the literature is to treat each time-frame independently
to enforce QoS within that frame
(
da Silva et al. 2020
)
. For example, to support real-time video streaming with delay requirement of 150 ms and negligible failure probability tolerance
(
Khan et al. 2024
)
, the scheduler can set the time-frame size to be 150 ms and provision full delivery of the required throughput per frame.
However, this approach neglects inter-frame QoS.
Specifically, environmental monitoring can allow delays of seconds to minutes, which correspond to thousands of orchestration time-frames, and does not demand immediate throughput delivery
(
Ho et al. 2005
)
, while data backup typically has no hard deadline requirement, in which the
data merely needs to be served eventually.
The design of QoS-based infrastructure sharing mechanisms thus requires a new QoS framework for OTT services that is powerful enough to ensure broad applicability yet analytically tractable enough to enable the provisioning of service guarantees.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose a novel QoS framework that captures delay and probabilistic service guarantees for OTT services. The framework is specified by three parameters: time-frame size, guaranteed delivery throughput and reliability. Specifically, our service provisioning is within a fixed time-frame, whose length
T_{s}
can be chosen to
capture stringent latency requirements. For every client
i
with type-
c
jobs, our framework specifies a guaranteed delivery throughput
\gamma^{c}_{i}
that is satisfied with probability at least
q^{c}_{i}
in each time-frame. Moreover, our model also takes into account queue stability of job requests to ensure long-term QoS for applications such as data backup, where timely but eventual service fulfillment is sufficient.
Closest to our work are
(
Neely et al. 2008
;
Hou et al. 2009
)
. In particular,
(
Neely et al. 2008
)
formulated a carefully designed network utility maximization problem (NUM) with stability constraints to jointly provision fairness and throughput guarantees and
(
Hou et al. 2009
)
provides QoS in the form of long-term guaranteed delivery ratio of deadline-constrained packets with probability 1. Our model extends NUM by incorporating both stability constraints and our proposed QoS framework. By setting
q^{c}_{i}=0
for all clients and jobs, our model reduces to the classical NUM formulation in
(
Neely et al. 2008
)
. For the required
\gamma^{c}_{i}
and
q^{c}_{i}\to 1
, our model aligns with
(
Hou et al. 2009
)
in providing guaranteed delivery ratio, while additionally satisfying queue stability.
Furthermore, our QoS model with appropriately chosen
\gamma^{c}_{i}
and
q^{c}_{i}
provides multi-time-frame delay guarantees.
This enables the jobs to be served over multiple time-frames to model applications with soft delay requirements (e.g. average delay) such as environmental monitoring and traditional Internet services. Finally, our framework’s modeling flexibility in tuning
T_{s},\gamma^{c}_{i}
and
q^{c}_{i}
allows for various combinations of all these models to provide heterogeneous QoS for different classes of applications.
To this end, we present a new QoS-based infrastructure sharing paradigm for NextG networks. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We present a novel QoS model that guarantees a specified OTT service within a bounded time-frame, which can be designed to capture hard delay requirements, with a pre-determined probability.
We demonstrate the application of our QoS model to a number of realistic scenarios.

•

We formulate the infrastructure sharing problem to ensure feasibility of the QoS requirements and stability of the queues.
We use upper-confidence bounds to linearize the probabilistic QoS constraints, and characterize the trade-offs between optimality of the service guarantees and the frame duration over which service is provisioned.

•

Based on the linearization technique, we develop the efficient algorithm, which minimizes the quadratic Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty, hence named Modified Drift-plus-Penalty (MDP), for the infrastructure sharing problem.
The MDP policy provably supports a stability region that is arbitrarily close to the original stability region, as the length of the time-frame increases. Moreover, we prove that the MDP policy attains an optimality gap that vanishes with the frame length. Empirical simulations demonstrate the efficiency of the MDP policy and its ability to handle realistic QoS requirements.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We present our system model in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we develop our new QoS framework and the formulation of the infrastructure sharing problem. In Section
4
, we propose the efficient MDP policy to solve the problem. The results on the optimality and stability guarantees of MDP are presented in Section
5
. In Section
6
, we demonstrate the application of our QoS framework in realistic scenarios and conduct empirical simulation of MDP to validate its ability to handle QoS constraints. We conclude the paper in Section
7
.

2.2.
System Model

Network Stakeholders:
We consider a resource allocation scenario with multiple Infrastructure Providers (InP) represented by
\mathcal{K}=\{1,2,...,K\}
and multiple clients represented by
\mathcal{I}=\{1,2,...,I\}
.
The InPs serve the clients to fulfill different types of jobs, indexed by
c\in\mathcal{C}=\{1,2,...,C\}
.
InP
k
is connected to and thus can serve a set
N(k)\subseteq\mathcal{I}
of clients. Similarly, client
i
is connected to and thus can be served by a set
N(i)\subseteq\mathcal{K}
of InPs. Let
E=\{(k,i):k\in\mathcal{K},i\in N(k)\}
be the set of all the links.
We consider an uplink system where the InPs receive jobs from the clients, which models a variety of real-world applications like video broadcasting or sensor monitoring; however, an analogous problem can be formulated for downlink system, and our results can be readily generalized to setting with both uplink and downlink transmissions.

Network Orchestrator:
The network orchestrator (NeO) is connected to all the InPs and clients to facilitate a centralized coordination over the network. From the system perspective, the NeO can be implemented via the Software-Defined Networking (SDN) paradigm, which separates the control plane from the data plane and provides a programmable management layer on top of the network; this approach of placing a SDN centralized controller on top of the underlying network for flexible control has been extensively utilized in resource sharing architectures
(
Qin et al. 2020
;
Leconte et al. 2018
)
. In our system model, the NeO helps facilitate the Service-Level Agreement (SLA) and meet QoS requirements by making scheduling decisions for the InPs.

System Time-Scales:
We consider two time-scales in this system model (see Figure
2
):

(i)

the orchestration time-frame
t\in[0,T-1]
over which the NeO gathers information such as incoming job requests and computes the scheduling decisions for the InPs to decide which clients and job types to serve. The time horizon
T
captures the entire lifespan of the system. The length of each time-frame
T_{s}
can be chosen to capture the latency requirement among applications of interest.

(ii)

the network operational time slot
\tau\in[0,T_{s}-1]
over which the InPs perform the actual scheduling within every orchestration time-frame
t
. Specifically, each time-frame
t
is divided into
T_{s}
equal operational time slots. The actual length of each operational time slot should be chosen in accordance to the transmission scheduling intervals of the InPs; for example, 5G InPs support sub-millisecond scheduling
(
Pedersen et al. 2016
)
, i.e. changing the schedule every 0.5 ms.

.

As the supported scheduling intervals for NextG network hardware are typically much smaller than the latency requirements of applications, we are interested in the regime of large
T_{s}
(of the order of hundreds as shown in Section
6.2
).

Policy Parameterization and Channel State Model:

At every operational time slot, each InP executes a randomized scheduling protocol that chooses the client to be connected to and the type of job to be served. To model the randomized scheduling procedure to be implemented at the InPs, we use

k
:

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i\in N(k)}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)=1,\forall k\in\mathcal{K},\tau\in[T_{s}].

For brevity, we write
\mathbf{p}(t,\tau)=\{\mathbf{p}_{k}(t,\tau)\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}

At the start of time-frame

T_{s}

Then, at every subsequent operational time slot

k

for serving jobs of type

p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)

\displaystyle d^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)=\mathbbm{1}\{\text{InP $k$ chooses to serve type-$c$ jobs from client $i$}\}

with

In this model, we assume that each client can connect to multiple InPs at every time slot

When InP

\displaystyle y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)=\mathbbm{1}\{\text{InP $k$ successfully serves type-$c$ jobs from client $i$}\}

Queue of Requests:
For any job entering the system, if it has not been served yet, it will be queued to be served later. We maintain such queues of requests at the orchestration time-scale.
For simplicity of technical exposition, we assume that all the links
(k,i)
have unit link capacity per operational time slot, during which one unit of traffic can be served.
At the start of orchestration time-frame
t

a^{c}_{i}(t)

c

\lambda^{c}_{i}=\mathbb{E}[a^{c}_{i}(t)]

The number of incoming new packets is bounded by a finite number
a^{c}_{i}(t)\leq A_{max}

Under the unit link capacity, in one time-frame of
T_{s}

\delta(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\min_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}:\lambda^{c}_{i}>0}\big\{\frac{\lambda^{c}_{i}}{|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}\big\}

The total number of times the client

Then, the total number of times it has received service for type-

(2)

\mu^{c}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k\in N(i)}y^{c}_{ki}(t)=\sum_{k\in N(i)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau),

which is the effective type-

The queue of type-

(3)

Q^{c}_{i}(t+1)=\big(Q^{c}_{i}(t)+a^{c}_{i}(t)-\mu^{c}_{i}(t)\big)^{+},\forall i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}.

2.3.
System Requirements

In this section, we first present the constraints that we impose on our decision variables
\mathbf{p}(t)
to model the system requirements.
Note that under unit link capacity setting, the link activation probability
r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)=\mathbb{E}[y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)]
is also the expected amount of type-
c
traffic that InP
k
serves client
i
in the current time slot. The management of the NeO, i.e. the control of prior probabilities
\mathbf{p}(t)
for scheduling, must take into account available resources at the InPs and clients, and ensure the stability of the system.

Transmission Rates of Clients:

At each operational time slot
\tau
within time-frame
t
, every client
i
transmits to InP
k
for type-
c
job service with probability
p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)
.
Thus, the client would have to make
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{k\in N(i)}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)
transmissions on average, which should not exceed the transmission rate
U_{i}>0
of the client:

(4)

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{k\in N(i)}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\leq U_{i},\forall i\in\mathcal{I},\tau\in[T_{s}].

Let
U_{max}=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\{U_{i}\}
be the maximum transmission rate of the clients.
This constraint (
4
) can be used to capture power control or bandwidth management in infrastructure sharing of networks.

Stability of Queues of Requests:
Stability has been an integral requirement in many communication networked systems
(
Neely et al. 2008
)
and infrastructure sharing networks
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
. We require the scheduling decisions to satisfy mean rate stability of the system
(
Neely 2010
)
:

(5)

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}Q^{c}_{i}(T)\big]}{T}=0,

or equivalently
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{Q}(T)\|_{1}]=o(T)
with
\mathbf{Q}(T)=\{Q^{c}_{i}(T)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
being the vector of the queue lengths. The constraint (
5
) requires that the job requests at all clients should eventually be served.

Service-Level Agreement:

Prior to the system deployment requiring interactions between various network stakeholders, a service-level agreement (SLA) is established to determine the QoS provided by the InPs to the clients. This phase of SLA is a common practice in infrastructure sharing
(
Stine and Bastidas 2014
)
. The SLA phase in our model is used to specify all the parameters for system requirements and QoS (Section
3.1
) to both the InPs and the clients. These parameters will then be given to the NeO to coordinate the system deployment.

2.4.
Modeling QoS

The modeling of system requirements, as presented above, can be well captured by linear constraints in the formulation.
More generally, this approach of leveraging linear constraints is heavily used by the vast literature on resource allocation for infrastructure sharing in network
(
Joshi et al. 2017
)
thanks to its tractability. In fact, it can be readily extended to also capture certain simplistic QoS requirements (e.g. throughput) that can be cast in linear form
(
Leconte et al. 2018
)
. However, restricting the problem formulation to such linear constraints fails to capture QoS requirements such as delay, reliability or guaranteed service.
In the next Section, we present our novel QoS framework that captures delay and probabilistic service guarantees and is specified by three parameters: time-frame size (
T_{s}
), guaranteed delivery throughput (
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
) and reliability metric (
\mathbf{q}
). The time-frame size (
T_{s}
) over which service is provisioned can be chosen to meet the stringent hard latency requirements of real-time applications. Our QoS model specifies the guaranteed delivery throughput (
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
) analogous to the average throughput constraint of some previous works
(
Kadota et al. 2018
)
, yet only requires it to be satisfied with some specified probability (
\mathbf{q}
).
Consequently, our framework allows the jobs to be served in multiple time-frames, thereby modeling applications with soft delay requirements (e.g. environmental monitoring), and provides long-term delay guarantees (in terms of time-frames) for such applications. At the same time, our framework can ensure hard delay guarantees of up to the time-frame size (
T_{s}
) by setting the reliability metric (
\mathbf{q}
) close to 1. Such hard delay requirements and delivery ratios have been considered in the literature
(
Hou et al. 2009
)
.

3.
QoS based Infrastructure Sharing Formulation

In this Section, we present our QoS based infrastructure sharing formulation.
We first propose our new QoS framework that can simultaneously capture delay, reliability and guaranteed service in Section
3.1
and then provide the full formulation for the infrastructure sharing control problem in Section
3.2
. .

3.1.
Model for QoS

First, the QoS constraint is specified within every time-frame
t
, i.e. over the
T_{s}
operational time slots therein. From the system point of view, the NeO can choose
T_{s}
such that the actual length of one orchestration time-frame is upper bounded by the most stringent delay requirements of all relevant applications, so that requested resources are always provisioned within the delay requirements.
Second, note that (under the unit link capacity) the total OTT service
\mu^{c}_{i}(t)
for type-
c
jobs at client
i
as in (
2
) is the sum of up to
|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}
service opportunities from at most
|\mathcal{K}|
InPs over
T_{s}
time slots. Thus, by enforcing a guaranteed delivery ratio
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}\mu^{c}_{i}(t)>\gamma^{c}_{i}
, we can provision the required service throughput to the client by controlling
\gamma^{c}_{i}\in(0,1)
. However, in many applications such as web-browsing or large data backup, where the delay requirement is milder, such guaranteed delivery ratio does not need to be necessarily satisfied within one time-frame.
Consequently, we further specify the reliability metric
q^{c}_{i}\in[0,1)
as the probability that the delivery ratio is guaranteed and impose the QoS constraint for every time-frame
t
as:

(6)

\displaystyle P\bigg(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}\mu^{c}_{i}(t)>\gamma^{c}_{i}\bigg)\geq q^{c}_{i},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}.

The set of
(T_{s},\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
, consisting of the frame size
T_{s}
, the guaranteed delivery ratios
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\{\gamma^{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
and the reliability metrics
\mathbf{q}=\{q^{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
, is part of the SLA between the network stakeholders, i.e. InPs and clients, and the NeO prior to the system deployment.
Note that setting
q^{c}_{i}=0
corresponds to no QoS requirement, where the constraint (
6
) trivially holds.
In Section
6
, we show through realistic examples how
T_{s},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{q}
can be chosen to exemplify real-world applications’ requirements.

3.2.
The Full Infrastructure Sharing Problem

Now, we proceed to present our full QoS based infrastructure sharing formulation. Given the guaranteed delivery ratios
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and reliability metrics
\mathbf{q}
pre-defined in the SLA, we formally define the feasible domain
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
of our link activation probabilities
\mathbf{p}(t)=\{p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\}_{\tau\in[0,T_{s}-1],(k,i)\in E,c\in\mathcal{C}}
that incorporates both the resource allocation constraints (Section
2.3
) and the probabilistic QoS constraints (Section
3.1
) as:

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})=\big\{\mathbf{p}(t)\in[0,1]^{T_{s}|E||\mathcal{C}|}\text{: such that \eqref{prob_constraint}, \eqref{pmax1}, \eqref{qos_constraint1}}\big\}.

After the scheduling probabilities
\mathbf{p}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
have been made at every time-frame
t
where
r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)=\mathbb{E}[y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)]
is the expected job service that client
i
receives from InP
k
for type-
c
jobs, a utility

is generated. We only require that the utility function

is continuous and concave, which hold in practical scenarios such as elastic traffic or fairness
(
Rakocevic et al. 2001
)
, and bounded within the feasible domain, i.e.
f(\mathbf{p}(t))\in[f_{min},f_{max}]
. We impose no structural assumption on
f(.)
for generality. As such,

can be designed to capture the InPs’ costs
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, the clients’ returns
(
Qin et al. 2020
)
or both, as well as administering additional factors like fairness
(
Altman et al. 2008
)
. Our goal is to maximize the long-term average
network utility while satisfying the realistic requirements, as described by
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
, and ensuring the stability (
5
) of the system:

\displaystyle\mathbf{(P):}\quad\max_{\mathbf{p}=\{\mathbf{p}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}}

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{p})\triangleq\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}f(\mathbf{p}(t))

such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}(T)]=o(T).

The above problem
(\mathbf{P})
is feasible if there exists a control policy
\pi

(
Neely 2010
)
.
Denote by
\mathbf{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
the stability region of the above problem
(\mathbf{P})
, describing the set of job arrival rate vectors
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\{\lambda^{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
whereby the problem
(\mathbf{P})
is feasible.

Definition 0 (Stability Region).

The stability region of the set of QoS specifications given by
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{q}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})=\{

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\{\lambda^{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{I}||\mathcal{C}|}:\text{$\exists\pi$ such that }

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{\pi}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q}),\forall t\geq 0\text{ and }\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}^{\pi}(T)]=o(T)\}.

In the SLA phase, the network stakeholders share their information such as the resource capacities of InPs and clients and the requested level of QoS (described through
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{q}
) with the NeO, whose job is to solve
(\mathbf{P})
to derive real-time scheduling decisions
\mathbf{p}(t)
for the InPs. In the next section, we proceed to develop an efficient control algorithm for the infrastructure sharing problem.

4.
Efficient Algorithm for QoS Based Infrastructure Sharing

In this section, we present the Modified Drift-plus-Penalty (MDP) policy as an efficient algorithm for our spectrum sharing problem
(\mathbf{P})
. We start by providing the preliminaries of the conventional Drift-plus-Penalty (DP) approach as well as its inability to handle the probabilistic constraints in Section
4.1
.
The algorithmic development of MDP is given in Section
4.2
and is based on a linearization technique to well approximate the probabilistic constraints.

4.1.
Inability of Drift-plus-Penalty to Handle QoS Constraints

4.1.1.
Complexity of the Probabilistic QoS Constraint

First, from (
2
), noting that each InP
k
independently initiates link activation, we know that
\mu^{c}_{i}(t)=\sum_{k\in N(i)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)
is the sum of
N(i)T_{s}
independent Bernoulli random variables
y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\sim Bern(r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau))
. Thus,
\mu^{c}_{i}(t)
follows Poisson Binomial distribution
(
Tang and Tang 2019
)
, so the probabilistic constraint (
6
) can be written as:

\displaystyle P\big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}\mu^{c}_{i}(t)>\gamma^{c}_{i}\big)\geq q^{c}_{i},\quad\forall i\in I,c\in\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor\gamma^{c}_{i}|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\rfloor}\sum_{A\in F_{l}}\prod_{(k,\tau)\in A}r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\prod_{(k^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})\in A^{c}}(1-r_{k^{\prime}i}p^{c}_{k^{\prime}i}(t,\tau^{\prime}))

(8)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 1-q^{c}_{i},\quad\forall i\in I,c\in\mathcal{C},

where
F_{l}
is the set of all subsets of size
l
that can be selected from
{\mathcal{K}\times[T_{s}]}=\{(k,\tau):k\in\mathcal{K},\tau\in[0,T_{s}-1]\}
. The constraint (
8
) is highly non-linear and even takes exponential time just to evaluate.

4.1.2.
The Drift-plus-Penalty Policy

The problem
(\mathbf{P})
can be considered as Network Utility Maximization (NUM) and solved via the DP framework
(
Neely 2010
)
. To achieve queue stability, we consider the Lyapunov potential:

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t))=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i\in I}Q^{c}_{i}(t)^{2},

and the drift of
\mathcal{L}(.)
conditioned on the queue lengths as:

(10)

\displaystyle\Delta(t)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t+1))-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(t))\big|\mathbf{Q}(t)\big].

The next Lemma, whose proof is given in Appendix
B
, establishes the drift bound.

Lemma 0.

For
B_{1}=|\mathcal{I}||\mathcal{C}|A_{max}^{2}+T_{s}^{2}|\mathcal{K}|^{2}
, we have:

(11)

\Delta(t)\leq B_{1}+\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i\in I}Q^{c}_{i}(t)\big(\lambda^{c}_{i}-\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}\sum_{k\in N(i)}r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\big).

Given the hyper-parameter
V>0
that can be tuned to control the utility-backlog trade-off, the conventional DP policy minimizes the upper-bound (given by (
11
)) of the drift-plus-penalty
\Delta(t)-Vf(\mathbf{p}(t))
, which is formally given by:

(12)

\mathbf{p}^{\textsc{DP}}(t)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\mathbf{p}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})}h_{t}(\mathbf{p}(t)),

where the objective
h_{t}(.)
is defined as:

(13)

h_{t}(\mathbf{p}(t))=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{i\in I}Q^{c}_{i}(t)\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}\sum_{k\in N(i)}r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)+Vf(\mathbf{p}(t)).

While the DP policy is known to be an utility-optimal stabilizing algorithm
(
Neely 2010
)
, solving (
12
) is equivalent to maximizing a concave objective over a domain comprised of highly non-linear constraints (
8
), thereby rendering it intractable.
In the next section, we present our algorithmic development that deploys a linearization technique to approximate the probabilistic constraints, thereby resulting in an efficient policy with polynomial time complexity.

4.2.
The Modified Drift-plus-Penalty Algorithm

To address the aforementioned challenge, we extend the technique from the robust optimization literature
(
Bertsimas and Sim 2004
)
to approximate the probabilistic QoS constraint (
6
) by linear constraints on
\mathbf{p}(t)
.
As for
q^{c}_{i}=0
, the constraint (
6
) trivially holds and corresponds to no QoS requirement, we consider the set:

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{(i,c):i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}\text{ such that }q^{c}_{i}>0\}

of clients and jobs that impose active QoS requirements.
The idea is that it is unlikely for all random variables
y^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)
to simultaneously deviate from their means, so resources are allocated to “protect" situations where only a number
\Gamma^{c}_{i}
, which is a hyper-parameter to be determined later, of variables exceed their means.
To formally describe the linearized constraint, we define
\hat{p}^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)=\max\{r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau),1-r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\}>0
and:

\displaystyle B^{c}_{i}(\Gamma^{c}_{i},

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t))=\max\limits_{S^{c}_{ti}\cup\{(k_{0},\tau_{0})\}:S^{c}_{ti}\in 2^{N(i)\times[T_{s}]},|S^{c}_{ti}|=\lfloor\Gamma^{c}_{i}\rfloor}

(15)

\displaystyle\bigg\{\sum_{(k,\tau)\in S^{c}_{ti}}\hat{p}^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)+(\Gamma^{c}_{i}-\lfloor\Gamma^{c}_{i}\rfloor)\hat{p}^{c}_{k_{0}i}(t,\tau_{0})\bigg\},

which serves as the UCB term in the linearized constraint as:

(16)

|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\gamma^{c}_{i}\leq\sum_{k\in N(i)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)-B^{c}_{i}(\Gamma^{c}_{i},\mathbf{p}(t)),\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S}.

Note that the constraint (
16
) can be equivalently converted into
|N(i)|T_{s}{|N(i)|T_{s}-1\choose\lfloor\Gamma^{c}_{i}\rfloor}2^{\lfloor\Gamma^{c}_{i}\rfloor+1}
linear constraints of
\mathbf{p}(t)
by untangling all the max operators in (
15
) and in
\hat{p}^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)
.
We establish the probabilistic guarantee implied by (
16
) in the following Theorem, whose proof is given in Appendix
A.1
.

Theorem 2.

If
\mathbf{p}(t)\in[0,1]^{T_{s}|E||\mathcal{C}|}
satisfies (
16
), we have:

(17)

\displaystyle P\big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}\mu^{c}_{i}(t)>\gamma^{c}_{i}\big)\geq 1-e^{-\frac{(\Gamma^{c}_{i})^{2}}{2|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}},\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S}.

To enforce the probabilistic QoS constraint (
6
), we can choose
\Gamma^{c}_{i}
such that
1-e^{-\frac{(\Gamma^{c}_{i})^{2}}{2|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}}=q^{c}_{i}
, or equivalently:

(18)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{c}_{i}=\sqrt{2|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\log\big((1-q^{c}_{i})^{-1}\big)}=\Theta(T_{s}^{1/2}).

As discussed in Section
4.1
, solving
\max_{\mathbf{p}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})}h_{t}(\mathbf{p}(t))
under the original DP policy is intractable due to the probabilistic constraint (
6
) in the feasible domain
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
. However, by Theorem
2
, this probabilistic constraint can be enforced by the linearized constraint (
16
).
To make the optimization problem tractable, we consider the linearized feasible domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
, which replaces the probabilistic constraint (
6
) in the definition of
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
by the above linearized constraint (
16
):

(19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})=\big\{\mathbf{p}(t)\in[0,1]^{T_{s}|E||\mathcal{C}|}\text{: such that \eqref{prob_constraint}, \eqref{pmax1}, \eqref{linearized_constraint1}}\big\}.

While the linearized constraint (
16
) implies the probabilistic QoS constraint,
it contains
B^{c}_{i}(\Gamma^{c}_{i},\mathbf{p}(t))
, which is a max operator over exponentially many linear constraints.
In the next Theorem, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A.2
,
we derive an equivalent form for
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
via duality, where the problem size is of polynomial order.

Theorem 3.

An equivalent form for
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
is given by:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})=\big\{

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t)\in[0,1]^{T_{s}|E||\mathcal{C}|}\text{: such that \eqref{prob_constraint}, \eqref{pmax1},}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\gamma^{c}_{i}\leq\sum_{k\in N(i)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)-\Gamma^{c}_{i}s^{c}_{i}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\sum_{k\in N(i)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}v^{c}_{ki\tau},\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad s^{c}_{i}\geq 0,\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle\text{ and }\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S},k\in N(i),\tau\in[T_{s}]:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad s^{c}_{i}+v^{c}_{ki\tau}\geq r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau),

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad s^{c}_{i}+v^{c}_{ki\tau}\geq 1-r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau),

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad v^{c}_{ki\tau}\geq 0\big\}.

Finally , we propose the Modified Drift-plus-Penalty (MDP) policy that replaces
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
by the linearized feasible domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
in the optimization problem (
12
) as follows:

(20)

\mathbf{p}^{\textsc{MDP}}(t)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\mathbf{p}(t)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})}h_{t}(\mathbf{p}(t)).

The full policy is depicted in Algorithm
1
. As
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
is a polyhedron of polynomial size by Theorem
3
, the MDP policy boils down to solving convex optimization (
20
) (i.e. concave maximization) over linear constraints and thus admits efficient polynomial-time solvers
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
.

T_{s},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{q}
.

for

t=0,...,T-1

do

1

Solve for the link activation probabilities
\mathbf{p}^{\textsc{MDP}}(t)=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{\mathbf{p}(t)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})}h_{t}(\mathbf{p}(t))
.

for

\tau=0,...,T_{s}-1
, within each time frame

do

2

[Scheduling]
Activate links
\{d^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\}_{k\in\mathcal{K},i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
(based on
\mathbf{p}^{\textsc{MDP}}(t,\tau)
) for serving jobs in time slot
\tau
.

end for

3

end for

4

Observe the arrivals
\{a^{c}_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
and compute the services
\{\mu^{c}_{i}(t)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}
via (
2
).

Update the queues as

Q^{c}_{i}(t+1)=\big(Q^{c}_{i}(t)+a^{c}_{i}(t)-\mu^{c}_{i}(t)\big)^{+},\forall i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}.

Modified Drift-plus-Penalty (MDP) policy

Our QoS model permits randomized scheduling via
\mathbf{p}(t,\tau)
in the policy parameterization.
This raises the question of whether there exists a
feasible
deterministic policy making deterministic scheduling decisions in every time slot, i.e.
p^{c}_{ki}(t,\tau)\in\{0,1\}
, which resembles a property (i.e. existence of feasible and optimal policy that is deterministic) inherent in many scheduling problems in the literature (e.g.
(
Neely et al. 2008
;
Nguyen and Modiano 2024
)
).
We answer this question negatively by constructing an example (Appendix
E
) in which the class of deterministic policies is insufficient to meet our probabilistic constraints. In contrast, our linearized feasible domain and thus the MDP policy can support these QoS requirements. In the following sections, we further derive theoretical guarantees for the MDP policy as well as how the proposed QoS model can provably provide throughput and delay guarantees.

5.
Guarantees for Modified Drift-plus-Penalty

In this section, we show that the MDP policy can support a stability regions arbitrarily close to the true stability region and achieve a vanishing optimality gap, as the time frame size
T_{s}
increases.
In Section
5.1
, we first establish the stability region characterization of the QoS-based infrastructure sharing problem

(\mathbf{P})
(see Corollary
4

7
and Corollary

5.1.
Stability Region Characterization

The MDP policy replaces the original feasible domain
\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
by the linearized feasible domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
in the optimization (
20
) for tractability.
While adopting
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
ensures the probabilistic QoS constraints, the question of how close this approximation is remains. For the regime of large
T_{s}
within interest (as discussed in Section
2.2

O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{s}}}\big)
in the following Theorem, whose proof is deferred to Appendix

Theorem 1.

If the following condition holds:

(21)

\displaystyle T_{s}>\frac{0.795^{2}}{(1-r_{max})|\mathcal{K}|\gamma^{c}_{i}(q^{c}_{i})^{3}}\triangleq K^{c}_{i},\quad\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S},

then we have the characterization:

(22)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\frac{K_{1}}{\sqrt{T_{s}}}\mathbf{1},\mathbf{q}),

where the constant
K_{1}
is defined as:

(23)

\displaystyle K_{1}\triangleq\frac{\sqrt{2\log\big((1-\|\mathbf{q}\|_{\infty})^{-1}\big)}}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{K}|}}+\frac{2\sqrt{U_{max}\pi e^{3}}}{|\mathcal{K}|}.

Theorem
1
establishes that, despite its hardness, we can linearize the probabilistic QoS constraint with vanishing approximation error
O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{s}}}\big)
.
We consider the approximate stability region as follows.

Definition 0 (Approximate Stability Region).

The approximate stability region given
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{q}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})=\{

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\{\lambda^{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{I}||\mathcal{C}|}:\text{$\exists\pi$ such that }

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}^{\pi}(t)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q}),\forall t\geq 0\text{ and }\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Q}^{\pi}(T)]=o(T)\}.

In the following Theorem, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
D.2
, we can further derive
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
in the form of polyhedron. Intuitively, it means that if
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
, there exists an optimal policy making stationary per-time-frame decision, which is a known property for linearly constrained control problem
(
Neely 2010
)
; thus, we drop the time frame
t
from the vector
\mathbf{p}
for clarity.

Theorem 3.

We have the following characterization of
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})=\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda^{c}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{|\mathcal{I}||\mathcal{C}|}:

(24)

\displaystyle\lambda^{c}_{i}\leq\sum_{k\in N(i)}\sum_{\tau=0}^{T_{s}-1}r_{ki}p^{c}_{ki}(\tau),\forall i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C},

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}=\{p^{c}_{ki}(\tau)\}_{\tau\in[0,T_{s}-1],(k,i)\in E,c\in\mathcal{C}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})\}.

Finally, we show that the approximate stability region
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
is close to the actual stability region
\mathbf{\Lambda}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
as demonstrated in the next Corollary directly following from Theorem
1
.

Corollary 0.

Under the condition (
21
) and for
K_{1}
in (
23
),

(25)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})\subseteq{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})\subseteq\widetilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\frac{K_{1}}{\sqrt{T_{s}}}\mathbf{1},\mathbf{q}).

5.2.
Utility-Optimality and Stability Results

We proceed to show that the MDP policy attains competitive performance guarantees on the supported stability region that is within
O\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{T_{s}}}\big)
distance from the true stability region.
For analysis, we make the following assumption that
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
has an interior point that supports the considered arrival rate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
in view of Theorem
3
.

Assumption 1 (Slater Condition).

For arrival rate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, there exists
\zeta>0
and
\mathbf{p}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
such that (
24

and
\forall(i,c)\in\mathcal{S}

(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004

is a natural assumption in practice.
The next Lemma establishes the sub-optimality gap between MDP policy and DP policy caused by replacing
{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})

Lemma 0.

\displaystyle 0\leq h_{t}(\mathbf{p}^{\textsc{DP}}(t))-h_{t}(\mathbf{p}^{\textsc{MDP}}(t))\leq

\displaystyle K_{2}\|\mathbf{Q}(t)\|_{\infty}\sqrt{T_{s}}

(27)

\displaystyle+\frac{VK_{2}(f_{max}-f_{min})}{|\mathcal{K}|\sqrt{T_{s}}},

Proof.

We consider:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}

It is thus left to bound

We note that

Application of the QoS Framework

In this section, we demonstrate the application of our proposed QoS framework in real-world scenarios.

6.1.
Throughput and Delay Guarantees

While our QoS model is designed to be generalized enough to handle a variety of QoS metrics, we show how it can be leveraged to directly imply the conventional throughput and delay guarantees. First, as in the following Corollary, whose proof is given in Appendix
F.1
, we can derive directly from the probabilistic QoS constraint (
6

Corollary 0.

The expected total OTT service for type-
c
jobs of client
i
is bounded by:

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mu^{c}_{i}(t)]\geq|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\gamma^{c}_{i}q^{c}_{i},\quad\forall i\in\mathcal{I},c\in\mathcal{C}.

The above result demonstrates how
T_{s},\gamma^{c}_{i}
and
q^{c}_{i}
can be chosen to enforce overall service guarantee for throughput-demanding applications. Besides throughput, a common QoS metric required by time-sensitive applications is delay, i.e. the requested job should be served within the required amount of time.
For applications with deterministic job arrivals such as streaming with stringent delay requirement, the time frame size can be chosen to meet the latency requirement and
q^{c}_{i}
is set close to 1 to ensure negligible failure probability, while
\gamma^{c}_{i}
is chosen in view of (
36
) to sustain the required throughput. Such guaranteed delivery ratio with probability 1 was considered in
(
Hou et al. 2009
)
.
On the other hand, many applications such as environmental monitoring only demand soft delay requirements, whereby jobs are allowed to be served in multiple time-frames within long-term average delay constraint.
We establish in the next Theorem an upper bound for the average queueing delay
W^{c}_{i}
that is only dependent on
\gamma^{c}_{i},q^{c}_{i}
and the first two moments of the arrival process. Moreover, real-world applications with stochastic job arrivals either have readily available first and second moments from measurement data or can be modeled as Poisson processes, which inherently provide well-defined first two moments.

Theorem 2.

Under the assumption that the second moment of the arrival process is finite and for any feasible scheduling probabilities
\mathbf{p}(t)\in\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
, we have the following bound on the average queueing delay of type-
c
jobs at client
i
if
\gamma^{c}_{i}q^{c}_{i}>\frac{\lambda^{c}_{i}}{|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}}
:

(37)

\displaystyle W^{c}_{i}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[a^{c}_{i}(t)^{2}]+T_{s}^{2}U_{i}^{2}+T_{s}U_{i}-2\lambda^{c}_{i}|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\gamma^{c}_{i}q^{c}_{i}}{2\lambda^{c}_{i}(|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}\gamma^{c}_{i}q^{c}_{i}-\lambda^{c}_{i})}.

Furthermore, a sufficient condition for

, i.e. the queueing delay is bounded by some given

(38)

\displaystyle\gamma^{c}_{i}q^{c}_{i}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[a^{c}_{i}(t)^{2}]+T_{s}^{2}U_{i}^{2}+T_{s}U_{i}+2(\lambda^{c}_{i})^{2}W^{*}}{2\lambda^{c}_{i}|\mathcal{K}|T_{s}(W^{*}+1)}.

(b)

Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new QoS framework for service provision in multi-infrastructure-sharing networks and the efficient MDP algorithm for the problem. We derive utility-optimality and stability gaurantees for MDP, and demonstrate the application of our QoS model in realistic scenarios.

Appendix E
Necessity of Randomized Scheduling and Single Server Example

Consider a single server serving 101 clients with only one type of job. For brevity, we omit the superscript
c
indexing the job type.
The channel statistics are the same, i.e.
r_{1i}=r_{max}=0.8
for all
i\in[1,101]

The orchestration time frame is
T_{s}=300
and the probabilistic constraints are specified by
\gamma_{i}=6.72\cdot 10^{-3}
and
q_{i}=2\cdot 10^{-4}
for all
i\in[1,101]
. Even under this simple setting with mild probabilistic QoS constraints, any deterministic policy fails to meet all the probabilistic constraints, as shown in the following Claim
1
.

Claim 1.

Under the considered setting and for any deterministic policy, at any time frame
t
, there exists some
i\in[1,101]
such that
P(\mu_{i}(t)>T_{s}\gamma_{i})=0
.

Proof.

For any deterministic policy, within time frame
t
, let
n_{i}(t)
be the number of time slots that the server activate link
(1,i)
with client
i
.
As there are
T_{s}
time slots, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{|I|}n_{i}(t)=T_{s}
. Thus, there exists some
i
such that
n_{i}(t)\leq T_{s}/|I|<3
, or
n_{i}(t)\leq 2
(as
n_{i}(t)
is an integer). Within time frame
t
, since the server attempts to serve client
i
for
n_{i}(t)
times, each of which is successful with probability
r_{max}
, the service that client
i
receives follows Binomial distribution as
\mu_{i}(t)\sim B(n_{i}(t),r_{max})
. Given that
n_{i}(t)\leq 2
, we obtain that
P(\mu_{i}(t)>T_{s}\gamma_{i})=P(\mu_{i}(t)>2.016)=0
.
∎

We know that the MDP policy seeks for the scheduling decision
\mathbf{p}(t)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
, which, if exists, by Theorem
2
would meet all the probabilistic QoS constraints. The following claim shows that, in fact, such feasible scheduling decision always exists.

Claim 2.

The domain
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\mathbf{q})
is non-empty.

Proof.

Consider the simple (randomized) scheduling decision
\mathbf{p}(t)
given by

p_{1i}(t,\tau)=\frac{1}{|I|}=\frac{1}{101},\forall\tau\in[T_{s}]
.
By direct verification, this choice of
\mathbf{p}(t)
satisfies the constraints

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}(\gamma,\mathbf{q})
, and hence the set is non-empty.
∎

(130)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2|\mathcal{C}||\mathcal{I}|\cdot\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(T))}.

By Jensen’s inequality, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\mathbf{Q}(T)\|_{1}\big]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{Q}(T)\|_{1}^{2}]\overset{\eqref{proof_lma_cauchy1}}{\leq}2|\mathcal{C}||\mathcal{I}|\,\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(T))]

\displaystyle\therefore\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{Q}(T)\|_{1}]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2|\mathcal{C}||\mathcal{I}|\,\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{Q}(T))]},

which concludes the proof of the Lemma.
∎

Appendix I
Properties of Convex Optimization over Linear Constraints

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, convex, and lower semicontinuous function, and let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Consider the primal problem:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{(Primal)}\quad\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&f(\mathbf{x})\\[2.84526pt]
\quad\text{subject to}&A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}.\end{array}

Suppose there exists a strictly feasible point
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
satisfying:

(133)

\displaystyle A\bar{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{b}-\zeta\,\mathbf{1}

for some
\zeta>0
, i.e. the Slater condition holds.
We consider an optimal solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}}f(\mathbf{x})
.

Lemma 0.

Let
f^{*}(.)
be the Frenchel conjugate of
f(.)
. The dual problem of (Primal) can be written in its Fenchel form as

\text{(Dual)}\quad\sup_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0}\left\{g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\triangleq-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-f^{*}\bigl(-A^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bigr)\right\},

and strong duality holds; that is, the optimal value of (Primal) equals the optimal value of (Dual).

Proof.

Define the Lagrangian for (P) by:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=f(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}(A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}),\quad\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0.

The dual function is then given by:

g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\{f(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}(A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})\Bigr\}=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\{f(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}\Bigr\}-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}.

Recall the definition of the Fenchel conjugate of
f(.)
:

f^{*}(\mathbf{y})=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}-f(\mathbf{x})\Bigr\}.

Thus, we have:

\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\{f(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}\Bigr\}=-\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Bigl\{-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}A\mathbf{x}-f(x)\Bigr\}=-f^{*}\bigl(-A^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bigr).

Thus, the dual function becomes:

g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-f^{*}\bigl(-A^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bigr).

The dual problem is to maximize
g(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
over
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0
, namely,

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0}\Bigl\{-\mathbf{b}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-f^{*}\bigl(-A^{\top}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bigr)\Bigr\}.

Under the stated assumptions (convexity of
f
, lower semicontinuity, and the Slater condition
A\bar{\mathbf{x}}<b
), standard results in convex optimization
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
guarantee that strong duality holds. Hence, the optimal values of the primal and dual problems are equal.
∎

Theorem 2 (Bound on the Optimal Dual Solution).

Let
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\geq 0
be an optimal solution to (Dual). We have:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{f(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})}{\zeta}.

Proof.

Since strong duality holds, we have:

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{*})=g(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})=\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})=f(\bar{\mathbf{x}})+\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\top}(A\bar{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\therefore f(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda^{*}_{i}\Bigl(b_{i}-[A\bar{\mathbf{x}}]_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{slate_appen1}}{\geq}\zeta\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda^{*}_{i}=\zeta\,\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|_{1}.

Thus, it follows that
\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{f(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})}{\zeta}
.

∎

Theorem 3.

Besides
\mathbf{x}^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}}f(\mathbf{x})
, we let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{A\mathbf{x}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{b}}}f(\mathbf{x})
be the solution to the primal problem with the perturbed boundary vector
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Assume that the Slater condition holds with constant
\zeta
for both right-hand sides
\mathbf{b}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{b}}
, i.e.
A\bar{\mathbf{x}}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\zeta\,\mathbf{1}
and
A\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\leq{\mathbf{b}}-\zeta\,\mathbf{1}
for some
\bar{\mathbf{x}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
.
Let
\kappa=\frac{1}{\zeta}\max\big\{f(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*}),f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*})\big\}
. We have the following bound:

0\leq|f(\mathbf{x}^{*})-f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*})|\leq\kappa\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{*}
be the optimal dual solution to the problem
\min_{A\mathbf{x}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{b}}}f(\mathbf{x})
.
Define the optimal value function given the boundary vector
\mathbf{b}
as:

v(\mathbf{b})=\min\{\,f(\mathbf{x}):\ A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}\,\}.

Since
v(\mathbf{b})
is convex in
\mathbf{b}
and
\lambda^{*}
is an optimal dual solution for the problem with right-hand side
\mathbf{b}
, it follows that
\lambda^{*}
is a subgradient of
v
at
\mathbf{b}
. By the convexity of
v
, we have:

\displaystyle v(\tilde{\mathbf{b}})\geq v(\mathbf{b})+\lambda^{*\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b}).

Similarly, we have:

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{b})\geq v(\tilde{\mathbf{b}})+\tilde{\lambda}^{*\top}(\mathbf{b}-\tilde{\mathbf{b}}).

Combining the above two inequalities, we obtain that:

(134)

\displaystyle\bigl|v(\tilde{\mathbf{b}})-v(\mathbf{b})\bigr|\leq\max\{\lambda^{*\top}(\mathbf{b}-\tilde{\mathbf{b}}),\tilde{\lambda}^{*\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b})\}.

By Holder’s inequality and Theorem
2
, we have:

(135)

\displaystyle\bigl|\lambda^{*\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b})\bigr|\leq\|\lambda^{*}\|_{1}\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{f(\bar{\mathbf{x}})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})}{\zeta}\,\|\mathbf{b}-\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\|_{\infty},

(136)

\displaystyle\bigl|\tilde{\lambda}^{*\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b})\bigr|\leq\|\tilde{\lambda}^{*}\|_{1}\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{f(\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*})}{\zeta}\,\|\mathbf{b}-\tilde{\mathbf{b}}\|_{\infty}.

Applying (
135
) and (
136
) to (
134
) and noting that
f(\mathbf{x}^{*})=v(\mathbf{b})
and
f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{*})=v(\tilde{\mathbf{b}})
, we conclude the required statement.
∎
