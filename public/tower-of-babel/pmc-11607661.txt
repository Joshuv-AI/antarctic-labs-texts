Effect of Regional Housing Hardship on Spatial Variation in Cancer Incidence: Does Housing Stress Increase Cancer Incidence?
Authors: Haishi Yu, Jinyu Huang, Yang Wang, Xiaoli Yue, Yingmei Wu, Hong'ou Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHousing hardship can create a range of health issues. However, little attention has been paid to the relationship between housing hardship and cancer incidence. This study examines the Yangtze River Economic Belt (YREB) in China as a case study to develop a model of factors related to housing hardship that can affect cancer incidence. This study employs spatial regression models to investigate the correlation between housing hardship and cancer incidence and further explores the variation in the correlation between urban areas (UAs) and non‐urban areas (NUAs). The research conclusions are as follows: (a) a palpable correlation exists between housing hardship and cancer incidence. The housing price‐to‐income ratio (HPIR) and the rental household proportion (RHP) are positively correlated to cancer incidence, whereas the per capita living area (PCLA) has a negative correlation with cancer incidence. (b) The differences in the impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence between the UAs and the NUAs are reflected mainly in the differences in the PCLA and the RHP. The PCLA has a strong association with cancer incidence in the UAs, whereas the RHP demonstrates a strong correlation with cancer incidence in the NUAs. (c) Significant spatial heterogeneity is observed in housing hardship in the YREB.Keywords: housing hardship, housing stress, housing price, housing overcrowding, cancer incidence, Yangtze River Economic Belt

Introduction

Residence constitutes a fundamental human activity, and the housing situation is closely correlated to one's sense of well‐being (Bratt, 2002; Costa‐Font, 2013). In certain cities, the degree of housing hardship is notably high and manifested in elevated living costs (high housing prices), residential congestion (small PCLAs), and residential instability (high rental ratio) (Burgard et al., 2012; Kole, 2022; Shamsuddin & Campbell, 2022). In such cities, residents face substantial housing pressure, which may compel them to incur considerable costs in their pursuit of comfortable living spaces. Such a situation can amplify residents' life stress and diminish their happiness, which may have an adverse effect on their health (Baker et al., 2020; Deidda, 2015; Xu & Wang, 2022). In addition, excessively high housing costs may force many residents to live in small spaces, which can lead to a lack of physical activity, poor indoor air circulation, and increased feelings of anxiety and repression, among other consequences (Chan et al., 2021; Kim et al., 2021; Reddy & Leslie, 2015). Hence, the impact of the degree of housing hardship on health should be analyzed. The cancer incidence in a region is a crucial indicator of the public health and residents' health in the area (Ellis et al., 2014). Therefore, the influence of housing stress factors, such as affordability, the population density in residential areas, and the percentage of rentals, on cancer incidence must be examined. However, little academic attention has been paid to such issues.

Housing hardship refers to the challenges faced by individuals or families in securing adequate housing, typically manifesting in unaffordable housing costs, insufficient housing quality, and poor living environments (Gold, 2020; Yoo et al., 2009). Previous studies have assessed housing hardship from various aspects, including housing affordability, overcrowding, and residential stability. The HPIR is a key indicator for evaluating housing affordability, offering insights into the financial challenges faced by homebuyers (Tsai, 2019). The PCLA is widely used in academic research to measure residential overcrowding and serves as an essential criterion in policy discussions regarding this issue (X. Wang & Liu, 2023). The RHP index comprehensively reflects the regional burden of housing affordability, residential stability, and environmental quality. On one hand, low‐income households often cannot afford homeownership and are forced to rent, meaning regions with high RHP face more severe housing burdens (Haffner & Boumeester, 2010); on the other hand, areas with high RHP are typically characterized by overcrowding, poor living conditions, and frequent relocations (Robinson & Steil, 2021).

The degree of housing hardship varies significantly across regions, which has prompted scholars to conduct relevant research. For instance, studies delved into regional disparities in housing prices, HPIRs (using income to assess the relative affordability of housing), PCLAs, and RHPs (Y. Wang, You, et al., 2023; Y. Wang et al., 2017). In terms of regional differences in housing prices, influenced by urban development, prices in core cities tend to surpass those in other cities (H. Zhang et al., 2016). Within UAs, prices in inner‐city areas typically exceed those in other areas (Bagheri & Shaykh‐Baygloo, 2021). The HPIR serves as an indicator of the housing market and affordability, which exhibits distinct spatial heterogeneity. For example, in Taiwan, the HPIR is significantly higher in Taipei City compared with other regions (André et al., 2014; P.‐F. Chen et al., 2011; Tsai, 2018). The PCLA also displays substantial disparity across regions, which is closely related to economic development, in which regions with a low density have large PCLAs (Froemelt et al., 2020; Huo et al., 2021). Furthermore, influenced by regional differences in income levels, social welfare systems, and other factors, housing hardship exhibits notable inequality and spatial heterogeneity (Béland et al., 2021; Nijman & Wei, 2020; Tang & Li, 2021).

Considerable disparity exists in cancer incidence across regions. Research perspectives on the factors that can influence cancer incidence primarily encompass environmental pollution (air, water, soil pollution, nuclear radiation, and so on), natural geographic environments, the climate, land use/land cover conditions, urbanization, healthcare service levels, economic development, education, and income (Anderson et al., 2023; Hauptmann et al., 2023; Hiatt & Beyeler, 2020; Lortet‐Tieulent et al., 2020; Ou et al., 2020; Turner et al., 2020; Williamson et al., 2021). Several studies have explored the impact of housing hardship on human health. Filali and Nkosi have identified overcrowded living conditions as significant risk factors for diseases such as tuberculosis, respiratory infections, and meningitis. In these environments, the confined space allows bacteria and viruses in droplets and aerosols, released during sneezing, coughing, or even regular conversation, to spread more easily, thereby increasing the risk of various diseases, including lung cancer (Filali, 2012; Nkosi et al., 2019). Jacobs and colleagues highlighted that older housing often contains deteriorating lead paint, and increased indoor humidity accelerates its degradation, leading to higher lead release, elevated blood lead levels, and an increased risk of lead poisoning among residents (Jacobs et al., 2009). Particularly concerning is that individuals burdened by heavy housing costs are often compelled to live in such dilapidated homes, putting them at greater immediate health risk. Lawrence and others also found that regions with a higher housing cost burden exhibit higher rates of premature cancer mortality (Lawrence et al., 2024). However, few studies have investigated the health effects of housing hardship from a large‐scale, macro‐level perspective, specifically examining the impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence. As housing issues become increasingly serious, the influence of housing hardship on cancer incidence should not be overlooked. Unfortunately, research in this particular domain is scarce.

To fill this gap, we examine the impact of three indicators of housing hardship (i.e., the HPIR, the PCLA, and the RHP) on cancer incidence. Specifically, we aim to determine whether regions with high housing pressure exhibit high cancer incidence to provide insights into housing pressure from a health perspective. Furthermore, we compare the differential effects of housing pressure on cancer incidence in UAs and NUAs. Our study contributes to the literature in three significant ways: first, by considering spatial variations in cancer incidence owing to the degree of housing hardship; second, by elucidating the significance and direction of the impact of housing pressure on cancer incidence; and third, by paying attention to the heterogeneity in the relationship between the degree of housing hardship and cancer incidence in UAs and NUAs.

The YREB spans the eastern, central, and western regions of China and contributes over 40% of the country's population and gross domestic product (GDP) (Shi et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023). Within the YREB, significant disparity exists in the housing pressure, accompanied by substantial spatial variation in cancer incidence. Hence, our analysis of the impact of the degree of housing hardship on cancer incidence, using the YREB in China as a case study, is representative and typical.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The YREB has been recognized as one of China's most dynamic economic zones, encompassing 11 provinces and municipalities. The area covers four provinces and municipalities in the southwest upstream region (i.e., Yunnan, Sichuan, Guizhou, and Chongqing), three provinces in the central midstream region (i.e., Hunan, Jiangxi, and Hubei), and four provinces and municipalities in the downstream region (i.e., Anhui, Zhejiang, Jiangsu, and Shanghai). Variations in the natural geography and socioeconomic progress are prevalent throughout the region. This study focuses on 1,058 counties within the YREB as the basic research units. The units are divided into UAs and NUAs based on their administrative level. A total of 459 county and district units are classified as UAs, and 599 units are classified as NUAs (Figure 1).

Research Design

Figure 2 illustrates the research framework. The research process is as follows: first, the spatial patterns of the three housing hardship indicators across the 1,058 counties within the YREB are illustrated. Second, spatial autocorrelation analysis is conducted to investigate the global and local spatial autocorrelation features of housing distress within the area. Third, a framework is devised to evaluate the variables that may affect the impact of housing distress on cancer incidence. Fourth, different models, namely, ordinary least squares (OLS), a spatial lag model (SLM), a spatial error model (SEM), and a spatial Durbin model (SDM), are employed to examine the degree and direction of the influence of housing distress on cancer incidence. Last, further analysis is conducted to ascertain the influence of housing hardship on cancer incidence in the UAs and NUAs.

The Model for Factors Influencing the Impact of Housing Hardship on Cancer Incidence

Assessment of housing hardship is the focal point of this study and serves as the explanatory variable in the model of factors that can influence the impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence. The HPIR, PCLA, and RHP reflect the degree of housing hardship across the counties within the YREB from purchasing pressure, residential congestion, and residential instability, respectively. The HPIR can measure housing affordability and is widely used to assess the housing market (M.‐C. Chen et al., 2007; Leung & Tang, 2023), and a high HPIR indicates considerable purchasing pressure. The PCLA reflects the size of residential spaces and the level of housing congestion and is significantly correlated to housing prices and residential satisfaction (F. Zhang et al., 2018). A small PCLA indicates considerable housing congestion. The RHP can gauge the stability of residence and the quality of housing conditions, and communities with a high RHP typically experience high population turnover and have poor housing conditions (Heffernan et al., 2021; Kang, 2021).

Cancer is a major health issue in China, and cancer incidence can reflect the regional health level. Cancer incidence can also reflect regional cancer risks and burden from the perspective of genetics and exposure to risk factors, which can serve as important bases for cancer prevention and early screening. Therefore, this study selects cancer incidence as the dependent variable. In terms of control variables, this study selected nine indicators related to cancer incidence from three aspects: economic factors, population characteristics, and natural environment. Economically, real GDP per capita not only reflects the level of cancer treatment but also the level of cancer diagnosis, which suggests a positive correlation with cancer incidence (You & Henneberg, 2018). Wages represent income levels, and theoretically, higher income reduces the likelihood of exposure to carcinogenic substances, leading to lower cancer incidence (Wu et al., 2018). Regarding population characteristics, education years per capita influence individuals’ health awareness and effective use of medical resources, thereby affecting cancer risk and diagnosis. For instance, lower educational attainment among men is associated with a higher incidence of gastric cancer (Smailyte et al., 2012). Household size can impact individual health through emotional support, economic assistance, and lifestyle factors, with larger households generally linked to lower cancer risk for both women and men in various countries (You et al., 2018). The secondary sector of the economy poses greater occupational exposure risks, and prolonged contact with harmful substances (such as chemicals and dust) may increase cancer risk (Keshava & Ong, 1999). Thus, regions with a higher proportion of employment in secondary sector of economy may also experience higher cancer risk among residents. Concerning the natural environment, PM2.5 concentration, temperature, precipitation, and atmospheric pressure all significantly impact cancer incidence. Studies have confirmed that both PM2.5 concentration and precipitation are associated with lung cancer incidence, with higher PM2.5 concentrations positively correlated with increased lung cancer rates (J. Li et al., 2020). Precipitation acts as a cleansing agent for air pollutants, especially particulate matter, and is therefore negatively correlated with lung cancer incidence (Liu et al., 2023). Temperature and atmospheric pressure are important factors affecting the incidence of skin and breast cancers (Czerniec et al., 2016; Masoumi et al., 2020).

Data Sources

The cancer incidence data are sourced from the Cancer Atlas in China (2018), in which the cancer incidence data are reported by 449 cancer registration points in the National Central Cancer Registry of China based on the number of cancer incidence cases and the registered population in 2014 (National Cancer Center/Cancer Hospital Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, 2019).

The distribution map of malignant tumor incidence in China adjusts the cancer incidence data using Segi's world standard population model and categorizes cancer incidence in China into 10 levels, that is, <130, 130–150, 150–160, 160–180, 180–190, 190–210, 210–230, 230–260, 260–340, and 340–560, with units of (1/100,000).

Based on the distribution map of malignant tumor incidence in China, we constructed a Geographic Information System (GIS) database for cancer incidence and its influencing factors. The process involved the following steps: (a) Database Construction: We developed a foundational GIS database for the YREB, covering 10 provinces and cities with three explanatory variables and nine control variables. (b) Data Extraction: We extracted the identification information for the 12 variables, including the names, codes, and coordinates of administrative divisions, as the base information for cancer incidence. Using the distribution map of malignant tumor incidence in China, we obtained the county‐level unit codes and their corresponding cancer incidence data. (c) Data Matching: We matched the cancer incidence data with the base information according to the codes and converted Segi's world‐standard cancer incidence rates into cancer incidence level data. Specifically, an incidence rate of <130 was categorized as Level 1, 130–150 as Level 2, and so on, with 340–560 classified as Level 10 (Figure 3). (d) Data Integration: Based on the spatial topology of the variables, we consolidated all the data, excluding county‐level units with missing data. This resulted in a GIS database comprising 1,058 county‐level units, including data on cancer incidence and its influencing factors.

Spatial pattern of cancer incidence in the Yangtze River Economic Zone.

The housing price data are obtained from Xitai data, Haowu data, and 58 Tongcheng data, which were collected in June 2014, as detailed in Y. Wang et al. (2022). Information on wages and the real GDP per capita is obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook for Regional Economy, the China County Statistical Yearbook, and the China City Statistical Yearbook. The 2010 data are collected. Data on the PCLA, the RHP, years of schooling per capita, household size, and the proportion of employment in the secondary sector of the economy are sourced from the tabulation on the 2010 Population Census of the People's Republic of China by County. The 2010 data are collected.

Data on PM2.5 concentration are obtained from the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group of Dalhousie University, and the data set is based on 0.01° × 0.01° raster data. After the annual average PM2.5 concentration values for each raster were obtained, the zoning statistics function in ArcGIS is used to obtain the annual average PM2.5 concentration values for each city, which demonstrates high data accuracy and is suitable for long‐time series analysis. Data on temperature, precipitation, and elevation are obtained from the Daily Surface Climate Dataset for China (V3.0) from the China Meteorological Data Service Center. The data on the four factors from 2010 to 2014 are averaged to eliminate the bias caused by the data in years with extreme weather.

Results

Spatial Differences and Spatial Autocorrelation Characteristics of Housing Hardship in YREB

Figure 4 shows the numerical distribution of the three indicators of housing hardship within the YREB. The distributions of the three indicators are relatively dispersed. The HPIR primarily ranges between 3.5 and 6.5. PCLA is concentrated between 29 and 39 m2, while RHP is mainly between 4% and 13%. All three indicators exhibit extreme outliers, with RHP showing a higher number of unusually high values and PCLA a higher number of unusually low values.

Box diagram of housing hardship.

We demonstrate the spatial pattern of housing hardship in the YREB using ArcGIS (Figure 5). Overall, we observe significant spatial variations in the indicators of housing hardship. The areas with a high HPIR are located downstream of the Yangtze River and concentrated primarily in the southern part of Jiangsu Province, Shanghai Municipality, and Zhejiang Province. Guizhou Province and Hunan Province have comparatively low HPIR. The areas with a large PCLA are relatively dispersed within the region, except Guizhou Province, with Zhejiang Province having a relatively large PCLA. The areas with an elevated RHP are concentrated primarily in Shanghai, the southern part of Jiangsu Province, and the northeastern part of Zhejiang Province.

To ensure the robustness of our conclusion, we further considered the AIC value as an additional criterion. Although both AIC and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) can be used for model selection, due to the large sample size and the complexity of the parameters in spatial econometric models, we concentrated on AIC analysis to effectively avoid overfitting or underfitting risks. A lower AIC value indicates a more optimal model, and in this study, the SDM had the lowest AIC value. Ultimately, the SDM model was identified as the preferred model for explaining the relationship between housing hardship and cancer incidence in the YREB, as it had the R 2 closest to 1, the highest log‐likelihood, and the lowest AIC.

The results in Table 4 show that the three indicators of housing hardship can significantly affect cancer incidence. Specifically, the HPIR and the RHP exhibit a positive correlation with cancer incidence. However, the impact of the HPIR on cancer incidence is stronger than that of the RHP. For every 1% increase in the HPIR, cancer incidence increases by a grade of 0.718%, whereas for every 1% increase in the RHP, the cancer incidence increases by a grade of 0.28%. Conversely, the PCLA is negatively correlated to cancer incidence. Specifically, for every 1% increase in the PCLA, cancer incidence decreases by a grade of 1.06%. Overall, the PCLA has the strongest impact on cancer incidence. Most importantly, the direction of the impact of the indicators on cancer incidence aligns with our theoretical expectations.

Regarding the control variables, seven indicators show a significant correlation with cancer incidence, namely, wages, years of schooling per capita, household size, proportion of employment in the secondary sector of the economy, PM2.5 concentration, temperature, and atmospheric pressure. Among the indicators, wages, proportion of employment in the secondary sector of the economy, PM2.5 concentration, and atmospheric pressure can positively affect cancer incidence. Notably, atmospheric pressure has the most significant impact, with a 1% increase resulting in a 3.262%‐grade rise in cancer incidence. Conversely, years of schooling per capita, household size, and temperature have a negative effect on cancer incidence. Specifically, for every 1% increase in temperature, cancer incidence rates decrease by a grade of 2.343%.

Table 5 reveals a significant association between housing hardship and cancer incidence in the UAs and the NUAs. In urban settings, the HPIR and the PCLA exhibit significance at the 0.01 level. Specifically, the HPIR is positively correlated to cancer incidence, with a 1% increase associated with a 0.718%‐grade rise in cancer incidence. Conversely, the PCLA demonstrates a negative correlation, with a 1% increase associated with a 1.385%‐grade decrease in cancer incidence.

In the NUAs, the HPIR is significant at the 0.05 level, whereas the RHP is significant at the 0.01 level, and both are positively correlated to cancer incidence. For the HPIR, a 1% increase is linked with a 0.352%‐grade increase in cancer incidence, whereas for the RHP, a 1% increase is linked with a 0.362%‐grade increase in cancer incidence.

The impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence varies by region. First, significance varies, with the effects of the PCLA and the RHP varying across regions. In the UAs, the HPIR and the PCLA significantly affect cancer incidence, whereas the RHP does not affect cancer incidence. In the NUAs, the effects of the HPIR and the RHP are significant, whereas the effect of the PCLA is not significant. Second, the impact intensity exhibits differences. Overall, the impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence is stronger in the UAs than in the NUAs. Specifically, in the UAs, the PCLA has the strongest impact on cancer incidence, whereas in the NUAs, the RHP has the strongest impact on cancer incidence.

Discussion

The impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence is multifaceted. First, as the HPIR increases, the cancer incidence rate also increases. A high HPIR implies that a significant portion of the resident's income is allocated to housing costs, which can exacerbate the housing pressure and their financial burden. A direct consequence is that the residents will be compelled to live in substandard housing conditions, such as small living spaces, inadequate sanitation facilities, and long commutes (K. Li et al., 2020). Influenced by their living environment, lifestyle, and the economic pressure, residents' physical and mental health may suffer, which may increase their risk of cancer (Marais & Cloete, 2014). This aligns with the findings of Pollack et al. in the United States, which suggested that excessive housing burden may harm residents' health (Pollack et al., 2010). Moreover, a high HPIR may lead to reduced expenditures in other areas, such as healthcare and physical activities. Second, overcrowded living conditions may affect cancer incidence. A decrease in the PCLA may hinder the maintenance of social distancing measures and increase the risk of unhealthy living conditions, such as bacterial and viral transmission, which may facilitate cross‐infections (Seidlein et al., 2021). Barker et al. indicated that overcrowding may be a primary determinant of gastric cancer (Barker et al., 1990). Overcrowding can also impact residents' mental health, with individuals living in crowded areas being at high risk of depression (X. Wang & Liu, 2023). Third, a high RHP is correlated to high cancer incidence rates. Compared with homeowners, renters face a higher risk of cancer and encounter more housing‐related issues (Sharpe et al., 2014). At the same time, renters are prone to feelings of instability owing to frequent relocations, which can hinder the formation of healthy lifestyle habits and stable mental states.

The disparity in the impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence between the UAs and the NUAs manifests primarily in the differences in the PCLA and the RHP. In UAs, which are constrained by urban land development and high population density, housing prices are high, living spaces are small, and overcrowding is prevalent. In such areas, an increasing number of individuals are residing in cramped quarters and thus may face a high risk of cancer. Conversely, in NUAs, living spaces are expansive, and people can afford housing with large living areas. Therefore, in NUAs, the PCLA is not correlated to cancer incidence. Regarding the relationship between the RHP and cancer incidence, UAs tend to be more inclusive and entail lower psychological stress compared with NUAs. Hence, the impact of the RHP on cancer incidence is not significant in UAs compared with NUAs.

Housing hardship in the YREB exhibits significant spatial autocorrelation. As a crucial economic region in China, the YREB is characterized by pronounced spatial disparities and agglomeration in terms of economic development, population size, industrial structure, environmental quality, and urban scale. For instance, the eastern part of the belt features closely interconnected cities with frequent exchanges of various factors, while the western region faces considerable challenges in coordination (D. Chen et al., 2023). These spatial disparities and agglomeration also contribute to the significant spatial autocorrelation observed in housing hardship across the region. Different areas within the belt face varying degrees of housing hardship. For example, the economically advanced provinces in the eastern part, such as Zhejiang, Shanghai, and southern Jiangsu, are burdened by high HPIR, leading to severe housing affordability issues, consistent with the findings of Zou et al. (2024). In Shanghai, the contradiction between high housing demand and limited land resources has resulted in persistent overcrowding (Y. Zhang & Chen, 2014). Similarly, the western provinces of Yunnan and Guizhou also face significant overcrowding issues. Moreover, many cities in the eastern part of the belt and provincial capitals in the western region have high RHP. The complexity of housing hardship in the YREB necessitates differentiated urban development and management strategies tailored to the specific challenges faced by different cities.

The incidence of cancer along the YREB displays significant spatial heterogeneity. In general, the downstream regions exhibit higher cancer incidence rates compared with the upstream areas, whereas certain counties in the upstream Chengdu Plain demonstrate elevated rates. The level of economic development is closely intertwined with cancer incidence (Guimarães Ribeiro et al., 2023). On the one hand, the downstream regions of the Yangtze River are characterized by advanced economic development and high levels of urbanization. Influenced by lifestyle factors such as food safety, sedentary habits, and irregular sleep patterns, as well as environmental factors, including air and water pollution and lack of green spaces, alongside industrial activities that produce carcinogenic substances, this region tends to have high cancer incidence rates. On the other hand, China is experiencing a consistent rise in the percentage of newly detected cancer cases among the elderly population. The high incidence of cancer in the downstream areas of the Yangtze River may be linked to the degree of aging in this region (Pilleron et al., 2021; S. Wang, 2020). Furthermore, the region benefits from relatively satisfactory medical conditions, with a large number of individuals actively seeking medical attention and participating in cancer screenings, thereby resulting in a high number of registered cancer cases.

In light of the relationship between housing hardship and cancer incidence, urban planning should focus on increasing PCLA, particularly in UAs. This can be achieved through rational planning and urban renewal, expanding living space in older city districts, and encouraging residents to relocate to suburban areas to alleviate overcrowding. Additionally, implementing diversified housing policies—such as constructing affordable housing and providing housing subsidies—can increase homeownership rates, especially in NUAs, thereby reducing economic burdens and mitigating health issues related to housing stress. Furthermore, cities within the YREB should develop differentiated policies based on the spatial heterogeneity of housing hardship to ensure that interventions are precise and effective. For instance, in the eastern part of the belt, efforts should be intensified to enhance housing security, adjust land supply structures, and increase residential land availability. In contrast, in the western regions, priorities should include improving infrastructure, advancing slum redevelopment, and enhancing housing conditions to boost residential attractiveness. Moreover, in cities with high RHP, it is essential to refine rental policies, establish a regulated rental market, and promote long‐term leasing to enhance residential stability.

This study has several limitations. First, data limitations. On one hand, the cancer incidence data used in this study are categorical, which, while providing a clear overview of cancer risk levels across different regions or populations, simplifies the complex incidence data and overlooks subtle differences within the same cancer category. On the other hand, it is important to acknowledge that cancer incidence may vary over time. Future research should aim to obtain more detailed cancer data to explore the health effects of housing hardship further. Second, the scope of the study. Our research is focused on the YREB. However, cancer incidence and housing hardship exhibit significant spatial variations. Future studies should consider expanding the research scope to a national or even global level to validate and deepen our findings with a larger sample size.

Conclusions

This study analyzes the impact of three indicators of housing hardship (i.e., the HPIR, the PCLA, and the RHP) on cancer incidence and reveals the health effects of housing hardship. This study also analyzes the differential effects of housing hardship on cancer incidence in UAs and NUAs. The research results are as follows: (a) a significant correlation exists between housing hardship and cancer incidence, with the HPIR and the RHP being positively correlated to cancer incidence and the PCLA being negatively correlated to cancer incidence. (b) The differences in the impact of housing hardship on cancer incidence between the UAs and the NUAs are reflected mainly in the differences in the PCLA and the RHP. The PCLA has a strong association with cancer incidence in the UAs, whereas the RHP demonstrates a strong correlation with cancer incidence in the NUAs. (c) Significant spatial heterogeneity is observed in housing hardship in the YREB.

This study reveals the complex relationship between housing hardship and cancer incidence, enhancing our understanding of the link between housing and health. Future research could focus on the following areas: (a) Mechanisms and causal inference. Future studies should further explore how housing hardship influences cancer incidence through intermediate variables such as lifestyle, psychological state, and environmental factors. Constructing mediation models or path analyses could provide a more precise understanding of the causal relationship between housing hardship and cancer incidence. (b) Fine‐scale research on housing hardship. Future research could examine the distribution characteristics of housing hardship indicators at smaller spatial scales (e.g., neighborhoods or communities) and their impact on cancer incidence. This would help identify high‐risk areas and inform regional health planning and resource allocation. (c) Longitudinal and dynamic studies. Given that housing conditions and health statuses may change over time, future research should consider conducting longitudinal studies to observe the dynamic changes in the relationship between housing hardship and cancer incidence.
