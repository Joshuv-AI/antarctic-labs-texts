Preventive effect of intraoperative antiseptic irrigation on surgical site infections after deep brain stimulation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Baobin Liu, Chengyou Hong, Wei Ding, Mingyu Zhao, Zhijuan Chen, Kexin Li, Mengyao He, Yuchi Zhang, Junjie Gong, Jing Li, Chang Liu, Zengguang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDeep brain stimulation (DBS) is an effective treatment for movement disorders. However, hardware-related infections following DBS surgery represent a serious complication, with incidence rates ranging from 2.6% to 10%. DBS hardware-related infections can lead to significant clinical deterioration, including loss of therapeutic stimulation benefits, recurrence of initial symptoms, potential hardware removal, and increased need for anti-infective therapy. Nevertheless, the key risk factors and potential preventive measures for these infections remain unclear. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of intraoperative prophylactic irrigation with hydrogen peroxide, 0.5% povidone-iodine solution, and their combination in reducing the rate of hardware infections following DBS surgery.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted, including patients who underwent DBS surgery between August 2016 and August 2024. Participants were categorized into one of four groups according to the intraoperative irrigation regimen used in clinical practice: saline, hydrogen peroxide, povidone-iodine, or combination. Only patients with complete operative and follow-up records were included. All patients were evaluated at four postoperative follow-up visits (1, 3, 6, and 12 months) to assess incision healing and surgical site infection (SSI) rates. Appropriate statistical analyses were employed to analyze variables of interest.ResultsThe overall surgical site infection (SSI) rate was 2.08% (8/385). The saline group exhibited a significantly higher infection rate compared to the intervention groups (4.2% vs. 1.03%, p = 0.039). The combination group did not demonstrate a synergistic effect (p > 0.05). Over approximately 8 years, 385 adult patients underwent 385 DBS procedures with bilateral electrode implantation, totaling 770 electrodes placed. Among these: Saline group: five SSI cases (5.26%) occurred within 1 year postoperatively. Pathogens identified: Staphylococcus aureus (n = 2) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (n = 3). Hydrogen peroxide group: 1 SSI case (1.02%) with Staphylococcus epidermidis identified. Povidone-iodine group: 1 SSI case (1.04%) with Staphylococcus aureus identified. Combination group: 1 SSI case with Enterobacter hormaechei identified.ConclusionAlthough, the characteristics of most patients evaluated in this study and operative duration were not associated with the risk of SSI, our data indicate that wound irrigation with hydrogen peroxide or 0.5% povidone-iodine solution may contribute to reduced SSI risk following DBS implantation.

Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a safe and effective neuromodulation technique widely used for treating movement disorders, particularly demonstrating significant efficacy in Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, and dystonia. Subsequently, Benabid and his team refined DBS technology in the 1980s, successfully treating Parkinson's patients and establishing its pivotal role in modern functional neurosurgery (3).

Although DBS surgery is generally safe, hardware-related complications associated with implantable medical devices remain a major clinical concern (1). Foreign body implantation may trigger risks including infection, skin erosion, electrode migration, lead fracture/failure, and implantable pulse generator (IPG) malfunction (2). According to a systematic review and meta-analysis by Jitkritsadakul et al. (4) encompassing 28 studies (n = 5,114 patients), the overall hardware complication rate was 10.45%, with infection being the most frequent (5.12%), followed by electrode migration (1.60%), lead fracture (1.46%), IPG failure (1.06%), other component failures (0.73%), and non-infectious skin erosion (0.48%).

DBS-related infections can lead to severe consequences: patients typically require hospitalization, surgical debridement, long-term antibiotic therapy, and in severe cases, partial or complete hardware removal with staged reimplantation (5). A retrospective cost analysis by Chen et al. (6) revealed that the average total treatment cost for DBS infections in the United States exceeds $75,505, primarily attributed to reoperations (43.2%), inpatient care (31.8%), and antibiotic therapy (25.0%). These interventions not only increase physical and psychological burdens but also disrupt neuromodulation therapy, resulting in rebound motor symptoms (7).

To mitigate post-DBS infection risks, multiple preventive strategies have been explored: (1) Preoperative skin preparation with 70% ethanol-based antiseptic solutions (8); (2) Perioperative systemic antibiotic prophylaxis; (3) Intraoperative topical vancomycin powder application (9); (4) Antibiotic-impregnated envelopes (AIE) for IPG wrapping (10).

Among these, a randomized controlled trial by Halpern et al. (24) demonstrated that preoperative patient-applied ethanol-based solutions significantly reduced Staphylococcus epidermidis colonization (p < 0.01), through direct correlation with infection rates remains unconfirmed. A retrospective study by Abode-Iyamah et al. (2) reported reduced infection rates from 6.4% to 1.7% (p = 0.03) with local vancomycin powder. However, most current evidence remains limited to single-center observational studies, with scarce direct comparisons between prevention strategies.

Addressing this research gap, we designed a single-center retrospective cohort study to evaluate the impact of two topical antiseptics-−3% hydrogen peroxide and 0.5% povidone-iodine solutions—for intraoperative wound irrigation on post-DBS infection rates. This study pioneers the novel application of wound irrigation, an established infection prevention measure in orthopedic arthroplasty, to functional neurosurgery, aiming to provide high-level evidence for diversified DBS infection control (11).

Methods

Study design and patient categorization

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study, approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (Approval No.: IRB2025-KY-447). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective, anonymized nature of the study.

The study included adult patients (≥18 years) with Parkinson's disease who underwent DBS surgery performed by Dr. Weidong Yang at Tianjin Medical University General Hospital between August 2016 and August 2024. Participants were stratified into four groups according to the intraoperative irrigation regimen used in clinical practice: (1) Saline group, (2) Hydrogen peroxide irrigation group, (3) Povidone-iodine irrigation group, (4) Combination irrigation group. The infusion plan is selected by the surgeon during the operation based on routine clinical practice, rather than being assigned according to a pre-determined research plan. Patients were not randomly assigned to any of the irrigation groups, and no randomization or allocation sequence was used. During the research period, there was no unified international guideline specifically stipulating the optimal intraoperative irrigation protocol for preventing infection in DBS surgery. In routine clinical practice, normal saline is usually used as the standard irrigation method, while hydrogen peroxide and povidone-iodine are also used as disinfection irrigation options. Additionally, in some cases, a sequential irrigation method of using hydrogen peroxide first and then povidone-iodine is adopted. The specific clinical factors influencing the choice of lavage regimens for individual patients were not predefined in the standardized protocols and were not systematically recorded in the medical records. According to the standard clinical procedures of our hospital, all DBS patients are required to undergo neuro-regulation adjustments and clinical evaluations at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after the surgery. During these routine follow-ups, it is also required to assess the healing of the incision and surgical site infection (SSI). SSI was determined according to CDC criteria (13+) through comprehensive medical record review. Only patients with complete operative and follow-up records were included. Throughout the entire research period, all four irrigation methods were employed simultaneously, rather than being introduced one after another at different time points.

Surgical procedure

All DBS procedures at our center were performed as single-stage implantations rather than staged surgeries. The regular surgical team consists of: a lead neurosurgeon, an assistant, a neurophysiologist, an anesthesiologist, a circulating nurse and an instrument nurse. All the operations were carried out under general anesthesia. Each case underwent multidisciplinary evaluation by a team comprising neurologists, neurosurgeons, and neuroradiologists to confirm diagnosis and select optimal targets: subthalamic nucleus (STN) or globus pallidus internus (GPi) for Parkinson's disease patients.

Electrode placement utilized MRI-based direct targeting coupled with frameless stereotaxy. Final lead positioning was confirmed through intraoperative electrophysiological verification:

The DBS hardware components, including the electrodes, extension cables and implantable pulse generator, are only opened from the sterile packaging after the target has been confirmed and before they are implanted immediately, in order to minimize air contamination.

Follow-up and endpoint definitions

Patients underwent routine postoperative clinical follow-up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery (12). Surgical site infection (SSI) was diagnosed according to CDC criteria (8). Microbiological confirmation was performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS).

Results

A total of 385 patients were enrolled (214 males, 171 females), with mean age 62.5 ± 8.8 years, fasting blood glucose 5.6 ± 1.2 mmol/L, and BMI 24.9 ± 3.1 kg/m2. ASA class II patients accounted for 86.7% of the cohort, while males constituted 55.6%. Mean operative duration was 211 ± 32 min. No significant differences existed in demographic characteristics or surgical parameters among the four groups (P > 0.05, Table 1).

To further clarify the characteristics of these infections, we analyzed their anatomical distribution and depth. The data showed that among the eight cases of infection, three occurred on the chest wall (related to the IPG pouch), three were located on the head (at the electrode implant site), and two were in the posterior auricular region (Supplementary Table 1). In terms of depth, all the identified infections were classified as deep incisional SSI related to surgical instruments or organ/space SSI (such as the pouch or electrode pathway), and no superficial infections limited to the skin surface were reported. There was no significant difference in the distribution of infection sites between the saline group and the intervention group.

Microbiological analysis revealed Staphylococcus aureus in three cases (two in the saline group vs. one in the povidone-iodine group), Staphylococcus epidermidis in four cases (three in the saline group vs. one in the hydrogen peroxide group), and Enterobacter hormaechei in one case (in the combination group). Gram-positive cocci were identified in seven cases, and Gram-negative bacilli in one case. The distribution of pathogens did not demonstrate clinically significant differences between the groups, as detailed in Table 3.

All eight SSI patients achieved complete clinical cure without recurrence during the 12-month post-treatment follow-up. None of the patients required hardware removal or staged reimplantation, confirming the efficacy of the combined debridement, irrigation, and antimicrobial therapy protocol. Post-treatment imaging and neurological assessments revealed no structural damage or neurological deficits related to infection or intervention, and all patients resumed regular DBS neuromodulation therapy with restored therapeutic benefits.

Discussion

The DBS surgical procedure is relatively complex, and surgeons change instruments significantly more frequently than in other neurosurgical operations. Factors such as the wide variety of instruments, high frequency of changes, and extensive operative field all increase the risk of infection. Although the operative time is relatively short, the intricate workflow elevates the probability of infection. Furthermore, the implantation of DBS-related devices (foreign bodies) significantly heightens the risk of surgical site infection (SSI).

Infection is the most common complication of DBS surgery, with reported rates ranging from approximately 2.6% to 10% (1). Deep brain stimulator infection not only interrupts therapeutic benefits but also significantly prolongs hospital stays and increases healthcare costs. Although multiple medical centers are exploring various strategies to reduce DBS hardware-related infection rates, there is still a lack of randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence; existing studies are predominantly retrospective cohort analyses. Beyond systemic antibiotic prophylaxis, the literature reports the local application of vancomycin powder (VP) to the surgical incision for SSI prevention, with lower SSI rates observed in patients receiving VP (though the association between VP and reduced SSI risk did not fully reach the pre-specified level of statistical significance).

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that intraoperative wound irrigation with either hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) or povidone-iodine (PVP-I) solution reduces the risk of postoperative DBS infection by 80.4%, with both antiseptics showing comparable efficacy. Both disinfectants have a long history of use in surgery. Hydrogen peroxide, a versatile chemical agent, is widely employed across medical fields, particularly in plastic surgery, orthopedics, general surgery, and neurosurgery (13). Its neurosurgical application dates back to cerebellar abscess debridement in 1893. The antimicrobial mechanism of hydrogen peroxide relies on the Fenton reaction: decomposition of H2O2 generates oxygen free radicals, which cause oxidative stress damage to microbial DNA, proteins, and other cellular structures, ultimately leading to pathogen death (14). As a broad-spectrum antiseptic, it effectively inhibits the growth of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and yeasts (15).

However, hydrogen peroxide application carries potential complications, primarily neurotoxicity, impaired wound healing, and gas embolism. Its negative impact on wound healing is mediated through damage to keratinocytes and fibroblasts (16). The substantial production of oxygen free radicals explains its neurotoxicity. The most severe complication is gas embolism caused by wound irrigation, potentially leading to neurological sequelae, hemodynamic instability, subcutaneous emphysema, limb ischemia, or even death (17). Therefore, the core protective measure when using hydrogen peroxide is meticulous dural closure to prevent its entry into the cranial cavity, which could cause intracranial pneumatosis, neural injury, or gas embolism. To mitigate embolism risk, we ensured thorough wound hemostasis before irrigation and immediately flushed any residual hydrogen peroxide with saline after irrigation.

Sole reliance on hydrogen peroxide irrigation has antimicrobial limitations: certain bacteria (e.g., common skin pathogens Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa) can secrete catalase, diminishing its bactericidal effect (18). Studies indicate that both hydrogen peroxide and povidone-iodine solutions effectively reduce SSI rates (19). Consequently, the other antiseptic agent selected for this study was povidone-iodine solution (PVP-I). As the most commonly used iodine preparation today, PVP-I is a stable complex of polyvinylpyrrolidone and elemental iodine. Free molecular iodine (I2) in the solution is its primary bactericidal component (20). I2 freely penetrates microbial cells and exerts its germicidal action through multiple mechanisms: blocking arginine/histidine hydrogen bond formation and binding to tyrosine phenolic groups; oxidizing sulfhydryl groups of cysteine/methionine, denaturing proteins; and binding to fatty acid carbon chains and nucleotides (adenine, cytosine, and guanine), disrupting nucleic acid structures and cell membrane integrity (21). I2 comprehensively damages microbial cell membranes, inhibits the respiratory chain, interferes with electron transport, blocks protein synthesis, and denatures nucleic acids. It is a broad-spectrum, non-resistance-inducing bactericide effective against bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa (22). Thus, PVP-I is an ideal surgical antiseptic.

Local application of hydrogen peroxide or diluted povidone-iodine to the incision enables targeted infection control. This study is the first to demonstrate that intraoperative irrigation significantly reduces the risk of postoperative DBS infection by 80.4%, with no observed complications such as impaired wound healing, gas embolism, or neurological injury. This finding aligns with studies in orthopedic joint arthroplasty (23). However, combined use did not demonstrate a synergistic effect, potentially due to the chemical neutralization reaction where H2O2 oxidizes iodide ions to form hypoiodous acid, reducing bactericidal efficacy (15).

After confirming balanced baseline characteristics (age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, fasting blood glucose, etc.) with no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) across the four patient groups, analysis of 385 DBS procedures revealed that antiseptic irrigation (H2O2, PVP-I, or their combination) significantly reduced SSI risk. The pooled intervention groups (*n* = 290) had an infection rate of 1.03%, representing an absolute risk reduction (ARR) of 4.23% compared to the saline group (*n* = 95, 5.26%; P = 0.039), and a relative risk reduction (RRR) of 80.4% (NNT = 24). Individual group comparisons did not reach significance (e.g., H2O2 group 1.04% vs. Saline group, P = 0.212), attributable to the rarity of infection events (total n = 8) and insufficient statistical power (32%) due to limited sample size per group (*n* ≈ 96). By pooling intervention groups sharing the same “chemical antimicrobial mechanism,” the sample size increased to 290 (three infections), boosting power to 82%, thereby avoiding a Type II error and adhering to CONSORT guidelines. The highly consistent infection rates across intervention groups (1.03%−1.04%) support homogeneity, and the lack of difference between H2O2 and PVP-I groups (P > 0.99) confirms equivalence. The absence of synergy in the combination group (P > 0.05) may relate to overlapping antimicrobial spectra or limited sample size. Although constrained by its single-center design and relatively small sample size, the results provide Level III evidence for DBS infection prevention. Future multicenter RCTs are needed to optimize protocols and assess long-term electrode compatibility.

Pathogen spectrum analysis indicated predominant staphylococci in the saline group, consistent with typical DBS postoperative pathogens. The occurrence of an Enterobacteriaceae infection in the combination group (n = 1) hints at a possible mechanism whereby antiseptic irrigation disrupts skin flora balance, facilitating Gram-negative colonization. Given the small number of infections, this interpretation is speculative and warrants investigation in larger cohorts.

Management of SSIs showed that all eight infected cases successfully retained hardware while controlling infection. Key factors included: (1) Prompt intervention: early detection and management prevented infection spread; (2) Thorough debridement and sequential irrigation: necrotic tissue removal followed by sequential irrigation using the combined prophylaxis group method (100 mL H2O2 + 100 mL PVP-I sequentially) to eradicate residual pathogens; (3) Primary closure: avoiding drain placement to prevent secondary drain-related infections. While this approach demonstrated efficacy, its validation requires RCTs due to the limited sample size.

Regarding the surveillance period

The new NHSN standard defines the SSI surveillance period as 90 days postoperatively, but literature suggests this may underestimate the true incidence. Therefore, this study extended surveillance to 1 year. Seven of the eight SSIs occurred within 90 days, with one appearing at 353 days (nearly 1 year). As each intervention group had only one infection (H2O2, PVP-I, and Combination), median time estimates have limited representativeness. However, the late infection in the combination group (353 days) may suggest: (1) incomplete eradication of early biofilm by the antiseptic; (2) long latency period of low-virulence pathogens (e.g., Enterobacteriaceae); (3) the need for extended follow-up to monitor delayed infections. Given the small number of SSI cases, future studies with larger samples are needed to validate this trend.

Of particular note

The Enterobacter hormaechei detected in the combination group is a nosocomial multidrug-resistant organism. This suggests antiseptic irrigation might selectively suppress resident skin flora (e.g., staphylococci), enabling colonization by resistant Gram-negative bacteria. A similar phenomenon has been reported in cardiac pacemaker implantation. Subsequent research should investigate the impact of irrigation solutions on the local microbiome.

Study limitations

The single-center design limits generalizability; the effect of irrigation solution concentration gradients was not assessed; mechanisms of biofilm formation were not studied. Future multicenter trials are required to validate the optimal irrigation protocol, coupled with microbiome studies to delve deeper into infection prevention mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that single-agent irrigation with either hydrogen peroxide or povidone-iodine effectively reduces the risk of postoperative DBS infection. However, the potential risks of the combination regimen require further validation. Future large-scale, long-term studies are needed to optimize intraoperative infection control strategies, complemented by microbiological and molecular mechanistic exploration to advance precision infection prevention practices.
