Characterizing Oral Microbiome and Periodontal Disease in Oral HPV-Positive (COMP-HPV) individuals with HIV: an observational longitudinal study protocol

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a major cause of oropharyngeal and other cancers, occurs more frequently among people with HIV (PWH). Despite antiretroviral therapy, HPV-related cancer incidence remains elevated in this group. Oral dysbiosis in PWH may impair mucosal immunity, promoting HPV persistence and inflammation. Periodontal disease, frequently observed in PWH, further contributes to microbial imbalance and immune dysregulation, increasing susceptibility to oral HPV infection. This study investigates the relationship among oral microbiome composition, periodontal disease and oral HPV infection behavior in PWH, considering immunologic and social determinants of health.MethodsThe characterizing oral microbiome and periodontal disease in oral HPV-positive individuals (COMP-HPV), an observational longitudinal study will enroll 500 PWH and follow them up for two years. Oral rinse for HPV testing and periodontal assessment will be collected every six months; saliva for inflammatory markers, oral rinse for microbiome and oral cytobrush for immunological profiling will be collected annually. Immune profiling will include high-dimensional flow cytometry and 10X RNA-sequencing to characterize innate and adaptive immune subsets, with emphasis on HLA-DR–positive populations, enabling evaluation of oral immune modulation during HPV infection. The study has four specific aims such as to examine associations between oral microbiome composition (16S and metagenomics) and oral HPV infection, including prevalence, incidence, persistence, and clearance; to assess the impact of periodontal disease on oral HPV infection and investigate whether the oral microbiome mediates this relationship; to determine how oral microbiome composition influences immunological responses in HPV-positive PWH and to evaluate the role of social determinants on oral microbiome composition and HPV infection. Data from this longitudinal study will be used to understand the natural history of oral HPV infection, the interplay with periodontal disease, microbial alterations, and immunological changes, providing evidence to guide interventions for reducing HPV-associated disease in PWH.Trial registration numberNot applicable.DiscussionThe COMP-HPV study aims to contribute to the body of research designed to investigate mechanisms underlying oral HPV infection among PWH to improve immune responses to reduce HPV infection and relevant carcinoma.

Background

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infections are among the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide, representing a significant global health concern due to their high prevalence and transmissibility. HPV is a leading cause of oral cancer, and the incidence of oral cancer is particularly high among PWH [1]. HPV accounts for at least 5% of all human cancers globally, including cancers of the cervix, anal canal, genitalia (vulva, vagina, and penis) and oropharynx [1, 2]. Each year, more than 600,000 new cases of HPV-related cancers are reported worldwide, resulting in approximately 300,000 deaths [3, 4]. While cervical cancer remains the most prevalent HPV-associated cancer globally and in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), rates of oropharyngeal cancer are increasing in many regions, including the USA and the UK, where oropharyngeal cancer has surpassed cervical cancer as the leading HPV-associated malignancy since 2012 [5–8]. Notably, the risk of this malignancy has increased nearly 50-fold over the past decade [4, 9, 10].

Oropharyngeal cancers are the second most common form of HPV-associated cancers in men, following anal cancers among PWH [4, 9]. Persistent oral HPV infection has been identified as the primary risk factor for HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer [4, 11]. Furthermore, the prevalence of HPV-associated lesions and cancers is rising among PWH [12–14]. HIV infection significantly increases the risk of oral HPV infection, with studies reporting higher rates of both overall and high-risk HPV subtypes among PWH [15–17]. However, most available data on this association come from developed countries and focus on young adults and older populations [15].

Recent molecular and epidemiological studies have demonstrated a clear role for HPV, particularly high-risk (HR) types such as HPV-16 in the aetiology of oropharyngeal cancers [10, 18]. Despite these advances, the epidemiology of oral HPV infection remains poorly understood. Initial evidence suggests that oral HPV infection, similar to cervical infection, is associated with sexual behaviour and immunosuppression [11, 12]. However, the extent to which findings from cervical HPV research can be extrapolated to oral infection is uncertain [6, 19–21]. Moreover, data on HPV transmission to various body sites through sexual practices remain limited.

HPV infection is closely linked with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC). While the incidence of HPV-unrelated OSCC associated with tobacco and alcohol use has declined in recent decades, HPV-related OSCC has risen significantly, particularly among white men and younger individuals likely reflecting changes in sexual behaviour [13].

Periodontal disease, commonly seen in PWH is a polymicrobial condition with pathological changes resulting from a complex inflammatory and immune host response [19, 20]. With the arrival of new sequencing technologies, periodontal disease has been linked to a broader dysbiosis in the oral microbiome. This dysbiosis further promotes a proinflammatory community and triggers tissue destruction augmented by the host immune response, which could subsequently lead to higher susceptibility to pathogenic microorganisms, such as oral HPV infection [17, 18, 22]. However, studies on the association between periodontal diseases and oral HPV infection are limited and evidence is particularly lacking in relation to the oral microbiome [23, 24]. Therefore, we propose to examine the association among the oral microbiome, periodontal diseases, and oral HPV infection, as well as the immunological underpinnings of oral HPV infection in the context of the oral microbiome, among PWH. Additionally, social determinants of health such as economic stability, education access and quality, health care access, neighborhood environment, and social and community context are increasingly recognized as key factors influencing overall health outcomes [25]. Their impact has been documented in areas such as HIV care, HPV infection, and the human microbiome [26–30]. Therefore, we will explore whether social determinants play a role in the association between the oral microbiome and oral HPV infection.

The findings of this study will provide critical evidence on whether and how periodontal disease and the oral microbiome influence the prevalence and persistence of oral HPV infection among PWH. The identification of key oral microbiota will guide the development of targeted interventions to reduce oral HPV infection and, in turn, lower the incidence of HPV-associated head and neck cancers (HNC) among PWH. Furthermore, addressing important social determinants of health will yield valuable implementation strategies to reduce health disparities and improve overall health outcomes in this population.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

The COMP-HPV, an observational longitudinal study will be conducted at the Oral Health Center of the Infectious Disease Program (IDP) at Grady’s Ponce Clinic in Atlanta, Georgia—one of the largest and most comprehensive facilities in the United States dedicated to the treatment of advanced HIV/AIDS. A total of 500 PWH will be enrolled and followed for two years, with data and biospecimen collection every six months or annually. This time frame was selected to capture the natural history of oral HPV infection.This study will follow participants over two years to understand how the oral microbiome, periodontal health, immune responses, and social factors together shape oral HPV infection among PWH. These data will help reveal how variations in microbial composition are linked to key oral HPV outcomes such as infection prevalence, new infections, persistence, and eventual clearance. Beyond microbial factors, the study will explore the role of periodontal disease as a potential contributor to oral HPV infection. By examining whether alterations in the oral microbiome mediate this relationship, we aim to uncover biological pathways connecting oral health to viral persistence. To further understand the body’s defense mechanisms, immunological profiling will be conducted to compare PWH with and without oral HPV infection. This analysis will provide insights into the immune processes that may promote or protect against HPV persistence within the oral cavity. Finally, recognizing that health is influenced not only by biological factors but also by social and environmental circumstances, we will assess how social determinants of health influence both the oral microbiome and HPV infection. Together, these integrated analyses will provide a holistic understanding of the biological and social factors that drive oral HPV infection and persistence in PWH.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants will be eligible for inclusion in the study if they are18 years of age or older and provided signed and dated informed consent. All participants will be required to have a documented diagnosis of HIV infection in their medical records and a Zubrod Performance Status of 0–2, as determined either from medical records or by evaluation from the study team [31]. Both male and female participants will be considered for enrollment and gender-diverse participants will be eligible for inclusion in this study. Each participant is required to have at least eight erupted teeth (i.e., teeth visible above the gum line), excluding root tips.

Participants with cognitive impairment, impaired decision-making capacity, or documented illiteracy in their medical record were excluded to ensure informed consent could be appropriately obtained. Participants requiring pre-medication for invasive dental procedures, such as bacterial endocarditis prophylaxis, periodontal surgery, root planning and scaling, gingivectomy, frenectomy, tooth extractions, or implant placement will be excluded. Individuals receiving antibiotic therapy for active infections, including HIV-related opportunistic infections, sinus infections, or sexually transmitted infections, will not be eligible for enrollment until at least two weeks after completion of antibiotic treatment. However, those receiving antibiotic prophylaxis for the prevention of opportunistic infections will be eligible without delay. Participants who reported the use of teeth-whitening or bleaching products will also be excluded from the study. For participants undergoing biopsy procedures, additional exclusion criteria will be applied. These included a CD4 count of less than 250 cells/µL (or CD4% below 14%), HIV viral load greater than 200 copies/mL, active or undiagnosed dental, oral or craniofacial infection and unhealed wounds in the oral or craniofacial tissues. Participants with an absolute neutrophil count below 500 cells/mm³, coagulopathy (INR > 2), platelet count below 50,000 cells/mm³, or those currently receiving anticoagulant therapy such as warfarin or factor Xa inhibitors will also be excluded.

Participant timeline

Upon successful screening and confirmation of eligibility, a total of 500 PWH individuals will be recruited. Approximately 100 patients attend the Oral Health Center each week, and preliminary data indicate that roughly 90% meet the eligibility criteria. Based on these estimates, we expect to enroll 500 participants over a period of approximately 24 months. The two-year follow-up period provides adequate time for completion of all laboratory procedures, including HPV testing, microbiome and immunologic assays, as well as subsequent data cleaning and analysis. Clinical evaluation and specimen collection

Following Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and the HIPAA authorization, trained research assistants (RAs) will screen the daily clinic schedules of participating physicians to identify potentially eligible patients. After reviewing medical records and obtaining confirmation of eligibility from the physicians, the RAs will approach eligible patients to obtain informed consent. Socioeconomic, behavioral, and lifestyle factors (excluding diet) will be assessed using self-administered questionnaires implemented through the online REDCap platform (Fig. 1). Participants may complete the questionnaires either at home or during their clinic visit, requiring approximately twenty minutes to complete. RAs will review submitted questionnaires daily to ensure completeness and address any discrepancies.

Dietary information will be collected through telephone interviewer-administered 24-hour dietary recalls, while clinical data will be abstracted from electronic medical records. Oral rinse samples will be collected at baseline and at each six-month study visit following standardized protocols used in our previous studies, in which we successfully enrolled PWH and collected oral rinse samples for oral HPV analyses [18, 32].

Periodontal status will also be examined at the same study visits. Additionally, oral rinse and cytobrush samples will be collected at baseline and 1 and 2 years for oral microbiome and immune profiling, and inflammatory markers. Biopsy samples will be collected only one time for eligible patients for single cell sequencing.

Data collection

Socioeconomic status (SES) covariates will be collected at baseline and at follow-up visits, as appropriate. A standardized self-reported questionnaire will capture demographic and socioeconomic variables, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, education, household income, and health insurance status. An SES index score will be generated using principal component analysis (PCA), following recommendations from previous study [33]. Both individual SES variables and the composite SES index will be included in the statistical analyses. Behavioural and lifestyle covariates will be collected at baseline, 12-month, and 24-month follow-up visits.

Dietary intake will be assessed using 24-hour dietary recalls, a method widely regarded as the most reliable for measuring short-term variations in food consumption [34]. At each data collection point, two interviewer-administered 24-hour recalls (one on a weekday and one on a weekend day) will be conducted via telephone. Dietary recalls will collect daily food, beverage and supplement use and avoid unusual events, such as holidays or illness. A computer-assisted, multiple-pass interview process will be employed to enhance recall accuracy. All dietary data will be collected, processed, and analysed using the most recent version of the Automated Self-Administered 24-hour Recall (ASA24), an interactive, web-based platform that prompts for detailed food descriptions and automatically codes and calculates nutrient intakes based on the USDA Food and Nutrient Database for Dietary Studies (FNDDS) [35]. Participants who encounter difficulties with the telephone interview will receive detailed instructions and secure online access to complete the ASA24 independently. For each participant, the USDA Healthy Eating Index (HEI), total energy intake and macronutrient composition (fiber, carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins) will be derived at each assessment point for use in data analyses. The HEI is an measure of diet quality that assesses alignment of dietary intake with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans [36]. Additionally, a dietary inflammation score will be calculated using validated methods developed by the co-investigator [37].

Alcohol and tobacco use will be assessed using standardized questions adapted from prior etiologic studies evaluating cancer risk factors. Participants will be queried on the frequency and quantity of alcohol consumption by beverage type. “One drink” will be defined as 12 ounces of beer, 4 ounces of wine, or 1.5 ounces of liquor. Tobacco use will include assessment of smoking status. Among current and former smokers, information on pack-years and years since cessation (if applicable) will be collected.

Sexual behaviors will be measured using a standardized self-reported questionnaire. For women, the number of lifetime sexual partners will be defined as the number of vaginal partners; for men, as the sum of vaginal and anal sex partners. Data on the lifetime number of oral sex partners and whether recent oral sex partners were new will also be collected. Self-reported sexual orientation (e.g., heterosexual men/women or men who have sex with men) will be documented.

Clinical covariates will be collected at baseline and at follow-up visits, as appropriate. These include time since HIV diagnosis, CD4 count, HIV viral load, serologic status, major clinical events (e.g., hospitalizations, surgeries), antiretroviral therapy history (types and duration), HIV-associated conditions (e.g., opportunistic infections, neoplasms), concomitant medications, and comorbidities. Comorbidities will be assessed using the validated Charlson Comorbidity Index [38]. All other clinical data will be extracted from electronic medical records at baseline and follow-up.

Periodontal status will be assessed at baseline and every six months for up to two years using the 2018 classification of periodontal diseases jointly established by the European Federation of Periodontology and the American Academy of Periodontology [39, 40]. Participants will be classified as having periodontitis if they exhibit interdental clinical attachment loss (CAL) in ≥ 2 nonadjacent teeth, or buccal/oral CAL ≥ 3 mm with a periodontal probing depth (PPD) > 3 mm at ≥ 2 teeth. Disease stage will be categorized according to the site of greatest interdental CAL: 1–2 mm (mild, Stage 1), 3–4 mm (moderate, Stage 2), and ≥ 5 mm (severe, Stage 3–4).

Social and neighborhood-level determinants of health will be collected at baseline and follow-up. County-level health factors will be derived from the County Health Rankings (CHR), which provide composite measures of health behaviors, clinical care, social and economic conditions, and physical environment [41]. Lower CHR scores indicate better county health status. Neighborhood disadvantage will be assessed using the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), developed by the Health Resources and Services Administration [42]. The ADI incorporates neighborhood-level indicators of income, education, employment, and housing quality, with lower scores reflecting less deprivation. Residential racial segregation (Black vs. White and non-White vs. White) will also be assessed using CHR data, based on an index of dissimilarity derived from the American Community Survey, where higher values indicate greater segregation [43, 44].

Laboratory measurements

Oral rinse samples and saliva samples will be collected using standard protocols and stored at − 80 °C until batch assays for oral HPV detection, inflammatory markers, and microbiome profiling [32]. Additionally, oral mucosal samples will be collected using cytobrushes for immune profiling. After cleaning the mucosal surface of excess mucus, the cytobrush will be swiped and rolled 10 times across the buccal mucosa on both sides of the mouth. The brushes will be placed in RPMI medium containing 20% human serum and transported at 4 °C to immunology laboratory within 4 h. Gingival collar punch biopsies (4 mm; 2 mm width) will also be obtained, placed in RPMI medium, and delivered for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) preparation.

HPV genotyping

DNA will be extracted from oral rinse samples collected at baseline and every 6 months for 2 years using the MagNA Pure LC DNA Isolation Kit III (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). HPV genotyping will be performed using the TypeSeq protocol, which detects 51 HPV genotypes (3, 6, 11, 13, 16, 18, 26, 28, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 39, 40, 42, 43, 44, 45, 51, 52, 53, 54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 62, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 76, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86, 87, 89, 90, 91, 97, and 114) including 13 high-risk types (HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 68a, 68b). All HPV testing will be performed at the CDC, which has extensive experience in HPV diagnostics.

Immune profiling

Immune profiling was conducted using the same approach as described in our previous studies [45, 46]. Cytobrush samples collected at baseline, 1 year, and 2 years will be used for immune profiling using multicolor flow cytometry. Approximately 1–2 × 10⁶ lymphocytes per sample were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies obtained from BD Biosciences – Pharmingen™ (San Diego, CA, USA) specific for human leukocyte antigen–DR isotype (HLA-DR, clone L243), cluster of differentiation CD14 (clone M5E2), CD86 (clone 2331), CD19 (clone HA58), CD123 (clone 9F5), CD3 (clone SP34-2), CD163 (clone GHI/61), and CD11b (clone ICRF44). Additional antibodies targeting CD33 (clone P67.6), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1, clone 29E.2A3), CD11c (clone 3.9), CD24 (clone 3G8), CD45 (clone HI30), and CD16 (clone 3G8) were obtained from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA). The CD66abc (clone TET2) antibody was purchased from Miltenyi Biotec (Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). To exclude non-viable cells, a LIVE/DEAD™ viability dye (Invitrogen, NY, USA) was used prior to analysis. Following surface staining, intracellular Arginase (clone L307.4) staining was performed according to the BD Biosciences intracellular staining protocol (BD 340973). Briefly, cells were incubated with Permeabilization Solution 2 for 10 min, followed by intracellular staining for 30 min at room temperature (RT). Samples are washed with FACS wash buffer and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. For extended storage (up to 16 h), samples will be fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde to preserve cell integrity. The samples will be acquired on an LSR Fortessa™ and a BD FACSymphony™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and a minimum of 500,000 total events will be collected for each. Using FlowJo™ software version X.0.7 (Tree Star, Inc., Ashland, OR). Live/dead fixable near IR dead cell staining kit (Invitrogen L10119) used to exclude dead cells and identify live cells. Subsequent analysis will be performed on CD45⁺ leukocyte populations. T cells (CD3⁺ marker), B cells (CD19⁺ marker), and CD3⁻CD19⁻ cell populations will be identified using CD3 and CD19 markers. To characterize HLA-DR populations, CD3⁻CD19⁻ cells will be selected to exclude T and B lymphocytes. Within this double-negative population, HLA-DR⁺ and HLA-DR⁻ subsets will be distinguished using forward scatter (FSC) and the side scatter (SSC) -HLA-DR population. Monocytic populations will be identified from HLA-DR⁺ cells using CD14 and CD16 markers. Based on their expression profiles, classical monocytes will be defined as CD14⁺CD16⁻, intermediate monocytes as CD14⁺CD16⁺, and non-classical monocytes as CD14⁻CD16⁺. From the CD14⁻CD16⁻ population, myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs) and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) will be gated using CD11c and CD123 expression, respectively. mDCs are identified as CD11c⁺CD123⁻, while pDCs will be defined as CD11c⁻CD123⁺ events. From the HLA-DR⁻ population, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) will be identified using CD33 and CD11b markers. Polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) will be defined as HLA-DR⁻CD11b⁺CD33⁺+, and M-MDSCs will be characterized as HLA-DR⁻CD11b⁺CD33⁺+. Functional markers such as Arginase-1, CD24, CD86, and PD-L1 will be evaluated within all innate immune subsets. Arginase-1 serves as an enzymatic indicator of immunosuppressive potential, particularly in myeloid cells. CD24 and CD86 will be assessed as activation and co-stimulatory markers, respectively, whereas PD-L1 expression represented an immune checkpoint molecule. Together, these analyses will enable comprehensive characterization of immune subsets and their functional states in relation to HPV infection and mucosal immune regulation. Specific Data will be processed using FlowJo, enabling high-dimensional analysis (e.g., SPADE, viSNE) of immune subsets [47, 48].

Single-Cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) will be employed to identify the cellular components orchestrating immune responses within the oral cavity of PWH. Single-cell suspensions (700–1200 cells/µL; minimum volume 50 µL) will be prepared from oral biopsy samples obtained from 20 participants and processed within 4 h of collection. Samples with a cell viability > 90% will be used for downstream analysis. Single-cell encapsulation will be performed using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Kit (10X Genomics, PN-1000121) on the Chromium Controller. Resulting libraries will be generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol and sequenced on an Illumina platform using standard paired-end chemistry. Sequencing depth for the 3′ and 5′ gene expression libraries will exceed 50,000 read pairs per cell. All scRNA-seq assays will be performed at the Emory Integrated Genomics Core (EIGC).

Inflammatory protein markers

Oral rinse samples (at baseline, year 1 and 2 ) will be used to measure inflammatory markers (IL1ra, IL1β, IL6, IL6sr, TNF-α, sTNFR2, IL-10, CRP, interferon (IFN)-γ, β2 Microglobulin (B2M), Prostagladin E2 (PGE2), Macrophage Inflammatory Protein (MIP)-1α, and Matrix Metalloproteinase (MMP)-9) using multiplex assays (Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [48]. All samples will be run in duplicate, and mean intra- and inter-assay CVs will be calculated and expected to be < 10%.

Oral microbiome

Oral microbiome data will be generated at baseline, 1 year, and 2 years using both 16S rRNA gene and shotgun metagenomic sequencing conducted at the Emory Integrated Genomics Core (EIGC). DNA Extraction, 16S rRNA & shotgun sequencing: DNA will be extracted from oral samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, CA) according to the standardized protocol from the manufacturer. Microbial diversity will be characterized by DNA sequence variation of the 16S rRNA gene. Oral microbiome composition and specific bacterial abundances will be determined through bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing with amplification of the hyper-variable V3-4 region. Pooled amplicons will be sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq instrument using reversible terminator chain extension for 250 cycles from each side of the 16S DNA insert. For each run, we expect more than 10 million high-quality paired reads (Q score > 30 at each base) for an average number of reads of at least 50,000 per sample after control DNA removal. Also, 7Gb of shotgun data per sample will be generated to achieve > 20× coverage of each microbe above 1% relative abundance using established protocols on the Illumina HiSeq platform [49–51].

Processing of 16S rRNA sequence data

Sequence reads will be demultiplexed and paired-end reads will be joined, followed by DADA2 or Deblur to correct amplicon sequence errors and filter the chimeric sequences [52, 53]. High-quality sequence reads will then be processed using the DATA2 algorithm to infer exact amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and subsequently assigned taxonomy, using the pre-trained classifier on the Greengenes and Silva reference databases [54, 55]. We will derive a phylogenetic tree and further measure the composition of oral microbiome through QIIME 2 diversity plugin. We will make use of HMP databases and tools when analyzing data and submitting data, samples, and nucleic acids to public repositories.

Shotgun metagenomic data processing

Whole-genome post-sequencing analyses will be performed using state-of-the-art bioinformatics pipelines. Given the rapid evolution of metagenomic analysis software, we will review benchmarking studies and conduct internal performance evaluations before finalizing the analysis workflow to ensure the use of the most accurate and efficient tools available [56, 57]. Post-sequencing, standard preprocessing steps will include read quality trimming with Trimmomatic and human genome read removal using Mash aligner and knead (bioBakery package) [55, 58–60]. Low-abundance contaminant reads will be filtered out based on control data using a recently described method [61]. Sequence quality metrics will be assessed for all samples, and additional sequencing will be performed as needed to ensure adequate depth.

Taxonomic profiling will be performed using OneCodex and MetaPhlAn to characterize species level data. Strain-level variation within major species will be identified using StrainPhlAn to detect single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels). Alternative analyses may employ Latent Strain Analysis or direct mapping of shotgun reads to reference genomes using BWA or PhredEM [36, 62–66].

Quality assurance procedures

In addition to our standard quality control procedures for assays, for all assays we will submit blinded duplicate samples from 2 to 5% of patient visits to monitor test-retest reliability.

Data analysis

Data analysis will be conducted using R, SAS, and specialized bioinformatics software. Oral HPV infection will be classified as prevalent if detected at baseline and as incident if first identified following a negative baseline result. Clearance of HPV infection will be defined as at least one subsequent negative test. Both prevalent and re-detected infections (e.g., +, −, −, +) will be excluded from incident analyses, whereas all other newly detected infections will be classified as incident [5]. Persistent HPV infection will be defined as detection at two or more consecutive visits. Sensitivity analyses will be performed for intermittent HPV infection, characterized by alternating positive and negative results across visits. Various statistical models will be applied depending on the aims and goals. All analyses will be repeated after restricting the dataset to high-risk HPV types only (as defined by the TypeSeq assay) and validated using extended whole genome sequencing (eWGS) data to ensure robustness of the findings [21, 67, 68].

Power calculation

Given a 20% attrition rate based on our previous experience, our recruitment target of 500 PWH will reach an analyzable sample size of 400 patients. For our primary aim, we will set the significance level of 0.0001 for a Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons for about 500 taxa after removing taxa with over 80% zeros values based on our preliminary data. We anticipate 400 observations of delta (change from baseline) at one year and two years follow up. Based on intra-class correction of 0.44 for repeated measures from our previous study in HNC, we will have the design effect (DE) of 1.44 (= 1 + 0.44[2 − 1]). Thus, in a longitudinal analysis, 400 observations at each of time point (one and two years) will be equivalent to 556 (= 2 × 400/DE) independent samples.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study aims to advance understanding of the complex interplay between oral microbiota, periodontal disease, immune regulation, and oral HPV infection PWH. Persistent high-risk HPV infection is a well-established risk factor for oropharyngeal cancer, a major type of HNC, and PWH experience higher incidence, earlier onset, and worse outcomes compared to the general population [1–9]. This highlights the urgent need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying oral HPV infection in this vulnerable population. The oral microbiome represents a dynamic ecosystem that plays a critical role in immune homeostasis and inflammation [10, 13]. Dysbiosis in PWH may compromise mucosal immunity and promote a proinflammatory environment, predisposing individuals to HPV infection [16–18]. While prior studies have suggested links between oral microbiome composition and HNC risk, evidence in PWH remains limited, with most studies cross-sectional, small in scale, or lacking metagenomic resolution [69–73]. The COMP-HPV study’s longitudinal design, combined with 16S and shotgun metagenomic sequencing, will provide comprehensive insights into microbial diversity, functional pathways, and their associations with HPV prevalence, incidence, persistence, and clearance.

Periodontal disease, a common inflammatory condition in PWH, may act synergistically with microbial dysbiosis to increase susceptibility to oral HPV infection [17, 18]. By evaluating the oral microbiome in the context of periodontal status, this study will clarify whether microbial-mediated inflammation contributes to HPV acquisition and persistence, thereby informing potential strategies to prevent or manage oral HPV infection in PWH. Identification of key microbial taxa associated with both periodontal diseases and HPV infection could inform targeted preventive or therapeutic interventions, particularly for individuals already exposed to high-risk HPV types for whom vaccination offers limited protection [74].

Immune dysregulation represents another critical axis linking HIV, oral microbiome, and HPV infection [75–77]. Alterations in local mucosal immunity, including shifts in innate and adaptive immune cell populations and inflammatory mediators, likely contribute to both microbial dysbiosis and viral susceptibility. By employing high-dimensional flow cytometry and 10X single-cell RNA sequencing, we aim to generate a comprehensive atlas of oral immune responses and identify mechanistic pathways through which oral microbiota influence HPV infection. This approach may uncover biomarkers for risk stratification and potential therapeutic targets.

Social determinants of health, including neighbourhood environment, structural inequities, and socioeconomic status, are increasingly recognized as drivers of health disparities [25–27, 29, 30]. In this study, the integration of social determinants with microbiome and immunological data provides a novel opportunity to understand how environmental and structural factors intersect with biological mechanisms to influence oral HPV infection. This is particularly relevant given that a significant proportion of our cohort is African American and experiences socioeconomic disadvantage, emphasizing the need for interventions that address both biological and social risk factors.

In summary, COMP-HPV study integrates microbial, immunological, periodontal, and social determinants of health perspectives to elucidate factors contributing to oral HPV infection among PWH. By identifying microbial and immune correlates of HPV acquisition, persistence, and clearance, the findings will inform targeted interventions, biomarker discovery, and precision health strategies. Furthermore, evaluating the influence of social determinants offers a framework for public health interventions to reduce disparities and improve outcomes for women and men living with HIV. Collectively, this study provides a unique opportunity to translate mechanistic insights into actionable strategies for reducing oral HPV burden and associated cancer risk in a high-risk population.

Findings from this study are expected to provide critical insights into the oral microbiota that may inform preventive and therapeutic strategies for managing oral HPV infection. Identification of microbial taxa significantly associated with periodontal diseases could guide the development of targeted interventions to reduce periodontal pathology, which may, in turn, decrease oral HPV acquisition and persistence. Moreover, understanding the immunological mechanisms underlying oral HPV infection will shed light on the interplay between local immunity, inflammation, periodontal health, and HPV persistence, thereby facilitating the identification of potential biomarkers for risk stratification and early detection. Future studies could validate these biomarkers and advance personalized precision health approaches.

The study’s collection of oral mucosal bio samples also offers an opportunity to investigate whether changes in the oral microbiome influence host DNA methylation, potentially altering gene expression and driving immune activation and inflammatory responses. Additionally, results examining social determinants of health will provide valuable evidence to inform community-level public health interventions. Samples collected at each study visit will serve as a biobank, enabling future exploration.

Despite these anticipated contributions, several limitations warrant consideration. First, while oral rinse samples provide a broad representation of the oral microbiota and have been successfully employed in large HIV studies, site-specific variations exist [17]. To address this, we will also collect swab samples of the oral mucosa and keratinized gingiva according to NIH Human Microbiome Project guidelines, allowing for comparative analyses if necessary. Inter-rater variability in periodontal assessment will be mitigated through standardized training and quality control. Although this study focuses on PWH in a longitudinal framework, generalizability to other populations should be interpreted with caution. Second, variability in periodontal status assessments could arise from differences between evaluators (dentists versus dental hygienists) [78]. To mitigate this, standardized training sessions led by Dr. Reznik will be conducted before study initiation, and inter-rater reliability will be monitored during data analysis. Third, although the study is powered to detect significant microbial taxa in our primary aim, if statistical significance is not achieved, analyses will focus on the top 20% of taxa relevant to each aim. Finally, due to budget constraints, single-cell sequencing will be performed on a subset of samples; however, the banked biospecimens will allow for expanded analyses in future studies with additional funding.

Overall, this study integrates microbiome, immunological, and social determinants of health perspectives to advance understanding of oral HPV infection and periodontal disease in PWH, with implications for biomarker discovery, intervention development, and personalized healthcare strategies.
