A case of peripartum cardiomyopathy in dichorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy
Authors: Kanis Fatema, Zhi Ying Tan, Uduwarage Ranaweera
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPeripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) is an uncommon life-threatening condition that is characterized by heart failure with reduced ejection fraction during late pregnancy or within 5 months of postpartum in the absence of other causes of heart failure. Despite advances in managing PPCM, the pathophysiology of it is still poorly understood. This article reviews the diagnostic challenges and management of PPCM, specifically highlighting a rare presentation of PPCM characterized by oxygen desaturation alone.

INTRODUCTION

Cardiac disease is the leading indirect cause of maternal death in the UK and peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) accounts for about 4.9% of it [1]. The mortality rate of PPCM is as high as 50% [2], yet diagnosis is frequently delayed because the symptoms closely mirror those typically experienced during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Early diagnosis and initial treatment are vital to reduce mortality rate and to increase the chance of full recovery.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 23-year-old Caucasian woman, third gravida, nulliparous, at 36 weeks of dichorionic diamniotic twin pregnancy, presented with preterm prolabor rupture of membrane (PPROM). On admission, she was newly diagnosed with pre-eclampsia, sFLT-1/PIGF ratio of 77 (reference range: <38). During antenatal period, her babies' growth was satisfactory. She has no cardiovascular risk factors. An emergency caesarean section was performed due to fetal distress following labor induction, resulting in the delivery of healthy twin infants. Post-operatively, her oxygen saturation decreased to 90-91% on air, however, she denied dyspnea, cough, orthopnea, fever, chest pain and hemoptysis. Clinical examination revealed bilateral basal crackles in the lungs. Serial electrocardiograms (ECG) showed sinus rhythm. Chest X-ray (CXR) revealed pulmonary edema and cardiomegaly. CT pulmonary angiogram ruled out pulmonary embolism. Blood tests conducted the following day revealed troponin levels of 47 ng/L (reference range: <13ng/L) and a B-type natriuretic peptide level of 6965 pg/mL (reference range: <100 pg/mL). The cardiologist advised an urgent echocardiography which showed an ejection fraction of 41% (Figure 1), fractional shortening of 19% and impaired left ventricular diastolic function. Following the diagnosis of PPCM, she was managed by a multidisciplinary team in the cardiac monitoring unit. She was treated with Furosemide 40mg twice daily, Bromocriptine 2.5mg once daily, Ramipril 1.25mg once daily and Spironolactone 12.5mg once daily. A repeated CXR, ECG and echocardiogram reported that patient had improved to baseline. She was counselled about the high recurrence rate of PPCM in subsequent pregnancies and provided long-acting reversible contraception on discharge. Her 4-week follow-up echocardiogram was normal.

DISCUSSION

Typically, women with PPCM exhibit symptoms of congestion such as dyspnea on exertion, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and lower extremity edema [2]. However, in this case, the diagnosis was made based solely on the presence of oxygen desaturation.

PPCM is a dilated cardiomyopathy defined as systolic cardiac failure with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <45% or fractional shortening <30%, or both in the last month of pregnancy or within five months of delivery. The true incidence is uncertain in the UK and the etiology remains unknown. The mortality rate of PPCM is as high as 50%. The main risk factor is being of black African origin, other risks include pre-eclampsia, advanced maternal age (35+), and multiple pregnancies.

The etiology of PPCM is not fully understood, but it has been suggested that factors like dilated cardiomyopathy, cardiovascular stress during pregnancy, and myocarditis may contribute to its development [3]. The recent hypothesis proposes that pregnancy-related vascular stress may trigger heart damage in women with a genetic or biological predisposition, as evidenced by familial clustering of PPCM cases and shared genetic mutations with dilated cardiomyopathy. Additionally, elevated levels of anti-angiogenic “vasoinhibin” prolactin fragments, driven by oxidative stress in the heart, are believed to cause endothelial and cardiomyocyte damage; inhibition of prolactin has shown promise in reducing these effects e.g. Bromocriptine. The anti-angiogenic protein sFlt-1, associated with hypertensive pregnancy disorders, may further contribute to PPCM by compromising vascular integrity and increasing myocardial stress, especially postpartum. Autoantibodies against cardiac proteins are also observed in some PPCM cases, though their direct role in the disease remains uncertain [1].

The management of PPCM usually involves conventional treatment for congestive heart failure. Fluid restriction is the mainstay of treatment whilst loop diuretics can be considered to relief symptoms of congestion. The ESC guidelines have also advised anticoagulation in patients with PPCM and LVEF ≤35% and in those who have received Bromocriptine as thromboembolism is a common complication of PPCM. There have been reports of the use of Levosimedan in unresponsive patients [3]. Levosimendan is a calcium sensitizing agent rather than a catecholamine and may thus lead to superior outcomes when used for inotropic support in PPCM.

50% of women eventually recover with the normalization of their ejection fraction. Patients would usually be warned about the high risk of relapse in subsequent pregnancies, which persisted long term in 20-50% of women [2].

CONCLUSION

To reduce mortality and morbidity rates, clinicians should include peripartum cardiomyopathy as a differential diagnosis in peripartum patients presenting with oxygen desaturation, ensuring timely medical intervention.
