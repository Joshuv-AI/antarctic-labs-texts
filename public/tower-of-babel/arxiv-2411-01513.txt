Abstract

We introduce a novel ultra-broadband ultraviolet and visible frequency comb light source covering more than
240\,$\mathrm{T}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
by resonant dispersive wave emission in a gas-filled hollow-core fiber waveguide. The light source allows tuning from
\approx 340\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
to
465\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
(
645\,$\mathrm{T}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
to
\approx 885\,$\mathrm{T}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
) with conversion efficiencies of
1.5\,\%
. Ultra-broadband absorption spectroscopy is demonstrated by studying nitrogen dioxide, a molecular species of major atmospheric relevance strongly absorbing across the ultraviolet and visible spectral region. We show that the coherence of the 80 MHz ytterbium fiber-based frequency comb seeding the frequency up-conversion process is conserved, paving the way toward further ultra-broadband (dual) comb spectroscopy across the ultraviolet/visible range.

Introduction

The ultraviolet (UV) spectral region is of high interest in various disciplines from fundamental scientific questions
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
to atmospheric sciences
[
6
,
7
]
and food science
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. For these studies, various light sources are used, depending on the requirements of the application. Arc lamps offer broad spectral coverage but no coherence and low brightness
[
12
]
. Synchrotrons provide high brightness, tuneability, and coherence up to MHz repetition rates with ps time resolution but their operation is expensive and beam time access is limited
[
13
,
14
]
. Alternatively, nonlinear up-conversion of laser radiation can coherently access the UV range with brightness and temporal resolution dependent on the driving laser. However, these sources often lack either tunability or bandwidth and most realizations are limited to a few
100\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
repetition rates
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. Supercontinuum generation in optical fibers provides a viable route for obtaining broadband UV radiation
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
but the multi-octave spectral coverage comes at the cost of low spectral power.

Advances in hollow-core fiber technology have enabled tunable, bright, and broadband radiation at MHz repetition rates. By engineering the waveguide dispersion, resonant dispersive waves (RDW) can efficiently generate deep ultraviolet (DUV)
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
and near ultraviolet (NUV)
[
26
,
27
]
radiation. Recently, the favorable temporal structure (
<3\,$\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}$
)
[
28
,
29
]
and tunability (
236\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
to
377\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
) of RDW emission were used in time-resolved experiments at kHz repetition rates to perform photoelectron imaging studies
[
2
]
and transient absorption spectroscopy
[
3
]
; a beamline for attochemistry studies is under construction
[
4
]
.

In this study, the tunability of RDW emission is used to generate UV and visible (VIS) radiation at repetition rates of
5\,$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
. The reliability and stability of the ultra-broadband frequency comb source are proven by conducting spectroscopic measurements across the full bandwidth.

\mathrm{NO_{2}}
is used as a spectroscopic prototype due to its broadband, feature-rich absorption response across the UV and VIS regions. It is an atmospheric trace gas of considerable significance, given its role in multiple chemical reactions occurring in the troposphere. Not only is the formation of ozone
[
30
]
, aerosols
[
31
]
, and PM2.5 directly linked to
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
concentration, but
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
also impacts human health and our surrounding ecosystem. Because one of the two main
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
sources is the internal combustion engine of cars, elevated concentrations are particularly observed in urban air during the morning and evening hours, coinciding with traffic rush hours
[
32
,
33
]
. The second main source of
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
is temperature control in buildings, which leads to higher concentrations in winter. Since long-term exposure has been linked to asthma
[
34
]
and high concentrations in a short period also have significant health effects, air quality monitoring is vital. The light source developed here allows to measure trace gases across a broad VIS and NUV spectrum, as demonstrated with
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
, opening the path toward multi-species detection.

2
Experimental Methods

2.1
Experimental setup

A pulse-picking Yb:fiber frequency comb system with a repetition rate adjustable between
250\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
and
80\,$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
delivers
250\,$\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}$
pulses with up to
8\,$\mu\mathrm{J}$
pulse energy. The pulses are compressed to about
60\,$\mathrm{f}\mathrm{s}$
by employing a Herriott-type multipass cell with
\mathrm{SiO_{2}}
as a nonlinear medium, similar in design to
[
35
]
. After the multipass cell, beam stabilization is used to minimize pointing variations. The remaining chirp of the broadened pulses is reduced with chirped mirrors (UltraFast Innovations). A half-wave plate and a thin film polarizer attenuate the pulse energy to about
1\,$\mu\mathrm{J}$
for optimum frequency conversion. The comb is launched into a gas-filled single-ring hollow-core photonic crystal fiber (SR HC PCF) for generating NUV radiation via RDW emission. The fiber consists of a core with a diameter of
(26.1\pm 1.9)\,$\mu\mathrm{m}$
(slightly elliptical) surrounded by a ring of six capillaries with diameters of
(11.6\pm 0.9)\,$\mu\mathrm{m}$
and a wall thickness of around
250\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
(
Figure 1
b). In the visible and near-infrared, the evacuated waveguide exhibits anomalous dispersion, which can be tailored by changing the pressure of the filling gas. In this study, the
11\,$\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
long fiber is filled with
20-40\,$\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}$
of argon. The small fiber core diameter allows for the generation of NUV radiation using
1030\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
pulses carrying energies as low as
(0.9-1.2)\,$\mu\mathrm{J}$
. The light exits the cell through an
\mathrm{MgF_{2}}
window followed by a second
\mathrm{MgF_{2}}
window at
45^{\circ}
used as a beam splitter. The transmitted beam is sent to a rotating grating spectrometer (APE WaveScan) to acquire reference spectra that account for changes in the spectrum due to coupling efficiency fluctuations. The reflected beam passes through the sample cell (
l=34\,$\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
) five times to increase the interaction path length to
170\,$\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}$
. The resulting light is coupled into a multimode fiber toward an optical spectrum analyzer (Yokogawa AQ6373E), offering
0.02\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
spectral resolution from
350
to
1100\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
.

Figure 1:

The output spectrum is monitored using a rotating grating spectrometer. b) Scanning electron microscope cross-section image of the full fiber (left) and the core structure (right). c) The beam traverses a mixture of
\mathrm{N_{2}}
and
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
in the sample cell with five passes. The transmitted light is detected with an optical spectrum analyzer.

2.2
Measurement protocol and referencing

The broad RDW is tuned to six different central wavelengths within the region of interest to determine the absorption across a broad wavelength range. Spectra are acquired in
39\,$\mathrm{s}$
each over
10\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
segments with
0.02\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
resolution. Each segment is an average of twenty spectra. The measurement sequence is repeated as follows to record the sample and reference spectrum as close in time to each other as possible: 1) measuring a reference spectrum with
10\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
bandwidth, 2) filling the cell with the sample gas, 3) measuring a
10\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
broad sample spectrum, 4) measuring a sample spectrum of a second adjacent
10\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
segment, 5) evacuating the cell, 6) measuring the reference spectrum of the corresponding adjacent
10\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
segment.
Despite evacuating and refilling the sample cell between measurements, sample consistency is ensured because the gas used is a standard commercial mixture of originally
500\,$\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{m}$

\mathrm{NO_{2}}
diluted by
\mathrm{N_{2}}
. The partial pressure in the cell is
(800\pm 10)\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}$
for all measurements.

A simultaneous reference spectrum is acquired with a rotating grating spectrometer. The final absorbance is calculated by
A=\log_{10}(I_{\mathrm{ref}}/I_{\mathrm{samp}})
, with
I_{\mathrm{ref}}=I_{\mathrm{OSA,ref}}/I_{\mathrm{RotGrat}}
and
I_{\mathrm{samp}}=I_{\mathrm{OSA,samp}}/I_{\mathrm{RotGrat}}
, accounting for the fluctuations in the fiber coupling efficiency by normalizing to the reference
I_{\mathrm{RotGrat}}
. A least squares fit of the absorbance computed from literature absorption cross-section data
[
36
]
to the experimental data determines the
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
concentration in the sample cell, with the concentration
c
as a free variable.

2.3
Comb stability characterization

To show that the frequency comb is conserved, a
f-2f
interferometer is implemented. The experimental setup uses a collinear geometry
[
37
,
38
]
. For this experiment, the amplifier operates at
10\,$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
repetition rate. The argon pressure within the fiber is set to
24\,$\mathrm{b}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{r}$
to generate an RDW at a central wavelength of
330\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
. The
f-2f
interferometry is performed between the frequency-doubled infrared driving field (
1030\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
) and the green tail (
515\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
) of the continuum. When these two combs are superimposed, a beat signal is generated with the frequency of the carrier-envelope-offset frequency (
f_{\mathrm{CEO}}
)
[
39
]
. For this experiment, the collimated light behind the fiber is refocused into an LBO crystal (type I phase matching to the
1030\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
fundamental beam) to generate its second harmonic. The two cross-polarized combs at
2f
from fiber and LBO are mixed with a half-wave plate and a polarizing beamsplitter cube. The green part of the spectrum is selected using a transmission grating and a slit and focused onto an amplified Si photodiode (Thorlabs PDA10A2). The beat notes are acquired using an electronic spectrum analyzer without any additional amplification.

The spectroscopic capabilities of the tunable and broadband light source are demonstrated by investigating the absorption of
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
. This gas features a large set of small absorption features across the NUV on top of a pronounced trend.
The measured absorbance of
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
is shown in red in
Figure 3
. The measured spectra were multiplied by a varying factor to correct the absorbance to the mean observed sample concentration of
(235\pm 59)\,$\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{m}$
of
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
. The factors and raw data are provided in Supplement 1. The updated Edlén formula
[
43
]
was used to convert the x-axis to vacuum wavelengths. In
Figure 3

[
36
]
for comparison. The literature data was acquired using an Xe arc lamp and a Fourier transform spectrometer.

The presented measurement matches almost all absorption features of
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
. The deviation at
370\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
can be explained by an observed drift of the laser pointing, which changed the spectrometer coupling and led to a baseline change. In
Figure 3
b, a close look at the feature aligned with the
357\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
RDW spectrum displays the high-resolution capabilities of the technique. In purple, the residual shows a standard deviation of
0.0087
. In
Figure 3
c, the absorbance is acquired with the tail of the RDW spectrum. In this case, the RDW has its maximum at
390\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
, far from the
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
features. Features like the double peak at
448\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
are still resolved, even with the spectral intensity being more than an order of magnitude lower. The standard deviation of the residual is approximately three times higher than the value observed in
Figure 3
b. The noise can be attributed to the emergence of spurious features, such as the modulation observed around
442\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$

The generated spectrum is less smooth in this region due to the higher soliton order of the driving pulses and the spectral anti-crossing between the guided mode and the core-wall resonances.

3.3
Comb structure of generated spectrum

The RDW is driven with a frequency comb. To confirm that the comb structure is conserved during the up-conversion,
f-2f
interferometry is used. The resulting beat signal is shown in
Figure 4
. To verify that the observed beat is caused by
f_{\mathrm{CEO}}
, the cavity dispersion is changed by setting a different oscillator pump current, leading to the expected shift of the beat frequency (
219\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
(blue) vs.
220\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{A}$
(green)). The seed oscillator is specified to be below
200\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$

f_{\mathrm{CEO}}
beat linewidth. Based on the observed
f_{\mathrm{CEO}}
beatnote width of
100\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
at
-20\,$\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}$
, an upper boundary for the increase of linewidth of
100\,$\mathrm{k}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
can be concluded. It should be noted that this figure represents the complete chain from oscillator to pulse picking, amplification, pulse compression, and broadening. These stages are not fully optimized yet for phase-locked operation and are known to introduce various amounts of phase noise
[
37
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
.

The stability of the source was ensured by selecting the experimental parameters and the species of the gas filling the fiber to minimize its ionization and thus avoid long-lived effects, which can impair the pulse-to-pulse stability
[
24
,
47
]
. Numerical simulations of the nonlinear propagation of the pulses in the HC-PCF based on the code described in
[
48
]
and using the Perelomov, Popov, Terent’ev ionization rate (as described in
[
49
]
) reveal that for the selected parameters the peak-free electron density remains below
10^{12}\,$\mathrm{e}\mathrm{l}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{t}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{/}{cm}^{3}$
, which is sufficiently low to avoid significant ionization-induced thermal effects
[
47
]
.
In a similar study involving RDW frequency conversion with an
f_{\mathrm{CEO}}
stabilized laser system, the
f_{\mathrm{CEO}}
variations below
240\,$\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
were characterized and it was concluded that the phase variation is about
330\,$\mathrm{m}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{d}$
RMS over
2.5
minutes
[
50
]
. The results of the present study indicate that the comb stability is also not impacted by high-frequency noise.

4
Conclusion

In this work, a broadband tunable laser frequency comb source is demonstrated with a repetition rate of
5\,$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
, covering more than
115\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
(
240\,$\mathrm{T}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
) across the UV/VIS spectrum. We utilized the broadband source to measure the detailed absorption of
\mathrm{NO_{2}}
from
350\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
to
465\,$\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}$
. The sample concentration was determined to be
(235\pm 59)\,$\mathrm{p}\mathrm{p}\mathrm{m}$
.
Additionally, our measurements show that the comb structure is conserved, even at a repetition rate of
10\,$\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}$
.
These results make ultra-broadband frequency comb spectroscopy in the UV/VIS attainable. A second laser system to perform comparable broadband measurements with the additional benefits of dual-comb spectroscopy is currently under construction. The broad spectral coverage will enable multi-species trace gas sensing throughout the NUV. Another application could be the tracking of reaction pathways. With the high photon flux, free space trace gas measurement campaigns over km path lengths, usually challenging due to high optical losses, come within reach.
