Regulation of antimicrobial peptides in Hermetia illucens in response to fungal exposure
Authors: Neta Herman, Tzach Vitenberg, Zvi Hayouka, Itai Opatovsky
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The black soldier fly (Hermetia illucens) is important for antimicrobial peptide (AMP) research due to its exposure to diverse microorganisms. However, the impact of different fungi on AMP abundance in H. illucens remains unexplored. We studied the induction of AMP expression under basal conditions and with three fungi: non-pathogenic Candida tropicalis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and pathogenic Beauveria bassiana, using RNA-sequencing and liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry. Under naive conditions, most AMPs belonged to the lysozyme, cecropin, and defensin classes, with defensins most abundant. We demonstrate that dietary supplementation with fungi is sufficient to induce AMP expression in H. illucens. However, exposure to C. tropicalis and B. bassiana also caused downregulation of certain AMPs, suggesting that these fungi may suppress or modulate the host immune response to aid in their survival and colonization. Evidently, S. cerevisiae and B. bassiana trigger similar AMP pathways, whereas C. tropicalis elicits a distinct reaction with upregulation of defensins and cecropins. Lysozymes were upregulated by S. cerevisiae and B. bassiana, but downregulated by C. tropicalis, potentially facilitating fungal survival in the larval gut. Understanding these mechanisms opens possibilities for leveraging AMPs to combat C. tropicalis, which is implicated in human diseases.

Introduction

The prolonged use of antibiotics contributes to the emergence and spread of drug-resistant microorganisms1. Antibiotic resistance poses a significant challenge in healthcare, leading to the urgency to explore alternative molecules that are less susceptible to bacterial resistance1–3. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) emerge as promising candidates for the next generation of antibiotics, as well as being innate immune modulators1–3.

AMPs are evolutionary conserved short proteins, naturally produced by various organisms, and representing the first line of defense against fungi, viruses and bacteria4,5. They are commonly classified according to their sources, their secondary structure, their biosynthesis, and their mechanism of action3. Despite these significant variations, AMPs share certain common features. These molecules are typically short in length (< 100 amino acids), possess a positive net charge, include a substantial proportion of hydrophobic residues and usually have an amphipathic structure, allowing them to remain soluble in aqueous environments1.

AMPs have various applications across diverse fields, including agriculture, the food industry, and medicine1–3. Their positive charge and amphiphilic nature facilitate their accumulation on polyanionic surfaces of microbial cells, including acidic polymers such as lipopolysaccharide in Gram-negative bacteria and wall-associated teichoic acids in Gram-positive bacteria2,5. Once inserted into the membrane, AMPs can disrupt the physical integrity of the lipid bilayer or translocate across the membrane to target internal components during inhibiting processes such as nucleic acid biosynthesis, protein biosynthesis, protease activity, and cell division2,5.

AMPs produced by insects have attracted great interest as they are one of the main reasons for the survival and adaptability of insects to their environment6,7. Specifically, AMPs produced by Hermetia illucens (the black soldier fly), which completes its life cycle on decaying organic matter, exposing it to a diverse microbial environment7, are of particular interest. Insects primarily produce AMPs in their fat bodies and hemocytes, which are then secreted into the hemolymph8. In H. illucens, 57 AMPs have been identified from various classes such as defensins, lysozymes, cecropins, attacins, diptericins, and knottin-like AMPs, based on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) predictions and bioinformatic analysis9.10.

AMPs can be constitutively expressed or influenced by encounters with microorganisms or different diets11,12. The larvae of H. illucens demonstrate a notably broad range of AMPs, with a significant portion being triggered by the consumption of a bacteria-rich diet11. A recent study identified 33 AMPs in the H. illucens hemolymph through mass spectrometry analysis (MS), with 13 of them showing stimulation in response to bacterial challenges12.

However, the impact of fungi on AMP expression has not been investigated. This is particularly interesting because the gut of H. illucens larvae contains a yeast-like fungal community, with Candida being the dominant genus13,14. Candida tropicalis is one of the most common yeasts in the environment15. Microbiota analyses in various animals have detected C. tropicalis, which is also associated with a variety of diseases15. Yet, H. illucens larvae fed with Candida showed changes in metabolic pathways, increased larval body weight, and improved nutrient profile16.

Beauveria bassiana isa saprobic fungus found naturally in soil worldwide, exhibiting parasitic behavior towards various arthropod species, placing it in the category of entomopathogenic fungi17. Farmers commonly use B. bassianato control insect pests in agricultural settings17. Recent investigations into the effects of the biocontrol agent B. bassiana on H. illucenshave revealed reductions in both larval and adult body weight, decreased adult emergence, and metabolic alterations17,18. High doses of B. bassianaon adult flies have resulted in high mortality and reduced egg count19.

In this study, we investigated the impact of C. tropicalis, isolated from the gut of H. illucens larvae, on the abundance of AMPs. For comparison, we also examined the effect of baker’s yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, which is not native to the H. illucens gut. We also studied the impact of the entomopathogenic fungi B. bassiana on AMP abundance in H. illucens. To test these effects we added the fungi to the larvae’s food to elicit a more natural response and examine its interaction with the environment, as opposed to other experiments that used oral administration of microorganisms with a Hamilton syringe.

We studied AMP production by H. illucens larvae under basal conditions and after exposure to fungi, using RNA-seq and Mass Spectrometry (MS) analysis. As most current knowledge about H. illucens AMP genes derives from RNA-seq data, MS analysis will fill the gap by providing insights into AMP production at the protein level.

We hypothesize that AMPs will be upregulated in response to any of the fungal treatments, as these would activate the larvae’s immune system. Given that C. tropicalis is native to the larvae’s gut, and B. bassiana is an entomopathogenic fungus, we expected to observe distinct immune responses, potentially reflected in the type or amount of AMP changes. This study explores the previously unexamined effects of fungal interactions on AMP expression in H. illucens, providing important new insights.

Results

AMP abundance in H. illucens larvae under basal conditions

From the RNA-seq data, we identified 86 AMP-expressed genes: 40 (46%) were classified as lysozyme, 23 as cecropin, 22 as defensin, and 1 as attacin (Fig. 1a). AMPs from the defensin class were the most highly quantified in both datasets. Effect of pathogenic and non-pathogenic fungi on mRNA expression of AMPs in H. illucens larvae

To determine how pathogenic and non-pathogenic fungi affect the mRNA expression levels of AMP genes in H. illucens, we raised H. illucens larvae on a standard diet supplemented with different fungi: non-pathogenic Candida tropicalis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, pathogenic Beauveria bassiana, and a control group (no fungi added). Four days after the fungal treatment we extracted RNA from the fat bodies and performed RNA-seq analysis.

We conducted differential expression analysis for the different treatments relative to the control using all expressed genes and then focused specifically on AMP-expressed genes in subsequent analyses. We visualized the data for AMP genes using volcano plots, where the P-values (in -log10 scale) are plotted against the gene expression fold changes (in log2 scale) relative to the control (Figs. 2a-c). We then focused on genes that met the cut-off criteria of P < 0.05 and an absolute fold-change > 1.5 compared to the control as previously done by others20–22. We then examined the overlap between the AMP genes that were upregulated and downregulated across the different treatments. Interestingly, among the upregulated AMP genes (Fig. 2d), all genes upregulated in the B. bassiana treatment were also upregulated in the S. cerevisiae treatment (Fisher’s exact test: P < 0.001), but the reverse was not true. The genes upregulated in the C. tropicalis treatment had minimal overlap with those in the S. cerevisiae treatment and no overlap with the B. bassiana treatment. For the downregulated genes (Fig. 2e), one AMP gene was present in both the B. bassiana and S. cerevisiae treatments, and another AMP gene was present in both the C. tropicalis and S. cerevisiae treatments. Similar to the upregulated genes, there was no overlap in downregulated genes between the C. tropicalis and B. bassiana treatments.

We classified the upregulated and downregulated genes into different AMP classes (Fig. 2f). In the S. cerevisiae and B. bassiana treatments, AMPs from the Lysozyme class were upregulated (17 and 6 genes, respectively), while in the C. tropicalis treatment, they were mostly downregulated. Conversely, in the C. tropicalis treatment, the upregulated AMP genes belonged primarily to the Defensin and Cecropin classes.

The control group clustered with the C. tropicalis treatment, whereas B. bassiana and S. cerevisiae treatments clustered together, emphasizing the similarity in AMP response following these two treatments (Fig. 3a). We observed a general increase in the expression levels of lysozyme AMPs in the S. cerevisiae treatment compared to the control. Five of these AMPs were significantly differentially expressed: HI_AMP36-lysozyme c-1-like G3, HI_AMP36-lysozyme c-1-like G4, HI_AMP34-lysozyme 1-like G1, HI_AMP34-lysozyme 1-like G2, and HI_AMP1003-lysozyme 2-like (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S2a).

In the B. bassiana treatment, we also noted an overall elevation in the expression levels of lysozyme AMPs, although this increase was less pronounced than in the S. cerevisiae treatment. One AMP, HI_AMP34-lysozyme 1-like G1, was significantly differentially expressed (Figs. 3a and S2a). Additionally, in the B. bassiana treatment, most cecropin and defensin AMPs showed a decrease in expression levels compared to the control. Two Cecropin AMPs, HI_AMP9-cecropin-like peptide 1 G4 and HI_AMP9-cecropin-like peptide 1 G1, were significantly differentially expressed (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S2a).

In the C. tropicalis treatment, we observed a general decrease in lysozyme AMPs and an increase in cecropin and defensin AMPs. The gene HI_AMP8-cecropin-like peptide 1 G3 was significantly differentially expressed (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. S2a).

To assess the AMP response at the protein level following fungal treatments, we conducted LC–MS/MS analysis on hemolymph collected three days after treatment. We generated a heatmap showing the quantification of all identified AMP protein groups categorized by their respective classes across the different treatments (Fig. 3b). Protein groups that changed significantly relative to the control (One way ANOVA; FDR < 0.1) are marked with asterisks (Fig. 3b) and can be visualized in greater detail in Supplementary Fig. S2b.

The control group clustered most closely with the S. cerevisiae treatment, followed by C. tropicalis, and finally B. bassiana (Fig. 3b). Compared to the RNA-seq data, a distinct pattern in AMP class responses was particularly evident in the B. bassiana treatment. In this treatment, lysozyme AMP protein groups generally showed a decrease in abundance relative to the control. Three lysozyme AMPs were significantly different: HI_AMP33-lysozyme-like, HI_AMP35-lysozyme 1-like, and HI_AMP36-lysozyme c-1-like (Fig. 3b and S2b). Conversely, most Defensin AMP protein groups displayed an increase in abundance, with one significantly different protein: HI_AMP17-defensin (Figs. 3b and S2b).

Discussion

This study presents a detailed analysis of AMPs in H. illucens larvae under both basal and fungal treatment conditions, utilizing RNA-seq and LC–MS/MS analyses. Under basal conditions, we identified 86 AMP genes using RNA-seq and confirmed 38 AMP protein groups using LC–MS/MS analysis. The differences observed between the RNA-seq data and LC–MS/MS data can largely be attributed to AMPs detected in the RNA-seq analysis but not identified in the LC–MS/MS data. Most of these AMP genes, only recognized in the RNA-seq data, belong to the lysozyme class. Consequently, in the RNA-seq analysis, lysozymes were the most represented AMP type, while in the LC–MS/MS data, defensins were the most abundant.

A possible reason for the lower identification of lysozyme-class AMPs in the LC–MS/MS data could be related to the peptide extraction method used prior to the LC–MS/MS experiment. We focused on extracting peptides to obtain proteins with lower molecular weights. The lysozyme-class AMPs have a higher molecular weight than other AMP classes9, possibly leading to their reduced recognition and detection in the LC–MS/MS analysis.

Another limitation of the LC–MS/MS data is that proteins that cannot be unambiguously identified by unique peptides—due to having only shared peptides—are grouped into a single protein group and quantified together. Consequently, several AMPs that were identified as distinct genes in the RNA-seq data were grouped together by LC–MS/MS because of the high similarity of their protein sequences.

Despite these limitations, we found a high degree of similarity between the highly quantified AMPs identified in the LC–MS/MS and RNA-seq data. Defensins emerged as the most expressed AMPs in both datasets, consistent with their crucial role in the innate immune system of insects against a wide range of pathogens23.

Previous studies have typically stimulated the production of AMPs in H. illucens through bacterial injection24,25. In this study, we demonstrate that supplementing the diet with fungi is sufficient to induce AMP expression. Our RNA-seq data showed that both yeast-like fungi (S. cerevisiae and C. tropicalis) triggered a significant upregulation of AMP genes.

In the RNA-seq data, the C. tropicalis and B. bassiana treatments also exhibited notable downregulation of other AMP genes compared to the S. cerevisiae response. This downregulation suggests an immune suppression or modulation mechanism that these fungi might employ to evade the host immune response or adapt to the host environment.

It has previously been suggested that bacteria may actively suppress AMP expression in host cells to resist AMP-mediated killing26–28. For example, while several bacterial species induce the expression of human β-defensin-2 in skin epithelial cells, the common skin pathogen Streptococcus pyogenes is a weak inducer of this AMP. Additionally, S. pyogenesis significantly more susceptible to killing by β-defensin-2 compared to other bacteria27. This mechanism is thought to play an important role in S. pyogenesability to evade innate defenses and cause skin disease29. Similarly, in H. illucens, knockdown of the immune genes Duox and TLR3 reduced the expression of AMPs, resulting in higher overall bacterial load but a decline in the abundance of native gut bacteria in the larvae30. Additionally, H. illucens larvae exposed to high bacterial loads also exhibited downregulation of several AMP genes11.

For B. bassiana, the negative effects on H. illucens larvae might trigger an immune depression response, via reduced AMP expression to conserve resources or direct interference with the immune signaling pathways. In the case of C. tropicalis, the downregulation of AMPs might facilitate its survival and colonization within the larval gut. This may involve the downregulation of specific AMPs that are potentially harmful to the yeast, allowing for a symbiotic or less antagonistic interaction within the gut environment. Further research examining the sensitivity of these fungi to the specific AMPs that were downregulated will help support this hypothesis. Gaining insight into the molecular mechanisms behind fungal resistance to AMPs is essential.

The overlap in upregulated AMPs between S. cerevisiae and B. bassiana and their clustering together in the RNA-seq-based heatmap indicate a shared or similar pathway of immune response against these fungi. In contrast, the C. tropicalis treatment resulted in a more distinct immune response. This difference could be due to the differing responses to native and non-native fungi in the H. illucens gut.

Lysozyme AMPs were predominantly upregulated in response to S. cerevisiae and B. bassiana. Although lysozymes are mainly known for breaking down bacterial cell walls, they also possess antifungal activity31.32. Specifically, the lytic action of lysozyme on Candidahas been reported several times33–35. Interestingly, the C. tropicalis treatment led to the downregulation of lysozymes, possibly indicating an adaptation mechanism that reduces immune pressure on the yeast.

Both defensins and cecropins were upregulated in response to C. tropicalis. Conversely, in the B. bassiana treatment, defensins and cecropins tended to be downregulated, emphasizing the contrasting immune responses toward these two fungi. Defensins are known for their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity36,37 and cecropins are effective against both bacteria and fungi23. These results suggest that C. tropicalis can survive in the H. illucens larval gut with the upregulation of defensins and cecropins. In contrast, B. bassiana may need to suppress the expression of these AMPs to survive. Further experiments are needed to test this suggestion.

In the LC–MS/MS results, the pattern of AMP class responses to the different treatments was less clear and not always consistent with the RNA-seq data. Specifically, we observed a decrease in lysozyme protein levels in the B. bassiana treatment, despite a general upregulation of lysozyme mRNA. This discrepancy could be explained by the fact that most of the upregulated lysozyme AMP genes following B. bassiana treatment were detected only in the RNA-seq data and not in the LC–MS/MS. The majority of lysozymes that showed a decrease in protein levels after B. bassiana treatment were not part of the general lysozyme upregulation observed in the RNA-seq data.

RNA-seq samples were collected four days after the fungal treatment, while LC–MS/MS samples were taken three days after the treatment. These temporal differences could affect the comparability of the data. Collecting RNA-seq samples earlier than LC–MS/MS sample collection could have yielded more comprehensive insights.

Additionally, post-transcriptional regulation, post-translational regulation and protein degradation can significantly alter the abundance and function of proteins, independent of mRNA levels38–40. Candida albicans has been shown to inactivate the His5 AMP through the hydrolytic action of secreted aspartic proteases (Saps), as part of its resistance mechanism41. This could also account for the discrepancies between the RNA-seq and LC–MS/MS results, potentially explaining the decrease in specific AMP protein levels following fungal treatment.

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of AMP production in H. illucens larvae under basal conditions and following exposure to various fungi. The findings highlight the dynamic nature of AMP responses in these larvae, influenced by both native and pathogenic microbial interactions. The differential AMP expression and production in response to fungal treatments underscore the complexity of the immune strategies employed by H. illucens. Understanding these mechanisms opens new avenues for exploring the use of AMPs in combating different fungi, specifically C. tropicalis, which cause human diseases. Future studies should explore the mechanistic pathways regulating AMP expression and action in H. illucens. Investigating the interactions between AMPs and other immune components in H. illucens could provide a comprehensive understanding of their immune defense strategies.

Methods

Hermetia illucens rearing

Hermetia illucens larvae were purchased from FreezEm Nachshonim, Israel. The larvae were reared on a sterile standard Gainesville diet comprising 30% alfalfa, 50% wheat bran, and 20% corn42 in a rearing chamber (27 ± 1.0°C, RH 70 ± 5%, and D/L 12/12 photoperiod).

Fungal culture

Candida tropicalis, previously isolated from the gut of H. illucens larvae16 (stored at −80°C in a glycerol stock) and commercial Saccharomyces cerevisiae commonly used for baking were utilized. The fungi were cultured in 10-ml aerobic tubes containing 6 ml yeast extract peptone dextrose broth (Difco™ YPD broth, Becton Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD USA) supplemented with the antibiotic chloramphenicol. These cultures were incubated at 30°C with shaking. After an initial incubation period of 3 h, the cultures were diluted to a final volume of 1 L and remained incubated under the same conditions for an additional 72 h. Finally, the fungal cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C.

Fungal treatment

The larvae were transferred to a sterile standard diet to reverse the growth pause induced by FreezEm technology in the food. Ten days after transferring the larvae to the sterile standard diet, they were divided into four distinct treatment groups: control, Beauveria bassiana, C. tropicalis, and S. cerevisiae. There were five biological replicates for each treatment, with each replicate comprising 50 larvae in 25 g standard Gainesville diet. The treatments were administered as follows:

1) Control (no fungi added) – 50 ml sterile water.

2) B. bassiana– commercial strain GHA-11.3% (Rimi, Petah Tikva, Israel). Fungal spore quantification was performed using a hemocytometer, and a concentration of 1077 cells/ml was selected (10 ml commercial strain and 40 ml water) as previously described18,19.

3) C. tropicalis – 1 g yeast pellet in 50 ml sterile water.

4) S. cerevisiae – 1 g yeast pellet in 50 ml sterile water.

RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

Four days after fungal treatment, five 5th instar larvae from each biological replicate (n = 4) were dissected and the fat body was collected into an Eppendorf tube on dry ice. The RNA was then extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Hybrid-R™-GeneAll, Seoul, Korea) and DNase removal was performed using a Clean-Up RNA concentrator kit (A&A BIOTECHNOLOGY, Gdansk, Poland). The libraries were then constructed using a + + NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit and a NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA USA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X instrument (2 × 150 base sequencing).

Analysis of RNA‐seq data

To analyze the RNA-seq data, we used the nf-core/rnaseq bioinformatics pipeline, version 3.14.043. The STAR–Salmon route was chosen for alignment and quantification. Reference genome and gene annotation of H. illucens (UCSC; Genome assembly GCF_905115235.1_ _iHerIll2.2.curated.2019112544,45) were used. The AMP genes identified in other datasets (A bioinformatic study of AMPs identified in Hermetia illucens10,11and UniProt46) that were not annotated in the NCBI RefSeq iHerIll2.2.curated.20191125 GTF file were manually added using the BLAT search genome tool.

To assess AMP mRNA expression levels in the control sample (basal conditions), FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) was calculated using edgeR. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR and RUVseq R packages for removing unwanted variation from RNA-seq data. Only genes with a read count > 5 in at least three different samples were included in the analysis. We applied a cut-off of P < 0.05 and absolute fold-change > 1.5 relative to the control for downstream analysis. Differentially expressed genes were defined with FDR < 0.1.

For simplicity and to facilitate comparison between the RNA-seq and LC–MS/MS data, we assigned new names (HI_AMP) to the AMPs, with the original names listed in Supplementary Table S1 (RNA-seq data). Genes labeled with the same AMP number followed by “Gn” (gene) indicate AMP genes that were identified as distinct entities in the RNA-seq data but grouped together in the LC–MS/MS analysis due to high similarity of their protein sequences. Genes numbered 1,000 or higher were detected in the RNA-seq analysis but not in the LC–MS/MS data.

Hemolymph collection and peptide extraction using organic solvents

Three days after the fungal treatment, 5th instar larvae were collected and their surface was sterilized using ethanol 70%. Hemolymph was collected by puncturing the cuticle in the larval head region and applying gentle pressure to extract the hemolymph. Approximately 40 larvae were used for hemolymph collection in each biological replicate. The hemolymph was then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min to separate the hemocytes, and the resulting supernatant was retained.

To extract the peptides from the hemolymph, precipitation with organic solvents (methanol, acetic acid, and water in a 90:1:9 ratio) was carried out in a ratio of 1:9 for sample and solvent. This mixture was subsequently centrifuged for 45 min at 16,000 g at 4 °C. The supernatant, containing the lower molecular weight compounds, was collected and subjected to vacuum drying to eliminate the organic solvent. The resulting extracts were reconstituted in sterile water, after which an equal volume of hexane was added. This step was designed to remove lipids. The samples were vortexed and then centrifuged at 16,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The upper fraction, containing the lipids, was discarded, and the lower fraction was stored at −20 °C until further use.

Proteolysis and mass spectrometry analysis

The analysis was conducted as previously described47; the protein samples were added to 8.5M urea, 100mM ammonium bicarbonate and 10mM dithiothreitol. The protein amount was estimated using Bradford readings (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California USA). The samples were reduced at 60 °C for 30 min, modified with 35.2mM iodoacetamide in ammonium bicarbonate at room temperature for 30 min in the dark and digested in 1.5M Urea, 17.6mM ammonium bicarbonate with modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI USA), overnight at 37 °C in a 1:50 (M/M) enzyme-to-substrate ratio. The resulting peptides were analyzed by LC–MS/MS using a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA) fitted with a capillary high performance liquid chromatography instrument (easy nLC 1200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA).

The peptides were loaded in solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) on a homemade capillary column (30 cm, 75-micron ID) packed with Reprosil C18-Aqua (Dr. Maisch GmbH, Germany).

The peptide mixture was resolved with a 5 to 28% linear gradient of solvent B (80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) for 60 min followed by a 28 to 95% gradient of 15 min and 15 min of 95% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid in water, at flow rates of 0.15 μl/min. Mass spectrometry was performed in a positive mode (m/z 300–1,800, resolution 60,000 for MS1 scan and 15,000 for MS2 scan) using a repetitively full MS scan followed by high collision dissociation (HCD, at 27 normalized collision energy) of the 18 most dominant ions (> 1 charges) selected from the first MS scan. A dynamic exclusion list was enabled with an exclusion duration of 20 s.

Protein identification

Protein identification was performed using Discoverer software (MaxQuant)48 against several putative protein datasets based on the H. illucens transcriptome datasets. A) Uniprot fasta database of Hermetia illucens, incorporating both reviewed and unreviewed sequences46; B) GCF_905115235.1_iHerIll2.2.curated.20191125_protein.faa, NCBI Genome assembly iHerIll2.2.curated.2019112544.45; and C) A bioinformatic study of AMPs identified in Hermetia illucens8,9.

The search protocol employed specific parameters: cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as a fixed modification, while methionine oxidation was considered a variable modification. Up to two missed cleavages were permitted. The "match-between-runs" feature was enabled, and a 1% FDR threshold was applied at both the peptide and protein levels. For protein identification, a minimum of two unique or razor peptides per protein was required. In MaxQuant, quantitative analysis involved using the label-free quantification (LFQ) method and intensity-based absolute quantification (IBAQ). Common protein contaminants were excluded from the analysis using the list provided in MaxQuant. Only proteins identified in more than two samples in at least one treatment group were included in the analysis. The LFQ and IBAQ values were log2-transformed and normalized by subtracting the median intensity of a sample from all individual intensities within that sample (e.g., intensity of protein A—median of all intensities from sample 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between control samples (n = 5) and treated samples (n = 5) were conducted on LFQ values using one-way ANOVA. Protein groups with adjusted P-values (FDR) < 0.1 were further analyzed using the post-hoc Tukey HSD test. The IBAQ values were used to assess protein abundance within the samples.

For simplicity and to facilitate comparison between the RNA-seq and LC–MS/MS data, we assigned new names (HI_AMP) to the AMPs, with the original names listed in Supplementary Table S2 (LC–MS/MS data).
