Intermittent air exposure enhances antioxidant capacity and stress resistance of oyster larvae
Authors: Xuesong Li, Chunmei Xiong, Lunyu Shen, Weilin Wang, Chuanyan Yang, Xiaoxu Zhou, Lingling Wang, Linsheng Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Two-cycle of intermittent air exposure at umbo larvae stage significantly increases survival rate of oyster larvae under high temperature stress.•Intermittent air exposure significantly enhances antioxidant capacity and expressions of CgHIF-1α and CgNrf2 of oyster larvae.•Intermittent air exposure in umbo larvae induces lasting higher stress resistance of juvenile oysters.

Introduction

Oyster aquaculture serves as a cornerstone of global shellfish production, supporting coastal economies, food security, and estuarine ecosystem restoration [[1], [2], [3]]. As a typical benthic filter-feeding mollusk with a complex life cycle, the Pacific oyster C. gigas undergoes a planktonic larval phase followed by settlement and metamorphosis into sessile juveniles [4]. This early developmental window is highly sensitive to environmental stressors, including temperature fluctuations, salinity shifts, hypoxia, pathogen exposure, and oxidative stress, all of which can impair larval survival, growth, and metamorphosis success [5]. In intensive hatchery and nursery systems, high larval mortality remains a major bottleneck, often linked to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, weakened antioxidant defense, and insufficient stress resilience [6]. Therefore, developing safe, scalable, and non-chemical strategies to improve larval viability and robustness is critical for sustainable oyster production.

Intertidal and off-bottom culture systems have been widely adopted to improve oyster performance in grow-out stages [[7], [8], [9]]. Among these practices, controlled intermittent air exposure has emerged as a low-cost and ecologically consistent management tool. Previous studies with juvenile and adult oysters have demonstrated that intermittent air exposure can reduce summer mortality, delay the proliferation of the protozoan parasite Perkinsus marinus (the causative agent of Dermo disease), enhance condition index, and reduce biofouling [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. In adjustable longline systems (ALS), daily intermittent air exposure during low tide has been associated with better physiological condition and lower pathogen loads compared with continuous submersion or weekly intermittent air exposure [15]. Although these benefits are well documented in older oysters, the underlying physiological mechanisms, especially the activation of antioxidant pathways and stress tolerance, remain poorly understood, and few studies have extended these findings to the vulnerable larval stage.

Oxidative stress arises when ROS production exceeds the scavenging capacity of antioxidant systems, leading to lipid peroxidation, protein damage, and DNA impairment, ultimately compromising cellular function and larval development [16,17]. In natural intertidal habitats, oyster larvae and early spat regularly experience brief emersion during tidal cycles, which may act as a mild hormetic stressor that primes antioxidant and stress-responsive pathways. Such preconditioning can enhance tolerance to subsequent environmental challenges, including high temperature, hypoxia, and pathogen exposure [18]. Despite this ecological relevance, whether intermittent air exposure can systematically enhance antioxidant capacity and stress resistance in oyster larvae has not been systematically investigated.

Furthermore, commercial oyster hatcheries rely heavily on stable, submerged rearing environments, which may limit the development of natural stress resilience [19]. While continuous submersion supports rapid growth, it may also leave larvae physiologically unprepared for field conditions, leading to high post-deploy mortality [20,21]. Especially, the umbo larva, the last stage before settlement, are relatively sensitive to environmental conditions. Intermittent air exposure during larvae culture may bridge this gap by stimulating adaptive physiological responses without compromising growth or development. This study aims to fill this knowledge gap by investigating the effects of intermittent air exposure of umbo larva on survival, development, antioxidant enzyme activities, ROS levels, and stress-related gene expression in C. gigas larvae. We hypothesize that appropriate intermittent air exposure regimes trigger hormetic effects that strengthen antioxidant capacity and improve resistance to environmental stressors. The results will provide a physiological basis for optimizing hatchery protocols and developing robust, stress-resistant oyster seed stock, supporting the stability and sustainability of coastal oyster aquaculture.

Materials and methods

Larval acquisition

Sixty adult oysters C. gigas of sexual maturity were obtained from a farm in Zhangzi island, Dalian, Liaoning province, China, and used for artificial fertilization. The spawning and harvesting of the oyster larvae were conducted according to the method described previously [22]. Approximately one day post-hatching, umbo larvae were isolated using a 300-mesh sieve. During the acclimation, larvae were kept in a density of 15 ± 2 individuals/mL at 22 ± 2 °C. All larvae in each the experiment came from the same spawning batch.

Intermittent air exposure treatment in umbo oyster larvae

The larvae intermittent air exposure was conducted according to transportation methods of bivalve seed [23]. Briefly, larvae at umbo stage were isolated using a 300-mesh sieve, and kept in temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and ambient humidity of 80% ± 5%, Wind speed: not controlled (indoor still air), Light intensity: 0–50 lx (during intermittent air exposure to minimize stress). Approximately 3.1 × 106 larvae at umbo stage were equally divided into five groups: one-cycle intermittent air exposure, two-cycle intermittent air exposure, three-cycle intermittent air exposure, four-cycle intermittent air exposure and control (no intermittent air exposure). Each group approximately 0.6 × 106 larvae were equally divided into three 200 L container, there were three biological replicates for each group, one 200 L cantainer is per replicate. The treatment of one-cycle intermittent air exposure contained a 12 h air exposure following a 12 h immersion in sterile seawater, lasting one day for a cycle. Then, the larvae were cultured in normal filtered seawater, and sampled at 24 h after intermittent air exposure treatment.

The untreated larvae cultured in the same situation were sampled synchronously as control group. The survival rates of larvae from each group were measured and recorded at 24 h post treatment. There were three biological replicates for each time point.

At 48 h after the intermittent air exposure cycle ended, 1000 larvae were sampled and placed in 8 L seawater at 28 °C for 12 h or at 30 °C for 10 h, and their mortality rate was recorded to evaluate their heat resistance. The extreme temperature is set according to sublethal stress influence oysters [24,25].

The growth of umbo larvae post intermittent air exposure treatment

The oyster larvae of the two-cycle of intermittent air exposure treatment group and the control group were sampled every two days and observed under light microscopy. The control group was untreated. Seawater in tanks was replaced after each treatment, with normal cultivation procedures followed. Shell lengths of larvae from each group were measured and recorded at 0, 2, 4, 6 days post cycle intermittent air exposure treatment. After thoroughly mixing the larvae in the tanks, we took a random 5 mL seawater sample from each tank for microscopic examination. To facilitate counting, the collected larvae were euthanized with alcohol. Under the microscope, deceased larvae were identified by their damaged internal structure and the absence of food in their intestines. Shell length was measured under a 100 × microscope using an ocular micrometer, and ImageJ software was used for counting and measurement.

To measure the feeding rate, 1000 larvae were sampled and placed in 1 L seawater at 48 h after the intermittent air exposure cycle ended, each with three replicates. The two-cycle intermittent air exposure treatment group and the control group were fed with Isochrysis galbana. The feeding rate of larvae from each group were measured and recorded at 0, 2, 4, 6 days post cycle intermittent air exposure treatment according previous report [26]. The feeding rate was calculated based on formula: F=(C0-Ct)*V/L*V*t, F is the feeding rate (cells/h), V is the volume of the experimental seawater (mL), L is the larval density (individuals/mL), C0 and Ct are the food densities in the water at the beginning and end of the experiment (cells/mL), respectively, and t is the experimental duration (h).

Total mRNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted by using Trizol Reagent according to manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of RNA were assessed by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gel and Nanodrop 2000 measurement, respectively. The first strand of cDNA was synthesized by using total RNA (treated with DNase I) as a template and oligo-dT-adaptor as primers according to the manufacturer's protocol (TaKaRa, China). The synthesis reaction was performed at 42 °C for 15 min, and terminated by heating at 85 °C for 5 sec. The cDNA mix was stored at −80 °C for following experiment.

In order to detect the antioxidant stress capacity of two-cycle intermittent air exposure treatment group, the oyster larvae of the two-cycle intermittent air exposure treatment group and the control group were sampled 24 h after the end of the intermittent air exposure cycle, two related transcription factors and one downstream gene were selected and their expression levels were monitored by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). These genes include the transcription factors CgHIF-1α (Hypoxia-inducible factor) [27], CgNrf2 (Nuclearfactor erythroidderived 2-like 2) [28], and the downstream antioxidant gene CgSOD (Superoxide dismutase) [29]. Specific primers for these target genes were designed (Table 1). The cDNAs prepared from different developmental stages larvae were used as templates after diluted to 1:20. The RT-qPCR reaction mixture (10 µL) consisted of 2 × SYBR Green PCR Master mix, 0.2 µL each of the forward and reverse primers, 2 µL of template cDNA, and 2.4 µL of DEPC water. Dissociation curve analysis was performed to determine the target specificity. The relative expression ratio of the target genes versus elongation factor (CgEF, CGI_10012474) gene was calculated using comparative Ct method (2-△△Ct method) and all data were given in terms of relative mRNA expression [30]. Total protein extraction and enzyme activity assay

The activities of antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase and catalase), hydrolytic enzyme (lysozyme) and of oyster larvae were determined according previous report [30]. Frozen larval samples were melted on ice and homogenized in 200 µL pre-cold PBS by using an electric homogenizer (MS 3, IKA, Germany). The homogenate was then centrifuged at 3500 × rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to precipitate insoluble tissue, and the protein concentration was determined and quantified with Bradford method. The supernatant was collected and assayed for detecting the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and lysozyme (LZM) following the manufacturer's instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute,China) by using a M1000 PRO absorbance microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). All assays were performed in three replicates (n = 3).

Survival rate under heat stress when the larvae developed to juvenile oyster

Three months later when the larvae developed to juvenile oyster, 80 juvenile oysters (shell length: 13.15 ± 1.75 mm, mean ± SD) were randomly selected from each group and transported from Zhangzi island Farm (Dalian, China) to the laboratory. After seven days acclimation, their high temperature tolerance was evaluated by exposing them to the high temperature condition (34 °C) for 24 h. The extreme temperature is set according to sublethal stress influence oysters [24,25]. The survival of juvenile oysters in each group was continuously observed and recorded after 6, 12, 18, 24 h.

Energy metabolism and antioxidant regulation of juvenile oyster under heat stress

The changes in activities of SOD and CAT and the glycogen content in the juvenile oyster from each group were measured after 24 h of high-temperature stress according to previous reports [31]. The 24 h time point was selected because it represents the end of the high-temperature stress exposure (34 °C for 24 h), which was designed to evaluate the stress resistance of juvenile oysters. The superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was assessed using the SOD Assay Kit based on the WST-1 method. The catalase (CAT) activity was assessed using the CAT Assay Kit based on the suspension and complexation of ammonium molybdate in CAT decomposition and the combination with H2O2. The Glycogen activity was assessed using the Glycogen Assay Kit based on under the action of concentrated sulfuric acid, dehydration can occur to produce sugar aldehyde derivatives.

Statistical analysis

All data were shown as mean ± S.D (N ≥ 3). The log-rank test in Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to evaluate the cumulative survival rate of oysters after heat stress under laboratory conditions. Data were initially assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance to deter mine suitability for one-way ANOVA, performed using SPSS statistics version 19 at a significance level of P < 0.05. Figures were plotted by means of GraphPad Prism 9.5.

Discussion

The present study provides comprehensive physiological and molecular evidence that, intermittent air exposure during the umbo larval stage significantly enhances antioxidant capacity and long-term stress resistance in C. gigas, offering a practical, low-cost strategy to alleviate high mortality during the planktonic-to-sessile transition and improve the stability of oyster hatchery production. As a typical metamorphic bivalve, oysters undergo a critical developmental window from umbo to pediveliger larvae, during which larvae prepare for settlement, attachment, and metamorphosis into sessile juveniles [4]. This period is inherently vulnerable to environmental fluctuations, especially elevated temperature, which frequently causes mass mortality in commercial hatcheries and limits the sustainable supply of high-quality oyster seeds [32]. Our findings demonstrate that precisely controlled intermittent air exposure, specifically two cycles of 12-hour air exposure followed by 12-hour immersion, functions as a mild hormetic stimulus that activates conserved adaptive pathways without compromising larval survival, growth, or development. These results extend the beneficial effects of intermittent air exposure well beyond juvenile and adult oyster grow-out practices, establishing a novel physiological preconditioning approach tailored to the most sensitive larval stages.

A central observation of this study is that two cycles of intermittent air exposure significantly improve the survival of umbo larvae under acute high-temperature stress, while excessive exposure (three or four cycles) reduces viability. This dose–response pattern aligns with the hormesis principle, in which moderate, repeated stress enhances resilience, whereas overexposure causes cumulative damage [33]. In commercial off-bottom and adjustable longline systems, similar optimal exposure regimes have been widely reported: daily short-term intermittent air exposure improves survival, condition index, and disease resistance in Crassostrea virginica, whereas prolonged or infrequent emersion increases mortality and reduces growth [15]. Our results confirm that such hormetic effects are conserved across developmental stages and can be strategically applied during early larviculture. Under sublethal heat stress (28 °C for 12 h or 30 °C for 10 h), larvae subjected to two-cycle exposure exhibited markedly higher survival than control larvae, indicating that intermittent air exposure preconditions cellular defense systems to withstand thermal challenges [34]. This is particularly meaningful because summer heatwaves and unstable water temperatures are major causes of larval collapse in hatcheries and nursery systems [31]. By activating adaptive responses during the sensitive umbo stage, hatcheries can substantially reduce heat-related losses without chemical interventions [35].

At the mechanistic level, this study reveals that intermittent air exposure strongly enhances antioxidant enzyme activities and upregulates key stress-responsive genes, including CgSOD, CgHIF-1α, and CgNrf2 [[27], [28], [29]]. The two-cycle treatment induced dramatic increases in SOD (9.42-fold) and CAT (5.29-fold) activities, along with significantly elevated transcript levels of CgSOD (2.67-fold), CgHIF-1α (3.57-fold), and CgNrf2 (3.06-fold) compared with controls. These molecular and biochemical changes represent a coordinated adaptive response aimed at neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), maintaining redox homeostasis, and enhancing cellular resilience. Oxidative stress is a primary driver of heat-induced damage in marine invertebrates: elevated temperatures accelerate ROS production, overwhelming basal antioxidant defenses and causing lipid peroxidation, protein denaturation, and DNA injury. The Nrf2 pathway is a master regulator of antioxidant gene expression, while HIF-1α mediates adaptive responses to fluctuating oxygen availability—both of which are activated during intermittent air exposure. By simultaneously boosting antioxidant enzyme activity and upregulating Nrf2 and HIF-1α, intermittent air exposure creates a robust defensive phenotype that enables larvae to tolerate subsequent environmental stress. This mechanism complements observations in adult oysters, where intermittent air exposure reduces pathogen loads and improves physiological condition; in larvae, these pathways are even more critical because developmental processes are highly sensitive to redox imbalance [17].

Notably, the stress resistance induced by transient intermittent air exposure during the umbo stage persists into the juvenile stage, representing a long-term physiological programming effect. When exposed to severe high-temperature stress (34 °C for 24 h), juveniles derived from the two-cycle treatment exhibited significantly higher survival than controls. Moreover, these juveniles maintained higher glycogen content (1.41-fold), as well as sustained elevations in SOD and CAT activities (1.14-fold and 1.21-fold, respectively). This long-lasting enhancement indicates that early-life intermittent air exposure does not merely produce acute physiological changes but triggers stable metabolic and defensive programming that supports robustness throughout later development [36,37]. Glycogen serves as a critical energy reserve for stress tolerance, metamorphosis, and settlement; higher glycogen levels improve the ability to withstand starvation, temperature fluctuations, and handling stress [38]. The sustained increase in antioxidant enzyme activities further ensures that juveniles remain resilient to field conditions after deployment [39]. In conventional hatchery systems, larvae are reared under stable submerged conditions that maximize growth but fail to induce such adaptive programming, resulting in high post-deployment mortality [40]. Our results demonstrate that brief, standardized intermittent air exposure during the umbo stage can effectively resolve this bottleneck by producing physiologically robust seed stock with persistent stress tolerance.

The optimal two-cycle protocol identified in this study balances stress preconditioning with normal development. Unlike three or four cycles, which reduced larval survival, two cycles of 12-hour exposure and 12-hour immersion did not impair baseline viability and significantly improved feeding rate and developmental progression. Accelerated feeding and development are critical for synchronous metamorphosis and attachment, which directly affect seed uniformity and production efficiency. In natural intertidal habitats, oyster larvae and early spat regularly experience tidal emersion; thus, the beneficial effects of intermittent air exposure reflect evolutionary adaptation rather than artificial manipulation [18]. This ecological relevance validates the practicality of integrating intermittent air exposure into hatchery protocols. Unlike genetic selection or chemical additives, intermittent air exposure is low-cost, easy to implement, environmentally safe, and compatible with large-scale commercial operations. It can be readily adopted in existing hatcheries using standard rearing systems without major infrastructure modifications.

These findings also carry important implications for climate-resilient aquaculture. As global warming increases the frequency and intensity of marine heatwaves, hatchery-produced oyster larvae face growing thermal risk [41]. Traditional management strategies rely on cooling systems, which are expensive and energy-intensive. In contrast, physiological preconditioning via intermittent air exposure provides a sustainable, cost-effective alternative that enhances intrinsic tolerance. By activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and HIF-1α-mediated oxygen homeostasis, this approach strengthens multiple layers of stress defense simultaneously, providing broad-spectrum resistance not only to heat but also to hypoxia, osmotic fluctuation, and oxidative challenge—common stressors in coastal and hatchery environments.

In summary, this study demonstrates that two cycles of intermittent air exposure at the umbo larval stage enhance acute and long-term stress resistance in C. gigas by activating antioxidant systems and key transcriptional regulators. The improved survival, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, elevated expression of CgHIF-1α and CgNrf2, accelerated development, and persistent resilience in juveniles collectively highlight the effectiveness of this simple preconditioning strategy. This work bridges the gap between grow-out management practices and early larval physiology, providing a scientifically grounded, operationally feasible solution to reduce mortality during the critical planktonic-to-sessile transition. By integrating standardized intermittent air exposure into larval rearing protocols, oyster hatcheries can improve seed quality, increase production stability, and enhance the climate resilience of the shellfish aquaculture industry. Future research should optimize exposure regimes across different larval stages, evaluate genotype-specific responses, and investigate interactions between aerial preconditioning and other environmental stressors to further refine this promising approach for global application.

While this study provides compelling evidence for the involvement of CgHIF-1α, CgNrf2, and CgSOD in the beneficial effects of intermittent air exposure, it is important to acknowledge a key limitation: the gene expression analysis was confined to a small set of preselected targets. The molecular mechanisms underlying air-exposure-induced hormesis are likely far more complex, involving global transcriptional reprogramming of stress response, metabolism, apoptosis, and immune signaling pathways. Therefore, future studies should employ unbiased, high-throughput approaches such as RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) or whole-transcriptome analysis to comprehensively characterize the gene expression landscape following intermittent air exposure. Such investigations could reveal previously unrecognized regulatory networks, including non-coding RNAs, alternative splicing events, and time-dependent expression dynamics. In addition, integrating transcriptomics with proteomics and metabolomics would provide a systems-level understanding of how mild aerial stress confers lasting resilience. These efforts will be essential for optimizing exposure regimes and identifying molecular biomarkers for real-time monitoring of larval physiological status in commercial hatcheries.
