Tanshinone IIA inhibits foam cell formation and alleviates atherosclerosis by regulating the Hedgehog signaling pathway through TRPM2 suppression
Authors: Yajuan Yin, Xiaocui Shi, Wenyun Tan, Xiao Zhang, Xiufang Jiang, Mei Liu, Jianan Xu, Da Liu, Le Wang, Mei Wei, Gang Liu, Fangfang Ma, Mingqi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of foam cells is crucial in the advancement of atherosclerosis (AS). Tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA), the primary lipophilic component of Salvia miltiorrhiza, has various pharmacological effects. Despite this, the precise role of Tan IIA in AS has not been fully elucidated. In this research, we employed ApoE−/− mice to establish an AS model. Oil Red O and HE staining indicated that Tan IIA obviously reduced plaque areas in both the aorta and aortic arch. Additionally, serum analysis revealed that Tan IIA notably decreased lipid and inflammatory factor levels in AS mice. In vitro studies showed that Tan IIA primarily prevented foam cell formation by enhancing cholesterol efflux rather than increasing lipid uptake. Mechanistic investigations reveal that Tan IIA suppresses the Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway by downregulating the expression of transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2) and subsequently inhibiting Ca²⁺ influx. This cascade ultimately attenuates foam cell formation and impedes the progression of AS. Overall, this research provides a solid theoretical foundation for the potential application of Tan IIA in the treatment of AS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51659-9.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Tanshinone IIA, Foam cell formation, TRPM2, Hedgehog signaling pathwaySubject terms: Cardiology, Cell biology, Diseases, Drug discovery, Physiology

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is an arteriosclerotic condition that mainly impacts the walls of medium and large arteries, serving as the key pathological contributor to many cardiovascular diseases1,2. The development of AS involves multiple factors and is not completely comprehended3. Macrophages, the primary immune cells within vascular lesions, are essential for AS progression4. During disease development, circulating monocytes accumulate beneath the endothelial layer and differentiate into macrophages. These macrophages engulf substantial quantities of oxLDL, leading to the loss of their antigen-presenting function and their transformation into foam cells5–7. Furthermore, oxLDL stimulates foam cells to secrete various pro-inflammatory cytokines, which accelerate plaque formation8. Thus, understanding the molecular mechanisms driving foam cell formation is critical for developing novel AS therapeutic strategies.

Statins are commonly prescribed in clinical practice to manage AS. While effective in controlling the condition to a degree, their use is often associated with adverse effects, including skeletal muscle injury, rhabdomyolysis, and hepatotoxicity9. This highlights the need for novel therapeutic agents to complement existing treatments. As a significant lipophilic constituent of Salvia miltiorrhiza, Tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA) offers numerous pharmacological advantages, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, as well as the ability to inhibit tumor growth10,11. Notably, studies have shown that Tan IIA could inhibit TNF-α-mediated endothelial inflammation and reduce monocyte adhesion, indicating its potential anti-AS effects12. However, the precise mechanisms underlying Tan ⅡA’s action in AS remain unclear.

Transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2), a non-selective cation channel, participates in multiple cellular signaling pathways, including oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis, by regulating intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations13. In terms of AS, one study demonstrated that TRPM2 accelerates the development of AS by increasing aortic reactivity to 5-hydroxytryptamine14. Moreover, TRPM2 knockout reduces plaque area in AS by attenuating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, thereby slowing AS progression15. The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway is broadly expressed across various cell types and plays vital roles in processes such as neural tube differentiation during embryogenesis, limb development, and stem cell homeostasis16. Recent experimental research confirmed the Hh pathway contributed to the pathogenesis of AS. For example, Zhang et al. demonstrated that Hh signaling contributes to AS progression by modulating foam cell formation and autophagy17. However, it remains uncertain if the anti-AS effects of Tan IIA are achieved by modulating the Hh pathway through TRPM2 regulation.

This investigation intends to elucidate the influence of Tan IIA on AS and the mechanisms involved. Our findings suggest that Tan IIA inhibits Hh signaling activation by downregulating TRPM2 to inhibit Ca²⁺ influx, thereby reducing foam cell formation and slowing AS progression.

Results

Tan IIA alleviates AS progression in mice

An animal model using ApoE⁻/⁻ mice was constructed to examine the particular effects of Tan IIA on AS. After twelve weeks of treatment, it was evident that Tan IIA significantly prevented weight gain caused by HFD in AS mice (Fig. 1A). Mice were then euthanized, and their thoracic and abdominal aortas were collected for ORO and HE staining. The results demonstrated that Tan IIA significantly decreased plaque areas in both the full-length aorta and the aortic arch (Fig. 1B–C). Additionally, serum was collected from three groups of mice to analyze the levels of lipid indicators, revealing that Tan IIA significantly lowered TC, TG, and LDL-C levels and raising HDL-C levels (Fig. 1D). Moreover, ELISA analysis revealed that Tan IIA remarkably decreased the serum concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 1E). Collectively, our outcomes revealed that Tan IIA could alleviate AS progression in vivo.

Tan IIA inhibits foam cell formation

To assess how Tan IIA affects the formation of foam cells, its optimal concentration for in vitro experimentation was determined. As indicated in Fig. 2A, Tan IIA concentrations exceeding 20 µM caused over 50% cell death, prompting the choice of 10 µM for subsequent experiments. Lipid accumulation was assessed using ORO staining, which revealed that Tan IIA treatment significantly reduced intracellular lipid content compared to the oxLDL group (Fig. 2B). Moreover, Tan IIA notably decreased the expression of pro-inflammatory factors induced by oxLDL (Fig. 2C). Foam cells development is tightly regulated by the interplay between cholesterol uptake and efflux. To investigate the underlying mechanism, we analyzed the mRNA levels of crucial receptors and transporters that contribute to these processes. Notably, Tan IIA significantly upregulated the expression of cholesterol efflux transporters ABCA1 and ABCB1, while exerting no noticeable effect on the oxLDL uptake receptors LOX-1 and SR-A1 (Fig. 2D). To further substantiate these results, Dil-oxLDL uptake and cholesterol efflux assays were conducted. These results demonstrated that Tan IIA significantly enhanced cholesterol efflux, while having no observable effect on Dil-oxLDL uptake (Fig. 2E–F). Consistent with qPCR results, western blot results confirmed a substantial increase in ABCA1 and ABCB1 protein expression following Tan IIA treatment (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these outcomes suggest that Tan IIA attenuates foam cell formation predominantly via facilitating cholesterol efflux.

Tan IIA inhibits foam cell formation via TRPM2 suppression

Previous studies have demonstrated that downregulating TRPM2 can inhibit the progression of AS18. To determine whether TRPM2 participates in the suppression of oxLDL-induced foam cell formation by Tan IIA, Western blot was used to assess TRPM2 protein expression. The results revealed that oxLDL stimulation markedly enhanced TRPM2 expression, whereas Tan IIA treatment significantly suppressed this upregulation (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we overexpressed TRPM2 in RAW 264.7 cells and verified the transfection efficiency through Western blot (Fig. 3B). The results indicated that, compared to the oxLDL + Tan IIA + Vector group, the oxLDL + Tan IIA + TRPM2 group exhibited significantly increased lipid accumulation and higher expression of pro-inflammatory factors (Fig. 3C-D). Furthermore, TRPM2 overexpression notably attenuated the Tan IIA-induced enhancement of cholesterol efflux and the upregulation of ABCA1 and ABCB1 protein expression (Fig. 3E-F). These results indicate that Tan IIA reduces foam cell formation via the downregulation of TRPM2.

Tan IIA alleviates AS progression in mice by inhibiting TRPM2

To evaluate whether Tan IIA inhibits the progression of AS in mice through TRPM2 suppression, TRPM2 was overexpressed in ApoE⁻/⁻ mice. Western blot analysis revealed that Tan IIA significantly decreased TRPM2 protein levels in the aortic arch of AS mice (Fig. 4A). ORO and HE staining further showed that, compared with the HFD + Tan IIA + Vector group, TRPM2 overexpression led to a significant increase in plaque area in both the full-length aorta and aortic arch (Fig. 4B-C). Moreover, TRPM2 overexpression markedly reversed the Tan IIA-induced decreases in serum lipid and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (Fig. 4D-E). These results imply that Tan IIA ameliorates AS in mice by inhibiting TRPM2.

Tan IIA suppresses the Hh signaling pathway by reducing TRPM2-mediated Ca²⁺ influx

Previous study has shown that Hh pathway inhibition can suppress the formation of foam cell17.Based on this, we hypothesized that the effect of Tan IIA on foam cell formation is associated with the Hh pathway. To test this, we employed Western to examine the expression of Hh pathway-related proteins. oxLDL stimulation markedly increased SHH, SMO, and Gli1 expression in RAW264.7, while Tan IIA treatment effectively suppressed their expression. Notably, TRPM2 overexpression significantly reversed the suppressive impact of Tan IIA on Hh pathway (Fig. 5A). To elucidate how TRPM2 regulates the Hh pathway, we first investigated whether TRPM2 directly interacts with core components of this pathway. Co-IP assays revealed that TRPM2 did not physically bind to SHH, SMO, or Gli1 proteins (Fig. 5B). Given that TRPM2 is a non-selective cation channel permeable to Ca²⁺, we speculated that TRPM2 might regulate the Hh pathway via intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling. Subsequent measurement of intracellular Ca²⁺ levels demonstrated that, compared with the oxLDL Tan IIA Vector group, TRPM2 overexpression significantly elevated the intracellular Ca²⁺ level, whereas co-treatment with the cell-permeable calcium chelator BAPTA-AM effectively abolished this increase (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, Western blot analysis demonstrated that BAPTA-AM treatment significantly reversed the upregulation of SHH, SMO, and Gli1 protein levels induced by TRPM2 overexpression (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings indicate that Tan IIA suppresses Hh pathway activation by downregulating TRPM2, which subsequently inhibits Ca²⁺ influx rather than through direct protein interactions.

Tan IIA prevents foam cell formation by suppressing the Hh pathway

To further explore whether Tan IIA’s inhibition of foam cell formation is associated with Hh pathway suppression, we applied SAG, a known Hh pathway agonist. ORO staining revealed SAG remarkably attenuated Tan IIA-induced reduction in lipid accumulation (Fig. 6A). SAG also reversed the Tan IIA’s inhibitory effects on inflammation (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, SAG abrogated the enhancement of cholesterol efflux induced by Tan IIA (Fig. 6C–D). These results indicate that Tan IIA reduces foam cell formation by inhibiting the Hh signaling pathway through TRPM2 downregulation.

Tan IIA alleviates AS in mice by suppressing the Hh pathway through TRPM2 inhibition

To determine whether Tan IIA exerts anti-AS effects in vivo through a similar mechanism, we examined SHH, SMO, and Gli1 protein expression in AS mice. Consistent with in vitro findings, HFD feeding markedly upregulated the expression of these proteins, while Tan IIA treatment remarkably suppressed their expression (Fig. 7A). Additionally, TRPM2 overexpression counteracted the suppressive impact of Tan IIA on Hh pathway (Fig. 7A). ORO and HE staining demonstrated that SAG markedly attenuated the Tan IIA-induced reduction in plaque area in the full-length aorta and aortic arch (Fig. 7B-C). In addition, SAG administration abrogated the Tan IIA-mediated decreases in serum lipid and inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 7D-E). Together, these findings suggest that Tan IIA inhibits the Hh signaling pathway through TRPM2 suppression, thereby attenuating the progression of AS.

Discussion

AS is a key contributor to the onset of cardiovascular diseases19. Due to the complex and multifactorial nature of AS, effective pharmacological treatments remain limited. Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen) is a traditional Chinese medicine, it offers several therapeutic benefits, including vasodilation, antioxidation, anticoagulation, and protection of myocardial and endothelial cells20. Tan IIA, primary lipophilic substance derived from Danshen, is commonly used for treating cardiovascular diseases21. Prior investigations confirmed that Tan IIA could protect the cardiovascular system through multiple mechanisms, such as improving blood flow and microcirculation by inhibiting platelet activation, thereby preventing cardiovascular disorders22. Furthermore, it has been shown that Tan IIA treats AS by regulating endothelial function and decreasing chronic inflammation23. This study employed HFD to induce AS mouse models. Following Tan IIA administration, a pronounced decline in body weight was observed, along with substantial reductions in lipid buildup within the aorta and aortic arch. Additionally, Tan IIA reduced serum inflammatory cytokines. These findings highlight Tan IIA’s promising potential as an anti-AS agent.

Foam cell formation from macrophages is a crucial early occurrence in AS development. Macrophages internalize excessive oxLDL, resulting in the formation of foam cells and subsequent accumulation in the vascular endothelium, thereby contributing to plaque development24. Inhibiting foam cell formation has been shown to effectively delay AS progression25. This study demonstrated that Tan IIA effectively reduces lipid accumulation and suppresses the inflammatory response in RAW264.7 macrophages. The formation of foam cells is governed by a complex interplay of mechanisms involving cholesterol uptake, efflux, and esterification26. To investigate the mechanism behind Tan IIA ‘s inhibition of foam cell formation, we examined the levels of key efflux transporters and cholesterol uptake receptors in RAW264.7 macrophages. According to qPCR analysis, Tan IIA remarkably elevated the expression of cholesterol transporters ABCA1 and ABCB1, while exerting no notable effect on the uptake receptors LOX-1 and SR-A1. ABCA1 and ABCB1 play essential roles in cellular cholesterol homeostasis. ABCA1 is responsible for transferring intracellular cholesterol and phospholipids to apolipoproteins27, whereas ABCB1 modulates intracellular cholesterol levels by regulating its absorption and secretion28. To further validate these findings, we performed Dil-oxLDL uptake and cholesterol efflux assays. These results found that Tan IIA enhanced cholesterol efflux without affecting oxLDL uptake. Additionally, Western blot analysis verified that Tan IIA treatment increased ABCA1 and ABCB1 expression in RAW264.7 macrophages. Collectively, findings indicate that Tan IIA prevents foam cell formation primarily by enhancing cholesterol efflux mechanisms.

TRPM2 is a cation channel that permits Ca²⁺ permeability, responds to oxidative stress, and is extensively present in various cell types like macrophages, vascular endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells. Research indicates that TRPM2 is crucial in processes like inflammatory responses, cell death, and angiogenesis by regulating Ca2+influx, which affects vascular stability and function, thus significantly contributing to AS development29,30. Increasing evidence indicates that downregulating TRPM2 might slow AS progression. For instance, active immunization with a TRPM2 peptide vaccine has been shown to effectively inhibit AS development in mice31. Furthermore, the absence of TRPM2 could inhibit the activation of the CD36 signaling cascade, thereby reducing foam cell formation, inflammatory responses, and impeding the progression of AS32. Thus, we hypothesize that the effects of Tan IIA in inhibiting AS may be associated with TRPM2. To test this, we performed Western blot analysis. The findings showed that TRPM2 protein expression was significantly elevated in oxLDL-treated RAW264.7 macrophages whereas Tan IIA treatment inhibited its expression. Next, we conducted rescue experiments by overexpressing TRPM2 in RAW264.7 macrophages. The results indicated that TRPM2 overexpression promoted the production of lipids and pro-inflammatory factors in cells, while inhibiting cholesterol efflux. Similarly, in vivo experiments demonstrated that Tan IIA treatment lowered TRPM2 protein level in aortic arch of AS mice. Additionally, TRPM2 overexpression counteracted the suppressive impact of Tan IIA on aortic plaque development and increased serum levels of lipids and inflammatory factors. Collectively, these findings imply that Tan IIA ameliorates AS by suppressing TRPM2, thereby reducing foam cell formation.

The Hh signaling pathway is essential for embryonic development in insects and mammals33. In recent years, numerous evidence highlights its pivotal involvement in AS pathogenesis. For instance, HHIPL1 promotes smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration by enhancing Hh pathway activity, thereby accelerating AS progression34. Conversely, suppression of Hh ligands is associated with enlarged atherosclerotic lesion area in advanced stages35. Based on this, we hypothesize that Tan IIA exerts anti-AS effects through suppressing Hh pathway. Our findings demonstrated that SHH, SMO, and Gli1 protein levels were significantly increased in oxLDL-treated cells and the aortic arch of AS mice, whereas Tan IIA reduced these protein levels. Notably, TRPM2 overexpression counteracted the inhibitory impact of Tan IIA on Hh pathway, suggesting that Tan IIA suppresses Hh pathway activation through TRPM2 downregulation.

To further elucidate how TRPM2 regulates the Hh pathway, we investigated whether TRPM2 directly interacts with its core components. Co-IP assays revealed no direct binding between TRPM2 and SHH, SMO, or Gli1. Given that TRPM2 is a member of the transient receptor potential channel family, and its activation leads to a marked increase in intracellular Ca²⁺ concentration36, we reasoned that Ca²⁺ signaling might serve as the link between TRPM2 and Hh pathway activation. Consistent with this hypothesis, TRPM2 overexpression elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ levels, whereas the Ca²⁺ chelator BAPTA-AM reversed this elevation and also abolished the TRPM2 overexpression-induced upregulation of SHH, SMO, and Gli1. These results demonstrate that TRPM2 regulates the Hh pathway through Ca²⁺ influx rather than direct protein-protein interaction.

To elucidate the role of Hh pathway in the anti-atherosclerotic effects of Tan IIA, we performed rescue experiments using the Hh pathway agonist SAG. The results demonstrated that reactivation of the Hh pathway reversed the anti-atherosclerotic effects of Tan IIA both in vitro and in vivo. Overall, these results imply that Tan IIA suppresses foam cell formation and AS progression by downregulating TRPM2, thereby reducing Ca²⁺ influx and inhibiting Hh signaling. Tan IIA is widely recognized as a multitarget pharmacological agent. Previous studies have demonstrated that Tan IIA alleviates AS by inhibiting the MAPK/NF-κB pathway to suppress vascular inflammation37. Additionally, Tan IIA can activate the PI3K/Akt/eNOS pathway to inhibit endothelial injury and ameliorate AS38. Notably, emerging evidence suggests potential crosstalk between these established targets and the Hh pathway. Specifically, key components of the Hh pathway (SHH, SMO) have been shown to regulate NF-κB activation in various diseases39,40. Moreover, the PI3K/AKT cascade is known to maintain the activity of SMO and Gli141. Thus, the TRPM2-mediated Hh activation identified in our study may intersect with these known pathways. Further investigation of this crosstalk will provide a more comprehensive understanding of Tan IIA’s anti-atherosclerotic mechanisms.

Although this study provides novel insights into the anti-atherosclerotic mechanism of Tan IIA, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the conclusions are derived primarily from in vitro cellular assays and animal models, which constrain the species translatability of the findings and their clinical applicability. Future studies should employ a broader range of animal models and incorporate analyses of clinical specimens to enhance the clinical relevance of the results. Second, this study focuses on the core regulatory TRPM2-Hh signaling axis but does not identify the upstream molecular targets or signaling networks through which Tan IIA modulates TRPM2 expression. Subsequent research should further elucidate these upstream functional targets and the crosstalk among interconnected signaling pathways to construct a more comprehensive molecular regulatory network underlying the anti-atherosclerotic effects of Tan IIA.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that Tan IIA effectively attenuates macrophage-derived foam cell formation and impedes AS progression by suppressing the TRPM2-mediated Ca²⁺ influx, which subsequently inhibits the activation of the Hh signaling pathway. These findings provide new insights into the anti-atherosclerotic mechanisms of Tan IIA and offer a strong theoretical foundation for its potential therapeutic application in AS treatment.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Tan IIA (Cat# T4952, Sigma, USA); SAG (Cat# HY-12848) and BAPTA-AM (Cat# HY-100545) were obtained from MCE (USA); Oil Red O (ORO) solution (Cat# G1260, Solarbio, China); HE staining kit (Cat# G1005, Servicebio, China); IL-1β ELISA kits (Cat# BSKM60085), IL-6 ELISA kits (Cat# bsk12004), TNF-α ELISA kits (Cat# bsk12002) were purchased from Bioss Biotechnology (China); DMEM medium (Cat# 11965092), and FBS (Cat# A5256701) were obtained from Gibco (USA); CCK8 kit (Cat# C0038) and Fluo-4 calcium assay kit were obtained from Beyotime (China); FuGENE HD transfection reagent (Cat# E2311, Promega, USA); cDNA synthesis kit (Cat# EG15133S, Yugong, China); ECL reagent (Cat# RPN418-1, Cytiva, China); cholesterol efflux assay kit (Cat# ab196985), goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cat# ab6721), primary antibodies against IL-1β (Cat# ab234437), IL-6 (Cat# ab290735), TNF-α (Cat# ab183218), β-actin (Cat# ab8227), ABCA1 (Cat# ab18180), ABCB1 (Cat# ab170904), SHH (Cat# ab308225), and Gli1 (Cat# AB307977) were purchased from Abcam (UK); antibodies against SMO (Cat# PA5-76145) and TRPM2 (Cat# PA1-46473) were obtained from Thermo Fisher (USA).

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) construction and production

To achieve TRPM2 overexpression in vivo, recombinant AAV serotype 9 carrying the mouse TRPM2 coding sequence was constructed. Briefly, the full-length mouse TRPM2 cDNA was cloned into the pAAV-CMV-MCS-3FLAG vector. AAV9 particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the pAAV-TRPM2 vector, pAAV9 capsid plasmid, and pHelper plasmid. Viral particles were purified by iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation and titrated by Quantitative PCR (qPCR). For in vivo delivery, mice were injected with 1 × 10¹¹ vg of AAV9-TRPM2 per mouse via tail vein.

Animal modeling

ApoE⁻/⁻ male mice (aged: 8 weeks; weight: 20–22 g) were obtained from Weitonglihua Corporation (Beijing, China). After one week of adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly assigned to different groups (n = 10) using a computer-generated random number sequence. The control group continued on a regular diet, while the remaining mice were given a high-fat diet (HFD) to develop the AS model. After eight weeks of HFD feeding, mice in the treatment group were subjected to daily gavage of Tan IIA (15 mg/kg). To activate the Hh signaling pathway, SAG (20 mg/kg), an Hh pathway activator, was administered intraperitoneally every two days. After four weeks, body weights of all mice were documented. The mice were then euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg), and aortic tissues and blood samples were collected. The sample size was determined using G*Power software (version 3.1). During the outcome assessment, the investigators were blinded to the group allocations to minimize bias. All experiments received approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval No. 20200385), and complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

AS lesion analyses

After the mice were euthanized, the heart and entire aorta was rapidly excised. Perivascular adipose tissue was meticulously excised, and the aorta was incised longitudinally along the midline. The samples were incubated in 0.5% ORO solution at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, excess stain was removed by differentiation in 60% isopropanol until a clear background was achieved. The stained aortas were then positioned flat against a clean white surface for image acquisition. After thorough PBS washing, the aortic arch was paraffin-embedded and sliced into 5 μm sections. Aortic arch sections underwent a 10-minute exposure to ORO at room temperature after undergoing dewaxing and rehydration. Excess stain was removed by differentiation in 60% isopropanol.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

Sections underwent dewaxing and dehydration before being stained with a HE staining kit, as per the manufacturer’s guidelines. Stained sections were subsequently observed and imaged under a microscope.

Serum lipid levels measurement

Blood samples from mice were kept at 4 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 3000 g for 5 min to isolate the serum. Serum lipid levels were measured using methods described in previous literature42.

ELISA

Mouse ELISA kits were employed to measure serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, following the manufacturer’s directions.

Cell culture

RAW 264.7 mouse macrophages were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. To maintain sterility, penicillin-streptomycin (1%) was included. Foam cell formation was induced through exposing the cells to 50 µg/mL oxLDL for 48 h. For drug treatment, to activate the Hh signaling pathway, cells were treated with 200 nM SAG for 24 h. For calcium chelation experiments, cells were incubated with 20 µM BAPTA-AM for 20 min.

CCK8 assay

Following overnight incubation in 96-well plates, RAW 264.7 cells were subjected to different doses of Tan IIA for 24 h. Each well received 10 µL of CCK8 reagent, and absorbance values were detected utilizing a microplate reader.

Cell transfection

After seeding cells into 6-well plates and achieving roughly 70% confluence, TRPM2 was introduced into the cells with FuGENE HD transfection reagent, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Following 24 h of transfection, the cells were harvested for further experiments.

ORO staining

RAW 264.7 cells were incubated with Tan IIA or a vehicle control in 6-well plates for 24 h. The cells were rinsed using PBS after treatment, fixed in paraformaldehyde and stained with ORO solution. The subsequent steps were carried out following the protocol used for aortic arch ORO staining.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

TRIzol reagent was employed to isolate RNA from aortic arch tissues and RAW264.7 macrophages. Following this, the cDNA synthesis kit was used to synthesize cDNA, and then qPCR was performed with the SYBR Green kit. Western blot

Proteins were isolated with RIPA buffer, and their levels were quantified by a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated using electrophoresis and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4 °C with appropriately diluted primary antibodies. Following washes, membranes were treated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected with ECL reagent and analyzed for gray intensity using ImageJ software.

Dil-oxLDL uptake detection

RAW 264.7 cells underwent a 24-hour pretreatment with Tan IIA, followed by a 6-hour incubation with 20 µg/mL Dil-oxLDL at 37 °C. Following treatment, cells were fixed using paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI. Following washes, fluorescence signals were visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope.

The detection of cholesterol efflux

The measurement of cholesterol efflux in RAW 264.7 macrophages was conducted using a Cholesterol Efflux Assay Kit following the previously published protocols43.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

RAW264.7 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors to extract total proteins, which were subsequently quantified. Equal amounts of total protein were incubated with an anti-TRPM2 antibody or an IgG isotype control at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, Protein A/G magnetic beads were added, and the mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 3 h. Following extensive washing, the immunoprecipitates were resuspended in 2× SDS loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. The supernatants were then harvested for Western blot analysis.

Intracellular Ca²⁺ levels measurement

Intracellular Ca²⁺ levels in RAW264.7 macrophages were measured using a Fluo-4 calcium assay kit. Following the indicated treatments, cells were washed with PBS and then incubated with 100 µL of Fluo-4 staining solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed with HBSS buffer to remove unbound dye. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used to conduct data analysis. To compare two groups, an unpaired t-test was utilized, and for multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was employed. Experimental data were expressed as mean±standard deviation (±s). P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. At least three biological replicates were performed for each experiment (n ≥ 3).
