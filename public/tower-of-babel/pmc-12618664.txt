Interpretable intrusion detection for IoT environments using a self-attention-based explainable AI framework
Authors: Kanta Prasad Sharma, Tapsi Nagpal, Tarak Vora, Anupam Yadav, Muhammad Irsyad Abdullah, B. Jayaprakash, Aditya Kashyap, G. Sridevi, A. Bhowmik, Bethelehem Burju Bukate
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of Internet of Things (IoT) devices has introduced critical cybersecurity challenges due to the large attack surface and diverse interconnected systems. This work proposes a novel self-attention deep neural network (SA-DNN) augmented with a learnable feature gating (LFG) mechanism. The LFG component dynamically emphasizes security-relevant features during training, while suppressing redundant data. This architecture enables seamless, end-to-end feature optimization. It enhances the robustness of the model across varied intrusion scenarios without the need for conventional manual or filter-based feature selection. The self-attention module efficiently captures long-range relationships within network traffic, while the LFG mechanism boosts the adaptability of feature representations. The framework is explainable through shapley additive explanations (SHAP) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) and provides transparency in intrusion detection decisions. Evaluations on IoT datasets BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT show superior performance of the proposed method over baseline and state-of-the-art methods, achieving accuracy values of 99.3% and 99.6%, respectively. Additional experiments on the general network dataset UNSW-NB15 demonstrate strong generalizability with 97.9% accuracy. This confirms the robustness of the model across diverse intrusion scenarios. The proposed approach provides a lightweight, scalable, and interpretable solution with strong generalizability across IoT and broader network security settings.

Introduction

Recently, the IoT has become popular due to enhanced technology, increased access to the Internet, and the generation of big data. But this growth has also come with increased risks, which can be noticed from the emergence of intrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT networks. IDS are highly useful for the detection of suspicious or unusual activity in such networks, raising alarms that help deter the exploitation of compromised devices. The distributed and massive nature of IoT networks, which include numerous and heterogeneous devices used for different purposes, has created new problems associated with data storage, computational capabilities, and security. Therefore, the creation of effective and responsive solutions is needed1–3.

On the basis of the applied model, traditional IDS techniques are broadly categorized into two types: those based on signature matching and those based on anomaly detection. The main strength of signature-based methods is that they rely on specific patterns of attack. Previous work shows difficulties in handling zero-day attacks, and such approaches involve trade-offs, including considerable storage and computation requirements, making them unfit for IoT4. Conversely, anomaly-based IDS methods identify variations from normal traffic patterns and thus can detect new attack patterns. However, anomaly-based systems often generate high false positive rates, as benign anomalies may also be flagged as malicious activities, highlighting the need for advanced detection methodologies5.

The proliferation of IoT devices has increased the potential for cyberattacks due to heterogeneous devices, limited computational resources, and dynamic network environments. Existing IDS solutions struggle with zero-day attacks, high false positives, and the need for manual feature selection, thereby creating a need for a robust, automated, and explainable intrusion detection framework.

IoT networks span across various layers of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model, where each layer is unique in terms of risk and attack surface. At the physical layer, potential adversarial behaviors include jamming and eavesdropping, where transmission of data may be disrupted or confidentiality compromised, respectively. The data link layer can face problems like collisions and replay attacks, which interfere with the rules used to send packets6. The network layer, responsible for moving data between devices, is vulnerable to sinkhole and Sybil attacks that can make the network unreliable. Likewise, at the transport layer, which is responsible for delivering data reliably, DoS and session hijacking attacks can flood the system or intrude and take over an unauthorized session, respectively. Finally, the application layer, which connects directly with users, is often targeted by malware, phishing, and ransomware attacks that try to steal data or disrupt services7,8.

New machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods also offer potential solutions to these difficulties. These techniques help design systems that are capable of learning across multiple layers of the OSI model to provide better ways of detecting attacks9,10. In particular, the application of attention mechanisms improves IDS performance because they allow models to focus on important features and dependencies in IoT network data. This capability is necessary for detecting complex attack patterns while excluding excessive noise and false alarms11. Consequently, this research on intrusion detection proposes an attention-based deep learning approach for dynamic and resource-constrained IoT environments.

In contrast to traditional approaches, this model avoids the use of filter-based feature selection by employing an end-to-end learning framework that independently extracts relevant features from IoT-specific datasets. Applying attention mechanisms helps the model achieve better results in identifying attacks across all layers of the OSI model and enhance protection against many types of cyber threats. Moreover, the adoption of post-hoc interpretability tools such as SHAP and LIME is useful for decision-making, since they make it easier to explain the results to other stakeholders and build trust in the system. For better practical usability, the suggested approach is evaluated on IoT-related dataset as well as on non-IoT datasets, offering a scalable, efficient, and explainable technique to secure networks.

While several recent works have leveraged deep learning and attention-based models for IoT intrusion detection, most existing approaches still suffer from static feature selection, limited interpretability, and restricted validation across heterogeneous datasets. To address these gaps, our work introduces a dynamic LFG mechanism within a Self-Attention framework, incorporates explainability through SHAP and LIME, and validates performance on both IoT-specific and general-purpose datasets. These aspects distinguish our approach from prior studies and establish its novelty in terms of adaptability, interpretability, and generalizability.

Although the primary focus of this research is intrusion detection in IoT environments, we additionally evaluate the framework on the UNSW-NB15 dataset. Unlike BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, which are IoT-specific, UNSW-NB15 captures a broader spectrum of modern network intrusions. Its inclusion allows us to examine the cross-domain adaptability of the proposed SA-DNN + LFG model and to demonstrate that the framework is effective not only for IoT security but also for general-purpose intrusion detection scenarios.

Related works

Intrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT have undergone significant development, with diverse approaches ranging from classical machine learning to deep learning and distributed paradigms. This section reviews important studies, pointing out both their strengths and weaknesses, and explains how our proposed SA-DNN + LFG framework positions into this context.

Kumar et al.12 proposed a unified IDS framework that brought multiple detection systems together under one structure. This modular design improved flexibility but it also added complexity and computational overhead, which limited its scalability. In recent years, deep learning has gained attention in IDS because of its ability to identify complex attack patterns. For example, Saba et al.13 used CNNs and LSTMs to capture spatial and temporal traffic features, achieving strong results in DDoS detection. However, their models required significant resources make them difficult to deploy on IoT devices with limited capacity. Similarly, Awajan14 showed that deep neural networks could scale in large IoT environments, but their reliance on high-dimensional data increased training time and reduced adaptability. These studies highlight the need for lightweight yet effective deep learning approaches. This gap is addressed in this work using learnable feature gating, which reduces redundancy without compromising detection accuracy.

Graph-based methods have also been explored. Lo et al.15 proposed E-Graph SAGE, which is a GNN-based framework to effectively capture spatial–temporal dependencies of multi-stage IoT attacks. Despite its strength in modeling relationships, the framework faces scalability issues in large IoT networks with sparse samples. Similarly, Li et al.16 applied semi-supervised learning with GANs (HDA-IDS) to detect outliers in skewed data distributions. Although effective in handling imbalanced datasets, GAN training remains computationally expensive. This limits the real-time applicability. Our proposed SA-DNN with attention avoids such overhead by capturing long-range relationships efficiently, while the LFG module adaptively emphasizes discriminative features to maintain scalability.

Lightweight IDS designs, such as those by Azimjonov and Kim17, optimized linear SVMs with feature selection to balance accuracy and computational cost. However, these simplified models often lack the flexibility to adapt to evolving attack types. Anwar et al.18 addressed privacy concerns using federated learning, which preserved data confidentiality by training models locally and aggregating updates centrally. While effective in enhancing privacy and fault tolerance, this approach introduces challenges in communication overhead and synchronization. Shahin et al.19 applied transfer learning to mitigate recalibration issues but its efficiency across heterogeneous IoT datasets remains uncertain. By comparison, our framework not only reduces computational cost through feature gating but also ensures interpretability with SHAP and LIME. This bridges the gap between efficiency, adaptability, and transparency.

Beyond these, optimization and distributed computing techniques have been explored to enhance IDS. Vakili et al.20 combined Grey Wolf Optimization with MapReduce for scalable IoT service composition, while Heidari et al.21 developed blockchain-based IDS for drone communication. These works demonstrated scalability and trustworthiness but introduced energy overhead and limited real-world validation. Similarly, Amiri et al.22,23 surveyed AI methods in IoT healthcare, and Zanbouri et al.24 optimized blockchain-IIoT environments using swarm intelligence. These contributions illustrate the breadth of AI applications but highlight persistent issues of complexity, energy cost, and generalizability. Our SA-DNN + LFG specifically addresses these by offering a lightweight and scalable IDS validated on multiple IoT and general datasets.

Recent research has also advanced intrusion detection in IoT and related domains using collaborative and federated learning approaches. For example, Khan et al.25 proposed a collaborative SRU network with dynamic behaviour aggregation, aiming to reduce communication overhead and enhance explainability in smart healthcare systems. Khan et al.26 introduced a Federated-Boosting scheme for consumer IoT, which addresses data imbalance and overfitting challenges while preserving device privacy through distributed training. Similarly, Khan et al.27 presented Fed-Inforce-Fusion, a federated reinforcement-based intrusion detection framework for Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) networks, which enhances privacy and security by leveraging reinforcement learning for adaptive aggregation. These studies highlight the growing role of federated and distributed learning for privacy-preserving, resource-efficient intrusion detection. However, they primarily focus on collaborative learning paradigms, whereas our work distinguishes itself by employing a self-attention-based architecture with learnable feature gating and post-hoc interpretability (SHAP and LIME) to provide a lightweight and transparent IDS tailored to IoT environments.

Dataset description

This study employs three publicly available datasets: N-BaIoT, BoT-IoT, and UNSW-NB15, for the training and evaluation of the proposed SA-DNN + LFG model. These datasets were selected due to their comprehensiveness, realistic representation of IoT and general-purpose network traffic, and wide acceptance as benchmarks in the intrusion detection research community. BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT are IoT-specific datasets that capture realistic smart device traffic under a variety of benign and attack scenarios, while UNSW-NB15 provides a broader perspective by including diverse modern attack categories beyond IoT-specific threats. Together, these datasets ensure that the proposed model is rigorously evaluated for both IoT environments and more general intrusion detection scenarios.

N-BaIoT dataset

Meidan et al.5 established the N-BaIoT dataset for benchmark testing of IoT intrusion detection systems when they made it publicly accessible through Kaggle in 2018. The tool functions as an industry-standard resource to combat smart IoT device botnet attacks because it specifically focuses on this security problem. Nine commercially available IoT devices composed of Edimax IP cameras and Philips B120N/10 baby monitors and Wemo smart plugs underwent an infection process to create this dataset. Two dominating botnets in the IoT field belong to them: Mirai and BASHLITE. The botnets conduct wide-ranging distributed denial-of-service attacks through vulnerable IoT devices. The dataset contains more than 7 million time series records, each characterized by 115 statistical features extracted from raw network traffic flows. These features include packet-level and connection-level statistics, such as:Packet jitter and entropy (e.g., H_L0.01_mean, HH_jit_L0.1_mean)Mutual information measures (e.g., MI_dir_L0.1_variance)Directional statistics for inbound/outbound flowsInter-arrival times, packet lengths, and flow durations

The traffic is labeled into 11 categories: one benign class and 10 attack types, including gafgyt_combo, gafgyt_junk, gafgyt_scan, mirai_syn, mirai_udpplain, and more. Each attack type corresponds to a specific mode of botnet exploitation, such as port scanning, TCP/UDP flooding, or malformed packet generation. These categories were chosen based on real-world attack signatures and thus reflect practical threats in modern IoT networks. In this research, it prefers to use specifically utilized data from the Danmini Doorbell, which includes both benign and malicious traffic related to multiple botnet attacks. This focused approach reduces complexity while maintaining representative traffic patterns necessary for robust model evaluation.

BoT-IoT dataset

The BoT-IoT dataset, developed by the University of New South Wales, Canberra, is a comprehensive benchmark dataset designed to evaluate intrusion detection systems within IoT environments. Generated in a realistic cyber range setting, the dataset simulates a wide range of normal and malicious traffic involving various IoT devices and services. It includes attack types such as Distributed Denial of Service, Denial of Service, OS and service scanning, keylogging, and data exfiltration, all performed over multiple protocols like TCP, UDP, and HTTP. The dataset is provided in multiple formats which includes packet captures, Argus flow files, and preprocessed CSV files with a total of over 72 million records. Each entry in the BoT-IoT dataset is represented by 46 extracted features, encompassing statistical, flow-based, and time-based characteristics. These features include packet size statistics, flow duration, inter-arrival times, source/destination IPs and ports, and protocol flags, among others. The dataset includes a class label identifying the traffic as either benign or one of several attack categories, making it suitable for supervised learning approaches.

UNSW-NB15 dataset

To further evaluate the adaptability of the proposed framework beyond IoT-specific traffic, the UNSW-NB15 dataset was incorporated. This dataset was created at the Cyber Range Lab of the Australian Centre for Cyber Security (ACCS) using the IXIA PerfectStorm tool to generate hybrid modern network traffic. It includes both benign traffic and nine categories of malicious activities: Fuzzers, Analysis, Backdoors, DoS, Exploits, Generic attacks, Reconnaissance, Shellcode, and Worms. These categories represent a diverse set of threat vectors, from protocol misuse and reconnaissance to malware injection and shellcode execution.

The dataset comprises approximately 2.54 million records with 49 features extracted using Argus and Bro-IDS tools. It is divided into a training set of 175,341 records and a test set of 82,332 records. Compared to BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, UNSW-NB15 is more challenging due to its overlap of attack classes, higher intra-class variability, and greater similarity between benign and malicious flows. These characteristics make it an effective benchmark for evaluating the generalizability, robustness, and resilience of intrusion detection frameworks.

Data preprocessing

To this end, in the proposed methodology, preprocessing techniques are applied to ensure that the IoT network traffic datasets used in training the SA-DNN + LFG model are properly standardized and encoded, with class distribution verified to be fairly balanced. The first steps include the elimination of some of the features in the dataset that are not destined to assist in intrusion detection, including pkSeqID, seq, and subcategory. More specific subcategories such as “Theft” are then removed in order to make the dataset even more relevant. Ports represented in hexadecimal are converted to decimal format to standardize numerical features.

Scaling

StandardScaler is a preprocessing utility and normalizes the features so that they have a mean of zero and a unit variance. This makes certain that the features participate as equal in the completion of model training so that features with higher variance will not overwhelm others. Feature extraction

The feature extraction strategies differ across datasets due to the distinct nature of their traffic characteristics. For the BoT-IoT dataset, correlation-based feature selection was employed, whereas for N-BaIoT, a Random Forest–based technique was applied. For the UNSW-NB15 dataset, we retained the feature set provided by the original creators, consisting of 49 statistical and flow-based attributes, which capture packet-level, time-based, and content-based characteristics of network traffic. This approach preserves the heterogeneity of UNSW-NB15 and allows testing the generalizability of the proposed framework without additional feature engineering.

BoT-IoT

In the case of BoT-IoT dataset, features like Total Length of Fwd Packets, Flow Bytes/s, Fwd Packet Length Mean and Fwd IAT Mean are considered. It has established that these features have a very high correlation with the behavior of the network and can distinguish normal activities from such attack categories as DoS and Reconnaissance. For example, Flow Bytes/s is an integrative parameter that reflects traffic load, and Fwd IAT Mean describes temporal irregularities. By focusing on these critical features, CFS reduces computational complexity and enhances the classification accuracy of the detection model.

N-BaIoT

Feature selection is performed by Random Forest algorithm for the N-BaIoT dataset because this algorithm allows to judge the importance of features for classification tasks. This ensemble-based classifier gauges the importance of each feature in deciding between normals and anomalies in IoT network traffic. The features that were selected comprised of ‘H_L0.01_mean’, ‘MI_dir_L0.01_mean’, ‘H_L0.1_mean’, ‘MI_dir_L0.1_mean’, ‘H_L0.01_variance’, ‘MI_dir_L0.01_variance’, ‘MI_dir_L0.1_variance’, ‘H_L1_mean’, ‘H_L0.1_variance’, and ‘HH_jit_L0.1_mean’, among others. These features represent statistical measures and mutual information values that capture patterns in data, such as entropy and direction-based variance.

UNSW-NB15

The UNSW-NB15 dataset includes 49 features generated using the Argus and Bro-IDS tools, spanning categories such as flow duration, packet size statistics, protocol flags, transaction content features, and connection behaviors. Examples include dur (record total duration), sbytes (source-to-destination bytes), dbytes (destination-to-source bytes), spkts (source packets), dpkts (destination packets), and sttl/dttl (source/destination TTL values). These features collectively capture traffic intensity, packet exchange behavior, and flow-level semantics. Since the dataset is already balanced across multiple modern attack categories, no additional manual feature reduction was performed. Instead, all 49 features were provided to the SA-DNN + LFG model, ensuring a fair assessment of its adaptability to non-IoT traffic.

Sampling process

The datasets used in this work exhibit varying degrees of class imbalance. For BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, severe imbalance exists between majority classes (e.g., normal traffic or DDoS) and minority classes (e.g., Keylogging, Data Exfiltration, gafgyt_combo). To address this, we applied a combination of random oversampling and random undersampling strategies. For UNSW-NB15, however, the dataset already provides a reasonably balanced distribution across its nine major attack categories (e.g., DoS, Exploits, Fuzzers, Backdoors, Shellcode, Worms, etc.) and normal traffic. Therefore, no additional resampling was applied to UNSW-NB15, allowing us to preserve the dataset’s native distribution for evaluating cross-domain generalizability.

Random oversampling

The random oversampling approach has been applied to increase the frequency of minority classes. For example, in the datasets, attack categories like Data Exfiltration or Keylogging might have far fewer samples compared to normal traffic or DDoS attacks. Oversampling duplicates these minority class instances to ensure their representation matches the majority classes. For the case of N-BaIoT dataset, minority classes like gafgyt_combo (59,718 samples before sampling) were increased using random oversampling to achieve a uniform distribution of 100,000 samples each. This process added more instances to the minority classes, improving their presence in the dataset and allowing the model to learn their behaviour effectively.

Random undersampling

It is also noted that after the oversampling, there is still quite imbalance in the dataset the reason being that normal traffic dominates most of the network connections. To do so, undersampling was carried on all classes and all of them were brought to a fixed level. For example, if each attack type was oversampled to 72,919 samples, normal traffic was undersampled to this same number. For the case of N-BaIoT dataset, classes such as mirai_udp which had 237,665 samples before sampling were reduced through random undersampling to a class size of 100,000 samples. This process helped to reduce the dominance of majority classes, ensuring that the model does not bias toward the larger classes during training.

Data splits

For all datasets, the samples were randomly shuffled and split into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% testing sets. Stratified sampling was applied where applicable to preserve class distributions. For UNSW-NB15, stratification was performed across all attack categories to ensure balanced evaluation.

Learnable feature gating layer

The ability of the proposed model can be enhanced by prioritize the discriminative features from the high-dimensional IoT traffic data. This study incorporates a Learnable Feature Gating layer before the classification stage. Unlike traditional feature selection techniques, the LFG layer enables the network to dynamically learn which features to emphasize or suppress during training through gradient-based optimization.

After applying random over-and under-sampling to balance the class distribution, the sampled feature vectors are passed through the LFG layer. This module applies a gating mechanism, where each input feature is modulated by a learnable gate value between 0 and 1. The gate values are determined by a shallow neural layer trained jointly with the rest of the model.

Classification

To address such challenges in intrusion detection in IoT networks, there is a proposed more enhanced model called Self-Attention Deep Neural Network. It incorporates self-attention mechanisms to prioritize significant patterns within the dataset and eliminate noise, enabling accurate classification of network traffic into predefined categories.

Architecture of SA-DNN

The SA-DNN architecture begins with an input layer that shares preprocessed features; for instance, normalized numerical representation of attributes of a specific network. Several fully connected layers then ensue, and they establish representations of features of progressively higher order through non-linear relations. These layers are mathematically defined as:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{z}=\mathcal{W}\cdot {x}_{i}^{\prime}+b$$\end{document}where, z is the layer output, W is weights matrix, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}_{i}^{\prime}$$\end{document} is the input vector and b is the bias term. Dropout layers are interspersed to mitigate overfitting by randomly deactivating neurons during training.

A distinctive aspect of SA-DNN is the integration of a self-attention layer. This layer computes attention scores to focus on the most relevant interactions between features. The attention mechanism operates as follows:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Attention(Q,K,V)=softmax\left(\frac{\mathcal{Q}{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{{d}_{k}}}\right)V$$\end{document}

Here, Q, K and V are the query, key, and value matrices derived from the input features, and dk represents the dimensionality of the key vector. This mechanism identifies and emphasizes critical temporal and spatial dependencies in IoT traffic. The final output layer employs a softmax activation function to produce class probabilities, determining the likelihood of each traffic sample belonging to specific attack or normal categories:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P\left(y=\left.c\right|x\right)=\frac{{e}^{{z}_{e}}}{\sum {j}^{{{e}{z}_{j}}}}$$\end{document}

This produces the likelihood of each sample belonging to specific categories, such as Normal traffic or various attack types. The model is trained by minimizing the categorical cross entropy loss function:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{c=1}^{C}{\mathcal{Y}}_{i,c}\text{log}{\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}}_{i,c}$$\end{document}where N is the number of samples, C is the number of classes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{Y}}_{i,c}$$\end{document} is the true label, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}}_{i,c}$$\end{document} is the predicted probability. Weight updates are performed using gradient descent:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W\to W-\eta \frac{\partial L}{\partial W}$$\end{document}where η is the learning rate. This iterative optimization process ensures that the model converges to an optimal set of parameters, enabling it to classify IoT network traffic with high accuracy while minimizing false positives.

The SA-DNN + LFG model has seven stages which unite deep learning components with attention and gating systems to boost IoT network intrusion detection. The input layer processes normalized and preprocessed feature vectors whose dimensions depend on the dataset while using ReLU activation between three dense layers with neuron counts of 128 followed by 64 followed by 32 respectively. The dropout layer rate set to 0.3 exists after each dense layer to stop overfitting. A Multi-Head Self-Attention layer with four heads and 16-dimensional key/query/value vectors follows, enabling the model to focus on critical inter-feature dependencies. Outputs from the MHSA are concatenated, passed through a feedforward sub-layer, and normalized. Next, the Latent Feature Gating module applies a sigmoid-activated gating mechanism to filter irrelevant latent features, improving the signal-to-noise ratio. A batch normalization layer further stabilizes training. The final classification block includes a dense layer with 16 neurons with ReLU activation function, followed by an output layer using softmax activation. The number of output classes is equivalent to five for BoT-IoT and up to eleven for N-BaIoT. This architecture, by combining attention and latent gating with dense representations, captures complex attack patterns and improves classification performance across diverse IoT datasets.

Notably, the architectural choices, including the use of four attention heads with 16-dimensional key, query, and value vectors along with a lightweight gating mechanism, were intentionally selected to ensure computational efficiency and enable deployment on resource-constrained IoT devices.

Explainable AI models

SHAP

SHAP explains predictions by computing Shapley values, which represent each feature’s marginal contribution toward the model output. For a model f(x), the SHAP value for a feature i is calculated as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\phi }_{i}=\sum_{S\subseteq N\backslash \left\{i\right\}}\frac{\left|S\right|!\left(\left|N\right|-\left|S\right|-1\right)!}{\left|N\right|!}\left[f\left(S\cup \left\{i\right\}\right)-f\left(S\right)\right]$$\end{document}where, S is a subset of features excluding i, N is the set of all features, f(S) is the model’s prediction considering only the features in S. In our work, SHAP highlights the most influential features in IoT traffic (e.g., protocol type, port number), thereby improving interpretability of the SA-DNN + LFG model.

LIME

LIME provides local explanations by approximating the complex model f with a simpler interpretable model g in the neighborhood of a given sample x. This is formalized as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Explanation(x)=arg\underset{g\in G}{\text{min}}\mathcal{L}\left(f,g,{\pi }_{x}\right)+\Omega \left(g\right)$$\end{document}where, g is the surrogate interpretable model, G is the set of interpretable models, L(f, g, πx) measures how well g approximates f locally around x, πx defines the locality around x, and Ω(g) enforces simplicity on g. In our work, LIME identifies why individual IoT traffic samples are classified as benign or malicious, complementing SHAP with case-specific interpretability.

Computational complexity analysis

The proposed SA-DNN + LFG framework involves three major components: preprocessing, feature selection, and classification. Preprocessing and feature extraction operations are linear in nature and scale as O(N·F), where N is the number of samples and F is the number of features. The Learnable Feature Gating (LFG) layer introduces only a lightweight overhead of O(F), since each feature is gated independently. The self-attention mechanism, which forms the core of SA-DNN, has a time complexity of O(F2·d) and space complexity of O(F2), where d is the dimensionality of the query vectors. The dense layers contribute an additional cost of O(N·F·H), where H denotes the hidden neurons.

During training, the total complexity can be approximated as: O(E·N·(F2 + F·H)), where E is the number of epochs. Inference scales linearly with the feature dimension (O(F·H + F2)), which ensures efficiency for real-time intrusion detection. The design of the SA-DNN + LFG, including the lightweight gating and compact attention module, makes the framework inherently suitable for deployment in resource-constrained IoT environments.

Explainability and model transparency

The interpretability of the SA-DNN + LFG model is significantly enhanced using SHAP and LIME, which contribute to greater trust, accountability, and transparency in the decision-making process of intrusion detection systems. These techniques provide both quantitative and qualitative insights. SHAP delivers a global view by identifying the most influential features across all predictions. This helps security analysts understand which traffic attributes such as flow duration, packet count and TCP flags consistently influence classification outcomes. This enables better system tuning and policy development. LIME provides local interpretability by perturbing the input and approximating the model’s behavior around a specific instance. This helps justify why a given case was flagged as malicious and supports incident response teams in root-cause analysis. Together, SHAP and LIME increase trust in deep learning models that are often perceived as black boxes. By making the detection rationale auditable and explainable, these methods support model validation, debugging, and potential regulatory compliance in security-critical IoT deployments. In IoT intrusion detection, it’s important to understand why the system flags certain network events as malicious, helping security analysts make informed decisions. To this end, the proposed SA-DNN combined with the LFG framework incorporates Explainable Artificial Intelligence techniques, specifically SHAP and LIME, to provide both global and local interpretability of the model’s predictions. By integrating SHAP and LIME, the SA-DNN combined with LFG model achieves not only high detection performance but also interpretability of its outcomes. This dual perspective enables global understanding of how features affect model behavior, along with localized justifications for specific predictions. These interpretability features contribute to better trust among cybersecurity professionals and facilitate debugging and refinement of the system during deployment. As such, explainability is a core aspect of the proposed framework, supporting both technical robustness and practical usability in real-world IoT intrusion detection scenarios.

Results and discussion

The experiments were conducted on Google Colab Pro, utilizing an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU with 15.36 GB VRAM, CUDA Version 12.2, and approximately 13.29 GB of system RAM. The environment also provided 113 GB disk space, ensuring efficient handling of data-intensive tasks and reducing computational overhead. To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed SA-DNN enhanced with a Learnable Feature Gating (LFG) module, experimental testing was carried out on two IoT-specific benchmark datasets, BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, which capture realistic IoT attack scenarios. In addition, to examine the generalizability of the model beyond IoT environments, we extended the evaluation to the UNSW-NB15 dataset, a widely used intrusion detection benchmark that contains diverse and modern attack types. This combination of IoT and non-IoT datasets allows us to validate both the suitability of the model for resource-constrained IoT networks and its adaptability to broader cybersecurity contexts.

Each experiment was repeated 10 times with different random seeds, and the reported results represent the mean and standard deviation of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score across these runs. This protocol ensures statistical reliability and avoids performance fluctuations due to stochastic training effects.

It is important to note that explainability experiments using SHAP and LIME were restricted to the IoT-specific datasets (BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT). This decision reflects their direct relevance to IoT environments and avoids interpretability bias due to heterogeneous feature sets in non-IoT benchmarks like UNSW-NB15. Robustness evaluation under adversarial conditions and noisy environments

To assess the resilience of the proposed SA-DNN + LFG model, additional experiments were conducted under two challenging scenarios: adversarial perturbations and varying levels of input noise. These tests were designed to simulate real-world conditions where attackers may attempt to evade detection through subtle feature manipulations or where IoT traffic data may be degraded due to signal noise, packet loss, or sensor inaccuracies. For adversarial robustness, a Fast Gradient Sign Method based attack was simulated by introducing perturbations with ε-values ranging from 0.01 to 0.1. The model retained an average detection accuracy above 89% up to ε = 0.05, demonstrating significant resistance to gradient-based evasion tactics. Beyond this threshold, accuracy gradually declined, but the performance degradation remained more graceful compared to conventional DNN baselines. In parallel, Gaussian noise with standard deviations (σ) of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 was injected into the feature vectors of both benign and malicious samples to simulate sensor-level distortions and environmental noise. The model maintained detection accuracy above 92% for σ = 0.05 and showed less than a 4% drop in F1-score across all tested noise levels.

The graph (Fig. 17) shows that the SA-DNN + LFG model maintains high accuracy under mild to moderate adversarial attacks and Gaussian noise, with performance gradually decreasing as perturbation intensity increases. This demonstrates the inherent robustness of the model and its ability to handle real-world uncertainties effectively. These outcomes highlight the effectiveness of the multihead self-attention mechanism and LFG in filtering out irrelevant perturbations and maintaining discriminative feature integrity.Fig. 17analysis of adversarial attacks for SA-DNN + LFG model.

Interpretability analysis using SHAP and LIME: quantitative and qualitative insights

To ensure transparency and trustworthiness in intrusion detection outcomes, SHAP and LIME were employed to interpret the predictions made by the SA-DNN + LFG model. These tools helped uncover which features most influenced specific detection decisions, enabling a deeper understanding of the model’s behavior.

Quantitative assessment

Feature attribution values generated by SHAP were aggregated across the dataset to determine globally influential features. For the N-BaIoT dataset, features such as packet_size_variance, flow_duration, and dst_port_entropy consistently received high SHAP values, indicating their strong relevance in identifying attack patterns. A feature importance ranking derived from SHAP showed over 85% alignment with LIME-based local explanations across multiple samples, validating consistency between global and local interpretability. Additionally, an average SHAP contribution threshold (≥ 0.015) was used to filter the top 10 impactful features. Detection performance using only these top features remained within 4% of the full model, suggesting that explanations can guide effective dimensionality reduction or model simplification without sacrificing accuracy.

Qualitative assessment

Visualizations from LIME provided case-specific insights by highlighting feature contributions for individual predictions say for instance: classifying Mirai-Attack traffic, LIME revealed how high flow_rate and low ttl_variance played decisive roles. These qualitative insights allow security analysts to validate predictions and trace decision logic in post-analysis workflows, supporting operational deployment in critical environments.

Impact and future directions

The insights derived from SHAP and LIME not only enhance model interpretability but also offer actionable guidance for refining intrusion detection strategies. For instance, features repeatedly shown to have minimal contribution could be pruned to reduce model complexity. Conversely, dominant features can inform future research focused on embedding domain knowledge into feature engineering or attention mechanisms. Moreover, these tools lay a foundation for adaptive IDS systems where model updates are driven by explainability feedback loops, improving resilience and transparency over time.

From a computational perspective, SA-DNN + LFG maintains efficient resource utilization. Thus, SA-DNN + LFG delivers a strong balance between detection performance and computational scalability. Computational overhead and resource efficiency

The proposed SA-DNN + LFG framework was implemented with four attention heads and 16-dimensional key, query, and value vectors, striking a balance between expressiveness and efficiency. This compact configuration enables modeling of complex inter-feature dependencies without incurring the quadratic scaling overhead typical of larger attention architectures. While Sect. "Computational complexity analysis" established the theoretical lightweight complexity of the framework, this section presents the corresponding empirical validation.

All experiments were conducted on Google Colab Pro, leveraging an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (15.36 GB GPU memory, 13.29 GB system RAM, CUDA 12.2). The average training time per epoch was approximately 28 s for the BoT-IoT dataset, 34 s for N-BaIoT, and 44 s for UNSW-NB15. Peak memory usage during training remained below 6 GB, and inference latency per instance was consistently under 3 ms, demonstrating the feasibility of near real-time deployment even on moderate edge hardware.

Compared with traditional filter-based feature selection methods such as Chi-square, mutual information, or ReliefF, which provide low-cost preprocessing but fail to capture temporal and nonlinear dependencies, the proposed approach integrates feature relevance learning directly into the model. This design removes the dependency on external feature engineering and allows dynamic adaptation to heterogeneous data distributions during both training and inference. Furthermore, the modularity of the attention mechanism permits pruning or reconfiguration for lightweight deployment on resource-constrained devices without retraining the full architecture.

From a practical standpoint, False Positive Rate (FPR) is a critical factor in intrusion detection systems. Excessive false alarms create alert fatigue and degrade operational efficiency. The SA-DNN + LFG achieves a substantially reduced FPR of 0.5% on BoT-IoT, 0.3% on N-BaIoT, and 0.8% on UNSW-NB15, consistently outperforming baselines such as BiLSTM + Attention (≈2%) and conventional classifiers like SVM and Random Forest (> 4%). By minimizing unnecessary alerts while maintaining high detection accuracy, the proposed framework ensures precision, reliability, and trustworthiness, making it suitable for deployment in both IoT-specific and general network intrusion detection contexts. These empirical observations reinforce the theoretical analysis by confirming that the framework’s lightweight design translates into practical efficiency for real-world IoT environments.

Conclusion and future work

In this work, we developed an interpretable intrusion detection framework for IoT environments by integrating SA-DNN with LFG mechanism. The proposed SA-DNN + LFG model demonstrated high accuracy on IoT-specific datasets, achieving 99.3% on BoT-IoT and 99.6% on N-BaIoT, and maintained strong generalizability on the broader UNSW-NB15 dataset with 97.9% accuracy. The model’s stability was further confirmed by reporting mean and standard deviation values across multiple experimental runs. For the IoT-specific datasets, SHAP and LIME were employed to provide interpretability of predictions, thereby enhancing transparency in feature contributions and decision-making. These results collectively validate the effectiveness, robustness, and explainability of the proposed framework for IoT intrusion detection, while demonstrating its adaptability to broader network intrusion scenarios.

In future work, we plan to extend the evaluation to additional datasets such as IoT-23 and TON_IoT to further assess cross-domain adaptability. We also plan to investigate adaptive learning strategies, including reinforcement learning for dynamic adaptation to evolving attack patterns and federated learning for privacy-preserving distributed training. These extensions are expected to strengthen the scalability and practical applicability of the framework in real-world IoT environments.
