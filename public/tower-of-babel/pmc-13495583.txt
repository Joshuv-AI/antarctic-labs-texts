Heart rate variability and heart rate in a sample of medicated and unmedicated adolescents with obsessive-compulsive disorder
Authors: Manuel Burkart, Sophie P Scheer, Lea L Backhausen, Magdalena K Wekenborg, Nicole Beyer, Maria McDonald, Veit Roessner, Nora C Vetter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlterations in autonomic nervous system activity – measured by heart rate variability (HRV) and heart rate (HR) - have been proposed as a relevant biopsychological mechanism in several psychiatric disorders. However, only few studies have examined these parameters in OCD, and evidence from pediatric samples is particularly limited. The aim of this study was to investigate autonomic activity in pediatric OCD.MethodsA five minute resting state electrocardiogram was recorded in adolescents with OCD (n = 64) and age- and IQ-matched healthy controls (HC, n = 52). Autonomic activity was compared using the logarithmized root mean square of differences between successive R-R intervals (logRMSSD), the logarithmized high-frequency HRV (logHF-HRV), the percentage of adjacent NN intervals differing from each other by more than 50 ms (pNN50) and HR. Associations with psychotropic medication status and symptom severity were assessed.ResultsAdolescents with OCD showed lower logRMSSD and higher HR than HC, whereas logHF-HRV and pNN50 did not differ between groups. No associations were found between cardiac measures and OCD symptom severity. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested lower logHF-HRV and logRMSSD in medicated adolescents with OCD.ConclusionsOur findings add to evidence for altered autonomic activity in pediatric OCD, reflected by elevated HR and lower RMSSD. The observed reduction in HRV did not increase with greater symptom severity and appeared most pronounced among medicated adolescents with OCD.SignificanceThe autonomic imbalance observable in adolescents with OCD is common across anxiety disorders and has been linked to cardiovascular risk.Keywords: Obsessive-compulsive disorder, Heart rate variability, Heart rate, Autonomic nervous system, Psychotropic medication, Symptom severity, Adolescents

Introduction

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is among the most prevalent psychiatric disorders in adolescence with a prevalence of 1–3% (Walitza et al., 2011). It is related to anxiety disorders and is characterized by obsessions that induce anxiety and compulsions performed to obtain temporary relief (World Health Organization, 2022). The disorder often follows a chronic course, with symptoms frequently persisting into adulthood, which may be prevented by early detection and treatment (Jans et al., 2007; Micali et al., 2010). However, despite marked impairments in quality of life, adolescents with OCD often conceal or downplay their symptoms, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment (Walitza et al., 2011; Wewetzer and Wewetzer, 2014). Accordingly, there is a need for additional objective, rapid and feasible diagnostic procedures for OCD, which may also serve as predictors of treatment response (Olbrich et al., 2022).

There has been growing interest in the autonomic nervous system (ANS) (Thayer et al., 2009) function, often assessed by heart rate variability (HRV), in OCD as well as in other psychiatric disorders, as this may facilitate the identification of objective biomarkers distinguishing between distinct disorders (Wang et al., 2025). HRV is defined as the quantification of variations between consecutive heartbeats over a defined period of time (McCraty and Shaffer, 2015). Under physiological conditions, the sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the ANS dynamically interact to maintain a balanced state of autonomic function (Böckelmann, 2012). Altered ANS function and decreased parasympathetic activity may underlie the link between adverse emotional states and physical disease (Hu et al., 2019; Thayer, 2020). Among HRV indices, high-frequency HRV (HF-HRV) belonging to the frequency-domain, and the root mean square of differences between successive R-R intervals (RMSSD) belonging to the time-domain, are the most prominent parameters of parasympathetic nervous system activity found in the anxiety literature (Chalmers et al., 2014). The percentage of adjacent NN intervals differing from each other by more than 50 ms (pNN50), as well as heart rate (HR), albeit only a less specific parameter of autonomic balance (Thayer, 2020), are also frequently reported in OCD studies.

Evidence of alterations in HRV and HR in OCD mainly comes from six studies in adults (Jüres et al., 2024; Olbrich et al., 2022; Pittig et al., 2013; Sandhya et al., 2022; Slaap et al., 2004; van Bennekom et al., 2020). The two earliest studies (Pittig et al., 2013; Slaap et al., 2004) were included in meta-analyses on anxiety disorders (Chalmers et al., 2014; Cheng et al., 2022), which reported reduced HF-HRV across different forms of anxiety disorders, but found no effect for OCD, despite one primary study showing lower HF-HRV in OCD compared to healthy controls (HC) (Pittig et al., 2013). Of note, OCD was underrepresented in these analyses, and both primary studies had small sample sizes and assessed only frequency-domain HRV parameters, which are associated with smaller effect sizes than time-domain parameters across anxiety disorders (Chalmers et al., 2014). Of the studies reporting HF-HRV published since then, two additional studies also found no difference between groups (Olbrich et al., 2022; Sandhya et al., 2022). In contrast, time-domain measures are more consistently associated with differences in autonomic regulation. As such, both the first study reporting such measures (Sandhya et al., 2022) and the most recent and largest study to date (Jüres et al., 2024) found evidence for lower RMSSD and pNN50 in OCD. While there is one study that did not find differences between groups in terms of pNN50 (Olbrich et al., 2022), no negative results for RMSSD have been reported by any study thus far. When interpreting these results, however, the potential impact of psychotropic medication status must be considered, as Jüres et al. (2024) did not exclude participants taking psychotropic medication, and subgroup analyses revealed that RMSSD was only diminished in medicated, but not in unmedicated patients with OCD compared to healthy controls. Consistent with this finding, the only study out of four that reported reduced HF-HRV also included medicated participants and did not find reduced HF-HRV when only considering unmedicated patients, although the sample size may have been too limited (Pittig et al., 2013). In summary, few adult studies have reported reduced HRV in OCD compared to HC, but conclusions remain complicated by differences in sample size, HRV parameters, and psychotropic medication status. Future studies with larger samples are needed that systematically assess all relevant HRV parameters and account for the influence of psychotropic medication status.

Regarding HR in adult OCD, Olbrich et al. (2022) identified elevated HR by statistically combining effect sizes from previous studies that had reported null results (Hoehn-Saric et al., 1995; Pittig et al., 2013; Slaap et al., 2004; Stein et al., 2006) and empirically confirmed this finding in their own sample. Similar to the HRV literature, the absence of group differences in earlier studies and one more recent study (Sandhya et al., 2022) may be attributable to small sample sizes (OCD n = 11–26, HC n = 14–39), whereas Olbrich et al. (2022) examined a comparatively larger sample (OCD n = 51, HC n = 28). The largest study to date (OCD n = 96, HC n = 112) also found elevated HR in OCD (Jüres et al., 2024). In that study, subgroup analyses by psychotropic medication status revealed that HR was increased only in unmedicated patients with OCD compared to HC. Notably, Olbrich et al.'s (2022) finding of elevated HR stemmed from an unmedicated sample. Taken together, evidence from studies with larger sample sizes consistently suggest that HR is elevated in adults with OCD. Preliminary data further indicate that psychotropic medication may exert a normalizing effect on HR.

In pediatric OCD, evidence on possible alterations of ANS activity is even more limited, with only three published studies to date (Rozenman et al., 2024; Thoustrup et al., 2025; Zahn et al., 1996). Importantly, these pediatric studies primarily focused on other research questions (emotion regulation, trend synchrony and family functioning), rather than on ANS alterations per se. All of the studies reported HR, but only one of them HF-HRV and RMSSD (Thoustrup et al., 2025), whereas another reported respiratory sinus arrhythmia (Rozenman et al., 2024), a parameter closely related to HF-HRV (Jüres et al., 2024), and the third assessed an HRV measure of questionable validity (Zahn et al., 1996). pNN50 was not reported in any of the studies. None of them found significant group differences in HRV, and HR was elevated only in the study with the smallest sample size (OCD n = 25, HC n = 23; Rozenman et al., 2024), but not in the studies with comparatively larger samples (Thoustrup et al., 2025: OCD n = 97, HC n = 85; Zahn et al., 1996: OCD n = 55, HC n = 58). Despite the scarcity of data, the discrepancy between pediatric and adult findings may indicate that ANS characteristics differ between pediatric and adult OCD (Zahn et al., 1996). Further studies are required to investigate whether this is indeed the case.

Beyond categorical group differences between patients with OCD and HC, it is also important to examine the association of continuous measures of OCD symptom severity and ANS measures. To date, only five studies have investigated this relationship, most of them in adults. Measures of symptom severity varied considerably across studies, with some using the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS; Havnen et al., 2013; Jüres et al., 2024; Sandhya et al., 2022) and the Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R; Jüres et al., 2024), and another the Revised Padua Inventory (Pittig et al., 2013). Three of the studies found no association between symptom severity and ANS measures despite the presence of group differences in cardiac parameters within the same samples (Jüres et al., 2024; Pittig et al., 2013; Sandhya et al., 2022). Notably, two studies with methodological deviations from the others reported associations between symptom severity and cardiac parameters. Zahn et al. (1996) combined ten different rating scales into four composite measures, but found a positive correlation with HR for only one of them in the only study of adolescents with OCD. Further, Havnen et al. (2013) recorded HRV in multiple postures in adults with OCD and found a negative correlation between HF-HRV and symptom severity exclusively in the standing position, but not in the more common seated position. Taken together, preliminary evidence suggests that OCD symptom severity is not reliably associated with cardiac parameters. Further studies, particularly in adolescents with OCD, are needed to systematically examine the relationship between ANS parameters and OCD symptom severity.

The aim of the present study was to address the existing research gaps by systematically investigating potential alterations in HR and HRV in a large sample of adolescents with OCD, as well as their association with symptom severity. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first study to comprehensively examine these parameters in this population. Investigating adolescents is essential, as it provides a unique perspective on the physiological manifestations of psychopathology in its early stage, when the influence of disease- and treatment history and lifestyle factors can be assumed to be smaller (Byrne et al., 2010; Koenig et al., 2016).

As evidence in pediatric OCD is very limited, we hypothesized that adolescents with OCD, in line with previous findings in adults, would exhibit higher resting HR and lower HRV compared to HC. HRV alterations were expected to be evident primarily in RMSSD, as studies in adults have consistently found group differences for this time-domain parameter and larger effect sizes have been observed for time- compared to frequency-domain measures across different forms of anxiety disorders. Furthermore, we expected no association between OCD symptom severity and HRV or HR. In addition, we exploratorily compared HRV and HR in subgroups with and without current psychotropic medication intake.

Methods

The study was part of a neuroimaging project on adolescents with OCD investigating reward/loss processing (McDonald et al., 2024) and the effects of an app-based mindfulness meditation training on emotion regulation and cognitive control (Rempel et al., 2023). The study was carried out in accordance with the latest guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Technical University Dresden (EK18012019). Participants and for those <18 years their legal guardians gave written informed consent. Participants received an allowance of a minimum of 10 €.

Recruitment and inclusion criteria

We recruited 94 adolescents with OCD and 62 healthy adolescents aged 10 to 19 years for the overall neuroimaging project via online advertisement, through the in- and outpatient clinic of the Department of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry at the University Hospital Dresden, and from psychotherapists in private practice. Patients in the OCD group had previously been diagnosed with OCD as primary disorder by board certified child and adolescent psychiatrists according to ICD-10 criteria (World Health Organization, 2008).

Participants were screened for exclusion criteria including neurological, developmental, or other primary psychiatric disorders, a history of psychosis, a current severe depressive episode, acute suicidal tendencies, and substance use disorders. Additionally, participants with an intelligence quotient <70 estimated by the number-connection test (ZVT; Oswald and Roth, 1987) were excluded. Following the initial screening, a clinical interview was conducted to assess lifetime psychiatric disorders in all participants. Adolescents in the OCD group were additionally administered the Children's Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (CY-BOCS; Scahill et al., 1997) by trained psychologists. A CY-BOCS <8 served as an exclusion criterion following Bossert-Zaudig and Niedermeier (2002). During the course of the study, 30 OCD patients were excluded due to an insufficient symptom severity as evidenced by the CY-BOCS (n = 10), MRI contraindications or aversion (n = 7), technical problems regarding ECG acquisition (n = 7), current use of antipsychotics (n = 3), and dropout (n = 3). In the control group, 10 participants were excluded due to either dropout (n = 4), mental health issues as per the clinical interview (n = 3), a severe traumatic brain injury in the medical history (n = 1), MRI contraindication (n = 1), or a missing body mass index (BMI) value (n = 1).

Participants

The final sample consisted of 64 adolescents with OCD and 52 matched HC (for sociodemographic and clinical characteristics see Table 1 and Supplementary Table A.1). The proportion of females did not differ between adolescents with OCD (n = 36) and HC (n = 27), χ2 (1, N = 116) = 0.08, p = .78. In the OCD sample, 22 participants had comorbid diagnoses (most frequently anxiety disorders, n = 8; Tourette's disorder, n = 6; depression, n = 4), and 28 participants currently took psychotropic medication (most frequently SSRIs, n = 27). Participants in the control group were free of psychotropic medication and psychiatric diagnoses. For detailed information on psychotropic medication status and comorbidities, see Supplementary Fig. A.1.

ECG recording

ECG recording took place after an MRI session, during which participants completed various paradigms, such as a reward or loss and an emotion regulation paradigm, as well as a resting state and structural MRI measurements. A five-minute ECG was subsequently recorded outside of the scanner in supine position. Data were recorded at a sampling rate of 500 Hz on a BrainAmp ExG MR (BrainProducts GmbH, Gilching, Germany) using BrainVision Recorder software (BrainProducts GmbH, Gilching, Germany). After cleaning the skin and applying ultrasound gel for better conductivity, two ECG electrodes were attached below the participants' left scapular. Participants were instructed to relax, to move as little as possible and to breathe normally.

HRV preprocessing and artifact removal

ECG raw data were exported into Brain Vision Analyzer 2.1 (BrainProducts GmbH, Gilching, Germany), where data were interpolated at 1000 Hz. A bandwidth filter was set at 30 Hz to remove low frequency changes such as baseline wandering and high frequency changes due to movement artifacts (Bansal et al., 2009). Data were then preprocessed using automatic R-wave detection and visual inspection, checking for movement artifacts, extrasystoles, and missing or incorrectly detected R-waves. A manual correction of the R-waves was performed if necessary. IBIs were subsequently calculated and exported to Kubios Standard Version 3.2.0 (Kubios Oy, Kuopio, Finland) for artifact correction and HRV parameter calculation. A maximum correction limit of 10% of the deflections per data set was applied. Further analyses included RMSSD, HF-HRV absolute power (frequency band 0.15–0.4 Hz), and pNN50 as HRV parameters. The entire five-minute time window was used for analysis. Additionally, HR was included in the quantification of ANS activity in accordance with previous studies (Jüres et al., 2024; Olbrich et al., 2022). Because HRV measures were not normally distributed, a log-transformation for RMSSD and HF-HRV was performed to reduce skewness. Due to its bounded nature as a percentage-based measure, we did not log-transform pNN50. HR data did not require transformation.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (Version 4.6.1; R Core Team, 2026) and JASP (Version 0.19.3., JASP Team, 2025). For the group comparison of HRV and HR, univariate analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) were performed. In accordance with previous studies, we included age (in years), sex, and BMI (kg/m2, based on self-reported height and weight) as covariates. These variables have been shown to influence HRV (Koenig and Thayer, 2016). As previous studies in psychiatric patients have also highlighted the importance of diurnal variation in cardiac parameters (Agorastos et al., 2013), with HRV showing an increase from approximately noon until night time and HR showing the inverse pattern in healthy individuals (Guo and Stein, 2003; Jarczok et al., 2019), we further included the time each assessment was conducted at as a covariate. We did not tightly control this factor during data collection for practical reasons. Outliers with standardized residuals greater than 3 were excluded from analysis. ANCOVAs were followed up with bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests. We calculated Pearson correlation scores of symptom severity and cardiac parameters. For all statistical tests, an alpha level of p < .05 was chosen.

Results

For results of the ANCOVAs adjusted for sex, age, BMI, and time of assessment of the HRV parameters and HR, see Table 2 and Fig. 1. Discussion

The main aim of this study was to systematically investigate alterations in HRV and HR in adolescents with OCD compared to HC, while accounting for the potential influence of symptom severity and psychotropic medication status. These questions have previously been examined in adult samples with somewhat mixed results (Jüres et al., 2024; Olbrich et al., 2022; Pittig et al., 2013; Sandhya et al., 2022; Slaap et al., 2004; van Bennekom et al., 2020). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate these alterations in adolescents with OCD while explicitly considering the additional factors of symptom severity and psychotropic medication status. As hypothesized, adolescents with OCD exhibited lower HRV and higher HR than HC, indicating altered ANS activity. No associations were found between symptom severity and cardiac parameters. Exploratory subgroup analyses further revealed that only those taking psychotropic medication showed lower HRV, whereas the elevated HR compared to HC was not driven by either patient group in particular.

Overall group differences in HRV and HR

Results for HRV revealed that logRMSSD was lower in adolescents with OCD, indicating altered ANS activity (Thayer et al., 2009). Despite being an important HRV parameter due to its robustness to respiratory influences, which were not controlled for in previous studies, RMSSD remains largely understudied in OCD. Most studies in adults have focused on frequency-domain parameters, and only two have reported RMSSD results, both showing lower values in OCD compared to HC (Jüres et al., 2024; Sandhya et al., 2022). In contrast, the only prior study in adolescents reporting RMSSD found no alterations (Thoustrup et al., 2025). This inconsistency in findings might be attributable to methodological differences. Specifically, Thoustrup et al. (2025) excluded adolescents taking antidepressants or antipsychotics, whereas Jüres et al.'s (2024) large adult sample and our adolescent sample did not. Moreover, a parent was present during ECG recording in the other pediatric study, which may have exerted a normalizing effect on HRV in adolescents with OCD (Thoustrup et al., 2025). In contrast, logHF-HRV did not differ between OCD and HC, consistent with evidence suggesting larger effect sizes for time versus frequency-domain parameters across different forms of anxiety disorders (Chalmers et al., 2014). Our null finding also aligns with previous studies in OCD, as only one adult study to date has reported lower HF-HRV (Pittig et al., 2013), while several adult (Olbrich et al., 2022; Sandhya et al., 2022; Slaap et al., 2004), and one pediatric study (Thoustrup et al., 2025) observed no group differences. Interestingly, despite the high correlation frequently observed between RMSSD and pNN50 (Agorastos et al., 2023), both time-domain measures with similar physiological interpretation, we did not find evidence for lower pNN50 in adolescents with OCD in our study. It has previously been noted that some mental disorders may be characterized by unique HRV profiles, making specific parameters more suitable than others (Wang et al., 2025). Taken together, current evidence suggests that altered parasympathetic nervous system activity in OCD may manifest primarily in RMSSD rather than HF-HRV or pNN50.

We found further evidence for altered ANS activity in the form of elevated HR in adolescents with OCD. While elevated HR has been consistently observed in adults with OCD, only one prior study has reported a similar effect in adolescents (Rozenman et al., 2024), whereas two others found no differences between groups (Thoustrup et al., 2025; Zahn et al., 1996). Zahn et al. (1996) suggested that the adult samples in previous research may have been characterized by greater symptom severity. In contrast, our data indicate elevated HR even in a sample that included mild cases (CY-BOCS scores between 8 and 15) with an overall moderate mean severity (M = 18). Interestingly, Thoustrup et al. (2025) found no differences in HR despite studying a more severe sample (CY-BOCS ≥16, M = 26). We therefore suggest that variability in the control group characteristics may account for some of the heterogeneity across adolescent studies. Although Zahn et al. (1996) did not report raw HR values, the other two studies in adolescents found comparable HR levels in their OCD samples (M = 81; M = 79), but markedly different HR in their control samples (M = 72, M = 78) (Rozenman et al., 2024; Thoustrup et al., 2025). Notably, adolescents in our control sample also differed from these studies in HR (M = 67), but the adolescents in our sample were slightly older, which may have contributed to these differences. Despite a growing body of literature documenting elevated HR in adult OCD, no clear picture emerges regarding HR in pediatric OCD, but our results suggest HR may also be elevated in this population.

Association of HRV and HR with symptom severity

As expected, we did not find an association between symptom severity and HRV or HR. This finding aligns with most previous research in OCD (Jüres et al., 2024; Pittig et al., 2013; Sandhya et al., 2022). To date, only one adult study has found an association between lower HRV and higher OCD symptom severity (Havnen et al., 2013), and one pediatric study found an association between elevated HR and increasing symptom severity (Zahn et al., 1996). Notably, both studies used multiple approaches to investigate the association between cardiac parameters and symptom severity, and only found relationships for some of them. As such, Zahn et al. (1996) used multiple composite measures and Havnen et al. (2013) recorded HRV in both the standing and seated position. In contrast, we employed more contemporary, gold-standard symptom measures (CY-BOCS and OCI-R; Rapp et al., 2016) and recorded cardiac activity in the supine rather than the standing position, which is more common in the literature. Studies replicating the alternative methodological choices are necessary to determine if the findings are robust or represent outliers.

Group comparison by psychotropic medication status

In our exploratory analysis of psychotropic medication status, we found reduced HRV only in medicated, but not in unmedicated adolescents with OCD compared to HC. This aligns with previous subgroup analyses of adult OCD (Jüres et al., 2024; Pittig et al., 2013) and adds evidence to the conclusion that there may be detrimental effects of psychotropic medication on HRV in OCD. A plausible explanation lies in the effects of SSRIs, the current first-line medication for OCD (Endres et al., 2025) and the predominant medication class in our sample. The influence of SSRIs on HRV across psychiatric disorders has been the subject of debate (Kemp et al., 2011; Licht et al., 2011). While conflicting results were reported in other research designs (Alvares et al., 2016; Fiani et al., 2023), several large longitudinal studies and a general population-based study reported adverse effects, i.e. reductions, on HRV (Hu et al., 2019; Kemp et al., 2014; Licht et al., 2010; Tegegne et al., 2018). Importantly, SSRI effects appear to vary by both medication class (Kemp et al., 2016) and psychiatric disorder (Alvares et al., 2016; Pittig et al., 2013). In contrast to previous findings (Jüres et al., 2024), we did not observe a possible normalizing effect of psychotropic medication on HR, given that the clinical groups did not differ from each other in this measure. Interestingly, as opposed to the overall group comparison, there were also no significant differences in HR when individually comparing the clinical groups to HC. As the means of both clinical groups were virtually equal to each other and to the overall mean, this finding may be due to reduced statistical power, a limitation that applies to the subgroup analysis as a whole.

Implications

Our findings carry several important implications. First, they suggest that HR is elevated and HRV is reduced in individuals with OCD, with the latter alteration possibly being most pronounced among medicated adolescents. Both elevated HR and reduced HRV are well established cardiovascular risk factors and have been linked to poorer cardiovascular health outcomes (Hillebrand et al., 2013; Ho et al., 2014). Indeed, evidence is emerging for an association between OCD and cardiovascular disease, although the underlying mechanisms, among others the role of psychotropic medications, remain unclear (de la Cruz et al., 2022). Interventions targeting HR and HRV may thus prove to be helpful in mitigating cardiovascular risk in OCD.

Second, cardiac parameters have previously been ascribed a possible role in the early detection of OCD. Such physiological indicators could complement existing clinical assessments, particularly in primary care settings where access to specialized diagnostics is limited (Zalpuri et al., 2025). In our study, however, the difference in HR was small, and the practical utility in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and incremental predictive value is likely to be insignificant. Given that we found no difference in HRV between unmedicated adolescents with OCD and HC, HRV may have even more limited utility in this regard, but may still be useful as a predictor of treatment response, with better responders characterized by higher HF-HRV prior to treatment (Olbrich et al., 2022). The absence of an association between OCD symptom severity and autonomic parameters in our study suggests that ANS alterations are not intricately linked to OCD symptoms, and that they are unsuitable to track treatment progress over time (Vloet et al., 2019).

Lastly, our findings also have implications for future research. We provide preliminary evidence suggesting potentially adverse effects of psychotropic medication on HRV in OCD, underscoring the importance of psychotropic medication status in HRV research. Consequently, conclusions drawn from studies including medicated patients should be interpreted with caution, as it may remain unclear whether reduced HRV is an inherent feature of pediatric OCD or a secondary effect of psychopharmacological treatment.

Strengths and limitations

Our study benefits from a large and well-matched sample, with the OCD group encompassing a broad range of symptom severity, and we additionally controlled for age, sex, BMI, and time of the assessment, four factors previously shown to influence autonomic activity (Koenig and Thayer, 2016; Agorastos et al., 2013).

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, we did not control for comorbidities, which were highly common in our OCD sample. While this reflects the clinical reality (Jans et al., 2007), a potential double-burden with comorbid conditions, particularly depressive disorders, has been associated with further reductions in HRV (Chen et al., 2014; Kemp et al., 2012; Koenig et al., 2016).

A second limitation is that alterations in ANS activity in anxiety disorders have previously been described as particularly associated with increased sympathetic activity, and similar overactivation appears to be present in OCD as well (Olbrich et al., 2022). Consequently, HRV parameters that primarily reflect the influence of the parasympathetic nervous system may be less suitable for characterizing such alterations.

Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of our research design precludes any causal conclusions. The causal directionality between altered ANS activity and psychiatric morbidity remains unclear (Hu et al., 2019). For example, it is uncertain whether OCD leads to elevated HR or whether an elevated HR predisposes individuals to developing OCD. Supporting the latter possibility, a longitudinal register-based study of Swedish men found that those in the highest HR quintile at conscription age had a 69% increased risk of developing OCD at follow-up compared to men in the lowest HR quintile (Latvala et al., 2016). Similarly, it remains unclear whether psychotropic medication contributed to the lower HRV observed in the medicated group, or whether patients taking psychotropic medication represent a clinically distinct subgroup characterized by lower HRV irrespective of psychotropic medication status.

Conclusion

This study with a large number of adolescents adds to a limited body of research on autonomic alterations in pediatric OCD. Our findings suggest that adolescents with OCD show an autonomic nervous system imbalance characterized by reduced parasympathetic activity that is common across anxiety disorders and has been linked to cardiovascular risk. While the reduction does not increase with greater symptom severity, psychotropic medication may contribute to the imbalance. Longitudinal studies are required to disentangle the temporal dynamics of OCD onset, autonomic dysregulation and psychotropic medication intake.
