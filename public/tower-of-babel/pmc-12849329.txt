A mixed-methods cross-sectional study on early contraceptive implant removal and its associated factors among women implanted attending family planning settings in Bangladesh
Authors: Abul Kalam Lutful Kabir, Shamsun Nahar, Emdad Hossain
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly contraceptive implant removal before the projected duration of use leads to not only high costs and resource wastage but also disclosures women to the risk of unintended pregnancies and accompanying difficulties, resulting in the threat of maternal mortality and morbidity if an alternative contraceptive is not immediately employed. Therefore, we aimed to assess and determine the prevalence and explore the associated factors for early implant removal among the women who had discontinued attending the family planning settings in Bangladesh.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed methods study was conducted between January and June 2025 at four government family planning settings. We collected both quantitative and qualitative data using a pretested structured questionnaire on sociodemographic, obstetric, contraceptives, and implant usage and its early removal through in-depth interviews. Additionally, we performed key informant interviews with the providers. We defined early removal as the discontinuation of the implant prior to the intended duration of three years.ResultsOut of 370 participants, 116 removed subdermal implants with a mean age of 28.33 ± 4.5 years (95% CI: 27.50-29.16). The proportion of implant discontinuation was 31.35% (n = 116, 95% CI: 26.84–36.25%) and the factors associated with implant discontinuation before the intended duration were irregular menstruation and dysmenorrhea (adjusted prevalence ratio [aPR] = 3.16, 95% CI: 2.39–4.19), desire to conceive (aPR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.68–2.63), husband’s opposition (aPR = 2.69, 95% CI: 2.07–3.49), switch to alternative contraceptives (aPR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.58–2.81) and self-reported side effects (aPR = 2.86, 95% CI: 2.09–3.93). From qualitative interviews, we found self-reported side effects and husband’s disapproval as the main reasons for early implant removal.ConclusionApproximately one-third of the study women had contraceptive implants removed early. Healthcare providers should be more focused on pre- and post-insertion counseling in client-centered communication and common side-effects management. Moreover, male partners should be encouraged, and community women in awareness programs must be engaged to reduce socio-cultural resistance.Keywords: Family planning methods, Contraceptives, Implants, Early removal, Women, Bangladesh

Introduction

Overpopulation in Bangladesh remains a national threat, although the fertility rate is slowly declining. Effective contraceptive methods, popularly known as family planning programs, become a challenge, and due to improper use of contraceptives, there is a chance of frequent unplanned pregnancies [1]. There are several options available for birth control systems, subsidized by the government of Bangladesh (GOB), that can benefit the prevention of unintended pregnancy, although some women can access those methods from other sources [2]. According to the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDSH) 2022 Key Indicators Report, the prevalence rate of contraceptives in Bangladesh is 64% among presently married women of 15–49 years, where 55% of women are currently getting modern contraceptive methods and 9% of them are dependent on traditional contraceptive methods The use of contraceptive options in Bangladesh since 1999–2000 has increased by 10% points, ranging from 54% to 64%. Between 2017 and 2018 and 2022, modern contraceptive methods improved from 52% to 55% [3]. In recent years, the demand for long-acting reversible hormonal contraceptives including intrauterine device (IUD) or LNG containing IUD and progestin implant especially LNG-releasing subdermal contraceptive implants has been doubled worldwide [4–6] and over the last few years due to availability in Bangladesh, the number of implant users increased manifold [3, 7, 8] because of most effective method of contraception with a failure rate with less than 1% [9–11]. This method is mostly suitable for females with poor compliance [12, 13], and fertility comes back with no or fewer side effects after immediate removal of the implant device [14], and also very safe for the breastfeeding woman [15]. Despite their advantages, implants are frequently discontinued by the women prematurely, often before the recommended duration which is evident by a study in twenty-one middle and low-income countries and the findings revealed that 59% of the users removed implants early [16]. In Bangladesh, the discontinuation rate of modern contraceptives persists at approximately 30%, with method-related side effects, especially among implant, injectable, and IUD users, accounting for over half of these cases of implants (51%). Among those switching or discontinuing, the most commonly cited reasons are the desire to conceive (31%) and adverse side effects (26%) [17]. Understanding the prevalence and underlying factors of early implant removal, including bleeding irregularities, inadequate pre-insertion counselling, myths and misconceptions, and partners’ opposition or societal pressure, is more comprehensive. Studies from comparable settings report similar issues, like heavy or irregular bleeding significantly contributes to early removal decisions [18], poor counseling about expected side effects and follow-up options further increases discontinuation risk, and concerns such as partner opposition, myths, and fertility intentions play influential roles [19].

Presently, only LNG containing two-rod implants is given to women for three years at no cost by the GOB under the Directorate General of Family Planning (DGFP) of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MOHFW) [20]. In this report, we aimed to quantify the prevalence of early implant removal before the recommended duration of use for three years among the women in Bangladesh and identify its socio-demographic, medical, and behavioral factors associated with early implant removal. In addition, we explored the role of counseling, partner involvement, and healthcare access in the decision to discontinue implants earlier, which will inform strategies to enhance counseling quality, manage side effects proactively, and reduce non-intentional discontinuation, ultimately contributing to the country’s family planning goals and broader reproductive health outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study setting, study design, and study population

A cross-sectional mixed-methods study involving quantitative and qualitative interviews was conducted from January to June 2025 among reproductive-aged women (18–45 years) who had attended to remove the implant at four government family planning service settings in the Dhaka division of Bangladesh. Based on the monthly client flow as well as the geographic proximity of the implant use, facility centers had been selected so that close supervision and monitoring of the participants and follow-up could be practically feasible.

Sample size and sampling

For quantitative data, the sample size for this study was calculated using Cochran’s formula, n = (Z)2 x P (1 - P)/d2, where Z is the Z statistic for a level of 95% confidence limit (Z = 1.96), P is the percentage considering the current proportion of implant use in Bangladesh as per the 2022 BDHS study [3], and d is the margin of error. With the addition of a 10% non-response rate, we finally obtained a total target sample size of 370, who were enrolled in this study. For qualitative sampling, we sampled 11 women using the convenience sampling technique from implant removed participants and four health care providers from family planning service centers.

Study variables

The outcome variable in our study was the time interval between the date of insertion and removal of the implant, which was dichotomously characterized as the early removal of contraceptive implant (Yes, if it is before three years of use) or not (No, if it is after three years of use). The present study included different independent variables, which were categorized into women’s socio-demographic characteristics, such as age, residence type, education level, occupation, family income, religion, and their husband’s age, education level, occupation, living status, and discussion with husband. Regarding medical and reproductive data variables, respondents’ number of living children, last child’s age, breastfeeding status, number of earlier pregnancies, previous delivery mode, abortion history, and plan to have more children. Additionally, in terms of knowledge, attitude, and perceptions towards contraceptive factors such as contraceptive methods used before implantation, having heard about implants and the source of information, reasons of choice, received pre-insertion counseling and clear instructions about unwanted complexity or adverse effects, and detailed information regarding implant removal from the providers. Finally, the variables concerning early implant removal, such as common or unwanted side effects, wanting to conceive, husband’s disapproval, switch to alternative method, etc., were included based on the literature about the outcome variable for data collection.

Data collection procedure

The data were collected from 9:00 am to 2:00 pm on work days by principal investigator and research associates with the assistance of employees served at four government family planning service settings. We used both structured and open-ended questionnaire through in-depth interviews with the implant users and providers (nurses and doctors) in the service centers for both quantitative and qualitative insights into socio-cultural influences and their perspectives. Since all the interviews were taken in the local language (Bangla) of Bangladesh, the questionnaire was written in English first, and then translated into Bangla. We monitored carefully with the implant provider’s team every day to find out the women who had come to remove the contraceptive implants at all service centers and collected information through face-to-face interviews. When enlisted for enrollment, all of the study participants were well-informed regarding the comprehensive purpose of the study and its detailed protocol. An informed consent form, having signature as well as verbal consent of the participants, was taken. Participants were informed that their contribution in this research was absolutely voluntary, no monetary incentive was offered for taking part in the study, and they had the freedom to withdraw at any time whenever they liked to do so. All the information collected from women was kept very private and confidential. The participants who did not have the record card of the implant insertion date were not included in this study.

Statistical analysis and data management

Quantitative data analysis was descriptive in nature. Using MS Excel and SPSS version 20, continuous variables were described as measures of central tendency and dispersion for continuous variables such as mean, median, range, and standard deviation. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and presented in frequency and bivariate tabular forms. Descriptive statistics outline discontinuation rates; bivariate and multivariate logistic regression identify the associated factors. Thematic analysis was conducted with all qualitative data to describe the perceptions of the study participants with implant usage and the reasons for implant selection and early discontinuation or removal of the implant [21].

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was reviewed thoroughly and approved for ethical clearance by the ethical review committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy of Dhaka University (Ref. No. Fa.Ph.E/047/2025). Data collection procedures have been maintained according to the ethical principles contained in the Declaration of the World Medical Association of Helsinki.

Results

Out of 370 contraceptive implants inserted women, 116 participants had removed implants before the intended duration of three years of use, enrolled into this study between January and June 2025, where 40 from Dhaka Medical College Hospital (DMC), 25 from Mohammadpur Fertility Services and Training Centre (Mohammadpur), 21 and 30 from Upazila Health Complexes, Sonargaon, Narayanganj (Sonargaon) and Keraniganj, Dhaka (Keraniganj), respectively shown in Fig. 1.

Socio-demographic characteristics

About one half of the women were in the age of greater than 30 years followed by 31.9% of the age group 25–30 years and 18–24 years (22.4%) and the proportion of their husband’s age were in the range of 18–30 years (52.5%), 31–40 years (31.8%), and greater than 40 years (15.5%). Based on the BMI of the study respondents, they were categorized into normal (64.6%), overweight (23.2%), and underweight (10.3%). Only two of them had obesity. The educational background analysis revealed that most of the study women and their husbands had no formal education (55.1% and 47.4%), primary level (25.8% and 29.3%), secondary level (16.3% and 6.9%), and higher education (2.6% and 16.3%), respectively. Most of the participants were from urban areas (74.1%) and all participants except five had been living together with their husbands (95.6%). Among the respondents, the majority belonged to the service or job (46.5%) followed by housewives (43.1%), and the remaining were students (7.7%) and others (2.5%) for employment. Regarding their partner’s occupation, around half of them were service holders (44.8%), businessmen (25.0%), day laborers (18.1%), and others (12.0%). In terms of the economic condition of the family, they were categorized into low (55.1% of monthly income: less than 20 K BDT), medium (33.6% of monthly income: between 20k and 40 K BDT), and high (11.2% of monthly income: greater than 40 K BDT). The distribution of age, residence, education, occupation, and family income was significantly different among the groups (p = 0.000) (Table 1).

Reproductive, contraceptive methods, and implant-related characteristics

The majority of participants (81.0%) had two living children, and most of their youngest child’s age was one year (64.6%), and 35.3% had a child of more than one year of age. Out of the women, 65 had been breastfeeding (56.0%) (p = 0.000) and 66.3% of the respondents had 4–6 no of pregnancies followed by 6 + pregnancy record (18.96%) and the rest had 0–3 pregnancies (14.65%) Around 70% of the women had experienced other than normal mood of delivery such as caesarian whereas the remaining had normal deliver mode (31.0%). A higher proportion of women having no history of MR/abortion (95.68%) who removed the implant early compared to those who had a previous history of abortion (4.3%) (p = 0.003). Among the study participants, most of them had a plan for more children (72.41%) compared to those who did not have one (27.5%) (p = 0.000). Before deciding on implant insertion, the majority of the women used contraceptives (69.8%) compared to those who had not used them earlier (30.17%) (p = 0.001). Of them, 53.4% of the participants had used oral contraceptive pills, followed by injectable (26.7%), and 19.8% of them had used other methods of contraceptives such as condom etc. previously.

Most of our study women who removed early implant had not heard about implant (72.4%) formerly compared to those had heard (27.5%) (p = 0.000) and regarding source of information, more than 50% of them heard about implant insertion from family welfare assistant (FWA) compared to other sources like friends or relatives (30.1%) and mass media TV, Radio, and Newspaper, etc. (13.7%) (p = 0.002). Before implant insertion, almost all of the participants consulted with their husbands, except for five. A larger proportion of the participants (83.6%) had reported that they had been counseled properly by the health care providers about the benefits and disadvantages, duration of action, mode of action, side effects, follow-up time, insertion and removal time, and complexity or adverse events of the device compared to those who had not been counseled (16.3%) (p = 0.000). Additionally, 62.0% of the women in our study had received concrete instructions about what they needed to do or where they had to go in case of any complexity or unwanted side effects or adverse events undergone and about implant removal details, respectively, compared to those who had not been informed (37.93%) (p = 0.000) (Table 2).

Factors associated with early implant removal among women at family planning settings in Bangladesh

Factors associated with early implant removal

During multivariate regression analysis, the substantial factors related to the early removal of contraceptive implants were age, education, occupation, breastfeeding, irregular menstruation and dysmenorrhea, desire to conceive again, partner’s disapproval, switch to an alternative contraceptive method, and some other self-reported side effects. The sociodemographic factors for implant removal before the intended duration revealed in the study that Participants aged 18–24 years had a 41% lower likelihood of discontinuation compared to women in older age groups (aPR = 0.59, 95% CI: 0.45–0.77, p = 0.000), women having no formal education were 3.97 times more probable (aPR = 3.97, 95% CI: 1.71–9.18, p = 0.001) associated to highly educated group and employed women had 1.47 times higher possibility of removing implant (aPR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.17–1.85, p = 0.001) compared to housewives. Among reproductive factors, breastfeeding women had a 34% lower likelihood of implant discontinuation compared to non-breastfeeding women (aPR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.51–0.86, p = 0.003). In addition, the study respondents reported irregular menstruation and dysmenorrhea as a major reason for early implant removal were 3.16 times more likely (aPR = 3.16, 95% CI: 2.39–4.19, p = 0.000) compared to those women who did not experience these two common side effects. In addition, the participants described that some self-reported side effects were 2.86 times higher possibility of enduring early implant discontinuation (aPR = 2.86, 95% CI:2.09–3.93, p = 0.000) compared to those who did not have. The women in this study who were interested in conceiving again had 2.10 times more prospect to remove the implant before the intended duration of three years (aPR = 2.10, 95% CI:1.68–2.63, p = 0.000) compared to those who were not interested in doing so. Moreover, the participants who reported their husband’s disapproval or opposition to continuing the contraceptive implant had a 2.69 times higher likelihood of early removal (aPR = 2.69, 95% CI: 2.07–3.49, p = 0.000) compared to others. Additionally, women who wanted to switch to an alternative method of contraceptives (e.g., pills, injectables, etc.) after implant removal were 2.10 times more expected to undergo early discontinuation (aPR = 2.10, 95% CI:1.58–2.81, p = 0.000) related to participants who were not informed of their willingness to remove the implant (Table 3).

Qualitative results

We conducted in-depth interviews of 11 implant users who agreed for in-depth interview to explore the factors associated with early contraceptive implant removal and took 4 health care providers, amounting to total 15 married women aged between 18 and 45 years having more than two children,. Out of the implanted women, six had no formal education, three completed primary schooling, and the remaining had a secondary level of education, whereas two were MBBS doctors and two had a nursing diploma among healthcare providers.

The results were categorized into four major themes: self-reported side effects, wanting to conceive again, husband’s opposition, and switch to an alternative contraceptive method.

Regarding self-reported side effects of contraceptive implants, irregular menstruation, abnormal menstrual bleeding, weakness, headache, dizziness, and weight gain, etc., have been stated as reasons for their early removal. Some of the implant users mentioned that due to prolonged bleeding during the menstrual period, their sexual life became hampered. As a result, their husband felt irritated towards them and got disturbed because of sexual interference between them for a long time, which ultimately caused their conjugal life to become uninterested and quarreling.

One of the women added that “I have been mentally tortured by my husband almost every day and cannot tolerate it now. He (partner) thinks that I am cheating with him day after day, and he is not getting feelings while having sex with me. That is why I have come to remove the implant inserted.”

Another participant said: “My husband has even threatened me that if I do not remove the inserted implant immediately from my body, he would get married again. Since he is too aggressive, I want to remove it today.”

She also mentioned that “If I am unable to do so today, it would not be possible to go home, and I may even have to commit suicide.”

Some of the women who had come to remove the implant had desired to conceive again to have more children.

Among them, one of the participants said, “We had two children, and one is a boy, but autistic. My family members expect that I take another baby boy to maintain the generation and become pregnant again. This is the reason I am here to remove the contraceptive implant.”

Moreover, because of uneasy feelings like sensation, some women wanted to remove the contraceptive implants and had an intention to use the alternative method of contraception as per their husbands’ expectations.

All the key informants had reported that they always tried their level best to manage the side effects from LNG-containing implants through counseling the users properly and giving some essential medicines (e.g. pain killers, contraceptive pills to regularize menstruation etc.) related with those common side effects but most of them had come with forceful determination to remove the implant inserted because of fear of husband who did not want implant like long-acting contraceptive methods rather they wished to use short acting contraceptive methods like oral pill or injection.

Discussion

About one third of the baseline study women (31.35%) in the present study who had been enrolled for implant insertion at four government family planning service settings in Dhaka division of Bangladesh discontinued the contraceptive implants between study period and the identified reasons associated with implant removal before intended duration were irregular menstruation and dysmenorrhea, self-reported side effects, wanted to conceive, husband’s disapproval and shift to alternative method of contraceptives after the implant had been removed. The proportion of early implant discontinuation in our present study was slightly low compared to a study performed in Bangladesh where the prevalence of early implant removal was 33% in a previous study done in Bangladeshi women enlisted for Norplant clinical trial [22] and the cumulative discontinuation rate following 5-years was 58.8% in another study among the women attending Dhaka Medical College Hospital, Institute of Postgraduate Medicine and Research and Mohammadpur Fertility Services and Training Center for the evaluation of efficacy, safety and acceptability of Norplant contraceptive subdermal implants for five years’ clinical study in Bangladesh [23] although this hormonal implant consisting of six rods are no longer used today worldwide. Moreover, the prevalence in the current study is quite less than have been observed in another cross-sectional research among the reproductive-aged married females attending a metropolitan city hospital in Bangladesh, where 68% of the study participants were willing to remove the implant early [7]. However, several studies were done on implant users in other Asian and African countries where various proportions of values on implant discontinuation had been reported [20]. Our study proportion prevails low implant removal to some extent compared to the previous study in Bangladesh because implants are a long-acting reversible hormonal contraceptive method with a success rate of about 99% [4–6]. Additionally, due to the availability of locally manufactured implants over the last few years and given at free of cost by the government of Bangladesh, the number of implant users increased manifold [3, 7, 8] because implants are the most effective method of contraception [9–11].

In our study, the proportion of women who had removed the contraceptive implant earlier or discontinued implant use before the intended duration are irregular menstruation and dysmenorrhea (38.8%), desire to conceive (33.6%), husband’s opposition (25.8%), switch to alternative method (15.5%), self-reported side effects (21.5%), and others (12.0%) (Table 3). These findings are significantly contradictory to a focused study performed in a small district of Bangladesh (Dinajpur district) among the participants who removed 6 rods containing a contraceptive hormonal implant (Norplant) before its five-year lifetime. Furthermore, it was observed from this study that 59% participants reported menstrual disorders as the primary reason of early removal of implant and around half of them who had removed implant both 22% for the first and second time request respectively and the rest (30%) upon several time appeals for removal of implant whereas reported side effects for example, dysmenorrhea and irregular bleeding and menstruation had been found (38.8%) of the current study results. In addition to this, other contributing factors encompassed the desire for conception (16%), adverse systemic reactions such as nausea or weight gain (7%), and partner or cultural influences, though less prevalent, such as husband’s opposition (3%) or religious views (3%) were somewhat comparable with our study [19, 24]. Some cases of early implant removal may be attributed to misconceptions and lack of knowledge about contraceptives. For example, some of the users’ spouses alleged that the implant was accountable for interrupting their sexual interest. Such concerns highlight the need for more comprehensive awareness campaigns targeting both women and their husbands, to improve understanding, dissipate myths, and support known contraceptive options. These findings highlight multifaceted contributors to early implant removal in Bangladesh, ranging from physiological side effects to inadequate counseling and social pressure, all of which merit deeper examination.

Limitations of the study

The current prospective cross-sectional mixed-method study has several limitations that should really be considered. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish causal relationships between early implant removal and its associated factors. Second, the study was conducted in four family planning centers only, which may not fully reflect practices in all family planning settings, limiting the generalizability of the study. Moreover, the sample size for qualitative data was very small in this study because we conducted this study within government family planning settings, where all types of contraceptive methods, including implants, are offered to women at no cost by the government of Bangladesh. Since all health care providers in these facilities are government employees, many of them showed little or no interest in participating in our study. This unwillingness seemed to stem from concerns about likely consequences, including fear of career loss or disciplinary action against them from their respective authorities.

Third, considering the argument that self-reported reasons for early implant removal are subject to recall and social desirability biases, and that setting records may contain incomplete information. Fourth, although qualitative interviews provided valuable context, interviewer influence and participants’ reluctance to disclose sensitive issues may have affected responses. Fifth, why women experiencing issues like irregular menstruation or dysmenorrhea had not been explored for prevention strategies. Finally, unmeasured factors such as provider skill, service accessibility, and broader cultural influences may contribute to residual confounding. Despite these limitations, the mixed-method approach strengthens the study by providing both statistical and contextual insights into early implant removal in Bangladeshi women.

Conclusion

Early discontinuation of implants remains a notable challenge, with approximately one-third of women electing its removal before the recommended duration in Bangladesh. The predominant reasons for early implant removal are mainly associated with side effects and inadequate counseling, indicating gaps in both users’ education and follow-up mechanisms. Strengthening comprehensive pre-insertion counseling, ensuring consistent post-insertion support, and addressing common side-effect concerns are important approaches to improve implant continuation rates. Integrating these measures into Bangladesh’s national family planning programs can optimize the effectiveness of contraceptive implants in contributing to reproductive health goals.
