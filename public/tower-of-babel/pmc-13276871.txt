Hospitalization Burden Estimates of Respiratory Syncytial Virus With Adjustment for Case Underascertainment in Adults Aged 18 to 65 Years in High-Income Countries: A Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis, and Modeling Study
Authors: You Li, Sebastien Kenmoe, Elizabeth Begier, Pia Wahi-Singh, Bhanu Wahi-Singh, Angela R Branche, Edward E Walsh, Caihua Liang, Reiko Sato, Bradford D Gessner, Harish Nair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) disease burden among adults aged <65 years is not well documented. We aimed to estimate the hospitalization burden of RSV in adults aged 18 to 64 years in high-income countries.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review (PROSEPRO: CRD42022377741) to identify studies reporting estimates of RSV hospitalization burden in the study population. For each study included, we followed a 2-step statistical approach to adjust for RSV case underascertainment related to the use of clinical specimen and diagnostic test. Random effects meta-analysis was conducted to estimate RSV hospitalization and mortality burden. We further compared estimates between the 18- to 64-year age group in this study and the ≥65-year age group in our previous study using the same methodology.ResultsBased on 8 studies, the adjusted annual RSV-associated hospitalization rate was 48 per 100 000 (95% CI, 31–77) in adults aged 18 to 64 years. According to 12 studies, more than three-quarters of RSV-associated hospitalized adults had at least 1 comorbidity: adults aged 50 to 64 years with asthma, congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary artery disease, or diabetes and adults aged 18 to 49 years with congestive heart failure or diabetes had comparable RSV hospitalization rates to those aged ≥65 years without these comorbidities. An overall 6.1% (95% CI, 5.2%–7.1%) of adults aged 18 to 64 years with RSV-associated hospitalization died in the hospital (3 studies).ConclusionsAdults aged 18 to 64 years with certain comorbidities have substantial RSV hospitalization burden comparable to those aged ≥65 years without these comorbidities and could be the additional target populations for RSV vaccination programs.

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) represents a substantial public health burden among infants [1] and older adults [2]. For infants, novel RSV prophylactic products, maternal vaccine (ABRYSVO; Pfizer), and long-acting monoclonal antibodies (nirsevimab; Sanofi/AstraZeneca) have been licensed as a passive immunization approach to protect this vulnerable population. For older adults, 3 RSV vaccine products are licensed—ABRYSVO, Arexy (GSK), and most recently mRNA-1345 (Moderna)—and are available as an active immunization approach [3].

RSV disease burden informs selection of the recommended target population and is one of the key inputs for economic evaluation of immunization programs. While a growing number of studies have estimated RSV disease burden among young children and older adults (eg, aged ≥65 years), this burden is not well documented for younger and middle-aged adults. License for RSV vaccines among younger adults is now available, and there is an urgent need to better understand the RSV disease burden in this age group to inform immunization strategies. Moreover, limitations associated with certain clinical specimen types and diagnostic tests can contribute to the underestimation of RSV disease burden among adults [4, 5]. Our previously published estimates of RSV hospitalization burden in older adults showed that the adjusted RSV hospitalization rate could be 2.2 times that of the unadjusted rate [6]. This highlights the importance of using multiple specimens in prospective incidence studies or adjusting for case underascertainment based on diagnostic testing limitations.

In this study, we aimed to estimate RSV-associated acute respiratory infection (ARI) hospitalization burden in adults aged 18–64 years in high-income countries after adjustment for case underascertainment resulting from variation in clinical specimen types and diagnostic tests used in the included studies. The primary objective was to estimate the RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rate. The secondary objectives were to estimate the proportion of ARI hospitalization testing positive for RSV, to determine the in-hospital case fatality ratio (hCFR) of RSV-associated ARI hospitalization, and to explore the RSV-associated ARI hospitalization risk associated with comorbidities. We also compared the estimates between adults aged 18–64 years in this study and those aged ≥65 years in a previously published study [6].

METHOD

Systematic Literature Review

Search Strategy

We conducted a systematic literature review (registered in PROSEPRO: CRD42022377741) to identify published studies reporting disease burden of RSV-associated ARI on hospital services in adults aged 18–64 years in high-income countries, as defined by World Bank Income Classification (Supplementary Appendix, p 1). Five English databases (MEDLINE, Embase, Global Health, Web of Science, and Global Index Medicus) were searched for relevant publications between January 1996 and November 2024, with no language restrictions. Modification of the search strategy (available in Supplementary Appendix, pp 2–4) was based on our previously published systematic review of RSV hospitalization burden in older adults in high-income countries [6].

Eligibility Criteria

In this study, ARI was a broad term used to capture short-term respiratory illness presenting with respiratory symptoms, consisting of upper and lower respiratory infections. Eligible studies should have reported at least 1 of the ARI-related outcomes of interest, as defined in the primary and secondary objectives in adults aged 18–64 years (or narrower age bands) in high-income countries. Considering the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on RSV epidemiology, only data collected prior to March 2020 were assessed for eligibility and subsequently included in the analysis. For studies that reported an RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rate, the data needed to be for at least 12 consecutive months, except where RSV seasonality was well established (eg, studies in temperate regions could test RSV only during the perceived RSV season). For studies that reported the RSV-positive proportion, data had to cover the entire year (ie, a 12-month interval) to account for RSV seasonality. No restrictions regarding the inclusion of at least 12 consecutive months were applied to studies that reported other outcomes, such as hCFR or comorbidities, as these outcomes were not expected to vary by calendar month. Studies were excluded if they focused only on cases with specific underlying medical conditions (eg, diabetes, asthma, coronary artery disease), without any noncomorbid RSV cases as a comparator. Studies were excluded that generated estimates based on excess morbidity approaches (eg, analysis of time series of aggregated cases) [7]. Moreover, similar to our previous analysis for older adults [6], studies were excluded if the case definition used was narrower than ARI, such as influenza-like illness and severe ARI (both requiring fever or history of fever [8, 9]) and community-acquired pneumonia (representing 14%–66% of RSV-related ARI hospitalizations [10–13], depending on the diagnostic criteria for pneumonia and admission criteria). This restriction helped to address heterogeneity associated with varied case definitions across studies and to enhance generalizability of the meta-estimates.

Screening, Extraction, and Quality Assessment

Based on the criteria, screening was conducted for all records after exclusion of duplicates, and the reference lists of all included studies were screened. Moreover, experts in the field were contacted to help identify additional eligible data. We used a data extraction template similar to our previously published study on RSV hospitalization burden in older adults, which included study-level characteristics and the results for each outcome of interest [6]. For studies that had available information on the comorbidity status of participants, we designed a supplementary template to extract the proportion of individual comorbidities among all hospitalized cases with RSV, as well as the hospitalization rate ratio (hRR) between those with a specific comorbidity and those without, where available; authors from studies were contacted for more detailed data if these data were not available in the publications. For each study, we conducted quality assessment using critical appraisal tools from the Joanna Briggs Institute according to the study design [14] (Supplementary Appendix, pp 5–6). As our study focused on estimating disease burden rather than assessing the association between exposures and outcomes, the question regarding confounding factors in the assessment form (“Were strategies to deal with confounding factors stated?”) for the cohort study design was modified as “whether the methodological approaches were transparently reported and the estimates were provided in a manner that allows for valid synthesis.” Each study was given an overall quality score based on the proportion of assessment questions receiving “yes,” indicating good quality: 0%–59%, low quality; 60%–79%, moderate; 80%–100%, high.

Literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment were all independently reviewed twice by S. K., B. W.-S., and P. W.-S., with any disagreements resolved through discussion within the review team.

Data Analysis

Adjustment for Clinical Specimen and Diagnostic Test

As in our previously published analysis [6], we adjusted for the use of specific clinical specimens and diagnostic tests in individual studies by a 2-step adjustment approach. The predefined gold standard for RSV detection was a combination of sputum, saliva, and nasopharyngeal swabs tested by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) plus paired serology. The first step adjusted for diagnostic tests based on the sensitivity of the sole use of PCR, viral culture, and paired serology by Falsey et al [15] and a recently published systematic review [4]. The second step adjusted for clinical specimen based on a multispecimen study (nasopharyngeal swab, saliva, and sputum specimens) in adults aged ≥40 years in United States [11]; as our study focused on adults aged <65 years, we included data for those aged 40–64 years provided by the study team. For sensitivity analysis, we used the same adjustment ratio as our previously published analysis in adults ≥65 years [6], referred to as the previous adjustment ratio. For both steps, we accounted for the innate uncertainty around the sensitivity results from the reference studies by applying a bayesian approach based on beta-binomial distribution; as a result, 1000 posterior samples of adjusted RSV-positive case estimates were generated per the originally reported RSV-positive number, yielding 1000 sets of adjusted data. The detailed adjustment factors used in this study can be found in the Supplementary Appendix (p 7).

Hospitalization Rate

We conducted a random effects meta-analysis to synthesize adjusted RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rates across studies per each set of adjusted data. The resulting 1000 sets of meta-estimates were then combined into 1 meta-estimate based on Rubin's rules [16]. The meta-analysis was conducted separately for 2 age groups, 18–49 and 50–64 years, yielding meta-estimated hospitalization rates for these 2 age bands. Based on the hospitalization rate estimates, the hospitalization episodes in high-income countries in 2019 for the 2 age bands were calculated by applying the population estimates for 2019 that were extracted from the United Nations World Population Prospects [17]. The overall hospitalization episodes for the age group of 18–64 years were calculated by combining the numbers for the 2 narrower age bands. Confidence intervals for the estimates for 18–64 years were calculated by conducting Monte Carlo simulation (1000 samples) of the meta-estimates for the 2 narrower age bands and subsequently taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting estimates for 18–64 years, as conducted previously [1]. For sensitivity analysis, we restricted our analyses separately to only prospective studies, only high-quality studies, and only studies that used PCR, to test the robustness of estimates. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis that directly synthesized the hospitalization rate estimates for 18–64 years, rather than combining the estimates for 18–49 and 50–64 years as in the main analysis.

Proportion of RSV in ARI Hospitalizatione

Similar to estimating the hospitalization rate, we used a random effects meta-analysis to synthesize the adjusted proportion of ARI hospitalization due to RSV infection, hereafter described as the RSV positivity rate. The meta-analysis was conducted separately for 3 age groups: 18–49, 50–64, and 18–64 years.

Hospitalization Burden by Comorbidity Status

For studies with available data by comorbidity status, we conducted additional exploratory analyses to understand the role of specific comorbidities in RSV hospitalization. The comorbidities of interest in this analysis, as determined from the published studies with relevant data, are as follows: any cancer, asthma, cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease (including liver cirrhosis or failure), congestive heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), coronary artery disease, diabetes, and stroke. In addition, we included 3 more categories indicating the number of comorbidities: ≥1, ≥2, and ≥3 comorbidities.

The first exploratory analysis synthesized the prevalence of each comorbidity category in hospitalized RSV cases by a random effects meta-analysis, stratified by 18–49 and 50–64 years, when there were ≥2 studies with available data. The second exploratory analysis synthesized the hRRs between cases with certain comorbidities and those without, stratified by the 2 narrower age bands. Moreover, when studies had data for the age group ≥65 years, we repeated the analyses for this age group for comparison. Using the prevalence of comorbidity and hRR estimates, we further estimated the RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rates among those with certain comorbidities and those without separately by age group (18–49, 50–64, and ≥65 years); a detailed method can be found in Supplementary Appendix (pp 9–10).

In-Hospital Mortality

We conducted separate random effects meta-analyses to synthesize the RSV hCFR for the 3 age groups: 18–49, 50–64, and 18–64 years. We further estimated the absolute number of RSV in-hospital deaths by applying the absolute number of RSV-associated ARI hospitalizations to the corresponding hCFR estimate. However, given that the hCFR estimate was based only on the identified RSV hospitalizations in individual studies (ie, not including those RSV cases missed by the testing method in those studies), we had to make an assumption on the hCFR for those missed by the RSV tests. To address this, we reanalyzed the study data from the multispecimen study in Louisville and Toronto [11] and compared the risks for severe outcomes—defined as intensive care unit stay, mechanical ventilation, or death—between RSV cases detected positive in nasopharyngeal swabs and RSV cases detected negative in nasopharyngeal swabs but positive in other clinical specimens. The results showed no significant differences in the risks for severe outcomes between the groups (Supplementary Appendix, p 8). Therefore, in this analysis, we applied the same hCFR to RSV cases identified by the study tests and RSV cases missed by those tests for estimating the absolute number of RSV in-hospital deaths.

Comparison Between Age Groups

We descriptively compared the estimated RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rate, proportion of RSV in ARI hospitalization, and RSV hCFR in our study population (18–64 years) with the previously published estimates for older adults (≥65 years) [6]. The prevalence of comorbidities in hospitalized cases and the hRR for comorbidities estimated in our analysis were also compared between the ages of 18–64 and ≥65 years.

Statistical Software

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.1.2).

RESULTS

As shown in Figure 1, a total 27 studies were included, with detailed study-level characteristics provided in the Supplementary Appendix (p 11) [12, 13, 18–42]. The studies provided data on RSV hospitalization burden between 1989 and 2020 from 11 countries, over half of which (15/27) were from United States. Among the studies, nasopharyngeal swabs (15/27) and PCR (21/27) were the most commonly used clinical specimen and diagnostic test, respectively. Most studies (22/27, 81%) were assessed as high quality with the remaining ones as moderate quality (for detailed quality assessment results, see Supplementary Appendix, pp 12–13).

PRISMA flowchart.

Eight studies [23, 26, 33, 35, 37, 39, 40, 42] reporting data on RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rate estimates were included in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Appendix, pp 14–15). After adjusting for the clinical specimens and diagnostic tests used in individual studies, the meta-estimated annual RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rate was 48 per 100 000 (95% CI, 31–77) in adults aged 18–64 years, corresponding to 373 000 (240 000–591 000) hospitalizations annually in high-income countries in 2019. The hospitalization rate for 50–64 years was approximately 5 times that of 18–49 years and was 70% lower than that for ≥65 years (Table 1). The adjusted hospitalization rates for all age groups were approximately 2.5 times as much as the unadjusted hospitalization rates. Predefined sensitivity analyses yielded similar estimates to the main analysis (Figure 2).

Data on the comorbidity status of hospitalized RSV cases were reported in 12 studies [12, 13, 18, 22, 23, 27, 28, 31, 36, 39–41] (Supplementary Appendix, pp 17–25). Generally, the prevalence of at least 1 comorbidity was lower among adults aged 18–49 years (78.4%; 95% CI, 60.9%–89.4%) as compared with 50–64 years (81.3%; 76.8%–85.2%) and ≥65 years (85.8%; 74.3%–92.6%) despite overlapping confidence intervals (Table 3). Furthermore, 3 studies [23, 31, 36] reported the RSV hRR for 5 comorbidities: asthma, congestive heart failure, COPD, coronary artery disease, and diabetes. Having any of the 5 comorbidities in any age groups was associated with an increased risk for RSV hospitalization; the corresponding hRRs ranged from 2.1 (95% CI, 1.6–2.8) for diabetes in ≥65 years to 31.6 (15.8–63.3) in 18–49 years for congestive heart failure. While the hRRs associated with asthma were comparable across age groups, the hRRs associated with the other 4 comorbidities decreased with increased age. Adults aged 50–64 years who had asthma, congestive heart failure, COPD, coronary artery disease, or diabetes and adults aged 18–49 years who had congestive heart failure or diabetes had comparable RSV hospitalization rates to older adults aged ≥65 years without these comorbidities (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

By systematically collecting data on RSV hospitalization burden and adjusting for case underascertainment related to variation in the use of clinical specimen and diagnostic test, this study is the first to quantify RSV hospitalization burden in adults aged 18–64 years in high-income countries. One goal of the current study was to assess burden in younger adults relative to the more standard vaccine target group of older adults aged ≥65 years. We ensured comparability between older and younger adults in the current study by using a statistical adjustment methodology for RSV case underascertainment that was similar to our previously published study [6] for older adults aged ≥65 years.

Overall, the adjusted RSV-associated ARI hospitalization rates among adults were approximately 2.5 times as much as the unadjusted hospitalization rates, suggesting that RSV hospitalization burden was substantially underestimated in individual studies that used a single clinical specimen or diagnostic test. It is critical to have accurate burden estimates for public health decision making since recently published cost-effectiveness analyses of RSV vaccination in older adults across multiple countries consistently suggested that hospitalization rate estimates were one of the most influential epidemiologic parameters for cost-effectiveness analyses, followed by hCFR estimates [43–45]. Moreover, National Immunization Technical Advisory Groups have consistently identified disease burden as one of the most important factors in determining whether to recommend vaccines [46]. Therefore, it is critical that such groups and government payer bodies have accurate rather than artificially conservative burden estimates, including those for understudied populations such as younger adults. Robust population-based surveillance studies with complete case ascertainment should remain the gold standard; however, these are expensive and time-consuming. In contrast, our approach of using existing data and adjusting for case underascertainment, while admittedly less robust, is relatively straightforward and provides a more accurate estimate.

In this study, we showed that adults aged 50–64 years who had asthma, congestive heart failure, COPD, coronary artery disease, or diabetes and younger adults aged 18–49 years who had congestive heart failure or diabetes had RSV hospitalization rates comparable to those aged ≥65 years without these comorbidities. Similar findings were reported in several studies in Scotland, Denmark, and United States [23, 31, 47]. These findings suggest that the presence of certain comorbidities constitutes a risk for RSV hospitalization independent of age. Comorbidities should be factored in as much as age has been for older adults when considering RSV vaccine recommendations.

Moreover, in addition to hospitalization, the meta-estimates for RSV hCFR in our study were generally similar across adults aged 18–49, 50–64, and ≥65 years. While hospitalization burden and thus absolute numbers of deaths increased with age, deaths among younger adults remained substantial and should be considered part of the overall population-based burden in this age group. Moreover, a previously published systematic review [48] showed that adults aged ≥18 years with cardiopulmonary conditions had an even higher hCFR (10.1%).

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, existing evidence on RSV hospitalization burden in the study population was relatively limited, and there were notable variations in the use of clinical specimens and diagnostic tests in individual studies; therefore, it was not possible to conduct a conventional meta-analysis. In this study, we applied statistical adjustment for the variations regarding clinical specimens and diagnostic tests, which helped reduce the statistical heterogeneity among the studies; nonetheless, there were sources of heterogeneity that remained, such as differences in study population, health care–seeking behavior, admission criteria, and quality of health care. These remaining heterogeneities could limit the generalizability of the study findings to countries or regions that were not included in this study. Moreover, the statistical adjustment was mostly based on evidence from North America and thus limits extrapolation to all high-income countries. In terms of time period, we excluded the post–COVID-19 pandemic data due to the perceived impact on RSV circulation, particularly for the first 1 to 2 years following the pandemic onset when different nonpharmaceutical interventions were in place. Consequently, our findings might not fully represent RSV epidemiology for the more recent period. Nonetheless, recent data indicate that RSV activity may have returned to prepandemic levels [49].

Second, we acknowledge the potential impact of selection bias due to reporting variations in the studies. For example, Hamilton et al [30] reported the comorbidity status of hospitalized RSV cases only for the study population across all age groups, rather than by individual age groups such as 18–49 and 50–64 years. There were also studies that did not report the relevant estimates for any of the common age groups, and their estimates could not be included in the meta-analysis or for comparison with other studies. For example, Arbefeville and Ferrieri [19] reported estimates in those aged 20–39 and 40–59 years, different from the age groups cited in the rest of the studies, and could be presented in only a narrative way. The selection bias further limited the generalizability of the study findings. Selection bias might also occur due to the scope and objectives of relevant published studies.

While our search strategy captured well the studies that focused on RSV as an important etiology of ARI, some studies that had a wider scope than RSV might not include it anywhere in the title, abstract, keywords, or other relevant fields that were indexed by the databases. Although we tried to address this by conducting citation searches of the studies, we could not fully rule out the possibility of missing potentially eligible studies.

Third, although the prevalence of comorbidities among hospitalized cases positive for RSV in this study added to the understanding of the role of comorbidities in RSV hospitalization burden, it should be noted that the estimated prevalence of underlying individual comorbidity in patients with RSV came from studies that may have varying case definitions as well as ascertainment strategies, and the point estimates are therefore not strictly comparable.

Last, we focused on hospitalization burden and did not consider postdischarge sequelae of RSV, which could be another important disease burden parameter for economic evaluation. A recently published systematic review showed that the most common clinical sequela in the entire adult population (general population ≥18 years) in high-income countries were cardiovascular events, including congestive heart failure [50].

In summary, our study demonstrated a substantial burden of hospitalized RSV infection and associated mortality among younger adults with comorbidities that is similar to older adults without comorbidities. These estimates provide important data for economic evaluation of RSV immunization programs and planning of immunization strategies. Moreover, despite the limitations of our approach, it is likely that this approach yields a more accurate burden estimate than a relatively weak or nonsystematic surveillance study. Future evaluations should compare results from our approach with robust surveillance data.
