Large-scale pleiotropic analysis across cancers reveals shared genetic mechanisms and identifies novel functional genes
Authors: Xiaohong Wu, Yuqing Yan, Wen Cao, Tian Wu, Jiaxing He, Dongyang Wang, Jing Gong, Xiaohui Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPleiotropic genetic loci have been increasingly reported in cancer, and identifying genetic variants with pleiotropic associations can reveal shared biological pathways influencing multiple cancers. Using summary statistics from genome-wide association studies for 37 cancer types (N = 433 836), we identified extensive genome-wide and local genetic correlations among cancers. Through pairwise pleiotropic analysis, we identified 75 243 significant pleiotropic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) across 372 cancer pairs, among which 3472 were lead SNPs with potential regulatory functions. Using FUMA and MAGMA, we identified 2527 pleiotropic risk loci and 4272 candidate pleiotropic genes. Notably, genes such as TERT (5p15.33), POU5F1B (8q24.21), and FANCA (16q24.3) exhibited widespread pleiotropy across multiple cancer types. Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the critical roles of pigment synthesis, metabolism, and apoptosis in skin-related cancers, while cross-cancer enrichment analysis emphasized pathways related to apoptosis, chromatin structure, and intermediate filaments. We also identified 33 novel functional genes harboring previously unreported cancer risk variants. Drug-gene interaction analysis revealed several repositionable FDA-approved drugs. Importantly, drug sensitivity assays demonstrated that bosutinib and cobimetinib exhibited promising therapeutic potential in breast cancer cell lines. Finally, we developed the PleioCancer database (https://gonglab.hzau.edu.cn/PleioCancer/), providing a comprehensive resource for cancer pleiotropy research. These findings have important implications for carcinogenesis cancer, prevention and treatment.

Introduction

Cancer remains significant global health burden, with complex and heterogeneous pathogenesis. Despite advances in early detection, diagnosis, and treatment, the incidence and mortality rates of cancer remain high [1]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of genetic variants associated with cancer risk, however, these variants account for only a fraction of genetic susceptibility [2]. Additionally, observed genetic correlations among various cancers suggest a shared genetic basis. For instance, GWAS findings indicate overlapping susceptibility loci across cancers, such as 1q32 (MDM4), 3q28 (TP63), and 4q24 (TET2) [3], which are pleiotropic and influence multiple cancers.

Previous studies have reported pleiotropic associations and shared genetic mechanisms among cancers. For example, Sato et al. identified five pleiotropic associations in a cross-population meta-analysis of 13 cancers in East Asian and European populations [4]. They further discovered shared biological pathways, including mitotic spindle, early estrogen response, and apoptosis, based on large-scale meta-analyses of breast and prostate cancer. Similarly, Rashkin et al. identified 100 independent pleiotropic variants across 18 cancers in two European cohorts, with the associated genes significantly enriched in 36 pathways [3]. However, pleiotropy remains underexplored in several rarer cancer types.

Pleiotropic analysis may also provide new targets and strategies for cancer prevention and treatment. Studies suggest that genetic loci failing to reach significance in single-cancer GWAS may become significant in joint analyses across multiple cancers [5], thus avoiding the cost increase associated with expanding sample sizes. Moreover, pleiotropic loci offer promising opportunities for drug repurposing and multi-indication drug development, thereby reducing the time and cost required for pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies during drug development. For example, metformin was initially used to treat diabetes [6] but was subsequently found to have therapeutic effects in multiple cancers through its targeting of PEN2 and modulation of the AMPK pathway [7, 8].

Despite these advances, several limitations remain in the current pleiotropy research in cancer. First, the identification of novel susceptibility loci via pleiotropy analyses is still incomplete, and numerous potential risk variants may remain undiscovered. Second, although previous studies have explored functional gene prioritization and drug repositioning based on pleiotropic loci [4, 9, 10], systematic analyses that connect pleiotropic genetic findings to underlying biological mechanisms and potential therapeutic applications remain limited. Third, the lack of publicly accessible and comprehensive databases dedicated to cancer pleiotropy hinders data sharing, cross-study replication, and the clinical translation of research findings. Therefore, a more comprehensive and systematic exploration of pleiotropy is necessary to better elucidate shared genetic architectures and facilitate precision oncology.

To address these gaps, we assembled GWAS summary statistics of 37 cancers and systematically explored multi-level pleiotropy across cancers, including genome-wide, SNP, gene, and biological pathway levels, to uncover potential shared genetic etiologies and identify new therapeutic targets (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S1). First, we quantitatively assessed genetic correlations between cancers at both the genome-wide and local levels. Subsequently, we employed a pleiotropy test based on the composite null hypothesis to identify shared genetic loci at the SNP level and employed MAGMA and enrichment analyses to detect pleiotropic signals at the gene level and shared biological pathways. Furthermore, we compared the identified pleiotropic loci with known cancer susceptibility loci and discovered several novel candidate risk loci and genes. In addition, through Summary data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis and gene-drug interaction analysis, we prioritized key functional genes and their potential targeted therapies. Finally, we developed PleioCancer, a comprehensive resource platform to support future research and translational applications in cancer pleiotropy.

Materials and methods

GWAS datasets

We collected GWAS summary statistics for 37 cancer types, each with more than 100 cases, from PubMed [11–13] and Finngen (Version R10, RRID: SCR_022254) databases (https://r10.finngen.fi/) [14]. Specifically, 10 datasets were obtained from PubMed literature and 27 were sourced from the Finngen database (https://r10.finngen.fi/). All studies were conducted on European ancestry to avoid population heterogeneity.

Then, we performed quality control on these GWAS summary statistics. The SNPs of all data were processed as follows: (i) all SNPs were mapped to the hg19 human reference genome; (ii) SNP IDs were unified to dbSNP build 156; (iii) SNPs with missing or duplicated rsIDs were removed; (iv) SNPs located within the major histocompatibility complex region (chr6: 25–35 Mb) were excluded due to its complex linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure; (v) SNPs located on sex chromosomes were removed; (vi) SNPs with a minor allele frequency < 0.01 were filtered out; (vii) common SNPs were extracted from each pair of cancers, and allele directions were aligned to ensure consistency across all datasets.

QTL datasets

The quantitative trait locus (QTL) data in this study are of the cis type, indicating that SNPs are significantly (P < 5 × 10−8) associated with molecular traits within 1 Mb.

The methylation QTL (mQTL) data were obtained from a meta-analysis of two European cohorts: BSGS (n = 614) and LBC (n = 1366) [15]. The methylation status in this study was measured using Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip, and the SMR-formatted mQTL data were downloaded from the YangLab website (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/data/SMR/LBC_BSGS_meta.tar.gz).

The gene expression QTL (eQTL) data were retrieved from the eQTLGen consortium (https://molgenis26.gcc.rug.nl/downloads/eqtlgen/cis-eqtl/SMR_formatted/cis-eQTL-SMR_20191212.tar.gz) [16]. The database includes 37 datasets encompassing blood samples from 31 684 healthy individuals of European ancestry and covering 16 989 cis-eQTL genes.

The protein QTL (pQTL) data were derived from plasma proteomics of 36 000 Icelanders using the SomaScan v4 platform [17]. The study identified 1944 proteins with significant cis associations. We excluded 41 proteins on sex chromosomes and 185 QTL SNPs with minor allele frequencies < 0.01 (5 of which were located on the X chromosome), and ultimately retained 1723 proteins. The pQTL summary data can be obtained on the deCODE genetics website (https://www.decode.com/summarydata/, RRID: SCR_003334).

Global and local genetic correlation analyses

To ensure the reliability of genetic correlation estimates, we first evaluated the heritability of each cancer using LD score regression (LDSC) (RRID: SCR_022801) [18, 19]. Given that heritability estimates can be biased by sample size and case–control imbalance in GWAS data, we calculated the effective sample size as input using the following formula: 4 / (1/Ncase + 1/Ncontrol) [20]. Cancers with a heritability Z-score > 2, which indicates a reliable heritability estimate, were retained for subsequent genetic correlation analyses to ensure the stability and robustness of the correlation results [21]. Then, we estimated global genetic correlations (rg) for pairwise cancers using LDSC, which regresses the product of SNP Z-scores from two traits on their LD scores. As part of the LDSC model, the cross-trait intercept was estimated to account for covariance arising from sample overlap and other confounding factors when estimating genetic correlation. To control for false positives from multiple testing, the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to adjust the P values and control the false discovery rate (FDR). A threshold of FDR < 0.05 was applied to identify significant global genetic correlations.

SUPERGNOVA [22] was employed to estimate local genetic correlations between paired traits to reveal shared local genomic regions. The method first employs LOGODetect to partition the genome into 2353 LD regions. Then, local Z-scores are adjusted using the eigenvectors of the regional LD matrix, and local genetic covariance is estimated via weighted least squares regression [23]. We used the European population genotype data from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 as the reference panel. This method is robust to sample overlap in GWAS data. Finally, FDR correction was performed separately for each cancer pair, and regions with FDR < 0.05 were considered to have significant local genetic correlations.

Pleiotropic SNP identification under composite null hypothesis

We applied the R package PLACO [24] to identify pleiotropic SNPs across pairwise cancers. PLACO is based on the composite null hypothesis that SNPs are not associated with at least one trait, for conducting pleiotropic association tests. The test statistic is defined as the product of the Z-scores of genetic variants across two traits. To avoid spurious associations caused by the extremely large effect, SNPs with squared Z-scores exceeding 80 were removed. Z-scores were de-correlated using a correlation coefficient matrix estimated from GWAS summary statistics to account for correlation induced by potential sample overlap [25, 26]. SNPs with P < 5 × 10−8 were considered significant pleiotropic variants.

Pleiotropic SNP annotation

We used the “SNP2GENE” module in the FUMA [27] online platform (https://fuma.ctglab.nl, RRID: SCR_017521) to define pleiotropic loci based on the identified pleiotropic SNPs. Specifically, European population genotypes from the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 were served as the reference. SNPs with r2 < 0.6 were considered independent significant SNPs, while SNPs with r2 < 0.1 were considered lead SNPs. If the distance between LD blocks of independent significant SNPs was <250 kb, they were merged into a single genomic region, which was defined as a potential pleiotropic locus. Therefore, each pleiotropic locus may contain multiple independent significant or lead pleiotropic SNPs.

To functionally characterize pleiotropic SNPs, we utilized multiple annotation strategies to assess their potential gene targets and regulatory effects. Three methods in FUMA were applied to map pleiotropic SNPs to genes, including ANNOVAR (RRID: SCR_012821) [28], cis-eQTL, and 3D chromatin interactions. Then, Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) (RRID: SCR_018393) score was used to predict the deleteriousness of each SNP, where a CADD score > 12.37 was considered as a harmful variant [29]. Additionally, the RegulomeDB (RDB) (RRID: SCR_017905) score was deployed to predict the likelihood of SNP regulatory function and chromatin status, ranging from 1 to 7, with 1 indicating high functionality and 7 indicating low functionality [30]. The chromatin states of the SNP regions were annotated using the 15-state ChromHMM model derived from 127 epigenomes provided by the Roadmap Epigenomics Project (RRID: SCR_008924). Chromatin states were assigned based on the most common state, with a value ≤7 indicating an open chromatin region. Additionally, for the selected epigenome, annotations were made based on the DNase peaks and chromatin states, where region 1 represents enhancers and region 2 represents promoters. The dyadic enhancer/promoter region was annotated for both. For each cancer type, we first calculated the proportion of pleiotropic SNPs annotated to each of the 15 chromatin states to generate the stacked bar chart. We further performed Fisher’s exact tests, using all annotated cancer GWAS SNPs as the background set, to evaluate the relative enrichment of pleiotropic SNPs in each chromatin state.

Sensitivity analyses for sample size imbalance and data source heterogeneity

To evaluate the robustness of our findings against differences in statistical power and data source heterogeneity, we performed two independent sensitivity analyses. The first subset included 20 cancers (190 cancer pairs) with more than 1000 cases from both published GWAS and FinnGen datasets, thereby excluding cancer types with limited sample sizes and low statistical power. The second, more homogeneous subset was restricted to FinnGen cancers with more than 1000 cases (12 cancers, 72 pairs), effectively reducing confounding heterogeneity from phenotype definitions, control selection, and potential sample overlap between published GWAS and FinnGen cohorts. In both subsets, we repeated the complete analytical pipeline for pleiotropic SNP identification and functional annotation, including genomic region distribution, eQTL-based regulation, chromatin interaction mapping, and multi-dimensional functional scoring. The results from the two subsets were systematically compared with those from the full set of 37 cancers.

Gene-based analysis

To identify candidate pleiotropic genes between paired cancers, we performed gene-based analysis using MAGMA (RRID: SCR_005757) [31] based on the PLACO results. We first downloaded the positions of 19,427 protein coding genes constructed based on NCBI 37.3 from the MAGMA website (https://cncr.nl/research/magma), and mapped SNPs to the genes (10 kb upstream and 1.5 kb downstream) according to genome coordinates. Then, MAGMA aggregated the P-values of all SNPs on each gene into the P value of a single gene. Genes located within or overlapping pleiotropic loci defined by FUMA (RRID: SCR_017521) were recognized as candidate pleiotropic genes. Among these, those with an FDR < 0.05 were further identified as statistically significant pleiotropic genes across cancers. Additionally, we employed P values from original single-cancer GWAS as input to identify genes associated with individual cancers.

Bayesian colocalization

We conducted Bayesian colocalization analyses on pleiotropic loci annotated by FUMA and candidate pleiotropic genes identified by MAGMA using the R package “coloc” (RRID: SCR_026041) [32] to further determine the shared causal variations of paired cancers in pleiotropic regions, rather than the results caused by LD. This method assumes a single causal variant and proposes five mutually exclusive hypotheses: (i) H0: Neither trait has a causal variation in this region; (ii) H1: Only trait 1 has a causal variant in this region; (iii) H2: Only trait 2 has a causal variant in this region; (iv) H3: The two traits have different causal variants in this region; (v) H4: Two traits share a causal variant in this region. Each hypothesis generates a posterior probability (PP), and the sum of the posterior probabilities of all hypotheses equals one. A higher posterior probability indicates greater support for the corresponding hypothesis. Regions with PP.H4 > 0.7 were considered to have potential shared causal variants in the pleiotropic loci or genes, and the SNP with the highest PP.H4 in this region was considered a candidate shared pathogenic variant.

Enrichment analysis

To explore the potential shared genetic mechanisms among cancers, we performed gene enrichment analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the R package “clusterProfiler” (RRID: SCR_016884) [33], based on gene sets from the Gene Ontology (GO) (RRID: SCR_002811) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (RRID: SCR_001120) databases. Pathways with FDR (i.e. p.adjust column) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

In gene enrichment analyses, we first selected genes showing pleiotropy across three skin-related cancers, including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma. These three cancers exhibited strong genetic correlations and clustered together in the global genetic correlation analyses. Additionally, we selected genes exhibiting pleiotropy across five or more cancer types. Enrichment analyses were then conducted separately to investigate the shared genetic mechanisms underlying skin cancers and pan-cancer pleiotropy.

We performed gene set enrichment analyses for each pair of cancers using Z-scores of all genes calculated by MAGMA. The GSEA method calculated the normalized enrichment score (NES), where positive and negative NES represent the degree of enrichment at the top and bottom of the ranked gene list, respectively. Pathways with NES > 2 and FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Analyses of summary-data-based Mendelian randomization

To identify methylation sites, genes, and proteins with potential genetically supported associations with cancer, we performed SMR (RRID: SCR_026042) [34] analyses by integrating mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL data with cancer GWAS datasets. mQTL and eQTL were also jointly utilized to identify methylation sites within a 2 MB window that influence gene expression, aiming to prioritize genes whose expression may be regulated by methylation and is genetically associated with cancer risk. SMR employs SNPs as instrumental variables to evaluate the association between an exposure and an outcome through shared genetic variants and incorporates the heterogeneity in dependent instruments (HEIDI) test to assess whether the observed associations are attributable to linkage disequilibrium (LD). Given that a single SNP often has a weak association with the trait, which reduces statistical power, multiple SNPs were used as instrumental variables (--smr-multi parameter) to obtain more stable and precise estimates of putative causal effects.

Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied separately for each cancer type and each molecular QTL category (mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL). Specifically, for each cancer–QTL category combination, all tested molecular traits were jointly corrected. SMR-supported associations with FDR < 0.05 and a HEIDI test P > 0.01 were considered significant, indicating that the observed associations were more likely driven by SNP pleiotropy rather than LD.

Identification of novel cancer SNPs and functional genes

To identify novel cancer risk SNPs and functional genes, we first retrieved all SNPs with genome-wide significant associations from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/, RRID: SCR_012745). The combined set of significant SNPs from both the GWAS Catalog and our study were considered as directly associated with cancer. We then calculated the LD between pleiotropic SNPs and directly associated SNPs, defining those located within 500 kb and with r2 > 0.5 as LD-associated SNPs [3, 4, 35–38]. The directly associated and LD-associated SNPs collectively constituted the full set of cancer-related variants (Supplementary Materials).

Novel pleiotropic SNPs and genes were identified according to the following criteria: (i) Among pleiotropic SNPs detected by PLACO, we excluded those associated with both cancers in the pair, including directly associated and LD-associated SNPs. The remaining SNPs were regarded as potential novel risk variants. (ii) If a cancer-related SNP was located within a gene region (defined as 10 kb upstream to 1.5 kb downstream), that gene was classified as a known cancer-associated gene. After excluding such genes, the remaining candidate pleiotropic genes identified by MAGMA at novel loci were considered candidate novel genes. (iii) Candidate novel genes were further prioritized by integrating the MAGMA and SMR results. A gene was retained as a potential novel functional pleiotropic gene only if it was significant in MAGMA (FDR < 0.05) and also showed significant SMR-supported associations with both cancers in the corresponding cancer pair (FDR < 0.05 and HEIDI P > 0.01).

Gene-targeted drug screening

The DrugBank [39] database (https://go.drugbank.com/, RRID: SCR_002700) was applied to obtain FDA-approved drugs targeting known genes.

Construction of the PleioCancer database

The PleioCancer database was developed to integrate the analytical results generated in this study together with publicly available GWAS summary statistics and molecular QTL datasets. It provides a unified platform for querying pleiotropic variants, candidate genes, SMR results, and functional annotations, facilitating exploration of shared genetic mechanisms across cancers.

The database employs a decoupled frontend-backend architecture. The frontend is built with the Vue 3 framework, and the backend is implemented using Flask, with data stored in MongoDB. For data visualization, ECharts was utilized on the frontend. The PleioCancer database is hosted on an Apache server, is publicly accessible online, and supports mainstream browsers such as Edge and Chrome.

Data availability

All additional data corresponding to the findings of this study are available within the article and its supplementary information files and from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Furthermore, all data presented in this study can be queried and visualized through our comprehensive PleioCancer database (https://gonglab.hzau.edu.cn/PleioCancer/).

Results

Summary information of cancer GWAS

We collected GWAS summary statistics of 37 cancer types (Fig. 1; Supplementary Fig. S1; Supplementary Table S1), with the number of cases ranging from 115 (Chronic myeloid leukaemia) to 122 188 (Prostate cancer). After quality control, the number of SNPs retained per cancer ranged from 7 611 226 in vulvar cancer to 9 286 980 in prostate cancer. Among these, 22 cancers contained significant risk SNPs (P < 5 × 10−8) (Supplementary Table S1), with prostate cancer having the highest number of lead SNPs (884), followed by breast cancer (408). SNP-based heritability estimates obtained via LDSC showed Z-scores > 2 for 20 cancers, indicating reliable heritability signals (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table S1). Among them, the Z-scores of nine cancers exceeded 4, suggesting high reliability. Especially, esophageal cancer had the highest heritability (h2 = 0.308), while ovarian cancer showed the lowest (h2 = 0.052).

Genetic correlation between cancer pairs

To ensure the stability of genetic correlation estimates, we limited the global and local genetic correlation analyses to the 20 cancers with heritability Z-scores above 2 (Fig. 2A), generating 190 cancer pairs for analysis.

LDSC estimated the genome-wide genetic correlations (rg) between cancer pairs (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S2). The results showed that 18 cancer pairs (9.5%) exhibited significant positive genetic correlations (FDR < 0.05), with an average rg of 0.298. No significant negative genetic correlations were observed. The strongest genetic correlation was observed between basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma (rg = 0.767, FDR = 4.84 × 10−19), while the lowest was between breast cancer and prostate cancer (rg = 0.116, FDR = 4.84 × 10−19). Additionally, 13.7% (26 pairs) of cancers showed suggestive evidence of genetic correlation (P < 0.05). After distance clustering based on genetic correlation, skin-related cancers (basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma) were grouped together (Fig. 2B), suggesting that these cancers may share a common genetic etiology. The bivariate LDSC cross-trait intercepts were close to zero for most cancer pairs, indicating limited covariance inflation attributable to sample overlap. Relatively higher intercepts were observed primarily among several cancer pairs derived from the FinnGen cohort, consistent with shared control participants within FinnGen (Supplementary Table S8).

Since global genetic correlation represents the average similarity across the entire genome, we further applied SUPERGNOVA to assess local genetic correlations between cancer pairs across 2353 LD regions defined by LOGODetect. The results showed that 267 regions exhibited significant local genetic correlations (FDR < 0.05) across 91 cancer pairs, involving 149 unique regions (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Table S3). 38.3% of significant regions exhibited local genetic correlations in at least two cancer pairs. Notably, 13 cancer pairs involving nine cancer types exhibited significant local genetic correlations within the chr1:182294372-183796074 region, which harbors multiple cancer-related genes, including GLUL and LAMC2.

A majority of cancer pairs with significant global genetic correlations (13 out of 18, 72.2%) also showed significant local genetic correlations in specific genomic regions (Fig. 2B). Additionally, SUPERGNOVA identified extra local genetic correlations in 78 cancer pairs that did not show significant global genetic correlations in the LDSC analyses.

Shared SNPs between cancer pairs and functional annotation

To systematically identify pleiotropic SNPs between cancers, we employed PLACO across all cancer pair combinations (37 cancer types and 666 pairs). A total of 75 243 pleiotropic SNPs (30 796 unique) were identified across 372 cancer pairs (P < 5 × 10−8), among which 3472 (2534 unique) were lead SNPs (r2 < 0.1) (Fig. 3A). The highest number of pleiotropic SNPs was identified between breast cancer and prostate cancer, with 7177 SNPs, including 203 lead SNPs. The number of identified pleiotropic SNPs was positively correlated with the total number of GWAS cases across the cancer pairs (Fig. 3B). Among the 3472 pleiotropic lead SNPs, 2046 (58.9%) had consistent effect directions in the GWAS summary data of the cancer pair, indicating that these variants may simultaneously increase or decrease the risk of cancer. In contrast, the remaining 1426 lead SNPs exhibited mixed directions, suggesting involvement of different biological mechanisms.

We assigned pleiotropic SNPs to 2527 risk loci using FUMA (Supplementary Table S4), involving 552 non-overlapping pleiotropic loci. Among all cancer pairs, the highest number of pleiotropic risk loci was identified between breast cancer and prostate cancer, with 114 loci. For the pleiotropic risk loci, several risk regions showed pleiotropy across 20 or more cancers (Table 1), including chr16:88578229-90244501 (16q24.2, 16q24.3), chr5:1123676-1543918 (5p15.33), chr8:127735049-128639116 (8q24.21), chr9:21692017-22125503 (9p21.3), and chr22:28195386-30905488 (22q12.1 and 22q12.2). Furthermore, Bayesian colocalization analyses showed that 345 pleiotropic loci (13.7%) exhibited significant colocalization (PP.H4 > 0.7). Among them, 62.9% (217) of the top SNPs (smallest PPLACO) at pleiotropic loci were the best shared causal variants (highest PP.H4). These results suggest that in the pleiotropic region, some SNPs may play a common causal role among multiple cancers.

Functional annotation of the 30 796 unique pleiotropic SNPs using ANNOVAR revealed that these variants were annotated to 1914 genes. While raw positional proportions indicated 62.7% of variants fell within intronic regions and 29.1% within intergenic regions (Supplementary Fig. S2A), regional variant density analysis identified splicing sites and untranslated regions as the compartments with the highest variant densities (Fig. 3C). eQTL analysis indicated that 27 157 (88.2%) pleiotropic SNPs regulated 2996 genes, identifying a total of 213 108 SNP-gene associations, with an average of eight genes regulated by one SNP (Fig. 3D). Chromatin interaction annotations identified 6378 (20.7%) pleiotropic SNPs interacting with 3782 genes, yielding 44 497 SNP-gene interactions, with an average of seven genes regulated by one SNP (Fig. 3E).

Integrating eQTL mapping and chromatin interaction analyses yields 14 188 SNP-gene associations were supported by two methods, involving 4516 SNPs and 1228 genes (Fig. 3F).

CADD scores indicated that 1348 pleiotropic SNPs were deleterious variants (CADD >12.37) (Supplementary Fig. S2B), with 36.1% being intronic variants, 25.1% being intergenic variants, and 15.7% being exonic variants. Three pleiotropic SNPs (rs11571833, rs202068855, rs9379084) had CADD scores > 30, and all were lead SNPs located in exonic regions. In addition, 148 pleiotropic SNPs showed moderate pathogenic potential, with CADD scores ranging from 20 to 30.

We further evaluated the potential regulatory functions of pleiotropic SNPs. RDB scores showed that 6.5% (2006) of pleiotropic SNPs had scores below 3 (Supplementary Fig. S2C), including 1023 SNPs with category 1 scores (supported by eQTL and transcription factor binding evidence). Two examples of potentially functional variants include rs884897 (RDB = 1a), which is in the intronic region of the LMTK2 gene and identified as a deleterious variant (CADD = 19.8), and rs17572495 (RDB = 1a), which is located in the 5′ UTR of the MAPT gene.

Functional annotation of pleiotropic SNPs from the eQTL and chromatin interaction intersection showed that 4516 SNPs were located in enhancer or dyadic regions, with 51.8% (2338) located in predicted chromatin open regions (Supplementary Fig. S2D). RDB predicted that 16.3% of SNPs (735) may have potential regulatory roles (RDB score < 3), and 7.0% (318) were considered deleterious (CADD >12.37). Furthermore, 35 pleiotropic SNPs were predicted to have potential functional roles in the above annotations.

We further characterized the genome-wide chromatin state distribution of all PLACO-identified pleiotropic SNPs across cancer types using the 15-state ChromHMM model from the Roadmap Epigenomics Project. A broadly consistent chromatin state composition pattern was observed across cancer types (Supplementary Fig. S2E). We next performed enrichment analysis to account for uneven genomic background in different regions. The forest plot showed that pan-cancer pleiotropic SNPs are significantly enriched within actively transcribed gene bodies and both weak and strong Polycomb-repressed chromatin domains (Fig. 3G).

Sensitivity analyses support the robustness of pleiotropic SNP functional profiles

Analysis of the two sensitivity subsets, namely the cross-cohort and FinnGen-only subsets both restricted to cancers with more than 1000 cases, 30 670 and 18 283 putative pleiotropic SNPs were identified, respectively, compared with 30 796 SNPs detected in the full 37-cancer analysis. Despite these differences in absolute SNP counts, the functional annotation characteristics remained highly consistent across all three analyses (Supplementary Table S7; Supplementary Fig. S3). The genomic region distribution patterns were nearly identical, with intronic SNPs accounting for 62.7%, 62.8%, and 65.8% of pleiotropic SNPs in the three analyses, respectively, and exonic SNPs consistently representing 3.0%. Similarly, the eQTL regulatory features, chromatin interaction patterns, and the proportions of SNPs located in open chromatin regions (~51.7%), with potential regulatory function (16.3%), or classified as deleterious (7.0%) were all highly concordant.

Candidate pleiotropic genes between cancer pairs

Among all cancer pairs, there were 8178 candidate pleiotropy genes (1875 unique) that overlapped with FUMA pleiotropic loci, of which 4272 (1182 unique) were identified as significant pleiotropy genes by MAGMA (FDR < 0.05). Most genes (42.6%) exhibit pleiotropy only in one pair of cancers, however, 52 genes had pleiotropy involving over 10 cancers (Fig. 4A), and five genes (TERT, CLPTM1L, ZNF276, FANCA, and VPS9D1) shared by over 20 cancers (Table 2). These genes with broad pleiotropy mainly encode proteins related to cellular stress responses, including apoptosis regulation, DNA repair, and cell cycle control. These processes are critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing tumor development caused by various stressors.

Shared genetic mechanisms in cancers

In genome-wide genetic correlation clustering analysis, skin-related cancers (basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma) were clustered into the same cluster, suggesting potential shared genetic mechanisms. Therefore, we performed enrichment analyses on 49 candidate pleiotropic genes shared among these three cancers. GO enrichment analysis identified 10 significantly enriched pathways in biological processes, 3 in cellular component terms, and 1 molecular function term (Fig. 4B). The results indicated that these genes were primarily involved in secondary metabolite synthesis, pigmentation—particularly melanin—and phenol-containing compound biosynthesis and metabolism, with pigmentation being a key factor in skin cancer development. Three genes (OCA2, SLC45A2, and TYRP1) are essential components of the pigment granule membrane, chitosome, and melanosome membrane, while three others (CTSK, CTSS, and DPEP1) regulate cysteine-type endopeptidase activity involved in apoptosis. No significant KEGG pathways were identified.

To further investigate shared genetic mechanisms across pan-cancer, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on 206 candidate pleiotropic genes shared among more than five cancers. After FDR < 0.05 correction, one GO term and 11 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched. The GO analysis revealed that four genes (ANXA9, CHRNA3, CHRNA5, and CHRNB4) were significantly enriched in the acetylcholine receptor activity pathway (GO:0015464, FDR = 0.025). KEGG analysis identified several canonical cancer-related pathways (Fig. 4C), including the p53 signaling pathway, cellular senescence, prolactin signaling, gastric cancer, human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 infection, hepatocellular carcinoma, the FoxO signaling pathway, the Hedgehog signaling pathway, and the cell cycle. Additionally, the enrichment of pathways related to Cushing’s syndrome and maturity-onset diabetes of the young suggests a possible shared genetic basis between these diseases and cancer.

We further repeated the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses in both sensitivity subsets to evaluate the potential influence of sample size imbalance and cross-cohort heterogeneity. The core oncogenic pathways, including p53 signaling, cell cycle, cellular senescence, gastric cancer, and human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 infection, were consistently and significantly enriched across both cohorts, with low FDR values and high enrichment ranks (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Gene set enrichment analyses were also performed across cancer pairs, identifying 1194 significantly enriched pathways (NES > 2, FDR < 0.05), including 1079 GO terms (401 unique) (Supplementary Table S5) and 115 KEGG pathways (43 unique) (Supplementary Table S6). Several GO terms were widely enriched across more than 20 cancer pairs, such as mitochondrial release of cytochrome c (GO:0001836), chromatin structure organization (GO:0030527), and intermediate filaments (GO:0005882). KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways such as bladder cancer (hsa05219), TGF-β signaling (hsa04350), systemic lupus erythematosus (hsa05322), pancreatic cancer (hsa05212), and drug metabolism-cytochrome P450 (hsa00982) across multiple cancer pairs.

Pleiotropic analyses identified novel cancer risk loci and functional genes

We constructed a reference set of known cancer-associated variants. by combining data from the GWAS Catalog and our primary GWAS summary statistics. The GWAS Catalog contributed 7706 genome-wide significant associations involving 4446 unique SNPs, while our summary statistics provided 78,791 associations involving 70 885 unique SNPs. Merging these two resources yielded a reference dataset of 73 161 unique cancer-associated SNPs across 88 672 SNP–cancer associations. After merging the two sources, the combined reference set comprised 73 161 unique cancer-associated SNPs and 88 672 SNP–cancer associations.

Using this reference set, we classified the 75 243 PLACO-identified pleiotropic SNPs across all cancer pairs according to their known association status. Among them, 10 123 SNPs were associated with both cancers in each pair, 59 829 SNPs were associated with only one cancer, and 5291 pleiotropic SNPs showed no association with either cancer. Among the 75 243 pleiotropic SNPs, 53 508 were located in candidate pleiotropic genes (within 10 kb upstream and 1.5 kb downstream), with 29 300 candidate pleiotropic genes associated with both cancers in each pair, and 967 candidate pleiotropic genes not associated with either cancer. Excluding the genes associated with both cancers, 1899 pairs of candidate pleiotropic genes and cancer associations showed that at least one cancer was not associated with the pleiotropic gene. These genes may represent potentially novel candidates for further investigation and might offer new therapeutic or diagnostic opportunities.

To prioritize functional pleiotropic genes, the 1899 novel pleiotropic gene–cancer associations were integrated with the SMR results derived from mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL analyses. Candidate genes were retained only when significant SMR-supported molecular associations were observed for each cancer in the corresponding cancer pair (FDR < 0.05 and HEIDI P > 0.01). Using this sequential prioritization framework, we found that 33 of these pleiotropic genes were prioritized functional genes for both two cancers (Fig. 5A). Colocalization analysis revealed 22 genes sharing causal variants between these cancer pairs (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. S5). Additionally, fifty methylation sites were identified as potential epigenetic mediators connecting pleiotropic SNPs to 13 functional genes based on significant mQTL associations (PmQTL < 5 × 10−8).

rs62005619 is a typical example of participating in pleiotropy regulation through molecular pathways. The minor allele of rs62005619 is associated with decreased methylation at cg12645742, leading to reduced SMAD6 expression. This reduction subsequently increases prostate cancer risk while decreasing colorectal cancer risk (Fig. 5B). This SNP is a deleterious variant in an active chromatin region (chromatin state = 1, CADD = 18.230), located in the 5′ UTR of the SMAD6 gene. It has strong regulatory potential (RDB = 1b) and may represent a novel risk locus for prostate cancer. Additionally, pQTL SMR analysis showed that elevated GDI2 protein expression is associated with a reduced risk of both breast cancer (β = −0.232, FDR = 0.010) and prostate cancer (β = −0.215, FDR = 0.002), highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Among the 33 prioritized functional genes, newly identified risk loci for HAUS6, MAFF, and CHEK2 received additional statistical support in the latest version of the FinnGen database (R12, 2024.12.04). These genes were identified as novel functional genes in thyroid cancer, basal cell carcinoma, and meningioma, respectively. With increasing sample sizes and statistical power, some SNPs that were previously not genome-wide significant in GWAS reached statistical significance in the updated data (P < 5 × 10−8) (Fig. 5C).

HAUS6 was identified as a pleiotropic functional gene between thyroid and prostate cancer, showing significant co-localization in GWAS of both cancers. Its LD region (chr9:18660695-19129349) exhibited significant local genetic correlation (corr = 0.874, FDR = 1.76 × 10−4). Among the 53 pleiotropic SNPs in HAUS6, 52 were significant only in prostate cancer GWAS, while one SNP was non-significant in both GWAS datasets. As the sample size for thyroid cancer increased from 1907 to 2859 cases, 31 SNPs reached genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) (Fig. 5C). Among these 53 pleiotropic SNPs, five were in the 3′ UTR (rs4977490, rs7856768, rs1048169, rs4977489, and rs4977492), while two were in exonic regions (rs13302748, rs13286328). Chromatin state annotations showed that all these SNPs were located in open chromatin regions, with four situated in enhancer regions (rs1154672, rs7026929, rs12003940, and rs10963932).

MAFF was identified as a pleiotropic functional gene across basal cell carcinoma, melanoma, and prostate cancer, with its region showing significant colocalization in GWAS. In pleiotropic analysis between basal cell carcinoma and melanoma, 30 novel basal cell carcinoma risk SNPs were identified within the MAFF gene, three of which also passed the pleiotropy test between basal cell carcinoma and prostate cancer. As the number of basal cell carcinoma cases in FinnGen cohort increased from 20 506 to 26 272, these SNPs reached genome-wide significance in GWAS. Among them, rs2267372 was located in the 5′ UTR of MAFF within an enhancer region. The A allele was associated with increased MAFF expression (eQTLGen Z-score = 15.532, P = 2.11 × 10−54), suggesting that this SNP may influence basal cell carcinoma development by regulating MAFF transcription.

CHEK2 was identified as a pleiotropic functional gene shared by meningioma and prostate cancer, harboring nine newly identified risk loci. Among these, rs182075939 and rs17879961 reached genome-wide significance as the sample size increased from 1316 to 1835 cases. Additionally, rs17879961 is a highly deleterious exonic SNP (CADD = 23.800), suggesting that it may play a critical role in cancer susceptibility by affecting protein function.

Sensitivity analysis further indicated that the identification of these novel functional genes was robust to the LD threshold used for defining known variants. Across r2 cutoffs ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 (with a fixed 500 kb window), the number of prioritized functional genes remained stable (total genes 31–34; unique genes 27–30) (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Drug-association analyses identified drug-targetable genes

We queried the DrugBank database for the 33 newly identified genes and found that CHEK2, CPNE1, MAP2K1, and SREBF1 are associated with existing FDA-approved targeted therapies (Fig. 5D). Notably, MAP2K1, identified as a pleiotropic gene in the pleiotropic analysis of prostate cancer and breast cancer, is a potential novel gene for breast cancer. This gene has FDA-approved targeted drugs used for melanoma treatment, including bosutinib and cobimetinib. These drugs were tested on the triple-negative breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231, showing half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of 7.009 μM for bosutinib and 9.874 μM for cobimetinib (Fig. 5E).

PleioCancer: an online tool for exploring cancer pleiotropy and shared mechanisms

We developed the PleioCancer database (https://gonglab.hzau.edu.cn/PleioCancer/) to offer online access to cancer pleiotropy-related findings and facilitate further exploration of shared genetic mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. The database contains 75 243 pleiotropic SNPs identified by PLACO across 372 cancer pairs and 4272 candidate pleiotropic genes identified by MAGMA. It also catalogs 5428 methylation sites, 1726 genes, and 229 proteins with potential causal associations to cancer, identified through SMR (vertical pleiotropy). Additionally, the database provides SMR results linking prioritized methylation sites to genes, along with corresponding GWAS, mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL datasets for associated cancers, SNPs, methylation sites, genes, and proteins offering users comprehensive resources to facilitate further exploration of pleiotropic mechanisms.

The homepage includes a quick search function, allowing users to retrieve relevant information by entering SNPs, methylation probes, gene symbols, genomic regions, or cancer types (Fig. 6A). The database consists of four modules: “Pleiotropic sites” and “SMR sites” allow users to search, browse, and download all significant results from pleiotropy and SMR analyses. Additionally, scatter plots visualize PLACO, SMR, GWAS, and QTL P values, as well as Z-scores within a 1 MB window around relevant loci (Fig. 6B). The “Network graph” module presents an interactive network visualization, incorporating five types of nodes (cancers, SNPs, methylation sites, genes, and proteins) and 10 types of relationships, including pleiotropy, mQTL, eQTL, pQTL, GWAS, methylation–gene potential causal associations, cancer–methylation potential causality, and cancer–gene/protein potential causality (Fig. 6C). The “Help” page provides an overview of the database and a user guide. The database will be regularly updated to incorporate new data and analytical results, with version documentation maintained to support reproducibility.

Discussion and conclusion

This study represents the first comprehensive effort to explore shared genetic factors across 37 cancer types. We observed widespread genome-wide and local genetic correlations among cancers. Further integrative analyses highlighted the potential genetic basis of pleiotropic genetic variants, loci, genes, and biological pathways, revealing the common genetic etiology of cancers. Additionally, the identification of novel risk loci and functional genes provides new directions for cancer research. Further drug repurposing analysis identified several potential pleiotropic gene targets, opening new possibilities for personalized cancer treatment. Finally, we constructed the PleioCancer database, providing researchers with a comprehensive resource platform for cancer pleiotropy.

Our study identified several pleiotropic risk loci and genes associated with more than 20 types of cancer. FANCA (16q24.3) is an important gene related to Fanconi anemia, a condition in which patients are at a higher risk of developing myeloid leukemia, liver tumors, head and neck cancers, and gynecological malignancies than the general population [40, 41]. The Fanconi anemia pathway is involved in a complex DNA damage signaling and repair network crucial for genomic stability, a key driver of malignancies [41]. The TERT gene (5p15.33) encodes the catalytic subunit of telomerase, which maintains telomere length and prevents cellular senescence, promoting cellular immortality and malignant transformation [42]. POU5F1B (8q24.21) is a weak transcriptional activator that has been reported to promote proliferation and migration in multiple cancers [43–45]. The CDKN2A and CDKN2B genes (9p21.3) are well-known tumor suppressors. CDKN2A (also known as p16) encodes a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, and CDKN2B, its paralog, is frequently mutated or deleted in various cancers [46–48]. This region also contains the CDKN2B-AS1 long noncoding RNA, which has been implicated in cancer-related processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition [49]. In addition, CHEK2 [50] and XBP1 [51] at 22q12.1 have been shown to play significant roles in cancer development.

Our pan-cancer pathway analysis revealed convergent biological mechanisms across malignancies. First, the enrichment of well-known cancer pathways (p53 signaling, cell cycle regulation, cellular senescence, FoxO, and Hedgehog) confirms that our framework effectively captures core tumor biology [52]. Second, cross-cancer analyses uncovered broader systemic pathways often missed by single-cancer GWAS, such as HTLV-1 infection [53], Cushing’s syndrome, MODY [54]. and prolactin signaling [55], suggesting shared immune, endocrine-metabolic, and signaling mechanisms across cancers. Tissue-specifically, melanin metabolism was selectively enriched in skin cancers. Furthermore, pairwise analyses identified mitochondrial cytochrome c release (GO:0001836) as a widely shared process [56, 57], reinforcing apoptosis dysregulation as a key pathogenic hallmark [58].

Several novel risk loci and functional genes were further supported in larger GWAS datasets of thyroid cancer (HAUS6), basal cell carcinoma (MAFF), and meningioma (CHEK2), revealing key players in cancer susceptibility and progression. HAUS6 is a subunit of the Augmin complex, which plays a crucial role in microtubule formation and chromosome segregation during cell division [59]. Chromosomal missegregation leading to aneuploidy is a key hallmark of many cancers [60]. MAFF (v-maf musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homolog F) is implicated in multiple cancers. Elevated MAFF expression has been reported in metastatic breast cancer, where it acts as an activator of the IL11/STAT3 pathway to promote tumor progression [61]. In contrast, studies in lung adenocarcinoma suggest that MAFF has tumor-suppressive effects [62]. CHEK2, a protein kinase, is a well-established multi-organ cancer susceptibility gene and one of the most frequently mutated genes in hereditary breast cancer [50].

Through drug repositioning analysis, we prioritized bosutinib and cobimetinib as candidate compounds for breast cancer. Our IC50 assays suggested preliminary anti-proliferative activity in the MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer cell line, providing proof-of-concept support for the computational predictions. This experimental evidence aligns with existing literature. Specifically, bosutinib is known to suppress breast cancer growth by inhibiting DNA damage repair pathways [63]. Likewise, MEK inhibition, including cobimetinib, has demonstrated anti-tumor activity in preclinical models of triple-negative breast cancer [64], and cobimetinib-containing combination regimens have entered clinical evaluation for breast cancer [65]. However, because our current validation is limited to a single cell line, these findings remain exploratory. Further evaluation across diverse breast cancer models, detailed mechanistic investigations, and in vivo studies are required to fully determine their therapeutic potential.

Compared with previous pan-cancer pleiotropy studies [3, 4, 66], our work achieves systematic advances in several respects. First, earlier efforts were largely confined to common solid tumors and encompassed a limited number of cancer types. We substantially expanded the scope to 37 cancer types, spanning common solid tumors, rare cancers, hematological malignancies, and central nervous system tumors (aggregate sample size >430 000), thereby addressing a gap in the shared genetic mechanisms in rare malignancies. Second, methodologically, previous studies primarily identified pleiotropic variants at the single-SNP level with limited downstream dissection, whereas we constructed an integrated framework spanning genome-wide genetic correlation estimation, pleiotropic SNP screening, locus definition, gene-level analysis, Bayesian colocalization, and multi-omics SMR-based causal inference. This multi-level framework enables hierarchical exploration from genetic variants to functional genes and biological pathways, providing a more comprehensive understanding of cross-cancer shared genetic mechanisms than single-layer analyses. Third, in translational application, most existing pan-cancer pleiotropy studies focused merely on genetic exploration and rarely linked genetic findings to therapeutic applications. We performed drug repurposing by mapping newly identified pleiotropic genes to targeted drugs using the DrugBank database. Finally, we constructed the open-access PleioCancer database as a reusable resource for future research.

Because the GWAS datasets varied substantially in sample size, the statistical power to detect pleiotropic associations differed across cancer pairs. Pairs with larger sample sizes yielded more pleiotropic variants, while those involving smaller GWAS datasets were likely underestimated. However, PLACO requires joint evidence from both cancers and is not driven solely by one well-powered GWAS. Thus, the observed differences reflect variation in statistical power rather than methodological bias. Pleiotropic associations involving cancers with relatively small GWAS remain incompletely characterized, and additional shared loci are expected to emerge as larger GWAS become available.

Our study has several strengths. First, this is a comprehensive investigation of the shared genetic etiology of multiple cancers. We used pleiotropy testing to identify novel risk loci and functional genes, avoiding the high costs associated with increasing sample sizes in individual cancer GWASs. Second, we applied SMR analysis to uncover molecular pathways linking methylation, gene expression, and cancer, providing mechanistic insights for further studies.

Although our analyses identified a large number of pleiotropic SNPs and genes, these findings represent different levels of evidence. Statistical pleiotropy detected from GWAS summary statistics indicates shared genetic associations between cancers but cannot, by itself, distinguish true shared causal variants from associations arising through LD, correlated traits, or highly polygenic genetic architecture. Consequently, the complete catalog of statistically significant associations should be regarded primarily as a discovery resource. To increase confidence in biological interpretation, we integrated multiple complementary analyses that provide progressively stronger evidence for shared genetic mechanisms. Functional prioritization identified 33 putative pleiotropic genes, 22 of which were independently supported by Bayesian colocalization, while representative novel genes (HAUS6, MAFF, and CHEK2) were further supported in the expanded FinnGen R12 dataset. Accordingly, we focus our biological discussion on these high-confidence genes backed by multiple lines of evidence, while the remaining associations serve as a valuable resource for future experimental validation.

However, our study has some limitations. First, our analysis was limited to European populations, and genetic background differences may limit generalizability to other ancestries. Second, the SMR analyses were based on blood-derived eQTL and mQTL datasets together with plasma-derived pQTL datasets. While these large population-based resources provide statistical power for pan-cancer analysis, they may not fully capture tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms within individual tumors. Future studies integrating large-scale tissue-specific QTL resources and functional validation are warranted. Third, this study relied exclusively on publicly available GWAS summary statistics rather than individual-level genotype data. We integrated 10 published GWAS datasets and 27 FinnGen GWAS datasets and performed rigorous harmonization across all datasets, including uniform European ancestry filtering, standardized SNP quality control thresholds, consistent effect allele alignment, and unified adoption of the 1000 Genomes LD reference panel. Nevertheless, several types of residual heterogeneity could not be fully eliminated, including subtle differences in clinical diagnostic criteria and tumor subtype classification across cohorts, inconsistent control definitions (healthy controls in published GWAS versus cancer-free controls from the same cohort in FinnGen), and potential sample overlap between published European GWAS and FinnGen cohorts that cannot be fully excluded using summary-level statistics alone. These residual heterogeneities may modestly affect the detection of weak pleiotropic signals. Fourth, although several prioritized genes were supported by multiple independent lines of evidence, this primarily reflects convergent statistical and molecular support rather than direct experimental validation of the proposed pleiotropic mechanisms. Therefore, additional independent replication studies and experimental functional validation will be essential to further establish the biological mechanisms underlying these pleiotropic associations. Fifth, the coloc framework assumes a single causal variant within each tested locus, which may not hold in complex cancer susceptibility regions such as 8q24 and 5p15.33, where multiple independent association signals have been reported. In such regions, allelic heterogeneity may influence the estimated posterior probabilities, potentially affecting the interpretation of shared causal variants.

In summary, our study elucidates pleiotropic genetic mechanisms across cancers, and highlights broadly shared risk loci, genes, and pathways. We also identify novel cancer risk loci and functional genes, providing potential drug targets for personalized cancer therapy. These findings offer critical theoretical and practical insights for understanding cancer genetics and developing cross-cancer prevention and treatment strategies.
