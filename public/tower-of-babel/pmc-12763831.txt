Clinical analysis of non-ketotic hyperglycemia with reversible cortical blindness: a case report
Authors: Litao Wang, Wenting Li, Fengjuan Zhang, Rujing Ren, Xiaoyu Xin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-ketotic hyperglycemia is a clinical syndrome caused by poor blood glucose control, which can lead to unilateral chorea, seizures, headaches, and other neurological deficit symptoms. Due to the lack of specificity of clinical symptoms and unclear pathogenesis, many patients are misdiagnosed and may delay treatment.Case presentationTwo male patients of Han nationality in China, one 61 years old and the other 33 years old, were admitted to our hospital with persistent hemianopia and headache, both with a background of type 2 diabetes mellitus and poor glycemic control on admission. They also had abnormal signals on magnetic resonance imaging of the head, manifested as focal subcortical hypointensity and cerebral gyrus hyperintensity. Through comprehensive evaluation of clinical features, imaging changes, and treatment effect, the diagnosis of non-ketotic hyperglycemia was considered to be relatively clear. After active diagnosis and treatment, reasonable control of blood glucose, clinical symptoms, and abnormal imaging changes disappeared completely. No evidence of recurrence was found in patients with follow-up for half a year.ConclusionClinicians should improve the necessity of non-ketotic hyperglycemia screening for patients with sudden headache, visual field defect, epilepsy, and hemichorea. This case report emphasizes the clinical diagnosis and treatment ideas and measures of non-ketotic hyperglycemia to increase the sensitivity of clinical diagnosis and the clinical detection rate.Keywords: Non-ketotic hyperglycemia, NKH, Diabetes, Clinical features

Introduction

Non-ketotic hyperglycemia (NKH) occurs because of partial insulin deficiency, with enough insulin to inhibit free fatty acid mobilization and ketoacidosis but not sufficient to transport glucose into cells. Severe hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity, and intracellular dehydration without ketoacidosis are the main pathophysiological characters of NHK. Hyperglycemia could mediate endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and cause neuronal damage [1]. NKH leading to acute neurological disorders is rare in clinical, but still, there are some cases that have exhibited that patients suffered from different debilitating symptoms. Some NKH patients present hemichorea, hemianopsia, seizure, and even coma [2]. Some patients showed acute stroke-like deficits associated with NKH. However, the definite mechanisms of hyperglycemia-induced cerebral injury remain speculative. Here, we will describe two cases of reversible NHK reversible cortical blindness, which have rarely been reported before. In this report, we present two cases and review the relevant literature by summarizing the details of clinical features, radiologic manifestations, possible pathophysiological mechanisms, and current therapies for both NKH-related encephalopathy and NKH-related epilepsy to create more awareness regarding the manifestations of NKH-related encephalopathy. In addition, this case report could help neurologists make early diagnoses and patient treatment plans to possibly avoid visual field defect and epilepsy.

First case description

A 61-year-old Han Chinese male patient was admitted to the hospital in February 2024 due to “blurred vision accompanied by headache for 9 days.” The patient had a medical history of hypertension, diabetes, acute promyelocytic leukemia, chronic hepatitis B, and proliferative diabetic retinopathy requiring surgery. The patient reported unexplained left visual field defects, followed by global headache, nausea, and a sensation of unsteadiness while walking. Initial evaluations at a local hospital, including cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) angiography of the head and neck, revealed punctate abnormal signals in the bilateral frontal and parietal white matter, as well as multiple arterial plaques and mild stenosis. The patient visited our hospital due to unsatisfactory treatment results at local hospital.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and articulate, but exhibited limited abduction of both eyes and left visual field defects (Fig. 1A). Neurological examination revealed suspicious bilateral pathological signs and mild neck stiffness. Laboratory tests showed significantly elevated blood glucose (fasting blood glucose was 19.08 mmol/L and 2-hour postprandial blood glucose 20.34 mmol/L) and glycated hemoglobin (9.2%) levels. The first lumbar puncture was performed on 20 February; cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis indicated increased glucose (11.34 mmol/L) and protein (1727.26 mg/L) levels.

During the hospitalization, enhanced cranial magnetic resonance venography (MRV) demonstrated a thinner left transverse sinus and internal jugular vein compared with the contralateral side. Susceptibility-weighted imaging of the head suggested possible scattered old microhemorrhages and suspicious DWI hyperintensities in the bilateral frontal and parietal lobes. Enhanced cranial MRI revealed signal abnormalities in the right occipital lobe and hippocampus (Fig. 2A–C), as well as scattered lacunar infarcts in the bilateral frontal lobes. Electroencephalography (EEG) showed diffuse low-potential theta waves, with slow-wave dominance in the right hemisphere and independent sharp-slow wave discharges in the frontal–temporal region, more pronounced on the right side.

After hospitalization, the patient received symptomatic management including intravenous immunoglobulin (IG) for immune modulation, high-dose steroid pulse therapy [500 mg intravenous glucose tolerance test (IVGTT) × 5 days] for antiinflammatory purposes, mannitol for dehydration, and insulin for glycemic control. The second lumbar puncture was performed on 26 February, CSF analysis showed normal glucose (6.72 mmol/L) and protein (72 mg/L) levels, and a positive 14 - 3- 3 protein result (Table 1). After the treatment, the deficit of the visual field was partially reversed (Fig. 1B), and the patient said no more red and green flash occurred. The patient was admitted to the hospital for examination again 1 month after being discharged. The MRI examination showed that the abnormal lesions had improved significantly (Fig. 2D–F).

Second case description

A 33-year-old Han Chinese male patient presented to our hospital with an abrupt onset of blurred vision in the right visual field of both eyes, accompanied by left-sided headache without nausea or vomiting. The patient had a 3-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, managed with metformin, but had not undergone regular blood glucose monitoring. There was no history of hypertension, migraine, alcohol abuse, or illicit drug use.

Ophthalmic evaluation revealed a visual acuity of 20/25 in the right eye (OD) and 20/20 in the left eye (OS). Intraocular pressures, pupillary responses, and extraocular motility were within normal limits. Visual field testing identified dense right homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing (Fig. 1C). Dilated fundus examination showed a normal optic disc with evidence of diabetic retinopathy. Neurological examination revealed no additional deficits or abnormalities.

Laboratory investigations indicated a serum glucose level of 540 mg/dL and an elevated osmolality of 348 mOsm/L. Spot urine analysis was negative for ketones. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 11.8%. Other laboratory parameters, including complete blood count, thyroid function tests, antiphospholipid antibody profile, antinuclear antibody, complement levels, glutamate decarboxylase antibody, insulin autoantibody, islet cell antibody, and cerebrospinal fluid analysis, were within normal ranges. Serologic testing for human immunodeficiency virus was negative.

Electroencephalography (EEG) revealed no epileptiform activity. Cranial imaging demonstrated abnormal lesions (Fig. 4A–C). T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI sequences revealed hyperintense lesions in the cortical gray matter of the left occipito-temporal lobes, with corresponding hypointense lesions in the subcortical white matter on FLAIR and T2-weighted images. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences showed mildly hyperintense regions. MRV showed no evidence of sinus thrombosis. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) indicated a reduction in N-acetylaspartate (NAA) levels in the left occipito-temporal regions, accompanied by mild elevations in choline and creatine (Fig. 5).

Following admission, the patient was treated with intravenous hydration and insulin therapy. His headache gradually resolved, likely due to the alleviation of cortical edema, and his visual symptoms improved concurrently. After 3 weeks, no visual field defects were observed, and 1 month post-discharge, a follow-up MRI at a local hospital demonstrated complete resolution of the previously identified abnormalities (Fig. 4D–F).

Discussion

Typically, patients with NKH exhibit elevated blood glucose levels and HbA1c. It has been documented that the average blood glucose level in patients with NKH is 481.5 mg/dL (range 169–1264 mg/dL), with an average HbA1c level of 14.4% (range 9.9–19.2%) [3]. NKH predominantly affects individuals with type 2 diabetes who are over the age of 50 years and is associated with a variety of neurological manifestations, including delirium, seizures, hemiplegia, dysphagia, hemianopsia, and hemisensory loss [4]. The basal ganglia and occipital lobe are particularly susceptible to injury in these cases. In patients with NKH, lesions in the basal ganglia can lead to hemichorea-hemiballismus (HC-HB). CT scans may reveal hyperdensity in the bilateral or contralateral globus pallidus (GP) or putamen, with high signal intensity on T1-weighted images [5]. Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain HC-HB in the context of hyperglycemia, including petechial hemorrhage, gemistocytes, and calcification. This condition is generally reversible in the majority of patients [6]. When NKH affects cortical or subcortical regions, it can induce NKH-related epilepsy and NKH-related encephalopathy [7].

On the basis of the clinical manifestations, laboratory results, imaging findings, and disease progression, the diagnosis of non-ketotic hyperglycemic encephalopathy is highly plausible.

Acute central nervous system (CNS) damage caused by non-ketotic hyperglycemia, as observed in the aforementioned cases, is relatively rare in clinical practice. However, similar cases have been documented. For instance, a 77-year-old female patient with a history of poorly controlled adult-onset diabetes mellitus (AODM) initially presented with stroke-like deficits. Following medical intervention and the normalization of blood glucose levels, her symptoms were completely reversed [8]. This underscores the critical importance of prompt and effective treatment in NKH to prevent neuronal or glial cell damage in the CNS, a finding corroborated by our cases. In contrast, other reported cases involving vision loss and visual field defects have resulted in irreversible symptoms, likely due to delayed diagnosis [9].

The mechanisms underlying NKH-related encephalopathy remain incompletely understood, leading to diverse perspectives within the academic community. One prevailing hypothesis suggests that hyperglycemia induces a hyperosmolar or near-hyperosmolar state in the extracellular fluid over a short period, causing varying degrees of neuronal dehydration. This in turn leads to insufficient cerebral cortical blood supply, with clinical manifestations varying depending on the extent and location of cortical involvement [10]. Despite the wide variability in clinical presentations of NKH-related encephalopathy, a common predisposing factor—hyperglycemia—is consistently observed. Cellular-level studies have further identified oxidative stress, nitric oxide dysregulation, glutathione metabolic system dysfunction, and endothelial impairment as contributing factors that individually or collectively disrupt brain metabolism, ultimately leading to diabetic encephalopathy [11].

Currently, the mechanism underlying epilepsy induced by NKH remains a subject of debate. It is widely recognized that brain cells primarily derive their energy supply through gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). In a hyperglycemic state, the tricarboxylic acid cycle is significantly inhibited, and increased GABA metabolism further elevates cerebral energy consumption. This disruption substantially interferes with neurotransmitter transmission, thereby promoting the thalamic-cortical feedback pathway and lowering the seizure threshold [12, 13]. Furthermore, the adverse effects of abnormal fluctuations in blood glucose levels predominantly impact the posterior regions of the brain, particularly the parietal and occipital lobes. This observation serves as a critical theoretical basis for the occurrence of focal or occipital seizures [14]. Kim et al. [15] propose that hyperglycemia can increase the permeability of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and BBB leakage may also contribute to epileptic activity. Persistent hyperglycemia can lead to alterations in intracellular and extracellular osmotic pressure, causing dehydration of nerve cells and a reduction in cell membrane stability, ultimately resulting in epilepsy [16]. Some studies indicate that a significant proportion of patients with non-ketotic hyperglycemic epilepsy exhibit underlying cerebral ischemic changes. Prolonged hyperglycemia may reduce regional cerebral blood flow, leading to reversible focal ischemic events without structural brain damage. These metabolic changes in the brain increase the likelihood of seizures [17]. Additionally, some scholars suggest that autoimmune mechanisms [18] and neurodegeneration hypotheses [19] may also play significant roles, though further research is required to substantiate these perspectives. NKH-induced epilepsy can manifest in various seizure types, including simple partial seizures, complex partial seizures, generalized tonic–clonic seizures, and in rare cases, status epilepticus. Among these, simple partial seizures are the most frequently observed [20]. Simple sensory seizures often involve the occipital lobe and may present as hemianopsia, quadrantanopia, visual hallucinations, visual distortions, or deflection attacks. In rare instances, patients may experience transient visual hallucinations accompanied by hemianopsia [21].

For the clinical diagnosis of NKH-related epilepsy, in addition to nonspecific clinical manifestations and abnormal blood glucose levels, it is necessary to conduct electroencephalogram (EEG), cranial imaging, and other auxiliary examinations. For patients with specific conditions, visual evoked potential (VEP) and 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) can provide a more comprehensive clinical assessment. In most cases, patients with non-ketotic hyperglycemic epilepsy exhibit abnormal imaging changes. CT typically reveals unilateral or bilateral high-density lesions, which should be carefully differentiated from hemorrhagic diseases. Cranial MRI demonstrates reversible T2-weighted cortical region abnormalities and high signal intensity in the FLAIR sequence, while the diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequence shows cytotoxic edema, which is considered a typical imaging feature of non-ketotic hyperglycemic epilepsy [22]. The subcortical low signal intensity observed on T2-weighted MRI may be associated with excitotoxic damage to neurons in the corresponding area caused by early cortical ischemia or seizures, leading to increased iron deposition and the accumulation of free radicals [4, 23]. Iwata et al. [24] suggest that in the early stages of the disease, there may be significant enhancement changes on gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MRI, potentially due to BBB damage. Additionally, decreased perfusion at the lesion site can be observed on PET/CT [25]. The imaging findings typically improve with symptom control, although the short signal on MRI-T1 may persist for an extended period.

There remains a notable absence of standardized guidelines or expert consensus regarding the diagnosis and treatment of non-ketotic hyperglycemic encephalopathy. In clinical practice, it is imperative to differentiate this condition from other diseases that present with similar symptoms, clinical manifestations, and imaging characteristics (as outlined in Table 2). Furthermore, it is essential to distinguish non-ketotic hyperglycemic encephalopathy from hypoglycemic encephalopathy, toxic encephalopathy, autoimmune encephalopathy, central nervous system infections, and other related disorders to minimize the risk of clinical misdiagnosis.

The primary therapeutic approach focuses on blood glucose control, judicious use of antiepileptic drugs, and supportive measures such as anti-infective therapy. During the acute phase of the disease, blood glucose levels should be managed through continuous insulin infusion via micropumps or intravenous administration. Once blood glucose is stabilized below 14 mmol/L, a gradual transition to oral hypoglycemic agents may be considered. It is important to emphasize that blood glucose reduction should be conducted gradually, as overly rapid correction may precipitate epileptic seizures. For patients experiencing non-ketotic hyperglycemic epileptic seizures, the efficacy of antiepileptic drugs alone is often limited. However, in cases of status epilepticus or frequent seizures, prompt control of seizures is essential. Clinically, carbamazepine or sodium valproate is recommended as the first-line treatment [32]. Caution should be exercised with phenytoin sodium and gabapentin, as they may induce insulin resistance and elevate blood glucose levels, while phenobarbital and diazepam can exacerbate impaired consciousness. Therefore, their use requires careful consideration. Furthermore, long-term administration of antiepileptic drugs is generally unnecessary. Once blood glucose levels are well controlled and no further seizures are observed clinically, the dosage may be tapered or discontinued on the basis of the patient’s condition.

Conclusion

This phenomenon has been characterized as NKH-related encephalopathy, manifesting neuronal dysfunctions such as hemianopsia and seizures. Given that type 2 diabetes represents a significant global health burden, it is imperative to comprehensively understand the diverse neurological manifestations associated with non-ketotic hyperglycemia. Our findings underscore the critical importance of integrating both laboratory results and diagnostic imaging for the accurate identification of NKH-related encephalopathy. Furthermore, prompt and aggressive therapeutic intervention is essential to prevent irreversible neuronal damage.
