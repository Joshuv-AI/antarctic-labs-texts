Bayesian Estimation of Corporate Default Spreads
Maksim Papenkov & \;\& Beau Robinette

Abstract
Risk-averse investors often wish to exclude stocks from their portfolios that bear high credit risk , which is a measure of a firm’s likelihood of bankruptcy. This risk is commonly estimated by constructing signals from quarterly accounting items, such as debt and income volatility. While such information may provide a rich description of a firm’s credit risk, the low-frequency with which the data is released implies that investors may be operating with outdated information. In this paper we circumvent this problem by developing a high-frequency credit risk proxy via corporate default spreads which are estimated from daily bond price data. We accomplish this by adapting classic yield curve estimation methods to a corporate bond setting, leveraging advances in Bayesian estimation to ensure higher model stability when working with small sample data which also allows us to directly model the uncertainty of our predictions.



Keywords
- Credit Risk Modeling ; Default Spread Estimation ; Bayesian Learning

1
Research Motivation

1.1
Problem

A single extreme credit event can significantly erode an investor’s accumulated wealth. While stocks for credit risky firms offer a high potential upside, for investors with a low tolerance for risk it may be desirable to exclude such firms from their portfolios entirely. Without sufficient data and an appropriate credit risk model, identifying risky firms is generally difficult - though an emerging literature has offered several viable model frameworks
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Utilizing a robust credit model, a risk-averse investor has the ability to avoid stocks with high credit risk, potentially providing a significant reduction to the drawdown risk of their portfolio.

1.2
Existing Solution (and its Limitations)

The standard approach towards modeling issuer credit risk is with a
structural credit risk model
that predicts some exogenous event (such as a bankruptcy) with a factor model
[
4
]
. Often, the factors used within such a model are derived from quarterly financial statements, primarily focusing on a firm’s quantity of debt and its estimated ability to repay it. While such models are generally very effective in practice, they have two critical limitations :

•

High-Latency
: Financial statements are prepared by accountants and take several weeks (or months) to process, and thus do not always represent the most recent information.

•

Low-Frequency
: Per SEC rules, financial statements are only issued quarterly, and if some high-impact even occurs in between financial statements, the model would become inconsistent with reality.

We can circumvent these issues by pivoting our attention away from quarterly financials to daily bond price data, which is directly influenced by the market’s perception around a firm’s ability to repay its debts.

1.3
New Solution (and its Value Proposition)

We introduce the
Bayesian Default Spread
which adapts classic methods for yield curve estimation to a corporate bond setting. Corporate bond prices are generally much more difficult to work with than government bonds - as the sample sizes are much smaller, the assets tend to be less liquid, and the data tends to be more noisy. We address these issues with Bayesian estimation, which allows us to regularize our parameters in a way that ensures stability despite the data limitations.

We structure this paper as a full methodological tutorial, beginning with first principals and gradually building up to the full Bayesian method. This new method offers the following benefits :

•

Low-Latency
: since corporate bonds are traded in large markets, their prices quickly adjust to reflect newly available information on a firm.

•

High-Frequency
: for many firms, their bonds are traded many times within a quarter, sometimes daily, providing a full time-series of changes throughout a year.

A clear limitation of our method is that not all firms issue bonds, limiting the utility of the Bayesian Default Spread as a credit risk signal to only large-cap firms. However, fixed income markets have significantly expanded in the last decade, and as the number of traded bonds has increased the viability of this method has improved in recent years.

2
Principals for Bond Pricing

Before delving into regression architectures, we’ll first discuss the necessary prerequisite math. Formally, a
bond
is a contract providing a deterministic sequence of
M
-many cash payments, such that each payment is denoted as
c_{t}
, represented in currency. These payments are temporally-indexed by
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

\mathcal{B}=\{c_{t}\}_{\mathcal{T}}=\{c_{t_{1}},c_{t_{2}},\dots,c_{t_{M}}\}

(1)

A bond’s
principal
is the value of the terminal payment and its
coupon
is the value of the interim payment. The coupon is quoted in terms of a
coupon rate
, which is a percentage of the principal, and a final coupon is paid alongside the principal. The total contract length is the bond’s
maturity
, which starts on the bond’s
issue date
and ends on the bond’s
maturity date
. The
coupon frequency
is the number of coupon payments made per year. Beyond this simple structure, there also exist many flavors of
exotic
bonds with additional complexity, such as variable rates and optionality, though we exclude them from our scope.

For our model we only consider
vanilla
bonds - senior debt with fixed coupon rates, no optionality, and no convertibility. Simply put, conditional on no default, a vanilla bond has a fully deterministic cash flow. For example, consider some particular two-year maturity vanilla bond
\mathcal{B}^{*}
with a principal of
\$1000
, a coupon rate of
5\%
paid semi-annually (
\$25
every six months), which has the following cash flow sequence :

\mathcal{B}^{*}=\{c_{0.5},\;c_{1},\;c_{1.5},\;c_{2}\}=\{\$25,\;\$25,\;\$25,\;\$1025\}

(2)

In general, the price of a bond is not simply equal to the sum of cash payments
\sum c_{t}
, but rather it is influenced by a set of external factors. For this, we require asset pricing theory.

2.1
Necessary Assumptions

To obtain a pricing model for a bond, we require a theoretical framework with necessary assumptions. First, we define a
discount function
as a mapping from time to the unit interval such that :

\text{d}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\text{d}(t)=1&\text{if }t=0\\
\text{d}(t)\in[0,1)&\text{if }t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\end{cases}

(3)

In practice, this function is
latent
and must be inferred from data, thus we can consider
\text{d}(t)
as a stochastic process, and assume that
uncertainty increases monotonically over time
:

\delta>0:\mathbb{V}\big[\text{d}(t)\big]<\mathbb{V}\big[\text{d}(t+\delta)\big]

(4)

For such a function to exist within a market, we must assume
a strictly positive risk-free rate exists
:

\exists\;r_{\text{risk-free}}:r_{\text{risk-free}}>0

(5)

Thus, for every bond we assume the existance of two separate discount functions :

1.

Time Value Discounting
:
a dollar today is worth strictly less than a dollar tomorrow
. Since we assume a strictly positive risk-free rate exists, then an investor is able to invest a dollar today and obtain strictly greater than a dollar back in the future. Such a discount function is constant across all issuers in a market, and can never be eliminated. We’ll denote this as
\text{d}_{\text{time}}(t)
.

2.

Default Risk Discounting
:
a risk of failing to receive payment decreases the price of a bond
. An investor is only willing to accept additional risk for additional compensation, and higher risk requires greater discounting. Such a discount function is unique to each issuer. We’ll denote this as
\text{d}_{\text{risk}}
(t).

We assume the risk-free rate corresponds to the US Treasury, and thus assume it has no risk of default. We define the following
degenerate
default risk discount function for the US Treasury as :

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:\text{d}^{*}_{\text{risk}}(t)=1

(6)

Big Idea
: The core purpose of this paper is to describe a general method for inferring
\text{d}_{\text{risk}}(t)
for any corporate issuer from observed bond price data, which will act as an ordinal bankruptcy prediction proxy signal that can be used for stock selection and portfolio construction.

2.2
Bond Pricing via Present Value

We can now bring together the cash flow sequence associated with a bond and a discount function to obtain a fair price for a bond. Utilizing the assumptions from the previous section, we define the
composite discount function
for a particular issuer as :

\displaystyle\text{d}_{\text{total}}(t)=\text{d}_{\text{time}}(t)\cdot\text{d}_{\text{risk}}(t)

(7)

The price
p
of bond
\mathcal{B}
is defined via the
present value
function, which is simply the sum of all future discounted cash flows. Notice that this is upper-bounded by the sum of the cash flows :

p=\text{PV}\big(\mathcal{B},\text{d}_{\text{total}}\big)=\sum_{t\in\tau}c_{t}\cdot\text{d}_{\text{total}}(t)\leq\sum_{t\in\tau}c_{t}

(8)

For convenient computation, we represent this linear algebraically by constructing the following vectors :

•

Cash Flow Vector
:
\mathbf{c}=\big[c_{t_{1}},c_{t_{2}},\dots,c_{t_{M}}\big]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M}

•

Discount Factor Vector
:
\mathbf{d}=\big[\text{d}(t_{1}),\text{d}(t_{2}),\dots,\text{d}(t_{M})\big]\in[0,1]^{M}

Thus, we the present value function reduces to the following inner product :

p=\text{PV}(\mathcal{B},\text{d}_{\text{total}})=\mathbf{c}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

(9)

We emphasize that the price of any bond incorporates
both
time value and default risk discounting simultaneously, and we must work to disentangle the two. Fortunately, since we assume that US Treasury bonds are risk-free, this disentanglement will be surprisingly easy to accomplish.

2.3
Yield Curves and Default Spread

While the discount function is mathematically convenient, it is more natural to compare firms in terms of
yield curves
, which are theoretical interest rates on zero-coupon bonds maturing at time
t
, defined by the following isomorphic transform :

\text{y}(t)=-\log\big[\text{d}(t)\big]\cdot t^{-1}\iff\text{d}(t)=\exp\big[-\text{y}(t)\cdot t\big]

(10)

Since we assume a positive risk-free rate exists, this implies that we can always construct a well-defined yield curve on US Treasuries. A
default spread
, sometimes called a
credit spread
is the difference between an issuer’s total yield and the US Treasury yield :

\displaystyle\text{s}\big(t\big)

\displaystyle=\text{y}_{\text{total}}\big(t\big)-\text{y}_{\text{Treasury}}\big(t\big)

(11)

\displaystyle=-\log\big[\text{d}_{\text{total}}\big(t\big)]\cdot t^{-1}+\log\big[\text{d}_{\text{time}}\big(t\big)\big]\cdot t^{-1}

(12)

\displaystyle=-\log\big[\text{d}_{\text{risk}}\big(t\big)]\cdot t^{-1}-\log\big[\text{d}_{\text{time}}\big(t\big)]\cdot t^{-1}+\log\big[\text{d}_{\text{time}}\big(t\big)\big]\cdot t^{-1}

(13)

\displaystyle=-\log\big[\text{d}_{\text{risk}}\big(t\big)]\cdot t^{-1}

(14)

The above derivation clearly demonstrates that the default spread is simply a monotonic function of an issuer’s
risk discount function
, providing an ordinal signal for ranking firms on the basis of credit-riskiness. We can estimate total default risk as the following integral over the default spread :

\text{risk}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\text{s}(t)\;dt

(15)

Summary in simpler terms :
in an approximately efficient market, a firm with high bankruptcy risk has bond prices that reflects this risk. Although we cannot directly observe a firm’s discount function, we can however estimate a default spread from data. In practice, this is good enough.

3
Data

While classical credit risk methods use quarterly fundamentals, we rely
only
on bond price data. It is important for the reader to keep in mind that bond markets have grown significantly in the past several years, and coverage for corporate bonds is gradually increasing. This makes proper backtesting particularly difficult, as the nature of bond markets now is much different from what it was even a decade ago. For our results section we will thus only focus on a few sub-samples for illustrative purposes.

3.1
Sourcing

We rely on the following data sources to construct a signal :

•

TRACE Bond Prices

[
5
]
: this includes daily closing prices for all publicly traded bonds, though for our purpose we are only interesed in corporate securities.

•

FISD Issue Data

[
6
]
: this includes descriptive data, such as issue and maturity dates, coupon rates and frequencies, and flags for things like optionality and convertibility.

•

Treasury Yield Curve
: we use this to convert a corporate yield into a corporate default spread.

As previously mentioned, we constrain our analysis to a subset of
vanilla
bonds, which are the simplest class of bonds with no random structure to their payout.

3.2
On-the-Run Bonds

In general, issuers often have multiple bonds in a market for a single maturity, which tend to have overlapping payouts. An empirical phenomenon that we must accommodate is the fact that when a firm issues a bond for a maturity that another issued bond already has, the liquidity of the older bond significantly falls. Thus, we only consider
on-the-run
bonds, which are the most recent issue of a particular maturity for an issuer.

For example, suppose a firm issued a 30-year bond last year, and another 30-year bond today. Only the bond issued
today
would be considered on-the-run, and the other would be excluded from our dataset. This is also something that must be accounted for when working with government bonds.

3.3
Dirty Prices

This is really just a note on data pre-processing. In practice, investors require compensation for selling a bond in-between coupon payments. We define
accrued interest
as the value of a partial coupon :
Let
t_{\text{next}}
be the time until the next coupon payment, let
t_{\text{coupon}}
be the coupon period, and let
c_{\text{next}}
be the cash value of the next coupon. Thus,
accrued interest
is simply defined as

\displaystyle a=c_{\text{next}}\cdot\frac{t_{\text{next}}}{t_{\text{coupon}}}

(16)

The
dirty price
is simply the sum of the
clean price
and the
accrued interest
:

\displaystyle p_{\text{dirty}}=p+a

(17)

To avoid a notational headache, for the remainder of this paper,
p
will refer only to the
dirty price
of a bond.

4
Regressions for Default Spread Modeling

4.1
Vasicek Exponential Spline

We estimate an issuer’s total discount function with the following
linear basis function model
1
1

1

In case this analogy is helpful to the reader, we can think of this framework as similar to a polynomial regression, in which we estimate
f(x)=\bm{\upphi}_{x}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\bm{\upphi}_{x}=[x,x^{2},x^{3},\dots,x^{K}]
. In both cases, we approximate a complicated function as a linear combination of simple functions, though a polynomial regression maps to
\mathbb{R}
while our discount model only maps to
[0,1]
.
, in which we use a linear combination of simple basis functions to approximate our true latent function. We specifically select
\upphi_{\alpha,k}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow[0,1]
to be a well defined discount function :

\text{d}^{*}_{\beta}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\upphi_{\alpha,k}(t)\cdot\beta_{k}=\bm{\upphi}_{t}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta}\in\big[0,1\big]

(18)

In this model, we represent a single discount factor for a particular payout time
t
as the inner product between latent
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
(which we will estimate via a linear regression) and
basis function vector

\bm{\upphi}_{t}
as :

\displaystyle\bm{\upphi}_{t}=\big[\upphi_{\alpha,1}(t),\upphi_{\alpha,2}(t),\dots,\upphi_{\alpha,K}(t)\big]\in\big[0,1\big]^{K}

(19)

There exist several reasonable choices for
\upphi
in the literature, such as polynomials, fourier series and exponential splines
[
7
]

[
8
]
. We select the
Vasicek basis

[
9
]
due to its flexibility. :

\upphi_{\alpha,k}(t)=\exp(-\alpha\cdot k\cdot t\big)\in\big[0,1\big]

(20)

Notice that with this basis
\phi_{\alpha,k}(0)=1
. To avoid a pathological basis function model in which
d(0)\neq 1
, we constrain
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}=1
. We’ll handle the details of this constraint in section
4.2
.
To price a bond using this discount function, we construct a Vasicek
discount factor vector
for a bond with
M
-many cash flows indexed by
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{M}\}
as :

\mathbf{d}^{*}=\big[\text{d}_{\beta}^{*}(t_{1}),\text{d}_{\beta}^{*}(t_{2}),\dots,\text{d}_{\beta}^{*}(t_{M})\big]=\bm{\Phi}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta}\in\big[0,1\big]^{M}

(21)

Here we define
basis factor matrix

\bm{\Phi}
, which is an
M
-dimensional extension of Equation
19
. This is simply a matrix of basis discount factors aligned with the payout times of cash flows for our bond. To simplify our task, we fix
\alpha=0.05
and suppress it in our notation :

\bm{\Phi}=\begin{bmatrix}\upphi_{1}\big(t_{1}\big)&\cdots&\upphi_{K}\big(t_{1}\big)\\
\vdots&\upphi_{k}\big(t_{m}\big)&\vdots\\
\upphi_{1}\big(t_{M_{n}}\big)&\cdots&\upphi_{K}\big(t_{M_{n}}\big)\end{bmatrix}\in\big[0,1\big]^{{M}\times K}

(22)

Thus, the price of a bond discounted by the Vasicek discount function is simply :

p=\mathbf{c}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{d}^{*}=\mathbf{c}^{\text{T}}\bm{\Phi}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

(23)

We emphasize that
p,\mathbf{c}
and
\bm{\Phi}
are known ahead of time, while only
\bm{\beta}
is latent - thus, a linear regression naturally follows. For our regression, we construct the following
basis price vector
as :

\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{c}^{\text{T}}\bm{\Phi}=\big[\text{PV}(\mathcal{B},\upphi_{1}\big),\dots,\text{PV}(\mathcal{B},\upphi_{K})\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

(24)

This naturally extends to the following
basis price matrix
for
N
-many bonds :

\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\text{PV}(\mathcal{B}_{1},\upphi_{1}\big)&\dots&\text{PV}(\mathcal{B}_{1},\upphi_{K})\\
\vdots&\text{PV}(\mathcal{B}_{n},\upphi_{k}\big)&\vdots\\
\text{PV}(\mathcal{B}_{N},\upphi_{1}\big)&\dots&\text{PV}(\mathcal{B}_{N},\upphi_{K})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times K}

(25)

Thus, given a set of
N
-many bonds, we define a linear regression of the form:

p_{n}=\mathbf{b}_{n}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta}+\varepsilon_{n}

(26)

To make notation more consistent with what the reader may be used to, for the remainder of the paper we will use the convention
y_{n}\coloneqq p_{n}
and
\mathbf{x}_{n}\coloneqq\mathbf{b}_{n}
. Now that we have defined the data structure that we will use to fit our regression, we must consider several numerical subtleties. Similar architectures have been discussed in the context of goverment bonds in
[

,

,

,

.

4.2
Numerical Considerations for Estimating
\bm{\beta}

As a naive initial case, we can consider an estimate of
\bm{\beta}
via
ordinary least squares (OLS)
, which is a convenient closed-form convex optimization approach :

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}}\Big\{\big\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta}\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}

(27)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{X}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{y}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

(28)

While OLS is a reasonable starting point for fitting a Vasicek exponential spline, unfortunately it isn’t sufficiently structured to model corporate bonds. We must generalize our approach and account for three additional considerations that we care about in practical implemenation.

Issue 1
:
if
\sum\beta_{k}\neq 1
, then
\mathrm{d}(0)\neq 1
, producing a pathological discount function.
Simply, it does not make sense to discount
\$1
today to anything other than
\$1
. The natural solution to this issue is to fit a constrained regression with
\|\bm{\beta}\|=1
, but equality constraints are a hassle to work with. Instead, we map our constrained problem in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
to an unconstrained problem in
\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
with the following data transform, allowing us to stay within a least-squares framework, defining
\mathbf{X}_{k}
as the
k
-th column of
\mathbf{X}
(see proof in Appendix
A
) :

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\big[\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}_{K}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

(29)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\big[\mathbf{X}_{1}-\mathbf{X}_{K},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{K-1}-\mathbf{X}_{K}\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times(K-1)}

(30)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\big[\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K-1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}

(31)

Issue 2
:
some observations are more reliable than others
. Per Axiom 3 in Section
2.1
, it follows that the price uncertainty of a bond is proportionate to its total term. Thus, when fitting a Vasicek spline, it is not appropriate to weight all observations equally in the objective function. For each bond, we define a weighting factor inversely proportion to its term as
w_{i}\propto M_{n}
, with which we construct diagonal
weight matrix

\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, which penalizes less-reliable observations via
weighted least squares (WLS)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}_{\text{WLS}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}}\Big\{\big\|\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}

(32)

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}\big)^{-1}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}

(33)

Issue 3
:
for some corporate issuers,
N<(K-1)
. While the above WLS method is perfectly suitable for estimating the Treasury discount function, since for Treasuries we often have
N>10
, things break down for issuers with
N<(K-1)
as
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
becomes uninvertible. Rather than restrict the expressivity of our model by decreasing
K
, we can instead resolve this issue by applying L2-regularization (“ridge regression”) on
\bm{\beta}
, which produces an always-invertible solution via
ridge weighted least squares (RWLS)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}_{\text{RWLS}}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\bm{\beta}}\Big\{\big\|\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\big\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}

(34)

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\big(\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{W}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{D-1}

(35)

Summary
: To simplify this architecture and avoid the clunky
tildes
above, we introduce the following transform, utilizing
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{B}
from Section
4.1
, and retain this notation for the remainder of the paper. We use
\mathbf{B}_{k}
to denote the
k
-th
column
of matrix
\mathbf{B}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\text{diag}\big([w_{1},\dots,w_{N}]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\text{ s.t. }w_{n}=f(M_{n})

(36)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\big(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{B}_{K}\big)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\mathbf{W}}\big[\mathbf{B}_{1}-\mathbf{B}_{K},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{K-1}-\mathbf{B}_{K}\big]\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N\times(K-1)}

(38)

We have not yet spoken on a reasonable method for selecting
\lambda
, though in practice an optimal value may be discovered through cross-validation. Alternatively, in the next section we introduce a broader approach to regularization via
Bayesian inference
that provides us with a general framework to incorporate additional constraints. Eventually, we will further expand this to a temporal model using state-space methods.

4.3
Estimating
\bm{\beta}
via Bayesian Inference

We generalize the regularization on our regression by introducing
Bayes Theorem
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}\big)}_{\text{posterior}}\propto\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\big(\mathbf{X}\mid\bm{\theta}\big)}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\;\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\theta}\big)}_{\text{prior}}

(39)

The
likelihood
defines the relationship between our data and the model parameters (similar to the OLS objective), the
prior
defines all constraints on our parameters (similar to the L2-regularizer), and the
posterior
ties it all together. The primary philosophical shift here is that we will now think of our parameters as
random variables
with corresponding probability distributions, which allows us to easily construct predictive confidence intervals that incorporate all of the uncertainty embedded in a model.

We can extend the
ridge regression
(Equation
34
) by introducing the
conjugate Bayesian regression
(utilizing
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{X}
defined in Equations
37
and
38
), which has the following generative model :

\displaystyle y_{n}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\big(y_{n}\mid\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\text{T}}\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}\big)

(40)

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\beta}\mid\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}\bm{\Lambda}\big)

(41)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{G}^{-1}\big(\sigma^{2}\mid\alpha,\gamma\big)

(42)

This model requires
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1)}
, positive semi-definite
\bm{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{(K-1),(K-1)}
, and
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. To simplify, we use a
Normal Inverse-Gamma
distribution on
\bm{\theta}=\{\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}\}
as :

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\theta}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta}\mid\sigma^{2}\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\sigma^{2}\big)=\mathcal{NG}^{-1}\big(\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\bm{\mu},\bm{\Lambda},\alpha,\gamma\big)

(43)

Using the notation that
\theta_{0}
is a prior parameter and
\theta_{*}
is a posterior parameter, we have posterior :

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\theta}\mid\mathbf{X}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{NG}^{-1}\big(\bm{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\bm{\mu}_{*},\bm{\Lambda}_{*},a_{*},b_{*}\big)

(44)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{*}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{X}+\bm{\Lambda}_{0}^{-1}\big)^{-1}

(45)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{*}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Lambda}_{*}\big(\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{y}+\bm{\Lambda}_{0}\bm{}{-1}\mu_{0}\big)

(46)

\displaystyle\alpha_{*}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}+\frac{N}{2}

(47)

\displaystyle\gamma_{*}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\big[\mathbf{y}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{y}+\bm{\mu}^{\text{T}}_{0}\bm{\Lambda}_{0}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{0}-\bm{\mu}_{*}^{\text{T}}\bm{\Lambda}_{*}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{*}\big]

(48)

Equipped with these posterior parameters, we construct the predictive distribution for
\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
as :

y_{n}\sim\mathcal{T}_{2\alpha}\Big(\mathbf{x}_{n}^{\text{T}}\bm{\mu}_{\beta},\frac{\gamma_{*}}{\alpha_{*}}\big[\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}\bm{\Lambda}_{*}\beta\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}^{\text{T}}\big]\Big)

(49)

To come full-circle, notice that for
\bm{\mu}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
\bm{\Lambda}_{0}=\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{I}
our estimate for
\bm{\mu}_{*}
is ridge-optimal :

\bm{\mu}_{*}=\big(\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{X}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\big)^{-1}\big(\mathbf{X}^{\text{T}}\mathbf{y}\big)=\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\text{RLS}}

(50)

Setting
\bm{\mu}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
\bm{\Lambda}_{0}=\lambda\mathbf{I}
is a good place to start, but we’ll be able to construct a far more interesting model by leveraging the fact that our data is temporal in nature. Bonds are constantly trading, and it is reasonable to assume that a recently estimated discount function is a reasonable prior for the current discount function. We can formalize this with the Kalman filter.

Sidebar :
For the reader unfamiliar with Bayesian learning, we can alternatively contextualize this process through the lens of convex optimization. For the conjugate Bayesian regression, the log-posterior is simply the sum of the log-likelihood (concave) and the log-prior (concave). The sum of concave functions is concave, and our parameters are defined over a convex set. Thus, conjugate posterior inference can be understood as unconstrained convex optimization.

4.4
State-Space
\bm{\beta}
with Kalman Filter

We posit that over a set of states
\mathcal{S}
we observe a stochastic parameter process for
\bm{\beta}
:

\big\{\bm{\beta}_{s}\big\}_{\mathcal{S}}=\big\{\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{S}\big\}

(51)

In plain English, we’re simply assuming that for an issuer, its discount function
evolves over time
as real-world events impact investor expectations of the future, which are reflected in traded bond prices. We model this parameter process as a
Discrete Brownian Motion
, which is both a
Markov process
(making it memoryless) and a
martingale
(making it trendless). In general, this is one of the simplest stochastic processes that we are able to construct, though it is nonetheless still useful :

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2},\dots,\bm{\beta}_{s-1}\big]=\bm{\beta}_{s-1}

(52)

This implies that
our best prediction for tomorrow’s yield curve is today’s yield curve with symmetric noise
.

The natural statistical approach for modeling a discrete Brownian motion is the zero-drift
Kalman Filter
, which recursively models our parameter
\bm{\beta}
with the following structure, similar models in
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. :

•

Data Model
:
\mathbf{p}_{s}=\mathbf{B}_{s}\bm{\beta}_{s}+\bm{\varepsilon}_{s}
(simply, the Vasicek model)

•

State Model
:
\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\beta}_{s-1}+\bm{\Delta}_{s}
with
\bm{\Delta}_{s}\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\Delta}_{s}\mid\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{s}\big)

You’ll notice that the State Model is an AR(1) model.
Here, we introduce
\bm{\Delta}_{s}
as the
temporal change
of
\bm{\beta}
, with no drift and uncertainty
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}
. Since both
\bm{\beta}_{s-1}
and
\bm{\Delta}_{s}
follow Gaussian distributions, which is closed under addition, we obtain the following prior on
\bm{\beta}_{s}
:

\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\sigma^{2}_{s}\big)=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\bm{\mu}^{*}_{s-1},\sigma^{2}_{s}\bm{\Lambda}_{s-1}^{*}+\bm{\Sigma}_{s}\big)

(53)

We can obtain a very convenient algebraic result by setting :

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{s}=\delta_{s}^{2}\cdot\bm{\Lambda}_{s-1}^{*}

(54)

The interpretation of this is that high-uncertainty parameters are more likely to evolve than low-certainty parameters, with
\delta_{s}^{2}
acting as a
variance amplifier
term, which is a hyperparameter specified by the user. Thus, our distribution now becomes :

\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\sigma^{2}_{s}\big)=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\bm{\mu}^{*}_{s-1},\big[\sigma^{2}_{s}+\delta_{s}^{2}\big]\bm{\Lambda}_{s-1}^{*}\big)

(55)

Since we treat
\sigma^{2}
as an unknown in our Bayesian conjugate regression, we require one more step to make this all practical. We instead encode this uncertainty in the prior on
\sigma^{2}
by constructing the following new random variable :

\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{s}=\sigma_{s}^{2}+\delta_{s}^{2}

(56)

Thus we specify the full prior for our Bayesian model as :

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s},\tilde{\sigma}_{s}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta}\mid\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}\big)

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\sigma^{2}_{s}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\big(\bm{\beta}_{s}\mid\bm{\mu}^{*}_{s-1},\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{s}\cdot\bm{\Lambda}_{s-1}^{*}\big)

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{s}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{-1}\big(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}_{s}\mid\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\gamma}\big)

(59)

Where we have the following hyperparameters (see Appendix
B
) :

•

\tilde{\alpha}_{s}=\gamma_{s-1}^{*-1}\cdot(\alpha^{*}_{s-1}-1)\cdot(\alpha^{*}_{s-1}-2)\cdot\big[(\gamma^{*}_{s-1}\cdot(\alpha^{*}_{s-1}-1)^{-1})+\delta_{s}^{2}\big]+2

•

\tilde{\gamma}_{s}=\big[\gamma^{*}_{s-1}\cdot(\alpha^{*}_{s-1}-1)^{-1}+\delta^{2}_{s}\big]\cdot(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}-1)^{-1}

And thus we have fully specified the model our state-space Bayesian approach for fitting the Vasicek exponential spline to corporate bonds in order to estimate a firm’s default spread.

Appendix C
Model Stability Notes

C.1
Variance Amplifier Hyper parameters

As mentioned in Section
4.4
we add a
variance amplifier
hyper parameter
\delta^{2}>0
to the prior on the coefficients that describe the yield curve. This is to combat the issue known as vanishing variance. This is problematic for model stability, because as variance tends toward zero the new calculated state will always be the same as the previous state.

This phenomenon tends to plague Markov models that model the new states variance as a function of the previous states parameters. However in this model we also model the
variance of the variance
as a function of the previous states parameters (see equations
47
and
48
for how
\alpha
and
\gamma
relate to the previous state)

This again is problematic as if VOV tends to zero then our previously defined hyper paramter
\delta^{2}
will ensure that our variance grows monotonically and infinitely. This will lead to overinflated and practically unusable confidence intervals as well as unstable coefficients. To address this we add a second hyper paramer
\epsilon>0
to the new states estimate of VOV. See Section
B
for algebraic details.

C.2
Rank Deficient Matrices

The user may encounter
rank deficient
matrices when using equation
45
to compute the covariance structure of the coefficients. In particular this issue will almost certaintly arise after the first iteration where the prior
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}
is no longer uninformative and is instead the previous states posterior. This is due to a well known result in linear algebra:
The Rank Product Theorem

\displaystyle\text{Rank}(AB)\leq\text{min}(\text{Rank}(A),\text{Rank}(B))

(73)

As many firms issue fewer bonds than
K
, from the theorem above one can verify that:

\displaystyle\text{Rank}({\mathbf{X^{T}X}})<K

(74)

Thus
\mathbf{X^{T}X}
is
rank deficient
and hence non-invertible. In the initial iteration equation
45
is solved by setting
\mathbf{\Lambda_{*}}=\lambda\mathbf{I}
(the ridge optimal solution). While this solution will work for all states of the model we can still impose a prior on the covariance structure between coefficients by setting
\mathbf{\Lambda_{*}}=\mathbf{\Lambda_{0}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}
for further iterations. This can be viewed as weakening the prior on the covariance matrix and is similar in spirit to our variance multiplier hyperparameters.
