Toward General Physical Intelligence for Resilient Agile Manufacturing Automation Journal: Advanced Engineering Informatics
Sandeep Kanta, Mehrdad Tavassoli, Varun Teja Chirkuri, Venkata Akhil Kumar, Santhi Bharath Punati, Praveen Damacharla, Sunny Katyara

Abstract
Agile and human-centric manufacturing stipulates resilient robotic solutions capable of contextual reasoning and safe interaction in unstructured environments. Foundation models particularly the Vision Language Action (VLA) models have emerged to fuse multimodal perception, reasoning and physically grounded action across varied embodiments into unified representation, termed as General Physical Intelligence (GPI). While GPI has already been described in the literature but its practical application and evolving role in contemporary agile manufacturing processes have yet to be duly explored. To bridge this gap, this practical review systematically surveys recent advancements in VLA models within GPI context, performs comprehensive comparative analysis of leading implementations and evaluates their readiness for industrial deployment through structured ablation study. Our analysis has organized state-of-the-art into five thematic pillars including multisensory representation learning, sim2real transfer, planning and control, uncertainty and safety measures and benchmarking. Finally, we articulate open research challenges and propose directions to better integrate GPI into next-generation industrial ecosystems in line with Industry 5.0.

Introduction

Market demands are incessantly evolving, prompting a paradigm shift from mass production to mass customization in industrial practices according to Industry 5.0 principles. However, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) often lack technological infrastructure and skilled workforce to properly establish such transitions. Hence, only 3% to 5% of SMEs have successfully embraced such requirements across Europe

To catalyze the required flexibility for customized manufacturing, industrial robots ought to carry out diverse tasks and interact seamlessly with their surroundings including human supervisors. Therefore, modern industrial robotic systems are equipped with multiple sensory modalities and machine learning techniques including deep neural networks and reinforcement learning agents to achieve optimal performance. However, according to Industry 5.0 vision, the proposed solutions need to handle variability across diverse tasks, environments and embodiments, which is a challenge that conventional machine intelligence methods often fail to address

State-of-the-Art Frameworks

In dynamic environments of agile manufacturing where human-robot collaboration is common and product lines are characterized by high variability, the role of GPI is indispensable. Achieving the required level of flexible automation hinges on the ability to synchronize perception, reasoning and physical action in real time that is often guided by kinetic-dynamic models of workspace

2.1
Multisensory representation learning

Robust perception that temporally accords sensory data with motor actions is a critical component of GPI for maintaining context awareness in unstructured settings. This alignment is foundational for visuomotor control where coherent representations stabilize performance during dynamic tasks. For instance, the TLA framework
[
28
]
exemplifies this by fusing language, vision and tactile modalities into unified latent space by augmenting policies for contact-rich manipulations, tasks central to industrial assembly and fastening. To address the computational demands of such models, frameworks like CACTI
[
29
]
improve scalability by incorporating generative data augmentation and compressed visual representations to reduce training time and cost without compromising throughput. However, vision alone is insufficient for many agile manufacturing tasks. Emerging sensorimotor fusion models that integrate audio and more importantly tactile feedback address this gap by capturing properties that cameras cannot i.e., object weight, surface texture or thermal conductivity
[
30
]

This multi-modal approach is imperative for industrial applications to enable robots to detect subtle physical cues i.e., incipient part slippage during grasp or precise insertion forces required for peg-in-hole tasks, greatly improving performance in both dexterous manipulation and stable locomotion

2.2
Data generation, simulation and sim-to-real transfer

The reliance of foundation models on vast and diverse datasets presents significant bottleneck for robotics where collecting real-world data is costly, time-consuming and potentially hazardous. Consequently, synthetic data generation and high-fidelity simulation have become cornerstones of modern robotic automation. One of the most established strategies to bridge simulation-to-reality gap is domain randomization
[
48
]
which enhances policy robustness by training on randomly varied physical and visual characteristics such as object textures, lighting conditions and physics parameters. Building on this, modern generative engines like RoboGen
[
49
]
and GenSim
[
50
]
employ diffusion and autoregressive models to create diverse and physics-consistent trajectories to significantly accelerate robot learning process. This is complemented by large-scale simulators such as RFUniverse
[
51
]
and knowledge bases like ActivityPrograms
[
52
]
that provide extensive libraries of objects and interactions. In an industrial context, this is the foundation of creating high-fidelity digital twins for virtual replicas of manufacturing cells that minimizes discrepancies between simulated and real-world scenarios.

Frameworks like DayDreamer
[
53
]
leverage learned world models for imagination-based planning to demonstrate competence in real-world industrial deployments such as shop-floor logistics and tabletop manipulation. Meanwhile, VC-1
[
54
]
and RoboCat
[
55
]
utilize cross-domain pre-training and self-improvement mechanisms respectively to enable generalization and continual adaptation to unseen environments. A significant milestone is Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset
[
56
]
which is unified collection of trajectories from 22 different robot embodiments. Models trained on this dataset such as RT-X
[
57
]
and Octo
[
58
]
have demonstrated unprecedented cross-embodiment generalization which is marking turning point in policy learning. These architectures are crucial for agile manufacturing as they combine data-driven robustness with modular designs to adapt across distinct objects, industrial scenes and industrial manipulators common in todays factories.

2.3
Planning and control frameworks

While large-scale simulation expedites robot skill acquisition but it cannot fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world manufacturing tasks. To bridge this gap, data from unstructured teleoperation and human demonstrations are often used to build more robust policies. Methods like MimicPlay
[
59
]
and MUTEX
[
60
]
leverage this data to construct structured task graphs that abstract skill sequences and enable cross-task generalization with minimal retraining, a key requirement for high-mix production lines. Reinforcement learning frameworks such as Adaptive Agent (AdA)
[
61

further modularize these graphs into hierarchical policies while loop-aware strategies like VOYAGER

and semantic-scene-graph methods

generate complete action sequence in single pass. In contrast, closed-loop systems iteratively replan by integrating new sensory information that make them more robust to unpredictable events that are prevalent on factory floors. This distinction is crucial for downstream implementation where LLMs are increasingly exploited to translate high-level natural language instructions into executable code via frameworks like ProgPrompt
[

and Code-as-Policies

]
. Recent hierarchical code-generation frameworks

This architectural modularity is proving critical for creating dependable robotic systems for open-world and resilient environments.

2.4
Uncertainty estimation and safety assurance

Safe and reliable operation is non-negotiable priority in manufacturing environments where robots must demonstrate unwavering robustness against uncertainties and unplanned events. This is especially true in agile settings where workspaces are shared with human operators, demanding not just collision avoidance but also predictable and trustworthy behavior. Modern approaches to safe GPI are multi-faceted to address uncertainty at levels of planning, action and execution. A foundational element of safety is quantifying model confidence. Before a robot even acts, it is important to know the limits of its understanding. To this end, methods like KNOWNO
[
74
]
integrate conformal prediction to quantify planner confidence in its own output. If confidence falls below predefined threshold, the system triggers safe stop or request human intervention to prevent actions based on erratic judgments. Complementary risk-aware controllers further enhance safety by enforcing safe distances or initiate fail recovery protocols to mitigate hazards in shared workspaces
[
75
]
.

Beyond static checks, safety is enhanced through probabilistic and adaptive actions. Instead of outputting single deterministic action, diffusion-based policies as seen in SUDD
[
76
]
and RDT-1B
[
77
]
, model full distribution over possible actions. This yields more robust and calibrated decision-making which is particularly effective for navigating high-dimensional control spaces found in complex assembly and material handling tasks. Safety is further enhanced by language-conditioned error handling and feedback-aware architectures. For example, the use of language-based motion plans in RT-H
[
70
]
allows system to intelligently backtrack or revise actions when real-world conditions deviate from the plan. Similarly, mixture-of-experts models like
\pi_{0}

[
78
]
dynamically selects from portfolio of policies to avoid unsafe actions when encountering novel and challenging scenarios. Finally, growing area of research is proactive risk mitigation through simulation. Planning-based frameworks like LLM-MCTS
[
79
]
and RAP
[
80
]
explicitly simulate and evaluate candidate action plans before execution. By integrating logical reasoning of LLMs with world model rollouts, these systems can foresee and preemptively avoid unsafe or ineffective behaviors. This capability is essential for qualifying robotic systems for complex and human-interactive environments to move the field closer to certified and safely deployable intelligence on factory floors.

2.5
Benchmarks and evaluation protocols

To ensure industrial readiness, the robotic systems must be systematically evaluated to track progress, identify generalization gaps and validate their safety and efficiency. This requires multi-layered approach to benchmarking that spans from controlled simulations to real-world physical tests by using metrics that capture not only task success but also operational value. Initial evaluation often occurs in simulation due to its safety, low cost and scalability. Platforms like VIMA-Bench
[
81
]
, Ravens
[
82
]
and RLBench
[
83
]
are invaluable for assessing model ability to generalize across structured tasks with controlled variability. These environments facilitate precise measurement of model capabilities in manipulation, tool use and object rearrangement when faced with novel objects and scenes. For agile manufacturing, this is crucial for cost-effectively testing how system might adapt to new product designs or component variations before physical deployment. To meet the generalization challenges posed by these benchmarks, a two-stage fine-tuning strategy is often employed where models are pre-trained on simpler tasks and refined on more complex ones
[
84
]
.

While simulation is powerful but performance in the physical world is the ultimate test. Real-world benchmarks like CALVIN
[
85
]
, ALOHA
[
86
]
and large-scale BridgeData V2
[
87
]
provide diverse tasks in physical environments with real sensors and actuators. These platforms are essential for evaluating system robustness to real-world noise, sensor delays and unmodeled physics. The ManiSkill2
[
88
]
benchmark further bridges this gap by providing broad suite of manipulation tasks that can be consistently compared across both simulation and reality thus offering more complete picture of model capabilities. For agile manufacturing, the evaluation must extend beyond task completion rates. The practical readiness of a GPI-enabled platform is measured by KPIs that reflect industrial value such as cycle time, generalization score to new tasks and overall performance index
[
89
]
. Furthermore, as robots become more intelligent, new metrics are emerging to evaluate their cognitive alignment and interaction quality. Frameworks like Inner Monologue
[
63
]
and E-CoT
[
90
]
introduce metrics for explainability, language grounding and iterative correction. These cognitive benchmarks are building trust and ensuring seamless coordination in human-robot teams. They are shaping evaluation of next-generation VLA systems not just by what they do but by how and why they do it as crucial step toward creating truly intelligent and dependable industrial partners.

3.1
Architectural Modifications for Industrial GPI

To anchor utility of GPI framework, we outline architectural modifications aimed at evolving state-of-the-art VLA models into shared GPI-compatible interface for industrial tasks.

1.

Gato: Originally a generalist agent that is adapted for GPI by augmenting its token stream to include proprioceptive
(p_{t})
, haptic
(h_{t})
and embodiment descriptor
(\sigma)
alongside predicted future states
(\hat{s_{t:t+H}})
. This provides richer context for its autoregressive sequence modelling. Fundamentally, by encoding object-action-effect affordance tuples, the model learns to predict the consequences of physical interactions to enable long-horizon planning essential for multi-step industrial assemblies
[
114
]
.

2.

RT-2: To enhance this VLA, we embed proprioceptive and haptic feedback into its token stream and introduce task-conditioned affordance priors via symbolic goal prompts
[
115
]
. The inclusion of planning module leverages dropout-based uncertainty estimation to allow transformed RT-2 to reason under stochasticity and generalize to new platforms with variable dynamics that is key requirement for factories with diverse hardware.

3.

PaLM-E: This model natively embeds sensory data with language. The GPI migration encapsulates full multimodal stream—proprioception, vision, haptics and embodiment descriptors into its encoder architecture. By representing all inputs as unified sequence of tokens, PaLM-E can reason across heterogeneous data streams to make it ideal for complex manipulation and quality inspection tasks in agile manufacturing
[
116
]
.

4.

OpenVLA: As open-source framework, OpenVLA is extended by infusing affordance-aware grounding and dynamic attention mechanisms. The model is modified to process task-relevant haptic and proprioceptive features while leveraging its powerful visual encoders (SigLIP
[
96
]
and DINOv2
[
95
]
) for fine-grained control primitives. Furthermore, we incorporate task-prompted attention masking to improve interpretability, critical feature for diagnostics, failure analysis and safety certification in industrial settings. This extension is pivotal for common tasks like industrial bin picking and assembly.

5.

VIMA: This transformer-based architecture excels at object-centric manipulation. To support GPI, we extend VIMA with affordance conditioning, a spatial goal embedding module and a dynamic memory component. We also introduce curriculum learning and skill composition replay to facilitate transfer across sequence of related tasks such as peg insertion, assembly and tool handovers which are common in warehouse intralogistics.

4
Challenges and Recommendations

The pursuit of General Physical Intelligence is set to redefine automation by moving from rigid and pre-programmed systems to adaptive robots that can flexibly perceive, plan and interact within diverse and unstructured environments. However, transforming today’s VLA models into industrially viable systems requires overcoming many fundamental challenges including;

4.0.1
Data Foundation for Physical Grounding

The overarching bottleneck impeding GPI is data itself. Physically intelligent systems require foundation of data that is fundamentally different from what is currently available. A "contact-rich data gap" exists in principle. Existing large-scale datasets predominantly focus on vision and language while omitting synchronized, high-frequency haptic (force-torque, tactile) and proprioceptive data streams essential for manufacturing tasks including assembly, fastening and finishing. Furthermore, sim2real gap is most pronounced in contact dynamics where inaccuracies in simulating force exchange, slippage and material deformation that cause policies to fail on deployment. To bridge this, we recommend:

1.

There is an exigent need for benchmarks that provide synchronized streams of vision (RGBD), natural language instructions, end-effector states, robot kinematics and high-frequency haptic feedback. These datasets should also be annotated with affordance information to link sensory data to physical possibilities
[
117
]
.

2.

Industrial viability requires more than task completion. Evaluation frameworks must evolve to include metrics that reflect real-world performance i.e., force-profile adherence for quality control, energy efficiency, compliant behavior and robustness to physical perturbations
[
118
]
.

3.

To train generalizable physical priors, new datasets should be generated in simulation with prime focus on contact dynamics. This involves extensive domain randomization of material properties (i.e., stiffness, friction, surface roughness) to ensure that learned policies are robust enough for the variability of real factories
[
119
]
.

4.0.2
Fusion and Semantic Grounding of Haptic Feedback

Fusing asynchronous and high-dimensional haptic signals with dense visual information and symbolic language is a non-trivial task. Most extant models fail to leverage rich temporal dynamics and nuanced signals within haptic data that are essential for inferring critical information, like contact state transitions, material compliance or physical intent
[
120
]
. Furthermore, semantically grounding linguistic commands like “press firmly” or “tighten until resistance” into precise and verifiable haptic strategies remains significant open problem for robotics community
[
121
,
122
]
to enable robots to achieve the level of dexterity required for many agile manufacturing tasks. Future work should prioritize following goals:

1.

Develop multi-modal fusion architectures with temporal cross-attention mechanisms
[
123
]
. This would allow robot to dynamically weigh the importance of different senses based on task context, for instance relying on vision to approach part and then shifting its focus to haptic feedback during final moments of precision insertion.

2.

Incorporate modality-specific encoders with learned priors over contact dynamics. This would enable language inputs to directly modulate robot physical behavior to allow it to translate abstract command like "handle delicately" into quantifiable control profile
[
124
]
.

3.

Fine-tune pre-trained VLA models using interaction-rich episodes that are deeply grounded in haptic feedback. This will enhance their internal models of physical causality and tactile affordances to move beyond simple object recognition to deeper understanding of how objects behave under physical forces
[
125
]
.

4.0.3
Generalization and Sim-to-Real Transfer in Physical Interaction

Unlike perception or language tasks, physical interactions are heavily dependent on dynamic system parameters such as object mass, friction coefficients and material compliance
[
126
]
. Generalizing policies across such variations, a daily reality in manufacturing where component suppliers or material batches change, has yet to be fully addressed. This problem is compounded by sim2real gap, which is most pronounced in contact dynamics. Inaccuracies in simulating force exchange, slippage or deformation of non-rigid parts cause policies trained in simulation to fail catastrophically
[
127
]
. Overcoming this requires creating more robust and adaptive policies. Future research should prioritize;

1.

Move beyond generic randomization to more targeted approaches. This involves focusing simulation on specific range of physical properties that robot is likely to encounter during industrial deployments to encourage learning of tailored and effective resilient policies
[
128
]
.

2.

Integrate meta-learning and zero-shot generalization capabilities. The goal is to develop VLA-haptic systems that can quickly adapt to new components or tasks with minimal real-world data that drastically reduce time and cost associated with production line changeovers
[
129
]
.

4.0.4
Hierarchical Planning and Feedback-Driven Control

Complex manufacturing workflows demand long-horizon planning where abstract goals must be decomposed into sequence of executable sub-tasks. Coordinating high-level plan often generated by VLA model with low-level controller to adapt to contact conditions in real time introduces significant layered synchronization problems. Furthermore, reliably handling execution failures, unexpected contact events and task transitions based on force thresholds requires resilient integration of feedback into controller
8
8

8

Crux is not synchronization between sequential planning layers but mismatch in temporal scales and uncertainty propagation in hierarchical stack.
. Future research must focus to;

1.

Develop hierarchical control architectures with bidirectional communication pathways. In such system, the high-level planner does not simply issue commands but the low-level controller also reports back critical information. For example, sensory feedback indicating slippage, unexpected resistance or force discontinuities would inform mid-level decision modules to adapt current strategy or alert high-level planner to flag anomaly such as part defect that requires completely new motion plan
[
130
]
.

2.

Automate learning of reusable temporal and functional task structures from demonstrations. By identifying and encoding fundamental skills (i.e., "pick" ,"align", "insert"), policies are constructed more efficiently and composed into more complex and long-horizon tasks. This encourages skill reuse across different production lines and speeds up process of programming new tasks
[
131
]
.

4.0.5
Real-Time Constraints, Interpretability and Safety

When scaled to billions of parameters, VLA models often fail to meet strict real-time demands of industrial control loops that frequently operate at high frequencies. Furthermore, their "black box" nature lacks interpretability that is required for rigorous failure analysis, safety validation and regulatory certification. This opacity is significant barrier to adoption in high-stakes manufacturing environments where system predictability is preeminent. Therefore, we recommend;

1.

Pursue aggressive model compression, distillation and quantization to create lightweight versions of GPI systems that can be deployed on cost-effective edge devices
[
132
]
. Utilizing real-time operating systems (RT-PREEMPT patches) is essential to guarantee microsecond determinism required for safe physical interaction.

2.

Incorporate XAI techniques specifically tailored to physically interactive systems. The goal is to create auditable models that link their actions directly to specific perceptual and haptic triggers to provide transparency to satisfy safety standards and regulatory certifications
[
133
]
.
