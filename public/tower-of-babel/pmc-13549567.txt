Frailty Measures for Adult Patients and Outcomes for Vestibular Schwannoma Surgeries: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Eric Michael Cohen, Emma Dunn, Bryan Clampitt, Jacob Parker, Mohammad Hassan A. Noureldine, Emily Coughlin, Rahul Mhaskar, Siviero Agazzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:To determine whether preoperative frailty [5-item/11-item modified frailty index (mFI-5/mFI-11)] independently predicts length of stay (LOS) and early facial nerve function after vestibular schwannoma (VS) resection when adjusting for tumor and operative factors at a single center with standardized postoperative care.METHODS:This was a single-center, institutional review board-approved, retrospective review of consecutive adults undergoing microsurgical VS resection from 2014 to 2025. Candidate predictors included mFI-5, mFI-11, age, temporal muscle thickness, tumor size, surgical approach, preoperative nonauditory cranial nerve deficits, immediate postoperative facial nerve function, and ventriculoperitoneal shunt requirement. Primary outcomes were LOS (days) and House-Brackmann (HB) grade at discharge, modeled with negative binomial and cumulative odds ordinal logistic regression, respectively (two-sided α = 0.05).RESULTS:Among 192 patients, larger tumor size was independently associated with longer LOS (adjusted incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.16 per cm, 95% CI, 1.03-1.31) and worse early facial nerve outcomes. The translabyrinthine approach was associated with shorter LOS than retrosigmoid (IRR 0.72, 95% CI, 0.53-0.96) and ventriculoperitoneal shunt prolonged hospitalization (IRR 2.51, 95% CI, 1.63-3.86). Discharge HB grade was most strongly associated with preoperative HB grade. In contrast, mFI-5 and mFI-11 were not independently associated with LOS or discharge HB (mFI-5 LOS IRR 1.14, 95% CI, 0.99-1.32, P = .07; mFI-11 IRR 1.08, 95% CI, 0.98-1.20, P = .13). Higher mFI-5 was associated with nonhome discharge univariably (P = .017). Substituting individual comorbidities for frailty indices did not materially alter findings.CONCLUSION:In VS microsurgery, tumor size rather than frailty was the dominant correlate of early postoperative outcomes after adjustment. Because frailer patients are often selected out before elective surgery, the null adjusted association does not prove frailty is clinically irrelevant but reflects its limited incremental value among already-fit patients. Counseling should prioritize tumor characteristics and surgical approach. These hypothesis-generating findings warrant prospective multicenter confirmation.

Frailty elucidates the variation between a patient's chronological age and physiological age.1 Frailty has been defined as a state of decreased physiological reserve associated with increased susceptibility to disability and unfavorable outcomes.1-3 Characterizing frailty in relation to brain surgery outcomes could improve preoperative counseling regarding expected length of stay (LOS), rehabilitation needs, and complication risk.

Several frailty measures are in use, including the 11-item and 5-item modified frailty index (mFI-11 and mFI-5).1,4-14 Temporal muscle thickness (TMT) on routine cranial imaging is a sarcopenia surrogate associated with outcomes in traumatic brain injury, stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and chronic subdural hematoma and is increasingly viewed as an imaging correlate of physiological reserve.15-19

The mFI summarizes comorbidity burden to approximate physiological reserve and stratify perioperative risk.20 Across neurosurgical populations, increasing frailty scores are associated with higher rates of postoperative complications and mortality, longer LOS, a lower likelihood of discharge to home, and greater resource utilization.20,21 Within skull base surgery, the mFI-5 independently predicts overall and life-threatening complications, mortality, and prolonged hospitalization.22 In intradural skull base procedures, higher mFI scores are associated with increased reoperation rates and medical and surgical complications and with reduced odds of discharge to home.23 In vestibular schwannoma (VS), preoperative frailty, measured by the mFI, correlates with poorer surgical outcomes and has informed the development of a VS-specific risk stratification model.24 Despite this evidence across neurosurgery and skull base oncology, data specific to VS remain sparse. This retrospective cohort study examines the relationship between preoperative frailty and surgical outcomes in VS to inform counseling and perioperative planning.

METHODS

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive adults who underwent microsurgical resection of sporadic VS at a single tertiary center between 2014 and 2025. The study received Institutional Review Board approval. Given the retrospective, deidentified design, a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act waiver was granted, and individual consent was not required. Reporting conforms to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology recommendations.25

All resections were performed by the same senior neurosurgeon, with patients identified from a prospectively maintained operative database. Indications for resection rather than observation or radiosurgery were determined by multidisciplinary discussion and included tumor size, documented growth, brainstem compression, progressive symptoms, and patient preference. Inclusion required the availability of both primary end points: LOS in days and House-Brackmann (HB) facial nerve grade at discharge. Cases missing an outcome were excluded from modeling for that end point; no imputation was performed. Patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 were excluded. Postoperative care is standardized across approaches and services, including routine immediate postoperative neuroimaging unless contraindicated.

Preoperative frailty was abstracted using mFI-5 and mFI-11. Candidate covariates, specified a priori and assessed for multicollinearity, were age, tumor size (per 1-cm increase), TMT,16 surgical approach (translabyrinthine vs retrosigmoid; reference = retrosigmoid), preoperative HB grade (per 1-grade worse), presence of a nonauditory cranial nerve deficit preoperatively (yes/no), and ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) requirement (yes/no). Tumor size was measured as the maximal tumor diameter, intracanalicular and extracanalicular, on contrast-enhanced axial MRI.

Primary outcomes were hospital LOS and HB grade at discharge as a measure of early in-hospital facial nerve function. Discharge disposition (home vs other) was prespecified as a secondary outcome but was analyzed univariably because of low event frequency. Postoperative complications (eg, cerebrospinal fluid leak, meningitis, readmission, reoperation, venous thromboembolism, delirium) were not prespecified end points and were beyond this analysis's scope.

Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± SD and categorical variables as counts (%). Frailty indices and HB scores were treated as ordinal and LOS as a count. Correlations were assessed with Spearman rho and 95% CIs. We modeled LOS using negative binomial regression to account for overdispersion of the count outcome. Results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95%CIs. Discharge HB grade was modeled with proportional odds ordinal logistic regression after confirming the proportional odds assumption; results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95%CIs. Variables meeting the entry criteria, excluding those with multicollinearity, were included in multivariable models. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the multivariable models substituting individual comorbidities for the frailty indices (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 1, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125). In a post hoc sensitivity analysis, we additionally repeated the multivariable LOS models excluding temporal muscle thickness (TMT) as a covariate, which increased the analytic sample from 150 to 173 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 2, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125). Two-sided α was set at 0.05. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 30 (IBM Corp.).

The LOS distribution was right-skewed (median 3 days [IQR 2-5]; range 0-50), and its variance exceeded its mean (variance-to-mean ratio ≈ 7.2); negative binomial regression was therefore preferred over Poisson regression. Poisson regression showed overdispersion (Pearson deviance 4.56); the negative binomial model reduced this (deviance 1.41), improved akaike information criterion (904.12-727.36), and improved significantly over the null model (likelihood ratio χ2 (9) = 56.753, P < .001). The proportional odds assumption was met (likelihood ratio χ2 (32) = 6.018, P = 1.000). Multicollinearity among candidate covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (all variance inflation factors <2). All models used complete-case data, with per-model sample sizes reported in the tables (eg, 150 patients for the multivariable LOS models). All consecutive eligible cases during the study period were included; no formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed. To limit confounding, tumor burden and operative covariates were prespecified and entered into the models; selection bias, confounding by indication, and missing data are addressed in the Limitations. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, and the analyses are considered hypothesis-generating. Analysis code is available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

RESULTS

Among 192 adults who underwent microsurgical resection, analyses were performed using complete-case data for each end point (no imputation). Patient characteristics are reported in Table 1. The median LOS was 3 days (IQR 2-5; mean 4.5 ± 5.7; range 0-50). Frailty (mFI-5 and mFI-11) correlated positively with worse preoperative HB grade (mFI-5: ρ = 0.154, 95% CI, 0.008-0.293, P = .034; mFI-11: ρ = 0.200, 95% CI, 0.056-0.337, P = .005) but did not correlate with age, TMT, tumor size, or LOS (all P > .05). Tumor size (2.91 ± 1.36 retrosigmoid vs 2.68 ± 0.97 translabyrinthine, P = .459) and TMT (P = .624) did not differ by surgical approach. Although the frailty indices were not significantly correlated with LOS on Spearman analysis, they were associated with LOS in univariable count regression modeling (below); this apparent difference reflects the differing assumptions of the 2 methods, and the univariable association did not persist after multivariable adjustment.

Univariable Associations With LOS

Both frailty indices were significant univariable predictors of LOS (IRR 1.207, 95% CI, 1.060-1.374, P = .004 for mFI-5 and IRR 1.155, 95% CI, 1.060-1.259, P = .001 for mFI-11). Larger tumor size, worse preoperative HB, presence of a nonauditory preoperative neurologic deficit, and VPS requirement were also associated with longer LOS in univariable analysis (Figure). Greater TMT and the translabyrinthine approach were associated with shorter LOS. Age was not a significant univariable predictor but was retained in multivariable modeling as a prespecified confounder. Several individual frailty components showed signal (eg, impaired functional status, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/pneumonia, cardiac valvular disease, prior stroke/transient ischemic attack, neurologic deficit); some were too infrequent to model (congestive heart failure N = 1; prior myocardial infarction N = 3). Multivariable Models for LOS

Frailty (mFI-5 in Model A, Table 3; mFI-11 in Model B, Table 4) was not an independent predictor of LOS after adjustment. Factors that remained independently associated with LOS in both models were tumor size (per 1-cm increase: IRR 1.160, 95% CI, 1.026-1.311 in Model A; 1.157, 95% CI, 1.023-1.309 in Model B), surgical approach (translabyrinthine vs retrosigmoid: IRR 0.715, 95% CI, 0.532-0.962 in Model A; 0.719, 95% CI, 0.534-0.968 in Model B), and VPS requirement (IRR 2.507, 95% CI, 1.629-3.857 in Model A; 2.524, 95% CI, 1.638-3.888 in Model B; both P < .001). In a sensitivity model in which individual frailty components were substituted for mFI-5/11 (Model C, Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 1, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125), VPS and tumor size remained significant, whereas surgical approach did not. Among individual components, only cardiac valvular disease retained significance, although the estimate is unstable given the small subgroup (N = 4). A post hoc analysis excluding TMT re-estimated the LOS models in the larger sample (N = 173), with materially unchanged results. Tumor size (IRR 1.16 per cm, 95% CI, 1.03-1.30, P = .013 in the mFI-5 model; IRR 1.16, 95% CI, 1.03-1.30, P = .016 in the mFI-11 model), translabyrinthine approach (IRR 0.70, 95% CI, 0.53-0.93, P = .013 and P = .015, respectively), and VPS requirement (IRR 2.62, 95% CI, 1.78-3.84, P < .001 and IRR 2.63, 95% CI, 1.80-3.87, P < .001) remained independently associated with longer LOS, whereas neither mFI-5 (IRR 1.13, 95% CI, 0.98-1.29, P = .096) nor mFI-11 (IRR 1.09, 95% CI, 0.99-1.20, P = .094) was independently associated with LOS. In the corresponding individual components model, tumor size (IRR 1.15, 95% CI, 1.03-1.29, P = .016) and VPS requirement (IRR 2.61, 95% CI, 1.79-3.79, P < .001) remained significant, and cardiac valvular disease again retained significance (IRR 2.81, 95% CI, 1.17-6.72, P = .021) (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 2, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125).

Discharge Facial Nerve Function (HB)

On univariable testing, worse discharge HB was associated with larger tumor size, worse preoperative HB, and a nonauditory preoperative neurologic deficit; neither mFI-5 nor mFI-11 was associated with discharge HB. In proportional odds models mirroring the LOS specifications, preoperative HB was the sole consistent independent predictor of worse discharge HB. In the model using individual frailty components, larger tumor size was also associated with worse discharge HB (OR 1.403 per cm, 95% CI, 1.011-1.948, P = .043). Because discharge HB grade is mechanistically driven by tumor size, facial nerve anatomy, intraoperative adhesions, and operative approach, the absence of an association with mFI is expected and should not be overinterpreted. The distribution of HB grades preoperatively, at discharge, and at the most recent documented follow-up is summarized in Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 3, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125.

Discharge Disposition (Exploratory)

Other-than-home discharge occurred in 16 of 190 patients with documented discharge disposition (8.4%) and was associated with higher mean frailty scores (mFI-5: 1.13 ± 1.20 vs 0.48 ± 0.66, P = .017; mFI-11: 1.88 ± 1.96 vs 0.70 ± 0.89, P = .010) on univariable analysis; multivariable modeling was not performed because of low event frequency. Mean mFI scores by discharge disposition are presented in Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 4, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125.

DISCUSSION

In our single-center, single-surgeon cohort, mFI-5 and mFI-11 correlated with baseline HB but did not independently predict LOS or early postoperative HB after adjusting for tumor size, VPS placement, and approach. LOS was most strongly associated with these factors, and early facial nerve outcome was most strongly associated with preoperative HB and tumor size in the individual comorbidities model. Once a patient is deemed an appropriate surgical candidate, frailty indices add limited incremental prognostic value for short-term postoperative outcomes.

VS microsurgery is highly elective; observation and radiosurgery are effective alternatives favored for smaller tumors.26-30 Consequently, medically frail patients rarely reach the OR, compressing the frailty range into a narrow “fit” window—unlike the broad distributions in meningioma, glioblastoma, and pituitary cohorts, where frailty reliably stratifies outcomes.4-6,10-13,20-24 Unsurprisingly, frailty loses significance once tumor-specific predictors are modeled. Our single-center, single-surgeon design, while limiting generalizability, reduces variability from technique, experience, and institutional pathways, strengthening the internal comparison of frailty against tumor and operative factors.

VS-specific literature bears this out. Tang et al24 showed that mFI-11 predicts complications in unadjusted National Surgical Quality Improvement Program analyses that cannot incorporate tumor size or hydrocephalus. In our data set, which models tumor size, approach, and shunt status directly, the frailty effect disappears—consistent with Licina et al31 across craniotomy studies.

Our study advances the existing VS-specific frailty literature in several respects. The most directly comparable single-center series, Steele et al32 examined 79 patients with large (Koos III-IV) tumors and found frailty predicted prolonged stay, but the restricted size range yielded wide CIs. Our cohort is more than twice as large (N = 192), spans all resected tumor sizes, and models tumor size continuously, disentangling frailty from tumor burden—not previously feasible in VS work. Casazza et al33 similarly linked frailty to length of stay and examined complications, which were not our prespecified end point; rather than duplicate that focus, we contribute granular operative and imaging data not previously reported together—TMT as a sarcopenia correlate, word-recognition data, and prospectively abstracted HB grading. The large data sets often cited here, Dicpinigaitis et al34 (National Inpatient Sample, ∼27 000 patients) and Goshtasbi et al35 (National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, ∼1400 patients), derive from administrative sources lacking tumor size, approach, shunt status, and facial nerve grade, and cannot account for the operative factors we show dominate early outcomes. Combining a single-surgeon design, standardized pathways, continuous tumor size modeling, and patient-level detail, our analysis gives a more controlled estimate of frailty's incremental value than either the smaller series or the coarser administrative cohorts.

Tumor size drives operative complexity, brainstem compression, adhesions, and cerebellar retraction, making it among the most consistent correlates of early outcomes. Multiple series, including Killeen et al,36 Grinblat et al,37 and de Boer et al,38 show that increasing VS size predicts early facial weakness and more complex surgery. Hearing preservation drops sharply beyond ∼25 mm, as shown by Bozhkov et al.39 VPS placement is another marker of disease burden, adding operative time and recovery demands. Tumor size and shunt requirement were more strongly associated with LOS than any frailty metric.

Our standardized postoperative pathway minimizes care-variation confounding. Within it, translabyrinthine patients had shorter stays than retrosigmoid patients, mirroring Gibbon et al,40 whose meta-analysis showed shorter LOS and better facial nerve outcomes with the translabyrinthine approach. Because our center does not select approach by tumor size, this reinforces that approach and tumor factors, not comorbidity burden, shape recovery. Nonetheless, approach selection is multifactorial—reflecting serviceable hearing, word recognition, tumor location and extension, brainstem compression, hydrocephalus, patient goals, and surgeon/neuro-otology preference—so the shorter translabyrinthine LOS may be partly confounded by indication and interpreted cautiously.

Together, these data show why frailty “falls out” after adjustment: in a population filtered to patients fit for elective surgery, tumor size and operative variables predominate. Frailty retains value unadjusted—consistent with Tang et al24 and the broad neurosurgical frailty literature4-6,10-13,20,22-24—but once tumor size and VPS status are incorporated, its independent contribution is attenuated. Importantly, loss of significance after adjustment does not establish absence of a clinically meaningful effect, given the narrow frailty range here. The exploratory univariable association of higher mFI-5 with non-home discharge suggests frailty may still inform discharge planning even when its contribution to LOS and facial nerve outcome is small. Counseling should therefore prioritize tumor characteristics and expected surgical complexity over frailty indices.

Limitations

This single-center observational analysis is subject to selection bias, limited generalizability, potential misclassification (frailty, HB), and likely residual confounding despite adjustment. Small subgroups, complete-case analyses, model assumptions, lack of multiplicity control, in-hospital–only outcomes, and unmodeled time trends further limit inference; results are hypothesis-generating and warrant prospective multicenter confirmation with standardized frailty assessment and longer follow-up. Specifically, HB grade at discharge reflects early in-hospital facial nerve function and may not capture later recovery, which is best assessed at 6 to 12 months postoperatively. The frailty distribution in our cohort was skewed toward low scores: 89.6% of patients had an mFI-5 of 0 or 1 and only 10.4% scored ≥2 (mFI-11: 81.8% scored 0%-1% and 18.2% scored ≥2). This reflects selection of clinically fit candidates and likely limited detection of frailty effects; our results thus evaluate frailty's incremental value among patients already deemed fit rather than show frailty is unimportant in VS surgery generally. TMT was missing in 17.2% of patients owing to variable imaging availability in the electronic health record rather than patient or outcome characteristics; this was not modeled formally, so TMT findings are exploratory. A post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding TMT (Supplemental Digital Content 1, Table 2, https://links.lww.com/NS9/A125) was materially unchanged, although limited TMT availability still constrains interpretation. Outcomes not captured—cerebrospinal fluid leak, meningitis, readmission, reoperation, delirium, venous thromboembolism, and longer-term facial nerve recovery—were beyond scope; conclusions are limited to LOS and discharge HB grade and should not be extrapolated to complication risk. Tumor burden was operationalized as maximal diameter; Koos grade and preoperative hearing/word recognition were not uniformly available as covariates, possibly incompletely capturing operative complexity. Approach selection was not randomized and is subject to confounding by indication.

CONCLUSIONS

In this single-center, single-surgeon cohort, validated frailty indices did not independently predict hospital LOS or early facial nerve function after adjustment for tumor and operative factors. Tumor size, VPS placement, and surgical approach were the principal determinants of early postoperative outcomes. Once a patient is selected for surgery, counseling should prioritize tumor characteristics and expected surgical complexity rather than aggregate frailty. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized frailty assessment and longer follow-up are warranted to validate these observations. Because frailer patients are typically selected away from elective surgery, these findings show limited incremental prognostic value of frailty among already-selected candidates rather than that frailty is unimportant; the absence of a significant adjusted association is hypothesis-generating and does not exclude a clinically relevant effect.
