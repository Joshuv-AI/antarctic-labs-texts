The relationship between oral frailty and cognitive function in elderly patients with chronic diseases: the chain-mediated effect of nutrition status and the inflammatory marker CRP
Authors: Yue Sun, Zhicheng Zhang, Shuoming Wang, Xia Yang, Qiong Li, Chengbiao Lu, Guodong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to investigate the mediating role of nutritional status and the inflammatory marker C-reactive protein (CRP) in the relationship between oral frailty and cognitive function in elderly patients with chronic diseases.MethodsA cross-sectional design was employed, and From June to December 2024, patients aged ≥ 60 years with chronic diseases from three tertiary hospitals in Henan Province were selected using convenience sampling. Data were collected using questionnaires, including the general information questionnaire, the Oral Frailty Screening Index-8 (OFI-8), the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short Form, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, and laboratory tests for CRP. Descriptive demographic analysis and Pearson correlation analysis were conducted using SPSS 26.0, and the model 6 in macro program Process 4.1 was employed to test the chain mediation effect.Results(1) Regression analysis showed that oral frailty had a significant direct impact on cognitive function (β = -0.154, P < 0.01), and nutritional status (β = 0.228, P < 0.001) and CRP (β = -0.200, P < 0.001) also had significant effects on cognitive function. (2) The results of the mediating effect analysis showed that the simple mediating effects of nutritional status and CRP between oral frailty and cognitive function were -0.088 (95% CI: -0.138 to -0.048) and -0.056(95% CI: -0.097 to -0.029), respectively, and the chain mediating effect was - 0.022 (95% CI: -0.040 to -0.010).ConclusionThis study found direct and chain-mediated effects of nutritional status and CRP between oral frailty and cognitive function. It suggests that cognitive function can be improved by early identification of oral frailty, improving nutritional status and reducing chronic inflammation levels to delay or even reverse the onset and progression of cognitive dysfunction in elderly patients with chronic diseases.Keywords: Oral frailty, Nutritional status, Inflammatory marker CRP, Cognitive function, Chain mediation

Introduction

With the accelerating global population aging, the health issues of elderly patients with chronic diseases have drawn increasing attention. Cognitive decline has become one of the key factors affecting the quality of life in this population [1], and is closely associated with various adverse outcomes such as rehospitalization, functional impairment, and mortality [2]. If not addressed early, cognitive decline may progress to mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and further develop into dementia, significantly increasing the caregiving burden and financial pressure on families and society [3]. MCI is an intermediate stage between normal aging and dementia, primarily characterized by a decline in memory and cognitive function [4]. Among patients with chronic diseases, the prevalence of MCI ranges from 18 to 35%, while that of dementia ranges from 8 to 12% both two to three times higher than in the general population—posing a serious challenge to global public health [5]. Therefore, early identification of cognitive decline in elderly patients with chronic diseases and timely intervention can help reduce the risk of adverse outcomes, improve individual quality of life, and alleviate the burden on families and society.

To more comprehensively address oral health issues in older adults, the concept of “oral frailty” has been proposed in recent years. As a novel framework, it not only reflects the overall status of oral function in older adults but also offers a novel perspective for systematically assessing the relationship between oral health and general health conditions. In 2020, the Japanese Dental Association defined oral frailty as a series of age-related changes in various aspects of oral conditions—such as the number of teeth, oral function, and oral hygiene—accompanied by a declining interest in oral health, reduced physical and psychological reserves, even impaired eating function and the overall effect of these changes is the deterioration of both physical and mental functioning [6]. The core clinical manifestations of oral frailty primarily include deteriorating oral health, impairments in chewing, swallowing, and salivation, oral pain, and reduced oral motor function [7]. Existing studies indicate that oral frailty is an important risk factor for malnutrition, sarcopenia, cognitive decline, and reduced quality of life in older adults [8–10]. Data show that the prevalence of oral frailty among older adults living in the community worldwide ranges from 18 to 38%, while it is as high as 58% among older adults with chronic diseases in China [11, 12]. Currently, the most widely used and convenient screening tool in China is the Oral Frailty Index-8 (OFI-8), developed by the Japanese Dental Association; its validity and reliability have been well established [13].

Multiple studies have indicated that factors such as impaired swallowing function and tooth loss related to oral frailty may potentially have connections with cognitive function in multiple aspects [14, 15]. Nutritional status plays a crucial role in maintaining the health of the elderly. Poor nutritional status can compromise immune function, further aggravate inflammatory responses, such as elevated CRP levels, and affect the body’s ability to regulate inflammation [16]. Elevated CRP levels have been proven to be associated with poorer cognitive function and an increased risk of dementia. The reason is that it may negatively affect cognitive function by exacerbating memory loss, reducing cognitive speed, and impairing executive function [17].

In summary, based on previous research on oral frailty, nutritional status, and CRP, most studies have focused on the relationships between two or three of these factors. The combined effects and potential connections among these factors remain unclear, and there is a lack of research on the mechanism of interaction among the four variables. That is, the specific pathways through which oral frailty in elderly patients with chronic diseases affects cognitive function have not been fully explored. Moreover, most studies have focused on the elderly population, with fewer studies on elderly patients with chronic diseases. This study aimed to examine the impact of oral frailty on cognitive function in elderly chronic disease inpatients. Furthermore, the study sought to further analyze the mediating roles of nutritional status and CRP. The study’s findings may inform the development of practical strategies for the prevention and improvement of cognitive dysfunction in elderly chronic disease patients.

Methods

Study participants and sample

This cross-sectional study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies (STROBE) statement checklist [18]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Medical University (No.:XYLL-20230322). This study used a convenience sampling method, with elderly patients with chronic diseases admitted to three tertiary hospitals in Henan Province from June to December 2024 as the research subjects. Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosed with at least one chronic disease by a secondary or higher-level medical institution; (2) At least 60 years old; (3) Informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria: (1) Those with language barriers or loss of consciousness; (2) Patients in the acute or terminal stage of a critical illness (survival period < 3 months); (3) Diagnosed with severe diseases such as malignant tumors or respiratory failure; (4) Diagnosed with mental illness or mental disorders. This study strictly adhered to the principle of informed consent. All participants voluntarily signed a written informed consent form after fully understanding the purpose, content, and procedures of the study.

According to the sample size calculation requirements, the sample size = number of dimensions × (10 - 20) × [1 + (10% -20%)]. This study’s variables include 15 dimensions (5 dimensions of oral frailty, 1 dimension of nutritional status, 1 dimension of CRP, and 8 dimensions of cognitive function), so the sample size should be at least 165 cases. The sample size of this study is 305 cases.

Research tools

General information questionnaire

Designed by the researchers after reviewing relevant literature and seeking the opinions of experts in geriatric chronic disease care, it includes age, gender, education level, monthly personal income, smoking, drinking, exercise habits, and falls or not in the past year.

Oral Frailty Screening Index-8 (OFI-8)

The Oral Frailty Screening Index-8 (OFI-8) was proposed by Japanese scholars Tanaka et al. [13]. in 2021 to identify oral frailty in the elderly. It was later translated and revised into the Chinese version of the Oral Frailty Screening Questionnaire for Older Adults by Chinese scholar Chen et al. [19].It includes five dimensions and eight items: denture use, chewing ability, swallowing function, social participation, and oral health-related behaviors. The score range is 0–11, with a higher score indicating more severe oral frailty. A score of ≥ 4 is considered to indicate the presence of oral frailty. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the OFI-8 has been validated to be 0.949.

Mini Nutritional Assessment Short Form (MNA-SF)

The Mini Nutritional Assessment Short Form (MNA-SF) was developed by Rubenstein in the United States in 2001 [20]. It is used to quickly identify the risk of malnutrition in the elderly. It is a simplified version of the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) scale and includes six items: body mass index, weight loss, appetite changes, acute illness or stress, bedridden status, and mental state. The score range is from 0 to 14, with a higher score indicating better nutritional status. According to the scoring system, nutritional status can be classified into three grades: 12–14 points as normal, 8–11 points as at risk, and 0–7 points as malnutrition. It has been verified that the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale is 0.737.

Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was developed by Professor Nasreddine in 2004 and is a standardized assessment used to screen for MCI [21]. The MoCA assessment includes eight dimensions such as attention, executive function, memory, and language, with a total score of 30 points. This scale also takes into account the influence of educational background. To address the bias caused by educational level on the results, 1 point was added to the MoCA score of elderly chronic disease in patients with an education duration of ≤ 12 years in this study [22]. When the MoCA score is ≥ 26 points, it indicates normal cognitive function. It has been verified that the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale is 0.82.

C-reactive protein (CRP)

C-reactive protein is a key indicator reflecting the inflammatory status of the body. An increase in its concentration indicates the presence of an inflammatory response in the body and can serve as an early warning signal for inflammation, providing a reference basis for the early identification and intervention of related diseases [23]. In this study, 5 ml of elbow venous blood was collected from all subjects in the morning on an empty stomach. After centrifugation, the serum CRP was measured using the latex immunoturbidimetric method. The normal content of the hs-CRP indicator is: 0 - 4 mg/L.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and lifestyle information was collected through questionnaires, including age (60-69years, 70-79years, 80 years and above), gender(male, female), Education Level(illiteracy, elementary school, junior high school, high school and above), Personal Monthly Income(less than 1000, 1000-3000, 3001-5000, 5000 and above), Smoking status (never, current, former), Drinking status(never, current, former), Fall within the past year.(no, yes) and Exercise habits (0 times, 1 - 3times a week, 4 times a week and above).

Data collection and quality control

Before the start of the survey, all researchers received standardized training to ensure they could guide participants to complete the questionnaire truthfully using consistent and standardized instructions. With the support and cooperation of the department heads, two trained researchers conducted face-to-face interviews with participants in a quiet room. Prior to the interview, the researchers explained the purpose and significance of the study, used a standardized script to guide participants, and clearly informed them that all data collected would be used solely for scientific research. Researchers assessed whether participants met the inclusion criteria, and after obtaining informed consent, the formal survey was conducted. Throughout the process, participants’ rights and privacy were fully protected. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants could withdraw from the study at any time. Anonymity was maintained in data collection to further ensure information security. After the questionnaire was completed, researchers checked the responses on the spot to ensure there were no missing or incomplete items before collecting the forms. The questionnaire data were entered independently by two researchers and then cross-checked by a third researcher to ensure accuracy. Logical checks and outlier screening were performed after data entry to ensure data completeness and consistency. For missing or potentially abnormal values, researchers reviewed the original records or contacted participants to verify and correct the data, thereby improving data quality and reliability.

Statistical analysis

The research data were entered into EpiData 3.1 software to establish a database and analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation±s), and t-tests or analysis of variance were used for comparisons between groups. Count data were expressed as frequency and proportion. The Harman single-factor method was used to test for common method bias. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were log-transformed and included in the Pearson correlation analysis to explore the correlations between oral frailty, nutritional status, CRP, and cognitive function. Given that the CRP data did not meet the requirements for normal distribution, it was log-transformed prior to conducting correlation and regression analyses. Other continuous variables were normally distributed and thus were analyzed in their original form. The model 6 in the macro program Process 4.1 was used for mediation and moderation effect analysis [24]. Bootstrap testing was used, with 5000 repeated samplings, to test the significance of the mediation effect. When the 95% CI of the indirect effect does not include 0, the mediation model is established. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Common method bias test

Harman’s single-factor method was used to detect common method bias. All items of oral frailty, nutritional status, and cognitive function were included. The percentage of variance explained by the first factor to the total variance explained was used as the criterion. The variance contribution rate of the first principal component was 27.028%, which was lower than the critical value of 40%, indicating that there was no significant common method bias in the research data and subsequent analysis could be conducted [25].

Based on the results of descriptive and correlational analyses, this study constructed a chain mediation model with oral frailty of elderly patients with chronic diseases as the independent variable, cognitive function as the dependent variable, nutritional status and CRP as the mediating variables, while controlling for age, gender, education level, personal monthly income, smoking status, drinking status, the number of weekly exercises, and whether there was a history of falls in the past year. The analysis indicated that oral frailty had a negative impact on nutritional status (β = −0.387, P < 0.001) and cognitive function (β = −0.154, P < 0.01), and a positive impact on CRP (β = 0.281, P < 0.001); nutritional status had a positive impact on cognitive function (β = 0.228, P < 0.001) and a negative impact on CRP (β = −0.280, P < 0.001); CRP had a negative impact on cognitive function (β = −0.200, P < 0.001). Discussion

Association between oral frailty and cognitive function in elderly patients with chronic diseases

The findings of this study indicate that oral frailty is negatively associated with cognitive function in elderly patients with chronic diseases. Multiple previous studies have also found that individuals with more severe oral frailty tend to have poorer cognitive function [26, 27]. A previous study showed that the absence of regular dental check-ups, brushing teeth less than twice daily, the presence of dental caries, and swallowing dysfunction were all independent risk factors for the onset of Alzheimer disease [15].Tooth loss, a significant indicator for evaluating oral frailty, has been found that for each missing tooth in the elderly, the risk of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) increases by 1.4%, and the risk of AD correspondingly rises by 1.1% [28].The potential reason could be that tooth loss leads to functional impairment of the chewing organs, subsequently reducing the ability to chew and bite. This functional decline leads to a decrease in chewing activities and cerebral blood flow, thereby influencing the oxygen and nutrient supply to the brain [29]. Additionally, the reduced stimulation of the peripheral receptors of the periodontal ligament can also impact cognitive function by weakening neural pathway connections and causing degeneration of the corresponding brain regions [30]. Concurrently, neuroimaging studies have also demonstrated that elderly patients with tooth loss exhibit a significant reduction in gray matter volume in brain regions closely related to memory, learning, and cognitive function. This structural alteration may serve as a crucial foundation for the decline in cognitive function and even the development of dementia [31]. Therefore, it is essential to support elderly patients with chronic diseases in developing regular oral examination habits, addressing oral health issues promptly, maintaining proper oral hygiene, and consistently wearing dentures in order to promote better oral health and cognitive function 4

The mediating role of nutritional status in the relationship between oral frailty and cognitive function among elderly patients with chronic diseases

The findings of this study suggest that nutritional status is positively associated with the cognitive function of elderly patients with chronic diseases. Nutritional status acts as a mediator between oral frailty and cognitive function. The underlying reason might lie in the fact that the deterioration of oral function alters the dietary structure of the elderly, resulting in reduced food intake and an increased risk of malnutrition [32]. Malnutrition as a risk factor for cognitive impairment in the elderly can lead to disrupted brain energy metabolism, thereby affecting the synthesis and transmission of neurotransmitters, ultimately contributing to cognitive decline [33, 34]. Simultaneously, the reduced tongue pressure associated with oral frailty might also make the formation of food boluses and swallowing more challenging, leading to weight loss and insufficient intake of essential nutrients by the brain [35]. These nutrients, such as vitamins, minerals, and fatty acids, are of great significance for brain development, function maintenance, and neuroprotection, and are closely intertwined with cognitive function [36].Based on this, clinical attention should be directed towards the nutritional management of the elderly, and nutritional intervention should be regarded as an important strategy for preventing cognitive decline [37]. By adjusting the intake of protein, energy, and trace elements, and designing dietary plans based on individualized nutritional assessment, the chewing and swallowing abilities of the elderly can be enhanced to promote nutrient intake, and the risk of cognitive dysfunction can be mitigated by improving the quality of the elderly’s diet.

The mediating role of CRP in the relationship between oral frailty and cognitive function among elderly patients with chronic diseases

The results of this study demonstrate that elevated levels of CRP are significantly negatively correlated with the decline in cognitive function among elderly patients with chronic diseases. As a biomarker for inflammation, CRP may exert negative effects on cognitive function through mechanisms such as activating microglia, damaging the blood-brain barrier, and triggering neuroinflammation, leading to a significantly elevated risk of dementia in the elderly with chronic inflammation compared to healthy individuals [38]. The mechanism might involve the over-proliferation of oral bacteria, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and oral diseases like periodontitis causing local oral inflammation, which subsequently triggers a systemic chronic inflammatory response [39, 40]. Specifically, in cases of oral frailty, alterations in the oral environment lead to microbial dysbiosis, resulting in excessive proliferation of periodontal pathogenic bacteria [39, 40].The endotoxins produced by periodontal pathogens induce an immune response within the body, leading to elevated levels of inflammatory factors such as CRP in the serum. Through activating intracellular signaling pathways in endothelial cells, inducing oxidative stress, and increasing the expression of adhesion molecules, the integrity of the blood-brain barrier is compromised, facilitating the penetration of oral pathogenic bacteria and their toxins into the blood-brain barrier [41]. triggering neuroinflammatory responses, and ultimately resulting in irreversible brain damage and permanent cognitive dysfunction [42]. Additionally, the loss of masticatory function can further exacerbate cognitive dysfunction by intensifying neural damage and chronic inflammatory stress [30]. Thus, it is evident that controlling the level of inflammation within the body holds significant importance for maintaining the cognitive function of elderly patients with chronic diseases. Medical personnel should attach importance to the management of chronic inflammation in elderly patients with chronic diseases and consider it as a crucial intervention target for preventing cognitive dysfunction.

The chain mediating effects of nutritional status and inflammatory factors between oral frailty and cognitive function

The structure of this study reveals that malnutrition and elevated CRP levels play a significant mediating role between oral frailty and cognitive dysfunction. The possible reason could be that patients with oral frailty, due to factors such as tooth loss restricting food choices, experience insufficient nutrient intake, resulting in malnutrition. This leads to weakened immune function, increased risk of infection, and intensification of the inflammatory response through multiple mechanisms [43]. For instance, deficiencies of key nutrients like vitamin D caused by malnutrition reduce their binding capacity to specific receptors in the central nervous system and may also trigger oxidative stress and neuroinflammation in the hippocampus [44]. Neuroinflammation can trigger abnormal deposition of beta-amyloid (Aβ) and inhibit its degradation, thereby causing brain damage in the early stages of the disease and significantly increasing the risk of cognitive dysfunction and dementia in the elderly [45, 46]. Although the present study model considers malnutrition as an antecedent variable leading to elevated CRP levels, it should be noted that a bidirectional relationship may exist between these factors [47]. On the one hand, inadequate nutrient intake may compromise the body’s anti-inflammatory capacity, resulting in elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as CRP [48, 49]. On the other hand, A state of chronic inflammation may further exacerbate malnutrition by suppressing appetite through its effects on appetite-regulating neuropeptides in the hypothalamus, or by impairing nutrient absorption in the gastrointestinal tract [50–52]. While the current model aligns with established theoretical frameworks, the potential reciprocal nature of this relationship necessitates future longitudinal studies to further validate the proposed causal pathways. Based on the aforementioned mechanisms, it is necessary to actively guide elderly patients with chronic diseases to cultivate good oral hygiene habits, ensure a diverse diet to meet nutritional requirements, and recommend nutritional intervention, particularly anti-inflammatory diets (such as the Mediterranean diet), to have a positive impact on cognitive function [53].

Despite achieving certain outcomes, this study has several limitations. The nature of the cross-sectional design constrains the inference of causal relationships. Future studies could conduct longitudinal research to further explore the causal links among variables. Second, this study was limited to elderly in patients with chronic diseases recruited from three tertiary hospitals in Henan Province. The sample may be more representative of individuals with more severe conditions or those with better access to medical resources. In addition, the findings may be influenced by the specific socioeconomic and cultural context of the local region, which could limit the generalizability and external validity of the results. Future studies should employ stratified, multicenter designs encompassing hospitals at different tiers (from primary to tertiary care) and diverse community settings to validate our results. Finally, the inflammatory factors in this study mainly relied on the laboratory indicator CRP for data collection. Future studies could incorporate more comprehensive clinical examination indicators to improve the accuracy of the research.

Conclusion

Nutritional status and inflammatory factors play important mediating roles between oral frailty and cognitive impairment in elderly patients with chronic diseases, highlighting the necessity of timely detection and comprehensive intervention. Future research should further explore ways to slow down oral frailty and cognitive decline in elderly patients with chronic diseases through effective nutritional intervention and reduction of chronic inflammation within the body, thereby enhancing the health status of this patient group. Given the cross-sectional design of the study, further validation through longitudinal studies is needed in the future.
