First aid educational needs among nursing officers in military primary clinics: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Seoyoung Baek, Jiyeon Lee, Yungyong Jeon, Mi-So Shim, Chang Gi Park, Gwang Suk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence regarding the first aid duties and educational needs of nursing officers in military primary healthcare facilities is limited. We aimed to develop a comprehensive first aid task inventory for nursing officers in such primary military clinics and to identify educational priorities using multiple analytic approaches.MethodsA cross-sectional mixed-methods design was employed. Nursing officer communities at primary military clinics across South Korea were targeted; 11 nursing officers participated in focus group interviews and 47 completed a nationwide survey. A multi-step process was conducted from August to December 2024, comprising a literature review, secondary data analysis of 939,530 emergency room visits from the Defense Medical Statistics Information System (2013–2022), focus group interviews, and a nationwide survey. Educational needs were assessed using both importance–performance analysis and the Borich needs assessment model.ResultsA total of 79 first aid tasks were identified, including 62 direct nursing and 17 emergency management duties. Both the importance–performance analysis and Borich needs assessment consistently prioritised 16 tasks, while seven additional tasks were prioritised by only one method—either importance–performance or Borich. High-priority tasks included caring for brain injury, chest injury, intraperitoneal organ injury, neural and spinal cord injury, crush injury, airway haemorrhage, inhalation burn, shock, blast and penetrating injuries, suicidal ideation and self-harm, and drowning.ConclusionsThis study provides the first systematic evidence on first aid duties and educational needs of nursing officers in primary military clinics in South Korea. The findings highlight the need for structured, military-contextualised continuing education programmes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04875-7.

Background

Military primary healthcare facilities in South Korea (hereinafter ‘primary military clinics’) are primary care medical units at the division level and below, functioning as the first point of access to healthcare for active-duty military personnel [1, 2]. These facilities are typically staffed with 1–10 army surgeons, 1–2 nursing officers, 1–10 medical non-commissioned officers, and 5–40 medics. These primary military clinics are responsible for the health management of 3,000–12,000 military personnel [2]. To strengthen the functioning of primary military clinics, the Ministry of National Defense in South Korea first deployed 12 nursing officers as public health nursing officers in 2008 [3]. As of 2023, approximately 80 nursing officers served in primary military clinics. Nursing officers in primary military clinics independently manage patient care, medical support, health promotion, infection control, preventive medicine, and administrative tasks. They plan, implement, evaluate, and report most tasks autonomously, therefore assuming broader responsibilities than nursing officers in other positions [1].

Owing to intensive training and physically demanding operational activities, military personnel are at increased risk of injury, including casualties from North Korean local provocations, as well as firearm and grenade accidents [4]. The incidence of environmentally related diseases, such as haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome, is also high [5]. Despite serving as primary medical institutions that must maintain emergency preparedness, primary military clinics face significant limitations in personnel and resources compared with hospitals, resulting in inadequate emergency response systems [2]. Nursing officers provide medical support and deliver integrated patient care encompassing assessment, treatment, evacuation coordination, and documentation when casualties occur [6]. Consequently, their competence in first aid may directly influence patient outcomes in emergency situations and is crucial for developing future managerial capabilities.

Although the Korea Armed Forces Nursing Academy operates disaster/emergency nursing training and requires basic life support certification for cadets [7], structured and continuous first aid training for nursing officers after deployment remains limited. Most nursing officers rely on sporadic unit-based training or self-directed learning, resulting in considerable variation in educational experiences across units and limited standardisation. Nursing officers in primary military clinics—typically junior officers—often have limited continuing education experience related to first aid. These officers are often assigned to divisions after 2–3 years of service in military hospitals, resulting in inadequate preparation for their differentiated responsibilities and adaptation difficulties [3]. Song and Kim [3] found that ‘first aid and evacuation during emergency incidents’ showed the highest job importance and ranked third in training needs among 52 survey items, confirming the critical need for specialized first aid education programmes. Chen and Liou’s [8] study with nursing students demonstrated that structured first aid training significantly improved knowledge, skills, and self-confidence in emergency care practices, further supporting the necessity for comprehensive first aid education. Therefore, providing an effective educational programme first requires a clear understanding of the first aid tasks performed in practice and the identification of priority training areas.

While the U.S. military utilises comprehensive guidelines such as the Joint Trauma System Clinical Practice Guidelines [9] and operates intensive educational programmes for military nursing officers, South Korea lacks such educational programmes. Although specialised training courses are available in various nursing areas [6] and the Army Health Report provides basic disease information [10], these resources contribute minimally to enhancing first aid competencies for division-level nursing officers.

Therefore, we aim to identify the educational needs for first aid among nursing officers in primary military clinics, providing foundational data for developing targeted first aid education programmes that reflect military-specific considerations. We use a mixed-methods approach to ensure comprehensive evaluation of training requirements [11]. The combination of Borich needs assessment and Locus for Focus models effectively identifies high-priority educational content by evaluating the gap between current knowledge and perceived importance [12, 13]. Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional mixed-methods design to develop a first aid task inventory (Phase 1) and identify educational needs among nursing officers in primary military clinics (Phase 2). The study followed an exploratory sequential procedure consisting of the two phases divided into four steps: (1) literature review, (2) secondary data analysis, (3) focus group interviews (FGIs), and (4) survey-based needs assessment. Findings from each step were integrated iteratively to develop and refine the first aid task inventory and identify educational priorities.

Participants and sampling

For the FGIs, participants were recruited via purposive sampling from professional nursing officer communities. Eligibility criteria included experience in a primary military clinic within the preceding six months to ensure familiarity with current protocols. This six-month threshold aligns with the military’s semi-annual rotation cycle, ensuring accurate retrospective recall. The 11 participants recruited for FGIs were divided into three groups based on their tenure (typically 18–24 months):

Interviews continued until thematic saturation was achieved.

The survey targeted nursing officers currently serving in primary military clinics. We excluded nursing civil servants and those who participated in the FGIs to ensure independent results. The minimum required sample size was estimated as 34, calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.7, for a paired t-test (two-tailed). Based on Cohen’s [14] criteria, an effect size of 0.5 (medium effect), significance level (α) of 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.80 were applied, requiring a minimum of 34 participants [15]. Given the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of prior empirical data from this specialised population, a medium effect size was considered appropriate for identifying educationally meaningful differences relevant to curriculum development. The final sample of 47 valid responses exceeded this requirement, ensuring sufficient power to identify educational priorities.

Study process

The research followed a sequential two-phase process to develop the first aid task inventory and evaluate educational needs.

Phase 1: Development of first aid task inventory

Literature review

Given the scarcity of research on first aid duties of nursing officers in primary military clinics, a targeted literature review was conducted from August to September 2023. Eight key documents were purposively selected, including military field manuals, official military health reports, and prior studies related to trauma care, occupational health nursing, and emergency preparedness (see Additional file 1). These documents were reviewed to identify common injury and disease patterns in military settings, as well as corresponding first aid competencies and nursing roles. Findings from the literature review informed the development of the preliminary first aid task inventory.

Secondary data analysis

The analysis included 939,530 patients who visited emergency rooms at primary military clinics over 10 years (2013–2022), excluding records with missing diagnosis data. Diseases and injury types were categorized using the Korean Standard Classification of Disease (KCD) codes, with data spanning KCD-6 through KCD-8 revisions during the study period. To improve the practical relevance of the inventory, high-frequency diagnostic categories were mapped to specific first aid tasks based on the KCD hierarchical structure, including both major and sub-categories. This process focused on operationalizing the duties of nursing officers as concretely as possible by listing tasks in their literal, field-applicable forms rather than abstracting them.

FGIs

A semi-structured interview guide was used to explore participants’ experiences with and perspectives on first aid educational needs. The guide’s core themes and probing questions were informed by the preliminary first aid task inventory derived from the preceding literature review and secondary data analysis (see Additional file 2). To ensure consistency, the interview guide was structured into two primary domains corresponding to the inventory: (1) direct nursing duties and (2) emergency management responsibilities. Before concluding each interview, participants were presented with the first aid task list and asked to confirm any additional content. Data were collected between October and November 2023. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using qualitative content analysis [16].

Phase 2: Identification of educational needs and priorities

The survey questionnaire consisted of educational needs assessment items and general characteristics. The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study (see Additional file 3). Based on the first aid inventory (79 items: 62 direct nursing duties and 17 emergency management duties), participants assessed performance and importance for each item. The performance and importance of first aid tasks were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale. Performance was assessed from 1 (cannot perform at all) to 5 (perform very well), and importance from 1 (not important at all) to 5 (very important). The internal consistency reliability of the instrument in this study was confirmed, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.98 for importance and 0.96 for performance. General characteristics included personal factors (gender, age, marital status, educational level, clinical experience, resuscitation certification) and military/occupational factors (rank, rank when transferring, length of military service, length of service at current workplace, division type, medical facility units, nursing specialties, work-related training experience, first aid training experience).

Data were collected from November 30 to December 15, 2023, using Google Survey. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling through research announcements in nursing officer communities at primary military clinics. The online survey was conducted via link access for those who met the selection criteria and voluntarily agreed to participate. Screening questions were presented on the first screen to confirm eligibility, and participants were informed in advance that responding to the first question would be considered consent to participate. Participants received convenience store gift cards (5,000 KRW) as appreciation.

Data analysis

Qualitative data from the FGIs were analysed using the content analysis method. The recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim, and meaningful statements were extracted and synthesized into higher-level categories based on similarities. To ensure trustworthiness, the analysis was reviewed by an external nursing professor with qualitative research expertise, and member-checking was conducted with three participants.

Quantitative data were analysed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics and paired t-tests were performed to summarize participants’ general characteristics and examine differences between importance and performance scores. Educational needs and priorities were determined using two methods to enhance validity through complementary interpretation of results: Borich’s [17] educational needs analysis, which considers importance and performance but applies weights to importance, and Martilla and James’s [18] importance–performance analysis (IPA), which derives priorities by positioning elements within four quadrants using performance and importance as two axes.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Armed Forces Medical Command (No. AFMC- 2023-09-001-001, dated September 12, 2023). Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained online.

Results

First aid task inventory development

A literature review, secondary data analysis, and FGIs were conducted to develop the first aid task inventory, ultimately deriving 79 tasks (62 direct nursing duties in emergency situations and 17 emergency management duties) as shown in Fig. 1.

First aid task derivation: literature review and secondary data analysis

First aid tasks were identified by reviewing eight documents. Diagnosis codes and names of 939,530 patients who visited emergency rooms at division-level primary military clinics over 10 years (2013–2022) were analysed to supplement the first aid task list identified through the literature review. Based on this analysis, several military-specific conditions were added owing to their relatively high incidence rates from frequent outdoor activities, including malaria, scrub typhus, and haemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome. In addition to animal contact identified in the literature review, contact with poisonous plants was frequently observed and therefore included. The inventory also incorporated psychiatric emergencies, including suicide attempts and self-harm, anxiety disorders, obsessive–compulsive disorder, adjustment disorders, and posttraumatic stress disorder. High-frequency diagnoses such as airway bleeding, asthma, acute gastroenteritis, appendicitis, food poisoning, and ear disorders were included in patient care duties. Furthermore, considering the collective living environment characteristic of military settings, infectious disease management was deemed highly important not only for patient care but also for unit-wide management. Therefore, nursing care for infectious diseases such as mumps, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19, and influenza was added to the task inventory (see Additional file 4).

Expanding the first aid task inventory: FGIs

Eleven nursing officers participated in FGIs to identify additional educational needs. Among the 11 participants, 10 (90.9%) were women and nine (81.8%) had under 5 years of work experience. The average work experience at primary military clinics was 18.6 months for past workers, 10 months for current workers (experienced), and 1 month for current workers (new).

As content analysis revealed similar themes between past and experienced current workers, their responses were integrated. Interview content was categorised into ‘direct nursing duties in emergency situations’ and ‘emergency management duties’. FGIs identified additional emergency management duties not found in the literature review or secondary data analysis. Key educational needs included understanding of military-prevalent diseases, understanding the functions and roles of the medical integrated situation centre, reporting according to the emergency medical support system, medical expense support guidance, patient triage and priority determination, use of first aid equipment and supplies, first aid education for nursing personnel/unit members, and mass infection and poisoning management. Post-resuscitation care, blood transfusion, and death management were excluded from the final task inventory as participant feedback revealed that these are not performed at primary military clinics (detailed qualitative findings are available in Additional file 5).

To determine high-priority tasks and ensure a methodologically balanced comparison with the 19 tasks derived from IPA Quadrant II, we selected the top 25% of items based on their Borich priority scores, yielding 20 core tasks. This cut-off threshold was established to achieve a comparable sample size between the two analytical methods for robust cross-validation. These high-priority tasks included caring for patients with great vessel injury, obstetric emergency, intracranial injury, cardiac injury, obstructive shock, drowning, blast injuries, and intraperitoneal organ injury. Among the 19 first aid tasks identified in Quadrant II through IPA, 16 were ranked within the top 20 in the Borich needs assessment. Seven tasks satisfied only one of these criteria (Table 2).

Discussion

We identified and prioritised educational needs related to first aid competencies of nursing officers working in primary military clinics. A multi-step process involving a literature review, secondary data analysis, FGIs, and nationwide survey enabled the construction of a comprehensive task inventory and identification of priority areas. By applying both IPA and the Borich needs assessment model, this study provides both a methodological contribution to nursing education research and practical guidance for curriculum design and professional training in military healthcare settings.

Methodological strengths

Our triangulated approach ensured that the final list of 79 inventory tasks reflected both prior evidence and the unique characteristics of military healthcare practice. This process was essential, given the absence of existing data regarding military nursing officers’ first aid duties, to ensure that the inventory was context-specific and constructed comprehensively.

We employed complementary analytic methods—IPA and Borich needs assessment—to determine educational priorities. Utilising these methods together allowed for more effective prioritisation of educational content, as the combined approach compensated for the limitations of each individual method and enhanced the practical relevance of selected priority items [19].

Lastly, the survey demonstrated strong representativeness. Considering that approximately 80 nursing officers were working in primary military clinics nationwide, the 47 valid responses represent about 59% of the entire population. This unusually high participation rate enhances the credibility and generalisability of the findings.

Educational priorities and practical implications

We identified 79 first aid tasks and prioritized them using IPA and Borich needs assessment. High-priority domains consistently included life-threatening conditions such as brain injury, shock, crush injury, blast and penetrating injuries, chest injury, abdominal/back injury, airway haemorrhage, inhalation burn, suicidal ideation and self-harm, and drowning. These tasks showed high importance but low performance, indicating critical gaps in preparedness and the need for targeted education focused on high-acuity trauma care.

The combination of early and appropriate interventions and timely evacuation to higher-level facilities is strongly associated with improved survival and reduced long-term complications. For instance, severe traumatic brain injury has been linked to a nearly 50% reduction in mortality and improved quality of life [20]. Prompt haemorrhage control and resuscitation in shock are critical, as death may occur within 2 hours of onset [21]. Likewise, early fluid resuscitation in crush injuries significantly reduces the risk of acute kidney injury, shock, and permanent disability [22, 23]. Although haemostasis and fluid resuscitation are prioritised in managing blast and penetrating injuries, several additional considerations should be documented or assessed. These include physical examination findings (e.g., fracture stabilization, wound contamination), precise location and severity of vascular injury, time and method of tourniquet application, type and amount of fluids or blood products administered, and use of haemostatic adjuncts [9, 24, 25].

For traumatic injuries, both immediate first aid and timely transfer to higher-level facilities are decisive for survival. To support this, the Armed Forces Medical Command operates an integrated one-stop system that provides real-time guidance on first aid, determines optimal transport routes and referral hospitals, and offers continuous support until hospital arrival [26]. Given that more than 70% of South Korea’s terrain is mountainous and many military units are geographically distant from civilian hospitals, helicopter evacuation plays a critical role. FGI participants further emphasised the need for specific guidelines and simulation-based training on communication with the control tower, ambulance equipment checks, and air evacuation procedures. Tasks such as verifying ambulance power supply, operating consoles and panels, handling stretchers, adjusting lighting at helicopter landing zones, and ensuring safe approaches are unfamiliar and complex for nursing officers, potentially delaying timely evacuation. Therefore, nursing officer education should integrate evacuation drills alongside direct first aid training to ensure comprehensive preparedness.

Secondary data analysis showed that emergency room visits related to mental and behavioural disorders accounted for only 4,861 cases (0.5% of the total) and intentional self-harm for just 92 cases (0.01% of the total); the low frequency may have limited nursing officers’ opportunities to manage such cases, resulting in low confidence. This suggests that even low-incidence but high-risk psychiatric emergencies can create significant educational demand, particularly within the collective living environment and psychological stressors of military settings [27]. Simulation-based training is particularly important for low-incidence but high-risk conditions, such as psychiatric emergencies and airway haemorrhage, as it enables repeated exposure and skill development without reliance on real-world case occurrence.

Meanwhile, the management of emergency situations—such as evacuation preparation, emergency medication administration, and use of first aid equipment—showed relatively lower priority compared with direct trauma care but was emphasised during the interviews, confirming its significance. Studies involving school health teachers and public health nurses also highlight the importance of patient assessment, haemorrhage control, communication during emergencies, and equipment use [28–32]. Emergency management components should be incorporated into future training programmes, with a focus on key tasks for each high-priority injury. This would enable nursing officers to develop integrated competencies combining clinical first aid and system-level management, enhancing comprehensiveness and practical relevance.

Limitations and recommendation for further research

This study has some limitations. First, although the survey achieved strong representativeness among nursing officers in primary military clinics, the findings may not be generalizable to officers in higher-level or specialized institutions. Second, performance was assessed using self-reported measures, which may have been influenced by subjective interpretation or social desirability bias. Third, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to capture how educational needs evolve over time or across different operational contexts. Finally, certain tasks such as blood transfusion and death management were excluded based on FGI findings reflecting current practice in primary military clinics, which may limit comparability with civilian or other military settings.

Future research should analyse educational needs by subdomains of specific injury types—such as medication use, equipment management, evacuation procedures, and initial assessment—to provide a more multidimensional basis for curriculum design. Additionally, intervention studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of training programmes developed based on the identified priorities. Qualitative follow-up research should further explore contextual barriers and facilitators to applying first aid competencies in real-world military practice.

Conclusions

This study systematically identified and prioritised educational needs related to first aid competencies among nursing officers in primary military clinics. We derived a comprehensive list of 79 first aid tasks and determined their educational priorities through IPA and Borich needs assessment. The findings highlight that nursing officers require strengthened competencies particularly in managing life-threatening trauma, military-specific diseases and injuries, and emergency management. These results provide evidence for the development of a standardised, military-contextualised continuing education programme that strengthens both clinical first aid capabilities and nursing officers’ ability to maintain unit-level health resilience in combat and collective living environments.
