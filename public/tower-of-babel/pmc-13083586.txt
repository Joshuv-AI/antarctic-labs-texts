Assessment of Food Safety Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices Among Fresh Market Vendors and Consumers in Dhaka City, Bangladesh: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Md. Ruhul Amin, Md. Mahbub Alam, Sneha Sarwar, Md. Jarif Mahbub, Afsana Habib Jui, G. M. Reza Sumon, Rudaba Khondker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsFood safety is a major public health concern in Bangladesh, particularly in urban areas where fresh markets are the primary source of food. The objective of the study was to examine food safety Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) among fresh market vendors and consumers in Dhaka, Bangladesh.MethodsCross‐sectional data were collected from 406 respondents (205 vendors and 201 consumers) from 2 fresh markets in Dhaka city in 2022. Data were collected using a pretested interview‐based questionnaire, and composite KAP scores were calculated.ResultsThe study found that vendors had inadequate food safety knowledge, a mean score of 12.4 ± 2.6 (77.4% correct responses), and a good level of attitude, 9.7 ± 1.5 (88% correct responses). However, food safety practices were inadequate (5.2 ± 1.1; 57.5% correct responses). Overall, the KAP score was inadequate (27.2 ± 4.0; 75.7% correct response) and positively associated with education and prior food safety knowledge. On the contrary, consumers demonstrated good knowledge (13.5 ± 1.9; 84% correct responses). Consumers' knowledge was also significantly associated with their educational status. Vendors' and consumers' risk perception and risk management were significantly associated with food safety knowledge. About 25.9% of the vendors and 41.8% of the consumers believed that the fresh markets were not regularly inspected. Both vendors (42.9%) and consumers (86.6%) emphasized the importance of regular inspections in the markets.ConclusionThe findings indicate critical gaps between knowledge, attitude, and actual practices of food safety among vendors, alongside limited trust in market inspection systems. Strengthening food safety requires routine inspections, targeted vendor training, consumer awareness campaigns, and coordinated efforts among authorities and communities.

Introduction

Food safety is a science‐based practice aimed at safeguarding food and human health by preventing exposure to harmful substances in the food supply [1]. It encompasses three key dimensions: food safety knowledge, food safety attitude, and food safety practice [2]. Food safety knowledge relates to the understanding and awareness of food‐related information obtained through education, training, or experience. Food safety attitude reflects an individual's perception, beliefs, and convictions concerning food safety. Food safety practice involves the practical application of food safety knowledge and attitudes in daily routines [3] emphasizing proper hygiene practices during food preparation, processing, packaging, transportation, and storage [4]. Together, these three dimensions form a comprehensive framework for promoting food safety at all levels of the food system [5].

The global interconnection between food safety, nutrition, and food security is undeniable. For instance, nearly 1 in 10 people worldwide (approximately 600 million individuals) suffer from illnesses caused by consuming contaminated food, leading to 420,000 deaths and a loss of 33 million healthy life years annually [6]. The impact is particularly severe in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including Bangladesh, which face annual economic losses of US$110 billion [7]. In Bangladesh, the prevalence of foodborne diseases is alarmingly high, primarily due to overpopulation, lack of infrastructure, and inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions [8]. Among these diseases, diarrheal diseases, enteric fever, and hepatitis are the most prominent in terms of their prevalence and impact [9]. Recent estimates indicate that approximately 30 million people in Bangladesh are affected by foodborne illnesses annually [10]. The burden of diarrheal diseases was particularly noteworthy in 2018, with approximately 1,122,681 reported cases of children at various healthcare facilities throughout the country [11]. These distressing figures underscore the urgent need for comprehensive food safety measures and targeted interventions to protect the health and well‐being of the Bangladeshi population.

Fresh markets, also known as kancha bazar, are traditional open‐air retail venues where small‐scale vendors sell perishable food items. Commonly referred to as “wet” or “fresh” markets, they play a pivotal role in ensuring access to food for lower‐income consumers [12, 13]. Fresh market products such as fruits and vegetables are increasingly integral to diets worldwide, with a growing emphasis on raw consumption, highlighting the critical importance of food safety in this setting [14]. However, these products often harbor biological, chemical, and physical hazards that pose significant risks to public health [15]. For instance, contaminants such as harmful bacteria, viruses, parasites, or chemical substances can cause over 200 diseases, ranging from gastrointestinal illnesses to severe conditions like cancer [6]. In Bangladesh, fresh markets are particularly popular as they offer fresh goods and a variety of produce at affordable prices. Almost 95% of the poor and 85% of the overall population in Dhaka purchase their food from fresh markets [16]. However, these markets present a significant risk of food contamination due to their inadequate infrastructure, including a lack of refrigeration and limited sanitation facilities, coupled with overcrowding and inconsistent food safety practices [17]. Empowering and supporting these vendors in these markets can enhance food safety practices, benefiting both consumers and the overall food supply chain.

Evidence assessing food safety knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) among consumers in Bangladesh is limited, though they are an important stakeholder in preventing foodborne illness [18]. While studies indicate that university students in Bangladesh possess satisfactory knowledge, but exhibit poor food safety handling practices [19, 20]. Similarly, rural Bangladeshis have satisfactory food safety knowledge, but fail to apply it effectively in practice [21]. Additionally, people in coastal areas of Bangladesh (79.6%) are not always aware of food safety issues when purchasing street‐vended foods [22]. Beyond Bangladesh, studies from Asia and Africa show that some consumers have good knowledge of food handling practices, but there is generally a lack of understanding of facility requirements for food processing, storage, and disposal [23, 24]. Moreover, an umbrella review indicated that consumers generally possess higher levels of food safety knowledge, whereas vendors often demonstrate inconsistent practices due to limited education and resources. The review emphasized the need for further research in underrepresented regions and on the dynamics between consumers and vendors [25]. In Bangladesh, several studies have focused on food safety KAP among specific types of food vendors and handlers in Bangladesh, such as those dealing with meat [26, 27], fish, restaurant [28], hospital [29], and street food [30, 31, 32]. However, there is little evidence regarding food safety KAP among fresh market vendors and consumers in urban areas of Bangladesh. Moreover, the Bangladesh Food Safety Authority (BFSA) collaborates with local authorities to oversee food handling and marketing in urban markets, and insights on vendors' and consumers' KAP can inform its efforts to enhance food safety and consumer protection [33]. This study aimed to fill the gap by examining the level of food safety KAP of fresh market vendors and consumers in Dhaka, Bangladesh, and to identify the factors associated with overall KAP.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

The study employed a cross‐sectional survey design to comprehensively assess the food safety KAP of both fresh market food vendors and consumers. It was conducted in two selected fresh markets: Mohammadpur Town Hall Market, located in Mohammadpur, Dhaka, and Mohakhali Fresh Market, situated in Mohakhali, Dhaka. Both were traditional urban retail venues where small‐scale vendors sell perishable food items. These markets were chosen to provide a diverse representation of the urban fresh market scenario in the capital city of Bangladesh. The study was carried out between November and December 2022.

Sample Size Estimation

The sample size for this cross‐sectional study was determined using purposive sampling techniques. The survey encompassed two distinct sampling segments: fresh market vendors and consumers. To ensure comprehensive coverage, all traders actively participating in the markets and all consumers frequenting these markets were targeted to participate in the survey. The sample size calculation followed a well‐established statistical formula, taking into consideration the desired level of precision and confidence. The formula used for sample size estimation was [34]: n=Z1−α22×P(P−1)d2 where P represents the estimated proportion of the indicator of interest (assumed to be 50% for respondents lacking adequate knowledge of food safety, since there was a lack of studies); Z1−α2 denotes the standard normal value corresponding to a 5% level of significance (1.96), and d signifies the desired level of error (5% or 0.05). Accounting for a 5% increment to accommodate potential nonresponse, the calculated sample size amounted to 404 respondents. However, data were collected from a total of 406 respondents from the two selected fresh markets, each with a focus on both vendors and consumers. Vendors were selected proportionately based on the size of the markets. From the Mohammadpur Town Hall Fresh Market, 145 vendors (a comparatively big fresh market), and from the Mohakhali Fresh Market, 60 vendors were selected for the survey. For the consumer survey, 101 consumers from Mohammadpur Town Hall Fresh Market and 100 consumers from the Mohakhali Fresh Market were interviewed.

Questionnaire Construction and Data Collection

This study used a KAP model (a structured questionnaire for the target population) to quantify and analyze KAP on food safety [28, 35, 36]. Structured questionnaires were designed as the primary data collection tool for both food vendors and consumers. These questionnaires were developed to elicit specific information related to food safety KAP. The vendors' survey questionnaire was divided into five sections, including demographic characteristics (8 questions), food safety knowledge (16 questions), attitudes toward safe food (11 questions), food safety practices (9 questions), and risk perception and management (5 questions). The vendors' food safety knowledge questionnaire was further divided into three subsections (personal hygiene, cross‐contamination, and food‐borne diseases). The consumers' survey questionnaire was also divided into four sections, including demographic characteristics (8 questions), food safety KAP (16 questions), perception of the source of contamination (multiple response questions), and risk perception and management (5 questions). The consumers' food safety KAP questionnaire was further divided into four subsections (consumers' food safety awareness/attitudes, knowledge of food safety management, knowledge of safe food and food poisoning, and personal hygiene/practice). These questionnaires were drawn upon food safety knowledge and practices from various global sources [28, 35], to precisely capture the desired information while keeping it as brief as possible. The goal was to gather feedback from a small but representative group of potential respondents. When designing the questionnaire, the main focus was on ensuring readability and comprehensibility, conciseness, ease of completion, and the ability to measure food safety perceptions specifically.

The questionnaire was pretested with a small sample of vendors and consumers to ensure clarity. The reliability coefficient of the questionnaire test (Cronbach's alpha, α) value for the vendors' survey questionnaire and the consumers' survey questionnaire were 0.797 and 0.712, respectively. Both scores were higher than the reference value (α > 0.7), which indicated the reliability and validity of the questionnaire [37]. Vendors and consumers were interviewed in person during regular business hours without prior notice. Prior to administering the questionnaire, the objectives of the study were explained to both the vendors and the consumers, and they were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. The questionnaire was presented in their native language and filled out through individual interviews. Each respondent was provided with ample time (20 min) to answer the questions.

Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice Score Calculation

For each of the knowledge statements, vendors who replied “True” were given a score of 1; otherwise, 0. Similarly, for the attitude statements, vendors who agreed with the statements were given a score of 1; otherwise, 0. For practice questions, vendors who answered “Yes” were given a score of 1; otherwise, they were given 0. Summing up the scores of individual sections, vendors' KAP scores were calculated. Each section differed in the number of statements and questions, so the scores were converted into percent scores for better comparison. All the scores were aggregated to create a composite KAP score. This score was then converted to a percentage score. To address the potential overestimation of KAP scores in the interview‐based, self‐reported questionnaire, we used higher cutoff points based on Bloom's classification system for more accurate categorization. Using Bloom's cut‐off points, the KAP score was classified into two categories. Vendors having KAP scores of more than 80% were categorized as “Good,” and the rest of them were “Inadequate” [38].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0. Data were scrutinized in relation to the objective of the study. Checking of inconsistencies, anomalies, missing values, and outliers (i.e., data cleaning) was done. The normality of the KAP scores was checked through the Shapiro–Wilk test and found non‐normal distributions (p < 0.05). Both descriptive and inferential analyses were performed. Descriptive statistics were used to identify the pattern and assess the consistency of the responses in each of the variables, with results presented as frequency and percentage distribution. To examine the association of the factors associated with the KAP score, the χ 2 test (for categorical variables) and Spearman's ρ test (for continuous variables) were performed. Furthermore, a bivariate logistic regression was conducted to see the magnitude of the effects of those factors that showed a significant association with the KAP score in the χ 2 test. For p values below 0.05 were considered significant [39].

Ethical Approval

All necessary ethical approvals were obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Faculty of Biological Sciences, University of Dhaka (Ref. No. 243/Biol.Scs). All interviewers received standardized training in neutral questioning techniques. Informed participation consent was obtained from all participants upon agreeing to participate in the study. All research participants' data were kept confidential, and access to the study data was restricted to the research team members.

Results

Vendors' Survey

Sociodemographic Characteristics

Among the 205 vendors interviewed, 60 were from Mohakhali Fresh Market, and 145 were from Mohammadpur Town Hall Market (Table 1). The majority were aged 25–35 years (31.7%) and were married (89.8%). About 73% of the vendors had been doing business for more than 10 years. The ownership of the marketplace was mostly rent‐based (71.2%). Overall, 38% of vendors had a secondary level of education, while a quarter (24.4%) of the vendors had no formal education. Only 17.1% of vendors reported having prior food safety training from any organization. About 52% of the vendors said they had no knowledge of food safety.

In terms of personal hygiene, most of the vendors replied positively to the statements about the essentiality of washing hands after using toilets (100%), handling garbage (96.1%), and raw meats (93.2%). However, only 53.7% of vendors were positive about washing their hands before preparing food and wearing gloves before eating ready‐to‐eat food (52.7%).

Attitude Toward Food Safety

A positive attitude is necessary to initiate desired behavior. On average, more than 80% of the vendors had a positive attitude (agreed with statements) toward food safety (including taking responsibility for food safety, gathering proper knowledge, avoiding work during sickness, participating in related training, etc.) However, only 68.3% of the vendors were motivated to use protective equipment at work (Table 3).

Vendors' Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) Score

Based on the correct responses, the vendors' KAP score was calculated (Table S1). On average, vendors were able to correctly answer 12.4 statements (77.4%) out of a total of 16 knowledge‐related statements. The attitude score was 9.7 (88%) out of 11 statements; for practice, it was 5.2 (57.5%) out of 9 questions. Adding all those scores, a composite KAP score was calculated. It was found that vendors' overall KAP score was 27.2 (75.7%) out of 36.

The percentage scores based on the responses were also given in Table S1. Vendors had the highest attitude score (88%), followed by knowledge score (77.4%) and practice score (57.5%). Ultimately, the overall KAP score stands at 75.7%, which means a vendor was, on average, 75.7% correct regarding KAP regarding food safety based on established food safety guidelines or standards recognized in the field.

Factors Associated With the Vendor's KAP Score

Vendors' overall KAP scores are significantly associated with their educational status and food safety knowledge (self‐reported) (Table S2). It is found that vendors having higher secondary levels of education had a better level of KAP scores than the vendors having secondary education (70.8% vs. 46.2%; χ 2 = 8.1, p = 0.044). The vendors who had prior food safety knowledge (self‐reported) had a better level of KAP score than those who had not (56.1% vs. 35.5%; χ 2 = 7.95, p = 0.005). It is also found that the vendors who rent the selling place had more “Good KAP” scores than the vendors who really own their places (49.3% vs. 35.6%; χ 2 = 3.19, p = 0.074). No significant association was found between the KAP score and vendors' age, marital status, years of business, and their types (p > 0.05).

In Table 5, the bivariate logistic regression model showed that vendors with higher secondary or more education were four times more likely to have good KAP scores than others (COR = 4.0, 95% CI: 1.4, 11.3; p = 0.01). Vendors with food safety knowledge had 2.3 times more odds of having a good KAP score than those who did not (COR = 2.3, 95% CI: 1.3, 4.1; p = 0.003).

Risk Perception and Management on Food Safety

About 70% of the vendors perceived that microorganisms, chemicals, and poor food handling practices are the risk factors for disrupted food safety systems (Table S3). On the other hand, about 70% of the vendors reported getting regularly inspected. However, only about 43% of vendors considered this act necessary. On average, a vendor responded correctly to 3.4 (67.0%) risk perception and management statements out of 5 based on established food safety guidelines or standards recognized in the field.

The correlation between the risk perception and risk management score and the vendor's KAP is given in Table S4. It was found that the risk perception and risk management scores were significantly correlated with the vendor's knowledge (rs = 0.58, p < 0.001) and attitude (rs = 0.425, p < 0.001). However, the practice had no significant correlation with the risk perception and management score.

Consumer Survey

Basic Characteristics of Consumers

A total of 201 consumers were interviewed from the 2 markets during the data collection period: 100 from Mohakhali Fresh Market and 101 from Mohammadpur Town Hall Market (Table 6). Among them, 66.2% were male, and 33.8% were female consumers. The majority (39.3%) of the consumers were between 25 and 35 years old. About 30.8% of consumers had a master's or higher‐level education. Most of the consumers (89.1%) purchased food produced for their own use or family consumption. About 30.3% of consumers purchase from these markets daily. Among them, 41.8% of the consumers had been shopping from those markets for more than 10 years.

Consumers' Perception of Sources of Contamination

When the consumers were asked about the source of contamination, about two‐thirds of the consumers (69.2%) reported that dirty and uncleaned market environments are the main source of food contamination (Table S5). The second highest source of food contamination is unhygienic and poor handling (66.2%). Chemicals (e.g., pesticides and heavy metals) were also considered responsible sources of food contamination by the consumers (64.2%), along with pests (63.7%).

Consumers' Food Safety KAP

When consumers were asked about food safety, 92.5% agreed that their health could easily be at risk because of unsafe food produce, and they paid attention to market hygiene (Table 7). More than 60% agreed that unsafe food produce is common in the domestic market food system, and there is a safety concern about fresh produce (fruits and vegetables) in that market. About 35% of the consumers believed that city corporations and the government do not regularly inspect the food supply chain. On the other hand, about 40% of them said that the Dhaka City Corporation does not regulate the safety of the food produce in these markets. More than 85% of consumers agreed with the statement related to knowledge of food safety and food poisoning that “food‐borne diseases were transmitted by handlers and solid waste disposals.” About 99% agreed that diseases are transmitted through food contamination. More than 95% of consumers agreed with statements related to personal hygiene, such as food contamination, health risks, hygiene, and separate toilet facilities for women. More than 90% agreed that “smoking should not be allowed in the market.” Out of 16 food safety KAP statements, a consumer correctly responded to 13.5 (84.2%) statements on average based on established food safety guidelines or standards recognized in the field.

Risk Perception and Risk Management

Most of the consumers (more than 85%) agreed with the statements related to the risk perception that the contamination of food produce can occur by microorganisms, pesticides, and poor food handling and hygiene practices (Table S7). When they were asked about the risk management of the market conditions, only 31.8% of consumers agreed that the market is regularly inspected, while the majority (86.6%) felt that there is a need for regular food safety risk assessment in the market. On average, a consumer responded correctly to 3.4 (67.0%) risk perception and management statements out of 5. Like the vendors, consumers' risk perception and risk management are significantly correlated with their food safety KAP (rs: 0.384, p < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study yielded insightful findings concerning vendors' KAP scores regarding food safety, along with their associated factors and perceptions of risk management. Additionally, the study reported consumers' knowledge of food safety, risk perceptions, and the determinants of their knowledge regarding food safety.

The KAP scores revealed that vendors had an inadequate level of knowledge of food safety but a positive attitude toward safe practices. However, the study findings highlighted that the level of food safety knowledge and positive attitude of the vendors were not translated into good practice. Supporting our findings, a study discovered that 72.5% of food vendors possessed an understanding of food safety principles, but the translation of this knowledge into practical actions was observed in only 0.5% of the food handlers [32]. Factors such as lack of education and training in food safety have been identified as key contributors to this challenge [40]. The overall findings about food safety knowledge, attitude, and risk perception among vendors were parallel with previous findings. Two recent Bangladeshi studies on various food handlers, like fish farmers, restaurant workers, and home food handlers, exhibited similar levels of food safety knowledge among them [28, 41]. Additionally, an umbrella review focusing on all regions identified poor practice, while having a positive attitude may be due to a lack of knowledge and resources [25]. However, food safety knowledge among the vendors was found to be poor among the street food vendors [27, 31]. Moreover, structural constraints such as limited infrastructure, inadequate sanitation facilities, economic pressures, and weak regulatory enforcement may hinder vendors from translating knowledge and attitude into practice.

The study found that food safety KAP scores were influenced by multifaceted factors. Higher levels of education and prior exposure to food safety knowledge were positively linked to elevated KAP scores. Similar findings in earlier literature also highlighted educational background, engagement in training, and desirable professional experiences as crucial factors in exercising food safety behavior [40, 42]. Additionally, a systematic review and meta‐analysis of LMICs also highlighted the protective effect of education and food safety training on the proper food safety practice of vendors [43]. Our study reported that around half of the vendors were either illiterate or primarily literate. Due to their low education levels, they may be unaware of the effects of unsafe foods on health and the economic burden of food‐borne illnesses [40]. Thus, recurrent food safety education sessions should be provided in workspaces with multiple approaches to improve vendors' understanding of food safety.

Despite possessing relatively good food safety knowledge and a positive attitude, the vendors' actual practices were inadequate. Earlier findings also suggest that the triad of food safety knowledge, attitude, and practice is not necessarily correlated [25, 28, 44]. The lack of adherence to food safety practices, even with sound knowledge, may stem from factors such as scarcity of time, staff, and logistic facilities [45]. Additional findings suggest that overconfidence among managers and owners who perceive their food operation units as low‐risk is also a factor for low compliance with the practices [46]. Continuous motivation and support from management might encourage vendors to implement food safety practices [28]. Training and repeated educational sessions should be provided to change behavior among vendors. Translating food safety knowledge into consistent practice requires a multifaceted approach. While education provides the foundation, practical implementation necessitates hands‐on training, clear action plans, ongoing support, integration into daily routines, and a supportive community environment. For example, short‐term, interactive vendor training sessions could focus on daily food safety practices, such as proper hygiene and food handling. Several studies underscored that recurrent emphasis on food safety practices through training and refresher courses not only had a desirable impact on food safety practices but also minimized the transmission of food‐borne diseases among vendors [28, 46].

Not only vendors but also consumers have a major role to play in maintaining food safety within the market premises. Proper food safety knowledge among consumers has the potential to minimize pathogenic attacks on food [47]. In contrast to the vendors, a good level of food safety knowledge and risk perception prevailed among consumers. This average score was comparable to previous studies [47, 48]. Consumers' level of KAP was also found to be associated with their educational qualifications in our study. The relationship between consumer education and their food safety knowledge was reported in earlier studies [47]. Predictably, due to pre‐existing knowledge, educated consumers have increased knowledge of food safety and improved awareness of foodborne disease risks [47].

Implications of the Research

Food safety risk perception and management are critical in ensuring food safety. For better implementation of food safety systems within markets and risk mitigation, the necessity of regular inspections was emphasized by both vendors and consumers. Though concerns have been raised about the inconsistency and insufficiency of food safety inspection methods, they represent a vital health protection strategy implemented by governments to mitigate the risk of foodborne illnesses [49]. Random and regular market inspections should be undertaken to monitor the food safety conditions of fresh markets. The use of food handler certification can improve food safety risk management in markets [50]. Moreover, previous Bangladeshi studies revealed that even with sufficient laws regarding food safety, poor implementation and minimal compliance result in a poor food safety situation throughout the country [40]. The BFSA can play a pivotal role in establishing food safety through appropriate regulatory actions. Recently, this governing body has started grading restaurants based on their food safety practices [51]. This activity should be extended to wet markets as well.

Strengths and Limitations

The study had several strengths. Unlike previous research, this study focused on diverse groups of existing vendors, providing a comprehensive picture of the fresh market food safety conditions in Dhaka City. Furthermore, the study incorporated not only the perceptions of vendors but also those of regular consumers of the fresh market. However, the study also has its limitations. The relatively small sample size with a purposive sampling design may limit the generalizability of the results. Further, subgroup analysis (market‐level difference) or adjustment was not feasible due to the limited sample size. Additionally, a cross‐sectional study cannot establish a causal relationship behind poor food safety practices. Moreover, relying on self‐reported data obtained through interview‐based questionnaires introduces potential biases, including response bias and social desirability biases, which may overestimate the KAP scores and compromise the accuracy and reliability of the findings. There is also a need for future studies using longitudinal or intervention‐based designs to assess the impact of inspection frequency on food safety behavior. Future research should consider alternative data collection methods for deeper learning or implement strategies to mitigate bias and enhance the validity of the results [52].

Conclusion

Our study found that vendors displayed a positive attitude but had limited yet somewhat promising knowledge of food safety, which they seldom put into practice. Consumers also demonstrated significant knowledge of food safety, which is associated with their educational qualifications. Both vendors and consumers stress the need for regular inspections in fresh markets to reduce risks. Ensuring regular food safety practices requires a multifaceted approach, including practical training, clear action plans, and community support. Government authorities should conduct routine market inspections and establish a clear monitoring mechanism to ensure compliance with food safety regulations. Targeted training programs for vendors should focus on practical, hands‐on food handling techniques. Consumer awareness campaigns can further strengthen community participation in maintaining safe food environments. Additionally, collaboration among local governments, market committees, and public health agencies is essential to ensure sustainable improvements in food safety practices.
