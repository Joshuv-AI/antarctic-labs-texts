Emerging biomodels to understand the pathophysiology of sepsis and evaluate new therapeutic strategies targeting extracellular histones
Authors: José Luis García-Gimenez, Marta Seco Cervera, Irene Cánovas-Cervera, Ana Belén Agúndez, Enric Dolz-Andrés, Gonzalo Mondaray-Muñoz, Rosa Calpe-Fortea, Elena Nacher-Sendra, Rebeca Osca-Verdegal, José Luis Aparicio-Collado, Jesús Beltrán-García, Antonio Sierra-Rivera, Lorena Peiró-Chova, Gloria Gallego-Ferrer, José Santiago Ibañez-Cabellos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracellular histones (extH) have emerged as key damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) driving multi-organ failure in sepsis. Despite their correlation with disease severity, organ-specific mechanisms of extH toxicity and targeted therapeutic strategies remain underexplored.Main bodyThis review dissects extH-mediated pathophysiology across vital organs including heart, lungs, kidneys, liver, and brain, through convergent pathways including TLR activation, NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, NETosis, oxidative stress, calcium influx, pyroptosis, and microvascular thrombosis. Conventional 2D cell cultures fail to recapitulate tissue architecture, multicellular interactions, and hemodynamic forces, while rodent models exhibit poor clinical translatability due to specific immune responses and physiology. Moreover, conventional 2D models and animal models do not usually cover the heterogenicity we can find in sepsis. In contrast, advanced human-relevant 3D biomodels offer transformative advantages: organoids faithfully recreate organ-specific cellular heterogeneity and developmental gradients; 3D-bioprinted biomodels provide precise spatial control of immune-endothelial-stromal interactions within biomimetic matrices; organ-on-chip platforms integrate physiological shear stress, dynamic flow, oxygen gradients, and real-time inter-organ communication, enabling study of extH-driven neutrophil adhesion, platelet aggregation, barrier dysfunction, and cytokine storms under clinically relevant conditions.ConclusionNext-generation 3D biomodels overcome traditional translational barriers, facilitating the comprehension of the pathophysiological mechanisms occurring in tissues during sepsis. Moreover, these advanced biomodels enable high-throughput screening of extH-neutralizing agents (e.g., heparinoids, anti-histone antibodies) and hemoperfusion technologies, thereby advancing precision intensive care medicine. By bridging mechanistic insights to clinical strategies that mitigate inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, thrombosis and long-term sequelae, these platforms promise transformative advances in sepsis management and intensive care outcomes.

Introduction

Extracellular histones (extH) are reshaping the landscape of biomedical research in critically ill conditions, particularly in sepsis. In recent years, a significant breakthrough has occurred, recognizing circulating histones as key mediators of tissue injury and organ failure [1,2]. During about last 15 years, research has demonstrated how extH can contribute to severity in critically ill patients by causing harmful host response and increasing hyperinflammation [3], driving immune cell recruitment and activation [4], impairing endothelial function which in turn promote endothelial cell death [5], and inducing platelets aggregation [6], thrombocytopenia [7] and coagulation hyperactivation [8].

Sources of extH include the production of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), the release by other cells which are damaged by innate immune cells responses, including macrophages, and endothelial cell death through apoptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis, which lead to disruption of the endothelial barrier, increased vascular permeability, and amplification of inflammation [2], thereby aggravating tissue injury and worsening disease severity.

At cellular level, recent research has focused on clarifying how extH activate deleterious and cytotoxic pathways [2]. However, to design new therapeutic strategies is noteworthy to completely understand how extH contribute to tissue damage and produce widespread organ injury.

In vitro cell culture models provide a controllable framework to investigate how different mediators identified during a sepsis episode (i.e. Damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), pathogen associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), reactive oxygen species (ROS) and cytokines, chemokines, etc.) perturb cellular and molecular pathways at multi-cellular level in specific cell types. By using primary immune cells (such as monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils) it is possible to evaluate the immune response regulation in sepsis. Other approaches using primary or immortalized cell lines for endothelial and epithelial populations also help to decipher high complex mechanisms and signaling pathways occurring after infection and during sepsis development. In those in vitro experiments, cells are typically exposed to bacterial products, including lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and gram-positive cell wall components, as well as bacterial lysates or septic patient serum, to reproduce the complex inflammatory milieu and key events observed in patients.

Moreover, researchers frequently use primary or immortalized cell lines to explore cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying pathological processes in target tissues which are seriously affected in a septic episode. The use of these cellular models has further helped to dissect pathogen–host interactions, and cytotoxic molecules to understand how cellular damage and disruption of signaling pathways occurs during sepsis, as well as to explore candidate therapies under controlled conditions. However, these in vitro models based mainly in monolayer 2D cell cultures only partially capture the complexity of the in vivo tissue organization and function and do not allow to explore organ cross-talk and systemic physiology of sepsis, so limiting the mechanistic studies and preclinical drug screening we need to advance in sepsis research.

In addition to this, it is noteworthy that not only cell-culture models but also traditional rodent models of sepsis fail to capture the human cell-to-cell type interactions, organ-specific complexity and patient heterogeneity of sepsis, so the design of advanced biomodels such as 3D bioprinted spheroids, Organ-on-a-Chip (OoC) and organoids may help to understand the intricate pathophysiology of sepsis and to look for new promising therapeutic approaches.

This review connects evidence on extH as key drivers of multiorgan dysfunction in sepsis with emerging human-relevant 3D biomodels as tools to investigate how DAMPs, including extH, contribute to tissue injury and organ damage in key affected organs. By integrating organ-specific mechanistic insights with advanced platforms such as organoids, 3D-bioprinted tissues, and OoC systems, we outline a framework for studying systemic inflammatory responses in sepsis and supporting the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

The use of advanced biomodels to study the pathophysiology of critically-ill conditions

More sophisticated three-dimensional (3D) cultures consisting of organoids, 3D-spheroids and 3D-bioprinted tissue constructs, and OoC devices have revolutionized biomedical research by enabling in vitro models closely mimicking human tissue architecture and function [9,10]. Unlike 2D monolayer cultures, these 3D models support complex cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions, spatial organization, and physiological gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and signaling molecules, which profoundly influence cellular behavior. This creates realistic microenvironments featuring hypoxic cores and proliferation zones, essential for studying tissue responses under normal and pathological conditions [10].

Organoids

Organoids are multicellular 3D structures that can be derived from pluripotent or adult stem cells self-organized into miniature organ-like structures encompassing multiple specialized cell types and maintaining tissue-specific spatial arrangements. Organoids allow accurate modeling of organ physiology including the kidney, lung, gut, liver, and brain [9]. Notably, organoids also offer a realistic environment to dissect the dynamics of microbial invasion and the impact of cytokine-induced dysfunction [[11], [12], [13]]. Organoids and 3D spheroids offer physiologically relevant platforms not only for modeling infections but also for antiviral drug testing and detailed three-dimensional studies. This has been already reviewed by Ryu et al. showing how organoids are being actively used as infectious disease models (i.e. brain, lung, liver, intestine, kidney, skin and blood vessel) supporting both mechanistic and translational research [14]. Moreover, patient-derived or genetically engineered organoids enable modeling of hereditary disorders and inter-individual variability in responses to injury, effectively linking developmental biology with disease phenotypes [15]. One of the main limitations hindering the clinical translation of organoids is their incomplete maturation, as they often retain fetal-like characteristics and do not fully reproduce adult tissue functionality. This is compounded by limited cellular complexity, particularly the absence of immune, stromal, and vascular components, as well as suboptimal tissue architecture and microenvironmental control. Additionally, variability across protocols and reliance on artificial extracellular matrices further restrict reproducibility and physiological relevance. Lot of research is being made in order to standardize organoid maturation across different tissues by combining specific growth factors with ECM microenvironment engineering and also mechanical stimulation [[16], [17], [18]]. Temporally biochemical stimulation (e.g., TGF-β, BMPs) to promote terminal differentiation is combined with physical stimuli based on cyclic mechanical stretching and/or vascular fluid shear stress to enhance cellular polarization and metabolic activity resembling native tissue [[19], [20], [21]].

Organoid limitations are particularly critical in sepsis, a systemic condition driven by circulating mediators and dynamic immune-endothelial interactions under flow. The absence of vascular perfusion and systemic connectivity prevents organoids from accurately modeling key processes such as histone-mediated toxicity, coagulation, and multi-organ crosstalk.

3D bioprinting

Complementing the use of organoids, 3D bioprinting fabricates biomimetic hydrogels from bioinks with precise spatial organization of cells, growth factors, and biomaterials mimicking native ECM, closely recreating native tissue and organ architecture [22]. By enabling controlled construction of complex biological structures, 3D bioprinting has become a key tool in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, particularly for generating in vitro 3D models that better reproduce physiological conditions than conventional 2D cultures or animal models and thus improve studies of biological processes, disease mechanisms, and drug responses [23]. Bioinks and bioprinted hydrogels must meet requirements for biocompatibility, bioactivity, rheological properties, and mechanical stability, ensuring both printing and cell viability, proliferation, and differentiation after bioprinting. The appropriate selection and formulation of the bioink is therefore a determining factor for the success of 3D bioprinting and further applications [23]. The election of the bioink components must be tailored to each organ specifically to accurately represent its biochemical and mechanical properties. For instance, gelatin (collagen derived polymer) hyaluronic acid, fibrin and elastin are some of the main components of hepatic/renal ECM commonly used in different bioink formulations [[24], [25], [26]]. Depending on the selected biomaterials, different crosslinking methods (physical and chemical crosslinking) will result in entangled hydrogels with tuneable mechanical properties like native tissues. Moreover, the possibility of developing bioinks based on decellularized ECM from different tissues as more realistic and biocompatible bioinks represents a promising approach for in vitro tissue modelling, although more research is needed to establish standardized reliable protocols [24,27].

Organ-on-a-chip

An ultimate emerging approach named Organ-on-a-Chip (OoC) technology aims to use microfluidic systems to not only reproduce 3D native tissue microarchitecture but also provide controlled dynamic flow of nutrients and oxygen, as well as mechanical shear stress to simulate in vivo dynamic conditions across organs and tissues. These platforms are essential to study intra- and inter-organ communication in health and disease, enabling the dynamic assessment of complex pathophysiological events such as leukocyte rolling, NET formation, and barrier integrity under pathological conditions [28,29].

Although the translational potential of OoC technology to study several pathological conditions at a multi-organic scale is high, there is still a lack of reproducibility control and manufacturing standardization across different laboratories and institutions. The structural complexity of these systems comprising microfluidics, spatial organization and biomechanical stimuli evidence the need of strict quality control and large-scale manufacturing validation. Most of the current efforts are focused on developing multi-organ standardized ‵plug and play' platforms with materials that minimize non-specific biomolecule adsorption such as polystyrene or cyclic olefin copolymers [[30], [31], [32], [33]].

Advanced biomodels mimic human tissue structure and complexity, enabling diverse applications (Table 1). In this regard, their spatial organization accurately recapitulates receptor expression, immune signaling, stromal interactions, and mechanisms of inflammation-driven injury, fibrosis, epithelial barrier dysfunction, immune-mediated damage, and host-pathogen interactions. As described above, extH have emerged as key DAMPs driving tissue-specific cytotoxicity and inflammation across multiple organs in sepsis [3,39], ultimately fueling multiorgan failure through mechanisms involving Toll-like receptor activation (TLR2/4/9), NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, complement engagement, and direct phospholipid membrane disruption leading to calcium influx, pyroptosis and apoptosis. These effects, spanning endothelial/epithelial toxicity, oxidative stress, cytokine storm, NETosis amplification, and microvascular thrombosis, can manifest distinctly in heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, and brain. Extracellular histones induce endothelial dysfunction and thrombosis

Sepsis is characterized by increased endothelial damage, vascular permeability, inflammation, and the development of thrombosis [40]. Moreover, by activating platelets, stimulating thrombin production, and disrupting anticoagulant systems, extH drive coagulopathy and the formation of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [40,41]. Consequently, small blood vessels become obstructed with clots even as bleeding risk increases, which further impairs organ function. The vicious cycle formed by extH-induced endothelial injury, immune system activation, and disturbed coagulation intensifies sepsis-associated complications [42,43]. This vicious cycle also stimulates inflammasome pathways, promotes pyroptotic cell death, and augments vascular leakiness, all of which collectively amplify systemic inflammation throughout the organism and accelerate disease progression [44].

The vascular endothelium, with a cumulative surface area exceeding 1000 m2 in adult humans, constitutes a primary target of extH toxicity during sepsis [1]. Through convergent molecular mechanisms, extH disrupt multiple aspects of endothelial homeostasis, leading to profound vascular dysfunction that underpins the development of multi-organ failure [1].

Cui et al. showed that histone exposure (50 μg/mL) increases endothelial permeability in human umbilical cord endothelial cells (HUVEC) by disrupting junctional complexes, with VE-cadherin and the tight junction protein ZO-1 relocating from the membrane to the to the cytoplasm in a phosphorylation-dependent manner [45] (Table 2). This barrier failure is reinforced by findings from Felmming et al., who observed in human dermal microvascular endothelial cells that endothelial injury generates soluble vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin fragments that further amplify inflammation and endothelial barrier disruption. Importantly, Biswass et al. described how heparin pre-treatment prevents barrier disruption, highlighting that charge-dependent interactions with glycosaminoglycans in the glycocalyx and membrane Recent evidence confirms that histone-induced VE-cadherin redistribution and junctional disassembly are major mechanisms of endothelial barrier failure in sepsis and related inflammatory disorders [69]. This process is tightly coupled to oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling initiated by extH via TLR4-dependent activation of NADPH oxidases and cyclooxygenase enzymes [70], leading to ROS accumulation and NF-κB activation. As a result, adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and E-selectin [70,71] are upregulated, promoting leukocyte recruitment and transendothelial migration and sustaining the inflammatory milieu characteristic of sepsis [69].

In parallel, extH bind to negatively charged glycocalyx components, triggering inflammatory signaling and activation of sheddases that degrade proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans [72]. Loss of the glycocalyx exposes the endothelial surface to direct leukocyte and platelet interactions and removes an important reservoir of anti-inflammatory mediators and growth factors [73,74].

Beyond barrier disruption, extH also impair vascular tone by downregulating eNOS expression and activity, reducing NO bioavailability and vasodilation [70,75]. Simultaneously, extH increase COX-2 expression and shift arachidonic acid metabolism away from vasodilatory prostaglandins toward the production of the potent vasoconstrictor thromboxane A2 [70]. This imbalance contributes to the paradoxical vasomotor phenotype of sepsis, characterized by systemic vasodilation accompanied by microvascular vasoconstriction, regional hypoperfusion, and impaired oxygen delivery despite preserved or elevated cardiac output [76].

Extracellular histones and coagulation

ExtH contribute to a paradoxical disruption of hemostasis by aberrantly activating the coagulation cascade in parallel with inflammation, endothelial injury, and cell death pathways [[77], [78], [79]]. Pyroptosis-associated exposure of phosphatidylserine (PS) on the outer plasma membrane provides a catalytic surface for tissue factor–driven coagulation, while histones directly activate platelets via P2X7 receptors and promote von Willebrand factor (vWF) release from endothelial Weibel–Palade bodies (WPB) through TLR-dependent and glycosaminoglycan-mediated mechanisms [80,81]. In flow-chamber experiments, Lam et al. showed that doses between 10 and 230 μg/mL histones rapidly induce long, platelet-coated ULvWF strings on the endothelial surface in a dose-dependent manner, confirming potent vWF secretion and disturbance of hemostasis [58] (Table 2).

extH lead to thrombocytopenia and excessive thrombin generation by initiating red blood cell (RBC) aggregation, activating platelets and endothelial cells, amplifying coagulation pathways, and inhibiting fibrinolysis [[82], [83], [84], [85]]. This dysregulation depletes key anticoagulant and procoagulant proteins, with marked reductions in antithrombin, prothrombin, and platelet counts leading to prolonged coagulation times [86]. Consistently, Eustes et al. demonstrated that plasma from patients with SARS-CoV-2 infection enhances platelet integrin activation, PS exposure, platelet-dependent thrombin generation, and thrombus formation, further illustrating histone-driven platelet activation in vivo [87]. Collectively, these changes induce a profoundly prothrombotic state, with thrombin–antithrombin complexes rising 10–50-fold in severe sepsis with high extH levels, thereby promoting DIC, tissue ischemia, and complications in septic shock [77].

In erythrocytes, extH induce aggregation even at low concentrations, increasing blood viscosity, venous thrombosis, and microcirculatory impairment, and can also promote PS-rich microparticle release and tissue factor (TF) activation, extending coagulation beyond the initial injury site [77]. Eryptosis induced by histone-driven oxidative stress (with PS exposure, caspase activation, and altered intracellular Ca2+) confers a procoagulant RBC phenotype that favors thrombin generation. Associated hemolysis releases PS-positive erythrocyte microparticles, free hemoglobin, and heme, which are linked to hypercoagulability, vascular occlusion, complement activation, endothelial activation, and ROS, thereby intensifying thromboinflammation (Fig. 1A) [77,89].

Effect of histones on secondary hemostasis

In vitro, extH at 100 to 1000 μg/mL disrupt the endothelial barrier, exposing the procoagulant subendothelium to circulating blood and triggering the extrinsic coagulation pathway through tissue factor (TF)–driven activation of the FVIIa complex, which then activates FX and the common cascade [77,82,88] (Table 2). In parallel, extH enhance intravascular TF expression in endothelial cells and monocytes via TLR2/TLR4-mediated activation of NF-κB and AP-1 signaling, and then convert this cryptogenic TF into an active form by promoting PS exposure and protein disulphide isomerase activity [77]. In parallel, extH stimulate platelet polyP release, which in turn activates FXI (Fig. 1B), and activate factor VII-activating protease (FSAP), raising circulating FVIIa levels and amplifying the initial procoagulant signal. In addition, extH binding to prothrombin enhances its cleavage by FXa and allows histones to function as alternative cofactors of the prothrombinase complex, lowering the threshold for thrombin generation independently of phospholipids (Fig. 1B) [77]. This systemic coagulation activation in septic shock consumes platelets and coagulation factors and contributes to DIC [86].

Histones effects on fibrinolysis

ExtH bind fibrinogen and fibrin, producing thicker, more rigid fibrin macromolecular networks that are relatively resistant to tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA)–mediated fibrinolysis without directly inhibiting plasmin activity. Activated factor XIII (FXIIIa) further stabilizes these clots by covalently crosslinking histones into the fibrin network and promoting high-molecular-weight fibrin structures that retain red blood cells, while additional non-covalent histone–fibrin interactions competitively hinder plasmin access and enhance clot robustness [77,90]. Locke and Longstaff showed that disrupting FXIIIa activity or blocking histone binding with heparinoids reverses this protection, 0,5 μg/mL of Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) blocked the crosslinking induced by 60 μg/mL of histones, and a higher dose of 1 μg/mL LMWH inhibited the crosslinking caused by 120 μg/mL of histones, indicating that histones stabilize clots partly by serving as plasmin substrates and shielding fibrin from degradation, an effect dependent on their positive charge since citrullination, which reduces histone charge, weakens fibrin binding [90].

Advanced biomodels to explore endothelium damage in sepsis

Conventional 2D HUVEC monolayers have been useful for studying endothelial responses to extracellular histones (extH), but they do not reproduce key in vivo features such as three-dimensional architecture, hemodynamic forces, multicellular interactions, and extracellular matrix cues, which limits their translational relevance for sepsis [34,91].

In contrast, advanced 3D biomodels provide more physiologically relevant platforms to study histone-mediated endothelial injury. In this context, vascular organoids (VOs), also known as blood vessel organoids (BVOs) are of special interest in the comprehension of different sepsis-related pathologic mechanisms and injury responses. Recent advances in VOs development have enabled the study of inflammation and endothelial dysfunction across different tissues and organs of special interest in sepsis [92]. For example, cardiac VOs of iPSCs-derived endothelial cells and cardiomyocytes exposed to inflammatory conditions reported the release of angiogenic molecules such as CCL2/MCP-1 and endothelin [93], which have been both found involved in sepsis [94,95].

In BVOs, extH reduced survival significantly at 500 μg/mL, and importantly, histone toxicity depended on cellular entry through a clatrin-mediated endocytosis, suggesting that uptake of extH is a key step in histone-mediated injury. In BVOs, internalized histones colocalized with LC3B, supporting a link between extH uptake and non-canonical autophagy-related responses involved in sepsis-related vascular dysfunction [57].

Despite the progress made in vascular organoids (VOs), the field is still at an early stage, with limited evidence on how septic mediators affect these 3D systems. A major limitation is the lack of a functional perfusable vasculature, which reduces their physiological relevance in sepsis. key vascular functions still depend on hemodynamic forces such as shear stress and intravascular pressure, which are absent in static cultures. For this reason, combining vascularized organoids with microfluidic organoid-on-chip platforms is a promising strategy to better reproduce vascular biology, immune cell trafficking, inter-organ communication, and disease processes [[96], [97], [98]]. In this regard, Yadav et al. developed a perfusable 3D xeno vessel-chip by seeding miniature pig aortic endothelial cells in a microfluidic OrganoPlate®, where they formed a confluent, lumenized endothelial layer within collagen ECM and reproduced small-to-medium artery architecture. When human neutrophils were introduced into the vessel lumen, the porcine endothelium induced robust NET formation independently of antibodies and complement, causing NET-associated ROS-mediated endothelial damage. This model is therefore useful for studying NET-driven cytotoxicity in transplantation contexts [99].

Moreover, these platforms may allow the study of endothelial-immune interactions, chemotaxis, adhesion molecule regulation, and therapy-related molecular changes under controlled flow conditions in the context of sepsis. They also support the analysis of septic inflammation on endothelial integrity and neutrophil-endothelial interactions, as shown by Kiani et al. [100], and to assess vascular perfusion and organoid maturation in iPSC-derived vascular organoid-on-chip systems, as reported by Gridol et al. [98]. Therefore, testing extH in these perfusable VO platforms could provide valuable insight into the systemic vascular damage mediated by extH in sepsis.

Monocytes are primary targets of circulating histones, exhibiting marked morphological and functional changes upon exposure [2,60,61]. In whole blood, histone doses (50–200 μg/mL) produce time and dose-dependent increases in monocyte distribution width (MDW), with greater cell volume, granularity, and vacuolization, without changes in absolute monocyte counts [2,61] (Table 2). In healthy-donor blood, extH mixtures at 50, 100, or 200 μg/mL increased MDW over 30, 60, and 180 min (Fig. 2B) [61]. These in vitro MDW values approached those observed in sepsis, providing a mechanistic link between extH exposure and the monocyte anisocytosis observed in patients [60,61,101,102].

In terms of cytokines, after 3 h of extH exposure, multiplex profiling demonstrated dose-dependent increases in 23 of 24 biomarkers, with G-CSF showing the largest fold change (9 × at 50 μg/mL, 15 × at 100 μg/mL, and 27 × at 200 μg/mL relative to control) [60]. IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8/CXCL8, MIP-1β/CCL4, and TNF-α increased markedly, supporting their involvement in cytokine-storm pathophysiology [60] and activation of Th17, Th1 and Treg cells. Network analysis revealed dense positive correlations among cytokines at 100 μg/mL of extH, consistent with self-amplifying inflammatory circuits in severe sepsis and COVID-19 [61,103]. Taken together, experimental and clinical data support a causal link between circulating histones and immune hyperactivation with elevated cytokines in sepsis [2,60,61,104,105].

Importantly, DAMPs are involved in NET release by interacting with TLRs signaling. For example, in liver ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, histones and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) released from apoptotic hepatocytes activate PAD4 via TLR4 and TLR9, inducing NETosis and sterile inflammation [106]. In parallel, NLRP3 inflammasome activation supports NET formation through PAD4-dependent membrane rupture and citrullination, and its inhibition reduces NET release and thrombosis in human and mouse neutrophils [107], highlighting MPO and neutrophil elastase as key NET scaffolds (Fig. 2B) [108]. Therefore, because extH activate NLRP3 inflammasome and apoptosis [56], and neutrophils express TLR4 and TLR9 [109], extH are likely to promote NETosis and pyroptosis, contributing to dysregulated innate immune responses.

Histones released from NETs act as potent signals that directly skew naïve CD4+ T-cell differentiation toward the Th17 lineage rather than simply being toxic debris. In vitro, NETs added to naïve T cells under Th17-polarizing conditions markedly increase IL-17 production; this effect is abolished by mCBS and restored by purified histone octamers, indicating that NET-derived histones are the active component [110]. At sub-cytotoxic concentrations, extH bind TLR2 on naïve CD4+ T cells and signal through MyD88, inducing RORγt, IRF4, IL23R, STAT3 phosphorylation, and selective expansion of IL-17+ and GM-CSF + Th17 cells without enhancing Th1 or Treg differentiation. In vivo, systemic histone administration after low-dose anti-CD3 stimulation similarly increases activated RORγt + IL-17+ CD4+ T cells in blood, and this Th17-biasing effect is conserved in human naïve CD4+ T cells [110]. Overall, this histone-TLR2-driven Th17 polarization creates a feed-forward loop in which neutrophil NETs amplify Th17 responses, which in turn promote ROS production, inflammation, and further NET formation (Fig. 2C) [111].

Advanced biomodels to explore immune response dysregulation in sepsis

It is noteworthy that immune cells require of the appropriate function of endothelial cells because they control when, where, and how leukocytes leave the blood, sense pathogens and cytokines, and maintain a non-leaky, non-thrombotic interface required for effective and targeted immunity [112].

Liu et al. reviewed the mechanisms by which activated neutrophils contribute to endothelial injury, including disruption of tight and adherens junctions and the formation of NETs. In parallel, endothelial cells exhibit organ-specific responses characterized by differential adhesion molecule expression, barrier dysfunction, and inflammatory signaling across lung, liver, kidney, and brain microvascular beds. The authors proposed an integrative strategy combining OoC microphysiological systems based on primary human endothelial and immune cells with multi-omics approaches, clinical phenotyping, and in silico modelling to define mechanistic sepsis endotypes and advance precision therapeutic strategies [28].

Co-culture of human endothelial monolayers with immune cells stimulates allogeneic T-cell proliferation and markedly augments PBMC responses and IL-2 production. This requires endothelial class II MHC expression, which is induced within 1–2 days by co-culture with T cells or by T-cell-derived IFN-γ, allowing endothelial cells to act as non-classical antigen-presenting cells. In turn, lymphocyte-derived cytokines such as IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-1 remodel endothelial morphology, up-regulate pro-adhesive and procoagulant properties, and increase leukocyte adhesion, illustrating bidirectional crosstalk that sustains and focuses the immune response in the vessel wall and adjacent tissues [113].

As described in section 3, OoC technologies can reconstruct perfused human microvascular networks with organ-specific endothelial cells and neutrophils, enabling dynamic assessment of leukocyte rolling, adhesion, transmigration, NET formation, glycocalyx damage, and barrier integrity under physiological shear. Adaptable to lung, kidney, or blood–brain barrier models with varied cytokine environments, they can quantify how DAMPs/PAMPs, mediators, or therapeutics impact junctional complexes, glycocalyx, and neutrophil–endothelial interactions in clinically relevant human setting. When integrated with transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, OoC assays facilitate dissection of intracellular signaling pathways (including PKCδ-, NF-κB-, and adhesion-dependent cascades) that connect immune activation to endothelial dysfunction, and provide a functional platform to evaluate targeted or repurposed therapies prior to clinical translation [28].

Extracellular histones induce myocardial dysfunction

Recent studies in patients and animal models have shown that high levels of extH are significantly associated with an increased risk of cardiac injury, the onset of left ventricular dysfunction, and de novo arrhythmias [7,114]. Intravenous administration of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled histones in wild-type mice revealed their rapid localization to the heart, observable as early as 30 min, indicating that extH accumulate early in heart [46] and impair in a dose-dependent manner, thereby promoting cardiac injury [115].

Furthermore, extH levels in plasma showed a strong correlation with classic biomarkers of cardiac damage, such as troponins [114]. In fact, circulating histones play a significant role in cardiovascular disease and cardiac injury more broadly [116], being released after adverse cardiovascular events such as ischemic heart disease, stroke, and coronary artery plaque rupture with intraluminal thrombosis [117,118].

Importantly, sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SIC) represents a serious yet often reversible cardiac impairment during sepsis, marked by diminished myocardial contractility, lowered ejection fraction, and chamber dilation [119,120]. Key drivers include widespread inflammation, ROS accumulation, impaired mitochondrial activity, and calcium dysregulation, culminating in cardiomyocyte injury and programmed cell death [121].

Calcium overload and contractile dysfunction mediated by extracellular histones in cellular models

Adequate calcium handling is essential for cardiomyocyte contractility. Extracellular histones rapidly disrupt this balance, inducing Ca2+ influx in mouse HL-1, rat ventricular, and human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, which raises diastolic Ca2+, broadens calcium transients, and impairs excitation–contraction coupling within minutes [46]. In adult rat cardiomyocytes, histone exposure also increases intracellular calcium and is associated with sinus bradycardia and ventricular bigeminy; in adult ventricular cardiomyocytes, extH mixtures at 0.5 to 100 μg/mL alter Ca2+ handling, whereas ≥500 μg/mL trigger overt apoptotic/necrotic injury [46] (Table 2).

At the cellular and molecular level, extH prolong Ca2+ decay time and reduce the frequency of calcium signals in human cardiomyocytes, changes that may be associated with bradycardia [47]. ExtH-induced calcium overload disrupts normal calcium cycling and activates PKCα, which translocates to the myofilaments and phosphorylates cardiac troponin I, reducing myofilament calcium sensitivity and systolic force generation [114]. Functionally, this causes negative inotropy, and PKCα inhibition largely restores force generation, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target. In parallel, reduced calcium rise in TLR2-or TLR4-deficient rat cardiomyocytes suggests that these receptors also contribute to Ca2+ overload, beyond the direct membrane effects of extH [47].

Mitochondrial and metabolic impairment contributing to cardiomyocyte cell death

The ability of extH to trigger ROS production in cardiomyocytes is well established, with dose- and time-dependent increases in cytosolic and mitochondrial oxidative stress. Increased ROS also oxidize calcium-handling proteins, raising cytosolic calcium and inhibiting sarcoplasmic endoplasmic reticulum-transporting ATPase (SERCA) [46,47] as well as inducing the mechanisms described above.

ExtH and HMGB-1 reduce viability in mouse HL-1, AC16 human cardiomyocytes, and iPSC-derived human cardiomyocytes via TLR2/TLR4 signaling, triggering inflammatory TNF-α/IL-1β/IL-6 cytokines release, ROS increase, mitochondrial membrane potential collapse, which in turn lead to ATP depletion, and cellular failure [46,47]. In this line, cardiomyocyte TLR2/TLR4 expression suggests receptor-mediated contributions, as their ligands induce IL-6/CXCL1 release and NF-κB-dependent contractile deficits [122,123].

Sepsis triggers cardiomyocyte death through multiple pathways, including apoptosis, necrosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, autophagy, and pyroptosis, exacerbating cardiac dysfunction in these terminally differentiated cells with limited regenerative capacity [124]. ExtH exhibit potent cytotoxicity across cardiomyocyte models. Sera from septic patients, containing >75 μg/mL histones, reduce murine HL-1 cardiomyocyte viability, while primary adult rat cardiomyocytes and human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs) showed dose-dependent toxicity from 20 μg/mL of extH mixtures [[47], [48], [49]]. Mechanistically, extH drive apoptosis and ferroptosis-like injury in HL-1, rat ventricular, and AC16 cardiomyocytes via Ca2+ overload and bioenergetic collapse, with H3 specifically promoting ferroptosis [[125], [126], [127]].

Three-dimensional biomodels of heart and their applications in sepsis research

The heart's high degree of specialization relies on a heterogeneous mixture of atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes, pericytes, fibroblasts, endothelial, smooth muscle, neuronal and immune cells, each with distinct subtypes [128]. The spatial organization and crosstalk among these cell populations, together with pacemaker-driven electrophysiological stimuli, coordinate chamber contraction and relaxation to sustain blood flow. Motivated by the need to reproduce this complex multicellular architecture and function, cardiac tissue bioengineering has advanced considerably in recent years. In this regard, ex vivo perfused mouse hearts have been used to study arrhythmias, biventricular dysfunction, stroke volume, and prolonged relaxation after histone challenge, all of which can be attenuated by histone-neutralizing antibodies [46,115]. Ex vivo Langendorff-perfused mouse hearts have been also used to confirm histone H1 as the predominant inducer of apoptosis and muscle damage by observing 40% LDH release at 5 μg/mL after 30 min of H1 injection [129].

As deduced, more complex models can help to understand the effect of extH on the heart function during sepsis, however there are not studies yet. A major limitation of engineered heart tissue (EHT) is the fetal-like phenotype of iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, prompting strategies to promote maturation of EHT [130]. Mechanical loading, electrical stimulation, and biochemical cues such as tri-iodo-L-thyronine has demonstrated to enhance structural organization, cell-to-cell coupling, cardiac marker expression, and metabolic and contractile maturation. Heart tissue-derived ECM hydrogels have been designed and obtained to further support differentiation and maturation, but their complex protein composition complicates reproduction of native mechanical properties and reduces synthesis efficiency and reproducibility [130]. Despite advances in bioprinting and the generation of individual myocardial components (i.e., vessels, valves, fibres, and chambers) EHT constructs remain small and integrating these elements into a fully functional, organ-scale tissue is still challenging [130]. However, 3D bioprinting strategies for generating cardiac tissues with high-density bioinks displaying aligned myocardium-like structure, rapid conduction, and appropriate chronotropic and inotropic responses to β-adrenergic stimulation and L-type calcium channel blockade [131], may provide a promising engineered heart tissue platform to study extH toxicity in sepsis. Complementing bioprinting, heart-on-a-chip systems that tri-culture iPSC-cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells under microfluidic perfusion may help to better reproduce myocardial architecture, endothelial alignment, barrier function, and cardiomyocyte maturation, while exhibiting clinically relevant electrophysiological and pharmacological responses [132], thereby offering a powerful tool to dissect extH effects during sepsis.

Extracellular histones contribute to acute lung injury

Sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are severe complications of infection, associated with high morbidity and mortality [133]. In sepsis, systemic inflammation drives widespread endothelial injury, increasing capillary permeability and worsening pulmonary edema, hypotension, and shock [134]. The main histological feature of ARDS is diffuse alveolar damage, with endothelial dysfunction and localized inflammation as central mechanisms Neutrophils accumulate in the pulmonary microcirculation, distort flow, and contribute to hypoxemia and interstitial/alveolar edema, while loss of alveolar–capillary barrier integrity impairs gas exchange and reduces compliance. Neutrophil-derived mediators, including proteases, ROS, NETs, and extH, further amplify inflammation and vascular injury [[135], [136], [137]].

Importantly, the pulmonary circulation receives the entire cardiac output and is continuously exposed to circulating DAMPs such as extH, which are elevated in sepsis and ARDS and act as potent mediators of endothelial injury, inflammation, and lung tissue injury [50,56,136], therefore correlating with disease severity [118].

In this regard, in sepsis-induced ALI and ARDS, circulating extH have emerged as clinically relevant biomarkers that closely reflect the extent of pulmonary injury, as well as mortality risk. Elevated plasma levels of nucleosomes, extH, and particularly citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3), have been consistently associated with worse oxygenation indices, greater pulmonary endothelial injury, and adverse outcomes in patients with sepsis and ARDS, supporting their utility for patient stratification and disease severity assessment [136,138,139]. In fact, in both clinical cohorts and experimental models, extH analysis in plasma or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid biospecimens correlates with the degree of alveolar–capillary barrier disruption, highlighting their potential value as lung-specific indicators of ongoing tissue damage [136,140].

Damage of pulmonary endothelium by extracellular histones

ExtH, especially the highly cationic H3 and H4, promote damage in pulmonary endothelium by disrupting the plasma membrane, triggering calcium influx and cell death, while simultaneously engage TLR2/TLR4 and activate NF-κB–dependent inflammatory pathways with oxidative stress, adhesion molecule upregulation, and cytokine release [71,139]. Kim et al. used human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) to dissect how extH disrupt lung endothelial barrier function and amplify inflammation. In human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells, purified H3/H4 at 10-50 μg/mL markedly increase permeability, with a clear inflammatory threshold at ≥30 μg/mL [50]. Bosmann et al. showed that purified calf-thymus histones (50 μg/mL) raise intracellular calcium, cause early LDH release, and reduce viability in mouse lung epithelial cells (MLE-12 and LA-4) in less than 1 h, demonstrating direct cytotoxicity toward type II-like alveolar epithelial cells [141].

ExtH also induce mitochondrial dysfunction, cytoskeletal rearrangement, glycocalyx damage, and loss of junctional proteins such as VE-cadherin [71], increasing endothelial permeability and promoting pulmonary edema. By sensitizing the endothelium to neutrophil-derived proteases and NETs, extH amplify inflammatory injury [51], and contribute to acute lung injury progression and alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction [118].

Alveolar epithelial cell injury in cellular models

As described in the previous section, extH, which are markedly elevated in the plasma and alveolar space of patients with sepsis and ARDS, directly injure the alveolar epithelium through membrane-disruptive mechanisms [142]. Using monolayer cell cultures of type I and type II pneumocytes, it was demonstrated how NET-associated and purified histones bound to the plasma membrane, triggering rapid calcium influx, membrane permeabilization, and histone concentration-dependent epithelial cell death. In alveolar epithelial cells, 50–200 μg/mL of calf thymus histones induce dose-dependent cytotoxicity and barrier disruption [142] (Table 2). Experimental studies further demonstrate that this cytotoxic effect markedly reduced the viability of these human alveolar epithelial cells, whereas histone-neutralizing antibodies or polyanionic compounds such as polysialic acid significantly attenuate cell injury, identifying histones as the principal toxic component within NETs [142].

Lung-specific inflammatory and immunothrombotic pathways

From the pathophysiological point of view, sepsis-associated ALI and ARDS can be conceptualized as the consequence of a coordinated thromboinflammatory and metabolic insult to the pulmonary microvasculature. Central to this process are Mac-1–dependent NET formation, platelet–leukocyte interactions, and metabolically driven endothelial cell death and macrophage polarization, which together converge to amplify endothelial dysfunction and microvascular obstruction [52,143].

Cellular models have been used to dissect how nucleosomes can potently activate macrophages, amplify NF-κB–dependent inflammatory signaling, and drive lung inflammation. To dissect these mechanisms, Dutta et al. used several cellular models: human and murine airway epithelial cells (BEAS-2B, MLE-12), endothelial cells (HUVEC), multiple macrophage models (U937-derived macrophages, murine bone-marrow–derived and alveolar macrophages, MH-S cells). Co-culture experiments were performed in trans-well systems where nucleosome-treated U937 macrophages were placed in inserts above BEAS-2B or HUVEC monolayers, allowing exchange of soluble mediators without direct cell contact; after 24 h, oxidative stress and proinflammatory markers were quantified to test nucleosome-activated macrophages propagation of secondary inflammatory injury to lung structural cells [104].

Yu et al. first exposed mouse alveolar macrophages (primary cells and MH-S cells) to extH (50 μg/mL) during 3 h to induce a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [59] (Table 2). These histone-primed M1 macrophages were then co-cultured with mouse or human neutrophils in transwells, allowing only soluble macrophage-derived factors to act on neutrophils. Under these conditions, neutrophils underwent robust NETosis, and cathepsin C was identified as the key macrophage-derived mediator driving NADPH oxidase–dependent ROS production and NET formation. Blocking cathepsin C in M1 macrophages markedly reduced NETosis and neutrophil-mediated injury, showing that histone-activated macrophages promote NET formation mainly through cathepsin C signaling [59].

Three-dimensional models of lung to study histone-induced lung injury

Although the work by Dutta et al. used co-culture conditions using different cells from lung tissue [104], actually they cannot represent the tridimensional architecture and complexity of the lung tissue. Therefore, 3D bioprinting represents a potential tool to reproduce the 3D architecture of the lung, to mimic the human lung microenvironment, including cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions, and to improve the study of the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. In fact, 3D bioprinted lung tissue has been used to model lung diseases (such as lung cancer, inflammation, asthma, or COPD) in a way that is as similar as possible to physiological reality [144,145].

Grigoryan et al. developed ventilatable lung mimetics with human lung fibroblasts in the interstitial compartment and epithelial-like cells lining PEGDA hydrogel airway lumens [146]. Lung 3D bioprinting deposits layer-by-layer alveolar/bronchial epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells with ECM-simulating hydrogels. These models enable three-dimensional organization and disease-like behaviors (proliferation, invasion, inflammation) mimicking human lung pathology [144]. Another biomodel explored influenza pathogenicity consisting of an extrusion bioink of alginate, gelatin, and Matrigel optimized for 3D A549 alveolar cell models. Influenza A infection showed human lung-like viral distribution and patterns (unlike 2D cultures), with the bioink promoting replication and inflammation, ideal for studying lung infection effects [147].

As for the alveolar structure, 3D bioprinting has also been used to manufacture a 3D multicellular alveolar model [148]. This model included human alveolar epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and microvessel endothelial cells arranged to form a thin barrier comparable to the human air-blood barrier. By reproducing the multilayered alveolar architecture with spatial control of each cell type, it overcomes key limitations of conventional 2D cultures.

Park et al. developed a vascularized airway-on-a-chip with a 3D-printed microvascular bed and differentiated epithelium coupled to a vascular network. Using porcine tracheal mucosa dECM bioink, they encapsulated human dermal microvascular endothelial cells and lung fibroblasts in PCL scaffolds, promoting endothelial self-organization into lumens and vessels. This airway–vascular interface modeled asthma-related inflammation and allergen-induced exacerbation, providing a basis for studying circulating histone cytotoxicity [149].

Finally, lung organoids have been also developed as advanced biomodels. In this regard, Susane Herold and colleagues developed vascular lung bronchoalveolar organoids of alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts that were microinjected with endothelial cells and subjected to LPS and/or viral infection, resulting in an enhanced release of proinflammatory cytokines leading to an enhancement of ICAM-1 expression [150]. Gu et al. generated vascularized lung organoids by co-inducing endodermal and mesodermal lineages from pluripotent stem cells, yielding coordinated airway epithelium, mesenchyme, and organ-specific endothelium in 3D. These self-organizing organoids featured FOXF1+ mesenchyme-surrounded branching airways and perfusable vascular networks lined by lung-type endothelial cells. This integrated vasculature recapitulates epithelial-mesenchymal-endothelial crosstalk, enhancing maturation and survival and providing physiological access for circulating factors, drugs, immune cells, and future extH studies [151].

3D bioprinted, organoids and lung-on-a-chip models offer significant advantages over conventional 2D cell cultures for the study of lung pathophysiological processes, especially in those in which there exist an interface between the airway and the vascular system. To date, there are no published studies specifically evaluating the cytotoxic effects of extH in 3D bioprinted lung tissues. However, existing bioprinting literature has extensively explored tissue exposure to external stimuli such as cytokines, toxins, and pro-inflammatory agents [152].

Extracellular histones produce acute kidney injury

Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SA-AKI) is a highly prevalent complication among patients with sepsis admitted to intensive care units [153], accounting for approximately 45–70% of cases [154] and showing significantly higher mortality rates [155]. It results from systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, altered vasoregulation due to imbalance between vasodilators and vasoconstrictors, microcirculatory dysfunction, intense endothelial activation, and direct cellular damage, with circulating histones further aggravating the injury [156], with circulating histones further exacerbating the injury [54,157]. These DAMP-driven mechanisms are especially relevant because SA-AKI may develop even with normal or increased cardiac output and apparently preserved global renal perfusion.

Nakazawa et al. showed renal I/R injury causes tubular epithelial necrosis, releasing extH that trigger neutrophil NETosis. Hypoxia/H2O2-injured HK-2 cell supernatants containing extH primed neutrophils for NETs (unlike hypoxia alone). NET-containing supernatants and purified extH/NETs induced HK-2 injury and epithelial cell necrosis and further NETosis. This vicious cycle of tubular necrosis, extH, and NETs amplifies damage and necroinflammation in acute kidney injury (Fig. 3). In neutrophils, 10–100 μg/mL of calf thymus histones were sufficient to induce robust NET formation, which then causes tubular damage in HK-2 cells [55] (Table 2). Moreover, van Smaalen et al. reported that warm ischemia during ex vivo porcine kidney perfusion markedly increased histone H3 release after 4 h, localizing to cytoplasmic blebs and debris around injured glomerular podocytes, endothelia, and mesangial cells. This interaction enables histones to act as antigens, promoting in situ immune complex formation upon binding IgG autoantibodies and facilitating antigen deposition (e.g., ssDNA bound only after histone priming), thus promoting macrophages and neutrophils recruitment of macrophages and neutrophils at the GBM (Fig. 3). Advanced biomodels to study histone-induced renal injury

Since renal tissue, including the GBM, comprises several cell types, it is relevant to explore extH-mediated deleterious effects on renal function using more sophisticated biomodels. As shown in the previous section, kidney toxicity is cell type- and dose-dependent: endothelial cells are highly sensitive and show significant injury at relatively low concentrations (≤30 μg/mL), whereas podocytes and parietal epithelial cells require higher histone levels to induce comparable cytotoxic effects.

Human kidney organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells allow physiological functional assays of tubular transport, cystogenesis, glomerular filtration, metabolism/endocrine function, renal injury and infection, using proximal-nephron–type organoids in vitro and vascularized organoid grafts in vivo [161]. Among the different assays that can be performed, in vivo filtration assays using kidney organoids engrafted under the mouse renal capsule and nephrotoxicity assays seems to be the most plausible assays to evaluate histone-induced renal injury.

Freedman's group engineered iPSC-derived human kidney organoids incorporating primary adult kidney endothelial cells that integrate into glomerular and peritubular vascular networks with podocytes and proximal tubules, recapitulating native renal microvasculature. IFN-γ stimulation induced inflammatory endothelial injury and pyroptosis in endothelial cells, modeling glomerulopathy via IFN-γ-driven pyroptotic angiopathy [162]. In addition, more sophisticated biomodels as the kidney-on-a-chip, especially proximal tubule-on-a-chip, models enable high-throughput nephrotoxicity screening by recapitulating 3D tubular architecture, shear stress, transporters, barrier integrity, and biomarker release (e.g., KIM-1, NGAL, HO-1, clusterin) in human renal cells, outperforming 2D monolayers. In fact, microfluidic platforms can even promote native proximal tubule phenotypes (transport, metabolism, junctions, endocytosis) and toxin sensitivity [163].

The described biomodels provide powerful platforms to investigate how extH mediate kidney damage in SA-AKI. They are also useful for dissecting the role of extH across kidney transplantation, where elevated posttransplant NETs correlate with delayed graft function, antibody-mediated rejection, tubular necrosis, and immune activation [164]. Owever, extH have not yet been systematically evaluated in advanced human-relevant biomodels such as kidney organoids or organ-on-chip platforms. This is a critical gap, since these models could clarify epithelial–endothelial interactions and offer a more physiologically relevant assessment of histone-induced renal damage in SA-AKI.

Extracellular histones produce acute liver injury

The liver plays a crucial role in metabolic and immunological homeostasis, which is compromised during sepsis [165]. Furthermore, its impairment disrupts normal function in distant organs, including the gut [166,167], kidneys [55,168] and lungs [169,170]. Consequently, linking damage caused by extH to liver injury represents significant challenges.

Damage of parenchymal cells and non-parenchymal cells by extracellular histones

Hepatocytes are the predominant cell type in the liver, being 60% of the total cells and 80% of the volume of the liver [171]. Hepatocytes act as both target and source of extH, initiating a vicious cycle in septic environments. In fact, it has been shown how hepatotoxicity mediated by carbon tetrachlorine (CCl4), which induce hepatocytes cell death increases circulating histone levels with peak values around 200 μg/mL [172]. Similarly, in models of liver I/R injury, which mimics septic hepatocellular stress, immunofluorescence reveals histone translocation from the hepatocyte nuclei to the cytoplasm as well as to the extracellular space [173,174]. Furthermore, exogenous administration of extH in a dose of 25 mg/kg in mice aggravates liver I/R injury but, neutralization of histones by antibodies has been shown to reduce hepatocellular damage and hinders the activation of the inflammatory cascade [173]. Moreover, conditioned media from hypoxic or necrotic hepatocytes induces NET formation, leading to further histone release that injures intact hepatocytes and may further recruit and activate neutrophils to generate additional NETs, thereby amplifying liver injury in a self-perpetuating cycle [106].

Interestingly, NETs have been shown to adhere and damage liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), which line the sinusoids and regulate blood flow and filtration [175]. NETs not only injure hepatocytes and LSECs but also activate Kupffer cells, a specialized form of macrophages that reside in the liver, which secrete proinflammatory cytokines [106]. When Kupffer cells were co-cultured in conditioned media from stimulated neutrophils, inflammatory cytokines and chemokines significantly increased [106]. However, it is noteworthy that Kupffer cells, and macrophages in general, can remove NETs via phagocytosis [[176], [177], [178], [179]]. Moreover, extH at a dose of 25 mg/kg in mice can also stimulate Kupffer cells, triggering the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [180]. Indeed, a study on extracellular vesicles containing nucleosome-DNA complex showed that the naked DNA does not induce Kupffer cell activation or cytokine secretion, but DNase treatment, which removes DNA but not histones, produced the activation of Kupffer cells [181]. In vitro treatment of LX2 human hepatic stellate cells with extH at concentrations between 2 and 10 μg/mL, significantly increased the collagen I production [172], which may contribute to fibrosis [182,183]. Furthermore, concentrations of 20 μg/mL of extH caused LX2 cell death [172] (Table 2). Conversely, the use of non-anticoagulant heparin or TLR4 neutralizing antibody, reduced the histone-enhanced collagen I production, as well the fibrosis in a hepatotoxic CCl4-treated mice, although levels of circulating histones were not diminished [172].

Advanced biomodels to explore liver injury in sepsis

As described above, traditional 2D in vitro models have provided important insights into histone-mediated hepatic toxicity across different liver-resident cell types. However, these models fail to account for the contribution of hepatic architecture, including the roles of spatial organization, cellular heterogeneity, and intercellular interactions in shaping the progression of hepatic injury during sepsis. In this context, advanced 3D hepatic biomodels are essential to accurately recapitulate the molecular mechanisms underlying the pathophysiological interplay between sepsis and liver injury.

It has been shown how circulating histone levels rise 5–6-fold in acute liver failure, with a median at around 5.6 μg/mL (ALF) versus healthy and chronic liver disease patients, correlating with mortality, disease severity, and cytokine levels (i.e. IL-6/8/18/TNF-α) [[184], [185], [186]]. ALF patient-derived serum or histone-spiked controls induce hepatocyte death (reversed by heparin), while mouse models confirmed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and injury mitigation via histone neutralization [184,[187], [188], [189]]. In post-liver transplant, extracellular histones rise sharply to a 5-to-6-fold increase due to I/R injury and primary graft dysfunction. These histones, alongside elevated MPO from neutrophil activation, S100A8/A9 (alarmins), and pro-inflammatory cytokines, directly promoted hepatocyte death in ex vivo culture models [189].

Different in vitro 3D culture systems have been developed to generate advanced hepatic biomodels for the study of liver function and liver disease. Currently, several human liver organoids (HLOs) have been used to recapitulate key pathological mechanisms in liver human diseases [190], drug-induced liver injury and cytokine release [191] and to investigate the impact of exogenous infections on hepatic function [[192], [193], [194]].

In the context of sepsis, Mosig et al. developed a vascularized organoid model of the hepatic sinusoid (vHLOs) to mimic early sepsis-associated liver dysfunction. This system consisted of a highly organized multicellular architecture incorporating HUVECs, macrophages, hepatocytes, and hepatic stellate cells. Stimulation with TLRs agonists induced inflammation-associated hepatic dysfunction, characterized by the release of proinflammatory cytokines (i.e. IL-6, IL-1β, and TNFα) and a reduction in endothelial VE-cadherin expression [195]. In another notable study, Taniguchi and colleagues reported the generation of iPSC-derived vascular HLOs containing resident Kupffer cells to model hepatic dysfunction in sepsis. Following treatment with LPS and IFN-γ, only Kupffer cell–containing HLOs exhibited proinflammatory polarization, as evidenced by increased expression of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-β), enhanced LDH release from apoptotic cells, accumulation of ROS, and endothelial cell dysfunction and death [196]. These events resulted in the downregulation of multiple molecular pathways associated with hepatic function, including ammonia metabolism, albumin synthesis, and cytochrome P450 enzyme activity (CYP3A4, CYP1A1, and CYP1A2), among others [196].

The combination of multiple hepatic cell types with biomimetic materials, such as hyaluronic acid, collagen, and elastin, represents an alternative strategy for developing 3D hepatic culture platforms with tunable microenvironmental properties through liver tissue engineering. Within this rapidly evolving field, Lee et al. developed a collagen-based bioink containing primary hepatocytes, HUVECs, and fibroblasts to bioprint 3D liver-like scaffolds that recapitulated key aspects of hepatic function, including increased albumin secretion and urea synthesis [197]. More recently, Moo-Yeal Lee et al. explored the bioprinting of vascularized liver organoids (vHLOs), generating functional vascularized constructs using a BEM-2 hydrogel matrix and demonstrating enhanced maturation compared with conventional HLOs [198].

Finally, given the critical role of blood flow and shear stress in maintaining hepatic function, liver-on-chip technologies have been developed to investigate multiple aspects of liver physiology and disease [[199], [200], [201]]. Notably, Kiehntopf et al. employed liver-on-chip systems to study hepatic dysfunction following exposure to CAAP48, a novel sepsis biomarker identified by his group [202]. Looking ahead, the integration of bioprinted vHLOs with existing liver-on-chip platforms, including infection-oriented models such as those developed by Robert Bals and colleagues [203], may enable the development of automated, high-throughput, and highly biomimetic systems for the reliable study of sepsis-induced hepatic injury.

Although no studies have yet examined the effects of extH in these advanced biomodels, a recent doctoral thesis has shown that liver organoids treated with antiretroviral therapy and combined antiretroviral plus anti-tuberculosis therapy exhibit proteomic enrichment of pathways associated with NET formation, suggesting the activation of NETosis-like inflammatory programs driven by overlapping chromatin remodeling and oxidative stress mechanisms [204] (R).

Histone-driven neurovascular injury as a central mechanism of sepsis-associated encephalopathy

Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) encompasses a spectrum of acute neurological manifestations ranging from inattention and fluctuating delirium to coma, seizures, and electroencephalographic abnormalities, and represents one of the most frequent and clinically impactful organ dysfunctions in sepsis [205]. Even in the absence of overt delirium, a substantial proportion of survivors develop persistent cognitive deficits, including impairments in attention, executive function, and memory, which can persist for months to years and significantly compromise long-term outcomes [206].

The brain is particularly vulnerable to circulating inflammatory insults because of its strict dependence on the integrity of the neurovascular unit (NVU), composed of endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, and neurons. Under physiological conditions, the blood-brain barrier (BBB) tightly regulates the entry of circulating molecules into the central nervous system, while resident microglia provide local immune surveillance. During sepsis, however, systemic cytokinemia, endothelial activation, coagulation abnormalities, and microvascular dysfunction converge to destabilize BBB integrity and promote neuroinflammatory cascades (Fig. 4) [205,207,208]. Within this context, circulating DAMPs, particularly extH, have emerged as key mediators linking systemic inflammation to brain dysfunction (Table 2). Although quantitative data in the central nervous system remain limited, circulating histone levels in sepsis have been reported in the range of 10-250 μg/mL, and concentrations within this range are sufficient to induce endothelial dysfunction, increase permeability, and promote cell death in vascular beds, including the cerebral endothelium [4,142,209]. Given the central role of the BBB as a highly specialized endothelial interface, these findings support a model in which histone-driven endothelial injury represents a primary initiating event in SAE pathogenesis. Extracellular histones drive inflammation, oxidative stress, and cell death in neurons and glial cells

At the cellular level, extH contribute to SAE through coordinated mechanisms involving neurovascular barrier disruption, innate immunity activation, and direct neural toxicity. Experimental extH concentrations reported in systemic inflammatory conditions such as sepsis span a broad range (10-250 μg/mL), with lower concentrations (10-50 μg/mL) sufficient to induce endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular permeability [4,136]. This disruption of the BBB facilitates the entry of plasma proteins, fibrin, complement components, and peripheral immune cells into the central nervous system, amplifying neuroinflammatory responses [208,211].

At the neurovascular interface, extH act as potent DAMPs, activating TLR2 and TLR4 on endothelial cells and infiltrating leukocytes, thereby triggering NF-κB and MAPK signaling and promoting the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules [39,70,212]. Within the CNS, microglia respond to histone exposure by adopting a neurotoxic phenotype characterized by increased production of TNF, IL-1β, ROS, and matrix metalloproteinases, further exacerbating barrier breakdown and inflammatory injury [66,213]. Extracellular histones and nucleosomes have also been linked to inflammasome activation and complement-mediated inflammatory amplification, although much of this evidence derives from vascular and immune systems rather than brain-specific models [56,67,107,214,215]. In parallel, extH exert direct cytotoxic effects independent of immune receptor signaling. Their strong cationic charge enables interaction with negatively charged neuronal and glial membranes, resulting in loss of ionic homeostasis and necrotic cell death at higher concentrations (≥50-100 μg/mL) [4,62]. Similar effects have been observed in astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, suggesting broader impairment of metabolic support and network stability within the septic brain [63,66].

These mechanisms are supported by experimental evidence across in vitro and in vivo systems. Exposure of brain endothelial cells, in vitro BBB models, and neuronal cultures to histones, particularly histones H3 and H4, induces endothelial activation, cytoskeletal contraction, junctional disassembly, increased paracellular permeability, and leukocyte transmigration [56,75] [216]. In primary cortical neurons, histones trigger rapid Ca2+ influx through membrane pore formation and secondary activation of voltage-gated calcium channels, causing growth cone collapse, neurite damage, mitochondrial depolarization, oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and caspase-mediated apoptosis (Fig. 4) [64,217,218]. In vivo, systemic histones or NETs induce endothelial swelling, vascular congestion, and perivascular edema in the CNS, while inhibition of NET formation preserves BBB integrity and reduces neuronal apoptosis and memory impairment [216]. Consistent with these findings, TLR4 mediates histone-induced neurotoxicity in microglia-like and neuronal systems by driving cytokine release, impairing phagocytosis, and exerting direct neurotoxic effects [62,63,66,218].

Advanced biomodels to study histone-driven brain dysfunction in sepsis

Accumulating evidence indicates that histone toxicity in SAE extends beyond overt neuronal loss to disrupt synaptic integrity and network-level function. Altered excitatory-inhibitory balance, impaired neurovascular coupling, and microvascular dysfunction likely underlie EEG slowing and fluctuating delirium in septic patients [65,219]. Microglial activation and aberrant synaptic pruning driven by HMGB1- and histone-associated pathways contribute to hippocampal long-term potentiation loss and persistent cognitive deficits [220]. Consistent with this view, selective regional vulnerability has been documented, with the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex exhibiting heightened susceptibility to histone- and NET-mediated microvascular injury and neuroinflammation [221,222], correlating clinically with deficits in memory and attention characteristic of SAE [223]. In parallel, histone-driven coagulation abnormalities, including cerebral microthrombi and fibrin deposition after BBB leakage, may contribute to diffuse cerebral hypometabolism [224,225]. Together, these mechanisms place extH at the intersection of neurovascular injury, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and coagulation disturbances in SAE [41]. However, the temporal relationships between circulating histone burden, regional brain vulnerability, and neurological manifestations in humans, and whether histone-targeted interventions can alter clinical trajectories, remain unresolved.

Conventional in vitro systems and animal models remain insufficient to capture the integrated, human-specific neurovascular dynamics of SAE. Static monocultures lack physiological shear stress, glycocalyx architecture, and multicellular crosstalk, while rodent models incompletely reflect human BBB properties, immune-neural interactions, and cognitive dysfunction. These limitations have driven the development of advanced human-relevant biomodels that better recapitulate blood-borne inflammatory injury to the brain. In this context, advanced biomodels offer complementary strengths: static systems provide mechanistic simplicity, animal models capture systemic responses, and OoC platforms uniquely reproduce human-specific neurovascular dynamics under controlled flow conditions.

Recent BBB-on-chip and neurovascular unit-on-chip platforms combine human brain microvascular endothelial cells with pericytes and astrocytes under controlled perfusion, yielding low baseline permeability and measurable responses to inflammatory challenge. These systems show that cytokines or endotoxemia-mimicking insults disrupt BBB integrity, induce endothelial activation and junctional remodeling, and promote leukocyte rolling, adhesion, and transmigration under flow [[226], [227], [228], [229], [230]]. Pericytes and shear conditioning enhance basement membrane organization and glycocalyx expression, providing partial resilience to inflammatory injury while defining thresholds beyond which barrier failure and neurovascular inflammation occur [231,232]. These features are especially relevant for modeling cationic histone-mediated BBB disruption, which depends on endothelial polarity, surface charge, and mechanotransduction. Brain-on-chip and neurovascular unit models then extend this framework by integrating neural and glial compartments, enabling direct study of vascular-to-neural signaling. Perfusion of inflammatory mediators across a human BBB into neuronal, astrocytic, and microglial populations recapitulates key SAE features, including microglial activation, synaptic dysfunction, metabolic stress, and altered neuronal activity measured by electrophysiology or multielectrode arrays [[232], [233], [234], [235]]. Importantly, Many of these biomodels have also been used for pharmacological testing, with quantitative rescue of endothelial, inflammatory, and neuronal endpoints [228,[236], [237], [238]]. This makes advanced neurovascular biomodels well suited to evaluate SAE therapies, including histone-neutralizing antibodies, heparinoids, DNase-based approaches, and TLR modulators, by testing BBB preservation, immune-cell recruitment, and neuronal function under blood-borne inflammatory stress. But, despite their potential, these platforms still face challenges related to standardization, reproducibility, and scalability, as well as limitations in fully recapitulating systemic immune interactions and long-term disease progression.

Looking forward, integration of these human biomodels with clinical sepsis cohorts offers a path toward truly translational SAE platforms. Perfusion with septic patient plasma or purified histone fractions, derivation of neurovascular unit components from patient-specific induced pluripotent stem cells, and benchmarking of chip-derived functional readouts against circulating biomarkers, neuroimaging findings, and cognitive trajectories could enable quantitative calibration of model responses to human disease [29,232]. Such clinically anchored biomodels have the potential to de-risk therapeutic development and accelerate the identification of effective interventions for histone-driven neurovascular injury in sepsis.

Future perspective

In human sepsis, NET markers (including citrullinated histone H3), nucleosomes and extH are consistently elevated and correlate with severity/mortality [41,207,239,240]. The dual role of circulating histones as both tissue damage effectors and biomarkers positions extH as ideal candidates not only for risk stratification and pharmacodynamic monitoring [241], as well as targets for treatments against sepsis.

Classical experiments evaluating the cytotoxic potential of extH and molecular mechanism mediated by these DAMPs have been evaluated using classical 2D cell culture (Table 2). Importantly, the translational impact of the use of advanced biomodels extends significantly from the basic research to explore pathological mechanisms to drug discovery and precision medicine (Fig. 5). Organoids accurately model organ physiology across the kidney, lung, gut, liver, and brain [9], which are key organs in sepsis pathophysiology. In fact, these organs also provide a realistic setting to study microbial invasion, cytokine-induced dysfunction, infections, and host-pathogen interactions, while supporting antiviral testing and translational research [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Patient-derived or genetically engineered organoids further enable modeling inter-individual variation, linking pathology development to phenotype [15]. In parallel, 3D spheroids and bioprinted tissue models support disease modeling and drug screening, and facilitate personalized therapy testing on patient-specific tissues [15]. Together with microfluidic OoC platforms and vascularized organoids, these advances help overcome diffusion and maturation limits, bringing in vitro systems closer to physiological fidelity [9,243].

In the short term, research should focus on standardizing extH exposure and response readouts in the advanced biomodels, using patient-derived material to assess reproducibility and inter-individual variability. These proof-of-concept studies would help validate extH-driven mechanisms and establish a preclinical framework for future histone-targeted interventions. In this context, 3D-bioprinted tissues and organoids offer physiologically relevant platforms to study organ-specific cytotoxic and inflammatory responses to DAMPs and PAMPs; however, evidence for extH effects in human-derived organoids remains scarce, with the exception of BVOs discussed in Section 3.5.

Importantly, these advanced biomodels have the potential of testing histone-targeted interventions in a human-like context, since circulating histone removal has emerged as a promising strategy for personalized sepsis therapy [2]. Several anti-histone approaches are under evaluation, including heparin-based therapies [244], anti-histone antibodies, protein C, small polyanions like mCBS [4,136,245,246], as well as DNases and PAD4 inhibitors [2,247], while new heparin-grafted composite microspheres enable simultaneous adsorption of endotoxin and circulating histones [248,249]. These strategies aim to preserve vascular integrity and reduce organ damage, so advanced biomodels can help optimize their efficacy against extH.

Although robust, histone-targeted interventions have not yet been validated in large-scale clinical trials, advanced biomodels already offer a strong translational route to dissect extH-driven mechanisms and test histone-removal strategies in human-like systems. To move this field forward, well-characterized organoids and standardized 3D-bioprinted platforms are needed, ideally linked to biobanks that can provide matched, patient-derived material for reproducible testing and population-level comparisons [[250], [251], [252]]. In this setting, living biobanks based on advanced biomodels would add particular value because they are dynamic, self-renewing systems that support disease modeling, drug discovery, and personalized medicine more effectively than static biospecimens [253,254]. They can also help capture inter-individual variability, which is especially important in sepsis [253].
