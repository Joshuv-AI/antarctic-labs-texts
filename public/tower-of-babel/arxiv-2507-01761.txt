Enhanced Generative Model Evaluation with Clipped Density and Coverage
Nicolas Salvy, Hugues Talbot, Bertrand Thirion

Abstract
Although generative models have made remarkable progress in recent years, their use in critical applications has been hindered by their incapacity to reliably evaluate sample quality. Quality refers to at least two complementary concepts: fidelity and coverage. Current quality metrics often lack reliable, interpretable values due to an absence of calibration or insufficient robustness to outliers. To address these shortcomings, we introduce two novel metrics, Clipped Density and Clipped Coverage . By clipping individual sample contributions and, for fidelity, the radii of nearest neighbor balls, our metrics prevent out-of-distribution samples from biasing the aggregated values. Through analytical and empirical calibration, these metrics exhibit linear score degradation as the proportion of poor samples increases. Thus, they can be straightforwardly interpreted as equivalent proportions of good samples. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that Clipped Density and Clipped Coverage outperform existing methods in terms of robustness, sensitivity, and interpretability for evaluating generative models.

1
Introduction

Generative models have achieved remarkable progress in recent years and are being actively explored in various fields, such as healthcare
pinaya2022brain
;
fernandez2024generating
;
tudosiu2024realistic
;
zhu2024diffusion
;
bluethgen2024vision

However, their deployment in high-stakes applications depends on the ability to reliably evaluate the quality of synthetic data, ensuring it can be trusted.
This evaluation is inherently challenging, especially for high-dimensional data. The true underlying distributions of these data are often unknown and complex. They also do not conform to known parametric families. This makes computing support or density infeasible in practice.
Current model evaluation often relies on metrics like
the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)
heusel2017gans
and FD-DINOv2
stein2024exposing
for images. These provide a single, compound score to represent overall sample quality, making it impossible to determine whether poor performance stems from a lack of realism or variety
sajjadi2018assessing
.

To address this issue, the quality of synthetic data can be broken down into at least two core concepts: fidelity and coverage. Fidelity assesses how similar each synthetic sample is to the input data
naeem2020reliable
. Conversely, coverage measures the extent to which synthetic samples represent the distribution of real data, taking into consideration how the rarity or commonness of real data is reflected in the synthetic samples.

To determine whether generative models can be truly dependable, particularly in sensitive applications, the metrics used to evaluate them must be trustworthy.
This means that they must be robust to outliers, sensitive to genuine deficiencies in the synthetic data, and provide interpretable scores.
Crucially, beyond relative comparisons (i.e., knowing that one model is better than another), metrics for which absolute values are meaningful are critical to ensure that the synthetic data can be used in applications.
Ideally, a score of
x
would indicate performance equivalent to having a proportion of
x
good samples and
(1-x)
bad ones.
As shown in
fig.

1
, current coverage metrics fail to achieve this.
We show in
fig.

4
that current fidelity metrics are similarly untrustworthy.

In this paper, we introduce
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
, two novel metrics designed to overcome these limitations. Our contributions are:

•

Trustworthy Evaluation
: Our metrics achieve robustness to outliers by clipping the contribution of individual samples to the aggregate score and, for
Clipped Density
, by limiting the radii of nearest-neighbor spheres used to measure the density.
This prevents out of distribution samples from unduly influencing the evaluation while maintaining sensitivity to actual quality issues.

•

Interpretable Absolute Scores
: Through empirical calibration in the case of
Clipped Density
and theoretical analysis in the case of
Clipped Coverage
, we ensure that the values degrade linearly with the proportion of bad samples.

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides background on existing metrics. Section 3 reviews related work. Section 4 introduces our
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
metrics. Section 5 details our experiments and results, and Section 6 discusses implications and limitations.

2
Background

We consider a setting in which we are given
N
i.i.d. samples
\{x^{r}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from an unknown data (reference, or real) distribution
p_{r}
and
M
i.i.d. samples
\{x^{s}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
generated from an unknown synthetic data distribution
p_{s}
.
In this section, we review relevant metrics for evaluating generative models that aim to disentangle two aspects of synthetic data: fidelity (how realistic each synthetic sample is) and coverage (how well the synthetic samples populate the real density distribution). These metrics serve as the basis for our proposed improvements.

Assuming a given support
S^{r}
of the real distribution and
S^{s}
for the synthetic distribution,
Precision

sajjadi2018assessing
;
simon2019revisiting
measures the proportion of synthetic samples that fall in
S^{r}
, while
Recall

sajjadi2018assessing
;
simon2019revisiting
measures the proportion of i.i.d. real samples that fall in
S^{s}
.

\displaystyle\text{Precision}=\mathbb{P}_{p_{s}}[S^{r}]=\mathbb{P}_{p_{s}}[S^{r}\cap S^{s}]

\displaystyle\text{Recall}=\mathbb{P}_{p_{r}}[S^{s}]=\mathbb{P}_{p_{r}}[S^{r}\cap S^{s}]

(1)

However, the underlying densities
d^{r}
and
d^{s}
and their support
S^{r}
and
S^{s}
are unknown, making such computation infeasible.
In practice,
improved

Precision
and
Recall

kynkaanniemi2019improved
estimate these distributions through the union of balls centered on each observed sample, with a radius equal to the distance
\text{NND}_{k}
from each center to its
k
-Nearest Neighbor
BURMAN1992132
. Here,
\text{NND}_{k}^{r}
denotes the distance to the
k
-nearest real data sample, while
\text{NND}_{k}^{s}
denotes the distance to the
k
-nearest synthetic data sample.

iPrecision

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{p_{s}}[\hat{S^{r}}\cap S^{s}]=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in\cup_{i=1}^{N}B(x^{r}_{i},\text{NND}_{k}^{r}(x^{r}_{i}))}

(2)

iRecall

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{p_{r}}[S^{r}\cap\hat{S^{s}}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{x^{r}_{i}\in\cup_{j=1}^{M}B(x^{s}_{j},\text{NND}_{k}^{s}(x^{s}_{j}))}

(3)

Yet,
iPrecision
and
iRecall
are strongly biased by out-of-distribution samples
naeem2020reliable
. Real outliers, being far from their nearest neighbors, create large balls and bias the estimation of
{S^{r}}
.
Similarly, bad samples in the synthetic data compromise the approximation of the synthetic support
naeem2020reliable
.

Density
and
Coverage

naeem2020reliable
aim to alleviate this issue. Instead of making a binary decision on whether a synthetic sample lies within the approximated real manifold,
Density
counts the number of real balls a synthetic sample falls into, normalized by
k
.
The rationale is that outliers with large balls only contribute
\frac{1}{k}
to the fidelity of a given synthetic sample, thus having a lesser contribution to the overall score than in
iPrecision
, where the sample fidelity would be 1
naeem2020reliable
.
With the definition given below, the
Density
metric represents the average relative density
d^{s}/d^{r}
.
In high-dimensional spaces where measuring density is impractical,
Density
instead examines balls centered around observed data points, providing a relative measure of how many synthetic points fall within these balls normalized by their mass
k
.
To avoid estimation biases when approximating the synthetic distribution that may contain many bad samples,
additional considerations are needed:

Coverage
adopts a distinct perspective
naeem2020reliable
.
Instead of counting the proportion of real samples within at least one synthetic ball,
Coverage
calculates the proportion of real samples that have at least one synthetic sample within their ball:
\text{Coverage}=\mathbb{P}_{p_{r}}[\hat{S^{r}}\cap S^{s}]
. It is the proportion of real samples that are
covered
.

\displaystyle\text{Density}=\frac{1}{kM}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},\text{NND}_{k}^{r}(x^{r}_{i}))}

\displaystyle\text{Coverage}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\exists j,x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},\text{NND}^{r}_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}

(4)

Although
Density
is less affected by outliers in the target distribution, it remains influenced by those
park2023probabilistic
.
Additionally,
Density
is not bounded by 1
naeem2020reliable
, making interpretation challenging when empirical estimates exceed this value
cheema2023precision
;
kim2023topp
. Similar to
iPrecision
and
iRecall
,
Coverage
is limited to analysing supports and does not detect density mismatches. A few synthetic samples can cover a high-density area of the real distribution by falling within many balls. Adding more synthetic samples in the high-density regions does not increase the score
park2023probabilistic
.

3
Related work

Recent works have aimed to improve these metrics.
Precision Cover
and
Recall Cover
, metrics analogous to
Precision
and
Recall
, have been introduced in
cheema2023precision
: they require at least
k^{\prime}>1
samples to be within a ball for it to be considered covered.
Another approach introduced in
park2023probabilistic
hinges on probabilistic metrics,
P-precision
and
P-recall
, which measure the probability of a synthetic (resp. real) element being within a random sub-support of the real (resp. synthetic) distribution.

To address the problem of large-radius balls,
khayatkhoei2023emergent
have employed a dual-perspective approach and introduced
symPrecision
and
symRecall
:
symRecall
is defined as the minimum of
Recall
and
Coverage
, while
symPrecision
is the minimum of
Precision
and the
complementary Precision
metric obtained by adopting the reversed perspective.

Some alternative approaches do not rely on nearest neighbors approximations.
\alpha
-Precision
and
\beta
-Recall

alaa2022faithful
employ a One-Class approach to estimate supports containing
\alpha
(and
\beta
) percent of a dataset. They measure the proportion of the other dataset present within supports of varying levels.
Topological Precision and Recall

kim2023topp
estimate the support with topologically conditioned density kernels.

However, none of these metrics address all the issues, as illustrated in
figs.

1
,
4
and
5
.
In the following section, we introduce metrics that are
i)
robust to outliers, and
ii)
provide clearly interpretable values.

4
Method

Our proposed metrics are designed to satisfy several properties. Firstly, they should be
robust to outliers
(Desideratum 1).
Secondly, the metrics should exhibit
linear score degradation
: if a proportion
x
of samples are bad, the score should decrease by
x
(Desideratum 2a). The metrics should be
normalized
between 0 and 1 (Desideratum 2b), allowing for the interpretation of values on their own.

These properties lead to straightforward interpretations: a score of
x
indicates that the synthetic dataset achieves the same fidelity or coverage as a dataset composed of a proportion of
x
real samples and
(1-x)
bad samples. A
y
difference between two datasets corresponds to
y
more bad samples in the equivalent scoring scenario.

4.1
Robustness to outliers

Figure 2
:
Clipped Density
corrects
Density
’s failure
: In a simple setup with a single bad synthetic sample,
Density
gives a value of
1
. By clipping the fidelity of individual samples to
1
, we obtain an adjusted score of
\frac{2}{3}
.

Figure 3
:
Correcting
Clipped Coverage
linear decay
: The
unnormalized Clipped Coverage
(blue) does not decrease linearly with the proportion of bad samples. We theoretically compute its expected behavior (orange) and correct it (green).

Figure

3
illustrates a failure case of the
Density
metric on a synthetic dataset containing a single bad sample, with
k=2
. In this configuration, the two centered points each receive a fidelity score of
\frac{3}{2}
, while the bad sample obtains a fidelity of
0
. Consequently, the overall dataset fidelity computes as
\frac{1}{3}\left(2\times\frac{3}{2}+0\right)=1
. While this is an example in 2 dimensions, the problem worsens in higher dimensions as the number of balls a sample can belong to increases
radovanovic2010hubs
, allowing one over-occurring sample to mask an increasing number of bad synthetic samples.

To prevent over-occuring samples from masking defects, we modify the aggregation approach for fidelity and coverage. The intuitive idea is to limit the contribution of each sample to the metric. The fidelity of any synthetic sample should not exceed 1, and similarly, the coverage of any real sample should be capped at 1. Applying this modification to
fig.

3
, the fidelity score becomes
\frac{1}{3}\left(2\times\min(\frac{3}{2},1)+0\right)=\frac{2}{3}
, which effectively detects the presence of the bad sample.

On the other hand, real outliers can have extremely large distances to their nearest neighbors, resulting in balls with significantly larger radii. In dimension
d
, the volume of a ball of radius
r
is proportional to
r^{d}
. Not only do outliers generate much larger balls, but any point in a low-density region does so, which dramatically skews the metrics. These balls have a fixed
mass

k
, but their
volume
varies.

For the fidelity metric, we are looking for each individual synthetic sample at the number of balls it falls within. Balls of large
volume
can contribute disproportionately more than the others. To ensure balanced contributions that limit outlier influence, we clip the radius of each ball to the median of the distances to the
k
-th nearest neighbor.

R_{k}(x^{r}_{i})=\min\left(\text{NND}^{r}_{k}(x^{r}_{i}),\operatorname{median}(\{\text{NND}^{r}_{k}(x_{l})\}_{l=1}^{N})\right)

(5)

For the metric of coverage, for each real sample, we compare the
mass
of synthetic samples in its ball to the
mass
of real samples. To balance the contributions of real points, the
masses
of their balls has to be fixed, hence no clipping to the radii is applied.

To further account for the
distribution
of real samples, we adopt an approach similar to
Clipped Density
for the proposed coverage metric. This results in the following metrics that satisfy Desideratum 1, robustness to outliers:

\displaystyle\text{ClippedDensity}_{\text{unnorm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\min\left(\vphantom{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},R_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}}\frac{1}{k}\smash{\overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},R_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}}^{\#\{\text{clipped real balls }x^{s}_{j}\text{ is within}\}}},1\right)\vphantom{\overbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},R_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}}^{\#\{\text{clipped real balls }x^{s}_{j}\text{ is within}\}}}

(6)

\displaystyle\text{ClippedCoverage}_{\text{unnorm}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\min\left(\vphantom{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},\text{NND}^{r}_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}}\frac{1}{k}\smash{\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},\text{NND}^{r}_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}}_{\#\{\text{synthetic samples within }x^{r}_{i}\text{'s ball}\}}},1\right)\vphantom{\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathbf{1}_{x^{s}_{j}\in B(x^{r}_{i},\text{NND}^{r}_{k}(x^{r}_{i}))}}_{\#\{\text{synthetic samples within }x^{r}_{i}\text{'s ball}\}}}

(7)

4.2
Interpretability

4.2.1
Clipped Density

Since
\text{ClippedDensity}_{\text{unnorm}}
is an average over synthetic samples, an
x
proportion of bad samples directly causes the score to decrease by
x
, satisfying Desideratum 2a.

To achieve normalization between 0 and 1 (Desideratum 2b), we require an ideal value of 1. We compute this ideal value empirically by evaluating the fidelity score of the real data using a leave-one-out strategy. For each sample
x^{r}_{i}
, we count the number of real balls
j
(where
j\neq i
) that contain it. This computation is efficient, as we already obtained the indices of real samples inside each ball during the radius computation.

We then normalize
\text{ClippedDensity}_{\text{unnorm}}
by dividing by the computed ideal value and clipping the result to 1, since scores exceeding 1 would lack meaningful interpretations.

The final normalized metric is:

ClippedDensity

\displaystyle=\min\left(\frac{\text{ClippedDensity}_{\text{unnorm}}}{\text{ClippedDensity}_{\text{real}}},1\right)

(8)

4.2.2
Clipped Coverage

The blue curve in
fig.

3
shows the score of
\text{ClippedCoverage}_{\text{unnorm}}
when the real and synthetic distributions are identical (far left) and as bad samples are progressively introduced into the synthetic distribution. To satisfy Desideratum 2, the score should follow
1-x
for
x
the bad sample proportion.

To correct the metric’s behavior, we start by deriving its expected value as a function of
x
.

Lemma 1
.

If the real and synthetic distributions are identical, that is,
\{x^{r}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{x^{s}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
are
N+M
i.i.d. samples from the same distribution, then the expected value of
\text{ClippedCoverage}_{\text{unnorm}}
is given by:

\mathbf{E}\left[\text{ClippedCoverage}_{\text{unnorm}}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{M}\min\left(\frac{j}{k},1\right){M\choose j}\frac{\beta(k+j,M-j+N-k)}{\beta(k,N-k)}

(9)

where
\beta
is the beta function.

The proof of
lemma

1
is provided in
appendix

A
.

To parameterize the curve, we now consider the case with a proportion
x
of bad synthetic samples.
Since such samples always lie outside any real ball, the expected score becomes equivalent to the ideal case in
lemma

1
but with only
M_{x}=\lfloor M(1-x)\rfloor
synthetic samples instead of
M
. We denote this as
f_{\text{expected}}(x)
. This result is shown in orange in
fig.

3
.

To satisfy Desideratum 2, the expected value of the metric should be
1-x
when there is a bad sample proportion
x
. We seek a function
g
such that
g\circ f_{\text{expected}}(x)=1-x
. Since
M_{x}
can only take values for integers
m
between
0
and
M
, it suffices to find
g
for
f_{\text{expected}}(M_{x}=m)
where
m\in\{0,\dots,M\}
.

We can efficiently compute
f_{\text{expected}}(M_{x}=m)
for all
m
(see
section

B.1
for details). Given these values, the function
g
can then be computed numerically. Since
f_{\text{expected}}
decreases with
x
, we first reverse it to obtain a sorted list of
f_{\text{expected}}
values. For a given
\text{ClippedCoverage}_{\text{unnorm}}
input score
s
, we find the index
i(s)
of
s
in the sorted list. The output of
g
is then given by
1-\frac{i(s)}{M}
.

Finally, the normalized metric is:

\text{ClippedCoverage}=g\circ\text{ClippedCoverage}_{\text{unnorm}}

(10)

With
Clipped Coverage
, we recover the desired behavior shown in green in
fig.

3
.

Metric Evaluation Tests

We evaluated metrics across various scenarios, with results summarized in

and

.

(yellow) and

4(c)
and
5(a)
, inspired by figure 5 of

), we progressively replaced both real and synthetic samples with out-of-distribution samples at the same rate.
Since the synthetic set remains ideal in all cases, scores should remain stable and close to their ideal value.
However,
\alpha
-Precision
and

-Recall
(purple),

and

(brown), and

and

(gray) show instability and deviate from the expected value.

and

, and contains a real outlier at

alongside a synthetic out-of-distribution sample at

For fidelity metrics (

), we observe that

(blue) and

-Precision

(blue) is non-symmetric due to the bad synthetic sample, which affects symRecall (green).

-recall
(purple) and

Third, to evaluate sensitivity and interpretability, we progressively introduced bad samples into the synthetic set (

and

).
For the coverage metrics, only

(brown) fails significantly,

(yellow), and

-Precision

).

We evaluated various generative models on CIFAR-10

, ImageNet

, LSUN Bedroom

, and FFHQ
kazemi2014one
using categories and 50000 samples from the data publicly shared by

256\times 256
using center cropping followed by bicubic interpolation downsampling, while FFHQ images were downsampled using Lanczos interpolation.
When possible, for conditional models, we kept an equal number of samples from each class (see
appendix

C
or
stein2024exposing
appendix A for more details).

As can be seen in
fig.

6
, on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, the
Density
values exceed 1, while the
Clipped Density
values remain stable.
Across all datasets, our results consistently indicate that diffusion models outperform GANs.
The range of values our metrics take seems to reflect the size of the training dataset: CIFAR-10 (50k samples, max score
\approx 0.4
), FFHQ (70k, max
\approx 0.4
), LSUN Bedroom (1.5M, max
\approx 0.7
), and ImageNet (14M, max
1.0
).
Interpreting the absolute scores, a value of
0.4
for CIFAR-10 and FFHQ suggests that even top generative models for smaller datasets, which are often the maximum size in practice, achieve results equivalent to 40% of good samples and 60% bad samples.
This highlights substantial room for improvement, an insight only possible because the absolute values of
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
are interpretable.

6
Discussion and conclusion

Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
represent significant advancements over existing metrics in terms of robustness, sensitivity, and interpretability. These improvements result from several key design choices.

To enhance robustness, we balanced the influence of individual samples by clipping their contribution to a maximum of 1. For
Clipped Density
, we further mitigate outlier impact by clipping the
volumes
of real nearest-neighbor balls, preventing overly large balls from dominating the fidelity score. In contrast, for
Clipped Coverage
, we purposefully maintained balls of constant
mass
to ensure balanced contributions. The contribution balancing prevents high-performing samples from disproportionately hiding low-performing ones. This careful handling of sample contributions and ball properties enhances robustness while preserving sensitivity.

For better interpretability, both metrics are calibrated to exhibit linear score degradation with increasing bad sample proportion.
Clipped Density
achieves this through normalization against the real data’s score. For
Clipped Coverage
, whose uncalibrated behavior is non-linear (as shown in
fig.

3
), we derived its expected behavior theoretically and applied a correction to ensure the desired linearity.

This combination of robust aggregation and principled calibration enables
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
to provide not only trustworthy relative comparisons between models but also meaningful absolute scores. This is important in practice, as it allows an assessment of whether a generative model’s output meets a required quality threshold. For instance, the interpretability of these absolute values reveals that generative models for smaller datasets often achieve only a fraction of their potential performance compared to those trained on larger datasets, highlighting significant areas for improvement.

Despite these improvements, some limitations remain.
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
represent an iteration on an accepted benchmark of tests that are progressively passed. This benchmark is not exhaustive, and there might be missing cases. Additionally, while we evaluate fidelity and coverage, other aspects are also relevant, such as
memorization
: are the generated samples simply a reproduction of training samples? Metrics for memorization include GAN-test
shmelkov2018good
, identifiability
yoon2020anonymization
, authenticity
alaa2022faithful
, and the calibrated
l_{2}
distance
carlini2023extracting
.

In conclusion,
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
provide a more trustworthy framework for generative model evaluation, offering robust, sensitive, and interpretable assessments of generative model performance.

Implementation details

B.1
Clipped Coverage

To compute
f_{\text{expected}}
efficiently for all
m
, we begin by precomputing log-gamma values for all
l
between
1
and
M+N+1
. Then, all required beta values and binomial coefficients can be computed as differences of three precomputed log-gamma values:
\log\text{-Beta}(a,b)=\log\text{-Gamma}(a)+\log\text{-Gamma}(b)-\log\text{-Gamma}(a+b)
and
\log\text{-}{n\choose k}=\log\text{-Gamma}(n+1)-\log\text{-Gamma}(k+1)-\log\text{-Gamma}(n-k+1)
.

For the computation of Clipped Coverage, we take an approach similar to that used in the proof of
lemma

1
: we compute the number of the
k
closest synthetic samples to a real sample that are contained within its ball. This can be computed using only nearest neighbor searches, which is more efficient than searching for the synthetic samples within the ball of the real sample.

Appendix H
Broader impact

We introduce robust metrics for generative model evaluation. These metrics highlight that sample quality remains a significant challenge, especially with limited data. By enhancing the assessment of generative models, our work can guide the development of more reliable ones, vital for data-scarce fields like healthcare. Conversely, we acknowledge that such models have the potential for misuse (e.g., deepfakes).

Appendix I
Comparison to a Recent Benchmark

A recent position paper
[
42
]
asserts that "all current generative fidelity and diversity metrics are flawed" and that "no metric is suitable for absolute evaluations." Our work directly addresses these concerns. In this section, we evaluate
Clipped Density
and
Clipped Coverage
on the benchmark introduced in
[
42
]
, using the authors’ original code. All tests in the benchmark are performed on synthetic data, with a default sample size of 1000.

The results are summarized in
tables

2
and
3
, which reproduce the tables from
[
42
]
with
Clipped Density
(Cl-Dens) and
Clipped Coverage
(Cl-Cov) added for comparison.
Here, T denotes a passed test, while
F
indicates a failure. The original benchmark distinguishes between high (H, support-based) and low (L, density-based) diversity metrics. Since we aim to measure coverage, which assesses densities, we interpret L as a success (T) and H as a failure (
F
).

We observed seven discrepancies with the original results, marked with a royal blue underline (
T

or
F
). These differences arise from the benchmark’s seed generation: the seed is based on a hash of the metric name, test name, and repetition index, but Python’s built-in
hash
function changes across different sessions unless
PYTHONHASHSEED
is set. As a result, seeds (and thus results) differ between runs.

Purple underlined entries (
F
) indicate cases where the benchmark’s criterion for success is, in our view, overly strict (see
section

I.1
).
Italic blue entries (
F
) indicate cases where the original implementation counts a failure, but we argue that for low diversity metrics, these should be considered successes (see
section

I.2
).

I.1
Harsh criteria

Purple underlined entries (
F
) correspond to cases where, in our view, the benchmark’s threshold for passing may be too narrow.

In the "Gaussian Mean Difference" test (
fig.

16
), which is analogous to our synthetic Gaussian translation test but without bad samples, the bound desideratum is not satisfied by
Clipped Coverage
in dimension 64 with no translation (0). In this case, the initial and synthetic distributions are the same.
Clipped Coverage
is valued at 0.947, while the criterion requires a value between 0.95 and 1.05.

Figure 16
:
Gaussian Mean Difference
: output from the original code for our metrics.

Similarly, in the "Mode Collapse" test (
fig.

17
), where real data is a mixture of two Gaussians spaced by
\mu
and the synthetic data is a single Gaussian, the value in dimension 64 with no translation (0, same initial and synthetic distributions) is 0.941, just below the same required threshold.

Figure 17
:
Mode Collapse
: output from the original code for our metrics.

I.2
Incorrect criterion

Italic blue entries (
F
) indicate cases where we disagree with the benchmark’s failure criterion.

In the "Mode Dropping + Invention" test (
fig.

18
), real data is a mixture of 5 Gaussians, and synthetic data progressively includes the 5 real modes and then 5 invented modes. Both real and synthetic sets always have 1000 samples.

Clipped Coverage
is marked as failing because it decreases when invented modes are added. However, this decrease is expected for low diversity metrics (density-based): when invented modes are included, real modes become under-covered as the total amount of synthetic data stays the same, so coverage metrics should decrease. Thus, this behavior should be considered as T or L, both a success for coverage metrics.

Mode Dropping + Invention
: output from the original code for our metrics.
