Controllable All-in-One Biomimetic Hollow Nanoscaffold Initiating Pyroptosis-Mediated Antiosteosarcoma Targeted Therapy and Bone Defect Repair
Authors: Qiming Ma, Shenglin Xu, Qian Wang, Yukang Que, Peng He, Rui Yang, Hao Wang, Ziheng Wu, Longze Xiao, Xingshi Yuan, Xingxing Li, Tangbing Xu, Yong Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyroptosis has gained attention for its potential to reinvigorate the immune system within the tumor microenvironment. However, current approaches employing pyroptosis inducers suffer from limitations. They primarily rely on single agents, lack precise targeting, and potentially disrupt the intricate bone formation microenvironment, hindering local repair of tumor-induced bone defects. Therefore, a therapeutic strategy is urgently needed that can effectively trigger pyroptosis while simultaneously promoting bone regeneration. This research introduces an all-in-one construct designed to address these limitations. It combines a cell-camouflaged shell with an autosynergistic reactive oxygen species (ROS) generating polymer. This construct incorporates a hollow core of manganese dioxide (HMnO2) embedded with the photosensitizer IR780 and disguised by the cell membrane of an M1 macrophage. The M1 macrophage membrane grants the construct stealth-like properties, enabling it to accumulate selectively at the tumor site. Upon laser irradiation, IR780 acts as an exogenous trigger for ROS generation while simultaneously converting the light energy into heat. Additionally, the hollow structure of HMnO2 serves as an efficient carrier for IR780. Furthermore, Mn4+ ions released from HMnO2 deplete glutathione (GSH) within the tumor, further amplifying ROS production. This synergistic cascade ultimately culminates in pyroptosis induction through caspase-3-mediated cleavage of gasdermin E (GSDME) upon laser activation. Meanwhile, the depletion of GSH by HMnO2 within the tumor microenvironment (TME) leads to the generation of Mn2+ ions. These Mn2+ ions establish a supportive milieu, which promotes the transformation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) into mature bone cells. This, in turn, promotes the repair of bone defects in rat femurs. Our findings strongly indicate that pyroptosis may be a strategy for osteosarcoma treatment, which presents a robust and versatile approach for targeted therapy and tissue regeneration in this patient population.Keywords: all-in-one nanoplatform, reactive oxygen species, pyroptosis, tumor therapy, bone repair

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) constitutes a significant form of malignant bone malignancy, primarily affecting the pediatric and young adult populations.1 Characteristically, most OS tumors exhibit osteolytic behavior, signifying their destructive capacity toward bone tissue, ultimately resulting in the formation of debilitating lesions.2 The current clinical treatment for OS primarily involves surgical resection combined with neoadjuvant chemotherapy. While this approach has improved patient outcomes to some extent, OS often exhibits high recurrence rates and aggressive bone destruction, significantly hindering treatment effectiveness.3,4 Therefore, there is an urgent need for a novel bifunctional nanoplatform that offers both highly effective antiosteosarcoma therapy and bone regeneration capabilities. Pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death involving inflammation, presents a potentially potent strategy for combating osteosarcoma.5,6 However, crucial questions remain regarding the mechanisms of pyroptosis in OS and how to effectively induce it. Notably, emerging near-infrared (NIR) light-mediated photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) offer unprecedented alternative therapeutic approaches.7,8 These noninvasive methods utilize photosensitizers or photothermal drugs that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) under laser irradiation. It is widely thought that these ROS can activate the caspase-3/GSDME or caspase-1/GSDMD signaling pathways and trigger pyroptosis through mitochondrial damage in cancer cells.9,10

IR780, a unique NIR fluorescent dye approved by the FDA, offers a promising avenue for osteosarcoma therapy.11 Notably, it demonstrates a robust absorption capacity within the optimal NIR I window (700–900 nm) and exhibits exceptional efficiency in converting absorbed light into heat (photothermal conversion).12 Recent research underscores its ability to trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) via a pyroptosis mechanism mediated by both PTT and the generation of reactive oxygen species from external sources.10 Furthermore, the photosensitive nature of IR780 allows for precise spatiotemporal control over pyroptosis induction within the tumor, minimizing the potential for undesirable inflammatory responses in surrounding healthy tissue. Intriguingly, additional studies suggest the potential for integrating photothermal or magnetic nanoparticles (NPs) into materials that actively promote bone formation.13,14 These nanoparticles, upon activation by external light or a magnetic field, can induce hyperthermia within the tumor microenvironment.15 This hyperthermia not only reduces the risk of local tumor recurrence but also promotes osteogenesis, ultimately leading to improved osseointegration. However, a significant challenge lies in designing a biomaterial that can simultaneously achieve both potent antitumor effects and efficient bone tissue regeneration, as these processes necessitate fundamentally different biological environments. Furthermore, the clinical application of free IR780 is hindered by several limitations. These include photobleaching, nonspecific biodistribution, and a short circulation time within the body. Additionally, the inherent hydrophobicity of IR780 renders it poorly soluble in water.16 Consequently, there is an urgent need to optimize IR780 dye formulations to enhance tumor-specific targeting and improve its water solubility for more effective clinical translation.

Biomimetic drug delivery systems utilizing native or cell-derived vesicles, including whole cells, extracellular vesicles (EVs), or cell membranes, have been actively explored to enhance tumor enrichment of photosensitizers and chemotherapeutic drugs.17,18 Research has focused on the extraction of cell membranes from red blood cells, neutrophils, or mesenchymal stem cells to achieve biomimetic functionalization of micro/nanorobots. M1 macrophage cell membrane-based vesicles, acting as natural nanoparticles, hold particular promise due to their preserved proteolipid composition and complex surface proteins.19−21 These features enable active tumor cell identification and binding through cell–cell adhesion, resulting in high tumor targeting potential. An additional challenge in tumor therapy involves the presence of intracellular GSH, which neutralizes reactive oxygen species to maintain cellular redox balance and thus reduce oxidative damage to tumor cells.22,23 To overcome this antioxidant defense mechanism, metal ions with high valence states, such as Fe3+, Cu2+, and Mn4+, are commonly employed to eliminate GSH.24−26 Notably, Mn4+ exhibits superior kinetic and energetic favorability for GSH depletion, particularly within the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment.

In recent years, manganese dioxide (MnO2) nanostructures have emerged as a promising class of therapeutic agents due to their unique ability to modulate the TME. These nanostructures readily react with GSH, a prevalent molecule within the TME, to decompose and generate Mn2+ ions. This process has been demonstrated to enhance the efficacy of antitumor treatment, especially radiation therapy and PDT.27−29 Furthermore, research suggests that the generated Mn2+ ions play a crucial role in several enzymatic systems essential for bone development, such as manganese-specific glycoacyltransferase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase.30 Notably, MnO2 nanoparticles degrade into biocompatible and hydrophilic Mn2+ ions that undergo kidney elimination, mitigating concerns regarding long-term toxicity, unlike nonbiodegradable inorganic nanomaterials.31,32 However, most previously reported MnO2 nanostructures fall under the categories of NPs, nanosheets, or nanocomposites combined with other NPs. These configurations are not ideal for achieving optimal drug loading capacity and precise control over the release of therapeutic payloads. Conversely, hollow nanostructures characterized by mesoporous shells and spacious cavities, exemplified by hollow MnO2 (HMnO2), have demonstrated exceptional potential as drug delivery systems (DDSs).32−34 These structures can efficiently encapsulate large quantities of therapeutic agents, and their release can be meticulously controlled by manipulating the shell structure or incorporating a coating. Intriguingly, the application of HMnO2 nanostructures as intelligent DDSs in OS has yet to be extensively explored, presenting a compelling avenue for future research.

This study proposes a novel all-in-one therapeutic strategy for osteosarcoma treatment and subsequent bone defect repair. The platform incorporates a hydrophobic NIR dye, IR780, adsorbed onto HMnO2 via a combination of electrostatic interaction and Mn–N coordination bonding. Subsequently, M1 macrophage membranes are coated onto the HMnO2 using a straightforward extrusion process. This results in a biomimetic nanoplatform capable of inducing a heat/ROS cascade, designated as HMn-IR@M1. First, the platform’s physicochemical properties were meticulously characterized. Second, its antiosteosarcoma properties were rigorously evaluated. IR780 serves a dual purpose: acting as a potent ROS inducer, generating cytotoxic ROS and significant heat upon laser irradiation. This spatiotemporally controlled mechanism effectively ablates OS cells. Furthermore, the inclusion of HMnO2 provides a crucial hollow scaffold for efficient IR780 loading. Additionally, Mn4+ ions released from HMnO2 deplete GSH, thus further amplifying the pyroptotic effect. This cascade ultimately triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, activates the caspase-3/GSDME signaling pathway, and culminates in the induction of OS-specific pyroptosis. Subsequently, the impact of Mn2+ ions on the maturation of BMSCs were investigated by studying the decomposition of HMnO2 by GSH within the TME. Moreover, in vivo experiments assessed the platform’s efficacy in repairing femoral defects in rats. In conclusion, this research introduces a groundbreaking nanoplatform that triggers a potent antiosteosarcoma response by leveraging pyroptosis through the generation of a substantial ROS cascade and photothermal effects. And the presence of Mn2+ fosters a favorable microenvironment for bone formation, highlighting the platform’s broad potential for therapeutic applications.

Results and Discussion

Establishment and Analysis of the Nanoplatforms

Scheme 1 illustrates a light-activated system designed to amplify ROS production, triggering tumor-specific pyroptosis and promoting subsequent bone regeneration. M1 macrophage-derived cell membrane vesicles boast several advantages for tumor therapy, including ease of modification, excellent biocompatibility, and inherent tumor-targeting capabilities.19 Therefore, the study employed lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation to first obtain M1 macrophages (M1-Raw264.7 cells). Subsequently, cell membranes were extracted from these M1 cells (“M1”) to create the biomimetic system. The preparation of HMnO2 nanoparticles involved a two-step process. First, a hard silica nanoparticle template was utilized to produce a uniform MnO2 layer via a reaction with potassium permanganate (KMnO4). The resulting nanoparticles were then incubated with a sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) solution. This process is believed to involve the reduction of KMnO4 by unreacted organosilica residues on the silica templates. Finally, a simple extrusion process was employed to coat M1 and IR780 onto the HMnO2 nanoparticles, resulting in the biomimetic ROS cascade nanoplatform (HMn-IR@M1) depicted in Figure 1a.

We constructed HMnO2-organic nanostructures (Mn4+–IR780) based on a published protocol, with a mass ratio of Mn4+ to IR780 of 10:2.27,32 To optimize the composition and produce a suitable HMn-IR@M1 platform, we investigated the mass ratio of Mn4+–IR780 to M1. Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were analyzed across a range of feed ratios using M1 concentrations of 10, 50, and 5 μg in 1 mL ultrapure water (Figure 1b). Based on these results, subsequent experiments focused on the manufacture of HMn-IR@M1 at a feed mass ratio of 10:10:2. Analysis revealed the average sizes of HMn@M1, IR@M1, and HMn-IR@M1 to be 180 ± 13, 218 ± 21, and 174 ± 6 nm, respectively. Additionally, their zeta potentials were measured at −12 ± 2, −17 ± 0.5, and −8 ± 4 mV, respectively (Figure 1c,d). Importantly, the average sizes and zeta potential of the synthesis process (sSiO2, sSiO2–MnO2, HMnO2, HMn-IR and HMn-IR@M1) were also analyzed in Figure S1a,b. These negatively charged surfaces contribute to the extended blood circulation of the nanovesicles. Meanwhile, the size distribution of HMn-IR@M1 in Figure S2a,b was uniform. The stabilities of HMn-IR@M1 in PBS with different pH were also assessed, the results revealed that HMn-IR@M1 remained stable for 9 days in PBS with the different pH (Figure S2c,d). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Figure S3) revealed a generally round shape for both HMnO2 and HMn-IR@M1, with the latter additionally exhibiting a discernible lipid layer. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping on HMn-IR@M1 (Figure 1e) confirmed the uniform distribution of elements like Mn, P, and Cl, further supporting the successful construction of this platform. Gel electrophoresis analysis of protein composition (Figure S4a) revealed the successful preservation of the M1 protein structure within HMn@M1, IR@M1, and HMn-IR@M1. Furthermore, Western blotting (Figure S4b) confirmed the retention of crucial membrane proteins (integrin α4, integrin β1, and CD80) on the nanovesicles,21 signifying the successful integration of M1 membranes into the final HMn-IR@M1 construct.

UV–vis spectroscopy further confirmed the composition of HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles (Figure 1f). The absorption spectrum of the HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticle suspension displayed a characteristic peak for IR780 near 790 nm, which was red-shifted compared to both the initial HMnO2 nanoparticle suspension and free IR780 dissolved in ethanol. This redshift indicates the successful encapsulation of IR780 within the hollow shell of HMnO2. The successful encapsulation of M1, IR780, and HMnO2 underwent validation using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Figure 1g). The infrared absorption peaks of the M1/HMnO2/IR780 composite largely resembled those of HMnO2, suggesting that a significant portion of the nanoparticles remained exposed on the material’s surface. However, additional peaks characteristic of cell membrane components (C=O and P–O stretching) were identified at 1436 and 1053 cm–1, respectively. Furthermore, the characteristic C–H absorption peaks of IR780 were evident at 1178, 1020, and 954 cm–1. These findings collectively support the successful synthesis of HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles in this work.

Next, we evaluated the nanoparticle’s photothermal performance. Briefly, temperature fluctuations were measured for solutions containing PBS, free IR780, and HMn-IR@M1 during laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 5 min).35 The temperatures of both free IR780 and HMn-IR@M1 samples significantly increased from 25 °C to around 60 °C, while the PBS control remained unchanged (Figure 1h,i). To further characterize this efficiency, we systematically varied both the concentration of IR780 and the applied laser power density. As illustrated in Figure 1j,k, a positive correlation was observed between each parameter and the temperature of HMn-IR@M1, ultimately reaching a plateau. The preservation of IR780s photothermal conversion efficiency within HMn-IR@M1 was also demonstrably confirmed in Figure S5 and the equations were shown as S(1)–S(4). Based on these observations, we selected specific parameters (IR780 concentration in HMn-IR@M1: 10 μg/mL, laser power density: 1.0 W/cm2, and irradiation time: 5 min) for subsequent investigations into photothermal stability.

Mn4+ possesses the ability to deplete glutathione within the tumor microenvironment, thereby increasing ROS levels and promoting tumor cell death.36,37 To assess the efficacy of HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles in depleting GSH, we employed the 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) assay. As depicted in Figures 1l and S6, a distinct absorption peak at 405 nm was documented upon adding DTNB to a solution containing GSH alone, signifying a substantial presence of GSH. Conversely, a progressive decline in the intensity of the DTNB peak at 405 nm was observed with increasing concentrations of HMn-IR@M1 and HMnO2 nanoparticles. This concentration-dependent attenuation can be attributed to a probable interaction between GSH and Mn4+ ions encapsulated within the nanoparticles. Furthermore, the persistence of a decreasing trend in the 405 nm absorption peak with extended coincubation periods (Figure 1m) underscores the remarkable efficiency of HMn-IR@M1 in depleting GSH. The ROS generating capacity of HMn-IR@M1 was further determined by green fluorescence probe of singlet oxygen sensor. As shown in Figure S7, almost no change in fluorescence intensity was found in solutions containing PBS, HMn@M1, or HMn-IR@M1, indicating that ROS was not produced in the absence of IR780 or laser irradiation. Under laser irradiation, the fluorescence intensity in HMn-IR@M1 dispersion increases significantly with the increase of irradiation time, revealing that the generation of ROS in HMn-IR@M1 upon laser irradiation. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis provided further validation, confirming Mn2+ as the predominant manganese oxidation state following the reaction between HMn-IR@M1 and GSH (Figure S8a,b). To quantify the encapsulation efficiencies and drug loading capacities of Mn4+ and IR780 within the HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles, we employed inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) and UV–vis absorption spectroscopy, respectively. The encapsulation efficiencies for Mn4+ and IR780 were determined to be 78.25% and 74.17%, respectively. Correspondingly, the drug-loading capacities for Mn4+ and IR780 were established to be 35.96% and 7.53%, respectively.

Internalization and Biodistribution of HMn-IR@M1

The critical role of immune cells in regulating tumor progression within the tumor microenvironment is increasingly recognized. M1 macrophages, for example, express CD80, a protein that can interact with specific receptors on tumor cells, facilitating targeted delivery. Inspired by this mechanism, researchers have developed biomimetic nanomaterials derived from M1 macrophages for improved tumor targeting and treatment efficacy.21,38 Herein, in vitro and in vivo investigations were conducted to assess the enhanced tumor cell and tumor accumulation capacity of HMn-IR@M1. Flow cytometry analysis (FCM) confirmed the successful retention and expression of the M1-specific marker protein CD80 on HMn-IR@M1 (Figure 2a,b). Next, we evaluated the in vitro cellular uptake efficiency of HMn-IR@M1 (visualized by red fluorescence of IR780) in tumor cells (stained blue for nuclei) using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). K7M2 cells treated with HMn-IR@M1 exhibited significantly brighter red fluorescence than those treated with PBS, free IR780 or HMn-IR (Figure 2c). This observation suggests superior drug enrichment within the cells, with saturation of the endocytosis process observed after 8 h. The findings from FCM analysis (Figure 2d,e) corroborated the CLSM data, demonstrating a strong correlation between CD80 expression and cellular uptake of HMn-IR@M1. Following this analysis, we determined that an 8 h incubation period for HMn-IR@M1 represents the optimal time frame before laser irradiation in forthcoming cellular studies. Overall, the in vitro data support the feasibility and potential of HMn-IR@M1 for targeted in vivo therapy, as the M1 macrophage outer shell facilitates efficient nanoparticle uptake by K7M2 cells.

We next evaluated the in vivo tumor accumulation potential of HMn-IR@M1 using a small animal fluorescent imaging system. K7M2 tumor-bearing mice received tail vein injections of the nanoparticles, and the biodistribution was monitored over time. As shown in Figure 2f,h, minimal fluorescence was observed around the tumor site in the free IR780 group. In contrast, the HMn-IR@M1 group exhibited significantly stronger tumor-associated fluorescence than the free IR780 group due to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect of HMn-IR@M1. The fluorescence intensity peaked at 24 h, and surprisingly, noticeable fluorescence persisted even at 36 h. This prolonged residence time is likely attributed to the biocompatibility of the cell membrane component, facilitating stable endocytosis by tumor cells and extending the half-life of HMn-IR@M1 in vivo. Collectively, these findings demonstrate the superior tumor targeting and accumulation capacity of HMn-IR@M1. Analysis of intratumoral accumulation data revealed that 24 h postinjection represented the optimal window for initiating laser irradiation in vivo experiments. To further validate the high degree of tumor selectivity exhibited by this biomimetic nanoplatform, ex vivo imaging of tumors and major organs was conducted 36 h after injection. As evidenced by Figure 2g,i, HMn-IR@M1 signals were predominantly localized within the tumor tissues, thus underlining the platform’s remarkable capacity for targeted tumor delivery.

Following the in vitro evaluation of photothermal performance for IR780-loaded nanoparticles, we investigated temperature fluctuations in vivo. 36 h after nanoparticle injection, mice from each treatment group were exposed to laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min. Real-time temperature fluctuations within the tumor areas were monitored using an infrared thermal camera. Compared to the PBS-treated group, animals receiving free IR780 exhibited a modest increase in local tumor temperature, reaching approximately 44.5 °C. Conversely, intratumoral injection of HMn-IR@M1 followed by laser irradiation resulted in a substantial elevation of tumor temperature in mice. Treated tumors exhibited a significant increase from 35.8 to 59.6 °C. This dramatic temperature change signifies both substantial drug enrichment within the tumors and effective photothermal conversion capabilities of HMn-IR@M1 in vivo (Figure 2j,k).

In Vitro Assessment of ROS Production and Pyroptosis Induction

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of HMn-IR@M1, we first employed the MTT assay to determine the appropriate treatment concentrations for inducing cytotoxicity in K7M2 tumor cells. This step aimed to establish effective drug concentrations before investigating the specific antitumor mechanisms of HMn-IR@M1. We designed a series of IR780 concentrations proportional to the drug loading capacities of Mn4+ (∼35.96%) and IR780 (∼7.53%) within HMn-IR@M1 (approximately a 5:1 ratio) to assess the tumor inhibitory potential of the platform. In the absence of laser irradiation, HMn-IR@M1 exhibited minimal cytotoxicity against K7M2 cells, although a slight dose-dependent effect was observed (Figure 3a). However, the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group displayed significantly lower cell viability compared to other groups at equivalent drug concentrations when exposed to laser irradiation (Figure 3b). Our data indicates that laser irradiation triggers the activation of IR780 encapsulated within HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles, leading to the production of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species and consequent oxidative cell death in tumor cells. Notably, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of IR780 on K7M2 cells was found to be approximately 2.0 μg/mL (Figure S9). In addition, in the absence of laser irradiation, HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles were also less toxic to nonpathological cells (hFOB1.19 and BMSCs) in a certain concentration range, indicating good biosafety of this nanoparticles (Figure S10a,b). To visualize the oxidative damage caused by HMn-IR@M1, we employed a live/dead cell assay. Viable cells fluoresce green, while dead or dying cells fluoresce red after treatment. Cells treated with PBS or HMn@M1, with or without laser irradiation, displayed predominantly green fluorescence with minimal red staining (Figures 3c and S11). This indicates that neither Mn4+ alone nor laser irradiation caused significant cell death in K7M2 cells. In contrast, cells in the “IR@M1 + L” and “HMn-IR@M1 + L” groups exhibited pronounced red fluorescence, indicative of oxidative damage. The “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group displayed a higher proportion of red-stained cells, suggesting that the presence of Mn4+ further amplifies the oxidative damage induced by HMn-IR@M1, consistent with the enhanced cytotoxicity observed in the MTT assay (Figure 3d). Based on these results, we selected 10.0 μg/mL Mn4+ and 2.0 μg/mL IR780 for subsequent cellular experiments.

We next investigated whether HMn-IR@M1 could further enhance intracellular ROS accumulation. To achieve this, we employed the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe, which becomes fluorescent green upon reacting with ROS. As shown in Figure 3e,f, green fluorescence was observed in cells treated with IR@M1 or HMn-IR@M1 following laser irradiation. Conversely, minimal green fluorescence was detected in the other groups. Overall, our findings confirm that IR780 can generate significant ROS under laser irradiation. Moreover, the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group exhibited the strongest green fluorescence intensity, indicating that Mn4+ efficiently augments intracellular ROS production. Flow cytometry analysis (Figure S12) corroborated the microscopic observations, revealing the highest ROS levels in K7M2 cells treated with HMn-IR@M1 and exposed to laser light. Tumor cells upregulate antioxidant GSH production when their redox balance is disrupted.39 Elevated GSH levels can counteract the antitumor effects of IR780 by scavenging ROS. We hypothesized that Mn4+ within HMn-IR@M1 might deplete GSH, leading to increased ROS formation, enhanced oxidative damage, and ultimately, improved tumor cell killing (Figure 3i). To investigate this hypothesis, we employed DTNB, a sulfhydryl (–SH) group indicator, to measure intracellular GSH levels. Increased absorbance intensity corresponds to higher GSH content. Cells treated with HMn@M1 or HMn-IR@M1 displayed significantly lower GSH concentrations than the PBS and IR@M1 groups (Figure 3g). These results suggest that Mn4+ within HMn-IR@M1 may indeed be responsible for GSH depletion.

Interestingly, chemodynamic therapy (CDT) is an emerging cancer treatment method, which converts H2O2 into hydroxyl free radicals (•OH) through fenton-like reaction, inducing severe oxidative stress to kill tumors,40 and with the rise of temperature, the efficiency of •OH is significantly improved.41 Therefore, CDT combined with PTT can achieve the effect of “1 + 1 > 2”. Liu et al. found that Mn@CaCO3/ICG@siRNA significantly improves the efficacy of PDT owing to the MnO2-mediated NPs produces more 1O2 than free ICG.42 In addition, Li et al. prepared HA@Cy-Cu NPs by using the coordination-driven assembly of cyanine dyes with Cu2+ ions. Cu2+ in NPs can convert H2O2 to •OH through GSH, thus down-regulating intracellular GSH level and up-regulating significant oxidative damage, showing excellent synergistic PTT/CDT.43 Furthermore, we explored the time- and concentration-dependent effects of Mn4+ on GSH consumption by HMn-IR@M1. The data (Figures 3h and S13) revealed that intracellular GSH levels progressively decreased with longer incubation times and increasing Mn4+ concentrations, supporting a time- and concentration-dependent GSH depletion mechanism by Mn4+.44 Taken together, our findings substantiate the potential of HMn-IR@M1 as a potent synergistic tumor therapeutic agent.

Pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death characterized by ROS-mediated mitochondrial damage and activation of the caspase-3/GSDME pathway, is increasingly recognized as a mechanism for eliminating tumor cells.45−47 We investigated whether HMn-IR@M1 could trigger pyroptosis in K7M2 cells via mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase-3/GSDME activation (Figure 4a). The JC-1 assay was employed to assess the impact of HMn-IR@M1 on mitochondrial activity. Mitochondrial dysfunction is indicated by a shift in JC-1 fluorescence from red (J-aggregates) to green (J-monomers). As shown in Figure 4b, only cells treated with IR@M1 or HMn-IR@M1 and exposed to laser irradiation displayed significant green fluorescence, indicative of mitochondrial damage caused by IR780. Conversely, no signs of mitochondrial damage (red fluorescence) were observed in any group not exposed to laser irradiation. The “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group exhibited the most pronounced mitochondrial damage, likely due to the combined effects of IR780-mediated ROS generation and GSH depletion by Mn4+, further amplifying oxidative stress. These observations from CLSM were corroborated by quantitative FCM analysis of mitochondrial polarization (Figure 4c). A significant shift in the cell population from the red to green fluorescence channels was observed in the “IR@M1 + L” and “HMn-IR@M1 + L” groups, with the latter group displaying the most prominent shift, suggesting a greater degree of mitochondrial dysfunction. The morphological changes in K7M2 cell mitochondria following different treatments were next visualized using TEM (Figures 4d and S14). The “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group displayed the most severe mitochondrial damage, characterized by swelling, membrane rupture, and disruption of cristae. These findings align with the CLSM and FCM data, collectively supporting the ability of HMn-IR@M1 to enhance ROS production and exacerbate mitochondrial damage. Previous studies have found that GSDMD-mediated mitochondrial damage triggers the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), further aggravates pyroptosis and amplifies downstream inflammatory responses.48 Meanwhile, mitochondrial damage also enhanced the ICD induced by pyroptosis and activating the antitumor immune response.49

To validate pyroptosis induction, K7M2 cells were examined following various treatments. Cells in the “IR@M1 + L” and “HMn-IR@M1 + L” groups displayed distinct microscopic hallmarks of pyroptosis, including balloon-like blebbing and cellular swelling, likely attributable to the enhanced mitochondrial damage caused by HMn-IR@M1 during laser irradiation (Figure 4e). Furthermore, the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group exhibited the highest percentage of cells undergoing pyroptosis compared to other groups (Figure 4f). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, a marker of compromised cell membrane integrity, was also employed to assess cell death. As expected, the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” treated cells displayed the highest LDH concentration (Figure S15). To further substantiate the activation of the caspase-3/GSDME pathway, we performed Western blot analysis to examine the expression of key proteins in different groups. Cleaved caspase-3 was abundantly expressed in K7M2 cells treated with “HMn-IR@M1 + L” (Figures 4g and S16). This was followed by an increase in the N-terminal fragment of GSDME, suggesting that aggravated mitochondrial damage may promote pyroptosis via the caspase-3/GSDME pathway.

Promotion of Osteogenic Differentiation In Vitro

Effective osteogenesis and promotion of bone regeneration following surgery are crucial for successful osteosarcoma treatment and bone defect repair. Manganese (Mn) is a critical metallic element found in the bone and cartilage matrix. Numerous studies have demonstrated that Mn ions can stimulate the growth and osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells.50,51 Indeed, Mn2+ plays a vital role in bone development as a key component of several enzyme systems, including manganese-specific glycosyltransferases and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylases.30 In this study, the research team was particularly interested in investigating whether HMn-IR@M1 could promote osteogenic differentiation, given the ability of high tumor GSH concentrations to reduce HMnO2 to Mn2+. To mimic the elevated GSH levels in the tumor microenvironment, BMSCs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 0.2 mM GSH before light or nonlight treatment with PBS, HMn@M1, IR@M1, and HMn-IR@M1. The MTT assay was first employed to evaluate the effects of these various nanoparticle compositions on BMSC proliferation in vitro (Figure S17). The results revealed that, in the absence of laser irradiation, none of the treatments (PBS, HMn@M1, IR@M1, or HMn-IR@M1) exhibited significant cytotoxicity or inhibitory effects on BMSCs proliferation. However, upon laser irradiation, IR@M1 partially suppressed BMSCs activity. Interestingly, HMn-IR@M1 treatment significantly mitigated the cytotoxic effects observed with IR@M1 alone. This observation suggests that Mn2+ generated by the oxidation reaction between HMnO2 and GSH may promote BMSCs proliferation.

To assess the influence of HMn-IR@M1 on the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs, we investigated the expression levels of crucial osteogenic differentiation markers, such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and calcium nodule formation.52 ALP staining revealed a statistically significant enhancement in ALP activity within BMSCs treated with HMn@M1 compared to the control groups (PBS, PBS + L, IR@M1 + L, and HMn-IR@M1 + L). Interestingly, the “IR@M1 + L” group, which incorporated the photosensitizer IR780, exhibited a cytotoxic effect on K7M2 cells but also negatively impacted the ALP activity of BMSCs. It is noteworthy that the ALP activity in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group was comparable to the PBS group, yet demonstrably higher than the “IR@M1 + L” group. This observation corroborates the promotional effect of Mn2+ on the early stages of osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs (Figure 5a,b). Furthermore, calcium nodules, a vital biomarker for evaluating the later stages of osteogenic differentiation on days 14, were visualized using alizarin red staining. As illustrated in Figure 5c, the “IR@M1 + L” group displayed a minimal number of calcium nodules and an almost unmineralized matrix. Conversely, the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group exhibited a noticeable deposition of calcium nodules. Quantitative analysis of the alizarin red staining (Figure 5d) further corroborated these findings, confirming a restoration of extracellular matrix (ECM) mineralization in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group compared to the “IR@M1 + L” group.

Western blot analysis (Figure 5k–m) further corroborated these results, demonstrating that the expression of Runx-2 and OPN proteins in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group was significantly higher compared to the “IR@M1 + L” group and comparable to the PBS group. In conclusion, our study demonstrates that HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles possess a dual functionality. Under laser irradiation, they can induce pyroptosis in osteosarcoma cells. Furthermore, the Mn2+ generated during this process promotes bone regeneration, potentially leading to improved bone repair efficiency while simultaneously inhibiting tumor growth.

In Vivo Assessment of Tumor Growth Inhibition and Pyroptosis Induction

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of HMn-IR@M1, a K7M2 tumor-bearing mouse model was established. As depicted in Figure 6a, five groups (n = 5) received different treatments: PBS control, PBS with laser irradiation, HMn@M1 alone, IR@M1 with laser irradiation, and HMn-IR@M1 with laser irradiation. Laser irradiation (808 nm, 10 min, 1.0 W/cm2) was administered 24 h postinjection via the tail vein. Body weight and tumor size were monitored every 2 days. Notably, no significant differences in body weight were observed between groups (Figure 6b). Additionally, analysis of key blood indicators (uric acid, creatinine, liver enzymes, total bile acid, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase) revealed minimal deviations from the PBS control group (Figure 6c), highlighting the excellent safety profile of HMn-IR@M1. Histological examination (H&E staining) of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) unveiled no apparent damage (Figure S18), further supporting the low systemic toxicity of HMn-IR@M1. Moreover, hemolysis assays demonstrated negligible hemolytic activity (<8%) and preserved erythrocyte morphology across a concentration range of 25–100 μg/mL (Figure S19), indicating good blood compatibility. Pharmacokinetic studies in mice revealed a prolonged circulation time of HMn-IR@M1, crucial for sustained tumor accumulation. Following intravenous injection, blood samples were collected at various time points, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader. As anticipated, HMn-IR@M1 exhibited significantly extended blood retention for up to 48 h (Figure S20), facilitating continuous systemic delivery within the animal model.

TUNEL and H&E staining of tumor tissues revealed a higher percentage of dead cells in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group compared to other groups (Figure 6h). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Western blot (WB) analysis of tumor tissues confirmed pyroptosis induction, as evidenced by increased expression of cleaved caspase-3 and GSDME-N in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group compared to other groups (Figure 7a–c). To gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying HMn-IR@M1-mediated tumor cell killing in osteosarcoma-bearing mice, we performed a comprehensive RNA-seq analysis of tumor tissues. This analysis revealed significant differential gene expression (DEG) in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group compared to the PBS group, with 1859 genes upregulated and 1327 genes downregulated (Figure 7f,g). Functional enrichment analysis of these DEGs identified a significant association with programmed cell death pathways (Figure 7h), with pyroptosis demonstrating the most statistically significant p-value. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) provided additional confirmation of pyroptosis pathway activation in the treated group (Figure 7i), with a normalized enrichment score of 1.64 and a p-value of 0.02. In conclusion, HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles, through their combined effects of oxidative damage and photodynamic therapy, demonstrate promising potential for tumor inhibition and offer an attractive avenue for cancer treatment.

HMn-IR@M1 Promotes Bone Generation in Healthy SD Rats

Due to their potential for concurrent tumor eradication and bone restoration, bifunctional biomaterials are rapidly captivating the scientific community, emerging as a revolutionary approach in the fight against osteosarcoma.53,54 In the context of postoperative treatment for tumor-associated bone defects, the ideal scenario involves eliminating residual tumor cells while promoting bone reconstruction. To evaluate the long-term osteogenic potential of HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles in a healthy environment, a bone defect model was established in the lateral epicondyle of the femur in healthy SD rats. Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) was employed to generate two-dimensional (2D) reconstructions of newly formed bone and to quantify the associated data. Nanoparticles with various compositions (HMn@M1, IR@M1 + L, HMn-IR@M1 + L) were injected into the lateral epicondylar defect site. The PBS and PBS + L groups served as controls (Figure 8a). Micro-CT analysis revealed no significant bone formation in the PBS and “PBS + L” groups around the femoral defect. The HMn@M1 group exhibited the greatest degree of bone formation, while the “IR@M1 + L” group displayed lower bone formation compared to both PBS and “PBS + L” groups, suggesting a potential negative effect on bone. Interestingly, the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group demonstrated recovery in bone formation compared to the “IR@M1 + L” group.

Quantitative analysis of newly formed bone revealed significant enhancement in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group compared to the “IR@M1 + L” group. Bone volume fraction (BV/TV) was 1.8-fold higher in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group (21.4%) than in the “IR@M1 + L” group (11.4%). Similarly, trabecular number (Tb.N) increased by 39.1%, trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) by 36.9%, and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) decreased by 31.8% in the “HMn-IR@M1 + L” group compared to the “IR@M1 + L” group, indicating restored bone structure (Figure 8b). H&E and Masson staining of newly formed bone tissue confirmed these findings. The “HMn@M1” and “HMn-IR@M1 + L” groups exhibited increased bone and collagen regeneration, particularly within the defect area (Figure 8c,d). Immunohistochemical staining for osteogenic markers COL-2, OCN, and Runx-2 further supported these observations (Figure 9a–c). The “HMn@M1” and “HMn-IR@M1 + L” groups displayed stronger positive staining for these proteins, indicating enhanced bone formation capacity. This observation suggests that HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles may, to some extent, counteract the detrimental effects of single PDT or PTT on BMSCs in bone tumors. In conclusion, these findings in healthy rats support the potential of HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles to promote bone regeneration while mitigating the bone-damaging effects associated with IR780-mediated therapies.

Conclusions

We successfully developed HMn-IR@M1, a light-activated theranostic nanoplatform based on M1 macrophage cell membrane vesicles. This platform offers dual functionality for targeted osteosarcoma therapy and promotion of osteogenic differentiation. M1 macrophage membranes endow HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles with high tumor uptake efficiency in osteosarcoma K7M2 cells and K7M2 tumor-bearing mice. Upon endocytosis, IR780 converts laser irradiation energy into heat and ROS. Furthermore, HMnO2 depletes GSH, leading to a further amplification of ROS production. Collectively, these effects culminate in mitochondrial dysfunction and caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis of osteosarcoma cells. On the other hand, in the TME, GSH decomposition of HMnO2 generates Mn2+ ions, which create a favorable milieu for promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and facilitating bone repair in a rat femoral defect model in vivo. This coassembly strategy, incorporating M1 macrophage cell membranes with HMnO2 and IR780, offers an effective solution to the limitations of single PTT/PDT therapies. Meanwhile, the all-in-one therapeutic approach has the potential to significantly accelerate the clinical translation of multifunctional nanoplatforms for targeted osteosarcoma treatment and the repair of tumor-associated bone defects. We anticipate that this model will hold broad applicability in various tumor treatment modalities while simultaneously promoting tissue repair.

Synthesis of the Hollow MnO2 Nanoplatform

Solid silica nanospheres (sSiO2) were synthesized following a previously established procedure with some optimizations.55 Briefly, 500 μL of NH3–H2O, 14 mL of ethanol, and 2 mL of deionized (DI) water were mixed intensely at 50 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of tetraethoxysilane (TEOS) was added dropwise to the mixture, which was then continuously stirred at 150 rpm for 1.5 h at 50 °C. Following purification, the resulting sSiO2 was dispersed in DI water. The nanoparticles were isolated via vacuum freeze-drying for 48 h, yielding solid sSiO2 nanoparticles, which were then stored at 4 °C.

100 mg of sSiO2 nanoparticles were first uniformly dispersed in 100 mL of ultrapure water (1 mg/mL) using ultrasonication. Then, 75 mL of a 10 mg/mL KMnO4 solution was slowly added dropwise under sonication. After magnetic stirring for 6 h (12,000 rpm), the mixture was centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min) and washed three times to obtain the sSiO2–MnO2 precipitate. Subsequently, 2.12 g of Na2CO3 was dissolved in 10 mL of water to prepare a 2 M solution. The sSiO2–MnO2 precipitate was then etched overnight at 60 °C in the 2 M Na2CO3 solution. The resulting HMnO2 were washed and centrifuged three times (12,000 rpm, 10 min) before being freeze-dried for 48 h under vacuum. Finally, the obtained HMnO2 nanoparticles were stored at 4 °C in a refrigerator.

Synthesis of HMn-IR@M1 Nanoparticles

The procedure began with dissolving 50 mg of IR780 in 10 mL of anhydrous ethanol by shaking. HMnO2 was then added to this dispersed phase in an appropriate amount. The solution underwent sonication with an acoustic vibrometer set to 100 W power for 6 min (5 s on, 5 s off). The power was then reduced to 60 W for another 4 min of sonication using the same on–off cycle. Magnetic beads were introduced, and the entire system was stirred in a magnetic stirrer for 24 h under light-protected conditions. The second day involved multiple rounds of washing and centrifugation (10,000 rpm for 5 min) with anhydrous ethanol and double-distilled water, leading to the final product: concentrated HMnO2–IR780 nanoparticles, referred to as HMn-IR.

To create HMn-IR@M1, researchers first obtained M1-Raw264.7 cells by incubating M0-Raw264.7 cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 48 h at a concentration of 100 ng/mL. The cell membrane of the M1-Raw264.7 cells, designated as “M1”, was then extracted using a membrane and cytosol protein extraction kit. The extracted M1 was subsequently dispersed in ultrapure water at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Following this, an appropriate amount of HMn-IR was weighed and dissolved in 9 mL of ultrapure water. To create the final product, 1 mL of a PBS solution containing 1 mg of M1 was mixed with the 9 mL dispersion containing HMn-IR. After repeated vortexing and ultrasonication for 10 min, the mixture was extruded through an Avanti mini extruder with progressively decreasing pore sizes (2 μm, 800, 400, 200, and 100 nm) to obtain HMn-IR@M1. The coated material was purified by differential centrifugal method and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. After removing precipitation, the residual suspension was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm at 4 °C for 30 min to obtain the purified HMn-IR@M1. Notably, both HMn@M1 and IR@M1 were prepared using the same method.

Characterization of HMnO2 and HMn-IR@M1

In a series of characterization techniques, the size and zeta potential of HMn-IR@M1 in various media were first determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument. TEM with a JEOL JEM-2100 revealed the overall morphology of HMn-IR@M1. A FEI Talos F200S TEM was then used to visualize the chemical composition and elemental mapping of the nanoparticles. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) identified the cell membrane proteins associated with each nanovesicle (HMn@M1, IR@M1, and HMn-IR@M1). The encapsulation efficiency of IR780 in HMn-IR@M1 was assessed using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (Edinburgh Instruments, DS5). FTIR spectroscopy (Bruker VERTEX 70) investigated the functional group composition and structure of HMn-IR@M1 nanoparticles. Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS, Agilent 7700X) quantified the amount of Mn4+ encapsulated. Finally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi) examined the change in manganese’s valence state before and after HMn-IR@M1 reacted with GSH.

Photothermal Properties of HMn-IR@M1 In Vitro

In a previous study, an infrared thermal imager (Fotric, 225 sL24) was used to record temperature changes. PBS buffer served as the control. Cells and Culture Conditions

Mouse Leukemic Monocyte Macrophage Cell Line (M0-RAW 264.7) and K7M2 cells were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS), Shanghai, China. BMSCs were collected from 3 week-old female BALB/c mice, as previously reported.56 Briefly, the femora and tibiae were separated, washed, and processed to extract bone marrow. The cells were then seeded into cell culture flasks and maintained in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. At approximately 80% confluence, the BMSCs were trypsinized and subcultured. In Vitro Cellular Endocytosis Studies

CLSM was employed to observe the cellular uptake of HMn-IR@M1. K7M2 cells were seeded onto 35 mm glass-bottomed culture dishes (MatTek) at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated for 16 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with either free IR780 or HMn-IR@M1 (at an IR780 concentration of 10 μg/mL) for various time points (0, 4, and 8 h). After each treatment, the cells were washed three times with PBS. Cell nuclei were then stained with Hoechst 33342. Following rinsing and collection of the cells, flow cytometry (FCM) was used to quantitatively measure cellular uptake.

In Vivo Fluorescence and Photothermal Imaging of HMn-IR@M1

All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the relevant laws and guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Center of The First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) (approved number: 2023 N(A)-0185). To establish K7M2 tumor-bearing mice, K7M2 cells (5 × 106 per mouse) were injected subcutaneously into the right thigh of female BALB/c mice. Once the tumor volumes reached approximately 200 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into two groups and intravenously injected with 100 μL of a solution containing either free IR780 or HMn-IR@M1 at an IR780-equivalent dose of 1.0 mg/kg body weight. To trace the nanomedicine distribution in vivo, the mice were imaged at 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 36 h after injection using a small animal imaging system (PerkinElmer, IVIS Lumina III).

For photothermal imaging in mice, mice with tumor volumes reaching approximately 200 mm3 were intravenously injected with 100 μL of PBS, free IR780, or HMn-IR@M1 (IR780 concentration was 200 μg/mL). After 4 h of administration, a near-infrared laser (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 5 min) was applied. Subsequently, the temperature changes were imaged using an IR thermal camera at different time points (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 min).

Pyroptosis

K7M2 cells were suspended in 2 mL of culture medium and seeded in a 6-well plate. After a 24 h incubation, the cells were treated with various agents (e.g., PBS, HMn@M1, IR@M1, etc.) for 12 h. The treatments included combinations with IR780 and Mn4+ at concentrations of 2 and 10 μg/mL, respectively. For the laser treatment group, cells were irradiated with a laser (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 3 min) 12 h after incubation with the nanoplatforms. Then, cell morphology was examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The percentage of pyroptotic cells (characterized by large, rounded cells with swollen organelles) was determined by counting the number of such cells in an image and multiplying by 100. The activity of LDH in cell secretions was measured using the LDH assay kit. Western blot analysis was performed to detect the levels of pyroptosis-related proteins, Gasdermin E N-terminal fragment (GSDME-N, Abcam, ab215191, 1:1000 dilution), and cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9664, 1:1000 dilution). Details of the LDH assay and WB analysis are provided in the Supporting Information.

In Vitro Osteogenic Differentiation

On days 7 and 14 of culture, cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. For ALP activity, cells were incubated with an alkaline phosphatase chromogenic kit (KGA353, KeyGEN BioTECH) for calcium cobalt staining and a separate ALP detection kit (KGT043S, KeyGEN BioTECH) for quantification. Mineralized nodules were stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS) (KGA363, KeyGEN BioTECH), and the staining intensity was analyzed quantitatively. Stained specimens were imaged using a fully automated stereomicroscope (SteREO Discovery, V20, Germany). To assess osteogenic differentiation, the expression levels of the osteogenic proteins Runx-2 and OPN were measured using immunostaining. Briefly, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Runx-2 (ab76956, Abcam, USA) and OPN (ab63856, Abcam, USA). Subsequently, cells were treated with appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h. Western blot analysis was performed to detect the levels of osteogenic-related proteins, Runx-2 (Proteintech, 20700-1-AP, 1:1000 dilution), and OPN (Proteintech, 25715-1-AP, 1:1000 dilution).

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was used to examine genes related to the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs: Runx-2, osteopontin (OPN). After lysing the cells using TRIzol reagent (Gibco-BRL, Gaithersburg, MD, USA), total mRNA was extracted. A reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan) was then used to convert the mRNA into cDNA. Using the ABI Prism 7300 equipment (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), a real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction was carried out using the SYBR Green PCR mix. In every study, the housekeeping gene GAPDH was used. The 2–ΔΔCt method was used three times to calculate the quantitative outcomes of the experiment. The primers used for each gene are listed in Table S1.

In Vivo Analysis of the Antitumor Effect

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the applicable regulations and standards of the Laboratory Animal Center of The First Affiliated Hospital of USTC (approval number: 2023 N(A)-0185). Female BALB/c mice (healthy, 6–8 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from Skbex Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). To establish K7M2 tumor-bearing mice, K7M2 cells (5 × 106) suspension was subcutaneously injected into the right hind legs of the mice. Once the tumor volumes reached approximately 200 mm3, mice were randomly divided into five groups: PBS, PBS + L, HMn@M1, IR@M1 + L and HMn-IR@M1 + L. HMn@M1, IR@M1, or HMn-IR@M1 were administered intravenously to the mice on day zero at doses corresponding to 5.0 mg/kg Mn4+ and 1.0 mg/kg IR780. Mice in the control group received an intravenous injection of PBS. At 24 h postinjection, mice in the PBS + L, IR@M1 + L and HMn-IR@M1 + L groups were exposed to laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min). Body weight and tumor volume were measured every other day throughout the observation period. After 12 days of treatment, the mice were euthanized, and their major organs and tumors were collected for further analysis. Details on the histological analysis are provided in the Supporting Information.

In Vivo Characterization of Osteogenic Properties

All experiments on female Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats from a commercial supplier in China (n = 3 per group) were approved by the Laboratory Animal Center of The First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTC) and conducted in compliance with relevant ethical guidelines. Eight-week-old female SD rats were randomly divided into five groups: PBS, PBS + L, HMn@M1, IR@M1 + L, and HMn-IR@M1 + L. The distal femur was chosen for creating the surgical defect. Anesthesia was induced intraperitoneally with xylazine (6 mg/kg) and ketamine (70 mg/kg). Under sterile conditions, a surgical approach was performed to expose the femoral condyle through the skin and muscle of the lateral patella via a skin incision. A 3 mm diameter and 5 mm deep hole was drilled through the femoral condyle along the longitudinal axis of the femur using a saline-cooled trephine. The different components of the nanomaterials were mixed with K7M2 cell suspension (5 × 106 cells) containing Matrigel, and 100 μL of the cell-nanomaterial suspension was injected into the femoral defect of rats according to the treatment groups (the concentration of Mn4+ in the HMn@M1 and HMn-IR@M1 groups was 10 μg/mL). The wound was then sutured. After wound healing, the rats received near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation (808 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min). Femurs with bone defects were collected 4 weeks after surgery for subsequent evaluation of bone formation.

Micro-CT and Histological Evaluation

Bone formation at the femoral defect in rats was analyzed using microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) scanning with a SkyScan 1172 Ex Vivo Micro-CT scanner (Belgium). The scans were then used to perform a two-dimensional reconstruction and analysis of the newly formed bone. Micro-CT parameters, including BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Th, and other trabecular bone properties, were computed using CTAn software (Bruker micro-CT software, Kontich, Belgium). Additionally, histology analysis was performed on stained sections using a light microscope.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed Student’s t tests for comparisons between two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for comparisons between multiple groups. Statistical significance is indicated by p-values: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and **P < 0.0001. Specific details about the statistical tests used for each figure are provided in the corresponding legends. GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software was used for all statistical analyses. ImageJ 1.51j8 software was used to quantify image data.
