Carotid stump syndrome in radiation-induced bilateral carotid occlusive vasculopathy
Authors: Xin Yee Liew, Carolyn Orr, Ferry Dharsono, Jerome Freund, David Prentice, Paul Parizel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo document the occurrence of radiation-induced vasculopathy as a delayed complication in the treatment of head and neck cancers.MethodsWe present the case history and review the imaging findings in a 51-year-old man with a history of nasopharyngeal cancer, treated with radiotherapy 10 years prior to admission, who presented with a stroke syndrome. The pathophysiology, clinical and imaging features, and development of collateral pathways due to radiation-induced vasculopathy are discussed.ResultsThe patient had occlusion of the right and left common carotid arteries, as well as of the left internal carotid artery and the right subclavian artery. The proximally occluded vessels ended in tapered “stumps”. CT angiography with Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP) and 3D Volume Rendering Technique (VRT) displayed the collateralisation of the occluded arteries via the thyrocervical trunks and ascending cervical arteries.ConclusionRadiation therapy for head and neck cancer is associated with a significantly increased incidence of radiation-induced vasculopathy and cerebrovascular ischaemic events. Carotid stump syndrome should be considered as a rare but serious cause of recurrent stroke and stroke-like symptomsKeywords: Radiation therapy, Radiation-induced vasculopathy, Carotid stump syndrome, Head and neck cancer, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Common carotid, Internal carotid, External carotid artery

Introduction

We present a unique case of a patient with a successfully treated head and neck cancer who developed multiple radiation-induced arterial occlusions, leading to significant cerebrovascular complications, including multiple ischaemic strokes. We report the clinical findings, imaging features, collateral pathways, and review the current literature on radiation-induced vasculopathy.

Case Presentation

A 51-year-old Cantonese man with a history of nasopharyngeal carcinoma presented with a two-week history of dysphasia, dysphagia and right upper limb numbness and weakness. The patient had a 30-pack year smoking history, and at the time of presentation he was still smoking around 10 cigarettes a day. He was on a low-dose statin for hyperlipidaemia but had no other cerebrovascular risk factors and no known family history of cerebrovascular disease. He had no history of atrial fibrillation, hypertension, diabetes, or previous cerebrovascular events. On admission his HbA1c was 5.5%, with an elevated LDL of 4.2, with BP 170/120s.

Ten years prior, in Hong Kong, the patient had received bilateral head and neck radiation therapy, for treatment of a nasopharyngeal carcinoma (Stage III T2 (bilateral choanae), T3A (left sphenopalatine fossa/pterygopalatine fossa), Union for International Cancer Control Classification III T3N2M0). The radiation field encompassed the primary nasopharyngeal tumour and bilateral cervical nodal regions. He had been treated with a standard curative dosing of 70 Gy over 35 fractions (at 8 MeV), using intensity-modulated radiotherapy (reduced dose to sensitive areas, higher dose to tumour). This was completed on 20th July 2015, with a further boost using an electron field of 4 Gy in 2 fractions. Radiation doses to the supra-aortic vessels were within the normal limits as per International Standards and Australian Standards. The inferior border of the radiation field was located near the subclavian arteries and we speculate these vessels may have received a lower, or peripheral dose rather than full therapeutic dosing. While written records were available from Hong Kong, we were unable to source imaging studies prior to his presentation.

Neurological examination demonstrated right arm weakness and sensory deficit, expressive dysphasia and mild dysphagia. MRI of the brain revealed several acute infarcts scattered in the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) and anterior cerebral artery (ACA) territories, including internal watershed distribution. Although the watershed infarcts could also occur in the setting of haemodynamic compromise, when seen together in our patient with multiple scattered territorial infarcts, the overall imaging pattern appears to be more consistent with an embolic mechanism, or a mixed embolic-haemodynamic process. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed mild left ventricle (LV) hypertrophy. CT angiography (CTA) documented occlusions of the right and left common carotid arteries (CCAs), the left internal carotid artery (ICA) and the right proximal subclavian artery (SCA), all terminating in tapered “stumps” (Fig. 1). There were prominent collateral networks (Fig. 2): anteriorly via the superior thyroid arteries (arising from the external carotid arteries) and the inferior thyroid arteries (via the thyrocervical trunks) and posteriorly via the ascending cervical arteries. The right ICA, both vertebral arteries and the basilar trunk were patent without flow-limiting stenosis or occlusion. No intracranial arterial stenosis was identified. Laboratory workup for vasculitis, including inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) and autoimmune serology (including ANCAs) was negative. Carotid ultrasound subsequently confirmed bilateral CCA occlusion, as well as left ICA occlusion, with the right ICA and ECA, and left ECA receiving perfusion via collaterals.

The case was reviewed in a multidisciplinary neuroradiology meeting. Based on imaging and prior history, the aetiology of the multiple cerebral infarcts and the neck vessel occlusions was deemed to be non-congenital (due to normal bony carotid canals) and likely related to previous radiation therapy and smoking. Although the unavailability of previous imaging does not allow direct documentation of the temporal evolution of the vascular abnormalities, the delayed presentation is consistent with the known latency of radiation-induced vasculopathy. The radiation doses to the carotid were within accepted limits, as per International Standards and Australian Standards. Additionally, the right subclavian artery was likely located at or near the inferior border of the radiation field and we speculate may have received a lower, or peripheral dose rather than full therapeutic dosing.

The patient was prescribed dual-antiplatelet therapy for three months, Aspirin 100 mg daily and Clopidogrel 75 mg daily, followed by lifelong aspirin monotherapy, as well as Atorvastatin 80 mg daily for secondary prevention. He was also counselled on the urgent need for smoking cessation. He was subsequently discharged to another tertiary hospital for further inpatient stroke rehabilitation.

Discussion

The introduction of chemo-radiotherapy for the treatment of head and neck cancer has significantly increased patient survival, with 5-year survival rates reaching up to 85% [1]. While radiation therapy remains a cornerstone in the management of these malignancies, it is associated with a spectrum of short-, medium-, and long-term complications, particularly vascular damage to the supra-aortic vessels. As head and neck cancers often metastasise to regional lymph nodes, the major arteries, including the common carotid arteries and subclavian arteries, are frequently included within the radiation field. As patient survival continues to improve, the long-term effects of radiation, particularly radiation-induced vascular damage and its role in cerebrovascular complications, have become an increasing concern [1]. This damage, which can lead to arterial narrowing, occlusion, and a heightened stroke risk, represents a growing challenge in the management of these patients.

Occlusion of the common carotid artery (CCA) is an uncommon finding, is typically unilateral, and has been identified 1–5% of stroke cases [2]. In a series of 5,400 carotid duplex ultrasound examinations, unilateral CCA occlusion was observed in only 0.24%, with no cases of bilateral occlusion reported [2]. Similarly, in a series of 812 digital subtraction angiograms, only a single case of CCA occlusion was identified [3]. This data highlights the rarity of this condition in clinical practice.

Bilateral CCA occlusion is exceedingly rare, with only seven cases reported in the literature [3]. Five of the seven had strokes (two in the posterior, and three in the anterior distribution), while the remaining two had transient ischaemic attacks. Collaterals were present in all via (i) muscular branches of the vertebral artery to the external carotid, (ii) from the thyrocervical or costocervical trunk to the external carotid, or (iii) from the vertebral artery via the circle of Willis. More recently, a case was reported in which bilateral CCA occlusion developed 10 years post-radiation therapy, presenting with a stroke similar to our case [4].

In addition to fixed vascular occlusion, embolisation from a residual carotid stump can be associated with ongoing or recurrent neurological symptoms. Despite the occlusion, the remaining carotid stump can serve as a source of thrombus formation and distal embolisation, potentially leading to recurrent transient ischaemic attacks and strokes in the ipsilateral cerebral hemisphere. This phenomenon, termed “carotid stump syndrome”, represents an important and often under-recognised mechanism of stroke in patients with carotid occlusive disease [5].

Conclusion

Radiation therapy for head and neck cancer is associated with a significantly increased incidence of radiation-induced vasculopathy and stroke. Routine follow-up with carotid ultrasound and the use of statin therapy for vascular protection may be warranted in these patients.

Despite the known risks, there is no standardised imaging follow-up protocol to detect radiation-induced vascular injury. Additionally, many patients with head and neck cancer belong to an older age group and have pre-existing vascular risk factors, making it difficult to differentiate whether the observed vasculopathy is a result of atherosclerosis, radiation therapy, or a combination of both. In our patient, the extent of vascular involvement and existing literature strongly suggest that radiation therapy has significantly contributed towards his stroke and the development of collateral pathways.
