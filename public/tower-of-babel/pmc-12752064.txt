Overwinter movement ecology of two Sparrow species in southeastern Ohio
Authors: Jacob E Morgan, Kelly A Williams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthropogenic development and land use changes, including habitat loss and fragmentation, have resulted in the decline of many North American birds. Overwintering birds need food resources to meet energetic demands, thermal refugia to reduce energetic costs, and cover to avoid predation. To address how overwintering birds navigate human-dominated landscapes, including the response to a prescribed burn, we used automated and manual radiotelemetry to quantify the overwinter habitat use by Slate-colored Juncos (Junco hyemalis) and American Tree Sparrows (Spizelloides arborea) in southeastern Ohio during the winters of 2023 and 2024. Slate-colored Junco mean home range size was 17.0 ha (95% CI: 15.1–18.9) while American Tree Sparrow mean home range size was 27.1 ha (95% CI: 24.3–30.1). Although habitat use and habitat breadth differed between the two species, both selected edges and used residential areas and roads. Juncos selected or used tall field and forests proportionally, depending on site and year. Slate-colored Juncos used shrublands proportionally but avoided short fields. In contrast, American Tree Sparrows used tall and short fields throughout the winter and we documented a shift in habitat use in response to prescribed fire. Before the prescribed fire, American Tree Sparrows selected shrublands and used forests proportionally, but after the burn, selected burnt fields, used shrublands, and avoided forests. Temporal variation in habitat use by Slate-colored Juncos and American Tree Sparrows highlights the importance in understanding how fluctuation in resource availability due to seasonal variation and management affect the use of habitat patches within the landscape. Background

Anthropogenic development has rapidly changed the landscapes that many species rely on. As a result of human expansion and land use changes, habitat loss and degradation have become leading contributors to biodiversity loss [1, 2]. Because the loss of habitat is often considered a primary catalyst of modern extinction [3, 4], evaluating how species use and move within present-day landscapes throughout the full annual cycle is imperative in conserving declining fauna populations. However, until 2015, fewer than 30% of avian ecology studies focused on nonbreeding seasons, resulting in a limited understanding of habitat use in overwintering bird populations [5]. For migratory birds, poor overwinter conditions can result in carry-over effects that impact success and survival during the subsequent migratory and breeding periods [6–8]. Evaluating how migratory birds use habitats within the overwintering period is crucial for developing effective conservation strategies to ensure the survival and resilience of migratory bird populations throughout the entire annual cycle.

Habitat patch use can be affected by different processes acting at fine to broad landscape scales [9]. Within a patch, resource availability [10, 11] and extreme weather conditions [12], often synergistically, are limiting factors for the survival of overwintering birds. Landscape cover, composition, and structure influence birds’ access to resources [13] and weather exposure [14], as well as the birds’ risk of predation [15]. At broader scales, anthropogenic expansion has resulted in the formation of isolated islands within generally inhospitable landscape matrices [16], further limiting birds’ access to survival requirements in winter. Fragmentation, or loss of patch connectivity, impairs movement, and thus access to resources [17–19], for many bird species. As site fidelity is common among migratory birds [20–22], returning migrants must navigate among these habitat islands to survive.

Migratory birds that overwinter in temperate regions often switch from insectivorous diets to alternative diets (granivory or frugivory) [23, 24] because of the decline in arthropod availability during the overwintering period [25]. Because of this diet change, many overwintering birds rely on grasslands and fields for food resources [26]. These habitats have been reduced by 80% in North America since the 1800s as a result of anthropogenic development, agricultural intensification, and succession from grassland to forests [27, 28]. With the loss of grasslands and fields, birds that rely on these habitats have faced steeper population declines than many other avian guilds [29]. Population declines can be exemplified by two Passerellid sparrows that overwinter in Ohio, Slate-colored Juncos (Junco hyemalis) and American Tree Sparrows (Spizelloides arborea). Slate-colored Juncos (Juncos) breed in the coniferous and mixed forests of northern United States and much of Canada, but winter in weedy fields, near forest edges, and close to roadsides [30]. Junco populations have declined by 42% [29, 31, 32], and American Tree Sparrows (Tree Sparrows) have declined by 53% between 1970 and 2014 [29]. Although Juncos are not considered a species of concern, Tree Sparrows have been classified as a Common Bird in Steep Decline by Partners in Flight [29, 33]. Tree Sparrows breed in the tundra across Alaska, USA and Canada, often near or above the tree line, but winter in fields, marshes, hedgerows, and open temperate forests [34].

The Ohio Bird Conservation Initiative’s Second All-Bird Conservation Plan emphasized the need to understand winter habitat quality, habitat composition, patch size, and patch use for Tree Sparrows [35]. Because Tree Sparrows and Juncos are similarly sized and often form mixed-species foraging flocks during winter, the two species make ideal candidates for a comparative study analyzing overwinter habitat use within the landscape intermix. We employed a combination of manual and automated telemetry to investigate the overwinter habitat use by Juncos and Tree Sparrows, focusing on habitat use within home ranges and roosting areas across three study sites in Athens County, Ohio. We focused on the home range scale as local conditions and habitat structure can have a disproportionate impact on habitat selection within species’ ranges [36, 37], further emphasizing the importance of examining spatial patterns in habitat use at finer scales. Furthermore, because landscape composition and habitat availability can vary between sites, we analyzed each site separately to account for these differences in habitat availability. We predicted that both species would select tall fields and avoid short fields, as tall field habitats provide more cover, food, and thermal refugia than short fields do. Additionally, we predicted that edge and forest habitats would be selected for roosting as these habitats provide thermal refugia for birds to more easily thermoregulate in winter [38–40]. Because Tree Sparrows breed in the tundra, we also predicted that the species would specialize more on large fields and shrub cover, while Juncos would exhibit a wider habitat breadth. By investigating how these species use habitat patches that are distributed within heterogenous landscapes, we can determine what habitats overwintering sparrows use, address habitat breadth tolerances and inform conservation initiatives and landowners on how to better conserve overwintering bird populations.

Methods

Study sites

We conducted our study from January – April 2023 at two study areas (New Marshfield [39.35° N, 82.22° W] and Ohio University Ridges Land Lab [39.32° N, 82.11° W]) and from December 2023 – April 2024 at three study areas (New Marshfield, Ohio University’s Ridges Land Lab, and Woodcock Nature Preserve [39.28° N, 82.22° W]) in the unglaciated Allegheny Plateau region of southeastern Ohio, USA in Athens County. New Marshfield is a rural intermix of privately owned forest, field, and edge habitats with residences and agriculture. Ohio University’s Ridges Land Lab (Ridges), adjacent to the University’s main campus, was previously managed by the Athens Mental Health Facility and consisted of pastures, orchards and second growth forest fragments. Currently, the Ridges is managed by Ohio University and covers 81 hectares of grassland, second growth forests, and university-operated buildings. The Woodcock Nature Preserve (Woodcock Preserve) is a 40-hectare public wildlife preserve near New Marshfield, Ohio. The preserve consists of second growth forests and grasslands. At Woodcock Preserve, grassland habitats are maintained with periodic prescribed burns.

Capture, banding, nanotagging, and automated telemetry

Between 21 January and 13 March 2023, we captured 46 Juncos using baited ground traps and mist nets and fitted 24 of these individuals with nanotags at New Marshfield. We attempted to capture Juncos at our banding station at the Ridges Land Lab (mix of small clearings, second growth forests and tall fields) but we were not successful. In winter 2023–2024, we relocated our Ridges Land Lab trapping station closer to university buildings and a cemetery where Juncos were regularly sighted. Between 2 December 2023 and 18 February 2024, we captured and banded 54 Juncos, nanotagging 37, and 24 Tree Sparrows, nanotagging 22, at all three study sites; however, we were not able to capture both species at all sites. We nanotagged 24 Juncos at New Marshfield, 13 Juncos at the Ridges, and 22 Tree Sparrows at Woodcock Nature Preserve.

We banded each individual with an aluminum U.S. Geological Survey band, 1–3 plastic color bands, and recorded wing chord (mm) and weight (g). We fitted each bird with a Lotek radio-transmitter (NTQB2-5–1 or NTQB2-2; Lotek Wireless Inc., Newmarket, Canada) using a 0.7 mm diameter elastic leg-loop harness made from Stretch Magic (43–44 mm for Juncos, 38 mm for Tree Sparrows). We determined the appropriate harness size by using the allometric function for leg-loop harnesses [41], adjusted based on conversations with experienced researchers. All auxiliary markers, including USGS band, color bands, and radio transmitter weighed < 3% of a bird’s body mass.

Each radio-transmitter emitted an individually identifiable Very High Frequency (VHF) signal at 166.380 MHz with a pulse rate between 13.3 and 25.9 s. The signals emitted by the transmitters were detected by Motus towers at each of our study sites. Motus towers [42] at New Marshfield and Ohio University Ridges Land Lab were installed during the summer 2021. In December 2023, we installed a “mini station” with a sensor gnome and omnidirectional antenna (range 500–1000 m) prior to nanotagging birds at Woodcock Nature Preserve and replaced the mini station with a Motus tower on 27 January 2024. Each Motus tower was equipped with four 5-element Yagi antennae to detect Lotek nanotags within an 8-km radius; however, this range is reduced by topography and other landscape features [43]. By visually inspecting data collected from the Motus towers, we monitored the movement and activity of nanotagged individuals.

Manual radiotelemetry

From 21 January to 14 April 2023, we used manual telemetry (handheld 3-element Yagi antenna and car-mounted omnidirectional antenna attached to an M2 Lotek SRX 1200 radio receiver) to track nanotagged Juncos eight times per week: four mornings (0600–1200) and four afternoons (1300–1800). From 2 December 2023–16 April 2024, we used manual telemetry to track nanotagged Juncos and Tree Sparrows at each field site six times per week. We monitored each site on two mornings (0600–1200), two afternoons (1300–1630), and two nights (1630–2100) during each seven-day period. We randomized the order of surveys each week but did not conduct telemetry when there was moderate to heavy precipitation. During each tracking session, we searched for nanotagged birds starting at the banding/tagging location and expanding our search radius up to 2 km. We chose a 2-km radius for the landscape scale because preliminary studies showed that Juncos moved as far as 0.75 km from the capture location and we wanted to improve the chances of detecting the nanotagged birds. We recorded the location of each detected bird (accuracy within 5 m) on a map and transcribed locations into Google Earth Pro [44]. If an individual bird did not appear to have changed location for more than two days (n = 4 Juncos, 1 Tree Sparrow), we used signal strength and antennae direction from the data collected by the Motus tower to guide manual telemetry efforts to resight the bird or recover the bird’s nanotag. We obtained permission from property owners to search for missing birds and recover transmitters if a bird died (n = 2 Juncos) or a tag was removed by the bird (n = 2 Juncos and 1 Tree Sparrow). We continued tracking nanotagged individuals until all birds departed on spring migration or were no longer detected by manual or automated telemetry.

Landcover classification

We classified landscape features within a 2 km radius circle around each capture location (1257 ha) by overlaying satellite maps [45] in ArcGIS Pro (vers. 3.1.3). We manually classified the satellite imagery as forests, edges, shrublands, tall fields, short fields, residential areas, roadways, or burnt fields (Fig. 1A–C) based on the National Land Cover Database’s landcover classifications and through in-person visual estimates and ground-truthing of satellite imagery. Burnt fields were unique to Woodcock Nature Preserve because a prescribed burn occurred on 8 February 2024.

Aerial view of the A) New Marshfield, B) Ridges, and C) Woodcock Nature Preserve study sites by landcover type (forest, edge, shrubland, tall field, short field, residential, Road, and River). The white diamond represents the location of our banding station at each site

We classified forest landcover as patches dominated by woody vegetation taller than 5 m. We delineated edge as a 4-meter transitional zone between two discrete landcover classes; however, hard edges were observed between some patches (e.g. forests, shrubs, tall fields) bordering anthropogenic habitats (e.g. short fields, residential areas, roadways) and were delimited as 2 meters wide to account for the abrupt transitions typically observed at these boundaries. We adjusted the width of edges as needed to account for observed feathered transitions. We classified shrublands as land dominated by woody vegetation shorter than 5 m (as opposed to shrubs within edges or isolated shrubs within fields). We delineated tall fields as fields with vegetation taller than 25 cm, while short fields had vegetation shorter than 25 cm tall. These short fields were typically hay fields or grazed pastures mowed in late summer or early fall. Although residential lawns had similar height as short fields, we delineated residential areas as a unique habitat class based on the increased disturbance posed by human activity in these areas. Residential areas included houses, barns, and businesses in addition to lawn. Additionally, some landowners maintained bird feeders on private property throughout the winter (personal observations and voluntary reports from landowners within our study areas). We classified roads as paved and gravel roadways, including driveways, present at each study site.

Data analyses

We conducted all analyses using R (vers. 4.3.1) [46]. To account for the dynamic shifts in avian behavior over the full daily cycle, we analyzed home range and roost site habitat use separately based on time of day: diurnal activity (0600–1630) and roost activity (1630–2100). To account for temporal autocorrelation that occurred during sampling, we used the mapped locations and time of detections of nanotagged birds to determine the Autocorrelated Kernel Density Estimate (function akde) for home ranges (95% isopleth) and roost sites (95% isopleth) in the ctmm package [47].

We used continuous time movement model selection (function ctmm.select) in the ctmm package to generate and rank models for each individual based on the presence of temporal and spatial autocorrelation [47]. Continuous-time movement models include Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) models for when autocorrelation is absent, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) models for when positional autocorrelation is present but movement direction changes randomly, and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Foraging (OUF) models for when both positional and directional (velocity) autocorrelation are present. The ctmm.select function also fits models with the appropriate variance structure including isotropic models which assume equal movement in all directions and anisotropic models which allow for directional differences in movement and space use.

We used integrated resource selection functions (iRSFs) in package ctmm (function rsf.fit) to evaluate habitat selection for each individual, where we used the autocorrelated kernel density home range and roost space estimates as the available habitat. Furthermore, we calculated the population-scale habitat selection for each species and site using the function mean.ctmm [47]. We adjusted the weight of data points (debias = TRUE) to account for animal locations that were temporally autocorrelated or had fewer observations, ensuring the results better represented overall patterns. We also excluded birds with fewer than 10 location fixes from our analysis. Due to a small sample size of roost site detections for Tree Sparrows (n = 7), we were unable to analyze roost site habitat selection for the species.

We ran iRSFs for each site separately because landscape composition and proportions vary between sites and habitat availability within the landscape matrix likely impacted habitat use [48, 49]. We ran iRSFs for each year separately as yearly fluctuations in weather conditions can create year-to-year variations in resource availability [50, 51]. To assess overall patterns across winters, we also ran a combined model that included data from both years for birds at New Marshfield. Because of a prescribed burn that occurred on 8 February 2024 at Woodcock Nature Preserve, we ran iRSFs for detections prior to the burn (pre-burn) and after the burn (post-burn) separately because changes in habitat composition could have impacted habitat use in Tree Sparrows (Additional File 2).

The most supported iRSF models varied by winter, site, and species. For winter 2023 (Jan–Apr), an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) model with anisotropic variance and a positional autocorrelation timescale of 9.5 h was best supported. For winter 2023–24 (Dec 2023–Apr 2024), the top model was an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Foraging (OUF) model with anisotropic variance accounting for positional (20.9 h) and velocity (12.2 h) autocorrelation. Across both winters at New Marshfield, the multi-year iRSF for Juncos was also best described by an OUF model (positional = 18.6 h; velocity = 12.7 h), while Juncos at the Ridges were best fit by an IID model with anisotropic variance. For Tree Sparrows, the pre-burn period was best explained by an OUF model (positional and velocity timescales = 22.3 h), whereas the post-burn period was best fit by an Independent and Identically Distributed (IID) model with anisotropic variance. Roosting Juncos at New Marshfield in winter 2023–34 were best modeled with an OU structure (positional = 28.1 h), while those at the Ridges were best fit by an IID model, both with anisotropic variance.

Using scaled individual iRSF outputs, we calculated the Euclidean dissimilarity of habitat use between Juncos and Tree Sparrows using the vegan package [52]. We performed our ordination using non-metric dimensional scaling with no transformation to the original dataset. Our best solution was three dimensions with a stress of 8.7%. To visualize the relationships in habitat use between each species, we plotted the 95% confidence ellipses based on the standard deviation of weighted averages, as well as the total space for each species using convex polygons. To test the hypothesis that habitat use between Juncos and Tree Sparrows differed, we ran a Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA) via the function adonis2 in the package vegan [52]. We ran 5000 permutations using species as a predictor variable. We used the betadisper function, a multivariate analogue of Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances, in the package vegan [52] to test the hypothesis that habitat breadth between species did not differ.

Results

Using manual telemetry, we mapped 1442 detections of Slate-colored Juncos (mean days per bird ± SE; 52.5 ± 3.55 days) on 56 survey days. On average, we detected Juncos every 30.4 ± 1.6 hours. Using manual telemetry, we mapped 1543 detections of Juncos (41.7 ± 2.8 days) and 579 detections of American Tree Sparrows (27.6 ± 3.6 days) on 111 survey days. On average, we detected birds every 55.5 ± 5.7 hours. The mean overwinter home range size for Juncos was 17.0 ha (95% CI: 15.1–18.9) while Tree Sparrow’s had a mean home range size of 27.1 ha (95% CI: 24.3–30.1). There was moderate variation in home range area for Juncos (CoV = 0.38) but Tree Sparrows exhibited no detectable variation among individuals (CoV = 0). The landscape included primarily forest cover (Range: 37–73%) but included all other identified landcover types (Table 1).

Home range habitat use

During both winters at New Marshfield, Juncos selected edges (β = 0.74 ± 0.36), used tall fields (β = 0.16 ± 0.30) and shrublands (β = 1.31 ± 1.91), and avoided short fields (β = −0.95 ± 0.60). Forests, residential areas, and roads were excluded from this model because the estimated selection coefficients were near zero, indicating use within proportion to availability. However, we observed temporal shifts in habitat use by year. Winter 2023, Juncos (n = 23) selected forests (β = 1.42 ± 1.12), edges (β = 1.83 ± 1.25), tall fields (β = 1.57 ± 1.17), and residential areas (β = 1.18 ± 1.16; Fig. 2A) at New Marshfield. Shrublands, short fields, and roads were excluded from the top population-level model because the estimated selection coefficients were near zero, indicating use within proportion to availability. During the winter of 2023–24, Juncos (n = 22) habitat selection differed from the previous year at New Marshfield, where Juncos selected edges (β = 1.01 ± 0.52) and residential areas (β = 0.42 ± 0.33) but avoided short fields (β = −0.89 ± 0.70; Fig. 2B). Forests, shrublands, tall fields, and roads were excluded from this model because the estimated selection coefficients were near zero, indicating use within proportion to availability.

At the Ridges, where the landscape included more impervious surface and increased human disturbance, Juncos (n = 13) selected edges (β = 0.87 ± 0.77 and tall fields (β = 1.10 ± 0.36) but used short fields proportionally (β = −0.41 ± 0.46); Figure 2C). Shrublands, tall fields, residential areas, and roads were excluded from this model because the estimated selection coefficients were near zero, indicating use within proportion to availability.

Habitat use for evening roost sites

During the winter of 2023–24, we mapped 554 detections of Juncos (mean days per bird ± SE; 15.0 ± 1.15 days) during 50 roost sampling periods. At New Marshfield, Juncos (n = 16) selected edges (β = 1.79 ± 0.86), tall fields (β = 1.79 ± 0.52), and forests (β = 0.47 ± 0.41), but avoided short fields (β = −2.28 ± 1.72; Fig. 3A). Discussion

We provide insight into the habitat selection of two sparrow species during the overwintering period. Slate-colored Juncos and American Tree Sparrows used edges more than expected within home ranges and Juncos used edges more than, or in proportion to, available habitat during roost periods. Both species used forests, residential areas, and roads within the home range. Although habitat selection among the two species overlapped, sensitivity to habitat patch size and home range size needs may have influenced the difference in overwinter presence between the two species [53]. For example, at the only site where we captured Tree Sparrows (Woodcock Nature Preserve in winter 2024), the largest tall field patch (8.1 ha) was much larger than the tall field patches at the other sites (New Marshfield and the Ridges; 0.71 and 0.31 ha respectively). With increasing landscape changes in the United States [54] and increased fragmentation [55, 56], understanding how birds use habitats within heterogeneous landscapes is necessary to sustain overwintering bird populations. Our study adopts a landscape ecology approach at the home range and roost site scale, emphasizing how spatial composition, configuration, and patch heterogeneity influence habitat use. This framework recognizes that ecological processes operate differently across scales [9, 57], and that finer scale landscape structure can strongly influence habitat selection.

The mean overwinter home ranges for Juncos was 17.1 ha (95% CI: 15.1–18.9 ha) and included forests, edges, tall fields, and residential areas. Similarly, Juncos home range size in Illinois was between 17 and 33 ha [58]. For Tree Sparrows, a species in which winter home range size has not been well documented, mean overwinter home range was 27.1 ha (95% CI: 24.3–30.1 ha) and included shrublands, edges, and tall fields. With a smaller habitat breadth (Fig. 4) and larger space requirement, Tree Sparrows may be more susceptible to habitat fragmentation than Juncos.

As predicted, based on our current understanding of overwintering Juncos [30] and Tree Sparrows [34], both species used tall fields in proportion to, or more than, available habitat within the home range. Tall field habitats often yield more seeds than short, grazed fields [59, 60]. Although foraging in dense grass swards can increase visual obstruction of food resources and predators [61], tall field habitats can provide more beneficial microclimates than short fields [62] for birds that need to mitigate energetic constraints caused by the exposure to extreme weather conditions in the winter [63]. Tall and weedy fields likely provided a reliable food source and cover from inclement weather conditions for the overwintering sparrows.

Edge habitats were selected by both species within winter home ranges and were used or selected by Juncos during roost periods. Tree Sparrows used edges in proportion to available habitat after the prescribed burn which aligned with our prediction that edge habitats would be selected to provide food, cover, and thermal refugia. Many species that rely on seeds during overwinter periods, such as Coal Tits (Parus ater), Blue Tits (Parus caeruleus), Great Tits (Parus major), Marsh Tits (Parus palustris), and Chaffinches (Fringilla coelebs), are found near edges or use edges to balance predator avoidance and foraging efforts [64]. Edge habitats can provide thermal benefits to birds using grasslands in the winter as wooded edges create temperature gradients along grassland-woodland transitions [38] that act as thermal buffers [39]. Juncos and Tree Sparrows likely used edge habitats to combat energetic constraints caused by harsh weather while balancing the trade-offs between resource acquisition and predator avoidance.

Both before and after the prescribed burn, Tree Sparrows used shrublands more than expected. Our results align with findings of Christmas Bird Count data that Tree Sparrows use brushy shrub-grassland habitats during the winter [33, 65]. Furthermore, Tree Sparrows breed at or above the tree line where woody vegetation is often limited to shrublands [34, 66]. Habitat fidelity, in which species use the same habitat type across the full annual cycle, can allow birds to decrease foraging effort due to the familiarity of the habitat [67]. Shrubland habitats can also better facilitate temperature regulation than short fields in extreme thermal conditions [62, 68], acting as thermal refugia when thermoregulatory costs in winter are high. However, the amount of early successional land cover, including shrublands, have decreased in the United States [69, 70], which may pose further risks for Tree Sparrows during the overwinter period.

After the prescribed fire, Tree Sparrows shifted habitat use to avoid forests and select burnt fields. We also observed other species foraging with Tree Sparrows including American Robins (Turdus migratorius), Eastern Bluebirds (Sialia sialis), and American Goldfinches (Spinus tristis) foraging in the burnt fields (Additional File 2). Mid-season shifts in habitat associations have been recorded in many species including in Yellow Wagtails (Motacilla flava) as habitat quality declines [71] and in Swainson’s Warbler (Limnothlypis swainsonii) after prescribed fires changed landcover composition [72]. In tropical savannas, early dry season prescribed burns increased foraging activity in granivorous birds, like the Gouldian Finches (Chloebia gouldiae), by removing litter and exposing mature grass seeds [73]. Other studies report that species, like Savannah Sparrows (Passerculus sanwichensis), displayed increased selection for recently burnt fields during the overwintering period [74]. The exploitation of post-burn patches has been observed in other ground-foraging bird species like American Robins [75]. The midwinter burn at Woodcock Nature Preserve likely exposed resources and increased the foraging efficiency [76]. Tree Sparrows likely avoided forests after the burn because the recently burned fields offered greater resource accessibility.

As predicted, Juncos used forest habitats more than expected (habitat selection) or in proportion to availability in the home range and roost sites. Using forests in winter limits exposure to weather by buffering extreme conditions more than non-forested habitats [40]. The use of forest patches by Juncos during the day is likely a result of the birds seeking more ideal microclimate conditions and cover from predators during the winter. Furthermore, during the breeding season, Juncos are found in forests and along forest edges [30, 77]. Similar to how Tree Sparrows used shrubland habitats, habitat fidelity, paired with the microclimatic benefits, likely influenced the Juncos’ use of forest cover within roost sites. Furthermore, because telemetry was not conducted during moderate or heavy precipitation, our dataset cannot provide insight on the use of forest habitats during those periods.

Juncos either avoided short fields or used short field habitat in proportion to availability as predicted, depending on the site and year. In contrast, Tree Sparrows used short field habitats likely because of habitat fidelity linked to the species breeding grounds in the tundra. Short field habitats, like those used for agriculture, limit the amount of resources available for overwintering granivorous birds [78]. Grazed and mowed fields likely do not offer enough resources to support overwintering seed-eating bird populations, as many granivorous bird species avoid grazed and sown fields [79]. Additionally, short fields provide little to no cover for birds to avoid predators or weather. Other granivorous sparrows, like Field Sparrows (Spizella pusilla) and Song Sparrows (Melospiza melodia), display preferences for vegetative cover during the winter, regardless of food availability [80].

Juncos and Tree Sparrows either selected or used residential areas in proportion to available habitat. Juncos and Tree Sparrows are known to visit birdfeeders regularly throughout the winter [30, 34]. With the exception of the Ridges, multiple birdfeeders were present on the landscape throughout the winters our study occurred. Juncos are highly susceptible to weather conditions in the winter, often increasing birdfeeder visitation when daily temperatures drop below average [81]. Furthermore, access to supplemental food in thermally challenging environments can increase overwinter survival for Juncos [32]. Juncos are often found in human-disturbed areas, even when birdfeeders are not present on the landscape [30]. The use of residential areas by Juncos and Tree Sparrows may be the result of foraging behaviors tied to weather-based energetic costs.

Juncos and Tree Sparrows used roads in proportion to available habitat. Our results coincide with previous documentation that Juncos are often found along roadsides during winter months [30], as we found Juncos foraging on or near roadways. Many bird species forage for grit [82] on roadways and can forage for salt [83] during the winter on roads treated with de-icing salts. Additionally, paved roads store solar heat that can benefit the birds thermally [82], even when roadways can impose mortality risks on birds, including predation [84] and vehicle collision [85].

Conclusions

The management of adequate habitat is necessary to support overwintering bird populations. The quality of overwinter habitat impacts the physical condition and survival of birds [86], with poor quality conditions creating carry-over effects that may decrease reproductive success on the breeding grounds [8]. Habitat quality and resource availability can also fluctuate yearly due to temporal variations in weather [50, 51] and habitat management practices [87]. As winter site fidelity can occur with Slate-colored Juncos [21] and American Tree Sparrows [88], the loss of overwinter habitat can be detrimental to returning migratory sparrows. Due to potential spatiotemporal shifts in habitat availability and quality, we observed shifts in the habitat use of Slate-colored Juncos and American Tree Sparrows within and between seasons. These observations highlight the need to assess habitat availability and changes across broader landscapes, quantify the specific resources birds use, and evaluate the habitat needs of other overwintering species that share these environments to better understand community-level habitat associations. Understanding these patterns can directly inform conservation strategies that address habitat quality, availability, and connectivity across multiple spatial scales.

Anthropogenic development and agricultural expansion in the United States has resulted in an 80% loss of grasslands since the 1800s [27]. More than 163,812 km2 of land in the United States is dedicated to lawns, parks, golf courses, and athletic fields [89], and lawn coverage increases annually by 8000 km2 [90]. Given these widespread changes in habitat availability and structure, research on habitat use and resource requirements provides a critical foundation for effective conservation action. Our study highlights the need to conserve and restore grassland and shrubland habitats and reduce short fields and lawns to provide overwintering sites for Slate-colored Juncos and American Tree Sparrows. Furthermore, the conservation and restoration of larger, unfragmented tall fields and shrublands should benefit overwintering Tree Sparrows. Converting residential lawns to habitat by increasing native plant diversity on residential properties increases fauna biodiversity and survival [91]. Developing management strategies that maintain and enhance tall field and shrub cover across residential and agricultural lands can provide essential resources for multiple overwintering species, supporting community-level biodiversity and informing targeted, landscape-scale conservation planning.
