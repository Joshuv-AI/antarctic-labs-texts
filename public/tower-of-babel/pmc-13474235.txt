The COMPASS-CAT risk assessment model for predicting in-hospital venous thromboembolism in patients with breast, lung, colorectal, and ovarian cancer: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hualin Wei, Wei Han, Xianbo Zhang, Jing Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the predictive accuracy of the comparison of methods for thromboembolic risk assessment with clinical perceptions and awareness in real-life patients with cancer-associated thrombosis (COMPASS-CAT) risk model against the established Khorana score for predicting in-hospital venous thromboembolism in patients with solid tumors.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 482 inpatients with breast, lung, colorectal, or ovarian cancer. Venous thromboembolism risk was stratified using the COMPASS-CAT and Khorana scores. The primary outcome was objectively verified in-hospital venous thromboembolism. Discriminative performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis.ResultsOf the 482 patients, 53 (11.0%) developed in-hospital venous thromboembolism. The COMPASS-CAT model (score ≥7) demonstrated higher specificity (98.13% vs. 81.77%) and positive predictive value (82.22% vs. 35.00%) compared with the Khorana score (score ≥2). The COMPASS-CAT model exhibited significantly better overall discrimination, with an area under the curve of 0.913 versus 0.695 for the Khorana score (p < 0.001). Univariate logistic regression revealed that a COMPASS-CAT score ≥7 was strongly associated with elevated venous thromboembolism risk (odds ratio = 20.66, 95% confidence interval: 9.96–42.83). A Khorana score ≥2 was also associated with an increased risk, but the magnitude was weaker (odds ratio = 3.26).ConclusionThe COMPASS-CAT model exhibited a promising discriminative trend over the Khorana score for identifying the risk of in-hospital venous thromboembolism. Its notably higher specificity and positive predictive value suggest that it may serve as a helpful supplementary tool for targeted thromboprophylaxis in the inpatient oncology setting.Keywords: Cancer, venous thromboembolism, risk assessment, COMPASS-CAT, Khorana score, hospitalized patients, cancer-associated thrombosis

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) represents a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among patients with cancer. Given the delicate balance between thrombotic risk and bleeding complications in this population, accurate risk stratification is paramount for guiding effective and safe thromboprophylaxis strategies. 1

Currently, the Khorana score serves as the standard risk assessment model (RAM) for cancer-associated thrombosis; however, it was originally derived from ambulatory patient cohorts. Subsequent validation studies have reported inconsistent predictive performance, often characterized by limited specificity and poor discrimination. Crucially, the Khorana score may fail to capture the dynamic and elevated risk profile of hospitalized patients, who face additional risks related to acute illness and immobilization.2,3

To address these shortcomings, the comparison of methods for thromboembolic risk assessment with clinical perceptions and awareness in real-life patients with cancer-associated thrombosis (COMPASS-CAT) model was developed, incorporating a broader range of variables, including cancer-specific therapies and cardiovascular comorbidities. 4 Despite its potential, comparative data in the inpatient setting remain sparse. Although the COMPASS-CAT model has theoretical advantages in capturing the multifactorial nature of VTE risk in patients with cancer, its predictive performance in the specific context of hospitalized patients remains underexplored. Hospitalized patients with cancer face compounded thrombotic risks from immobilization, acute illness, invasive procedures, and central venous catheter use, factors that may enhance the discriminative power of the COMPASS-CAT model. Dedicated validation studies in the inpatient setting are urgently needed to inform evidence-based thromboprophylaxis strategies. Therefore, the present study aimed to validate the discriminative performance of the COMPASS-CAT model for predicting in-hospital VTE in patients with solid tumors and to compare its predictive accuracy with that of the traditional Khorana score. 5

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The present study was a retrospective analysis based on a prospectively maintained institutional cancer-associated thrombosis registry at the Department of Oncology, Hebei General Hospital (Shijiazhuang, China). The establishment of this prospective database and the subsequent use of its anonymized data for retrospective analyses were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hebei General Hospital (Approval Number: 20190354; granted on 17 May 2019). Because of the observational nature of this specific retrospective analysis and the use of deidentified registry data, the requirement for additional written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Ethics Committee. For the current study, electronic medical records and registry data for eligible patients admitted between October 2020 and October 2023 were extracted and analyzed between January 2024 and June 2024 by authorized investigators.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2024. All patient data were completely deidentified before data extraction and analysis to ensure confidentiality. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 6 To minimize information bias, a blinded adjudication process was employed: Investigators calculating risk scores were blinded to VTE outcomes, and radiologists interpreting imaging studies were blinded to the patients’ risk scores.

Study population

Consecutive adult inpatients (aged ≥18 years) admitted to the Department of Oncology between October 2020 and October 2023 were recruited to the present study. Eligible participants had histologically or cytologically confirmed malignancies of the breast, lung, colorectum (colon or rectum), or ovary. To ensure the validity of risk assessment, inclusion was restricted to patients who received active systemic anticancer therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy) during the index hospitalization and underwent routine lower-extremity venous duplex ultrasonography for VTE screening. At our institution, routine lower-extremity venous duplex ultrasonography is performed consecutively for all admitted oncology patients at baseline before the initiation of systemic therapy, irrespective of symptom presentation. Therefore, VTE screening was systematic rather than symptom-driven. Only patients with complete clinical data required for calculating both the COMPASS-CAT and Khorana scores were included.

Patients were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (a) presence of multiple concurrent primary malignancies; (b) unclear or indeterminate pathological diagnosis; (c) history of VTE diagnosed before the index admission; (d) clinically suspected VTE that was not confirmed by objective imaging studies; (e) therapeutic anticoagulation initiated before admission for indications other than VTE (e.g. atrial fibrillation); or (f) incomplete medical records or missing data elements required for risk score calculation.

Outcome definition

The primary endpoint was in-hospital VTE, defined as a composite of newly diagnosed deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and/or pulmonary embolism (PE) occurring between hospital admission and discharge. DVT was objectively confirmed by compression venous duplex ultrasonography, with diagnostic criteria requiring noncompressibility of the deep venous segments. PE was diagnosed by computed tomography pulmonary angiography demonstrating intraluminal filling defects within the pulmonary arterial tree. In patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast, high-probability ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scintigraphy served as the diagnostic alternative. All imaging studies were interpreted by board-certified radiologists. To ensure data accuracy, VTE events were independently adjudicated by two investigators through a comprehensive review of medical records, radiology reports, and discharge summaries. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third senior investigator.

RAMs

Risk stratification was performed for all eligible patients using both the COMPASS-CAT model and the Khorana score. Scores were calculated using clinical and laboratory data obtained within 24 h of hospital admission. This early time point was selected because it reflects the clinically relevant time for initial risk stratification and thromboprophylaxis decisions, allowing clinicians to act prospectively rather than retrospectively.

The COMPASS-CAT model integrates cancer-associated and patient-specific risk factors into a weighted scoring system. Points are assigned as follows: anthracycline- or platinum-based chemotherapy (6 points); recent emergency admission (5 points); personal history of VTE (4 points); presence of a central venous catheter (3 points); stage IV malignancy (3 points); time since cancer diagnosis ≤6 months (2 points); platelet count ≥350 × 109/L (2 points); presence of cardiovascular risk factors (2 points); antiangiogenic therapy (2 points); recent hospitalization for acute medical illness within the previous 3 months (1 point); and hormonal therapy (1 point). A cumulative score of ≥7 was classified as high risk for VTE.

The Khorana score stratifies risk based on five baseline characteristics: cancer site (2 points for stomach or pancreas; 1 point for lung, lymphoma, gynecological, bladder, or testicular cancer); platelet count ≥350 × 109/L (1 point); hemoglobin <100 g/L or use of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (1 point); leukocyte count >11 × 109/L (1 point); and body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35 kg/m2 (1 point). In alignment with recent comparative literature, a cutoff score of ≥2 was used to define the high-risk cohort.

Data collection

Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were comprehensively extracted from the electronic medical record system of the hospital. Collected variables included demographic details (age, sex, and BMI), tumor-related characteristics (cancer type, clinical stage, and time since diagnosis), comorbidities (with specific attention to cardiovascular risk factors), and treatment exposures (chemotherapy regimen, antiangiogenic agents, hormonal therapy, and central venous catheter use). Laboratory parameters, including hemoglobin levels, platelet counts and leukocyte counts, were recorded using the first values obtained on admission.

Thromboprophylaxis practices

During the study period (October 2020 to October 2023), mechanical thromboprophylaxis (e.g. graduated compression stockings) was encouraged but not uniformly documented in the medical records. Pharmacological thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin was not routinely administered to all hospitalized patients with cancer at our center. The decision to initiate pharmacological prophylaxis was made on an individual basis by the attending physicians after balancing the risk of thrombosis against the risk of bleeding, particularly in patients with thrombocytopenia or recent invasive procedures, and also considering the economic burden on patients. This practice pattern likely contributed to the observed VTE incidence of 11.0% in our cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± SD and were compared using the unpaired Student's t-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

The discriminative performance of the RAMs was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the curve (AUC), along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), was calculated for both models. Diagnostic metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and likelihood ratios, were derived.

To evaluate the strength of the association between risk scores and VTE events, univariate logistic regression models were fitted separately for the COMPASS-CAT and Khorana scores, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. As the present study was designed to validate and compare prespecified, published models rather than to derive a new model, the original scoring algorithms were applied without modification. Consequently, variable selection procedures, collinearity diagnostics (e.g. variance inflation factors), and model recalibration (e.g. the Hosmer–Lemeshow test) were not performed. All statistical tests were two sided, and p <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Data analyses were conducted using the Free Statistics software platform (version 2.1; Beijing Qingsi Technology Co., Ltd.; Beijing, China; https://www.clinicalscience.cn/).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 482 hospitalized patients were included in the final analysis. The cohort had a mean age of 64.5 ± 12.4 years, and 39.2% of the patients were male. Lung cancer was the most prevalent malignancy (53.9%), followed by colorectal (21.2%), ovarian (11.8%), and breast cancer (10.6%). The majority of patients (82.8%) had advanced disease (clinical stage III–IV). During hospitalization period, 53 patients (11.0%) developed VTE, comprising 45 cases of isolated DVT, 5 cases of isolated PE, and 3 cases of concurrent DVT and PE. Detailed baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared with in patients without VTE, those who developed VTE exhibited significantly higher rates of cerebrovascular disease (20.8% vs. 0.7%; p < 0.001) and central venous catheter use (24.5% vs. 7.9%; p < 0.001). Additionally, the VTE group had a higher prevalence of abnormal laboratory parameters, including platelet counts >350 × 109/L (15.1% vs. 2.8%; p < 0.001), hemoglobin levels <100 g/L (30.2% vs. 8.2%; p < 0.001), and leukocyte counts >11 × 109/L (18.9% vs. 6.8%; p = 0.002). Regarding risk stratification scores, a high-risk COMPASS-CAT score (≥7) was identified in 69.8% of patients with VTE compared with only 1.9% of non-VTE patients (p < 0.001). Similarly, a high-risk Khorana score (≥2) was more frequent in the VTE group (79.2% vs. 18.2%; p < 0.001).

Diagnostic performance

The diagnostic accuracy metrics for both RAMs are presented in Table 2. Using the standard cutoff of ≥7, the COMPASS-CAT model demonstrated high specificity of 98.13% (95% CI: 96.37–99.17%) and a PPV of 82.22% (95% CI: 67.95–91.49%). In contrast, the Khorana score (cutoff ≥2) exhibited considerably lower specificity (81.77%) and PPV (35.00%), although its sensitivity was numerically higher than that of the COMPASS-CAT model (79.24% vs. 69.81%). Notably, both models maintained excellent NPVs (>96%), indicating good reliability for ruling out VTE.

In the univariate logistic regression analysis (Table 3), patients with a COMPASS-CAT score ≥7 had a markedly increased risk of VTE (OR = 20.66, 95% CI: 9.96–42.83; p < 0.001) compared with patients with lower scores. Discussion

The present study demonstrated that the COMPASS-CAT model outperformed the Khorana score in predicting in-hospital VTE in patients with breast, lung, colorectal, and ovarian cancer. The observed VTE incidence of 11.0% in the present cohort is notably higher than the 2%–5% typically reported in ambulatory cancer populations.7,8 This discrepancy likely reflects the distinct risk profile of hospitalized patients, who face compounded thrombotic risks related to acute illness, immobilization, and invasive procedures. 9 The present findings are consistent with recent epidemiological data from Asian populations with cancer, which challenge the historical perception of lower VTE risk in this demographic by demonstrating a rising incidence comparable to that in Western cohorts. 10

The COMPASS-CAT model achieved excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.913) compared with the Khorana score (AUC = 0.695). Its superior performance was primarily due to higher specificity (98.13% vs. 81.77%) and PPV (82.22% vs. 35.00%). These findings have important clinical implications. Only 35% of patients classified as high risk by the Khorana score developed VTE, compared with 82% of those identified by the COMPASS-CAT model. Universal thromboprophylaxis based on the Khorana score would treat a number of low-risk patients, potentially causing unnecessary bleeding complications and substantial economic burden.1,2 Clinically, the high specificity (98.13%) and PPV (82.22%) of the COMPASS-CAT imply that it can effectively identify the minority of patients who truly require thromboprophylaxis, thereby minimizing “false alarm” treatments and unnecessary exposure to bleeding risk. Conversely, the high NPV (>96%) observed for both models provides clinicians with confidence to withhold anticoagulation in low-risk patients, thereby avoiding unnecessary bleeding complications without compromising safety.

The limitations of the Khorana score observed in the present study are consistent with findings from other comparative analyses. For example, the Vienna Cancer and Thrombosis Study demonstrated that adding biomarkers such as D-dimer and P-selectin improved prediction over the Khorana score alone.11,12 Similarly, the prophylaxis of thromboembolism during chemotherapy trial (PROTECHT) score, which added chemotherapy agents into the Khorana model, has shown improved discrimination but still lacks the comprehensive assessment of patient-specific comorbidities found in the COMPASS-CAT model. 13

The exceptional discriminative performance of the COMPASS-CAT model observed in the present study (AUC = 0.913) is notably higher than the AUCs typically reported in ambulatory validation cohorts. We hypothesize that this discrepancy arises from a distinct site and selection bias inherent to our study design. Our cohort was heavily weighted toward advanced-stage disease (82.8% stage III–IV) and patients undergoing intensive inpatient interventions, directly amplifying the prevalence of variables that are heavily weighted in the COMPASS-CAT model. Therefore, this high AUC represents the model's performance in a concentrated, high-risk inpatient demographic, rather than generalized performance across unselected outpatient populations. Furthermore, although the application of ambulatory-derived RAMs to hospitalized patients requires careful methodological justification, hospitalization itself is a potent transient risk multiplier. In the absence of universally validated inpatient-specific oncology RAMs, testing the boundaries of comprehensive tools such as the COMPASS-CAT model in this setting provides critical real-world data for clinicians facing immediate prophylactic decisions.

Our findings are consistent with those of recent comparative studies. Abdel-Razeq et al. 5 reported that the COMPASS-CAT model outperformed the Khorana score in patients with lung cancer receiving chemotherapy and immunotherapy, although the absolute AUC values were lower (0.65 vs. 0.56). Spyropoulos et al. 14 reported that the COMPASS-CAT model showed modest discrimination (AUC = 0.63) in ambulatory outpatients. The higher AUC observed in the present study likely reflects the inclusion of hospitalized patients with higher baseline VTE risk and multiple concurrent prothrombotic factors.

The COMPASS-CAT model may perform particularly well in hospitalized patients because it incorporates treatment-related factors (chemotherapy type, antiangiogenic agents, and central venous catheter use) and cardiovascular comorbidities. 4 In the present cohort, central venous catheter use was a significant predictor (OR = 3.12), highlighting the importance of procedure-related risk factors in the inpatient setting. This finding is consistent with previous reports showing that central venous catheters are a major contributor to VTE in hospitalized patients with cancer. 15 Furthermore, the study population included patients receiving newer therapies. Recent evidence suggests that immune checkpoint inhibitors are independently associated with an increased risk of VTE, a factor not captured by older models such as the Khorana score but partially accounted for through the treatment-related variables included in the COMPASS-CAT model.16,17 Crucially, the Khorana score lacks variables that reflect acute in-hospital hazards. By contrast, the COMPASS-CAT model specifically weights “central venous catheter” and “recent emergency admission,” factors ubiquitous in hospitalized oncology care. The present data confirms that these procedure-related and acute-status variables are potent drivers of VTE risk in inpatients, explaining the better performance of the COMPASS-CAT model over the ambulatory-based Khorana score.

The findings of the present study support targeted thromboprophylaxis strategies. A COMPASS-CAT score ≥7 identified a high-risk subgroup (PPV 82.22%) that would likely benefit from pharmacological prophylaxis. Current guidelines increasingly support the use of direct oral anticoagulants for thromboprophylaxis in high-risk patients with cancer, offering an effective alternative to low-molecular-weight heparin. 18 However, bleeding risk remains an important clinical consideration. Although hypertension, abnormal renal and liver function, stroke, bleeding history or predisposition, labile international normalized ratios, elderly, and drugs or alcohol concomitantly (HAS-BLED) and other bleeding risk scores are available, their performance in patients with cancer receiving anticoagulation is often suboptimal, necessitating careful individualized assessment. 19 The high NPV (>96%) for both models in the current study suggested that patients at low risk have a very low probability of VTE and may not require intensive surveillance, thereby avoiding unnecessary bleeding risk. The superior specificity of the COMPASS-CAT model also allows more precise targeting of thromboprophylaxis, potentially reducing the number needed to treat (NNT) and avoiding the costs and bleeding risks associated with treating false-positive patients, which were more common with the Khorana score. Although a formal cost-effectiveness analysis was beyond the scope of this retrospective study, we believe that preventing VTE events in the high-risk group may offset the costs of prophylactic anticoagulation, particularly given the substantial economic burden of treating established VTE.

The present study has several strengths. Consecutive hospitalized patients who underwent systematic VTE screening using objective imaging were included, ensuring the diagnostic accuracy of VTE events and minimizing misclassification. Both models were calculated using admission data, thereby simulating real-world clinical decision making. Furthermore, a direct head-to-head comparison was performed within the same study population. A notable finding of the present study is the high discriminative ability of the COMPASS-CAT model (AUC = 0.913), which exceeded that reported in several previous studies conducted in ambulatory settings. This discrepancy may be attributable to the specific characteristics of the study population. The present study exclusively included hospitalized patients, who inherently have a higher baseline risk of VTE because of factors such as immobilization and acute illness, potentially amplifying the predictive value of risk factors included in the model. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that both the COMPASS-CAT and Khorana scores were originally developed for ambulatory patients with cancer. Applying them to an inpatient population represents a real-world assessment of their utility in a different clinical context. For example, assigning the “recent emergency admission” variable to a patient currently hospitalized requires a clear and consistent definition, which in the present study referred to an emergency admission preceding the current hospitalization. This highlights the need for RAMs specifically developed and validated for the inpatient setting.

Notably, the present study also has several important limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations and healthcare systems. VTE risk profiles and hospitalization practices may differ in Western populations and other Asian regions; therefore, regional validation is warranted. In addition, the findings are strictly applicable to the inpatient setting and should not be extrapolated to ambulatory outpatients, where the risk dynamics differ. Second, given the relatively small number of observed VTE events (n = 53) in our cohort, we deliberately restricted our analysis to univariate logistic regression. According to the widely accepted “Events Per Variable (EPV)” rule in statistical modeling, performing multivariable analyses with numerous clinical variables in this context could lead to severe statistical overfitting and unstable parameter estimates. Consequently, we were unable to adjust for all potential confounding factors (e.g. age, clinical stage, and central venous catheter use). Furthermore, although calibration assessments (e.g. the Hosmer–Lemeshow test) are valuable for novel models that generate continuous probabilities, the present study aimed to validate a pre-existing scoring system using an established, strict binary cutoff. Therefore, our univariate findings and traditional binary diagnostic metrics should be considered exploratory.

Third, and critically, risk scores originally developed for ambulatory patients were applied to an inpatient cohort. This poses challenges in interpreting certain variables and underscores that the high performance observed may be specific to this clinical setting. Furthermore, our inclusion criteria required patients to have undergone routine venous duplex ultrasonography. This requirement may have inadvertently selected a cohort with a higher baseline suspicion or risk of VTE, contributing to the high incidence rate (11.0%) and the exceptionally high discriminative performance of the COMPASS-CAT model. This selection bias means our results may not be generalizable to unselected inpatient populations. Finally, the retrospective design prevented the granular collection of detailed pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis, including dosing, duration, and subsequent minor bleeding events, which directly impact the net clinical benefit of any screening tool. Future prospective validation studies incorporating comprehensive analyses of anticoagulation therapy, bleeding outcomes, and cost-effectiveness modeling are strictly required to justify the implementation of COMPASS-CAT–guided prophylaxis in routine inpatient care. 20

Prospective, multicenter validation studies are needed to confirm these findings across diverse populations and clinical settings. Future studies should evaluate whether COMPASS-CAT–guided thromboprophylaxis improves clinical outcomes without increasing bleeding complications. Risk prediction models should also incorporate emerging biomarkers, such as circulating tumor DNA and tissue factor, which have shown promise for improving VTE prediction. 21 Cost-effectiveness analyses are also essential, as VTE markedly increases the economic burden of cancer care. 22 In addition, patient-centered research should address preferences regarding thromboprophylaxis by balancing the benefits of VTE reduction against the inconvenience and risks of anticoagulation. 23

In conclusion, the COMPASS-CAT model demonstrated promising discriminative performance compared with the traditional Khorana score for predicting in-hospital VTE in patients with breast, lung, colorectal, and ovarian cancer. The model’s enhanced specificity and PPV of the model facilitate more precise identification of high-risk individuals, thereby optimizing the selection of candidates for targeted thromboprophylaxis. Although further validation in multicenter cohorts is warranted, these findings suggest that the COMPASS-CAT represents a robust and potentially helpful tool for refining VTE risk stratification in hospitalized oncology population. Future research should also investigate whether COMPASS-CAT–guided risk stratification can be used to tailor the intensity and duration of thromboprophylaxis, moving beyond a simple binary decision to initiate or withhold prophylaxis, thereby optimizing individualized anticoagulation strategies.
