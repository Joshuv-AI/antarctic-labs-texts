Evaluation of myocardial fibrosis and cardiac function in fibrotic lung diseases: a prospective study
Authors: Süleyman Ekiz, Kaan Esen, Sibel Naycı, Yüksel Balcı, Hasan Hüsnü Yüksek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research sought to elucidate the possible fibrotic effects of lung diseases accompanied by fibrosis on the heart with mapping methods. In this prospective study, 35 patients with fibrotic lung disease and 17 healthy volunteer participants with no known disease were enrolled. Myocardial T1 relaxation value, lung parenchymal T1 relaxation value, diastolic dysfunction measurements were performed in the patient and control groups with a 1.5 T MR device. ECV value was calculated only in the patient group due to ethical concerns. In patients with fibrotic lung disease, myocardial native T1 relaxation value was statistically significantly higher in the patient group compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Myocardial ECV value was found to be higher in patients with fibrotic lung disease compared to literature data. In the patient group, the native T1 relaxation value of the lung parenchyma in the area thought to be morphologically fibrotic was statistically significantly higher than the native T1 relaxation value of the lung parenchyma in the control group (p < 0.001). The native T1 relaxation value of the morphologically normal lung parenchyma in the patient group was significantly higher than the lung native T1 relaxation value of the control group (p < 0.001). High myocardial native T1 relaxation values ​​and ECV in the patient group suggest that fibrotic lung diseases, like other systemic diseases affecting the heart, may also affect the myocardium. The development of cardiac MRI techniques will facilitate the effect of fibrotic lung diseases on myocardial fibrosis.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is currently the most commonly used imaging modality for soft tissue imaging. Furthermore, functional assessment of myocardial motions, mapping techniques and extracellular volume measurements make cardiac MRI an indispensable imaging method in evaluating myocardium1. The non-invasive imaging method used in the diagnosis of myocardial fibrosis today is cardiac MRI. The methods used in MRI are T1 mapping and post-contrast myocardial uptake2. Fibrotic lung diseases (FLD) are a group of diseases that progress with chronic progressive fibrosis in the lung. Although the etiology of the disease is not known, it has been stated that smoking, environmental exposure, and genetic predisposition may be necessary in etiology. It is known that fibrosis is progressive in fibrotic lung patients, and the average life expectancy is five years. FLD are usually observed in the 6th–7th decades and may be accompanied by many comorbid diseases due to the patient’s age. Studies have observed that FLD may be associated with cardiac diseases3. In recent years, studies have been conducted on the pathophysiological pathways of fibrosis in FLD4,5. Inflammation, oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction are three common mechanisms in the pathophysiology of chronic fibrotic disorders4. We hypothesize that myocardial fibrosis may also develop in FLD due to the systemic effects of these mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective study was conducted on participants presenting for cardiac MRI to assess possible cardiac involvement in patients followed with fibrotic lung disease between September 2022 and February 2023.

Due to their clinical and radiological compatibility, histopathologic diagnosis was not required for the patients with FLD included in the study. Patients with history of MI, pulmonary hypertension, kidney disease, known cardiomyopathy, sarcoidosis, known valvular heart disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), allergy to contrast material, history of cardiotoxic drug use, claustrophobia, thought to be unable to cooperate with breathing and command, patients with pacemakers or implanted cardiac devices, pregnant or suspected pregnancy, and patients under the age of 18 were excluded from the study. The control group consisted of individuals with no contraindications to MRI and no known lung or heart disease.

There are no studies in the literature related to our study. Therefore, based on the power analysis conducted by Saunders et al6. For the difference in expiratory breath-hold (inspiratory T1 (1.11–1.22) to be statistically significant between the patient and control groups, a minimum of 34 cases per group should be studied, assuming a Type I error of 5% and a test power of 80%.

Ninety-eight patients with FLD were evaluated for the study. 60 patients were excluded because they did not fulfill the study criteria, 2 patients were excluded because they could not establish breathing coordination during extraction, and 1 patient was excluded because of arrhythmia (Fig. 1).

This prospective, controlled study enrolled 35 patients with FLD and 17 healthy volunteer participants without known disease. Regarding gender distribution, 22 of the patient group were male and 13 were female, and 11 of the volunteer group were male and 6 were female. The mean age in the patient group was 61.69, while it was 34.76 in the volunteer group (range 18–80 years). All participants and/or their legal guardians have provided informed consent forms. The study protocol was approved by Mersin University non-interventional clinical research ethics committee (Date: 22.06.2022 Decision No: 429). Our study was supported by the Scientific Research Projects Unit of Mersin University under code 2022-2-TP3-4767. All procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2008. The authors affirm that human research participants provided informed consent for publication of the images in Figures. The data that support the findings of this study are not openly available due to reasons of sensitivity and are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Data are located in controlled access data storage at Mersin University Medicine Faculty Hospital.

MRI protocol

All imagings were performed with 1.5 T MR device (Siemens Magnetom Aera; Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 32 channel matrix coil. Retrospective ECG triggering method was used for imaging.

Multislice T2 HASTE images were acquired to evaluate the overall anatomy with parameters: TE 27 ms, TR 700 ms, field of view (FOV) 263 × 360 mm, matrix 160 × 320, slice thickness 6 mm, distance factor 20%, flip angle 160˚, transverse orientation, bandwidth 710 Hz/px.

The classical MOLLI technique suitable for the 533 model was used for T1 mapping. According to increasing TI times, 8 images showing sequences were obtained and T1 map was obtained from these images. The parameters of the sequence used in T1 mapping were; TI value 100, 180, 535, 895, 985, 1220, 1760, 2280 ms, TE 1.1 ms, TR 277 ms, FOV 341 × 400 mm, matrix 144 × 256, slice thickness 8 mm, distance factor 20%, flip angle 35°, orientation transverse, bandwidth 1085 Hz/px.

Late phase contrast-enhanced images were obtained at 7 min after intravenous administration of 0.1 mmol/kg gadoterate dimeglumine (Dotarem; Guerbet, Aulnay-sous-Bois, France) at a rate of 3 mL/s. After contrast, TI scout images were obtained first. Afterwards, 2-chamber and 4-chamber PSIR images were created using a breath-hold and phase-sensitive inversion recovery (PSIR; Phase-Sensitive Inversion Recovery) sequence for late contrast imaging. Post contrast T1 mapping of the myocardium and lung was performed at 15 min.

Pre- and postcontrast T1 value from myocardium, current hematocrit value and pre- and postcontrast blood T1 value were used for extracellular volume measurement. T1 mapping was performed for the lung and heart. In T1 mapping of left ventricule, 2 short axis images were taken from the apex and midventricular level.

The patient’s available CT images in the system were evaluated before T1 mapping in the lung. The area thought to be morphologically fibrosis was identified. (Fig. 3) The patient group underwent manual ROI measurement from the morphologically normal area and the area thought to have fibrosis. (Fig. 4) In the healthy group, single voxel manual ROI measurement was performed.

Two-chamber, 4-chamber and short axis cine images were acquired using the TRUEFISP technique. Magnitude and phase images were obtained using phase contrast MRI Venc value which was determined during flow imaging automatically by the device. The resulting images were evaluated during acquisition and the optimal Venc value was determined manually during acquisition. The Venc value was set between 80 and 130. TMF curves were obtained and classified into 4 types. (Fig. 5) E/A ratio and left atrium volume were used to differentiate the obtained curves. Two-plane area-length method was used for left atrial volume measurement. At the end of systole, LA area and AP length were calculated manually from 2 to 4 chamber images for both four chambers (A1-L1) and 2 chambers (A2-L2) and LA volume was obtained with the formula 0.85xA1xA2/L. Patients with pulmonary hypertension according to the Echo results available in the system were excluded from the study and therefore pulmonary venous flow evaluation was not performed.

All images were uploaded to Syngo.via (Siemens Healthcare), a server-provided imaging software, for analysis. Measurements were performed separately by two radiologists with 4 and 15 years of experience.

Statistical analysis

Patient data collected within the scope of the study were analyzed with the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) for Windows 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) package program. Frequency and percentage for categorical data and mean and standard deviation for continuous data were given as descriptive values. For comparisons between groups, the “Independent Sample T-test” was used for two groups, and the “Pearson Chi-Square Test” was used to compare categorical variables. The results were considered statistically significant when the p-value was less than 0.05. The agreement between the two radiologists was calculated by “Intra Class Correlation” (ICC). Linear regression models were created to explain age-related changes in myocardial T1 values due to the age difference between the patient and control groups. Since our dependent variable, T1 values are continuous, Linear Regression models are created by adding both Age and Group (Patient and Control) as independent variable to the model, adding only Age and also, adding only Group as independent variable, in order to determine which explains the myocardial T1 values better.

The correlation between the cardiac native T1 relaxation value and ECV in fibrotic lung group patients, the native T1 relaxation value of the lung parenchyma with fibrosis, and the native T1 relaxation value of the normal lung parenchyma was calculated using bivariate Pearson correlation analysis.

Discussion

This research, investigating the possible cardiac effects of lung diseases with fibrosis, determined that the global native myocardial T1 relaxation values ​​of the left ventricular wall were higher in the patients with fibrotic lung disease than the control group. Various cardiac diseases can affect T1 relaxation time. T1 relaxation time is prolonged in conditions that cause myocardial edema and fibrosis. Acute myocardial infarction and myocarditis are the leading acute clinical pathologies that cause edema in the myocardium. Amyloidosis may lead to prolonged T1 relaxation times secondary to interstitial deposition of amyloid protein and expansion of the extracellular space. Myocardial fibrosis occuring in cardiomyopathies and chronic myocardial infarction is one of the main reasons of prolonged T1 relaxation time. Studies in the current literature have shown that systemic diseases such as pulmonary hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and chronic renal failure also prolong myocardial T1 relaxation time7,8. None of the above mentioned pathologies were present in our patient group. However, no sufficient clinical studies investigating the possible fibrotic changes in the myocardium due to fibrotic lung diseases have been published. Although dysfunction of left and right ventricule and pulmonary arterial hypertension in idiopatic pulmonary fibrosis have been reported, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of T1 mapping analysis and ECV quantification of myocardium in fibrotic lung diseases8–11. Murtha et al. stated that cardiac and pulmonary fibrosis are different in etiology, but developmental processes have many similarities12. It is generally thought that the common theme in both fibrosis is that it develops through fibroblasts and inflammatory cells. As an expected result, the extracellular matrix shows a significant increase. Fibrotic lung diseases that are the subject of our study are generally seen in the 5th-6th decades, and the average age of our patient group is also high. At this point, it is necessary to mention the effect of a physiological process such as aging on T1 relaxation time and the pathological processes discussed above. Studies in the current literature show that age and gender can also affect T1 relaxation time, but no definitive consensus exists and the results are conflicting. The mechanism of myocardial fibrosis in the senescent myocardium may be the increase of collagen fibrils in the aging heart13. Age was significantly associated with T1 indices for men in the study reported by Liu et al.14. Although the general belief is that T1 relaxation time prolongs with age, Rosmini et al. reported that myocardial T1 relaxation time is slightly lower with increasing age and stated that T1 relaxation time is longer in women than men15. In another study, conducted by Özcan et al., lower T1 values were detected in women over 50 age than those under 5016. In our study, myocardial T1 values were found to be not statistically significantly elevated with age. The change of ECV with aging is also controversial. ECV was independent of age in the study of Rosmini et al15. Ugander et al. showed a continuous relationship between age and the ECV of ″normal appearing″ myocardium17. As a result, due to these different data in the literature, it is not possible to reach a definitive conclusion about the effect of changes that may develop in the myocardium with aging on the results of our study.

Increased ECV is often due to excessive collagen deposition and is a robust measure of myocardial fibrosis apart from amyloid18. Native T1, post-contrast T1 ​​and hematocrit values ​​are the basis for calculating ECV. The advantages of ECV are that it is standardized with the hematocrit value and can be estimated quantitatively, independent of the patient and the device19. While T1 relaxation time generally varies in conditions affecting the myocardium, ECV varies only in extracellular pathologies. Therefore, ECV is more critical in the case of myocardial fibrosis. Studies have reported that ECV might be used as a prognostic factor in determining mortality of myocardial infarction20,21. However, the requirement for using contrast material for ECV measurement and the 15-minute wait after contrast material administration may limit its use in daily practice22. Since contrast material could not be administered to the control group due to ethical reasons in this study, it was calculated only in the patient group. So, the data we found in the patient group were compared with the literature. Garg et al. reported that the normal ECV value was 25.3 ± 3.5%23. In the current study, the mean ECV value in the patient group was calculated as 26.9 ± 3.48%, which was higher than the literature data.

The use of T1 mapping in the lung is limited. T1 mapping has been used in cystic fibrosis patients to detect early regional pulmonary disease in children24. In another study performed in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients, a significant difference between T1 values of fibrotic lung tissue and morphologically normal lung tissue was detected both before and post-contrast images25. Saunders et al. compared the T1 mapping technique performed with the Look-Locker technique in patients who could not cooperate in breathing with a control group. They reported that the T1 relaxation time was shorter in IPF patients, whereas in our study, we used the T1 mapping technique during deep inspiration6. T1 relaxation time in the fibrotic segments was statistically higher than the normal segments in the patient group (p = 0.001). Additionally, the native T1 value of the morphologically normal-appearing lung parenchyma in patients with pulmonary fibrosis was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.001). Therefore, in patients with pulmonary fibrosis, early interstitial changes in morphologically normal parenchyma, might prolonge T1 relaxation time before visible fibrotic changes. However, for clinical benefits more supporting data are needed.

In the current study, diastolic dysfunction was detected in 22 patients in the patient group, and no diastolic dysfunction was detected in the control group. It is well known that diastolic left ventricular function is altered substantially with aging in healthy people26. However, myocardial fibrosis is also among the causes of diastolic dysfunction11,27,28. In case of myocardial fibrosis, the relaxation and contraction of the ventricles are impaired due to increased myocardial stiffness29. Therefore, it does not seem possible to reveal the real cause of diastolic dysfunction in our patient group with a mean age of 61.69 and who did not have diseases that could affect the heart such as diabetes or cardiomyopathy.

Limitations

The most important limitation of our study is the difference in mean age between the patient and control groups and the small number of patients. The age difference between groups was addressed using statistical methods, and it was demonstrated that the age difference had no effect.

Another limitation is that the ECV value was only measured in the patient group. Our data has been compared with data from the literature. It is thought that more useful data could be obtained from studies where ECV measurements were performed in the control group.

Our study has demonstrated that myocardial involvement may occur in FLD patients; however, there is no evidence of long-term effects and histopathological findings. In light of the findings we have presented in the literature, future studies may be conducted to investigate long-term effects and histopathological data.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the relationship between lung and myocardial fibrosis in patients with fibrotic lung disease. The higher myocardial T1 relaxation values ​​and ECV in the patient group suggest that, as in other systemic diseases affecting the heart, lung diseases with fibrosis might affect the myocardium. Cardiovascular causes are the 2nd most common cause of death in patients with FLD. We think that clinical and pharmacological studies on this subject will benefit the mortality and morbidity of patients.
