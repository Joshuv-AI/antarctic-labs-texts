Clinical evidence on integrated Chinese-Western medicine for rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Yanfang Ma, Shouyuan Wu, Xuan Yu, Hui Liu, Zijun Wang, Yandong Feng, Ke Xu, Yaolong Chen, Zhaoxiang Bian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChinese medicine (CM) and the integrated Chinese-Western medicine (ICWM) have been recognized and commonly used in the management of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). This study aimed to leverage the AI-empowered Clinical Evidence for Integrated Chinese-Western Medicine (ACE-iMed) platform to identify clinical evidence on CM and ICWM for RA, categorize and assess it by literature type, and provide evidence summaries.MethodsFollowing the ACE-iMed methodology, we first constructed a dedicated literature database for RA and identified clinical questions (CQs) and outcomes of interest to clinicians in the management of RA. Furthermore, for each specific CQ, we performed target searches and screening within the database, extracted data, assessed risk of bias/methodological quality, performed data analysis and provided evidence summaries.ResultsFollowing literature searches and deduplication, 11,635 records were used to construct the RA and CM literature database. Across 18 questions covering ten major CM interventions and two comparisons, 556 records were ultimately identified, including 17 clinical practice guidelines/consensus statements, 35 systematic reviews, and 504 randomized controlled trials. Seven (38.9%) of the 18 CQs were supported by evidence from all three literature types. This study selected and presented evidence summaries for two CQs. Details and summaries of included studies for all CQs are available on the ACE-iMed platform.ConclusionThis study and relevant ACE-iMed platform provide a structured overview of clinical evidence on both CM and ICWM questions regarding RA, which can support clinicians in accessing evidence rapidly and identifying evidence distribution patterns and research gaps, and may inform future research and evidence-based practice.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, ongoing, systemic autoimmune inflammatory disease primarily affecting synovial joints, leading to pain, stiffness, swelling, progressive joint damage, and functional impairment.1 It most commonly affects most joints, such as the fingers, wrists, shoulders, knees, ankles, and feet, and usually occurs in the same joints on both sides of the body.2,3 The specific causes for RA are still unknown, but its risk may be increased by genetic (female sex, age) and environmental factors (smoking, obesity) .4 The global prevalence of RA is estimated to range from 0.5% to 1%, with approximately 70% of women and 55% of people older than 55 years.1 Without effective treatment, the disease may lead to reduced quality of life and functional ability, as well as increased risk of numerous complications and comorbidities, resulting in a heavy personal, societal, and economic burden.5,6

RA is not curable, but early diagnosis and effective treatment can reduce symptoms and slow the disease progression. Recommended by multiple clinical practice guidelines, remission or low disease activity is the initial treatment goal of RA, and the treat-to-target strategy is the fundamental management approach.2,7 The conventional synthetic Disease-Modifying Antirheumatic Drugs (csDMARDs) are typically used as the initial treatment. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, biologics, and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) are the viable medication options, depending on the patient's medical history and symptom severity.1,8 Lifestyle changes and patient education are beneficial as well.9,10 People with RA can benefit from well-established Western medical treatment regimens supported by robust evidence from randomized controlled trials, but these treatment regimens are also limited by variable patient response, safety concerns, and the high cost of some biologics.11,12

Chinese medicine (CM) has currently been recognized as a complementary approach in the management of RA. It typically includes Chinese herbal medicine (CHM), acupuncture, moxibustion, and other exercise therapies, emphasizing pattern differentiation, symptom relief, and holistic patient management.13 Increasing mechanistic and clinical evidence suggests that CM is effective in improving clinical symptoms and patient-reported outcomes, with fewer side effects.14, 15, 16, 17

Integrated Chinese-Western medicine (ICWM) in the treatment of RA can leverage the complementary advantages of both approaches.18,19 Evidence-based multidisciplinary guidance will help clinicians and patients manage RA better. Therefore, our working group initiated the development of the AI-empowered Clinical Evidence for Integrated Chinese-Western Medicine (ACE-iMed) platform. As one of a series of articles introducing this platform, this study focuses on RA and uses the ACE-iMed platform to summarize the clinical evidence on both CM and ICWM questions of interest to clinicians in RA management, categorize and assess evidence by literature types, and provide evidence summaries.

Methods

As part of the ACE-iMed series, details on the methods and processes are available in Liu H et al.20 We constructed the disease literature database for RA and defined clinical questions (CQs) and outcomes. A total of 18 questions were finalized; the specific questions and outcomes are listed in Table 1 and Supplement 1, respectively.

Exclusion criteria included publications in languages other than Chinese or English; dissertations, conference abstracts, or studies for which the full text could not be obtained; studies involving combinations of multiple CM interventions; and studies with obvious data errors.

Literature searches and screening

We adopted a two-step search strategy to ensure comprehensive coverage and alignment with clinically relevant questions. First, we constructed a broad disease-specific database by searching for RA combined with a wide range of CM interventions. This step aimed to maximize sensitivity and avoid missing potentially relevant studies. All databases were searched from their inception to September 6, 2025, and the detailed search strategies were provided in Supplement 2. Second, for each predefined CQs, we applied PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome)-based filtering within the disease-specific database, using specific intervention-related search terms and eligibility criteria tailored to each CQ. The literature screening was supported by a large language model with predefined prompts and included manual review by human investigators at several stages. Details on the literature searches and screening are available in Liu H et al.20

Quality/risk of bias assessment of included studies

The methodological quality of CPGs/CSs was evaluated using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II).24 The methodological quality of SRs was assessed by A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews 2 (AMSTAR 2).25 The risk of bias of RCTs was evaluated by The Risk Of Bias instrument for use in Systematic reviews - Randomized Controlled Trials (ROBUST‑RCT).26 Details on the processes are available in Liu H et al.20

Data extraction and analysis

For each included study, we extracted three basic items for all three literature types (CPGs/CSs, SRs, and RCTs): title, year of publication, and journal of publication. The other information, including developing organization, target population, grading system for evidence quality, grading system for strength of recommendations, recommendations, supporting evidence, databases searched, search time frame, basic characteristics of the population of interest, sample size, interventions/comparators/outcomes and their effect sizes, and conclusions, were extracted accordingly based on the specific literature type. Data analysis and figure generation were carried out in Microsoft Excel 2019 and R (version 4.5.1), respectively. Details on the processes are available in Liu H et al.20

Results

Study characteristics

Through systematic literature searches of WanFang, CNKI, MEDLINE (via PubMed), the Cochrane Library, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) and eight standard websites, we yielded 13,851 records on CM interventions for RA. After removing duplicates, 11,635 records remained and were used to construct an RA literature database. We then performed targeted searches and screening within the database for each specific clinical question. Across all questions, 673 records were initially included; after removing overlap between questions, 556 records were ultimately included. Quality/risk of bias of included studies

By using AGREE II, the methodological quality score of the 17 included CPGs/CSs ranged from 34.7% to 77.1%, and nine (52.9%) scored above 60%. In the 35 SRs, the highest score of AMSTAR 2 was 15, and the lowest was 5.5, with 30 (85.7%) scoring greater than 9. Among the 504 included RCTs, the scores of ROBUST-RCT ranged from 0.5 to 5.5, and 375 (74.4%) scored above 3. Clinical questions and evidence

The details and summaries of included studies for all CQs are available on the ACE-iMed platform (www.aceimed.org). We selected the two with the most included studies and for different CM interventions, i.e., CQ 1 and CQ 7, and provided evidence summaries here.

CQ1: For people with RA, how do the efficacy and safety of integrated oral CHM and WM compare with WM alone?

Fifteen CPGs/CSs (published 2013–2025) addressed this question or provided recommendations/suggestions, and 21 SRs (published 2012–2025) and 281 RCTs (published 2002–2025) provided relevant evidence.

CPGs/CSs: Among 15 CPGs/CSs, eleven recommended/suggested combining oral CHM and CWT to improve one or more of the following: clinical efficacy (ACR20/50/70 response rate or DAS28 response rate), clinical symptoms (joint pain, joint swelling, or morning stiffness), or inflammation markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C‑reactive protein, or rheumatoid factor). The other four did not specify whether CHM should be used alone or integrated with WM in their recommendations. Furthermore, two of 15 CPGs/CSs indicated that integrating oral CHM and WM can lower the incidence of adverse events or liver dysfunction;27,28 two suggested integrating WM when oral CHM alone is insufficient.29,30 The basic characteristics and recommendation details of 15 CPGs/CSs are presented in Supplement 3.

CQ 7: For people with RA, how do the efficacy and safety of integrated external therapy of CHM (e.g., plaster/patch, bath, fumigation, wet compress) and CWT compare with CWT alone?

Seven CPGs/CSs (published 2018–2025) addressed this question or provided recommendations/suggestions, and two SRs (published in 2023 and 2025) and 43 RCTs (published 1998–2025) provided relevant evidence.

CPGs/CSs: Seven CPGs/CSs recommended various external therapies for CHM in treating RA, such as plasters/patches, baths, fumigation, wet compresses, iontophoresis, but none of them explicitly stated whether these therapies should be integrated with CWT. Among them, four stated targeted RA disease stages applicable to external therapies of CHM (active, stable, and remission);30,52, 53, 54 furthermore, one of the four emphasized that the active stage should focus on dispelling cold and dampness/clearing heat and relieving Bi (painful obstruction), while the remission stage should focus on tonifying the liver and kidney/promoting blood circulation and activating the channels.30 Three specified the names of external CHMs and their procedures.53, 54, 55 The basic characteristics and recommendation details of seven CPGs/CSs are presented in Supplement 4.

SRs: A network meta-analysis of 49 RCTs indicated that integrating wax therapy, acupoint plaster, or fumigation of CHM and CWT was associated with improvements in DAS28, duration of morning stiffness, and VAS than CWT alone.56 The other SR examined a subgroup of RA people (one RCT) and found that, compared with CWT alone, ointment massage therapy combined with CWT can lower VAS scores.57

RCTs: The sample sizes of 42 RCTs ranged from 22 to 889. The various external therapies of CHM were investigated, including plasters/patches (17, 40.5%), fumigation (16, 38.1%), steaming washing (4, 9.5%), baths (2, 4.8%), iontophoresis (1, 2.4%), combination of steaming washing and acupoint plaster (1, 2.4%), and a combination of plasters/patches and baths (1, 2.4%). Among the outcomes of our interest, the top five reported most frequently in the included RCTs were DMS (34, 80.9%), SJC (21, 50.0%), TJC (19, 45.2%), VAS (18, 42.9%), and DAS28 (11, 26.2%). Details of all included RCTs can be found on the ACE-iMed platform.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This study addressed 18 clinical questions for RA, identified a total of 556 relevant CPGs/CSs, SRs, and RCTs, covering ten major CM interventions, such as herbal medicine, acupuncture, moxibustion, tuina, and cupping. Each intervention included two common comparisons, i.e., CM interventions versus CWT, and integrated CM interventions and CWT versus CWT. Of these, seven (38.9%; CQs 1–5, 7 and 9) questions were supported by evidence from all three literature types; nine (50.0%; CQs 6, 8, 10, 11, 13 and 15–18) questions were supported by two types of literature, with no evidence from SRs; and two (11.1%; CQs 12 and 14) questions were only mentioned by CPGs/CSs, with no evidence from SRs and RCTs. The question about the efficacy and safety of integrated oral CHM and CWT has the most substantial evidence, with the largest amount of evidence in each literature type. Although we only presented details of two questions in this study, currently, all RA-related questions and their evidence and summaries can be accessed on the ACE-iMed platform (www.aceimed.org).

Strengths and limitations

This study first performed systematic searches to construct an RA literature database and then specific searching in the literature database for each clinical question, as well as collating them by literature types, which provided a strong and readily traceable evidence body for the disease. Beyond basic bibliographic information (e.g., year, journal), this study also provided key intervention and implementation details from each study by literature types to support clinical decision-making, e.g., recommendations in CPGs/CSs, their supporting evidence, and the grading system of evidence quality and recommendation strength used. Notably, we applied artificial intelligence methods in the stage of study selection, quality assessment, and evidence summary for included studies, which can be used for the evidence scoping of other diseases in the future.

However, we acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, the identification of clinical questions and outcomes was not based on a large-scale stakeholder survey and therefore may not fully reflect the priorities of clinicians and patients. Second, literature screening, methodological quality assessment, and evidence summary were assisted by artificial intelligence tools; although specialized prompts were employed and sensitivity/specificity were tested for some questions, residual errors or biases remain possible. Third, this work only provided narrative summary and did not perform additional quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) of the included data; consequently, it cannot generate new quantitative effect estimates and support definitive clinical recommendations.

Clinical and research implications

ICWM has attracted considerable public interest and is commonly used in clinical practice for RA management. The CM interventions are diverse and complex, and the quantity and quality of evidence supporting their integration or comparison with conventional medicine remain limited. It is well known that, compared with conventional medicine, CM exhibits a substantial gap between clinical practice and the evidentiary base; therefore, clinicians should exercise caution when considering interventions for which evidence is insufficient or absent. Although some CPGs/CSs recommended certain CM interventions, these may not be supported by SRs or RCTs, and their clinical applications should be contingent on further high-level and high-quality evidence. Standardized, scientifically grounded, effective, and safe treatment is essential for RA, which is not only associated with patient benefit but also with healthcare resource utilization. Our ACE-iMed platform provides evidence overviews and summaries of CM interventions to support researchers and clinicians in identifying research gaps and making evidence-informed decisions.

Conclusion

Leveraging AI-assisted systematic searching, categorization, assessment and summary, this study and the relevant ACE-iMed platform provide a structured overview of clinical evidence on both CM and ICWM questions of interest to clinicians in the management of RA. They encompass commonly used CM interventions in the real-world clinical practice, which can support clinicians in identifying evidence distribution and research gaps, and we believe they may support clinicians in making evidence-based decisions.
