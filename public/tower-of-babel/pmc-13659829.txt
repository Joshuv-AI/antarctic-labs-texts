Lactate Promotes Liver Regeneration by Increasing Fatty Acid Oxidation Through GCN5-Mediated H3K9 Lactylation
Authors: Xun Qiu, Hanzhi Xu, Lijun Meng, Yunguo Lei, Yawen Tan, Zunqiang Xiao, Xiao Tang, Xinyu Yang, Fengqiang Gao, Yingying Zhang, Libin Dong, Zhoucheng Wang, Yuqi Gong, Xinyang Zhong, Nasha Qiu, Ruhong Zhou, Kai Wang, Xiao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsLactylation has been implicated in the repair and regeneration of multiple organs, including the skin and the heart. The liver serves as a vital organ for lactate metabolism, whereas the roles of lactate and lactylation during liver regeneration remain unclear.MethodsMice were subjected to 70% partial hepatectomy, and the liver-to-body weight ratio and proliferative markers were measured. Protein–protein interactions were detected by co-immunoprecipitation. The pivotal site of GCN5 for catalysis was predicted by theoretical modeling. CUT&Tag and bulk RNA sequencing assays were used to identify downstream genes regulated by H3K9 lactylation. Murine hepatocyte organoids and human liver tissues were used to validate the effects of H3K9 lactylation.ResultsLactate supplementation notably improved the liver-to-body weight ratio and upregulated hepatic levels of Ki-67, proliferating cell nuclear antigen, and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine incorporation at 48 hours after partial hepatectomy, whereas hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice showed impaired liver regenerative capacity. In vitro experiments indicated that increased intracellular lactate upregulated H3 lactylation and promoted hepatocyte proliferation. Mechanistically, GCN5, rather than P300 or MOF, was identified as the pivotal enzyme catalyzing H3K9 lactylation. Furthermore, lactate treatment promoted the interaction between GCN5 and H3K9 during liver regeneration. Glu639 was identified as the critical amino acid residue in GCN5 for catalyzing H3K9 lactylation. CUT&Tag and bulk RNA sequencing results further revealed that H3K9 lactylation upregulated the expression of Lipin1 and its mediated transcription of fatty acid oxidation–related genes. The proregenerative effects of lactate and H3K9 lactylation were validated using murine hepatocyte organoids and human liver tissues.ConclusionsLactate facilitates liver regeneration through promoting GCN5-mediated H3K9 lactylation, which upregulates Lipin1 transcription and subsequent Lipin1-mediated fatty acid oxidation in hepatocytes.Keywords: Epigenetics, Fatty Acid Metabolism, GCN5, Histone Lactylation, Liver Regeneration

Summary

Lactate supplementation facilitates liver regeneration by increasing H3K9 lactylation, a modification that upregulates the expression of Lipin1 and its mediated transcription of fatty acid oxidation–related genes. GCN5 (Glu639) is identified as the pivotal enzyme for catalyzing H3K9 lactylation.

What You Need to Know

Background

Although lactate and histone lactylation have been implicated in tissue repair and regeneration, the epigenetic mechanisms linking lactate signaling to liver regeneration remain largely unknown.

Impact

Lactate promotes liver regeneration through GCN5-mediated H3K9 lactylation, which enhances Lipin1-dependent fatty acid oxidation, stimulates hepatocyte proliferation, and supports effective liver regrowth following partial hepatectomy.

Future Directions

Further studies are needed to determine how lactate signaling promotes Lipin1 nuclear translocation, and whether GCN5 or Lipin1 can be therapeutically targeted to enhance liver regeneration.

Partial hepatectomy (PHx) stands as the primary means for the radical treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).1,2 However, the morbidity of postoperative liver failure can reach 11.0%, which is closely attributed to the high incidence of small-for-size syndrome following extensive hepatectomy.3 Notably, the regenerative capacity is a critical determinant influencing the recovery of liver function and the sustenance of systemic homeostasis after hepatic injury.4, 5, 6 Therefore, exploring the underlying mechanisms involved in liver regeneration and identifying strategies to enhance the regenerative ability remain required and urgent.

Lactylation is a newly discovered form of protein modification, which is defined as the modification of lysine residues on proteins by lactate-generated lactyl-coenzyme A (lactyl-CoA).7 Similar to acetylation and other acyl-lysine modifications, lactylation is capable of regulating gene transcription through its action on histones, and it can also modulate protein activity by acting upon nonhistone proteins.8,9 Recently, lactylation has been shown to participate in the regeneration and repair of various tissues and organs (eg, skin, skeletal muscle, and heart).10, 11, 12, 13 Lactate-triggered lactylation of pyruvate kinase M2 is indicated to reprogram proinflammatory macrophages into a reparative phenotype, thus facilitating wound healing.14 In ischemic heart disease and heart failure, lactylation and its substrate lactate exhibit critical roles in improving cardiac function by promoting the expression of reparative genes and maintaining sarcomeric structure.11,15

The liver serves as a vital organ for lactate metabolism.16 In HCC, lactate and its derived lactylation have been reported to enhance hepatic stemness and proliferative potential.17,18 For instance, the intracellular lactate-derived lactylation of H3 at lysine 56 and of aldolase A promotes the maintenance of liver cancer stem cells, thereby supporting HCC growth.19 Additionally, lactate has also been shown to facilitate the proliferation of HCC cells through upregulating the lactylation of cyclin E2.20 Regarding PHx, it has been reported that major hepatectomy typically does not lead to hyperlactatemia, but elevated serum lactate levels or impaired lactate clearance following PHx have been associated with poor clinical outcomes.21,22 However, the changes in hepatic lactate level and the roles of lactylation during liver regeneration remain unreported. The regenerating liver undergoes a transient yet complex transcriptional reprogramming, during which epigenetic modifications play important roles in controlling gene activation and silencing.23 Therefore, our aim was to investigate the regulatory roles that lactate and histone lactylation play in liver regeneration, along with the underlying mechanisms.

Here, we indicated that the hepatic levels of lactate and H3 lactylation decreased in the early stages post-PHx. Exogenous sodium lactate (NALA) supplementation significantly enhanced the hepatocyte proliferative ability and facilitated liver regeneration, whereas hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice exhibited impaired hepatic regenerative capacity. In vitro, increased intracellular lactate levels upregulated H3 lactylation and promoted hepatocyte proliferation, which were validated using murine hepatocyte organoids and human liver tissues. GCN5 served as the pivotal enzyme catalyzing H3K9 lactylation (H3K9la), and NALA treatment promoted the catalytic effects of GCN5 on H3K9 during liver regeneration. In addition, Glu639 (E639) was identified as the critical amino acid residue of GCN5 required for H3K9la catalysis. CUT&Tag and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) results further revealed that H3K9la upregulated the transcription of Lipin1 and its downstream genes involved in fatty acid oxidation (FAO). Taken together, our results suggest that lactate facilitates liver regeneration through enhancing GCN5-mediated H3K9la, which in turn upregulates Lipin1 transcription and subsequent Lipin1-mediated FAO in hepatocytes.

Results

Lactate and Histone Lactylation Promote Liver Regeneration

We initially measured the hepatic levels of lactate and histone lactylation during liver regeneration after PHx. As shown in Figure 1A, lactate was downregulated at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-PHx. Consistently, the levels of H3PanKla, H3K9la, and H3K18la were remarkably reduced (Figure 1B). To explore the effects of lactate and lactylation on liver regeneration, we treated mice with different doses of NALA before and after surgery. The results showed that mice treated with 0.05 g/kg NALA had the most regenerative capacity, reflected by the highest liver-to-body weight (LW:BW) ratio and the highest expression level of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) (Figure 1C and D). NALA treatment also increased the hepatic levels of lactate and histone lactylation (Figure 1E and G). Moreover, increased protein expression of proliferative markers (PCNA and Ki-67) and elevated messenger RNA (mRNA) levels of cell cycle regulators and stemness-related genes were found in livers treated with NALA (Figure 1F–H).

Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) catalyzes the reduction of pyruvate to lactate and serves as a key enzyme driving lactate production.24 To further investigate the role of lactate in liver regeneration, hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout (Ldhafl/fl;Alb-Cre) mice were subjected to PHx. The results showed that Ldha deficiency significantly reduced the hepatic lactate levels and LW:BW ratio (Figure 1I and J). Then, we assessed serum lactate levels in both groups. No significant differences were observed between control and knockout mice at any examined time point, indicating that hepatocyte-specific deletion of Ldha does not significantly affect systemic lactate dynamics during liver regeneration (Figure 1K). However, the greater fluctuations observed in knockout mice may suggest a reduced capacity to maintain lactate homeostasis, especially during the rapid regenerative phase (36–72 hours). Moreover, Ki-67 staining and Western blotting analyses demonstrated that Ldha deficiency impaired the regenerative potential of hepatocytes following PHx (Figure 1L and M). Consistently, the hepatic mRNA levels of cell-cycle regulators and stemness-related genes were downregulated in hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice (Figure 1N). Altogether, these results suggest that elevations of lactate and histone lactylation could facilitate liver regeneration after PHx.

To further determine whether exogenous lactate supplementation could rescue the impaired regenerative phenotype caused by Ldha deficiency, we administered NALA to hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice following PHx (Figure 2A). The results showed that NALA supplementation significantly increased the hepatic lactate levels and LW:BW ratio in Ldha knockout mice (Figure 2B and C). This improvement largely restored the LW:BW ratio to the level observed in control mice treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Notably, despite comparable hepatic lactate levels between NALA-treated groups, the LW:BW ratio in Ldha knockout mice remained lower than that in control mice receiving NALA. Consistently, Ki-67 immunostaining and 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation assays demonstrated that NALA supplementation markedly increased the number of proliferating hepatocytes in Ldha knockout mice, reaching levels comparable to or slightly higher than those in control mice treated with PBS (Figure 2D and E). Similarly, Western blotting and real time-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses revealed that NALA supplementation substantially rescued the impaired regenerative capacity in Ldha knockout mice to approximately the baseline level of control mice, yet it failed to achieve the enhanced regenerative response induced by NALA in control mice (Figure 2F–H). Taken together, these findings indicate that exogenous lactate supplementation can effectively rescue the regenerative defects caused by Ldha deficiency to a basal level, whereas Ldha may also contribute to liver regeneration through lactate-independent mechanisms.

Intracellular Lactate Levels Modulate Hepatocyte Proliferation and Stemness

To explore the effects of lactate on hepatocytes, we treated AML12 cells with NALA or MCT1 inhibitor (AZD3965) to increase the intracellular lactate levels. As shown in Figure 3A, NALA enhanced AML12 cell proliferation in a time- and dose-dependent manner. The number of colonies formed by hepatocytes was significantly increased in the group treated with NALA (Figure 3B). We subsequently observed that NALA treatment upregulated the levels of H3PanKla, H3K9la, and H3K18la (Figure 3C) and mRNA levels of critical cell-cycle regulators and stemness-related genes (Figure 3D and E). Consistent with the conclusion from NALA treatment, inhibition of MCT1 upregulated the intracellular lactate levels and improved the proliferation and colony formation of hepatocytes (Figure 3F–I). The levels of H3PanKla, H3K9la, and H3K18la and expression levels of cell-cycle– and stemness-related genes were also increased in cells with MCT1 inhibition (Figure 3J–L). Hence, our results reveal that the elevation of intracellular lactate levels could enhance the proliferation and stemness of hepatocytes.

In a continued effort to investigate the relationship between lactate levels and hepatocyte proliferation, we further assessed the proliferative and self-renewal abilities of AML12 cells following pharmacologic or genetic modulation of LDHA, with or without NALA supplementation. Pharmacologic inhibition of LDHA with GSK2837808A (Figure 4A–F), as well as LDHA knockdown (Figure 4G–M), significantly reduced intracellular lactate levels and H3 lactylation, accompanied by a decreased proliferative capacity of cells. In contrast, NALA supplementation effectively rescued these inhibitory effects. Furthermore, primary hepatocytes were isolated from Ldhafl/fl and Ldhafl/fl;Alb-Cre mice and subjected to similar analyses. Lactate levels and H3 lactylation were significantly lower in hepatocytes derived from Ldha knockout mice compared with controls. Upon NALA treatment, both lactate levels and H3 lactylation were markedly increased in the control and knockout groups; however, the levels in Ldha knockout hepatocytes remained slightly lower than those in control cells treated with 10 mM NALA (Figure 5A–C). Consistently, Cell-Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and RT-qPCR analyses demonstrated that 10 mM NALA supplementation restored the proliferative capacity of Ldha-deficient primary hepatocytes to a level comparable to that of control cells treated with PBS (Figure 5D–F). Notably, LDHA expression was upregulated following PHx, in contrast to the trend observed for H3 lactylation (Figure 6A and B). Therefore, although LDHA regulates lactate production and influences H3 lactylation, it is not the core factor that regulates H3 lactylation during liver regeneration.

GCN5 Is Required for H3 Lactylation in Hepatocytes

Next, we set out to ascertain the enzymatic mechanism of H3 lactylation, and we tested enzymes that could potentially act as acyltransferases catalyzing histone lactylation, including MOF, GCN5, and P300.15 We found that all 3 transferases could interact with H3, H3K9la, and H3K18la (Figure 7A). To investigate which acyltransferase is most critical for H3 lactylation, we transfected human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK293T) cells with plasmids expressing H3 and plasmids expressing MOF, GCN5, or P300. The results from co-immunoprecipitation showed that GCN5 overexpression significantly upregulated the levels of H3PanKla, H3K9la, and H3K18la compared with MOF and P300 (Figure 7B). We further found that, although the protein levels of GCN5 did not change after NALA treatment, the interaction between GCN5 and H3 was markedly enhanced (Figure 7C).

Then, we treated AML12 cells with GCN5 knockdown using small interfering RNA (siRNA) and measured the effects on the proliferative and self-renewal capabilities of cells. Both mRNA and protein levels showed that GCN5-siRNA1 and GCN5-siRNA3 had better knockdown efficiency than GCN5-siRNA2 (Figure 7D and E). GCN5 knockdown significantly attenuated the proliferation and colony formation of hepatocytes, which could not be rescued by NALA readdition (Figure 7F–H). Interestingly, the protein levels of GCN5 were upregulated in the early phase after PHx (Figure 8A). Given the hypoglycemia after PHx and the involvement of GCN5 in cellular energy metabolism (Figure 8B),25,26 we hypothesized that the increase in GCN5 protein levels was due to hypoglycemia. Therefore, we treated AML12 cells with low-glucose medium, and the results were consistent with our hypothesis that low glucose could induce GCN5 upregulation (Figure 8C and D). Altogether, these results suggest that GCN5 serves as the key acyltransferase for H3 lactylation in hepatocytes during liver regeneration, whereas lactate, rather than GCN5, is the pivotal factor contributing to the reduction of H3 lactylation during this process.

E639 Is the Crucial Site of GCN5 for Catalyzing H3K9 Lactylation

To identify which lysine residue of H3 is mainly catalyzed by GCN5 to influence H3PanKla levels, we transfected plasmids expressing H3, H3K9R, or H3K18R into 293T cells with GCN5 overexpression and assessed the effects of K9R and K18R mutations on H3PanKla levels. Both the K9R and K18R mutations reduced the GCN5-mediated H3 lactylation, but the reduction was more pronounced in the K9R mutation group (Figure 9A). We further found that the amount of H3K9la bound to GCN5 increased in regenerating liver tissues treated with NALA (Figure 9B), suggesting that NALA promoted the catalytic activity of GCN5 on H3K9 residue during liver regeneration.

To gain a deeper understanding of the interaction between GCN5 and H3, theoretical modeling was used to predict the formation of their complex. The input sequences corresponded to the N-acetyltransferase domain (the catalytic domain of GCN5) and a 13-mer peptide derived from H3 containing K9 as the central residue. Figure 9C shows the ranked first result, in which the peptide was embedded into the catalytic pocket. We further found the interaction between the positively charged Arg8 residue of H3 and the negatively charged E639 residue of GCN5 seemed to play a critical role in their binding. Therefore, a mutation (E639Q) experiment was carried out to validate this prediction. The results showed that GCN5-mediated H3K9la was distinctly decreased in cells transfected with GCN5 (E639Q) plasmids, and the binding of GCN5 (E639Q) to H3K9la was also remarkably reduced (Figure 9D). In hepatocytes, GCN5 (wild-type [WT]) overexpression upregulated the level of H3K9la, whereas the E639Q mutation markedly attenuated this effect (Figure 9E). Through CCK8 and RT-qPCR assays, we found that the overexpression of the GCN5 WT promoted hepatocyte proliferation as well as the transcription of cell-cycle– and stemness-related genes. However, these effects were abolished in cells transfected with GCN5 (E639Q) plasmids (Figure 9F–H). These results suggest that E639 constitutes the critical site within GCN5 in catalyzing H3K9la.

H3K9 Lactylation Facilitates the Transcription of Fatty Acid Oxidation–Related Genes

To screen candidate target genes of H3K9la in hepatocytes during liver regeneration, we treated hepatocytes with either PBS or 10 mM NALA and subjected them to a CUT&Tag assay using anti-H3K9la antibody (Figure 10A). The data showed that, in comparison with the control group, NALA treatment led to an increase in the lactylation levels of 4893 gene regions and a decrease in those of 4675 gene regions (Figure 10B). The areas with changed lactylation levels were predominantly in the distal intergenic regions (Figure 10C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that H3K9la-modified genes were largely involved in the phosphoinositide 3-kinase–Akt signaling pathway, the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway, and the Wnt signaling pathway (Figure 10D). To identify H3K9la-marked genes in hepatocytes, RNA-seq was also conducted in hepatocytes cultured with or without NALA (Figure 10A). KEGG analysis indicated that the genes affected by H3K9la were mostly associated with cellular senescence, the mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathway, hepatitis C, and carcinogenesis (Figure 10E). Next, we combined the CUT&Tag and bulk RNA-seq data to determine the genes with both increased H3K9la and upregulated transcription (Figure 10F). Gene Ontology (GO) term analysis demonstrated that the upregulated genes with increased H3K9la predominantly participate in the regulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, the phosphoinositide 3-kinase–Akt signaling pathway, and the triglyceride (TG) metabolic process (Figure 10G).

Hepatic transient steatosis represents one of the most typical metabolic hallmarks in the early stage of liver regeneration.26 It has been reported that fatty acids, rather than glucose, emerge as the predominant energy source during liver regeneration.26,27 Hence, we sought to further investigate the regulatory role of H3K9la-mediated genes involved in the TG metabolic process during liver regeneration. Four genes (Agmo, Lipin1, Lipin2, and Srebf1) were significantly upregulated at both the H3K9la modification levels and mRNA expression levels after NALA treatment (Figure 10H). Consistently, transcription levels of the 4 genes were downregulated in liver tissues of hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice (Figure 10I). Lipin1 has been reported to play a critical role in maintaining lipid homeostasis, and its deficiency can cause lipid metabolic disorders.28,29 Therefore, we further explored the effects of H3K9la-upregulated Lipin1 expression on liver regeneration. In regenerating livers, the protein levels of Lipin1 were found to be significantly upregulated in NALA-treated mice and downregulated in hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice (Figure 11A). Then, we assessed hepatic lipid accumulation at 48 hours post-PHx and found that NALA treatment reduced the TG levels and decreased the intrahepatic lipid droplet accumulation, whereas Ldha deficiency upregulated the TG levels and promoted lipid accumulation (Figure 11B). Because Lipin1 could translocate into the nucleus to act as a transcriptional coregulator, promoting the transcription of FAO-related genes and regulating lipid metabolism,30,31 we measured the nuclear and cytoplasmic protein levels of Lipin1 in regenerating liver tissues. In mice treated with NALA, nuclear levels of Lipin1 and its downstream genes involved in FAO were upregulated, whereas hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice exhibited the opposite trend (Figure 11C and D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that lactate elevates the expression of FAO-related genes through increasing H3K9la-regulated Lipin1 expression.

The Proregenerative Effect of H3K9 Lactylation Is Verified in Hepatocyte Organoids and Clinical Samples

Murine hepatocyte organoids have been shown to be highly effective in mimicking the proliferative state of hepatocytes after PHx, thus enabling a more in-depth study of this regenerative process in vitro.32 Therefore, the proregenerative potential of lactate and H3K9la was further evaluated in murine primary hepatocytes as well as in the hepatocyte organoids derived from them. Consistent with the results of cell-based and in vivo experiments, exogenous NALA treatment increased the level of GCN5-mediated H3K9la and enhanced the proliferative ability of primary hepatocytes (Figure 12A). In hepatocyte organoids, treatment with 10 mM NALA for 72 hours induced a remarkable increase in organoid size (Figure 12B and C) and in the expression levels of H3K9la and PCNA (Figure 12D).

We then performed RNA-seq to compare the transcriptomes of hepatocyte organoids treated with PBS or NALA. Our analysis of differentially expressed genes demonstrated that NALA supplementation led to significant alterations in the gene expression patterns of the organoids (Figure 12E). In GO analysis, NALA-mediated genes were mainly involved in the regulation of cell adhesion, the positive regulation of cell population proliferation, and the fatty acid metabolic process (Figure 12F). The mRNA levels of Lipin1, Acadvl, and Cpt1α were elevated in organoids treated with NALA, whereas other FAO-related genes showed marginal differences (Figure 12G and H). In addition, both of the hepatocyte functional markers (Alb and Cyp3a11) and the fetal hepatocyte marker Afp were upregulated after NALA treatment, indicating that NALA plays a dual role in promoting proliferation and maintaining function (Figure 12I). Notably, the mRNA levels of Ldha and Ldhb were not affected by NALA treatment (Figure 12J).

To further validate these findings in an Ldha-deficient context, we generated hepatocyte organoids from control (Ldhafl/fl) and Ldhafl/fl;Alb-Cre mice, followed by treatment with PBS or NALA and subsequent analyses. Under PBS conditions, Ldha-deficient organoids exhibited significantly reduced size and impaired proliferative capacity compared with the control organoids. Importantly, NALA supplementation markedly rescued the growth defects in Ldha-deficient organoids, although not to the extent observed in the control organoids treated with NALA (Figure 12K). We next assessed the expression of genes associated with hepatocyte proliferation, function, and Lipin1-mediated mitochondrial FAO by RT-qPCR. Consistent with our observations in WT organoids, Ldha-deficient organoids displayed significantly reduced expression of these genes, and NALA supplementation partially restored their expression in Ldha-deficient organoids (Figure 12L and M).

Next, the relevance between H3K9la level and regenerative potential was evaluated in human liver tissues. Nine patients with liver cancer, who underwent surgical resection, were analyzed. The expression levels of H3K9la and PCNA were measured in adjacent normal liver tissues by Western blotting, and the correlation analysis showed that PCNA levels were positively correlated with H3K9la levels (Figure 13A and B). Taken together, these results demonstrate that H3K9la elevates the regenerative potential of hepatocytes.

Discussion

For the timely recovery of liver size and function after injury, histone post-translational modifications (hPTMs) play a critical role by regulating gene activation and silencing.23,33 Several studies have reported that histone methylation and acetylation modulate liver regeneration.34, 35, 36, 37 Here, we revealed that another hPTM (ie, H3K9la) and its substrate lactate facilitated liver regeneration through upregulating Lipin1 and downstream FAO-related genes in hepatocytes. Elevating intracellular lactate through NALA supplementation or MCT1 inhibitor treatment enhanced hepatocyte proliferation and colony formation, whereas LDHA inhibition or depletion suppressed hepatocyte replication. Although NALA markedly improved the proliferative capacity in Ldha-deficient settings, the restoration remained incomplete, suggesting that the impaired regenerative capacity caused by Ldha deficiency is, at least in part, lactate-independent. Previous studies have reported that NAD+ promotes liver regeneration,38,39 raising the possibility that reduced NAD+ availability upon Ldha deletion contributes to impaired hepatocyte proliferation. In addition, LDHA could exert functions beyond its glycolytic activity. For example, in cancer, LDHA can promote cell proliferation independently of its metabolic activity by activating Rac1 signaling through interaction with Rac1-GTP.40 Furthermore, LDHA has been reported to localize to the nucleus and regulate gene transcription via interactions with single-stranded DNA or hypoxia-inducible factor-1α.41,42 Together, these findings suggest that LDHA regulates liver regeneration through both lactate-dependent and -independent mechanisms, highlighting further investigation into its noncanonical roles in hepatic repair.

In the process of regeneration, the hepatic metabolic panorama undergoes considerable alterations.26 Hypoglycemia and the resulting transient steatosis represent 2 typical metabolic hallmarks in the early stage of liver regeneration.26 Previous studies have shown that hypoglycemia serves as a proregenerative signal, whereas glucose supplementation would impede liver regeneration.43,44 During this stage, fatty acids emerge as the predominant energy source.26,27 Due to the reduced mitochondrial redox potential caused by enhanced lipid oxidation, the mitochondrial utilization of pyruvate is suppressed, rendering glucose an unsuitable energy source for regenerating livers.26 In this study, we found that hepatic lactate levels significantly decreased after PHx. Interestingly, the expression of LDHA was increased following PHx, which we interpret as a compensatory metabolic adaptation to the depleted lactate pool. Consistent with this interpretation, our data showed that exogenous lactate supplementation decreased hepatic LDHA levels at 48 hours post-PHx (Figure 2F). Additionally, impaired mitochondrial utilization of pyruvate may lead to its cytosolic accumulation, which may in turn contribute to LDHA upregulation.26 Therefore, the reduction in lactate is more likely not caused by LDHA deficiency, but by reduced glucose flux into glycolysis and increased lactate consumption. Taken together, our findings suggest that lactate supplementation, rather than glucose, appears to be a preferable option to facilitate liver regeneration.

Studies have demonstrated that the levels of lactate-derived lactyl-CoA and those of acyltransferases are the 2 principal factors that affect the lactylation level.45 Our study investigated the effects of 3 representative members of writers for histone lactylation, including MOF, GCN5, and P300. The results indicated that the 3 enzymes could interact with H3 and catalyze the lactylation at lysine sites, whereas GCN5 was the primary enzyme that catalyzed H3 lactylation. Consistently, Wang et al reported that GCN5 is the main acyltransferase mediating the H3K18la in circulating monocytes after myocardial infarction.15 In addition, our results showed that the expression of GCN5 was upregulated post PHx, suggesting the enzyme was not the core factor that led to the decreased H3 lactylation. Given that low-glucose medium could induce GCN5 overexpression, we considered that the upregulation of GCN5 expression was likely caused by hypoglycemia post-PHx. On the contrary, hepatic lactate levels were downregulated after surgery, consistent with the trend observed for H3 lactylation. Therefore, our results suggest that reduced lactate availability is the primary driver of decreased H3 lactylation during liver regeneration.

Lysine 9 and lysine 18 are 2 well-recognized sites on H3 that could be modified by lactyl-CoA.23 To explore the major lysine lactylation site regulated by GCN5, plasmids expressing H3, H3K9R, or H3K18R were transfected into 293T cells with GCN5 overexpression. The results indicated that K9 was the main site catalyzed by GCN5. By utilizing molecular docking and conducting co-immunoprecipitation experiments, we further identified E639 as the critical functional residue of GCN5 for catalyzing H3K9la. Notably, H3K9la CUT&Tag analysis revealed that, following NALA treatment, genomic regions with increased H3K9la levels were predominantly distributed within the distal intergenic regions. These observations are in line with previous reports showing that H3K9 exerted a critical role in controlling particular distal intergenic regions.46 In addition, hPTMs in these regions have been reported to differ considerably across cell types and could potentially determine cell identity.47 Our findings further highlight the pivotal role of distal intergenic regions in modulating chromatin accessibility and gene activation during liver regeneration.

As fatty acids are the predominant energy source during liver regeneration, we screened TG metabolism–related genes with increased H3K9la enrichment and transcriptional activation, and focused on Lipin1 as a key candidate.28,31 In this study, we found that NALA upregulated Lipin1 expression through H3K9la and promoted its nuclear translocation, thereby enhancing transcription of FAO-related genes to support the energetic demands of hepatocyte proliferation. This finding is consistent with a previous study showing that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α–induced FAO facilitates liver regeneration.27 Notably, Lipin1 also functions as a cytoplasmic phosphatidic acid phosphatase converting phosphatidic acid (PA) to diacylglycerol.48 In acetaminophen-induced hepatotoxicity, characterized by severe metabolic stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, reduced Lipin1 has been reported to increase PA levels and activate compensatory regenerative signaling pathways.49,50 Collectively, these findings indicate that Lipin1 plays a highly context-dependent role in liver regeneration, driven by its dual functions as a cytoplasmic PA phosphatase and a nuclear transcriptional coregulator.

To better validate the effect of lactate on hepatocyte proliferation during liver regeneration in vitro, we utilized the murine hepatocyte organoid model, the growth condition of which can satisfactorily simulate the proliferative state of hepatocytes following PHx.32 Consistently, NALA supplementation led to increased proliferation of organoids, as well as upregulated expression of Lipin1 and its downstream genes. We further observed an elevation in the expression of both hepatocyte functional markers (Alb and Cyp3a11) and the fetal hepatocyte marker Afp following NALA treatment. This finding implies that NALA promotes both hepatocyte proliferation and functional maintenance, thereby preventing ineffective proliferation during liver regeneration. In contrast, Ldha-deficient organoids exhibited impaired growth, along with a reduced expression of genes associated with hepatocyte proliferation, function, and Lipin1-mediated mitochondrial FAO. However, Cpt1α expression was not decreased in Ldha-deficient organoids. This discrepancy suggests that, although hepatocyte organoids can largely recapitulate the regenerative process, certain genes (eg, Cpt1α) may still be influenced by the lack of the in vivo context.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that lactate and histone H3 lactylation promote liver regeneration in mice after PHx. Mechanistically, lactate upregulates Lipin1 and its related FAO through enhancing GCN5-mediated H3K9la in hepatocytes (Figure 14). The E639 residue of GCN5 is critical for H3K9la catalysis. Moreover, the association between liver regenerative potential and H3K9la levels was further validated using murine hepatocyte organoids and human liver tissues. Our results suggest that lactate supplementation might represent a potential therapeutic strategy for promoting liver regeneration after PHx.

Schematic diagram showing the proposed mechanistic model of lactate-accelerated liver regeneration. Upon hepatectomy, the decrease in lactate level within hepatocytes results in a reduction of the GCN5-mediated H3K9la. The murine hepatocyte cell line AML12 (CRL-2254) and the HEK293T cell line were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and Beyotime Biotechnology. Both cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humanized atmosphere with 5% CO2 in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Wisent) and 100 μg/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen). The cellular transfection of short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs; Genechem), siRNAs (Tsingke), and plasmids (Tsingke) was performed using jetPRIME (Polyplus) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, cells were plated in a 6-well plate at 60% confluence and transfected with shRNAs, siRNAs, or plasmids, followed by cell collection 48 to 72 hours later. Reagents

The MCT1 inhibitor AZD396515,51 (S7339) and the LDHA inhibitor GSK2837808A (S8590) were purchased from Selleck. NALA was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (71,718), and EdU was purchased from MedChemExpress (HY-118411).

Animal Experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice (22–24 g) were subjected to 70% PHx. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, followed by ligation and resection of the left lateral lobe and the median lobe separately. The experimental protocols were authorized by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, Zhejiang Center of Laboratory Animals (No. ZJCLA-IACUC-20010152).

To investigate the effects of lactate on liver regeneration, both pharmacologic (NALA) and genetic (hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout) approaches were employed. For NALA supplementation, mice received intraperitoneal injections of NALA or PBS 6 hours prior to surgery, followed by daily injections until sacrifice at the indicated timepoints. Hepatocyte-specific Ldha knockout mice were generated by crossing Ldhafl/fl C57BL/6J mice (GemPharmatech) with Alb-Cre transgenic C57BL/6J mice (GemPharmatech). In selected experiments, Ldhafl/fl and Ldhafl/fl; Alb-Cre mice were treated with NALA or PBS as described above to assess whether exogenous lactate supplementation could rescue the observed phenotypes. To label the proliferating hepatocytes, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 50 mg/kg EdU 6 hours prior to sacrifice.52

To monitor changes in blood glucose levels following PHx, peripheral blood glucose levels were measured at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, and 168 hours after surgery via tail bleed. Briefly, the tail tip was disinfected, and 1 to 2 mm of tissue was excised to obtain blood. Glucose levels were measured using a portable glucometer and expressed as mg/dL.

Human Liver Tissue Samples

This study was conducted according to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. Approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital (Affiliated People’s Hospital) (No. KT2025046), a total of 9 samples of adjacent normal liver tissues were collected from patients with HCC who underwent PHx in this hospital. All patients included in the current study have provided written informed consent and agreed to the application of their tissue samples.

Co-Immunoprecipitation Assay

Except for the radioimmunoprecipitation assay solution being weak in strength, the method of obtaining the protein supernatant is the same as that in the Western blotting analysis. The supernatants were incubated with anti-MOF/anti-GCN5 (Abcam)/anti-P300/anti-Flag/anti-Myc/control IgG (CST) for 1 hour at room temperature, and then the protein A/G MagPoly Beads (Smart-Lifesciences) were added, followed by incubation for 1 hour at room temperature. The complexes were washed 3 times with 1× PBS with Tween-20 and resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer. The immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted from the beads through heating at 100 °C for 10 minutes and finally were analyzed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibody.

Cell Viability Assay

The cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4000 cells per well. After 24 hours, the original medium was replaced by 200 μL of medium with or without NALA, AZD3965, or GSK2837808A. CCK-8 solution (MCE) (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium: CCK-8 = 10:1) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 90 minutes at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Colony Formation Assay

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 800–1000 cells per well, and the medium was refreshed every 3 days. After 14 days, the cells were fixed and then stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 minutes.

Histologic Staining, Immunohistochemistry, and 5-Ethynyl-2′-Deoxyuridine Incorporation Assay

Mouse liver tissues were processed for both paraffin and frozen sectioning. For paraffin sections (5 μm), tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Immunohistochemical staining for Ki-67 (Abcam) was performed to assess the proliferative ability of hepatocytes. In addition, EdU incorporation assays were conducted on paraffin sections to evaluate de novo DNA synthesis. For cryosections, fresh liver tissues were directly embedded in optimum cutting temperature compound and snap-frozen, followed by sectioning at 8–10 μm. These frozen sections were utilized for Oil Red O staining to visualize intrahepatic lipid accumulation.

Lactate and Triglyceride Measurement

The concentrations of lactate in serum, hepatic tissues, cell lysates, and culture medium were determined using a Lactate Assay Kit (Solarbio). Briefly, the tissues and cells were homogenized and centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to enzymatic colorimetric analysis according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the quantification of intrahepatic lipids, the TG levels in liver tissues were measured using a TG Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) following the provided instructions.

Primary Hepatocyte Isolation

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from WT, Ldhafl/fl, and Ldhafl/fl;Alb-Cre mice using a 2-step collagenase perfusion method as previously described.53 Briefly, a catheter was inserted into the portal vein, and the liver was perfused with prewarmed perfusion medium at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. Once the liver visibly turned white, the inferior vena cava was quickly incised. The catheter was firmly fixed in position, and the perfusion rate was adjusted to 7 mL/min, with perfusion continuing for approximately 10 minutes. Subsequently, the liver was perfused with prewarmed digestive medium containing collagenase type IV, Mg2+, and Ca2+ at a rate of 8 mL/min for 3–5 minutes. During this process, the outlet was intermittently pressed with a cotton swab to facilitate the digestion process. After perfusion, the liver was excised and placed in the culture medium. The liver capsule was torn open, and the culture medium containing cells was collected and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to remove debris. High-purity hepatocytes were obtained through sequential low-speed centrifugations (50 g, 3 minutes) followed by optimal Percoll gradient centrifugation (200 g, 10 minutes).54 The purified murine primary hepatocytes were washed twice with cold culture medium, counted, and then seeded into culture plates. Following cell attachment, the monolayers were subjected to PBS or NALA treatment for subsequent analysis.

Murine Hepatocyte Organoid Culture and Analysis

The purified murine primary hepatocytes were washed twice with cold culture medium, counted, and mixed with Matrigel (Corning) in plates. For each well of a 24-well plate, 20,000–50,000 cells were seeded. Once the Matrigel solidified, liver organoid medium (JFKR) was added. Fourteen days after seeding, the organoids were mechanically fragmented and reseeded into new Matrigel. During the culturing period, the medium was refreshed at least once every 3 days. After treatment with either PBS or 10 mM NALA, the organoids were photographed and then harvested for Western blotting, RT-qPCR, or bulk RNA-seq analysis (LC Science).

CUT&Tag, Bulk RNA Sequencing, and Functional Enrichment Analyses

The AML12 cells treated with or without 10 mM NALA were subjected to CUT&Tag and bulk RNA-seq analyses. A total of 100,000 cells/sample were prepared for CUT&Tag analysis (PTM Bio), and the primary antibody included anti-H3K9la and anti-IgG negative control (PTM Bio). For RNA-seq analysis, total cellular RNA was isolated using the Total RNA Isolation Kit (Yeason), and the second generation of RNA-seq was performed by BGI Genomics. Furthermore, the KEGG analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes (P < .05) from CUT&Tag and RNA-seq analyses. The GO term analysis was utilized for pathway enrichment analysis of genes with increased H3K9la modifications and upregulated transcription.

Theoretical Modeling

An AlphaFold-multimer was used to predict the complex formed by mouse GCN5 (Q9JHD2) and histone H3.1 (P68433).55,56 The input sequences are that of N-acetyltransferase, which is the catalytic domain of GCN5, and that of a 13-mer peptide extracted from histone H3.1, which includes the lactylation site Lys9 as its central residue. Among all generated complexes, the interaction between positively charged Arg8 of H3.1 and negatively charged E639 of GCN5 was assumed to play a critical role in binding of the 2 proteins.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) and R language (version 4.0.3), and continuous data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparison of groups was achieved with the Student t test or 1-way analysis of variance plus a post hoc test. Values of P < .05 were considered as significantly different. The Pearson test was used to perform correlation analyses.
