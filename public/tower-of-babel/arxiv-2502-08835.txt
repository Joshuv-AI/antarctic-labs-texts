Abstract
We propose a new bundle-based augmented Lagrangian framework for solving constrained convex problems. Unlike the classical (inexact) augmented Lagrangian method (ALM) that has a nested double-loop structure, our framework features a single-loop process. Motivated by the proximal bundle method (PBM), we use a bundle of past iterates to approximate the subproblem in ALM to get a computationally efficient update at each iteration. We establish sub-linear convergences for primal feasibility, primal cost values, and dual iterates under mild assumptions. With further regularity conditions, such as quadratic growth, our algorithm enjoys linear convergences. Importantly, this linear convergence can happen for a class of conic optimization problems, including semidefinite programs. Our proof techniques leverage deep connections with inexact ALM and primal-dual principles with PBM.

Augmented Lagrangian method
. Many algorithms have been developed to solve

[
2
,
7
]
. In this paper, we focus on the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM), which is a conceptually simple yet algorithmically powerful framework for constrained optimization
[
15
,
16
,
17

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{x\in\Omega}\;\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k}),

Related works
. One main approach to acquiring an acceptable

is to develop another iterative algorithm as a subroutine, such as the accelerated proximal gradient methods

or Frank-Wolf methods
[

]

This approach is useful when one can exploit the problem-specific properties to get a solution to (

Another approach is to design a sequence of simpler sets

to approximate

involves a positive semidefinite constraint such as

This leads to simpler linear programs or second-order cone programs

A third approach, known as the Burer-Monteiro (BM) factorization

37

Our contributions.

In this paper, we move beyond the iALM and introduce a new

augmented Lagrangian framework.
Our key idea is to leverage

Finally, our algorithm

A Bundle-based Augmented Lagrangian Framework and
BALA

This section presents a new bundle-based augmented Lagrangian framework and a specific algorithm

. Similar to (inexact) ALM, our approach is essentially based on a primal and dual framework, utilizing both primal and dual information to update iterates. We recall the primal problem is shown in

in the form of

\displaystyle g^{\star}:=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;g(y),

where we denote
g(y):=-\theta(y),\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Note that the optimal value is related as
g^{\star}=-\theta^{\star}

3.1
A conceptual idea of inner approximations

The primal update
1a
is the computationally expensive step in ALM, often due to the potentially complicated convex set
\Omega
. For example,
\Omega
often involves the set of positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices in many applications
[
8
,
45
]
. Even testing the membership of

typically requires a complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{n}^{3})
, where

denotes the PSD matrix dimension.

Unlike the classical choice of getting an inexact solution

\Omega
by another simpler convex set
\Omega_{k}\subset\Omega
, such that the following approximated subproblem admits an efficient and exact solution

w_{k+1}\in\argmin_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\;\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k}).

(12)

For example, one may use the set of diagonally dominant matrices to approximate PSD matrices, leading to a set of linear constraints. This idea has indeed been used widely in other contexts (but not in the ALM framework); see e.g.,
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
. When applying this approximation idea in the ALM, if we directly replace the primal update
1a
with
12

. An
\epsilon
-optimal solution would mean to have a very accurate inner approximation, which poses two challenges 1) constructing an accurate approximation

is itself a non-trivial problem
[
46
]
and 2) solving
12
exactly with this
\Omega_{k}
might not be computationally simple
[
32
]
.

To avoid the challenges, our key idea is to exploit the past iterates to iteratively update the approximation
\Omega_{k}
, which can guide our search process and reach a global minimizer. This is analogous to the PBM that iteratively approximates the original (potentially complicated) convex function
f
.

3.2
A novel bundled-based inner approximation

Our starting point is to relax the requirement of obtaining an
\epsilon
-optimal solution to
1a
from
12
. Instead, we will use
w_{k+1}
to update the next inner approximation
\Omega_{k+1}\subset\Omega
.
It is important to note – and we will elaborate on this later – that these approximations are typically not nested, i.e.,
\Omega_{k}\not\subseteq\Omega_{k+1}
. At each iteration, the inner approximation
\Omega_{k}
can be chosen to be very simple, enabling an exact and computationally efficient solution to
12
. Indeed, each
\Omega_{k}

could be as simple as a
line segment
, such that
12
admits an analytical solution (no other subroutines are required).

To achieve this, we will use a dual function to guide the update of
\Omega_{k+1}
and employ a testing step similar to the PBM. Each iteration of our bundled-based augmented Lagrangian framework has three steps: a) computing a pair of primal and dual candidates from
12
; b) testing the descent progress; c) updating the approximation
\Omega_{k+1}
.

Step a) Computing a pair of candidates.

We compute an exact optimal solution
w_{k+1}
to
12
as our primal candidate. Any convex inner approximation
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
and dual initial point
y_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are acceptable for the first iteration. We perform a dual ascent on
\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(w_{k+1},\cdot)
to get a dual candidate:

z_{k+1}=y_{k}+\rho(b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}).

(13)

Step b) Testing the descent progress.
We then test if the candidate
(w_{k+1},z_{k+1})
makes sufficient descent progress. In particular, we examine the cost drop of the dual function, i.e.,

g(y_{k})-g(z_{k+1})
. Ideally, we want the dual cost to decrease sufficiently.
Since we have approximated the primal augmented Lagrangian in
12
, it is useful to define an induced
approximated
dual function

g_{k}(y)=-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}(x,y).\vskip-2.84526pt

(14)

Instead of fixing a constant threshold, we use a dynamic test

g(y_{k})-g(z_{k+1})\geq\beta(g(y_{k})-g_{k}(z_{k+1})),

(15)

where
\beta\!\in\!(0,1)
. The term
g(y_{k})-g_{k}(z_{k+1})
can be viewed as an approximated dual cost drop. By definition, we have

g(z_{k+1})\!=\!-\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}(x,z_{k+1})\geq\!-\!\min_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}(x,z_{k+1}).

(16)

Then, if
15
is satisfied, the true cost drop
g(y_{k})-g(z_{k+1})
would be at least
\beta
close to the approximated dual cost drop. In this case, we have found a good candidate
(w_{k+1},z_{k+1})
, and we update the iterates
x_{k+1}=w_{k+1}
and
y_{k+1}=z_{k+1}
(this is called as a
descent step
). Otherwise, we set
x_{k+1}=w_{k}
and
y_{k+1}=z_{k}
(this is called as a
null step
).

Step c) Updating the inner approximation.
Regardless of a descent or null step, we update
\Omega_{k+1}
as follows.

Assumption 3.1
.

The approximation set
\Omega_{k+1}
satisfies

1.

Proper set:

\Omega_{k+1}
is convex and closed;

2.

Inner approximation:
we have
\Omega_{k+1}\subseteq\Omega
;

3.

Dual information:
we require
v_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1}
, where
v_{k+1}\in\Omega
satisfies
g(z_{k+1})=-\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})
;

4.

Primal information:
if the step
k
is a null step, then we require
w_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1}
.

Essentially, we use a
bundle
of past points, including
v_{k+1}
(from its dual) and
w_{k+1}
(from its primal), to construct the inner approximation. These rules in updating
\Omega_{k+1}
are crucial for the convergence guarantees, and they share a similar structure with
Assumption

2.1
. As alluded to at the beginning of this section, the set
\Omega_{k+1}
can simply contain only a line segment connecting
v_{k+1}
and
w_{k+1}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Omega_{k+1}=\{\alpha v_{k+1}+(1-\alpha)w_{k+1}:0\leq\alpha\leq 1\}.

In this case, the subproblem
12
can be extremely simple to solve, as it becomes a convex quadratic program (QP) with a scalar variable (see
Appendix

A
for computational details).

4.3
Linear convergences under regularity conditions

We have established the sublinear convergences of
BALA
when the construction of
\Omega_{k}
satisfies
Assumption

3.1
. We here show linear convergence for
BALA
under a stronger assumption on
\Omega_{k}
and the dual function
g
defined in (
11
).

Our intuition is inspired by the recent advances in linear convergences in inexact ALM and PPM
[
20
,
22
,
48
]
. All these results require a
quadratic growth
condition, i.e., there is a constant
\alpha>0
such that

g(y)-g^{\star}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\cdot\mathrm{dist}^{2}(y,\Omega_{\mathrm{D}}),\hskip 10.00002pt\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(18)

where
\Omega_{\mathrm{D}}
is the optimal solution set in
11
. As our
BALA
is closely related to iALM and PBM (see
Appendix

B
), we may thus expect linear convergence when
g
satisfies quadratic growth
18
. However, the iALM assumes an oracle to obtain an inexact point
x_{k+1}
satisfying (
3
). In contrast,
BALA
is single-loop and incorporates all the intermediate steps to update the next point
x_{k+1}
(i.e., null steps). We need another key assumption: there exists a
\gamma>0
, such that the approximated dual function
g_{k}
in (
14
) satisfies

g_{k}(y)\leq g(y)\leq g_{k}(y)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2},\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(19)

This condition means that the lower approximation function
g_{k}
captures the true function
g
well up to a quadratic error.

Theorem 6

(Linear convergences)
.

Suppose that the dual function
g
satisfies (
18
) and the approximation function
g_{k}
in (
14
) satisfies (
19
) for all
k\geq T
with a constant
\gamma>0
. Choose
\beta\in(0,1/2]
and an augmented Lagrangian constant
\rho\geq\frac{1}{\gamma}
in
BALA
. For all iterations
k\geq T
, we have:

1.

Every iteration is a descent step, i.e., (
15
) is satisfied;

2.

There exists two constants
\mu_{1}\in(0,1),\mu_{2}>0
such that
\mathrm{dist}(y_{k+1},\Omega_{\mathrm{D}})\leq\mu_{1}\cdot\mathrm{dist}(y_{k},\Omega_{\mathrm{D}})
and
\max\{g(y_{k})-g^{\star},\|\mathcal{A}x_{k}-b\|^{2},|\left\langle c,x_{k}\right\rangle-p^{\star}|^{2}\}\leq\mu_{2}\cdot\mathrm{dist}(y_{k},\Omega_{\mathrm{D}})
.

The proof is given in
Section

D.7
, which relies on an abstraction of the analysis techniques in
[
5
,
4
]
that focus on semidefinite programs (SDPs). Indeed, our algorithm

can be directly applied to SDPs, where all steps in
Algorithm

3
have explicit constructions. We outline the details of

when applied to solving SDPs in

closely resembles the classical spectral bundle method in
[
49
]
; see
[

,

]
for recent discussions.

We further note that the quadratic growth

holds for a class of SDPs
[
5
,
22
]
that arise in a range of applications, e.g., Max-Cut
[

]
and matrix optimization
[

. The quadratic closeness condition (

captures the rank of an optimal solution in SDPs

can be constructed in a simple set while ensuring (

enjoys fast linear convergences (guaranteed by

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a novel bundled-based Augmented Lagrangian framework and a new algorithm
BALA
. Our algorithm enjoys a sublinear convergence under a mild assumption and achieves fast linear convergence when certain regularity conditions are satisfied. The superior performance of
BALA
is demonstrated in numerical experiments. We are interested in applying
BALA
to solve large-scale conic programs. The connections between
BALA
with other first-order algorithms, such as momentum-based and Frank-Wolfe-type algorithms, are also interesting.

Appendix A
Computations in
BALA

We here discuss computation details in our proposed
BALA
(see
algorithm

3
). At every iteration
k
,
BALA
performs three updates: a) computing a pair of primal and dual candidates from
12
; b) testing the descent progress; c) updating the approximation
\Omega_{k+1}
. Typically, computing the primal candidate
w_{k+1}
is the most computationally intensive step, which needs to solve the subproblem
12
.
The update of a dual candidate from
13
is computationally trivial.

A.1
Analytical solution to the subproblem
12

For convenience, we reproduce the subproblem
12
below

w_{k+1}\in\argmin_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\;\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})=\left\langle c,x\right\rangle+\left\langle y_{k},b-\mathcal{A}x\right\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|b-\mathcal{A}x\|^{2}.

(A.1)

This is a convex problem with a quadratic cost function. Since the cost function is quadratic, the complexity of solving
A.1
largely depends on the constraint set
\Omega_{k}
. Our idea of
BALA
is to design a simple constraint set
\Omega_{k}
so that
A.1
can be solved efficiently. Our requirement regarding the update of
\Omega_{k}
is listed in
Assumption

3.1
.

In particular, the inner approximations
\Omega_{k}
should contain a bundle of past iterates to approximate
\Omega
. As listed in
Assumption

3.1
, it can be as simple as a line segment, including
v_{k}
(from its dual) and
w_{k}
(from its primal), i.e.,

\Omega_{k}=\mathrm{conv}(v_{k},w_{k}):=\{\alpha v_{k}+(1-\alpha)w_{k}:0\leq\alpha\leq 1\}.

In this case, the subproblem
A.1
becomes an equality-constrained quadratic program with a scalar variable
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
. Its analytical solution is given by a saturation operator,
\alpha^{\star}=\mathrm{sat}(\phi)
, given below

\displaystyle\mathrm{sat}(\phi)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\phi<0,\\
\phi,&\text{if }0\leq\phi\leq 1,\\
1,&\text{if }\phi>1,\end{cases}

where we have defined

\phi=\frac{\rho\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}(b-\mathcal{A}w_{k}),v_{k}-w_{k}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}y_{k}-c,v_{k}-w_{k}\right\rangle}{\rho\|\mathcal{A}(v_{k}-w_{k})\|^{2}}.

As shown in
Theorems

3
,
4
and
5
, this simple update already guarantees the asymptotic and sublinear convergences of our algorithm
BALA
. Depending on different applications, one may use a few more past iterates to construct the inner approximations
\Omega_{k}
, which may give a better convergence behavior. Indeed, as we prove in
Theorem

6
,
BALA
enjoy linear convergences if 1) the dual optimization satisfies a
quadratic growth
condition
18
, and 2) the inner approximation
\Omega_{k}
is chosen such that the induced dual approximation is
quadratically close
to the original dual function
19
. In this case, one may want to choose a few more points to construct
\Omega_{k}
that requires slightly more computation to solve
A.1
.

For example, in SDP applications, if an optimal solution is of low rank, a good strategy is to design an inner approximation
\Omega_{k}
that captures the rank of the optimal solutions
[
5
,
4
]
. In this situation, the subproblem can also be solved efficiently as it only involves a small semidefinite constraint. Moreover, linear convergence is also guaranteed under mild assumptions (e.g., strict complementarity).
We present further details of applying
BALA
to solve SDPs in
Appendix

F
.

A.2
Computations in the descent progress test and inner approximation update

Aside from the main computation of solving the subproblem
12
, we discuss the computation for the test (
15
) and update the set
\Omega_{k+1}
. We first discuss the computation for the test (
15
). At iteration
k
, we recall that the test requires the knowledge of the three values
g(y_{k})
,
g(z_{k+1}),
and
g_{k}(z_{k+1})
. In general, obtaining
g(y_{k})
and
g(z_{k+1})
requires solving an optimization problem and the complexity depends on the difficulty of
\Omega
. However, when dual function
g
admits an analytical form, obtaining
g(y_{k})
and
g(z_{k+1})
simply requires two evaluations of the function. This is indeed the case when the compact set
\Omega
is well-structured. For example, three common symmetric cones (nonnegative orthant, second-order cone, and positive semidefinite cone) with the natural bound admit this property.

•

(Nonnegative orthant) Let
\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\|x\|_{1}\leq a\}
with
a\geq 0
. The dual function
g
can be shown as

g(y)=-\left\langle b,y\right\rangle+a\max\{(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{1},\ldots,(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{n},0\},

where
(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{i}
denotes the
i
-th element of the vector
\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c
.

•

(Second-order cone) Let
\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\|x_{1:n}\|\leq x_{n+1},x_{n+1}\leq a\}
with
a\geq 0
. The dual function
g
takes the form

g(y)=-\left\langle b,y\right\rangle+a\max\{\|(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{1:n}\|-(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{n+1},0\},

where
(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{1:n}
denotes the first
n
elements of
\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c
.

•

(Positive semidefinite cone) Let
\Omega=\{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}:\mathop{\bf tr}(x)\leq a\}
with
a\geq 0
. The dual function
g
becomes

g(y)=-\left\langle b,y\right\rangle+a\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c),0\}.

In addition, if the step
k-1
is a null step, then we have
g(y_{k})=g(y_{k-1})
. If the step
k-1
is a descent step, then we have
g(y_{k})=g(z_{k})
. Thus, one can keep track of progress to save computations.

On the other hand, the function value
g_{k}(z_{k+1})
can be retrieved readily once the subproblem (
12
) is solved. In particular, as we will see in (
B.1
), it holds that

\displaystyle-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2}.

Thus, once
w_{k+1}\in\min_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})
and
z_{k+1}=y_{k}+\rho(b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1})
are constructed,
g_{k}(z_{k+1})
can be recovered as

\displaystyle g_{k}(z_{k+1})=-\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(w_{k+1},y_{k})-\frac{1}{2\rho}\|z_{k+1}-y_{k}\|^{2},

which is computationally trivial.

We next discuss the complexity of the update of
\Omega_{k}
. Viewing
Assumption

3.1
, we see that the only part that requires computation is to find a point
v_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1}
such that
g(v_{k+1})=-\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})
. In other words, it requires finding a point
v_{k+1}
that realizes the dual function value in the Lagrangian function. In general, this requires solving an optimization problem. However, in some cases, it can be done by computing the eigenvector of
\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-c
. Specifically, consider the above three concrete examples of nonnegative orthant, second-order cone, and positive semidefinite cone. It can be shown that
v_{k+1}
can be computed in the following ways:

•

(Nonnegative orthant) Let
p(y)=\max\{(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{1},\ldots,(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{n},0\}
. We have

\displaystyle v_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0},&\text{if}~p(z_{k+1})=0,\\
ae_{i},&\text{if}~p(z_{k+1})>0,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a zero vector, and
e_{i}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-c)_{i}=p(z_{k+1})
.

•

(Second-order cone) Let
p(y)=\max\{\|(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{1:n}\|-(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{n+1},0\}
. We have

\displaystyle v_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0},&\text{if}~p(z_{k+1})=0,\\
av_{+},&\text{if}~p(z_{k+1})>0,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a zero vector, and
v_{+}
is the eigenvector of
\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-c
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\|(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{1:n}\|-(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-c)_{n+1}
in the sense of the second-order cone. We refer readers to
[
52
]
for more details regarding the eigenvalue/eigenvector in the second-order cone.

•

(Positive semidefinite cone) Let
p(x)=\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-C),0\}
. We have

\displaystyle v_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{0},&\text{if}~p(z_{k+1})=0,\\
av_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}},&\text{if}~p(z_{k+1})>0,\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
is a zero matrix, and
v_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the eigenvector of
\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-c
such that
v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-c)v_{+}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-c)
.

Primal-dual Principles in

In this section, we discuss the relationship among

(
Algorithm

3
), PBM (
Algorithm

2
), and iALM (
Algorithm

1
). In
Section

B.1
, we reveal
a deep primal and dual relationship between

in

and the PBM in

, analogous to the connection between the exact ALM and PPM revealed in

. In

B.1
Primal-Dual interplay between
BALA
and PBM

In this subsection, we show that running
BALA
is equivalent to applying PBM for the dual problem (
D
). This is analogous to the connection in
[
15
]
: running an exact ALM on the primal is equivalent to executing an exact PPM on its dual. With this connection, many proofs in the main text (including
Theorems

3
and
4
) are simplified and can be adapted from the PBM counterparts (note that the proof of
Theorem

5
relies on a recent result in the ALM framework
[
21
]
and has not been established in the literature on PBM). This is also the same as a classical strategy for analyzing (exact or inexact) ALM since the seminal work
[
15
]
.

We first present an important lemma that connects the dual update in
BALA
with a proximal step.

Lemma 5
.

The dual update
z_{k+1}
from
13
in
BALA
is the same as the proximal step on the induced approximated dual function, i.e.,

z_{k+1}=\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2},

where
g_{k}
is defined in
14
.

Proof.

Our proof idea is based on primal and dual analysis and the KKT optimality condition. Consider the bundled-based augmented Lagrangian subproblem (
12
) (or equivalently
A.1
) in
BALA
at the iteration
k
. We can reformulate this problem in the following form

\displaystyle-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})=

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\Omega_{k}}-\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})

(B.1a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}-\mathcal{L}(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2}

(B.1b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\max_{x\in\Omega_{k}}-\mathcal{L}(x,y)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2}

(B.1c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2},

(B.1d)

where
B.1a
is a simple sign switch from minimization to maximization,
B.1b
is from the definition of the augmented Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})
in
2
,

B.1c
swaps the minimization and maximization operation since the set
\Omega_{k}
is compact and convex
[
53
, Corollary 37.3.2]
, and finally in the last equality
B.1d
, we have defined an induced approximated dual function (see
14
)

g_{k}(y)=\max_{x\in\Omega_{k}}-\mathcal{L}(x,y)=-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}(x,y),\hskip 18.49988pt\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Once (
A.1
) is solved with a primal candidate
w_{k+1}
, we can compute the optimal solution to (
B.1d
) by

y_{k}+\rho(b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1})
, which is the same as the construction of the dual candidate
z_{k+1}
in
BALA
(see
13
). This is the KKT optimality condition for the inner optimization, i.e.,

0=\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b+(y^{\star}-y_{k})/\rho\,\Rightarrow\,y^{\star}=y_{k}+\rho(b-\mathcal{A}x^{\star}).

This completes the proof.
∎

In other words, the construction of the dual candidate
z_{k+1}
from
13
in
BALA
is equivalent to performing a proximal step on approximated dual function
g_{k}
centered at
y_{k}
. We remark that the equivalence in
Lemma

5
is not entirely surprising since when we have
\Omega_{k}=\Omega
, it recovers the finding in
[
15
]
that an exact ALM step on the primal problem is equivalent to an exact PPM step on its dual.

Another interesting connection is that the induced approximated dual function
g_{k}
satisfies the requirements in
Assumption

2.1
for PBM introduced in
Section

2.2
. We have the following result.

Theorem 7
.

Consider the inner approximation
\Omega_{k+1}\subset\Omega
in
BALA
. Let
g_{k+1}(\cdot)\!=\!-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k+1}}\mathcal{L}(x,\cdot)
be an approximated dual function of
g
. Then, the fact that
\Omega_{k+1}
satisfies
Assumption

3.1
implies that the function
g_{k+1}
satisfies
Assumption

2.1
.

Proof.

Recall that the points
w_{k+1}
and
z_{k+1}
correspond to the primal and dual candidates in
BALA
; see the updates in
12
and
13
.
We verify that the requirements in
Assumption

3.1
imply those in
Assumption

2.1
one by one.

•

Firstly, as
\Omega_{k+1}\subseteq\Omega
, it is immediate to see that

\displaystyle g(y)=-\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}(x,y)\geq-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k+1}}\mathcal{L}(x,y)=g_{k+1}(y),\hskip 18.49988pt\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(B.2)

This indicates that the induced approximation
g_{k+1}
serves a global lower approximation of the original dual
g
. This also highlights the principle that imposing a restriction on the primal problem results in a relaxation on its dual.

•

Secondly, we have that

\displaystyle g_{k+1}(y)=-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k+1}}\mathcal{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq-\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},y)

(B.3a)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})+\left\langle z_{k+1}-y,b-\mathcal{A}v_{k+1}\right\rangle

(B.3b)

\displaystyle=g(z_{k+1})+\left\langle z_{k+1}-y,b-\mathcal{A}v_{k+1}\right\rangle,

(B.3c)

\displaystyle=g(z_{k+1})+\left\langle\mathcal{A}v_{k+1}-b,y-z_{k+1}\right\rangle,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(B.3d)

In
B.3a
, we use the fact that
v_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1}
is a feasible solution (recall that
\Omega_{k+1}
contains
v_{k+1}
by the third requirement in
Assumption

3.1
). The equality in
B.3b
applies the fact that the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(x,y)
is linear in
y
, and the equality in
B.3c
uses
\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})=-g(z_{k+1})
by the third requirement in
Assumption

3.1
.

Recall that we further have
\mathcal{A}v_{k+1}-b\in\partial g(z_{k+1})
; see
Lemma

7
. Thus,
B.3
indicates that
g_{k+1}
includes the subgradient lowerbound of
g
at
z_{k+1}
.

•

Lastly, we have the following inequality

\displaystyle g_{k+1}(y)=-\min_{x\in\Omega_{k+1}}\mathcal{L}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq-\mathcal{L}(w_{k+1},y)

(B.4a)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(z_{k+1})-g_{k}(z_{k+1})-\left\langle c,w_{k+1}\right\rangle-\left\langle y,b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}\right\rangle

(B.4b)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(z_{k+1})+\mathcal{L}(w_{k+1},z_{k+1})-\left\langle c,w_{k+1}\right\rangle-\left\langle y,b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}\right\rangle

(B.4c)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle z_{k+1},b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}\right\rangle-\left\langle y,b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}\right\rangle

(B.4d)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle z_{k+1}-y,b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}\right\rangle

(B.4e)

\displaystyle=g_{k}(z_{k+1})+\left\langle y-z_{k+1},(y_{k}-z_{k+1})/\rho\right\rangle,\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(B.4f)

In
B.4a
, we have used the fact that
w_{k+1}
is a feasible point in
\Omega_{k+1}
(recall that
\Omega_{k+1}
contains
w_{k+1}
by the fourth requirement in
Assumption

3.1
when step
k
is a null step). The equality in
B.4b
is a simple reformulation, and
B.4c
is by the definition of
g_{k}
and the fact
w_{k+1}\in\argmin_{x\in\Omega_{k}}\mathcal{L}(x,y_{k})
since
B.1c
. The equality in
B.4d
is due to the simple calculation

\mathcal{L}(w_{k+1},z_{k+1})-\left\langle c,w_{k+1}\right\rangle=\left\langle z_{k+1},b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}\right\rangle.

In
B.4f
, we use the update that

z_{k+1}-y_{k}=\rho(b-\mathcal{A}w_{k+1}).

Finally, we note that
(y_{k}-z_{k+1})/\rho\in\partial g_{k}(z_{k+1})
is the optimality condition for the problem

z_{k+1}=\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}g_{k}(y)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2}.

This establishes that
g_{k+1}
aggregates the subgradient of
g_{k}
at
z_{k+1}
.

To summarize, the three inequalities
B.2
,
B.4
and
B.3
confirm the three requirements in
Assumption

2.1
, respectively.
∎

Theorem

7
confirms that the function
g_{k}
is a valid lower approximation for the dual function
g
in PBM. Thus, with the view of (
B.1
), we see that the dual candidate
z_{k+1}
in
BALA
is the same as the solution of a proximal subproblem with the approximation function
g_{k}
in PBM for (

).
Finally, by the definition of
g_{k}
(
14
), we observe that the testing criterion (

) in

is the same as that in PMB for (
D
). Thus, we conclude that

is equivalent to PBM for (
D
) with the sequence of lower approximation functions
\{g_{k}\}
induced by the sequence of inner approximation sets

from

.

Remark B.1

(Insights beyond PBM)
.

While
Lemma

5
and
7
present an exact primal and dual relation between our algorithm
BALA
and the well-established PBM, we believe that our bundle-based Augmented Lagrangian framework provides complementary insights and extends beyond the proximal bundle framework. First, as we will demonstrate in
Section

B.2
, our algorithm also has a close relationship with the inexact ALM framework. Then, all classical developments in inexact ALM may be adapted into our algorithmic framework. Second, our algorithm
BALA
essentially uses a line segment between
v_{k+1}
(representing the current dual information) and
w_{k+1}
(aggregating the past primal information) to guide our iterative progress. This perspective may suggest potential links to other first-order methods, such as momentum-based algorithms
[
54
]
and Frank-Wolfe-type algorithms
[
27
,
55
]
, though establishing an exact connection requires further investigation and is left for future work. Finally, our bundle-based Augmented Lagrangian framework also complements the viewpoints of interior-point and penalty methods
[
1
, Chapter 11]
in addressing
\Omega
. Our idea is to approximate the complicated convex set
\Omega
using a line segment while interior-point and penalty methods use a suitable barrier or penalty function.
\square

B.2
BALA
in the iALM framework

As highlighted in
Section

3
,
BALA
is designed in the general framework of ALM. It also shares a similar algorithmic structure with the iALM in
algorithm

1
. We here establish a close relation between
BALA
and
algorithm

1
. This relationship allows us to utilize a recent result in
[

]
to prove the improved sublinear rate in
Theorem

5
.

Our algorithm

features a single-loop process, unlike the explicit nested structure in the classical iALM. Still, the iterates

and

in

are updated only for the descent step, i.e., the descent test

is satisfied. In contrast, the iterates and the objective function remain the same between two consecutive descent steps, i.e., when

is not satisfied. Thus, we expect that the satisfaction of

means that the updates
x_{k+1}
and
y_{k+1}
provide meaningful progress. Indeed, as shown in
Lemma

1
, each update of
x_{k+1}
and
y_{k+1}
implies that
x_{k+1}
is an inexact solution to the original subproblem (
1a
) with the inexactness quantified by

. For convenience, we re-iterate
Lemma

1
in the following result.

Lemma 6
.

If step
k
of
BALA
satisfies the criterion
15
, then
x_{k+1}
is an
\epsilon_{k+1}
-suboptimal solution to
\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})
in
3
, i.e., we have

\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x_{k+1},y_{k})-\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}_{\rho}(x,y_{k})\leq\epsilon_{k+1},

with the suboptimality measure as

\epsilon_{k+1}=(g(y_{k})-g(y_{k+1}))/\beta.

Lemma

6
is just a restatement of
Lemma

1
. Thus, the proof is the same as that in
Lemma

1
.
Both
BALA
and iALM employ the same gradient ascent strategy for the dual update. In this sense, it is clear that our
BALA
in
algorithm

3
is closely related to the iALM in
algorithm

1
.
Moreover, we can interpret that the goal of the null steps in
BALA
is to search for an acceptable primal point
x_{k+1}
, which is similar to the spirit of (
3
) in iALM. Instead of applying another iterative algorithm to solve (
3
) aiming to satisfy (
4
),
BALA
solves the sequence of problems of the form (

) with the sequence of inner approximations
\{\Omega_{k}\}
to satisfy (

). Note that the sequence of problems of the form (

) is guided by an approximated dual function (see
Lemma

5
). As guaranteed by
Lemma

, the number of total null steps is upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. Our discussions in
Appendix

A
have shown that all the computations in
BALA
can be extremely efficient. With the interpretation in
Lemma

6
, many recent developments in the iALM framework (such as
[

]
) might be adapted to our
BALA
algorithm. We will use this insight to prove
Theorems

and

.

Appendix C
Connection to (sub)gradient methods

This section discusses some connections between
BALA
and the dual (sub)gradient method. To start with, we review a classical result in convex optimization.

Lemma 7
.

Consider the function
g
in (
D
), and let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. If
x^{\star}
solves
\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}(x,y)
, then
\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b
is a subgradient of
g
at
y
, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\in\partial g(y)
.

Proof.

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. It holds that

\displaystyle g(z)=-\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}(x,z)\geq-\mathcal{L}(x^{\star},z)

\displaystyle=-(\left\langle c,x^{\star}\right\rangle+\left\langle z,b-\mathcal{A}x^{\star}\right\rangle)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle c,x^{\star}\right\rangle+\left\langle z,\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle c,x^{\star}\right\rangle+\left\langle y,\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\right\rangle+\left\langle z-y,\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-(\left\langle c,x^{\star}\right\rangle+\left\langle y,b-\mathcal{A}x^{\star}\right\rangle)+\left\langle z-y,\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\right\rangle

\displaystyle=g(y)+\left\langle z-y,\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\right\rangle,

where the first inequality plugs in a feasible point
x^{\star}
, the fourth equality adds and subtracts the term
\left\langle y,\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b\right\rangle
, and the last equality uses the fact that
x^{\star}
solves
\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}(x,y)
.
Since
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is arbitrary, the above confirms that
\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b
is a subgradient of
g
at
y
.
∎

Given a
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
Lemma

7
confirms that if we can find a point
x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
g(y)=-\mathcal{L}(x^{\star},y)=-\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathcal{L}(x,y)
(i.e.,
x^{\star}
archives the dual function value in the Lagrangian function at
y
), then
\mathcal{A}x^{\star}-b
is one valid subgradient of the dual function
g
. This result is standard since the Lagrangian function
\mathcal{L}(x,y)
is linear in
y

[
47
, Chapter 4]
.
Recall that we have used
Lemma

7
in the proof of
Theorem

7
, especially in
B.3
.

We have the following relationship between
BALA
and the dual subgradient method.

Lemma 8
.

If the iteration
k
in
BALA

Proof.

If iteration

The key insight in the proof of

Another perspective is to look at the approximated dual function

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{L}(v_{k},y)

Remark C.1

(Improved performance compared to naive subgradient methods)
.

While
Lemma

8
highlights a close relationship between our algorithm
BALA
and the standard dual subgradient method,
BALA
enjoys significantly improved convergence behaviors. In particular, the standard dual subgradient method would accept
z_{k+1}
as the next iterate, whereas
BALA
updates
z_{k+1}
only if the reduction in the objective value meets at least a fraction of the predicted decrease; see
15
. Additionally, for null steps,
BALA
will also
aggregate information from past iterations into the next inner approximation. This seemingly subtle refinement enables us to establish both asymptotic and sublinear convergence results in
Theorems

5
,
3
and
4
for any augmented Lagrangian constant
\rho
. However, subgradient methods typically require a carefully controlled diminishing step size sequence to guarantee convergence
[
47
, Theorem 7.4.]
; an improperly chosen step size may result in slow progress, oscillatory behavior, or, in some cases, failure to converge.
\square

Thus, the two constants

\displaystyle\max\{g(y_{k})-g^{\star},\|\mathcal{A}x_{k}-b\|^{2},|\left\langle c,x_{k}\right\rangle-p^{\star}|^{2}\}

Appendix F
Application to Semidefinite Programs

In this section, we provide details on the application of
BALA
to solving semidefinite programs (SDPs). SDPs have a wide range of applications across various fields. Rather than reviewing these in depth (see
[
2
]
), we simply emphasize that SDPs form a broad and powerful class of conic programs, encompassing linear programs (LPs) and second-order cone programs (SOCPs) as special cases. Therefore, all the discussions below can be easily adapted to solve any LPs and SOCPs, as well as conic programs involving a mixture of LP, SOCP, and positive semidefinite constraints.

F.1
SDPs with a trace constraint

The standard primal and dual SDPs are optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}

\displaystyle\left\langle C,X\right\rangle

(F.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject~to}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}X=b,

\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+},

\displaystyle\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}

\displaystyle\left\langle b,y\right\rangle

(F.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject~to}

\displaystyle C-\mathcal{A}^{*}y\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}.

where
C\in\mathbb{S}^{n},b\in\mathbb{R}^{m},
and the linear map
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{S}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
are the problem data. As well see below, applying
BALA
to solve
F.1
and
F.2
closely resembles the classical Spectral Bundle Method (
SBM
), originally introduced in
[
49
]
; also see recent discussions in
[
5
,
4
]
.

We assume the set of the optimal solutions to
F.1
is compact, then there exists a constant
\gamma\geq 0
such that
\mathop{\bf tr}(X^{\star})\leq\gamma
for all optimal solution
X^{\star}
in
F.1
. This assumption is mild and less restrictive than that
[
49
]
, where the equality trace constraint
\mathop{\bf tr}(X)=\gamma
is needed.
Therefore, adding the constraint
\mathop{\bf tr}(X)\leq\gamma
into
F.1
does not affect the optimal solution set. Let
\Omega=\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}:\mathop{\bf tr}(X)\leq\gamma\}
.
We can now consider the following primal and dual problems

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}

\displaystyle\left\langle C,X\right\rangle

(F.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{subject~to}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}X=b,

\displaystyle X\in\Omega,

\displaystyle-\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;g(y)=

\displaystyle-\left\langle b,y\right\rangle+\gamma\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-C),0\}.

(F.4)

It is clear that
F.3
is of the form of our problem
P
as the constraint set
\Omega
becomes compact.
The fact that
F.4
is the dual problem of
F.3
can be seen from the identity

\displaystyle\gamma\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-C),0\}

\displaystyle=\max\{\gamma\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}y-C),0\}

\displaystyle=\max_{X\in\Omega}\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}y-C,X\right\rangle,\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Further details on the trace constraint in primal and dual SDPs can be found in
[
4
]
.

F.2
Applications of
BALA
in solving SDPs

We can now directly apply

to solve

Step a): primal and dual candidate.
We perform the following two steps to acquire a pair of primal and dual candidates

\displaystyle W_{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\argmin_{X\in\Omega_{k}}\;\left\langle C,X\right\rangle+\left\langle y_{k},b-\mathcal{A}X\right\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|b-\mathcal{A}X\|^{2}

It is clear that

Remark F.1

(Linear convergences in solving SDPs)
.

In the context of SDPs, the quadratic growth property has been known to hold locally around the solution set
[
57
,
50
,
5
,
22
]

[

,
28
]
. Moreover, it is also shown that semi-smooth Netwon-CG methods are effective in solving the subproblem in iALM
[

]
. However, the semi-smooth Newton-CG method requires the computation of the second-order information of the Lagrangian function, which can be complicated and computationally challenging. On the other hand, recently, the

has been revisited and shown to enjoy linear convergence

in (

in (

such that

\displaystyle g_{k}(y)\leq g(y)\leq g_{k}(y)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-y_{k}\|^{2},

provided the constraint set
\Omega_{k}
in

captures the rank of the primal solutions in
F.1
and strict complementarity holds
[
5
]
. This suggests that
SBM
is particularly effective when the primal SDP

admits low-rank solutions as the subproblem (
F.5
) can be solved efficiently. We also provide some numerical evidence in
Figure

.

F.3
Proof of
Theorem

9

Firstly, recall that we have
\Omega_{k}=\{\eta\bar{X}_{k}+V_{k}SV_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}:\eta\geq 0,S\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{r},\eta+\mathop{\bf tr}(S)\leq\gamma\}
and
V_{k}
is orthonormal, so clearly
\Omega_{k}\subseteq\Omega=\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}:\mathop{\bf tr}(X)\leq\gamma\}
.
Secondly, we argue that we can find
v_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})

\displaystyle=-g(z_{k+1})

\displaystyle=-\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle+\gamma\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C),0\}.

Suppose
\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C)<0
. Then choosing
v_{k+1}=0\in\Omega_{k+1}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle-\gamma\max\{\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C),0\}

\displaystyle=-g(z_{k+1}).

On the other hand, suppose
\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C)\geq 0
. Let
v_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C)v_{+}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C)
. As
v_{+}\in\mathrm{Span}(V_{k+1})
by the design of
V_{k+1}
, we know there exists a unit vector
e\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that
V_{k+1}e=v_{+}
. Let
\eta=0
,
S=\gamma ee^{\mathsf{T}}
, and

v_{k+1}=\eta\bar{X}_{k+1}+V_{k+1}SV_{k+1}^{\mathsf{T}}=\gamma V_{k+1}ee^{\mathsf{T}}V_{k+1}^{\mathsf{T}}=\gamma v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}.

We see that
v_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(v_{k+1},z_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\left\langle C,v_{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle z_{k+1},b-\mathcal{A}v_{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle C,\gamma v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle+\left\langle z_{k+1},b-\mathcal{A}(\gamma v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle C,\gamma v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle-\left\langle z_{k+1},\mathcal{A}(\gamma v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle+\left\langle C-\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1},\gamma v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle-\gamma\left\langle\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C,v_{+}v_{+}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle b,z_{k+1}\right\rangle-\gamma\lambda_{\max}(\mathcal{A}^{*}z_{k+1}-C).

Lastly, by the design of
V_{k+1}
, there exists a
l\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r_{\mathrm{p}}}
such that

and

. Choosing

and

, we see that

\eta\bar{X}_{k+1}+V_{k+1}SV_{k+1}=\eta^{\star}_{k}\bar{X}_{k}+V_{k}Q_{2}\Lambda_{2}Q_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}+V_{k}Q_{1}\Lambda_{1}Q_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}V_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}=W_{k+1}\in\Omega_{k+1},

where the first equality applies (
F.7c

To generate an optimal solution, we construct a positive definite matrix

G.2.2
Binary Quadratic Program

Our third numerical experiment considers the binary quadratic program (BQP) with the form

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle x^{\mathsf{T}}Qx+q^{\mathsf{T}}x

subject to

\displaystyle x_{i}^{2}=1,\hskip 9.24994pti=1,\ldots,n,

where
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are problem data. BQP is a typical example in the field of combinatorial optimization. As BQP is also a polynomial optimization, it can be relaxed as an SDP using the moment relaxation
[
60
]
. It is also known that the SDP raised from the moment relaxation admits low-rank solutions. When the relaxation is second-order, empirical evidence shows that the moment relaxation is tight. Each entry of the symmetric matrix
Q
and the vector
q
is generated randomly following the uniform distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
.

Our fourth numerical experiment considers optimizing a polynomial over a unit sphere

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle p(x)

subject to

\displaystyle x^{\mathsf{T}}x=1,

where
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

n
variables. Similar to

p

\displaystyle p(x)=\mathbf{c}^{\mathsf{T}}[\mathbf{x}]

where
[\mathbf{x}]
is the standard monomial of degree four with
n
variables and
\mathbf{c}
is the corresponding coefficient vector. We then generate the vector
\mathbf{c}
randomly with each entry drawn from a normal distribution with mean
0
and variance
1
.
