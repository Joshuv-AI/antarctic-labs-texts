Experimental Investigation of the Influence of Composition Changes in Shale on Pore Structure and Fractal Characteristics under Different HCl Concentrations
Authors: Zepeng Sun, Qipan Liu, Lin Gan, Yashi Liu, Zhifu Wei, Yue Ni
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcidification is a promising stimulation technique for improving the pore structure of low-permeability shale reservoirs and enhancing shale gas production. Variations in the mineral composition and acid concentration play a significant role in altering the pore systems in shale reservoirs, thereby affecting shale gas transport and production. To investigate the effect of shale composition on pore structure and fractal characteristics under hydrochloric acid (HCl) treatment, experiments were carried out on carbonate-rich (68%), carbonate-medium (45%), and carbonate-poor (2%) shales with different concentrations of HCl solution (1, 5, 10 wt %). X-ray diffraction (XRD), low-temperature nitrogen adsorption, and fractal theory were performed to investigate variations in mineral composition, pore structure, and fractal characteristics of the unreacted and reacted shales. The results illustrated that the HCl treatment significantly reduced the content of carbonate minerals, and the dissolution degree of carbonate minerals increased with the increase in HCl concentration. More meso- and macropores were generated for carbonate-rich and carbonate-medium shale, while more micro- and mesopores were generated in carbonate-poor shale, which led to the increase in total pore volume (TPV) and total specific surface area (TSSA) at different degrees. Moreover, the pore surface roughness (D1) and structure complexity (D2) were reduced with the increase of HCl concentration for carbonate-rich shale, while the alteration of D1 and D2 showed an opposite trend for carbonate-poor shale. Additionally, D1 decreased and D2 increased for carbonate-medium shale. Relevant results could provide theoretical references for the acid fracturing of shale reservoirs with varying mineral compositions.

Introduction

The progress of multistage hydraulic fracturing stimulation techniques in horizontal wells has contributed to a significant increase in the production of oil and gas from shale formation over the past decade. These advanced production techniques are aimed at changing the pore structure and providing permeable channels to mobilize fluid in the shale reservoir.1 However, the production of shale gas suffers from a rapid decline in the initial year,2 which mainly relates to the increase of effective closure stress caused by the declining pore pressure.3 Keeping fractures open and reducing the closure stress can maintain and prolong the shale reservoir productivity. Recent studies showed that acid fracturing can effectively enhance the microfractures and secondary pores in the shale rock.4,5 Therefore, the acid fracturing stimulation technology attracts much attention in enhancing gas migration, improving microstructure, and optimizing acid types in shale reservoirs.

A shale is a rock composed of organic matter and inorganic minerals,6 and its complex geometry nanopore structure forms the storage site and migration channel for shale gas.7 Previous studies revealed that the fracture conductivity and initial structure of shale can be destroyed by the physicochemical interactions between shale and fracturing fluid.8,9 Among water-based stimulation fluids, hydrochloric acid (HCl) is the most common additive used to initiate hydraulic fracturing in shale reservoirs.10,11 The interaction between HCl and minerals of shale has been extensively studied.12,13 The carbonate minerals (calcite and dolomite) in shale formation can react with HCl to produce dissolution pores and channels.14 Sheng et al. investigated the generation of microfracturing behaviors in the Longmaxi carbonate-rich shale after acid-soaking experiments, and the acid dissolution microfractures were observed within calcite and organic matter regions.15 Although the acid treatment has a direct effect on the dissolution of carbonate minerals in shale, the dissolution can lead to an increase in pH and secondary mineral precipitation. Noël et al.16 studied the effect of calcite dissolution on pore-space modification and matrix chemical reactivity. They found that the spatial distribution and relative abundance of calcite were the main factors controlling the chemical saturation state and pH with acidic hydraulic fracture fluid progressing into the matrix. Nevertheless, the characteristics of the pore structure changes in shale with different carbonate minerals during acid fracturing still lack clarity.

Unlike the dissolution of carbonate minerals, quartz remains stable during acid fracturing, which could form a rough plane and improve fracture conductivity.5 Shovkun and Espinoza17 studied the effect of local dissolution of carbonate on the stress state of shale formation caused by acidizing. The results indicated that the dissolution of carbonate minerals led to an increase of vertical-to-horizontal stress anisotropy, which may result in the reactivation of critically oriented natural fractures in shale reservoirs. Asgar et al.18 demonstrated that the surface area and pore volume were higher in the silica-bearing and silica-lean shales than the silica-rich shales after reacting with 1 M HCl solution, owing to the abundance of reactive clay and carbonate phases. Additionally, combined with the acid and proppant, the generated etching profiles can remain open even after the closure stress buildup.19 Therefore, the efficiency of subsurface reservoir stimulation through the acid treatment is quite correlated with the mineral composition of the shale reservoir.

Moreover, different pH of the acidic hydraulic fracture fluid could cause different geochemical scenarios including mineral dissolution and precipitation, which directly leads to changes in pore structure characteristics of shale reservoir.20,21 Tan et al.22 reported that the pH of fracturing fluid had a significant impact on shale fracture roughness, and the alkaline fracturing fluid possessed a stronger transformation ability for the shale reservoir with a high pyrite content. Medina-Rodriguez et al.23 confirmed that the dissolution of carbonate minerals was predominant at low pH, and the silicate and clay dissolution contributed slightly to the formation of secondary pores at high pH. In most cases, researchers mainly focus on understanding the geochemical mechanisms of mineral dissolution and precipitation induced by pH.4,24 However, the effect of mineral composition on the pore structure and fractal characteristics of shale at different pH values during shale–HCl reactions is still a complicated process and needs further research. In addition, evaluating the pore structure parameters combined with the fractal dimension is beneficial for understanding the flow patterns of shale gas.25

Therefore, in this study, we further explore how the mineral composition contributes to the alteration of pore structure and fractal characteristics and bring attention to the role of different HCl concentrations during acid fracturing. For this purpose, samples with a wide range of carbonate mineral content (2 to 68%) were collected and used to react with HCl (1, 5, 10 wt %). Based on the results of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and low-temperature nitrogen (N2) adsorption measurement, the alteration in mineral composition, specific surface area (SSA), pore volume (PV), and the fractal dimension of shale after interaction with HCl was discussed. Moreover, the relationships between shale compositions and pore structure as well as the fractal dimension were obtained. The study results will provide guidance for a clear understanding of the impact of shale compositions on the reservoir pore structure after the acid treatment and a valuable reference for acid fracturing development in shale gas exploitation.

Materials and Methods

Sample Preparation and Experimental Procedure

Three distinct shale samples with different mineral compositions were utilized and labeled as A, B, and C. Samples A and C were taken from the Lower Cambrian Niutitang formation located at Guzhang County in Hunan Province and the Youyang County in Chongqing City, respectively. Sample B from the Lower Silurian Longmaxi formation was obtained from Changning County, Sichuan Province. These collected outcrop samples were sent to the laboratory and crushed to 40–60 mesh before experiments. The mineral compositions of the three shale samples were analyzed through XRD (Table 1). The results illustrated that these shale samples were mainly composed of quartz, feldspar, dolomite, calcite, pyrite, and clay minerals. However, the weight fraction of each mineral varied with the shale samples. Sample A contained the largest amount of carbonate minerals (68%), while sample C possessed the lowest amount of carbonate minerals (2%). Sample B exhibited a medium content of carbonate minerals (45%).

Mineralogical Composition of Shale

Fifty grams of crushed shale samples were reacted with 250 mL of HCl solution in a glass beaker. The HCl solutions included three concentrations (1, 5, 10 wt %). Three weight percent KCl was added to the solutions to reduce the effects of water-induced clay swelling during the experiment. A previous study demonstrated that the pressure effect on mineral solubility is negligible.26 Moreover, considering the security, the experiments were conducted at ambient temperature and pressure. To determine the influence of the fracturing fluid injection phase, the reaction time was set to 20 days. After the reaction, the shale samples were washed with distilled water and subsequently dried in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h to minimize the impact of moisture. The dirged shale samples were stored in sealed bags at room temperature until XRD and low-temperature N2 adsorption were taken.

Experimental Methods

The XRD and low-temperature N2 adsorption measurements were carried out on shale samples before and after the acidizing experiment. The relevant testing procedures and setting parameters of each experimental equipment are discussed below.

XRD Measurement

Bulk mineralogy was obtained by the XRD technique (Rigaku D/Max-III B). The diffractometer was operated using Cu Kα radiation at a current of 40 mA and voltage of 40 kV. The scanning range was from 3 to 70° with a scanning rate of 2.0°/min and a step size of 0.02°. The extracted shale of 40–60 mesh was crushed to a powder sample of 200 mesh for XRD testing.

Low-Temperature N2 Adsorption Experiment

The pore structure parameters of unreacted and reacted shale samples were determined by ASAP 2020 HD88. Before the measurement, the crushed shale samples (40–60 mesh) were placed in an outgas station and automatically degassed for 20 h at 110 °C. Then, the degassed samples were exposed to N2 at 77.15 K with a relative equilibrium adsorption pressure (adsorption equilibrium pressure/saturation pressure, P/P0) in the range of 0.0001–0.995. SSA was calculated based on the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) theory.27 PV was obtained using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) theory.28 Pore size classification followed the criteria established by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC), namely, micropore (<2 nm), mesopore (2–50 nm), and macropore (>50 nm).

Results and Discussion

Mineral Composition

Mineral compositions of the unreacted and reacted shale samples are presented in Figure 1. The XRD results demonstrated that the mineral composition of each shale changed perceptibly when it reacted with HCl at different concentrations. For sample A, the content of carbonate minerals decreased significantly and regularly with an increased HCl concentration. The initial content of carbonate minerals dropped from 68 to 4% after reaction with HCl of 10 wt % concentration, and the calcite was not detected (Figure 1a). In addition, the higher the concentration of HCl, the more carbonate minerals were involved in the reaction. By contrast, the content of other minerals showed an increasing trend. Owing to the measurement of XRD providing a relative rather than absolute composition,31 the increasing content of other minerals was related to the dissolution of carbonate minerals, which may not be true evidence of the formation of these minerals during the shale–HCl reaction.

Mineral composition of shale samples under treatment of different concentrations of HCl ((a–c) represent samples A–C, respectively).

For sample B, the content of carbonate minerals decreased by 15.6% (from 45 to 38%) and 68.9% (from 45 to 14%), respectively, under the 1 and 5 wt % HCl treatment (Figure 1b). However, the dolomite and calcite were not detected in the shale sample after reacting with 10 wt % HCl (Figure 1b). The measured variation characteristics of carbonate minerals reflected that mineral dissolution occurred in the experiments, and the reaction degree was related to the concentration of HCl. Compared to the largest changes in carbonate minerals in sample B, the changes in quartz, plagioclase, pyrite, and clay minerals may be caused by the renormalization of the remaining phases after the dissolution of carbonate minerals. For sample C (Figure 1c), the calcite completely dissolved after reaction with 1 wt % HCl. Dolomite decreased from 1 to 0% after reaction with 5 wt % HCl. The quartz, plagioclase, and clay minerals had a slight increasing change. However, the content of pyrite decreased by 37.5% (from 8 to 5%) after reacting with 10 wt % HCl (Figure 1c).

The observed mineral alterations could potentially be attributed to the complex chemical reactions occurring between the shale and HCl solutions. Pore Structure Analysis

Pore Shape

The shapes of N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms for the unreacted and reacted shale samples are presented in Figure 2a–c. The sorption amount of shale samples showed a rapid increase (P/P0 < 0.2) – slow increase (P/P0 = 0.2–0.8) – rapid increase (P/P0 > 0.8). The adsorption capacity indicated that the micropore filling and monolayer adsorption were dominated at P/P0 < 0.2, the mesopores were gradually filled and multilayer adsorption started at P/P0 = 0.2–0.8, and the capillary coalescence and adsorption saturation occurred at P/P0 > 0.8.32 Additionally, a hysteresis loop was clearly observed in the desorption curve at a P/P0 of 0.4–1.0. According to the IUPAC classification,33 the N2 adsorption isotherms of the shale samples belonged to Type IV and the hysteresis loops were ascribed to Type H2. The features of N2 adsorption isotherms suggested that the main pores of shale samples before and after the HCl treatment were dominated by 2–50 nm mesopores with heterogeneous planar surfaces, and the pore type was mainly inkbottle-shaped pores.30

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms for shale samples with the treatment of different concentrations of HCl ((a–c) represent samples A–C, respectively) and the changes of N2 adsorption capacity (d).

After the HCl treatment, the adsorption/desorption curves had no significant change in samples A–C. Nevertheless, the hysteresis loop areas of the reacted shales increased compared with the unreacted shales (Figure 2a–c). The larger size of the hysteresis loop suggested that treatment with HCl did not alter the type of pores but rather enhanced pore opening.34 The changes in the pore shapes suggested that acid fracturing could improve the microporous property and optimize the pore fracture connectivity of the shale reservoir. Moreover, the adsorption capacity of N2 saturation can reflect the pore space in shale.32 After the HCl treatment, the N2 saturated adsorption capacity increased with the HCl concentration in all shale samples (Figure 2d). The results demonstrated that acid fracturing can unblock fluid seepage pores and fractures, enhance gas adsorption space, and increase pore volume. Moreover, the N2 adsorption volume of sample C increased significantly after the HCl treatment, followed by samples B and A, indicating that acid fracturing contributed to unblocking micropores and mineral-induced fractures in carbonate-poor shale, and mineral composition had a significant effect on the reactions between shale and HCl. It appears that alterations in the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms have a direct impact on the pore structure parameters, such as PV and SSA, which will be explained in the following sections.

Pore Volume and Specific Surface Area

Based on the data of N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms, PV and SSA were obtained using the BJH and BET models, respectively. The pore structure parameters of all shale samples before and after the HCl treatment are listed in Table 2. For sample A, the total pore volume (TPV) showed a significant increase of 28.4, 62.1, and 65.2% after reacting with 1, 5, and 10 wt % HCl, respectively (Figure 3a). With the increase in HCl concentration, the total specific surface area (TSSA) increased by 37.7, 64.4, and 14.7%, respectively, and the increasing rates exhibited a trend of first rising and then declining (Figure 3b). The percentage of mesopores gradually increased with the HCl concentration, resulting in an expansion of the TPV (Figure 3a) and TSSA (Figure 3b). The results indicated that the high concentration of HCl caused more dissolution of carbonate minerals, which generated more mesopores and led to the increase of TPV and TSSA in carbonate-rich shale.

Moreover, the relative rate of change of TPV and TSSA improved when the HCl concentration increased. Therefore, the mineral composition heterogeneity of the shale reservoir directly affected the distribution of pores within the shale matrix and the alteration of the pore structure parameters after acid fracturing.

Pore Size Distribution

To explore the distribution characteristics of PV and SSA for the shale samples before and after the reaction, the pore size distribution results obtained by N2 adsorption are displayed in Figure 5. For sample A, the incremental PV of each shale reacting with HCl of different concentrations gradually increased with the increase of the pore size (Figure 5a). It can be observed that there were three peaks at approximately 9, 50, and 140 nm, and the PV was primarily occupied by meso- and macropores with a diameter exceeding 9 nm for the unreacted and reacted shale samples. Compared with the unreacted shale sample, the incremental PV of the reacted shale increased to various degrees (Figure 5a). Before 20 nm, the incremental PV of the shale treated by 5 wt % HCl was the largest. The increase in incremental PV was positively correlated with the HCl concentration (Figure 5a). Meanwhile, the cumulative PV of the shales treated by 5 and 10 wt % HCl was basically the same. The SSA distributions of sample A before and after the reaction with HCl are shown in Figure 5b. The incremental SSA of the shale treated by 5 wt % HCl was much larger than that of others for the pore diameter <20 nm, which led to the largest cumulative SSA. Nevertheless, for the pore diameter >20 nm, the shale reacting with 10 wt % HCl resulted in a greater increase in pore area, which indicated that more dissolution pores larger than 20 nm were created. The result of the distribution characteristics of PV and SSA for sample A suggested that more mesopores less than 20 nm were formed after the 5 wt % HCl treatment, while more meso- and macropores larger than 20 nm were generated after the 10 wt % HCl treatment.

Pore volume (a, c, e) and pore area (b, d, f) distribution of unreacted and reacted shale samples: (a, b) represent sample A; (c, d) represent sample B; and (e, f) represent sample C, respectively.

For sample B, the incremental PV and SSA of the shale treated by HCl of different concentrations increased to various degrees compared with the level of the unreacted shale, and the increase was positively correlated with the concentration of HCl (Figure 5c,d). The reason was that more carbonate minerals were dissolved as the HCl concentration increased, which raised the PV and SSA of sample B. The incremental PV distribution showed that mesopores between 2 and 50 nm exhibited more significant changes than other pores (Figure 5c), indicating that the mesopores were the most affected by the reaction with HCl. The mesopore increase of the reacted shale caused the increase in cumulative PV. In addition, the incremental SSA of the 10 wt % HCl-treated shale was approximately twice that of the unreacted shale for a pore diameter of less than 10 nm (Figure 5d). This suggested that lots of pores <10 nm were generated in sample B after the 10 wt % HCl treatment, and these small pores led to a significant increase in the cumulative SSA.

For sample C, the incremental PV and SSA of the shale treated by HCl of different concentrations were obviously higher than the unreacted shale (Figure 5e,f). The cumulative PV and SSA of the reacted shale were approximately 5 to 7 and 12 to 14 times that of the unreacted shale, respectively. As shown in Figure 5e,f, the incremental PV and SSA increased sharply for the shale treated by 1 wt % HCl, while they increased little as the concentration of the HCl increased. This illustrated that an excessive concentration of HCl can no longer cause the fracturing fluid to change the pore structure more seriously. Overall, acidification could cause the nanopores, including micro-, meso-, and macropores to become enlarged, opened, and connected with each other to form new and larger pores in the carbonate-poor shale reservoir.

Fractal Dimensions

Fractal dimensions obtained from N2 adsorption are an effective and important method to characterize the irregular structures and complex surfaces of shale reservoir rocks.35,36 The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms indicated that the N2 adsorption amount grew rapidly at a P/P0 of 0.45, and there were two different adsorption mechanisms. The increase in the adsorption amount was primarily attributed to monolayer or multilayer adsorption when P/P0 < 0.45. However, the capillary condensation led to the increase in the adsorption amount for P/P0 > 0.45.37 Therefore, the fractal dimensions calculated below and above the relative pressure of 0.45 were recorded as D1 and D2, respectively. D1 reflects the pore surface roughness, and D2 reflects the pore structure complexity.38D value generally varies between 2 and 3, and the upper value of 3 corresponds to a rough or irregular surface, while the lower value of 2 corresponds to a perfectly smooth surface.39

The fractal dimensions and FHH plots of unreacted and reacted shale samples are illustrated in Table 3 and Figure 6. The calculated D values of all unreacted and reacted shale samples were between 2 and 3, and all of the correlation coefficients were greater than 0.9 (Table 3). The pore structure of all experimental shale samples possessed strong fractal characteristics. For sample A, the fractal dimension D1 and D2 exhibited an increase first and then tended to decrease (Figure 6b). When the shale was treated with 1 wt % HCl, the D1 and D2 increased to a certain extent. However, as the concentration of HCl increased from 5 to 10 wt %, the D1 and D2 values gradually decreased. The results indicated that the pore surface and pore structure of shale became rougher and more complex after the 1 wt % HCl treatment, which led to the enhancement of heterogeneity. The reason for this phenomenon could be closely related to the generation of mesopores with a diameter of 2–20 nm caused by the dissolution of carbonate minerals. However, as the concentration of HCl increased from 5 to 10 wt %, more meso- and macropores were formed owing to the removal of carbonate minerals. A previous study reported that the removal of mineral particles can simplify the pore structure and reduce the fractal dimension.40 Therefore, for carbonate-rich shale, a high concentration of HCl was able to make the pore surface less rough, thereby reducing the complexity of the pore structure.

For sample B, the evolution of D1 and D2 showed an opposite trend with the increasing HCl concentration (Figure 6d). In detail, the D1 of the 1 and 5 wt % HCl-treated shale samples was higher than that of the unreacted shale sample, while their D2 was lower than that of the unreacted shale sample, indicating that the pore surface roughness increased, and the pore structure became more homogeneous. However, the pore surface of the shale became smooth, and the pore structure became more complex after the 10 wt % HCl treatment. Based on the analysis of the pore size distribution of sample B, the acidification mainly affected the mesopores with a diameter of 2–10 nm. The changes in fractal dimensions were mainly related to the degree of dissolution of carbonate minerals after treatment with different concentrations of HCl, which affected the generated numbers of dissolution mesopores.

For sample C, the values of D1 and D2 gradually increased after the HCl treatment (Figure 6f), which illustrated an increase in the surface roughness and pore complexity. A previous study reported that the fractal dimension showed a positive correlation with both TSSA and TPV, and a larger TSSA and TPV may lead to a rougher surface and more complicated pore structure.41 After the acid treatment for sample C, the TSSA and TPV gradually improved with an increasing concentration of HCl. The increase of D1 and D2 was probably because acid dissolved more carbonate minerals and pyrite, and some generated dissolution nanopores enhanced the surface roughness and pore structure complexity.

In order to describe the relationship between carbonate minerals content and fractal dimension, the relative change rates of D1 and D2 with carbonate minerals content are presented in Figure 7. As the shale with the increased amount of carbonate minerals was treated by 1 and 5 wt % HCl, the relative change rates of D1 and D2 exhibited a rapid decrease and then a slight increase. However, after treatment with 10 wt % HCl, the relative change rates of D1 and D2 gradually reduced with the increase of carbonate mineral content. The results showed that under the action of acid fracturing fluid, the influence of low content of carbonate mineral on the fractal dimension of shale was greater than that of the high content, and the impact of moderate content was minimal. Moreover, when the carbonate mineral content was below 45%, increasing the HCl concentration had a greater impact on D1 than on D2.

Relationship between Fractal Dimensions and Shale Pore Structure

The mineral composition heterogeneity of shale directly affects the uniformity and regularity of its pore structure and surface.30 Studies have demonstrated that the etching of shale pore surface and the enhancement of mesopore structure can lead to an increase in surface roughness, but the removal of mineral particles can lead to a reduction in surface roughness.42,43 To further investigate the correlation between the fractal dimension and the pore structure (PV and SSA) of shale, a discussion of their relationship was presented.

As illustrated in Figure 8, the values of D1 and D2 had no obvious correlation with TPV and TSSA for sample A. However, D1 and D2 had a good positive correlation with the micro-PV and SSA, and the relations between fractal dimension and meso-PV and SSA were poor. The results illustrated that the increases in D1 and D2 for sample A after the HCl treatment were most strongly correlated with the number growth of micropores. This was due to a stronger structure inhomogeneity for a smaller pore size and a larger fractal dimension of the pore structure.44 In addition, the fractal dimension had no obvious correlation with macro-PV. Nevertheless, it showed a good positive correlation with the macro-SSA. Previous studies demonstrated that SSA had a significant influence on the fractal dimension, and the shale with higher SSA generally exhibited larger fractal dimensions.35,45 This is mainly due to the fact that the fractal dimension is primarily related to irregularities in pore geometry and complexity of the pore system rather than the pore space.46

Relationship between fractal dimensions and the pore structure of carbonate-rich shale (sample A).

As illustrated in Figure 9, D1 of sample B showed a slight negative correlation with the TPV and TSSA. Instead, a slight positive correlation was discovered between pore structure parameters (TPV and TSSA) and D2. In addition, the relationships between fractal dimensions (D1 and D2) and different types of pores (micro-, meso-, and macropore) exhibited the same correlation trend (Figure 9), and the correlation coefficients were micropore > macropore > mesopore. The results indicated that the increase of PV and SSA for the carbonate-medium shale could make the pore surface smoother and the pore structure more complex. Based on the combined results presented in Figures 3 and 5, the increased proportion of meso- and macropores after the HCl treatment increased the average pore size. Shale with a larger average pore size could lead to a smooth pore surface.47 Furthermore, many closed small mesopores were generally opened after the HCl treatment due to the dissolution of carbonate minerals, resulting in a more complex pore structure.

Relationship between fractal dimensions and pore structure of carbonate-medium shale (sample B).

Fractal dimensions showed a good positive relationship with the TPV and TSSA with a correlation coefficient higher than 0.95 for sample C (Figure 10). The relationships demonstrated that shales with greater TPV or TSSA possessed a larger fractal dimension, which was consistent with previous findings.48,49 Moreover, the positive correlation between the fractal dimensions and the pore structure parameters increased the surface roughness and led to a complicated pore structure. For micro- and mesopores, a strong positive linear relationship between fraction dimensions D1 and D2 and the PV and SSA with coefficients higher than 0.90 was observed. However, a slight positive correlation trend was found for the macropore. Previous research has demonstrated that shale with a higher micropore volume and micropore surface area could result in a more complicated pore structure and higher roughness of the pore surface.41 The increase in the number of micro- and mesopores of carbonate-poor shale after acidification resulted in an increase in TPV and TSSA, as well as an increase in the pore surface roughness and structure complexity.

Conclusions

In this paper, three types of shales with different carbonate mineral contents were treated with HCl. The alteration of mineral composition, pore structures, and fractal dimensions characteristics of shale were investigated using XRD and low-temperature N2 adsorption. The key conclusions were summarized as follows:

(1)The mineral composition of the three types of shales showed relative alterations owing to the shale–HCl interactions. The carbonate mineral content decreased significantly and regularly with increased HCl concentration. Specifically, the carbonate mineral content of carbonate-rich, -medium, and -poor shale decreased by 94, 100, and 100%, respectively, under the 10 wt % HCl treatment.

The mineral composition of the three types of shales showed relative alterations owing to the shale–HCl interactions. The carbonate mineral content decreased significantly and regularly with increased HCl concentration. Specifically, the carbonate mineral content of carbonate-rich, -medium, and -poor shale decreased by 94, 100, and 100%, respectively, under the 10 wt % HCl treatment.

(2)After the HCl treatment, the N2 sorption amount and hysteresis loop areas obviously increased with the HCl concentration for all shales, but the type almost did not change. These changes in the pore shapes suggested that acid fracturing could improve the microporous property and optimize the pore fracture connectivity of the shale reservoir.

These changes in the pore shapes suggested that acid fracturing could improve the microporous property and optimize the pore fracture connectivity of the shale reservoir.

(3)Pore size, TPV, and TSSA showed a significant increase after the HCl treatment of all three types of shales. Under the effect of dissolution of carbonate minerals, more mesopores less than 20 nm were formed for carbonate-rich shale and more pores <10 nm were generated in carbonate-medium shale. However, under the dissolution impact of carbonate minerals and pyrite in carbonate-poor shale, many closed pores opened and the proportion of micro- and mesopores increased.

Moreover, the relationship between fractal dimensions (D1 and D2) and pore structure parameters (TPV and TSSA) in carbonate-rich, -medium, and -poor shale showed no obvious correlation, slight correlation, and significant correlation, respectively.
