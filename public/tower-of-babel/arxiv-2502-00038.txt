The Spectral Barycentre of a Set of Graphs with Community Structure
François G. Meyer

Abstract
The notion of barycentre graph is of crucial importance for machine learning algorithms that process graph-valued data. The barycentre graph is a “summary graph” that captures the mean topology and connectivity structure of a training dataset of graphs. The construction of a barycentre requires the definition of a metric to quantify distances between pairs of graphs. In this work, we use a multiscale spectral distance that is defined using the eigenvalues of the normalized graph Laplacian. The eigenvalues – but not the eigenvectors – of the normalized Laplacian of the barycentre graph can be determined from the optimization problem that defines the barycentre. In this work, we propose a structural constraint on the eigenvectors of the normalized graph Laplacian of the barycentre graph that guarantees that the barycentre inherits the topological structure of the graphs in the sample dataset. The eigenvectors can be computed using an algorithm that explores the large library of Soules bases. When the graphs are random realizations of a balanced stochastic block model, then our algorithm returns a barycentre that converges asymptotically (in the limit of large graph size) almost-surely to the population mean of the graphs. We perform Monte Carlo simulations to validate the theoretical properties of the estimator; we conduct experiments on real-life graphs that suggest that our approach works beyond the controlled environment of stochastic block models. Keywords: Barycentre graph; Soules basis; Fréchet mean; Laplacian Spectral distance; statistical analysis of graph-valued data.

1.7
State of the art

The requirement given by
Eq.

13
can be approximately achieved if
\bm{\Psi}
is
an “average on
\OG(n)
” of the distribution of bases of eigenvectors associated with the
respective Laplacian matrices
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)}
of the graphs in the sample. To this goal several
authors have proposed to align the eigenvectors of the respective graph adjacency matrices

[
37
]
or Laplacian matrices
[
76
]
. Rather than working with the
eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian, the authors in
[
46
]
seek the
barycentre graph on the cone of symmetric positive semidefinite matrices. They characterize
the space of graph Laplacians, a subset of this cone, to develop statistical tests about the
location of the Fréchet mean. Others
[
36
]
have proposed numerical methods to find
the best stochastic block model (SBM) whose eigenvalues match the sample mean eigenvalues.

1.8
The stochastic block model

Experiments performed on random graph ensembles, with adjustable parameters, provide a
mechanism to quantify the performance of graph-valued algorithms

63
]

provide theoretical guarantees for the algorithms presented in this paper, we analyse the
algorithms when the graphs are sampled from a random graph model: the stochastic block model
(e.g.,
[
1
]

In the next subsections, we define the stochastic block model, justify the importance of

1.8.1
Definition

We define the general stochastic block model
{\thesbm\big(\bm{p},q,n\big)}
. Let

be a partition of the vertex set
[n]
into
M
blocks (or communities). We define the
vector

to be the edge probabilities
within each block, and
q
to be the edge probability between blocks. The entries

of the adjacency matrix
\bm{A}
are independent (up to symmetry) and are
distributed with Bernoulli distributions with parameter
p_{m}
if
i
and
j
are in the same
block

, and parameter
q
if
i
and
j
are in distinct blocks.

We often represent
{\thesbm\big(\bm{p},q,n\big)}
by the matrix of edge probabilities, or matrix of
connection probabilities,
\bm{P}
defined by
Eq.

11
. We sometimes consider a balanced
version of the model where all blocks have the same size,
{\lvert B_{m}\rvert}=n/M
, (in that case
we assume without loss of generality that

is a multiple of
M
), and all the edge
probabilities are equal,
p_{1}=\cdots=p_{M}
. We denote this probability space by

{\thesbm\big(p,q,n\big)}
, since

is a scalar and no longer a vector.

1.8.2
Motivation for using stochastic block models

The stochastic block model represents the quintessential exemplar of a graph with community
structure. It has been used extensively in the study of complex real-life graphs

[
35
,
74
]
. Stochastic block models have also been shown to provide
universal approximants (under various norms or distances) (e.g.,

[
3
,
36
,
44
,
61
]
and references therein), and can therefore
be used as building blocks to analyse more complex

graphs. In fact, it was shown in
[
81
]
that the structural analysis of a large
graph is equivalent to the approximation of that graph using stochastic block models,
of decreasing complexity as the scale of the analysis becomes coarser.

Stochastic block models also provide a discrete version of step graphons

[
11
,
26
,
39
]
, which are dense in the space of graphons for the topology
induced by the cut-norm
[
39
]
. Finally, stochastic block models are amenable to a
rigorous mathematical analysis, and are indeed at the cutting edge of rigorous probabilistic
analysis of random graphs
[
1
]
.

1.9
Content of the paper: overview of the results

The main contribution of this work is a fast algorithm to compute the barycentre of a set of
graphs with community structure. The barycentre is determined using a Laplacian spectral
pseudo-distance. We solve the problem defined by
Eq.

13
by relaxing the
optimization problem
Eq.

4
and taking

\bm{\lambda}({\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]})=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\bm{\lambda}\right]
.

We then construct
\bm{\Psi}
using the large library of Soules bases

[
30
,
70
]
, which were designed specifically to solve similar inverse
eigenvalue problems.
This work is significant because it opens the door to the design of
new spectral-based graph synthesis
[
8
,
69
]
that have theoretical
guarantees. We publicly share our code to facilitate future work
[
57
]
.

3
Soules Bases: definition and properties

Soules bases
[
29
,
70
]
were invented to provide a solution to the following
symmetric nonnegative inverse eigenvalue problem: find
\bm{\Psi}\in\OG(n)
such that

\bm{\Psi}\mspace{4mu}{\diagonal\big(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\mspace{-2mu}\big)}\mspace{14mu}{\mspace{-12mu}\bm{\Psi}^{T}}\in\mathcal{S}
. Soules bases
provide a large family of solutions to this problem.

3.1
Definition of Soules Bases

A Soules bases is an orthogonal matrix that is constructed iteratively by applying a
product of Givens rotations to a fixed vector
\bm{\psi}_{1}
with nonnegative entries. The
construction starts at the coarsest level (
l=1
) with a normalized vector
\bm{\psi}_{1}
with
nonnegative entries, whose support is the interval
I^{(1)}=[n]
. Hereunder, our analysis
assumes that we always choose
\bm{\psi}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\scalebox{0.8}{$\small n^{-1/2}$}\bm{1}
.

At any given level
l
, the set
[n]
is partitioned into
l
ordered intervals
I^{(l)}_{q},1\leq q\leq l
. When progressing from level
l
to
l+1
, one chooses an interval,

I^{(l)}_{q}=[i_{0},i_{1})
, and one chooses an index
{i^{\ast}}\in[i_{0},i_{1}]
and defines

I_{q}^{(l+1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}[i_{0},{i^{\ast}}]
, and
I_{q+1}^{(l+1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}[{i^{\ast}}+1,i_{1}]
(see

Fig.

3
-right). The split of
I^{(l)}_{q}
into
I^{(l+1)}_{q}
and

I^{(l+1)}_{q+1}
triggers the construction of the Soules vector
\bm{\psi}_{l+1}
(see

Fig.

3
-left), defined by

\bm{\psi}_{l+1}(i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{{\big\|\bm{\psi}(i_{0}:i_{1})\big\|}}\left\{\begin{aligned} \mspace{14mu}\displaystyle\frac{{\big\|\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}}+1:i_{1})\big\|}}{{\big\|\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0}:{i^{\ast}})\big\|}}\bm{\psi}_{1}(i)\mspace{16mu}&\text{if}\mspace{16mu}i_{0}\leq i\leq{i^{\ast}},\\
-\displaystyle\frac{{\big\|\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0}:{i^{\ast}})\big\|}}{{\big\|\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}}+1:i_{1})\big\|}}\bm{\psi}_{1}(i)\mspace{16mu}&\text{if}\mspace{16mu}{i^{\ast}}+1\leq i\leq i_{1},\\
0\mspace{128mu}&\text{otherwise,}\end{aligned}\right.

(27)

where the vectors
\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0}:i_{1}),\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0}:{i^{\ast}})
, and
\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}}+1:i_{1})
are

n
-dimensional vectors whose nonzero entries are extracted from
\bm{\psi}_{1}
at the corresponding indices,

\begin{aligned} \bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0}:i_{1})&=[0\;\cdots\;0\mspace{12mu}\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0})\;\cdots\;\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}})\;\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}}+1)\;\cdots\;\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{1})\;0\;\cdots 0]^{T},\\
\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0}:{i^{\ast}})&=[0\;\cdots\;0\mspace{12mu}\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{0})\;\cdots\;\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}})\mspace{8mu}0\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\mspace{2mu}0]^{T},\\
\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}}+1:i_{1})&=[0\mspace{8mu}\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots 0\mspace{8mu}\bm{\psi}_{1}({i^{\ast}}+1)\;\cdots\;\bm{\psi}_{1}(i_{1})\;0\;\cdots 0]^{T}.\end{aligned}

(28)

The iterative subdivision process can be described using a binary tree (see

Fig.

4
-right) where a new vector is created at each node that has two
children. We observe that
\bm{\psi}_{k}
and
\bm{\psi}_{k^{\prime}}
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
, are
either nested, or they do not overlap; whence
{\langle\bm{\psi}_{k},\bm{\psi}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle}=0
, and

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\psi}_{1}\cdots\bm{\psi}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
is an orthonormal matrix

[
29
]
; see
[
75
]
for a similar construction of Walsh-Hadamard
packets and
[
18
]
for an analogous construction of complex-valued packets.

3.2
Properties of Soules Bases

Using
Eq.

27
, we derive the following lemma with a proof by induction.

Lemma 3

(See
[
29
]
)
.

Let
\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\psi}_{1}\cdots\bm{\psi}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
be a Soules basis
constructed according to
Eq.

27
. Then,

\forall m=1,\ldots,n,\mspace{16mu}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}\geq 0,\mspace{8mu}\text{and}\mspace{16mu}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}=\id.

(29)

Finally, we have the fundamental property of Soules bases.

Lemma 4

(See
[
29
]
)
.

Let
\bm{\Psi}
be a Soules basis constructed according to
Eq.

27
. Let

\bm{\Lambda}=\mspace{4mu}{\diagonal\big(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\mspace{-2mu}\big)}\mspace{14mu}\mspace{-12mu}
, where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
. Then, the off-diagonal entries of
\bm{\Psi}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Psi}^{T}
are
non-negative. In addition, if
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
, then
\bm{\Psi}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Psi}^{T}\geq 0
.

We note that there has been some recent interest in Soules bases to solve various inverse
eigenvalue problems
[
24
,
65
]
.

Remark 1
.

The result in
Lemma

4
relies on the fact that the sequence of eigenvalues
is decreasing. On the other hand, the eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}
are by nature ranked in
ascending order (the index

of eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}
encodes the frequency of
the corresponding eigenvector). Given an ascending sequence of eigenvalues of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}
,

, we would like to apply

Lemma

4
to reconstruct a Laplacian matrix using a Soules basis. Since the
off-diagonal entries of a normalized Laplacian

are nonpositive, we need to work with

. Then,
0=\lambda_{1}>-\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq-\lambda_{n}
, and we can use

Lemma

4
to construct
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
such that

\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}=\bm{\Psi}\mspace{4mu}{\diagonal\big(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\mspace{-2mu}\big)}\mspace{14mu}\mspace{-14mu}\bm{\Psi}^{T},\mspace{16mu}\text{where}\mspace{8mu}\mathscr{L}^{\ast}_{ij}\leq 0\mspace{8mu}\text{if}\mspace{8mu}i\neq j.

(30)

Since we choose,
\bm{\psi}_{1}=n^{-1/2}\bm{1}
, we have
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}\bm{1}=\bm{0}
, and therefore

\mathscr{L}^{\ast}_{ii}\geq 0
. While the signs of the entries of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
match
those of a normalized Laplacian, there is no guarantee that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\ast}
be a valid
normalized Laplacian (but see a definite answer in the case of the combinatorial
Laplacian,
\bm{L}=\bm{D}-\bm{A}
in
[
24
]
).

4.2
Spectral clustering of the nodes.

The greedy construction of the Soules basis necessitates that
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]
be “well-aligned” –
in the sense that nodes are aggregated in clusters wherein the local node connectivity is
approximately constant. This required step is equivalent to the estimation of a piecewise
constant graphon for each observed graph in
[
49
]
. We use a spectral clustering
method based on the eigenvectors of the normalized graph Laplacian

[
22
,
58
,
68
]
to organize nodes into clusters; the algorithm only
requires the knowledge of the number
M
of clusters. This prerequisite step only provides a grouping of the nodes based on
the connectivity measurements (see
Fig.

5
). After this coarse clustering, the
adjacency matrices
\bm{A}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{A}^{(T)}
are not aligned: one cannot match the
nodes from one graph to another. In short, this step corresponds to the approximation of
each
\bm{A}^{(t)}
using a step graphon (e.g.,

[
3
,
11
,
36
,
39
,
49
,
53
,
61
,
78
]
).
As demonstrated in the experiments, the clustering of the nodes into communities is not
always accurate. Fortunately, the choice of the Soules vectors in
Algorithm

1
only relies
on the coarse scale eigenvectors. These eigenvectors are determined by computing
\lvert{\langle\bm{\psi}_{l}\bm{\psi}_{l}^{T},\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]\rangle}\rvert^{2}
. The support of
\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}
is large
for
k=2,3,\ldots
, and
\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}
is piecewise constant. The random noise
in
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]
– created by the incorrect assignment of nodes to the wrong community –
is partly suppressed when computing
\big\lvert{\langle\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T},\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]\rangle}\big\rvert^{2}
.

4.2.1
Algorithmic Complexity

The optimization problem given by
Eq.

13
cannot be solved with a
brute-force search, since we search
\bm{\Psi}
over the orthogonal group

\OG(n)
. Polynomial-time numerical methods
[
12
]
have been devised to take
advantage of the lower dimension of this Riemannian submanifold. In comparison, the
computational complexity of
Algorithm

1
is linear in
n
. We need to process
M
levels;
the discovery of
\bm{\psi}_{m}
at each level

\bigg\lvert{\langle\bm{\psi}_{m+1}\bm{\psi}_{m+1}^{T},\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]\rangle}\bigg\rvert^{2}

n
times, for an overall complexity
of
Mn
.

The following lemma proves that the first

Lemma 5
.

Let
\bm{P}
be the population mean adjacency matrix of
{\thesbm\big(\bm{p},q,n\big)}
defined by

\bm{P}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}(p_{m}-q)\bm{1}_{B_{m}}\bm{1}_{B_{m}}^{T}+q\bm{J},

(33)

where the
M
blocks
\{B_{m}\}
form a partition of
[n]
. Let

be
the leaves in the binary Soules tree (these are the
M
intervals that are no longer split,
see
Fig.

4
-right) after
M
steps of
Algorithm

1
. Then, the
M
blocks

\{B_{m}\}
in
Eq.

33
coincide with
M
the intervals
\{J_{l}\}
. The entries of the
matrix
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}
satisfy

\sum_{k=1}^{M}\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\lvert J_{m}\rvert}}&\text{if}\mspace{8mu}(i,j)\in J_{m}\times J_{m},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(34)

where
{\lvert J_{m}\rvert}
is the length of the interval
J_{m}
.

The proof of
Lemma

5
can be found in
Section

8.1
. The proof relies on two
different facts. The first shows that a top-down exploration of the Soules binary tree,
when the first Soules vector is
\bm{\psi}_{1}=\scalebox{0.8}{${n}^{-1/2}$}\bm{1}
, always
creates a matrix
\bm{E}_{M}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\bm{\psi}_{k}\bm{\psi}_{k}^{T}
that is piecewise constant on
square blocks aligned along the diagonal, and zero outside of the blocks (see

Corollary

2

have nested supports.

The second result specifically addresses the construction of each
\bm{\psi}_{k}
in

Algorithm

1
. We prove in

9
that at each level

\bm{\psi}_{k}
returned by

of the edge probability matrix
\bm{P}
. At level

1
has

Corollary 1
.

Let
\bm{\Psi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\psi}_{1}\cdots\bm{\psi}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

5.1
Estimation of the degree matrix of
{\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-2mu}\pr\mspace{-2mu}\big]}

We observe that
Lemma

5
yields an estimate of the location of the blocks
B_{m}\times B_{m}
,
1\leq m\leq M
associated with
{\thesbm\big(\bm{p},q,n\big)}
. An estimate of the degree matrix,

{\widehat{\bm{D}}}

B_{m}
of
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]

\widehat{d}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{m}\times B_{m}}\big[\widehat{\mathbb{E}}_{T}\mspace{-2mu}\left[\pr\right]\big]_{ij},\mspace{16mu}1\leq m\leq M.

(36)

A quick calculation confirms that the estimator
\widehat{d}_{m}
concentrates
around the “within block” expected degree
\overline{d\mspace{4mu}}_{m}
(see

Eq.

14
). We have

\widehat{d}_{m}=T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{(i,j)\in B_{m}\times B_{m}}a^{(t)}_{ij}.

(37)

Therefore

\overline{d\mspace{4mu}}_{m}

\widehat{d}_{m}

\forall m\in[M],\forall\;T\geq 1,\forall\;\delta>0,\mspace{2mu}\mathbb{P}\mspace{-2mu}\left(\mspace{-2mu}\Big\lvert\widehat{d}_{m}-\overline{d\mspace{4mu}}_{m}\Big\rvert\geq\delta\mspace{-2mu}\right)\leq\exp{\displaystyle\big(-4T\delta^{2}/({\lvert B_{m}\rvert}({\lvert B_{M}\rvert}-1))\big)}.

(38)

We recall that
\overline{d\mspace{4mu}}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}{\mathbb{E}\mspace{-2mu}\big[\mspace{-1mu}d_{ii}\mspace{-1mu}\big]}={\lvert B_{m}\rvert}\big\{p_{m}+(n/{\lvert B_{m}\rvert}-1)q\big\}

p_{i}\gg q

\widehat{d}_{m}
is asymptotically
unbiased when the sample size

.

Discussion

In this work, we address the following question: when one computes a barycentre graph
using a spectral pseudo-distance, one needs a basis of eigenvectors to reconstruct the
barycentre graph: the distance only yields the eigenvalues. This paper contains several
original contributions: (1) we formally define a criterion for estimating the
eigenvectors, (2) we devise an algorithm to estimate the eigenvectors using the library of
Soules vectors, (3) we provide a theoretical justification for the algorithm (in the
context of the analysis of balanced Stochastic Block Models), and (4) we conduct
experiments that suggest that the algorithm performs as expected beyond the ensemble of
balanced stochastic block models. As many papers in the research area of data
representation and analysis, this manuscript combines algorithms with theory, where the
theory is sometimes developed in a more restricted framework.

This work opens the door to further studies. Our experiments suggest that the method works
beyond the restricted scope of balanced SBM, and one should be able to prove that

Algorithm

1
solves
Eq.

13
when graphs are sampled from

{\thesbm\big(\bm{p},q,n\big)}
. A second extension concerns the application of
Algorithm

1
to graphs
that have different sizes. Because we work with the normalized graph Laplacian, one can
always compare interpolate a discrete spectrum on the interval
[0,2]
, making the
comparison of two graphs of different size possible. Finally, the analysis of the
performance of the algorithm with graphs sampled from the preferential attachment models

[
82
,
9
]
would have a significant practical impact. While less is
known about the eigenvalues of the normalized Laplacian
[
38
,
34
]
, the
topology of the preferential attachment model (with the high-degree vertices) is
frequently found in real world networks, and therefore this model is worthy of attention.
