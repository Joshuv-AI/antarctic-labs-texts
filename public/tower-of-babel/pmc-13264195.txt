An unusual presentation of meningococcal meningitis
Authors: Ayyad Zartasht Khan, Fredrik Fineide, Tor Paaske Utheim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo present a unique case of meningococcal meningitis initially manifesting as isolated ocular symptoms in a 19-year-old contact lens wearer.ObservationsThe patient presented with a two-day history of unilateral red and painful eye without systemic symptoms, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of contact lens-associated keratitis. A bacterial swab from the inferior fornix returned positive for N. meningitidis, prompting further evaluation. Diagnostic workup by the internal medicine service supported the diagnosis of meningitis and the patient was treated according to local guidelines.Conclusions and ImportanceThis case underscores the importance of maintaining a relatively low threshold for obtaining samples for microbiological testing in ocular surface conditions.

Case report

A 19-year-old contact lens wearer presented with a two-day history of red and painful right eye to the Eye Department. Eleven months prior to the current presentation, the patient had been successfully treated with topical chloramphenicol ointment for a culture-positive Corynebacterium macginleyi-keratitis on the same eye. General medical history was significant for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), for which she was taking methylphenidate capsules. At presentation, best corrected visual acuity was 0.8/0.63 Snellen and intraocular pressure was 16/17 mmHg (iCare IC 100, iCare, Finland). Except for a combined conjunctival and ciliary hyperemia on the right eye, slit lamp examination of both eyes, including fundus examination, was unremarkable. Specifically, assessment using fluorescein sodium dye revealed no corneal staining and there was no purulent discharge. A right-sided contact-lens associated keratitis was suspected and the patient was prescribed combination eye drops containing dexamethasone 1 mg/mL and chloramphenicol 5 mg/mL four times a day for one week. She was instructed to return for follow-up after one week and to avoid contact lenses for at least six weeks.

Despite negative fluorescein staining, a bacterial swab was obtained from the right inferior fornix and was sent to the lab. After seven days, the microbiological report returned showing positive cultures of Neisseria meningitidis. Upon receipt of the microbiological report, the patient was contacted and requested to present at the Emergency Department to the on-call internal medicine physician.

In the Emergency Department, the patient reported light headache, neck stiffness, and fever that had started concurrent with her eye symptoms. Her eye symptoms had improved on initiated therapy. She reported attending a festival few days prior to symptom onset. The patient had stable vital signs and was afebrile. Physical examination revealed a positive Kernig's sign but no petechiae. The bloodwork was normal except for a slightly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) level at 19 mg/L. White blood cell count (WBC) was within normal range, i.e. at 8.0 × 109/L. Computed tomography (CT) of the head and neck showed mucosal thickening of the nasal sinuses. Lumbar puncture (LP) showed clear fluid and a normal opening pressure of 10 cm H2O. Blood cultures and LP cultures were negative.

The patient was admitted to the internal medicine ward and was started on intravenous penicillin G 3000 mg four times a day and dexamethasone 9000 mg four times a day. She remained admitted for three days after which she was discharged upon clinical remission with per oral phenoxymethylpenicillin 1320 mg four times a day for five more days. The patient returned to her usual state of health and her eye symptoms resolved.

Discussion

This case is educational as it illustrates an unusual presentation of meningococcal meningitis where ocular symptoms and signs aided in the diagnosis.

Meningitis is defined as an inflammation of the meninges, i.e. the membranes covering the central nervous system (CNS).1 Meningitis can have infectious or non-infectious etiologies, where the former includes microorganisms such as bacteria and viruses.1 Common organisms responsible for bacterial meningitis are Group B Streptococcus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and N. meningitidis.1 N. meningitidis, a Gram-negative encapsulated diplococcus is especially common in community-acquired acute bacterial meningitis in children and young adults.2 The clinical presentation of bacterial meningitis is highly variable, ranging from transient fever to rapidly progressive disease that can result in death within hours of symptom onset.3 This necessitates a high degree of clinical suspicion and a careful search for clues of disease to establish the diagnosis. Early detection is especially important in preventing outbreaks.4 Fever, neck stiffness, and altered mental status have traditionally been considered the classic triad of bacterial meningitis; however, real-world data indicate presentation with the complete triad to be low 5, 6, 7. Physical exam may reveal positive Kernig's or Brudzinski's signs; however, their absence does not reliably rule out the diagnosis.8 As bacterial meningitis can be a life-threatening condition, early treatment should be endeavoured.9 Although blood cultures and LP should be obtained prior to initiation of antibiotic therapy, this should not delay appropriate parenteral antibiotic therapy, which should be initiated on suspicion.10 Meningococcal vaccines are effective,11 and have significantly reduced the global burden of disease.12,13 They not only lower the incidence of invasive disease but also decrease pharyngeal carriage, reducing transmission within populations. It is therefore crucial that clinicians encourage up-to-date vaccination, particularly in young adults and individuals with risk factors.

Although N. meningitidis is primarily associated with meningitis and septicemia, a growing body of medical literature have documented its ability to cause ocular infections, particularly primary meningococcal conjunctivitis (PMC).14,15 While PMC is rare, it is clinically significant due to its potential to precede invasive meningococcal disease. Several reports describe patients presenting with conjunctival hyperemia, mucopurulent discharge, or keratitis, with ocular cultures later confirming N. meningitidis as the causative organism. In a 2019 review, Parikh et al. found that 10–29% of PMC cases progressed to invasive disease within days of ocular symptom onset, and emphasized that systemic antibiotic therapy, rather than topical treatment alone, substantially reduced this risk of PMC progressing to invasive disease.15 More recent case reports, including those involving immunocompetent adults16 and neonates,17 reaffirm the need for prompt microbiological testing and systemic intervention. What distinguishes the current case is that the ocular findings were mild and non-specific, without conjunctival exudate or corneal staining, and the diagnosis of meningococcal meningitis was triggered solely by the positive result of a routine conjunctival swab, an approach that would not be standard in many clinical settings without overt corneal involvement. As such, it adds a valuable and unusual clinical perspective to the expanding spectrum of meningococcal disease presentations.

Our patient had history, physical exam findings, and to some degree bloodwork that was enough to initiate treatment for bacterial meningitis by the on-call internal medicine service. Blood cultures were negative. Negative were also cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cultures, however, these were collected after initiation of parenteral antibiotic therapy. Obtaining blood and CSF cultures during antibiotic therapy is associated with a significant loss of pathogen detection,18,19 which can explain the CSF culture negativity in our patient. Blood cultures were obtained prior to initiating intravenous antibiotic therapy, but returned negative. Of note, our patient was prescribed a seven-day course of combination eye drops containing dexamethasone 1 mg/mL and chloramphenicol 5 mg/mL, which she had been compliant to before her in-hospital diagnostic workup and treatment. Even though chloramphenicol penetrates the blood‐brain barrier,20 is bactericidal for N. meningitidis, and has demonstrated efficacy in meningococcal meningitis (intramuscular and intravenous),21,22 we doubt that the topical chloramphenicol eye drops the patient was using prior to the hospital admission reached bactericidal levels in plasma. Therefore, blood culture negativity in our patient remains unexplained to us.

Interestingly, the same eye had previously cultured positive for Corynebacterium macginleyi, an organism associated with ocular surface disease in contact lens users.23 While there is no known pathogenic relationship between Corynebacterium macginleyi and N. meningitidis, repeated infections in the same eye raise the question of altered local defense, tear film abnormalities, or subclinical epithelial compromise. Although our patient had no systemic immunodeficiency such as HIV and was otherwise healthy, one should be aware that recurrent ocular infections may warrant systemic evaluation.

This case underscores a broader point relevant to clinicians: systemic infections or inflammatory diseases can initially manifest with ocular symptoms alone (Table 1), often preceding or even occurring in the absence of more classical systemic signs. Monkeypox, for instance, has been reported to present with conjunctivitis or blepharitis in early stages, particularly during recent outbreaks in non-endemic regions.24 Tuberculosis can present as granulomatous uveitis or scleritis, even in the absence of pulmonary involvement.25 Similarly, syphilis26 and Lyme disease27 may cause uveitis as an early sign of systemic infection. In immunocompromised individuals, including those with HIV, ocular presentations such as cytomegalovirus retinitis28 or conjunctival Kaposi sarcoma29 may precede a formal diagnosis of systemic disease. Autoimmune conditions like sarcoidosis or Behçet's disease often involve the eye early, with manifestations such as uveitis or scleritis.30 Additionally, COVID-19 has been associated with conjunctivitis as a primary or sole symptom.31 These diverse examples illustrate the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and support a low threshold for systemic investigation when ocular findings are unexplained, atypical, or unresponsive to local therapy.

In the case reported herein, a conjunctival swab was obtained despite the absence of corneal staining or ulceration. This approach may not reflect standard practice in many clinical settings. The decision was supported by several contextual factors: the patient was a contact lens wearer, had a recent history of culture-confirmed microbial keratitis in the same eye, and had been prescribed a combination eye drop containing dexamethasone (that could potentially worsen infections). Based on prior clinical experience, the authors maintain a deliberately low threshold for obtaining microbiological samples. In this instance, that strategy enabled early identification of a potentially fatal infection. The case highlights the diagnostic value of proactive microbiological testing in patients with subtle or inconclusive ocular findings. While such practices may not be feasible in all geographic regions or resource-limited settings, this approach has proven diagnostically valuable in the authors’ clinical experience.

In conclusion, this case emphasizes the importance of clinical vigilance and diagnostic flexibility when evaluating ocular complaints, particularly in contact lens wearers or individuals with a history of ocular infections. The absence of corneal staining or other overt signs of keratitis did not preclude a serious underlying etiology. Instead, the decision to obtain a conjunctival swab, based on clinical context and a low threshold for testing, proved critical in uncovering a potentially fatal systemic infection. While ocular manifestations of N. meningitidis are rare, this case reminds us that even mild or non-specific ocular symptoms can be a harbinger of invasive disease. It also highlights the value of interdisciplinary collaboration, prompt systemic workup, and early treatment, all of which contributed to a favorable outcome in our patient. As the spectrum of meningococcal disease presentations continues to evolve, clinicians must remain aware of its atypical manifestations, maintain a broad differential, and consider microbiological testing even in the absence of classic findings.
