What goals does China’s medical insurance fund supervision aim to achieve? A quantitative analysis based on national-level policies
Authors: Jinpeng Xu, Feier Cheng, Xinyan Jiang, Xinhui Zuo, Weixue Yin, Lijuan Cui, Dongxue Wang, Zheng Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe goals of medical insurance fund supervision will influence the selection of supervisory methods and their effectiveness. This study aims to identify the multiple goals of China’s medical insurance fund supervision and to examine the hierarchy and proximity among these goals.Methods133 national-level policies related to the goals of medical insurance fund supervision in China were selected. Frequency ranking and social network analysis were used to explore the hierarchical relationships among these goals. The Ochiai coefficient in the similarity matrix was used to assess the proximity. Clustering analysis was performed via the generation of a dendrogram, while multidimensional scaling was applied to validate the clustering results.ResultsFund security was the most frequently cited goal (11.01%) among the 84 goals, followed by ending insurance fraud (8.55%) and protecting the rights and interests of citizens (6.38%). Efficiency and economy-related goals, such as fund security and intelligence, were prioritized over public-oriented goals like social participation and justice. The correlation coefficients among the goals ranged from 0.000 to 0.602, with the strongest association observed between fairness and justice (r = 0.602).ConclusionsThe supervision of China’s medical insurance fund involves diverse goals that are interrelated and centered on the core objectives of ensuring fund security, combating medical insurance fraud, and safeguarding citizens’ rights and interests. They can be distilled into five categories: fairness, citizenship, efficiency, administration, and social welfare. These findings can provide empirical evidence for the governance of goal conflicts in medical insurance fund supervision and the construction of an assessment framework for supervisory efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13775-6.Keywords: Medical insurance fund supervision, Goals, Policy analysis, Quantitative analysis

Introduction

Medical insurance fraud refers to deliberate deception or misrepresentation by individuals or organizations to unlawfully obtain medical insurance fund or benefits [1–3]. This problem has become a widespread issue globally, with financial losses to the healthcare system due to fraudulent activities increasing every year [4–7]. According to estimates from the National Healthcare Anti-Fraud Association (NHCAA) in 2022, medical insurance fraud causes an annual loss of approximately $68 billion to the U.S. healthcare system. Similarly, European countries like the United Kingdom and Germany report medical insurance fraud losses accounting for 5% to 10% of their healthcare budgets. In developing nations such as India and Brazil, these losses are estimated to range between 6% and 12% [8, 9]. In China, medical insurance fraud typically involves inappropriate billing practices, non-standard diagnostic or treatment behaviors, and even the fabrication of medical services, among other violations. Recent estimates suggest that such fraudulent activities account for 7–8% of total national healthcare expenditures, underscoring the severity of the problem [10]. In 2024, Chinese authorities recovered approximately $3.77 billion from fraudulent insurance claims [11], highlighting the urgent need for stronger governance and supervision measures.

Various countries have implemented a range of strategies to strengthen the supervision of medical insurance fund [12, 13]. King [14] summarizes the U.S. experience in medical fraud prevention, emphasizing the importance of stronger legislation, stricter enforcement, the establishment of anti-fraud systems, and the improvement of fraud detection techniques. Moreover, the development of the internet and digital technology has accelerated the adoption of big data, blockchain, and machine learning to automate fraud detection, making it a key area of research in recent years [15, 16]. For example, Yang and Hwang [17] propose a data-mining framework that incorporates clinical pathways to develop an adaptable and extensible fraud detection model. Additionally, since citizens both contribute to and depend on medical insurance funds, fraudulent activities threaten their financial and health-related interests, underscoring the importance of public participation in fund supervision [18, 19]. In this context, public reporting has been widely recognized as an effective mechanism for detecting and preventing medical insurance fraud [20, 21].

In summary, most existing research on the supervision of medical insurance fund has focused primarily on technical governance, with scholars adopting two main approaches. The first approach seeks to strengthen law enforcement by improving the legal framework, establishing specialized enforcement teams, fostering cross-sectoral collaboration, and enhancing deterrence against medical insurance fraud by enrollees [22–24]. The second emphasizes the use of intelligent tools, including big data-driven fraud detection and social supervision [25–27]. These strategies have enhanced the efficiency and precision of supervision. However, they primarily address the question of “how to supervise” rather than the more fundamental question of “what the goals of supervision should be”. These goals not only shape policy design but also influence the choice of supervisory methods and their overall effectiveness. Despite their significance, existing research provides limited discussion on the goals of medical insurance fund supervision, leaving the fundamental question of what these efforts aim to achieve largely unanswered.

Moreover, supervision of medical insurance fund inevitably involves conflicts among different goals. For example, while digital technologies enhance supervisory efficiency, they also raise concerns about data security and privacy protection [28]. Similarly, a strong focus on fraud prevention may increase compliance costs for healthcare providers and may even affect the delivery of medical services. In 2007, Jørgensen and Bozeman [29] argued that value-based goals do not exist in isolation but are interconnected through hierarchical relationships, proximity, and causality. Conflicts among goals often arise from an unclear understanding of these relationships, and resolving them requires uncovering their underlying structure. However, in China’s medical insurance fund supervision, these interconnections remain unexplored.

Based on the above, the specific goals that China’s medical insurance fund supervision aims to achieve remain unclear, and how these goals relate to one another in terms of priority and proximity also lack definitive answers. Public policy reflects the pursuit of specific goals by governments and public institutions. In recent years, China has introduced several policies aimed at strengthening the legal framework of medical insurance and addressing persistent issues of fraud and abuse. Building on this foundation, this study aims to conduct a quantitative analysis with two key objectives: (1) to identify the multiple goals embedded in China’s medical insurance fund supervision policies; and (2) to examine the hierarchy and relative proximity among these goals. The findings will provide empirical evidence to help address goal conflicts in China’s medical insurance fund supervision, contribute to the construction of an assessment framework for future supervisory efforts, and offer insights for other countries facing similar challenges.

Materials and methods

Data collection

The data for this study were obtained from policies regarding the supervision of medical insurance fund in China, primarily issued by the State Council, the National Healthcare Security Administration, the National Health Commission, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security, and the Ministry of Finance of China. Relevant government websites and the Pkulaw database were searched, covering the period from 1998, when China’s Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance was established [30], to December 31, 2024. These sources collectively cover the main issuing authorities and core national-level policies on the supervision of medical insurance fund. Cross-checking and supplementation between official government websites and the Pkulaw database ensured the representativeness and completeness of the dataset.

The search terms used were: (1) healthcare security, medical insurance, or health insurance; (2) fund supervision, fund security, or supervision and management; (3) insurance fraud, reporting rewards, or related terms. Policies were included if they (1) were issued at the national level by entities such as the National People’s Congress, the State Council, or relevant ministries, either independently or jointly; (2) were related to medical insurance fund supervision and clearly stated the goals, such as “ensuring fund security and improving fund utilization efficiency” or “safeguarding the integrity of funds and protecting citizens’ legitimate rights”; and (3) were classified as laws, regulations, decrees, notices, circulars, measures, opinions, or decisions while excluding documents such as approvals, proposals, and recommendations. Ultimately, 133 policies were selected for analysis. A detailed description of the study design and policy selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Methods

Identification of the goals of medical insurance fund supervision

NVivo 12.0 Plus was used to code 133 policy texts, extracting key statements related to the goals of medical insurance fund supervision and transforming them into general expressions to identify diverse goals. To ensure reliability, two graduate students specializing in medical insurance fund supervision carried out the coding process. Before formal coding, both coders received standardized training and jointly discussed and clarified the criteria for category divisions and judgments. Each researcher then independently identified goal-related keywords within the policies. Multiple words with the same meaning were merged into one representative word. For example, the phrase “establish an efficient, responsive, and well-coordinated cross-regional collaborative supervision mechanism” was coded as “Collaboration”, and “extend medical insurance supervision from healthcare institutions to the medical service practices of healthcare providers” was coded as “Standardized medical practices”. Upon completion, the “coding comparison” function in NVivo was used to assess consistency. For coding points with a Cohen’s kappa coefficient less than 0.9 [31], a two-stage adjudication process was employed: the coders first discussed to seek consensus, and any persistent disagreements were referred to the supervisor for final arbitration. This process systematically addressed all coding discrepancies and secured the reliability and validity of the coding results.

Analysis of the hierarchical relationships among goals

Frequency ranking combined with social network analysis (SNA) was used to explore the hierarchical relationships among the goals. As a mixed-methods approach that integrates qualitative and quantitative analysis, SNA provides a framework for assessing interactions between qualitative exploration and quantitative measurement [32]. In SNA, the feature item ​ serves as the network node i, while the degree of co-occurrence ​ represents the strength of the edge connection between nodes. This approach enables a quantitative analysis of the hierarchical structure and relationships within the network and has been increasingly applied in policy studies [33, 34].

In this study, represents the various goals of medical insurance fund supervision, collectively forming a network of these goals. Social network analysis was conducted using NetDraw and UCINET 6.0 to determine the position of each goal within the network. To eliminate the influence of nonsignificant goals, only the top 30 goals, ranked by frequency, were included. Selecting too few goals would risk omitting important policy objectives, whereas including too many could introduce noise and complicate the analyses. The chosen cutoff balances comprehensiveness and interpretability, capturing the most relevant goals for the study. In SNA, degree centrality is a fundamental measure used to assess the influence of nodes [35, 36]. Based on the coding results, a word matrix and co-occurrence matrix were generated to analyze the importance of each goal by calculating its degree centrality [37]. A higher degree centrality indicates a more central role within the network, indicating that the goal holds greater importance and serves as a core objective of China’s medical insurance fund supervision.

Specifically, Degree centrality is calculated via absolute degree centrality (Degree), normalized degree (NrmDegree), and degree centrality proportion (Share) [38]. Absolute degree centrality refers to the number of direct connections (edges) a node has within a network, reflecting how many other nodes are directly linked to that node. Relative degree centrality is the ratio of a node’s degree centrality to the maximum possible degree centrality, providing a standardized measure of centrality. The degree centrality proportion represents a node’s degree centrality as a share of the total degree centrality across all nodes, indicating its relative contribution to the overall network structure. These measures help identify the most central or influential nodes on the basis of their connections, offering insights into the relative importance of each goal in the network of medical insurance fund supervision.

Analysis of proximity and categorization of goals

To examine the proximity among the goals of China’s medical insurance fund supervision, clustering analysis was performed on the constructed word matrix, followed by the generation of a similarity matrix and a dendrogram. Correlation coefficients in the similarity matrix were used to assess the proximity of the supervision goals. These coefficients ranged from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a closer relationship between the two goals. A coefficient of 0 represented no correlation, while a coefficient of 1 indicated a strong correlation [39].

To generate a similarity matrix, the co-occurrence matrix was normalized using Ochiai coefficients to mitigate the impact of large discrepancies in co-occurrence values [40]. The Ochiai coefficient is a widely used measure that considers frequency while minimizing distortion caused by excessively high occurrence counts [41, 42]. Based on the similarity matrix, multidimensional scaling (MDS) was applied to categorize the goals and validate the clustering analysis results. MDS is a statistical technique commonly used to visualize the similarity or dissimilarity of data in a low-dimensional space and is often represented as a perceptual map. A shorter distance between two objects indicates a stronger similarity in their relationship [43]. In this study, SPSS 22.0 was used for MDS. The data were standardized using the Z score method, and a multidimensional scaling plot of China’s medical insurance fund supervision goals was generated [44].

Results

Distribution of policies

Figure 2 illustrates the trend in the number of policies over time. Between 1998 and 2014, the number of policies related to medical insurance fund supervision goals fluctuated moderately. However, since 2015, the number of policies has been on the rise, peaking in both 2016 and 2021 with 13 policies each. This increase likely reflects the growing attention of Chinese policymakers to improving the governance, efficiency, and financial security of the medical insurance fund in response to the rapid expansion of health coverage and the evolving challenges in fund management. In particular, the sharp rise in 2016 coincides with the integration of the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance and the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme, which likely prompted policymakers to place greater emphasis on fund supervision.

Multiple goals of China’s medical insurance fund supervision

A total of 690 goal codes were extracted from 133 policies, resulting in 84 distinct goals. As shown in Fig. 3, these goals encompass economic and efficiency-oriented objectives, such as real-time, precision, fund efficiency, and supervisory efficiency, as well as democratic goals, including citizens’ rights and interests, justice, public participation, and social participation.

The network of goals of China’s medical insurance fund supervision

In summary, the results of the frequency ranking and degree centrality analyses were largely consistent, revealing a clear hierarchy of goals in China’s medical insurance fund supervision. This hierarchy can be summarized as follows: Fund security > Ending insurance fraud > Rights and interests of citizens > Collaboration > Intelligence > Standardized supervision > Comprehensiveness > Standardized medical practices > Real-time > Supervisory efficiency > Social participation > Information sharing > Justice > Fund efficiency > Precision > Information security > Legitimacy, among others. Efficiency- and economy-related goals, such as fund security and intelligence, were prioritized over public-oriented goals, such as social participation and justice.

When set to 2 dimensions, the model’s Stress was 0.27943, and the R-squared was 0.55304. To improve the fit, models with additional dimensions were tested. However, although the higher-dimensional model had better statistical indicators, its complexity made it difficult to interpret and apply.

Multidimensional scale analysis mapping of the goals

The primary purpose of MDS is not to maximize absolute fit but to maintain the relative distance relationships between objects in the lower-dimensional space, effectively reflecting their inherent structure [45]. Although the 2-dimensional model does not achieve a perfect fit, it accurately preserves the relative positioning and proximity of the points, thereby reliably reflecting the similarities and differences between the objects. Therefore, the 2-dimensional model was retained. In the resulting map, highly similar words cluster together, forming groups, where closer proximity indicates a stronger association between the words. The distribution of the 30 goals across the two-dimensional quadrants of Fig. 6 suggests that they can be categorized into five themes. The results of the multidimensional scaling analysis are largely consistent with the dendrogram clustering results.

Discussion

Based on national policies, this study is the first to identify and analyze multiple goals and their structural relationships in the supervision of China’s medical insurance fund. While China has actively advanced the supervision of medical insurance fund, most existing research has focused on innovations in supervisory technologies or methods, leaving the underlying supervisory goals largely unexamined. By addressing this gap, this study not only enhances the understanding of supervisory objectives but also provides empirical evidence that may help reconcile goal conflicts and assess the effectiveness of their implementation.

Specifically, this study revealed that China’s medical insurance fund supervision pursued multiple goals, including ensuring fund security, protecting citizens’ rights and interests, improving supervisory efficiency, promoting fairness and justice, and supporting social development. These goals encompassed both mission-based objectives, such as efficiency and effectiveness, as well as nonmission-based objectives, such as justice and openness [46]. Mission-based objectives refer to the core functions of supervision that focus on achieving operational efficiency and effective fund management, whereas nonmission-based objectives emphasize broader social and ethical values. Distinguishing between the two highlights the dual orientation of China’s medical insurance fund supervision, which balances instrumental performance with public value creation. Based on the DID model, Wang, Song [47] reported that supervision helps curb unnecessary medical demand, reduce the overuse of healthcare resources, and contribute to narrowing disparities in healthcare access between urban and rural residents. The results reflect that the supervision of medical insurance fund has made progress toward achieving mission-based goals, but its impact on nonmission-based goals remains unassessed.

A hierarchical analysis of these goals revealed that China’s medical insurance fund supervision focused primarily on securing funds, preventing fraud, and safeguarding public rights while also considering other objectives. To achieve these goals, the Chinese government has implemented various measures. In 2019, the Notice on Carrying Out the “Two Pilots, One Demonstration” Initiative for Medical Insurance Fund Supervision was issued, aiming to curb fraud through digital and credit-based supervisory innovations [48, 49]. Moreover, induced demand and moral hazard are key contributors to fund deficits [50–52], as unnecessary medical treatments further exacerbate inefficiencies and waste [53]. While most of these behaviors do not constitute fraud, they still pose a significant threat to fund sustainability. To address this, China has continuously refined its payment systems, transitioning from fee-for-service and capitation payment models to Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRGs) and Diagnosis-Intervention Packets (DIP) payment systems in an effort to control medical expenditures and regulate provider behavior [54, 55]. Similar to China, many other countries face limited healthcare resources and rising medical costs, making the enhancement of supervision efficiency and the reduction of waste central objectives of their policy agendas.

Notably, compared with the objectives of other countries, China’s medical insurance fund supervision at that stage placed greater emphasis on efficiency and financial security, while goals such as social participation, public participation, fairness, openness, and justice had lower relative priorities and were not the core focus. On one hand, this may have occurred because the system was still in its early stages of development, with the government primarily driven by technological and economic efficiency considerations, which to some extent undermines its public value attributes [56]. On the other hand, although fairness and justice are often emphasized in the formulation of public policies, their implementation tends to focus more on effectiveness and utility [57]. Compared with equity- or justice-related objectives, performance or efficiency indicators are usually more measurable and manageable, making them more salient for policymakers.

However, as China’s supervision system continues to mature, striking a balance between efficiency and equity will inevitably become a central challenge for future policy design. In this regard, international experience offers valuable insights. For instance, the United States has advanced efficiency in healthcare delivery through value-based payment models, while simultaneously safeguarding the basic medical needs of low-income groups by expanding programs such as Medicare and Medicaid. Additionally, in the U.S., qui tam lawsuits play a crucial role in combating medical insurance fraud, with the vast majority of major cases initiated through whistleblower reports [58, 59]. In European countries such as Germany, market competition has been integrated into well-established social health insurance systems through tiered payment schemes and risk-adjustment mechanisms. These countries have adopted refined fund management models aimed at balancing equity and efficiency. Moreover, research on policy transfer indicates that experiences from countries with different institutional and socioeconomic contexts, such as Ghana, can offer insights for adapting social health insurance policies to China [60]. By drawing on practices from these countries, particularly in terms of transparency and public participation, China can move beyond a narrow focus on fund security to incorporate greater equity and citizen engagement into policy design, thereby achieving a more comprehensive balance of supervision goals. Of course, variations in socioeconomic conditions, political systems, and institutional contexts mean that international experiences cannot be transplanted wholesale, and careful adaptation to China’s specific circumstances will be essential.

Further analysis revealed that the various goals of medical insurance fund supervision were not independent but rather interconnected. Among all the goals, strong correlations were observed between Fund security and the Rights and interests of citizens, Fairness and Justice, Medical expenses and Standardized medical practices, Information sharing and Collaboration, Intelligence and Real-time, and Intelligence and Comprehensiveness. This suggests that improvements in any specific goal will simultaneously promote the progress of related objectives. For example, when healthcare providers adhere to necessary and appropriate medical procedures while avoiding excessive tests and treatments, patients’ medical expenses can be effectively controlled. It is important to note, however, that although the supervision goals are closely related, the exploratory nature of this study does not permit causal inference, and it would be inappropriate to conclude that improvement in one goal directly causes progress in others.

Overall, the goals of China’s medical insurance fund supervision can be categorized into five broad dimensions: fairness, citizenship, efficiency, administration, and social welfare. These dimensions form a comprehensive framework aimed at upholding justice, enhancing public well-being, improving supervisory efficiency, strengthening administrative oversight, and promoting societal progress. Our findings not only reflect the multidimensional nature of public values in China’s medical insurance fund supervision, but also reveal the structural relationships and inherent tensions among these values, providing an empirical validation of the public values identified by Jørgensen and Bozeman [29] through literature analysis in the specific context of China. While these dimensions provide a useful structure for understanding the supervisory goals, their practical application in policy design must account for the complex trade-offs that may arise between these goals. By accounting for all five goal clusters, policymakers can achieve a more balanced approach, mitigating the risk that overemphasis on a single objective will produce negative effects on others.

This study provided some insights into guiding and advancing the development of China’s medical insurance fund supervision. First, the supervision of medical insurance fund encompasses multiple goals, and the inherent trade-offs among these goals mean that prioritizing certain objectives may inevitably constrain the achievement of others [61]. By analyzing the hierarchical relationships and proximities among these goals, this study clarified their underlying relationships. This, in turn, can help policymakers set reasonable priorities when dealing with conflicting objectives and facilitate coordination between different goals in the process of policy implementation. Second, this study can provide a reference for the construction of an evaluation framework for the supervision of China’s medical insurance fund, which can help measure the realization of various supervision goals and improve supervision strategies. Finally, this study may offer valuable insights for other countries with similar medical insurance systems, helping them to better coordinate and optimize multiple policy objectives when formulating supervisory policies.

Several limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. The primary limitation was the subjective judgment involved in policy selection and coding, which may affect the objectivity of the analysis. Furthermore, the multidimensional scaling analysis did not achieve an optimal fit, which may have introduced some minor inaccuracies in the categorization of the goals. In addition, this study focused primarily on analyzing the hierarchical relationships and proximities among the goals, without delving into the causal relationships between them. Future research could employ quantitative methods to further examine the mechanisms underlying these interactions. Finally, this study did not fully account for the dynamic changes in supervisory goals over time. Future research could incorporate long-term policy data to analyze how these goals evolve in response to shifts in the policy environment and socioeconomic conditions.

Conclusions

In the context of national-level policies, the supervision of China’s medical insurance fund was guided by diverse goals, which could be systematically categorized into fairness goals, citizenship goals, efficiency goals, administrative goals, and social goals. These objectives were not independent but interrelated, centering on core priorities such as ensuring fund security, preventing medical insurance fraud, and protecting citizens’ rights and interests. Moreover, they also considered secondary goals such as efficiency, justice, and standardization. The findings of this study can provide empirical evidence for managing goal conflicts in China’s medical insurance fund supervision, contribute to the development of an assessment framework for future regulatory efforts, and offer insights for supervising medical insurance fund in other comparable countries.
