Evaluation of heparin gel’s effects on stage II pressure ulcers: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Raheleh Khani, Leila Behbood, Masoud Fallahi, Shahab Rezaeian, Foroud Shahbazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPressure ulcers are common in critically ill patients and can delay recovery. Heparin improves blood flow and reduces inflammation, which may help wound healing. This study compared an optimized heparin hydrogel with a standard hydrocolloid dressing for treating Stage II pressure ulcers in critically ill patients.MethodsEighteen heparin hydrogel formulations were prepared; eight met key physicochemical and stability criteria, and one optimized 1% formulation based on release profile, stability, and microbiological safety was selected for clinical testing. Patients were randomly assigned to receive either the heparin hydrogel or a hydrocolloid dressing every 3 days alongside standard wound care. Wound length, width, and surface area, as well as the Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing (PUSH) score at baseline and on days 3, 7, and 14 after enrollment.ResultsEighty-four patients were enrolled in the study, and 56 completed the trial (26 in the heparin hydrogel group and 30 in the hydrocolloid control group). The two groups were similar in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics. Both groups improved wound healing over time, but patients who received the heparin hydrogel had greater reductions in PUSH scores, wound length, width, and surface area by day 14 (p < 0.05). The median healing time was 7 days (95% CI 5.8–8.2) in the heparin group and 10 days (95% CI 8.8–11.2) in the hydrocolloid group (p < 0.001). No local or systemic adverse events were reported during the study.ConclusionsHeparin hydrogel treatment was well-tolerated and effectively accelerated wound recovery in patients with stage II pressure ulcers. Nonetheless, further research is warranted to elucidate the role of heparin hydrogel in pressure ulcer management.Keywords: Heparin, Hydrogel dressing, Pressure ulcer, Stage II, PUSH score

Introduction

Pressure ulcers represent a significant global health concern [1]. It has been estimated that about 12% of hospitalized patients develop different grades of pressure ulcers [2]. Different incidences of pressure ulcers have been reported among critically ill patients. Some studies indicate that approximately 8–12% of patients in intensive care units are affected by pressure ulcers [3, 4]. However, a recent meta-analysis showed that the prevalence of pressure ulcers among Iranian intensive care unit (ICU) patients was 19% [5]. Pressure ulcers frequently develop in patients with limited mobility, particularly at anatomical sites subjected to prolonged pressure. The sacrum, heel, lateral malleolus, and greater trochanter are common locations for pressure ulcer formation. Pressure ulcers are linked to a substantial reduction in quality of life. Furthermore, the annual economic burden of pressure ulcers is estimated at approximately $11 billion [1].

Advanced age, physical frailty, decreased level of consciousness, unintentional weight loss, malnutrition, compromised tissue perfusion, tobacco use, dehydration or edema, anemia, heart failure, sepsis, multiorgan dysfunction, and excessive perspiration are widely recognized as significant risk factors for the development of pressure ulcers [6]. Higher ulcer stages, poor management, older age, malnutrition, and limited mobility can slow the healing of pressure ulcers [7, 8]. This delay increases the chance of complications, such as cellulitis, osteomyelitis, bacteremia, and sepsis [9]. Pressure ulcers also raise the risk of hospital-acquired infections and death by about 25% and 50%, respectively [10]. Globally, they are estimated to cause nearly six million deaths each year [8]. Several risk assessment tools—such as the Waterlow, Norton, and Braden scales—are commonly used to predict the likelihood of pressure ulcer development [11]. For classification, the National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) system is widely used. The 2016 update defines four stages (I–IV) and includes categories for unstageable and deep tissue pressure injuries [12].

Good nutrition, regular repositioning, daily skin checks, minimizing shear forces, and maintaining proper moisture balance are essential in preventing and managing Stage II pressure ulcers [9]. Various advanced dressings—such as hydrogel, alginate, foam, and hydrocolloid—are commonly used for prevention and treatment [13, 14]. However, availability, cost, and hypersensitivity reactions can influence the application of this treatment [13]. Hydrogels are three-dimensional polymer networks with chemical (permanent) and physical (temporary) cross-links. Their functional groups (–OH, –NH₂, and –COOH) allow them to absorb and retain large amounts of water [15]. Hydrogel dressing can alleviate pain, facilitate wound repair, optimize the wound microenvironment, and suppress bacterial proliferation. The semisolid dosage forms of hydrogels allow for improved drug stability throughout the shelf-life, enable controlled release of therapeutic agents, and possess an appropriate physical appearance for applications involving mucocutaneous tissues [16].

Heparin is an anticoagulant commonly used for prophylaxis and the treatment of coagulation disorders. Heparin has anti-inflammatory, angiogenic, epithelializing, and antioxidant properties that promote wound healing [17, 18]. Subcutaneous injection of nadroparin, a low molecular weight heparin, has been associated with an increased healing rate of chronic venous leg ulcers in the elderly [19]. Heparin has the potential to enhance endothelial function and reduce micro-thrombosis. These findings and the mechanisms described above suggest that heparin may be a practical therapeutic approach for patients with pressure ulcers [19].

The Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing (PUSH) has been well-validated for pressure ulcer healing [20, 21]. However, other parameters, including length, width, and surface, can help determine wound healing [21]. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of 1% heparin hydrogel compared with hydrocolloid dressing in the treatment of stage II pressure ulcers among ICU patients.

Materials and methods

Heparin gel preparation

This study developed eighteen candidate heparin hydrogel formulations using carboxymethyl cellulose, propylene glycol, glycerol, water, parabens, and 1% heparin. Eight formulations were selected based on viscosity, appearance, pH, homogeneity, and drug-release profile. From these, one optimized formulation was chosen for clinical evaluation, composed of 5% carboxymethyl cellulose (gelling agent), 5% glycerol (humectant), 0.1% paraben (preservative), 1% heparin, and purified water up to 100%. Formulation characteristics were evaluated, including drug release, homogeneity, viscosity, pH, and spreadability. To quantify heparin content, 1 mL of gel was diluted with 9 mL of sodium phosphate buffer, and 2 mL of this solution was mixed with 2 mL of toluidine blue. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was washed twice with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid and 0.2% sodium chloride solutions, then dissolved in 80% ethanol containing 0.1% sodium hydroxide. Absorbance was measured on days 1 and 7 and monthly thereafter for 6 months; values represented the mean of three replicates. Formulation appearance and pH were evaluated on days 1, 7, and 30, then monthly for 6 months for samples stored at 25 ± 2 °C. Homogeneity was examined by light microscopy and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Spreadability was also measured according to the previously described method [22].

Study design and randomization

This single-center, randomized, controlled pilot trial was conducted at Imam Reza Hospital, affiliated with Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either heparin hydrogel or hydrocolloid dressings using a simple random-number generator with a block size of four.

Ethical approval

The Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences approved the trial (IR.KUMS.REC.1398.826). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives. The trial was registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20190624043993N1).

Sample size estimation

Because no prior data were available, we estimated the sample size using the non-central t distribution (NCT) method within Whitehead et al.’s two-stage external pilot trial framework. This approach, which yields a lower inflation rate than the upper confidence limit (UCL) method, is designed to minimize the combined sample size of both the pilot and definitive trials. Assuming a small standardized effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.20), 90% power, and a two-sided α = 0.05, the optimal pilot sample size was calculated as ~ 25 participants per arm. Allowing for an anticipated 10% attrition rate, we, therefore, targeted 28 participants per arm for our pilot study [23].

Participants

Adult patients with acute stage II pressure ulcers, as defined by the National Pressure Ulcer Advisory Panel (NPUAP), who had no contraindications to heparin and signed the informed consent, were included in the study. Patients exhibiting signs of infection, stage I, III, or IV pressure ulcers, collagen vascular disease, renal or hepatic disorders, smoking, alcohol or opium use, malnutrition, thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 50,000), active bleeding, anemia, clinical deterioration, or death during the study were excluded. Patients who received phosphodiesterase inhibitors, anticoagulants, statins, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, or cyclosporine were also excluded [1, 24, 25].

Interventions

All patients were managed on pneumatic beds and repositioned every 2 h. Each patient received oral or enteral nutrition along with intravenous multivitamins containing thiamine (10 mg), riboflavin (4 mg), nicotinamide (40 mg), pantothenic acid (5 mg), pyridoxine (4 mg), vitamin C (500 mg), and zinc sulfate (5 mg). All wounds were cleansed with saline solution before dressing application. In the intervention group, a 1% heparin hydrogel was applied in a layer approximately 2–3 mm thick, bordered with zinc oxide, and covered with a simple non-adherent gauze dressing. Dressings were replaced every 72 h. Treatment was discontinued in cases of significant bleeding, hematoma, or suspected heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The control group received hydrocolloid dressings following identical wound-care procedures and replacement intervals.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were the PUSH score, wound surface area, length, and width, assessed on days 3, 7, and 14 after treatment allocation. The secondary outcome was the time to complete re-epithelialization (The PUSH ≤ 1), which was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods.

The PUSH score incorporated three domains: wound surface area (length × width, scored 0–10), exudate amount (0–3), and tissue type (0–4), yielding a total score ranging from 0 (healed) to 17 (worst) [26]. Baseline variables included age, sex, comorbidities, Braden scores, body weight, body mass index, and wound location, which were recorded. All wound assessments were performed by a single experienced nurse.

Safety assessment

Local events (bleeding, hematoma, dermatitis, pain, infection) and systemic events (clinically significant bleeding, thrombocytopenia) were monitored. Vital signs, wound inspection, and routine labs were reviewed. Adverse events were graded per DAIDS v2.1 and assessed for causality [27].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between groups using independent-samples t tests or the Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square (χ2) tests. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to compare changes in PUSH scores, wound length, width, and surface area over time between groups. The secondary outcome, time to wound re-epithelialization, was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests. the analysis was performed per protocol (n = 30 control, n = 26 intervention). All analyses used a two-sided p value of < 0.05 and were conducted using SPSS for Windows, version 16.0.

Results

A formulation containing 5% CMC, 5% glycerol, 0.1% paraben, and water up to 100% was selected. The pH of the preparation during the first 180 days after preparation ranged from 6.9 to 7.01. No visual changes were observed during this period. The measured viscosity was also 41,704.7 millipascal seconds (mPa·s) (Table 1). Scanning electron microscope (SEM) images showed a uniform and homogeneous preparation (Fig. 1). Approximately 50% and 100% of heparin were released after 1.2 and 4 h, respectively (Fig. 2).

Heparin release profile

Between November 2019 and September 2020, 110 patients with stage II pressure ulcers were screened; 26 were excluded. The remaining 84 were randomized (44 control, 40 intervention). Fourteen patients in each group were lost to follow-up, leaving 30 in control and 26 in intervention patients for analysis (Fig. 3). The most frequent reason for loss to follow-up in both groups was early discharge, accounting for 17 of 28 cases (61%). Baseline wound length, width, and surface area were similar between groups (Table 2). The mean age of participants was 63.7 ± 14.5 years (range 27–92). The median Glasgow Coma Scale score was 10.0 (IQR 6.0–13.0). The median Braden score was 10 (IQR 9–12) in the intervention group and 11 (IQR 9–12) in the control group, with no significant difference (p = 0.81). The most common location of pressure ulcer was the buttock (40% in the control group vs. 54% in the intervention group), followed by the sacrum (17% vs. 15%). Ankle wounds occurred only in the control group (17%), while scapular wounds were observed exclusively in the intervention group (8%). Heel (7% vs. 8%), trochanteric (7% vs. 0%), and elbow (13% vs. 15%) ulcers were similarly distributed. Overall, the groups had no significant difference in wound location distribution (p = 0.32). Using off-loading devices was frequent in both groups and comparable (0.83 ± 0.37 vs. 0.92 ± 0.27; p = 0.42) (Fig. 4).

Although no significant differences in PUSH scores were observed between the groups on day 3, by days 7 (p = 0.033) and 14 (p = 0.003), the intervention group demonstrated significantly lower PUSH scores than the control group (Table 3). Mean wound length was comparable at baseline and day 3, but was significantly shorter in the intervention group by days 7 (p = 0.046) and 14 (p = 0.012). Mean wound width decreased steadily in both groups, and by day 14, wounds were nearly resolved in the heparin group compared to the control group (p = 0.012). The median wound surface area progressively decreased in both groups, with a numerically smaller residual surface area in the heparin group. By day 7, all wound parameters had a greater improvement in the heparin hydrogel group compared to the hydrocolloid control group. Mean wound length and width decreased by 81% and 80% in the heparin group, compared with 52% and 60% reductions in the control group. The median wound surface area declined by 95% in the heparin group vs. 76% in the control group. By day 14, nearly complete closure was achieved in both groups; however, the extent of reduction remained higher with heparin hydrogel (length 99%, width 99%, surface 100%) than with hydrocolloid dressing (length 86%, width 89%, surface 100%). PUSH scores followed a similar trend, decreasing from 11.15 ± 2.41 to 3.69 ± 3.45 (a 67% reduction) by day 7 and to 1.12 ± 0.59 (a 90% reduction) by day 14 in the heparin group, compared with reductions of 42% and 77% in the control group, respectively (Fig. 4). Both groups showed significant within-group improvements from baseline to day 14 in wound length, width, surface area, and PUSH score (all p < 0.001); however, the heparin hydrogel group demonstrated significantly greater reductions compared with the hydrocolloid group (between-group p < 0.05 for all outcomes (Table 4).

The heparin hydrogel demonstrated a significantly higher probability of complete re-epithelialization than the hydrocolloid group. The median time to complete re-epithelialization healing time was 7 days (95% CI 5.8–8.2) in the heparin group vs. 10 days (95% CI 8.8–11.2) in the control group (p < 0.001), indicating a significantly faster re-epithelialization rate in the intervention group (Fig. 5). Larger initial wounds are strongly associated with longer healing times (r = 0.61). Conversely, patients with higher Braden scores (indicating a lower risk of pressure sores) tend to experience shorter healing times (r = −0.59). For each one-point increase in a patient's Braden score, the odds of healing by day 14 increase by 83% (Odds Ratio = 1.83). Other underlying risk factors, including malignancies, age, gender, level of consciousness, and need for mechanical ventilation, influenced the recovery rate. The healing rate was similar between females and males across different groups (p = 0.86) (Fig. 6).

No probable or possible treatment-related side effects were detected, and no serious adverse events occurred. Specifically, no cases of local bleeding, hematoma, contact dermatitis, wound infection, or heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) were observed in either group.

Discussion

This study investigated the comparative effects of heparin hydrogel and hydrocolloid dressings in treating stage II pressure ulcers. Initially, several formulations were developed incorporating carboxymethyl CMC, glycerol, propylene glycol, parabens, and water. The formulation selected for further evaluation demonstrated favorable characteristics, including appropriate appearance, pH, release kinetics, spreadability, homogeneity, and viscosity. Stability assessments indicated that the product maintained its physical properties and spreadability for at least 6 months. In the clinical application, both heparin hydrogel and hydrocolloid dressings were replaced every 72 h, with the release profile analysis revealing that heparin was released steadily over 4 h.

Hydrogel dressings help keep wounds moist and support natural tissue repair by donating moisture to the wound bed and maintaining a semi-occlusive barrier that promotes autolytic cleaning and epithelial growth, especially in dry or necrotic wounds. Their water content also cools the wound surface, easing pain and reducing inflammation. In addition, hydrogels can increase angiogenesis, counter oxidative stress, and speed up tissue regeneration. Compared with hydrocolloid dressings, they are handy for wounds with little exudate that benefit from added hydration, though they usually need a secondary cover to prevent drying out [28–30]. In contrast, hydrocolloid dressings incorporate gelling polymers such as gelatin, sodium carboxymethylcellulose, and pectin to create a moist, occlusive interface that sustains autolysis and typically adheres longer than hydrogel formulations [28]. Most evidence is derived from small sample size studies, and there is not enough high-quality evidence indicating that one dressing is more or less adequate than another [31, 32]. Therefore, selecting appropriate dressings for pressure ulcer stage II depends on availability, exudate amount, presence of infection, and wound thickness [28].

This pilot study showed a significant reduction in wound length, width, surface, and PUSH score throughout the study in both groups. However, by day 14, the heparin hydrogel group had a significantly better response than the hydrocolloid groups. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that topical heparin accelerates wound healing [33]. Some studies have shown the benefits of heparin in wound healing and shown that topical heparin use not only improves wound healing but also has some potential benefits, including reducing pain and analgesic consumption, decreasing antibiotic use, and may further reduce the need for graft surgery and hospitalization in patients with second- and third-degree burns [34–36]. However, due to the patients' level of consciousness, the need for analgesics was not evaluated in the present study.

The current study used re-epithelialization as the main indicator of wound improvement. By day 14, a higher rate of re-epithelialization was observed in the heparin hydrogel group compared with the hydrocolloid group. Previous studies in patients with Stage II pressure ulcers have reported a mean healing time of approximately 17.5 days [37, 38]. Healing time is strongly influenced by ulcer chronicity, nutritional status, age, and comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease [37–39]. A baseline ulcer size ≤ 3.0 cm2 and a PUSH score ≤ 6 have been identified as predictors of faster healing [39, 40]. In the present study, a smaller wound surface area and a higher Braden score were independent predictors of early re-epithelialization.

Topical heparin use has also been associated with favorable wound-healing outcomes in various vascular and infusion-related disorders, with reported healing rates of up to 99% after 5–28 days of treatment [41]. In this study, most patients had baseline wound areas smaller than 3 cm2, which likely contributed to the shorter time to complete re-epithelialization. The relatively younger age of participants (63.66 ± 14.47 years vs. 83.9 [82.7–85.1] years in the current study), along with the fact that all ulcers developed during recent hospitalization rather than being long-standing, may also explain the faster healing and earlier achievement of a PUSH score of 1 [39]. Although previous studies have reported variable follow-up durations and healing rates, extended follow-up may provide a more accurate assessment of long-term outcomes (Table 5). In the study by Palese et al., 24 and 38 out of 269 patients with Stage II pressure ulcers achieved healing by days 7 and 14, respectively [39]. Similarly, a small randomized trial in cancer patients with infected pressure ulcers found significantly shorter healing times with hydrogel compared with foam dressings (7.17 ± 5.12 vs. 12.83 ± 5.04 days) [42].

Previous studies have reported varying results. A recent meta-analysis showed that hydrogel dressings vs. other types of dressings for various ulcer types demonstrated complete healing rates of 55.16% ± 32.30% vs. 41.67% ± 29.07%, and healing times of 31.11 ± 21.74 days vs. 38.15 ± 24.36 days, respectively [43]. However, a network meta-analysis demonstrated that hydrocolloid dressings are more effective than foam, hydrogel, and gauze dressings regarding healing time and wound surface reduction [44]. Several mechanisms have been proposed for topical heparin. Microvascular thrombosis and erythrocyte aggregation may prolong the wound healing process in chronic venous ulcers [45]. Heparin rapidly enhances endothelial cell function and exhibits anticoagulant and anti-angiogenic properties. In addition, it improves microcirculation, modulates cytokine release, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects. These mechanisms are believed to underlie heparin’s therapeutic impact on various types of ulcers [46, 47].

The findings of this study indicate that treatment with heparin hydrogel significantly enhanced the healing rate compared to hydrocolloid dressings. Although hospital costs were not explicitly calculated, the reduced duration of hospital stays observed with heparin hydrogel treatment suggests a potential reduction in overall hospital expenses. Furthermore, among nursing home residents with stage II pressure ulcers, hydrocellular dressings and polyurethane derivatives demonstrated superior efficacy relative to hydrocolloid dressings in both healing rates at 6 weeks and time to complete healing [48]. Hydrogel formulations are characterized by their non-irritating and non-reactive properties, making them suitable for application on dry wounds, including chronic, necrotic, and pressure ulcers. Nonetheless, the use of hydrogels necessitates the application of a secondary dressing [49].

As seen during the study, no significant side effects, including thrombocytopenia and hematoma, are associated with using heparin hydrogel. However, our formulation was well-tolerated in the present study, and no notable adverse effects were observed. These results are consistent with data from a recent meta-analysis that showed that hydrogel dressings are well-tolerated, and no significant side effects related to this dressing were reported [43].

Strength and limitations

Despite the development of a novel heparin formulation and its evaluation in patients with stage II pressure ulcers, where it was associated with faster healing compared to hydrocolloid dressings-several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the preliminary pilot design limited the sample size. Because no prior data were available, we applied Whitehead’s two-stage recommendations for designing pilot studies and assumed a small standardized effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.20), resulting in a target of 28 patients per group. However, based on our observed effect (median recovery: 7.0 vs. 10.7 days; hazard ratio ≈ 1.53), Freedman’s method suggests that a definitive trial would require approximately 80 patients (40 per arm) to achieve 90% power. Second, approximately 33% of patients were lost to follow-up due to early discharge, death, or clinical deterioration, which may have influenced the final results. Third, all wound assessments were based on direct clinical examinations without a standardized photographic protocol [50].

Conclusion

This study's results demonstrated that the wound improvement rate was significantly higher with heparin hydrogel than with hydrocolloid dressings. Larger, adequately powered randomized trials are warranted to confirm these findings and further evaluate long-term safety and efficacy.
