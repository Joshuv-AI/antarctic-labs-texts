Perioperative opioid prescribing concerns in anterior cervical surgery
Authors: Darcy T Fay, Nam V Huynh, Aaron J Buckland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOpioid prescriptions are increasingly used to treat cervical pain. This violates most opioid prescribing guidelines and is associated with worse clinical outcomes, prolonged post-operative opioid use, and dependence. This study aims to analyse the impact of preoperative opioid prescribing on perioperative and long-term opioid use following anterior cervical surgery.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of consecutive patients undergoing 1–2 level anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), cervical disc replacement (CDR), or hybrid procedures by a single surgeon in Melbourne, Australia (Oct 2020–Feb 2024). SafeScript data was used to quantify opioid prescribing. Outcomes included postoperative opioid use, morphine milligram equivalents (MME), complications, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).ResultsSixty-one patients were identified, 28 opioid-sensitised (OS) and 33 opioid-naïve (ON). Patient demographics and complications between groups were similar. More OS patients required opioids at 6 weeks (3.0% vs. 25.0%, P=0.01) and 6 months postoperatively (0.0% vs. 29.0%, P=0.001), but not at 1 year (6.1% vs. 17.9%, P=0.15). Patients prescribed preoperative opioids for <1-month have an increased risk of opioid use at 6 weeks [odds ratio (OR) =12.2, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.4–104.7, P=0.02] and 1–3 months postoperatively (OR =14.4, 95% CI: 1.7–122.4, P=0.02). Opioid prescribing 3–6 months preoperatively was associated with postoperative use at 6 weeks (OR =11.3, P=0.003), 3 months (OR =30.7, P<0.001), 6 months (OR =23.0, P<0.001) and 12 months (OR =8.6, P=0.01) postoperatively. Six-month postoperative MME was higher amongst OS patients (2,265 vs. 577 mg, P=0.04) and correlated with MME prescribed 1-month (r=0.553, P<0.001) and 3-month preoperatively (r=0.691, P<0.001). Preoperative Neck Disability Index (NDI), visual analogue scale (VAS)-neck and VAS-arm were similar between groups, but OS patients had a lower EuroQol-5 Dimensions-3 Level (EQ-5D-3L) index (0.56 vs. 0.68, P=0.03). Postoperative NDI, VAS-neck, VAS-arm and EQ-5D-3L index were similar at 6 weeks, 6 months and 12 months.ConclusionsOS patients undergoing anterior cervical surgery are more likely to chronically use opioids postoperatively compared to ON patients. Duration of preoperative opioid use significantly predicts prolonged postoperative use, with no corresponding improvement in PROMs.Keywords: Opioid, anterior cervical surgery, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), cervical disc replacement (CDR), patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Preoperative opioid exposure was strongly associated with prolonged postoperative opioid use after anterior cervical surgery. Even short preoperative exposure (<1 month) increased postoperative opioid requirements at 6 months. Despite higher opioid use, there were no significant differences in patient-reported outcomes [Neck Disability Index (NDI), visual analogue scale (VAS) neck/arm pain, EuroQol-5 Dimensions-3 Level (EQ-5D-3L)] at 6 weeks, 6 months, or 1 year postoperatively.

What is known and what is new?

• Preoperative opioid use is common in cervical spine pathology and has been associated with increased postoperative opioid use and poorer outcomes in some spine cohorts. However, Australian data with patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) correlation are limited.

• This Australian SafeScript-monitored study demonstrates that preoperative opioid exposure predicts prolonged postoperative opioid use after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) or cervical disc replacement (CDR). The study quantifies the relationship between preoperative and postoperative morphine milligram equivalents (MME) and shows no meaningful PROM improvement despite increased opioid use.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Preoperative opioid prescribing in cervical spine disease should be minimised. Earlier specialist referral, preoperative opioid tapering, and opioid-sparing perioperative pathways may reduce chronic postoperative opioid dependence without compromising surgical outcomes.

Introduction

Cervical spine surgeries such as anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and cervical disc replacement (CDR) are commonly used for the management of degenerative disc disease and cervical myelopathy (1,2). Patients with neck and arm pain are often prescribed opioid analgesia in the initial stages of symptomatic disease (3,4). Although ACDF reduces opioid dependence for most patients, there is an increasing trend for opioid prescriptions in patients with cervical pain (3,5,6). This trend typically violates most opioid prescribing guidelines and is associated with worse clinical outcomes compared to alternative regimens, increasing the risk of prolonged post-operative opioid use and dependence (5,7-9).

Opioid use and dependence are increasing in the context of elective orthopaedic surgeries and is associated with postoperative morbidity and mortality (9). It has been demonstrated that pre-operative opioid analgesia is a risk factor for long-term post-operative opioid use (8,10,11). The management of pre-operative and postoperative pain following ACDF and CDR often incorporates opioid analgesia as a mainstay (12). This is despite the growing understanding of the risks associated with opioid prescribing. The literature demonstrates that there is substantial reliance on opioids for pain management in these populations. Evidence broadly suggests that spinal surgery is shown to reduce opioid dependence for most patients. However, it has also been demonstrated that a small proportion of patients who are prescribed post-operative opioids develop new opioid dependence (8,10,11). This underscores the need for careful patient monitoring and tailored pain management strategies. There is currently a paucity of data that analyses the role of pre-operative opioid use and its impact on post-operative opioid use in patients undergoing ACDF, CDR or ACDF/CDR hybrid surgery specifically. Furthermore, there is no well-defined relationship between post-operative opioid use and patient outcomes in this population.

This study is novel in that it examines preoperative and postoperative opioid prescribing patterns in Australian patients undergoing anterior cervical surgery using real-time prescription monitoring data (SafeScript). To our knowledge, no prior study in this population has evaluated the quantitative relationship between preoperative opioid exposure and postoperative opioid dependence with concurrent patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) analysis. This study aims to analyse the impact of preoperative opioid prescribing on perioperative and long-term opioid use following anterior cervical surgery. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-1-0052/rc).

Methods

A retrospective analysis of consecutively enrolled patients in a prospective clinical registry, who underwent either ACDF, CDR, or a hybrid of ACDF/CDR, performed by a single spinal surgeon between October 2020 and February 2024 were included. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ramsay Health Care Human Research Ethics Committee (No. 2020/ETH/9011). Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision cervical fusion or disc replacement at this index procedure.

Patient demographics, treatment and surgical characteristics, complications and reoperations were collected from anaesthetics, operative and patient medical records. Postoperative complications were classified using the Clavien-Dindo classification (13). Minor complications included those that caused minor deviations from the normal postoperative course, required blood transfusion or pharmacological intervention (Clavien-Dindo I–II [18]). Major complications were classified as those that required further endoscopic, radiological or surgical management, life-threatening complications or death (Clavien-Dindo III–V [18]). All follow-up data was limited to 2 years following the date of surgery.

Preoperative and postoperative opioid prescription and postoperative neuroleptic and muscle relaxant prescription data were obtained from SafeScript (Department of Health, Victoria, Australia). SafeScript records all prescribed and dispensed opioids, including the name and registration of all prescribing physicians (surgeons and others) and the location where the medication was dispensed. Only dispensed prescriptions were included to account for prescriptions provided to patients but were not filled. The authors believe this minimizes under-reporting unless the patient obtained opioid analgesia from another unprescribed source. Opioid scripts were not routinely prescribed beyond 2 weeks postoperatively and an early postoperative opioid taper was performed. Thus, prolonged opioid use was defined as ongoing use beyond 2 weeks postoperatively.

Patients were grouped as opioid-naïve (ON) and opioid-sensitised (OS). ON was defined as no opioid prescriptions dispensed within 1 month prior to surgery. The primary outcome variable was defined as 6-month postoperative opioid use. Secondary outcomes included postoperative MME dispensed and PROMs. Morphine milligram equivalents (MME) were calculated using the opioid dose equivalence calculation table from The Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetics, Faculty of Pain Medicine (14).

Three PROMs were utilised in this study: Neck Disability Index (NDI), visual analogue scale (VAS) for neck pain and arm pain and EuroQol-5 Dimensions-3 Level (EQ-5D-3L). Minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for NDI was defined as an improvement of more than 17.3% from the patient’s preoperative score (15).

Statistical methods

Independent samples t-tests and chi-squared analyses were used to compare ON and OS groups as appropriate. Bivariate correlation and univariate binary logistic regression were utilised to analyse associations and relationships between variables. Sub-analysis was conducted to assess the association between postoperative MME and demographic factors and complications by independent sample t-tests as appropriate. A post-hoc power analysis was performed. All statistical analysis were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Software (29.0, Armonk, NY, USA), with significance set at P<0.05.

Results

Sixty-two patients were examined for eligibility. One patient underwent further spinal surgery and was excluded. A total of 61 patients were included, with a mean follow-up of 19.29 months [standard deviation (SD) 5.55]. Twenty-six patients underwent ACDF, 28 patients underwent CDR and 7 patients had a hybrid ACDF/CDR performed. Thirty-three patients were classified as ON and 28 patients were OS.

Demographics in ON and OS patients

No differences were noted in demographic factors between ON and OS patients, including average age (55.626 vs. 55.49 years, P=0.97 respectively), gender distribution (57.60% vs. 64.30%, P=0.59), body mass index (BMI; 22.551 vs. 28.18 kg/m2, P=0.65), smokers (3.00% vs. 10.7%, P=0.23) and diabetics (3.10% vs. 3.80%, P=0.88). The distribution of ON and OS patients who had ACDF (51.50% vs. 32.10%, P=0.13), CDR (39.40% vs. 53.60%, P=0.27) and hybrid ACDF/CDR (9.10% vs. 14.30%, P=0.53) was similar. The mean operative time (82.27 vs. 85.93 min, P=0.53), estimated blood loss (EBL; 20.91 vs. 20.91 mL, P=0.91) and length of stay (LOS; 0.97 vs. 1.0 days, P=0.73) were also similar between groups. A similar proportion of ON and OS patients required opioids in the first 2 weeks postoperatively (78.80% vs. 89.30%, P=0.27). Beyond 2 weeks post-operatively, a significantly larger proportion of OS patients required opioids at 6 weeks (3.0% vs. 25.0%, P=0.01) and 6-months (0% vs. 29%, P=0.001) postoperatively. Additionally, there were no patients who used neuroleptics or muscle relaxants in the post-operative period (Table 2).

Postoperative opioid use at 3, 6 and 12 months and odds ratios (ORs)

Preoperative opioid usage of any duration and all timepoints within 1-year prior to surgery was associated with postoperative opioid use at 6 weeks, 6 months and 12 months (Figure 2). Notably, patients using opioids for 3 to 6 months preoperatively were significantly more likely to be using opioids at 6 weeks [OR =11.25, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.33–54.44, P=0.003], 6 months (OR =23.00, 95% CI: 3.95–133.85, P<0.001) and 1 year (OR =17.25, 95% CI: 2.95–101.04, P=0.002). Patients using opioids for greater than 6 months preoperatively demonstrated a similar pattern at 6 weeks (OR =5.57, 95% CI: 1.23–25.33, P=0.03), 6 months (OR =10.74, 95% CI: 1.95–58.45, P<0.001) and 1 year (OR =8.57, 95% CI: 1.55–47.49, P=0.01). ON patients were very unlikely to use opioids at 6 weeks (OR =0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.71, P=0.02) and 6-months (OR =0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.65, P=0.01) postoperatively.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that preoperative opioid use is a significant predictor of prolonged postoperative opioid requirements in patients undergoing ACDF, CDR, or hybrid ACDF/CDR. OS patients demonstrated significantly higher MME at 6 months compared to ON patients. Specifically, 6-month postoperative MME requirements were almost four times higher among OS patients, and ongoing opioid use at 6 months and beyond was significantly more common in the OS group. These findings align with existing evidence that preoperative opioid use is a major risk factor for long-term postoperative opioid dependence (8,10,16,17).

However, other risk factors for increased and chronic postoperative opioid use in the literature include high BMI, high postoperative opioid dose, preoperative benzodiazepine use, drug abuse, depression and anxiety (10,18). The present study did not note significant differences in BMI and did not collect data on mental health conditions. Furthermore, patients prescribed opioids for short durations preoperatively (<1 month) had significantly increased odds of postoperative opioid use at both 6 weeks and 6 months postoperatively. Therefore, suggesting that even small quantities of preoperative opioid use may contribute to prolonged postoperative dependence. This is consistent with Schoenfeld et al. (2017), who demonstrated that ON spine surgery patients were significantly less likely to develop long-term opioid dependence (19). The same study suggests that spine surgeries are not the major drivers of long-term post-operative opioid use by demonstrating that 0.1% of ON patients continued opioids at 6 months postoperatively. This study found similar results, where 0% of ON patients continued opioids at 6-months post-operatively, compared to 29% of ON patients using opioids at the same timepoint.

The quantity and duration of preoperative opioid use are strong predictors of prolonged postoperative opioid requirements. This underscores the importance of minimising preoperative opioid exposure through timely surgical referral, non-opioid pain management strategies, and patient education. Patients with chronic opioid use should ideally undergo preoperative tapering programs to optimise postoperative recovery and minimise the risk of prolonged postoperative opioid use (9). Segebarth et al. (2021) demonstrated that adequate postoperative pain control is a possibility with multimodal opioid free pain management pathways following anterior cervical surgery for degenerative disease (20). Similarly, Silver et al. (2023) found that cannabis users who underwent ACDF filled fewer opioid prescriptions postoperatively (21). Lovecchio et al. (2021) suggested 12×5 mg oxycodone as a standardised post-operative prescription accompanied by education on appropriate use (22). Reed et al. (2022) have suggested a protocol that may help limit the role of opioids in spine surgery (23).

United States opioid prescribing guidelines have demonstrated reduced postoperative opioid prescribing without compromising pain control or increasing readmission rates (24-26). In addition, standardised multimodal and opioid-free pain management pathways offer a viable alternative, providing adequate pain control compared to traditional opioid-based regimens (20). No such guidelines exist in the preoperative setting to the authors’ knowledge, however, their development may be of benefit. Clinically, these results reinforce the importance of avoiding preoperative opioid prescribing for cervical radiculopathy and myelopathy. Even limited preoperative exposure (<1 month) was associated with a 12-fold increase in postoperative opioid use. This underscores the need for early specialist evaluation and adoption of opioid-sparing perioperative pathways.

This study supports previously described patterns of opioid prescribing for spinal conditions in the primary care setting. Mathieson et al. described increasing opioid prescriptions for managing spinal pain over nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) amongst general practitioners (6). Additionally, patients presenting to emergency departments for back pain are frequently prescribed opioids, even when the pain is not secondary to a primary spinal pathology (3). Excessive opioid prescriptions in primary care and emergency settings likely contribute to preoperative opioid sensitisation, perpetuating a cycle of opioid dependence that extends into the postoperative period.

Despite the clear association between preoperative opioid use and prolonged postoperative dependence, this study found no significant differences in PROMs, including NDI, VAS for neck and arm pain, and EQ-5D-3L index at 6 weeks, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively. Interestingly, no difference in the likelihood of meeting the Minimally Clinically Important Difference (MCID) was observed amongst ON and OS patients at any timepoint except 6 months post-operatively. This may suggest that postoperative opioid use has a minimal role in improving surgical outcome scores, but rather OS patients may require higher doses of opioids to achieve similar outcomes to their ON counterparts. Hills et al. (2020) found that preoperative opioid use was associated with worse functional outcomes and lower rates of achieving clinically meaningful improvements (27). Additionally, studies suggest that opioids provide limited benefit in managing chronic spinal pain preoperatively and may contribute to worse long-term outcomes through mechanisms such as opioid-induced hyperalgesia and psychological dependence (7,8,20,28,29). Furthermore, hyperalgesia, tolerance and dependence may be potential confounders of postoperative opioid requirements.

The association between preoperative opioid exposure and long-term postoperative use is multifactorial, involving physiological, psychological, and behavioural elements (4,30). Preoperative opioid use contributes to tolerance, dependence, and central sensitisation. All of which contribute to higher postoperative opioid requirements. Psychiatric comorbidities, namely anxiety and depression, are established risk factors for prolonged postoperative opioid use (10). In this study, OS patients demonstrated significantly worse preoperative EQ-5D-3L scores, suggesting a lower baseline quality of life as well as greater pain and functional impairment.

This study reported no complications in either ON or OS patients. This is consistent with the findings of Armaghani et al. (2014), who reported no association between preoperative opioid use and major perioperative complications in spine surgery patients (31).

Cervical sagittal alignment is an important determinant of postoperative pain and functional recovery. A recent large-scale magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based study has shown age and sex-related variation in cervical lordosis and kyphosis in asymptomatic populations (32). Thereby suggesting that preoperative sagittal imbalance may influence postoperative pain and analgesia requirements. However, radiographic parameters were not recorded in this cohort, and this relationship could not be directly examined. Future studies incorporating radiographic data may clarify whether spinal imbalance independently predicts prolonged opioid use.

Limitations

The small sample size (n=61) in this study may decrease the generalisability and external validity of the above findings. Additionally, the small population size reduces statistical power, limiting the precision of outcome estimates. Additionally, the single surgeon, single centre nature of the study provides minimal diversity in patient demographics. Multicentre studies involving multiple surgeons and a larger cohort size are planned to further validate the results of this study as well as influence practice guidelines.

While SafeScript data provides an accurate record of prescribed opioids, actual opioid consumption was not directly measured, and some patients may not have used all dispensed medication, thus overestimating opioid use. Orosz et al. (2022) found that opioid-naive patients use less opioids than prescribed, suggesting that their true MME amount may be smaller (33). While prescriber information was available via the SafeScript database, the indication of prescription was not provided, further attributing to potential overestimation of pre- and postoperative opioid requirements. This study also excluded inpatient opioid use, meaning true perioperative MME may be underestimated. However, given that the average LOS was less than 1 day, the authors believe this would not significantly alter the results of the study. Lastly, the lack of non-operative patients hinders the ability to determine the impact of cervical surgery on opioid consumption. Due to the modest cohort size, non-significant findings may reflect Type II error rather than the absence of the true difference. Post-hoc power analysis demonstrated that some non-significant results of important outcomes were inconclusive. Future multicentre studies with larger cohorts are warranted.

Conclusions

Preoperative opioid exposure significantly increases the risk of prolonged postoperative opioid use in patients undergoing anterior cervical surgery, without clear benefits for pain relief or functional improvement. Incorporating preoperative screening, opioid-sparing perioperative protocols, and evidence-based procedure-specific opioid prescribing guidelines into routine practice may help reduce opioid dependence and improve patient outcomes. Future research should aim to incorporate larger, multicentre studies to validate these findings.
