Impact of ICME sheath planarity on Earth’s outer radiation belt
Authors: Kalpesh Ghag, Ankush Bhaskar, Anil Raghav, Omkar Dhamane, Mary Hudson, Kishor Kumbhar, Mariyam Karari, Mohit Shah, Ajay Kumar, Ganesh Shitole
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A planar magnetic structure refers to a compressed and ordered region in interplanetary space where magnetic field vectors are predominantly confined to a plane. Due to enhanced plasma parameters associated with them, they play a significant role in space weather dynamics. To examine their geoeffectiveness, we investigate the impact of planar and non-planar sheaths of Interplanetary Coronal Mass Ejections on the outer radiation belt electrons, using in-situ particle data from NASA’s Van Allen Probes mission. A superposed epoch analysis on electron flux data revealed a significant reduction in electron flux during planar sheath passages as compared to non-planar sheaths, possibly driven by stronger magnetospheric compression resulting in intensifying magnetopause shadowing. Notably, electron flux levels exhibit a significant post-passage increase, surpassing pre-passage levels three days later. For the first time, the efforts taken to highlight the interplay between planar sheath-driven magnetospheric compression and radiation belt dynamics offer new insights for improving space weather forecasting.

Introduction

The Earth’s magnetosphere traps energetic particles, including electrons, ions, and protons, through gyration, bouncing, and drift motions within the magnetic field, forming the Van Allen radiation belts1. The outer Van Allen belt and slot region, in particular, exhibit considerable fluctuations in relativistic electron intensity during geomagnetic storms, highlighting their highly dynamic nature2–6.

Previous studies have extensively investigated the outer electron radiation belt’s response to geomagnetic storms. O’Brien et al., (2001)7 analyzed electron data (0.3-1.5 MeV and >2 MeV) from NOAA’s GOES and LANL spacecraft at geosynchronous orbit (GEO), finding that traditional geomagnetic indices were not strongly correlated with electron response at GEO. Instead, factors such as storm duration, sustained high solar wind speed, elevated Pc5 ultra-low frequency (ULF) wave power, and enhanced substorm activity during the recovery phase were better predictors of enhanced electron flux. Reeves et al., (2003)8 later reported that out of 276 moderate to intense storms (Dst < -50 nT), 53% resulted in enhanced electron flux, 19% caused depletion, and 28% produced no significant change, highlighting the complex interplay of acceleration and loss mechanisms in the outer radiation belt.

NASA’s Van Allen Probes, launched in August 2012, have significantly advanced our understanding of radiation belt dynamics, especially regarding storm-time electron enhancement and loss processes9. Murphy et al., (2018)10 used Van Allen Probes data to identify a two-phase response during geomagnetic storms: a dominant loss process during the main phase and prevalent particle acceleration during the recovery phase. Studies have also shown correlations between outer belt enhancements of relativistic electrons and several solar wind-magnetosphere coupling factors, including elevated solar wind velocity, southward interplanetary magnetic field (IMF), and plasma parameters11–14. Moreover, Hietala et al., (2014)15 and Kilpua et al., (2015)14 refined our understanding of how the solar wind driver type influences the evolution of outer belt electrons during geomagnetic storms. Their work focused on differentiating between interplanetary coronal mass ejection (ICME)-driven events based on the distinct solar wind properties within the sheath (the turbulent leading part of ICME) and ejecta (an ordered flux rope-like structure) substructures. Hietala et al., (2014)15 observed that storms driven by the CME sheath were particularly effective at causing long-lasting depletions of outer belt electrons, as measured by > 2 MeV electron fluxes at GEO. Kilpua et al., (2015)14 further emphasized that the > 2 MeV electron response at GEO is not random. They proposed that loss mechanisms dominate during both CME sheath and ejecta-driven storms, while storms driven by a combination of CMEs, corotating interaction regions (CIRs), and subsequent fast solar wind are particularly effective for producing outer belt enhancements.

Recent studies have revealed that the ICME sheath and its ejecta can dynamically transform into planar magnetic structures (PMSs)16. Recently, Shaikh et al., (2022)17,18 showed that ICME substructures molded into PMS can exhibit enhanced plasma properties and magnetic field intensities that are nearly double those observed in non-planar ICMEs. Consequently, PMSs are expected to play a crucial role in the effects of space weather. This finding is substantiated by studies showing that a PMS-molded ICME triggered the most intense geomagnetic superstorm of the 21st century on November 20, 200319. Additionally, a statistical study by Ghag et al., (2023)20 supports the notion that planar ICMEs are more geoeffective than non-planar ICMEs. In the consensus view, key factors contributing to geomagnetic storms include the southward component of the magnetic field, the duskward convection electric field, solar wind proton density, and solar wind dynamic pressure21–26. These parameters are typically enhanced in planar ICME structures, leading to a notable enhancement in magnetopause current (when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} is positive) and in ring current (when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} is negative). In a recent study by Ghag et al., (2024)27, they attributed the cause of the extreme geomagnetic storm (SYMH \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx -231~nT$$\end{document}) that occurred on April 23–24, 2023 to an ICME planar sheath. Consequently, both case studies and statistical research suggest that PMSs are likely to be highly geoeffective19,20,27,28.

Given their significant impact, it is crucial to understand how ICME-associated PMSs affect the inner magnetosphere and the ionosphere-thermosphere system. Ghag et al., (2024)27 noted that the high compression caused by PMSs can push the magnetopause boundary inward to around 6 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_E$$\end{document}, which poses risks to geosynchronous satellites. For the first time, the present study aims to bridge the gap between our understanding of interplanetary dynamics and its effects on the magnetosphere by examining the distinct impacts of planar and non-planar sheaths on the outer radiation belt’s dynamics.

Data and method

In this study, we selected ICME events from 2012 to 2017 using the catalog developed by Shaikh et al., (2020)18. This catalog examines the plasma properties of ICME sheaths transformed through the PMS method. Previous studies outline criteria for identifying PMSs17,29–31. A region qualifies as a PMS if it meets: (1) a wide azimuth angle distribution, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0^{\circ }< \phi < 360^{\circ }$$\end{document}, (2) high planarity, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{<B_n>}{|B|} \le 0.2$$\end{document}, and (3) efficiency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R = \lambda _2 / \lambda _3 \ge 3$$\end{document}31,32. Here, B is the IMF magnitude, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_n = \vec {B} \cdot \hat{n}$$\end{document} is the magnetic field component normal to the PMS plane, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{n}$$\end{document} derived from Minimum Variance Analysis (MVA)33. MVA yields eigenvalues (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _1, \lambda _2, \lambda _3$$\end{document}) in descending order and corresponding eigenvectors (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_1, e_2, e_3$$\end{document}). Perfect planarity occurs when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_n = 0$$\end{document}, and a low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{|B_n|}{B}$$\end{document} indicates vectors are nearly parallel to the plane29,34. The ratio of the component of the magnetic field is normal to the PMS plane, and the total magnetic field should be less than 0.20. The MVA technique has been employed to identify the normal direction along which the magnetic field variation is minimal. The catalog found 35 planar sheaths and 39 non-planar sheath events. To analyze the radiation belt response to these selected planar and non-planar sheaths, we used electron flux data from the Radiation Belt Storm Probes Energetic Particle Composition and Thermal Plasma (RBSP-ECT) Relativistic Electron-Proton Telescope (REPT) on NASA’s Van Allen Probes spacecraft35,36. We utilized level 3 data from the RBSP-ECT, covering 4 energy channels from approximately 1.8 MeV to 6 MeV. Moreover, we used International Monitor for Auroral Geomagnetic Effects (IMAGE) magnetometer data to identify the ULF wave activity during the planar and non-planar sheath region.

Superposed epoch analysis

Superposed epoch analysis (SEA) is a statistical technique that consolidates information from comparable yet separate occurrences by establishing a reference time, referred to as epoch time, within each event. This epoch time is a benchmark for calculating averages of various parameters within the specified category. The time markers for each event are selected to ensure that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t = 0$$\end{document} (the epoch time) corresponds to a common characteristic in the aggregated data37. This tool is used in various statistical studies of solar wind38, ICMEs39,40, and geomagnetic storm41,42. In this study, we performed a two-dimensional SEA, where data was structured in both L-shell and time43–48. We chose the onset of the shock as the epoch time (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t = 0$$\end{document}) and analyzed data spanning three days before and after the shock onset, bin-averaging in L-shell (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta L = 0.3$$\end{document}) and time (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t = 3$$\end{document} h). Subsequently, the mean was calculated, and the standard error was determined following the method outlined in Yermolaev et al, (2014)49.

Discussion

In general, the large amplitude and long-duration southward IMF (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document}), solar wind density (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_p$$\end{document}), solar wind velocity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_p$$\end{document}), and solar wind dynamic pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_d$$\end{document}) are key parameters influencing geoeffectiveness (see, e.g.,21,22,24,51). The role of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_p$$\end{document} in geomagnetic storms is complex. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_p$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V_p$$\end{document} do not show a direct correlation with geomagnetic indices. However, solar wind dynamic pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_d = N_p m_p V_p^2$$\end{document}) significantly impacts geomagnetic activity. Fenrich et al. (1998)52 suggested that the ring current injection rate increases during the passage of enhanced dynamic pressure. Moreover, Wang et al. (2003)53 empirically showed the dynamic pressure effect on the ring current injection rate using the Dst index. They also suggest a low ring current decay rate in high dynamic pressure conditions. Additionally, Shaikh et al. (2020)18 and Shaikh et al. (2022)17 found that the magnitudes of these plasma parameters, along with the southward/northward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} within planar sheaths, are almost double compared to non-planar sheaths. This suggests that planar sheath crossovers may cause enhanced compression and erosion of the magnetosphere, leading to the magnetopause boundary shifting closer to Earth.

Previous studies have shown that sheaths associated with PMS exhibit enhanced IMF and plasma parameters, such as the magnitude and duration of southward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} and dynamic pressure, which drive their geoeffectiveness. To understand why these parameters are particularly pronounced in planar sheath events, we consider the mechanism proposed by Kataoka et al. (2005)54, who suggest that magnetic field lines drape around the shock and magnetic cloud, forming a planar structure in the ICME sheath. Neugebauer et al. (1993)34 proposed that interplanetary fast shocks can amplify and align preexisting discontinuities in the solar wind, leading to the formation of PMS. As a fast-forward shock propagates toward Earth, it carries draped IMF field lines along its leading edge, resulting in a magnetic field highly inclined relative to the ecliptic plane. This configuration elevates the vertical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} component, and its sustained duration during propagation likely contributes to prolonged southward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document}. Additionally, plasma pile-up ahead of the shock increases proton density, leading to elevated dynamic pressure at Earth’s bow shock. These processes likely account for the enhanced geoeffectiveness of PMS sheaths. Yermolaev et al. (2012)55 reported that sheaths have a higher occurrence rate and efficiency in triggering geomagnetic storms. Ghag et al. (2023)20 further noted a greater proportion of extreme storms associated with PMS sheaths. These findings suggest that the inherent geoeffectiveness of sheaths, driven by dynamic IMF and plasma conditions, is amplified in planar events due to their more inclined magnetic fields and denser plasma compared to non-planar sheaths.

The geoeffectiveness of sheaths has been a topic of significant discussion in recent studies. Huttunne et al. (2004)56 examined geomagnetic storms from 1997 to 2002 using WIND and ACE spacecraft data, demonstrating that post-shock streams and sheath regions of ICMEs caused the largest portion of intense magnetic storms. Additionally, Guo et al. (2011)57 suggested that during ICME-driven storms, particularly those driven by magnetic clouds, the solar wind-magnetosphere coupling functions and geomagnetic indices exhibit a slower and more gradual increase and recovery compared to sheath-driven storms. In contrast, sheath-driven storms tend to have larger peak values in these parameters. Kilpua et al. (2015)14 suggested that sheaths generally decrease radiation belt flux more than ejecta and other solar wind drivers. In this study, we showed how planarity enhances the sheath’s ability to deplete radiation belt electron flux.

Ni et al. (2016)58 demonstrate that electron fluxes in the radiation belt decrease following a peak in dynamic pressure under northward IMF \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} conditions, while significant dropouts occur during dynamic pressure pulses associated with southward IMF \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document}, highlighting the role of IMF orientation in flux dynamics. They further note that deeper earthward magnetopause erosion can lead to prompt flux dropouts at L \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 5, emphasizing the impact of magnetopause dynamics on electron losses. Moreover, Cervantes et al. (2020)59 quantify the effects of loss mechanisms in the outer electron radiation belt using RBSP, GOES 15, and GOES 16 data, finding that magnetopause shadowing causes more substantial losses, between 50%/h and 70%/h of the maximum phase space density , compared to EMIC wave scattering, which accounts for 10%/h to 30%/h of the maximum PSD. This underscores the dominant role of magnetopause shadowing in driving electron flux dropouts, particularly under conditions of enhanced solar wind pressure and magnetopause erosion. In our analysis, we observed high dynamic pressure and enhanced southward\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} during PMS sheath events, which likely causes greater magnetopause erosion and compression in these cases, leading to the pronounced depletion of electron fluxes observed in PMS sheath events compared to Non-PMS events.

The “Dst effect” is an adiabatic, reversible process that explains electron flux dropouts resulting from the storm-time ring current development and the subsequent decrease in the inner magnetosphere’s magnetic field strength60,61. This effect is marked by a strong correlation between the reduction in energetic electron fluxes and the decline in the Dst index during the main phase of a geomagnetic storm. Furthermore, the formation of a partial ring current near the dusk sector43,62, causes magnetic field line stretching, leading to localized electron losses through changes in magnetic field topology. In the context of PMS events, which exhibit higher flux dropouts, the Dst effect is particularly relevant due to the sudden magnetic field changes during the main phase, amplifying the observed depletion in electron fluxes.

The past studies demonstrate flux depletion during the main phase of a geomagnetic storm, which is attributed to magnetopause shadowing and radial diffusion caused by enhanced ULF and EMIC wave activity63,64. Magnetopause shadowing causes particles previously on closed drift shells to cross the magnetopause and be lost. Magnetopause shadowing during sheath crossings results from a strongly compressed magnetosphere due to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_d$$\end{document}, which scatters electrons to larger distances and enhances their loss rates. Therefore, we conclude that during the PMS-sheath crossover, the observed electron flux dropout is primarily driven by magnetopause shadowing3,65,66. Radiation belt electrons exhibit common responses during geomagnetic storms, including MeV electron dropouts in the outer belt during the storm’s main phase, followed by replenishment in the early recovery phase67–69. The enhancement of MeV electrons at specific L-shells varies with each storm and depends on the storm driver. Murphy et al. (2018)10 described two distinct behaviors of relativistic outer belt electrons: significant loss during the main phase and rapid acceleration during the recovery phase. The pre-storm and post-storm flux features are quantified by Reeves et al. (2003)8 using GEO data.

In Fig. 3, we observe that post-sheath flux enhancement is more pronounced in the case of planar sheaths. During the recovery phase of the geomagnetic storm, the southward IMF \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} begins to recover and increase in the radiation belt’s seed, relativistic, and ultra-relativistic particle populations. At the same time, solar wind proton velocity either peaks or begins to decline, boosting ULF wave power during this second phase of geomagnetic storms10,70. Numerous studies have linked solar wind parameters to ULF wave activity in the magnetosphere67,71–73. PMSs amplify solar wind parameters, potentially enhancing ULF wave activity during their passage, which may drive increased particle fluxes in the outer radiation belt post-sheath. Investigating ULF wave activity alongside PMS planarity offers valuable insights into their roles in flux dropouts. To explore this, we analyzed ULF wave activity using IMAGE magnetometer data from stations within the L-shell range of 2.5 to 6.5 during the period of interest. ULF power was computed in 10-min intervals over a 24-h window, spanning -24 h before to -24 h after the sheath onset. A bandpass filter of 1 to 10 mHz is applied, and a superposed epoch analysis is performed to examine variations in ULF wave activity. The top panel of Fig. 4 illustrates the integrated ULF wave power during planar sheath events, while the bottom panel displays the ULF power for non-planar sheath events. During planar sheath events, we observe significant ULF power penetration in the region 3.5 < L < 5. In contrast, non-planar sheath events exhibit negligible ULF power. This highlights the significant role of planar structures in enhancing ULF wave activity in the magnetosphere and particle energization.

In addition to radial diffusion driven by ULF waves, other key mechanisms contribute to the acceleration and loss of radiation belt particles, including local acceleration via cyclotron resonance with whistler-mode chorus waves and inward radial transport due to substorm dipolarization. Locally generated whistler-mode chorus waves significantly influence electron acceleration and loss in the outer radiation belt74–76. Cyclotron resonance interactions with dayside chorus, hiss, and lightning-generated whistler waves can drive both electron acceleration and scattering loss77,78. Drozdov et al. (2020)78 demonstrate that hiss and chorus waves provide essential scattering of near-equatorial mirroring electrons, with chorus waves enhancing loss in the presence of EMIC waves. Additionally, substorm dipolarization is known to inject keV electrons into the radiation belts, but observations and modeling over the past two decades show that it can also inject MeV electrons in some cases79–82. These effects are amplified during storms driven by high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$B_z$$\end{document} and dynamic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{dy}$$\end{document}, making PMS sheath-driven storms particularly geoeffective.

(2024)27 demonstrated that the magnetopause moved as close to as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 6 ~R_E$$\end{document} during the latest geomagnetic storm on April 23, 2023, using the empirical model by Shue (1998)50 and GOES 16 data. The figure clearly shows a skewed distribution towards lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_E$$\end{document} values for planar sheath events, with a notable peak around a MP distance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 6~R_E$$\end{document}. This analysis provides evidence of the magnetopause boundary shifting closer to Earth during planar sheath events. In conclusion, this study highlights the critical role of planar sheaths in shaping magnetopause dynamics and the loss of radiation belt electron fluxes. During planar sheath crossovers, enhanced solar wind parameters boost ULF wave activity, which drives electron acceleration and flux increases during the storm recovery phase. Further exploration of wave activity and sheath planarity could shed light on these processes. Our findings emphasize the importance of ICME sheath planarity in determining their geoeffectiveness, with implications for both planar and non-planar sheaths. Understanding sheath planarity in advance could improve forecasting and modeling of radiation belt impacts. Additionally, the complex interactions within the Solar-Magnetosphere-Ionosphere-Thermosphere (SMIT) system warrant further investigation.
