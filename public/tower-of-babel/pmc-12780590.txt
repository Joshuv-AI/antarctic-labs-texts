Trends of mortality among patients with malignant neoplasms of digestive system in the United States from 2007 to 2021
Authors: Yu Hua, Yuting Zhang, Shangcheng Yan, Yichun Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile malignant neoplasms (MNs) of digestive system (MNDS) remain a leading cause of cancer mortality, evolving patterns of cause-specific deaths in the modern treatment era are poorly characterized. This study aimed to depict the trends in cause-specific mortality among MNDS patients in the U.S. from 2007 to 2021, stratified by clinical and demographic factors, with particular focus on the growing impact of non-neoplasm causes of death (CODs).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed incidence-based mortality rates from 22 registries of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results program. Patients diagnosed with MNDS in 2000–2021 who died in 2007–2021 were included. Mortality rates were age-adjusted to the 2000 U.S. standard population. Trends of mortality rates were quantified by annual percent changes (APCs), calculated with Joinpoint regression. We stratified the cohort by tumor site, age at death, sex, race, and tumor stage. In each subgroup, mortality rates were classified by COD into MNDS, non-digestive system MNs, non-MNs, non-neoplasm diseases, and unknown COD. In the overall cohort, non-neoplasm CODs were further subclassified.ResultsFrom 2007 to 2021, 1,318,354 deaths occurred among MNDS patients, corresponding to an average mortality rate of 619.27 [95% confidence interval (CI): 618.2, 620.34] per 1,000,000 person-years. The overall mortality increased by 0.53% per year (95% CI: 0.40–0.67%; P<0.001), driven by the increasing non-neoplasm mortality of 3.15% per year (95% CI: 2.59–3.78%; P<0.001) despite declining MNDS-specific mortality (APC: −0.51%; 95% CI: −0.65% to −0.36%; P<0.001). Both sexes demonstrated similar mortality patterns. Colorectal MNs demonstrated a decline in MNDS-specific mortality (APC: −1.46%; 95% CI: −1.58 to −1.34%; P<0.001), but increasing non-neoplasm mortality (APC: 2.72%; 95% CI: 2.15–3.39%; P<0.001) led to overall mortality increase (APC: 0.25%; 95% CI: 0.05–0.47%; P=0.01). Meanwhile, patients with pancreatic, biliary, and anal MNs experienced mortality increases from both MNDS-specific and non-neoplasm CODs. Notably, medical complications displayed the fastest acceleration (APC, 8.58%; 95% CI: 4.97–14.17%; P<0.001) among non-neoplasm CODs. American Indian or Alaska Native was the only race with non-decreasing MNDS-specific mortality (APC: 0.63%; 95% CI: −0.56% to 1.95%; P=0.22) alongside the fastest-growing non-neoplasm mortality (APC: 4.23%; 95% CI: 3.21–5.52%; P<0.001). Localized-stage patients had the most rapid non-neoplasm mortality increase (APC: 6.42%; 95% CI: 5.60–7.43%; P<0.001) across tumor stages.ConclusionsNon-neoplasm diseases have become the dominant determinant of mortality increases in MNDS patients. This paradigm change requires transforming MNDS care to address comorbidities alongside cancer treatment, particularly for high-risk subgroups.

Highlight box

Key findings

• From 2007 to 2021, U.S. malignant neoplasms (MNs) of digestive system (MNDS) patients experienced rising overall mortality (0.53%/year) driven by non-neoplasm mortality (3.15%/year), offsetting declining MNDS-specific mortality (−0.51%/year). This pattern was consistent across sexes. Medical complications showed the fastest-increasing mortality among all non-neoplasm causes (8.58%/year).

• Non-neoplasm mortality increased significantly across all strata. Small intestine (non-significant), pancreatic, biliary, and anal MNs demonstrated rises in MNDS-specific mortality. While colorectal MNs had decreasing MNDS-specific mortality (−1.46%/year), rising non-neoplasm mortality (2.72%/year) led to increasing overall mortality (0.25%/year). American Indian or Alaska Native (AIAN) patients among all races faced the most rapid non-neoplasm mortality growth (4.23%/year) with non-decreasing MNDS-specific mortality (0.63%/year). Localized-stage patients exhibited the fastest-growing non-neoplasm mortality among all stages (6.42%/year).

What is known and what is new?

• MNDS survival has improved through screening and therapy advances, with disparities existing across clinical and demographic groups.

• We reported the comprehensive landscape of mortality trends in MNDS patients in the U.S. during the past 15 years. We first discovered that non-neoplasm causes now dominate mortality trends, with medical complications rising most rapidly. AIAN populations and patients with pancreatobiliary, small intestine, and anal MN represented the most vulnerable groups with increases in both MNDS-specific and non-neoplasm mortality.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Focus of MNDS should expand beyond cancer control to address the growing burden of comorbidities. Systematic comorbidity management should be integrated into MNDS survivorship programs. Policies should target vulnerable groups to reduce disparities in both MNDS and non-neoplasm outcomes.

Introduction

Malignant neoplasms (MNs) of digestive system (MNDS) constitute a major public health burden, representing a leading cause of mortality worldwide (1). In the U.S., these malignancies collectively account for the highest number of cancer deaths, with colorectal and pancreatic cancers ranking as the second and third most lethal cancers, respectively, in 2025 (2). A dynamic epidemiological landscape varying across clinical and demographic subgroups lies behind these static statistics.

Recent decades have witnessed divergent mortality trends among MNDS. While screening programs, risk factor reduction, and therapeutic advances have driven declining mortality rates for colorectal and gastric MNs, other malignancies, such as pancreatic and hepatic MNs have shown less favorable trajectories (3). Despite these overall trends, disparities across age, sex, race, and stage groups have been observed, though existing studies have largely focused on individual tumor types (4-7).

Furthermore, with improved prognosis for many cancers, a new challenge has emerged: competing mortality risks from non-neoplasm conditions, such as circulatory (8,9) and chronic liver disease (10). The coronavirus disease (COVID) pandemic further complicated this landscape, with evidence suggesting sharply increased mortality among cancer patients (11). While we increasingly recognize these trends, a comprehensive assessment of cause-specific mortality patterns in MNDS patients and their variations by demographic and clinical factors remains lacking.

To address these gaps, this study systematically evaluated the trends in age-adjusted incidence-based mortality rates from 2007 to 2021 among MNDS patients using data from 22 registries of the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results program (SEER22). We analyzed the overall and cause-specific mortality trends and stratified them by tumor site, age, sex, race, and tumor stage, with special emphasis on MNDS-specific and non-neoplasm mortality. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jgo.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jgo-2025-636/rc).

Methods

Data source and study population

This observational retrospective cohort study utilized National Cancer Institute (NCI)’s SEER database “Incidence-Based Mortality—SEER Research Limited-Field Data, 22 Registries (excl IL and MA), Nov 2023 Sub [2000–2021]”, which covered approximately 41.9% of the U.S. population based on 2020 census. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was exempt from ethical review by the Institutional Review Board of Affiliated Dongtai Hospital of Nantong University and informed consent was not required as the study utilized only publicly available, de-identified data from SEER database.

Patients diagnosed with MNDS [Behavior code International Classification of Diseases for Oncology third edition (ICD-O-3): Malignant; Site recode ICD-O-3 2023 Revision Expanded: Digestive System] in 2000–2021 who died in 2007–2021 were included.

The cohort was stratified by tumor site (esophagus, stomach, small intestine, colorectum, anus/anal canal/anorectum, liver, biliary system, pancreas, and others), sex (female, and male), age at death (<55, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, and 85+ years), race [White, Black, American Indian or Alaska Native (AIAN), Asian or Pacific Islander (API), and Hispanic], and tumor stage (localized, regional and distant).

Outcomes

Incidence-based mortality rates were calculated to link deaths to tumor diagnosis. All rates were adjusted by age based on the 2000 U.S. standard population and expressed as the number of cases per 1,000,000 persons per year. Within each stratified cohort, mortality rates were classified by cause of death (COD) into (I) MNDS, (II) non-digestive system MNs, (III) non-MNs, (IV) non-neoplasm diseases, and (V) unknown COD. In the overall cohort, non-neoplasm CODs were further subclassified according to “COD to site recode ICD-O-3 2023 Revision Expanded (1999+)” as septicemia, other infectious/parasitic diseases including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), COVID, diabetes mellitus, Alzheimer’s disease, circulatory disease, pneumonia/influenza, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and allied conditions, stomach/duodenal ulcers, chronic liver disease/cirrhosis, nephritis/nephrotic syndrome/nephrosis, complications of pregnancy/childbirth/puerperium, congenital anomalies, certain conditions originating in perinatal period, symptoms/signs/ill-defined conditions, accidents/adverse effects, suicide/self-inflicted injury, homicide/legal intervention, complications of medical/surgical care, and other COD. Annual percent changes (APCs) were calculated to describe trends of mortality rates for the period 2007–2021.

Statistical analysis

Age-adjusted incidence-based mortality rates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated in SEER*stat (version 8.4.4, NCI, Bethesda, Maryland, U.S.). Data processing and visualization were performed with Python (version 3.13.5). APCs were estimated in Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.3.0, NCI, Bethesda, Maryland, U.S.). The analyses were performed by fitting log-linear models via a weighted least squares method, assuming uncorrelated errors. The empirical quantile method was used to compute the 95% CIs for the APCs. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Overall and site-specific trend of mortality rate

During 1,994,617,152 person-years of observation from 2007 to 2021, 1,318,354 patients with MNDS experienced death, yielding an average overall mortality rate of 619.27 (95% CI: 618.2–620.34) per 1,000,000 person-years. The average and annual age-adjusted incidence-based mortality rates used in this study are provided in the Supplementary (Tables S1-S6).

The overall mortality rate per 1,000,000 person-years increased significantly from 590.22 (95% CI: 585.85–594.61) in 2007 to 645.85 (95% CI: 641.9–649.82) in 2021 with an APC of 0.53% (95% CI: 0.40–0.67%; P<0.001). While MNDS-specific mortality rate declined (APC: −0.51%; 95% CI: −0.65% to −0.36%; P<0.001), the most striking increase was observed in mortality rate due to non-neoplasm diseases (APC: 3.15%; 95% CI: 2.59–3.78%; P<0.001) which was the primary driver of the overall mortality rate increase during the study period (Figure 1A).

Trends in age-adjusted incidence-based mortality rates of patients with MNDS from 2007 to 2021, SEER22 [2000–2021], stratified by tumor sites (A-J), non-neoplasm diseases (K) and sex (L-M). APC, annual percent change; CI, confidence interval; COD, cause of death; COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; COVID, coronavirus disease; MNDS, malignant neoplasms of digestive system; NR, not reported; SEER, Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results.

Esophageal MNs exhibited reduced overall mortality rate (APC: −0.49%; 95% CI: −0.79% to −0.16%; P=0.005; Figure 1B), driven by the significant declines in MNDS-specific mortality rate (APC: −1.30%; 95% CI: −1.47% to −1.12%; P<0.001), which compensated for the increase in non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 2.96%; 95% CI: 1.77–4.34%; P<0.001). Gastric MNs showed the similar trend (overall, APC: −0.61%; 95% CI: −0.86% to −0.36%; P<0.001; MNDS-specific, APC: −1.74%; 95% CI: −2.06% to −1.41%; P<0.001; non-neoplasm, APC: 3.13%; 95% CI: 2.20–4.23%; P<0.001; Figure 1C). Overall mortality rates of other tumor sites increased significantly (Figure 1D−1J).

Although colorectal MNs also had a declining MNDS-specific mortality rate (APC: −1.46%; 95% CI: −1.58% to −1.34%; P<0.001), the sharp rise in non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 2.72%; 95% CI: 2.15–3.39%; P<0.001) offset these gains, resulting in a significant increase of overall mortality rate (APC: 0.25%; 95% CI: 0.05–0.47%; P=0.01; Figure 1D).

In patients with small intestine MNs, non-neoplasm mortality rate increased at the most rapid pace observed (APC: 6.76%; 95% CI: 5.92–7.80%; P<0.001) despite MNDS-specific mortality rate increased without statistical significance (APC: 0.61%; 95% CI: −0.26% to 1.55%; P=0.13; Figure 1E). MNDS-specific mortality rate of hepatic MNs decreased since 2015 (2007–2021 APC: 0.15%; 95% CI: −0.66% to 1.02%; P=0.65), while non-neoplasm mortality rate rose steadily (APC: 3.98%; 95% CI: 3.34–4.76%; P<0.001; Figure 1F).

Contrarily, both MNDS-specific (APC: 0.32%; 95% CI: 0.24–0.40%; P<0.001) and non-neoplasm mortality rates (APC: 3.39%; 95% CI: 2.14–4.79%; P<0.001) showed progressive increase among pancreatic MN patients (Figure 1G). This trend was also observed in anal (MNDS-specific, APC: 1.27%; 95% CI: 0.78–1.79%; P<0.001; non-neoplasm, APC: 5.57%; 95% CI: 4.49–6.92%; P<0.001; Figure 1H) and biliary MNs (MNDS-specific, APC: 1.80%; 95% CI: 1.54–2.08%; P<0.001; non-neoplasm, APC: 4.22%; 95% CI: 2.78–5.90%; P<0.001; Figure 1I).

Additionally, patients with other MNDS had the most rapid increase in overall (APC: 4.86%; 95% CI: 3.17–6.97%; P<0.001) and MNDS-specific mortality rates (APC: 4.96%; 95% CI: 3.32–7.01%; P<0.001; Figure 1J).

Non-neoplasm cause-specific trend of mortality rate

As increases in non-neoplasm mortality were observed across all tumor sites, we further focused on detailed non-neoplasm CODs. All non-neoplasm CODs had stable or increasing mortality rates (Figure 1K) except for other infectious diseases, which showed a significant decline (APC: −2.17%; 95% CI: −4.14% to −0.19%; P=0.03). However, emerging COVID had a mortality rate of 26.89 (95% CI: 26.08–27.72) per 1,000,000 person-years in 2020, which was responsible for the sharp increase in non-neoplasm mortality rate in 2019−2020 (Figure 1A).

Chronic diseases except for ulcers (APC: 1.77%; 95% CI: −1.10% to 5.09%; P=0.18) were increasing (Figure 1K), including circulatory disease (APC: 1.21%; 95% CI: 0.93–1.52%; P<0.001), diabetes (APC: 3.09%; 95% CI: 2.71–3.51%; P<0.001), Alzheimer’s disease (APC: 7.31%; 95% CI: 6.04–9.00%; P<0.001), COPD (APC: 0.87%; 95% CI: 0.04–1.79%; P=0.04), hepatic disease (APC: 4.36%; 95% CI: 3.76–5.06%; P<0.001) and renal disease (APC: 1.99%; 95% CI: 0.95–3.20%; P<0.001).

Medical complications had the fastest growing mortality rate observed (APC, 8.58%; 95% CI: 4.97–14.17%; P<0.001; Figure 1K). Septicemia (APC: 1.94%; 95% CI: 0.90–3.13%; P<0.001), accidents (APC: 4.69%; 95% CI: 3.75–5.81%; P<0.001), suicide (APC: 2.23%; 95% CI: 0.42–4.31%; P=0.02) and other COD (APC: 4.37%; 95% CI: 3.51–5.39%; P<0.001) also had increasing mortality rates.

Sex-specific trend of mortality rate

Both sexes experienced remarkably similar trends in mortality over time (Figure 1L,1M). In both groups, mortality rates due to non-neoplasms increased significantly from 2007 to 2021 (female, APC: 3.07%; 95% CI: 2.54–3.66%; P<0.001; male, APC: 3.11%; 95% CI: 2.52–3.77%; P<0.001), compensating for the decrease in those due to MNDS (female, APC: −0.47%; 95% CI: −0.61% to −0.33%; P<0.001; male, APC: −0.61%; 95% CI: −0.84% to −0.37%; P<0.001), leading to the overall increasing mortality rates (female, APC: 0.53%; 95% CI: 0.37–0.70%; P<0.001; male, APC: 0.47%; 95% CI: 0.31–0.64%; P<0.001).

Age-specific trend of mortality rate

Across all ages, patients aged <55 years was the only group showing improving in overall mortality rate (APC: −0.20%; 95% CI: −0.31% to −0.09%; P<0.001), owing to the decreasing MNDS-specific mortality rate (APC: −0.43%; 95% CI: −0.66% to −0.22%; P<0.001) although non-neoplasm mortality rate was increasing (APC: 1.52%; 95% CI: 0.74–2.34%; P<0.001; Figure 2A). While patients aged 55–64 showed increasing non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 3.14%; 95% CI: 2.29–4.13%; P<0.001), its effect was largely offset by stable MNDS-specific mortality rate (APC: −0.26%; 95% CI: −0.98% to 0.47%; P=0.44), yielding unchanged overall mortality rate (APC: 0.29%; 95% CI: −0.16% to 0.76%; P=0.17; Figure 2B).

APC, annual percent change; CI, confidence interval; COD, cause of death; MNDS, malignant neoplasms of digestive system; SEER, Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results.

Conversely, patients aged 65–74 years had increasing overall mortality rate (APC: 0.48%; 95% CI: 0.21–0.78%; P<0.001; Figure 2C) owing to rising non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 3.37%; 95% CI: 2.44–4.48%; P<0.001) despite a significant decline in MNDS-specific mortality rate (APC: −0.25%; 95% CI: −0.44% to −0.03%; P=0.03). These trends were similar in patients aged 75–84 (overall, APC: 0.38%; 95% CI: 0.17–0.60%; P<0.001; MNDS-specific, APC: −0.70%; 95% CI: −0.86% to −0.54%; P<0.001; non-neoplasm, APC: 2.68%; 95% CI: 2.03–3.42%; P<0.001; Figure 2D) and 85+ years (overall, APC: 1.36%; 95% CI: 1.17–1.57%; P<0.001; MNDS-specific, APC: −0.89%; 95% CI: −1.09% to −0.68%; P<0.001; non-neoplasm, APC: 3.72%; 95% CI: 3.20–4.34%; P<0.001; Figure 2E).

Race-specific trend of mortality rate

AIAN patients had a concerning trend, where MNDS-specific mortality rate remained non-significantly increasing (APC: 0.63%; 95% CI: −0.56% to 1.95%; P=0.22) but the non-neoplasm mortality rate increased at the highest APC observed of 4.23% (95% CI: 3.21–5.52%; P<0.001), driving the fastest increase in overall mortality rate (APC: 1.56%; 95% CI: 0.60–2.68%; P=0.002; Figure 2F).

In White patients, non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 3.26%; 95% CI: 2.73–3.88%; P<0.001) increased and resulted in the increase of overall mortality rate (APC: 0.71%; 95% CI: 0.60–0.83%; P<0.001), though MNDS-specific mortality rate was decreasing (APC: −0.39%; 95% CI: −0.52% to −0.25%; P<0.001; Figure 2G). The Hispanic patients showed a similar pattern (overall, APC: 0.64%; 95% CI: 0.36–0.96%; P<0.001; MNDS-specific, APC: −0.34%; 95% CI: −0.53% to −0.11%; P=0.003; non-neoplasm, APC: 3.93%; 95% CI: 2.74–5.47%; P<0.001; Figure 2H).

With a decreasing MNDS-specific mortality rate at −1.41% per year (95% CI: −1.57% to −1.23%; P<0.001) and an increasing non-neoplasm mortality rate at 3.12% per year (95% CI: 1.86–4.60%; P<0.001), the overall mortality rate of Black patients remained stable (APC: −0.15%; 95% CI: −0.53% to 0.26%; P=0.48; Figure 2I).

API patients had the slowest increase in non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 2.10%; 95% CI: 1.21–3.17%; P<0.001) and the fastest decrease in MNDS-specific mortality rate (APC: −1.50%; 95% CI: −1.83% to −1.14%; P<0.001), making them the only group with decreasing overall mortality rate (APC: −0.74%; 95% CI: −0.92% to −0.54%; P<0.001; Figure 2J).

Stage-specific trend of mortality rate

Patients with localized MNDS experienced an increasing MNDS-specific mortality rate of 0.74% per year (95% CI: 0.28–1.25%; P=0.002) and the most rapid acceleration in non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 6.42%; 95% CI: 5.60–7.43%; P<0.001) which became the dominant contributor for overall rate since 2015 (APC: 3.57%; 95% CI: 3.04–4.19%; P<0.001; Figure 2K).

Patients with regional MNDS presented a slower increasing overall mortality rate of 1.26% per year (95% CI: 0.82–1.75%; P<0.001), where the decline in MNDS-specific mortality rate after 2012 (2007–2021 APC: −0.35%; 95% CI: −0.89% to 0.24%; P=0.25) was offset by the continuously increasing non-neoplasm mortality rate (APC: 5.46%; 95% CI: 4.85–6.23%; P<0.001; Figure 2L).

Non-neoplasm mortality rates increased slowest in the distant-stage group (APC: 3.18%; 95% CI: 1.96–4.57%; P<0.001), where MNDS (APC: 0.30%; 95% CI: 0.09–0.52%; P=0.008) accounted for most of the mortality rate (APC: 0.61%; 95% CI: 0.48–0.74%; P<0.001; Figure 2M).

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of mortality trends among patients with MNDS in the U.S. during the past 15 years, revealing a critical transition in patterns of cause-specific deaths. While MNDS-specific mortality has declined, this progress has been offset by a sharp rise in mortality from non-neoplasm diseases, leading to a net increase in overall mortality. These trends were identical in both sexes but varied significantly by tumor site, age, race, and stage, highlighting disparities that warrant clinical and public health interventions.

Non-neoplasm mortality now drives the increase in overall mortality among MNDS patients, suggesting that while advancements in detection and treatment have reduced deaths directly attributed to MNDS, patients face heightened risks from comorbidities, particularly chronic conditions. These findings align with prior studies showing that cancer survivors increasingly die from non-cancer causes, which might be a metabolic syndrome from cancer-directed therapies (12). The prominent success in prolonging MNDS survival further contributed to this phenomenon which inadvertently created more population and time at risk for competing CODs. On the other hand, cancer diagnosis may also overshadow other medical concerns for patients and clinicians (13).

We first reported that the fastest mortality rise was from medical complications among all non-neoplasm CODs. This trend coincided with the general trend in the U.S. (14) but at a higher rate, further underscoring the vulnerability of the MNDS population. With the growing use of new drugs and techniques to MNDS, improving awareness of adverse events and medical errors is urgently required at the individual, organizational and national levels (15).

MNDS-specific mortality showed significant decrease in upper digestive tract MNs, likely reflecting the successful implementation of endoscopic screening strategies (16). Advances in perioperative chemotherapy (17,18) and refined surgical techniques (19,20) may have also collectively contributed to these declines. In colorectal MNs, the reduction in MNDS-specific mortality similarly underscored the widespread screening colonoscopy, which facilitates earlier diagnosis and dissection (21). Unfortunately, this benefit was completely counterbalanced by the rising non-neoplasm mortality, resulting in an increase overall mortality trend that represents a reversal from the previous decreasing trend before 2007 (22).

In contrast, pancreatobiliary MNs exhibited increasing MNDS-specific mortality, reflecting the persistent detection and therapeutic challenges (23,24). The rise in non-neoplasm mortality among these patients further compounds their poor prognosis, emphasizing the need to actively provide palliative care for select patients.

Intriguingly, the trend of mortality specific to MNDS in hepatic MNs demonstrated a distinct upward convex curve, a pattern that paralleled the trajectory observed in hepatitis C virus (HCV)-related hepatocellular carcinoma (25). The decline beginning in 2015 is likely attributable to advancements in antiviral therapy for HCV initiated in 2014 (26). Given that hepatitis virus infection remains the leading cause of hepatic MN-related death, enhancing hepatitis virus screening and expanding access to antiviral treatment for eligible patients (with and without hepatic MNs) is essential to sustain this promising decline in cancer-specific mortality.

Furthermore, we identified two rare malignancies, anal and small intestine MNs, that exhibited mortality trends different from other digestive tract MNs. The non-significant increase in small intestinal MN mortality may be attributed to a lack of routine screening methods (27) and limited systemic therapeutic options (28). Meanwhile, the rising trend in anal MNs aligned with another study in the U.S. population and is likely driven by human papillomavirus (HPV) infection (29). While HPV vaccination has reduced the incidence of premalignant lesions, this benefit may take years to translate into a reduction in mortality from anal MNs. Moreover, unlike other HPV-associated MNs, such as cervical cancer, screening programs are not widely implemented for anal MNs (30). For these rare MNDS, future efforts should focus on developing less invasive, cost-effective screening methods and facilitating their broader adoption.

The trends of declining MNDS-specific mortality but increasing non-neoplasm mortality in older patients (65+ years) reveal the growing burden of comorbidities in geriatric MNDS survivorship. As success has been made in their cancer treatment (31), tailored assessment and management of comorbidities must now be prioritized. Comprehensive therapy may be considered for extremely old patients (85+ years) as non-neoplasm diseases have become the major COD since 2017, indicating they may benefit less from cancer-directed therapy.

The mortality trend patterns in AIAN patients highlighted severe disparities in both cancer and non-cancer healthcare. Conversely, although API were considered a group with higher burden in MNDS, they were the only group with declining overall mortality from 2007 to 2021, attributed to both better cancer control and slower comorbidity growth, possibly due to higher socioeconomic status and active cultural attitudes toward preventive healthcare (32). The observed MNDS burden of AIAN versus API population further emphasizes the health inequity that demands systematic interventions (33).

Moreover, mortality trends in localized-stage patients suggested they had extremely good cancer treatment efficacy, but overall health risks might be inadequately addressed. Given the continuously improving cancer survival in early MNDS, particular attention should be paid to integrating cancer-specific surveillance with comorbidity management.

This study benefits from a large, population-based cohort with an extended observation period and detailed COD data, enabling robust stratification by demographic and clinical characteristics. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. A key limitation in interpreting these trends is the potential interdependence between non-neoplasm and MNDS-specific mortality, as conditions such as septicemia, peptic ulcer, and cirrhosis may develop as complications of cancer or as sequelae of its treatment. Consequently, some deaths classified under non-neoplasm diseases may partially represent indirect consequences of MNDS. However, diseases accounting for the highest mortality rates (circulatory diseases, COVID, COPD and Alzheimer’s disease) had weaker relation to MNDS themselves, suggesting that this bias may only have a modest effect on our main findings. Second, as with other SEER-based studies, potential misclassification and incomplete data may affect accuracy (34). Third, although SEER provides high-quality cancer data, it lacks other key determinants of cancer outcomes such as comorbidities, lifestyle factors, socioeconomic status and healthcare accessibility. Fourth, recent advancements in cancer therapy may not be fully reflected in the observed mortality patterns. Finally, while the U.S. represents a diverse population with the second largest cancer burden globally, generalizing these findings to other healthcare systems should be done cautiously, as variations in screening practices, treatment protocols, and comorbidity prevalence may lead to different trends elsewhere. Future research incorporating granular clinical and socioeconomic data, as well as contemporary treatment effects, will further refine our understanding of these evolving patterns.

As we continue to make progress against MNDS, we must ensure that the gains in cancer control translate into improvements in patients’ overall health. Future clinical strategies should integrate routine comorbidity assessment throughout management of MNDS, especially for high-risk groups. Health administrators should develop targeted initiatives to reduce disparities in vulnerable populations. Meanwhile, we encourage future MNDS studies to incorporate both oncologic and non-oncologic factors.

Conclusions

Using a nationwide database, this study discovered a recent shift in MNDS, in which non-neoplasm diseases have become the dominant determinant of mortality increase. Nevertheless, these findings must be interpreted with the recognition that non-neoplasm diseases are partially linked to the consequences of MNDS and its treatment. We identified distinct clinical and demographic subgroups with unique mortality patterns, revealing significant health disparities and previously unrecognized risks. Future focus of MNDS should expand beyond cancer control to address the growing burden of comorbidities.
