The CCL5/CCR5/SHP2 axis sustains Stat1 phosphorylation and activates NF-κB signaling promoting M1 macrophage polarization and exacerbating chronic prostatic inflammation
Authors: Chen Jin, Fei Zhang, Hailang Luo, Boyang Li, Xue Jiang, Christopher J. Pirozzi, Chaozhao Liang, Meng Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveChronic prostatitis (CP) is a condition markered by persistent prostate inflammation, yet the specific cytokines driving its progression remain largely undefined. This study aims to identify key cytokines involved in CP and investigate their role in driving inflammatory responses through mechanistic and therapeutic exploration.MethodsA 48-cytokine panel test was conducted to compare the plasma cytokine profiles between participants with CP-like symptoms (CP-LS) and healthy controls. Experimental autoimmune prostatitis (EAP) models were used for functional validation, with further mechanistic studies performed through in vivo and in vitro assays. Pharmacological inhibition was applied using maraviroc, and pathway inhibitors to assess therapeutic potential.ResultsOur analysis identified CCL5 as one of the most prominently elevated cytokines in CP-LS patients. Further validation in the EAP model mice confirmed elevated CCL5 levels, highlighting its role in driving prostatic inflammation. Mechanistic studies revealed that CCL5 interacts with the CCR5 receptor, promoting M1 macrophage polarization and activating key inflammatory signaling pathways, including Stat1 and NF-κB, as indicated by increased phosphorylation of Stat1 and p65. In vitro, CCL5 combined with LPS stimulation amplified these effects, further promoting M1 polarization. CCL5 also sustained Stat1 activation by inhibiting its dephosphorylation through reduced interaction with SHP2, leading to prolonged inflammatory signaling. Single-cell transcriptomics confirmed high CCR5 expression in macrophages, correlating with inflammatory pathways. Pharmacological inhibition of CCR5, or its downstream signaling, significantly reduced macrophage-driven inflammation both in vivo and in vitro.ConclusionThese findings establish the CCL5/CCR5 axis as a critical driver of persistant prostatic inflammation and present it as a potential therapeutic target for CP.Graphic AbstractThe graphic abstract illustrates the central role of the CCL5/CCR5 axis in promoting chronic prostatitis progression. The interaction also inhibits SHP2-mediated Stat1 dephosphorylation, sustaining prolonged inflammatory signaling. Pharmacological inhibition of CCR5 or its downstream pathways attenuates macrophage-driven inflammation, highlighting this axis as a critical driver and potential therapeutic target for chronic prostatitis. Introduction

Chronic prostatitis (CP), characterized by persistent inflammation of the prostate gland, frequently leads to discomfort, urinary complications, and a diminished quality of life [1, 2]. Despite extensive research efforts, the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underpinning chronic prostatitis have been only partially elucidated. Its persistent and nonhealing nature presents a significant challenge for both clinicians and patients, highlighting the need for continued investigative efforts into its molecular and cellular dynamics.

Increasing evidence suggests that immune dysfunction is the pathological foundation for the onset and progression of CP [3, 4]. Most studies have reported that the differentiation of CD4 + T cells toward Th17 and Th1 subsets is a major factor in the pathogenesis of CP. However, a recent study revealed that blocking macrophage-mediated TNF can reduce prostate inflammation and epithelial hyperplasia [5], highlighting the crucial role of macrophages in prostate immunity and the progression of benign diseases.

Cytokines play pivotal roles in modulating immune cell function, often creating a feedback loop that perpetuates immune dysregulation and drives chronic inflammation [6]. Many studies have identified key cytokines, such as CXCL10 [7], CXCL12 [8], CCL2 [9], CCL3 [9], GM-CSF [10], and TNF-α [11], as critical contributors to CP by promoting immune cell recruitment and differentiation. However, comprehensive, systematic studies that screen for cytokines associated with the occurrence and progression of CP are lacking. To address this gap, we conducted extensive profiling of 48 cytokines in the plasma of participants with chronic prostatitis-like symptoms (CP-LS) and healthy controls (HCs), aiming to clarify the roles of these key cytokines in the pathogenesis of CP. We identified one of the predominant elevated cytokines, CCL5, a potent chemoattractant crucial for the recruitment and activation of various leukocytes at sites of inflammation [12–16]. In general, CCL5 is secreted by lymphocytes to promote the homing of CCR5-expressing CD4 + T cells to sites of inflammation, where it exerts pro-inflammatory effects [17]. Recent studies have shown that CCL5 can also promote the M1 macrophage phenotype, while targeting its receptor CCR5 or neutralizing CCL5 promotes the M2 phenotype [12, 18].

Given that immune dysregulation in the prostate microenvironment is characterized by abnormal proportions of Th1 [7, 19–21], Th17 [19, 21–24], and M1 [25, 26] proinflammatory cells, we investigated the role of the CCL5/CCR5 axis in the progression of prostatitis and identified key target cells via an experimental autoimmune prostatitis (EAP) mouse model. We found that CCL5 exacerbates prostatic inflammation in this murine model by promoting M1 macrophage infiltration rather than Th1 and Th17 cell infiltration. Mechanistically, CCL5 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway and inhibits Stat1 dephosphorylation in macrophages, thereby sustaining the proinflammatory phenotype. Notably, treatment with maraviroc [27–29], a CCR5 inhibitor, effectively reversed these pathological changes, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the CCL5/CCR5 axis.

Materials and methods

Participant recruitment and sample collection

Male patients under the age of 35 diagnosed with CP-LS, along with age-matched HCs, were recruited. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the hospital's ethics committee on June 10, 2020 (PJ-2020–07–11), with the informed consent from all participants. Plasma samples from 24 CP-LS patients and 9 HCs were collected at the Urology Outpatient Clinic of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University from July 2020 to April 2021, and processed as previously described [30]. Briefly, blood was collected in EDTA-containing tubes, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3–5 min, and the supernatant was stored at a −80°C freezer for future use. All CP-LS patients had undergone at least two types of pharmacotherapy, including antibiotics, anti-inflammatory agents, alpha-blockers, and Chinese patent drugs, without success. None of the participants had a history of fever, cold, or urinary tract infections in the past two weeks.

Inflammatory cytokine measurements

The collected plasma was thawed and diluted fourfold. Fifty µl of the diluted sample was used for cytokine measurement via a 48-plex Human Cytokine Screening Panel in a 1 × 96-well plate following the manufacturer’s instructions (Bio-Rad, Cat. # 12007283). The plate was read on a Luminex detector (Luminex Corporation, X-200). Data acquisition and analysis were conducted via Milliplex Analyst (Milliplex, Version 5.1.0.0 standard), generating an output file in Excel. The representative assay working ranges, assay sensitivities, and precisions are described in detail by Jiang et al. [31].

Antibodies and reagents

The reagents and antibodies used in the experiments (see in Table S1 and Table S2), along with their manufacturer instructions.

EAP induction and behavioral tests

Male NOD (non-obese diabetic) mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 20–25 g, were obtained from the Nanjing Biomedical Research Institute, Nanjing University (China). They were housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) facilities. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval no. LLSC20241750).

In accordance with previous methods [32], prostate tissue from Sprague‒Dawley (SD) rats was mixed evenly with Complete Freund’s Adjuvant (CFA) to generate prostate antigen (PAg). Mice were immunized with 200 µl of PAg (0.3 g/mouse) or saline solution subcutaneously. The injections were administered at six various sites per mouse, including the sides of hindfootpad, lower abdomen, and tail root. The mice were immunized on days 0 and 28, and sacrificed on day 42. Recombinant murine CCL5 was intravenously injected every day at a dose of 4 μg/kg, and maraviroc and JSH-23 [33–35] were intraperitoneally injected at doses of 20 mg/kg and 2 mg/kg, respectively, starting on day 28.

Prior to sacrifice, a behavioral test was conducted to assess pelvic pain resulting from EAP and reagent administration via the von Frey method, which is consistent with previous studies [36]. A series of forces (0.04, 0.16, 0.40, 1, and 4 g) were applied to the lower abdomen of each mouse 10 times. The responses were categorized into three specific behaviors: (i) jumping, (ii) immediate licking or scratching to the tested area, and (iii) lower abdomen retraction. The response rate was recorded as the number of positive responses divided by 10.

Histopathological examination

After fixation in 4% formaldehyde solution for 24 h, prostate tissues were processed by paraffin embedding and cut into 0.5 µm sections. For pathological analysis, the tissues were subjected to hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining. The severity of inflammation was evaluated via established criteria [37]. The grading of histopathological inflammation ranged from 0 to 3: 0, with no inflammatary cells within glandular stromal region; 1, mild, well-defined perivascular infiltration of mononuclear cells; 2, moderate perivascular infiltration of mononuclear cells; and 3, severe perivascular infiltration, hemorrhage, and substantial infiltration of mononuclear cells in the parenchyma.

Immunohistochemistry

Prostate tissue sections were incubated with an anti-CCL5 antibody (1:200, 4°C, 12–16 h), an anti-phospho-p65 antibody (1:100, 4°C, 12–16 h), or an anti-phospho-Stat1 antibody (1:100, 4°C, 12–16 h) and incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:400, RT, 1 h). The immune complexes were further stained with DAB (Solarbio, Cat# DA1016). The tissues were subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin and bluing buffer, dehydrated and mounted on coverslips. Representative images were taken via a digital pathological slide scanner (KF-PRO-005, KFBIO/Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH). For the quantification of immunostaining, the H-score system was used. The staining intensity was multiplied (scored as 0 for no, 1 for weak, 2 for moderate, and 3 for intense staining) by the percentage of glandular stromal region cells (ranging from 0–100%).

Immunofluorescence

Prostate tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by boiling in 0.01 M citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) for 40 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 20 min. Following permeabilization and blocking, the slides were incubated in a moist chamber at 4°C 12–16 h with various primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-CCR5 (1:100) in combination with rabbit anti-iNOS (1:100) to specifically label M1 macrophages (CCR5 + iNOS +) and with rabbit anti-CD68 (1:1000) to label general macrophage populations (CCR5 + CD68 +). The slides were subsequently incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. Costaining was subsequently conducted via TYR-520 (1:1000) and TYR570 (1:500) via tyramide signal amplification (TSA) technology. Then, the slides were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) and then mounted under anti-faded coverslips. Representative images showing the colocalization of iNOS with other biomarkers were collected with a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic MIDI, 3DHISTECH).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Plasma was obtained from the peripheral blood of the mice following the previously described procedure. Plasma cytokine levels in immunized mice were measured using a specific ELISA kit for CCL5 (Elabscience, Cat# E-EL-M0009), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions derived from mouse spleens were prepared according to our previous study [22, 38, 39]. To assess immune cell subsets relevant to our findings, we stained Th1 cells with CD3-conjugated FITC (0.5 μg/test), CD4-conjugated APC (0.25 μg/test), and IFN-γ (0.25 μg/test); Th17 cells with CD3-conjugated FITC (0.5 μg/test), CD4-conjugated APC (0.25 μg/test), and IL-17 (0.25 μg/test); and M1 macrophages with CD11b-conjugated FITC (0.25 μg/test), F4/80-conjugated APC (1.00 μg/test), and CD86-conjugated PE (1.00 μg/test). All staining were performed for 1 h at 4°C. The stained cells were captured via a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), and the data were processed with CytExpert 2.0 (Beckman Coulter).

RNA extraction and RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from prostate tissues via TRIzol (Cat# T9424, Sigma‒Aldrich), and libraries were prepared following the Illumina standard protocol (Cat# NR604-01/02). An Agilent 4200 bioanalyzer was used to access the concentration and size distribution of the cDNA library prior to sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. High-throughput sequencing was carried out following the manufacturer's instructions (Illumina). Raw reads were filtered using Seqtk and mapped to the genome with HISAT2 (v2.0.4) [40]. Gene fragments were quantified via stringtie (v1.3.3b) [41, 42] followed by normalization with the trimmed mean of M values (TMM) method. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using limma [43], with a fold change threshold of > 2, chosen to capture biologically meaningful changes in gene expression, and a significance level of P-value < 0.05 to ensure statistical robustness. The results of the differential gene analysis were visualized via volcano plots generated with ggplot2 [44], and key genes from the selected pathways were visualized via heatmaps created with the pheatmap [45] package. For pathway analysis, the GSVA [46] package was used to perform gene set variation analysis (GSVA), which allowed us to assess pathway activity differences between the groups.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Cat# 10564011; Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat# 26140079; Thermo Fisher) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Cat# 15140122; Thermo Fisher). Mycoplasma contamination was monitored every two months via a Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Cat# rep-mysnc-50; InvivoGen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All experiments were conducted within ten cell passages, ensuring that the cells were maintained in the logarithmic growth phase.

Quantitative reverse transcription (qRT)-PCR

RAW264.7 cells were plated at a density of 0.8 to 1.2 × 106 cells per well in laminin-coated 6-well plates. After allowing the cells to adhere for 2 h, they were treated with the indicated doses and durations of the reagents. Total RNA was extracted via an RNA extraction kit (Cat# R1055; ZYMO RESEARCH) following the instruction of manufacturer. The RNA concentration and A260/A280 ratio were measured via a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher). RNA samples with a concentration greater than 50 ng/µL and an A260/280 ratio of ≥ 1.9 were then reverse-transcribed into cDNA (Cat# 4368814; Thermo Fisher). Real-time quantitative PCR was subsequently conducted via the gene-specific qPCR primers listed in Table S3, along with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Cat# 11184ES08; YEASEN) on a QuantStudio 3 (Thermo Fisher). The housekeeping gene Actb was employed as the internal control for normalization of gene expression.

Western blot and immunoprecipitation

For the western blotting, cell pellets were washed once with cold PBS, and lysed on ice for 30 min in RIPA buffer (Cat# R0278; Sigma‒Aldrich) supplemented with phosphatase and protease inhibitor (Cat# 1861281; Thermo Fisher). The lysates were sonicated on ice twice and subsequently cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The collected supernatant was combined with loading buffer (Cat# 161074; Bio-Rad), boiled for 5–7 min, and loaded onto a sodium dodecyl sulfate‒polyacrylamide (SDS‒PAGE) gel for electrophoresis.

For the immunoprecipitation, approximately 5 × 107 cells were lysed in 2 ml NP40 lysis buffer (0.5% NP-40, 50 mM Tris–HCL pH = 8.0, 5 mM EDTA, 250 mM NaCl). After sonication and centrifugation, 5% of the supernatant was collected as the input sample. The remaining supernatant was pre-cleared with 25–30 μL of protein A/G agarose beads (Cat# HY-K0202, MedChemExpress) for 30 min at 4°C with gentle rotation. The supernatant was then equally transferred into the new tubes and incubated with a specific antibody or control rabbit/mouse IgG 12–16 h at 4°C under rotation. Next day, 25 μL of fresh beads were added to each tube to bind the antibody–antigen complex for 2–4 h at 4°C. After four washes with NP40 lysis buffer containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors, the immunoprecipitates combined with loading were boiled for 5–7 min and subsequently subjected to SDS‒PAGE.

The proteins separated by gel electrophoresis were transferred onto Immun-Blot PVDF membranes (Cat# 1620177; Bio-Rad). The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C for 12–16 h on a shaking platform. After the membranes were washed with TBST, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added at dilutions ranging from 1:7000 to 1:10000. Protein detection was performed using Pierce ECL substrate (Cat# 21,050; Thermo Fisher) or SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Cat# 34095; Thermo Fisher) and visualized with an iBright imager (Thermo Fisher).

ISRE-GFP reporter assays and IncuCyte monitoring

ISRE-GFP reporter-expressing cells were generated by transducing RAW264.7 cells with ISRE-GFP lentivirus (Cat# LVP937-P, GenTarget) at a multiplicity of infection of 1–2 in a laminin-coated 24-well plate. After 24 h of transduction, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh DMEM. The transduced cells were then selected with puromycin (Cat# P8833; Millipore Sigma) at a concentration of 2 µg/mL for several days until all nontransduced cells died. The surviving cells were subsequently maintained in 1 µg/mL puromycin. The selected RAW264.7 cells were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 2,500–4,000 cells per well.

The reagents were added according to the specified conditions, and the cells were incubated in the IncuCyte system, which was set to calculate the GFP signal area, mean signal intensity, and cell confluence per well on an 8-h scanning interval to monitor GFP signaling. We summarized the recorded total integrated intensity (expressed as GCU µM2 per image), which was calculated by multiplying the mean signal intensity by the signal area, and then dividing by the degree of cell confluence. This value was normalized to that of day 0 and the untreated control group to quantify the activation of the Stat1 signaling. Representative images were also captured and downloaded from the IncuCyte system.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were conducted at least twice independently. The number of mice utilized for in vivo experiments are indicated in the figure legend and statistical analyses included independent-samples t tests, single-factor repeated-measures ANOVA, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons, and two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons to evaluate differences across groups. Visual representations of the data were created via GraphPad Prism (v9.0) and R software (v4.0.2).

Results

CCL5 expression is upregulated in CP-LS patients and EAP mice

To pinpoint the crucial cytokines in CP, we comprehensively analyzed the expression of 48 cytokines in 24 CP-LS patients compared with nine healthy controls via the Human Cytokine Screening Panel (Table 1, and Table S4). This panel was chosen as it is a high-performance, broad screening panel capability, including a biologically relevant set of adaptive immunity, pro-inflammatory, and anti-inflammatory cytokines. As indicated in Fig. 1A and B, key cytokines, such as RANTES/CCL5, IL-5, IL-9, IL-17, MIG, GRO-alpha, HGF, MIP-1 beta, IL-4, and PDGF-BB, exhibited significantly different expression levels between the HCs and CP-LS groups. Among the analyzed markers, CCL5 presented the highest plasma abundance in the CP-LS group. Moreover, CCL5 expression demonstrated the strongest correlation with the earlier onset of CP-LS (r = 0.5412, P-value = 0.0063) (Fig. 1C, S1). Despite these findings, the role of CCL5 in CP remains underexplored. We employed an EAP mouse model to investigate the function of CCL5 in CP pathogenesis and progression. Consistent with the patient data, CCL5 expression was markedly elevated in the plasma of EAP mice compared with control mice (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, intense CCL5 staining in the prostatic interstitium indicated obvious upregulation of CCL5 expression in the EAP model compared with control mice (Fig. 1E). These findings indicate a potential correlation between elevated CCL5 levels and the development of CP. CCL5 exacerbates EAP progression through eliciting M1 macrophage infiltration

To study the role of CCL5 in CP, we administered mouse recombinant CCL5 (mCCL5) to healthy control and EAP mice (Fig. 2A). Histopathological and behavioral results revealed that mCCL5 treatment resulted in increased immune cell infiltration and higher response frequency (reflects pain severity) in the EAP groups compared to untreated EAP and control groups (one-way ANOVA, P-value < 0.05; Fig. 2B‒D). Furthermore, IHC staining revealed increased CCL5 expression in the prostatic interstitium among the EAP and EAP with CCL5 treated mice (Fig. 2E). Quantitative analysis of the staining intensity revealed notable upregulation of CCL5, which was correlated with inflammatory infiltration (Fig. S2A). On the basis of the reported pivotal role of CCL5 in immune cell regulation, we performed flow cytometry on spleen tissues from mice to identify potential proinflammatory cells affected by CCL5. Interestingly, mCCL5 did not increase the proportion of proinflammatory CD4 + lymphocytes (CD3 + CD4 +), including the Th17 (CD3 + CD4 + IL-17 +) and Th1 (CD3 + CD4 + IFN-γ +) subtypes (Fig. 2F-K). Similarly, the proportion of macrophages (F4/80 + CD11b +) remained consistent across all groups (Fig. 2L-M). Notably, the proportion of M1 macrophages (F4/80 + CD11b + CD86 +) increased in the spleens of EAP mice following mCCL5 treatment, an effect that was less pronounced in control mice (Fig. 2N-O). This phenotype was further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining of prostate tissue (Fig. S2B). These findings indicate that CCL5 increases M1 macrophage infiltration in EAP mice. We next sought to determine whether it could be a driving cause of CP progression.

Blocking CCR5 diminishes EAP prostatic inflammation through inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization

CCR5 is the receptor with the highest affinity for CCL5. The CCL5‒CCR5 axis is typically associated with a proinflammatory phenotype in the context of autoimmune diseases and chronic inflammation. To investigate whether the increased proinflammatory phenotype associated with M1 macrophages mediated by CCL5 in the EAP model is due to its interaction with CCR5, we first reanalyzed the distribution of Ccr5 expression across various cell subsets within the mouse prostate via exploring single-cell transcriptomic data. Our analysis revealed significant infiltration of macrophages and T cells in both adult and older mice, providing a representative profile of the prostate immune environment (Fig. 3A, see Online Materials and Methods). As shown in Fig. 3B, Ccr5 is predominantly expressed on monocytes/macrophages and T lymphocytes, with higher expression in macrophages than in T lymphocytes. Figure 3C shows UMAP density plots of Ccl5, Ccr5, T lymphocytes (Cd3d, Cd3e), and macrophages (Cd68, Cd86, Cd163), illustrating their expression and distribution. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that CCR5 is expressed on iNOS + macrophages in the EAP model, with a greater proportion observed in the mCCL5-treated EAP mouse group, compared with control set (Fig. 3D). To further elucidate the link between the CCL5/CCR5 axis function and the M1 macrophage phenotype, we stimulated RAW264.7 cells with LPS in vitro to simulate the M1 proinflammatory phenotype observed in EAP followed by mCCL5 treatment. We found that mCCL5 alone had a minimal effect on the expression of M1 marker genes, including Nos2, Tnf, and Il1b (Fig. S3A-B). However, under costimulation with LPS, mCCL5 significantly upregulated the transcription of Nos2, Tnf, and Il1b in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3E). Additionally, iNOS protein expression increased under the same costimulation conditions with mCCL5 and LPS (Fig. 3F). Further functional experiments confirmed that blocking CCR5 with maraviroc, a known CCR5 receptor antagonist, attenuated the mCCL5-mediated upregulation of iNOS at both the RNA and protein levels, as well as the transcriptional levels of Tnf and Il1b (Fig. 3G-H). These findings are consistent with the in vivo results, suggesting that CCL5 enhances, rather than initiates, the proinflammatory response in the EAP model. Additionally, the increased M1 macrophage phenotype is attributed to the CCL5/CCR5 axis promoting M1 macrophage polarization.

Therefore, we further administrated maraviroc, in the in vivo study. As expected, maraviroc treatment significantly reduced inflammation, as well as the pain response rate, in the EAP groups compared with both the untreated and mCCL5-treated EAP groups (Fig. 3I-K), These results, along with earlier findings, indicate that CCL5 aggravates EAP progression through its high-affinity interaction with CCR5. Next, we investigated the impact of blocking CCR5 on CCL5-mediated M1 macrophage infiltration. As shown in Fig. 3L-M, maraviroc treatment reduced the proportion of M1 macrophages (F4/80 + CD11b + CD86 +) in the spleen compared with both the mCCL5-treated EAP and untreated EAP mouse groups but did not influence the proportion of M0 macrophages (F4/80 + CD11b) (Fig. S3C-D). Consistent results were observed via prostate immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3N), indicating a reduction in local proinflammatory status in the prostate following CCR5 blockade.

Taken together, these results underscore the critical role of the CCR5 receptor in mediating prostatic inflammation. Inhibiting CCR5 with maraviroc effectively reversed the prostatic inflammation and M1 macrophage polarization induced by mCCL5 administration, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

In addition to NF-κB, the CCL5/CCR5 axis also engages Stat1 signaling, which further drives EAP inflammation. As aforementioned, CCL5 appears to prolong the activated Stat1 signaling. Therefore, we applied an IFN-stimulated response element (ISRE)-GFP lentivirus to transduce RAW264.7 cells, enabling us to monitor the status of Stat1 signaling. After adding IFN-γ to stimulate Stat1 signaling, we used IncuCyte to detect the GFP signal every 8 h. Analysis revealed that mCCL5 enhanced IFN-γ-mediated GFP expression (Fig. 6G-I) at low levels of IFN-γ stimulation (≤ 1 ng/ml) (Fig. S6C-D), which was reversed by maraviroc and fludarabine [47–50] (a Stat1 phosphorylation inhibitor) (Fig. 6J-L). Furthermore, qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that both maraviroc and fludarabine reduced the mCCL5-enhanced expression of iNOS and IRF1 at both the RNA and protein levels (Fig. 6L-M). The decreased phosphorylation levels of p65 and Stat1 further confirmed the reduced activation of the Stat1 and NF-κB signaling pathways following maraviroc treatment (Fig. 6N).

Many studies have explored the role of the NF-κB pathway in chronic prostatitis (CP), but the activation mechanism of Stat1 remains underreported. It is possible that the sustained phosphorylation of Stat1 is influenced by the CCL5/CCR5 axis, either by inhibiting phosphatase binding to Stat1 or promoting kinase interactions. We examined the correlation between phosphatase mRNA expression (Dusp18, Socs3, Ptpn1, Ptpn11, Ptpn2, Pias1, Ptpn6, Socs1, Ptp4a1, Ppp1ca, Ptpn61, Ptpn12) and IFN-γ pathway activity scores. The results revealed that Ppp1ca and Ptpn11 were highly expressed and negatively correlated with pathway activation (Fig. 6E). Given the importance of SHP2 (encoded by Ptpn11) in prostate diseases [51–54], we performed co-immunoprecipitation experiments, which showed that mCCL5 treatment inhibits the interaction between SHP2 and Stat1 in RAW264.7 cells after LPS stimulation (Figs. 6O, S6F). These findings suggest that the sustained phosphorylation of Stat1 mediated by the CCL5/CCR5 axis may result from a reduced interaction between SHP2 and Stat1. Together, our study revealed the critical role of the CCL5/CCR5 axis in promoting chronic prostatitis progression. We demonstrated that CCL5, which is secreted within the prostate microenvironment, binds to CCR5 on macrophages, activating both the NF-κB and Stat1 signaling pathways, which in turn promotes the M1 macrophage phenotype. Notably, inhibiting the CCL5/CCR5 axis, blocking Stat1, or blocking NF-kB signaling effectively reduced inflammation, suggesting a potential therapeutic approach for treating chronic prostatitis. Discussion

Chronic prostatitis remains a significant clinical challenge because of its complex etiology, high recurrence rates, and limited efficacy of current treatment options [55, 56]. Despite its widespread prevalence, the underlying mechanisms driving CP pathogenesis, particularly persistent inflammation and immune dysregulation, remain poorly understood. While cytokines are thought to play pivotal roles in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment of the CP, previous studies have focused largely on isolated markers [22, 57, 58]. These studies fail to provide a comprehensive understanding of the critical cytokines that drive disease progression. In our study, we aimed to bridge this gap by conducting a systematic analysis of 48 common cytokines in participants with CP-LS and healthy controls. Our results revealed that CCL5 is one of the most significantly elevated cytokines in CP-LS patients, with the highest abundance among the analyzed cytokines, and is strongly correlated with disease progression, suggesting its central role in CP pathogenesis.

CCL5, a chemokine originally described as RANTES [59, 60], is predominantly produced by CD8 + T cells [61], epithelial cells [62], fibroblasts [63] and platelets [64]. It has been implicated in various inflammatory disorders [65–69], yet its role in chronic prostatitis has received limited attention. We found that CCL5 exerts its proinflammatory effects predominantly through its interaction with CCR5, its primary receptor, which is highly expressed on immune cells such as macrophages and T cells. In our EAP mouse model, CCL5 was significantly elevated in both plasma and prostate tissues, mimicking the cytokine profile observed in CP-LS patients. Notably, the administration of mCCL5 increased immune cell infiltration and M1 macrophage polarization in EAP mice without affecting Th1 or Th17 cell populations. This selective effect on macrophage polarization underscores the central role of M1 macrophages in aggravating inflammation in CP. Importantly, blocking CCR5 with maraviroc significantly reduced M1 macrophage polarization and alleviated prostatic inflammation in EAP model mice. These findings indicate that the CCL5/CCR5 axis is a critical mediator of CP progression.

One of the key findings of our study is the dual activation of the NF-κB and Stat1 signaling pathways by the CCL5/CCR5 axis. The NF-κB pathway is well established as a key regulator of inflammation [70–75], and our data demonstrate that CCL5 amplifies inflammation in CP primarily through NF-κB-mediated M1 macrophage polarization. This is supported by the significant reduction in prostatic inflammation and alleviation of pelvic pain following treatment with maraviroc or with the NF-κB inhibitor JSH-23. Flow cytometry and histological analysis confirmed reduced M1 macrophage infiltration in prostate and spleen tissues, reinforcing the central role of NF-κB in macrophage-driven immune activation. In addition to its role in NF-κB, our study revealed the critical role of Stat1 in CP progression. CCL5’s ability to sustain Stat1 phosphorylation through the inhibition of SHP2-mediated dephosphorylation represents a novel mechanism by which the CCL5/CCR5 axis prolongs the inflammatory response. Prolonged Stat1 activation plays a significant role in maintaining the proinflammatory phenotype of M1 macrophages [76–80], as demonstrated by enhanced IFN-γ-induced Stat1 signaling following mCCL5 treatment. Inhibition of Stat1 with fludarabine significantly reduced the expression of key inflammatory mediators, such as iNOS and IRF1, further confirming the role of Stat1 in CP pathogenesis. These findings imply that targeting the CCL5/CCR5 signaling axis has substantial therapeutic potential, particularly in diseases characterized by chronic macrophage activation, such as CP, where traditional anti-inflammatory treatments often fail to prevent relapse.

The therapeutic implications of our findings are substantial. Originally developed for HIV treatment, maraviroc has shown promise in modulating immune responses across various inflammatory and autoimmune conditions. Our study expands its applicability to CP, where maraviroc effectively disrupts the CCL5/CCR5 axis, thereby reducing M1 macrophage polarization and alleviating prostatic inflammation. Similarly, targeting the NFκB and Stat1 signaling pathways yielded comparable anti-inflammatory effects, highlighting these pathways as potential molecular vulnerabilities in CP that could be leveraged for therapeutic purposes. However, while our study provides critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying CP, certain limitations must be acknowledged. One limitation is that this study primarily focuses on the cytokine profile of young adults. Although this group represents the population with the highest incidence of CP-LS, it does not fully capture the progression of the disease. Supplementing the study with data from middle-aged CP patients would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying etiology of CP. Another limitation is the use of murine models, which, while informative, may not fully capture the complexity of human CP, particularly in terms of species-specific differences in immune system dynamics, hormonal influences, and cytokine profiles. These variations suggest that further research is needed to validate our findings in clinical settings. Additionally, the roles of other cytokines and immune cell types in CP progression, as well as interactions between key signaling pathways, such as NF-κB and Stat1, warrant further exploration to better understand the intricate regulatory networks driving CP. Future studies should focus on validating these therapeutic strategies in clinical trials while also investigating the broader cytokine and signaling landscapes involved in CP pathogenesis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the crucial role of the CCL5/CCR5 axis in promoting chronic prostatic inflammation progression through the dual activation of the NF-κB and Stat1 signaling pathways. Targeting this axis, particularly through CCR5, NF-κB and Stat1 inhibition, is a promising therapeutic strategy for managing CP. By advancing our understanding of CP progression, these findings pave the way for the development of more effective, multitargeted therapies that may provide long-lasting relief for patients suffering from this debilitating condition.
