Genetic Determinants of Left Atrial Function Are Associated With Stroke
Authors: Oliver B Vad, Christian Paludan‐Müller, Søren Z Diederichsen, Emily A McKaige, Laia M Monfort, Pia R Lundegaard, Litten Bertelsen, Laura Andreasen, Jesper H Svendsen, Morten S Olesen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile atrial fibrillation (AF) is a key risk factor for stroke, recent studies have suggested that atrial cardiomyopathy may also increase stroke risk in the absence of AF. This study aimed to evaluate genetic determinants for left atrial (LA) function and volumes and risk of stroke.Methods and ResultsPolygenic scores (PGSs) for LA measures were constructed and used in 380 017 individuals in the UK Biobank, without history of ischemic stroke, systemic arterial embolism, or AF at enrollment. Risk of ischemic stroke was estimated using time‐to‐event models, considering all‐cause death and incident AF as competing events. Systemic arterial embolism was investigated as a secondary end point. The PGS for LA passive emptying fraction was associated with risk of ischemic stroke. Individuals with a PGS for LA passive emptying fraction in the lowest quartile had a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.12 for ischemic stroke compared with those in the top quartile (P=0.004). These associations were robust in a subgroup free of prevalent cardiovascular disease at baseline (HR for lowest quartile versus top quartile, 1.22; P<0.001). No associations were found for 4 other PGSs for LA measures or for risk of systemic arterial embolism. Two‐sample Mendelian randomization was conducted between LA measures and cardioembolic stroke and indicated a protective effect of higher LA functional measurements.ConclusionsThe PGS for LA passive emptying fraction was significantly associated with incident ischemic stroke when considering AF as a competing risk. Consistent results were found in Mendelian randomization analyses. These findings suggest that atrial dysfunction may influence stroke risk independently of AF.Keywords: atrial myopathy, genetics, ischemic stroke, Mendelian randomization, polygenic riskSubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Ischemic Stroke, Genetics

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Stroke is a major cause of death and disability worldwide, 1 with the majority of strokes being of ischemic pathogenesis. 2 The incidence of cardioembolic stroke (CES), a type of ischemic stroke associated with more severe outcomes, is expected to increase several‐fold in the coming decades. 3 Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, is often attributed as a key risk factor for CESs and often presents with incident stroke or transient ischemic attack. 2 However, the LOOP (Implantable Loop Recorder Detection of Atrial Fibrillation to Prevent Stroke) study, a large randomized clinical trial, did not identify a significant protective effect of AF screening on ischemic stroke risk. 4 In a similar vein, a large meta‐analysis reported that ongoing AF was observed in only a minority of individuals with ischemic stroke. 5

Recent studies have suggested that atrial dysfunction (ie, atrial cardiomyopathy) may also constitute a prothrombogenic substrate independently of AF. 6 , 7 Various indicators of atrial cardiomyopathy and their associations with ischemic stroke have been examined in this context. Kamel et al evaluated protein and electrocardiographic markers of left atrial (LA) remodeling in a cohort of individuals without AF and observed an association with subsequent ischemic stroke of these markers. 8 Another study found associations between LA function and white matter hyperintensities in an AF‐free cohort of individuals undergoing continuous cardiac rhythm monitoring. 9

Large‐scale integration of genetics and cardiac imaging data has elucidated part of the genetic background of cardiac structure and function, 10 , 11 including genetic variation underlying LA function and volumes. 12 Information derived from large genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) can be useful in both disease prediction and to explore intricate pathophysiological relationships. Polygenic scores (PGSs), which leverage genetic variation from GWAS summary statistics, are playing an increasingly important role in cardiovascular risk stratification, 13 and several studies have investigated polygenic associations between stroke and other traits such as kidney function, 14 depression, 15 and mood disorders. 16 This study aimed to investigate a potential shared polygenic architecture between LA function and volumes and ischemic stroke, independent of AF. PGSs were constructed for 5 LA measures and evaluated in a cohort of >380 000 individuals free of ischemic stroke and AF at baseline. This was complemented by Mendelian randomization analyses to ascertain potential causal relationships between LA function and volumes and CES.

Methods

Data Availability

The summary statistics of the LA measurements used to calculate the PGSs are publicly available at https://zenodo.org/record/5074929. Weights for the PGS calculated in this study will be made publicly available through the Polygenic Score Catalog 17 upon publication. Bona fide researchers can apply for access to individual‐level data from the UK Biobank at https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/enable‐your‐research/apply‐for‐access.

Study Cohort

The study was conducted in the UK Biobank under application number 43247. The UK Biobank is a large population‐based prospective cohort study composed of genetic and clinical information on almost 500 000 individuals. All participants provided written informed consent, and the biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi‐centre Research Ethics Committee in the United Kingdom. Individuals were enrolled between 2006 and 2010. Details of the UK Biobank, including genotyping methods, have been described by Bycroft and colleagues. 18

Samples were filtered on the basis of quality control criteria provided in UK Biobank resource 531. Individuals with disagreement between reported and genetically determined sex were excluded. Individuals with missing values for body mass index or sex were also excluded. As the PGSs were based on genetic data from individuals of European ancestry, we excluded individuals of non‐European ancestry in the subsequent analyses. European ancestry was defined using self‐reported ethnicity and the first 6 principal components. Data fields used for the definition of phenotypes, covariates, and ancestry are summarized in Table S1. The study design is illustrated in Figure 1.

LA Measurements

We used previously published summary statistics from 1 of our earlier GWASs on LA structure and function. 12 These genetic associations were based on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) data from 35 658 individuals of European ancestry from the UK Biobank. The atrial measurements included LA maximum volume (LAMax) and LA minimum volume (LAMin), as well as 3 measurements for LA function: LA total emptying fraction (LATEF), LA active emptying fraction (LAAEF), and LA passive emptying fraction (LAPEF). The extraction of measurements from cMRI scans and subsequent GWASs have previously been described in detail. 12 We explored the correlation between measured and genetically predicted LA measurements by applying the PGS in the individuals with cMRI data (described in detail in Data S1).

PGSs for LA Function and Volumes

We constructed a PGS for each of the 5 LA measurements using the continuous shrinkage algorithm (polygenic risk score–continuous shrinkage). Weights for each single‐nucleotide polymorphism were calculated using the second version of the algorithm (polygenic risk score–continuous shrinkage–auto). This algorithm employs a Bayesian approach that automatically learns from data and offers the advantage that it does not require validation in another data set. The detailed methodology behind the algorithm has been described elsewhere. 19 Using PLINK, 20 individual‐level PGSs were estimated for each LA trait, according to number of risk alleles and posterior effect size of the variants. We excluded individuals that had undergone cMRI of the atria to avoid sample overlap with the 35 658 individuals from the previous GWAS on LA measurements and consequent risk of bias due to model overfitting.

Study Cohort, Outcomes, and Statistical Analyses

We constructed a nested case–control study, with enrollment in the UK Biobank as the index date. Individuals diagnosed with ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, or AF before or at the index date were excluded, as well as individuals who were part of the previous GWAS on LA measurements. Proportional hazards were estimated using cause‐specific Cox regressions adjusted for sex, age at enrollment, body mass index, and the following risk factors at baseline: ischemic heart disease, hypertension, heart failure, and diabetes. Body mass index was evaluated per unit increase and age‐per‐year increase. Death and incident AF during follow‐up were considered competing events. Participants were followed from index date, until first incident ischemic stroke, death, incident AF, or end of follow‐up (July 1, 2022), whichever came first.

Each PGS was evaluated per SD increase in the above‐described Cox regression models. For significant associations, the score was also evaluated with the cohort stratified into 2 groups on the basis of PGSs above or below the cohort median and stratified by PGS quartiles. The group with PGS above the median was used as the reference. To assess potential nonlinear relationships between PGSs and ischemic stroke, we employed penalized smoothing spline models with 4 degrees of freedom. Crude cumulative incidence of ischemic stroke was estimated using the Aalen–Johansen estimator, again with AF occurrence and death as competing risks. As a secondary outcome, we estimated incident arterial embolism in a separate model, again considering incident AF and all‐cause death as competing events.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). 21 Cox regressions were carried out with the survival package. Cumulative incidences were calculated using the prodlim package, and statistical differences between cumulative incidences of groups were calculated using Gray's test, implemented in the cmprsk package. Penalized smoothing spline models were performed using the pspline function integrated in the survival package, with default parameters. Data were visualized in R, using ggplot2. Results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using Bonferroni correction, and P values <0.01 (0.05/5) were considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity and Subgroup Analyses

In a sensitivity analysis, we applied a blinding period of 1 year following enrollment, setting the index date as 365 days after enrollment, and excluding individuals with ischemic stroke, AF, systemic arterial embolism, or death before this date. In a subgroup analysis, the PGSs were evaluated in a subcohort free of cardiovascular disease, excluding individuals with prevalent ischemic stroke, AF, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, diabetes, heart failure, systemic arterial embolism, and valvular heart disease at baseline. In this subgroup, individuals reporting use of cholesterol‐lowering medication or blood pressure medication were also excluded. Prescription medication was based on self‐report at enrollment (Table S1). Individuals who did not know if they took prescription medication or preferred not to answer were also excluded. In another subgroup analysis, the cohort was stratified by age (≥60 years and <60 years at index date). Significant associations in the primary analysis were assessed in each subgroup separately.

Mendelian Randomization

Mendelian randomization is a method to infer potential causal relationships between distinct traits, using genetic variants as instruments to proxy the effect of an exposure on an outcome. 22 For each of the 5 LA measurements, we selected genetic instruments from genome‐wide significant single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (P<5×10−8). Instruments were pruned for linkage‐disequilibrium using an R 2 cutoff of 0.001. For the outcome data set, we used summary statistics for CES, from a European ancestry subset of the MEGASTROKE (Multiancestry Genome‐Wide Association Study of 520,000 Subjects Identifies 32 Loci Associated With Stroke and Stroke Subtypes) consortium (GWAS catalog ID: GCST006910), 23 representing 7193 cases and 406 111 controls. We conducted 2‐sample Mendelian randomization, using the inverse variance weighted method as the primary analysis, with weighted median as a sensitivity analysis. Evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the intercept of the Egger regression (P for non‐0 intercept <0.05), and heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q statistic (P <0.05). For exposures with too few genome‐wide significant instruments to perform Egger regression and weighted median analysis, we conducted sensitivity analyses using a slightly relaxed P value cutoff of P<5×10−7. The analyses were conducted using the R packages TwoSampleMR and MendelianRandomization.

Association With Ischemic Stroke

We observed a significant association between the PGS for LAPEF and the risk of ischemic stroke. When analyzed as a continuous variable, the LAPEF PGS conferred a reduced risk per SD increase (HR per SD, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93–0.99]; P=0.006). Accordingly, a lower PGS (score below the 50th percentile) conferred an HR of 1.10 for ischemic stroke (95% CI, 1.05–1.16; P=0.0004) compared with individuals with a PGS at or above the 50th percentile. When comparing individuals on the basis of LAPEF PGS quartiles, a dose–response‐like association was observed (Figure 2A). We did not observe evidence of a nonlinear relationship between the LAPEF PGS and rates of ischemic stroke in the primary analysis (P nonlinearity=0.085). Spline representations are presented in Figure 2B. Applying a 1‐year blinding period did not substantially alter the results (Table S2).

Associations in Disease‐Free and Age‐Stratified Subgroups

Applying the PGS for LAPEF in a subgroup of 253 893 individuals without cardiovascular comorbidities replicated the estimates. Here, a 1‐SD higher PGS conferred an HR of 0.95 (95% CI, 0.91–0.98; P=0.007) for ischemic stroke. Individuals with lower PGS (below the 50th percentile) had an increased HR of 1.15 (95% CI, 1.06–1.24; P=0.0005) for ischemic stroke compared with those with a PGS at or above the 50th percentile. Those with a score in the lowest quartile had an HR of 1.22 (95% CI, 1.09–1.36; P=0.0005) compared with those in the top quartile (Figure 2A). In spline representations, we observed evidence of a nonlinear relationship (P nonlinearity=0.010), with higher ischemic stroke rates in individuals with a PGS in the upper tail end (Figure 2C).

When stratified by age, estimates were consistent in individuals aged ≥60 years at inclusion. Here, a 1‐SD increase in the PGS LAPEF conferred an HR of 0.96 per SD increase (95% CI, 0.93–0.99; P: 0.015). Consistent effects were observed when stratified into PGS quartiles (HR for lowest quartile versus highest quartile, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.02–1.22]; P=0.013). In the individuals aged <60 years at the index date, the estimates were no longer statistically significant (HR per SD increase, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.92–1.02]; P=0.20), although a similar trend was observed when stratified by PGS quartiles (Figure 3A). We did not observe evidence of a nonlinear relationship between the ischemic stroke rates and the PGS for LAPEF when stratifying by age (≥60 years, P nonlinearity=0.159; <60 years, P nonlinearity=0.687). Spline representations are illustrated in Figures 3B and 3C.

Cumulative Incidence of Ischemic Stroke on the Basis of LAPEF Polygenic Score

For the LAPEF PGS, the increased HR observed in Cox regressions also translated into a significantly higher cumulative incidence of ischemic stroke (Figure 4). At 14 years after enrollment (upper quartile of follow‐up period), individuals with a LAPEF PGS below the 50th percentile had an absolute risk of 1.57% (95% CI, 1.51–1.63) for incident ischemic stroke. Comparatively, the absolute risk was 1.41% (95% CI, 1.35–1.46) for individuals with a LAPEF PGS at or above the 50th percentile. Gray's test indicated a statistically significant difference between the 2 groups (P=3.63×10−5).

Association With Systemic Embolism

As a secondary outcome, we also assessed HRs of incident systemic arterial embolism. During follow‐up, 1037 individuals had an incident systemic embolism (0.3%). We found a nominally significant association for the PGS for LAPEF with an effect direction consistent with that of ischemic stroke (HR per SD increase, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.88–1.00]; P=0.049). Stratified by PGS quartiles, we observed a trend toward increased risk for those with genetically predicted reduction in LAPEF that was not statistically significant (HR for lowest quartile versus highest quartile, 1.14 [95% CI, 0.96–1.36]; P=0.15).

Inference of Causal Relationships

Five instruments were identified for LATEF and LAAEF each, 2 two were identified for LAMax, LAMin and LAPEF, respectively. We observed a protective effect of higher genetically predicted LATEF (OR per SD increase, 0.46 [95% CI, 0.25–0.83]; P=0.010), LAPEF (OR per SD increase, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.28–0.91]; P=0.026), and LAAEF (OR per SD increase, 0.66 [95% CI, 0.46–0.96]; P=0.028). We did not observe any significant associations for increased LAMax (OR per SD increase, 0.92 [95% CI, 0.48–1.77]; P=0.81) or LAMin (OR per SD increase, 2.26 [95% CI, 0.92–5.51]; P=0.074; Figure 6). In weighted median analyses, the results for LATEF (OR per SD increase, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.36–1.03]; P=0.065) and LAAEF (OR per SD increase, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.40–1.05]; P=0.079) were attenuated and were not statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study leveraged PGSs on the basis of >35 000 cMRI scans to further elucidate the complex relationship between atrial function and risk of stroke. In a large cohort of >380 000 individuals, we found a significant association between a genetically predicted reduction in LAPEF and increased risk of ischemic stroke. This association was robust with more pronounced estimates in a subgroup free of prevalent CVD.

The connection between AF, LA dysfunction, and stroke is still not completely understood. While embolism of cardiac origin is thought to be an important pathophysiological mechanism, clinical trials on AF detection after stroke have found similar rates of AF following cryptogenic stroke 24 and following stroke attributed to large‐ or small‐vessel disease. 25 Similarly, clinical trials have not been able to demonstrate superior efficacy of anticoagulation initiation in patients with embolic stroke of undetermined source, compared with aspirin treatment. 26 , 27 Our findings indicate that atrial dysfunction in itself may be an independent risk factor for ischemic stroke. Stratification into PGSs, indicated a dose–response‐like relationship between lower LA passive emptying fraction and higher rates of ischemic stroke. This relationship appeared to be linear in the primary analysis, although we did observe evidence of some degree of nonlinearity in a subanalysis on individuals free of prevalent cardiovascular disease.

The LAPEF measurement is an indicator of LA conduit function, 28 which involves the passive transfer of blood from the pulmonary veins to the left ventricle during early diastole. LA dysfunction has been associated with an increase in interstitial fibrosis and structural changes, 29 leading to atrial remodeling (ie, atrial cardiomyopathy). 30 Over time, compensatory mechanisms such as dilation of the LA and increases in LA pressure further exacerbate LA dysfunction. Lower LAPEF has previously been associated with AF severity 31 and reoccurrence following catheter ablation. 32

We observed no significant associations between PGSs for LA volumes, LATEF or LAAEF and risk of incident ischemic stroke. A few previous studies have already addressed potential associations between LA measurements and risk of stroke. 9 , 33 , 34 , 35 Most of these were conducted on relatively small cohorts with older individuals and have offered somewhat conflicting results. Russo et al found that greater LA volumes and lower LA emptying fraction were associated with increased risk of infarction and white matter hyperintensities in a cohort of 455 stroke‐free individuals. 35 A later study by Habibi et al only found an association for LATEF in models adjusting for incident AF, 34 although it should be noted that this study used a composite end point of both ischemic stroke and transient ischemic attack. In 200 AF‐free patients undergoing continuous cardiac monitoring, Bertelsen et al showed an independent association of LAPEF with an increased Fazekas score but found no associations with other LA functional measurements. 9 The Fazekas score measures white matter hyperintensities, an indicator of subclinical cerebrovascular disease.

The disparity in results between studies could potentially be caused by different measuring techniques (echocardiography versus cMRI) or end point definitions (ischemic stroke, composite end points, subclinical cerebrovascular disease, or different stroke subtypes) or be influenced by bias through residual confounding or confounding by indication. Our cohort did not contain information on stroke subtypes, and it is possible that some atrial measurements are better predictors for specific subtypes of stroke rather than ischemic stroke in general. Moreover, age‐related changes in LA function is well established, with a reduction in LAPEF and an increase in LAAEF observed with increasing age. 36 Given that an individual's genetics are fixed at zygote formation, using genetically predicted measurements of LA function may provide more robust estimates that can complement the initial findings from previous studies. Although the effect estimates observed here were relatively modest, they were independent of AF and were consistent with more pronounced effect estimates in a subgroup of individuals free of prevalent cardiovascular disease.

As the UK Biobank participants had not undergone continuous cardiac monitoring, we cannot exclude that some individuals may have had undetected subclinical AF. However, we note that the influence of subclinical AF on stroke risk is uncertain. The recent NOAH‐AF (Non–Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulants in Patients With Atrial High‐Rate Episodes Without ECG‐Diagnosed Atrial Fibrillation) trial found no significant reduction on stroke risk following anticoagulation initiation in patients with device‐detected atrial high‐rate episodes. 37 Another study, the ARTESIA (Apixaban for the Reduction of Thromboembolism in Patients With Device‐Detected Subclinical Atrial Fibrillation) trial, found a significant protective effect of anticoagulation among individuals with subclinical AF. 38 It is notable that the stroke incidence in individuals with subclinical AF (1.24% per year in aspirin arm) was markedly lower than that observed in previous trials on clinically manifest AF (3.7% per person‐year in aspirin arm). 39 This is consistent with the LOOP study, in which AF screening alone did not significantly reduce the risk of ischemic stroke, despite a 3‐fold increase in AF detection and anticoagulation initiation. 4 Interestingly, a post hoc analysis from the same study found a benefit of AF screening on subsequent stroke risk in individuals with elevated NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels but not among those with lower levels. 40 NT‐proBNP is a natriuretic peptide that has previously been described as a marker for atrial cardiomyopathy, 41 underlining that atrial cardiomyopathy may be an important pathogenetic prerequisite for stroke. 6

Mendelian randomization represents another method for assessing the impact of the genetic determinants of LA volumes and function on stroke risk. This approach complements the PGSs and may be more robust to bias from horizontal pleitropy. 42 In Mendelian randomization analyses, higher LATEF, LAPEF, and LAAEF were all associated with protective effects on the risk of CES. These results indicate a potential causal relationship between LA function and stroke risk, consistent with the findings of an increased risk of stroke in individuals with a genetically predicted reduction in LAPEF. While we did not find evidence of horizontal pleiotropy in the present analysis, its presence cannot be completely ruled out. Hence, the approach requires that potential horizontal pleiotropic pathways be considered and triangulation of evidence from other sources be included. 43 , 44

Atrial remodeling could also be induced by heart failure, hypertension, and obesity, or atrial fibrillation itself. 30 , 45 , 46 As these are also risk factors for ischemic stroke, 2 they constitute potential horizontal pleiotropic pathways which may in part drive the observed associations. In the present study, we evaluated genetic determinants of atrial structure and function through both Mendelian randomization and through PGSs. This approach allowed us to adjust for some of these factors shared risk factors as covariates when analyzing the PGSs. Both the PGS assessment in the UK Biobank and the Mendelian randomization approach using summary statistics from other sources showed a consistent association between lower LA function, specifically LAPEF, and risk of ischemic stroke. This is congruent with evidence from several observational imaging studies that showed associations with similar directions of effect. 9 , 35 While the consistency of evidence from several sources supports the robustness of the observed results, it should be noted that some degree of bias or residual confounding cannot be excluded.

When interpreting the results of this study, one must also consider its potential limitations. As mentioned above, the identified effect estimates were modest in size. Thus, the current PGSs are unlikely to be used as independent predictors of ischemic stroke in a clinical setting. Instead, they should be regarded as instruments that provide new pathophysiological insights into atrial cardiomyopathy and dysfunction as important, novel pathogenetic components of ischemic stroke. While Mendelian randomization on an independent outcome data set showed associations between LA functional measurements and CES, we note that the UK Biobank did not contain information on subtypes of ischemic stroke (eg, TOAST [Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment] classification), which limited our ability to assess the PGSs in the context of CES specifically. Moreover, while using genetically predicted LA measurements may be relatively robust against bias introduced by confounding in observational studies, we cannot exclude that unknown factors may also have introduced bias into our study design. In this context, it should be noted that the general makeup of the UK Biobank cohort could possibly introduce a healthy‐volunteer bias. It should also be noted that while the PGSs used here were based on cMRI scans from >35 000 individuals, it is possible that they were not powerful enough genetic instruments to adequately elucidate all true associations. The Mendelian randomization analyses were limited by relatively few genome‐wide significant instruments, and it should be noted that analyses indicated evidence of heterogeneity for LAMin and LAAEF in tests with relaxed instrument selection. Finally, the study was limited to individuals of European ancestry. While this approach limits the risk of bias due to population stratification, one cannot assume that our results are generalizable to all other population groups. Therefore, the relationship between atrial dysfunction and cerebrovascular disease warrants further study in other cohorts with a varied spectrum of imaging modalities and ischemic stroke subtypes.

In summary, our study provides new insights into the link between atrial dysfunction and ischemic stroke, with a robust association between LAPEF and incident ischemic stroke. Mendelian randomization supported an association between LA functional measures and CES. These findings corroborate previous theories of atrial cardiomyopathy as an important pathogenetic component in ischemic stroke and illustrate how PGSs may serve as instruments to elucidate complex pathophysiological relationships.
