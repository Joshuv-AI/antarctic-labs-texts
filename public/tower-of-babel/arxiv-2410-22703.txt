On tail inference in scale-free inhomogeneous random graphs
Daniel Cirkovic, Tiandong Wang, Daren B.H. Cline

Abstract
Both empirical and theoretical investigations of scale-free network models have found that large degrees in a network exert an outsized impact on its structure. However, the tools used to infer the tail behavior of degree distributions in scale-free networks often lack a strong theoretical foundation. In this paper, we introduce a new framework for analyzing the asymptotic distribution of estimators for degree tail indices in scale-free inhomogeneous random graphs. Our framework leverages the relationship between the large weights and large degrees of Norros-Reittu and Chung-Lu random graphs. In particular, we determine a rate for the number of nodes k ⁡ ( n ) → ∞ k(n)\rightarrow\infty such that for all i = 1 , … , k ⁡ ( n ) i=1,\dots,k(n) , the node with the i i -th largest weight will have the i i -th largest degree with high probability. Such alignment of upper-order statistics is then employed to establish the asymptotic normality of three different tail index estimators based on the upper degrees. These results suggest potential applications of the framework to threshold selection and goodness-of-fit testing in scale-free networks, issues that have long challenged the network science community.



1
Introduction

Early research in network science found that many real-world networks exhibit power-law degree distributions
[
1
,
2
,
20
,
33
]
. Loosely speaking, this means that the proportion of nodes with degree exceeding
j
is approximately proportional to
j^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
and
j
sufficiently large. Here,
\alpha
is called the tail index and networks satisfying this property are commonly referred to as “scale-free”. The prominence of scale-free networks led to the development of models that could imitate such behavior, which in turn allowed researchers to study the recurring consequences that the scale-free property had on a variety of network characteristics. Prominent examples of network models that emerged during this time are the classes of preferential attachment and inhomogeneous random graphs (IRGs) (see for instance
[
4
,
12
]
).

Despite the interest in the scale-free property and, by extension, the large degrees of random graphs, statistical inference procedures focused on the extremal behavior of the degree distribution are underdeveloped. Often, large degrees in a network are the most impactful on its structure. Empirical investigations have found that the connectivity of scale-free networks is vulnerable to the deletion of targeted high degree vertices
[
3
]
. Theoretical studies of popular scale-free network models also confirm the outsized influence large degree nodes have on connectivity
[
6
]
. Hence, in order to make reliable inference on a wide variety of network properties, network scientists require provably accurate statistical procedures that describe the tail behavior of degree distributions.

When examining the large degrees of scale-free networks, the foremost object of interest is the power-law tail index
\alpha
. Yet, only recently has the Hill estimator
[
25
]
, a popular estimator of the tail index, been shown to be consistent in the preferential attachment and inhomogeneous random graph models
[
8
,
13
,
39
]
. To the best of our knowledge, there are no results confirming asymptotic normality of this or any other tail index estimator in scale-free random networks. Furthermore, thresholding methods that estimate the value beyond which a power-law tail fits best, such as the minimum distance method, have no theoretical basis for application in scale-free networks
[
14
,
19
]
. Such lack of tools for assessing the goodness of fit has even provoked controversies regarding the applicability of the scale-free model in network science
[
11
,
26
,
38
]
.

In this paper, we take a vital first step towards providing valid statistical inference for the extreme-value behavior of large degrees in scale-free random networks. In particular, we provide a framework for proving the asymptotic normality of tail index estimators in scale-free IRGs. Inhomogeneous random graphs assign to each node an independently and identically distributed weight that models its degree. It is well-known that the node degrees concentrate strongly around their weights when the weights are large. This dynamic causes the degree distribution to adopt the tail behavior of the weight distribution
[
8
]
. Our framework leverages this relationship between the weights and degrees, but interrogates it further. In particular, for the Norros-Reittu and Chung-Lu random graph models, we determine a rate for the number of nodes
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
such that, with probability tending towards 1, the node with the
i
-th largest weight will have the
i
-th largest degree for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k(n)\}
. We refer to this phenomenon as the
alignment of upper order statistics
. Under the alignment of upper order statistics, we prove that when applied to the degrees of inhomogeneous random graphs, three different tail index estimators can be approximated by their weight-based counterparts. This approximation gives rise to the asymptotic normality of tail index estimators when applied to IRGs, the first such result for scale-free networks.
The alignment of upper order statistics thus provides a potential avenue for future work that verifies the validity of multiple extreme-value procedures employing the upper degrees of inhomogeneous random graphs.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we start by discussing important concepts in IRGs, and give conditions under which our asymptotic results will be derived. We then summarize the key finding, i.e. the alignment of upper order statistics, in Section
3
, and apply it to three different tail index estimators in Section
4
. Simulation results and concluding remarks are given in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively, and Section
7
collects all technical proofs.

Notation

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we employ the notation
[n]
to refer to the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. If
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
are a collection of random variables, we let
X_{[n]}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}\}
. For real numbers
x
and
y

x\wedge y

2
Scale-free inhomogeneous random network models

2.1
Ordered inhomogeneous random network

Inhomogeneous random graph models assign each node a latent weight that confers some notion of connectivity or popularity relative to other nodes in the network. A larger weight is then typically associated with a greater number of links. In order to explicitly analyze the extent to which a large weight confers a large degree, we consider
ordered
inhomogeneous random network models. Instead of assigning identically distributed weights to each node independently, the ordered inhomogeneous network matches the label of each node with the rank of its allocated weight. In said scheme, the largest weight is always assigned to node one, the second largest to node two and so on. Such an ordering is often imposed when the weights are deterministic (see for example
[
7
,
36
]
). Assuming that the weights are drawn from a continuous distribution, then an inhomogeneous random network may be recovered from an ordered version by selecting a permutation of the node labels uniformly at random. As such, the typical degree distribution from an ordered inhomogeneous network is the same as its unordered counterpart.

We now formally introduce the two ordered inhomogeneous random network models under consideration. Suppose
W_{[n]}
are independent and identically distributed weights from a positively supported continuous distribution function
F
and let
W_{(1)}(n)\geq W_{(2)}(n)\geq\dots\geq W_{(n)}(n)
denote the associated decreasing order statistics. Further, let
L(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{(i)}(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}
. Both random networks model the symmetric adjacency matrix
A(n)=\{A_{ij}(n)\}_{i,j=1}^{n}
by conditioning on the latent weights
\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}
. The first model under consideration is the Norros-Reittu multigraph which assumes that

\displaystyle A_{ij}(n)\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\overset{\text{ind}}{\sim}\text{Poisson}\left(\frac{W_{(i)}(n)W_{(j)}(n)}{L(n)}\right),\qquad\text{for }1\leq i\leq j\leq n.

(1)

Let
D_{i}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(n)
denote the degree of node
i
for
i\in[n]
. From (
1
), we have that

\displaystyle D_{i}(n)\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\sim\text{Poisson}\left(W_{(i)}(n)\right),\qquad\text{for }i\in[n],

(2)

since
D_{i}(n)
is the sum of conditionally independent Poisson random variables. We secondly consider the Chung-Lu model which assumes that

\displaystyle A_{ij}(n)\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\overset{\text{ind}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}\left(\frac{W_{(i)}(n)W_{(j)}(n)}{L(n)}\wedge 1\right),\qquad\text{for }1\leq i\leq j\leq n.

(3)

Under (
3
), note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[D_{i}(n)|\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{W_{(i)}(n)W_{(j)}(n)}{L(n)}\wedge 1\right),\qquad i\in[n].

As mentioned earlier, despite working in an ordered model, inhomogeneous random graphs may be recovered by taking a random permutation of the node labels.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\pi
is a permutation drawn uniformly at random from
S_{n}
, the set of all permutations on
[n]
. Define for every
i,j\in[n]
such that
i\leq j
,
\tilde{A}_{ij}(n)=A_{\pi(i)\pi(j)}(n)
. Then
\{\tilde{A}_{ij}(n)\}_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
is distributed according to an inhomogeneous random graph model.

As such, the ordered and unordered models share the same typical degree distribution. Lemma
1
is proved in Section
7.1
. We next investigate the scale-free nature of inhomogeneous random networks.

2.2
Scale-free property and order statistics

For random networks, the scale-free property is most generally defined through regular variation of the
asymptotic
degree distribution
[
26
,
30
,
38
]
. That is, we say a random network is scale-free if for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{i}(n)=k\right)\rightarrow\ \mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}=k\right),\qquad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty,

(4)

for some random variable
\mathcal{D}
with regularly varying tail. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}>k\right)=l(k)k^{-\alpha},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(5)

for some slowly varying function
l
and
\alpha>0
. It is well-known that, for a wide class of inhomogeneous random graphs, regular variation of the weight distribution implies regular variation of the asymptotic degree distribution
[
8
,
13
]
. In particular, for the Norros-Reittu random network, regular variation of the weight distribution implies regular variation of the typical degree distribution for all
\alpha>0

[
8
]
. For the Chung-Lu model, regular variation of the weight distribution implies regular variation of the asymptotic degree distribution when
\alpha>1

[
9
]
. In both settings, the asymptotic degree distribution has a mixed-Poisson form where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{D}=k\right)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-w}w^{k}}{k!}\mathbb{P}\left(W_{1}\in dw\right),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Regular variation of the asymptotic degree distribution emerges from the weights due to the strong concentration of the Poisson distribution for large rates. Obtaining rates at which the upper degrees concentrate around large weights will later play a large role in quantifying how the regular variation property translates from the weights to the degrees.

We henceforth assume that the distribution of
W_{1}
is regularly varying with tail index
\alpha>0
. For the Chung-Lu random graph, we will restrict the tail index to
\alpha>2
. When working with the order statistics
W_{(1)}(n),\dots,W_{(n)}(n)
, regular variation is often more conveniently expressed in terms of the quantile function. Let
U(t)=F^{\leftarrow}(1-1/t)
for
t\geq 1
where
F^{\leftarrow}(y)=\inf\{x:F(x)\geq y\}
. In addition, let
\gamma=1/\alpha
. According to
[
35
]
, Remark 3.3, regular variation of
W_{1}
with tail index
\alpha>0
is then equivalent to the statement

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{U(tx)}{U(t)}=x^{\gamma},\qquad x>0.

(6)

Hence, we may write
U(t)=x^{\gamma}\ell(t)
for some slowly varying function
\ell
. Further, using Rényi’s representation for exponential order statistics, we have the following distributional characterization for the order statistics:

\displaystyle\left(W_{(1)}(n),W_{2}(n),\dots,W_{(n)}(n)\right)\overset{d}{=}\left(U\left(e^{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{E_{j}}{j}}\right),U\left(e^{\sum_{j=2}^{n}\frac{E_{j}}{j}}\right),\dots,U\left(e^{\frac{E_{n}}{n}}\right)\right),

(7)

where
E_{1},E_{2},\dots,E_{n}
are independent unit rate exponential random variables
[
5
, Section 4.4]
.

In order to achieve asymptotic normality of most tail index estimators based on the upper order statistics, it is common to impose a second order condition on
U

[
16
]
. The second order condition assumes that there exists a
\rho\leq 0
and a function
A
with constant sign such that for
x>0

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\frac{U(tx)}{U(t)}-x^{\gamma}}{A(t)}=x^{\gamma}\frac{x^{\rho}-1}{\rho}.

(8)

When
\rho=0
, the limiting function in (
8
) is understood to be
x^{\gamma}\log x
. The second order condition provides a rate of convergence for (
6
). In addition, when
\rho<0
, the first order relationship is simplified to
U(t)\sim ct^{\gamma}
where
c=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-\gamma}U(t)

[
21
]
. The second order condition may be equivalently restated in terms of the survival function
[
21
, Theorem 2.3.9]
, and (
8
) can be seen to hold for the Fréchet distribution with
\rho=-1
, the t-distribution with
\rho=-2
, and the Burr distribution with
1-F(x)=(1+x^{-\rho/\gamma})^{1/\rho}
,
\rho<0
, among other distributions.

We further assume that there exists a
t_{0}>0
and
\eta\in[0,1)
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
and
x\geq 1

\displaystyle\frac{U(tx)}{U(t)}\geq(1-\eta)x^{\gamma}+\eta.

(9)

Hence taking
\eta=0
in (
9
) is equivalent to stating that
\ell
is eventually non-decreasing. For
\eta\in(0,1)
, (
9
) allows
\ell
to be non-increasing, but ensures that
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\ell(x)\geq(1-\eta)\ell(t_{0})
. Thus, in conjunction with the second order condition with
\rho<0
, (
9
) is not very restrictive. In terms of the tails of
F
, (
9
) implies that for all
z\geq U(t_{0})
and
x\geq 1

\displaystyle\frac{1-F(zx)}{1-F(z)}\geq\left(\frac{x-\eta}{1-\eta}\right)^{-\alpha}.

(10)

For
\eta=0
, the condition (
9
) naturally holds for the Pareto distribution and it can analytically be shown to hold for the half-Cauchy distribution, the Fréchet distribution and the Burr distribution. Reminiscent of the distributions considered in
[
23
,
24
]
, the tail function
1-F(x)=\frac{2+x}{x^{2}}
for
x\geq 2
satisfies (
10
) for, say,
\eta=1/4
. Condition (
9
) will lead to a lower bound for the spacings of consecutive order statistics, an important ingredient in our proof strategy.

3
Alignment of upper order statistics

For inhomogeneous random networks, it is well understood that a large node weight typically confers a large degree. As mentioned in Section
2.2
, this is due to the strong concentration of the degrees around the weights, conditional on the weights being large. Hence, we expect the largest weights to play a fundamental role in determining the upper degrees. In this section, we find that the large weights not only determine the upper degrees, but that the ordering of the large weights governs the ordering of the upper degrees. Let
R_{i}(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}1_{\{D_{i}(n)\leq D_{j}(n)\}}
denote the number of nodes with degree at least as large as the degree of node
i
. For scale-free inhomogeneous networks we establish a growth rate on the number of nodes,
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
, such that with probability tending towards
1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\left(R_{1}(n),R_{2}(n),\dots,R_{k(n)}(n)\right)=\left(1,2,\dots,k(n)\right).

(11)

Our proof also shows that, with probability tending towards
1
, no ties occur among the upper
k(n)
degrees so that we indeed find the that the ordering of the upper
k(n)
weights is preserved by the ordering of the upper
k(n)
degrees. We refer to the statement (
11
) as the alignment of upper order statistics. The requirement on
k(n)
is presented in Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
U
satisfies (
6
) and (
9
).
Suppose that the network is generated under either a Norros-Reittu model with
\alpha>0
or Chung-Lu model with
\alpha>2
. Further suppose
k(n)
is such that
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
and
k(n)\log^{\alpha}(n)/n^{\frac{1}{4\alpha+1}}\rightarrow 0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left(R_{1}(n),R_{2}(n),\dots,R_{k(n)}(n)\right)=\left(1,2,\dots,k(n)\right)\right)\rightarrow 1.

Theorem
1
is proved in Section
7.5
. It is pursued in three steps. First, we establish that the spacings between the upper
k(n)
order statistics of regularly varying random variables are sufficiently large. This is done by establishing high probability upper and lower bounds on the upper order statistics of regularly varying random variables. Then we show that the first
k(n)
degrees concentrate around their weights at a sufficiently fast rate. In fact, this rate determines how far apart the upper order statistics need to be, which in turn determines how fast
k(n)
is allowed to grow. The main technical ingredients here are Bennett’s inequality for the Norros-Reittu model and Chernoff’s inequality for the Chung-Lu random graph. Assuming that the first and second steps are achieved, one may determine that

\displaystyle D_{1}(n)>D_{2}(n)>\dots>D_{k(n)}(n),

with probability tending towards one as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, the final step required to prove the alignment of the upper order statistics is to show that, with probability tending towards one, none of the degrees
\{D_{i}(n)\}_{i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]}
exceed
D_{k(n)}(n)
. This sequence of steps can be more precisely outlined by showing that each of the following sequences of events,

\begin{split}S(n)=&\left\{\forall i\in[k(n)],W_{(i)}(n)-W_{(i+1)}(n)>2\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\right\},\\
C(n)=&\left\{\forall i\in[k(n)],\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|<\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\right\},\\
M(n)=&\left\{D_{k(n)}(n)>\max_{i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]}D_{i}(n)\right\}.\end{split}

(12)

occur with probability tending towards one as
n\rightarrow\infty
. That is,
S(n)
,
C(n)
and
M(n)
refer to the events described in the first, second and final step, respectively. Theory regarding the events
S(n)
and
C(n)
can be found in Sections
7.3
and
7.4
, respectively. Statements for
M(n)
are developed in Section
7.5
.

4
Application to tail index estimators

In the statistical inference of scale-free networks, a fundamental task is the estimation of the tail index
\alpha
. However, for network data in particular, this is a difficult problem. Theoretical justification for procedures used to estimate the tail index are difficult to obtain due to the degree dependence present in network data. In fact, only consistency of the well-known Hill estimator for the power-law tail index has been verified in two non-trivial network models: the preferential attachment model
[
39
]
and the inhomogeneous random graph
[
8
]
. To the best of our knowledge, the current literature contains no results regarding the asymptotic normality of
any
tail index estimator for network data. Further, a variety of practical difficulties also emerge when applying tail index estimators to real-world network data
[
38
]
. For example, ties in integer-valued data often lead to the erratic behavior of tail index estimators
[
29
]
.

In this section, we find that the alignment of upper order statistics also provides a path to proving the asymptotic normality of tail index estimators when applied to degrees from the Norros-Reittu and Chung-Lu random graphs. Although we focus on three estimators, the presented techniques can in principle be applied to most tail index estimators based on the largest degrees of an inhomogeneous random graph. The three estimators under consideration are the Hill
[
25
]
, Pickands
[
31
]
and probability weighted moment (PWM) estimators
[
27
]
. All three estimators target the reciprocal of the tail index,
\gamma\equiv 1/\alpha
. In the iid setting, asymptotic normality has been verified for the Hill and Pickands estimators when
\alpha>0
and for the PWM estimator when
\alpha>2
. In all three cases, asymptotic normality relies on the second-order condition for
U
presented in Section
2.2
; see Chapter 3 of
[
21
]
for detailed discussions.

Our strategy to prove asymptotic normality of these estimators when applied to the network degrees is to approximate them by their weight-based counterparts. We denote a generic tail index estimator based on the degrees by
\hat{\gamma}_{D}(n)
and its weight-based counterpart by
\hat{\gamma}_{W}(n)
. In all three cases, the upper-order statistic alignment allows us to write
\hat{\gamma}_{D}(n)
as a function of the degrees
D_{1}(n),D_{2}(n),\dots
rather than the order statistics. From there, an approximation by
\hat{\gamma}_{W}(n)
is obtained in the ordered inhomogeneous random graph model.

4.1
Hill and Pickands estimators

In this section, we prove an approximation of the degree-based Hill and Pickands estimators by their weight-based counterparts. The Hill estimator based on the upper
k(n)
degrees is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{D}(n)=\frac{1}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\log\left(D_{(i)}(n)/D_{(k(n))}(n)\right).

(13)

The weight-based Hill estimator,
\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{W}(n)
, is defined by replacing
D_{(i)}(n)
in (
13
) with
W_{(i)}(n)
for
i\in[k(n)]
. The Pickands estimator based on the upper
4k(n)
degrees is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Pick}}_{D}(n)=\frac{1}{\log 2}\log\left(\frac{D_{(k(n))}(n)-D_{(2k(n))}(n)}{D_{(2k(n))}(n)-D_{(4k(n))}(n)}\right),

with its weight-based counterpart defined similarly. In either case, the goal is to prove a statement of the form

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{\gamma}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}_{W}(n)\right|\xrightarrow{p}0,\qquad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(14)

For the Hill and Pickands estimators the approximation is handled similarly since both involve a difference of logarithms. A formal statement of (
14
) is given in Lemma
13
. Asymptotic normality of the centered and scaled tail index estimators is again ensured through the second order condition on
U
. That is, by assuming (
8
) and that
k(n)A(n/k_{n})\rightarrow 0
, we have that for
\ell\in\{\text{Hill},\text{Pick}\}

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{W}(n)-\gamma\right)\Rightarrow N\left(0,\tau^{\ell}\right)

(15)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
where
\tau^{\text{Hill}}=\gamma^{2}
and

\displaystyle\tau^{\text{Pick}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}(2^{2\gamma+1}+1)}{4(\log 2)^{2}(2^{\gamma}-1)^{2}}.

See Theorems 3.2.5 and 3.3.5 of
[
21
]
for more details. Since
2(2^{\gamma}-1)^{2}<2^{2\gamma+1}+1
for
\gamma>0
, it is easily seen that
\tau^{\text{Pick}}>\gamma^{2}/2(\log 2)^{2}>\tau^{\text{Hill}}
. The statements (
14
) and (
15
), in conjuction with Slutsky’s theorem, give asymptotic normality of the tail index estimators based on the degrees. Asymptotic normality of the Hill and Pickands estimators for Norros-Reittu and Chung-Lu random graph models is presented in Theorem
2
. Theorem
2
is proved in Section
7.6
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
U
satisfies (
6
) and (
9
).
Suppose that the network is generated under either a Norros-Reittu model with
\alpha>0
or Chung-Lu model with
\alpha>2
. Suppose the second order condition (
8
) holds with
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
,
k(n)\log^{\alpha}(n)/n^{\frac{1}{4\alpha+1}}\rightarrow 0
and
k(n)A(n/k(n))\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then for
\ell\in\{\textup{Hill},\textup{Pick}\}

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{D}(n)-\gamma\right)\Rightarrow N\left(0,\tau^{\ell}\right),\qquad\text{as }n\rightarrow\infty.

(16)

The key finding in Theorem
2
is that despite the degree dependence present in IRGs, the tail index estimators are asymptotically normal with the same asymptotic variance as in the iid case. Though, as we discuss in the conclusion, we do not expect the same result to hold for other scale-free random graph models.

4.2
Probability weighted moment estimator

The degree-based PWM estimator based on the upper
k(n)
degrees is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}^{\text{PWM}}_{D}(n)=\frac{\hat{I}^{(1)}_{D}(n)-4\hat{I}^{(2)}_{D}(n)}{\hat{I}^{(1)}_{D}(n)-2\hat{I}^{(2)}_{D}(n)},

where for
q=1,2

\displaystyle\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)=\frac{1}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\left(\frac{i}{k(n)-1}\right)^{q-1}\left(D_{(i)}(n)-D_{(k(n))}(n)\right).

(17)

As before, the weight-based version is defined by replacing the degree order statistics by the weight order statistics. Typically, asymptotic normality of the PWM estimator is achieved by showing that the probability weighted moments in (
17
) are jointly asymptotically normal and then applying the delta method
[
21
]
. Hence, we assume that the weighted-based probability weighted moments,
(\hat{I}^{(1)}_{W}(n),\hat{I}^{(2)}_{W}(n))
, are asymptotically normal and then approximate the degree-based moments by their weighted counterparts. In particular, we prove in Lemma
14
that for
q\in\{1,2\}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)-\hat{I}^{(q)}_{W}(n)\right|\xrightarrow{p}&0\end{split}

(18)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Asymptotic normality of the centered and scaled
(\hat{I}^{(1)}_{W}(n),\hat{I}^{(2)}_{W}(n))
is again ensured through the second order condition on
U
. That is, by assuming (
8
) and that
k(n)A(n/k_{n})\rightarrow 0
, one may achieve that

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\hat{I}^{(1)}_{W}(n)/a(n/k(n))\\
\hat{I}^{(2)}_{W}(n)/a(n/k(n))\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\\
\frac{1}{2(2-\gamma)}\end{pmatrix}\right)\Rightarrow N\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}\right),

(19)

with
a(t)\equiv\gamma U(t)
for
t>1
and for
q,r\in\{1,2\}

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have taken a vital first step towards rigorously justifying statistical procedures for inferring the tail behavior of degree distributions from scale-free random networks. This is achieved by leveraging and further probing the relationship between the large weights and large degrees in inhomogeneous random graphs. By approximating the upper degree order statistics by their corresponding weight order statistics, we prove the asymptotic normality of three tail index estimators, each of which exhibited behavior analogous to the iid setting. We comment that this behavior likely only holds for the class of IRGs. Simulations not presented here indicate that tail index estimators computed on network models with temporal dependence, such as the preferential attachment network, do not reflect the same asymptotic behavior.

The alignment of upper order statistics provides a general technique for inspecting the performance of extreme-value methods when applied to the degrees from scale-free IRGs. As mentioned earlier, a matter that hinders the application of tail index estimators to real-world networks is choosing
k(n)
. In future work, we aim to prove consistency of a double bootstrap procedure used to estimate the optimal choice of
k(n)

[
15
]
. Simulation results indicate that this procedure performs well for scale-free networks, especially in comparison to other popular methods
[
38
]
. Moreover, we may finally provide a goodness-of-fit test for the scale-free model, the absence of which has led to much controversy in the network science community
[
11
,
26
,
38
]
. In particular, we envision extending the tests of
[
18
]
and
[
28
]
to scale-free networks.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(1-e^{-\frac{E_{i}}{\alpha i}}<\frac{2}{1-\eta}\sqrt{5\log(n)/U\left(e^{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{E_{j}}{j}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(1-e^{-\frac{E_{i}}{\alpha i}}<\frac{2}{1-\eta}\sqrt{5\log(n)/U\left(e^{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{E_{j}}{j}}\right)},A_{i}(n)\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}(A^{c}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(1-e^{-\frac{E_{i}}{\alpha i}}<\frac{2}{1-\eta}\sqrt{5\log(n)/U\left(\frac{1}{a(n)}\frac{n}{i}\right)}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}(A^{c}_{i}(n))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}r_{i}(n)+\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}s_{i}(n).

Define
m_{i}(n)=\frac{2}{1-\eta}\sqrt{5\log(n)/U\left(\frac{1}{a(n)}\frac{n}{i}\right)}
. Note that for
i\in[k(n)]
,
m_{i}(n)\rightarrow 0
since by (
9
) and (
23
), for
n
sufficiently large

\displaystyle U\left(\frac{1}{a(n)}\frac{n}{i}\right)\geq U\left(\frac{1}{a(n)}\frac{n}{k(n)}\right)\geq(1-\eta)U\left(\frac{n}{a(n)k^{4\alpha+1}(n)\log^{\alpha}(n)}\right)\log(n)k^{4}(n).

For
r_{i}(n)
, suppose that
n
is large enough so that
m_{k(n)}(n)<1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}r_{i}(n)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(e^{\frac{E_{i}}{\alpha i}}<(1-m_{i}(n))^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\left(1-\left(1-m_{i}(n)\right)^{\alpha i}\right),

and by the mean value theorem

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor}\left(1-\left(1-m_{i}(n)\right)^{\alpha i}\right)+\alpha\sum_{i=\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor+1}^{k(n)}im_{i}(n)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor\left(1-\left(1-m_{k(n)}(n)\right)\right)+\alpha\sum_{i=\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor+1}^{k(n)}im_{i}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor m_{k(n)}(n)+\alpha\sum_{i=\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor+1}^{k(n)}im_{i}(n).

By (
9
),
U\left(\frac{1}{a(n)}\frac{n}{i}\right)=U\left(t_{0}\frac{1}{a(n)t_{0}}\frac{n}{i}\right)\geq(1-\eta)\left(\frac{1}{a(n)t_{0}}\frac{n}{i}\right)^{1/\alpha}U(t_{0})
for
n
sufficiently large and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}r_{i}(n)\leq

\displaystyle\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor m_{k(n)}(n)+\alpha\sum_{i=\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor+1}^{k(n)}im_{i}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-\eta}}\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor\sqrt{5t_{0}^{1/\alpha}\log(n)\left(a(n)\frac{k(n)}{n}\right)^{1/\alpha}/U(t_{0})}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{\sqrt{1-\eta}}\alpha\sum_{i=\lfloor 1/\alpha\rfloor+1}^{k(n)}i\sqrt{5t_{0}^{1/\alpha}\log(n)\left(a(n)\frac{i}{n}\right)^{1/\alpha}/U(t_{0})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\sqrt{\log(n)\left(a(n)\frac{k(n)}{n}\right)^{1/\alpha}}\right)+O\left(\sqrt{a^{1/\alpha}(n)\log(n)\frac{k(n)^{\frac{4\alpha+1}{\alpha}}}{n^{1/\alpha}}}\right)

Since
a(n)=o\left(n/\left(\log^{\alpha}(n)k^{4\alpha+1}(n)\right)\right)
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}r_{i}(n)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We now turn to the terms
s_{i}(n)
. Define
\epsilon_{i}(n)=\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{1}{j}-\log(n/ia(n))
. Note that we may invoke Lemma
3
since
\epsilon_{i}(n)\geq\log(\frac{n+1}{n}a(n))\rightarrow\infty
and thus for
n
sufficiently large

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}s_{i}(n)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(U\left(e^{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{E_{j}}{j}}\right)<U\left(\frac{1}{a(n)}\frac{n}{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(U\left(e^{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{E_{j}}{j}}\right)<U\left(e^{\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{1}{j}-\left(\sum_{j=i}^{n}\frac{1}{j}-\log(\frac{n}{a(n)i}\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}2\exp\left\{-\frac{i}{4}\log(a(n))\right\},

and for
n
large enough so that
a(n)>1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\left(-1+\frac{1}{1-a^{-\frac{1}{4}}(n)}\right)=2\left(\frac{a^{-\frac{1}{4}}(n)}{1-a^{-\frac{1}{4}}(n)}\right).

Thus
\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}s_{i}(n)\rightarrow 0
and the proof is complete.
∎

7.4
Degree concentration

Recall that in order to achieve the alignment of the upper order statistics, we require that the degrees of nodes with large weights sufficiently concentrate around said weights. This section develops concentration inequalities for the degrees in the Norros-Reittu and Chung-Lu models. The first result concerns the rate at which
D_{i}(n)
concentrates around
W_{(i)}(n)
for
i\in[n]
in the Norros-Reittu model. It is a straight-forward application of Poisson concentration results for large rates
[see
10
,
32
, for example]

Lemma 6
.

For any
i\in[n]
, under the Norros-Reittu model

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\geq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\right)\leq n^{-5/4}.

(24)

Proof.

See that for any
i\in[n]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\geq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\frac{\left(\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)^{2}}{2\left(W_{(i)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)}\right\}.

When
W_{(i)}(n)>5\log(n)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)^{2}}{2\left(W_{(i)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)}=

\displaystyle\frac{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}{2\left(W_{(i)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\right)}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}{4W_{(i)}(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{5}{4}\log(n).

On the other hand, when
W_{(i)}(n)\leq 5\log(n)

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)^{2}}{2\left(W_{(i)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)}=

\displaystyle\frac{5^{2}\log^{2}(n)}{2\left(W_{(i)}(n)+5\log(n)\right)}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{5^{2}\log^{2}(n)}{20\log(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{5}{4}\log(n).

Hence, overall

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\geq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\right)\leq n^{-5/4}.

∎

From Lemmas

Lemma 7

Assume that

Proof.

Note that from Lemma

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\geq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)},W_{(k(n))}(n)\geq 5\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\geq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)},W_{(i)}(n)\geq 5\log(n)\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}\left(\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\geq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\mid\{W_{(i)}(n)\}_{i\in[n]}\right)1_{\left\{W_{(i)}(n)\geq 5\log(n)\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[n^{-5/4}1_{\left\{W_{(i)}(n)\geq 5\log(n)\right\}}\right]

Lemma 8

7.5
Upper order statistic alignment

We may now combine Lemmas
5
,
7
and

to state a general result about the upper
k(n)
node degrees drawn from either the Norros-Reittu model or the Chung-Lu model with
\alpha>2
.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
U
satisfies (
6
) and (
9
). Assume that the network is generated under either the Norros-Reittu random graph with
\alpha>0
or the Chung-Lu random graph with
\alpha>2
. Suppose
k(n)
is such that
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
and
k(n)\log^{\alpha}(n)/n^{\frac{1}{4\alpha+1}}\rightarrow 0
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{1}(n)>D_{2}(n)>\dots>D_{k(n)}(n)\right)\rightarrow 1,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

Simply note that

\displaystyle\left\{D_{1}(n)>D_{2}(n)>\dots>D_{k(n)}(n)\right\}\supset

\displaystyle\left\{\forall i\in[k(n)],\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|<\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\right\}\cap

\displaystyle\left\{\forall i\in[k(n)],W_{(i)}(n)-W_{(i+1)}(n)>2\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\right\}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle C(n)\cap S(n),

since on
C(n)
and

we have that for all

\displaystyle D_{i}(n)-D_{i+1}(n)>

\displaystyle W_{(i)}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}-W_{(i+1)}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i+1)}(n)}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle W_{(i)}(n)-W_{(i+1)}(n)-2\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle 0.

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{D_{1}(n)>D_{2}(n)>\dots>D_{k(n)}(n)\right\}^{c}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(C^{c}(n)\cup S^{c}(n)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(C^{c}(n)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(S^{c}(n)\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where we have applied Lemma
5
as well as Lemma
7
in the Chung-Lu case and Lemma
9
in the Norros-Reittu case.
∎

The last step towards proving alignment of upper order statistics is to ensure that no degree

,

, exceeds

. This is proved in the following lemma

Lemma 12
.

Assume that
U
satisfies (
6
) and (

). Assume that the network is generated under either the Norros-Reittu model with

or the Chung-Lu model with

. Suppose

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{k(n)}(n)>\max_{i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]}D_{i}(n)\right)\rightarrow 1.

We aim to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{k(n)}(n)\leq\max_{i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]}D_{i}(n)\right)\rightarrow 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty

Define the events

\displaystyle B_{1}(n)=

\displaystyle\left\{\max_{i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]}D_{i}(n)<W_{k(n)+1}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{k(n)+1}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right\}

\displaystyle\left\{D_{k(n)}(n)>W_{(k(n))}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B_{1}^{c}(n)\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]:D_{i}(n)\geq W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\exists i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]:D_{i}(n)\geq W_{(i)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle 0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In addition, Lemmas

and

give that

as

. Hence, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W_{(k(n))}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}\leq W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)\rightarrow 0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(W_{(k(n))}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}\leq W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)+\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)}\vee 5\log(n)\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)\leq 2\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(W_{(k(n)+1)}(n)<5\log(n)\right).

Both terms in the previous display converge to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
by Lemmas
5
and
4
respectively. Hence the proof is complete.
∎

We are now able to prove the main result, Theorem
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Simply notice that the event

\displaystyle\left\{\left(R_{1}(n),R_{2}(n),\dots,R_{k(n)}(n)\right)=\left(1,2,\dots,k(n)\right)\right\}

contains the event

\displaystyle\left\{D_{1}(n)>D_{2}(n)>\dots>D_{k(n)}(n)\right\}\cap\left\{D_{k(n)}(n)>\max_{i\in[n]\setminus[k(n)]}D_{i}(n)\right\},

and apply Theorem
5
and Lemma
12
.
∎

7.6
Approximating tail index estimators

In this section we provide proofs showing that the tail index estimators based on the degrees may be approximated by their weight-based counterparts under the alignment of upper order statistics. We first prove the approximation for the Hill and Pickands estimators.

Lemma 13
.

Assume that
U
satisfies (
6
) and (
9
). Suppose the network is generated under either the Norros-Reittu model with
\alpha>0
or the Chung-Lu model with
\alpha>2
. Let
k(n)
be such that
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
,
k(n)\log^{\alpha}(n)/n^{\frac{1}{4\alpha+1}}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then for
\ell\in\{\textup{Hill},\textup{Pick}\}

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{W}(n)\right|\xrightarrow{p}0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

For both tail index estimators, we aim to prove that for any
\epsilon>0
, as
n\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{\gamma}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}_{W}(n)\right|>\epsilon\right)\rightarrow 0.

It suffices to prove that as
n\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{\gamma}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}_{W}(n)\right|>\epsilon,S(n),C(n),M(n)\right)\rightarrow 0.

since
P(S^{c}(n))
,
P(C^{c}(n))
and
P(M^{c}(n))
all tend towards zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
by Lemmas
5
,
7
,
9
and
8
. We begin with the Hill estimator. Recall that on
S(n)\cap C(n)\cap M(n)
,
\left(D_{(1)}(n),\dots,D_{(k(n))}(n)\right)=\left(D_{1}(n),\dots,D_{k(n)}(n)\right)
so that

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{W}(n)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\log\left(D_{i}(n)/W_{(i)}(n)\right)-\sqrt{k(n)}\log\left(D_{k(n)}(n)/W_{(k(n))}(n)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(n)-1}}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\left|\log\left(D_{i}(n)/W_{(i)}(n)\right)\right|+\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\log\left(D_{k(n)}(n)/W_{(k(n))}(n)\right)\right|.

Note that for
x,y\geq 0
, if
x>y

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\right|=\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{x-y}{y}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{x-y}{y}=\frac{|x-y|}{x\wedge y}.

(25)

On the other hand, if
y>x

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)\right|=\left|-\log\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\right|=\log\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{y-x}{x}\right)\leq\frac{y-x}{x}=\frac{|x-y|}{x\wedge y}.

(26)

Hence on
S(n)\cap C(n)\cap M(n)
we may further bound

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{W}(n)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\frac{\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|}{D_{i}(n)\wedge W_{(i)}(n)}+\sqrt{k(n)}\frac{\left|D_{k(n)}(n)-W_{(k(n))}(n)\right|}{D_{k(n)}(n)\wedge W_{(k(n))}(n)},

and on
C(n)
specifically, we have that
\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|\leq\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}
so that

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\frac{\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}}{W_{(i)}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(i)}(n)}}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{k(n)}\frac{\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}}{W_{(k(n))}(n)-\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{W_{(i)}(n)}{5\log(n)}}-1}+\sqrt{k(n)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{W_{(k(n))}(n)}{5\log(n)}}-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sqrt{k(n)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{W_{(k(n))}(n)}{5\log(n)}}-1}.

In summary, we have achieved that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Hill}}_{W}(n)\right|>\epsilon,S(n),C(n),M(n)\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(2\sqrt{k(n)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{W_{(k(n))}(n)}{5\log(n)}}-1}>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{W_{(k(n))}(n)}{5k(n)\log(n)}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(n)}}<2\epsilon^{-1}\right),

Hence it suffices that
W_{(k(n))}(n)/k(n)\log(n)\xrightarrow{p}\infty
. Since
W_{(k(n))}(n)/U(n/k(n))\xrightarrow{p}1

[
35
, see Theorem 4.2 of]
, it suffices that
U(n/k(n))/k(n)\log(n)\rightarrow\infty
. Using (
9
), this is easily seen to hold under growth rate restrictions on
k(n)
.

We now follow a similar strategy for the Pickands estimator. Refine
S(n)
,
C(n)
and
M(n)
by employing the upper
4k(n)
order statistics instead of
k(n)
. Again, on
S(n)\cap C(n)\cap M(n)
,
\allowbreak\left(D_{(1)}(n),\dots,D_{(4k(n))}(n)\right)=\left(D_{1}(n),\dots,D_{4k(n)}(n)\right)
so that

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Pick}}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Pick}}_{W}(n)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{\log 2}\left|\log\left(\frac{D_{k(n)}(n)-D_{2k(n)}(n)}{W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)}\right)\right|+\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{\log 2}\left|\log\left(\frac{D_{2k(n)}(n)-D_{4k(n)}(n)}{W_{(2k(n))}(n)-W_{(4k(n))}(n)}\right)\right|.

Further, using (
25
) and (
26
)

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{D_{k(n)}(n)-D_{2k(n)}(n)}{W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)}\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\left|D_{k(n)}(n)-W_{(k(n))}(n)\right|+\left|D_{2k(n)}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)\right|}{\left(D_{k(n)}(n)-D_{2k(n)}(n)\right)\wedge\left(W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)\right)},

and on
C(n)
we have that

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}}{W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)-2\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}}.

In addition, on
S(n)

\displaystyle W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)=\sum_{i=k(n)}^{2k(n)-1}\left(W_{(i)}(n)-W_{(i+1)}(n)\right)\geq 2k(n)\sqrt{5\log(n)W_{(2k(n))}(n)},

so that

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{D_{k(n)}(n)-D_{2k(n)}(n)}{W_{(k(n))}(n)-W_{(2k(n))}(n)}\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{W_{(k(n))}(n)/W_{(2k(n))}(n)}}{k(n)-\sqrt{W_{(k(n))}(n)/W_{(2k(n))}(n)}}\equiv J_{1}(n).

In a similar fashion, it can be shown that on
S(n)\cap C(n)\cap M(n)
,

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{D_{2k(n)}(n)-D_{4k(n)}(n)}{W_{(2k(n))}(n)-W_{(4k(n))}(n)}\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{W_{(2k(n))}(n)/W_{(4k(n))}(n)}}{2k(n)-\sqrt{W_{(2k(n))}(n)/W_{(4k(n))}(n)}}\equiv J_{2}(n).

Hence, in summary, we have shown that for the Pickands estimator

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Pick}}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\text{Pick}}_{W}(n)\right|>\epsilon,S(n),C(n),M(n)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{k(n)}(J_{1}(n)+J_{2}(n))/\log 2>\epsilon\right).

It is straightforwardly seen that
W_{(k(n))}(n)/W_{(2k(n))}(n)\xrightarrow{p}2^{\gamma}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
so that
\sqrt{k(n)}J_{1}(n),\sqrt{k(n)}J_{2}(n)\xrightarrow{p}0
and the proof is complete.
∎

We may now prove the main result regarding the Hill and Pickands estimator, Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

For
\ell\in\{\text{Hill},\text{Pick}\}
, simply rewrite

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{D}(n)-\gamma\right)=\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{D}(n)-\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{W}(n)\right)+\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\ell}_{W}(n)-\gamma\right).

The first converges in probability to zero by Lemma
13
, while the second is asymptotically normal by Theorems 3.2.5 and 3.3.5 of
[
21
]
. Hence, we may apply Slutsky’s Theorem to achieve the result.
∎

The approximation of the probability-weighted moment estimator requires a slightly different proof strategy. Instead of approximating the degree-based estimator directly, we approximate the probability-weighted moment estimates. We may then apply standard techniques to achieve asymptotic normality of the degree-based PWM estimator.

Lemma 14
.

Assume that
U
satisfies (
6
) and (
9
). Assume that the network is generated under either the Norros-Reittu or Chung-Lu model with
\alpha>2
. Let
k(n)
be such that
k(n)\rightarrow\infty
,
k(n)\log^{\alpha}(n)/n^{\frac{1}{4\alpha+1}}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then for
q\in\{1,2\}

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}-\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\right|\xrightarrow{p}0,

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that as
n\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}-\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\right|>\epsilon,S(n),C(n),M(n)\right)\rightarrow 0.

since
P(S^{c}(n))
,
P(C^{c}(n))
and
P(M^{c}(n))
all tend towards zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
by Lemmas
5
,
7
,
9
and
8
. Of course on
S(n)\cap C(n)\cap M(n)
we have

\displaystyle\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)=\frac{1}{k(n)-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\left(\frac{i}{k(n)-1}\right)^{q-1}\left(D_{i}(n)-D_{k(n)}(n)\right),

so that

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}-\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{a(n/k(n))(k(n)-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\left(\frac{i}{k(n)-1}\right)^{q-1}\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{a(n/k(n))(k(n)-1)}\left|D_{k(n)}(n)-W_{(k(n))}(n)\right|\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\left(\frac{i}{k(n)-1}\right)^{q-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{a(n/k(n))(k(n)-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\left|D_{i}(n)-W_{(i)}(n)\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{k(n)}}{a(n/k(n))}\left|D_{k(n)}(n)-W_{(k(n))}(n)\right|.

Further, we may employ the concentration bound on
C(n)
to achieve that

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}-\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{5k(n)\log(n)}}{a(n/k(n))(k(n)-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)-1}\sqrt{W_{(i)}(n)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\sqrt{5k(n)\log(n)W_{(k(n))}(n)}}{a(n/k(n))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{\sqrt{5k(n)\log(n)W_{(1)}(n)}}{a(n/k(n))}.

In summary, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{k(n)}\left|\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}-\frac{\hat{I}^{(q)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\right|>\epsilon,S(n),C(n),M(n)\right)\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(2\frac{\sqrt{5k(n)\log(n)W_{(1)}(n)}}{a(n/k(n))}>\epsilon\right).

Since
\gamma W_{(1)}(n)/a(n)=O_{p}(1)
it suffices that
\sqrt{k(n)a(n)\log(n)}/a(n/k(n))=o(1)
and using (
9
) it further suffices that
\sqrt{k(n)\log(n)a(n)}k^{1/\alpha}(n)/n^{1/\alpha}=o(1)
. This is easily achieved when
\alpha>2
and thus the proof is complete.
∎

We may now prove the main result regarding the PWM estimator, Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

We may rewrite

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\hat{I}^{(1)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\\
\frac{\hat{I}^{(2)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\\
\frac{1}{2(2-\gamma)}\end{pmatrix}\right)=

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\hat{I}^{(1)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\\
\frac{\hat{I}^{(2)}_{D}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\hat{I}^{(1)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\\
\frac{\hat{I}^{(2)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\hat{I}^{(1)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\\
\frac{\hat{I}^{(2)}_{W}(n)}{a(n/k(n))}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\\
\frac{1}{2(2-\gamma)}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Note that under the Euclidean norm, the first term converges in probability to zero as
n\rightarrow\infty
by Lemma
14
. The second term is asymptotically bivariate normal by Proposition 4.1 of
[
17
]
. Hence, we may apply the converging together theorem to achieve that

\displaystyle\sqrt{k(n)}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\hat{I}^{(1)}_{D}(n)/a(n/k(n))\\
\hat{I}^{(2)}_{D}(n)/a(n/k(n))\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\\
\frac{1}{2(2-\gamma)}\end{pmatrix}\right)\Rightarrow N\left(\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{11}&\sigma_{12}\\
\sigma_{21}&\sigma_{22}\end{pmatrix}\right),

with covariance matrix as determined in (
19
). Finally, applying the delta method gives the result.
∎
