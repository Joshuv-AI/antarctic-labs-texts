Abstract

The aim of this work is to study the Airy and Schrödinger operators on
looping-edge graphs, a class of metric graphs consisting of a circle and
a finite number
N
of infinite half-lines attached to a common vertex.
For the Airy operator, we characterize all extensions generating unitary
and contractive dynamics in terms of self-orthogonal subspaces and linear
operators acting on indefinite inner product spaces (Krein spaces)
associated to the boundary values at the vertex. Employing similar
abstract techniques, we then describe a systematic way to produce self-adjoint extensions of the Schrödinger operator that are compatible with prescribed boundary relations on looping-edge and
\mathcal{T}
-shaped graphs.

35Q51, 35Q55, 81Q35, 35R02; Secondary 47E05.

. Schrödinger equation, Airy equation, quantum graphs, Krein spaces, extension theory of symmetric operators.

1.
Introduction

Nonlinear evolution models on metric graphs arise as models in wave
propagation, for instance, in quasi-one-dimensional systems (e.g.,
meso- or nanoscale) that resemble a thin neighborhood of a graph. These
models provide a convenient means to study various physical effects in
the real world, both from theoretical and experimental perspectives. The
flexibility in setting the geometry of the graph configuration allows for
the creation of different dynamics. To mention a few examples of
bifurcated systems: Josephson junction structures, networks of planar
optical waveguides and fibers, branched structures associated with DNA,
blood pressure waves in large arteries, nerve impulses in complex arrays
of neurons, conducting polymers, and Bose-Einstein condensation (see

Recently, nonlinear models on graphs, such as the nonlinear
Schrödinger equation, the sine-Gordon model, and the Korteweg-de Vries
model, have been studied extensively (see
[

equivalent to a system of PDEs defined on the edges (intervals), where
the coupling is determined exclusively by the boundary conditions at the
vertices (known as the “topology of the graph”), which governs the
dynamics on the network via groups. This area of study has attracted
significant attention, particularly in the context of soliton transport.
Solitons and other nonlinear waves in branched systems provide valuable
insights into the dynamics of these models.

Addressing these issues is challenging because both the equations of
motion and the graph topology can be complex. Additionally, a critical
aspect that complicates the analysis is the presence of one or more
vertices where a soliton profile, arriving at the vertex along one of
the edges, undergoes complicated behaviors such as reflection and the
emergence of radiation. This makes it difficult to observe how energy
travels across the network. As a result, the study of soliton
propagation through networks presents significant challenges. The
mechanisms for the existence and stability (or instability) of soliton
profiles remain unclear for many types of graphs and models.

We recall that a metric graph

A wave function

The primary aim of this manuscript is to develop a self-contained and
systematic theory of linear dynamics for both the Airy and the
Schrödinger operators on looping-edge graphs, intended to serve as a
reference for future nonlinear applications. We emphasize that this
abstract theory is presented as a standalone contribution: the dynamical
part is established here independently, so that subsequent studies on
existence, stability, controllability, or stabilization of nonlinear
waves on these graphs can build upon it without having to reconstruct
the linear theory from scratch.

For the Airy operator, to the best of our knowledge, no results on the
characterization of linear dynamics on looping-edge graphs appear in
the existing literature. We fill this gap by providing, in terms of
Krein space techniques, a complete description of all boundary conditions
at the vertex that make the Airy operator the generator of a unitary
group or a contraction semigroup. For the Schrödinger operator, our
approach offers an additional structural advantage compared to the classical methods: it allows one to
prescribe a priori the type of vertex interactions desired (for
instance, continuity of derivatives at the vertex) and then to
systematically identify all self-adjoint extensions compatible with
those conditions. In this way the theory does not merely produce a
parameter family of extensions, but organizes them according to
physically or geometrically motivated constraints.

Subsequently, Angulo and Cavalcante

and bump profiles on balanced graphs with specific boundary conditions

investigated the existence of solitary waves on a

(\alpha_{\mathbf{e}})_{e\in E}

(\beta_{\mathbf{e}})_{e\in E}

on each edge, with coupling conditions at the vertex
\nu=0

[

Results on the dynamics of the KdV model on looping-edge graphs, such
as local well-posedness of the Cauchy problem and existence and stability
of solitary waves, are not known in the literature to the best of the
authors’ knowledge. The theory developed in this manuscript is intended
to fill that gap at the linear level and to lay the groundwork for
future nonlinear investigations. Several applications are already
emerging from the framework presented here. On the one hand, the
boundary system based on separation of first derivatives, developed in
Section 4.3, has already proven effective in the analysis of dispersive
equations on metric graphs: the technique was used in
[
42
]
to
construct explicit solitary-wave solutions for the KdV equation on
star graphs, where the first-derivative coupling at the vertex plays a
distinguished role. Moreover, the domains generated via separation of
derivatives in Example

directly recover the looping-edge analogue to the vertex conditions employed in

[
6
,
48
]

controllability of the KdV on networks with bounded and unbounded domains. In general these abstract theory can be used to yield the class of boundary conditions needed for controllability
problems on metric graphs, in fact the class of extensions
A_{Y,R_{1}}
constitute
the functional-analytic backbone of an ongoing program on exponential
stabilization and controllability of the KdV equation on
\mathcal{G}
,
currently in an advanced stage of research. As a concrete illustration of the use of contractive dynamics, in
Section
4.4
we show that adding a damping term
-\gamma\mathbf{U}

to the Airy evolution under the boundary conditions of
Example

yields exponential decay of the

at rate
\gamma

boundary structure rather than from the dispersive coefficients.

Organization of the article.

Section 2 collects the necessary preliminaries, including notation,
the Stone and Lummer–Phillips theorems, and the relevant theory of
operators on Krein spaces. Section 3 analyzes the free Airy operator
and computes its deficiency indices on the looping-edge graph.
Section 4 is the core of the paper and is devoted to the Airy operator
on looping-edge graphs. It is divided into three parts:
Section 4.1 treats the case of an even number of half-lines and
characterizes extensions generating unitary dynamics;
Section 4.2 handles an arbitrary number of half-lines and
characterizes extensions generating contractive dynamics;
Section 4.3 develops an alternative boundary system based on
the separation of derivatives, providing a further class of
extensions with contractive dynamics.
Finally, Section 5 applies the same abstract framework to the
Schrödinger operator on looping-edge graphs, characterizing
its self-adjoint extensions and the associated unitary dynamics.

2.1.
Notation

Let us introduce some notation related to the boundary values of a function posed in
\mathcal{G}
. For
\textbf{U}=(\phi,\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{N})
, we denote the vector of values of
U
and its derivatives at the vertex with

\begin{split}\ &\vec{\mathbf{U}}:=\vec{\mathbf{U}}(L)=(\phi(-L),\ \phi(L),\ \psi_{1}(L),\ \dots,\psi_{N}(L))^{T},\\
\ &\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}:=\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}(L):=(\phi^{\prime}(-L),\ \phi^{\prime}(L),\ \psi_{1}^{\prime}(L),\ \dots,\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L))^{T},\\
\ &\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}:=\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}(L):=(\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L),\ \phi^{\prime\prime}(L),\ \psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(L),\ \dots,\psi_{N}^{\prime\prime}(L))^{T}.\end{split}

For the components of
U
, we denote the derivatives values at the vertex with

\partial\phi(-L):=\begin{pmatrix}\phi(-L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)\end{pmatrix},\ \partial\phi(L):=\begin{pmatrix}\phi(L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\mbox{ and }\partial\psi_{j}(L)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{j}(L)\\
\psi_{j}^{\prime}(L)\\
\psi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}.

We adopt concatenation of vectors in vertical stack. For instance,

\begin{pmatrix}\partial\phi(-L)\\
\partial\phi(L)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi(-L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)\\
\phi(L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}.

For convenience we denote for
\psi=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{N})

(2.1)

\partial\psi(L):=\begin{pmatrix}\partial\psi_{1}(L)\\
\partial\psi_{2}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\partial\psi_{N}(L)\end{pmatrix},\ \partial\psi_{even}(L):=\begin{pmatrix}\partial\psi_{2}(L)\\
\partial\psi_{4}(L)\\
\vdots\\

2.2.
Stone and Lumer-Phillips Theorems

In this subsections we recall some well-known features of group theory.

Let
X
be a Banack space and
X^{\prime}
its dual space. Consider
\mathcal{J}(x):=\{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}\mid\langle x,x^{\prime}\rangle=\|x\|^{2}=\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}\}
. Note
\mathcal{J}(x)
is non-empty by Hahn-Banach Theorem. Moreover, if
X
is a Hilbert space then
\mathcal{J}(x)
consists of a single element.

Recall that a linear operator
(A,D(A))
on a Banach space
X
is called dissipative if
\|(\lambda-A)x\|\geqslant\lambda\|x\|
.

Proposition 2.1
.

An operator
(A,D(A))
is dissipative if and only if for every
x\in D(A)
there exists
j(x)\in\mathcal{J}(x)
such that

(2.2)

\mbox{Re}\langle Ax,j(x)\rangle\leqslant 0.

If
A
is the generator of a strongly continuous contraction semigroup, then (
2.2
) holds for all
x\in D(A)
and arbitrary
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{J}(x)
.

Proof.

See Proposition 3.23 in
[
28
]

∎

Note in the case
X
is a Hilbert space, condition (
2.2
) reduces to
\mbox{Re}(Ax\mid x)\leqslant 0
.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Lummer-Phillips)
Let
(A,D(A))
be a densely defined operator on a Banach space
X
. If both,
A
and
A^{*}
are dissipative, then the closure
\overline{A}
of
A
generates a contraction semigroup on
X
.

Proof.

See Corollary 3.17 in
[
28
]
.
∎

We also recall the Stone’s theorem for unitary groups.

Theorem 2.3

(Stone)
.

Let
(A,D(A))
be a densely defined operator on a Hilbert space
H
. Then
A
generates a unitary group
(T(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on
H
if and only if
A
is skew-self-adjoint, i.e.,
A^{*}=-A
and
D(A^{*})=D(A)
.

2.3.
Operators on Krein Spaces

Following
[
49
]
we introduce the abstract notions of self-orthogonal subspaces and operators based on indefinite inner product spaces.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Y
be a linear space, let
X\subset Y
be a subspace and
w
a sesquilinear form defined on
(Y\times Y)^{2}
. The space
X
is called
w
-self-orthogonal if

(2.3)

X=X^{\perp_{w}}\equiv\{(x,y)\in Y\times Y\mid w((x,y),(\tilde{x},\tilde{y}))=0\mbox{ for all }(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})\in X\}.

Definition 2.2
.

A Krein space
(\mathcal{K},(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{\mathcal{K}})
is a complex linear space
\mathcal{K}
on which a indefinite inner product
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{\mathcal{K}}
is defined, namely, the function
(x,y)\in\mathcal{K}\times\mathcal{K}\to(x\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies the following axioms:

i)

Linearity in the first argument:

(\alpha x_{1}+\beta x_{2}\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}}=\alpha(x_{1}\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}}+\beta(x_{2}\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}}

for all
x_{1},x_{2},y\in\mathcal{K}
and all complex numbers
\alpha,\beta
;

ii)

antisymmetry:

(x\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}}=\overline{(y\mid x)_{\mathcal{K}}}

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{K}
;

iii)

nondegeneracy: if
(x\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}}=0
for all
y\in\mathcal{K}
, then
x=0
.

Thus, the function
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{\mathcal{K}}
satisfies all the properties of a standard inner product
with the possible exception that
(x\mid x)_{\mathcal{K}}
may be nonpositive for
x\neq 0
.

We recall that if
dim(\mathcal{K})=n
(
\mathcal{K}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
) every indefinite inner
product
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{\mathcal{K}}
on
\mathcal{K}

4.1.1.
Extensions generating unitary dynamics

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

.
X
is
w
-self-orthogonal if and only if there is a
(\mathcal{B}_{+},\mathcal{B}_{-})
-unitary operator
\mathcal{L}
such that

.

Proof.

We follow the same ideas of
[
41
]
. For sake of completeness we carry on with the details. Let
X
be
w
-self-orthogonal and let
(x,y)\in X
. By definition we have
w((x,y),(x,y))=0
, that is,

(4.11)

(x\mid x)_{+}=(y\mid y)_{-}.

Since
X
is a subspace, by setting
x=0
we get
0=(y\mid y)_{-}
. Since
(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{-}
is non-degenerated it must be the case
y=0
. By linearity we can conclude that
X
is the graph of a linear operator
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{B}_{+}\to\mathcal{B}_{-}
. Also note that if
y=0
it also implies
x=0
; which means
\mathcal{L}
is injective.

To see
\mathcal{L}
is densely defined, let
z\in D(\mathcal{L})^{\perp}
and
x\in D(\mathcal{L})
. We have

(4.12)

0=(z\mid x)=(B_{+}B_{+}^{-1}z\mid x)=(B_{+}^{-1}z\mid x)_{+}=w((B_{+}^{-1}z,0),(x,\mathcal{L}x)).

Since
x\in D(\mathcal{L})
is arbitrary, we conclude
(B_{+}^{-1}z,0)\in X^{\perp_{w}}=X=G(\mathcal{L})
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is injective, it must be the case
B^{-1}_{+}z=0
. The latter implies
z=0
. A symmetric argument shows the image
R(\mathcal{L})
is dense.

Let us now see
\mathcal{L}^{\#}=L^{-1}
. First note for
x\in D(\mathcal{L})
and
y\in R(\mathcal{L})
, from (
4.11
), we have

(\mathcal{L}x\mid y)_{-}=(x\mid\mathcal{L}^{-1}y)_{+}.

The latter implies
R(\mathcal{L})\subset D(\mathcal{L}^{\#})
with
\mathcal{L}^{\#}y=\mathcal{L}^{-1}y
;
i.e.
,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\#}
. On the other hand, if
y\in D(\mathcal{L}^{\#})
with
\mathcal{L}^{\#}y=x
we have

(\mathcal{L}z\mid y)_{-}=(z\mid x)_{+}\ \ \mbox{for all }z\in D(\mathcal{L}).

Note the latter is equivalent to say

w((z,\mathcal{L}z),(x,y))=0\ \mbox{ for all }z\in D(\mathcal{L}),

which implies
(x,y)\in X^{\perp_{w}}=X=G(\mathcal{L})
. Thus,
(y,x)\in G(\mathcal{L}^{-1})
. We conclude
\mathcal{L}^{-1}=\mathcal{L}^{\#}
; proving
\mathcal{L}
is unitary.

Conversely, let
\mathcal{L}
be a
(\mathcal{B}_{+},\mathcal{B}_{-})
-unitary operator with
X=G(\mathcal{L})
. Let
x\in D(\mathcal{L})
. For any
z\in D(\mathcal{L})
we have
(\mathcal{L}x\mid\mathcal{L}z)_{-}=(x\mid z)_{+}
. That is,
w((x,\mathcal{L}x),(z,\mathcal{L}z))=0
for any
z\in D(\mathcal{L})
. We conclude
(x,\mathcal{L}x)\in X^{\perp_{w}}
,
i.e.
,
X\subset X^{\perp_{w}}
. On the other hand, if
(x,y)\in X^{\perp_{w}}
we have
w((x,y),(z,\mathcal{L}z))=0
for all
z\in D(\mathcal{L})
,
i.e.
,
(\mathcal{L}z\mid y)_{-}=(z\mid x)_{+}
for all
z\in D(\mathcal{L})
. By definition of
\mathcal{L}^{\#}
the latter implies
y\in D(\mathcal{L}^{\#})=D(\mathcal{L}^{-1})
with
\mathcal{L}^{-1}y=\mathcal{L}^{\#}y=x
. We conclude
(x,y)\in G(\mathcal{L})=X
. Hence
X=X^{\perp_{w}}
as desired.
∎

.

4.2.
Boundary system for arbitrary amount of half-lines

For this part we no longer seek unitary dynamics. We allow the dynamics to be
generated by a
C_{0}
-semigroup of contractions. We consider
\mathcal{G}

consisting of a finite amount
N
of half-lines attached to the vertex
L
,
with no restrictions on the signs of the coefficients of the Hamiltonian. We
still virtually treat
-L
as a terminal vertex.

Definition 4.4
.

Consider the spaces
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cong\mathcal{C}_{0}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3(N+1)}

equipped with the non-degenerate indefinite inner products

(4.24)

(x\mid y)_{1}:=(C_{1}\,x\mid y),\qquad(x\mid y)_{0}:=(C_{0}\,x\mid y),

where

C_{1}:=\operatorname{diag}(B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{N}),\qquad C_{0}:=\frac{1}{N+1}\operatorname{diag}(B_{0},B_{0},\dots,B_{0}).

Both
(\mathcal{C}_{1},(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{1})
and
(\mathcal{C}_{0},(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{0})

are Krein spaces.

4.2.1.
Boundary trace maps and the Green identity

We introduce boundary trace maps
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{0}:D(A_{0}^{*})\to\mathbb{C}^{3(N+1)}
by

(4.25)

\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}:=\begin{pmatrix}\partial\phi(-L)\\[2.0pt]
\partial\psi_{1}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\partial\psi_{N}(L)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}_{1},\qquad\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}:=\begin{pmatrix}\partial\phi(L)\\
\partial\phi(L)\\
\vdots\\
\partial\phi(L)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}.

In terms of these maps, the Green identity for
A_{0}^{*}
reads

(4.26)

[A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}]+[\mathbf{U},A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V}]=(\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}\mid\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{V})_{1}-(\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}\mid\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{V})_{0}.

4.2.2.
Boundary relations, Krein adjoints, and contractive extensions

Definition 4.5
.

A
boundary relation
is a linear subspace
R\subset\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{0}
.
Its
Krein adjoint

R^{[*]}\subset\mathcal{C}_{0}\times\mathcal{C}_{1}
is defined by

(4.27)

(y,x)\in R^{[*]}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;(y_{1}\mid y)_{0}=(x_{1}\mid x)_{1}\quad\text{for all }(x_{1},y_{1})\in R.

The relation
R
is called a
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{0})
-contraction
if

(4.28)

(y\mid y)_{0}\leqslant(x\mid x)_{1}\qquad\text{for all }(x,y)\in R.

When
R=\operatorname{graph}(\mathcal{L})
for a linear map

\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{0}
, the Krein adjoint satisfies

R^{[*]}=\operatorname{graph}(\mathcal{L}^{\#})
with

(4.29)

\mathcal{L}^{\#}:=C_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}C_{0},

and contractivity of
R
reduces to
\mathcal{L}^{*}C_{0}\mathcal{L}-C_{1}\preceq 0
.

Remark 1
.

The
multivalued part
of
R
is

\operatorname{mul}(R):=\{y:(0,y)\in R\}
.
Whenever
\operatorname{mul}(R)\neq\{0\}
, the relation
R
is not the graph of
any operator, and no matrix
\mathcal{L}
can encode the corresponding
boundary conditions. Concretely,
(0,y)\in R
imposes

\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}=0
, i.e.

\phi(-L)=\phi^{\prime}(-L)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)=0,\quad\psi_{j}(L)=\psi_{j}^{\prime}(L)=\psi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(L)=0,\quad j=1,\dots,N,

while leaving
\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}
unconstrained. These are

Dirichlet-type
conditions at the incoming edges; they are valid
generators of contraction semigroups whenever the corresponding relation is
contractive, but are inaccessible to the operator-based framework.

Definition 4.6
.

Given a boundary relation
R\subset\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{0}
, define

(4.30)

A_{R}\mathbf{U}:=-A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U},\qquad D(A_{R}):=\bigl\{\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{0}^{*}):(\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U},\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U})\in R\bigr\}.

Let us describe the adjoint of
A_{R}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

A_{R}^{*}=-A_{R^{[*]}}
, where
A_{R^{[*]}}
acts as
A_{0}^{*}
with domain

(4.31)

D\!\left(A_{R^{[*]}}\right):=\bigl\{\mathbf{V}\in D(A_{0}^{*}):(\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{V},\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{V})\in R^{[*]}\bigr\}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{R})
and
\mathbf{V}\in D(A_{0}^{*})
.
Using (
4.26
) and
A_{R}\mathbf{U}=-A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U}
,

(4.32)

[A_{R}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}]=[\mathbf{U},A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V}]+(\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}\mid\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{V})_{0}-(\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}\mid\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{V})_{1}.

Hence
\mathbf{V}\in D(A_{R}^{*})
if and only if

(y_{1}\mid\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{V})_{0}=(x_{1}\mid\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{V})_{1}\quad\forall\,(x_{1},y_{1})\in R,

which by (
4.27
) is equivalent to

(\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{V},\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{V})\in R^{[*]}
.
In this case
A_{R}^{*}\mathbf{V}=A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V}=-(-A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V})=-A_{R^{[*]}}\mathbf{V}
.
∎

We now obtain dissipativity via contractivity in the following sense:

Proposition 4.5
.

A_{R}
is dissipative if and only if
R
is a
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{0})
-contraction.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{R})
, set
(x,y):=(\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U},\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U})\in R
.
The Green identity (
4.26
) applied to the pair
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})
gives

(4.33)

2\operatorname{Re}[A_{R}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}]=(y\mid y)_{0}-(x\mid x)_{1}.

Hence
\operatorname{Re}[A_{R}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}]\leqslant 0
for all
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{R})

if and only if
(y\mid y)_{0}\leqslant(x\mid x)_{1}
for all
(x,y)\in R
,
which is exactly (
4.28
).
∎

Remark 2
.

We note contractivity of
R
implies contractivity of
R^{[*]}
in this finite-
dimensional setting. In fact, by the polar decomposition of an invertible Hermitian matrix we can write
C_{0}=J_{0}H_{0}
and
C_{1}=J_{1}H_{1}
with
H_{0},H_{1}
positive definite and

J_{0},J_{1}
fundamental symmetries of the respective Krein spaces. The
contractivity condition (
4.28
) for
R
, when expressed through the
similarity transforms
H_{0}^{1/2}
and
H_{1}^{1/2}
, becomes a Hilbert-space
contraction condition for an equivalent relation. Its adjoint in the Hilbert
sense is again a contraction, and undoing the similarity transforms shows that

R^{[*]}
satisfies (
4.28
) with the roles of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
and

\mathcal{C}_{1}
interchanged. Consequently the
dissipativity of
A_{R}
automatically entails the dissipativity of

A_{R}^{*}=-A_{R^{[*]}}
, without any additional hypothesis on
R
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
R\subset\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{0}
be a linear boundary relation.
Then
A_{R}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup of contractions on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})

if and only if
R
is a
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{0})
-contraction.

Proof.

Suppose
R
is contractive. By Proposition
4.5
,
A_{R}
is dissipative.
By Remark
2
,
R^{[*]}
is also contractive, so Proposition
4.5

applied to
R^{[*]}
gives that
A_{R}^{*}=-A_{R^{[*]}}
is dissipative. The
Lumer–Phillips theorem then yields that
A_{R}
generates a contraction
semigroup.

Conversely, if
A_{R}
generates a contraction semigroup, the Lumer–Phillips
theorem implies
A_{R}
is dissipative, and Proposition
4.5
gives that
R

is contractive.
∎

4.2.3.
Examples

We now present extensions
A_{R}
generating semigroups of contractions, framed
as boundary relations. The first example corresponds to an operator-type
relation (graph of a matrix), while Remark
1
describes the
additional Dirichlet-type extensions that fall outside the operator framework.

Example 4

(
\delta
-type interactions on a tadpole graph)
.

Let
N=1
,
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}
, and let
m_{1},\dots,m_{4}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Define the matrix

(4.34)

M:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&0&0\\[2.0pt]
\dfrac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{2}&1&0&1&0&0\\[6.0pt]
m_{1}&m_{2}+m_{3}&2&m_{2}&0&0\\[2.0pt]
0&0&0&1&0&0\\[2.0pt]
\dfrac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{2}&1&0&1&0&0\\[6.0pt]
m_{3}&2&0&m_{4}&0&2\end{pmatrix},

and consider the boundary relation

R:=\operatorname{graph}(M)\subset\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{0}
,
i.e.

(4.35)

R=\bigl\{(x,Mx):x\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\bigr\}.

By Definition
4.5
,
R
is a
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{0})
-contraction
if and only if the matrix
M^{*}C_{0}M-C_{1}
is negative semidefinite. A direct
computation shows that a sufficient condition is

(4.36)

\alpha_{0}<0,\qquad m_{4}\leqslant\tfrac{3}{2},\qquad 4m_{1}-(m_{2}^{2}+m_{3}^{2})\leqslant 2.

By Theorem
4.6
, under (
4.36
) the operator
A_{R}
generates a

C_{0}
-semigroup of contractions.

The Krein adjoint relation is
R^{[*]}=\operatorname{graph}(M^{\#})
with

M^{\#}=C_{1}^{-1}M^{*}C_{0}
, given explicitly by

(4.37)

M^{\#}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0&0&0\\[2.0pt]
\dfrac{-m_{2}-m_{3}}{2}&\dfrac{1}{2}&0&-1&\dfrac{1}{2}&0\\[8.0pt]
\dfrac{m_{1}-1}{2}&\dfrac{-(m_{2}+m_{3})}{4}&\dfrac{1}{2}&\dfrac{m_{3}}{2}&\dfrac{-(m_{2}+m_{3})}{4}&0\\[8.0pt]
0&0&0&1&0&0\\[2.0pt]
0&0&0&0&0&0\\[2.0pt]
\dfrac{m_{2}}{2}&-\dfrac{1}{2}&0&\dfrac{m_{4}-1}{2}&-\dfrac{1}{2}&\dfrac{1}{2}\end{pmatrix},

which is also a
(\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{1})
-contraction under (
4.36
),
in agreement with Remark
2
.

Explicitly, the domain of
A_{R}
consists of all
(\phi,\psi)\in D(A_{0}^{*})

satisfying
(\Gamma_{1}(\phi,\psi),\,\Gamma_{0}(\phi,\psi))\in R
, that is,

(4.38)

\begin{split}&\phi(-L)=\phi(L)=\psi(L),\\[4.0pt]
&\phi^{\prime}(L)-\phi^{\prime}(-L)=\left(\tfrac{m_{2}+m_{3}+2}{2}\right)\phi(-L),\\[4.0pt]
&\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)-2\psi^{\prime\prime}(L)=(m_{3}+m_{4})\,\psi(L)+2\,\phi^{\prime}(-L),\\[4.0pt]
&\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)-2\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)=(m_{1}+m_{2})\,\phi(-L)+(m_{2}+m_{3})\,\phi^{\prime}(-L).\end{split}

In particular, setting
m_{4}=m_{1}=0
,
m_{3}=-2
, and writing
z=m_{2}/2\in\mathbb{R}
,
the conditions (
4.38
) reduce to

(4.39)

\begin{split}&\phi(-L)=\phi(L)=\psi(L),\qquad\phi^{\prime}(L)-\phi^{\prime}(-L)=z\,\phi(-L),\\[4.0pt]
&\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)-\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)-\psi^{\prime\prime}(L)=z\,\phi^{\prime}(-L)+(z-1)\,\phi(-L).\end{split}

The adjoint operator
A_{R}^{*}=-A_{R^{[*]}}
has domain consisting of all

(\phi,\psi)\in D(A_{0}^{*})
satisfying

(\Gamma_{0}(\phi,\psi),\,\Gamma_{1}(\phi,\psi))\in R^{[*]}
, i.e.

(4.40)

\begin{split}&\phi(-L)=\phi(L)=\psi(L),\qquad\psi^{\prime}(L)=0,\\[4.0pt]
&-\tfrac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{2}\,\phi(L)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\phi^{\prime}(L)-\phi(-L)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi^{\prime}(L)=\phi^{\prime}(-L),\\[4.0pt]
&\tfrac{m_{1}-1}{2}\,\phi(L)-\tfrac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{4}\,\phi^{\prime}(L)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)+\tfrac{m_{3}}{2}\,\phi(-L)-\tfrac{m_{2}+m_{3}}{4}\,\psi^{\prime}(L)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L),\\[4.0pt]
&\tfrac{m_{2}}{2}\,\phi(L)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\phi^{\prime}(L)+\tfrac{m_{4}-1}{2}\,\phi(-L)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\psi^{\prime}(L)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)=\psi^{\prime\prime}(L).\end{split}

Remark 3
.

A canonical example of extensions not reachable by linear operators is the
partial Dirichlet relation

(4.41)

R_{j}:=\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{0}:x_{j}=0\bigr\},

where
x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}
is the
j
-th block of
x
, which imposes

\partial\psi_{j}(L)=0
(i.e.
\psi_{j}(L)=\psi_{j}^{\prime}(L)=\psi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(L)=0
) while
leaving
\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}
unconstrained. Such relations are contractive
whenever the residual quadratic form
(y\mid y)_{0}
is non-positive on

\{0\}\times\operatorname{mul}(R)
. No finite matrix
\mathcal{L}
can encode these
boundary conditions, since that would require
\mathcal{L}\cdot 0=y\neq 0
,
which is impossible for a linear map.

4.3.
Boundary system separating derivatives

A finer description of the boundary behavior is obtained by separating
the quadratic form involving first derivatives from those involving the
function values and second derivatives.

The technique of separating the first-derivative component from the
boundary system, developed in the present subsection, is not merely a
formal device. It has already proven to be an effective tool in the
qualitative analysis of dispersive equations on metric graphs: in

[
42
]
, this separation of derivatives was exploited to construct solitary-wave solutions for the
Korteweg–de Vries equation on star graphs. Furthermore, the class of
extensions
A_{Y,R_{1}}
introduced below, together with the contraction
semigroups they generate, constitutes the functional-analytic backbone
of an ongoing program aimed at the stabilization and controllability of
the KdV equation on metric graphs.

We recall the notation

\vec{\mathbf{U}}
,
\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}
,
\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}

introduced in Subsection 2.1.

Lemma 4.7
.

For any
\mathbf{U}=(\phi,\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{N})
and

\mathbf{V}=(\tilde{\phi},\tilde{\psi}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\psi}_{N})
in
D(A_{0}^{*})
,

(4.42)

[A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}]+[\mathbf{U},A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V}]=\bigl(D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\mid\vec{\mathbf{V}}\bigr)+\bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}\mid\vec{\mathbf{V}}\bigr)+\bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\mid\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime\prime}\bigr)-\bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}\mid\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}\bigr),

where

(4.43)

D_{\alpha}:=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{0},-\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}),\qquad D_{\beta}:=\operatorname{diag}(\beta_{0},-\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{N}).

Definition 4.7
.

Consider the spaces
\mathcal{K}_{+}\cong\mathcal{K}_{-}\cong\mathbb{C}^{N+1}

equipped with the weighted inner products

(4.44)

(x\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}:=(D_{\alpha}^{+}\,x\mid y),\qquad(x\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}:=(D_{\alpha}^{-}\,x\mid y),

where

(4.45)

D_{\alpha}^{+}:=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{N}),\qquad D_{\alpha}^{-}:=\frac{1}{N+1}\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{0},\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{0}).

Both
(\mathcal{K}_{\pm},(\cdot\mid\cdot)_{\mathcal{K}_{\pm}})
are Krein spaces.

The matrices
D_{\alpha}^{-}
and
D_{\alpha}^{+}
split
D_{\alpha}
: the former
collects the coefficient of
\phi(L)
and the latter collects all remaining
coefficients. Specifically, (
4.42
) and the identity

(4.46)

\bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}\mid\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}\bigr)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(-L)\\
\tilde{\psi}_{1}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\psi}_{N}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\right)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}-\left(\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(L)\\
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\right)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}

yield the split Green identity

(4.47)

\begin{split}[A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}]+[\mathbf{U},A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V}]&=\Bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\;\Big|\;\vec{\mathbf{V}}\Bigr)+\Bigl(\vec{\mathbf{U}}\;\Big|\;\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{V}}+D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime\prime}\Bigr)\\
&\quad-\left(\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(-L)\\
\tilde{\psi}_{1}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\psi}_{N}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\right)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}+\left(\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\;\middle|\;\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(L)\\
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{\phi}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}\right)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}.\end{split}

4.3.1.
Boundary relations for the first-derivative part

We introduce derivative trace maps

\Lambda_{+},\Lambda_{-}:D(A_{0}^{*})\to\mathcal{K}_{\pm}
by

(4.48)

\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U}:=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U}:=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}.

Definition 4.8
.

A
derivative boundary relation
is a linear subspace

R_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}_{+}\times\mathcal{K}_{-}
.
Its
Krein adjoint

R_{1}^{[*]}\subset\mathcal{K}_{-}\times\mathcal{K}_{+}

is defined by

(4.49)

(y,x)\in R_{1}^{[*]}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;(y_{1}\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}=(x_{1}\mid x)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}\quad\text{for all }(x_{1},y_{1})\in R_{1}.

The relation
R_{1}
is called a
(\mathcal{K}_{+},\mathcal{K}_{-})
-contraction if

(4.50)

(y\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}\leqslant(x\mid x)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}\qquad\text{for all }(x,y)\in R_{1}.

When
R_{1}=\operatorname{graph}(\mathcal{L})
for a linear map

\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{K}_{+}\to\mathcal{K}_{-}
, the Krein adjoint satisfies

R_{1}^{[*]}=\operatorname{graph}(\mathcal{L}^{\#})
with

\mathcal{L}^{\#}:=(D_{\alpha}^{+})^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}D_{\alpha}^{-}
,
and (
4.50
) reduces to

(D_{\alpha}^{+})^{-1}\mathcal{L}^{*}D_{\alpha}^{-}\mathcal{L}-I\preceq 0
.

4.3.2.
Extensions generating contractive dynamics via splitting derivatives

Definition 4.9
.

Let
R_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}_{+}\times\mathcal{K}_{-}
be a linear boundary relation
and let
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{N+2}
be a closed subspace. Define

(4.51)

\begin{split}D(A_{Y,R_{1}}):=\Bigl\{\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{0}^{*})\;\Big|\;&\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y,\quad D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y^{\perp},\\
&\text{and}\quad(\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U},\,\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U})\in R_{1}\Bigr\},\end{split}

with
A_{Y,R_{1}}\mathbf{U}:=-A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U}
.

The subspace condition
\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y
and the orthogonality condition

D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y^{\perp}
together
govern how function values and second derivatives interact at the vertex.
The relation condition
(\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U},\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U})\in R_{1}

governs the first-derivative coupling independently.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
R_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}_{+}\times\mathcal{K}_{-}
be a linear boundary relation
and
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{N+2}
a closed subspace. Then

A_{Y,R_{1}}^{*}=-A_{Y,R_{1}^{[*]}}
, where
A_{Y,R_{1}^{[*]}}
acts as
A_{0}^{*}

with domain

(4.52)

\begin{split}D\!\left(A_{Y,R_{1}^{[*]}}\right)=\Bigl\{\mathbf{V}\in D(A_{0}^{*})\;\Big|\;&\vec{\mathbf{V}}\in Y,\quad\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{V}}+D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime\prime}\in Y^{\perp},\\
&\text{and}\quad(\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{V},\,\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{V})\in R_{1}^{[*]}\Bigr\}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{Y,R_{1}})
and
\mathbf{V}\in D(A_{0}^{*})
.
Using (
4.47
) and
A_{Y,R_{1}}\mathbf{U}=-A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U}
,

(4.53)

\begin{split}[A_{Y,R_{1}}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}]&=[\mathbf{U},A_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V}]-\Bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\;\Big|\;\vec{\mathbf{V}}\Bigr)-\Bigl(\vec{\mathbf{U}}\;\Big|\;\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{V}}+D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime\prime}\Bigr)\\
&\quad+(\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U}\mid\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{V})_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}-(\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U}\mid\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{V})_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}.\end{split}

We need
[A_{Y,R_{1}}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}]=[\mathbf{U},A_{Y,R_{1}}^{*}\mathbf{V}]

for all
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{Y,R_{1}})
.

Taking
\mathbf{U}
with
\vec{\mathbf{U}}=\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}=0

gives
(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}\mid\vec{\mathbf{V}})=0
for all

D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}\in Y^{\perp}
, hence
\vec{\mathbf{V}}\in Y
.

For arbitrary
x\in Y
, taking
\mathbf{U}
with

\vec{\mathbf{U}}=x
and
\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime}=0
, the condition

D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y^{\perp}

and (
4.53
) yield

\Bigl(x\;\Big|\;\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{V}}+D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime\prime}\Bigr)=\Bigl(D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\;\Big|\;\vec{\mathbf{V}}\Bigr)=0,

hence
\frac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{V}}+D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime\prime}\in Y^{\perp}
.

At this point (
4.53
) reduces to the
requirement that for all
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{Y,R_{1}})
,

(\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U}\mid\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{V})_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}=(\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U}\mid\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{V})_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}.

Since
(\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U},\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U})\in R_{1}
, the definition
(
4.49
) of the Krein adjoint shows this is equivalent to

(\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{V},\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{V})\in R_{1}^{[*]}
.
∎

We now obtain, once more, dissipativity via contractivity.

Proposition 4.9
.

A_{Y,R_{1}}
is dissipative if and only if
R_{1}
is a

(\mathcal{K}_{+},\mathcal{K}_{-})
-contraction.

Proof.

For
\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{Y,R_{1}})
, set
(x,y):=(\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U},\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U})\in R_{1}
.
The split Green identity (
4.47
) applied to
(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U})
,
together with the conditions
\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y
and

D_{\alpha}\vec{\mathbf{U}}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{2}D_{\beta}\vec{\mathbf{U}}\in Y^{\perp}
, gives

(4.54)

2\operatorname{Re}[A_{Y,R_{1}}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}]=(x\mid x)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}-(y\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}.

Hence
\operatorname{Re}[A_{Y,R_{1}}\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}]\leqslant 0
for all

\mathbf{U}\in D(A_{Y,R_{1}})
if and only if
(y\mid y)_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}\leqslant(x\mid x)_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}

for all
(x,y)\in R_{1}
, i.e.
R_{1}
is contractive.
∎

Remark 4
.

The same Hilbert-space similarity argument as in Remark
2

shows that contractivity of
R_{1}
automatically implies
contractivity of
R_{1}^{[*]}
. Hence dissipativity of
A_{Y,R_{1}}
entails
dissipativity of
A_{Y,R_{1}}^{*}
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
R_{1}\subset\mathcal{K}_{+}\times\mathcal{K}_{-}
be a linear boundary relation
and
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{N+2}
a closed subspace. Then
A_{Y,R_{1}}
generates
a
C_{0}
-semigroup of contractions on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
if and only if
R_{1}

is a
(\mathcal{K}_{+},\mathcal{K}_{-})
-contraction.

Proof.

If
R_{1}
is contractive, Proposition
4.9
gives dissipativity of

A_{Y,R_{1}}
, and Remark
4
gives dissipativity of

A_{Y,R_{1}}^{*}
. The Lumer–Phillips theorem yields the conclusion.
Conversely, if
A_{Y,R_{1}}
generates a contraction semigroup it is
dissipative, and Proposition
4.9
gives contractivity of
R_{1}
.
∎

4.3.3.
Examples

For simplicity we take
N=1
(tadpole graph) and
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}
.

Example 5
.

For
m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
with
m_{3}\neq 0
, consider the operator

\mathcal{L}:=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}&m_{2}\\
m_{3}&-\tfrac{m_{1}m_{2}}{m_{3}}\end{pmatrix}

and the operator-type relation
R_{1}:=\operatorname{graph}(\mathcal{L})\subset\mathcal{K}_{+}\times\mathcal{K}_{-}

Its Krein adjoint is

with

\tfrac{m_{2}}{2}&-\tfrac{m_{1}m_{2}}{2m_{3}}\end{pmatrix}.

Both

and

m_{3}^{2}+m_{1}^{2}\leqslant 2,\quad m_{2}^{2}(m_{1}^{2}+1)\leqslant 2,\quad m_{1}(m_{3}^{2}-m_{2}^{2})=0,\quad m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}\leqslant 1,\quad\frac{m_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}}{m_{3}^{2}}+m_{3}^{2}\leqslant 2.

In the case

In particular,

1&-1\end{pmatrix}
.

The next example characterizes extensions that maintain the looping-edge
structure (
\phi(-L)=\phi(L)
) and include continuity at the vertex.

Example 6
.

Let

and

be a

Case

5

&\psi^{\prime}(L)=0,\quad\alpha_{1}\psi^{\prime\prime}(L)+\tfrac{\beta_{1}}{2}\psi(L)=\alpha_{0}\bigl(\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)-\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)\bigr)\Bigr\},\end{split}

and by Theorem
4.10
,
A_{Y_{1},R_{1}}
generates a contraction semigroup on

Case

The domain of

Example 7
.

Assume
\alpha_{j}\equiv\alpha
and
\beta_{j}\equiv\beta
for all

. Let

so that

For

arbitrary and parameters

m_{j}^{2}\leqslant N+1

Its Krein adjoint relation is

with
\mathcal{L}^{\#}=\frac{1}{N+1}\mathcal{L}

ensures that both

applies.

The corresponding extension

\begin{split}D(A_{Y,R_{1}}):=\Big\{\mathbf{U}\in H^{3}(\mathcal{G})\;\Bigm|\;&\phi(-L)=\phi(L)=0,\quad\psi_{1}(L)=\psi_{j}(L)\text{ for all }j=1,\dots,N,\\

&\sum_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(L)=\frac{-\beta}{2\alpha}\,N\psi_{1}(L)\Big\},\end{split}

.

4.4.
A simple application to exponential stabilization

For simplicity we work within the setting of Example
6
(case
z=0
) on the
tadpole graph
\mathcal{G}
with
N=1
, taking
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=\alpha>0
and

\beta_{0}=\beta_{1}=\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. The operator
A=A_{Y_{0},R_{1}}
from (
4.59
),
together with the relation

R_{1}=\operatorname{graph}\!\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}
,
acts on all
\mathbf{U}=(\phi,\psi)\in H^{3}(\mathcal{G})
satisfying

\phi(-L)=\phi(L),\qquad\psi(L)=0,\qquad\phi^{\prime}(-L)=\phi^{\prime}(L),\qquad\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(L),

together with
\psi^{\prime}(L)=0
. This last condition follows directly from
R_{1}
:
the equation
\Lambda_{-}\mathbf{U}=\mathcal{L}\,\Lambda_{+}\mathbf{U}
reads

\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\phi^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
\psi^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix},

and subtracting the two rows forces
\psi^{\prime}(L)=0
, after which either row gives

\phi^{\prime}(L)=\phi^{\prime}(-L)
. Fix
\gamma>0
and consider the damped problem

(4.63)

\partial_{t}\mathbf{U}(t)=A\,\mathbf{U}(t)-\gamma\,\mathbf{U}(t),\qquad t>0,\qquad\mathbf{U}(0)=\mathbf{U}_{0}\in D(A).

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
\mathbf{U}(t)
be a classical solution of (
4.63
). Then the
energy
E(t):=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{U}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}
satisfies

E(t)\leqslant e^{-2\gamma t}\,E(0),\qquad t\geqslant 0,

or equivalently
\|\mathbf{U}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}\leqslant e^{-\gamma t}\|\mathbf{U}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.
In particular, the solution decays to zero exponentially at rate
\gamma
,
independently of the dispersive coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Proof.

We first address the existence of classical solutions to (
4.63
).
Since
A_{Y_{0},R_{1}}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup of contractions on

L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
by Theorem
4.10
, the operator
A-\gamma I
is dissipative
for every
\gamma>0
. Moreover,
(A-\gamma I)^{*}=A^{*}-\gamma I
, and since
A^{*}

is also dissipative (as noted in Remark
4
), so is
A^{*}-\gamma I
.
The Lumer–Phillips theorem therefore guarantees that
A-\gamma I
generates a

C_{0}
-semigroup of contractions on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, and in particular that
(
4.63
) admits a unique classical solution

\mathbf{U}\in C^{1}([0,\infty);L^{2}(\mathcal{G}))\cap C([0,\infty);D(A))

for every
\mathbf{U}_{0}\in D(A)
.

Writing (
4.63
) componentwise gives

\phi_{t}=-\alpha\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}-\beta\phi^{\prime}-\gamma\phi
on
(-L,L)
and

\psi_{t}=-\alpha\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}-\beta\psi^{\prime}-\gamma\psi
on
(L,\infty)
.
Differentiating the energy and substituting the equations of motion yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E(t)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\!\int_{-L}^{L}\!\phi\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\,dx-\beta\!\int_{-L}^{L}\!\phi\phi^{\prime}\,dx-\gamma\!\int_{-L}^{L}\!\phi^{2}\,dx-\alpha\!\int_{L}^{\infty}\!\psi\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}\,dx-\beta\!\int_{L}^{\infty}\!\psi\psi^{\prime}\,dx-\gamma\!\int_{L}^{\infty}\!\psi^{2}\,dx.

We claim that all terms except those with factor
-\gamma
vanish identically.
Indeed, integrating by parts twice on the loop edge,

\int_{-L}^{L}\phi\,\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\,dx=\Bigl[\phi^{\prime\prime}\phi\Bigr]_{-L}^{L}-\int_{-L}^{L}\phi^{\prime\prime}\phi^{\prime}\,dx=\bigl(\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)\phi(L)-\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)\phi(-L)\bigr)-\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\phi^{\prime}(L)^{2}-\phi^{\prime}(-L)^{2}\bigr),

and every boundary term cancels upon applying
\phi(-L)=\phi(L)
,

\phi^{\prime}(-L)=\phi^{\prime}(L)
, and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(-L)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)
, giving

\int_{-L}^{L}\phi\,\phi^{\prime\prime\prime}\,dx=0
.
Besides, since
\phi\phi^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\phi^{2})^{\prime}
,

\int_{-L}^{L}\phi\,\phi^{\prime}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\phi(L)^{2}-\phi(-L)^{2}\bigr)=0,

again by
\phi(-L)=\phi(L)
. On the other hand, functions in
D(A)
belong to

H^{3}(L,\infty)
, so
\psi
,
\psi^{\prime}
, and
\psi^{\prime\prime}
all decay to zero as

x\to\infty
. Integrating by parts on the half-line therefore gives

\int_{L}^{\infty}\psi\,\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}\,dx=\Bigl[\psi^{\prime\prime}\psi\Bigr]_{L}^{\infty}-\int_{L}^{\infty}\psi^{\prime\prime}\psi^{\prime}\,dx=-\psi^{\prime\prime}(L)\psi(L)+\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime}(L)^{2}=0,

where the last equality uses
\psi(L)=0
and
\psi^{\prime}(L)=0
. Similarly,

\int_{L}^{\infty}\psi\,\psi^{\prime}\,dx=\tfrac{1}{2}[\psi^{2}]_{L}^{\infty}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\psi(L)^{2}=0
.
Consequently all boundary contributions vanish and the energy equation reduces to

\frac{d}{dt}E(t)=-\gamma\|\mathbf{U}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}=-2\gamma\,E(t).

An application of Grönwall’s lemma to the differential inequality

E^{\prime}(t)\leqslant-2\gamma E(t)
gives the conclusion.
∎

Remark 5
.

The vanishing of all boundary terms is not a coincidence. It is a direct
manifestation of the fact that
A_{Y_{0},R_{1}}
generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup of
contractions on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
: by Theorem
4.10
, this is equivalent to
the contractivity of
R_{1}
, which in turn forces the boundary terms arising in
the Green identity to be non-positive. In the particular case at hand they
vanish altogether, reflecting the precise balance between the subspace
Y_{0}

and the relation
R_{1}
in the definition of the domain. The systematic
exploitation of this connection between the contractivity of the boundary
relation, the structure of the domain, and the resulting energy dissipation is part of an ongoing work for the KdV equation on general metric graphs.

5.
The Schrödinger operator on looping-edge and
\mathcal{T}
-shaped graphs

In this section we employ the same techniques used in the case of the free Airy operator to characterize the extensions of the Schrödinger operator on a looping-edge and a graph determined by the edges
e_{0}=[-L,L]
and
e_{j}=[L,\infty)
considering
-L
as a
terminal vertex
. This strategy will allow us in the future to study the dynamics of NLS model (
1.1
) specially on
\mathcal{T}
-graphs.

Let us now consider the Schrödinger operator

(5.1)

\mathcal{H}_{0}:\{u_{e}\}_{e\in E}\mapsto\left\{-\frac{d^{2}}{dx_{e}^{2}}u_{e}\right\}_{e\in E},

seen as a densely defined operator on
L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
with domain
D(\mathcal{H}_{0})
defined on (
1.3
). Note we would have
D(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})=H^{2}(\mathcal{G})
.

5.1.
Deficiency indices of the free Schrödinger operator

For the symmetric operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in (
5.1
) we have that free solutions of
-\partial_{x}^{2}u_{e}\pm iu_{e}=0
are given as linear combinations of complex exponentials of the form

u_{e}=c_{1}e^{\frac{(1\pm i)}{\sqrt{2}}x_{e}}+c_{2}e^{-\frac{(1\pm i)}{\sqrt{2}}x_{e}}.

We note that
e^{\pm\frac{(1\pm i)}{\sqrt{2}}x}
belong to
L^{2}(-L,L)
. On the other hand, on a half-line
[L,\infty)
, it would be square integrable only if the real part of
\pm\frac{(1\pm i)}{\sqrt{2}}
is negative. Along each edge
e=[L,\infty)
we would therefore always have that the deficiency indices are
{d_{+}}_{e}={d_{-}}_{e}=1
.

In a looping-edge graph with
N
half-lines attached at the vertex we would have for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
that the deficiency indices
d_{\mp}=dim(Ker(\mathcal{H}_{0}\pm iI))
satisfy
d_{+}=d_{-}=2+N
.
From the von Neumann and Krein theory, one can always expect self-adjoint extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and therefore unitary dynamics.

5.2.
Boundary systems

In this subsection we adapt the ideas developed in the case of the Airy operator, for the Schrödinger operator posed on
\mathcal{G}
.

Definition 5.1
.

We define on the graph of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}
,
G(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})=\{(\textbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{U})\mid\textbf{U}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})\}
, the standard skew-symmetric form
\Lambda:G(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})\times G(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})\to\mathbb{C},
defined by

(5.2)

\Lambda((\textbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{U}),(\textbf{V},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{V})):=[\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{U},\textbf{V}]-[\textbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{V}].

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{J}=\mathbb{C}^{N+2}
. Define the standard skew-symmetric sesquilinear form on

\mathcal{J}\oplus\mathcal{J}
by

(5.3)

\rho\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\bigr):=\langle y_{1},x_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{J}}-\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{J}},\qquad(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathcal{J}\oplus\mathcal{J},

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\mathcal{J}}
is the
standard inner product on
\mathbb{C}^{N+2}
.

Definition 5.3
.

We define
\mathbb{F}
to be the surjective boundary map

(5.4)

\begin{split}\mathbb{F}:&\ G(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})\to\mathcal{J}\oplus\mathcal{J}\\
&(\mathbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U})\mapsto\bigl(\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U},\,\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}\bigr),\end{split}

where

\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi(-L)\\
\phi(L)\\
\psi_{1}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{N}(L)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{\prime}(-L)\\
-\phi^{\prime}(L)\\
\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L)\\
\vdots\\
\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L)\end{pmatrix}.

Proposition 5.1
.

The quintuple
(\Lambda,\mathcal{J},\mathcal{J},\mathbb{F},\rho)
is a
boundary system
for
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, that is,

\Lambda((\textbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{U}),(\textbf{V},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{V
}))=\rho\bigl(\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{U}),\,\mathbb{F}(\mathbf{V},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\mathbf{V})\bigr).

Proof.

The proof follows by integration by parts.
∎

5.3.
Self-adjoint extensions via boundary systems

The following theorem, taken from
[
49
, Theorem 3.3]
, gives a complete
parametrization of all self-adjoint extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
in terms of
the boundary system constructed in the previous subsection.

Theorem 5.2
.

An operator
\mathcal{H}
is a self-adjoint extension of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
if
and only if there exist a subspace
X\subset\mathcal{J}=\mathbb{C}^{N+2}

and a self-adjoint operator
\mathcal{L}:X\to X
such that

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{X,\mathcal{L}}
, where

(5.5)

D(\mathcal{H}_{X,\mathcal{L}})=\Bigl\{\mathbf{U}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{G})\;\Big|\;\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}\in X,\quad\mathcal{L}\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}=P_{X}\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}\Bigr\}.

Here
P_{X}:\mathcal{J}\to X
is the orthogonal projection onto
X
.

Theorem
5.2
suggests a practical procedure for constructing self-adjoint extensions by prescribing boundary conditions in two stages. First, one translates the desired
k
conditions (with
k<N+2
) into conditions on
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
, which can then be directly incorporated into the definition of a matrix
M:X\to X
. The second stage consists of completing the remaining (unconstrained) entries of
M
so that it becomes self-adjoint, and of determining the resulting conditions imposed by the relation
M\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}=P_{X}\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}
. We exemplify the procedure next.

5.4.
Examples

Example 8

(Self-adjoint extensions on a looping-edge graph)
.

We seek all self-adjoint extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
whose domain
includes only the condition looping condition

. This condition constrains

in a way that the condition

Consider the orthonormal basis for

In this basis the coordinate vectors of

and

are, respectively,

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}\mathbf{U}=\bigl(\sqrt{2}\,\phi(-L),\;\psi_{1}(L),\;\ldots,\;\psi_{N}(L)\bigr)^{\!\top},\qquad\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}\mathbf{U}=\Bigl(\tfrac{\phi^{\prime}(-L)-\phi^{\prime}(L)}{\sqrt{2}},\;\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L),\;\ldots,\;\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L)\Bigr)^{\!\top}.

\mathbf{b}&M^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},\qquad a\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N},\quad M^{\prime}\in M_{N}(\mathbb{C})\text{ Hermitian},

.

Expanding

(5.7)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(-L)-\phi^{\prime}(L)

\displaystyle=2a\,\phi(-L)+\sqrt{2}\,\mathbf{b}^{*}\vec{\psi}(L),

\displaystyle\vec{\psi}\,^{\prime}(L)

\vec{\psi}\,^{\prime}(L)=(\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L),\ldots,\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L))^{\!\top}

.

The self-adjoint extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{0}

Remark 6
.

The

5.5.
Extensions generating unitary dynamics parameterized by linear subspaces

We now emulate the strategy of separating derivatives used for the Airy operator. We note in this case there is no actual separation of derivatives rather a simple reference frame to build some of the self-adjoint realizations.

Proposition 5.4
.

For any
\textbf{U},\textbf{V}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})
we have

(5.12)

[\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{U},\textbf{V}]-[\textbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{V}]=\left(Q\vec{\textbf{U}}^{\prime}\mid\vec{\textbf{V}}\right)-\left(Q\vec{\textbf{U}}\mid\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\right)=\left(Q\vec{\textbf{U}}^{\prime}\mid\vec{\textbf{V}}\right)-\left(\vec{\textbf{U}}\mid Q\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\right),

where

Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1,\dots,1).

Definition 5.4
.

Given a subspace
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{2+N}
consider the operator
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
, acting as
\mathcal{H}_{Y}\textbf{U}=\mathcal{H}_{0}\textbf{U}
, with domain

(5.13)

D(\mathcal{H}_{Y}):=\left\{\textbf{U}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})\mid\ \vec{\textbf{U}}\in Y\ \mbox{and }Q\vec{\textbf{U}}^{\prime}\in Y^{\perp}\right\}.

The next proposition determine explicitly the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

For any subspace
Y\subset\mathbb{C}^{2+N}
the operator
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
is a self-adjoint extension of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that the adjoint of
\mathcal{H}_{Y}
is given by
\mathcal{H}_{Y}^{*}\textbf{V}=\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*}\textbf{V}
with domain

(5.14)

D(\mathcal{H}_{Y}^{*}):=\left\{\textbf{V}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})\mid\ \vec{\textbf{V}}\in Y\ \mbox{and }Q\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\in Y^{\perp}\right\}=D(\mathcal{H}_{Y}).

It is obvious from the definition of
D(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
that if
Q\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\in Y^{\perp}
and
\vec{\textbf{V}}\in Y
then
\textbf{V}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{Y}^{*})
.

Let us prove the converse. Let
\textbf{V}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{*})
. By definition
V
is in
D(\mathcal{H}_{Y}^{*})
if and only if
[\mathcal{H}_{Y}\textbf{U},\textbf{V}]=[\textbf{U},\mathcal{H}_{Y}^{*}\textbf{V}]
for all
\textbf{U}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
.

Let
x\in Y^{\perp}
be arbitrary. There exists
\textbf{U}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
such that
\vec{\textbf{U}}=0
and
Q\vec{\textbf{U}}^{\prime}=x
. From (
5.12
), we must have

\left(x\mid\vec{\textbf{V}}\right)=\left(Q\vec{\textbf{U}}^{\prime}\mid\vec{\textbf{V}}\right)=0.

Since
x
was arbitrary, it must be the case
\vec{\textbf{V}}\in(Y^{\perp})^{\perp}=Y
.

Similarly, for
x\in Y
there exists
\textbf{U}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{Y})
such that
\vec{\textbf{U}}^{\prime}=0
and
\vec{\textbf{U}}=x
. From (
5.12
) we conclude it must be the case

\left(x\mid Q\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\right)=\left(\vec{\textbf{U}}\mid Q\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\right)=0.

We therefore have
Q\vec{\textbf{V}}^{\prime}\in Y^{\perp}
.
∎

Example 10

(Extensions with prescribed continuity on the derivatives)
.

We seek all self-adjoint extensions of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
whose domain includes the
condition

(5.15)

\phi^{\prime}(L)=\psi_{1}^{\prime}(L)=\cdots=\psi_{N}^{\prime}(L).

From the boundary map definition and because of the sign in the second component, the conditions

\phi^{\prime}(L)=\psi_{j}^{\prime}(L)
are equivalent to

(\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U})_{2}+(\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U})_{j+2}=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,N,

or, setting
v_{j}:=e_{2}+e_{j+2}\in\mathcal{J}

v_{j}^{\,*}\,\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}=0,\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.

Since the prescribed conditions place no restriction on
\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}
,
we take the full space
X=\mathcal{J}
in Theorem
5.2
.
The domain condition then reads
\mathcal{L}\,\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}=\Gamma_{1}\mathbf{U}

for all
\mathbf{U}\in D(\mathcal{H}_{X,\mathcal{L}})
.
Substituting into (
5.16
),

v_{j}^{\,*}\,\mathcal{L}\,\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}=0\qquad\text{for all }\Gamma_{0}\mathbf{U}\in\mathcal{J},

which must hold
structurally

(5.17)

\mathrm{row}_{j+2}(\mathcal{L})=-\,\mathrm{row}_{2}(\mathcal{L}),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.

We require
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{*}
.
The row constraints (

\mathrm{col}_{j+2}(\mathcal{L})=-\,\mathrm{col}_{2}(\mathcal{L})

Enforcing both simultaneously and reading off the diagonal entries (which must be real),

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}a&\beta&-\beta&\cdots&-\beta\\[4.0pt]
\bar{\beta}&c&-c&\cdots&-c\\[2.0pt]

.

(a,c,\operatorname{Re}\beta,\operatorname{Im}\beta)

Expanding

\displaystyle=a\,\phi(-L)+\beta\,\sigma,

\displaystyle=\bar{\beta}\,\phi(-L)+c\,\sigma.

The remaining

-
(

5.7.
A simple application to instability of standing waves of the
cubic NLS

Let
D_{\mathrm{ext}}=D(\mathcal{H})
where
D(\mathcal{H})
is defined
in (
5.9
) with
M^{\prime}=0
. That is,
D_{\mathrm{ext}}

defines a self-adjoint extension of the Laplacian inducing pure periodic
conditions on the loop and independent pure Neumann conditions on each
half-line.

We seek real-valued standing waves

\mathbf{U}(t,x)=e^{i\omega t}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\omega}(x)

with
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\omega}=(\phi_{\omega},\psi_{1,\omega},\dots,\psi_{N,\omega})\in D_{\mathrm{ext}}
.
Substituting into (
1.1
) with
p=1
, each component of
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\omega}

satisfies an independent stationary NLS equation
f^{\prime\prime}+f^{3}-\omega f=0
on
its respective edge.

For the loop component we consider a positive, real,
2L
-periodic solution
constructed via the Jacobi dnoidal function. By Theorem 2.1 of
[
9
]
,
for
\omega>\omega^{*}:=\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{2L^{2}}
, the function

(5.21)

\phi_{\omega}(x)=\eta_{1}\,\mathrm{dn}\!\left(\frac{\eta_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}\,x;\,k\right)

with elliptic modulus
k\in(0,1)
determined by

(5.22)

k^{2}(\eta,\omega)=\frac{2\omega-2\eta^{2}_{2}}{2\omega-\eta^{2}_{2}},\qquad\eta^{2}_{1}+\eta^{2}_{2}=2\omega,\qquad 0<\eta_{2}<\eta_{1},

solves the stationary loop equation. The half-line stationary equation is
solved by the
half-soliton

\psi_{\omega}(x)=\sqrt{2\omega}\,\operatorname{sech}\!\bigl(\sqrt{\omega}(x-L)\bigr),\qquad x\in[L,+\infty),

which is the restriction to
[L,+\infty)
of the full soliton centred at

x=L
. The Neumann condition
\psi_{\omega}^{\prime}(L)=0
holds automatically
because the full soliton is even about
L
. For
j=1,\dots,n\leqslant N
we
therefore set
\psi_{j,\omega}\equiv\psi_{\omega}
, and for

j=n+1,\dots,N
we set
\psi_{j,\omega}\equiv 0
. The standing wave
profile under study is thus (see Figure
3
)

(5.23)

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\omega,n}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\omega}=\bigl(\phi_{\omega},\,\underbrace{\psi_{1,\omega},\dots,\psi_{n,\omega}}_{n\text{ half-solitons}},\,\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{N-n\text{ zero-solitons}}\bigr)\in D_{\mathrm{ext}},\qquad\omega>\omega^{*}.

Figure 3.
Schematic of the standing wave profile
\Phi_{\omega,n}
with
N=3
and
n=2
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
L>0
and
N\geqslant 1
. For every
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and every

\omega>\omega_{*}
, the orbit

\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega})=\bigl\{e^{i\theta}\bigl(\phi_{\omega}(\cdot+s),\,\psi_{1,\omega},\dots,\psi_{n,\omega},0,\dots,0\bigr):\theta\in\mathbb{R},\;s\in\mathbb{R}/(2L\mathbb{Z})\bigr\}

is orbitally unstable in
H^{1}(\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

The decoupled structure of
D_{\mathrm{ext}}
reduces global well-posedness
on
H^{1}(\mathcal{G})
to independent problems on each edge. On the loop

[-L,L]
with periodic boundary conditions, global well-posedness in
H^{1}

is classical and follows from semigroup theory together with conservation of
energy and charge. On each half-line
[L,+\infty)

with Neumann boundary condition
\psi^{\prime}(L)=0
, well-posedness is reduced to
the full-line case via the reflection method: extending
\psi
to an even
function on
\mathbb{R}
embeds the problem into the cubic NLS on

\mathbb{R}
restricted to even initial data, which is invariant under the
flow. Global solutions for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
are then obtained from the
conservation of energy and charge via the Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define the initial datum

\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(0):=\bigl(\phi_{\omega},\;\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon},\;\psi_{2,\omega},\dots,\psi_{n,\omega},\;0,\dots,0\bigr),

where

\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}(x)=\sqrt{2(\omega+\varepsilon)}\,\operatorname{sech}\!\bigl(\sqrt{\omega+\varepsilon}(x-L)\bigr)

is the half-soliton at the shifted frequency
\omega+\varepsilon
.
Since
\omega\mapsto\psi_{1,\omega}
is
C^{1}
as a map into

H^{1}([L,+\infty))
, the mean value inequality in Banach spaces
gives

(5.24)

\|\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(0)-\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega}\|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{G})}=\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}-\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{H^{1}([L,+\infty))}\leqslant C_{\omega}\,\varepsilon,

where

C_{\omega}=\sup_{\omega^{\prime}\in(\omega,\,\omega+1)}\|\partial_{\omega}\psi_{1,\omega^{\prime}}\|_{H^{1}([L,+\infty))}<\infty
.
In particular,

\mathrm{dist}_{H^{1}}(\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(0),\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega}))\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Since every component of
\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(0)
is an exact stationary
profile at its respective frequency, each component satisfies its own NLS
equation with its boundary conditions. By the uniqueness of
H^{1}
solutions,
the global solution is therefore given by

(5.25)

\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\bigl(e^{i\omega t}\phi_{\omega},\;e^{i(\omega+\varepsilon)t}\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon},\;e^{i\omega t}\psi_{2,\omega},\;\dots,\;e^{i\omega t}\psi_{n,\omega},\;0,\dots,0\bigr).

At the time
t_{\varepsilon}:=\pi/\varepsilon
one has

e^{i(\omega+\varepsilon)t_{\varepsilon}}=e^{i\omega t_{\varepsilon}}\cdot e^{i\pi}=-e^{i\omega t_{\varepsilon}}
,
so the solution becomes

(5.26)

\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})=e^{i\omega t_{\varepsilon}}\bigl(\phi_{\omega},\;-\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon},\;\psi_{2,\omega},\dots,\psi_{n,\omega},\;0,\dots\bigr),

that is, the first half-line component has flipped sign relative to all
others.

We now derive a lower bound on the distance from the orbit at time

t_{\varepsilon}
. Take any orbit element

\mathbf{V}=e^{i\theta}(\phi_{\omega}(\cdot+s),\psi_{1,\omega},\dots)\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega})

and set
\mu:=\theta-\omega t_{\varepsilon}
. Since the components are
supported on disjoint edges and

\|(1-e^{i\mu})\psi_{j,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geqslant 0
for
j=2,\dots,N
,
we obtain

(5.27)

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})-\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}^{2}&\geqslant\|\phi_{\omega}-e^{i\mu}\phi_{\omega}(\cdot+s)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}+e^{i\mu}\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\\
&=A+2\cos(\mu)\bigl(P-q(s)\bigr),\end{split}

where

\begin{split}A&:=2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\\
P&:=\langle\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon},\psi_{1,\omega}\rangle_{L^{2}([L,+\infty))},\\
q(s)&:=\langle\phi_{\omega},\phi_{\omega}(\cdot+s)\rangle_{L^{2}([-L,L])}.\end{split}

Since
\cos\mu\in[-1,1]
, minimising the right-hand side of
(
5.27
) over
\mu
and then taking the infimum over

(\theta,s)
gives

(5.28)

\inf_{(\theta,s)}\|\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon})-\mathbf{V}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\geqslant A-2\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}/(2L\mathbb{Z})}|P-q(s)|.

It is shown in Appendix
A
that

A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|\geqslant\min\!\bigl(4\sqrt{\omega},\;2q_{\min}\bigr)=:c_{0}^{2}>0,

where
q_{\min}:=\min_{s}q(s)>0
and
c_{0}
depends only on
\omega
,
L
,
and
k
, but not on
\varepsilon
. Combining with (
5.28
) we conclude

(5.29)

\mathrm{dist}_{H^{1}}(\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}),\,\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega}))\geqslant\mathrm{dist}_{L^{2}}(\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}),\,\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega}))\geqslant c_{0}>0.

Finally, given
\delta>0
arbitrary, one can choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough that

C_{\omega}\varepsilon<\delta
. Then (
5.24
) gives

\mathrm{dist}_{H^{1}}(\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(0),\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega}))<\delta
,
while (
5.29
) gives

\mathrm{dist}_{H^{1}}(\mathbf{U}_{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{\Phi}_{\omega}))\geqslant c_{0}
.
This is precisely the opposite of orbital
stability, and the proof is complete.
∎

Appendix A
Lower bound computation

In this appendix we establish the inequality

A-2\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}/2L\mathbb{Z}}|P-q(s)|\geqslant\min\!\bigl(4\sqrt{\omega},\;2q_{\min}\bigr)=:c_{0}^{2}>0,

used in Section
5.7
.

Since
\phi_{\omega}
and
\psi_{\omega}
are strictly positive, both
P
and

q(s)
are strictly positive. The map
s\mapsto q(s)
is continuous on the
compact set
\mathbb{R}/2L\mathbb{Z}
, so it attains strictly positive
extrema
0<q_{\min}<q_{\max}
. The maximum is attained at
s=0
, with

q_{\max}=q(0)=\|\phi_{\omega}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
, as a consequence of the
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the invariance of the
L^{2}
norm under
periodic translation. Since
P
is independent of
s
, the convexity of
the absolute value gives

\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|\leqslant\max\bigl(|P-q_{\min}|,\;|P-q_{\max}|\bigr).

We argue in three cases according to the position of
P
relative to

[q_{\min},q_{\max}]
.

Case 1:
P\leqslant q_{\min}
.

Since
q(s)\geqslant q_{\min}\geqslant P
for all
s
, one has
|P-q(s)|=q(s)-P

and the supremum is attained at
s=0
:

\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|=q_{\max}-P=\|\phi_{\omega}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}-P.

Substituting back and cancelling the loop norm terms:

\begin{split}A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|&=2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2P\\
&=\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2P.\end{split}

All three terms are non-negative and

\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=2\sqrt{\omega}
, so

A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|\geqslant 2\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=4\sqrt{\omega}>0.

Case 2:
q_{\min}\leqslant P\leqslant q_{\max}
.

Here
|P-q_{\min}|=P-q_{\min}
and
|P-q_{\max}|=q_{\max}-P
, so

\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|=\max(P-q_{\min},\;q_{\max}-P).

Subcase 2a:
q_{\max}-P\geqslant P-q_{\min}
.

The supremum equals
q_{\max}-P=\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-P
, and the same
computation as in Case 1 yields

A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|=\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2P\geqslant 4\sqrt{\omega}>0.

Subcase 2b:
P-q_{\min}>q_{\max}-P
.

The supremum equals
P-q_{\min}
. Since

P\leqslant q_{\max}=\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, we have

2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2P\geqslant 0
, and therefore

\begin{split}A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|&=2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2P+2q_{\min}\\
&\geqslant\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2q_{\min}\geqslant 2q_{\min}>0.\end{split}

Combining both subcases, in Case 2:

A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|\geqslant\min\bigl(4\sqrt{\omega},\;2q_{\min}\bigr)>0.

Case 3:
P>q_{\max}
.

Since
P>q(s)
for all
s
, one has
|P-q(s)|=P-q(s)
and the supremum
is attained at the minimizer of
q
:

\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|=P-q_{\min}.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

P\leqslant\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}
,
hence

\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2P\geqslant\bigl(\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}-\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}\bigr)^{2}\geqslant 0.

Therefore:

\begin{split}A-2\sup_{s}|P-q(s)|&=2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2q_{\min}+\bigl(\|\psi_{1,\omega+\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1,\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-2P\bigr)\\
&\geqslant 2\|\phi_{\omega}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+2q_{\min}\geqslant 2q_{\min}>0.\end{split}

Collecting all cases, we conclude that

A-2\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}/2L\mathbb{Z}}|P-q(s)|\geqslant\min\!\bigl(4\sqrt{\omega},\;2q_{\min}\bigr)=:c_{0}^{2}>0,

where
c_{0}^{2}
depends only on
\omega
,
L
, and the elliptic modulus

k=k(\omega)
, but not on
\varepsilon
.
