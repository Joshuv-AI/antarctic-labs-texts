Genomic analysis of virulence and antimicrobial resistance in Klebsiella isolates from farmed sika deer
Authors: Yu-Zhe Sun, Shuo Liu, Nai-Chao Diao, Xue-Min Li, Qing-Long Gong, Fei Liu, Ruopeng Cai, Xue Leng, Jian-Ming Li, Xiao-Xuan Zhang, Rui Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKlebsiella species are important opportunistic pathogens whose antimicrobial resistance and virulence evolution have attracted increasing attention. However, their prevalence and genomic characteristics in farmed sika deer remain unclear.ResultsA total of 69 Klebsiella isolates (13.40%) were recovered from 443 fecal samples of farmed sika deer. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that all isolates were resistant to ciprofloxacin and sulfonamides (100%), and 17 isolates were identified as multi-drug resistant (MDR). Biofilm assays showed that 63.77% of isolates exhibited biofilm-forming ability. Whole-genome sequencing of 27 representative deer-derived isolates identified 108 antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and diverse virulence factor genes (VFGs), mainly associated with adhesion factors and iron acquisition systems (ent, fep, and iutA). Mobile genetic elements (MGEs), particularly tnpA and IS91, exhibited the highest detection frequencies. Comparative analysis with 511 publicly available genomes identified 263 ARGs, including 17 shared between sika deer- and human-derived strains, and 13 shared MGEs, suggesting a potential role of sika deer as a reservoir of resistance determinants. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated close genetic relatedness among strains from different hosts, with frequent clustering of deer-derived and human-derived isolates. MLST analysis identified 180 sequence types (STs), with ST11 as the dominant clone across hosts, while several emerging MDR STs (e.g., ST442, ST35, and ST4501) were detected in deer-derived isolates. Correlation and Mantel analyses further revealed a significant association between ARGs and MGEs.ConclusionThese findings suggest that farmed sika deer may serve as a potential reservoir for ARGs and contribute to their dissemination across hosts, possibly mediated by MGEs. The observed genetic relatedness between animal- and human-derived strains highlights the potential risk of cross-species transmission. This study underscores the importance of farmed sika deer in the maintenance and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance and highlights the necessity of integrated One Health surveillance approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12974-7.Keywords: Klebsiella, Virulence, Antimicrobial resistance, WGS, Farmed sika deer

Introduction

The genus Klebsiella belongs to the family Enterobacteriaceae and comprises encapsulated, non-motile, rod-shaped Gram-negative bacteria [1]. These organisms are widely distributed in natural environments and commonly colonize the skin, respiratory tract, and intestinal microbiota of healthy individuals [2]. As typical opportunistic pathogens, Klebsiella species can cause a variety of infectious diseases, including urinary tract infections (UTIs), respiratory tract infections, wound and soft tissue infections, and septicemia [3]. In recent years, Klebsiella has become one of the major pathogens associated with hospital-acquired (healthcare-associated) infections and is also an important risk factor for severe community-acquired infections, particularly in immunocompromised populations such as patients with diabetes, the elderly, and individuals with chronic alcohol abuse [4, 5].

Currently, the genus Klebsiella comprises multiple species, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, K. indica, K. terrigena, K. spallanzanii, K. huaxiensis, K. oxytoca, K. grimontii, K. pasteurii, K. variicola, and K. michiganensis [6]. Among these, K. pneumoniae is classified into three subspecies based on the high similarity observed in DNA–DNA hybridization studies: K. pneumoniae subsp. pneumoniae, K. pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae and K. pneumoniae subsp. rhinoscleromatis [7, 8]. Notably, K. pneumoniae subsp. ozaenae and K. pneumoniae subsp. rhinoscleromatis are closely associated with the development of atrophic rhinitis (ozena) and rhinoscleroma, respectively [9].

Antimicrobial resistance in Klebsiella has become a major research focus in both medical and veterinary fields. With the widespread and sometimes inappropriate use of antibiotics in clinical treatment and animal husbandry, resistance levels of Klebsiella to multiple classes of antimicrobials have continued to rise. In particular, the emergence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains poses significant challenges to the clinical management of infections [10]. Among these, extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing and carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae have spread rapidly worldwide and have been listed by the World Health Organization as priority pathogens requiring urgent surveillance and control [11, 12].

Moreover, the resistance mechanisms of Klebsiella are highly diverse. In addition to classical mechanisms such as β-lactamase production, alterations in antimicrobial target sites, and overexpression of efflux pump systems, resistance is also closely associated with biofilm-forming ability [13, 14]. The combined effects of these factors facilitate bacterial colonization, survival, and persistent infection within the host, thereby further increasing the difficulty of infection control and clinical treatment.

Against this background, systematic investigation of Klebsiella isolation and identification, antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and resistance mechanisms at the genomic level is of great scientific significance for clarifying their epidemiological characteristics and resistance risks. The present study aims to isolate Klebsiella strains from farmed sika deer and to comprehensively analyze their antimicrobial resistance patterns, biofilm-forming capacity, and the distribution of associated resistance genes, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the prevention and control of Klebsiella infections and the rational use of antimicrobial agents.

Materials and methods

Sample source

From May to October 2024, a total of 443 fecal samples were randomly collected from farmed sika deer in Heilongjiang, Liaoning, Jilin, and Shandong provinces of China. During sampling, moist and freshly excreted feces were preferentially selected, and portions in contact with the external environment were discarded. The central part of each fecal sample was collected using sterile disposable polyethylene (PE) gloves. The collected samples were immediately placed into sterile disposable sampling tubes, labeled, and transported to the laboratory as soon as possible under refrigerated conditions at 4 °C for bacterial isolation.

Strain isolation

Klebsiella was isolated using a combination of selective enrichment and selective plating. Briefly, one gram of fecal sample was inoculated into nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 4–6 h. The enrichment culture was then streaked onto MacConkey Inositol–Adonitol–Carbenicillin (MIAC) selective agar plates containing carbenicillin (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China) and incubated at 37 °C for bacterial isolation. Typical dark pink colonies were selected and purified by three-sector streaking to obtain single colonies. Purified isolates were subjected to Gram staining, and those confirmed as Gram-negative rods were further processed for genomic DNA extraction. The 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR using universal primers (Supplementary Table 1). PCR products were verified by electrophoresis on 1.0% agarose gels and subsequently submitted to Anhui General Biology Engineering Co., Ltd. for Sanger sequencing. The resulting sequences were compared against the NCBI database using the BLAST program (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) to achieve preliminary identification.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

A total of 14 antimicrobial agents representing different classes were tested. These included imipenem (IPM), meropenem (MEM), ceftazidime (CAZ), cefotaxime (CTX), azithromycin (AZM), ciprofloxacin (CIP), enrofloxacin (ENR), gentamicin (GEN), amikacin (AMK), tetracycline (TET), tigecycline (TIG), sulfamethoxazole (SOX), and florfenicol (FLR), which were tested using the Mueller–Hinton (MH) agar dilution method. Polymyxin B (POLB) was tested using MH broth by the broth microdil ution method (Supplementary Table 2). The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for ENR and TIG were interpreted according to the EUCAST guidelines (https://www.eucast.org/), while the MICs for the other antibiotics were interpreted according to the CLSI guidelines (CLSI M100, 34th edition, 2024).

All antimicrobial agents were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used as the quality control strain to validate the antimicrobial stock solutions and ensure the reliability of the susceptibility testing results.

Biofilm formation

A single colony was inoculated into MH broth and cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm to the logarithmic phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6–0.8). The bacterial suspension was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard and diluted 1:100 in MH broth. Subsequently, 200 µL of the diluted suspension was added to each well of a sterile 96-well polystyrene microplate. Each isolate was tested in triplicate, with sterile MH broth serving as the negative control. Plates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h.

After incubation, wells were gently washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic cells and air-dried. Adherent biofilms were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 15 min, followed by washing and drying at 37 °C for 1 h. The bound dye was solubilized with 200 µL of 95% ethanol for 10 min. The destaining solution was transferred to a new microplate, and absorbance was measured at 590 nm (OD₅₉₀). Biofilm-forming ability was classified according to the optical density cutoff value (ODc), defined as the mean OD₅₉₀ of the negative control plus three standard deviations. Isolates were categorized as follows: OD ≤ ODc, non-biofilm producer; ODc < OD ≤ 2ODc, weak; 2ODc < OD ≤ 4ODc, moderate; OD > 4ODc, strong biofilm producer [15].

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly

To further elucidate the transmission characteristics of virulence factor genes (VFGs) and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in sika deer-derived Klebsiella at the genetic level. A total of 27 isolates with representative antimicrobial resistance profiles were selected for second-generation high-throughput whole-genome sequencing (WGS), particularly those exhibiting resistance to two to three classes of antimicrobial agents. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed using fastp (v0.23.4) [16] with default parameters. High-quality reads were then assembled into draft genomes using SPAdes (v3.15.5) [17] with the parameters “--cov-cutoff auto --careful -k 21,33,55,77,99”.

In addition, to enable comparative genomic analysis, whole-genome sequencing data of 511 Klebsiella strains from different hosts in four provinces of northern China were retrieved from the NCBI database (Supplementary Table 3).

Bioinformation statistic

The quality of genome assemblies was assessed using QUAST (v5.2.0) [18]. The completeness and contamination of each strain were assessed using CheckM2 (v1.0.1) [19]. Taxonomic identification was primarily performed using GTDB-Tk (v2.3.3) [20]. To further validate the taxonomic assignments, Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) analysis was conducted using FastANI (v1.33) against reference genomes (GCF_020525545.1: K. variicola, GCF_000240185.1: K. pneumoniae, GCF_042137965.1: K. grimontii, GCF_020099175.1: K. quasipneumoniae, GCF_019048125.1: K. aerogenes, GCA_030505655.1: K. ornithinolytica, GCA_900636985.1: K. oxytoca, GCA_015139575.1: K. michiganensis ), with a threshold of 95% for species delineation.

Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was performed for all Klebsiella isolates using MLST software (v2.3.0) [21] with the PubMLST database, applying the parameter: “-scheme Klebsiella.”

ARGs, VFGs, and MGEs annotation

ARGs were identified using ResFinder (v4.7.0) [22] with the parameters “-s klebsiella -l 0.6 -t 0.9 -c -acq”. MGEs were identified by aligning gene sequences against the MGE Database [23] using BLASTN (v2.13.0) with the parameters “-evalue 1e-5 -perc_identity 80 -qcov_hsp_perc 80”. VFGs were identified through alignments with the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) [24] using DIAMOND (v2.1.8.162) [25], with criteria of min score > 60% sequence identity and > 50% query coverage.

Statistics and visualization

Phylogenetic trees based on amino acid sequences were constructed using PhyloPhlAn (v1.0) [11] with default parameters. Based on MLST profiles (allelic profiles of seven housekeeping genes), a minimum spanning tree (MSTree) was generated using the MSTree V2 algorithm implemented in GrapeTree (v1.5.0). To evaluate the association between ARGs and MGEs, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated among ARG categories and visualized as a heatmap. Furthermore, a Mantel test (based on Jaccard distance with 999 permutations) was performed to assess the correlation between ARGs and MGEs, and Mantel links were overlaid onto the ARG correlation heatmap for visualization. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using iTOL (Interactive Tree Of Life) [26], and all other visualizations were performed in R (v4.5.2).

Results

Distribution of Klebsiella

Based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis, a total of 69 Klebsiella isolates were recovered from 443 fecal samples of sika deer, with an overall isolation rate of 13.40% (69/443). Significant differences in Klebsiella isolation rates were observed among different regions (P < 0.01). Specifically, the highest isolation rate was recorded in Jilin Province at 23.96% (23/96), followed by Liaoning Province at 20.13% (32/159). In contrast, relatively lower isolation rates were observed in Shandong Province and Heilongjiang Province, at 8.00% (4/50) and 7.25% (10/138), respectively (Fig. 1A).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing and biofilm formation ability

MIC testing showed that among the antibiotics evaluated, the 69 Klebsiella isolates exhibited resistance to only five antimicrobial agents. Notably, extremely high resistance rates were observed for CIP and SOX, with both reaching 100% (69/69). In addition, resistance to POLB was detected in 14.49% (10/69) of the isolates, whereas lower resistance rates were observed for TET and FLR, at 8.70% (6/69) and 10.14% (7/69), respectively (Fig. 1B). All isolates remained fully susceptible to the remaining tested antibiotics. Based on the criterion of resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes, a total of 17 isolates were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR) (Fig. 1C).

The biofilm formation assay showed that, among the 69 Klebsiella isolates in this study, 44 (63.77%) strains exhibited biofilm-forming ability. Of these, 7 were classified as strong biofilm producers, 7 as moderate producers, and 30 as weak biofilm producers (Fig. 1D).

Analysis of VFGs in the isolates

A total of 2,083 virulence factor genes (VFGs) were identified in the 27 Klebsiella isolates through alignment against the VFDB database. Functional classification showed that adherence-related genes accounted for the largest proportion (25.4%), followed by nutritional/metabolic factors (21.75%) and effector delivery system–associated genes (16.55%) (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 3).

At the gene repertoire level, a total of 96 distinct virulence genes were identified. Among these, tufA, acrB, and ompA exhibited relatively high prevalence across the isolates, suggesting that these genes are commonly distributed virulence-associated factors within this population. In addition, several iron acquisition–related virulence genes were detected, including enterobactin synthesis and transport genes (ent family, fep family, and fes), yersiniabactin system genes (irp and the ybt family), and the salmochelin-associated gene iroE, indicating that this bacterial population possesses the genetic basis for iron acquisition. At the individual isolate level, strain L_66 harbored the highest number of virulence genes, with adherence-related genes remaining the dominant category. Of all identified virulence genes, 40 were associated with adherence, indicating that adherence-related factors constitute a substantial proportion of the virulence gene repertoire in this population (Supplementary Table 3). In addition, we conducted a joint analysis of virulence genes and mobile genetic elements. Based on previous studies, it is assumed that if the distance between the two is less than 5 kilobases, they may potentially have genetic mobility [27]. Unfortunately, none of the combinations showed such potential mobility.

Analysis of ARGs and MGEs

In this study, whole-genome sequencing of 27 isolates identified a total of 108 ARGs, belonging to 24 resistance gene types and associated with 21 antibiotics across six antimicrobial classes. Among them, resistance phenotypes to trimethoprim, chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, and nalidixic acid showed relatively high detection frequencies, each accounting for 12.8% (51/397) (Fig. 2B). At the genotypic level, the oqxB gene (25.0%, 27/108) and oqxA gene (22.2%, 24/108) were the most frequently detected, followed by the fosA gene (21.3%, 23/108) (Fig. 2C). In addition, isolate L_60 harbored the highest number of predicted ARGs (Fig. 2D). When focusing on MGEs in the isolates, tnpA was found to be predominant, accounting for 80.6% (214/310), followed by IS91 at 13.87% (43/310) (Fig. 2E). Notably, no MGEs were detected in the L_3 isolate (Fig. 2F).

In the 511 publicly available genomes, we detected 263 antibiotic resistance genes, with 100 resistance genes unique to human-derived strains and 3 resistance genes unique to sika deer-derived strains. Notably, there are as many as 17 shared resistance genes between sika deer-derived and human-derived strains (Fig. 3A). Additionally, 66 MGEs were detected in these publicly available genomes, with 13 of them also found in the sika deer-derived strains, mainly including the tnpA family, int2, int3, and IS91 and IS679 (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table 3). These results suggest that farmed sika deer may serve as a potential reservoir for ARGs, highlighting the risk of cross-species transmission.

Species identification and phylogenetic analysis

A total of 538 Klebsiella strains were included in this study, comprising 410 from Shandong, 54 from Hebei, 48 from Liaoning, 15 from Jilin, and 11 from Heilongjiang. Among these strains, K. pneumoniae was the predominant species (n = 482), followed by K. variicola (n = 31), K. quasipneumoniae (n = 8), K. grimontii (n = 6), K. aerogenes (n = 4), K. ornithinolytica (n = 4), K. oxytoca (n = 2), and K. michiganensis (n = 1). Among these, the Klebsiella isolates recovered from sika deer were identified as K. variicola (n = 19), K. pneumoniae (n = 5), and K. grimontii (n = 3) (Supplementary Table 3).

The phylogenetic tree constructed based on core genome sequences shows that K. pneumoniae, as a dominant species, forms the most expansive clonal cluster. Phylogenetic topology analysis reveals significant genetic relatedness between strains from different host sources. Notably, strains isolated from sika deer and fur-bearing animals frequently cluster with human clinical strains in the same evolutionary branch. This cross-host aggregation phenomenon suggests the potential for pathogen spillover and interspecies transmission between farmed wild animals and humans, highlighting the critical role of the “animal-human-environment” interface in the regional transmission and evolution of Klebsiella species (Fig. 3C).

MLST analysis of K. pneumoniae

For K. pneumoniae, a total of 180 sequence types (STs) were identified through MLST (Supplementary Table 3). According to previous studies, several of these MLST types have been the focus of primary research. These include the high-virulence K. pneumoniae (hvKp) lineages, such as ST23, ST11, and ST29, as well as the “high-risk” emerging multi-drug-resistant (MDR) lineages, ST395, ST307, and ST15 [28]. However, none of these predominant STs were detected among the sika deer-derived isolates; only ST442, ST35, ST4501, ST187, and ST370 were identified in this subset.

Genetic diversity assessment of the included K. pneumoniae isolates via MLST revealed a population structure characterized by distinct clonal expansion and inter-host distribution. Among all identified STs, ST11 was determined to be the predominant dominant clone, occupying a central position in the MSTree. It exhibited robust evolutionary adaptability, expanding radially through multiple Single-Locus Variant (SLV) branches. The MSTree topology further unveiled a complex cross-host transmission network: core clonal complexes represented by ST11, ST15, ST37, and ST35 not only encompassed the majority of human clinical samples but were also widely distributed across farmed wildlife samples, including foxes, minks, and sika deer, demonstrating a high degree of genetic homology (Fig. 3D).

Correlation analysis between ARGs and MGEs

Correlation analysis revealed that ARG categories were predominantly positively correlated, with no evident strong negative correlations. Among them, aminoglycoside resistance genes showed relatively strong positive correlations with multiple other ARG categories, particularly with trimethoprim, macrolide/lincosamide, and phenicol resistance genes. Quinolone/efflux-associated resistance genes also exhibited a high level of concordance with tetracycline resistance genes. In addition, a clear positive correlation was observed between polymyxin and bleomycin resistance genes (Fig. 3E).

Mantel analysis further indicated that MGEs were generally associated with the composition of ARGs, although the strength and significance of these associations varied. Transposases, insertion sequences, plasmid replicons, and accessory MGEs showed widespread associations with multiple ARG categories, whereas integrases exhibited relatively fewer connections (Fig. 3E). Several MGE-ARG combinations reached higher levels of statistical significance (P < 0.05, r-value > 0.25), suggesting that the co-distribution of ARG categories may be accompanied by coordinated changes in MGE composition. Overall, these results indicate a close relationship between resistome structure and MGE composition.

Discussion

Klebsiella are of considerable importance in veterinary medicine and can lead to substantial economic losses in the livestock industry. A meta-analysis conducted in China reported pooled prevalence rates of 13% and 9.18% for Klebsiella [29, 30]. In the present study, 69 Klebsiella isolates were recovered from 443 fecal samples of farmed sika deer, yielding an overall isolation rate of 13.40%, which is comparable to previously reported levels but shows a slightly higher trend [31, 32]. This discrepancy may be associated with differences in sample sources as well as farming environmental conditions.

Capsule, extracellular polysaccharides, iron acquisition systems, and lipopolysaccharide are widely regarded as key determinants of high virulence in Klebsiella [33]. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a high detection rate of adherence-associated virulence factors, suggesting a potentially strong capacity for host colonization. Adherence is considered a critical step in the early establishment of Klebsiella infection, facilitating bacterial attachment to host cell surfaces and the maintenance of persistent infection [34].

In this study, mrkD and fimH were detected in all sequenced isolates, consistent with previous reports, further indicating the widespread distribution of adherence-related factors within the Klebsiella virulence repertoire [35]. Additionally, several iron acquisition–associated virulence genes were identified, among which entB, fepA, and iutA showed a 100% detection rate, generally consistent with the high prevalence reported in the study by Li et al. [35]. The presence of these genes suggests that the isolates possess a strong genetic potential for iron uptake, which is recognized as an important adaptive mechanism for bacterial survival and proliferation within the host.

The emergence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance are primarily driven by the widespread spread of resistance genes. Among Klebsiella isolates from different sources included in this study, the most frequently detected resistance genes were sul1, oqxA, oqxB, and tetA, which confer bacterial resistance through distinct mechanisms. Sulfonamide resistance genes exhibit a high prevalence among Gram-negative bacteria worldwide [36, 37]. Jiang et al. reported that the overall detection rate of sul genes in Gram-negative bacteria can reach approximately 90%, with sul1 being particularly prevalent, which is consistent with the findings of this study [38]. The sul1 gene encodes a variant of dihydropteroate synthase that can replace the drug-sensitive enzyme, thereby reducing the affinity of bacteria for sulfonamides and leading to resistance [39]. Additionally, sul genes are frequently located on MGEs, facilitating their dissemination in the environment and among bacterial populations [40]. Therefore, they hold important public health significance and warrant close attention. Furthermore, among the 27 sika deer-derived Klebsiella isolates in this study, the detection rates of oqxA and oqxB were 100% and 88.9%, respectively, which are comparable to the overall prevalence observed in Klebsiella from other sources. Similar prevalence rates were reported by Amereh et al. [41], further indicating that the oqxAB efflux pump–associated resistance mechanism is widely distributed among Klebsiella from different host origins.

A study conducted in Europe reported that Klebsiella isolates from acute mastitis cases showed the highest resistance rate to tetracycline (19%), which was markedly higher than the 8.7% observed in the present study [42]. This discrepancy may be associated with differences in regional antibiotic usage policies, farming practices, or the background of resistance gene dissemination. Additionally, no resistance to cephalosporins was detected among the isolates in this study, which is consistent with the findings of de Jong et al. [42], suggesting that this class of antibiotics may still retain good antimicrobial efficacy against the relevant bacterial populations. Notably, all isolates in this study exhibited a resistant phenotype to SOX, which is consistent with the findings of Yang et al. in foxes and Liu et al. in minks from northern China [43, 44]. This suggests that SOX may be widely used in farmed wildlife in northern China, thereby exerting sustained selective pressure on bacterial populations and promoting the widespread occurrence of resistance phenotypes. However, no known sulfonamide resistance genes (such as sul1, sul2, or sul3) were detected in this study. This “phenotype–genotype discrepancy” may be attributable to alternative, non-classical resistance mechanisms, such as the activity of multidrug efflux pump systems. Previous studies have demonstrated that the oqxAB efflux pump genes are associated with resistance to multiple antibiotics, and their overexpression can reduce intracellular drug concentrations, thereby contributing to the development of resistant phenotypes [45]. Furthermore, some isolates in this study showed resistance to POLB, but no known colistin resistance genes were identified. This finding suggests that resistance in these strains may arise from chromosomal mutations, lipopolysaccharide modifications, or alterations in regulatory systems, which require further molecular investigation to be fully elucidated.

Biofilms are widely regarded as an important factor influencing bacterial antimicrobial resistance [46]. The establishment of a biofilm structure not only reduces the inhibitory effects of antibiotics on bacteria but also enhances bacterial survival under exposure to disinfectants and other adverse environmental conditions [47, 48]. In this study, a total of 44 isolates capable of forming biofilms were identified, exhibiting strong, moderate, and weak levels of biofilm-forming ability. It is noteworthy that Tremblay et al. [49] reported no significant difference in antibiotic tolerance between strains with weak biofilm-forming capacity and those with strong biofilm-forming capacity. This finding suggests that the presence of a biofilm itself, rather than the degree of its formation, may be a key factor influencing antimicrobial resistance. In other words, even low-level biofilm formation may substantially contribute to bacterial tolerance to antimicrobial agents.

Insertion sequences (ISs) are a class of mobile, short repetitive DNA elements that can alter genomic architecture through transposition, thereby contributing to genomic plasticity and the regulation of gene expression in prokaryotes [50]. Previous studies have demonstrated that, in addition to mediating genomic rearrangements, certain IS elements may serve as potential promoters or regulatory sequences for adjacent genes, thereby influencing the expression of functional genes in members of the family Enterobacteriaceae [51, 52]. Whole-genome sequencing in the present study revealed the presence of the IS91 element in all 27 Klebsiella isolates derived from deer. IS91 belongs to the rolling-circle transposition family, whose mechanism differs from the classical “cut-and-paste” model and confers the potential to mobilize adjacent DNA fragments during transposition. Therefore, the presence of IS91 suggests a degree of genomic structural plasticity in these isolates and indicates its possible involvement in the rearrangement, amplification, and potential dissemination of antimicrobial resistance or virulence genes. Although no direct co-localization between IS91 and functional genes was observed in this study, the widespread distribution of this element still implies a certain level of genetic mobility in these strains.

In this study, we systematically investigated the distribution of ARGs and MGEs across different sources, including humans, sika deer, and other farmed wildlife. The results showed that farmed sika deer shared 17 resistance genes with human- and other animal-derived strains, suggesting that they may serve as a potential reservoir of antimicrobial resistance genes and may participate in the transmission of these genes among different hosts via environmental mediation [53–56]. Meanwhile, a total of 13 MGEs (such as tnpA family transposases, int2 and int3 integrases, and IS91 and IS679 insertion sequences) were detected in strains from sika deer, humans, and other animal sources, further highlighting the potential role of farmed sika deer in harboring and disseminating genetic elements associated with gene transfer [57]. Phylogenetic analysis indicated that sika deer-derived strains frequently clustered with human-derived strains within the same evolutionary branches, suggesting a potential risk of cross-species transmission. MLST analysis showed that K. pneumoniae comprised 180 STs, among which ST11 was the dominant clone in both human and farmed wildlife samples. Although commonly recognized high-risk STs (such as ST23, ST11, and ST29) were not detected in sika deer isolates [58–60], several emerging multidrug-resistant (MDR) STs, including ST442 and ST35 [61], were identified. These findings suggest that, although typical hypervirulent Klebsiella lineages were not observed in sika deer, they may still act as carriers of emerging MDR clones, thereby contributing to the persistence and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance. Mantel analysis further demonstrated a significant correlation between MGEs and the composition of ARGs, emphasizing the critical role of genetic mobility in shaping the distribution of antimicrobial resistance [62, 63]. Overall, the coexistence of ARGs and MGEs may enhance the potential for cross-species transmission, highlighting the need for integrated surveillance strategies that encompass both human populations and farmed wildlife.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the samples were collected from four provinces in China, which may not fully represent the overall prevalence of Klebsiella in farmed sika deer nationwide. Second, although whole-genome sequencing was performed on 27 representative isolates, not all strains were sequenced, which may limit comprehensive interpretation of population structure and genomic diversity. In addition, the analysis of antimicrobial resistance mechanisms was primarily based on bioinformatic prediction of known resistance genes, without functional validation of potential chromosomal mutations or gene expression regulation. For the few isolates showing discrepancies between phenotypic resistance and genotypic findings, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be elucidated in future studies.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the prevalence, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and genomic features of Klebsiella spp. isolated from farmed sika deer, combined with comparative analysis of publicly available genomes from multiple hosts. The findings reveal that deer-derived Klebsiella populations harbor diverse antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence-associated factors, with a notable prevalence of biofilm-forming ability and multidrug resistance phenotypes. The identification of shared ARGs and MGEs between deer- and human-derived strains, together with the close phylogenetic relatedness and overlapping MLST profiles across hosts, highlights the potential role of farmed sika deer in the maintenance and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance within the animal–human–environment interface. Moreover, the significant association between MGEs and ARG composition underscores the importance of genetic mobility in shaping resistance patterns. Overall, these results emphasize the necessity for continuous genomic surveillance and integrated “One Health” approaches to better understand and mitigate the spread of antimicrobial resistance across different ecological niches.
