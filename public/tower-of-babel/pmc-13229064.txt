Pneumonia-associated microbial species and stabilized chlorine dioxide–containing oral care products
Authors: Hadeel Ayoub, Charu Sharma, Richard L. Gregory
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study evaluated the antifungal and antibacterial effects of stabilized chlorine dioxide–containing oral care products against yeast and bacterial species associated with pneumonia.MethodsSix yeast species (Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Cryptococcus neoformans, Candida dubliniensis, Candida krusei,Candida tropicalis) and 5 bacterial species (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa) were grown in Sabouraud dextrose broth and brain-heart infusion media, respectively, overnight. Each microorganism was treated with individual chlorine dioxide–containing ClōSYS (Rowpar Pharmaceuticals) products (Silver Multi-Benefit Fluoride Mouthwash, Sensitive Fresh Breath Mouthwash, Ultra Sensitive Mouthwash, Oral Spray, Silver Toothpaste, Sensitive Toothpaste, Fluoride-free Toothpaste) at serial dilutions. Chlorhexidine gluconate (0.12%) mouthrinse and water were positive and negative controls, respectively. Treated species were grown overnight. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was evaluated. Five microliters of each culture were transferred to blood agar plates incubated overnight, and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) and minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBC) were evaluated.ResultsAll undiluted products produced antifungal effects against tested yeast species. Similarly, antibacterial effects were observed for all products except for S pneumoniae and H influenzae, which were resistant to killing by some products. All tested products produced fungistatic or bacteriostatic effects against tested fungal or bacterial species. Different concentrations of each product are required to produce MIC, MFC, and MBC for each species tested. Some species required an equal or higher MIC than MFC and MBC.ConclusionsStabilized chlorine dioxide–containing ClōSYS oral care products exhibit antifungal and antibacterial effects against common yeast and bacterial species associated with pneumonia.

Why Is This Important?When studying viral infections, an equally important aspect to explore is the secondary infections that follow the primary infection. Some of these secondary infections could be as serious as the primary infection. The COVID-19 pandemic has been extensively explored regarding its severity, fatality, and complications. Most hospitalized patients with COVID-19 are put on ventilators to allow proper oxygen intake. One significant complication is developing ventilator-associated pneumonia, which has been reported to be fatal. This study evaluated the effectiveness of stabilized chlorine dioxide–containing ClōSYS (Rowpar Pharmaceuticals) oral care products against different bacterial and yeast species. Results from this study showed antifungal and antibacterial effects against common yeast and bacterial species associated with pneumonia. These oral care products could completely kill the species or inhibit their growth. The significance of this study is directly related to how ventilator-associated pneumonia, a serious concern associated with the use of ventilators among patients with COVID-19, can potentially be prevented or controlled through a promising, unconventional approach, which is the use of these oral care products. Future studies are still needed to evaluate the long-term effects of these products in clinical trials.

Introduction

Respiratory infection outbreaks are still among the top 6 leading causes of death globally to different extents among countries and income groups.1 Previous pandemics, such as the influenza pandemic, and viral infections, including seasonal influenza, were shown to be associated with secondary infections (eg, bacterial infections), resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates, particularly in older adults.2,3 The mechanism behind this association is strongly related to the immune-mediated damage induced by the high viral load. This results in dysregulation in immune responses. As a result, bacterial and fungal species colonization occurs, leading to epithelial damage in respiratory tract tissues.4,5

The oral microecosystem is highly diverse, consisting of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other microorganisms. More than 700 bacterial and 100 fungal species partially constitute the oral microbiota.6,7 In a healthy oral cavity, the microecosystem is dynamically balanced. The oral cavity serves as a gateway to the respiratory system. It has been widely documented that within the microecosystem, numerous species reside in the oral cavity and are associated with respiratory infections and other systemic diseases.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14 For example, pneumonia pathogens include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Haemophilus influenzae, and Klebsiella pneumoniae.15,16 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease is associated with K. pneumoniae, P aeruginosa, and S pneumoniae.17 In addition, cystic fibrosis pathogens include P aeruginosa and S aureus.18,19 The respiratory pathogens mentioned as well as pathogens associated with asthma are present in the oral microecosystem.

As described above, all microorganisms must maintain a dynamic balance through mutualism, competition, and antagonism. Poor oral hygiene leads to disturbance of the microecosystem and dominance of many pathogenic species. Consequently, it is expected to observe an association between oral health and systemic health, as described in the literature.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14

Many studies explored several aspects of the COVID-19 pandemic. Patients with COVID-19 acquire bacterial and yeast respiratory tract infections secondary to the viral disease. Some microorganisms associated with the COVID-19 infection are S pneumoniae, K pneumoniae, S aureus, H influenzae, P aeruginosa, Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, Candida dubliniensis, Candida krusei, Candida tropicalis, and Cryptococcus neoformans.20, 21, 22, 23 Moreover, Shafran et al23 compared the impact of secondary bacterial infections on the clinical course and mortality in COVID-19 (n = 642) and influenza (n = 742) patients. Patients with COVID-19 had higher rates of bacterial infections than those with influenza (12.6% vs 8.7%). Secondly, the average time from admission to bacterial growth was longer in patients with COVID-19 than patients with influenza (4 days vs 1 day). The most common bacterial coinfections were P aeruginosa (32% vs 25% of influenza- and COVID-19 infected patients; P = .50) and S aureus (17% vs 22%; P = .60). A significant difference was observed in the percentage of Enterococcus infections between patients with influenza and those with COVID-19 (0% vs 8.6%; P = .018). Significant differences were also observed in late Corynebacterium striatum and K pneumoniae infections. Furthermore, the causes of death in COVID-19 fatalities were often associated with secondary bacterial infections. The association of the number of secondary bacterial infections with death remained significant on age adjustment, minimal blood oxygen saturation, maximal C-reactive proteins, and minimal albumin levels, all variables of disease severity in patients with COVID-19 (1 infection: 2.48-fold [range, 1.16-5.29]), P = .019; > 1 infection: 7.64-fold [range, 2.46-27.00], P < .001), but not in the infected patients with influenza (1 infection: 1.11-fold [range, 0.38-2.92], P = .80; > 1 infection: 1.21-fold [range, 0.22-5.51], P = .80).23 Therefore, it is critical to explore and evaluate the impact of antimicrobial agents on COVID-19 coinfections as an approach to prevent serious complications. This is a serious issue that requires immediate attention, as reports are starting to be released about the high number of patients with COVID-19 who underwent mechanical ventilation and who exhibited bacterial and fungal ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP).24

ClōSYS (Rowpar Pharmaceuticals) oral care products containing stabilized chlorine dioxide (SCD) exhibit antimicrobial properties against different bacteria,25,26 fungi,26 and viruses.27,28 Our study aimed to determine in vitro fungistatic, fungicidal, bacteriostatic, and bactericidal effects of the SCD-containing ClōSYS toothpastes, oral rinses, and oral sprays on bacteria and fungi implicated in the onset of pneumonia. The minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC), and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for each product against each bacterial and yeast species were measured and evaluated.

Methods

Study design

This in vitro study investigated 7 oral care products containing SCD. The study started by growing all species overnight in the appropriate medium. Then, test products were prepared by preparing 7 different dilutions of each of the 7 products. After treating each strain with the different products at different dilutions, MFC, MBC, and MIC were evaluated to determine the effectiveness of each product at each concentration. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Tested products

ClōSYS oral care products, namely Silver Multi-Benefit Fluoride Mouthwash, Sensitive Fresh Breath Mouthwash, Ultra Sensitive Mouthwash, Oral Spray, Silver Toothpaste, Sensitive Toothpaste, and Fluoride-free Toothpaste were provided by Rowpar Pharmaceuticals. Chlorhexidine gluconate (0.12%) mouthrinse (MilliporeSigma) and distilled water were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Oral rinses and oral spray products were used without modification. A toothpaste solution, free of suspended particles, was prepared by mixing 1 part of the toothpaste with 2 parts of phosphate-buffered saline (ie, 1:3 dilution). This slurry was vortexed for 10 seconds, centrifuged (10 minutes, 4 °C, 27,000 × g), and syringe filtered through a 0.22-μm filter (Thermo Scientific, Remel) to obtain a clear supernatant. Serial dilutions of all tested products were prepared at no dilution, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1:16, 1:32, and 1:64. Antifungal and Antibacterial Activity

The tested yeast species were C albicans (ATCC 10231), C glabrata (ATCC 2001), C neoformans (ATCC 32045), C dubliniensis (ATCC MYA-178), C krusei (ATCC 28870), and C tropicalis (ATCC 750). In addition, 5 bacterial species were tested. The tested bacterial species were S pneumoniae (ATCC 33400), K pneumoniae (ATCC 13883), H influenzae (ATCC 33391), S aureus (ATCC 12600), and P aeruginosa (PA14).

Starter culture preparation of the yeast species was obtained by growing each species overnight in 5 mL of Sabouraud dextrose broth (Fisher Scientific) by transfer of a single colony from a fresh blood agar plate culture. Bacterial species were grown in brain-heart infusion medium, supplemented with 0.5% (5 g/L) yeast extract (Fisher Scientific) and 5% vitamin K (vol/vol) and menadione. All cultures were incubated aerobically at 5% carbon dioxide and 37 °C for 24 hours.

After growing overnight cultures, 10 μL aliquots of each broth culture were transferred to each well of a sterile 96-well microtiter plate (Fisher Scientific). Each bacterial or fungal sample was mixed with 190 μL of undiluted appropriate media (Sabouraud dextrose broth or brain-heart infusion medium; negative controls) or the corresponding treatments at different dilutions (study groups). All groups were incubated aerobically at 5% carbon dioxide and 37 °C for 24 hours. The MIC of each product against each species was determined by measuring the absorbance of the overnight broth using a microplate spectrophotometer (SpectraMax 190; Molecular Devices at an absorbance optical density of 595 nm.

To evaluate the antibacterial and antifungal effects of all products, the viability of the microorganisms was tested. Five μL of each culture fluid from each sample in the microtiter plate were plated on blood agar plates (Thermo Scientific) and incubated for 24 hours. The plates were examined for the presence or absence of colonies to determine the effect of each product and to determine the minimum MFC and MBC values of each product, which is defined as the minimum concentration of an agent that inhibits the viability of the inoculum. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Nonparametric Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to compare each pair of groups, as the data were not normally distributed. A 5% significance level was used for each test. Analyses were performed using SAS, Version 9.4 (SAS Institute). Analysis of variance was used to compare the effects of the agent’s exposure on growth. Pairwise comparisons were made using Fisher protected least significant differences method to control the overall significance level at 5%. The distribution of the measurements was examined, and a transformation of the data (eg, natural logarithm, square root, and rank) was used for the analyses, when required, to satisfy the analysis of variance assumptions. The coefficient of variation was estimated to be 0.5. With 4 wells of each dilution, the study had 80% power to detect a ratio of 1.8 (80% increase in means) between any 2 treatment conditions, assuming 2-sided tests each conducted at a 5% significance level.

MIC for bacterial species was as low as less than 6.25 and as high as 500 ppm of SCD, a value higher than the highest concentration required by the yeast species to reach the MIC. Overall, bacterial species required higher concentrations of SCD to achieve MIC and MBC than fungal species in most products, with few exceptions.

MFC and MBC values for most products were equal to or higher than MIC concentrations. An exception is the Oral Spray, in which equal or higher concentrations were needed to inhibit the growth of 3 of the 11 tested species compared with concentrations needed to kill the same species (Tables 4 and 5). Finally, the overall MIC, MFC, and MBC data were lower than any of the concentrations of SCD in commercially available products.

Discussion

Increasing evidence is being released showing the seriousness of VAP after invasive mechanical ventilation in COVID-19 cases. Furthermore, the fatalities among COVID-19 cases are not only because of overall viral invasion but of superinfections; VAP is a perfect example of this scenario.23,24,30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35

Although the gold-standard approach to treating pneumonia is the use of antibiotics, previous studies discussed the significance of some other modalities that prevent the onset or manage the severity of the infection.36,37 Among these modalities is evaluating how good oral hygiene can prevent VAP in intensive care units.36,37 Our study aimed to evaluate the in vitro reduction or killing of microorganisms associated with pneumonia using SCD-containing ClōSYS oral care products. The main finding in this study was that all undiluted products, which reflect the commercially available products, could exhibit fungicidal effects against all tested yeast species and bactericidal effects against 3 of the 5 tested bacterial species.

MIC for each product was also determined. MIC is a significant parameter that reflects another approach to infection control by fungistatic or bacteriostatic effects. As in many infectious diseases, killing the microorganism responsible for the infection is not always the approach taken. Instead, controlling the growth of that microorganism can be a potential alternative. An example is fluoride’s effect on dental biofilm. Although fluoride has a bactericidal potential, this is not the approach to control biofilm’s cariogenicity when incorporating fluorides in toothpaste and oral care products. Instead, the fluoride’s concentration in available toothpaste controls the biofilm’s cariogenicity (ie, acid production and exopolysaccharide formation).38 Therefore, even though some ClōSYS products did not kill 2 pneumonia-associated bacterial species, they do have the potential to inhibit the growth of these species, an important factor in controlling the disease. Although we cannot determine a specific reason for S pneumoniae and H influenzae exhibiting higher resistance than other species, we speculate structural differences, such as the cell wall structure, play a role. This area needs further investigation. The finding in this study that MIC values for tested fungi and bacteria were lower than the concentration of SCD in commercial products is not surprising. The variation in MIC values of an individual product for different bacteria and fungi was expected as the accessible biomolecules on the bacterial or fungal cell wall that are oxidized by chlorine dioxide are different.

One variable that was not evaluated here was the preventive effect of these products against biofilm formation. We agree this could have validated whether or not ClōSYS products play a role against serious illnesses and superinfections. Our study focused on the interaction between tested products and more mature biofilm (24 hours). Future studies must explore the antibiofilm potential of the tested products. In our laboratory, a study was conducted to evaluate the antibiofilm potential (S mutans biofilm) of these fluoride-free mouthwashes (A.C. Valdivia-Tapia, F. Lippert, P.F. Castelluccio, J.A. Cury, A.P. Ricomini-Filho, R.L. Gregory, unpublished data).

It is difficult to conclude the most effective product on the basis of the MIC, MFC, and MBC values. For example, among mouthrinses, the Ultra Sensitive Mouthwash required 4 times more concentration of SCD than the Silver Multi-Benefit Fluoride Mouthwash to kill K pneumoniae and P aeruginosa and 2 times the concentration to kill C glabrata. However, both products required the same concentration of SCD to produce an inhibitory effect against C albicans and K pneumoniae. Such a variation could be attributed to the physicochemical properties of other components in each product, an area needing further investigation.

MFC values for all ClōSYS products for tested yeast species were lower than the concentration of SCD in the respective commercial product. MBC values for the bacteria that were killed by tested products were lower than the concentration of (SCD) in the respective commercial product except for (1) MBC values for the Ultra Sensitive Mouthwash for K pneumoniae and P aeruginosa and (2) for the Sensitive Fresh Breath Mouthwash for P aeruginosa, which were the same as in the respective commercial product.

One final finding in the study was how killing a microorganism does not always require a higher product concentration than the concentration required to inhibit its growth. An example is Oral Spray, in which none of the species evaluated in this study required a higher product concentration to kill the microorganism than what was needed to inhibit its growth. In contrast, Kommerein et al39 tested the antibacterial effect of a product derived from medicinal plants and essential oils. They found that a slightly higher product concentration than that needed for MIC completely killed the bacterial species tested.39 Similarly, Katagiri et al40 found that the antifungal effects of several mouthwash ingredients (eg, chlorhexidine gluconate and tea tree oil) are concentration dependent. The MBC or MFC is complementary to the MIC. If MIC shows inhibition, plating the bacteria onto agar might still result in organism proliferation because the antimicrobial did not kill the bacteria. The significance of MBC takes place when testing the potency of several antimicrobial agents is conducted simultaneously. Another use of MBC is evaluating formulation problems wherein the formulator suspects that the active ingredient is being “bound up” by other ingredients.29 Therefore, this might explain the lower MBC and MFC values found in this study compared with MIC.

In our study, all microorganisms were grown in broth, treated with corresponding products, and then transferred to blood agar plates to monitor viability and growth. The antimicrobial agents were bound by the broth used for the MIC, and when transferred to the blood agar plates, they became more available. Alternatively, because the MBC test uses colony-forming units as a proxy measure of bacterial viability, it can be confounded by antibacterial agents that cause aggregation of bacterial cells. Examples of antibacterial agents which do this include flavonoids and peptides.41,42 This would cause the antimicrobial agent to have a different killing effect than the inhibition observed in the MIC assay. A third possible explanation is that the rate of killing of the agent is different with these microbes than the rate of inhibition.43 For example, some agents may be able to lyse the cell wall or membrane of certain microbes and cause cellular death, whereas higher concentrations of that same agent may agglutinate in solution and not penetrate the cell wall, resulting in less inhibition.

One major limitation of our study is that biofilm formation was not evaluated. In addition, our study was conducted in vitro, under optimal conditions, which do not reflect actual clinical situations. A third limitation is limiting the experiments to a single strain per yeast or bacterial species. This is a challenge, especially with the rapidly developing variants of microorganisms observed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Future studies must evaluate the potential of these products against biofilm formation. Future studies may also focus on investigating the long-term effect of ClōSYS products through clinical trials to monitor users of the products, their oral health, and full pulmonary functions. Another future study could investigate different strains of the same yeast or bacterial species, especially with the mutants and variants that are quickly developing. Candida auris is an emerging organism of concern in hospital settings regarding transmissibility and multidrug resistance. C auris should be tested in future studies. Finally, using a more sophisticated biofilm model, such as a pH cycling model that reflects the oral environment, may lead to more clinically relevant findings.

Conclusions

This in vitro study evaluated the fungicidal, bactericidal, fungistatic, and bacteriostatic effects of SCD-containing oral care products against the most common yeast and bacteria associated with pneumonia. They are over-the-counter and cost-effective products with potential to prevent more serious conditions, such as reducing the proliferation of pneumonia. Further clinical studies are needed to determine the in vivo efficacy.
