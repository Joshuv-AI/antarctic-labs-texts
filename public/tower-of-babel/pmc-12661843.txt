Altered music emotion experiences of congenital amusia: from the perspective of reward
Authors: Zhishuai Jin, Sizhu Huyang, Qixiong Li, Lichen Jiang, Yajun Yan, Ming Xu, Jinyu Wang, Qiaoping Lian, Lu Xia, Daxing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA biological model of auditory learning posits that the auditory cortex interacts with the cognitive, sensorimotor, and reward systems to improve sound learning in real-world listening. Congenital amusia is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in fine-grained pitch discrimination. Although previous studies have investigated the auditory processing, sensorimotor and cognitive abilities of amusia, the characteristics of musical reward in amusia remain unclear.MethodWe recruited 44 individuals with congenital amusia (22 females; 19.66 ± 1.92years), identified with the Montreal Battery of Evaluation of Amusia (MBEA; cut-off ≤ 21.5) and 44 matched controls(23 females; 19.61 ± 1.65years). Participants completed the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire(BMRQ) to assess derived pleasure from engagement in musical activities, and the Multiple Mood Scale (MMS) to evaluate emotional experiences (heightened, tragic, romantic, blithe) elicited by minor- and major-key music.ResultsThe amusia group reported lower overall BMRQ scores and lower scores on all five BMRQ subscales (social reward, mood regulation, emotional evocation, sensory-motor and musical seeking) compared to controls. For the musical emotion task, both groups experienced similar musical emotions for major music, whereas the amusia group rated minor-key music as significantly less tragic and more blithe. Additionally, overall BMRQ scores were negatively associated with blithe and heightened ratings for major-key music, whereas these associations were positive in controls.ConclusionsThese findings suggest individuals with amusia exhibit music-specific deficits in the pleasure derived from music, together with an alteration in emotion experience to minor-key music. Overall, the findings indicate an alteration in the use of pitch-derived cues when evaluating musical emotions and their relation to reward. From a reward perspective, these findings guide neuroimaging studies toward clarifying how pitch-based cues influence music-induced emotion experiences in congenital amusia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03664-2.

Background

Congenital amusia is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized primarily by impairments in music pitch discrimination, with an estimated prevalence of 1.5–4% in the general population [1, 2]. Although pitch deficits are the prominent feature of amusia, they may not fully represent the underlying neural or cognitive mechanisms. For example, individuals with amusia exhibit deficits in the discrimination of small pitch differences in music, whereas discrimination of large pitch differences can remain preserved [3]. This suggests that amusia may involve a specific difficulty in processing subtle pitch variations, rather than a generalized auditory processing disorder. Kraus and White-Schwoch [4] proposed a biological model of auditory learning that may help explain these specific difficulties in amusia. According to their model, auditory learning involves the interaction among the auditory cortex and cognitive, sensorimotor and reward systems, which collaborate to guide and improve sound learning in real-world situations. Deficits in any of these interacting systems could lead to the characteristic difficulties in pitch discrimination seen in amusia. While other hypotheses suggest that amusia may arise from dysfunction in the auditory cortex [5], sensorimotor disorder [6] or cognitive learning deficits [7], their model provides a more integrated framework. It suggests that these mechanisms may not be independent but rather interact to impair auditory learning processes.

A central component of musical reward is the ability of music to induce emotions. However, studies on emotional processing in congenital amusia have showed inconsistent results. On one hand, several studies suggest that individuals with amusia exhibit reduced sensitivity in recognizing and experiencing musical emotions. For example, Jiang et al., [8] evaluated the emotional intensity of Western and Indian melodies in both amusia and control groups. While the control group rated Western melodies as less emotionally intense than Indian melodies, no such significant difference was found in the amusia group. This suggests that the differences of emotional sensitivity in amusia may be context-dependent, potentially influenced by cultural factors or the type of music. McDonald and Stewart [1] observed that individuals with amusia engaged in less musical activities and displayed apathy towards music, a finding later supported by Cousineau, who reported that most individuals with amusia exhibited lower desire to participate in music activities [9]. On the other hand, some researchers argued that while congenital amusia primarily affects the recognition of emotions, the emotional experience may remain unaffected. Leveque et al. [10] examined the emotional recognition and emotional experience using musical stimuli and facial expressions. Their results showed that while individuals with amusia were impaired in recognizing emotion of musical stimuli, their emotional experience, specifically the emotional intensity they felt, was similar to that of the control group. Furthermore, other studies have found that, under laboratory conditions, the recognition of music emotions in individuals with amusia did not differ significantly from that of controls. Most of them were able to identify four emotions such as happiness, sadness, fear and peacefulness, conveyed through unfamiliar music clips [11]. These results may indicate changes in the musical reward processing systems of congenital amusia.

The musical reward effects manifest in multiple aspects, influencing emotional experiences, regulating emotions, promoting sensorimotor coordination, serving as a tool for social bonding, and stimulating individual interest and motivation. These effects are primarily mediated by dopaminergic pathways in the mesolimbic system, including structures such as the nucleus accumbens and ventral striatum. These regions release dopamine in response to pleasurable music, contributing to feelings of enjoyment and motivation further engagement with music [12]. Emotional engagement with music plays a crucial role in the reward process, as dopamine release is triggered not only by sensory stimuli but also by emotional experiences associated with music. For instance, structural elements of music, such as the dichotomy between major and minor keys, directly shape emotional responses, thus modulating reward processing [13]. In Western tonal music, the major key (characterized by a bright tonic-third interval) is typically associated with positive emotions such as joy and warmth, while the minor key (featuring a lowered third) evokes negative emotions like sadness or tension [13]. This emotional contrast is based on the interplay between acoustic properties and culturally learned associations, which together activate fronto-striatal circuits, integrating the prefrontal cortex and striatum to generate motivational and affective responses [13–17]. However, disruptions in emotional recognition, as seen in some individuals with amusia, may impair this process, reducing intrinsic reward value by diminishing sensitivity to structural-emotional cues like keys [12, 18].

This study aimed to comprehensively investigate musical reward sensitivity and music-induced emotional experiences in individuals with congenital amusia from the perspective of reward. We assessed pleasure derived from engagement in musical activities with the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ) across five subscales (social reward, mood regulation, emotion evocation, sensory-motor and musical seeking), and evaluated emotional experiences to major- and minor-key excerpts with the Multiple mood Scale (MMS) [19–21]. To control for non-music influences on reward and motivation, participants also completed the Chinese versions of the Physical Anhedonia Scale (PAS), the Revised Social Anhedonia Scale (RSAS), and the Behavioral Inhibition/Activation System Scale (BIS/BAS). Guided by the biological model of auditory learning proposed [4], which emphasizes interactions among the auditory cortex, cognitive, sensorimotor and reward systems, we hypothesized 1) individuals with congenital amusia would report lower overall BMRQ and subscale scores than controls, reflecting reduced pleasure from music-related activities; 2) they would show lower MMS ratings to both major and minor-key excerpts, indicating altered emotion experiences with music; 3) they would show smaller key-related differences in emotional experience than those observed in controls. Clarifying these patterns will help determine whether disrupted integration underlies altered music-emotion experiences in amusia and will guide future neurobiological investigations of the underlying mechanism.

Methods

Participants

An a priori power analysis in G*Power (two-tailed independent-samples t test, α = 0.05, 1–β = 0.80) indicated that detecting an effect size of 0.60 would require 45 per group (N = 90). Individuals were prescreened with the Montreal Battery of Evaluation of Amusia (MBEA, cut-off ≤ 21.5), and then eligible participants individuals into the laboratory study. Inclusion criteria were: native Mandarin speakers, right-handed, age18-25 years, no history of professional musical training, and no neurological or psychiatric disorders. Right-handedness was confirmed with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI) to minimize variability due to hemispheric lateralization in pitch and language networks, which may influence auditory and musical processing. All participants underwent binaural audiometry using pure tone audiometry and were confirmed to have normal hearing. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

A total of 88 individuals were enrolled: 44 individuals with amusia (22 males and 22 females) and 44 matched controls (21 males and 23 females). Groups were matched on age and years of education; intelligence were screened with Chinese-revised Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) [22], and all scores were above 85.

Ethical statement

Participants were recruited via convenience sampling from universities in Changsha between June 2022 and September 2023. The study protocol was approved by Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (Approval ID: LYF20220108) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaires and task

Amusia screening

Congenital amusia was identified with the MBEA following Peretz et al. [23]. The MBEA comprises six subtests: three melodic tests that assessing pitch dimension (scale, contour, interval), two temporal tests evaluating timing (rhythm, meter), and one test assessing musical memory. The MBEA has been validated as a reliable screening tool for congenital amusia in Eastern cultures [2, 3]. In line with the validation study in Chinese samples (e.g., Nan et al.,), participants with an average MBEA score below 21.5 across the six subtests were classified as having amusia [1]. Musical questionnaire

The MMS was adapted from Taniguchi’s framework linking music to affective states [19] and the factor structure reported Kawakami [20, 24, 25]. It contains 62 emotion words, categorized into four dimensions: Heightened (e.g., Fiery, Overwhelmed, Passionate; 20 items); Tragic (e.g., Sad, Sorrowful, Gloomy; 16 items); Romantic (e.g., Fascinated, Dear, Serene; 15 items); Blithe (e.g., Happy, Merry, Bouncy; 11 items). After each excerpt, participants rated how strongly each word described their felt emotion on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all, 4 = very much). The MMS has been validated in previous studies involving both musicians and non-musicians [20].

Musical reward and anhedonia measures

Participants completed the Chinese version of the BMRQ [26], which measures overall music-reward sensitivity; higher total scores indicate greater pleasure derived from musical experiences. The BMRQ evaluates five dimensions: social reward, which refers to the pleasure derived from sharing music with others, such as group participation in musical activities or experiencing music in a social context; mood regulation, which involves using music to regulate one’s emotions, including enhancing positive emotions or alleviating negative feelings; emotion evocation, which is experiencing strong emotions, such as joy, sadness, or excitement, elicited by music; sensory-motor, which involves physical responses to music, such as dancing, tapping, or other forms of motor activity in response to rhythm and melody, and musical seeking, which refers to the intrinsic drive to seek out and engage with musical experiences for personal enjoyment, emotional fulfillment, or self-expression. The Chinese version of BMRQ demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) [27].

To assess individual differences in anhedonia and motivation, additional measures included the Chinese versions of the Physical Anhedonia Scale (RPAS; Cronbach’s α = 0.83), Revised Social Anhedonia Scale (RSAS; Cronbach’s α = 0.76), the Chinese version of the Behavioral Inhibition (BIS; Cronbach’s α = 0.87) and Behavioral Activation System Scale (BAS; Cronbach’s α = 0.87). This allowed for distinguishing music-specific reward differences from general anhedonia.

Music emotion task

Stimuli

We used published piano excerpts in which musical key (major/minor) was manipulated with an expert via transposition while holding other features constant [20]. Each excerpt lasted about 30 s. For Glinka’s La Separation, the excerpt comprised 16 bars, rendered at quarter note = 80 in F minor and in the corresponding F-major transposition. For Granados’s Allegro de Concierto (Op.46), the excerpts comprised 9 bars rendered at quarter note = 70 in G major and in the G-minor transposition.

Procedure

The task was conducted individually in a sound-attenuated room. Participants sat approximately 100 cm from a computer monitor, and musical stimuli were presented through a pair of calibrated loud-speaker at a comfortable volume. To prevent task-induced affect from biasing responses on self-reported questionnaires, these measures (BMRQ, RPAS/RSAS, BIS/BAS) were completed before the listening task.

During the music emotion task, excerpts were presented in a pseudo-randomized order, with key (major/minor) counterbalanced across participants to minimize sequence effect. Immediately after each excerpt, participants were instructed to rate the emotions they experienced while listening to the music using the MMS. The instructions were: “You will hear a series of musical excerpts. After each piece, please rate the intensity of the emotions you felt while listening, using the scale provided. " They were given as much time as they needed to evaluate the music emotion and were free to rate the descriptive words or phrases at their own pace. The laboratory session lasted approximately 50 min. MBEA used for amusia screening was administered on a separate screening day prior to the laboratory session and lasted approximately 60 min.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed in SPSS 24.0. Group differences in demographic and behavioral variables were assessed using χ² tests for gender and independent-samples t-tests for age, year of education, IQ, and overall MBEA and subtest scores. Distribution was checked using the Shapiro–Wilk test; when normality was violated, non-parametric alternatives were used. Homogeneity of variance was checked with Levene’s test.

BMRQ factor scores were calculated based on the factor analysis procedure recommended by Mas-Herrero et al., [26]. Group differences in overall BMRQ and subscales, as well as RPAS, RSAS, and BIS/BAS scores, were examined using independent-samples t-tests.

For the music emotion task, four two-way mixed-design analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted, one per MMS dimension (Heightened, Tragic, Romantic, and Blithe), with group (amusia vs. control) as a between-subjects factor and key (major vs. minor) as the within-subjects factor. Significant effects were followed by Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests. Post-hoc power analyses (α = 0.05, two-tailed) were conducted in G*Power 3.1 using the observed effect sizes (Results are provided in Supplementary Table S1).

Pearson correlations were conducted separately within each group between the overall BMRQ score and each MMS dimension. Correlations involving BMRQ subscales are reported in Supplementary Material. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests, partial ηp² for ANOVAs, and r for correlations; conventional benchmarks were used (small/medium/large: d = 0.20/0.50/0.80; ηp² ≈ 0.01/0.06/0.14; r = 0.10/0.30/0.50).

Results

Eighty-eight participants (amusia: n = 44; controls: n = 44) completed the study. Groups did not differ in gender, age, years of education, or IQ (all ps > 0.05). The amusia group scored significantly lower than controls on the MBEA average and on the melodic, temporal and memory subtests (all ps < 0.001). Demographic data and test results are presented in Table 1.

Analysis of music reward sensitivity between amusia and control

Relative to controls, the CA group showed significantly lower overall BMRQ scores (Fig. 1; p < 0.001, d = 1.08) and lower scores on each BMRQ subscale (all p values ≤ 0.002; ds = 0.68–0.94). Groups did not differ on physical anhedonia, social anhedonia, behavioral activation, depression or anxiety (all ps > 0.05). However, a marginally significant difference was found for the behavioral inhibition scores, with the amusia group having a slightly lower score than controls (t = − 1.87, p = 0.065) (Table 2).

MMS dimension 1: heightened emotion

The ANOVA results (Table 3) corresponding to heightened emotion revealed no significant main effect of Group or Key on MMS ratings (p > 0.05). The Group × Key interaction was also not significant (p > 0.05).

MMS dimension 2: tragic emotion

The ANOVA results corresponding to tragic emotion showed significant main effects of Group (F(1, 86) = 11.52, p = 0.001, ηp²= 0.12) and Key (F(1, 86) = 155.52, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.66). There was also a significant Group × Key interaction (F(1, 86) = 19.92, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.20, large; Table 4). Simple effects showed that under minor-key music, the amusia group reported rated tragic emotion words significantly lower than controls (Table 7; Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.001), whereas no group difference emerged under major-key music (adjusted p > 0.05).

MMS dimension 3: romantic emotion

For romantic emotion (Table 5), a significant main effect of Key was observed (F(1, 86) = 66.01, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.43, large), with higher ratings for major-key than minor-key excerpts. The Group main effect was not significant (p > 0.05). and the Group × Key interaction did not reach significance (p = 0.066).

MMS dimension 4: blithe emotion

Discussion

This study examined music reward in individuals with congenital amusia from two perspectives: derived pleasure from engaging in musical activities, captured by the BMRQ, and emotion experiences in response to major and minor key excerpts, measured by the MMS. Compared to controls, individuals with amusia showed significantly lower BMRQ total scores and lower scores on all five dimensions (social reward, mood regulation, emotion evocation, sensory-motor and musical seeking), indicating reduced sensitivity to musical reward. However, the groups did not differ on general reward sensitivity, as measured by BAS/BIS, and anhedonia scales, which suggests that reward-related differences between groups are music-specific rather than domain general. On the music emotional task, emotional experiences to major-key music were comparable across groups, whereas minor-key music induced lower tragic and higher blithe ratings in the amusia group. Finally, the associations between BMRQ scores and emotion experiences diverged by group: in amusia, BMRQ scores were negatively related to blithe ratings for major-key music; while in controls, higher BMRQ scores were positively related to heightened and blithe ratings for major-key music.

Reduced music reward sensitivity of amusia

The amusia group showed significantly lower scores on the total BMRQ as well as on all five dimensions, including social reward, mood regulation, emotion evocation, sensory-motor and musical seeking. These findings suggest reduced sensitivity to music-related activities in individuals with amusia. This result is consistent with previous studies reporting that many individuals with amusia exhibit reduced interest or emotional engagement with music, indicating that the emotional and motivational appeal of music may be diminished in this population [1].

Importantly, no significant group differences were found in general reward sensitivity, physical and social anhedonia, or emotional states. This finding implies that their music reward deficits are unlikely to be due to a general dysfunction of the reward system, but rather to impairments in auditory processing that interfere with the extraction of emotional cues. Specifically, individuals with amusia show difficulties in perceiving temporal patterns and melodic contours, such as detecting pitch changes in music and emotional prosody in speech [28]. These broader auditory processing impairments, including impaired fine-grained pitch discrimination [11], likely hinder the integration of emotional cues within musical structure (e.g., detecting tension-resolution patterns or dynamic prosodic variations). Since pitch and temporal patterns are essential for conveying emotional meaning in music [29], these impairments reduce the emotional salience of musical stimuli, diminishing both emotional engagement and the reward derived from music [30]. Therefore, the reduced music reward observed in amusia is likely a result of disruption in the auditory and emotional integration pathway, rather than a direct consequence of a dysfunction in the reward system.

Altered music emotion sensitivity in minor-key music among individuals with amusia

Emotions induced by music are an important component of its reward value. In this study, both groups showed typical emotional patterns in response to musical key. Major-key music tended to induce heightened, romantic and blithe emotions while reducing feelings of tragedy. Conversely, minor-key music was more likely to elicit tragic emotions and diminish heightened and blithe emotions. These patterns align with the emotional connotations of musical keys, whereby major keys are generally perceived as bright, uplifting and associated with positive affect, while minor keys are associated with somber, melancholic, or dramatic emotions, reflecting negative valence and heighted tension [13, 31, 32].

However, group differences emerged for minor-key music. Compared to controls, the amusia group reported lower tragic and higher blithe ratings to minor excerpts, with no group differences under major-key music. This combination of findings indicates an altered processing of negative emotional cues conveyed by the minor key. Rather than reflecting an absence of emotional experience, this pattern may point to an alternative appraisal in congenital amusia [28]. When subtle pitch variations that convey negative valence are hard to access, listeners may rely on other musical feature leading to a more neutral or positive interpretation. Specifically, the findings could reflect a sequence in amusia have reduced access to fine grained pitch information, the cue to valence mapping become less reliable, and attention is reallocated toward other features.

Although romantic emotion ratings did not differ between groups, participants experienced more romantic emotions in response to major-key music, consistent with prior studies on musical valence [33]. Major-key music is typically associated with positive and pleasant affect, including happiness and warmth, which aligns with the core attributes of the romantic dimension such as affection, intimacy, and tenderness. The romantic dimension may depend less on fine-grained pitch perception and more on global musical features, such as tempo or harmonic richness, as well as on listeners’ life experiences [33]. In major-key contexts, the emotional clarity conveyed by harmonic structure and rhythmic organization appears sufficient for listeners, including those with congenital amusia, to experience romantic emotions despite reduced access to subtle pitch cues.

Heightened emotions, which index general arousal or excitement, showed no main effects of group or key, suggesting that arousal-based responses to music remain largely intact in amusia. From the perspective of cognitive appraisal theory [34], emotions reflect evaluative interpretations rather than direct readouts of acoustic input. Impaired pitch perception in amusia may therefore disrupt appraisals of emotional valence particularly for minor-key music, where recognition relies more heavily on subtle pitch cues. This account explains why the amusia group diverged from controls in pitch-related dimensions, such as tragic and blithe ratings under minor-key excerpts, while showing preserved responses in romantic and heightened dimensions.

Dissociation between musical reward sensitivity and emotional experience among individuals with amusia

Correlation analyses revealed additional differences between groups. In the control group, higher BMRQ scores were positively related to blithe and heightened ratings for major-key music, which supports the view that motivation to engage with music is closely linked to positive emotional experiences in typical listeners [35]. In the amusia group, higher overall BMRQ scores were negatively associated with blithe and romantic ratings for major-key music. This inverse relationship indicates that while some individuals with amusia retain a motivation to engage with music, they may not experience the expected emotional pleasure or rewards from it. This dissociation between music reward and emotional experiences has been observed in music-specific anhedonia [30, 36] and may reflect disruptions in affective appraisal rather than a lack of musical interest. Specifically, individuals with amusia may have difficulty processing the emotional cues in music, particularly those related to pitch and tonal shifts. These impairments could disrupt the interaction between the auditory cortex and subcortical reward systems, such as the nucleus accumbens, which plays a central role in music-induced pleasure. This reduced functional connectivity between auditory regions and the reward network may explain why amusic individuals report lower emotional rewards from music, despite maintaining their motivation to engage with it.

In addition, emotion states selectively influenced emotional experiences in the amusia group. For minor-key music, higher depression and anxiety scores were associated with more tragic ratings and with lower blithe ratings. These associations were absent in the control group. This result suggests that when external musical cues are not adequately perceived because of pitch deficits, internal stats exerts a greater influence on emotional appraisal [13, 37, 38].

Reconsidering the consistent reduction in BMRQ scores across all domains in the amusia group may attributed to several factors. First, individuals with amusia may show reduced sensitivity to emotional cues in music, especially those associated with sadness and tension in minor-key music, which can limit their access to emotionally rewarding experiences [39]. Second, early auditory processing difficulties may discourage musical engagement over time, thereby reducing the opportunities to develop emotionally meaningful associations with music [9]. Third, even when motivation is retained, the absence of corresponding emotional resonance, as indicated by the negative correlations between reward sensitivity and emotional ratings, suggests a disconnect between the desire to engage with music and the capacity to derive affective reward from it in individuals with congenital amusia.

The reduced musical reward sensitivity and the altered emotional experiences observed in congenital amusia may reflect disruptions in the interaction between cognitive and reward systems. Prior neuroimaging research has identified structural and functional anomalies in frontotemporal networks in amusia [40–43], which may affect connectivity with subcortical reward regions such as the nucleus accumbens and putamen [12, 44–47]. As a neurodevelopmental disorder, congenital amusia may involve early alterations in frontal cortical development that potentially weaken integration with limbic reward circuits. Our behavioral data are consistent with this account, although they do not directly demonstrate neural dysfunction.

Limitations and future directions

The stimulus set was small and limited to Western tonal music. Participants completed 62 emotion items after each excerpt, which may induce fatigue or memory decay for the just-heard music. Future researches should use larger and more diverse stimulus sets (e.g., including classical, popular, and non-western traditions), pre-validate valence and arousal in the target population, assess familiarity, and parametrically vary acoustic features (e.g., tempo, loudness, harmonic density, timbre) while incorporating computational acoustic descriptors as predictors.

A second limitation is the absence direct neural or physiological measures. Further research should directly test this framework by examining whether functional connectivity between fronto-striatal and auditory networks relates to BMRQ scores at rest and during listening, and whether striatal responses to key (major/minor) and pitch-cue manipulations are reduced in individuals with congenital amusia.

Finally, the sample was culturally homogeneous. All participants were native Mandarin speakers. Linguistic factors may influence music perception and emotion recognition, so future studies should recruit more diverse populations across languages and cultures to enhance generalizability.

Conclusions

This study found that individuals with congenital amusia exhibit reduced musical reward sensitivity and altered emotional experiences in response to minor-key music. Specifically, they reported lower tragic and higher blithe ratings for minor-key music, while romantic and heightened emotional experiences were preserved. In the amusia group, higher music reward sensitivity was related to less positive rating for major-key music, which points to a weaker alignment between musical engagement and positive emotion experiences induced by music. In controls, higher musical reward sensitivity was related to more positive rating for major-key music. Taken together, these findings indicate a specific alteration in how pitch cues shape affective evaluation and reward in congenital amusia. Future neuroimaging studies are needed to clarify these interactions in congenital amusia by examining frontotemporal and striatal networks during the processing of musical mode and other acoustic features.
