Clinical characteristics and prognostic analysis of sepsis in immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised patients: a prospective cohort study in the emergency department
Authors: Wei Hu, Yingxiu Wu, Chengfei Bi, Kairui Ren, Nan Zhang, Huadong Zhu, Yan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the clinical characteristics and prognosis of immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised patients with sepsis and to develop a novel mortality prediction model for these patients.MethodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted between February and October 2023 in the emergency department (ED) of Peking Union Medical College Hospital and included 145 sepsis patients (67 with immunocompromised state and 78 without). The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression were employed to assess the relationship between immune status and mortality risk, identify independent risk factors, and develop a predictive nomogram.ResultsThe 90-day mortality rate in immunocompromised patients was significantly higher than that in the control group (55.2% vs. 34.6%, p = 0.013). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis identified immunocompromised state as an independent risk factor for mortality (HR = 2.980, 95% CI: 1.679–5.288; p < 0.001). In the subgroup analysis, autoimmune diseases (HR = 3.926, 95% CI: 1.763–8.744), poorly controlled diabetes (HR = 4.432, 95% CI: 1.638–11.992), and malignant neoplasms (HR = 3.195, 95% CI: 1.203–8.486) were significantly associated with an increased mortality risk. After statistical adjustment, seven factors were significantly associated with sepsis mortality (p < 0.05), including respiratory tract infection, urinary tract infection, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, immunocompromised state, septic shock, and urgent surgery. The nomogram model based on these factors demonstrated strong predictive ability and accuracy, with a C-index of 0.808 (p < 0.001).ConclusionImmunocompromised state is an independent risk factor for mortality in sepsis patients, and different types of immunosuppression exert varying impacts on prognosis. The nomogram model developed on the basis of these findings offers valuable guidance for individualized risk assessment in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13417-0.Keywords: Sepsis, Immunocompromised, Mortality, Prognostic analysis, Nomogram

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening condition characterized by organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated host response to infection, which may progress to septic shock [1]. Epidemiological data from 2017 indicate that approximately 48.9 million cases of sepsis occurred globally, resulting in 11 million sepsis-related deaths, accounting for 19.7% of all global mortality [2]. Notably, the in-hospital mortality rate for septic shock ranges from 40% to 60% [3]. The median hospitalization cost for each sepsis patient was $32,421, while the median ICU treatment cost was $27,461 [4]. These findings not only emphasize the significant global burden of sepsis but also highlight the critical need for precise prognostication of patient outcomes and the systematic optimization of clinical interventions.

Studies suggest that immunosuppression plays a central role in the disability and mortality associated with sepsis [5–8]. Jonathan et al. [9] collected and analyzed immune cells from the spleen or lungs of sepsis patients within 30 to 180 min post-mortem, revealing a significant decrease in the production of both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, upregulation of inhibitory receptors (including PD1), expansion of regulatory T (TReg) cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and downregulation of activation pathways mediated by CD28 and Human Leukocyte Antigen-DR (HLA-DR). These findings indicate the involvement of multiple, overlapping immune suppression mechanisms across innate and adaptive immune systems.

In recent years, as our understanding of various diseases has deepened, the incidence of conditions such as cancer and rheumatic immune diseases has increased. Furthermore, advances in techniques like hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and solid organ transplantation, along with the use of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and cytotoxic drugs, have significantly elevated the number of immunocompromised patients. However, limited data are available regarding the pathophysiology and outcomes of sepsis patients with immunosuppression, and findings across studies remain inconsistent. It is speculated that immunocompromised state may worsen clinical outcomes in sepsis [5]. A prospective observational study by Paiva José-Artur et al. demonstrated that, compared to non-immunocompromised patients, septic shock was more prevalent in the immunocompromised group, although mortality rates between the two groups were similar (31.1% vs. 28.9%; p = 0.468), and immune dysfunction was not identified as a risk factor for sepsis prognosis (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.66–1.43) [10]. In contrast, Mallory Sheth et al. found that sepsis patients with autoimmune diseases had a 27% reduction in the 30-day mortality risk, with the strongest correlation observed in patients with septic shock (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54–0.93, p = 0.014) [11]. This discrepancy suggests that the relationship between immunosuppression and sepsis prognosis may be influenced by various factors, and the underlying reasons for the observed improvements in clinical outcomes remain unclear. It is noteworthy that the majority of existing studies are based on Western populations, which may limit the applicability of these findings to Chinese populations due to differences in genetic backgrounds, environmental factors, and medical practices. These factors may potentially influence the effect of immunocompromised state on sepsis prognosis.

Building on the identified research gaps and with the aim of offering valuable insights for future studies, we analyzed the clinical data of sepsis patients with immunocompromised state. Our objective was to identify high-risk subgroups within the immunocompromised population and potential risk factors that influence sepsis prognosis, while also developing a predictive nomogram model to enhance clinical application.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted in the emergency department (ED) of Peking Union Medical College Hospital in Beijing, China. Between February 2023 and October 2023, all adult patients (≥ 18 years old) diagnosed with sepsis within 24 h of emergency department admission were enrolled. Sepsis was defined according to the Sepsis-3 criteria [1]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (approval number: PUMCH-K22C1863).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1)symptom onset > 1 week duration; (2) anticipated hospital stay of less than 48 h; (3) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (4) prior urgent surgery at another hospital; (5) inability to provide informed consent; (6) participation in other interventional sepsis research within the preceding six months; and (7) overlapping cases (i.e., patients meeting the criteria for multiple subgroups).

Patients were categorized into two groups based on diagnostic criteria for immunocompromised status.A patient was classified as immunocompromised if any of the following conditions were met: (1) congenital or acquired immune deficiency; (2) HIV-positive status; (3) solid tumours or haematological malignancies; (4) organ or stem cell transplantation; (5) splenectomy; (6) neutropenia lasting at least 48 h (neutrophil count < 1.0 × 109/L); (7) autoimmune diseases requiring sustained corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, polymyositis/dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, overlap syndrome, systemic vasculitis, inflammatory myopathies, seronegative spondyloarthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, myasthenia gravis, Gravis' disease); (8) diabetes with prolonged poor glycaemic control [12, 13].

Data collection and study outcomes

Before initiating empirical antibiotic therapy, participants underwent a comprehensive clinical history review and physical examination. Basic laboratory tests, including chemistry panels, haematological tests, arterial blood gas analysis, and microbiological investigations, were conducted. Patient data were extracted from the electronic medical records system. A standardized survey (Table S1) was used to collect demographic characteristics, vital parameters, and laboratory measurements at admission. Treatment decisions were individualized based on the attending physician’s judgement in accordance with established guidelines. Data on treatments administered outside the ED were not collected.

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality within 90 days of sepsis diagnosis. Secondary outcomes included 28-day mortality and early case fatality rate, defined as death from any cause within 48 h of sepsis confirmation.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All statistical tests were two-sided, with the significance level set at p < 0.05. Clinical characteristics and outcome variables were compared between immunocompromised and non-immunocompromised patients. Variables with more than 20% missing data were excluded from analyses, and multiple imputation was performed for variables with less than 20% missing data. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) in cases of severe deviation from normal distribution, and were compared between groups using the t-test or Mann–Whitney test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages (counts) and compared using the chi-squared test. Mortality outcomes were assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and differences between groups were evaluated using the log-rank test. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was used for collinearity diagnosis of the covariates in the model. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses adjusted by confounding factors were used to assess the relationships between continuous variables with 90-day mortality outcomes. Risk prediction for the primary outcome was estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression models before and adjusting for confounding factors, including age, gender, chronic heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, chronic kidney failure, and cirrhosis. As a sensitivity analysis, LASSO regression combined with multivariable Cox regression (LASSO-Cox approach) was further performed in R to verify the robustness of the variable selection strategy.

The predictive accuracy gained by adding each clinically significant independent factor to the basic model was assessed using both discrimination and calibration statistics. Discrimination was assessed using net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), which measure improvements in predictive performance [14, 15]. Additionally, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was used to determine whether the inclusion of these factors improved the ability to distinguish between patients who died from sepsis and those who survived. Calibration was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), which assess consistency between the predicted probabilities and observed risks, as well as overall model fit following the incorporation of clinically significant independent factors. The net benefit of adding these factors to the basic model was visualized using decision curve analysis (DCA) [16]. The x-axis of decision curve analysis graph denotes the thresholds for sepsis mortality, and the y-axis denotes the net benefits for different thresholds.A higher net benefit is indicated when the model curve is farther from the grey solid line (assuming all patients experience sepsis mortality) and the black solid line (assuming no patients experience sepsis mortality). The farther the curve from these reference lines, the higher the net benefit.

To facilitate clinical interpretation, a nomogram was developed to predict 12-day, 36-day, and 60-day sepsis mortality based on baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, clinical treatment parameters, and clinically significant independent factors. The nomogram was constructed using the ‘rms’ package (regression modelling strategies) in R version 4.0.2 (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/rms/index.html). The predictive accuracy and discriminative ability of the nomogram were assessed using the concordance index (C-index) and calibration curves. The 45° line in the calibration curve represents perfect prediction. The closer the curve is to this line, the more consistent the predicted probabilities are with actual observations. Models for predicting outcomes in sepsis patients were internally validated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications. At each resampling step, the models were refitted, and their discrimination and calibration were evaluated using both the bootstrap samples and the original dataset. The performance differences between these datasets were then calculated and averaged across all 1000 replications to derive the optimism-adjusted C-statistics.

Results

Clinical characteristics

This study included 145 of 310 sepsis patients, comprising 78 non-immunocompromised and 67 immunocompromised patients. The latter group consisted of 18 patients with autoimmune diseases, 18 with neutropenia, 19 with uncontrolled diabetes, and 12 with malignant neoplasms (Fig. 1). Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of these patients. Overall, immunocompromised patients had a significantly higher 90-day mortality rate (55.2% vs. 34.6%, p = 0.013). Compared with non-immunocompromised sepsis patients, immunocompromised patients had higher 28-day and early mortality rates, although these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.153 and p = 0.273, respectively). Within the immunocompromised subgroup, the 90-day mortality rate was highest in patients with autoimmune diseases (72.2%), followed by those with neutropenia (55.6%), malignant neoplasms (50.0%), and diabetes (42.1%).

Demographic analysis revealed a significantly lower proportion of male patients in the immunocompromised group (50.7% vs. 70.5%, p = 0.015), whereas no significant difference in age was observed between the two groups (60.976 ± 9.890 vs. 61.295 ± 17.048 years, p = 0.889). Regarding comorbidities, the prevalence of cirrhosis was higher in the non-immunocompromised group (9.0% vs. 1.5%, p = 0.049). Analysis of infection source revealed significant differences between two groups. Abdominal infections were more common in non-immunocompromised patients (56.4% vs. 38.8%, p = 0.034), whereas respiratory tract infections showed an insignificant trend toward higher prevalence in the immunocompromised group (46.3% vs. 34.6%, p = 0.153). Aetiological analysis indicated that fungal infections were more prevalent in immunocompromised patients (35.8% vs. 19.2%, p = 0.025), and polymicrobial infections were also more common in this group (49.3% vs. 30.8%, p = 0.023). Laboratory parameters, including white blood cell count (median: 5.73 vs. 12.80 × 109/L, p < 0.001), lymphocyte count (median: 0.26 vs. 0.49 × 109/L, p < 0.001), and platelet count (median: 87.00 vs. 111.00 × 109/L, p = 0.034), revealed significant haematological abnormalities in immunocompromised patients. Immunological parameters also demonstrated significant impairment, reflected by reduced IgM levels (median: 0.475 vs. 0.70 g/L, p = 0.004), lower C3 levels (0.620 ± 0.244 vs. 0.800 ± 0.257 g/L, p = 0.040), lower C4 levels (0.139 ± 0.058 vs. 0.212 ± 0.095 g/L, p = 0.011), and a significantly reduced systemic immune inflammation index (SII) (median: 642.286 vs. 2140.267, p < 0.001). Regarding clinical management, non-immunocompromised patients required urgent surgical intervention more frequently (38.5% vs. 19.4%, p = 0.012).

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that immunocompromised patients had significantly lower cumulative survival than their non-immunocompromised counterparts (Fig. 2A; log-rank test, p = 0.0082). In the autoimmune subgroup (Fig. 2B), the impact of immune status on survival was even greater (log-rank test, p = 0.0027).

Univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that immunocompromise was significantly associated with an increased risk of death in patients with sepsis (HR = 1.928, 95% CI 1.174–3.168; p = 0.010). Multivariate analysis confirmed this association, indicating that immunocompromised patients had nearly a three-fold higher mortality risk than non-immunocompromised patients (HR = 2.980, 95% CI 1.679–5.288; p < 0.001). To clarify the impact of specific immunocompromising conditions on prognosis, we performed subgroup analyses within the immunocompromised cohort. Autoimmune disease was the strongest predictor of poor outcome, with multivariate analysis demonstrating an almost four-fold increase in mortality (HR = 3.926, 95% CI 1.763–8.744; p < 0.001). Uncontrolled diabetes was not significant in univariate analysis (HR = 1.505, 95% CI 0.683–3.313; p = 0.311); however, after covariate adjustment it conferred a four-fold higher risk (HR = 4.432, 95% CI 1.638–11.992; p = 0.003). Malignant neoplasms were likewise associated with a three-fold increase in mortality after adjustment (HR = 3.195, 95% CI 1.203–8.486; p = 0.020) (Table 2).

Identification of significant prognostic factors for sepsis

The risk factors for 90-day mortality identified through univariate analysis are summarized in Table S2. As shown in Table 3, the univariate Cox regression analysis revealed several factors significantly associated with sepsis prognosis. After stepwise adjustment for potential confounders, the effect sizes of these factors remained consistent, supporting the robustness of the associations. The site of infection was a major determinant of sepsis outcomes. Urinary tract infections were strongly associated with increased mortality (HR = 2.473, 95% CI: 1.371–5.488, p = 0.004), corresponding to a 147% higher risk, and respiratory tract infections similarly elevated the risk of death (HR = 2.078, 95% CI: 1.264–3.421, p = 0.004), representing a 108% increase. Each 1-point increase in SOFA score was associated with an 18.2% rise in mortality risk (HR = 1.182, 95% CI: 1.095–1.276, p < 0.001), underscoring its prognostic value in sepsis. The RCS analysis shows a non-significant nonlinear relationship between SOFA and 90-day mortality (Figure S1). Septic shock was linked to a 91.7% higher mortality risk (HR = 1.917, 95% CI: 1.059–3.473, p = 0.032). An immunocompromised state significantly worsened prognosis (HR = 2.343, 95% CI: 1.346–4.081, p = 0.003), conferring a 134% higher mortality risk compared with patients with normal immune function. Mechanical ventilation was associated with increased mortality (HR = 1.643, 95% CI: 0.993–2.721, p = 0.053), approaching statistical significance in the fully adjusted model, possibly reflecting combined effects of disease severity and treatment needs. Urgent surgery substantially reduced mortality risk (HR = 0.204, 95% CI: 0.092–0.449, p < 0.001), corresponding to a 79.6% reduction, and intensive care unit admission also exerted a protective effect (HR = 0.356, 95% CI: 0.184–0.689, p = 0.002), reducing mortality by 64.4%. Collinearity diagnostics indicated VIF values below 5 for all factors, suggesting no meaningful multicollinearity. Accordingly, seven variables were included as predictors in the final multivariable prediction model (Figure S2). As a sensitivity analysis, LASSO-Cox regression identified six independently significant variables (all p < 0.05)-immunocompromised state, SOFA score, septic shock, ICU admission, urgent surgery, and urinary tract infection-all of which were core components of the primary model, confirming the robustness of the variable selection (Figure S3).

Prediction performance of seven independent significant factors

To evaluate the impact of incorporating significant predictive factors on model performance, we compared the basic model with the enhanced full model (Table 4, Figure S4). The full model demonstrated substantially improved fit, with the AIC decreasing from 615.9 to 540.5 and the BIC decreasing from 684.4 to 635.7. The NRI and IDI analyses demonstrated that adding the significant factors to the basic model substantially improved reclassification performance in both the derivation and validation cohorts. The AUROC increased from 0.746 (95% CI: 0.667–0.826) to 0.910 (95% CI: 0.864–0.955, p < 0.001). To evaluate the model’s clinical usefulness, we conducted decision curve analysis to compare the net benefits of the basic and full models across a range of risk thresholds (Fig. 3). The full model consistently yielded greater net benefit than the basic model across most threshold values.

Establishment of the prediction nomogram model

To enhance clinical applicability, we developed a nomogram to predict short-term survival outcomes in patients with sepsis (Fig. 4). The model incorporates age, sex, and seven additional variables with significant predictive value, quantifying the contribution of each through a structured scoring system. Total scores range from 0 to 30 and correspond to survival probabilities at 12, 36, and 60 days. The score is inversely associated with survival probability, which declines from 0.9 (90%) in low-risk patients to 0.1 (10%) in high risk patients. This scoring system provides a practical tool for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in clinical practice. By relying on readily available clinical parameters, clinicians can more effectively manage individual patients and make informed decisions. To assess the nomogram’s predictive accuracy, calibration curves were generated to evaluate performance at multiple time points (Fig. 5). The results indicated strong agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 12, 36, and 60 days post-diagnosis, consistent with the C-index of 0.808 (p < 0.001). The constructed nomogram models were internally validated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 replications, demonstrating good internal discrimination (bootstrap C-statistic of 0.782) and excellent calibration between predicted and observed risk.

Discussion

This study employed a prospective cohort design to investigate the association between immunocompromised status and mortality risk in patients with sepsis. A total of 145 patients were enrolled and followed for 90 days. The findings demonstrated that immunocompromised patients had a significantly higher 90-day mortality rate. A multicentre prospective study by Violaine Tolsma et al. [17], which included 1,981 ICU patients with severe sepsis or septic shock, reported similar 28-day crude mortality rates between immunodeficient and non-immunodeficient patients (31.3% vs. 28.8%). However, after adjustment for confounding factors, immunodeficiency emerged as an independent risk factor for 28-day mortality (HR = 1.37, 95% CI: 1.12–1.67). Similarly, Yanqing Chen et al. [18] found higher in-hospital mortality among immunocompromised patients. These findings are partially consistent with our results. In our study, no significant differences were observed in 28-day or early mortality rates; however, extending the follow-up to 90 days revealed a substantial difference in crude mortality between the two groups. After adjusting for covariates such as age and infection site using a multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, immunocompromised patients exhibited nearly threefold higher mortality risk compared with non-immunocompromised patients. These results indicate that immunocompromised status is an important independent predictor of poor prognosis in sepsis, and the increased effect size observed in the multivariate analysis underscores the need to consider immunosuppression comprehensively when evaluating clinical outcomes.

This study analyzed clinical and laboratory data from patients with sepsis to identify characteristics associated with immunosuppression. Notably, numerous studies [19–26] have demonstrated that the accumulation of such adverse factors is strongly linked to poor prognosis in sepsis, providing a solid pathophysiological basis for the unfavorable outcomes observed in immunocompromised patients. Given the broad and heterogeneous definitions of immunosuppression, we focused on the most common types for subgroup analysis.

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves of patients with autoimmune diseases receiving long-term corticosteroid or immunosuppressive therapy showed a distinct and persistent separation, indicating a markedly increased mortality risk in sepsis patients with autoimmune diseases. Subsequent multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated elevated mortality risks across subgroups, where patients with autoimmune diseases remained at higher risk after covariate adjustment, diabetic patients with poor glycaemic control exhibited a fourfold increase in mortality risk, and immunosuppression related to malignant neoplasms was associated with a threefold increase in mortality. These findings suggest that these subgroups may serve as independent prognostic indicators for sepsis outcomes. Multiple studies [27–30] have shown that hyperglycaemia adversely affects immune function by impairing leukocyte activity, inducing chronic low-grade inflammation, and promoting cardiovascular damage, thereby worsening sepsis prognosis. The influence of malignant neoplasms on sepsis outcomes has also been confirmed in several studies [31, 32]. Hugo Gonzalez et al. [33] analysed mechanisms by which tumours evade immune surveillance—such as secreting immunosuppressive factors (e.g., TGF-β, IL-10) and recruiting inhibitory immune cells (e.g., Tregs, MDSCs)-thus limiting the host’s ability to eliminate tumours and pathogens. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy may further damage rapidly proliferating immune cells. These mechanisms help explain the subgroup outcomes observed in our analysis. However, our findings regarding autoimmune diseases differ from those of previous reports. Mallory Sheth et al. [11], using MIMIC-III data, found a paradoxical protective effect, with a 27% reduction in 30-day mortality among sepsis patients with autoimmune diseases. This effect was most pronounced in septic shock and appeared independent of long-term immunomodulatory therapy. The discrepancy may stem from differences in study design, patient selection, inclusion criteria, follow-up duration, or earlier ICU admission among autoimmune patients due to disease severity and immunosuppression. Our study utilized the Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria, observed no significant group differences in SOFA scores or septic shock incidence, and included a longer follow-up period. We hope these findings will support future efforts to develop personalized sepsis treatment strategies and refine prognostic models and interventions informed by multifactorial risk assessments.

Numerous studies have sought to identify prognostic factors for sepsis, including demographic characteristics, medical history, and laboratory biomarkers. However, many reported significant factors are difficult to reproduce. Beyond the limited statistical power resulting from small sample sizes, poor reproducibility is largely attributable to insufficient adjustment for confounding variables. Various approaches, including statistical adjustments and exploratory analyses, can be used to control or mitigate the influence of confounders [34–36]. After applying appropriate statistical adjustments, we identified seven independent and statistically significant predictors of sepsis mortality, including respiratory tract infection, urinary tract infection, ICU admission, SOFA score, immunocompromised status, septic shock, and urgent surgery [37, 38]. Given the multistep and multifactorial pathophysiology of sepsis, no single factor is expected to play a dominant role. Therefore, constructing a multivariable risk prediction model is more meaningful. Accordingly, we developed a nomogram based on factors significantly associated with sepsis prognosis and independent of demographic covariates. This model demonstrated robust predictive performance and provides a practical prognostic tool for immunocompromised sepsis patients in the Chinese population. Future studies will aim to validate these findings in larger cohorts.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting our findings. First, the single-centre design may restrict the generalizability of the results until they are validated in independent cohorts, although it may more accurately reflect real-world clinical practice. Second, the relatively small size of the immunocompromised subgroup limited our ability to perform detailed statistical analyses on how specific types of immunosuppression influence patient prognosis. Third, the classification of immunocompromised status relied on broad diagnostic categories, which may oversimplify the true immunological heterogeneity, particularly in patients whose immune function fluctuates with disease activity and therapeutic regimens. Future prospective studies incorporating dynamic immune profiling are warranted to refine this stratification. Finally, although the 60-day survival calibration was validated, long-term predictive performance of this model requires further assessment. Future research should incorporate multicentre cohorts, refine classifications of immunosuppressive conditions, and investigate dynamic risk assessment approaches to enhance personalized treatment strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, septic patients with immunocompromised state exhibit distinct clinical features, including a higher 90-day mortality rate compared to non-immunocompromised patients. Subgroup analyses identified autoimmune diseases, poorly controlled diabetes, and malignant neoplasms as independent risk factors that influenced sepsis prognosis. We identified seven independent prognostic factors: respiratory tract infection, urinary tract infection, ICU admission, the SOFA score, immunocompromised state, septic shock and urgent surgery. On the basis of these factors, we developed a clinically useful nomogram. With further external validation, this model is anticipated to have broad applicability.
