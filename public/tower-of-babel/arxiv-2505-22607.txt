Introduction

1.1
Background

A minimal representation is an infinite-dimensional irreducible representation
of a simple Lie group with the smallest Gelfand–Kirillov dimension. However,
at the same time, it can be thought of as a manifestation of large symmetry of
the space acted on by the group, and hence, it is expected to control global
analysis on the space effectively. This is the idea of
global analysis of
minimal representations
initiated by T. Kobayashi
[
19
,
20
]
,
which led a transition from algebraic representation theory to analytic
representation theory. See also
[
15
, Section VII]
for an excellent survey.

From the viewpoint of global analysis of minimal representations, the
classical Fourier transform on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
can be
interpreted as a unitary inversion operator in the Weil representation, which is
a unitary representation of the metaplectic group
\mathrm{Mp}(N,\mathbb{R})
on
the Hilbert space
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)
(see
[
14
]
for more details) and
decomposes into two irreducible components, each of which is a minimal
representation. Promoting this interpretation,
Kobayashi–Mano
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
introduced the
Fourier
transform on the light cone
as a unitary inversion operator in an
L^{2}
-model
of a minimal representation of
\mathrm{O}(p,q)
and developed a new theory of
harmonic analysis. The special case
(p,q)=(N+1,2)
, where the model
Hilbert space is isomorphic to
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N},\lvert x\rvert^{-1}{\rm d}x\bigr)
, is studied
in
[
21
,
23
]
.

After that, Ben Saïd–Kobayashi–Ørsted
[
3
,
4
]
introduced a family of

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triples of differential-difference operators

\mathbb{H}_{k,a}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
indexed by two parameters
k
and
a
,
and defined the
(k,a)
-generalized Laguerre semigroup

\mathscr{I}_{k,a}(z)=\exp\left(\frac{z}{\rm i}\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}-\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}\bigr)\right),\qquad\RePart z\geq 0

and the
(k,a)
-generalized Fourier transform

\mathscr{F}_{k,a}={\rm e}^{\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{2}\frac{2\langle k\rangle+a+N-2}{a}}\mathscr{I}_{k,a}\left(\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{2}\right)
.
Here,
k
is a combinatorial parameter derived from the Dunkl operators,
and
a>0
is a deformation parameter. The
(k,a)
-generalized
Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{k,a}
includes some known transforms:

•

The
(0,2)
-generalized Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{0,2}
is
the classical Fourier transform.

•

The
(0,1)
-generalized Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{0,1}
is
the Hankel transform, or the Fourier transform on the light cone
for
(p,q)=(N+1,2)
.

•

The
(k,2)
-generalized Fourier transform
\mathscr{F}_{k,2}
is
the Dunkl transform
[
13
]
.

The parameter
a
therefore provides a continuous interpolation between
the two minimal representations of the simple Lie groups

\mathrm{Mp}(N,\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{O}(N+1,2)
.

1.2
Results of the paper

Let
\mathfrak{g}_{k,a}=\lspan_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl\{\mathbb{H}_{k,a},\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+},\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}\bigr\}\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
Ben Saïd–Kobayashi–Ørsted
[
4
, Theorems 3.30 and 3.31]
showed that,
for
a>0
, the action of
\mathfrak{g}_{k,a}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N},w_{k,a}(x){\rm d}x)

(see (
3.3
) for the definition of
w_{k,a}
)
lifts to a unique unitary representation of the universal covering Lie group
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
and found
its irreducible decomposition explicitly; the Hilbert space
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N},\allowbreak w_{k,a}(x){\rm d}x\bigr)
decomposes discretely
with finite multiplicities into relatively discrete series representations
of
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
.
Furthermore, we investigated in
[
17
]
the case
a<0
,
which provided an extension of the parameter
a
.

In the present paper, we study the behavior as
a\to 0
.
Although the operators
\mathbb{H}_{k,a}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}
or
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}

are not well-defined for
a=0
, the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}_{k,a}\cong\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
contracts
to a three-dimensional commutative Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{k,0}\cong\mathbb{R}^{3}

as
a\to 0
.
Such a contraction of Lie algebras (or corresponding Lie groups) was earlier
formalized by Inonu–Wigner
[
18
]
, where it is referred to as
a
contraction of groups
. Classical examples include the contraction from
the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(3)
(resp.
\mathrm{O}(2,1)
) to the Euclidean motion group

\mathrm{O}(2)\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which reflects the geometric phenomenon that the sphere of
curvature
\kappa>0
(resp. the hyperbolic plane of curvature
\kappa<0
)
approaches the flat Euclidean plane as
\kappa\to 0
.

We then consider the action of
\mathfrak{g}_{k,0}
on
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N},w_{k,0}(x){\rm d}x\bigr)

(note that the weight function
w_{k,a}
is well-defined even for
a=0
).
As an analog of the result in the case
a\neq 0
, we describe that
the joint spectral decomposition for the operators in
\mathfrak{g}_{k,0}
on

L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N},w_{k,0}(x){\rm d}x\bigr)
(Theorem
3.4
)
and show that it lifts to a unique unitary representation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

(Theorem
3.6
). This is the main result of the paper.
In contrast to the case
a\neq 0
, this spectral decomposition involves only
the continuous spectrum.

Moreover, we discuss formulas for operator semigroups with infinitesimal
generators in
\mathfrak{g}_{k,0}
(see Theorems
3.8
and
3.13
).
In particular, we describe the integral kernel of
\exp\bigl(z\lvert x\rvert^{2}\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}\bigr)

as an infinite series, which, in some low-dimensional cases, can be expressed
in a closed form using the theta function
(Theorems
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
).
Although the
(k,a)
-generalized Laguerre semigroup and
the
(k,a)
-generalized Fourier transform are not well-defined for
a=0
,
the operator semigroup
\bigl({\rm e}^{-z}\exp\bigl(z\lvert x\rvert^{2}\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}\bigr)\bigr)_{\RePart z\geq 0}

may be viewed as the “renormalized”
(k,a)
-generalized Laguerre semigroup
for
a=0
(Theorem
3.15
).
Note that explicit formulas and estimates for the integral kernels of
the
(k,a)
-generalized Laguerre semigroup and
the
(k,a)
-generalized Fourier transform have been extensively studied
in Ben Saïd–Kobayashi–Ørsted
[
4
, Sections 4.3–4.5 and 5.2–5.4]

and subsequent papers
[
2
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
16
,
27
]
up to the present.
There are also unpublished results by Mano and related results by Demni
[
9
]
.

Thus, this paper analyzes representation-theoretic aspects of contraction of
Lie algebras in the framework of
(k,a)
-generalized Fourier analysis.
We note that, recently, Benoist–Kobayashi
[
5
, Theorem 1.2]
discovered
a relationship between
limit algebras
(see Section 1.4 of their paper) of

\mathfrak{h}=\Lie(H)
in
\mathfrak{g}=\Lie(G)
and
L^{2}
-analysis of
G/H

in the context of tempered unitary representations. It can be viewed as an
application of the notion of contraction of Lie algebras to representation theory.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we review Dunkl theory and the differential-difference operators

\mathbb{H}_{k,a}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}
introduced
by Ben Saïd–Kobayashi–Ørsted to the extent necessary for later use.

2.1
The Dunkl Laplacian

Throughout this paper, we fix a reduced root system
\mathscr{R}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
That is, we suppose that
\mathscr{R}
satisfies the following conditions:

•

\mathscr{R}
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
,

•

\mathscr{R}
is stable under the orthogonal reflection
r_{\alpha}

with respect to the hyperplane
(\mathbb{R}\alpha)^{\perp}
for all

\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
, and

•

\mathscr{R}\cap\mathbb{R}\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}
for all

\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
.

Note that we do not impose crystallographic conditions on roots and do not
require that
\mathscr{R}
spans
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

The subgroup of
\mathrm{O}(N)
generated by all the reflections
r_{\alpha}

is called the
reflection group associated with
\mathscr{R}
.
We say that a function
k\colon\mathscr{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
multiplicity
function
if it is invariant under the natural action of the reflection group.
We usually write
k_{\alpha}
instead of
k(\alpha)
. We say that
a multiplicity function
k
is
non-negative
if
k_{\alpha}\geq 0

for all
\alpha\in\mathscr{R}
. The
index
of a multiplicity function

k
is defined as

\langle k\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}k_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}^{+}}k_{\alpha},

where
\mathscr{R}^{+}
is any positive system of
\mathscr{R}
.

For a (not necessarily non-negative) multiplicity function
k
,
the
Dunkl Laplacian

\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}

(see
[
10
]
and
[
11
, Definition 1.1]
) is defined by

\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}F(x)=\mathrm{\Delta}F(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}^{+}}k_{\alpha}\left(\frac{2\langle\alpha,\nabla F(x)\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}-\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}\frac{F(x)-F(r_{\alpha}(x))}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle^{2}}\right),

where
\mathrm{\Delta}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\bigr)^{2}
is the classical Laplacian
and
\nabla=\bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\dots,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{N}}\bigr)
is the classical gradient operator.
When
k=0
, the Dunkl Laplacian
\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}
reduces to the classical
Laplacian
\mathrm{\Delta}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)
denote the space of polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

\mathcal{P}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)
denote its subspace of homogeneous polynomials of degree
m
.
The space of
k
-harmonic polynomials of degree
m

(see
[
10
, Definition 1.5]
) is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)=\bigl\{p\in\mathcal{P}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)\mid\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}p=0\bigr\},

and the space of
k
-spherical harmonics of degree
m
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)=\bigl\{p\rvert_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\mid p\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)\bigr\}.

When
k=0
, these are reduced to the space
\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)

of classical harmonic polynomials and the space
\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)

of classical spherical harmonics, respectively.

We write
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)=\bigl\{p\rvert_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\mid p\in\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)\bigr\}
.
The following fact is a generalization of the decomposition of

\mathcal{H}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)
and
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)
into the spaces

\mathcal{H}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)
of classical spherical harmonics.

Fact 2.1

(
[
10
, pp. 37–39]
)
.

For a non-negative multiplicity function
k
,
we have the direct sum decomposition

\mathcal{H}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)=\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)

and the orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1},w_{k}(\omega){\rm d}\omega\bigr)=\sideset{}{{}^{\oplus}}{\sum}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr),

where the weight function
w_{k}
with respect to the standard measure

{\rm d}\omega
on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
is defined by

w_{k}(\omega)=\prod_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}}\lvert\langle\alpha,\omega\rangle\rvert^{k_{\alpha}}=\prod_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}^{+}}\lvert\langle\alpha,\omega\rangle\rvert^{2k_{\alpha}}.

2.2
Dunkl’s intertwining operator and the Poisson kernel

For a non-negative multiplicity function
k
, Dunkl introduced a linear
automorphism
V_{k}
of the space
\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)
of polynomials
that satisfies a certain intertwining property (
Dunkl’s intertwining operator
).
See
[
12
, Definition 2.2 and Theorem 2.3]
for the definition
and a characterization of
V_{k}
. We note that
V_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
.

The following integral representation of Dunkl’s intertwining operator
V_{k}

was obtained by Rösler.

Fact 2.2

(
[
26
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
k
be a non-negative multiplicity function.
For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, there exists a unique probability Borel measure

\mu_{k,x}
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that

V_{k}p(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}p(\xi)\,{\rm d}\mu_{k,x}(\xi)

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{N}\bigr)
. Moreover, the support of
\mu_{k,x}
is
contained in the ball
\bigl\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\mid\lvert\xi\rvert\leq\lvert x\rvert\bigr\}
,
and we have

\mu_{k,x}(S)=\mu_{k,gx}(gS)=\mu_{k,rx}(rS)

for any element
g
of the reflection group,
r>0
,
and Borel set
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Let
k
be a non-negative multiplicity function and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
We consider the orthogonal projection
\Pi_{k}^{(m)}

from
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1},w_{k}(\omega){\rm d}\omega\bigr)

onto
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)
and its normalized integral kernel
P_{k}^{(m)}
,
which is called the
Poisson kernel
of the space of
k
-spherical harmonics
of degree
m
. That is, the function
P_{k}^{(m)}

on
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
is characterized by the formula

\Pi_{k}^{(m)}p(\omega)=\frac{1}{\vol_{k}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}P_{k}^{(m)}\big(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\big)p\big(\omega^{\prime}\big)w_{k}\big(\omega^{\prime}\big)\,{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}

(2.1)

for all
p\in L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1},w_{k}(\omega){\rm d}\omega\bigr)
, where

\displaystyle\vol_{k}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{N-1}}\,{\rm d}w_{k}(\omega).

(2.2)

Equivalently, the Poisson kernel
P_{k}^{(m)}
is given by

P_{k}^{(m)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\vol_{k}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)\sum_{j=1}^{d}p_{j}(\omega)p_{j}(\omega^{\prime}),

where
(p_{1},\dots,p_{d})
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)
,
regarded as a subspace of
L^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1},\allowbreak w_{k}(\omega){\rm d}\omega\bigr)
.

The Poisson kernel
P_{k}^{(m)}
can be expressed in terms of Dunkl’s intertwining
operator and the Gegenbauer polynomials. To state this result, we first prepare
some notation. For
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
and
{m\in\mathbb{N}}
,
we consider the Gegenbauer polynomial
C_{m}^{\nu}
defined by the generating
function

\bigl(1-2t\xi+\xi^{2}\bigr)^{-\nu}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}C_{m}^{\nu}(t)\xi^{m},

(2.3)

and the renormalized Gegenbauer polynomial
\widetilde{C}_{m}^{\nu}
defined by

\widetilde{C}_{m}^{\nu}(t)=\frac{m+\nu}{\nu}C_{m}^{\nu}(t).

(2.4)

For
\nu=0
, we define
\widetilde{C}_{m}^{0}
by the limit formula
(see
[
1
, equation (6.4.13)]
)

\widetilde{C}_{m}^{0}(t)=\lim_{\nu\to 0}\widetilde{C}_{m}^{\nu}(t)=\begin{cases}1,&m=0,\\
2T_{m}(t),&m\geq 1,\end{cases}

(2.5)

where
T_{m}
denotes the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind, which is
characterized by the formula
T_{m}(\cos\theta)=\cos m\theta
.

Fact 2.3

(
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Let
k
be a non-negative multiplicity function and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
The Poisson kernel
P_{k}^{(m)}
is given by

P_{k}^{(m)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=V_{k}\big(\widetilde{C}_{m}^{\frac{2\langle k\rangle+N-2}{2}}\big(\langle\mathord{\text{\textendash}},\omega^{\prime}\rangle\big)\big)(\omega).

Remark 2.4
.

When
k=0
, we have
V_{0}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
, so that
the formula in Theorem
2.3
reduces to

P_{0}^{(m)}\bigl(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\bigr)=\widetilde{C}_{m}^{\frac{N-2}{2}}\bigl(\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle\bigr).

See
[
1
, Theorem 9.6.3 and Remark 9.6.1]
.

Remark 2.5
.

In the case
N=1
, we have
\mathbb{S}^{0}=\{\pm 1\}
and
the formula in Theorem
2.3
reduces to

P_{k}^{(m)}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1,&m=0,\\
\sgn(\omega\omega^{\prime}),&m=1,\\
0,&m\geq 2,\end{cases}

which corresponds to the fact that

\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\big(\mathbb{S}^{0}\big)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}1,&m=0,\\
\mathbb{C}\sgn,&m=1,\\
0,&m\geq 2.\end{cases}

Here,
1
and
\sgn
denote the constant function and the sign function on

\mathbb{S}^{0}=\{\pm 1\}
, respectively.

2.3
The differential-difference operators
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{H}_{k,a}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}}

Let
k
be a multiplicity function and
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.
We recall the definition of the differential-difference operators

\mathbb{H}_{k,a}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}
and
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
from
[
4
, equation (3.3)]
:

\mathbb{H}_{k,a}=\frac{2}{a}E_{x}+\frac{2\langle k\rangle+a+N-2}{a},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}=\frac{\rm i}{a}\lvert x\rvert^{a},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}=\frac{\rm i}{a}\lvert x\rvert^{2-a}\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}.

Additionally, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the following differential
operators on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{k,a}^{(m)}=\frac{2}{a}E_{r}+\frac{2\langle k\rangle+a+N-2}{a},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+\,(m)}=\frac{\rm i}{a}r^{a},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-\,(m)}=\frac{\rm i}{a}r^{-a}(E_{r}-m)(E_{r}+m+2\langle k\rangle+N-2).

These are the radial parts of
\mathbb{H}_{k,a}
,
\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}
and

\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}
respectively in the following sense.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
k
be a multiplicity function,
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
p\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}_{k,a}(p\otimes f)=p\otimes\mathbb{H}_{k,a}^{(m)}f,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+}(p\otimes f)=p\otimes\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{+\,(m)}f,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-}(p\otimes f)=p\otimes\mathbb{E}_{k,a}^{-\,(m)}f,

where
p\otimes f
denotes the function
r\omega\mapsto p(\omega)f(r)

on
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

The first and second equations are clear. We now prove the third equation.
We use the polar coordinates
x=r\omega
, where
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and

\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
. We extend
p\in\mathcal{H}_{k}^{m}\bigl(\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\bigr)

to a
k
-harmonic polynomial of degree
m
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which we again
write
p
. Then, since
\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}p=0
, we have

\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}(p\otimes f)(x)=\mathrm{\Delta}_{k}(r^{-m}f(r)p(x))=[\mathrm{\Delta}_{k},r^{-m}f(r)]p(x),

(2.6)

where
[\mathord{\text{\textendash}},\mathord{\text{\textendash}}]
denotes the commutator of operators.
For a radial function

, the commutator
{[\mathrm{\Delta}_{k},g(r)]}

can be computed by the Leibniz rule as

\displaystyle[\mathrm{\Delta}_{k},g(r)]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\Delta}(g(r))+2\langle\nabla(g(r)),\nabla\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathscr{R}^{+}}k_{\alpha}\frac{2\langle\alpha,\nabla(g(r))\rangle}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle}

\displaystyle=g^{\prime\prime}(r)+\frac{1}{r}g^{\prime}(r)(2E_{x}+2\langle k\rangle+N-1)

\displaystyle=r^{-2}\bigl(E_{r}^{2}g(r)+E_{r}g(r)(2E_{x}+2\langle k\rangle+N-2)\bigr).

Setting

and applying this commutator to

,
we have

\displaystyle[\mathrm{\Delta}_{k},r^{-m}f(r)]p(x)

\displaystyle\qquad=r^{-2}\bigl(E_{r}^{2}(r^{-m}f(r))+E_{r}(r^{-m}f(r))(2E_{x}+2\langle k\rangle+N-2)\bigr)p(x)

\displaystyle\qquad=r^{-m-2}\bigl((E_{r}-m)^{2}f(r)+(E_{r}-m)f(r)(2E_{x}+2\langle k\rangle+N-2)\bigr)p(x)

\displaystyle\qquad=r^{-m-2}\bigl((E_{r}-m)^{2}+(E_{r}-m)(2m+2\langle k\rangle+N-2)\bigr)f(r)p(x)

\displaystyle\qquad=r^{-2}(E_{r}-m)(E_{r}+m+2\langle k\rangle+N-2)f(r)p(\omega)

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl(p\otimes r^{-2}(E_{r}-m)(E_{r}+m+2\langle k\rangle+N-2)f\bigr)(x).

(2.7)

The third equation follows from (
2.6
) and (
2.7
).
∎

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-zm^{2}\bigr)\widetilde{C}_{m}^{0}(\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-zm^{2}\bigr)T_{m}(\cos\theta)

\displaystyle=1+2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-zm^{2}\bigr)\cos m\theta=\vartheta\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\theta,\frac{\rm i}{\pi}z\right).

Hence, we have

\displaystyle K_{0}\bigl(r\omega,r^{\prime}\omega^{\prime};z\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi z}}\exp\left(-\frac{\bigl(\log r-\log r^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}}{4z}\right)\times\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-zm^{2}\bigr)\widetilde{C}_{m}^{0}\bigl(\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{4\pi z}}\exp\left(-\frac{\bigl(\log r-\log r^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}}{4z}\right)\vartheta\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle,\frac{\rm i}{\pi}z\right).

∎

Proposition 4.3
.

In the case
N=4
, for
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\RePart z>0
, we have

\displaystyle K_{0}\bigl(r\omega,r^{\prime}\omega^{\prime};z\bigr)={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}\sqrt{4\pi z}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\log r-\log r^{\prime})^{2}}{4z}\right)\bigl(rr^{\prime}\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\bigl(1-\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle^{2}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial v}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle,\frac{\rm i}{\pi}z\right)

for
r,r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
.
Here, we take the branch of
\arccos\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle
such that

\arccos\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle\in[0,\pi]
.

Proof.

Since

\widetilde{C}_{m}^{1}(t)=(m+1)C_{m}^{1}(t)=(m+1)U_{m}(t)
,
where
U_{m}
denotes the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind, which is
characterized by the formula
U_{m}(\cos\theta)=(\sin(m+1)\theta)/(\sin\theta)
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-z(m+1)^{2}\bigr)\widetilde{C}_{m}^{1}(\cos\theta)&=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-z(m+1)^{2}\bigr)\cdot(m+1)\frac{\sin(m+1)\theta}{\sin\theta}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\exp\bigl(-zm^{2}\bigr)\cdot m\sin m\theta\\
&=-\frac{1}{4\pi\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial v}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\theta,\frac{\rm i}{\pi}z\right).\end{split}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle K_{0}(r\omega,r^{\prime}\omega^{\prime};z)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi z}}\exp\left(-\frac{\bigl(\log r-\log r^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}}{4z}\right)\bigl(rr^{\prime}\bigr)^{-1}\times\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\exp(-z(m+1)^{2})\widetilde{C}_{m}^{1}\bigl(\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle\bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad=-\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}\sqrt{4\pi z}}\exp\left(-\frac{\bigl(\log r-\log r^{\prime}\bigr)^{2}}{4z}\right)\bigl(rr^{\prime}\bigr)^{-1}

\displaystyle\phantom{\qquad=}{}\times\bigl(1-\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle^{2}\bigr)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\partial\vartheta}{\partial v}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\arccos\langle\omega,\omega^{\prime}\rangle,\frac{\rm i}{\pi}z\right).

∎
