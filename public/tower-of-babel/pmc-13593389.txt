Contemporary Treatment and Survival Outcomes in Mantle Cell Lymphoma: A National Real‐World Analysis From REALYSA, a LYSA Cohort
Authors: Morgane Cheminant, Patrick Fogarty, Sylvain Carras, Clémentine Sarkozy, Cédric Rossi, Luc Fornecker, Bijou Fontanet, Adrien Chauchet, Sandra Leguyader‐Peyrou, Laure Lebras, Lucie Oberic, Loïc Ysebaert, Ghandi Damaj, Gaelle Labouré, Ludovic Fouillet, Stéphanie Guidez, François Lemonnier, Krimo Bouabdallah, Thomas Gastinne, Nadine Morineau, Sandy Amorim, Fabrice Jardin, Julie Abraham, Roch Houot, Catherine Thieblemont, Franck Morschhauser, Rémy Gressin, Steven Le Gouill, Olivier Hermine, Fanny Cherblanc, Alain Monnereau, Aurélien Belot, Hervé Ghesquieres, Benoit Tessoulin

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough clinical trials showed outcomes improvement in MCL, their applicability to real‐world practice remains limited. Using the prospective REALYSA registry (NCT03869619), we evaluated contemporary management, highlighting evolving practices and differences by age. Among 295 patients, 63% were ≥ 65 years, 48% had a high‐risk MIPI score with Ki‐67 ≥ 30% in 53% and aggressive morphology in 11%. Younger patients received high‐dose cytarabine (93%) and ASCT (78%). Older patients were treated with bendamustine‐rituximab (28%), R‐CHOP (18%), and low‐dose cytarabine (20%). Two‐year event‐free (EFS‐2y) and overall (OS‐2y) survivals were 80% and 89% in < 65y; 60% and 77% in ≥ 65y. Among 238 patients responders after induction, rituximab maintenance (RM) use was frequent but lower in older patients (88% vs. 76%). In time‐dependent Cox models adjusted for age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65) and MIPI, RM was associated with a lower hazard of EFS events (HR 0.48; 95% CI: 0.25–0.92), although residual confounding related to treatment selection may remain. In the second‐line setting (n = 72), most patients were POD24 (89%) and 56% received ibrutinib, yet outcomes remained poor (CR 24%, ORR 35%, median EFS and OS of 6.3 and 25.6 months, respectively). These findings underscore the need for improved strategies for older and high‐risk patients. Retrospective landmark analyses suggest a potential association between RM and improved EFS but are limited by baseline differences between groups.

Introduction

Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) is a rare and incurable subtype of B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, accounting for approximately 5%–6% of cases. Over the past decade, first‐line treatment strategies have evolved significantly, improving patient outcomes relative to the historical median overall survival of 3–5 years [1, 2, 3]. The integration of high‐dose cytarabine during induction [4], autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) for eligible patients [5], and anti‐CD20 maintenance therapy [6, 7] has established new standards of care, resulting in unprecedented long‐term survival [8, 9, 10].

The landscape has expanded with the emergence of new therapeutic strategies, including combinations of immunochemotherapy with BTK inhibitors [11, 12], novel targeted therapies [13, 14, 15, 16], and immunotherapy‐based salvage treatments such as CAR T‐cells or bispecific antibody therapies [17, 18, 19]. These advances have also introduced uncertainty regarding the optimal first‐line approach, particularly for transplant‐ineligible patients. Furthermore, the generalizability of clinical trial findings to routine practice is often limited by strict eligibility criteria and uneven access to novel agents. For example, in France, ibrutinib was initially available only through a Temporary Use Authorization (TUA) program from 2014 to 2017 and has been accessible in community pharmacies since August 2017.

In this context, real‐world data are essential to better understand treatment patterns and outcomes in clinical practice. To address this need, we analyzed real‐world management strategies and clinical outcomes of patients with MCL enrolled in the French national REALYSA cohort between 2018 and 2022, in the modern therapeutic era.

Materials and Methods

Patients

The REALYSA study (NCT03869619) was approved by ethics committee and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The cohort methodology has been described elsewhere [20, 21] (Supporting Information S1: Data). Patients were enrolled across 35 French centers between 2018 and 2022, with a data cut‐off in May 2024. The included set (IS) was defined as all patients who received a first‐line (1L) systemic therapy. The non‐trial 1L set comprised all patients from the IS, except those who received their 1L treatment as part of an interventional clinical trial. The non‐trial 2L set comprised all patients who have initiated a second line of treatment. REALYSA captures several key variables associated with high‐risk MCL [22], including the MIPI score, blastoid/pleomorphic histology and Ki‐67 proliferation index > 30%.

Treatment, Response Evaluation and Outcomes

The definition of broad classes of treatment therapies is described in the Supporting Information S1: Data. The “Time window for evaluation” is defined as the date of the end of the treatment and up to the earliest of date of first maintenance, date of a new treatment line and 4 months after the end of the treatment (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1). Treatment groups were described using estimates, confidence intervals, and graphical presentation rather than formal hypothesis testing because the analyses were descriptive and not designed for causal treatment comparisons. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from initiation of first‐line therapy to death from any cause. OS after second‐line therapy (OS2L) was defined as the time from the start of second‐line treatment to death from any cause. Event‐free survival (EFS) was measured from the initiation of first‐line therapy to the earliest occurrence of disease progression, start of second‐line treatment, or death. Similarly, EFS after second‐line therapy (EFS2L) was calculated from the start of second‐line treatment to disease progression, initiation of a third‐line therapy or death. POD24 was defined as progression/relapse or lymphoma‐related death within 24 months from initiation of first‐line therapy. Patients censored or with non‐lymphoma related death before 24 months were excluded from the POD24 analysis.

ASCT and Rituximab Maintenance Analysis

These analyses should be interpreted as descriptive analyses within selected, landmark‐eligible patients rather than as evidence of treatment effect. To minimize age‐related biases and focus on patients who were potentially eligible for autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) or rituximab maintenance (RM), two analytical subsets were defined. The Responder Set included patients from the non‐trial 1L set who achieved a complete or partial response at end‐of‐induction (EOI). The Exploratory Set comprised patients from the non‐trial 1L set, aged 60–70 years and in response at EOI, to further limit confounding due to age and fitness. Given that rituximab maintenance was typically initiated around 2 months after EOI, with some cases beginning beyond 6 months, a 6‐month landmark analysis was applied to mitigate immortal time bias. This approach enabled classification of most patients who received maintenance and excluded those with early events who would have been ineligible. The same approach was used for ASCT with a 2‐month landmark analysis. To complement the landmark analysis, we also fitted a Cox model including either ASCT or RM as a time‐dependent variable. Models were adjusted for relevant baseline covariates to control for potential confounding factors, particularly those related to treatment timing and patient selection.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and First‐Line Treatment

A total of 344 patients were treated in first line and included in the whole analysis population. Of these, 49 (14%) were excluded because they were enrolled in interventional clinical trials, leaving a non‐trial 1L population of 295 patients (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2). Overall, 71% of the patients were recruited from University Hospitals, 15% from General Hospitals, 10% from cancer centers, and 4% from private clinics. Most of the patients were male (76.9%) and the median age at diagnosis was 68.9 years. Approximately two‐thirds (62.7%) of patients were aged ≥ 65 years, contributing to a high proportion of high‐risk MIPI scores (48%). Most patients had a good performance status (ECOG 0–1 in 86%), with advanced‐stage disease (Ann Arbor stage III–IV) observed in 91% and B symptoms present in 31%. Blastoid/pleomorphic histology was present in 11% of MCLs. A high Ki‐67 proliferation index (≥ 30%) was observed in 53% of patients with available Ki‐67 (n = 66 missing), resulting in a high‐risk MIPI‐combined score in 25% of cases (Table 1). Among patients aged < 65 years, high‐risk MIPI and Ki‐67 ≥ 30% were observed in 17% and 60%, respectively. In the 65–80‐year age group, these features were present in 58% and 46%, respectively.

In the first‐line setting, immunochemotherapy regimens were the most frequently administered (Table 2). Among younger patients, intensive regimens were more frequently used, with high‐dose cytarabine being the most common (n = 102, 92.7%). Autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) was performed in 98 patients (33.2%), exclusively following high‐dose cytarabine‐based induction. As expected, ASCT was predominantly conducted in patients under 65 years of age (N = 86), representing 78.2% of this subgroup and 86.9% of those who achieved complete or partial response to induction (N = 99). In patients aged over 65 years, BR was the most frequently administered regimen (N = 52, 28.1%), followed by low‐dose cytarabine‐based regimens (N = 37, 20.0%) and R‐CHOP (N = 34, 18.4%). Patients over 80 years of age (n = 39) predominantly received BR (33%), followed by low‐dose cytarabine‐ or anthracycline‐based regimens (28%), and ibrutinib (15%). Most patients received rituximab maintenance therapy (N = 200, 67.8%), both in younger (N = 87, 79.1%) and older patients (N = 113, 61.1%). Notably, rituximab maintenance was administered to 88% of younger and 76% of older patients who responded to induction therapy. Among patients over 65 years treated with BR, 69% subsequently received rituximab maintenance.

Outcomes in Newly Diagnosed Mantle Cell Lymphoma Patients

In line with the recommendation, PET‐scan is the most reported imaging modality (N = 211, 74.8%) for response evaluation. Overall response rates were significantly higher in patients under 65 years, with complete and overall response rates of 81% (95% CI: 72–88) and 90% (95% CI: 83–95), respectively, compared to 72% (95% CI: 64–79) and 85% (95% CI: 78–90) in patients 65–80 y/o. Notably, among patients aged > 80 years, the complete response rate was only 46% (95% CI: 29–63). As of the current data cut‐off, the median follow‐up duration was 29 months (range: 0.5–59.4). At 24 months, the EFS‐2y was 67% (95% CI: 61–73) and OS‐2y was 81% (95% CI: 76–86). Among patients under 65 years of age, the EFS‐2y and OS‐2y were 80% (95% CI: 71–87) and 89% (95% CI: 81–94), respectively, compared to 60% (95% CI: 52–67), and 77% (95% CI: 70–82), respectively, in patients over 65 years of age (Figure 1A).

Complete response (CR) rates among patients > 65 years and treated with BR (n = 52), R‐CHOP (n = 34) or low‐dose cytarabine‐based regimens (n = 37), were 69% (95% CI: 55–81), 68% (95% CI: 49–83), and 60% (95% CI: 42–76), respectively. These treatment subgroups differed in several features, including subgroup size, treatment selection, and potential unassessed confounders. EFS‐2 y was 67% (95% CI: 52–79) for BR, 62% (95% CI: 43–77) for R‐CHOP, and 59% (95% CI: 41–73) for low‐dose cytarabine‐based regimens. OS‐2 y was 78% (95% CI: 63–87) for BR, 78% (95% CI: 60–89) for R‐CHOP, and 84% (95% CI: 67–92) for low‐dose cytarabine‐based regimens. Among patients aged over 80 years (n = 39), EFS‐2y and OS‐2y were 46% (95% CI: 30–61) and 66% (95% CI: 49–79), respectively, despite treatment with BR in 33%, low‐dose cytarabine–based regimens in 18%, and ibrutinib‐based therapies in 15% of cases (Supporting Information S1: Table 1).

High‐Risk Mantle Cell Lymphoma

At diagnosis, 69% of patients exhibited at least one high‐risk feature (n = 205/295), defined as a high‐risk MIPI score, blastoid or pleomorphic histology, or Ki‐67 ≥ 30%. This notably high proportion may reflect a broad risk classification, potentially limiting the ability to clearly identify patients with truly high‐risk disease. EFS‐2 y and OS‐2y for patients with blastoid or pleomorphic morphology were 58% (95% CI: 39–72) and 67% (95% CI: 48–80), respectively (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3A). For patients with a high‐risk MIPI score, EFS‐2y and OS‐2y were 53% (95% CI: 44–61) and 70% (95% CI: 62–78), respectively (Figure 1B). Among those with a Ki‐67 index ≥ 30%, EFS‐2y and OS‐2y were 64% (95% CI: 54–72) and 79% (95% CI: 70–85), respectively (Supporting Information S1: Figure 3B). Within the non‐trial 1L population, 73 patients were censored prior to 24 months and cannot be evaluated for POD24 status. Censored patients had fewer adverse disease features, including blastoid/pleomorphic variants, ECOG PS 2–4 and high Ki‐67. First‐line treatment and transplant rates were similar, while maintenance was more frequent among censored patients (78% vs. 64%); by definition, all censored patients were in overall response. Of 222 patients evaluable for POD24, 94 (41%) progressed, died or started a new treatment within 24 months.

Impact of Autologous Stem Cell Transplantation in Patients Aged 60–70 Years

Compared to those w/o ASCT (n = 197), patients who received ASCT (n = 98) were younger (88% vs. 12% aged < 65), with better PS (95% vs. 82% ECOG 0‐1) and presented lower MIPI‐combined score (4% vs. 37% of high‐risk MIPI‐c respectively). They also received higher rates of rituximab maintenance (94% vs. 55%), Supporting Information S1: Table 2. To minimize age‐related bias, we assessed the impact of ASCT in patients aged 60–70 years who were in response at the end of induction (EOI), using a 2‐month landmark analysis. Within these 76 patients, 37 received ASCT (49%) and 39 did not (51%). The impact of potential confounding factors is reduced given the comparable PS and age, although patients who received ASCT exhibited lower MIPI‐combined scores (6% vs. 19% high‐risk MIPI‐c, respectively). In this exploratory, limited‐size subset, EFS‐2y from the landmark timepoint (EOI+2 months) was 81% (95% CI: 62–91) in the ASCT group and 80% (95% CI: 62–90) in patients who did not receive ASCT (Supporting Information S1: Figure 4). These findings were consistent across sensitivity analyses using alternative landmark points at EOI+15 days, +1 month and in time‐dependent Cox models considering MIPI score and performance status, however, the limited sample size precludes any definitive conclusion regarding the effect of ASCT.

Impact of Rituximab Maintenance

In the non‐trial 1L population, there were 238 patients in CR/PR at end‐of‐induction (EOI), of which 192 went on to receive rituximab maintenance and 46 did not receive any maintenance. Median follow‐up was 29.5 months in the population in response at EOI. Patients without maintenance were older (74% vs. 56% aged > 65) and more likely to have a high‐risk MIPI score (58% vs. 35% high MIPI). In contrast, patients who received R‐maintenance were more likely to be treated with high‐dose cytarabine (53% vs. 24%) and transplant (47% vs. 13%). Among patients receiving R‐maintenance at the 6‐month landmark, median EFS was not reached compared to 28.5 months in patients without maintenance (HR 0.60; 95% CI: 0.26–1.4). Median OS was not reached in both groups (HR 0.57; 95% CI: 0.21–1.5, Supporting Information S1: Figure 5). These findings are based on descriptive analyses and may be influenced by residual confounding related to treatment selection. In time‐dependent Cox models adjusted for age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65) and MIPI risk category (low/intermediate vs. high), initiation of R‐maintenance was associated with a lower hazard of EFS events (HR 0.48, 95% CI: 0.25–0.92) and of OS events (HR 0.58, 95% CI: 0.23–1.45) in this descriptive, adjusted time‐dependent model (Table 3).

Outcomes in Patients With Relapsed Mantle Cell Lymphoma and Causes of Deaths

At second line initiation, 74% of patients were over 65 years old, and 9 (12%) presented with central nervous system (CNS) involvement. In total, 77 patients received treatment, mainly outside interventional clinical trials, resulting in 72 patients in the non‐trial 2L population. Within this non‐trial 2L population, 89% were identified as POD24 patients following first‐line therapy (n = 64/72). Ibrutinib was the most frequently used agent, administered to 56% of patients (n = 40/72), either as monotherapy or in combination with rituximab (n = 6). Four additional patients received ibrutinib in combination with venetoclax, and two patients received ibrutinib with chemotherapy due to CNS involvement at relapse. Immunochemotherapy was administered in 25% of cases. Lenalidomide was used in only two patients. Overall, CR and ORR were 24% (95% CI: 14–36) and 35% (95% CI: 23–48). With a median follow‐up duration of 25 months (range: 0.0–44.6), the median EFS2L was 6.3 months (95% CI: 3.5–11.1) with a prolonged median OS2L of 25.6 months (95% CI: 8.1–not reached) after relapse (Figure 2). Among the 40 patients treated with ibrutinib, 34 were over the age of 65 (64% of this subgroup). Despite modest response rates (CR rates at 22%), outcomes were comparable to those receiving non‐BTKi regimens, with an EFS2L‐2y of 24% (95% CI: 10–42) and an OS2L‐2y of 51% (95% CI: 32–67) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6).

Overall, 65 (22%) deaths were reported, including lymphoma (n = 28, 43%), infection (n = 11, 17%, including six COVID‐19‐related deaths), secondary malignancy (n = 7, 11%), treatment‐related cardiac or other disorders (n = 2, 3%), other causes (n = 11, 17%), and unknown causes (n = 6, 9%).

Discussion

This analysis of a prospective national French cohort provides an overview of real‐world management of MCL in the contemporary treatment landscape, including the use of rituximab maintenance in first‐line therapy and ibrutinib‐based regimens at relapse. Despite encouraging short‐term outcomes, older, high‐risk and relapsed patients represent major therapeutic challenges.

Clinical characteristics were consistent with previously reported findings [23, 24, 25]. However, our cohort included a higher proportion of high‐risk patients, with 48% classified as high‐risk by MIPI overall compared to other datasets (17%–33%) [23, 26]. In contrast, the prevalence of blastoid variants (10%–11.5%) was consistent across cohorts. In real‐life, standard prognostic factors showed a less pronounced impact than reported in European trials, where high‐risk MIPI was associated with a 5‐year OS of only 34% [27], likely reflecting advances in salvage therapies. Similarly, histological features, including Ki‐67 index and aggressive variants, failed to reach prognostic significance, possibly due to the lack of standardized pathological assessment outside controlled trial settings. Collectively, these findings underscore the challenges of implementing histology‐driven, risk‐adapted treatment strategies in routine clinical practice.

Treatment strategies differed from other registries. In the Flatiron cohort, BR was the most frequently used first‐line regimen in patients over 65, accounting for 49% of cases, followed by R‐CHOP (15%). In contrast, among French patients aged 65–80, BR was the most used regimen in 26%, followed by R‐CHOP in 21%. Younger patients were predominantly treated following the LyMa protocol [7], which includes four cycles of R‐DHAP, intensification with ASCT, and RM.

As in clinical trials [3], younger patients achieved excellent outcomes in the real‐world setting, with a 2‐year EFS of 80%, translating into a 2‐year OS of 89%. Due to the limited number of younger patients who did not receive RM [28, 29], its specific contribution could not be formally assessed in this subgroup. The widespread use of ASCT was confirmed, with 87% of responding patients under 65 undergoing the procedure. However, its benefit in the context of RM remains unclear. Our exploratory analysis, restricted to 76 patients aged 60–70 years, was underpowered to detect a clinically meaningful difference and should not be interpreted as evidence of the absence of an ASCT effect. Since the pivotal randomized trial supporting its use [5, 30], treatment strategies have evolved, incorporating high‐dose cytarabine [4], ibrutinib [11, 31], and routine RM [6, 7]. Emerging data suggest no added benefit in patients achieving undetectable MRD post‐induction [32]. Although ASCT has been proposed for high‐risk patients [31], other suggest it does not prevent early relapse in the era of RM [29].

Older patients exhibited a 2‐year EFS of 63% and a 2‐year OS of 77%, which is notably higher than previously reported in real‐world studies [23, 25]. Outcomes across immunochemotherapy regimen (R‐CHOP, BR, or dose‐adapted cytarabine) appeared broadly similar, but are presented descriptively, as confounding factors preclude meaningful comparisons between treatment subgroups. These results align with the BR arms of clinical trials in this population [12, 26], likely due to the broader use of RM. Recent trials combining BTK inhibitors have shown promising efficacy but with increased toxicity [12, 26], while emerging chemo‐free approaches offer encouraging preliminary results [14, 16, 33].

Our study showed an overrepresentation of early‐relapsing patients, attributable to the limited follow‐up period, with only eight relapses occurring beyond 24 months. Patients with early relapse had particularly poor outcomes [29, 34, 35], confirmed in our real‐world data, with an EFS of less than 6 months at the time of progression. Notably, the median OS in this high‐risk subgroup was longer than previously reported [36], which may reflect improved access to effective salvage therapies, including CAR T‐cells therapy.

This analysis has several limitations. Considerable efforts were made to ensure the REALYSA cohort was representative of care centers nationwide. Although the study period was a relatively stable therapeutic era, some temporal and center‐related heterogeneity in treatment practices may have contributed to residual selection bias, particularly for high‐risk and young patients, who may be referred to or managed differently in academic centers. Moreover, the cohort does not allow for direct comparison of treatment strategies, as treatment choices were largely influenced by patient characteristics, primarily age and fitness, particularly regarding eligibility for ASCT. This study also highlights the widespread use of RM in France, which supports the need for comparisons with real‐world cohorts from other countries employing alternative strategies, to better address this clinical question. In addition, the relatively short follow‐up period limits the ability to assess long‐term outcomes.

These findings highlight the central role of rituximab maintenance in MCL treatment in France, while emphasizing the need to optimize strategies for older patients. Improving risk stratification remains essential to identify high‐risk individuals prone to early relapse. This real‐world cohort may serve as a foundation for future clinical trials and as an external comparator arm to address these unmet needs.
