Effect of basis choice on quantitative parameter estimation in accelerated subspace reconstructions
Authors: Haoran Bai, Ke Dai, Yueqi Qiu, Jianfeng Bao, Xiao Wang, Yong Zhang, Hao Chen, Zhiyong Zhang, Philip K. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work assesses the effects of different simulated and data-driven subspaces on quantitative parameter estimation in highly accelerated subspace reconstructions. Three different methods for generating basis were applied in a locally low rank subspace reconstruction: (1) a data-driven basis derived from fully sampled low-resolution data, (2) a simulated basis from a dictionary with uniform T2 tissue distribution, and (3) a simulated basis with white matter (WM) and gray matter (GM) oversampled. Obtaining basis vectors from a dictionary with non-uniform tissue distributions can be interpreted as obtaining basis vectors using a weighted singular value decomposition to a dictionary generated with uniform tissue spacings. Fully sampled echo planar spectroscopic imaging (EPSI) datasets of six subjects (including three brain tumor patients) were retrospectively undersampled with 9-shot and 3-shot ky-t traversals. The effect of regularization was assessed by comparing T2 maps and qualitative images of fully sampled and retrospectively undersampled images. Under 3-shot retrospective undersampling, images reconstructed with the basis generated from a uniform T2 distribution basis had altered signal evolutions, resulting in statistically significant WM/GM T2 overestimations with mean differences of 10–15 ms. Applying a WM/GM oversampled basis improved T2 accuracy but still induced T2 bias. The data-driven basis provided the most accurate T2 estimates. Highly accelerated subspace reconstructions cause bias in quantitative relaxation maps. Data-driven subspaces can reduce this bias.

Introduction

Quantitative MRI aims to provide objective biomarkers of tissue properties, offering possible insights for disease diagnosis, progression monitoring, and treatment assessment [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. Locally low rank (LLR) subspace reconstructions [[6], [7], [8], [9]] exploit signal redundancy of adjacent voxels to reconstruct undersampled data. Subspaces are formed by applying the singular value decomposition (SVD) to a set of expected signal evolutions, and retaining the singular vectors associated with the first few singular values. However, under high acceleration, regularization can lead to loss of image details, reduced spatial resolution, and altered temporal signal evolutions, thereby introducing bias in parameter estimation [10,11]. Such distortions may cause systematic bias in the estimated quantitative parameters, potentially confounding the differentiation between pathological and healthy tissues [[12], [13], [14]]. We show that the number of occurrences (or local density) of each expected signal evolution strongly impacts the resulting subspace and quantitative parameter estimates. The choice of subspace has implications on the characterization of pathologies that may lie outside the expected distribution.

Existing basis generation methods can be broadly categorized into two types: model-based and data-driven. The model-based approach estimates basis vectors from an ensemble of simulated signal evolutions. In healthy individuals, simulations can be conducted over a range of parameters (such as T1 and T2) to cover normal tissues like white matter (WM), gray matter (GM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). For example, basis vectors for MR fingerprinting [9,15,16] have been pre-calculated using linearly spaced T1 and T2 values. However, linear parameter ranges may fail to capture the actual distribution of dominant tissues, such as white and gray matter in brain imaging, resulting in suboptimal representations. To address this limitation, Tamir et al. [[17], [18], [19]] performed a data-driven simulation to pre-compute subspaces using the distribution of T2 values in the anatomy of interest. However, pathological tissues often exhibit different T2 values (e.g., meningioma 90–120 ms, IDH-mt glioma ∼150 ms, IDH-wt glioma ∼120 ms, enhancing multiple sclerosis (MS) lesions ∼100 ms, non-enhancing MS lesions ∼135 ms) [[20], [21], [22]] compared to WM (60–90 ms) and GM (90–110 ms) [[23], [24], [25]] at 3 T. Such data-driven simulations may still fail to account for signal evolutions of pathological tissues.

Data-driven bases are widely used in dynamic MRI [[26], [27], [28]] and MR spectroscopic imaging (MRSI). SPICE (SPectroscopic Imaging by exploiting spatiospectral CorrElation) employs a data-driven approach to estimate subspaces with a set of low-resolution calibration data for achieving fast, high-resolution MRSI [29,30]. The data-driven subspace can better capture underlying relaxation processes, and can provide robustness to signal-model mismatches from unaccounted system imperfections. Other examples of data-driven subspaces include k-parametric domain reconstructions which were evaluated for T1 and T2 mapping [31], or Echo Planar Time-resolved Imaging (EPTI) [32,33] for T2/ T2∗ mapping.

In this study, we investigate the effect of regularization on simulated and data-driven bases subspace reconstructions using fully sampled echo planar spectroscopic imaging (EPSI) data acquired in six subjects, including three subjects with pathologies. We first show that the tissue parameter density used for generating dictionaries can be recast as adjusting weights of a weighted SVD applied to a dictionary with uniform tissue distributions. The weighted SVD interpretation shows that subspaces can be adjusted to improve the representation of certain tissues at the expense of others, and illustrates the potential for overoptimistic performance when applied only to healthy volunteers. Fully sampled EPSI data was retrospectively undersampled with 9-shot and 3-shot EPTI ky-t traversals. We compared reconstructions using a data-driven basis generated from fully sampled low-resolution data, and simulated bases generated from dictionaries with uniform or WM/GM oversampled T2 distributions. Images reconstructed from highly accelerated 3-shot data exhibited large deviations in signal evolutions when compared to the fully sampled reference, as well as statistically significant T2 overestimation bias of 10–15 ms. Less aggressively accelerated 9-shot data exhibited little T2 bias. These findings have considerable implications on the clinical translation of quantitative MRI using highly accelerated subspace reconstructions.

Theory

Subspace reconstructions employ a reconstruction prior based on expected temporal signal evolutions. For quantitative MRI, signals are expected to follow relaxation processes (most commonly T1/ T2) according to the Bloch equation. Basis vectors are generated by applying a SVD to a dictionary of simulated signal evolutions and retaining the first few singular vectors. Some works [9,32] employ linearly spaced relaxation parameters for dictionary generation. However, such uniform parameter distributions may fail to capture the actual distribution of predominant tissues in the anatomy of interest.

We will show that the application of SVD to signal evolutions simulated with such non-uniform spacings can be framed as performing a weighted singular value decomposition where the weights are proportional to the square root of the local density. The weighted SVD formulation provides a conceptual framework for explainable reconstruction performance and how well subspace reconstructions generalize. This perspective is important since applying a weighted SVD to a dictionary of simulated tissue evolutions will improve the representation of specific tissues or parameter ranges (usually chosen to be healthy tissues) at the expense of others, and may hinder assessment of pathologies that lie outside the prior distribution.

Methods

Subspace reconstruction

The subspace reconstruction pipeline is shown in Fig. 3. After coil compression, the fully sampled data were performed with 9-shot and 3-shot EPTI undersampling mask. Calibration data was obtained from low-resolution center k-space and used to estimate B0 field maps and even/odd phase differences. The locally low rank subspace reconstruction is formulated as:(10)minα12‖y−UFSBΦKα‖22+λ‖Rr(α)‖∗

U is the ky-t sampling mask, F is the Fourier transform, S is the sensitivity map, and B is the phase induced by B0 inhomogeneity and even/odd phase differences. ΦK is the basis which can be obtained from simulation or data-driven approaches, α is the coefficient images, and ‖·‖∗ denotes the nuclear norm. The operator Rr extracts a block from each temporal coefficient image centered around voxel r and reshapes each block into a column of a matrix. The final time-resolved, multi-contrast images is a linear combination of basis ΦK and α.

EPTI utilizes ky-t traversal to mitigate blurring and distortions while enabling the acquisition of multi-echo, multi-contrast images. EPTI encoding utilizes a continuous readout, where data points are spaced temporally by echo spacing (ESP) and by RPE lines in the phase encoding (PE) direction. Each shot covers Rseg lines in the PE direction, with adjacent line-sections interleaved. T2 estimation

The relaxation model of the GESE is shown in Fig. 2(D). Following the excitation pulse, the signal is a gradient echo (GE) signal with a R2 +R2′ decay. After the refocusing pulse, signal follows R2−R2′ decay before the spin echo (SE), and R2 +R2′ decay after the spin echo. The signal evolution at each voxel was modeled as:(11)S(TE)={M0exp⁡(−TE(R2+R2′))GEM0exp⁡(−TESE∗R2)exp⁡(−(TESE−TE)(R2−R2′))SE(TE<TESE)M0exp⁡(−TE∗R2)exp⁡(−(TE−TESE)(R2+R2′))SE(TE≫TESE)where TE is the echo time and TESE is the echo time of the spin echo. The T2=1/R2 was estimated with MATLAB's lsqnonlin function using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm, and initialized with R2=0.01ms−1.

Basis generation

Three different bases were generated to compare the effect of regularization on reconstruction. The data-driven subspace (ΦD) was generated from low-resolution fully sampled images at each echo with a matrix size = 216 × 32 × 144 (kx × ky × t). Signal evolutions were extracted from each voxel and used to form a dictionary.

In the model-based simulation, basis vectors were generated by applying SVD to a dictionary of signal evolutions simulated with a uniform T2 distribution (ΦU) and a WM/GM oversampled T2 distribution(ΦO). By applying the same experimental parameters as the fully sampled data, the possible signal evolutions can be simulated with extended phase graphs (EPG) [35,36]. Similar to previous studies [32], a uniform spaced T2 distribution ranging from 1 to 400 ms with a step size of 1 ms was used for simulation. Since WM and GM accounts for the majority of brain tissue, a WM/GM oversampled dictionary was generating by oversampling the T2 range corresponding to WM/GM (60–110 ms) by a factor of 10, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Dictionary spacings for subspace generation vary considerably between works [9,15,16,32], but generally range between 2 and 10. The relatively high oversampling factor of 10 employed in this work was chosen not to represent the actual tissue distribution, but to emphasize the bias effects induced by dictionary oversampling. The dictionary also included B1 inhomogeneity factors of 0.6–1.2 to match signal intensity variations in GESE after the refocusing pulse.

Data acquisition

Fully sampled ky-t EPSI were acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3T scanner with a 20-channel head/neck coil (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the appropriate institutional review board. The fully sampled datasets including 3 healthy subjects (Subject 1: 28-year-old male, Subject 2: 34-year-old male, and Subject 3: 24-year-old female) and 3 brain tumor subjects (Subject 4: 57-year-old male with vestibular schwannoma, Subject 5: 67-year-old male with meningioma, and Subject 6: 47-year-old male with meningioma). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review boards of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (Approval No.: E20240286I). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants (or their legal guardians) included in the study.

The fully sampled acquisition parameters were as follows: field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm2, in-plane resolution = 1.1 × 1.1 mm2, slice thickness = 3 mm, number of shots = 216, number of echoes 144 (48 GE, 96 SE), TErange of GE = 4.3–48.0 ms, TErange of SE = 60.0–149.0 ms, ESP = 0.93 ms, TR = 3.5 s. The 9-shot and 3-shot EPTI undersampled data were retrospectively undersampled in ky-t space from the fully sampled data by Rseg = 24 for 9-shot and Rseg = 72 for 3-shot retrospective undersampling.

The first K = 4 singular vectors of each dictionary were used in subspace reconstruction, which captured 99% of the energy when applied to the low-resolution data in every case. As shown in Fig. 2(E), the cumulative energy of the simulated subspaces with first K = 4 basis vectors is 99.01% (uniform T2 distribution basis) and 99.05% (WM/GM oversampled basis), compared to 99.88% for the data-driven basis. Coil compression was performed using ESPIRiT [37] to reduce the data to 11 virtual channels, thereby alleviating the computational burden of LLR subspace reconstruction and improving SNR by suppressing redundant and low-SNR coil components. Alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) [38] was used to solve the reconstruction problem. Berkeley advanced reconstruction toolbox (BART) [39] was used for the LLR subspace reconstruction, Other processing was performed with MATLAB (R2019a, MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, USA). The maximum number of iterations was 100 and regularization parameter λ was set to 0.1.

Discussion

This study assessed the effect of regularization on T2 estimation in subspace reconstructions using data-driven and simulated bases with fully sampled EPSI data (serving as a ground truth) retrospectively undersampled with different EPTI ky-t traversals. The basis used for reconstruction significantly influenced the T2 estimates under high acceleration. Biases introduced during reconstruction affect the clinical applicability of highly accelerated subspace reconstructions. The data-driven basis consistently outperformed simulated bases, particularly at higher acceleration factors, as shown in Fig. 7. The data-driven basis were derived from calibration data with 32 PE lines. The 32 calibration PE lines was selected to balance reconstruction accuracy and the effective retrospective scan time. As shown in Appendix A. Supplementary data Fig. S1, reconstruction results remained robust when 32 or more calibration PE lines were used. When fewer PE lines were used, the low-resolution calibration data failed to adequately characterize the signal evolutions and thus the resulting subspaces. Furthermore, the low-rank constraint became less effective in suppressing residual artifacts, leading to notable T2 estimation errors. The subspaces generated from simulated dictionaries apply simplified signal models based on the Bloch equation. Measured tissue signal evolutions may deviate from the analytical models because of imperfections such as imperfect refocusing, although B1 inhomogeneity was included in the dictionary to account for this.

Compared to data-driven subspaces, simulated subspaces offer a flexible and reproducible alternative without additional scans. Subspaces are normally generated from discretized sampling of the parameter space, but we allowed the spacing between samples to approximate the local density. The simulated WM/GM oversampled basis reduced the reconstruction error for healthy tissues and had a smaller T2 estimation bias compared to the reconstruction with a uniform basis. However, based on the weighted SVD interpretation, WM/GM oversampling may lead to increased reconstruction error because the T2 distribution used in WM/GM oversampling cannot fully represent the true physiological tissue distribution, especially for pathological tissues with relaxation properties deviating from the WM/GM range. For example, pathologies with differing T2 like schwannomas and meningiomas may be insufficiently represented, which can introduce systematic biases in T2 estimates of these tissues. Clinical evaluation of subspace reconstructions cannot be based on healthy volunteers, since applying a greater weighting to healthy tissues during dictionary generation can create seemingly improved reconstructions at the expense of pathological tissues. However, there are certain applications, such as measuring brain volume [40,41] or segmenting WM/GM, where a WM/GM oversampled dictionary may be appropriate.

While simulated subspaces may not fully capture the actual T2 distribution, data-driven subspaces can still be biased towards tissues that most commonly occur in the dataset. This is problematic because pathologies are often small and therefore prone to partial voluming in low-resolution images, where their signals are mixed with adjacent tissues. For example, if IPartialVolumed is the signal of a low-resolution voxel that integrates signals from four adjacent high-resolution voxels with equal contribution, including three WM voxels and one pathology voxel, IPartialVolumed is:(12)IPartialVolumed=34IWM+14IPathology

Partial voluming not only reduces the effective weight of the pathological signal within the voxel, but also renders the blended signal incapable of accurately reflecting the true signal evolution of the pathology.

With highly accelerated 3-shot undersampling, T2 estimation biases of 10–15 ms were observed, which is large relative to the normal WM/GM T2 range of 60–110 ms at 3 T. The bias of 10%–20% induced by excessive undersampling is on the order of errors induced by unaccounted physical effects in parametric modeling, such as gradient nonlinearity in ADC estimation [42], B1 inhomogeneity in MR fingerprinting [43], or magnetization transfer effects in T1 estimation [44]. A less aggressive undersampling factor of 9 shots exhibited less bias in T2 maps. Acquisitions in this study were performed with a 20-channel head/neck coil. While higher channel count coils that can achieve higher undersampling factors are available, the dependence of T2 estimation bias on parallel imaging power represents a challenge for clinical translation of quantitative subspace reconstructions. Since the geometry factors of different receive coils drastically vary, acquisitions on different hardware may fail to produce consistent results in pathological tissues.

Under different acceleration factors, alternate regularization factors may be required to suppress greater aliasing. Appendix A. Supplementary data Fig.s S2–S3 show the reconstruction results with different regularization strengths for both 9-shot and 3-shot retrospective undersampling. λ = 0.1 visually provided a balanced trade-off between residual aliasing and reduction of block artifacts for both 9-shot and 3-shot reconstructions. Shrinkage bias, where the energy in the coefficient images is reduced as the regularization factor increases, is shown in Appendix A. Supplementary data Fig. S4. Example reconstructions at TE = 100 ms obtained from the reconstructed coefficient images are shown in Appendix A. Supplementary data Fig.s S5-6.

This study has several limitations. First, the data-driven basis relies on fully sampled low-resolution images. The data-driven basis used in this work implicitly incorporates effects of B1 inhomogeneity, improving its compression power. Although this is one factor that may have reduced the bias in estimated T2, the simulated subspaces also incorporated B1 effects. For 9-shot retrospective undersampling, reconstructions obtained using simulated bases exhibited little T2 bias. This indicates that biases observed in the retrospective 3-shot reconstruction scan are mostly due to regularization effects, and not due to unaccounted B1 inhomogeneity. Besides, the reconstruction time is relatively long. On a Linux workstation (Intel Xeon Gold 6230, 2.10 GHz; NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti), reconstruction requires approximately 100 s per slice with a simulated basis and 110 s per slice with a data-driven basis. The latter includes the additional computational cost of data-driven basis estimation from the low-resolution calibration data.

Conclusion

Relaxation parameters estimated from highly accelerated subspace reconstruction are sensitive to the tissue distribution used for generating the subspace. Applying SVD to a dictionary generated with non-linear spacings is equivalent to applying a weighted SVD to a dictionary generated with linear spacings. Reconstructions using a data-driven basis had less T2 estimation error than reconstructions using simulated bases in highly retrospectively undersampled data.
