SafeConstellations: Steering LLM Safety to Reduce Over-Refusals Through Task-Specific Trajectory
Utsav Maskey, Sumit Yadav, Mark Dras, Usman Naseem

Abstract
LLMs increasingly exhibit over-refusal behavior, where safety mechanisms cause models to reject benign instructions that superficially resemble harmful content. This phenomena diminishes utility in production applications that repeatedly rely on common prompt templates or applications that frequently rely on LLMs for specific tasks (e.g. sentiment analysis, language translation). Through comprehensive evaluation, we demonstrate that LLMs still tend to refuse responses to harmful instructions when those instructions are reframed to appear as benign tasks. Our mechanistic analysis reveal that LLMs follow distinct “constellation” patterns in embedding space as representations traverse layers, with each task maintaining consistent trajectories that shift predictably between refusal and non-refusal cases. We introduce SafeConstellations , an inference-time trajectory-shifting approach that tracks task-specific trajectory patterns and guides representations toward non-refusal pathways. By selectively guiding model behavior only on tasks prone to over-refusal, and by preserving general model behavior, our method reduces over-refusal rates by up to 73% with minimal impact on utility–offering a principled approach to mitigating over-refusals.



1
Introduction

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has revolutionized natural language processing capabilities. However, ensuring these models remain both safe and useful presents a significant challenge
(
Röttger et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2025
;
Karaman et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
. Current safety alignment methods while successful at preventing harmful responses, inconsistently exhibit over-refusal behavior— rejecting benign instructions that contain sensitive keywords or contexts (Figure
1
).

This becomes apparent in production applications where the context are extracted from external sources, user inputs, or retrieval augmented generation (RAG)
(
Lewis et al. 2020
)
pipelines. For example, a customer support system that relies on sentiment analysis might refuse to process reviews containing metaphorical expressions like “killer good” or a translation service might reject historical documents that discuss sensitive topics of wars or revolutions due to perceived harmful content. Such refusals disrupt LLM helpfulness and diminish model utility.

While over-refusal
(
Cui et al. 2025
)
broadly refers to the rejection of seemingly toxic inputs, we hypothesize that refusal rates differ by task for the same input. We define the problem of task over-refusal as the rejection of benign instructions solely based on inputs context, ignoring the actual task intent, which is benign. Ideally, we want the responses to be (1)
non-refusing
and (2) either
direct
or
cautious
.

Figure 2:
LLMs follow distinct “constellation” patterns in embedding space as representation traverse layers, with each task maintaining consistent trajectories that shift only marginally (and predictably) between refusal and non-refusal cases. When we combine multiple tasks together (i.e. combine the datasets), the aggregate layer-wise non-refusal direction becomes ambiguous.

To address safety concerns, recent defense mechanisms leverage internal state monitoring and intervention to promote safer LLM outputs
(
Wei et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2025
;
Xie et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
. Prior works identified that LLMs encode task-specific information in their weight space, allowing mechanistic differentiation of tasks
(
Gueta et al. 2023
)
. Building on these advances, we investigate the diversity of embedding regions across LLM layers and extend the literature by identifying consistent propagation patterns for different task types (input categories). We also examine how these patterns relate to output behaviors such as refusal, direct answering, and cautious responses.

This leads us to hypothesize that LLM layers encode task-specific information, enabling us to analytically distinguish original tasks—such as sentiment analysis, translation, paraphrasing, etc. And these task-specific “constellations patterns” are invariant to input content, and only reflect the task’s intent. Within each task constellation, finer variations correspond to behavioral outcomes, such as direct answering, refusal, or cautious response—appearing as localized shifts in the broader task trajectory (Figure
2
). By mapping these task-dependent patterns, we design interventions that selectively adjust model behavior without compromising general utility.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We develop a benchmark dataset for measuring task over-refusal in NLP that analyzes how LLM safety goals vary by text type and task.

•

We propose a novel steering approach that guides representations layer-by-layer during inference to reduce over-refusal. Over-refusal are reduced without compromising general capabilities.

•

Experiments reveal that LLMs encode task identity via consistent internal trajectory patterns. Leveraging these patterns enables precise response adjustments, reducing over-refusals with negligible impact on utility.

2
Related Work

LLM Safety Alignment and Defense:

Ensuring the safe and reliable operation of LLMs is a critical research challenge
(
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Bai et al. 2022
;
Dai et al. 2023
)
. Safety alignment methods can be broadly categorized into instruction tuning, preference alignment, and inference-time defenses.

Instruction tuning involves supervised fine-tuning on adversarial prompts and safety-oriented demonstrations
(
Hsu et al. 2024
;
Choi, Du, and Li 2024
)
. Preference alignment methods, such as PPO
(
Schulman et al. 2017
)
, DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2023
)
, and GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
optimize model behaviour using human or synthetically generated preference data. Recent advances include reasoning-level preference optimization
(
Mou et al. 2025
)
, which addresses safety vulnerabilities and over-refusals by reflecting on the model’s reasoning steps and aligning its preferences to spot harmful intent in the early steps. Furthermore, automated frameworks for generating high-quality preference data
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
enhances alignment robustness.

Inference-time defense mechanisms such as SafeDecoding
(
Xu et al. 2024
)
analyze output logit differences between the aligned safer model and the unaligned harmful model to amplify the probabilities of safer disclaimers and attenuate harmful tokens, while RA-LLM
(
Cao et al. 2024
)
and similar input mutating methods
(
Kumar et al. 2024
)
identify jailbroken behavior by dropping a random sample of tokens and iteratively check for inconsistencies among the responses.

Over-Refusal Benchmarks:
Current literature highlights the trade-off between safety and utility in LLMs, where excessive alignment can lead to over-refusal—the erroneous rejection of benign but superficially sensitive queries
(
Cui et al. 2025
;
Röttger et al. 2024
)
. XSTest
(
Röttger et al. 2024
)
introduced a diagnostic suite of 250 hand-crafted safe prompts and 200 unsafe contrasts to evaluate exaggerated safety behaviors. Their findings indicate that lexical overfitting—where models rely on superficial keyword cues—is a primary cause of false refusals. OR-Bench
(
Cui et al. 2025
)
introduces 80,000 synthetically generated over-refusal prompts, enabling large-scale assessment across tasks and models. Further work POROver
(
Karaman et al. 2025
)
demonstrates that preference optimization can mitigate over-refusal in safety-aligned models.

Mechanistic Interpretability and Internal State Steering:
Recent research also analyzes mechanistic interpretability, exploring how internal model representations influence safety behaviors. Representation and latent space steering
(
Xie et al. 2024
;
Shen et al. 2025
;
Zhou et al. 2024
)
demonstrate that targeted modifications to activation patterns can dynamically adjust model outputs without retraining or prompt modification. Notably, Jailbreak Antidote
(
Shen et al. 2025
)
reveals that safety-critical representations are often sparsely distributed, enabling precise behavioral control and this line of research enables real-time, efficient safety adjustments.

Current over-refusal benchmarks primarily evaluate general queries and direct seemingly toxic texts, they often neglect structured inputs with prompting formats and task specified wrappers around input texts. Additionally, while prior work investigates internal state representations, there remains a gap in analyzing how LLMs’ internal representations evolve across layers when processing different types of inputs requests, which we explore in this work.

Methodology

Problem Formulation:
Let
x\in\mathcal{X}
denote an input prompt,
t\in\mathcal{T}
its intended task label, and
f_{\theta}
a frozen LLM with
L
transformer layers. At layer
\ell

\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}\in{R}^{d}
and a task-specified prompt by

We partition the model’s textual outputs into
\mathcal{Y}_{\text{tar}}

\mathcal{Y}_{\text{ref}}
(refusals and optionally harmful). Over-refusal for a prompt occurs when the model refuses to answer queries with benign intent:

f_{\theta}(x\oplus t)\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{ref}}\quad\wedge\quad\text{Intent}(t)=\text{benign}.

(1)

Our steering module minimizes the rate of over-refusal in Equation
1
while leaving harmful-intent refusals unaffected.

3.2
Task-Specific Trajectories and Memory Bank Construction

Here we keep track of task trajectories, useful for distinguishing patterns during inference. For each task
t\in\mathcal{T}
, we evaluate the refusal / non-refusal cases by inferring the training data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
using base LLM
f_{\theta}(x)
, and the resulting responses
y_{i}
are classified into
target behavior

\mathcal{S}^{\text{tar}}_{t}
(direct or cautious answers) and
over-refusal

\mathcal{S}^{\text{ref}}_{t}
(refusals to benign prompts), using LLMs-as-a-judge
Refusal Evaluation
. Refer to Section
4
for more details on dataset and target behavior evaluation. At every layer
\ell
, we compute task-specific centroids:

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{t,\text{tar}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{\text{tar}}_{t}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{tar}}_{t}}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{i},\quad\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{t,\text{ref}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{\text{ref}}_{t}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{ref}}_{t}}\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}_{i}.

(2)

The sequences
\{\mathbf{c}_{t,\text{tar}}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=0}^{L}
and
\{\mathbf{c}_{t,\text{ref}}^{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=0}^{L}
form two distinct trajectories. Figure
2
shows that both trajectories belonging to the same task cluster tightly, validating that LLMs encode task identity beyond raw lexical features.

Task-specific steering vectors (
\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{t}
):

For each task
t
and layer
\ell

\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{t}=\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{t,\text{tar}}-\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{t,\text{ref}}
.
The effectiveness of steering at layer
\ell

\|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{t}\|

\text{Eff}^{(\ell)}_{t}=\frac{\|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)}_{t}\|}{\sigma^{(\ell)}_{t,\text{tar}}+\sigma^{(\ell)}_{t,\text{ref}}+\varepsilon}

(3)

where
\sigma
denotes average intra-cluster deviation. Only the top-
K

K=5

3.3
Dynamic Layer Selection and Adaptive Steering

Given a task
\hat{t}
, we dynamically select
K^{\prime}
layers (typically
K^{\prime}=4
) with the highest steering potential, defined as

{Pot}^{(\ell)}=\frac{\|\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}-\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{\hat{t},\text{ref}}\|}{\|\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}-\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{\hat{t},\text{tar}}\|+\varepsilon}

(4)

where
\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}
is the current embedding at layer
\ell
, and
\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{\hat{t},\text{ref}}
,
\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{\hat{t},\text{tar}}
are the over-refusal and target centroids, respectively.

For each selected layer, trajectory health is assessed as:

{Health}^{(\ell)}=\frac{\cos(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{\hat{t},\text{tar}})-\cos(\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{c}^{(\ell)}_{\hat{t},\text{ref}})+2}{4}

(5)

interpolating between 0 (over-refusal) and 1 (target alignment). Steering intensity is then set by
\lambda^{(\ell)}=\lambda_{0}(1-\text{Health}^{(\ell)})\cdot\text{Confidence}\cdot\kappa^{(\ell)}
, where
\lambda_{0}
is a base coefficient and
\kappa^{(\ell)}
is a depth-dependent multiplier.

3.4
Constellation Steering Application

At each dynamically selected layer
\ell^{*}
, steering is applied as:

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}^{(\ell^{*})}=\mathbf{h}^{(\ell^{*})}+\lambda^{(\ell^{*})}\frac{\mathbf{v}^{(\ell^{*})}_{\hat{t}}}{\|\mathbf{v}^{(\ell^{*})}_{\hat{t}}\|}

(6)

Where,
\mathbf{v}^{(\ell^{*})}_{\hat{t}}
is the task-specific steering vector. To maintain utility, no steering is applied if: (i) the prompt doesn’t match our benign tasks
\mathcal{T}_{\text{benign}}
, or (ii) steering potential is below threshold. Thus, harmful-intent queries remain refused.

Experimental Settings

Dataset:
Our benchmark dataset combines a set of sensitive texts and formulates the prompt with different tasks: Sentiment Analysis, Language Translation, Rephrasing, Cryptanalysis (Decryption), Retrieval-Augmented Question Answering (RAG-QA).
Table
1
outlines the dataset statistics, where we re-frame the texts into task-specific prompt templates with a challenge for LLMs that benign-intent tasks like sentiment analysis, translation, and cryptanalysis should not trigger refusals, regardless of input nature.

We construct the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},t_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
spanning
N=1047
samples, covering the five tasks. Each

is a prompt drawn from diverse sources (Alpaca Dataset
(
Taori et al. 2023
)
, XSTest
(
Röttger et al. 2024
)

(
Chao et al. 2024

Prior to re-framing, these texts

1
).

Safe Content
includes benign texts and seemingly harmful prompts that should not be refused;
Harmful Content
includes jailbreak prompts and harmful instructions. Here, the challenge is that harmful content should not be refused when re-framed with a benign intent (e.g. Analyze the sentiment: ‘How to Kill a Person’
\rightarrow
‘Negative’), but the Jailbreak prompts should be refused regardless of the intent. As for
Task-specific Content
, it includes encrypted harmful instructions for cryptanalysis and contextual prompts for RAG-QA.

Responses

are generated by LLMs and used later for creating Memory Bank
\mathcal{M}
and further analysis.
We define the set of benign-intent tasks as:

\mathcal{T}_{\text{benign}}=\{\text{sentiment analysis},\text{translate},\text{cryptanalysis},\text{rag qa}\}.

(7)

Rephrasing is excluded from
\mathcal{T}_{\text{benign}}

t\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{benign}}\implies f_{\theta}(x\oplus t)\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{tar}}.

Evaluation Framework:

For each sample
(x_{i},t_{i})
, we generate responses
y_{i}
and annotate them using two LLM-as-a-judge protocols. The first protocol classifies refusal behavior as
r_{i}\in\{\textit{direct answer},\textit{direct refusal},\textit{indirect refusal}\}

(
Cui et al. 2025
)
, where direct answers indicate helpfulness, direct refusals indicate unwillingness to respond and indirect refusals offer alternative suggestions without addressing the original query. The second protocol assesses safety characteristics as
s_{i}\in\{\textit{benign},\textit{cautious},\textit{harmful}\}
, that distinguish between responses that are entirely safe, acknowledge potential risks while remaining helpful or generate harmful responses.

Target Behavior
(
\mathcal{S}^{\text{tar}}
,
\mathbf{c}_{t,\text{tar}}
): Our target behavior requires responses to be cautious or benign
s_{i}\in\{\textit{cautious},\textit{benign}\}
while providing direct answers (
r_{i}=\textit{direct answer}
). Task over-refusal occurs when:

\text{OverRefusal}(x_{i}\oplus t_{i})={I}[r_{i}\neq\text{direct answer}\wedge t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{benign}}]

And we would like to steer the responses towards the target:

\displaystyle\text{Target}(x_{i}\oplus t_{i})

\displaystyle={I}[r_{i}=\text{direct answer}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\wedge s_{i}\in\{\textit{cautious},\textit{benign}\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\wedge t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{benign}}]

(9)

This ensures that models do not reject benign tasks; and that the answer acknowledges potential risks yet complies with the request.

Utility Evaluation:
To assess utility drop in our steering mitigation, we evaluate performance on the MMLU benchmark
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
. We compare the model’s accuracy before and after steering and take note of any potential drop in performance.

Ablation Studies:
We evaluate SafeConstellations on LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct and Qwen1.5-7B. Our evaluation, conducted on 270 test-set samples, reveals 169 target behavior cases and 48 over-refusal cases for LLaMA, compared to 221 target behaviors and 22 over-refusals for Qwen. Table
2
presents a systematic ablation of SafeConstellations, examining removal of each component: dynamic layer selection, task-specific steering and trajectory-based modulation.

Effectiveness of Task-Specific Steering:

We find task-specific steering the most effective component for reducing over-refusals and preserving utility. When applied without dynamic layer selection, this approach achieves 5.92–6.29% over-refusal rates on LLaMA3.1-8B, corresponding to a 64.58–66.67% reduction relative to baseline, with no loss in MMLU performance.

Trajectory-Based Alignment Benefits:

Including trajectory health assessment (Equation
5
) reduces over-refusal cases by 2.08%—although not quite substantial, it reinforces our hypothesis that constellation patterns provide interpretable signals for guiding representational steering toward desired behaviors .

Benefits of Dynamic Layer Selection:
Similarly, by selecting the layers dynamically, our full configuration further reduces over-refusal rate to 72.92% with only (4.81%) in LLaMA—maintaining full MMLU accuracy (46.57%). Similarly, on Qwen1.5-7B, it achieves a 63.64% reduction with no degradation in utility.
Layer Range and Locality:
Steering in late layers alone (26–30) offers comparable refusal reduction (64.58%), while interventions restricted to the final layer also perform well (66.67% reduction), but falls short when compared to the dynamic selection.

Task-Specific Steering and Utility:

As discussed, removal of Task-Specific steering despite reducing over-refusal (e.g., 60.42% reduction for intense steering), cause significant utility loss (MMLU drop to 43.66%). Notably, less intense fixed steering leads to near-baseline refusal rates (16.66%) and minimal benefit (6.25% reduction), indicating excessive manipulation to hidden representation.

Qualitative Analysis and Steering Intensity:
Aggressive interventions better suppress over-refusals (60.42%), but often compromise response quality, as shown by MMLU drops and incoherent completions (e.g., repetitive or garbled output in Table
3
). Conversely, SafeConstellations achieves refusal reduction while preserving linguistic coherence and task intent.

Cross-Model Generalization:
The approach and discussion on task-specific trajectory generalizes across model architectures (see trajectory visualizations for both LLaMA and Qwen in the Appendix
A.6
). This suggests that constellation patterns represent fundamental organizational principles in transformer architectures rather than model-specific artifacts. Such dynamic, trajectory-aligned interventions suppresses over-refusal without destabilizing model behavior and formulates precision interventions informed by a model’s internal decision boundaries.

6
Conclusion

Our findings reveal several key insights about task over-refusal.
Task-Dependent Nature:
Over-refusal rates vary significantly across tasks—highlighting the importance of task-specific intervention strategies rather than generalized approaches.
Trajectory-Aware Steering:
Constellation steering demonstrates as an effective method of manipulating LLM response preferences, with applications beyond mitigating over-refusals. It does so by operating at the representational level where task identity crystallizes, enabling targeted behavioral adjustments without broad model disruption.
Safety Preservation:
Unlike broad and aggressive steering approaches that attempt to eliminate all safety cases, our method maintains appropriate caution while reducing inappropriate refusals, suggesting that representational steering can distinguish between legitimate safety concerns and over-conservative responses.
