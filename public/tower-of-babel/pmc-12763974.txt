Analysis of prenatal medication use and placental epigenetic gestational age in extremely low gestational age newborns (ELGANs) highlight relationships to aspirin use during pregnancy
Authors: Katelyn K. Huff, Catherine M. Bulka, Jeliyah Clark, Kyle R. Roell, T. Michael O’Shea, Rebecca C. Fry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral maternal exposures such as acetaminophen during pregnancy have been previously associated with altered placental CpG methylation. Epigenetic gestational age measures biological aging using DNA methylation from gestational tissues such as placenta and cord blood. We hypothesize that placental epigenetic gestational age (eGA) could serve as a biomarker for maternal exposures or pathological processes that influence placental development. To investigate relationships between maternal exposures and placental eGA, we evaluated prenatal medication use (antibiotics, acetaminophen, aspirin, and ibuprofen) in relation to placental epigenetic gestational age acceleration (eGAA) using the Robust Placental Clock (RPC). We also examined associations between these four exposures and the methylation levels of the 558 individual CpGs comprising the RPC. Using data from the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborns (ELGAN) study (N = 408), we ran linear mixed-effects regression models that accounted for multiple births to the same mother. We further stratified by infant sex assigned at birth to assess effect measure modification.ResultsWe observed a positive association between prenatal aspirin use and eGAA at borderline significance (β: + 0.35 weeks gestation; 95% CI: − 0.01, 0.72). When stratified by sex, this finding was significant among females (β: + 0.63 weeks gestation; 95% CI: 0.10, 1.17) but not males (β: − 0.05 weeks gestation; 95% CI: − 0.60, 0.49). Prenatal aspirin use in female pregnancies was further associated with ten differentially methylated RPC CpGs, mapping to genes including Retinoid X Receptor Alpha (RXRA), a nuclear receptor that can function as transcription factor and thereby influence multiple processes critical to placental development.ConclusionThese findings highlight that prenatal aspirin use is associated with placental eGAA in a sex-dependent manner, and RPC CpG methylation is sensitive to maternal medication exposures. Additional research is required to validate these eGAA observations, and future research should investigate the mechanisms underlying these relationships and their potential long-term clinical implications in the infant.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01988-9.

Background

Prescribed and over-the-counter (OTC) medication use during pregnancy is widespread, with 90% of pregnant people using at least one medication [1]. However, while many of these medications are considered generally safe when taken in low doses over short durations [2], some of these medications have been associated with pregnancy complications as well as adverse birth and developmental outcomes [3–5]. Previous work has suggested that several medications used during pregnancy can impact gestational age at birth. For example, use of acetaminophen, particularly during the third trimester of pregnancy, has been associated with decreased gestational age or higher risk of preterm birth of less than 37 weeks of gestation [6, 7]. In contrast, prenatal low-dose aspirin use has been associated with lower risk of preterm birth [8].

The mechanisms by which these pharmaceuticals may influence gestational age are not fully understood. One potential route is through epigenetic regulation including DNA methylation which occurs at cytosine-(phosphate)-guanine (CpG) loci to serve as a key switch for gene expression [9, 10]. Specifically, epigenetic age, calculated using ‘clock’ algorithms based upon DNA methylation levels at specific age-related CpG loci in the genome, can more accurately indicate one’s biological age and health status compared to chronological age alone [11–14]. The use of NSAIDs and antihypertensives have previously been associated with accelerated epigenetic aging in adult tissues [15, 16]; however, prenatal exposures and their potential implications for the fetus are not well characterized. To accomplish this, algorithms derived from fetal tissue like umbilical cord blood [17, 18] and placental tissue [19, 20] can be utilized to more accurately estimate epigenetic gestational age (eGA).

To date, very few medications have been investigated for their impacts on eGA in fetal tissues, with only one study citing associations between prenatal selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and eGA deceleration in umbilical cord blood samples [21]. In contrast to assessments in cord blood, measures in the placenta serve as an indicator of effects in a critical target organ that develops during pregnancy. The placenta serves in direct communication between the pregnant person and fetus by facilitating the transfer of nutrients, gases, hormones, and antibodies [22]. It also acts as a dynamic monitor for environmental cues during pregnancy, which can alter its development and functioning. For example, in utero acetaminophen exposure is associated with differential placental CpG methylation annotating to genes related to placental maintenance as well as inhibition of first trimester trophoblast invasion in vitro [10, 23].

Presently, four placental epigenetic clock algorithms have been defined for eGA estimation. The first was derived by Mayne et al. and comprises 62 CpG probes using placental DNA methylation data from both the Infinium Human Methylation27 (27 K) and Methylation450 (450 K) BeadChip arrays [19]. In contrast, Lee et al. later developed three more distinct placental clocks using a larger training set from Infinium Human MethylationEPIC (EPIC) and 450 K arrays: (1) the Control Placental Clock (CPC), comprising 546 CpGs for unaffected pregnancies; (2) the Robust Placental Clock (RPC), comprising 558 CpGs and considered unaffected by pregnancy complications; and (3) the Refined RPC (rRPC), comprising 395 CpGs for uncomplicated term pregnancies [20]. When comparing the performance of placental clocks, the RPC consistently exhibited stronger correlations to gestational age than Mayne et al.’s clock, providing more reliability in its application to diverse datasets [24, 25].

In the present study, we aimed to elucidate the following epigenetic mechanisms by which commonly used medications may influence placental biology and later fetal trajectories. First, leveraging the unique dataset of the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborns (ELGAN) cohort, we hypothesized that self-reported prenatal medication use (i.e., acetaminophen, antibiotics, aspirin, and ibuprofen) would be associated with placental eGA alternations estimated using the RPC. Secondly, differential methylation at key regulatory RPC CpGs that may influence placental biology and yield long-term consequences in the infant or transgenerational effects based on the developmental origins of health and disease (DoHAD) hypothesis [26]. Thus, we further evaluated the hypothesis that these medications would be associated with the methylation levels among the individual 558 RPC CpGs. Linear mixed regression modeling was conducted to account for multiple births in our sample, and both overall and sex-stratified analyses were performed to evaluate potential sexually dimorphic epigenetic modifications which have previously been observed in placenta [27–29].

Methods

ELGAN recruitment

The analyses discussed in this study utilized data collected from the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborn (ELGAN) birth cohort, which was originally designed to investigate possible contributors to risk of neurological disorder development in extremely prematurely born infants [30]. The study recruitment and sample collection has been described elsewhere [30]. In brief, women who gave birth before reaching 28 weeks of gestation at one of the 14 participating ELGAN sites were invited to participate in the study. All study procedures received approval from the Institutional Review Board at each of the 14 study sites. Mothers gave informed consent after hospital admission before or shortly following delivery, resulting in a total of 1249 mothers and 1506 infants enrolled in ELGAN. From the 1411 placentas retrieved at the time of delivery, our sample comprised 411 placentas for which DNA methylation levels were previously quantified [31].

Chronological gestational age ascertainment

Chronological gestational age was estimated based on ultrasound examination before the 14th week of gestation (62%), or for infants conceived via assisted reproductive technology, on the dates of embryo retrieval or intrauterine insemination [32]. If this information was unavailable, estimations were made using the following sequential approach: (1) ultrasound examination at 14 or more weeks (29%), (2) menstrual dating without ultrasound confirmation (7%), or (3) gestational age recorded in the log of the neonatal intensive care unit (1%) [32].

Placental sample collection

Placentas were collected into a sterile exam basin and biopsied in the sampling room as reported previously [33]. Briefly, samples were taken at the midpoint of the longest distance between the cord insertion and edge of the placental disk. Using sterile technique, the amnion was peeled away to expose the chorion, and traction was applied to the chorion and underlaying trophoblast tissue. A sample was cut from the base of this fetal tissue, collected in a sterile 2 mL cryovial, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Each sample was then stored at − 80 °C until further processing.

DNA extraction and DNA methylation assay

DNA extraction from placental samples and methylation assays were carried out as previously described [34, 35]. To detail, genomic DNA sequences were isolated utilizing All Prep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) and quantified and normalized with Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following respective manufacturer’s protocols. Extracted DNA samples were shipped on dry ice to the core facility where methylation assays were performed. For the assays, DNA underwent bisulfite conversion using EZ DNA Methylation Kits (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) and then analyzed on Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) which measures methylation levels across more than 850,000 CpG probes.

Average methylation levels at individual CpG sites were estimated as β-values, representing the ratio of methylated over unmethylated allele intensities [36]. Background correction and normalization of β-values was performed using normal-exponential out-of-band (noob) correction and functional normalization methodologies, respectively [37, 38]. For the evaluation of potential batch effects, principal component analysis (PCA) suggested that plate was a significant source of variation. ComBat procedure with the surrogate variable analysis (sva) package was used for removal of batch effects, followed by PCA [39, 40].

Estimating placental epigenetic gestational age using the robust placental clock (RPC)

To account for participants’ extreme prematurity (< 28 weeks gestation), the Robust Placental Clock (RPC) was selected to estimate placental epigenetic gestational age (eGA) since this clock algorithm is largely unaffected by pregnancy complications which could reduce the length of gestation [20]. The RPC calculates the weighted average of placental DNA methylation β-values from 558 CpGs that were originally selected using penalized elastic net regression to accurately predict gestational age [20]. To derive epigenetic gestational age acceleration (eGAA), we regressed predicted eGA onto chronological gestational age and extracted the model residuals. Positive residual values indicate ‘accelerated’ eGA whereas negative residual values indicate ‘decelerated’ eGA. We excluded three samples with RPC-estimated age acceleration residuals greater than three standard deviations from the mean as potential outliers [41]. Medications used during pregnancy

The following four medications used during pregnancy in the ELGAN cohort were assessed in this study: (1) acetaminophen, (2) antibiotic, (3) aspirin, and (4) ibuprofen uses during pregnancy. Note that these exposures were characterized as binary variables (no/yes) and thus cannot determine dosage or timing inferences. Acetaminophen, antibiotic, aspirin, and ibuprofen exposures were considered ‘yes’ if the mother self-reported taking “Tylenol,” “Antibiotic,” “Aspirin or aspirin-containing medicine,” or “Ibuprofen (Advil, Nuprin, and Motrin)” at least once during pregnancy but prior to entering the hospital for delivery, respectively. If an individual did not indicate taking the respective medication, exposure was considered ‘no.’

Covariate selection

To minimize confounding by extraneous factors in the models, potential confounders were identified using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Supplemental Fig. 1) [42]. For each medication of interest in relation to placental eGAA, the following seven covariates were selected as the minimally sufficient adjustment set: (1) pre-pregnancy BMI (continuous), (2) maternal age (continuous, years), (3) smoking exposure while pregnant (no/yes), (4) parity (0, 1, 2 +), (5) public health insurance (no/yes), (6) maternal educational attainment (high school diploma or less, some college, college degree or greater), and (7) condition(s) indicating medication use during pregnancy.

Specifically, several conditions that could indicate use of prescribed and over-the-counter medications during pregnancy were considered to account for potential confounding by indication bias in this study [43]. Linear mixed regression was used to determine if any of the following conditions (no/yes) were associated with medication use: (1) vaginal or cervical infection, (2) fever > 100.4⁰F (38⁰C), (3) flu, (4) pneumonia, (5) asthma, and (6) urine, bladder, or kidney infection (Supplemental Table 1). Vaginal infection and fever were associated with prenatal acetaminophen use (adjusted p value or q < 0.1) and thus were included as covariates in the acetaminophen model. Vaginal infection, pneumonia, asthma, and urinary infection were associated with prenatal antibiotic use and included in the antibiotic model. Note that placental cell type and infant sex assigned at birth (female/male) were considered a precision variable and effect measure modifier, respectively. Since eGA is highly sensitive to cell type, cellular composition of each placental sample was estimated using the R package planet (v0.99.4), as described elsewhere [41, 44]. Because of the prematurity of our sample, whose births spanned 23–28 gestational weeks, the average of the first and third trimester placental reference libraries was calculated to appropriately estimate placental cell-type variation in the second trimester [41]. Principal components analysis (PCA) of cell-type proportions was performed, and the first two eigenvectors were further included in these analyses as precision variables.

Statistical analysis

In the first set of analyses, linear mixed regression models were run to examine associations between each exposure (i.e., prenatal acetaminophen use, prenatal antibiotics use, prenatal aspirin use, and prenatal ibuprofen use) and placental eGAA as the outcome while adjusting for key covariates mentioned above. Mixed-effects modeling was used to account for associations among multiples born to the same mother. Additionally, to examine the role of sex as a potential effect measure modifier, these analyses were also stratified for infant sex assigned at birth.

In secondary analyses, placental CpG methylation levels at each of the 558 CpG sites comprising the RPC clock were individually regressed on each medication exposure. Again, linear mixed-effects modeling was ran, adjusting for the same covariates and stratifying by infant sex. To correct for multiple comparisons, Benjamini and Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated to determine which loci were most strongly associated with maternal exposures (adjusted p value or q < 0.1) [45].

Additionally, some mothers exposed to the medications of interest during pregnancy, especially aspirin users, also utilized infertility therapies to get pregnant as shown in Supplemental Table S2. While prior work did not find significant associations between eGAA in cord blood tissue among infants conceived via assisted reproductive technology compared to naturally conceived infants [46], other studies suggest that assisted reproductive technologies may influence placental development as well as DNA methylation levels [47, 48]. Thus, it is possible that assisted reproductive technologies could influence eGAA estimations by the Robust Placental Clock. To account for this, we conducted a sensitivity analysis examining prenatal medication use and placental eGAA that adjusts for the use of infertility therapy (no/yes) in addition to the other covariates used in the main models (Supplemental Table S3). ELGAN mothers who self-reported as having the following treatments were characterized as ‘Yes’ for using any infertility therapy to get pregnant: (1) intrauterine insemination, (2) fertility drug such as Clomiphene or Perganol, and (3) procedure such as in vitro fertilization or intracytoplasmic sperm injection.

Furthermore, a subset of mothers in this study reported taking more than one of the medications of interest during pregnancy (Supplemental Table S2). To account for the use of multiple medications, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed that adjusts for other medications in addition to the other aforementioned covariates (Supplemental Table S3). For example, in this sensitivity analysis for prenatal aspirin use in respect to placental eGAA, the following covariates were included in the model: acetaminophen use, antibiotic use, ibuprofen use, infertility therapy, pre-pregnancy BMI, maternal age, smoking exposure while pregnant, parity, public health insurance, maternal educational attainment, health conditions, and placental cell type. All statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software version 4.4.1.

Results

Participant characteristics

Maternal and infant demographic characteristics of this ELGAN sub-cohort with RPC measurements available (N = 408 out of 1506 neonates enrolled at birth into the ELGAN cohort) are detailed in Table 1. Within this sub-cohort, mothers self-identified as White (61.6%), Black (28.7%), or Other (9.7%). The average age of these mothers was approximately 29 years old with a mean pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) of 25.5 kg/m2. Approximately 34.4% reported utilizing public insurance; over half (60.5%) reported having an education greater than a high school degree; and 10.5% reported smoking while pregnant. Moreover, there were 196 (48.0%) female and 212 (52.0%) male infants with an average gestational age of approximately 26 weeks.

Prenatal medication use was self-reported. Over half of mothers (54.4%) used acetaminophen during pregnancy and 31.0% used antibiotics. Use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) was reported with 4.8% taking aspirin and 7.6% taking ibuprofen during pregnancy. Among mothers in this sample, vaginal or cervical infections (17.6%) and urine, bladder, or kidney infections (10.1%) were reported more often than other conditions or disorders like fever (5.3%), flu (6.5%), pneumonia (1.0%), or asthma (6.5%).

Performance of the robust placental clock (RPC) in the ELGAN cohort

The epigenetic gestational ages (eGA) were calculated for each placental sample using the Robust Placental Clock (RPC) [20]. Clark et al. previously showed that this ELGAN sub-cohort exhibited a strong correlation between eGA relative to chronological GA, with a resulting Spearman correlation coefficient of r = 0.61 (p < 0.01) and median absolute error (MAE) of 2.02 gestational weeks [41]. Associations between epigenetic gestational age acceleration (eGAA) and medication use during pregnancy

Four maternal medications used during pregnancy were examined in this analysis: acetaminophen, antibiotics, aspirin, and ibuprofen. There was a borderline significant association between prenatal aspirin use and placental eGAA among the overall population (β: + 0.35 weeks gestation; 95% CI: − 0.01, 0.72) (Fig. 3). The β estimate is interpreted as follows: the exposed group has an average 0.35-week eGAA compared to those unexposed to aspirin during pregnancy. Notably, when stratified by infant sex, prenatal aspirin use was significantly associated with a 0.63 week or 4.4 day (95% CI: 0.10, 1.17) acceleration in placental eGA among female infants but not male infants (β: − 0.05 weeks gestation; 95% CI: − 0.60, 0.49). Further, two sensitivity analyses evaluating prenatal medication use in relation to eGAA were performed to address 1) the potential influence of assisted reproductive technology and 2) multiple medications used during pregnancy. To account for assisted reproductive technologies, Model 2 in Supplemental Table S3 adjusts for use of infertility therapies in addition to the eight other covariates (i.e., pre-pregnancy BMI, maternal age, smoking during pregnancy, parity, public health insurance, maternal education, health conditions, and placental cell type) included in the main analytic models. Model 2 results remained consistent with the main models, with only aspirin use during pregnancy being associated with placental eGAA in female infants (β: 0.71 weeks gestation; 95% CI: 0.17, 1.26). Additionally, when accounting for multiple medications used during pregnancy in Model 3 in Supplemental Table S3, results from this sensitivity analysis were similar with aspirin use during pregnancy being associated with a + 0.72 week acceleration in eGA (95% CI: 0.17, 1.27) for female infants.

Associations between individual robust placental clock (RPC) CpG Loci and medication use during pregnancy

To pursue the second hypothesis of this study, associations between each of the four medications and methylation levels at each of the 558 RPC CpGs were evaluated. As summarized in Table 2 in the overall sample, after adjusting for multiple comparisons, prenatal use of acetaminophen was associated with seven differentially methylated CpGs (annotating to six genes); antibiotic use associated with two CpGs (annotating to one gene); aspirin use associated with three CpGs (annotating to one gene); and ibuprofen use associated with nine CpGs (annotating to seven genes) (q < 0.1). Note that additional details surrounding these differentially methylated RPC CpGs for each medication can be found in Tables 3, 4, 5 and Supplemental Tables 3, 4, 5. When stratified by infant sex, associations between each of the medications and methylation levels at the individual RPC CpGs were distinct with limited overlap. Furthermore, these differentially methylated RPC CpGs were generally located within a gene body or transcription start site regions. Discussion

Certain medications used during pregnancy have been associated with birth outcomes and later health trajectories [5, 6]. However, the biological mechanisms that underlie these relationships are not fully understood. The placental epigenetic clock has been described as a tool to measure biological age in newborns [19, 20]. In this study, we examined maternal medication use during pregnancy in relation to (1) placental epigenetic gestational age acceleration (eGAA) and (2) methylation at individual RPC CpG loci using data from the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborns (ELGAN) cohort. Among the four medications evaluated, prenatal aspirin use was associated with a + 0.63 week acceleration in eGA in placentas among female infants. Additionally, we found that each medication used during pregnancy was associated with methylation levels at specific RPC CpGs in a sex-dependent manner.

While prenatal aspirin use has not been previously explored in relation to eGA, the positive association between its use and placental eGAA observed in this study contrasts findings from other biological aging metrics. Specifically, low-dose aspirin use was associated with decelerated biological aging estimated using an epigenetic multi-tissue clock in colon tissue as well as the metabolic clock of gestation, which estimates biological age through global metabolic profiling in plasma samples [49, 50]. However, it is important to consider that these studies utilized different populations, clock models, and tissue types which might contribute to inconsistencies across studies [24]. Furthermore, we found it interesting that ibuprofen was not associated with placental epigenetic gestational aging since both aspirin and ibuprofen are NSAIDs. Distinctions between these two NSAIDs’ pharmacologic effects within placental tissue [51] as well as the extent of exposure (i.e., dosage and frequency of use) could potentially underlie these differing outcomes.

Low-dose aspirin use during pregnancy has been associated with reduced risks of recurrent preterm birth as well as pregnancy complications such as preeclampsia that can influence placental development and increase risk of being born prematurely [8, 52]. Notably in the past decade, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and Society for Maternal–Fetal Medicine advised the prescription of low-dose aspirin to populations at high risk of developing preeclampsia [53]. While Mayne et al. showed that placentas from individuals with early onset preeclampsia exhibited higher eGA than chronological gestational age [19], a multisite cohort study by Ladd-Acosta et al. found that preeclampsia was associated with decelerated eGA at birth estimated using CpG methylation from umbilical cord blood [54]. Taken together, it is possible that the eGAA in the present study could be a positive health indicator in relation to prenatal aspirin use given the extremely premature population, perhaps allowing placentas to ‘catch-up’ to normal development in ELGAN. However, inconsistencies around this hypothesis prompt further investigation into the precise mechanisms underlying the relationship between pregnancy conditions, aspirin use during pregnancy, and epigenetic gestational age.

While further investigating the RPC CpGs associated with prenatal aspirin use in female pregnancies, there were associations with methylation levels at ten CpGs that annotated to genes including C5orf47, CABP7, TEX264, and RXRA. When heterodimerized to peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma, retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRA) acts as a transcription factor to contribute to placental development by regulating fatty acid transport and metabolism, the differentiation of cytotrophoblasts into syncytiotrophoblasts, and the synthesis of essential pregnancy hormones such as human chorionic gonadotropin [55, 56]. Low-dose aspirin can selectively inhibit thromboxane A2 and decrease oxidative stress-induced lipid peroxidation in the placenta [57, 58]. The dampening of inflammatory responses may modulate the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway which triggers downstream RXR/PPAR heterodimer activity [59],

Additionally, while the functions of CABP7, TEX264, and C5orf47 have yet to be established specifically in placental tissue, studies have suggested roles of calcium binding protein 7 (CABP7) in cytokinesis and regulation of lysosome clustering [60] and testis expressed 264 (TEX264) in ER autophagy and DNA repair in other tissues [61, 62]. If these functions are retained in the placenta, disruption to such cellular maintenance genes could have implications for proper placental development during pregnancy. Particularly, autophagy is required for proper trophoblast invasion and remodeling during the placentation process [63]. It is worth noting that aspirin has previously been shown to induce protective autophagy in other tissues [64, 65], and more investigation would be warranted to examine the potential mechanism of the medication on the regulation of these RPC-related genes and placental aging.

While prenatal acetaminophen, antibiotics, and ibuprofen use were not associated with placental eGAA in this study, each of these medications were associated with methylation levels for several RPC CpGs. For instance, prenatal acetaminophen use was significantly associated with lower methylation levels at cg12013591 in the transcription start site of LDLR in both the overall and female-specific analysis. Placental lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) mediates transport of cholesterol, an essential nutrient utilized for synthesizing progesterone and estrogen during pregnancy, from maternal serum to fetus [66, 67]. Abnormal LDLR expression in placenta been associated with pathologies such as intrauterine growth restriction [68, 69], and lower levels of placental cholesterol can lead to dysregulation of allopregnanolone production, a neurosteriod utilized in fetal nervous system development, indicating potential long-term effects in the fetus [70, 71]. Additionally, ibuprofen was associated with a higher methylation level at cg08493294 in the body of DNMT3A in the overall sample. DNA methyltransferase 3A (DNMT3A) is an enzyme that promotes early epigenetic reprogramming via de novo DNA methylation during embryonic and germ cell development [72, 73]. Downregulation of placental DNMT3A has been shown to impair trophoblast migration and invasion and associate with adverse outcomes such as early embryo growth arrest and pre-eclamptic phenotypes [74, 75]. Thus, while we did not observe associations directly with eGAA in the placenta, differential methylation of RPC CpGs could influence later-in-life health outcomes as described by the DoHAD hypothesis, and future research should investigate the mechanisms and implications of these relationships.

Distinct sex-specific differences were observed for the associations between methylation levels at RPC CpGs and each of the medications used during pregnancy. These differences align with several prior studies that show sexually dimorphic patterns among DNA methylation in placenta as well as epigenetic aging [27, 29, 76]. Meakin et al. hypothesized that molecular and phenotypic differences could be evolutionarily and hormonally driven—with female placentas prioritizing placental reserve capacity relying on glucocorticoid signaling while male placentas optimize growth- and metabolism-related utilities based on androgen signaling [77]. Furthermore, epigenetic regulators like DNA methyltransferases can silence germline genes in the placenta, potentially lending to aberrant expression of sex-specific genes during development [73]. Such underlying sex-specific features may contribute to sexual dimorphism observed in the findings of this study as well as among birth outcomes [78, 79].

Overall, these results contribute to a growing literature investigating links between maternal exposures and epigenetic aging and fill a knowledge gap by focusing on epigenetic gestational aging (eGA) especially in placental tissue. However, this study is not without limitations. First, this study utilized the ELGAN birth cohort which is comprised of premature pregnancies (less than 28 weeks of gestation). While Lee et al.’s RPC model was constructed with a wide variety of placental DNA methylation training datasets (n = 1,102) to remain unaffected by adverse pregnancy conditions [20], the derived analyses may exhibit some loss of generalizability across all gestational ages. Second, while several covariates were included in these models based on a DAG approach and explored in sensitivity analyses, information was not collected regarding other health conditions such as headache that could contribute to confounding by indication. Third, medications used during pregnancy examined in this study (i.e., acetaminophen, antibiotics, aspirin, and ibuprofen) were collected as binary records (no/yes) and, thus, cannot reflect dosage or exposure duration. Finally, limited subsets of participants were exposed to prenatal aspirin and ibuprofen in this analytic sample. Thus, results should be carefully interpreted, and replication of this study using validation cohorts consisting of full-term pregnancies and higher representation of the exposures with dose- and trimester-specific data would strengthen these findings.

Conclusions

Epigenetic gestational clocks are interesting molecular tools for measuring biological aging and identifying potential health biomarkers for later life outcomes. Specifically, use of the placental epigenetic clock for such developmental studies could offer a more precise aging and health benchmark since the placenta is the main fetal-maternal interface during gestation. To our knowledge, the present study is among the first to evaluate these maternal medication uses during pregnancy in context of placental epigenetic aging, revealing sex-specific associations with altered methylation of the individual RPC CpGs that largely annotate to genes enriching placental development. We highlight a significant association between aspirin use during pregnancy and placental eGAA in female pregnancies. In contrast to epigenetic aging metrics in adult tissues, the interpretations of eGA acceleration or deceleration have been inconclusive in prior literature. While placental eGAA has been associated with several adverse birth outcomes including early onset preeclampsia, it has also been linked to positive outcomes like improved fetal growth in female offspring and shorter stays in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) [19, 29, 41]. Thus, more investigation is required to evaluate the clinical interpretation of eGAA, specifically in placenta, as an indicator of risk or protection for the infant. Furthermore, this study has several limitations to consider when interpreting results, including small exposure group size, that calls for additional validation studies. Future work should also investigate these relationships using mixture-based approaches to assess polypharmacy; examine the mechanism by which aspirin may modulate placental clock methylation in a dose or timing-dependent manner; and explore associations between placental eGAA and later child health trajectories.
