Enhancing Soft Happiness via Evolutionary Algorithms
Mohammad H. Shekarriz, Dhananjay Thiruvady, Asef Nazari

Abstract
For 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 1 0\leq\rho\leq 1 , a ρ \rho -happy vertex v v in a coloured graph shares colour with at least ρ ​ deg ​ ( v ) \rho\mathrm{deg}(v) of its neighbours. Soft happy colouring of a graph G G with k k colours extends a partial k k -colouring to a complete vertex k k -colouring such that the number of ρ \rho -happy vertices is maximum among all such colouring extensions. The problem is known to be NP-hard, and an optimal solution has a direct relation with the community structure of the graph. In addition, some heuristics and local search algorithms, such as Local Maximal Colouring ( LMC ) and Local Search ( LS ), have already been introduced in the literature. In this paper, we design Genetic and Memetic Algorithms for soft happy colouring and test them for a large set of randomly generated partially coloured graphs. Memetic Algorithms yield a higher number of ρ \rho -happy vertices, but Genetic Algorithms can perform well only when their initial populations are generated by LMC or LS . Statistically significant results indicate that both Genetic and Memetic Algorithms achieve high average accuracy in community detection when their initial populations are enhanced using LMC . Moreover, among the competing methods, the evolutionary algorithms identified the greatest number of complete solutions.



1
Introduction

Evolutionary algorithms offer an adaptive framework for solving the optimisation challenges inherent in real-world networks, where the complexity of interconnections demands flexible search techniques
[
11
]
. By emulating the fundamental mechanisms of natural selection, including mutation and crossover, these algorithms can effectively navigate large multimodal solution spaces commonly encountered in network design and analysis
[
8
]
. Their ability to optimise configurations across domains such as social networks, cybersecurity, and neural systems has been demonstrated to enhance both system efficiency and resilience
[
2
]
. The flexibility inherent in multi-purpose evolutionary approaches allows the simultaneous balancing of often conflicting requirements, such as performance and robustness, thereby providing practical solutions in dynamic environments
[
8
]
.

Our attention here is to use evolutionary algorithms for
soft happy colouring
, a graph colouring problem that is introduced in 2015 by Zhang and Li
[
25
]
. It seeks to maximise the number of
\rho
-happy vertices
for
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, i.e., those vertices whose portion of same colour neighbours is at least
\rho
. If
\rho=1
, the problem of soft happy colouring is called “happy colouring" and a 1-happy vertex is called a “happy vertex”
[
25
]
.

Most published research papers on the subject focus on happy colouring rather than soft happy colouring, see
[
14
,
15
,
22
,
23
,
26
]
. Finding a happy colouring in a connected partially coloured graph presents a significant challenge, and research has predominantly considered the problem. For instance, an integer programming formulation combined with the construct, solve, merge & adapt (CMSA) was used in
[
14
]
, tabu search was used in
[
23
]
and
[
15
]
, and evolutionary algorithms and hybrid approaches combining with mixed integer program techniques were examined in
[
22
]
.

The development of happy colouring is motivated by the principle of
homophily

[
16
]
in social networks — a concept that is effectively embodied by the community structure of graphs
[
9
]
. A
community

[
5
]
in a graph is a group of vertices of sufficient size, within which the interconnections are markedly denser than the connections with vertices outside the group. This concept is illustrated in Figure
1
for a graph with 11 communities.

Nevertheless, conventional happy colouring (i.e.
\rho=1
) does not entirely partition the vertex set into happy colour classes in the case of connected graphs. This is primarily because in connected graphs, any colouring that employs more than one colour will inevitably include some unhappy vertices
[
14
]
. The problem is even more pronounced in dense and large graphs, where almost no happy vertices can be found
[
14
]
. However, in soft happy colouring the
proportion of happiness
,
0\leq\rho\leq 1
, merely pushed this limit and shown to have a strong relation with the community structure of graphs
[
20
]
. More importantly, colour classes of a
\rho
-happy colouring can represent the graph’s communities
[
19
]
. The colouring of the graph in Figure
1
demonstrates this relation as it is a complete 0.6-happy colouring whose colour classes are aligned with the communities of the graph.

Figure 1
:
A connected graph with 11 communities, which are grouped together. Grey lines are inter-community edges. The colouring here is aligned with the communities and is a complete 0.6-happy colouring. The objective of
community detection
is finding such a vertex partition.

Real-world networks frequently exhibit community structures. In social networks, for instance, distinct groups often form around common interests or affiliations, thereby facilitating efficient information exchange and collective behaviour
[
18
]
. In the realm of cybersecurity, network architectures often reveal modular divisions that can mirror coordinated clusters of activity
[
24
]
. Similarly, neural networks display community structure through the formation of functionally specialised regions within the brain
[
21
]
. Detecting communities also helps increase the robustness of networks through enhancing the intercommunity links
[
7
]
. These cross-disciplinary examples underscore that community structure is a fundamental attribute of complex systems. By examining evolutionary algorithms for soft happy colouring, it is possible to see whether they are useful in revealing the community structure of graphs.

The conventional type of evolutionary algorithms, namely
Genetic Algorithms
(GAs), utilises only the canonical genetic operators - selection, crossover, and mutation - to evolve a population of initial solutions over successive generations. While they usually offer robust global search capabilities, their convergence tends to be comparatively slow, primarily due to the random nature of their search process and lack of problem-specific refinement
[
4
]
.
Another type of evolutionary algorithms,
Memetic Algorithms
(MAs), have been devised to represent a powerful hybridisation of evolutionary search and local optimisation techniques, to enhance the convergence speed
[
17
]
. The approach of Memetic Algorithms has shown to be successful in diverse areas such as scheduling, routing, and bioinformatics
[
6
]
. We introduce GAs and MAs in Section
3
in details.

Algorithms for soft happy colouring consist of
Greedy-SoftMHV
and
Growth-SoftMHV
, which were introduced in
[
25
]
,
Local Maximal Colouring (LMC)
and
Neighbour Greedy Colouring (NGC)
from
[
20
]
, and three more heuristic-improvement algorithms
Local Search
(
LS
),
Repeated Local Search
(
RLS
), and
Enhanced Local Search
(
ELS
) introduced in
[
19
]
. Among these,
LMC
and
LS
(and sometimes
RLS
, when given a suitable input) proved to be fast and effective, and can be used in designing metaheuristics.

In this paper, we introduce Genetic and Memetic Algorithms for soft happy colouring. The initial populations can come from random or fast heuristic solutions such as
LMC
or
LS
. For Memetic Algorithms, we integrate local search heuristics of
LS
and
RLS
with the evolutionary process. The algorithms are tested for a large set of randomly generated graphs, which shows the outperformance of Memetic Algorithms. For the average ratio of
\rho
-happy vertices to the total number of vertices, the Memetic Algorithm that integrated
RLS
over a population generated by
LS
attained the first rank among the tested evolutionary algorithms. The highest average of community detection accuracy belongs to the algorithms that use
LMC
to generate their initial populations. We also show that our results are statistically significant using the Welch
t
-test.

In Section
2
, we introduce underpinning technical background, including theoretical insights into soft happy colouring. An overview of established heuristics and evolutionary methodology come in Section
3
, while Section
4
explores our experimental results and analysis. Section
5
concludes the paper by highlighting possibilities for future research.

2
Theory behind the problem

A graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
is a combinatorial object consisting of its vertex and edge sets
[
3
]
. For our theoretical purposes, we need our graphs modelled by a random graph model
[
1
]
. Thus, we suppose that our graphs are modelled by the Stochastic Block model (SBM)
[
12
,
13
]
, a random graph model tailored for community-structured graphs. Specifically, we use a simplified version of the SBM, denoted by
\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
, which is the probability space of
n
-vertex graphs with
k
nonoverlapping (i.e. vertex-disjoint) communities. The edge probability inside a community is
p
while an intercommunity edge has the probability of
q
. To have a meaningful and detectable community structure is guaranteed, we always suppose that
q
is smaller than
p
, for example
q<\frac{p}{2}
. Moreover, for simplicity and comparability to other available results in the literature, we assume that the size of communities is almost equal to
\frac{n}{k}
.

Assume that certain vertices of a graph
G
are precoloured with
k
colours (where
k\geq 2
), and let
\sigma
be a
k
-colouring that extends this partial colouring. In this context, a vertex
v
is termed
\rho
-happy
if at least
\lceil\rho\cdot\deg(v)\rceil
of its neighbours are assigned the colour
\sigma(v)
. A vertex colouring
\sigma
is defined as a
soft happy colouring
(with
k
colours) for
G
if it maximises the number of
\rho
-happy vertices among all such
k
-colouring extensions of the partial colouring.

Used notations in this paper follow notations of
[
19
,
20
]
. A vertex is
free
if it is not precoloured. When all vertices of
G
are
\rho
-happy by
\sigma
, we write
\sigma\in H_{\rho}
. The notation
H_{\rho}(G,\sigma)
or simply by
H_{\rho}(\sigma)
represents the number of
\rho
-happy vertices of a colouring
\sigma
of the graph
G
,
G\in H_{\rho}
means that communities of
G
induce a complete
\rho
-happy colouring, and for a vertex colouring
\sigma
,
ACD(\sigma)
is the ratio of vertices whose colours align with their community membership. The
ratio of
\rho
-happy vertices
in a colouring
\sigma
is

\alpha(\sigma)=\frac{H_{\rho}(\sigma)}{n}.

(2.1)

It is demonstrated in
[
20
]
that soft happy colouring is intimately connected to the community structure of graphs, showing that if
\rho
is sufficiently small, every vertex in a dense graph in the SBM can become
\rho
-happy. More precisely, suppose that
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
,
n=|V(G)|
,
2\leq k
,
0<q<p<1
,
0<\rho\leq 1
, and
0<\varepsilon<1
. It is proved that if
\rho
is less than a threshold,
then with a high probability the communities of
G
induce a
\rho
-happy colouring on
G

[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
. The threshold is

\xi=\max\left\{\min\left\{\mathrm{ln}\left(\frac{\frac{k}{n}\mathrm{ln}(\varepsilon)+pe+(k-1)q}{p+(k-1)q}\right),\;\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}\right\},\;0\right\}.

(2.2)

It is also established that this favourable outcome becomes more pronounced as the number of vertices increases. For a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
, it is stated in
[
20
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
19
, Theorem 2.1]
that the probability of communities induce a complete
\rho
-happy colouring approaches 1 when
n\rightarrow\infty
and

0\leq\rho<\tilde{\xi}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi=\frac{p}{p+(k-1)q}.

However, when
\rho>\tilde{\xi}
, it is very unlikely to find a complete
\rho
-happy colouring, and when
n\to\infty
, almost no
\rho
-happy vertex would be found
[
19
, Theorem 3.2]
.

When
\rho
is very small, any colouring with no relation to community structure can have a large proportion of
\rho
-happy vertices. For a graph in the SBM
G\in\mathcal{G}(n,k,p,q)
, the lower bound

\mu=\frac{q}{p+(k-1)q}

(2.3)

is stated for
\rho
to expect a complete
\rho
-happy colouring has high correlation with the community structure of
G

[
19
]
. When
0\leq\rho<\mu
, a vertex of one community can have the same colour as the vertices of other communities and still be
\rho
-happy. Consequently, realising the soft happiness condition for this case is easier, but the solution’s colour classes might not give a good quality community detection
[
19
]
.

For a graph in the SBM, the alignment with the original communities of the graph of a complete
\rho_{2}
-happy colouring is higher than that of a complete
\rho_{1}
-happy colouring when
\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}

[
19
]
. And when
\mu\leq\rho\leq\tilde{\xi}
, colour classes of a complete
\rho
-happy colouring can present a community structure for a graph in the SBM
[
19
]
.

Methodology

3.1
Known heuristics for soft happy colouring

In
[
25
]
two heuristics were introduced for soft happy colouring. One of them,
Greedy-SoftMHV
, gives only one colour to all the free vertices, the one with maximum
\rho
-happiness. The other,
Growth-SoftMHV
, splits the free vertices into some groups, and prioritises the search of vertices of one group over another.
NGC
is an extension to
Greedy-SoftMHV
, which is introduced in
[
20
]
. We use neither of these algorithms for designing the evolutionary algorithms.

Local Maximal Colouring (LMC)
is introduced in
[
20
]
and has linear time complexity (
\mathcal{O}(m)
, where
m
is the number of edges). Moreover, its output is highly correlated with the graph’s community structure. In the design of the
LMC
, there is no dependence on the proportion of happiness
\rho
, but some randomness is employed. In its main loop, the algorithm randomly chooses a vertex
v
from the intersection of uncoloured vertices and neighbours of already coloured vertices, and colours
v
with the colours that appear the most in
N(v)
.

are precoloured vertices whose colours, respectively red, green and blue, must not be changed. At a time, the vertex
v
is selected. Because it has two red, one green and one blue neighbours (as well as two uncoloured neighbours), the colour red has the maximum number. Therefore, the colour red is given to
v
.

Two other versions of local search algorithms were introduced in
[
19
]
, namely
Repeated Local Search (RLS)
and
Enhanced Local Search (ELS)
. The former is similar to
LS
, only refills the set
U
with
\rho
-unhappy vertices when checking vertices in
U

\rho
-happy vertices in the entire vertex set, any time it decides about a vertex colour.

Due to high time complexity,
ELS
produces poor results in a reasonable amount of time, and thus is not suitable for designing metaheuristics. On the other hand, although
RLS
can be much more time costly if its input is a partially coloured or a randomly coloured graph, it is useful if it inputs a solution by
Growth-SoftMHV
(which is very time costly on its own), or when it is run on a solution by
LS
. In the latter case, it can make a slight difference in a reasonable amount of time
[
19
]
. As a result, it can be useful for designing metaheuristic algorithms for soft happy colouring, especially when a solution by
LS
needs to be enhanced.

The results of the practical tests show that
LMC
and
LS
are very fast and effective heuristics whose randomness in selecting
\rho
-unhappy free vertices changes their output every time they are used
[
20
,
19
]
. Thus, it is natural to use them in the design of evolutionary algorithms, especially to create initial populations. Furthermore,
LS
is a fast and effective improvement algorithm, so it is an obvious choice for the enhancement needed in Memetic Algorithms
[
19
]
. Other improvement algorithms require significantly more time than
LS
, and their use instead of
LS
is not justified unless the initial population is made solely by
LS
. When
LS
is being used to form the initial population, it adds no enhancements if being run again, the case for which we use
RLS
in our Memetic Algorithms.

3.2
Evolutionary Algorithms

Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs)
[
10
]
are a subset of metaheuristic optimisation techniques inspired by the principles of natural selection and biological evolution. They are widely used to solve complex optimisation problems, particularly when traditional methods struggle due to non-linearity, a vast search space, or the high complexity of other existing methods. By mimicking the evolutionary processes found in nature, namely reproduction, mutation, and selection, EAs aim to evolve solutions toward optimal or near-optimal configurations over iterative cycles, termed generations. Due to the complex nature of soft happy colouring problems, it is interesting and useful to develop EAs for these problems to see how the solutions can evolve towards optimal solutions.

The working mechanism of EAs begins with the initialisation of a population comprising feasible solutions. Most EAs start with populations of random solutions, but having an initial population of feasible solutions with structures could be a game-changer. At each generation, individual solutions from the population undergo evaluation through
selection procedure
or a
fitness function
, which in our case is selecting colourings with the highest numbers (or ratios) of
\rho
-happy vertices. Operators of EAs, namely crossover (recombination) and mutation, introduce diversity by combining the genetic material of parent solutions and institute small randomly alterations, respectively. This stochastic variation ensures exploration of large parts of the solution space and helps to avoid premature convergence. Figure
4
presents a flowchart of EAs.

Key operations like crossover and mutation play pivotal roles in EAs. Crossover combines segments of genetic information from parent individuals to produce offspring that inherit traits from both, fostering the exploitation of existing high-quality solutions. In contrast, mutation introduces random changes to an individual’s genetic code, promoting exploration and maintaining diversity within the population. This balance between exploration and exploitation is essential for the efficacy of EAs, as it ensures both the thorough exploration of the search space and the refinement of promising areas.

Figure 4
:
This is flowchart of EAs. The improvement step (pale pink dashed-rectangle) is skipped in GAs.

EAs can have another step usually called improvement, shown in pink in Figure
4
. Memetic Algorithms (MAs) as an advanced class of EAs incorporate an improvement algorithm (usually a local search method) to enhance the optimisation process. They combine the global exploration capabilities of evolutionary strategies with the fine-tuning strength of local search techniques
[
10
]
. Here, EAs without improvement steps are just Genetic Algorithms (GAs).

The foundational concept of MAs draws inspiration from the idea of cultural evolution, where memes — units of cultural information — spread and adapt through a population. Within MAs, individuals undergo not only genetic operations, such as crossover and mutation, but also a local optimisation phase that improves their fitness before proceeding to the next generation. This dual mechanism effectively speeds up its convergence, making them particularly adept at solving complex, large-scale optimisation problems. It was shown previously that effective local search strategies can be devised for soft happy colouring
[
19
]
, hence warranting memetic approaches for tackling this problem.

We propose GA and MA for soft happy colouring. Each of these comes from three different types: (a) randomly generated initial population denoted by
Rnd

LMC

3.3
Memetic algorithm

As mentioned, MAs follow almost similar setting to GAs, the main difference is the involvement of an improvement algorithm that, at each step, enhances the solutions stored in the population. Since this improvement repeats several times during a run of the algorithm, it is very important to use a very fast and effective local search algorithm to allow time for effective evolution of the population. Based on the results of
[
19
]
, the improvement algorithm
LS
is chosen due to its low time-complexity, effectiveness in such a short time, employing some randomness in its procedure, and good experimental records
[
19
]
.

For generating the initial population of feasible colourings, again we use
Rnd
and
LMC
. There was no point in generating a population from outputs of
LS
if it gets improved by the same algorithm, and the result will be similar to GA’s when it starts with a population of outputs of
LS
. Therefore, in this case, we use
RLS
instead of
LS
for the local search component of the algorithm. Our proposed MA is presented in Algorithm
3

4
Experimental evaluation

In this section, we explain the extensive tests we run to compare our EAs for soft happy colouring. There are six such algorithms, namely
GA (Rnd)
,
GA (LMC)
,
GA (LS)
,
MA (Rnd)
,
MA (LMC)
, and
MA+RLS (LS)
. In the names of the tested algorithms, the name within parentheses denotes how the population is generated.

First in Section
4.1
, details about the instances and chosen parameters are given. We compare solutions generated by the algorithms for their number of
\rho
-happy vertices in Section
4.2
. As said, the main objective of the algorithms is to maximise the number of
\rho
-happy vertices. Meanwhile, because a complete
\rho
-happy colouring gives a vertex partition through its colour classes, which can be considered as the graph’s communities. A secondary objective of a soft happy colouring algorithm is to maximise
ACD(\sigma)
, which leads to a higher alignment of colour classes with the original community structure of the graph. We compare algorithms for this objective in Section
4.3
.

4.1
Instances and parameters

The EAs are tested for a set of 28,000 randomly generated partially coloured graphs in the SBM, as introduced in
[
20
, Section 5.2]
. The graphs are stored in DIMACS format and are publicly available
1
1

1

at
https://github.com/mhshekarriz/HappyColouring_SBM
.

The 28,000 graphs are defined for
200\leq n<3,000
vertices, 10 instances for each
n
. For each graph, other parameters are randomly chosen so that
k\in\{2,3,\ldots,20\}
,
p\in(0,1]
,
q\in(0,\frac{p}{2}]
, and
\rho\in(0,1]
.

In each graph, a subset of the vertices is precoloured. This was done during the generation of instances, so that the colour number for each precoloured vertex agrees with its community number; if two vertices of a community are precoloured, they cannot be of different colours. For each graph, a random number
pcc
is chosen from the set
\{1,2,\ldots,10\}
, and from each community,
pcc
number of vertices are precoloured. A study on how
pcc
can affect soft happy colouring or the accuracy of community detection of algorithms is given in
[
19
, Section 6.5]
.

To run an EV, we need to decide on two parameters: population size and mutation factor. Before the test, we ran the algorithms with different values of these parameters for a set of 1000 randomly generated precoloured graphs. The test shows acceptable performance of both GAs and MAs when the population size is around 20 and the mutation factor is around 0.005. We then fixed these parameters in all the tested EAs in order to have a smooth outcome comparable with each other and also with other methods.

All tests were given a time limit of 600 seconds. A lower time limit prevents the EAs from completing some (at least 3) iterations of their main loops.

5
Conclusion

We have implemented Genetic and Memetic Algorithms for soft happy colouring. We tested these algorithms for a large set of randomly generated precoloured graphs in the SBM by using populations generated by
LMC
,
LS
, or randomly generated populations. We gave 600 seconds to each of these variations and reported and analysed the algorithms’ performances in Section
4
. Our main objective was to find the maximum number of
\rho
-happy vertices in each of the instances, while secondly, it was desired to have a colouring that aligned with their original communities.

Among the algorithms we tested,
MA+RLS(LS)
demonstrated the highest average ratio of
\rho
-happy vertices, while
MA(LMC)
outperforms others when
\mu\leq\rho\leq\tilde{\xi}
. It also has the best average accuracy of community detection among these algorithms. On average,
GA(Rnd)
performed not as well as other algorithms. The difference between
MA(Rnd)
and it in each of the objectives is huge, reminding us of the unforgettable role of the improvement algorithm
LS
.

Our findings also verified the theoretical results of
[
20
,
19
]
. Furthermore, the heuristic / improvement algorithm
LS
performed quite quickly and effectively. Therefore, one may think of using it to design metaheuristics (and matheuristics) other than evolutionary ones.

As soft happy colouring relies on optimisation techniques, implementing a mixed-integer program can be one possible research direction for future considerations. It can be used find how far is a solution by a metaheuristic algorithm from an optimal solution.
