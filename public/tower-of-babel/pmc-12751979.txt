Influence of psychological capital on the research ability of master’s degree students in medical academic degrees: the mediating role of research investment and the moderating role of mentorship guidance
Authors: Zhiqiang Wang, Mengting Zhang, Jinzhong Jia, Jin Yu, Qingsong Yang, Jing Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch ability is a key indicator for assessing the quality of training and academic competitiveness among postgraduate students in medical academic programs. Psychological capital, as an important positive psychological resource, has not yet been systematically investigated in terms of its influence mechanism on research ability within the context of medical postgraduate education.MethodsThis study employed convenience sampling. Between June and July 2023, an online questionnaire survey was conducted among 3,892 medical academic master’s students from 70 Chinese universities through the Sojump online platform. Hierarchical regression and moderated mediation analyses were performed via SPSS 27.0 and the PROCESS macro (version 4.1).ResultsPsychological capital not only significantly and positively predicts the research ability of medical academic master’s students but also has an indirect effect via research investment. Moreover, mentorship guidance plays a dual moderating role in this mediation process: it strengthens the impact of psychological capital on research investment (β = 0.031, P < 0.05) and increases the efficiency of converting research investment into research ability (β = 0.053, P < 0.01).ConclusionsPsychological Capital and Mentorship Guidance play a crucial role in enhancing Research Investment and Research Ability among Master of Medical Studies students. This empirical study enriches the evidence base on the mechanisms through which positive psychological resources promote research competence, provides a foundation for strengthening graduate medical education through policies centered on Psychological Capital and Mentorship Guidance, and offers both theoretical insights and practical implications for optimizing the cultivation model of scientific research talent.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08305-1.Keywords: Psychological capital, Research investment, Mentorship guidance, Research ability, Moderated mediation effect

Research background

In the context of comprehensively advancing Chinese-style modernization and thoroughly implementing the Healthy China strategy, medical science and technology innovation has been prioritized for development. There is an urgent need for a large number of high-level medical research-oriented talents to provide critical support [1]. As an important new force within the innovation systems of research institutions and higher medical colleges and universities, the training quality of medical academic master’s students (hereinafter referred to as “masters of medical studies”) is directly linked to the innovation capacity of the national medical and health system and the talent reserve of the biomedical industry.The improvement of research ability has thus become a key aspect of medical postgraduate education reform and a crucial measure to promote the continuous development of the national medical and health care industry. The development of research competence among academic-track medical postgraduate students is influenced by a constellation of interrelated factors. As research in learning psychology advances, increasing attention has been directed toward the role of non-cognitive factors in shaping students’ research performance and holistic development. Psychological Capital—a core construct in positive psychology encompassing self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience—has been empirically demonstrated to enhance academic achievement among medical students [2], promote sustained Research Investment in scientific endeavors [3], and strengthen mental well-being [4]. However, compared to undergraduate populations, empirical investigations into Psychological Capital among academic-track Master of Medical Studies students remain limited.

In contrast to foundational research training at the undergraduate level, the education of academic-track medical postgraduates emphasizes the systematic acquisition of research skills under structured Mentorship Guidance, aiming to cultivate independent Research Ability. These students face more complex research demands and clearly defined career expectations, frequently encountering challenges related to research design, experimental implementation, and knowledge translation. This high-pressure research environment places heightened demands on their Psychological Capital. Sustained Research Investment is essential for the progressive development of Research Ability. Within this context, Mentorship Guidance may play a moderating role in the mediating pathway through which Psychological Capital influences Research Ability, thereby reflecting a Moderated Mediation Effect.

Therefore, this study focuses on the medical field and aims to elucidate the mechanism by which Psychological Capital affects the Research Ability of academic-track Master of Medical Studies students. Specifically, it examines the mediating role of Research Investment and the moderating role of Mentorship Guidance, with the goal of clarifying the internal dynamics among these key variables. The findings provide empirical evidence for understanding the psychological mechanisms underlying research competence in medical postgraduates and offer theoretical insights to support the optimization of national strategies for cultivating high-level innovative medical talents.

Literature review

The connotation and evaluation system of research ability

Research ability is widely recognized as a core objective in the training of Masters of Medical Studies and has garnered significant attention both domestically and internationally. The literature primarily categorizes the evaluation of research ability into three models: output-oriented, activity-oriented, and comprehensive. The output-oriented model conceptualizes research ability as an individual’s academic output, typically measured by indicators such as the number of published papers and citation frequency. This approach is commonly used in the evaluation of doctoral candidates and early-career faculty within higher medical education systems across Europe, America, and East Asia [5, 6]. However, some scholars argue that this model overlooks the dynamic development of methodological skills, critical thinking, and innovative potential during the early stages of training, thereby offering an incomplete assessment of the progression of research ability [7].

The activity-oriented model emphasizes the specific abilities and skills demonstrated at each stage of the research process, such as problem identification, literature retrieval, and data analysis. This evaluative framework focuses on the evolving nature of research capabilities within actual research experiences and is particularly effective in assessing participatory and process-oriented competencies [8]. Nevertheless, it faces challenges in terms of standardization in practical settings and may neglect overarching competencies essential for effective research practice [9].

The comprehensive model posits that research competence encompasses all the abilities required to conduct scientific inquiry, including domain-specific skills such as research design and statistical analysis, as well as general competencies such as teamwork and resilience under pressure. This model is especially relevant for groups with limited research experience, as it enables holistic identification of capability gaps and developmental needs [10]. Given that the development of research ability at the master’s level emphasizes the integration of specialized knowledge and general skills in practical contexts, a single evaluation model is insufficient to capture this complexity. Notably, cross-national variations exist in assessment approaches: Western developed countries, particularly in Europe and the United States, prioritize a combination of academic output and research process indicators, whereas China and other Asian nations are increasingly adopting multi-dimensional comprehensive evaluation frameworks [11]. The comprehensive model addresses the limitations of unidimensional approaches in international scholarship by integrating the strengths of both output- and activity-based perspectives—balancing attention to scholarly outcomes with emphasis on procedural competencies and broader personal as well as professional qualities. Therefore, this study adopts the comprehensive framework to assess the research ability of medical academic-degree master’s students, aiming to provide a scientifically grounded basis for understanding their dynamic capability development through a more holistic evaluative approach.

The impact of psychological capital and research investment on research ability

Research ability is developed and strengthened through the combined influence of environmental support and individual characteristics. At the individual level, psychological capital—an important representative of noncognitive factors—has recently emerged as a key variable influencing the academic and research performance of Masters of Medical Studies [12]. Psychological capital refers to the positive psychological resources individuals develop during their growth and adaptation processes. It is primarily composed of four core dimensions: self-efficacy, hope, resilience, and optimism. This construct demonstrates strong measurability, developability, and manageability. According to the Conservation of Resources theory, individuals are motivated to actively acquire, maintain, and utilize valuable resources. As a unique form of capital that extends beyond human and social capital, psychological capital plays a crucial role in enhancing postgraduate students’ academic performance. Individuals with high levels of psychological capital tend to demonstrate greater focus, proactively seek academic resources, critically evaluate research designs, and engage in effective teamwork and innovative behaviors. A substantial body of empirical evidence has consistently demonstrated a strong positive association between elevated levels of psychological capital and improved research ability and academic achievement among master of medical studies [13, 14].

In addition to its direct effects, psychological capital also contributes to the development of research ability through various indirect mechanisms, with research investment widely recognized as the most critical mediating pathway. Research investment reflects both the depth and breadth of a graduate student’s engagement in research activities and constitutes a central variable in the process of research ability formation. Graduate students with high levels of psychological capital tend to achieve sustained improvements in both research ability and outcomes through increased research investment [15]. For example, Wang et al. showed that psychological capital can enhance research ability by promoting greater research investment and fostering self-regulated learning behaviors [16]. Research conducted by Li et al. indicated that psychological capital can indirectly enhance postgraduate students’ research investment and overall research ability by strengthening academic self-efficacy [17]. Empirical findings from Norwegian universities further support this relationship, revealing a significant positive association between psychological capital and both research investment and research ability, with the latter partially mediating the relationship [18]. In conclusion, psychological capital not only significantly enhances postgraduate students’ levels of research investment but also serves as a key predictor of research ability and outcomes.

Moderating role of mentorship guidance

Compared with the student-centered, personalized guidance and structured feedback commonly practiced in graduate medical education in North America and Europe, mentorship in China tends to emphasize the authority of the mentor and the student’s sense of team affiliation [8]. Mentorship is widely recognized as a critical external environmental factor that supports the development of research ability among Masters of Medical Studies, encompassing evaluations of the mentor as an individual, the mentorship process, and the interpersonal dynamics between mentors and students. According to social exchange theory, students regulate their academic attitudes and behaviors on the basis of both their own efforts and accomplishments and the recognition and other returns they receive from their mentors, with the goal of maximizing personal gains. Therefore, mentorship not only enhances students’ psychological resources but also significantly promotes their adaptability, self-efficacy, and innovative behaviors.

Empirical research conducted by Deane et al. indicates that graduate students are more likely to engage in research and expand their academic networks when their mentors possess strong subject matter expertise, demonstrate a forward-looking academic vision, and uphold exemplary academic integrity [19]. Moreover, systematic guidance and regular feedback from mentors can significantly improve graduate students’ research efficiency and scholarly output, thereby playing a crucial role in the development of their academic competencies. Mahsood et al. highlighted in a retrospective study on the well-being of doctoral students that a positive teacher‒student relationship can significantly enhance students’ adaptability and creativity, acting as an “amplifier” in the transformation of research investment into research ability [20]. As a vital environmental support factor, mentorship not only directly facilitates the development of students’ research ability but also serves as a key catalyst and moderator in the pathway through which psychological capital is translated into research ability. The implementation of a student-centered mentorship model that encourages diverse feedback and provides psychological support reflects a prominent trend in contemporary international medical education reform [6].

Research objectives and hypotheses

Research on the psychological capital of medical students in China began at a relatively late stage and has focused predominantly on undergraduate populations. However, studies examining Master of Medical studies, which are at a critical stage of academic and research development, are lacking. In particular, the interaction pathways and underlying mechanisms linking psychological capital to research ability among this group have been insufficiently explored. Furthermore, prior research has inadequately addressed the environmental factors that influence the development of psychological capital, with limited attention given to the role of mentorship guidance as a key moderating variable. As a result, a comprehensive theoretical framework in this area has yet to be fully established [8].

This study addresses this research gap by integrating conservation of resources theory and social exchange theory, extending the ‘psychological input‒output’ framework and clarifying its mechanisms. It proposes and empirically tests an innovative dynamic interaction model that incorporates psychological capital, research investment, research ability, and mentorship guidance. The objective is to provide both theoretical insights and practical implications for improving the medical postgraduate training system and informing the precise formulation of educational policies.

Data source

This study collected data through the Sojump online platform between June and July 2023 via convenience sampling. Although convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of research findings, this study drew data from 70 higher education institutions, encompassing both independent medical colleges and comprehensive universities, thereby offering a broad and representative overview of postgraduate master’s education for medical academic-degree students in China. Participation in the survey was entirely voluntary. To ensure data authenticity and sample quality, a dual restriction mechanism based on the IP address and device identification was implemented to prevent duplicate submissions and invalid responses.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) participants must be currently enrolled students pursuing a master’s degree in a medical discipline accredited by the Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China; (2) they are willing to participate in the study and provide informed consent; and (3) they are able to independently and accurately complete the questionnaire. Following rigorous quality control procedures, a total of 3,892 valid responses were obtained, representing multiple disciplines, such as basic medicine, clinical medicine, public health, and pharmacy. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University (Approval Number: IRB00001052-20074). All participants provided written informed consent. The research strictly complied with the ethical guidelines and principles of data confidentiality as outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurement tools

This study employed standardized and structured questionnaires to assess the key variables. All the measurement scales were developed on the basis of well-established domestic and international research frameworks and were appropriately adapted to reflect the unique characteristics of the Master of Medical Studies population in China. Following a pilot test, the questionnaires demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity.

Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PCQ)

The Psychological Capital Questionnaire developed by Luthans et al. was used in this study. The Chinese version of the questionnaire was revised and validated by Zhang Kuo [21] and other scholars. It consists of four dimensions, self-efficacy, hope, optimism, and resilience, with a total of 26 items. Each item is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 indicates “strongly disagree” and 7 indicates “strongly agree.” A higher total score indicates a greater level of psychological capital among those with Master of Medical studies. In this study, the internal consistency reliability of the scale, as measured by Cronbach’s α, was 0.935, demonstrating excellent internal consistency.

Research Investment Scale (RIS)

The assessment of research investment was based on the Research Investment Scale developed by Liu Pingping et al. [22], which was adapted from the Work Engagement Scale originally proposed by Schaufeli et al. This measurement tool consists of three distinct dimensions: cognitive input, emotional input, and behavioral input within the context of research activities. The scale contains 10 items and uses a 5-point Likert-type response format ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree “) to 5 (“Strongly Agree”). A higher total score indicates a greater level of research investment among Masters of Medical Studies. In the sample population of this study, the scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.963.

Research ability scale (RCS)

The research ability assessment tool was systematically adapted from the established “Medical Workers’ Research Ability Scale” [23] in China. This adaptation incorporated recommendations from the Association of American Medical Colleges and Federation for Medical Education regarding the evaluation of postgraduate research competencies.The final version of the scale comprises two primary dimensions, i.e., research activity capabilities and general capabilities, encompassing a total of 12 specific items. A 5-point Likert scale was used (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a greater level of self-perceived research ability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this questionnaire was 0.981, indicating excellent internal consistency.The English version of the questionnaire utilized in this study is included as Supplementary Material.

Mentorship guidance scale (MGS)

The mentorship guidance scale was adapted from the medical postgraduate satisfaction scale developed by Zhang Chi’s team [24] and was specifically designed to align with the policy requirements of the mentor responsibility system. The scale consists of three dimensions, namely, personal evaluation of the mentor, evaluation of guidance quality, and mentor‒student interaction, encompassing a total of 16 items. A 5-point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 (“Extremely Dissatisfied”) to 5 (“Extremely Satisfied”), with higher scores indicating a greater level of perceived mentorship quality among Masters of Medical Studies. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient for the scale was 0.991, indicating excellent internal consistency.The English version of the questionnaire utilized in this study is included as Supplementary Material.

Demographic and academic variables

The participants’ basic demographic and academic background information—including gender, age, experience with college/university transfer, major change, and supervisor type—was collected to control for potential confounding variables.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted via IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 and the PROCESS macro (Version 4.1). Descriptive statistics were calculated for the primary variables as well as for the participants’ demographic and academic characteristics. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, whereas categorical variables were described in terms of frequencies and percentages. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine the relationships among the key variables. Hierarchical regression analysis and PROCESS macro Model 4 were utilized to investigate the mediating role of Research Investment in the association between Psychological Capital and Research Ability. The bootstrap method, which is based on 5,000 resamples and a 95% confidence interval, was applied to test the mediation effect. A mediation effect was considered statistically significant if the confidence interval did not contain zero. To assess the moderated mediation effect, the PROCESS macro Model 59 was employed to analyze the conditional indirect effects of the moderator variable at different levels. Furthermore, the Harman single-factor test was performed to address potential common method bias. All the statistical tests were two-tailed, with the significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Basic characteristics of the sample

Among the total participants, 32.3% (n = 1,256) were male, and 67.7% (n = 2,636) were female. With respect to college transfer, 43.2% (n = 1,680) of the students experienced such a transition, whereas 56.8% (n = 2,212) did not. Concerning major reallocation, 39.3% (n = 1,529) of the participants had undergone this change, whereas 60.7% (n = 2,363) had not. Furthermore, 43.1% (n = 1,679) were supervised by doctoral advisors, and 56.9% (n = 2,213) received supervision from master’s advisors.

Common method variance test

As the primary variables in this study were collected through self-reported responses from postgraduate students, there is a potential risk of artificial covariation between the independent and dependent variables. This risk arises from the homogeneity of the participant group and the uniformity of the testing conditions. To assess the presence of common method bias, the Harman single-factor test was performed, which involved an exploratory factor analysis including all measurement items. The results revealed six factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1. The variance explained by the first factor was 26.69%, which is below the critical threshold of 40%. This suggests that the data are not significantly affected by common method bias.

Mediation effect test

Testing the moderated mediation model

Using the moderated mediation model (Model 59) proposed by Hayes in the SPSS PROCESS plugin, this study investigated the moderating role of mentoring guidance in the mediating pathway “Psychological Capital → Research Investment → Research Ability” (Table 5). After controlling for key demographic and academic variables, the results indicated that, in the first path, the interaction term between Psychological Capital and Mentorship Guidance had a statistically significant positive effect on Research Investment (β = 0.031, P < 0.05), suggesting that Mentorship Guidance enhances the positive influence of Psychological Capital on Research Investment, thereby supporting Hypothesis H3. Further analysis revealed that the interaction term between Research Investment and Mentorship Guidance also significantly and positively predicted Research Ability (β = 0.053, P < 0.01), indicating that Mentorship Guidance strengthens the effect of Research Investment on Research Ability, thus confirming Hypothesis H4. The adjusted R² values for the two moderation models were 0.414 and 0.479, respectively, with both F values being statistically significant (344.388 and 474.777, respectively, P < 0.001), demonstrating a good overall model fit. Importantly, the direct effect of the interaction between Psychological Capital and Mentorship Guidance on Research Ability was not significant (β = 0.002, P > 0.05), suggesting that Mentorship Guidance does not moderate the direct relationship between Psychological Capital and Research Ability; therefore, Hypothesis H5 was not supported.

Simple slope plot of the moderating effect

To more intuitively illustrate the moderating effect among variables, simple slope graphs (Figs. 2 and 3) were constructed to depict how variations in the level of mentoring guidance influence the relationships between variables. The results indicate that when Mentorship Guidance is at a higher level (M + 1SD), the positive predictive effect of Psychological Capital on Research Investment is significantly stronger. Similarly, the facilitating effect of Research Investment on Research Ability is notably greater under high levels of Mentorship Guidance than under low levels. These findings further confirm that Mentorship Guidance exerts a dual moderating effect: it enhances the conversion efficiency of Psychological Capital into Research Investment and strengthens the influence of Research Investment on Research Ability. In other words, mentorship guidance significantly moderates both stages of the indirect pathway through which psychological capital influences research ability via research investment. As the level of mentoring guidance increases, the mediating role of research investment becomes more pronounced, thereby supporting the moderated mediation model proposed in this study.

Discussion

Building on the dual theoretical foundations of Conservation of Resources Theory and Social Exchange Theory, this study systematically investigates the mechanisms by which Psychological Capital, Research Investment, and Mentorship Guidance influence the Research Ability of Masters of Medical Studies through empirical analysis. The key findings are as follows:

First, Psychological Capital has a significant direct positive effect on the Research Ability of Masters of Medical Studies, indicating that Psychological Capital can be effectively translated into research competence. This result aligns with Mao’s finding in a study of medical graduate students that “psychological capital was positively correlated with academic success and adaptability” [25]. From an internal structural perspective, the dimensions of Psychological Capital exhibit notable variation: the hope dimension scores higher, suggesting that medical graduate students maintain positive expectations regarding research goals; in contrast, the resilience dimension is relatively weaker, reflecting a limited capacity to cope with stressful situations such as experimental failure and manuscript rejections. Therefore, the training of medical graduate students should prioritize resilience development to enhance their ability to cope with research challenges and support long-term professional growth, thereby generating greater marginal benefits. Moreover, Psychological resources can significantly influence the application of academic achievements and performance across diverse industries [26]. Compared with related studies in the engineering field [14], the high-pressure and high-failure-rate nature of medical research may further amplify the role of Psychological Capital in this population, implying that the mechanisms through which Psychological Capital influences outcomes may vary across disciplinary contexts.

Second, research investment serves as a key mediator between psychological capital and research ability. Liu also observed in a model of graduate research participation that psychological capital can enhance research investment, which in turn influences research performance [27]. Specifically, psychological capital contributes to research ability by promoting research investment. Notably, current behavioral investment is relatively strong, whereas emotional investment in terms of internal identification still has considerable room for improvement. A 2023 empirical study on Norwegian higher education found that even when individuals lack initial interest in the research field, high levels of psychological capital can promote sustained research investment [28]. Clearly, in contexts marked by insufficient intrinsic motivation, psychological capital can serve as a compensatory psychological resource supporting students’ research investment. Therefore, future efforts to optimize the structure of research investment should focus on enhancing students’ emotional identification simultaneously, rather than solely emphasizing task completion, in order to establish a more balanced and sustainable investment framework.

Additionally, mentoring guidance has a dual moderating effect characterized as “front-end enhancement and back-end reinforcement” within the Psychological Capital–Research Investment–Research Ability mediating chain. At the front end, mentorship guidance significantly amplifies the effect of psychological capital on research investment, with students receiving high levels of mentorship showing a stronger conversion effect than those receiving low mentorship [20]. At the back end, mentorship further enhances the efficiency of converting Research Investment into Research Ability through mechanisms such as manuscript revision and research feedback, with higher conversion rates observed in the high-mentorship group. It has been indicated that in higher education teaching, mentorship guidance and other environmental support resources can serve as supplementary factors [29]. Furthermore, mentorship guidance does not significantly moderate the direct relationship between psychological capital and research ability; instead, its influence primarily operates through optimizing the mediating mechanisms rather than altering the core effect of psychological capital. This finding supports the perspective of social exchange theory (SET), which states that environmental support facilitates positive cycles by reinforcing resource conversion pathways [30].

Recommendations

On the basis of the aforementioned findings, the following recommendations are proposed in alignment with Psychological Capital intervention strategies:

Firstly, at the individual level, medical master’s students should strengthen their awareness of autonomous psychological capital development and formulate scientific and structured enhancement plans. To address the relatively weak resilience dimension, institutions should implement systematic training in failure case reviews and stress scenario simulations, incorporating cognitive restructuring techniques to enhance psychological resilience.Secondly, at the supervisory level, differentiated guidance strategies should be established. A routine communication mechanism should be developed that integrates weekly academic progress reporting with ongoing psychological state assessments, supplemented by timely stress-relief support when needed. Furthermore, at the policy level, the multi-dimensional quality assurance system should be strengthened by integrating psychological capital development into postgraduate training quality evaluation indicators. The supervisor support and training system should be enhanced by including psychological counseling competence in supervisor selection criteria and instituting a quarterly evaluation mechanism for supervisor-mentee relationship quality.

Limitations and future perspectives

This study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the capacity to infer causal relationships among the variables. Although significant correlations were observed between Psychological Capital, Research Investment, Research Ability, and Mentorship Guidance, the directionality of these relationships cannot be determined on the basis of the current design. Future research should adopt longitudinal methodologies to explore the temporal development and dynamic interactions of these variables throughout the graduate training process. Moreover, although the sample included Masters of Medical Studies from various institutional levels and medical disciplines, it may not be fully representative of the broader population of Masters of Medical Studies at the national or international level. Therefore, future research should involve further validation of this model through application in medical projects across diverse countries and regions, thereby enhancing the universality and applicability of the findings.

Conclusions

This study utilizes a moderated mediation model to elucidate the underlying mechanism through which psychological capital influences research ability via research investment, and clarifies the moderating role of mentorship guidance. The findings not only extend the integrative theoretical framework of Conservation of Resources Theory and Social Exchange Theory—validating psychological capital as a critical individual psychological resource—but also provide practical and actionable intervention pathways for the development of Master of Medical Studies students across three domains: individual learners, supervisory practices, and policy support. These results hold substantial reference value and practical significance in mitigating research burnout within medical education, enhancing medical research innovation capacity, and improving the quality of mentor–student relationships. In conclusion, strengthening psychological capital development and optimizing mentorship guidance constitute key strategies for improving research training quality among Medical Studies students and ensuring their effective contribution to health-related technological innovation.
