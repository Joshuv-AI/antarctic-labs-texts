Towards the β \beta function of SU ⁡ ( 2 ) \mathrm{SU}(2) with adjoint matter using Pauli–Villars fields
Biagio Lucini

Abstract
The family of SU(2) theories with matter transforming in the adjoint representation has attracted interest from many angles. The two-flavour theory, known as Minimal Walking Technicolor, has a body of evidence pointing to it being in the conformal window with anomalous dimension γ ∗ ≈ 0.3 \gamma_{*}\approx 0.3 . Perturbative calculations would suggest that the one-flavour theory should be confining and chirally broken; however, lattice studies of the theory have been inconclusive. In this contribution we present a first look at efforts towards the computation of the beta function of these theories using the gradient flow methodology. Following an exploration of the phase diagram of the two theories with Wilson fermions and additional Pauli–Villars fields, we tune the bare fermion mass to near the chiral limit, and subsequently generate ensembles at five lattice volumes and a range of lattice spacings.

Introduction

In the last two decades
the
\mathrm{SU}(2)
gauge theories with various numbers
N_{\textnormal{f}}
of adjoint fermion flavours
have been studied extensively on the lattice.
This family of theories is interesting in a number of contexts:
the
N_{\textnormal{f}}=2
theory, for example,
provides the minimal possible realisation of Walking Technicolor.
Numerous studies have placed it in the conformal window;
recent work
[
1
]
shows
a mass anomalous dimension of 0.304(4) at finite lattice spacing
from the scaling of the Dirac mode number,
while earlier step-scaling studies
[
2
]
indicate
an anomalous dimension of
0.263(4){}^{+0.012}_{-0.015}
.
The
N_{\textnormal{f}}=1
theory cannot by itself break electroweak symmetry,
although it can be extended in such a direction in theories such as Ultra Minimal Walking Technicolor,
and is interesting in the context of being able to
identify the lower end of the conformal window,
and to observe near-conformal behaviour.
It is also relevant to studies of topological phase transitions
via ’t Hooft anomaly matching.
Recent work
[
1
]
finds
a relatively small continuum limit mass anomalous dimension of 0.170(7)
by extrapolating values computed using the mode number at finite lattice spacing.

The fixed point in the
N_{\textnormal{f}}=2
theory,
and any possible remnant of a fixed point in the
N_{\textnormal{f}}=1
theory,
can only be found at relatively strong coupling.
Such couplings are challenging to reach on the lattice,
as in this regime the lattice spacing becomes large,
and a bulk phase transition can mean that results are not connected to the continuum limit.
Recent work
[
3
,
4
]

has suggested that introducing Pauli–Villars (PV) fields into the action
may reduce lattice artefacts at strong coupling
and hence widen the range of couplings at which a theory may be studied.

2
Summary of the theory

We generate ensembles using the Wilson gauge action

S_{\mathrm{G}}=\beta_{0}\sum_{p}\Tr\left[1-\frac{1}{2}U(p)\right]

and Wilson fermion action,

S_{\mathrm{F}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\textnormal{f}}}\overline{\psi}_{\alpha}(x)(i\not{D}-m)\psi_{\alpha}(x)
,
where
N_{\textnormal{f}}\in\{1,2\}
,
as in our previous work
[
1
]
.
To this,
we add
N_{\textnormal{PV}}
species of PV field each with mass
m_{\textnormal{PV}}
,
implemented as a Hasenbusch mass term
with a very heavy mass
in the denominator.

While previous work
[
3
,
4
]

has smeared these PV fields,
in this work we use unsmeared fields.
Theoretically,
since this has the effect of adding a single plaquette contribution to the action,
this would be expected to have no effect on the lattice artefacts,
leading mainly to a shift in the value of
\beta_{0}
.
In this work we will test that prediction.

We make use of the RHMC and HMC algorithms respectively for
N_{\textnormal{f}}=1
and
2
.
The HiRep
[
5
,
6
]
code is used in both cases,
and is run both on CPU
(for the phase diagram)
and on A100 GPUs
(for larger volumes).
GNU Parallel
[
7
]

is used to manage large swarms of small jobs.
Meson correlation function and gradient flow histories
are computed using HiRep on CPUs.

4
Computing the running coupling and
\beta
function

In this section we will summarise the methodology of computing
the running coupling and
\beta
function
described by the authors of Ref.
[
4
]
,
to which we refer for further detail.

We may evolve our field configurations under the diffusion equation
defined by the gradient of the Wilson action
(the Wilson flow),
as described in Ref.
[
9
]
.
The running coupling at finite volume and lattice spacing
can be computed as a function of the flow time
t
as

g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2}(t;L,g_{0}^{2})=\langle t^{2}E(t)\rangle
,
where the energy density
E
may be computed on the lattice via a variety of operators;
in this work we consider the plaquette and clover operators.
From this,
the
\beta
function may be computed as

\beta(t;L,g_{0^{2}})=t\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2}(t;L,g_{0}^{2})
,
where we may fit subsets of the volumes considered,
and compute the fit results’ mean weighted using a modified
Akaike Information Criteria.

Both
g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2}
and
\beta
may be extrapolated to the infinite-volume limit
using a linear fit form in the reciprocal of the lattice volume

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2}(t;L,g_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2}(t;g_{0}^{2})+C_{g^{2}}L^{-4}\;,

\displaystyle\beta(t;L,g_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle=\beta(t;g_{0}^{2})+C_{\beta}L^{-4}\;.

(1)

These may then be interpolated against each other to obtain
the
\beta
function at arbitrary
g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2}
,
using

\beta^{\mathrm{int}}(t,g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2})=g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{4}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2n}\;,

(2)

where
N
is fixed to the lowest value that gives a reasonable fit.
This allows the
\beta
function to be scanned across a range of running couplings,
and at each of these a continuum limit may be taken by extrapolating the linear Ansatz

\beta^{\mathrm{int}}(t,g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2})=\beta^{\mathrm{cont}}(g_{\mathrm{GF}}^{2})+\frac{C_{\mathrm{cont}}}{t}\;.

(3)

Analysis workflow

The data analysis pipeline is written using Snakemake
[
10
]
,
connecting individual tools each of which makes use of pyerrors
[
11

.
This allows simple rules
(for example,
how to transform one or more input data files
to an output containing one or more statistical quantities)
to be easily composed,
independent steps to be parallelised,
and redundant computations to be skipped.
To generate every plot in this contribution,
excluding Fig.
4

starting from the raw output files transferred from the HPC resources used to generate them,
requires the single command:

snakemake --cores 6 --use-conda

be run twice,
once per
N_{\textnormal{f}}

This then launches 344 (for
N_{\textnormal{f}}=1
) + 81 (for
N_{\textnormal{f}}=2
) job steps,
taking 5 minutes to run on six cores of an Apple M1 Pro CPU.
All required software packages are installed automatically;
no setup is required beyond installing Snakemake.

7
Conclusions

We have computed the gradient flow beta function
for
\mathrm{SU}(2)
with
N_{\textnormal{f}}=2
flavours of adjoint Dirac fermion,
with the addition of
N_{\textnormal{PV}}=5
, 10, and 15 unsmeared PV fields.
We have also studied the parameter space of the equivalent
N_{\textnormal{f}}=1
theory.
Unlike results seen in work performed with smeared PV fields
[
4
]
,
we do not see a substantial change in the phase diagram
or the range of running coupling that may be observed
as
N_{\textnormal{PV}}
is increased,
instead only seeing a constant shift in the phase diagram as a function of the bare coupling;
this is consistent with theoretical expectations.

We now aim to repeat this work with smeared PV fields,
such that we may compute the
\beta
function at stronger coupling,
including observing the fixed point in the
N_{\textnormal{f}}=2
case,
and any remnant of a fixed point in the
N_{\textnormal{f}}=1
case.
