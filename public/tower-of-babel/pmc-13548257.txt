ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective medical record management is crucial for timely access to patient information and quality care. Issues like loss, misplacement and delays in retrieval lead to longer wait times and reduced staff efficiency yet these operational challenges are often neglected in healthcare improvement efforts.Aim of the projectThis project seeks to improve medical record management at Wallaga University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, increasing the baseline from 35% to 90% between 15 September and 10 December 2025.MethodsA quality improvement approach was implemented using sequential Planplan--Dodo--Studystudy--Aact (PDSA cycles. Key interventions included staff training on filing and tracking, numerical reorganisation of folders, separation of active/inactive files, a digital tracking system, assignment of folder responsibilities, privacy measures and routine audits with feedback. Effectiveness was evaluated using quantitative indicators (retrieval rates, lost folder rates, waiting times, satisfaction surveys) and qualitative feedback from stakeholders.ResultsPerformance improved significantly from 41% to 95% over seven PDSA cycles. Lost folders decreased from 13% to 2%, delayed retrieval dropped from 25% to 3% and timely folder provision rose from 72% to 95%. Patient satisfaction increased from 40% to 90%, while healthcare provider satisfaction grew from 60% to 85%. Qualitative feedback highlighted reduced conflicts, improved workflow, enhanced accountability and fewer duplicate records.ConclusionThe structured quality improvement intervention effectively enhanced medical record management, showcasing the efficacy of PDSA cycles combined with practical interventions. Sustained improvements are anticipated with ongoing monitoring and staff engagement. This model can be adapted by other hospitals facing similar challenges, contributing to more efficient, patient-centred healthcare delivery.Keywords: Quality improvement, PDSA, Management

Introduction

Problem description and impact

Inefficient medical record management can negatively affect patient safety and healthcare outcomes by delaying access to previous clinical information, increasing the risk of incomplete clinical decision-making, unnecessary diagnostic repetition and medication-related errors. Poor record availability also compromises continuity and quality of care, particularly for returning patients who require previous medical history, treatment plans and follow-up information.1 2

From an operational perspective, ineffective medical record systems increase waiting times, consume healthcare workers’ time through prolonged searching and replacement of missing folders and reduce service efficiency. For healthcare organisations, inadequate medical record management affects data quality, reporting accuracy, clinical governance, accreditation readiness and achievement of institutional performance goals.3 Therefore, improving medical record management was identified as a critical system-level priority at Wallaga University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (WUCSH).4

Available knowledge

Medical records are documents that explain all details about the patient’s history, clinical findings, diagnostic test results, preoperative and postoperative care, patient’s progress and medications.5 Medical records are kept in the interest of both the patient and clinician. Proper filing of patient’s medical records ensures easy retrieval and contributes to decreased patient waiting time at the hospital and continuity of care.6

Medical record management in the medical record room refers to the organised process of receiving, arranging, storing, retrieving, protecting and updating patient medical records to ensure accuracy, confidentiality and timely availability for patient care and administrative use.1 7–9

According to a recent study, the absence of medical records has a detrimental impact on the timely and effective delivery of healthcare services. This deficiency often leads to increased wait times for patients seeking treatment and, in some instances, results in healthcare providers treating patients without access to their medical histories. The research emphasises that missing files significantly delay patient assistance, which impedes nurses and doctors in delivering prompt care.10

A well-structured medical record filing system, supported by proper training, logistics and consistent monitoring and supervision, reduces the likelihood of issuing multiple folders and prevents misfiling. Hybrid systems can further minimise the risk of lost or misplaced files through better tracking and indexing, which is crucial for maintaining accurate patient records. Additionally, managing loose paperwork and merging temporary notes with original records leads to a more organised and streamlined record-keeping process. This combination of improved tracking and organisation not only enhances the reliability of patient information but also facilitates easier access for healthcare professionals when they need to retrieve important data.6 11 12

First, a previous medical record completeness rate study conducted in the same area showed an improvement from 53% to 80%. However, many medical record quality studies do not explicitly measure rates of folder loss, redundancy or misfiling. Instead, they tend to focus on aspects such as completeness, legibility and consistency, including quality improvements implemented at WUCH in 2023.13

Rationale

This highlights a significant gap: while many audits emphasise the quality of documentation, they often overlook the tracking of losses within the record room. When institutions implement structured interventions—such as a defined handover system, regular audits and an appropriate tracking system—both the completeness of records and issues related to record room management improve significantly.

The ramifications of poor medical record management extend to various stakeholders, including patients who face delays in treatment and potential errors in care; healthcare providers who experience frustration and inefficiencies in accessing patient information; administrative staff who struggle with insurance processing and record retrieval; regulatory bodies concerned about compliance with operational standards and the community, which suffers from decreased trust and satisfaction with hospital services. This study aims to address this gap by emphasising the need for comprehensive tracking alongside quality assessments.

Methods

Context

WUCSH is a teaching facility that offers both academic programmes and healthcare services. It is situated in the western region of Ethiopia, approximately 327 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city. Each month, the hospital serves around 20 232 patients, with 56% being returning patients who possess a medical record number and 44% being new patients.

Patient inquiries vary throughout the week, peaking on Mondays and declining on Fridays. During nights and weekends, only emergency cases are handled, highlighting the necessity for efficient resource allocation during busy periods.

The medical record room (MRR) is staffed by 18 employees, along with six additional pooled workers, which include two runners, two cashiers and two credit service workers.

Before the intervention, WUCSH primarily used a paper-based medical record system. Although some hospital functions had partial digitalisation, including patient registration and selected administrative processes, clinical documentation and medical folder storage remained paper-based. Full implementation of an electronic health record (EHR) system had not yet been achieved due to limitations related to infrastructure readiness, financial requirements, system implementation capacity and institutional digital transformation stage.

However, the intervention introduced a simple digital tracking approach to improve medical folder identification, monitoring and retrieval while preparing the foundation for future EHR adoption. EHRs could address several root causes of record loss by enabling unique patient identification, electronic storage, controlled access, automated tracking and real-time availability of clinical information.

Project team composition

The improvement initiative was implemented by a multidisciplinary team representing both administrative and clinical services. The team included one Information technology professional, one quality improvement team lead, two nurses from the outpatient department, two nurses representing inpatient and emergency services, one midwifery representative, the chief executive director and four senior physicians from different clinical disciplines.

The project team was selected based on direct involvement in medical record generation, utilisation, management and quality improvement activities. Members included representatives from clinical services, nursing, health information management, information technology, quality improvement and hospital leadership.

Although departments such as radiology, laboratory, pharmacy and administration were not permanent members of the core improvement team, their roles were considered during intervention planning because the project primarily focused on medical folder management and retrieval processes. These departments were indirect users of patient records and were engaged through consultation and feedback mechanisms when required.

The simple digital record referred to a basic electronic tracking system developed to support medical folder management rather than a complete EHR system. The system included patient identification information, folder location status, movement tracking and retrieval monitoring.

It supported record retrieval by allowing staff to identify whether a folder was available, misplaced, transferred or returned to storage. The system improved accountability and reduced time spent searching for missing records.

Measurement strategy and evaluation of the improvement intervention

To evaluate the effectiveness of the interventions, different study methods were used before and after the intervention. A standardised medical record management checklist was applied to measure how well staff followed the improved folder handling procedures. In addition, direct physical observation of the medical record room was conducted to assess workflow, organisation and the arrangement of patient folders before and after changes were made. Qualitative feedback was also gathered from medical record staff, clinicians and patients to understand their experience with folder retrieval time, lost folders and overall service satisfaction.

Balancing measures

Balancing measures were included to identify unintended consequences of the intervention. While improving record retrieval, it was important to ensure that the intervention did not create additional workload, reduce staff efficiency, compromise confidentiality or increase documentation burden.

The balancing measures included staff workload perception, compliance with confidentiality requirements and time required for maintaining the tracking system.

Analysis

The effectiveness of the interventions was analysed using both quantitative and qualitative approaches. Quantitative data were assessed using run charts (figure 2) and percentage calculations to compare changes in timely folder retrieval, folder loss and patient waiting time before and after the intervention. A standardised medical record management checklist also provided measurable scores on staff compliance with improved filing procedures. Direct physical observation of the medical record room supported these measurements by documenting improvements in workflow and filing organisation.

Run chart diagram for improving medical record management performance, WUCSH, 2025. WUCSH, Wallaga University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Qualitative data from healthcare workers’ feedback and observations were analysed using thematic analysis. The process involved familiarisation with the data, initial coding, grouping similar codes, development of themes, review and refinement of themes and interpretation of findings.

Two members of the improvement team independently reviewed the data and discussed differences until consensus was reached. Manual coding was used because of the relatively small volume of qualitative data.

Ethical consideration

This study was conducted as a quality improvement project aimed at strengthening medical record management and improving folder retrieval processes. No experimental interventions were performed, and no identifiable patient data were collected or used beyond routine administrative information. According to institutional policy at WUCSH, quality improvement activities that focus on enhancing existing service processes do not require formal ethical committee approval. Administrative permission was obtained from hospital leadership prior to initiating the project.

Results

The cumulative results from the PDSA cycles show significant improvement in medical record management, meeting the predefined assessment criteria, increased from 41% at baseline to 95% following implementation of the quality improvement interventions. The proportion of medical records meeting the predefined quality standards increased steadily during the implementation period, improving from 41% at baseline to 95% following completion of the quality improvement intervention: 41% in cycle 1, 47% in cycle 2, 53% in cycle 3, 67% in cycle 4, 85% in cycle 5, 87% in cycle 6 and reaching 95% by cycle 7 (figure 2)

Before intervention, 13% of patient’s folders received new cards after long searching and confirm lost. 25% patients stay long to get their folders even they get it. Only 72% repeat patients get their folders within time frame.

Following implementation of the interventions, substantial improvements were observed in medical record management. The proportion of patients whose folders were lost and who were subsequently issued new folders decreased to 2%. Similarly, the proportion of patients who experienced prolonged waiting times while their folders were being searched decreased to 3%, representing a 22 percentage-point reduction from baseline. Qualitative findings further supported these improvements. Prior to the intervention, card room workers reported significant challenges in managing patient folders due to the absence of clear procedures, frequent folder misplacement and recurrent conflicts with patients and healthcare providers. Hospital management also identified lost folders as one of the most common complaints received from patients. Patients frequently expressed dissatisfaction because their folders could not be located promptly, often resulting in delays in service and disagreements with medical record staff. Healthcare providers reported that patients were sometimes issued new cards despite having existing records and that some providers kept patient folders in their offices to prevent them from being lost. Following implementation of the interventions, participants reported improved organisation, reduced folder loss, smoother workflow and fewer complaints from patients and staff.

After the intervention, card room staff reported that work processes became simpler, conflicts significantly decreased and the work environment improved. Top management observed a noticeable reduction in patient complaints regarding lost folders. Patients expressed improved satisfaction, stating that folder retrieval was faster than before. Healthcare workers also reported a reduction in duplicate cards and easier access to patient folders, which improved clinical workflow.

Discussion

Overall medical record management performance, calculated as the percentage of reviewed medical records meeting the predefined quality standards, increased from 41% at baseline to 95% after implementation of the quality improvement interventions. The proportion of lost folders decreased from 13% to 2%, while delayed folder retrieval dropped from 25% to 3%, indicating substantial improvement in operational efficiency. Additionally, timely folder provision increased from 72% to 95%. Satisfaction outcomes also improved, with patient satisfaction rising from 40% to 90% and healthcare provider satisfaction increasing from 60% to 85%. These results were further supported by qualitative findings, which revealed reduced conflicts, improved workflow and a noticeable decline in complaints from patients and clinical staff.

The findings suggest that the systematic application of PDSA cycles is effective in addressing operational gaps in medical record management. The dramatic reduction in lost folders and retrieval delays indicates that the problem was primarily due to weak organisational structures rather than resource shortages. Improved satisfaction levels imply that streamlined processes reduce stress for staff and enhance patients’ confidence in receiving timely care. By establishing clearer procedures and improving accountability, the intervention successfully tackled a persistent bottleneck within the facility. Overall, these results demonstrate that structured quality improvement approaches can significantly strengthen service delivery using simple, practical solutions.

The improvement observed in this study is consistent with existing evidence demonstrating that quality improvement approaches, particularly PDSA cycles, can effectively strengthen healthcare processes and service delivery. Previous studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries have reported improvements in medical record availability, reduced patient waiting times and enhanced workflow efficiency following the introduction of standardised record management procedures, staff training and monitoring systems.1 14 15

Similarly, guidance from the WHO emphasises that effective health information management depends on standardised procedures, accountability mechanisms and continuous quality monitoring to ensure timely access to patient information and support clinical decision-making.1 16

The substantial improvement observed in this study, from 41% to 95% compliance with medical record management standards, exceeds improvements reported in some comparable interventions and may be attributable to the combined effect of staff engagement, leadership commitment, routine supervision and the introduction of a simple digital tracking system. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of infrastructure and technological investment for improving record management; however, our findings suggest that significant gains can also be achieved through low-cost organisational interventions that strengthen existing processes and clarify responsibilities.17 18 Furthermore, the observed improvements in patient and provider satisfaction are consistent with literature demonstrating that reliable access to medical records contributes to continuity of care, improved communication among healthcare professionals and greater patient confidence in healthcare services.19–21 These findings support the view that strengthening medical record management is not only an administrative activity but also an important component of patient safety, quality of care and health system performance.1 17 19 21

This study has several strengths, including the use of both quantitative and qualitative data, which allowed for a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of the intervention. Stakeholder involvement—ranging from patients to record staff and management—helped create strong ownership and improved the likelihood of sustaining the improvements. Additionally, the intervention relied on practical, low-cost strategies, making it easily adaptable to similar healthcare settings. However, the study also has limitations. It was conducted in a single facility, which limits generalisability to other hospitals. The follow-up period was relatively short, making it difficult to assess the long-term sustainability of the improvements. Furthermore, there is potential for response bias in satisfaction surveys, as participants may have been influenced by awareness of ongoing changes within the hospital.

To address the balancing measures observed during the intervention, several mitigation strategies were implemented. To reduce staff burnout, refreshment services were arranged for staff participating in the audit and folder re-arrangement activities. To minimise work overload and its effect on patient waiting time, the team used a mass campaign approach on weekends, while limiting the number of workers involved during regular working days (about three per day) and increasing to six workers during low patient-flow days such as Fridays. To reduce dust exposure, staff were provided with PPE (disposable gowns and masks), and a cleaning campaign was conducted. To prevent errors during sorting and re-arrangement, a team of trained and dedicated health professionals was assigned to provide continuous supervision while simultaneously registering lost, redundant and misplaced cards for proper tracking and correction.

Conclusions

The study successfully achieved its aim of improving medical record management, as evidenced by the increase in timely folder retrieval from 72% to 95%, the reduction in lost folders from 13% to 2% and significant improvements in both patient and healthcare provider satisfaction. The intervention demonstrated that systematic, well-structured PDSA cycles can effectively address operational bottlenecks and enhance workflow efficiency.

Given the simplicity, low cost and clear procedural changes introduced, the improvements are likely to be sustainable if continued monitoring, staff engagement and management support are maintained. Regular audits and refresher training can help ensure that staff adhere to the standardised processes over time.

Furthermore, the intervention has strong potential for scale-up to other hospitals facing similar challenges with medical record management. The principles of standardising procedures, improving accountability and streamlining workflow can be adapted to different settings, making this a feasible model for wider implementation across the healthcare system.
