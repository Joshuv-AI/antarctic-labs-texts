From X to Z: generational differences in ageism among Iranian nurses
Authors: Mohammad Mehdi Rajabi, Mahsa Talesh, Reza Negarandeh, Fatemeh Khoshnavay Fomani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAgeism is a form of discrimination based on age. It is defined as negative attitudes, beliefs, or behaviors toward older individuals. In healthcare settings, ageism can lead to reduced care quality, patient dissatisfaction, and poorer health outcomes. One potential factor contributing to ageism among healthcare providers is generational differences. Nurses from different generational cohorts (X, Y, and Z) have differences in socialization, experience, and cultural context, which can lead to variation in ageism. This study aimed to investigate generational differences in ageism among nurses in Iran, specifically examining ageism across Generation X, Y, and Z nurses. A correlational cross-sectional study was conducted among 210 nurses working at two major referral hospitals in Tehran, Iran. Participants were recruited using stratified random sampling. They were categorized into three generational groups: Generation X (45–60 years), Generation Y (29–44 years), and Generation Z (22–28 years). Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and a validated ageism scale. Statistical analyses included one-way ANOVA, post hoc tests, and hierarchical regression.ResultsSignificant differences were observed in total ageism scores across generational cohorts. Generation Z nurses reported the highest scores (52.19 ± 7.95), followed by Generation Y (45.93 ± 5.96) and Generation X (43.71 ± 7.90). Post hoc analyses indicated that Generation Z scored significantly higher on ageism than both Generation Y and Generation X (P < 0.001). Hierarchical regression confirmed that generational cohort remained a significant predictor of ageism after adjusting for demographic variables, with Generation Z exhibiting higher scores (P < 0.001). These results highlight generational differences in ageism, with Generation Z nurses displaying higher total scores. This suggests the need for targeted interventions, such as geriatric education, communication skills training, and mentorship programs, to reduce ageist attitudes among younger nurses. Further research is required to explore the underlying causes of these generational differences and develop strategies to mitigate ageism in nursing practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07891-7.

Introduction

Population aging is a major bio-social change of the 21st century. It is driven by declining fertility, increased life expectancy, and healthcare advances [1]. This trend is called a “demographic time bomb” and poses serious social and economic challenges. The WHO estimates that by 2050, the global population aged ≥ 60 will exceed 2 billion (21% of the world population) [2]. In Iran, population aging is progressing rapidly. The proportion of individuals aged ≥ 65 in Iran increased from 3.9% in 1956 to 6.1% in 2016. It is projected that this age group will comprise approximately 11% and 17% of the total population by 2036 and 2051, respectively. Accordingly, Iran is considered as a country undergoing a rapid transition toward an aging population [3].

Entering old age is accompanied by extensive physiological, psychological, and social changes. Older adults often experience multiple health problems and require increased use of health-care services, highlighting the need for specialized attention and care within the health system [4, 5]. In this context, nurses, as primary providers of health care and the professionals with the most direct contact with patients, play a vital role in meeting the needs of older adults [6].

One factor that can influence the quality of nursing care for older adults is ageism [7]. First introduced by Robert Butler, ageism is a form of age-based discrimination that can occur at individual, organizational, and societal levels [8]. It refers to negative attitudes, beliefs, or behaviors toward older individuals based on their age [9]. Studies have demonstrated that ageism can lead to patient dissatisfaction, reduced quality of care, delayed recovery, and poorer health outcomes, including increased mortality, worsened mental and physical health, a higher burden of comorbidities, impaired cognitive performance, and diminished quality of life [10–13]. Given the broad impact of ageism on health outcomes in older adults, identifying factors that contribute to ageism among nurses is essential.

Ageism is shaped by multiple factors, including psychological characteristics, cultural beliefs, educational level, personal experiences, and social context [14, 15]. One potential factor contributing to ageism is generational differences. Nurses from different generations (X, Y, and Z) have been socialized within distinct social, cultural, and educational environments [16], which may influence their attitudes toward older adults and the expression of ageism. Generation Z, defined as individuals born between 1997 and 2012, is the first generation to grow up with digital technologies and the internet from early childhood [17]. Extensive exposure to social media and digital lifestyles has influenced their values, attitudes, and social interactions [18]. Generation Z nurses may hold different attitudes toward older adults, shaped by digital information, media stereotypes, and globalized cultures [19]. Greater access to social media and information may increase awareness of older adults’ rights. However, technological gaps and lifestyle differences may also contribute to ageist views in generation Z [20]. In contrast, older generations of nurses (X and Y) may hold different perspectives due to greater direct contact with older adults and socialization within more traditional cultural contexts that emphasize respect for older people. The generational differences in experience, culture, and social environment may lead to variations in attitudes toward older adults, which can ultimately influence the occurrence of ageism.

With Iran’s rapidly aging population and growing healthcare needs, research on ageism is essential. Given generational differences in social and cultural environments, this study hypothesized significant ageism differences among Generation X, Y, and Z nurses. This study examined generational variations in nurses’ ageism. The findings may improve older adult care quality and clarify the role of generational differences in nursing practice.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The study was conducted in January 2026 at Imam Khomeini and Shariati hospitals in Tehran, two major referral and teaching centers affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS). These hospitals have a high patient volume and employ a large nursing workforce recruited from across the country. Therefore, they provide an appropriate and broadly representative setting for such studies [21].

Participants

The study population included nurses from three generational cohorts. Ages were considered at the time of the study. Generation X included those born 1965–1980 (aged 46–61). Generation Y included those born 1981–1996 (aged 30–45). Generation Z included those born 1997–2012 (aged 14–29) [17]. In Iran, nurses typically enter the profession at age 22 and retire after 30 years of service. Older and younger cohorts were not included because they are generally retired or have not yet entered the profession. Inclusion criteria were holding at least a bachelor’s degree in nursing, having at least one year of clinical nursing experience, and willingness to participate in the study.

Sample size and power

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1. A one-way ANOVA for three groups was used to estimate the number of participants, assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), a significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.90. The calculation indicated that at least 207 participants were needed. In this study, 210 nurses were recruited. Since the goal was to compare ageism across three generational groups, the sample was divided equally, with 70 participants in each group.

Instruments

Data were collected using a two-part electronic form, which included a demographic questionnaire and an ageism questionnaire.

Demographic Questionnaire: This section collected nurses’ demographic information, including age, gender, marital status, and education. Participants were grouped into three generations based on age: Generation X (46–61), Generation Y (30–45), and Generation Z (22–29).

Self-Directed and Other-Directed Ageism Questionnaire in Clinical Settings: This instrument, developed by Mehri et al. [22] in Iran, consists of 20 items across two dimensions: self-directed and other-directed ageism (See Supplementary File 1). In this instrument, self-directed ageism reflects the view that older adults contribute to the discrimination they experience (e.g., poor personal hygiene reduces care) [22]. It should be noted that self-directed ageism in the present study is not identical to the broader concept of internalized ageism, which refers to older adults’ own endorsement of negative age stereotypes [23]. Other-directed ageism arises from external factors such as caregivers’ attitudes or healthcare system characteristics (e.g., lack of caregiver motivation) [22]. Items are rated on a six-point Likert scale [1–6], with total scores ranging from 20 to 120; higher scores indicate higher ageism. Scores of 20–53, 54–87, and 88–120 represent weak, moderate, and severe ageism, respectively [24]. Mehri et al. reported a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.62, a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90 for the overall instrument, and an intra-cluster correlation of 0.98. The internal consistency for the self-directed and other-directed subscales was 0.75 and 0.91, respectively, and 0.91 for the entire instrument [22]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.83 for the self-directed subscale, 0.93 for the other-directed subscale, and 0.89 for the total scale, indicating good internal consistency.

Data collection

After receiving ethical approval from the TUMS Ethics Committee, the researcher (MT) visited the hospitals. Wards willing to participate were identified, and the study objectives and ethical issues were explained to the head nurses. Participants were recruited using stratified random sampling. Nurses were categorized into Generations X, Y, and Z based on age. From each generation, 70 nurses were randomly selected using Microsoft Excel 2013. The selected nurses were contacted and given a link to the electronic questionnaires. Collecting data electronically allowed participants to respond in a low-stress environment and take enough time for each question. If a nurse declined to participate, another nurse from the same generation was randomly selected.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (Approval No. IR.TUMS.FNM.REC.1404.251) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before completing the survey. Participation was voluntary. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without any consequences. Personal identifiers were not collected to ensure anonymity.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages, were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and ageism scores. The demographic variables were categorical. Therefore, comparisons of these variables between generations were conducted using chi-square tests. Independent t-tests were used to examine differences in ageism scores across the categories of demographic variables. As the groups did not significantly differ in demographic characteristics, these variables were not treated as confounders. One-way ANOVA was used to compare ageism scores across the three generations, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment for significant differences. Hierarchical regression was used to examine predictors of ageism in two steps. In Model 1, demographic variables (gender, marital status, and educational level) were entered to adjust for their potential confounding effects on total ageism. In Model 2, generational groups were added to determine whether it significantly improves the model fit and explains unique variance in ageism after controlling for demographics. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 210 nurses participated in the study. The majority of participants were female (71.0%), married (58.6%), and held a bachelor’s degree in nursing (85.2%). No statistically significant differences were observed between generations for gender (P = 0.09), educational level (P = 0.19), and marital status (P = 0.11). Therefore, the generations were similar in terms of demographic variables (Table 1).

Ageism scores

The mean self-directed ageism score was 15.51 (SD = 4.07; range 7–24). According to the possible score range for this subscale (6–36), it can be considered a weak-to-moderate level of ageism. The mean other-directed ageism score was 31.76 (SD = 7.04; range 16–49). Based on the score range for this subscale (16–96), it also reflects a weak-to-moderate level of ageism. The mean total ageism score was 47.28 (SD = 8.13; range 28–67). According to the scale’s cutoff points, this score suggests a weak level of ageism among the nurses (Table 2).

Generational differences in ageism scores

The results revealed that the total ageism scores in all generational cohorts were at a weak level. However, the analysis revealed that Generation Z had the highest score (52.19 ± 7.95), followed by Generation Y (45.93 ± 5.96) and Generation X (43.71 ± 7.90), with significant differences (P < 0.001, η²=0.196). Post hoc tests showed Generation Z differed significantly from both Generation Y and X (P < 0.001). Similar patterns were seen for the subscales. For self-directed ageism, Generation Z scored highest (17.00 ± 3.90) versus Generation Y (14.84 ± 3.83) and Generation X (14.70 ± 4.12), with significant differences (P < 0.001, η²=0.067). For other-directed ageism, Generation Z also scored highest (35.19 ± 6.78), followed by Generation Y (31.09 ± 5.91) and Generation X (29.01 ± 6.99), with significant differences (P < 0.001, η²=0.134) (Table 4).

Predictive role of generation in ageism

Hierarchical multiple regression was conducted in two steps to examine the predictive role of generational cohort on total ageism after controlling for demographic variables. In Model 1, demographic variables (gender, marital status, and educational level) were entered as predictors. This model was not statistically significant (R² = 0.001, P = 0.989) and none of the demographic variables were significant individual predictors (all P > 0.05). In Model 2, generational cohort (Generation X and Generation Y, with Generation Z as the reference group) was added. The overall model was significant and explained 20.4% of the ageism variance (R² = 0.204, P < 0.001). The addition of generational cohort resulted in a significant increase in explained variance (ΔR² = 0.204, P < 0.001). In the final model, both Generation X (B = − 8.80, β = − 0.51, P < 0.001) and Generation Y (B = − 6.54, β = − 0.38, P < 0.001) had significantly lower total ageism scores than Generation Z and none of the demographic variables were significant (all P > 0.05). These findings indicate that generational cohort is a significant predictor of ageism independent of demographic characteristics (Table 5).

Discussion

The present study examined ageism among nurses across three generational cohorts, Generation X, Generation Y, and Generation Z. The results indicated significant differences in ageism scores across generations, with Generation Z reporting the highest levels. Generational group was a strong predictor of ageism, independent of demographic variables, including gender, marital status, and educational level.

In this study, nurses exhibited a low level of total ageism. When examining the subscales, self-directed and other-directed ageism were low-to-moderate. Previous studies in Iran and Indonesia reported moderate ageism [24, 25]. The lower scores in this study may reflect the sample composition, as two-thirds of participants in the current study were from Generations X and Y.

The results revealed that ageism was not significantly associated with age, marital status, or educational level. In line with our findings, Melia and Choowattanapakorn [25] reported that educational level was not related to nursing practices for hospitalized older adults; however, they found a significant relationship between gender and ageism, which contrasts with our results [25]. Similarly, Hosseini et al. [24] observed no significant relationship between marital status or educational level and ageism, but contrary to our findings, they reported lower ageism scores in females than in males [24]. The differences in findings may be attributed to cultural and contextual factors. Our study focused on nurses in Tehran, the capital of Iran, a large and more modern city. In contrast, Melia and Choowattanapakorn [25] conducted their study in Bandung, Indonesia [25], and Hosseini et al. [24] in Ardabil, Iran [24], both smaller cities with more traditional settings. It can be hypothesized that the ongoing transition of traditional gender roles and the active participation of women in society and the workforce have narrowed the gap between male and female nurses’ attitudes and behaviors. This may explain the lack of significant differences between genders in our study.

The findings revealed significant generational differences in ageism. Although ageism scores were weak across all generations, Generation Z nurses exhibited higher self-directed, other-directed, and total ageism compared to Generations X and Y. This may be due to the early career stage and limited experience with older adults, which can increase reliance on stereotypes. Generational Cohort Theory suggests attitudes are shaped by the social, cultural, and technological context in which people are raised. Generation Z has extensive access to social media, which may expose them to biased attitudes toward older adults [26]. Additionally, Generation Z has less contact with older family members and fewer intergenerational interactions. Social Learning Theory emphasizes that attitudes are learned through observation and interaction [27]. Observing negative behaviors or attitudes toward older adults in social media, society, or the workplace may lead to higher ageism in Generation Z. Moreover, limited contact with older adults prevents the formation of positive and accurate attitudes and can exacerbate negative ones. Furthermore, older adults often require more intensive and challenging care due to complex health problems, which may increase workload pressure on nurses. As Generation Z nurses are in the early phase of their careers, providing such care may be demanding, intensifying negative attitudes toward older patients. Additionally, limited exposure to geriatric-focused education and fewer meaningful interactions with older adults during nursing training may reinforce negative perceptions and ultimately increase ageism. Supporting this explanation, Baseri Arani et al. [15] reported that nurses’ attitudes toward old age are significantly associated with age-discriminatory behaviors in the care of older adults, with more positive attitudes linked to fewer ageist behaviors [15]. A systematic review also found that workload and work-related factors, such as stress or lack of personnel, may be associated with ageism, and that workers with less knowledge about aging and less experience are more likely to have higher ageism scores [12]. This study provides evidence of generational differences in ageism among nurses. Further research is recommended to explore the underlying causes of these differences and to examine additional factors that may contribute to ageism in nursing.

Implications of findings

Generation Z nurses in this study showed higher ageism than Generations X and Y. This indicates that targeted interventions should focus more on younger nurses. Recommended strategies include ethical workshops and training on working with older adults. Activities designed to challenge negative attitudes are also suggested. Further workshops should focus on communication skills, empathy building, and therapeutic interactions. Mentorship programs that pair younger nurses with experienced colleagues could bridge generational gaps. Integrating more geriatric nursing content into training programs may promote more positive attitudes toward elderly care.

Strength and limitations

This study is among the few investigations examining ageism in Iran and has several strengths. The sample was recruited from two major referral hospitals in Tehran, which enhances the representativeness of the sample. Moreover, participants in each generational cohort were selected using random sampling, thereby increasing the representativeness of each generational group and strengthening the validity of comparisons of ageism scores. Collecting data electronically allowed participants to respond in a low-pressure environment, which can improve response accuracy. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sample was drawn from only two hospitals in Tehran, which limits generalizability to other regions or healthcare settings in Iran. Self-reported measures may be subject to social desirability bias. Additionally, other factors that could influence ageism—such as workplace characteristics, workload, specific clinical experience with older adults, or cultural attitudes—were not assessed in this study. Future research should include nurses from multiple regions to improve generalizability. Further studies should also explore factors such as workplace characteristics, clinical experience, workload, and cultural attitudes. Qualitative approaches and evaluations of targeted interventions, including training or mentorship programs, could provide deeper insights and practical strategies to reduce ageism.

Conclusion

In this study, Generation Z nurses showed higher levels of ageism compared with Generations X and Y, indicating that generational differences influence attitudes toward older adults. The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions for younger nurses. Education on geriatric care, communication skills, and empathy, combined with mentorship programs and increased interaction with older adults during training, may help reduce ageist attitudes and improve the quality of care.
