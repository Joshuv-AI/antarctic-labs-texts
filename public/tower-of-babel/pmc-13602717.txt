Emergency Department length of stay: a mixed-methods study in four Ethiopian public hospitals
Authors: Demessie Diriba Feyisa, Gada Edae Moti, Girma Gudina Debisa, Gadisa Tadasa Shaldo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEmergency Department (ED) length of stay is a key performance indicator of healthcare services, as prolonged stays are associated with adverse patient outcomes, increased financial burden, and higher mortality. This study aimed to assess ED length of stay and associated factors among patients in public hospitals in Kellam Wollega, Ethiopia, 2025.MethodsAn institution-based mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted in four public hospitals in the Kellam Wollega Zone, Ethiopia, from April 1–30, 2025. For the quantitative component, 399 adult ED patients were selected using systematic random sampling and interviewed using a structured questionnaire. Clinical and service-related data were supplemented through review of patient charts and ED logs. Descriptive statistics summarized patient characteristics, while multivariable logistic regression identified factors associated with prolonged ED length of stay (p < 0.05). For the qualitative component, 18 healthcare providers were purposively selected for in-depth interviews. Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated through triangulation to strengthen interpretation.ResultsSome 390 participants completed the questionnaire, yielding a response rate of 97.7%. More than half, 208 (53.3%; 95% CI: 48.2–58.4), experienced prolonged length of stay. Multivariable analysis showed that night-time presentation (AOR = 17.8, p < 0.001), comatose status (AOR = 2.8, p = 0.04), and referral after the ED visit (AOR = 8.0, p = 0.02) were significantly associated with prolonged stays in the ED. Qualitative analysis identified 12 length of stay assessment points, categorized into two themes: patient-related and hospital-related factors.ConclusionMore than half of the patients experienced a prolonged ED length of stay. Factors significantly associated with prolonged stay included time of presentation, triage category, mental status, post ED visit destination, absence of a laboratory test menu, consultation delays, and lack of inpatient beds.

Introduction

The Emergency Department (ED) is the primary entry point to hospitals for nearly all patients with serious conditions [1]. It provides uninterrupted healthcare services 24h a day, 365 days a year, for patients requiring urgent treatment. This department serves as the backbone of health facilities by offering the first line of care upon arrival, both during routine events and in times of disaster [2,3]

According to the Ethiopian Hospital Services Transformation Guideline, the maximum duration of stay allowed in the ED is 24h. Patients who require close observation and emergency treatment (such as IV fluid administration and a loading dose of IV antibiotics) may be transferred to a bed in the emergency services and kept for a maximum of 24h [4].

Prolonged ED length of stay (LOS) is a global challenge that negatively affects quality of care [5]. Although this is a worldwide issue, the rate of prolonged stays in ED is significantly higher in low and middle income countries (LMIC) compared to high income countries [6]. Studies conducted in Canada and Korea showed that approximately 11% and 23% of patients, respectively, experienced a prolonged LOS in the ED [7,8] . Sub-Saharan African countries have also recognized that prolonged LOS has become a daily reality, affecting the functionality of the ED, and have emphasized the need for further research to reduce prolonged LOS in the ED [9]. For example, evidence from Botswana shows that more than half of patients had a prolonged LOS [10]. In Ethiopia, some reports suggest that more than two-thirds of patients who visited the ED experienced prolonged LOS [11,12].

Prolonged LOS in ED significantly affects patients economically, as treatment costs tend to increase with longer hospital stays. Prolonged LOS also disrupts hospital bed occupancy rates, places additional strain on healthcare resources, and poses a serious risk to patient safety by increasing the likelihood of hospital-acquired infections, morbidity, and mortality [13]. Patient satisfaction is often reduced under such conditions [14].

Improving the performance of ED should be a public health priority, given the growing global demand for emergency care [15,16]. Most previous studies have primarily focused on patients as the target population when identifying potential causes of prolonged LOS in the ED [10,17,18]. However, certain contributing factors, particularly those related to healthcare professionals and hospital systems may not be fully known or understood by patients alone. Therefore, this study includes both patients and healthcare professionals, which is essential for comprehensively identifying and addressing the underlying causes of prolonged LOS in the ED.

The findings of this study provide valuable insights for hospital managers by providing current data that can guide interventions to address unexpected prolonged stays in the ED. Furthermore, since no previous research has examined the factors contributing to extended ED length of stay (LOS) by involving both patients and healthcare professionals, this study establishes a foundation for future investigations. The primary aim of this study was to assess patients’ LOS in the ED and associated factors among public hospitals in Kellam Wollega, Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting and design

An institution-based convergent parallel mixed-methods design was employed in public hospitals in the Kellem Wollega Zone from April 1 to April 30, 2025. In this design, quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently and analyzed separately, and the findings from both components were then integrated during the interpretation phase to provide a comprehensive understanding of factors contributing to prolonged length of stay in the ED [19]. The study was conducted in four public hospitals in Kellem Wollega Zone: Dambi Dollo University Comprehensive Specialized Teaching Hospital (DDUCSTH), Kake Hospital, Gidami Hospital, and Hawa Galan Hospital. These hospitals provide healthcare services to over 1.19 million people in the area, according to the 2021 Central Statistical Agency (CSA) population projection report, with a catchment area of approximately 10,995 km². Reports from the Zonal Health Office indicate that approximately 28,000 patients visit the adult emergency departments of these hospitals annually.

Quantitative study

All patients who presented to the ED of public hospitals in Kellam Wollega Zone during the study period and met the inclusion criteria were eligible. Patients who died on arrival before receiving healthcare interventions were excluded from the study. In addition, patients triaged as green (non-urgent) were excluded from the study. As the primary objective of this study was to assess length of stay among patients requiring urgent and emergent care, the analysis was limited to higher-acuity cases. The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula, considering a 38.4% proportion of prolonged ED length of stay[17]. After adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size was 399.

Data collection technique

Data were collected through prospective patient interviews and record review. First, data were obtained from each hospital on the total number of patients treated in the ED during the preceding year. Based on this information, the average monthly ED patient volume was calculated for each hospital. Since April reflected the average patient load across the hospitals, data collection was conducted during this month. The calculated sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital according to its average monthly ED patient volume. A systematic random sampling technique was then applied to recruit patients upon presentation to the ED. The sampling interval (k-value) was determined by dividing the estimated monthly ED attendance by the allocated sample size for each hospital. The first participant was selected using a lottery method at the start of data collection, and subsequent patients were enrolled at regular intervals (Supplementary File 1). Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire to obtain socio-demographic information and mode of arrival, while clinical and service-related variables were extracted from patient medical charts and ED logbooks using a standardized data extraction checklist.

Data quality control

Prior to data collection, one-day training was provided for the supervisors and data collectors on the objectives of the study, data collection procedures, and ethical considerations. In addition, the questionnaire was pre-tested on 5% (20 patients) of the sample size two weeks before the actual data collection at Ayira Hospital. Based on the pre-test results, ambiguous words and unclear concepts were corrected accordingly. Throughout the data collection period, close supervision was conducted. The collected data were checked daily by the supervisors for completeness and clarity, while the investigators monitored the overall quality of the data collection process.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were checked, coded, and entered into EpiData version 3.1 and then exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. A binary logistic regression model was used to assess the association between the dependent and independent variables. Initially, bivariable logistic regression analysis was performed, and variables with a p-value ≤ 0.25 were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to control for potential confounding variables. Crude odds ratios (COR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to measure the strength of associations. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05. The goodness of fit of the model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.51), indicating adequate model fit. Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values ranging from 1.2 to 1.8, suggesting no significant multicollinearity.

Qualitative study

All healthcare professionals who had worked in the ED for at least six months were eligible. Healthcare professionals with less than six months of ED experience were excluded from the study. In-depth interviews were conducted with healthcare professionals working in Kellem Wollega hospitals to explore factors related to prolonged hospital stays. Participants were selected purposively based on their professional experience and involvement in patient care. Interviews were conducted until data saturation was achieved, meaning no new themes or information emerged from additional interviews [20]. A total of 18 (n = 18) healthcare professionals participated in the qualitative interviews.

Qualitative data were transcribed verbatim and translated into English. After familiarization through repeated reading of the transcripts, the data were coded, and similar codes were grouped into themes. The themes were then reviewed, defined, and clearly described. Finally, findings were triangulated with quantitative results to enhance interpretation and validity.

An exploratory descriptive qualitative approach was employed for the qualitative component. Healthcare professionals working in the ED of the selected public hospitals were recruited using a maximum variation purposive sampling technique to capture diverse professional roles and levels of experience. Data were collected through face-to-face, in-depth interviews conducted by trained data collectors using a pretested interview guide. With participants’ written informed consent, interviews were audio-recorded to ensure accuracy and completeness of the data. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new themes emerged.

Trustworthiness of the findings was ensured by applying Lincoln and Guba’s rigor criteria, including credibility, transferability, dependability, and conformability. Audio recordings were copied to a computer as a backup, and written field notes were kept securely by the principal investigator until the transcriptions were checked for accuracy and completeness.

Ethical clearance

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Dambi Dollo University (Ref. No. 8622/DDU/15), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Qualitative study

Eight participants were female and ten were male. The professional backgrounds of the participants included seven comprehensive nurses, four general practitioners, one surgeon, three emergency and critical care nurses, and three adult health nursing professionals. The participants’ work experience ranged from 3 to 18 years, with a median of 10 years. Their ages ranged from 30 to 51 years, with a median age of 42 years. Regarding their current roles, thirteen participants were staff members, three were nursing directors, and two were medical directors. From the qualitative data, a total of twelve different ED length of stay assessment points emerged, which were categorized into two major themes.

Theme 1: patient related factors

This theme highlighted patient characteristics linked to prolonged length of stay in the ED. Most participants identified time of presentation, case severity, financial constraints, nature of the disease, age, residence, and patient preferences as factors contributing to extended stays in the ED.

Participants reported that case severity is one of the factors contributing to prolonged length of stays in the ED. “Sometimes, patients who are unconscious or have unstable vital signs […] cannot be admitted or referred until their vital signs stabilize.”

Financial constraints are another factor contributing to prolonged stays in the ED. “Some patients are brought to the ED by strangers […] and may be unable to afford necessary laboratory tests or prescribed medications. Financial constraints are a major factor contributing to prolonged length of stay in the ED.”

Participants highlighted that time of presentation was another factor contributing to prolonged length of stay in the ED. “Timing of patient presentation is a key factor contributing to prolonged length of stay in the ED. Some patients arrive at night when a doctor is unavailable […] and may remain in the ED for >24 h until consultation is possible.”

The nature of the disease contributes to prolonged length of stay in the ED. “Conflict-related injuries requiring legal documentation […] may result in prolonged stays in the ED when the authorized person for assessments and documentation is unavailable.”

Theme 2: hospital related factors

Most participants identified hospital-related factors as contributing to a prolonged length of stay in the ED. The most frequently reported issues included absence of medications, unavailability of laboratory tests, delays in consultation, shortage of beds, and an inadequate healthcare professional to patient ratio.

Participants reported that delays in consultation contribute to prolonged ED length of stays. “Some senior doctors are very busy with dual responsibilities […] and are often unavailable for timely consultations, even for critical patients. Delays in consultation are a common cause of prolonged stays in the ED.”

Participants highlighted that bed shortages contribute to prolonged ED length of stays. “Recently, a severe malaria epidemic led to many patients receiving care in corridors due to bed shortages […]. The number of patients requiring admission far exceeds available beds, making bed shortage a critical problem.”

Participants reported that delays in laboratory investigations, often due to unavailable tests or equipment malfunctions, contribute to prolonged stays. “When laboratory investigations are unavailable or machines malfunction, patients are sent elsewhere […]. This process can take a long time and contribute to prolonged ED stays.”

Discussion

A prolonged length of stay in the ED adversely affects the functionality of the ED and the quality of patient care [9]. Many countries use length of stay as a quality indicator to measure ED performance and to assess the overall development of their healthcare systems [21]. This study aimed to assess the length of stay in the ED and identify the factors associated with it.

Accordingly, more than half of the patients stay at the ED for >24h. This finding is lower than the study conducted in Botswana (72.5%) [22] and southern Ethiopia (91.5%) [12]. Conversely, it is higher than findings from studies conducted in Australia (25%) [23], Indonesia (3%) [24], Iran (10.2%) [25], Eastern Ethiopia (42.25%) [26], southwestern Ethiopia (38.4%) [17], and northwestern Ethiopia (46.5%) [18]. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in the data sources, quality of healthcare services, and adequacy of the healthcare workforce.

The quantitative finding that patients presenting in the evening and at night had longer LOS compared with those presenting in the morning is strongly supported by the qualitative findings of this study. Participants consistently identified time of presentation as a key factor contributing to prolonged ED LOS, particularly during evening and night hours. Qualitative accounts highlighted reduced availability of diagnostic and therapeutic services, limited staffing, delayed consultations, and slower decision-making processes outside regular working hours. These findings indicate that prolonged ED stay among evening and night presenters is not incidental but is driven by systemic and organizational constraints associated with off-hour service delivery. The convergence of quantitative and qualitative evidence enhances the credibility of the results and underscores time of presentation as a critical determinant of ED length of stay in this setting. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Iran [25], Canada [8], and south-west Ethiopia [17]. A possible explanation is that senior consultations, as well as diagnostic and therapeutic tools, are less likely available at night [27].

Consistent with the findings of this study, research conducted in the Netherland [28] and Southwest Ethiopia [17] also showed that patients with altered mental status had a higher likelihood of prolonged LOS in the ED. The finding that patients with altered mental status had longer ED stays is triangulated by qualitative evidence indicating that hospital policies require extended monitoring and stabilization for patients with unstable vital signs or unconsciousness.

Similar to studies conducted in Southern [12] and Eastern Ethiopia [26], this study found that patients who did not undergo imaging studies spent less time in the ED compared to their counterparts. This could be attributed to the fact that the time required for diagnostic workups contributes to an increased LOS in the ED. Additionally, this study revealed that the destination after the ED visit is significantly associated with a prolonged LOS. This finding is supported by the studies conducted in the Netherlands [28] and Sweden [29].

Overcrowding is a worldwide issue that could lead to a crisis in the delivery of high-quality healthcare services [27,28]. The qualitative result explored overcrowding as a factors contributing to prolonged length of stay in the ED. This result is consistent with findings of studies done in Sweden [29], southern Ethiopia [12], and Northwest Ethiopia [18]. A possible reason for this is that overcrowding limits the availability of essential resources such as beds, medical staff, and diagnostic services, thereby delaying patient assessment, treatment, and transfer. When ED operates beyond their capacity, the workflow becomes inefficient, leading to bottlenecks at every stage of care [30]. The qualitative result has also explored that the shortage of beds in the inpatient ward contributed to the prolonged length of stay in the ED. This finding is in line with a study conducted in Southern Ethiopia[12].

Consistent with the findings of a study conducted in Eastern Ethiopia [26], the result of the qualitative study shows a shortage of an available laboratory test menu was reported to contribute to a prolonged LOS in the ED. Limited diagnostic capacity can delay clinical decision-making, thereby extending the time patients remain in the ED. Furthermore, participants explored delays in specialist consultations as another contributing factor. Patients in the ED often require evaluation and decisions from senior clinicians regarding admission, referral, or discharge.

Limitation of the study

This study was conducted over a one-month period; therefore, patient presentations may have been influenced by seasonal or short-term temporal variations. Additionally, the cross-sectional design assessed factors affecting ED length of stay at a single point in time and did not permit causal inferences. Primary diagnoses were grouped broadly, which may have obscured condition-specific patterns. As a result, future studies could use more detailed classifications to better explore determinants of prolonged ED stays. This study did not assess delays related to the referral process among referred patients. The exclusion of green-coded patients may limit the generalizability of the findings, as these patients constitute a portion of ED attendees. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect patients with urgent and emergent conditions only. Furthermore, some data extracted from patient medical records were incomplete; where possible, missing information was supplemented through direct patient interviews.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrated that the majority of patients remain in the ED for >24h. Several factors were significantly associated with prolonged ED length of stay, including time of presentation, primary diagnosis, triage category, mental status, need for imaging studies, destination after ED visit, absence of a laboratory test menu, delays in consultation, lack of inpatient beds, and overall overcrowding. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve patient flow and reduce delays in emergency care. Hospital administrators and emergency department managers should strengthen bed management systems, enhance the availability of diagnostic services, streamline consultation processes, and implement strategies to mitigate overcrowding.

Dissemination of the results

Results from this study were shared with stakeholders via informal presentations at the data collection sites. Additionally, the findings were disseminated through formal reports to Dambi Dollo University’s Institute of Health, Department of Nursing, and the Kellam Wollega Zone Health Office.
