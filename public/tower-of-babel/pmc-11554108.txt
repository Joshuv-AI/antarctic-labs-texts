Posttraumatic stress symptoms in parents of children with newly diagnosed cancer: 1-year trajectories and relationship variables as predictors
Authors: Alexandra J Vogt, Lasse Bartels, Michael Grotzer, Guy Bodenmann, Kurt Leibundgut, Jochen Rössler, Markus A Landolt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe way in which parental posttraumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) unfold in the first year after a cancer diagnosis in their child is poorly understood. The aims of this study were to identify parental PTSS trajectories and to examine couple-related predictors (dyadic coping and we-disease appraisals), sociodemographic predictors (education and sex), and medical predictors (child’s physical impairment) of trajectory membership.MethodA 1-year prospective study was conducted, and 157 parents of children newly diagnosed with cancer were assessed. PTSS was measured with the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale for DSM-5 (PDS-5) at 3–6 weeks (T1), 6 months (T2), and 12 months (T3) after the diagnosis. The trajectories were identified with Latent Class Growth Analysis, the predictors were explored with binomial logistic regression.ResultsTwo distinct trajectories were found. The majority of parents (86.0%) followed a low PTSS trajectory, characterized by initially low scores that slightly decreased over 12 months. In contrast, 14.0% of parents displayed a stable, high PTSS trajectory. A high trajectory of child’s physical impairment and low scores in parental dyadic coping were significantly associated with the likelihood of a high parental PTSS trajectory.ConclusionsThe findings provide new insights into the critical period of the first year after a child’s cancer diagnosis. While most parents display a resilient, low PTSS trajectory, a minority consistently experience high PTSS levels. Child’s physical impairment and dyadic coping should be considered as predictors for early identification of vulnerable parents. Incorporating dyadic coping in parental support following a childhood cancer diagnosis could be beneficial for parental mental health.

Each year, about 400,000 children worldwide are diagnosed with cancer (Ward et al., 2019). Such a diagnosis often entails invasive, painful, and time-consuming treatments, confronting fear and death, and a complete change in daily life (e.g., change in daily routines, frequent or long hospital stays, absence from school). The diagnosis and treatment place an enormous psychological burden on parents, increasing their risk for symptoms of anxiety, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (Katz et al., 2018; Pai et al., 2007; Pinquart, 2019). According to pooled prevalences from the meta-analysis by van Warmerdam et al. (2019), 21% of parents of children with cancer meet the criteria for anxiety disorders, 28% for depression, and 26% for PTSD, which is significantly higher than for noncancer parental controls. However, the included studies in this meta-analysis exhibited significant variability in the time since diagnosis. Examination of parental posttraumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) over time has suggested distinct trajectories (Dolgin et al., 2007; Sharp et al., 2022), with a majority of parents showing resilience in terms of low PTSS trajectories after a child’s cancer diagnosis, while some parents show high PTSS scores that remain elevated over the course of the disease. However, our understanding of trajectories in parental PTSS following the diagnosis remains limited, as does our knowledge of potential risk factors for unfavorable trajectories. Identifying these risk and resilience factors is particularly relevant for early detection and appropriate support for parents at risk (Bruce, 2006). To our knowledge, the role of relationship variables as potential predictors of parental PTSS trajectories has been largely overlooked in the literature, highlighting the contribution of this study in addressing this critical gap.

The Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, originally proposed by Lazarus and Folkman (1984), provides a theoretical framework for understanding the development of stress in individuals, and can be used to identify pertinent predictors for parental mental health following a child’s cancer diagnosis. The model’s starting point is a stimulus that can trigger stress, in this case, the diagnosis of cancer in the child. The individual’s perception of this stimulus undergoes two distinct appraisal processes: In the primary appraisal process, the situation is evaluated as either positive, irrelevant, or stressful. If the stimulus is perceived as stressful, a secondary appraisal follows, involving an assessment of available resources. The assessment of the situation and resources is influenced by environmental and individual variables (Cox & Griffiths, 2010; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). Characteristics of the child’s illness, e.g., its severity, duration, and treatment intensity, as well as the child’s treatment have been discussed in various studies as a crucial environmental variable for parental well-being (Landolt et al., 2003; Sloper, 2000). Additionally, personal variables like the sex of the parent have been identified as significant predictors of parental mental health in in many studies (e.g., Pai et al., 2007; Pinquart, 2019). Various studies have also reported education level as a significant predictor for PTSS following a potential traumatic event (e.g., Brewin et al., 2000; Tang et al., 2017). According to the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping, the coping process follows the appraisal process (Cox & Griffiths, 2010; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). In current research, there is growing emphasis on two coping factors that influence parental mental health: Dyadic coping (Bodenmann, 2016) and the concept of “we-disease” (Kayser et al., 2007). The coping processes delineated in the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping exert a significant influence on an individual’s well-being, health, and social behavior (Cox & Griffiths, 2010; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). However, these relationship variables have not yet been investigated in the context of parental PTSS, a gap that this study aims to address.

According to the assumptions of this model, the mental health of parents of children with cancer should be directly or indirectly influenced by the following five identified and frequently discussed variables: the severity of the child’s illness, the parental sex and level of education, the quality of common/joint dyadic coping, and the presence of parental shared appraisals according to “we-disease” (Bodenmann, 2016; Kayser et al., 2007; Landolt et al., 2003; Pinquart, 2019; Tang et al., 2017).

Characteristics of the child’s illness, such as its severity, duration, and treatment intensity, along with the child’s treatment itself, have been discussed in various studies as significant risk factors affecting parental well-being (Landolt et al., 2003; Sloper, 2000). In terms of sex, studies comparing the well-being of mothers and fathers of a child with cancer often found that mothers report a greater burden than fathers (Pai et al., 2007; Pinquart, 2019). Furthermore, a low level of education appears to be a risk factor for PTSS after experience a potential traumatic event (Brewin et al., 2000; Tang et al., 2017).

Dyadic coping is a systemic concept reflecting how partners manage stress together, signifying a shared experience beyond individual coping, involving stress communication and joint strategies. Dyadic coping involves both partners actively engaging in the stress management, highlighting the importance of mutual support and collaboration (Bodenmann, 2016). This concept applies to everyday stress and severe situations like illness in the family (Leuchtmann & Bodenmann, 2017), and is crucial for couples’ well-being (Landolt et al., 2023), especially in contexts like cancer (Traa et al., 2015) and child-related stress (Roth et al., 2024). To our knowledge, dyadic coping has not yet been investigated as a predictor of parental PTSS after a childhood cancer diagnosis. However, there are studies that report that dyadic coping is a significant predictor of PTSS in populations that have experienced other potential traumatic events, such as veterans or work accident victims (Lambert et al., 2015, 2018). There are also studies in the field of childhood cancer that report that dyadic coping is a predictor of parental mental health (Van Schoors et al., 2019). On the other hand, “we-disease” illustrates viewing illness as a shared challenge, enhancing unity and resource sharing (Kayser et al., 2007). The concept of “we-disease” suggests that when one partner is affected by a health issue, it impacts both partners, transforming the stress into a shared challenge (Leuchtmann & Bodenmann, 2017). This may include but is not limited to both parents actively participating in their child’s treatment decisions, coordinating care responsibilities, providing emotional support to each other, and jointly managing the practical aspects of their child’s illness, such as medical appointments and communicating with healthcare providers. This view aligns with the systemic perspective of stress and coping, emphasizing that both partners are affected, and both have resources to support each other. Dyadic coping and we-disease are therefore two concepts that are related and can overlap. While there has been research on cancer as a we-disease (Kayser et al., 2007), to our knowledge there has been no research on the association between we-disease appraisals and PTSS.

To date, there has been a lack of longitudinal studies looking at parental PTSS over the course of the particularly critical first year after the child’s cancer diagnosis and attempting to identify predictors of the course of parental PTSS. By identifying specific risk and resilience factors, vulnerable parents can be recognized and supported at an early stage. In particular, it is still unclear what influence dyadic coping and we-disease appraisals have on parental PTSS.

Enhanced knowledge about the impact of risk and resilience factors such as the severity of the child’s illness, the parental sex and level of education, the quality of common/joint dyadic coping, and parental we-disease appraisal would inform psychological and medical practitioners about the complex interplay between these factors and parental PTSS after a child’s cancer diagnosis. This might help clinical and medical practitioners to help tailor interventions according to the needs of parents from the start of treatment, and as a result, could help to prevent parents from following a negative developmental trajectory and better adapt to the child’s illness. The investigation of relationship variables such as dyadic coping and we-disease as potential predictors is particularly relevant here, as they can be assessed and potentially influenced at an early stage after the child’s cancer diagnosis. Unlike stable demographic factors such as sex or education, relationship variables are dynamic and can be influenced through interventions, making them crucial targets for early assessment and intervention (Bodenmann & Shantinath, 2004).

The aim of this study was to evaluate parental PTSS trajectories over the course of 12 months after their child’s cancer diagnosis. Furthermore, the present study aimed to identify predictors of parental PTSS trajectories. Based on the literature, we expect to find two or three parental PTSS trajectory classes (Dolgin et al., 2007; Sharp et al, 2022). Further, based on the existing literature and the Transactional Model of Stress and Coping (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984), we hypothesize that parental sex, level of education, the severity of the child’s illness, levels of dyadic coping, and we-disease were associated with parental PTSS trajectories.

Methods

Procedures and participants

Data on parents of children with cancer were taken from a study conducted at the University Children’s Hospital of Zurich and the University Hospital of Berne. The main objective of the study was to investigate the longitudinal influence of parental factors on quality of life in children with recently diagnosed cancer. A sub-objective of the study was to investigate the influence of relationship variables on parental PTSS, which is reported in the present article. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from both the local Ethics Committee in Zurich (No. 2015-0086) and Berne (No. 067/2015). Potential participating families were informed that the aim of the study was to understand how families cope with a child’s cancer or tumor over time. Parents were eligible for participation if their child was aged between 0 and 17 years and had been newly diagnosed with cancer or tumor based on the list of the Swiss Childhood Cancer Registry. The caregiver parent needed to live together with another partner in a committed relationship for at least 1 year, as this study examined relationship variables as possible predictors. The relationship did not have to be between the child’s mother and father but could also be a new relationship of one of the parents. Dyads (mother and father or one parent with a new partner) or individuals could participate, but they had to be in a relationship. Due to the challenging and stressful situation in which a family finds itself after a child has been diagnosed with cancer, the parents were given the opportunity to participate as individuals and not as a dyad. It was also possible that no parent of a participating family took part at a specific measurement time, but then took part again at a later time. Further, families were excluded if the child had exhibited previous severe mental or physical disabilities, as assessed by a physician. Moreover, the participants were required to be fluent in German. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving information regarding the voluntary nature of their participation and their right to withdraw from the study at any time. Families did not receive financial compensation for participation. The TREND statement checklist is available as Supplementary Material 2.

Assessments were conducted at the following three time points: 3–6 weeks following the child’s cancer diagnosis (T1), 6 months after the diagnosis (T2), and 12 months after the diagnosis (T3). The data was collected using paper-pencil questionnaires, which were sent to the participants by post. A total of 164 participants (79 fathers and 85 mothers) took part in the study. Overall, seven participants were excluded. Among the seven excluded participants, four did not report parental PTSS at any of the three assessment points. Further, three participants were excluded because they reported a “worst potential traumatic event” other than the child’s cancer diagnosis, which meant that the reported PTSS could not be clearly attributed to the child’s cancer diagnosis. In total, data of 157 parents (73 fathers and 84 mothers) were analyzed. Of the 157 participants, 138 participated as dyads, while 19 participated as individuals. Of the 69 participating dyads, in 11 dyads, only one of the two partners participated in at least one measurement point. The sample flowchart and reasons for participant attrition can be found in Figure 2.

Sample flow chart.

The variables “child sex,” “child age,” and “type of cancer diagnosis” were recorded for nonparticipating and participating families. No significant differences were found between the groups with respect to child sex (χ2(1)=1.120, p=.290), child age (t(228)=1.626, p=.134), or type of cancer diagnosis (χ2(3)=3.438, p=.329). Excluded participants did not differ significantly from analyzed parents in the type of the child’s cancer diagnosis, the child’s physical impairment, parental sex, level of education, we-disease appraisal, and dyadic coping.

Measures

Trauma exposure

Trauma exposure was assessed with the self-report 12-item trauma checklist of the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (PDS) (Foa, 1995). Items are scored dichotomously as either “yes” (1) or “no” (0). For the purpose of the study, we added an additional item, “intensive medical interventions, e.g., after a heart attack, stroke, etc.” Notably, the PDS-item “life-threatening illness” was scored as present in all parents due to the child’s cancer diagnosis.

Posttraumatic stress symptoms

Parental PTSS were assessed using the German translation (Wittmann et al., 2021) of the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale for DSM-5 (PDS-5), a questionnaire with good to excellent psychometric properties reported (Foa et al., 2016). The PDS-5 consists of 24 items that measure the severity of PTSD symptoms experienced in the past month based on the DSM-5 criteria. Symptoms are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with (0) indicating “not at all or only once in the last month”, (1) “once or less per week/sometimes,” (2) “2 to 5 times per week/half of the time,” and (3) corresponding to “5 times or more per week/almost always.” Additional items necessary for PTSD diagnosis assess symptom duration, functional impairment, and potential medication effects. To calculate the severity of PTSS, we calculated the mean score of all DSM-5 PTSD symptom items. Based on the proposal by Bennett (2001), a maximum of 15% of the items in the present study can be missing in order to calculate the mean. A maximum of three missing items (13%) were considered for the calculation of PTSS. Three participants with more than three missing items were excluded for all analyses involving the severity of PTSS. For individuals with up to three missing values, these were replaced by the mean of the other symptom items. In the present study, the PDS-5 exhibited good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α of .89, .92, and .93 at each time point, respectively).

Predictor variables

Participants were asked to report their highest level of education at T1. Parental education was thereby assessed separately for mothers and fathers and categorized according to the five levels of the Swiss education system (Swiss Conference of Cantonal Ministers of Education, 2017). Parental sex was self-assessed. At T1, participants were also asked to rate their dyadic coping and their we-disease appraisals. In this study, dyadic coping was measured with the Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI) (Bodenmann, 2008) by calculating the mean score of all 35 items. The DCI comprises the subscales stress communication (self and partner), supportive dyadic coping (self and partner), delegated dyadic coping (self and partner), negative dyadic coping (self and partner), and common dyadic coping. The items are scored on a 5-point scale ranging from 1=“never/very rarely” to 5=“very often.” Higher scores indicate better dyadic coping. Five missing items (14%) were allowed for the calculation the sum score. Participants with more than five missing items were excluded. If a participant had a maximum of five missing values, the missing items were replaced with the individual’s mean score of the present items. The scale exhibited good internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s α of .93).

We-disease was measured with the We-Disease Questionnaire (WDQ) (Vogt et al., 2024) by calculating the mean score of all 10 items. The items are scored on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0=“strongly disagree” to 5=“strongly agree.” Higher scores indicate better we-disease appraisals. In the validation study by Vogt et al. (2024), the WDQ demonstrated good reliability and validity. The instrument can be found in the Supplementary Material 1.

Participants with more than one missing item (10%) were excluded from the analysis. One missing item was allowed for the calculation of the mean score and was replaced with the individual’s mean score of the other 9 items. The scale exhibited good internal consistency in the present study (Cronbach’s α of .77).

Lastly, physical impairment was measured using a single item at all three time points. The responsible doctor assessed the child’s current physical impairment on a 5-point scale: 0=“no impairment,” 1=“minor impairment,” 2=“moderate impairment,” 3=“severe impairment,” and 4=“very severe impairment.” To incorporate information about the course of the child’s physical impairment throughout the first year following the cancer diagnosis, a latent class growth analysis (LCGA) was conducted using data from all three time points. Based on the fit indices and the relevance of the content interpretation, a 2-class solution was chosen (fit indices of the three class solutions can be found in the Supplementary Material 2). A total of 123 parents were assigned to class 1, characterized as the “low physical impairment” trajectory. These children exhibited low initial impairment (intercept: 1.01, SE=.54, p=.060), which remained stable over time (slope: −.19, SE=.48, p=.684). Class 2, described as “high physical impairment” trajectory, included 34 parents whose children experienced higher levels of impairment (intercept: 2.38, SE = 1.19, p=.046). The physical impairment of this class remained high over time (slope: −.39, SE = 1.59, p=.807). The individual class membership of participating parents in these two classes of physical impairment was used as a predictor for the trajectory of parental PTSS.

Statistical analyses

In a first step, we conducted LCGA without covariates or distal outcomes, using Mplus, version 8.5 (Muthén & Muthén, 2017). LCGA uses a full maximum-likelihood estimation procedure that effectively accounts for missing data. We compared four LCGA models with different numbers of classes, ranging from 1 to 4. To compare these models, we considered several fit indices, including the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (with lower values indicating a better fit), and the Lo-Mendell Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRA-LRT). Additionally, we considered entropy (with higher values indicating a better fit; Reinecke, 2014) and we took the size of the smallest class into account. Class sizes that were smaller than 5% of the total sample were excluded (Weller et al., 2020). In a second step, predictors of trajectory membership were explored by conducting binary logistic regression analysis using SPSS, version 29.0 (IBM Inc.). The trajectory classes were regressed on five priori selected predictors, namely parental sex, parental level of education, parental we-disease at T1, parental dyadic coping at T1, and the 1-year trajectory of the child’s physical impairment. Due to the rather small sample size, a bootstrapping procedure was conducted during the logistic regression, repeated 1,000 times to enhance the robustness of the results.

Results

Characteristics of the sample

Overall, 84 mothers and 73 fathers participated in this study. Of the total, 138 parents took part as a dyad and 19 as individuals. The sample consisted mainly of parents with a medium or high level of education. On an average, the participants reported 2.22 (SD = 1.27) potential traumatic events (range of PTEs: 1–8). The mean PTSS sum score was 11.90 (SD = 8.56) at T1, 10.41 (SD = 9.21) at T1, and 9.28 (SD = 9.65) at T3. At T1, 26.7% (n = 40) of the parents met the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for PTSD, at T2 14.8% (n = 19), and at T3 11.4% (n = 13). The previously defined minimum class size also indicated a 2-class solution. This 2-class solution delineated two distinct trajectories of parental PTSS over the initial year following a child’s cancer diagnosis (see Figure 3). The first class, comprising 86.0% (n = 135) of the sample, represented a “low PTSS” trajectory characterized with low initial scores (intercept: .48, SE=.03, p<.001) and a slightly decreasing slope over the course of 12 months after the diagnosis (slope: −.08, SE=.03, p=.001). Predictors of PTSS trajectories

The child’s physical impairment and parental dyadic coping were found to significantly predict the parental PTSS-trajectories (see Table 3). Parents of children with higher initial levels of physical impairment and a pronounced decrease over time were less likely to belong to the “low PTSS” class than parents of children with a lower initial level of physical impairment. The odds for belonging to the “low PTSS” class were 2.83 times higher for parents who displayed higher values in dyadic coping than for parents with lower values in dyadic coping. Sex, level of education, and we-disease appraisals of the parents were no significant predictors of parental PTSS.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify and understand the patterns and predictors of parental PTSS trajectories over the first year after a child’s cancer diagnosis. The focus was on the relationship variables dyadic coping and we-disease, and other potential predictors (sex, education, and the child’s physical impairment), which were examined due to their theoretical and empirical relevance.

Two distinct PTSS trajectories in parents of a child with cancer in the first year following diagnosis were found: a low trajectory and a high trajectory of PTSS. The findings indicate that the majority of the parents (86.0%) followed a trajectory characterized by a low level of PTSS, which further decreased over time. In contrast, 14.0% of the parents exhibited a high trajectory of PTSS over the course of 12 months after their child’s cancer diagnosis. The fact that some parents who met the PTSD criteria according to DSM-5 at T1 were classified in the “low PTSS” trajectory can be explained by the spontaneous natural remission of PTSS within the first few months (Morina et al., 2014).

Although previous studies have identified three or more distinct trajectories (e.g., Dolgin et al., 2007), a recently published study by Sharp et al. (2022) also found two distinct trajectories of parental PTSS following a childhood cancer diagnosis. Comparing our results with those of Dolgin et al. (2007), who identified three trajectories in a sample of 212 caregivers, and Sharp et al. (2022), who found two trajectories in a sample of 255 caregivers, it is noteworthy that our study, with a sample size of 157 caregivers, also identified two trajectories. This suggests that the number of trajectories observed does not seem to be influenced by sample size, indicating that other factors may play a more significant role in determining the number of trajectories. Similar to the finding of our study, the majority of parents in the study by Sharp et al. (2022) displayed a resilient trajectory, with a significantly decreasing slope. However, the second trajectory class identified in our study differs from the results reported by Sharp et al. (2022). Our study showed initially elevated PTSS scores that remained consistently, while in the study by Sharp et al. (2022) these scores increased over time. Different measurement time points are considered as a possible reason for this inconsistency. Whereas the present study focused on three time points within the first year after the diagnosis, the study by Sharp et al. (2022) assessed PTSS four times over a 5-year period, with symptoms increasing particularly between years 1 and 3. Nevertheless, in line with Sharp et al. (2022), our findings suggest that parents experiencing distress related to their child’s cancer are likely to remain distressed.

The second aim of this study was to identify predictors of parental PTSS trajectories. Sex, level of education, and we-disease did not prove to be significant predictors for the parents’ PTSS trajectories. While prior research has often indicated that women experience more severe PTSS trajectories (Holbrook et al., 2002; Pinquart, 2019), our study along with Sharp et al. (2022) results, suggests that fathers do not significantly differ from mothers in terms of PTSS trajectories following a childhood cancer diagnosis. This absence of significant sex differences could be attributed to the increasing involvement of fathers in childcare, as they play a significant role in their child’s experiences, potentially leading to the development of PTSS similar to those observed in mothers. The reason for the lack of significance of the level of education as a predictor may be explained by the generally high level of education and socioeconomic status in Switzerland, coupled with the broad access to therapeutic support enabled by the Swiss health care system. Notably, viewing an illness as a shared issue or challenge (we-disease appraisals) was not a significant predictor of PTSS trajectories. This could be due to the fact that the values were assessed at T1, shortly after the cancer diagnosis. The question arises as to whether the view of the disease as a shared challenge changes during the course of the disease and whether we-disease is more suitable as a predictor when measured at later time points. In contrast, high parental dyadic coping, reflecting the parents’ joint stress management, was a significant factor in the group with a low PTSS trajectory. These findings align with a study by Van Schoors et al. (2019), which also identified dyadic coping as a significant protective factor for parental mental health following a childhood cancer diagnosis. However, this study evaluated parental depression, anxiety, and stress, not parental PTSS and did not have a longitudinal design. Considering dyadic coping as a significant predictor, it is possible that we-disease appraisal may not be as crucial. This could be because dyadic coping, which encompasses we-disease appraisal, holds more importance for parental PTSS. Lastly, the child’s physical impairment over time (T1–T3) emerged as another significant predictor. If the child had low physical impairment over time, parents were more likely to show a resilient trajectory of PTSS over the time course. Physical impairment is an indicator for illness, which can trigger parental PTSS. Moreover, the child’s physical impairment is associated with more everyday stress, which in turn reduces the parents’ ability to cope with PTSS (Landolt et al., 2003; Sloper, 2000).

This study has various strengths, particularly the prospective longitudinal design that allows insights into the critical period of the first 12 months following the diagnosis. Moreover, causal effects over time can better be examined with such a design. Furthermore, this study included both mothers and fathers, which is still an exception in this field of research (Fisher et al., 2023). Nevertheless, this study has also some limitations. First, parental PTSS was assessed only after their child’s cancer diagnosis. It remains unclear whether parents had PTSD/PTSS or other mental disorders prior to the diagnosis which could potentially have influenced their PTSS trajectories. Secondly, the study did not assess whether parents underwent psychotherapeutic treatment during the research period. Thirdly, the sample size was relatively small. In LCGA, small trajectory classes which we ignored due to their limited size, were combined into larger trajectory classes. Therefore, additional relevant trajectories might have remained uncovered. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that one of the primary reasons for participant dropouts was the perception of high stress or lack of available time. It is plausible that these factors may be related to a more challenging course of the child’s illness, potentially biasing the findings of our study in a too positive direction. A further limitation of this study was that the WDQ was validated, partially using the sample of the present manuscript. This overlap in sample usage raises potential concerns about the generalizability and robustness of the findings derived from this measure. Consequently, the results obtained from this measure should be interpreted with caution. Physical impairment as an indicator of child’s illness severity was assessed by treating physicians in this study. However, there is evidence that that the perception of disease severity is a better indicator of psychological outcomes than objective indices (e.g., Balluffi et al., 2004). Therefore, future studies should take the perception of disease severity as predictor into account. This study allowed parents to participate either as dyads or as individuals, resulting in both independent and dependent data. We analyzed the dyadic data using the same approach as for the individual data. Future studies examining relationship variables as predictors of parental PTSS should focus exclusively on dyads.

These findings hold practical implications. While the majority of parents are resilient, a small group continues to experience consistently elevated PTSS levels. It is crucial to identify parents with elevated PTSS levels shortly after diagnosis and provide them with timely medical information and psychosocial support to mitigate the risk of persisting PTSS. Particular attention should be given to parents whose child experiences severe physical impairment and who exhibit limited coping strategies as a couple. Considering the findings, enhancing dyadic coping as a valuable resource within the therapeutic context is recommended. Results suggest that the evidence-based Couples Coping Enhancement Training (CCET; Bodenmann & Shantinath, 2004), which has proven to be an efficacious program for enhancing dyadic coping, could be adapted for parents to better deal with the illness of their child. The aim of the CCET is to help both partners acquire new adaptive behaviors and to strengthen existing ones. The program is 18 hr and consists of six modules: knowledge of stress and coping, improvement of individual coping, enhancement of dyadic coping, exchange and fairness in the relationship, improvement of martial communication, and improvement of problem-solving skills (Bodenmann & Shantinath, 2004). As studies on PTSD and dyadic coping already indicated the importance of this concept in the treatment of PTSD (e.g., Lambert et al., 2015, 2018), an adaptation of CCET for PTSD and particularly parents confronted to the cancer diagnosis of their child is easily feasible. Future investigations concerning PTSS trajectories among parents after a childhood cancer diagnosis could benefit from analyzing only dependent samples of parents, evaluating trajectories within larger samples and shorter intervals during the critical first year. In addition to dyadic coping within the relationship, perceived social support outside a relationship was shown to be predictive of parental PTSS (Manne et al., 2000). Future studies should also focus on social support, examining the combined effect of relationship variables such as dyadic coping and we-disease, and social support from outside the relationship on parental PTSS trajectories.

In conclusion, this study identified two distinct trajectories of parental PTSS during the first year following a childhood cancer diagnosis, highlighting the child’s physical impairment and levels of parental dyadic coping as clinical relevant factors for severe parental PTSS levels.
