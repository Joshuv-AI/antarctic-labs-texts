Entropy of Soft Random Geometric Graphs in General Geometries
Oliver Baker, Carl P. Dettmann

Abstract
We study the effect of the choice of embedding geometry on the entropy of random geometric graph ensembles with soft connection functions. First we show that when the connection range is small, the entropy is dependent only on the dimension of the geometry and not the shape, but for large connection ranges the boundaries of the domain matter. Next, we formulate the problem of estimating entropy as a problem of estimating the average degree of a graph with the binary entropy function as its connection function. We use this formulation to study the effect of boundaries on the entropy, and to estimate the entropy of soft random geometric graphs in complicated geometries where a closed form pair distance density is not available.

Waxman’s model can be modified to allow for more general connection functions. We call this model the

,

and entropy

,

]

,
11
]
. In statistical physics, the canonical ensemble of spatial networks is the one that maximises the entropy with respect to a set of constraints. This results in an SRGG ensemble whose connection function takes a Fermi-Dirac form

,

,

In this work, we quantify the effect of the embedding geometry on the entropy of the SRGG. In Section

A universality result for SRGG ensembles in the small connection range limit (

A scaling law for the entropy in the large connection range limit, where the embedding geometry

contribute

These scaling laws imply a qualitative gap in compressibility between SRGG and Erdős–Rényi ensembles in the short-range regime.

In Section

Preliminaries

2.1
Asymptotic notation

We begin with a brief overview of the asymptotic notation used in this paper. Given two real-valued functions
f
and
g
, we say
f(x)=\mathcal{O}\left(g(x)\right)
as
x\rightarrow\infty
if there exist constants
c\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and
x_{0}
in the domain of
f
and
g
such that
f(x)\leq cg(x)
for all
x>x_{0}
. Likewise,
f(x)=\mathcal{O}\left(g(x)\right)
as
x\rightarrow 0
if
f(x)\leq cg(x)
for all
x<x_{0}
. If
f(x)=\mathcal{O}\left(g(x)\right)
and
g(x)=\mathcal{O}\left(f(x)\right)
then we write
f(x)=\Theta(g(x))
(or equivalently,
g(x)=\Theta(f(x))
. If
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)/g(x)=0
, then we say
f(x)=o(g(x))
as
x\rightarrow\infty
, and if
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}f(x)/g(x)=0
, then we say
f(x)=o(g(x))
as
x\rightarrow 0
. Finally, if
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}f(x)/g(x)=1
, then we write
f(x)\sim g(x)
as
x\rightarrow\infty
(and similarly for
x\rightarrow 0
).

2.2
Soft Random Geometric Graphs

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mu)
be a probability space equipped with a metric induced by the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
, and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Except for Section
4.2.2
, the measure
\mu
will represent the Lebesgue measure. Define
D=\sup_{x,y\in\Omega}\|x-y\|
, the
diameter
of the domain
\Omega
. A
Soft Random Geometric Graph
(SRGG) is a graph
G=(V,E)
where
V=\{1,...,n\}
, and
E
is constructed as follows. Distribute points
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in V}
in
\Omega
according to probability measure
\mu
. Let
r_{ij}:=\|Z_{i}-Z_{j}\|
and
p:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow[0,1]
, then we will include each edge
(i,j)
in the edge set
E
independently with probability
p(r_{ij}/r_{0})
where
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is known as the
typical connection range
. If we take
p(r/r_{0})=\mathbb{I}(r/r_{0}<1)
then we recover the classical
hard
random geometric graph, and if we take
p(r/r_{0})=q
for a constant
q
, the SRGG is equivalent to the Erdős-Rényi (ER) random graph
G(n,q)
.

There are several standard choices for the connection function
p
. In the wireless communications literature, a standard choice is the connection function

p(r/r_{0})=\exp\left(-\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)^{\eta}\right)

(1)

which is known as the
Rayleigh fading
model, and is derived through the signal to noise ratio of a wireless channel (see e.g.
[
18
]
), which is governed by the
path-loss exponent

\eta
which tends to be between 2 and 6 in practical applications. This model is also of theoretical interest because
\eta=0
gives the ER graph, and
\eta\rightarrow\infty
recovers the hard RGG. Other popular choices of connection function in other fields include the Fermi-Dirac connection function
p(r/r_{0})=(1+\exp(\alpha+r/r_{0}))^{-1}
which is derived from maximum entropy statistics of the SRGG model
[
4
]
, and
non-homogeneous
connection functions like
\tilde{p}(x/r_{0},y/r_{0})=W_{x}W_{y}\min\left(1,\left(\frac{\|x-y\|}{r_{0}}\right)^{-\alpha}\right)
where
W_{x},W_{y}
are
weights
associated with
x
and
y
. Connection functions like
\tilde{p}
can give us a
power-law
degree distribution (see e.g.
[
16
]
). Many other connection functions are explored in
[
12
]
.

To fix some notation, we denote by
f(r)
the probability density function of the distance between two random points distributed according to
\mu
in
\Omega
,
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{2}}
to be the random vector representing the distances between each pair of
n
random points in
\Omega
. We note that the function
f
is not well defined for the Cantor set, see Section
4.2.2
.
\mathcal{G}(n,r_{0},\eta)
is the ensemble of SRGGs on
n
nodes with a Rayleigh fading connection function with parameters
r_{0}
and
\eta
. We will abbreviate this to
\mathcal{G}
when the parameters are either clear from context or unimportant in the context. Finally, denote by
X_{ij}
the Bernoulli random variable which represents the existence of edge
(i,j)
in the SRGG.

In general we will consider connection functions of the form
p=p(r/r_{0})
, i.e. those that take the ratio of the distance and connection range as the argument. The exception is in Section
4.2.1
, where we will make the dependence on
r_{0}
implicit and write for example
p(r)=\exp(-(r/r_{0})^{\eta})
. This is because in this section, we will treat connection functions with different connection range parameters as different connection functions, rather than treating
r_{0}
as a parameter that we can take limits with respect to.

2.3
Entropy of Soft Random Geometric Graphs

If
\mathcal{G}
is an ensemble of random graphs on
n
nodes (not necessarily SRGGs), we define the
entropy
of the ensemble as

H(\mathcal{G}):=-\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\mathbb{P}\left(G\right)\log\mathbb{P}\left(G\right)

(2)

where the logarithm is to base
e
. In
[
10
]
some simple bounds were derived for the entropy of the SRGG ensemble. First, we must note that the entropy of the ensemble
G
is equivalent to the joint entropy of the edge variables,
H(\{X_{ij}\}_{i<j})
where
X_{ij}=1
if
(i,j)\in E
and is 0 otherwise. Then, using Shannon’s independence bound we obtain

H(\mathcal{G})\leq\sum_{i<j}h_{2}(\mathbb{P}\left(X_{ij}=1\right))=\binom{n}{2}h_{2}(\bar{p})

(3)

where
h_{2}(p)=-p\log p-(1-p)\log(1-p)
is the binary entropy function, and
\bar{p}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}p(\|x-y\|/r_{0})dxdy
is the
average connection probability
. We may also write
\bar{p}=\int_{0}^{D}f(r)p(r)dr
, where
f
is the density function of the distance between two random points in
\Omega
. As a lower bound, we condition on the locations of nodes, or equivalently on the pair distances between nodes. We refer to
[
10
]
for a more in-depth explanation.

H(\mathcal{G})\geq H(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})=\binom{n}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}h_{2}(p(\|x-y\|/r_{0}))dxdy

(4)

It was shown in
[
19
]
that (
4
) is also the limiting entropy-per-edge of the SRGG ensemble as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Specifically,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H(\mathcal{G})}{\binom{n}{2}}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}h_{2}(p(\|x-y\|/r_{0}))dxdy

(5)

These results motivate that
H(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})
is an important object to study in the field of SRGG entropy. To ease notation, we will introduce the conditional entropy-per-edge of the SRGG, which we denote as
\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R}):=\binom{n}{2}^{-1}H(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})
. We will investigate first how this quantity scales in the limiting cases of very small and very large typical connection ranges.

3
Entropy Scaling in the Connection Range

3.1
Small Connection Range

In this section, we will analyse the initial slope of the conditional entropy of SRGGs. Whilst we restrict the connection function here to the case of Rayleigh fading, we maintain significant generality in the domain
\Omega
. We will assume only the following, which will give us an asymptotic expression for the distance distribution for small
r
.

Assumption 1
.

The domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

A1.

has a non-empty interior

A2.

has a boundary that is piecewise smooth, has a finite number of corners, and is of finite length.

We also stress that, with
D:=\sup\{\|x-y\|:x,y\in\Omega\}
as the diameter of
\Omega
and
f(r)
as the density of pairwise distances in
\Omega
, the integral in (
4
) can be written as

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0},\eta)|\mathcal{R})=\int_{0}^{D}f(r)h_{2}(p(r/r_{0}))dr

(6)

which, as in
[
10
]
, is (once multiplied by
\binom{n}{2}
) the entropy of an SRGG conditioned on the node positions. Note that we have dropped the dependence on
n
, since this quantity is independent of the number of nodes. Therefore, as well as graphs in the limit of high density, the result below will hold for the entropy of an SRGG where we know the node locations. We will now prove that for small connection ranges
r_{0}
, the conditional entropy of SRGGs with Rayleigh connection functions scales as
\Theta(r_{0}^{d})
, where
d
is the dimension of the domain. More precisely:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\Omega
satisfy the conditions of Assumption
1
, and
0<\eta<\infty
. Then

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0},\eta)|\mathcal{R})\sim\frac{s_{d-1}r_{0}^{d}}{\eta}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{\eta}\right)\left(\frac{d}{\eta}+\zeta\left(\frac{d}{\eta}+1\right)-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{k^{-\frac{d}{\eta}}}{k-1}\right)

(7)

as
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
, where
s_{d-1}
is the surface area of the unit
d
-ball,
\Gamma
is the gamma function and
\zeta
is the Riemann-Zeta function.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

The result of this theorem is predicated on the fact that for a suitably regular domain, the small-scale distance density (
f(r)
for small
r
) behaves like
s_{d-1}r^{d-1}
. For some geometries, we are able to also find the next highest term in the expansion. For example, when
\Omega=[0,1]
,
f(r)=2-2r
. This means that we can obtain a better approximation to the conditional entropy. Following exactly the same procedure as in the proof of Theorem
1
, we find that for
r_{0}
small,

\displaystyle\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0},\eta)|\mathcal{R})\approx\frac{s_{d-1}r_{0}^{d}}{\eta}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{\eta}\right)\left(\frac{d}{\eta}+\zeta\left(\frac{d}{\eta}+1\right)-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{k^{-\frac{d}{\eta}}}{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{a_{d}r_{0}^{d+1}}{\eta}\Gamma\left(\frac{d+1}{\eta}\right)\left(\frac{d+1}{\eta}+\zeta\left(\frac{d+1}{\eta}+1\right)-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{k^{-\frac{d+1}{\eta}}}{k-1}\right)

(8)

where
a_{d}
is the coefficient of
r^{d}
in
f(r)
. The asymptotic in the conclusion of Theorem
1
is independent of the choice of
\Omega
, except for the dimension of the space. This kind of result is not unique to the Rayleigh fading connection function. The main idea in the proof is to approximate the pair distance density
f(r)
with the leading order monomial
f(r)=s_{d-1}r^{d-1}
. The coefficient in Theorem
1
comes from the expression

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})\sim\int_{0}^{\sqrt{r_{0}}}s_{d-1}r^{d-1}h_{2}(p(r/r_{0}))dr

(9)

After a change of variables
t=r/r_{0}
we find that we will see the dimension-dependent growth if and only if

\frac{1}{s_{d-1}r_{0}^{d}}\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})\sim\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{0}}}}t^{d-1}h_{2}(p(t))dt\in(0,\infty)

(10)

As an example, the Rayleigh fading connection functions satisfy this property, as does the Fermi-Dirac connection function. So by the same reasoning as in Theorem 1, there exists a constant
C_{p,d}
so that the Fermi-Dirac connection function behaves as
C_{p.d}r_{0}^{d}
as
r_{0}\to 0
. The power-law connection function behaves differently. For the power-law connection function

p_{PL}(r/r_{0})=\min(1,(r/r_{0})^{-\alpha})

(11)

the integral in (
10
) is

\int_{1}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{0}}}}t^{d-1}h_{2}(p_{PL}(t))dt=\int_{1}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{0}}}}t^{d-1}(-t\log t^{-\alpha}-(1-t^{-\alpha})\log(1-t^{-\alpha}))dt

(12)

\geq\int_{1}^{\frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{0}}}}\alpha t^{d-\alpha-1}\log tdt

(13)

which diverges whenever
d\geq\alpha
. We also have

\int_{1}^{1/\sqrt{r_{0}}}t^{d-1}h_{2}(p_{PL}(t))dt\leq 2\log 2\int_{1}^{1/\sqrt{r_{0}}}t^{d-1-\alpha}dt

(14)

which clearly only converges if
d<\alpha
.

We can conclude that for
d\geq\alpha
, the initial rate of increase of the conditional entropy is faster than
r_{0}^{d}
, but when
d<\alpha
, the conditional entropy grows at the same rate as we have seen in the Rayleigh fading and Fermi-Dirac connection functions.

\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
in Figure

The results in this section show that the limiting behaviour of the conditional entropy as

is the same for any geometry of the same dimension. Effectively, they say that the small

behaviour is determined by the nodes that are far from the boundaries, since the boundary structure of the domain does not influence the limiting entropy. We will explore this idea further in Section

, but deteriorates it when

Suppose now that

. The following result shows that there is an explicit dependence on the ‘moments’ of the domain

.

\mathcal{G}(n,r_{0},\eta)

. Then, as

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0},\eta)|\mathcal{R})=\frac{1+\eta\log r_{0}}{r_{0}^{\eta}}\mathbb{E}[R^{\eta}]-\frac{\eta}{r_{0}^{\eta}}\mathbb{E}[R^{\eta}\log R]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2\eta}}\log r_{0}\right)

3.3
Compressibility of SRGGs vs ER Graphs

In this section, we will apply the previous two sections in the area of graph compression. It is well known that for any random sequence, the fundamental lower bound for the amount of information required to represent that sequence is the entropy of the distribution that generated it. In our setting, to compress a random network ensemble
\mathcal{G}
, we require at least
H(\mathcal{G})
bits (or nats since our logarithm is to base
e
) to compress the network. A question we may want to ask is whether the SRGG is any easier to compress than other graph models, for example the Erdős-Rényi (ER) graph. Of course, the correlation between the edges in the SRGG means it has lower entropy, but can we say anything more? A common quantity in the graph compression literature used to quantify the ease of compressing a graph structure is the ‘compressibility’ of the structure
[
7
,
20
]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be an ensemble of random graphs, with entropy
H(\mathcal{G}_{n})
, and edge set
E_{n}
. The compressibility of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
is the ratio

C_{n}:=\frac{H(\mathcal{G}_{n})}{\mathbb{E}[|E_{n}|]}

(16)

We say an ensemble is compressible if
C_{n}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

It is straightforward to check that the Erdős-Rényi ensemble is compressible. Indeed,

C_{n}^{ER}=\frac{\binom{n}{2}h_{2}(p)}{\binom{n}{2}p}=\frac{h_{2}(p)}{p}=\Theta(1)

(17)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Also, the SRGG is compressible:

C_{n}^{SRGG}=\frac{H(\mathcal{G}_{n}^{SRGG})}{\binom{n}{2}\bar{p}}\geq\frac{\int_{0}^{D}f(r)h_{2}(p(r))dr}{\bar{p}}=\Theta(1)

(18)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. However, (perhaps counter-intuitively), the
sparse
ER graph is incompressible (see e.g.
[
20
]
). To construct an ER graph with the same expected proportion of links as the SRGG, we simply use
G(n,\bar{p})
. Then we are interested in estimating the quantity

\Delta C_{n}:=C_{n}^{ER}-C_{n}^{SRGG}\geq\frac{h_{2}(\bar{p})-\int_{0}^{D}f(r)h_{2}(p(r/r_{0}))dr}{\bar{p}}\geq 0

(19)

The lower bound becomes exact in the large
n
limit
[
19
]
, so we can define the compressibility difference in the large
n
limit as

\Delta C:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Delta C_{n}=\frac{h_{2}(\bar{p})-\int_{0}^{D}f(r)h_{2}(p(r))dr}{\bar{p}}

(20)

Using the asymptotic expressions derived in the previous sections leads to the following result.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an ensemble of SRGGs with a Rayleigh fading connection function with connection range
r_{0}
, path loss exponent
\eta
, and average connection probability
\bar{p}=\bar{p}(r_{0})
. Then the compressibility difference in the large
n
limit between the SRGG ensemble
\mathcal{G}
and
G(n,\bar{p})
,
\Delta C
satisfies

\lim_{r_{0}\rightarrow 0}\Delta C=\infty

(21)

\lim_{r_{0}\rightarrow\infty}\Delta C=0

(22)

Proof.

See Appendix
A.3
.
∎

Intuitively the difference between the small
r_{0}
case and the large
r_{0}
case is that when
r_{0}
is large, the connection function is effectively 1 everywhere, so there is not much difference between the SRGG and the ER graph. However, when
r_{0}
is small, the SRGG connection function maintains a very small section where the connection probability is high, and therefore maintains its high clustering which improves the compressibility. We can also say that if the connection function satisfies the integrability condition (
10
), then the divergence of
\Delta C
as
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
will also occur.

4
The Entropy Graph

In this section, we tackle the issue of estimating the conditional entropy-per-edge of the SRGG for general geometries and connection functions. We begin by writing the conditional entropy-per-edge as an expectation over distances.

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})=\int_{0}^{D}h_{2}(p(r/r_{0}))f(r)dr=\mathbb{E}_{R}[h_{2}(p(R/r_{0}))]

(23)

Now by analogy with the expression for the expected edge density of the SRGG,
\bar{p}=\int_{0}^{D}f(r)p(r)dr
, (
23
) can be thought of as the expected edge density of a SRGG with connection function
h_{2}\circ p
. We will call this graph the ‘
entropy graph
’. This observation makes the task of estimating
H(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})
significantly easier, as now instead of requiring the function
f
and numerically estimating the integral, we can now estimate the conditional entropy by a Monte-Carlo simulation of network edge counts. It also allows us to directly quantify the effect of geometric boundaries on the conditional entropy of the network ensemble.

4.1
The Effect of Boundaries for Small Connection Ranges

The formulation of the conditional entropy-per-edge
H(\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{R})
as an edge count of the network allows us to think about the network complexity in a new way. Below, we will discuss the
entropy mass
of a point
x
, which can be thought of as the ‘amount of uncertainty’ a point at
x
contributes to the overall ensemble entropy.

Definition 2

(Entropy Mass)
.

For a SRGG ensemble with connection function
p
on
\Omega
, We define the entropy mass at a point
x\in\Omega
as

H_{x}(\mathcal{G}|\mathcal{R})=\int_{\Omega}h_{2}(p(\|x-y\|))dy

(24)

Recall that in this section, we will not treat
r_{0}
as a variable, and instead treat
r_{0}
as a part of the connection function. Hence, we have not included
r_{0}
in the definition of entropy mass, and will treat connection functions that have the same functional form but different parameter

as different connection functions entirely. It is useful here to draw an analogy with the ‘connectivity mass’ from the SRGG connectivity literature. It is shown by Penrose in

(25)

Where the
connectivity mass

M(x)=\int_{\Omega}p(\|x-y\|)dy
clearly appears in the expression. We point out that our
entropy mass
is the
connectivity mass
of a SRGG with connection function
h_{2}\circ p
. Unfortunately, our connection function
h_{2}\circ p
does not satisfy the regularity conditions set out in
[
22
]
as for example it is non-monotone. However, it is still a useful object to study. In
[
12
]
, a general formula for the connectivity mass is given in terms of the boundary components of the domain. The results of the next few sections can be viewed as an application of the methodology of
[
12
]
to the problem of graph entropy. This seems to be an unexpected use case for research into connectivity, and prompts the question of whether there are other use cases that this technology can be used for.

In Figure

H_{(1/2,1/2)}(\mathcal{G})\approx\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}h_{2}(p(\|(r\cos\phi,r\sin\phi)\|))rdrd\phi

=2\pi\int_{0}^{1}h_{2}(p(r))rdr

but a node on the middle of the edge has entropy mass 0 in the direction that points outside of the box, so its region that we need to integrate over is only the semicircle,

4.2
Entropy Estimation for General Geometries

Here, we will present two further examples that demonstrate the use of the entropy graph formalism.

4.2.1
Wedge - Corner Effects by Connectivity-Style Analysis

In this section we will follow the methodology of
[
12
]
to establish the form of the entropy mass at each point in a wedge. The idea of using a wedge geometry is that it is representative of a general boundary component in a 2D polygonal domain. We define the wedge with angle
\theta
in polar coordinates as the following set,

W_{\theta}:=\{(r,\phi):0\leq\phi\leq\theta,0\leq r\leq R\}

In
[

]

p(r)=p(0)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}a_{\alpha}r^{\alpha}

\alpha,a_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}

\rho=h_{2}\circ p

\rho(r)=\rho(0)+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}a_{\alpha}r^{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}b_{\beta}r^{\beta}\log r

(32)

where again,
b_{\beta},\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\beta>0

p(0)=1
then the constant term
\rho(0)
is 0. We denote by
\alpha_{\min}
and
\beta_{\min}
the smallest elements of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
respectively. This means that the methods of
[
12
]
do not immediately apply, but the general principles still work, with a little extra effort. We assume the following:

Assumption 2
.

1.

The entropy function
\rho
can be written in the form (
32
)

2.

\rho
is piecewise smooth with nonsmooth points at a discrete and possibly empty set
\{r_{k}\}
for

, where

is the number of such discontinuities.

3.

The integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}r\rho(r)dr
is finite.

4.

All derivatives of
\rho(r)
are monotone for sufficiently large
r
.

Under this assumption, we will show that the entropy mass for a polar point
(r,\omega)
in the wedge

situated close to the corner is given by

H_{(r,\omega)}(\mathcal{G})=\theta\rho_{1}+\rho_{0}r(\sin\omega+\sin\omega^{\prime})+\rho(0)\frac{r^{2}}{2}(\sin\omega\cos\omega+\sin\omega^{\prime}\cos\omega^{\prime})+\mathcal{O}\left(r^{3},r^{\alpha_{\min}+2},r^{\beta_{\min}+2}\log r\right)

where

The derivation is given in Appendix

. It is interesting to compare the results to those in
[

. In fact, the form of the entropy mass (

7
, but are very cumbersome so are not reproduced here. It is only at the
\mathcal{O}\left(r^{3},r^{\alpha_{\min}}.r^{\beta_{\min}}\log r\right)
term where we see the first

term, which is the first difference between the entropy mass and the connectivity mass.

4.2.2
Cantor Set - Complicated Geometry with Unavailable
f(r)

In this section, we consider an SRGG embedded in the Cantor set (or equivalently, an SRGG in
[0,1]
with Cantor-distributed nodes). We define the Cantor set with ratio
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
\alpha>2
iteratively as follows. We start with
\mathcal{C}_{0}:=[0,1]
. Then, we remove all but the leftmost and rightmost
\alpha
-ths of the set, so that

\mathcal{C}_{1}:=[0,1/\alpha]\cup[1-1/\alpha,1]

(34)

In general, defining
L(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha}x
and
R(x)=1-\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\alpha}x
, we have
\mathcal{C}_{k}=L(\mathcal{C}_{k-1})\cup R(\mathcal{C}_{k-1})
, and
\mathcal{C}=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{C}_{k}
. In Figure
6
, the entropy curves for this choice of
\Omega
are shown. This is an example of when the entropy graph formalism is useful for estimating conditional entropy when we do not have
f(r)
. This is also an interesting counterexample to the intuition that the entropy curves should be unimodal. The binary entropy function
h_{2}
is unimodal, but this does not mean the entropy of the SRGG is.

The goal here is to explain why the conditional entropy of the SRGG in the
\mathcal{C}_{k}
for a large
k
displays these log-periodic oscillations, with a ‘gradient’ of the Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set, which is
\log 2/\log(\alpha)

[
13
]
. It is important to note that the usual definition of the uniform distribution, that is, taking the normalised Lebesgue measure, will not work here, since the Cantor set is a set of zero Lebesgue measure. Therefore, we will define the uniform distribution on the Cantor set as the normalised
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\mathcal{C}
, where here
d=\log(2)/\log(\alpha)

[
13
]
. For convenience we will denote by
F(r)
now the CDF of the displacement between two random points, that is, if
X,Y
are random points in
\mathcal{C}
, then
F
is the distribution of
R:=X-Y
.

Figure 6:
Left: The entropy curve of a SRGG with Rayleigh connection functions with
\eta=4
for
\alpha=1/3,1/4,1/6,1/10
. Right: The same curves on a log-log plot. The gradient of the line connecting the local maxima in the log-log plot is
\dim_{H}(\mathcal{C})
.

Our aim is to explain the log-periodic oscillatory behaviour of the entropy curve in Figure
6
. Provided that the connection function satisfies some decay and differentiability conditions (Assumption
3
in Appendix
B.2.3
), we will show that the conditional entropy-per-edge
\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})
may be written as

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})\sim 2r_{0}^{d}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{2}+2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}R_{m}\cos\left(\theta_{m}+2\pi m\frac{\log r_{0}}{\log{\alpha}}\right)\right)

(35)

as
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
where for
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
R_{m},\theta_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
are constants to be determined. We begin with the function

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})=\int_{-1}^{1}h_{2}(p(|r|/r_{0}))dF(r)

(36)

=2\int_{0}^{1}\rho(r)dF(r)

(37)

where we have introduced the function
\rho(r)=h_{2}(p(|r|/r_{0}))
. For
s\in\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathcal{M}[\rho;s]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho(r)r^{s-1}ds
be the Mellin transform of
\rho
, and for
s\in\mathbb{C}
, define the Cantor moment
C[F;s]:=\int_{0}^{1}r^{s}dF(r)
. The next results will allow us to swap the order of integration. They may also be of general use in the study of distance distributions in the Cantor set.

Lemma 1
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{[}0,1]
, then

F(r)=\frac{1}{2}F(\alpha r)+\frac{1}{4}F(\alpha r-(\alpha-1))+\frac{1}{4}F(\alpha r+(\alpha-1))

(38)

Proof.

See Appendix
B.2.1
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Im(s)\neq\frac{2\pi im}{\log\alpha}
for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. The Cantor distance moments
C[F;s]
are given by

C[F;-s]=\frac{1}{(2\alpha^{-s}-1)}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{2l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-2l}C[F;2l]

(39)

where for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\binom{z}{k}=\frac{(z)_{l}}{k!}
, with
(z)_{k}=z\cdot(z-1)\cdot...\cdot(z-k+1)
as the Pöchhammer symbol. Further
C[F;-s]
is a meromorphic function.

Proof.

See Appendix
B.2.2
.
∎

By Lemma
2

C[F;-s]
has simple poles at
-s_{m}=-\frac{\log 2}{\log\alpha}-\frac{2\pi im}{\log\alpha}
for every
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. In particular, this means it is analytic on the half-planes
\Re(s)<\frac{\log 2}{\log\alpha}
and
\Re(s)>\frac{\log 2}{\log\alpha}
. Using a change of variables
t=r/r_{0}
, it is easy to see that

\mathcal{M}[\rho;s]=r_{0}^{s}\psi(s)

(40)

where
\psi(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{s}h_{2}(p(t))dt
, which we have assumed to be integrable for
\Re(s)>0
(Assumption
3
). So, for
\Re(s)>0
we can use the Cauchy-Riemann equations to show that
\psi(s)
is also analytic.
Then,
\mathcal{M}[\rho;s]
and
C[F;-s]
have a common strip of analyticity, and therefore Parseval’s theorem for the Mellin transform allows us to write

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})=\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{c_{l}-i\infty}^{c_{l}+i\infty}M[\rho;s]C[F;-s]ds

(41)

where
c_{l}
is a real number in the interval
(0,\log(2)/\log(\alpha))
. We may use the residue theorem on a rectangular contour surrounding the poles
s_{m}
. The details are technical, and so are relegated to Appendix
B.2.3
.

Theorem 4
.

The conditional entropy-per-edge of the SRGG with a connection function satisfying Assumption
3
in the Cantor set is given by

\overline{H}(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})=2r_{0}^{d}\left(\frac{R_{0}}{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}R_{m}\cos\left(\theta_{m}+2\pi m\frac{\log r_{0}}{\log\alpha}\right)\right)+o(r_{0}^{d})

(42)

as
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
, where
d=\log(2)/\log(\alpha)
, and
R_{m}
and
\theta_{m}
are the modulus and argument of
\frac{\psi(s_{m})}{2\log\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}(t+\alpha-1)^{-s_{m}}dF(r)
respectively.

Proof.

See Appendix
B.2.3
.
∎

We can make a number of observations. It can be clearly seen that the leading-order behaviour of the conditional entropy as
r_{0}\rightarrow 0
is a log-periodic oscillation. Also, this result clearly mirrors Theorem
1
, since the leading order behaviour is a constant multiplied by
r_{0}^{d}
, except here
d
is the Hausdorff dimension of the Cantor set.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we have studied the entropy of a family of soft random geometric graph ensembles in the limiting cases of a very small and very large connection range. In Section
3
we derived the asymptotic scaling of the conditional entropy for SRGGs with Rayleigh fading connection functions. We showed that in the small
r_{0}
limit the conditional entropy scales as
\Theta(r_{0}^{d})
for general connection functions provided they satisfy an integrability condition. It is very likely that this can be used to derive the limiting conditional entropy for sparse ensembles whose connection ranges vary with
n
. We then used these results to show that the difference in the amount of conditional entropy-per-edge between ER graphs and SRGGs diverges when the probability of a connection gets small, but goes to 0 for large connection ranges. This suggests that algorithms to compress SRGGs should take advantage of the clustering present in the networks.

In Section
4
we introduced the ‘entropy graph’ as an object to study SRGG entropy. The quantity
H(\mathcal{G}(r_{0})|\mathcal{R})
may be written as an expectation over distances which corresponds to an edge-level property of the SRGG. This allows us to approach the problem of estimating the conditional entropy of the SRGG as an average degree problem. We demonstrated that this formalism is useful when
f(r)
is unavailable due to the complexity of the underlying geometry by characterising the conditional entropy in a general boundary component, and in a fractal domain. Potential applications of this idea include designing sensor networks in complicated domains where we want to measure the uncertainty of the global network structure. In particular, it suggests that in a random spatial network, there is location-induced heterogeneity in the amount of information each node requires to represent. Finally, we note that this idea opens up the field of spatial network entropy to approaches involving stochastic geometry, and motivates the study of soft random geometric graphs with non-monotone connection functions.

as

Proof.

r=x-y=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x_{n}-y_{n}}{\alpha^{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{w_{n}}{\alpha^{n}}

where

with

uniformly distributed in the Cantor set as

\mathbb{P}\left(R<r\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{W_{n}}{\alpha^{n}}<t\right)

(129)

where now

independently with probability
1/4,1/2,1/4
respectively. Now since this sum is absolutely convergent, we can express this probability as

\mathbb{P}\left(R<r\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{W_{1}}{\alpha}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{W_{n}}{\alpha^{n}}<r\right)

B.2.2
Proof of Lemma
2

The proof of this result uses the change of variables formula for Lebesgue integration.

Lemma 5

(Change of Variables Formula
[
6
]
)
.

A measurable function
g
on
X_{2}
is integrable with respect to the pushforward measure
f*\mu=\mu\circ f^{-1}
if and only if the composition
g\circ f
is integrable with respect to the measure
\mu
. In that case, the integrals coincide, i.e.,

\int_{X_{2}}gd(f*\mu)=\int_{X_{1}}g\circ fd\mu

(133)

where
X_{1}=f^{-1}(X_{2})

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We have

C[F;-s]=\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(r)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(\alpha r)+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(\alpha r+(\alpha-1))+\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(\alpha r-(\alpha-1))

(134)

Using the change of variables formula, we can re-write each integral in turn:

\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(\alpha r)=\int_{0}^{\alpha}(\alpha r)^{-s}dF(r)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(r)

(135)

=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}C[F;-s]

(136)

where the second inequality follows because
dF(r)=0
when
r>1
(and
\alpha>2
by its definition). Then, the second integral is

\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(\alpha r+(\alpha-1))=\int_{\alpha-1}^{2\alpha-1}\left(\frac{r-(\alpha-1)}{\alpha}\right)^{s}dF(r)=0

(137)

since
\alpha-1>1
. Finally,

\int_{0}^{1}r^{-s}dF(\alpha-(\alpha-1))=\int_{1-\alpha}^{1}\left(\frac{r+(\alpha-1)}{\alpha}\right)^{-s}dF(r)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\int_{-1}^{1}(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)

(138)

We can further write this as

\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\int_{-1}^{1}(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\left[\int_{-1}^{0}(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)+\int_{0}^{1}(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)\right]

(139)

=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}(-r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)+\int_{0}^{1}(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)\right]

(140)

where we have used the change of variable formula and the fact that
dF(r)=dF(-r)
again on the first integral. Now, note that
(\alpha-1)-r
is positive for
r\in[0,1]
, as is
r+(\alpha-1)
. Therefore, we can perform a Taylor expansion of the integrands as follows:

(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}=(\alpha-1)^{-s}\left(\frac{r}{\alpha-1}+1\right)^{-s}

(141)

=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{l}r^{l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-l}

(142)

and likewise

(-r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{l}(-r)^{l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-l}

(143)

where we have the generalised binomial coefficient

\binom{-s}{l}=\frac{(-s)(-s-1)\cdots(-s-l)}{l!}=\frac{(-s)_{l}}{l!}

(144)

where
(\cdot)_{l}
is the Pochhammer symbol. So, substituting back into (
140
) gives

\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\int_{-1}^{1}(r+(\alpha-1))^{-s}dF(r)=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-l}\int_{0}^{1}\left[r^{l}+(-r)^{l}\right]dF(r)

(145)

=\frac{1}{\alpha^{-s}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-l}(C[F;l]+(-1)^{l}C{[F;l])}

(146)

Combining the three integrals together gives

C[F;-s]=\frac{1}{2\alpha^{-s}}C[F;-s]+\frac{1}{4\alpha^{-s}}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-l}(C[F;l]+(-1)^{l}C{[F;l])}

(147)

and rearranging gives

C[F;-s]=\frac{1}{(2\alpha^{-s}-1)}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\binom{-s}{2l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-2l}C[F;2l]

(148)

It remains to show that this sum is well-defined, that is, convergent. First, it is easy to see that for any positive integer
l
,

C[F;l]=\int_{0}^{1}r^{l}dF(r)\leq\int_{0}^{1}dF(r)=\frac{1}{2}

(149)

and so

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left|\binom{-s}{2l}(\alpha-1)^{-s-2l}C[F;2l]\right|\leq\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\left|\binom{-s}{2l}\right|(\alpha-1)^{-\Re(s)-2l}

(150)

now for a fixed
s
, we have

(\alpha-1)^{-\Re(s)-2l}\left|\binom{-s}{2l}\right|\sim\frac{(-1)^{2l}}{\Gamma(s)}(2l)^{\Re(s)-1}(\alpha-1)^{-\Re(s)-2l}

(151)

which decays exponentially fast as
l\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, the sum converges absolutely.
∎
