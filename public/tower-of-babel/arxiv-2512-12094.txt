Leveraging Symmetry Merging in Pauli Propagation
Yanting Teng, Su Yeon Chang, Manuel S. Rudolph, Zoë Holmes

Abstract
We introduce a symmetry-adapted framework for simulating quantum dynamics based on Pauli propagation. When a quantum circuit possesses a symmetry, many Pauli strings evolve redundantly under actions of the symmetry group. We exploit this by merging Pauli strings related through symmetry transformations. This procedure, formalized as the symmetry-merging Pauli propagation algorithm, propagates only a minimal set of orbit representatives. Analytically, we show that symmetry merging reduces space complexity by a factor set by orbit sizes, with explicit gains for translation and permutation symmetries. Numerical benchmarks of all-to-all Heisenberg dynamics confirm improved stability, particularly under truncation and noise. Our results establish a group-theoretic framework for enhancing Pauli propagation, supported by open-source code demonstrating its practical relevance for classical quantum-dynamics simulations.

The Role of Symmetry.

Consider a unitary
U
composed of
L
layers
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}
. For instance, the
l
-th unitary layer
U_{l}
can be a trotterized Hamiltonian layer
U_{l}=\prod_{j}e^{-ih^{(1)}_{j}t}e^{-ih^{(2)}_{j}t}
for the translational invariant Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{j}h^{(1)}_{j}+h^{(2)}_{j}
or only a sublayer satisfying the symmetry
U_{l}=\prod_{j}e^{-ih^{(1)}_{j}t}
. Let
G
be a finite symmetry group and
A_{g}
be the representation of
g\in G
. Each layer is invariant under
G
defined by the
group action

g\cdot(\,)
:

g\cdot(U_{l})=U_{l}\Longleftrightarrow A_{g}U_{l}A_{g}^{-1}={U_{l}},\quad\forall\,g\in G.

(1)

The main goal of this paper is to more efficiently determine how operators evolve under these symmetric unitaries.
If the initial state (
\rho
) is also symmetric such that
g\cdot(\rho)=\rho
, then expectation values of normalized Pauli strings
s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}:=1/\sqrt{2^{n}}\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
related by a symmetry transformation are identical by the cyclic property of trace,

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,s]=\mathrm{Tr}[g\cdot(\rho)s]=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,g\cdot(s)],\quad\forall g\in G.

(2)

Intuitively, evolving a Pauli string
g\cdot(s)
related by symmetry is the same as evolving
s
.

Under the evolution of a symmetric unitary, this redundancy allows observables to be computed from a reduced set of representative
n
-qubit Pauli strings, denoted as
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
. While we focus on unitary dynamics, our framework generalizes for open quantum systems. We illustrate this flexibility in our numerical example by adding noise layers modeling a local Lindbladian dynamics
32
;
11
.

II
Symmetry-merging Pauli propagation

Given an initial state
\rho
and the evolution unitary
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}
with discrete symmetry
G
, our goal is to compute the expectation value of the evolved target observable
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,U^{\dagger}OU]
.
At a high level, the
standard PP
algorithm evolves a target observable
O
using the Heisenberg equation of motion. The evolved operator expectation value can be expanded in the Pauli basis leading to a
Pauli path integral
,

\displaystyle U^{\dagger}OU=\sum_{s_{0},\cdots s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[s_{L}O]\prod_{l=1}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[s_{l-1}U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}]s_{0}.

(3)

Here, the summation over normalized Pauli strings
s_{l}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
enumerates all possible paths during the evolution (see Supplementary Materials
A
).
Here we propose avoiding redundant computations by tracking only a minimal set of representative Pauli strings from each orbit under
G
by introducing
symmetry merging
.

More precisely, consider any Pauli strings
s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
throughout the evolution, we use
\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s):=\{g\cdot(s)\,|\,g\in G\}
to denote the
Pauli orbits
of
s
under the group action defined in Eq. (
1
). The
Pauli orbit representatives
, denoted as
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n}
, provide a natural minimal set of Pauli strings that need to keep track of. The orbits of a full set of orbit representatives form a disjoint union of all Pauli strings
\mathcal{P}_{n}=\coprod_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s)
.

For example, as sketched in Fig.
1
, we consider a
3
-qubit system under symmetry group
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
, represented by translation operators
\hat{I},\hat{T},\hat{T}^{2}
. Here, the group action is defined by translating a Pauli string to the left, e.g.
\hat{T}\cdot(IXI)=XII
. The Pauli orbit of an (unnormalized) string
XII
is
\operatorname{Orb}_{\mathbb{Z}_{3}}(XII)=\{IIX,IXI,XII\}
as illustrated in Fig.
1
a
. The set of complete orbit representatives
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}=1/\sqrt{2^{n}}\{III,XII,YII,ZII,\dots,XXX,\cdots\}
contains
24
elements and is given by Example
3
. For more detailed definitions and a review of group theory, we refer the reader to Supplementary Materials
B
.

At a high level, symmetry PP involves two subroutines: a) perform standard PP; b) merge Paulis to their orbit representatives
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
(Fig.
1
b
and Fig.
1
c
). It is formally given by Algorithm
1
.
In particular, the merging subroutine of step
2
identifies all Pauli strings equivalent under symmetry transformations and merges them into their corresponding representative of their orbit. The key intuition is that when a quantum circuit exhibits symmetry, many Pauli strings contribute to the expectation value equally under the unitary transformation.

E
, we outline merging subroutines for step
5
and discuss their trade-offs compared to standard PP. More specifically in Supplementary Materials
E.2

III
Analytical guarantees

We provide rigorous justifications to symmetry PP algorithm outlined in the previous section. First, we show that symmetry PP outputs the correct result for the dynamics of an operator in Theorem
1
. Then we compare its space complexity comparing to the standard PP in Theorem
2
. Additionally, we comment on time complexity in Supplementary Materials
D
.

Algorithm 1

Symmetry-merging Pauli propagation (symmetry PP)

1.

Prior to evolution, merge Paulis that are related by a symmetry transformation such that

\widetilde{O}_{L}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[O\,g\cdot(s)]s\,.

(4)

2.

Symmetry-merging propagation: For each layer
l=L,L-1,\cdots,1
, to the following:

(a)

Compute the Heisenberg evolved observable
O_{l-1}=U_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{O}_{l}U_{l}
in the Pauli basis.

(b)

Merge Paulis by orbit representatives
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
:

\widetilde{O}_{l-1}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[O_{l-1}\,g\cdot(s)]s.

(5)

3.

Compute the inner product between the initial state and the merged observable
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,\widetilde{O}_{0}]
.

Theorem 1

(
Symmetry PP
)
.

Let
\rho
be a symmetric initial state and
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}
be the
L
-layer unitary. Suppose each
l
-th layer has a discrete symmetry
G
with representations
A_{g}
such that
\left[A_{g},\,\rho\right]=\left[A_{g},\,U_{l}\right]=0,\forall g\in G
. Let
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,U^{\dagger}OU]
be the target expectation value of an observable
O
. Then the expectation value of the merged operator given by Algorithm
1
as
\widetilde{O}_{0}
equals the target value

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,\widetilde{O}_{0}]=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,U^{\dagger}OU].

(6)

Computing the expectation value using only the Pauli representatives is sufficient under symmetric evolutions.
We provide a proof of Theorem
1
in Supplementary Materials
C
. While the present theorem is applicable to exact evolution, we perform numerical experiments and analyze the effects of truncations commonly adopted in standard PP. A major limiting factor of standard PP is the memory computational cost of storing Pauli strings being propagated. We now discuss the key advantage of memory saving using symmetry PP.

Theorem 2

(
Space complexity
(informal))
.

Let
G
be the discrete symmetry group of an
n
-qubit system. The symmetry PP defined by Algorithm
1
reduces the space complexity lower bounded by a ratio

r=\frac{\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert}{4^{n}}<1,

(7)

compared to standard PP, where
\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert
corresponds to the number of Pauli orbit representatives.

A smaller ratio represents more saving on Pauli strings. The lower bound
r
is saturated when the evolved operator spans the full operator space, typically relevant for a long-time evolution under ergodic quantum dynamics.
The number of orbit representatives
\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert
depends on the symmetry group
G
governing the system. While it is difficult to generalize the bound as a function of the number of qubits
n
, we have found that in the asymptotic limit of
n\gg 1
, the space saving ratio is inversely proportional to the group size
r\in\Theta(\frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert})
. In the following, we give two examples and provide their proofs with a summary of space complexity for different groups in Supplementary Materials

for interested readers.

Example 1

(Translation symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
in
1d
)
.

Consider the translational symmetry group
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
on
n
qubits. If
n
is a prime number,

r=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{4^{n}+4(n-1))}{4^{n}}\right).

(8)

For
n\gg 1
, we have a linear space saving
r\in\Theta(\frac{1}{n})
.

Example 2

(Permutation symmetry
S_{n}
)
.

Consider the permutation group
S_{n}
. The space saving ratio is

r=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\binom{n+3}{3}\;.

(9)

For
n\gg 1
, the number of orbit representatives scales as
\Theta(n^{3})
, leading to an exponential improvement in space complexity
r\in\Theta\left(\frac{n^{3}}{4^{n}}\right)
.

IV
Numerical examples

Example: periodic 1
d
Ising model.

The tilted-field Ising model is described by the Hamiltonian:

H_{\text{Ising}}=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}-h_{z}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}-h_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},

(10)

where
\langle ij\rangle
enumerates the nearest-neighbor sites and
h_{x}(h_{z})
is the transverse (longitudinal) field. It is known that in the absence of the longitudinal field, the Hamiltonian is integrable using Jordan Wigner transformations
33
such that only a subspace is explored, which is spanned by a polynomial in
n
number of Pauli operators. Away from the integrable limit (
h_{z}=0
), this model has been widely used to study thermalization and ergodic dynamics
29
;
48
. In our numerical results, we consider the field strengths
g_{x}=1.4,g_{z}=0.9045
studied in prior works
46
. Under periodic boundary conditions, the system has the translation symmetry
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
.

The Hamiltonian dynamics is unitary and can be trotterized to
L=\frac{t}{\delta}
layers as
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\prod_{\langle ij\rangle}e^{-i\delta Z_{i}Z_{j}}\prod_{i}e^{-ih_{z}\delta Z_{i}}\prod_{i}e^{-ih_{x}\delta X_{i}}
. We only merge after a full layer of Hamiltonian trotterization, hence the merging procedure can be further improved if we merge after a minimal symmetric layer, e.g. a single layer of
ZZ
, or
Z
, or
X
Pauli gates. Consider an initial Pauli operator
Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil}
located in the middle of the periodic chain at site
\lceil n/2\rceil
, the evolution of this operator is given by
U^{\dagger}Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil}U=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}c_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}
, where
\alpha
enumerates Pauli strings
s_{\alpha}
with coefficients
c_{\alpha}
and
N
denotes the number of Pauli strings with nonzero coefficients. Using symmetry PP, the merged operator
\widetilde{Z}_{\lceil n/2\rceil}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\tilde{N}}\tilde{c}_{\alpha}\tilde{s}_{\alpha}
has a reduced number
\tilde{N}
of Pauli representatives
\tilde{s}_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
by merging contributions from symmetry-equivalent Pauli strings in the same orbit
\tilde{c}_{\alpha}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{Tr}[\hat{T}^{j}(\tilde{s}_{\alpha})U^{\dagger}Z_{\lceil n/2\rceil}U]
(see Fig.
1
b
).

In Fig.
2
, we numerically verify that
N>\tilde{N}
for all circuit layers, therefore leading to a reduced number of Pauli strings throughout the simulations as discussed in Eq. (
5
). The memory saving increases with the circuit layers as more Paulis are generated. The circuit depth necessary to generate Pauli dynamics exploring the full operator space increases with the number of qubits
n
, as seen by the shifted elbows. The reduction in the number of Paulis needed to be propagated saturates to the theoretical limit in Example
1
at high enough layers. In practice in order for Pauli propagation to be numerically feasible, the number of Paulis generated are typically truncated so that the evolved operator does not span the full operator space. To tackle the dynamics of a more difficult dynamics, we adopt numerical truncations in symmetry PP in the next example.

Example: all-to-all XXZ Heisenberg model.

To demonstrate how symmetry PP allows us to simulate physically relevant dynamics, we investigate the dynamics of the all-to-all interacting Heisenberg model with long-range power law interactions decaying with a distance defined on a
2
-dimensional lattice over an exponent
\alpha
.
This model is a rich playground, as the ground state phase diagrams exhibit a complex structure in entanglement and critical phenomena
22
. Moreover, it is also relevant for quantum simulations and metrology in atom-based platforms such as the Rydberg atom arrays, dipolar molecules and trapped ion arrays
42
;
10
;
39
. The power-law XXZ Hamiltonian is given by

H_{\text{XXZ}}=-J_{\perp}\sum_{i<j}\frac{X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}+(\Delta+1)Z_{i}Z_{j}}{|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}|^{\alpha}},

(11)

where
J_{\perp}
is the interaction strength and
\Delta
is the relative anisotropic coupling strength and
\mathbf{r}_{i}
is the position of the
i=1,\cdots,n
-th atom. For
\Delta=0
, the Hamiltonian reduces to the invariant model with
SU(2)
spin rotational symmetry. In the absence of spin-orbit coupling
\alpha=0
, the Hamiltonian also has rotation and permutation symmetry and the dynamics can continually be simulated classically.

The unitary is the trotterized Hamiltonian evolution
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\prod_{i<j}e^{-ih_{ij}\delta}
where the time step is
\delta=t/L
and
h_{ij}=-J_{\perp}\left(X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}+(\Delta+1)Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)/|\mathbf{r}_{i}-\mathbf{r}_{j}|^{\alpha}
is the local Hamiltonian operator. It is possible to merge after only evolving with sublayers to further improve the efficiency. Because pairs of Pauli operators commute with each other e.g.
\left[X_{i}X_{j},\,Y_{i}Y_{j}\right]=0
, we can further decompose the rotation into Pauli rotations as
e^{i\left(\theta_{x}X_{i}X_{j}+\theta_{y}Y_{i}Y_{j}+\theta_{z}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)}=e^{i\theta_{x}X_{i}X_{j}}e^{i\theta_{y}Y_{i}Y_{j}}e^{i\theta_{z}Z_{i}Z_{j}}
. Alternative strategies include higher order trotterization with multi product formula
16
, finding a minimum set of grouped operators
9
and randomized trotterization
12
;
15
. In the present example, we have numerically observed that the trotterization order affects the relevant observable expectation values minimally.

We will study the total spin operator
39

\mathbf{S}^{2}=\sum_{\mu=x,y,z}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\mu,i})^{2},

(12)

where
\sigma_{\mu,i}
are spin-half Pauli matrices (e.g.
\sigma_{x,i}=\frac{1}{2}X_{i}
).
The measurement operator and unitary evolution are translationally invariant, therefore we apply our merging algorithm with translation symmetries along both
x
and
y
directions independently. While there exists other specialized methods such as discrete truncated Wigner approximation
64
, this dynamics is highly non-trivial for MPS-based simulations
39
owing to the all-to-all connected circuit topology. In our naive MPO simulations, we observe difficulty in convergence for simulation time
t\bar{J}\lvert\Delta\rvert>0.02
using a bond dimension up to
400
.

In Fig.
3
, we demonstrate that symmetry PP allows us to simulate the dynamics more accurately than standard PP. To simulate effects of presence of noise, our numerical simulations include a layer of depolarizing noise with strength
\gamma
after each trotterization step. The noise layer can be viewed as a time discretized model for a local Lindbladian
11
;
32
. In addition, we adopt a coefficient truncation strategy such that only terms with coefficients greater than a truncation threshold
\epsilon
are kept
51
. For instance, a truncated operator is given by
O^{\text{trunc}}=\sum_{\alpha\,|\,c_{\alpha}\leq\epsilon}c_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}
for some small
\epsilon
. We study the effect of different truncation thresholds on simulation accuracy and find that symmetry PP achieves faster convergence even for a larger truncation threshold
\epsilon=10^{-3}
(circles) compared to a truncation threshold at
\epsilon=5\times 10^{-5}
(solid line). In comparison, standard PP diverges at earlier times (dashed vs. solid lines). Moreover, symmetry PP with a higher truncation threshold
\epsilon=0.05
agrees with standard PP at a much lower threshold (
\epsilon=0.001
). This can be understood by realizing that symmetry merging tends to increase the magnitudes of the coefficients, in turn increasing the Pauli strings that are kept per threshold. While it is inevitably exponentially costly to simulate long-time dynamics, given a fixed budget of memory and computation time, symmetry PP converges to longer time and leads to more accurate results hence reducing the computational resources for a target accuracy.

V
Discussion

We have established a group-theoretic symmetry-merging framework for Pauli propagation, called symmetry PP. By propagating only a set of Pauli orbit representatives, we reduce the memory cost by a factor related to the size of the group. Such a memory saving is highly impactful for future improvements using graphics processing unit (GPU) because the acceleration hardwares typically have more constraints in memory. Most recently, related works have also taken symmetry into consideration via merging in PP
35
;
28
. Theoretically, we provide rigorous guarantees for the correctness and computational complexity. Then we demonstrate the effectiveness of symmetry PP for an ergodic dynamics of
1d
tilted-field Ising model and an all-to-all Heisenberg dynamics. We observe an improved stability in symmetry PP simulations, despite subtleties in the memory saving due to an unavoidable exponentially large space of Pauli strings. It would be interesting to analyze the improvement by considering magic monotones in resource theory
18
;
59
.

For future work, it would be interesting to explore symmetric merging strategies in other propagation based methods such as Majorana propagation for fermionic systems
37
;
2
;
3
;
20
or displacement propagation for bosonic system
60
.
This includes any future merging strategies for other discrete symmetries.
More ambitiously, developing propagation subroutines
for continuous symmetries will be highly relevant and may yield substantial reductions in computational cost. An abstract analysis of this direction has already been initiated using representation theory in Ref.
17
. Such progress would open new avenues for investigating the role of symmetries in physically relevant quantum Floquet dynamics, such as those previously explored in random circuits with
U(1)
symmetry
30
;
45
.

A
Pauli Path Methods

In this appendix, we review the framework of Pauli path based simulations, adopting the conventions of Pauli propagation
51
.
Let us consider an approximately maximally mixed state, commonly studied in operator spreading,

\rho=\frac{\mathbb{I}+aO}{\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbb{I})},

(13)

which is perturbed by a small amplitude
a
.
The evolution of the state is determined by the evolution of the perturbation operator

O^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}OU.

(14)

Here, the operators live in the
n
-qubit Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, with an inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}[A^{\dagger}B]
. The operators can be represented by matrices
M_{2^{n}}(\mathbb{C})
.
To analyze quantum dynamics, it is useful to vectorize these operators in an enlarged Hilbert space, called the Liouville space
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}^{*}

26
. In the vectorization formalism, an operator
A
on
\mathcal{H}
can be uniquely mapped to a vector
\mbox{$|A\rangle\rangle$}\in\mathcal{L}
as the following:

A=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\mbox{$|i\rangle$}\mbox{$\langle j|$}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\mbox{$|A\rangle\rangle$}=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\mbox{$|i\rangle$}\otimes\mbox{$|j^{*}\rangle$},

(15)

where
\{\mbox{$|i\rangle$}\}
represents an orthonormal basis in the Hilbert space.
|j^{*}\rangle
is the complex conjugation of
|j\rangle
, corresponding to the dual vector
\langle j|
. In the simple cases of the computational basis, the complex conjugation does nothing and is often abbreviated in vectorization formalism. The unitary acting on the original operators are superoperators in the Liouville space. In particular, its adjoint action is

U\otimes U^{*}\mbox{$|A\rangle\rangle$}=\mbox{$|UAU^{\dagger}\rangle\rangle$}.

(16)

In the rest of the sections, it is useful to define the
normalized
Pauli strings

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\left\{\frac{\mathbb{I}}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{X}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{Y}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{Z}{\sqrt{2}}\right\}^{\otimes n}.

(17)

Consider the basis given by the normalized Pauli operators,
|A\rangle\rangle
can be represented as

\mbox{$|A\rangle\rangle$}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[s^{\dagger}A]\,\mbox{$|s\rangle\rangle$}.

(18)

In the following, we will often interchangably write
A=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[sA]\,s
because
s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
forms a Hermitian basis.
The adjoint action of the unitary can then be represented in the Pauli basis as

U\otimes U^{*}=\sum_{ij}(U\otimes U^{*})_{ij}\,\mbox{$|s_{i}\rangle\rangle$}\mbox{$\langle\langle s_{j}|$},\quad(U\otimes U^{*})_{ij}=\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{j}U^{\dagger}s_{i}].

(19)

The Liouville matrix representation
(U\otimes U^{*})_{ij}
in the Pauli basis is often referred to as the
Pauli transfer matrix
(PTM) in Pauli propagation. While we focus on unitary evolutions, our analysis in this formalism can be generalized to quantum channels. The PTM for any quantum channels in the Pauli basis is always real. For instance in the case of a unitary evolution, its complex conjugate is the same in the Pauli basis gives

(U\otimes U^{*})^{*}_{ij}=\mathrm{Tr}[(U^{\dagger})^{T}(s^{\dagger}_{j})^{T}U^{T}(s^{\dagger}_{i})^{T}]=\mathrm{Tr}[s_{i}Us_{j}U^{\dagger}]=(U\otimes U^{*})_{ij},

(20)

where we have used that Pauli operators are Hermitian
s_{i}^{\dagger}=s_{i}
as well as cyclic and invariant properties of trace
\mathrm{Tr}[(AB)^{T}]=\mathrm{Tr}[AB]=\mathrm{Tr}[BA]
. In in the Heisenberg picture, the PTM is given by the transpose of that in the Schrodinger picture

(U^{+}\otimes U^{T})_{ij}=\mathrm{Tr}[U^{\dagger}s_{j}Us_{i}]

(21)

Suppose the unitary circuit is composed of
L
layers as
U=U_{L}U_{L-1}\cdots U_{2}U_{1}
, then the evolved expectation of an operator in the Heisenberg picture is

U^{\dagger}OU=U_{1}^{\dagger}U_{2}^{\dagger}\cdots U_{L-1}^{\dagger}U_{L}^{\dagger}(O)U_{L}U_{L-1}\cdots U_{2}U_{1}.

(22)

The Pauli decomposition of the initial observable is

O_{L}:=O=\sum_{s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Os_{L}]s_{L}.

(23)

For layers
l=L,L-1,\cdots,1
-th layer, a partially evolved observable is given by recursively applying each layer of the unitaries

O_{l-1}=U_{l}^{\dagger}O_{l}U_{l}

(24)

For instance, the last layer of the evolution can be written as a linear combination of applying the PTM from Eq. (
21
) to
s_{L}
as

O_{L-1}:=U_{L}^{\dagger}(O_{L})U_{L}=\sum_{s_{L},s_{L-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[Os_{L}\right]\mathrm{Tr}\left[U_{L}^{\dagger}s_{L}U_{L}s_{L-1}\right]s_{L-1}.

(25)

Expanding such evolution iteratively in the Pauli basis and denoting the final operator as
O_{0}
leads to the
Pauli path integral

O_{0}:=U^{\dagger}OU=\sum_{s_{0},s_{1},\cdots s_{L-1}s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[s_{L}O]\prod_{l=1}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[s_{l-1}U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}]s_{0}.

(26)

In the rest of the appendices, our goal is to improve the computation using symmetry analysis.

B
Symmetry and Group Theory

In this self-contained section, we review the terminologies that we use from group theory, which can be found in standard texts
7
;
49
.

Definition 1

(
Group
(finite))
.

A finite set
G
with an operation
*
forms a
group

(G,*)
if the following holds:

1.

The identity element is in the group:
\exists e\in G,\quad e*g=g*e=g,\quad\forall g\in G
.

2.

Every element has an inverse:
\forall g\in G,\exists a\in G\implies g*a=a*g=e
.

3.

The operation
*
is associative:
g*(a*b)=(g*a)*b,\quad\forall g,a,b\in G
.

In the following, we will usually omit the operation and refer to a group as
G
. Rather than focusing on the abstract definition of a group, it is more useful to think about their
action
on a set.

Definition 2

(
Group action
)
.

The action of a group
G
on a set
X
is denoted as

g\cdot(x),\quad\forall g\in G,\,x\in X.

(27)

The group action defines an equivalence relation on
X
:
x\sim y
if
\exists\,g\in G
such that

y=g\cdot(x).

(28)

The set of elements reached by acting
G
on an element
x
is called an
orbit
.

Definition 3

(
Group orbit
)
.

The orbit of
x\in X
under the action of group
G
is

\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x):=\{g\cdot(x)\,|\,g\in G\}.

(29)

Each orbit
\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x)
is an
equivalence class
of
x
, and two elements
x,y
are equivalent under the group action if they are in the same orbit
y\in\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x)
.

Because each orbit is a set containing elements that are reachable by the action of a group, in this sense these elements in an orbit are redundant descriptions of each other. Therefore it is sufficient to only consider a representative for each orbit.

Definition 4

(
Orbit representatives
)
.

A complete set of orbit representatives is a set
R^{G}_{X}:=\{x_{i}\}\subseteq X
such that

X=\coprod_{x_{i}\in R^{G}_{X}}\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x_{i}),

(30)

where
\coprod
denotes a disjoint union.

Here the disjoint union emphasizes that different orbits do not intersect or otherwise they are the same orbit.
Applying a group action on Pauli strings, we define
Pauli representatives
under translation symmetry.

Example 3

(
Pauli representatives (
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
)
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
be the set of normalized Pauli strings, and
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
be the symmetry group consisting of
n
group elements
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\{\hat{I},\hat{T},\cdots,\hat{T}^{n-1}\}
. The group action of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
on Pauli string
s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is

\operatorname{Orb}_{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}(s)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\hat{T}^{j}\cdot(s).

(31)

Define the action of translation as shifting the system to the left such that
\hat{T}(IZI\cdots I):=ZII\cdots I
(up to normalization). Let
s_{0}=1/\sqrt{2}^{n}(ZII\cdots I)
, then its orbit consists of all the Pauli strings under translation

\operatorname{Orb}_{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}(s_{0})=\left\{\frac{ZII\cdots I}{\sqrt{2}^{n}},\frac{IZI\cdots I}{\sqrt{2}^{n}},\cdots,\frac{III\cdots Z}{\sqrt{2}^{n}}\right\}.

(32)

A complete set of orbits partition the Pauli strings into a disjoint set. For instance, for
n=3
qubits, the set of unique orbits representatives (up to normalization) are

\displaystyle\{

\displaystyle III,XII,YII,ZII,XXI,XYI,XZI,YXI,YYI,YZI,ZXI,ZYI,ZZI,XXX,XXY,XXZ,

\displaystyle YYY,YYX,YYZ,ZZX,ZZY,ZZZ,XYZ,XZY\}.

(33)

In total, there are
24
representatives and we show how to calculate its cardinality in Example
4
.

Next, we formalize how to count the size of the orbits of any groups using Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1

(
Burnside’s lemma

7
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and
R^{G}_{X}
be a complete set of orbit representatives of a finite set
X
. Then the cardinality of the representatives is given by

\lvert R^{G}_{X}\rvert=\frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert X^{g}\rvert,

(34)

where
X^{g}
denotes the set of elements that are invariant under left action of
g

X^{g}=\{x\in X\,|\,g\cdot(x)=x\}.

(35)

Proof.

Consider the sum in the r.h.s.

\sum_{g\in G}\lvert X^{g}\rvert=\lvert\{(x,g)\,|\,x\in X,g\in G\,,\,g\cdot(x)=x\}\rvert.

(36)

The sum can be rewritten as

\sum_{g\in G}\lvert X^{g}\rvert=\sum_{x\in X}\lvert\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(x)\rvert,

(37)

where
\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(x)=\{g\,|\,g\cdot(x)=x,\,g\in G\}
is the stabilizer of
x
. Consider an element
x\in X
, the orbit-stabilizer theorem counts how much
x
is stabilized and moved in orbits

\lvert G\rvert=\lvert\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x)\rvert\times\lvert\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(x)\rvert,

(38)

leading to

\sum_{x\in X}\lvert\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(x)\rvert=\sum_{x\in X}\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x)\rvert}.

(39)

By definition of orbit representatives,
X
is the disjoint union of orbits so the sum is

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\lvert\operatorname{Stab}_{G}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in R^{G}_{X}}\sum_{x\in\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(r)}\frac{\lvert G\rvert}{\lvert\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(x)\rvert},

(40)

\displaystyle=\lvert G\rvert\lvert R^{G}_{X}\rvert.

(41)

Therefore, combing with Eq. (
37
), we have arrived at the result

\sum_{g\in G}\lvert X^{g}\rvert=\lvert G\rvert\lvert R^{G}_{X}\rvert.

(42)

∎

Equipped with Lemma
1
, we can then count the representatives under group actions for Pauli strings.

Corollary 1

(
Pauli representatives cardinality

7
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
be a complete set of orbit representatives of the normalized
n
-qubit Pauli strings
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Then the cardinality of the representatives is given by

\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert=\frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}^{g}\rvert,

(43)

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{g}
denotes the set of Paulis that are invariant under left action of
g

\mathcal{P}_{n}^{g}=\{s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\,|\,g\cdot(s)=s\}.

(44)

In the following examples, we reserve the notation
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
for the
n
-qubit
Pauli representatives
under a group
G
.

Example 4

(
Translation symmetry

\mathbb{Z}_{n}
)
.

Consider the translational symmetry group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
on
n
qubits. The cardinality of the group is
\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rvert=n.
According to Corollary
1
, the number of orbit representatives is given by computing the size of fixed Paulis under the group action

\lvert\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}_{n}\rvert=\frac{1}{\lvert\mathbb{Z}_{n}\rvert}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\hat{T}^{j}}\rvert.

(45)

Mathematically, this is a classical combinatorics problem of counting
p
-ary necklaces
with
n
beads, where each bead can be one of
p
colors (for counting Pauli strings
p=4
corresponding to
\{I,X,Y,Z\}
).
The number of distinct
necklace
combinations is given by

\lvert\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}_{n}\rvert=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}4^{\operatorname{gcd}(j,n)},

(46)

where
\operatorname{gcd}(a,b)
represents the greatest common divisor between two integers
a
and
b
. Here,
j=n
corresponds to applying the identity element and is equivalent to counting the size of the Pauli strings
\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}\rvert=4^{n}
. For
1\leq j<n
, a length-
n
Pauli string invariant under shift of length
j
has periodicity of
\operatorname{gcd}(j,n)
. Moreover, there are
4^{\operatorname{gcd}(j,n)}
number of such strings given by the first periodic subset and the rest of the necklace is fully fixed.
In particular, for
n=2^{m}
, this can be simplified into

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}_{n}\rvert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}4^{\textrm{gcd}(j,n)},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(4\cdot\frac{n}{2}+4^{2}\cdot\frac{n}{4}+4^{4}\cdot\frac{n}{8}+\cdots+4^{n/2}+4^{n}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}4^{2^{k-1}}\cdot 2^{m-k}+4^{n}\right)\leq 2\cdot\frac{4^{n}}{n}\;,

(47)

as the number of indices
j
with
\textrm{gcd}(j,n)=2^{k-1}
for
k=1,\cdots,m
is
\frac{n}{2^{k}}
.

On the other hand, if
n
is a prime number, then,

\lvert\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}_{n}\rvert=\frac{1}{n}(4^{n}+4(n-1))).

(48)

Therefore by Theorem
2
, we have a linear saving ratio
r\in\Theta(\frac{\lvert\mathcal{R}^{\mathbb{Z}_{n}}_{n}\rvert}{4^{n}})\sim\Theta(\frac{1}{n})
using symmetry Pauli propagation.

Example 5

(
Permutation symmetry

S_{n}
)
.

Consider the permutation symmetry group
S_{n}
. The number of orbit representatives can be found using the stars and bars formula, leading to:

\lvert\mathcal{R}^{S_{n}}_{n}\rvert=\binom{n+3}{3}:=\textrm{Te}_{n+1}\;.

(49)

This is also called the tetrahedral number
\textrm{Te}_{n}=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}{6}

53
.
For large
n
, the number of orbit representatives scales as
\Theta(n^{3})
, leading to
r\in\Theta\left(\frac{n^{3}}{4^{n}}\right)
improvement in terms of space complexity.

Example 6

(
Dihedral group

D_{n}
)
.

The
Dihedral group

D_{n}
corresponds to a group of the reflectional and rotational symmetry of an regular
n
-sided polygon. By definition,
D_{n}
consists of
2n
elements:
n
rotational symmetries and
n
reflectional symmetries.
Therefore, we have a linear time saving
r\in\Theta(\frac{1}{2n})
using symmetric merging. This can be viewed as a natural extension of translational symmetry with periodic boundary condition (c.f. Example
1
), where rotational operations on the vertices of the polygon are interpreted as cyclic translations along its perimeter.

C
Symmetry-merging Pauli Propagation

In this section, we prove the correctness of symmetry Pauli propagation under exact evolution. First, we turn the intuition that evolving a Pauli string is the same as evolving its orbit representative into a rigorous statement via Lemma
2
.

Lemma 2

(
Expectation equivalence for orbit representatives
)
.

Let
G
be the symmetry group and
U
be a symmetric unitary. Suppose a function over Pauli strings
f:\mathcal{P}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is also invariant under
G
such that
f(g\cdot(s))=f(s),\,\forall g\in G
. Then the expectation value after propagating a Pauli string
s_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is equivalent to that from propagating any representative in the same orbit
g\cdot(s_{1}),\,g\in G
. More precisely the following holds:

\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Ug\cdot(s_{1})\,U^{\dagger}s_{0}]f(s_{0})=\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}U^{\dagger}s_{0}]f(s_{0}),\quad\forall g\in G.

(50)

Proof.

Let
A_{g}
be the representation of the the symmetry group so that
\left[A_{g},\,U\right]=0
. The action of
g
on
s_{1}
can be written as its inverse action on
s_{0}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[Ug\cdot(s_{1})\,U^{\dagger}s_{0}]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[UA_{g}s_{1}A_{g}^{(-1)}U^{\dagger}s_{0}],

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}U^{\dagger}A_{g}^{(-1)}s_{0}A_{g}],

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}\,U^{\dagger}g^{(-1)}\cdot(s_{0})].

(51)

Substituting the summation over the Pauli group with its orbit representatives, the expectation value we want to evaluate can be rewritten as

\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Ug\cdot(s_{1})\,U^{\dagger}s_{0}]f(s_{0})=\sum_{g_{0}\in G}\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}U^{\dagger}g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]f(g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})),\quad\forall g\in G.

(52)

Using Eq. (
C
) and Eq. (
52
), we have

\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Ug\cdot(s_{1})\,U^{\dagger}s_{0}]f(s_{0})=\sum_{g_{0}\in G}\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}\,U^{\dagger}g^{(-1)}g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]f(s_{0}),

(53)

where we have also used that
f
is invariant under the symmetry transformation. Therefore, we can relable the sum over the group element by
\tilde{g}_{0}=gg_{0}
so that

\sum_{g_{0}\in G}\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}\,U^{\dagger}g^{(-1)}g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]f(s_{0})=\sum_{\tilde{g}_{0}\in G}\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}\,U^{\dagger}\tilde{g}_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]f(s_{0}).

(54)

Using the fact that
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
is a complete set of orbit representatives, the r.h.s becomes

\sum_{\tilde{g}_{0}\in G}\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}\,U^{\dagger}\tilde{g}_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]f(s_{0})=\sum_{s_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Us_{1}U^{\dagger}s_{0}]f(s_{0}).

(55)

∎

Using Lemma
2
, we provide a proof of Theorem
1
by induction.

Theorem 1

(
Pauli Symmetric Merging
)
.

Let
\rho
be the initial state and
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}
be an
L
-layer unitary. Suppose they have symmetry
G
with representations
A_{g}
such that

\left[A_{g},\,\rho\right]=\left[A_{g},\,U_{l}\right]=0,\quad\forall g\in G.

Let
\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,U^{\dagger}OU]
be the target expectation value of an observable
O
. Then the output value given by the symmetry-merging propagation algorithm via Algorithm
1

\widetilde{O}_{0}=\sum_{s_{0},\dots,s_{L}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{0},\dots,g_{L}\in G}\,\prod_{l=1}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}g_{l}\cdot(s_{l-1})]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s_{0}],

(56)

equals the target value:

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,\widetilde{O}_{0}]=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,U^{\dagger}OU].

(57)

Proof.

We prove the theorem by
induction
on the number of layers
l
. For conceptual simplicity of the proof, we consider the merging process from the
beginning
layers of the unitary circuits, even though in Heisenberg evolution we perform propagation from the final layers.

Base Case:
(
m=2
)
Consider the expectation value after evolution of
L
layers of the unitary:

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,U^{\dagger}OU]=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}^{\dagger}\right)O\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}\right)\right].

(58)

Expanding in the Pauli basis leads to the conventional Pauli path integral

\sum_{s_{0},\dots,s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Os_{L}]\prod_{l=1}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s_{0}].

(59)

To consider symmetric merging, we rewrite the beginning two layers
l=1,2
of the unitary evolution in terms of orbit representatives and their symmetry group as

\sum_{s_{2},\dots,s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Os_{L}]\prod_{l=3}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{s_{1},s_{0}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{1},g_{0}\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{2}^{\dagger}s_{2}U_{2}g_{1}\cdot(s_{1})]\mathrm{Tr}[U_{1}^{\dagger}g_{1}\cdot(s_{1})U_{1}g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})].

(60)

Because the initial state is symmetric under
G
, the Pauli strings in the same orbit contribute equally to the expectation value:

\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,g\cdot(s)]=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s],\quad\forall g\in G.

(61)

Let
f(s_{0})=\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s_{0}]
, by Lemma
2
we can simplify the last two terms as:

\sum_{s_{2},\dots,s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}[Os_{L}]\prod_{l=3}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{s_{0},s_{1}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{0},g_{1}\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{2}^{\dagger}s_{2}U_{2}g_{1}\cdot(s_{1})]\mathrm{Tr}[U_{1}^{\dagger}s_{1}U_{1}g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho\,s_{0}].

(62)

The expressions of the form
\mathrm{Tr}[U_{2}^{\dagger}s_{2}U_{2}g_{1}\cdot(s_{1})]\mathrm{Tr}[U_{1}^{\dagger}s_{1}U_{1}g_{0}\cdot(s_{0})]
is saying that after Pauli propagation until layer
l=2
, we can simply propagate the orbit representatives in the last layer
l=1
.
We establish the merged form up the initial two layers.

Inductive Step:

Suppose that for the beginning
m-1
layers, we can merge Pauli strings into symmetry orbits. We show that this also holds for the
m
-th layer.

By the inductive assumption, propagating the orbit representatives up to the first
m-1
layers gives an exact expectation value:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left[\rho\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}^{\dagger}\right)O\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s_{m},\dots,s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\prod_{l=m+1}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{s_{0},\dots,s_{m-1}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{0},\dots,g_{m-1}\in G}\prod_{l=1}^{m}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}g_{l}\cdot(s_{l-1})]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s_{0}].

(63)

Now, rewriting the Pauli string in the next layer
l=m
of the unitary evolution up to
m+1
layers in terms of orbit representatives, the expectation value on the r.h.s. becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{s_{m+1},\dots,s_{L}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\prod_{l=m+2}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{s_{m}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\sum_{s_{m-1}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{m-1}\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m+1}^{\dagger}s_{m+1}U_{m+1}s_{m}]\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m}^{\dagger}s_{m}U_{m}g_{m-1}\cdot(s_{m-1})]f(s_{m-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m+1},\dots,s_{L}\\
\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\prod_{l=m+2}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m-1},s_{m}\\
\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{m-1},g_{m}\\
\in G\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m+1}^{\dagger}s_{m+1}U_{m+1}g_{m}\cdot(s_{m})]\,\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m}^{\dagger}g_{m}\cdot(s_{m})U_{m}g_{m-1}\cdot(s_{m-1})]f(s_{m-1}),

(64)

where
f
is the following function

f(s_{m-1})=\sum_{s_{0},\dots,s_{m-2}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{0},\dots,g_{m-2}\in G}\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}g_{l}\cdot(s_{l-1})]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s_{0}].

(65)

By the inductive assumption,
f
is invariant under the symmetry action such that
f(g\cdot(s_{m-1}))=f(s_{m-1}),\,\forall g\in G
.

Therefore, applying the same symmetry merging argument using Lemma
2
for layer
m
, leads to the following:

\sum_{s_{m-1}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{m-1}\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m}^{\dagger}g_{m}\cdot(s_{m})U_{m}g_{m-1}\cdot(s_{m-1})]f(s_{m-1})=\sum_{s_{m-1}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{m-1}\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m}^{\dagger}s_{m}U_{m}g_{m-1}\cdot(s_{m-1})]f(s_{m-1}),

(66)

Using Eq. (
66
), we can simplify Eq. (
64
) to

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m+1},\dots,s_{L}\\
\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\prod_{l=m+2}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m-1},s_{m}\\
\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g_{m-1},g_{m}\\
\in G\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m+1}^{\dagger}s_{m+1}U_{m+1}g_{m}\cdot(s_{m})]\,\mathrm{Tr}[U_{m}^{\dagger}s_{m}U_{m}g_{m-1}\cdot(s_{m-1})]f(s_{m-1}),

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m+1},\dots,s_{L}\\
\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\end{subarray}}\prod_{l=m+2}^{L}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}s_{l-1}]\sum_{s_{0},\dots,s_{m}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g_{0},\dots,g_{m}\in G}\prod_{l=1}^{m}\mathrm{Tr}[U_{l}^{\dagger}s_{l}U_{l}g_{l}\cdot(s_{l-1})]\mathrm{Tr}[\rho s_{0}],

(67)

which says that we achieve the same expectation value after symmetric merging up to
m
layers.

Thus, by induction, Pauli strings can be merged at every layer, completing the proof.
∎

D
Computational Complexity

In this section, we provide a rigorous analysis of computational cost of symmetry PP. First we provide a proof of Theorem
2
in the main text.

Theorem 2

(
Space complexity
)
.

Let
G
be the discrete symmetry group acting on the set of
n
-qubit normalized Pauli strings
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Suppose the observable at
l
-th layer has support on all of Pauli strings
\mathrm{Tr}[O_{l}s]\neq 0,\,\forall s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. The symmetry Pauli propagation defined in Algorithm
1
outputs an observable
\widetilde{O}_{l}
and reduces the space complexity by a ratio

r=\frac{1}{4^{n}\lvert G\rvert}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}^{g}\rvert<1,

(68)

where
\mathcal{P}^{g}_{n}=\{g\cdot(s)\,|\,s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
corresponds to group action on Pauli strings.

Proof.

The relative space complexity with respect to standard Pauli propagation is determined by the number of distinct operator trajectories that need to be evolved under symmetry Pauli propagation. The group
G
acts on the normalized Pauli operators
\mathcal{P}_{n}
through a left action
g\cdot(s)
, corresponding to symmetry transformations. For instance, consider the
3
-qubit Pauli group under translation group
\mathbb{Z}_{3}=\{\hat{I},\hat{T},\hat{T}^{2}\}
. Then the action of
\hat{T}
on a Pauli string is e.g.
\hat{T}\cdot(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}^{3}}IZI)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}^{3}}IIZ
.
This action partitions
\mathcal{P}_{n}
into disjoint orbits:

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\coprod_{r_{i}\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(r_{i}),

(69)

where
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
is a complete set of orbit representatives (Definition
4
) of the Pauli group.
Each orbit corresponds to a set of Pauli strings related by the symmetry, so evolving one representative per orbit suffices by Lemma
2
. By assumption,
O_{l}
has support on all Paulis and the merged observable using symmetry-merging algorithm is

\widetilde{O}_{l}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}s\left(\sum_{g\in G}\mathrm{Tr}\left[g\cdot(s)O_{l}\right]\right).

(70)

Consider the case where
\widetilde{O}_{l}
has support over all the representatives, then the number of effective Pauli trajectories equals the number of distinct orbits,

\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert=\frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}^{g}\rvert,

(71)

by Burnside’s lemma (Corollary
1
).
This quantity directly measures how many unique classes of Pauli operators remain after merging symmetry-equivalent Paulis. If the coefficients for some of the representatives are zero, then the support of the merged observable will be less than the size of the orbit representatives

\lvert\widetilde{O}_{l}\rvert<\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert.

(72)

In the absence of symmetry,
\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}\rvert=4^{n}
, so naive Pauli propagation requires
\mathcal{O}(4^{n})
updates in the worst case. We can upper bound the relative support comparing the merged observable and the full observable being propagated as

\frac{\lvert\widetilde{O}_{l}\rvert}{\lvert O_{l}\rvert}\leq\frac{\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert}{4^{n}}.

(73)

Therefore we define a
ratio

r
that quantifies the fraction of operator space that are distinct under symmetry transformation as

\displaystyle r:

\displaystyle=\frac{\lvert\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}\rvert}{4^{n}},

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{g\in G}\lvert\mathcal{P}_{n}^{g}\rvert.

(74)

Thus, symmetry-merged Pauli propagation reduces the exponential prefactor by the average group orbit size.
The complexity improvement depends on the symmetry group and the sparsity of invariant Paulis under its action.
∎

We have formally considered the space complexity by counting the Pauli support in Theorem
2
, assuming that the observable
O_{l}
has a full support in
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. In practice, the difference between merged and original observables depends on how the Paulis contributions are distributed in their orbits. In general, we may benefit from choosing the number of layers for merging as a hyperparameter. In this work, we consider the simplest scenario of utilizing the symmetry Pauli propagation for all the layers and we comment on scenarios where a more sophisticated design might be beneficial.

Observation 1
.

Consider an observable that is supported exactly on the orbit representatives

O=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[sO\right]s.

(75)

Then the symmetry Pauli propagation leaves the observable unchanged
\widetilde{O}=O
and they have the same amount of Pauli support
\lvert\widetilde{O}\rvert=\lvert O\rvert
.

This leads to the intuition that if an observable is sparsely distributed across different Pauli orbits, then it may not be beneficial to do symmetry-merging. Next we consider scenarios where it is highly beneficial to perform symmetry merging.

Observation 2
.

Suppose an observable is uniformly supported on Paulis contained in a single orbit represented by
s_{0}
such that

O=\sum_{s\in\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s_{0})}\mathrm{Tr}[sO]s.

(76)

By Lagrange’s theorem, the size of an orbit is a divisor of the group size such that
\lvert G\rvert/\lvert\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s)\rvert\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\,\forall s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. The relative support of a merged observable is then lower bounded by the size of the group

\frac{1}{\lvert G\rvert}\leq\frac{\lvert\widetilde{O}_{l}\rvert}{\lvert O_{l}\rvert}.

(77)

And the equality is achieved precisely when the size of the orbit is equal to the group size
\lvert\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s)\rvert=\lvert G\rvert
.

Finding the largest orbit for an arbitrary group is an open question, to the best of our knowledge
44
, hence this lower bound is not necessarily achievable. Nevertheless, for most common symmetry groups appearing in the context of actions on Pauli strings in quantum simulations, our lower bound in Observable
2
is usually satisfied. In the rest of the section, we analyze the time complexity of our algorithm and comment on its relations to practical simulations.

On a general level, there is a trade-off between the additional time spent performing merging and the time saved from propagating fewer Pauli operators. The more time we spent on merging, the more time we also save by needing to propagate fewer Paulis. We provide a theoretical bound on the time complexity aspects of symmetry PP in Proposition
1
as well as a numerical analysis of such time in practice in Supplementary Fig.
1
.

Proposition 1

(
Time complexity
)
.

Let
U=\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}
be an
l
-layered unitary symmetric under group
G
and suppose each symmetric layer contains
K_{l}
number of Pauli gates such that
U_{l}=\prod_{k=1}^{K_{l}}V_{k}
. Denote by
\lvert O_{l}\rvert
the number of Pauli terms in the operator expansion at layer
l
, and by
\lvert\tilde{O}_{l}\rvert
the corresponding number after symmetry merging.
The total computational time of the symmetry Pauli propagation has two contributions as

T_{\mathrm{sym}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}(T_{m,l}+T_{p,l}),

(78)

where
T_{m,l}
represents time spent merging Paulis and
T_{p,l}
represents time spent on propagation.
More precisely, we have

\displaystyle T_{m,l}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\lvert\tilde{O}_{l-1}\rvert\lvert G\rvert),

(79)

\displaystyle T_{p,l}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(2^{K_{l}}\lvert\tilde{O}_{l}\rvert),

(80)

with
K_{l}
the number of local gates in layer
l
acting on distinct supports.

Proof.

We separate the analysis into two components: the merging cost
T_{m,l}
and the propagation cost
T_{p,l}
.

(1) Merging time complexity.

At layer
l
, the observable evolves as

O_{l-1}=U_{l}^{\dagger}\tilde{O}_{l}U_{l},

(81)

where
\tilde{O}_{l}
denotes the post-merged operator from the previous layer.
In the brute-force merging procedure (Supplementary Materials
E.1
), each Pauli in
\tilde{O}_{l-1}
is checked against its symmetry orbit under
G
, requiring at most
\lvert G\rvert
group actions per element.
Hence, the worst-case merging time is bounded by

T_{m,l}=\mathcal{O}(\lvert\tilde{O}_{l-1}\rvert\lvert G\rvert).

(82)

(2) Propagation time complexity.

Each layer
U_{l}
consists of
K_{l}
Pauli gates
V_{k}
(e.g., two-qubit gates) written as

V_{k}=e^{i\theta P},

(83)

for a rotation angle
\theta
and Pauli string
P
. Given a Pauli string
Q
, the evolved operator is

V_{k}^{\dagger}QV_{k}=\begin{cases}Q,&\left[P,\,Q\right]=0\\
\cos(\theta)Q-i\sin(\theta)PQ,&\{P,Q\}=0.\end{cases}

(84)

Propagating a single Pauli string with a Pauli gate results in at most
2
Paulis, and across the layer produces at most
2^{K_{l}}
output terms.
Therefore, the propagation time for symmetry-merged propagation is

T_{p,l}=\mathcal{O}(2^{K_{l}}\lvert\tilde{O}_{l}\rvert),

(85)

compared to
\mathcal{O}(2^{K_{l}}\lvert O_{l}\rvert)
for standard Pauli propagation without merging.

Combining the two contributions,

T_{\mathrm{sym}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Big[\mathcal{O}(\lvert\tilde{O}_{l-1}\rvert\lvert G\rvert)+\mathcal{O}(2^{K_{l}}\lvert\tilde{O}_{l}\rvert)\Big].

(86)

Define an effective reduction ratio
r_{l}=\lvert\tilde{O}_{l}\rvert/\lvert O_{l}\rvert
, which is upper bounded by the ratio in Theorem
2
as
r_{l}<r
, the total runtime relative to standard Pauli propagation scales as

\frac{T_{\mathrm{sym}}}{T_{\mathrm{PP}}}\simeq\frac{\sum_{l}(2^{K_{l}}r_{l}\lvert O_{l}\rvert+\lvert G\rvert\lvert U_{l}^{\dagger}\widetilde{O}_{l}U_{l}\rvert))}{\sum_{l}2^{K_{l}}\lvert O_{l}\rvert}.

(87)

The symmetry Pauli propagation thus improves the time complexity of Pauli propagation by reducing the number of Pauli trajectories that must be evolved, at the cost of a modest merging overhead that scales with the symmetry group size.

∎

E
Symmetry-merging Subroutines for Pauli Propagation

In this appendix, we outline the practical steps for the merging subroutine in step
2
that applies to any symmetry group. This process identifies all Pauli strings that are equivalent under symmetry transformations and merges them into a single representative from their orbit. By reducing redundant terms, we improve the efficiency of Pauli propagation. First we outline the exhaustive merging procedure in Supplementary Materials
E.1
. Finally we outline the practical algorithms via lowest integer mapping for symmetry merging subroutines in Supplementary Materials
E.2
.

E.1
A general subroutine: exhaustive search

When propagating an observable
O
through a quantum circuit, the number of Pauli strings can grow exponentially. However, if the system has a symmetry group
G
, many of these Pauli strings are redundant because they transform into one another under symmetry operations. Instead of tracking all Pauli terms individually, we can group them into symmetry orbits and only propagate a reduced set of representatives.

At the
l
-th layer of the circuit, the observable is rewritten as:

\widetilde{O}_{l}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}}\sum_{g\in G}\mathrm{Tr}[O_{l}\,g\cdot(s)]s,

(88)

where
\mathcal{R}^{G}_{n}
are the non-equivalent orbit representatives (defined in Definition
4
). After merging, only this reduced set of Pauli strings needs to be propagated through the circuit. The merging procedure follows these steps:

1.

Initialize the merged Pauli Set
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}=\varnothing
:

Start with a set
\mathcal{D}
of Pauli strings with nonzero amplitudes in the decomposition of the observable
O
:

\mathcal{D}=\{(s,\mathrm{Tr}[Os])\mid\mathrm{Tr}[Os]\neq 0,\,s\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}.

(89)

2.

Loop Over Each Pauli String and Check Symmetry Equivalence:

For each Pauli string
s
in
\mathcal{D}
, check whether it belongs to an existing symmetry orbit. Apply all symmetry transformations
g\in G
to generate the orbit of
s
:

\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s)=\{g\cdot s\mid g\in G\}.

(90)

If another Pauli string
\widetilde{s}
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}=\{\left(\widetilde{s},a_{\widetilde{s}}\right)\}
is already in the orbit, so that
\widetilde{s}=g\cdot s
for some
g
, merge their coefficients:

a_{\widetilde{s}}\leftarrow a_{\widetilde{s}}+\mathrm{Tr}[Os].

(91)

Otherwise, store
s
as a new representative in
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}\leftarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}+\left(s,\mathrm{Tr}[Os]\right)
.

3.

Construct the Merged Observable:

The final grouped observable is given by:

\widetilde{O}=\sum_{s\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}}a_{s}s.

(92)

While this exhaustive merging procedure significantly reduces memory overhead by tracking only symmetry-distinct Pauli strings, it can be computationally expensive when the symmetry group
G
is large. To address this, alternative merging methods, such as canonical forms given by the lowest integer representations (see Algorithm
2
and Algorithm
3
), may be more practical especially for certain symmetries like permutation groups.

E.2
Practical subroutines: lowest integer mapping

In this section, we discuss our practical implementations considering two examples of translation and permutation symmetry. In general, finding a minimal set of representatives is a challenging task for an arbitrary symmetry.
In this work we adopt a canonical mapping based on the
lowest integer representation
, which provides a consistent and symmetry-agnostic way to identify orbit representatives.

Definition 5

(
Lowest integer representation
)
.

Let
G
be the symmetry group, and
\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s_{0})
be the orbits of a Pauli string
s_{0}
. Define a binary representation of a Pauli string by the a base 4 numeral such that a pair of bits represent a Pauli operator

I\rightarrow 00,\,\,\,X\rightarrow 01,\,\,\,Y\rightarrow 10,\,\,\,Z\rightarrow 11.

(93)

For an
n
-qubit Pauli string, this defines a binary vector
b\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}
.
Its corresponding integer representation is

\operatorname{int}:\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z},\qquad\operatorname{int}(b)=\sum_{i=0}^{2n-1}b_{i}\,2^{i}.

(94)

The
lowest integer representation
of
s_{0}
is then defined as

\mathrm{argmin}_{s\in\operatorname{Orb}_{G}(s_{0})}\operatorname{int}(s).

(95)

The binary representation convention is equivalent to the more familiar
symplectic representation
of Pauli strings,

P=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X^{a_{i}}Z^{b_{i}},\qquad a_{i},b_{i}\in\{0,1\},

(96)

where multiplication of Pauli operators corresponds to addition modulo two in the symplectic vector space
51
.
Here, we follow the base-
4
numeral conventions as Ref.
51
