Exploring the drivers of low DPT3 vaccination coverage and the implications in rural, urban and peri-urban Uganda: a cross-sectional study of Hoima and Wakiso districts of Uganda
Authors: Oliver Ombeva Malande, Deogratias Munube, Richard Muhindo, Barnabas Kigen, Julia Jerono Songok, Kamulegeya John, Johanna Catharina Meyer, Brian Godman, Victor Eneojo Adamu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn a recent observational study, we reported findings on barriers to effective immunization in rural Hoima District, western Uganda, where it emerged that rural contexts may not have same determinants as peri-urban or urban contexts driving underimmunization. In a continuation of that work to incorporate peri-urban and urban barriers to immunization, this study reports a follow up on the recommendations of our earlier publication, through a case study of (peri) urban Wakiso district, and incorporates and compares the findings from both districts to propose unified recommendations to address low immunization coverage (under 90%) in Uganda. The aim was therefore to determine and describe the barriers to the uptake and utilization of immunization services in Hoima and Wakiso districts of Uganda and propose interventions, strategies and approaches that can help address the problem of vaccine hesitancy and underimmunization in Uganda.MethodsThis was a mixed methods study, that utilized interviews with child caregivers, for the quantitative design and focus group discussions (FGDs) with caregivers of children eligible for vaccination, and single in-person in-depth interviews with health workers, and immunization focal persons (KIs) for the qualitative part in both Wakiso (per-urban) and Hoima (rural) districts of Uganda.ResultsIn this study, majority of the caregivers (369/643) were 21–25 years of age, female, married and protestants, with basic secondary education. The study found that 91% (588/643) of respondents consider vaccines safe, 85% (547/643) consider vaccines effective, 95% (611/643) have complete trust in vaccines and 13% have some misgivings regarding vaccines. Both Districts are under-immunized (Hoima 81% and Wakiso 75.3% DPT3 coverage). Factors independently associated with low DPT3 coverage include: low trust in vaccines, being a single parent caregiver, those who consider vaccines unsafe, relying on social media as source of vaccine information, those who did not receive any education on vaccines during immunization visits, and those who have misgivings about vaccines. Additional drivers of underimmunization include access difficulties, geographical barriers, inadequate funding, cold chain inadequacies, inadequate social mobilization, vaccine stock outs, high training needs for health workers, and adverse events following immunization.ConclusionsThis study found that both Hoima and Wakiso districts are under-immunized given a DPT3 vaccine coverage less than 90%. The Ministry of Health needs to train Health Workers through improved mentorship, enhance outreach services, social mobilization, and build trust in communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27328-x.Keywords: Vaccine, Under-immunization, Low DPT 3 coverage, Rural, Peri-urban, Hesitancy, Key drivers, Uganda

Background

The highest proportion of deaths and diseases occurring in poor and developing countries, also referred to as low-and middle-income countries (LMICs), follow infectious diseases that are largely vaccine-preventable [1–5]. Consequently, immunization is a key strategy to ensure children live to adulthood, in addition to averting more than 4 million deaths annually, especially in the very poor or low-income communities of the world [6, 7]. We are aware that some interventions aimed at increasing the global availability of immunization services have been added to the Abuja Declaration on health, whose theme was to promote allocation of resources to health through resolutions that mirrored those under the child survival action convention for political mobilization [8]. These aims informed the health-leaning sections of the United Nations’ Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), which were later replaced by the current Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The focus of the third SDG is on reducing the deaths of children under the age of 5 years to less than 25 deaths for every 1000 live births by the year 2030.

Recent reports point to a prolonged overall stagnation in vaccination coverage levels globally, with no vaccine shown to exceed 90% coverage at the global level [9, 10]. This stagnation has undoubtedly been impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic in recent years both in terms of lock-down measures appreciably impacting on vaccination rates with an envisaged impact on future morbidity and mortality unless pro-actively addressed [11–13]. Alongside this, ongoing controversy and misinformation surrounding the combination measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine in children is still affecting vaccine uptake, with similar unfounded concerns that the polio vaccine causes AIDS, sterility and cancer, impacted on its uptake in Africa. Examples such as these, alongside the extensive conspiracy theories surrounding COVID-19 vaccines, have raised major concerns regarding the strategies that are currently being used to address vaccine hesitancy globally [14–17]. In addition, whether additional interventions are now necessary to tip the scales and to move the world much closer to achieving SDG 3.b.1, that targets universal vaccination coverage worldwide. An important strategy going forward is to reduce missed opportunities against vaccination and address current low acceptancy of vaccines where these exist owing to vaccine hesitancy and prevailing under-immunization [18, 19].

Overall, refusal and hesitancy to vaccines are now the central threats to national immunization programs meeting their targets, and considered one of the 10 biggest public health threats globally [20]. The WHO defines hesitancy as “a delay in the acceptance of or the refusal of vaccines by communities despite the availability of vaccination services.” Vaccine hesitancy is widespread and context specific, with the WHO SAGE working group on vaccine hesitancy designing a vaccine hesitancy matrix to facilitate understanding the situation within a specific context. The matrix categorizes the reasons behind vaccine hesitancy into individual and group factors, contextual factors, and vaccine or vaccination-specific factors [21].

Besides hesitancy, there are inequalities and inequities in the provision of immunization services and coverage globally, with these often being masked by national or regional summarized immunization data, not representing the true picture [11, 22, 23]. In sub-Saharan Africa, including Uganda, over 170 child deaths per 1000 live births are reported under the age of 5 years [9]. Manifestations of inequities and inequalities in this region include inadequate access to vaccines, sustained low vaccination coverage, low education level of caregivers, a young age of some caregivers owing to teenage pregnancies, poor or low social economic status of caregivers, as well as inadequate male partner or fathers’ involvement in care delivery [2, 24–27]. A deeper and broader understanding of these factors associated with poor vaccine uptake is critical and needed to improve vaccination coverage in Uganda and beyond.

Despite multiple previous efforts in the immunization, there remains a gap in the understanding of the reasons for low DPT3 immunization rates (referred to as under-immunized) and recurrence of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) in Uganda. Alongside this, the need for further recommendations regarding interventions that can help address this problem building on identified concerns in Uganda and across Africa. We are aware that there are reports from Uganda and around the region attempting to investigate and explain reasons for sub-optimal uptake of available vaccines amongst many communities in the region [6, 27]. Some of the barriers identified in these reports include difficulties in language hampering effective communication as well as difficult terrain and poor road network, making transportation difficult when trying to access immunization centers for delivery of supplies or even for supervision and mentorship [19, 27–30]. There are additional barriers in these reports, affecting access or availability or acceptance of immunization services in Uganda and countries with similar contexts in sub-Saharan Africa. These include a large population with inadequate to low education levels; a generally high number of adolescent or teenage pregnancies that end up in early parenthood; conflict ridden borders including DR Congo or South Sudan with Uganda that has communities constantly on the move and high numbers of refugees housed in for instance in the Hoima District in Uganda [19, 27–30]. Consequently, this study was undertaken to address this information gap by aiming to quantify just how appreciable is problem of vaccine hesitancy and low DPT 3 coverage is in Uganda, and to what extent the related determinants and drivers contribute to the reported low utilization of immunization services in urban and rural districts of Uganda. The aim was therefore to determine and describe the barriers to the uptake and utilization of immunization services in Hoima and Wakiso districts of Uganda and propose interventions, strategies and approaches that can help address the problem of vaccine hesitancy and low DPT 3 coverage in Uganda. This includes ascertaining the current situation and attitudes among child caregivers.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study mixed methods study, that utilized consisting of questionnaire interviews with child caregivers, for the quantitative design and focus group discussions (FGDs) with caregivers of children eligible for vaccination, and single in-person in-depth interviews with health workers, and immunization focal persons (KIs) for the qualitative part in both the districts of Wasiko (per-urban) and Hoima (rural) districts of Uganda, conducted from 10th March 2022 to 30th August 2022. The study entailed structured interviewer-administered questionnaires through in-depth interviews (IDI) with child caregivers. A list of open-ended questions tool was used for key informants (KIIs), who were mainly focal vaccination personnel from Wakiso and Hoima districts. The focus groups and KIIs comprised the main sources of qualitative data for the study, while further additional data came from records at district and national offices in the Ministry of Health. DPT3 coverage was used a marker for immunization coverage as is standard EPI practice [31]. This study utilized both quantitative and qualitative methods to maximize on the advantages of triangulation – by enhancing the study validity, maintaining credibility, and improving reliability of the findings. This is because the use of multiple methods and data sources broadened the reach of the findings, enabled the qualitative FGDs to address matters not adequately exhausted in the questionnaires until saturation was achieved, minimizes bias in the study and helped provides a holistic and comprehensive analysis on the study while overcoming limitations.

Study population and setting

The study population of 311 respondents from Hoima and 332 from Wakiso consisted of caregiver-child pairs (care giver with a child aged 9–59 months) attending vaccination services in any of the designated study health facilities that offer immunization services. The two districts were chosen in consultation with the Uganda EPI program for high burden (poorly performing) rural, urban and peri-urban populations. Wakiso district was chosen for this research as it is the fastest growing District in Uganda, located in Central region, around Kampala city, with a population of over two million consisting of urban and peri urban communities and having recurrent outbreaks of vaccine preventable diseases and had never achieved over 90% vaccine coverage [32]. The district has two counties (Kyadondo and Busiro) and a municipality (Entebbe); 15 sub-counties, 146 parishes and 704 villages. Hoima District is located in Western Uganda in the Bunyoro-Kitara region. It is a rural district, also performing poorly in immunization indices, with a large refugee population as well as highly mobile immigrant communities from the DR Congo potentially providing a very different viewpoint. Hoima district is made of thirteen sub-counties, of 54 parishes, which comprise 643 villages.

Sampling (size and technique)

The WHO 30 cluster sampling technique was used to determine the sample size [33]. This technique for immunization coverage uses a cluster survey design and assigns the minimum number from the target population that should be sampled and surveyed in order to provide statistically valid data. The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) Cluster Survey Design recommends the use of 30 clusters taking at least 7 respondents from each cluster to give a total of 210 quantitative samples [33, 34]. In this study, to allow for no response, 3 respondents were added per cluster, which resulted in a total minimum quantitative sample size of 300 per district. Hoima district has 13 sub counties, 54 parishes, and 643 villages, while Wakiso district has 15 sub counties, 146 parishes and 704 villages. Using the sub counties as clusters, 6 sub counties in Wakiso were considered (3 urban, 3 peri-urban for Wakiso, and all 6 rural for Hoima) to provide an even distribution of respondents, with 50 minimum caregiver-child pairs from each of the 6 sub-counties.

Study variables and inclusion criteria

Independent variables comprised the following predictor variables: Age of the caregiver; marital status; religion; level of education; occupation; sex of the caregiver; distance to the health facility; waiting time before receiving immunization services at the facility or outreach site. The dependent variable incorporated the acceptance of immunization services.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for quantitative study arm entailed the following: A caregiver with at least one child (9–59 months); resident of Hoima/Wakiso district; can give the required information; resided in the relevant district for at least 12 months; providing consent for his/her child to participate in the study; child’s vaccination book record/vaccination card available on the day of interview. The exclusion criteria included a caregiver of a child aged 9–59 months in their home who is unable to provide the required information due to mental/other incapacity.

Data processing (collection and analysis)

The quantitative arm entailed a structured interviewer-administered piloted questionnaire. The interview guide used for focus group discussions and in-depth interviews was developed for this study specifically, and an English language version has been provided as a supplementary file. This tool was pre-tested through a test-retest approach on two segments of 5 each and 6 each in Hoima and 8 each in per-urban and 8 each in urban Wakiso each at different times and it returned satisfactory desired results. It is these 11 participants for Hoima [27] and 32 participants for Wakiso that produced the additional sample number added to the target sample of 300 for each district; giving a final sample size of 311 for Hoima District and 332 for Wakiso District. The additional sample (11 plus 32) results were added to the results and analyzed. Focus group discussions and KIIs made the qualitative data. FGDs were conducted either in Lunyoro, Luganda or English depending on language best understood by participants in that session. Both qualitative and quantitative data were collected from 10th March to 30th August 2022, with closure of follow up visits of data collection on 30th August 2022. In the case of additional secondary Hoima data, where applicable, this is adopted as referenced from our related work [27].

The Luganda/Lunyoro focus group discussion recordings were translated into English, taken through reverse translation reliability, and transcribed and saved as Microsoft Word™ documents. These were then imported into NVivo 12 for coding of the data, followed by a thematic analysis technique. The research team, comprising investigators and assistants jointly reviewed the transcripts and identified the coding frame. In this process, one person first did coding for one or two transcripts, then followed by recoding by another person. Following this, they performed transition from open codes to themes as well as the definitions and relationships between the latter.

For the quantitative component, data from the pre-coded questionnaires were entered onto a Microsoft Excel™ spreadsheet. Data were subsequently transferred into STATA version 14 for analysis where bivariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. All factors with a P-value less than 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were entered into a model designed for logistic regression. This sought to determine the independent or multivariable factors associated with coverage, as is the standard definition by those who received DPT3 vaccine dose. These findings were further refined through Poisson regression analysis to determine health system factors independently associated with completion of immunization schedules. Descriptive analysis of the data was expressed as means ± standard deviations, percentages and frequencies, with associations between variables analyzed using the chi-square test. Differences between means were assessed using the student’s t-test. Respondents’ responses on different variables, especially on vaccine confidence, trust, safety, effectives and misgivings were described as proportions. P-values of below 0.05 were considered statistically significant and confidence intervals of 95% were used. The focus group discussion participants were chosen through purposive sampling in order to achieve maximum variation in the sample. They were conducted in the local language (or in the language spoken and understood by all participants) by a moderator and a note taker. The discussions were audio recorded and then later transcribed, with transcripts being reviewed by two separate reviewers to avoid bias in transcription and message translation. To further reduce selection bias, the FGD participants were different from those who had participated in the individual caregiver-child interviews.

Findings from the focus group discussions and key informant interviews

Theme I: explores the knowledge, attitudes and the perceptions of caregivers towards immunization

The FGDs revealed a satisfactory level of knowledge amongst caregivers about immunization. Most of them understood that immunization protects their children from vaccine preventable diseases .

“… vaccines are important in prevention of illnesses and diseases like being lame due to polio.” (FGD - Buseruka; single mother of two children and no formal education).

Theme II: this reports on the state of immunization services in Hoima and Wakiso districts

Caregivers were asked for their view regarding the overall delivery of immunization services within the district. Their responses indicated that the services were available but had limitations:

One of the KI noted the following about their immunization coverage:

"We do static immunization on Tuesday and outreach on Thursday. For newly born babies still within the facility, they receive immunization in the hospital on day one. In a month we immunize at least They rage 150–200 and some who come from very far miss out on the immunization services."

In one of the districts, the interviewee described that the health staff work on rotational basis in order to manage staff shortages:

"We have a shortage but we rotate so in this unit, we have 1 senior nursing officer, 4 nursing officers, 2 enrolled midwives and 2 nursing assistants. That makes nine in total. But right now, we have one staff in the clinic."

An EPI focal person from Dwooli Health Centre III had this to say about the current cold chain system;

“Yes we have; We have a fridge, we monitor the temperatures, we have vaccine control books, we have child registers, so we are able to make our system. Power supply is gas but has a problem of inconsistent supply. It can go off, it’s at night or weekend and you don’t have transport to get what to replace it yet it’s supposed to. Even our fridge is old model. It goes off even when gas is still on. About 3 times since 2013 when I came here. We have reported that and they are planning replacement of a solar fridge. It freezes so easily too so you have to keep defrosting. It takes about a day to fix”.

Mothers who participated in the FGD at Buchayaya Health Centre III confirmed the importance of educating caregivers about immunization:

“Health workers should continue to advertise immunization services. We should be told the importance of immunization. Health facilities should stock more vaccines in the health facilities. The people should be educated well on the vaccines that they introduce because some people have poor perceptions on the vaccines”.

Similarly, at Kikube HC III, one of the mothers in the FGD said that outreach serves could be improved further by saying, “The doctors should sensitize caregivers and educate them. The health workers should give outreach service. Caregivers should be convinced to come or through giving them incentives to make them bring their children for immunization.”

Findings from the FGD held at Tonya Health Centre II suggested the following measure to create demand for immunization services, they noted;

“Health workers should mobilize the community and inform them of scheduled immunization dates. Health workers do not have transport to do outreach services; this facility has 9 villages under it. The government should give health workers transport”.

Theme III: exploring the gaps in immunization service provision promoting low vaccine uptake or completion of recommended schedules

The Sebigoro Health Centre III, Assistant Clinical officer had this to say; “The challenge is actually transportation especially when we have emergencies”.

The nurse in charge of Kikube Health Centre said: “The challenge we face at the facility is lack of transport and the staff who go for outreach are women and cannot ride boda boda”.

The LCI chairman of Hoima Hospital Cell said about the location of Hoima: “Because Hoima is on the border, we have many people coming from Congo, and after giving birth, you find a woman spent three months before bringing the baby for immunization because they go back to Congo and don’t return at all for necessary immunization”.

The health worker in charge of Buchayaya Health Centre III reported this regarding vaccine supply; “The district stores supply us vaccines, the supply is adequate but at times we get stock out of BCG and measles vaccines. Like a month ago, the whole district did not have BCG vaccine, and the previous month measles vaccine was out of stock.”

The VHT Kabwooya HC III had this to say about vaccine supply and stock outs; “The vaccines are inadequate, because sometimes mothers come and don’t find the vaccines. We get outreach services every Saturday.”

Regarding communication difficulties with caregivers, the head of Tonya Health Centre said; “Most of our patients originate from Alur and do not speak Runyoro. A few speak Kiswahili. This makes communication a big problem”.

Sebigoro Health Centre III Assistant in charge also commented on language barrier as a challenge to immunization uptake by saying: “We have a challenge of language barrier, some caregivers don’t know English or Kiswahili so and communization is difficult with them.”

Cultural and religious barriers

In relation to culture influencing decisions regarding vaccines, the VHT Buhimba East said: “I have heard in neighboring village that because God told them not to take their children for immunization or to school.”

The LCI Secretary Buhimba East on the matter of religious barriers pointed out that; “A local group says that even Jesus was not immunized so there is no need for immunization for their children.”

The VHT Buhanika said: “There is a religious cult called “egiri ya Yesu” that teaches against immunization and vaccines.”

The VHT Kabwooya HC said: “The major thing is cultural barrier and some clans believe that they shouldn’t be immunized. The people have poor perspectives on immunization. The people are illiterate”.

A respondent in an FGD at Bujumbura HC III, pointed out that; “There are some religions that don’t encourage mothers to come for immunization; they say that immunization makes children get more diseases. There are men who don’t give women transport to go to hospital yet the hospital is far.”

Theme IV: ways in which the capacity of health workers in Hoima and Wakiso districts can be strengthened in order to reach at least 90% of the unvaccinated children

The former LC III Kabwoya had this to say about ways of strengthening capacity of Health workers. “I would like to ask the government to put up more immunizing health facilities in the parishes so that the services are closer to the people.”

Sensitization about a program is one of the most effective way of getting communities respond to a program from the government or acquire knowledge about what it is about. The LCI Hoima Hospital Cell had this to say about sensitization;

"The VHT systems should help to sensitize the people about immunization in communities."

The VHT Kikube HC III had this to say about mobilization and sensitization of mothers, he said that;

"They should facilitate us to reach all corners in the days of immunization. Of recent we have been using house to house mobilization and it’s not effective, some people are not reached. Even the days we are given to mobilize are less for mass immunization and for outreaches, they should put more points."

The Hoima Regional Hospital cell had this to say about sensitization and mobilization;

“Through mobilization and sensitization of the people in the villages and it should be done by the health staff therefore we need to increase the health staff in the district”.

The former LC III Chairman mentioned a statement emphasizing outreaches;

"I would also like to request any NGO or government to facilitate outreach services if the facilities are not up."

The VHT Butema also emphasized the need for outreaches he said that; “We need to introduce more outreaches to cover more areas”.

The VHT Kiragura parish had this to say on transport issues for health workers, he pointed out that;

“We need to support these health workers because transport is poor. It causes time delay and so forth for these health workers”.

THEME V: identification, reporting and management of AEFIs

Respondents were asked whether any of their children had experienced any adverse events following immunization, and health workers asked if the caregivers attending their facility had noticed or recorded any AEFIs; if so, how often does this happen, what would you attribute it to, do you have a reporting system for AEFIs; is this system standardized; is there a reporting tool available for this and what are the measures taken to prevent this from recurring.

The EPI focal person from Kigorobya Health Centre IV had this to say about AEFIs she had this to say;

“We receive reports about adverse events. Recording and reporting rarely occurs. Health workers need to be trained better on reporting”.

The matters of AEFIs were clearly articulated by the Assistant Incharge Sebigoro Health Centre, he had this to say about the matters;

“It only happened once in one month, it was attributed to DPT, I believe the focal person reported about it. Yes, we have reporting system, we discussed it in our meeting and we hope reporting improves”.

The EPI Focal Person Hoima regional referral hospital said this about AEFIs:

"We normally talk about AEFIs and similar reactions for children after immunization and give advice on what to do but we have not had any AEFIs. We have an emergency tray because as long as we give an injection, we expect any. So they emergency tray is to help us in sorting out any effects. Those at home, we advise they come to the hospital very fast. Our main emphasis is educating the mothers."

Discussion

We believe this is the first study to identify the main barriers to effective immunization that drives under-immunization (low DPT 3 coverage) in the Hoima and Wakiso Districts of Uganda. The drivers of under-immunization in our study include vaccine stock outs (due to unreliable vaccine supply), problems with access to health facilities that offer immunization services (due to transportation related difficulties, very long distance that clients have to travel to health facilities and a very difficult geographical terrain that is hard for vehicles or motor cycles to navigate). Across many areas of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), terrain and transport difficulties have been documented as significant drivers of poor immunization service uptake [35]. The caregivers may desire to have their children vaccinated, but the access barriers pose a real threat to immunization schedule completion. In Uganda, this problem is widespread, and has been reported in other parts of the country, especially Yumbe district in far north western part of the country along Congo Border, with similar findings in studies in Nigeria and Kenya [5, 19, 35, 36].

The findings from Hoima district reveal that the district has adequate Health Facilities with capacity to provide required immunization services if the vaccines and personnel could be adequately provided. Indeed, the ratio of facility to population has been previously described as satisfactory; however, the lack of adequate staff numbers and cold chain infrastructure capacity remain key drivers of under-immunization (low DPT 3 coverage) [5, 19, 27, 35–37]. Some of the health facilities have no vaccine refrigerators or have refrigerators that are malfunctional. These facilities have to depend on other neighbouring health facilities for storage of vaccines. However, these supporting facilities are often located very far away, and coupled with the district’s poor road terrain, provide major barriers to the movement of the vaccines to the vaccination centres and back for storage. Some of the health facilities without refrigerators include Buraru, Kisiha, Nsozi Health Centres. A key concern in Uganda is that this is a widespread problem, and contributes to missed opportunities for immunization. Alongside this, contributes to long waiting time at both static and outreach centers of immunization, a factor that is recognized as a major driver of under-immunization (low DPT 3 coverage). Previous reports have attributed this problem to inadequate supplies of vaccines, lack of vaccine storage and inadequate numbers of health workers to operate the immunization services [19, 38, 39]. These supply and access barriers have been described widely in studies in sub Saharan Africa, where the failure of countries to allocate 15% of their GDP to immunization as per the Abuja declaration has left them stretched in their effort to balance pressing demands [6, 22, 24, 35, 40].

In Wakiso, an urban/peri-urban set up for example, the problems of inadequate staff numbers at health facilities, the unreliable supply of vaccine stocks, and long waiting hours are comparable to rural Hoima. Wakiso however relies more on electricity than gas to power the vaccine refrigerators, and the unreliable electricity supply, coupled with inadequate allocation of financial resources to effectively run the immunization programs, remains a significant barrier to increasing immunization rates. This was similar to reports in Yumbe, and elsewhere in sub Saharan Africa (SSA). The commitment of health workers in both Wakiso and Hoima, whilst laudable, is frustrated by poor transport infrastructure and terrain in Hoima or poor allocation of transport finances in both districts. As a result, making it difficult even for central district immunization officers to supervise and mentor and support health facilities.

Wakiso District currently lacks an adequate number of health facilities to provide required immunization services, and the facilities suffer shortage of health workers, vaccine stock outs, stable electricity supply, and inadequate financial resources to ensure outreach programs. Vaccine stock outs have been attributed to unreliable vaccine supply, while the prevailing difficulties in accessing far located health facilities, which offer immunization services, are linked to inadequate transport finances. The cold chain team is committed, motivated and well mapped out to cover the district. Besides frequent vaccine stock outs especially affecting BCG and oral polio and rota virus oral vaccines, some of the health facilities in Wakiso lack adequate vaccine fridges. The problem of lack of fridges is complicated by unstable electricity supply to facilities in Wakiso district.

The health teams in both districts were also generally not well versed with programs for managing adverse events following immunization (AEFIs). The Ministry of Health in Uganda has a system in place, complete with a committee of adverse events causality assessment. However, the implementation of its functioning has not been adequately developed across these districts, with very few health workers having knowledge how to use it. The launch of measles – rubella (MR) vaccines was associated with marked antivaccine sentiments and hesitancy, especially fears around alleged autism and convulsions and neurological sequalae building on unfounded and now retracted findings of Wakefield [41], with the impact still persisting today [42]. There are still cults and religious groups in Wakiso and Hoima that have been linked to anti vaccine messages and sentiments building on misinformation.

Generally, the communities in the two districts in Uganda accept vaccines and immunization. The health workers as well were found to be hard working, committed, but overwhelmed with work, reflected by the numbers of caregivers served by each health worker being currently very high. This affects their ability to offer outreach services to help address concerns with parents and their children being able to reach facilities. The lack of adequate funding, especially for fuel and transport, exacerbates this problem. Some outreach sites are also very far from the facilities and some are hard to reach due to a hilly narrow road that are difficult to use in rainy seasons. This problem as well affects the caregivers, who find it difficult to take their children to the Health Facilities or outreach sites for immunization. Besides frequent vaccine stock outs especially affecting BCG and the oral polio and rotavirus oral vaccines, some of the health facilities in Hoima have no vaccine fridges and therefore have to depend of other neighbouring health facilities for storage of vaccines. This issue becomes even more challenging if some of the facilities with working fridges are located far away, a situation that is worsened by the district’s poor road terrain. The problem of lack of fridges is complicated by poor access to gas supplied for the fridges.

This study found that the level of acceptance of immunization services and vaccines in general among communities in Wakiso and Hoima districts are satisfactory, with caregivers being receptive to both old and new vaccines, even for those who would get vaccination from outreach centres. The large turn out and long lines of caregivers waiting for vaccination at the outreach centres is testimony of acceptable levels of vaccine acceptance that needs to be sustained. Demand creation and sustainability requires deliberate efforts by the government and ministry of health and other key stakeholders to ensure they support and equip the immunization program with the required enabling infrastructure [6, 22, 24, 35, 40]. The rationale for having outreach programs was to help expand access to the immunization programs, especially for communities and populations that would for one reason or another not travel to static immunization centres. The lack of adequate funding to support this program, especially provision of transport for staff and vaccines, coupled with the difficult to navigate geographical terrain greatly hampers the provision of an effective immunization program. This barrier affects both health workers and caregivers, who struggle to access health facilities, and often give up. Caregivers reported that they would rather stay home and run their own businesses or go to the farms than report to facilities where they will find vaccine snot available or very long lines with extended waiting hours. There are additional drivers of low DPT immunization identified in Hoima and Wakiso that relate to inability of children (with long-term illnesses, orphans, children born premature, children catered for by elderly grandmothers as caregivers) to attend immunization visits or scheduled appointments. The challenge involved in transporting these children to the facilities are enormous to these poor parents, tied to the high levels of poverty and financial need, factors that jointly affect effective delivery of immunization services. These are the populations that would be best served by an effective mobile outreach program, that can deliver vaccines as close to their residence as possible.

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study filled a critical gap in knowledge by identifying and defining the drivers of poor immunization in Hoima and Wakiso Districts. The biggest problems identified centred around access difficulties, inadequate funding, geographical barriers, vaccine stock outs, Cold chain inadequacies, high training needs for health workers and inadequate social mobilisation. Out of this study, we expect to see strengthened immunization services in Wakiso and Hoima districts; improved knowledge, communication and skills in the immunization service delivery by health workers as a way of encouraging parents to come for the services; enhanced community engagement, and participation in immunization, uptake of immunization services and empowerment to demand for immunization services and adhering to recommended immunization schedules; generation of guidelines and tools for assessing vaccine hesitancy and low DPT immunization; reduction in the out breaks and the occurrence/recurrence of common vaccine preventable disease in Wakiso and Hoima districts; improved ministry of health and government funding and investment into immunization especially in fighting the problem of vaccine hesitancy and improved numbers of children who survive up to the fifth birthday, due to reduced morbidity and mortality as a result of vaccine preventable diseases following improved uptake and availability of immunization services in Hoima and Wakiso Districts.

Conclusions

This study found that low immunization rates in Hoima and Wakiso districts are due to frequent stock outs, difficult to access immunization centres, inadequately trained health workers, communities not adequately mobilized to utilize vaccination services, a poor understanding, identification and reporting of Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI), which are drivers of reduced uptake of some vaccines.

Recommendations

There is need for the ministry of health to support the outreach component of immunization in Hoima district in terms of transportation, mentorship and ensuring timely supplies, to ensure vaccines reach all health centres from the district vaccine stores and reduce stock outs. There is need for the ministry of health and the stakeholders in immunization to always train health professionals on current and new vaccines. This training should also target focal persons involved in immunization, village health teams and health promotion officers within the community and communication to caretakers. The ministry of health through UNEPI should educate and engage the health workers on the currently existing system for identifying, recording, treating and reporting AEFIs. It is critical that the ministry of health puts in place interventions to increase vaccine acceptance, focusing on better communication, use of social media, religious influences, enabling culture, gender disparities hindering immunization, political stability and support, and improving road network and access to facilities.
