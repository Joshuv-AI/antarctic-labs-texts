AbstractCase SummaryWe here present a case of a patient admitted with acute recurrent pancreatitis. An electrocardiogram (ECG) was obtained to assess clinical deterioration after presentation. The ECG showed sinus tachycardia with severe convex ST-segment elevation in the inferior and anteroseptal leads, with reciprocal ST-segment depression in the lateral leads, worrisome for acute extensive myocardial infarction. The ECG normalized after 6 hours.Take-Home MessageThe unique ECG findings are described as a “spiked helmet sign,” a pattern of ST-segment elevation seen in critically ill patients in the absence of acute myocardial infarction/ischemia.Key Words: electrocardiographic artifact, spiked helmet sign, ST-segment elevation

Case Report

A 42-year-old male patient presented to a rural hospital with a 2-day history of epigastric pain radiating to the back. The patient had a known history of alcohol abuse, hypertriglyceridemia, and pancreatitis. His physical examination showed normal vital signs, normal cardiovascular examination findings, and abdominal tenderness with minimal rebound. Initial electrocardiographic (ECG) and troponin test results were normal. The patient's abdominal pain worsened overnight. The patient became tachycardic and diaphoretic. His abdomen became more distended and quite firm. An ECG was obtained (Figure 1), which showed sinus tachycardia with severe convex ST-segment elevation in the inferior and anteroseptal leads, with reciprocal ST-segment depression in the lateral leads and prolonged QT interval. Response to ECG Challenges

Spiked helmet sign (SHS): ST-segment elevation is noted in multiple leads. Leads III and aVF show a sharp peaked R-wave followed by a concave ST-segment elevation right after the R-wave peak. The shape of the QRS complex is very similar to a military spiked helmet. When consecutive beats are viewed, the peculiar ST-T waves show a “wavy” undulating pattern that seems continuous, if the QRS complexes were to be deleted. What is unique about this pattern differentiating it from an ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction is that the upward baseline shift starts before the onset of the QRS complex. No Q or abnormal T waves are noted. Reciprocal ECG changes probably just represent mirror-image reflection of ST-segment elevation. Heart catheterization showed normal coronary arteries. Repeat troponin levels were normal.

Discussion

An SHS was first described by Littmann et al1 in 2011 in a cohort of 8 critically ill patients. The peculiar ST-segment elevation was consistent with myocardial infarction; however, all patients had negative cardiac markers. The shape of the QRS complex resembled the Pickelhaube, a spiked helmet worn by the Prussian and German soldiers in the 19th and early 20th centuries. A high mortality rate was reported (6 of 8 patients). A catastrophic event involving the abdomen or chest was the most common underlying pathology, with either elevated intra-abdominal or intrathoracic pressure. ST-segment elevation seems to occur in the inferior leads during acute abdominal events and in the precordial leads during thoracic events. Other pathologic conditions associated with SHS included sepsis, subarachnoid hemorrhage, cocaine abuse, and stellate ganglionectomy.2

The etiology of the SHS is uncertain. Littmann et al1 suggested that pseudo–ST-segment elevation is due to repetitive epidermal stretch in association with nearby pulsatile flow or due to an acute rise in the intrathoracic or intra-abdominal pressure.

An SHS reported due to a left leg lead placed over a visibly pulsatile aorta strongly suggests that transferred arterial pulsations could cause the ST-segment elevation and explains the synchronicity of the upward shift of the baseline preceding and following the QRS complex.3

SHS remains exceedingly rare. Approximately 40 cases were reported by 2023.2 Mortality up to 59% was documented. A few more cases have been reported since. The number of cases is expected to rise once clinicians become familiar with the condition.

Resolution of SHS occurred after treatment of hypoxia, correction of metabolic acidosis, resection of ischemic bowel, nasogastric decompression, correction of acidosis, and sometimes spontaneously.2 In our patient, correction of acidosis and improvement of pancreatitis led to resolution of SHS.
