Evaluating the effectiveness and utilization of the primary emergency care training in Nepal: a longitudinal mixed methods study
Authors: Rabindra Bhandari, Arpana B C Kalaunee, Archana Amatya, Yadu Chandra Ghimire, Madhab Prasad Lamsal, Deepa Chitrakar, Babu Ram Bhusal, Anjila Thapa, Abigail Knoble, Pravin Paudel, Ruma Rajbhandari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency care is essential for the management of the growing burden of disease and traffic injuries in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Nepal, nearly one in ten deaths in 2017 were attributed to injury. The Primary Emergency Care (PEC) training package of 6 days duration was developed to equip the various health care cadres with knowledge and skills to deliver emergency response. The study evaluates the effectiveness of the training, skill utilization, and the significance of an enabling work environment on application of acquired knowledge and skills.MethodsA mixed-methods evaluation was conducted using the four-level Kirkpatrick Model (KM) to assess the PEC training. Participant details and training scores were obtained from training records of 295 training graduates for level one results. Pre/post-knowledge tests were compared using paired t-tests. Eleven health facilities were purposively selected for follow up, assessing 48 PEC graduates via knowledge assessments, structured clinical skills assessments, and simulations. Qualitative data were collected via 19 in-depth interviews, 10 key informant interviews with hospital leadership, and 11 focus group discussions (FGDs) with non-PEC-trained staff. These qualitative data were thematically analyzed using Ms-excel.ResultsThere was a significant 35.9% ± 17.3% increase in knowledge after the PEC training (p = 0.001), with a significant 7.2% ± 12.9% decline at study follow up (p = 0.001), although there was no significant difference by length of time since the training (p = 0.652). Paramedics showed the greatest loss in knowledge compared to other health cadres (p = 0.005). Trainees reported increased confidence, and workplace observations indicated a positive “ripple effect” of peer-to-peer knowledge sharing among PEC and non-PEC staff.ConclusionsThe PEC training significantly improved knowledge and skills with evidence of sustained knowledge retention although a modest but significant decline was observed over time along with evidence of workplace application of skills. Limitations existed in measuring patient level outcomes as per the KM, positive spillover effects along with an enabling environment were reported in emergency departments as result of the training. The training is a feasible, low-cost approach to strengthening emergency care in resource-limited settings like Nepal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01593-w.Keywords: Primary emergency care, Effectiveness, Training evaluation, Knowledge, Skills retention, Emergency, Basic life support, Healthcare, Professionals

Introduction

Every year, 90%of 1.29 million traffic accident deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. However, emergency care to address these needs are chronically under prioritized, staff are poorly trained, and infrastructure and equipment remain lacking even as service access has expanded [2–5]. Further, the need for quality emergency care is growing with road traffic accidents being projected to become the third leading cause of death in LMICs by 2030 [6]. The ongoing epidemiological transition towards noncommunicable diseases is increasing the need for emergency care systems that can manage more complex cases [7, 8]. Recent estimates by the World Health Organization (WHO) has shown 9.6% of all causes of death attributed to injuries in 2021 and a staggering 40.9% of all injury mortality estimated to be caused by road traffic accidents [9]. If emergency care access and quality were improved, an estimated 58% of annual mortality in LMICs could be reduced [10, 11]. Recognizing this, WHO has drafted several resolutions to call for an improvement in emergency care, emphasizing the training of service providers [12, 13].

In 2017, 9.2% of all deaths in Nepal were attributed to injuries, the majority being related to transportation, and in line with global trends, the proportion has been steadily increasing [14]. The road traffic injuries have been observed at an alarming rate in the last three FYs (FY starts in mid-July) from 44,397 in 2022/2023 to 70,832 in 2024/2025 as per the Health Management Information System (HMIS) [15]. Although literature on emergency care in Nepal is limited, especially in rural areas, it is estimated that the number of additional people expected to be protected from health emergencies is projected to be 15.2 m (9.8 –19.5 m) by 2025 compared to 2018 [16, 17]. Around the country, district or peripheral hospitals are expected to provide 24 h emergency service, but the actual readiness of these facilities to provide quality emergency care is poor [4, 18]. In these settings, care is usually provided by health assistants (HAs) with three years of formal training, Medical Officers (MOs, M.B.B.S degree), and Medical Doctorate in General Practice (MDGPs, family physicians) [19]. Although MDGPs are the most qualified to provide emergency care, in reality HAs and MOs provide the majority of the services in rural areas, and lack regular emergency care training [20]. Even 90% of surveyed MDGPs requested further training to upgrade their acute emergency care skills, suggesting widespread need [21]. This need has been recognized with emergency medicine as a specialty, shortly following the establishment of the first National Trauma Center in 2015 in the country [18].

In the year 2018/19, the Primary Emergency Care (PEC) training, a comprehensive six-day (total 42 instructional hours) training package, was developed by the National Health Training Center (NHTC) under the Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP) in support of Nick Simons Institute in consultation with the team of senior doctors working at the emergency department of various tertiary level hospitals as a technical working group [22]. This training package is for those health care providers working in the emergency department (ED) of hospitals and Primary Health Care Centers (PHC) where they need to manage a wide range of emergencies with limited resources, deemed to address this gap. This is an off-site, in-service and group based, clinical course. The training comprises five modules (a) Basic Life Support, (b) Common Medical Emergencies, (c) Primary Trauma Care, (d) Mass Casualty Management, and (e) Common Orthopedic Emergencies [23]. After the package was drafted, the pilot batch training was conducted, then finalized with trainers’& participants’ feedback in 2019 and has been conducted in different batches at various training sites under NHTC accreditation since then. Between 2019 and January 2023, 295 healthcare workers received PEC training.

The competency is gained through practicing in models with clinical scenarios. The training is delivered through classroom teaching, small group discussion, hands-on skill practice and simulation with real case-based scenarios by the trainers/doctors certified by NHTC. The overall assessment is done by the group of trainers within the training. There is an initial and final knowledge assessment with multiple choice questionnaires at the beginning and end of training for all modules, whereas skill assessment is done through checklists of each module. In order to be certified, they should attain at least 85% in the assessments.

During this study, the trainers/doctors were consulted for the onsite assessment of the knowledge, skill retention and behavior of the PEC graduates along with the field research team and emergency department head of the selected site. A special skill station similar to OSCE was set within the emergency room of the selected site where the knowledge, skill and behavior of the PEC graduates were assessed using standard checklists during their performance followed by a semi-structured interview.

This study was a first-time evaluation of the PEC training program to assess training effectiveness and skill utilization within PEC trainees’ actual working environment in Nepal. Available literature on the evaluation of emergency medicine training programs lacks a comprehensive evaluation beyond the learner, with little known regarding the work environment, skill retention, or utilization; a gap this study aims to mitigate [24–26]. Findings can inform policymakers, healthcare providers, and stakeholders during making decisions regarding the implementation and improvement of PEC training programs in Nepal and similar resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and conceptual framework

This study utilizes a pre- and post-interventional descriptive, mixed-methods design with quantitative and qualitative analysis using the 4-level Kirkpatrick Model (KM), fulfilling criteria set by Rybarczyk et al. to evaluate emergency medicine training programs in LMICs [24, 27]. The KM evaluates outcomes of the program at four levels. Level One evaluates trainees’ satisfaction with the trainers and training program. This study utilizes initial training data from graduates to assess satisfaction and course feedback. Level Two evaluates trainees’ knowledge and skills gained from the program. This study utilizes pre/post test data from PEC graduates to assess knowledge gained during the course. Levels Three and Four address changes in trainees’ behavior or performance and long-term sustainability of the program and its effects, respectively. Survey data at the time of the study were used to assess skill utilization in the field, and in-person skill assessments of PEC graduates at purposively selected hospital sites were completed. Hospital sites were also evaluated for an enabling environment. Data was stratified by cadre, years of experience, and hospital type for further analysis. See Fig. 1A.

Study site and sampling

A total of 295 participants belonging to three healthcare cadres (nursing, paramedics and medical doctors - based on pre-training qualifications) were trained in 15 batches of PEC training as of January 2023. For KM Level One, the training evaluation forms of all 295 participants were utilized. For KM Level Two, retrospective pre/post test questionnaire scores were available and matched for 271 PECs (91.9%). For KM Levels Three and Four, 11 study sites were purposively selected based on geographical representation, current PEC employment status, and the number of PEC graduates working for six months or longer at the site after the PEC training. Selection for the 11 study sites and 48 graduates (16.3%) were based on the survey results obtained from 255 PEC graduates. See Fig. 1B.

For qualitative data to assess Levels Three and Four, semi-structured interviews with one PEC graduate, one ED in-charge/medical superintendent, and one focus group discussion (FGD) with the Emergency Department (ED) team from each sampled hospital was conducted. To create a balance between cadres, one from each profession from each sampled hospital were purposively selected. In hospitals where there was more than one trained member from the same profession, the provider with the longest duration of experience in service delivery after receiving the training was interviewed.

Data collection

The data collection involved quantitative and qualitative techniques through standard tools and guidelines where retrospective data review, online survey through mailed questionnaire, onsite assessment and interviews conducted with PEC graduates while interviews, discussion and site observation were conducted with the department incharges, MeSu and non-PEC staff for enabling environment. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants before conducting the interview, discussion and tape recording the interaction. See Table 1.

For the qualitative data collection, the topic guides and FGD guides were developed in reference to the PEC training manual and consultation with PEC training resource persons. The tools were pretested at APF hospital, Kathmandu then required revision and uniformity on the question pattern was maintained. Major components of the topic guides for trained personnel included: the experience and perception of PEC graduates regarding the utilization of knowledge and skills gained during the training, the relevance of the training content, the facilitating and hindering factors in skill utilization. In addition to this, the contents in the topic guides and questionnaire of KII and FGD with Non-PEC trained personnel included; observed changes to the ED as a result of the newly acquired knowledge and skills by the PEC-trained ones.

Similarly, we have tried to maintain the participant diversity during data collection within the PEC trained ones (i.e. selected from all categories Medical, Paramedics and Nurses) for KII as well as the non-PEC trained ones (Medical, Paramedics and Nurses) for FGD. Additionally, KII were conducted with ER incharges and Medical Superintendent of selected hospitals for taking their perspectives on usefulness and effectiveness of PEC training. The saturation was decided when no new responses on the discussion were noted during the interviews or the focused group discussion from the participants. See Table 1.

Reflexivity and credibility in the study

The authors demonstrated reflexivity by acknowledging their professional backgrounds and discussing how this may have influenced data interpretation. The assessment of PEC graduates was done by consultants who were the Primary Emergency Care trainers and professional practitioners on the emergency service for a long term. The graduate’s assessment was conducted setting a skill station similar to OSCE with in their work site in the presence of the Emergency Department head of selected sites. Thus, the consultants ensured a pressure free environment for conducting the assessment within the hospital. This also provided the space for peer debrief and discussion within the survey team. Similarly, regular team discussions were conducted to reflect on and refine emerging themes. Additionally, during developing the study report and manuscript, authors have thoroughly reviewed the field notes and reports from the consultants.

Outcome measures

KM level 1: Reaction

A training evaluation form including subjective and objective questions covering classroom teaching, practical skill exposure, and administrative aspects of the training was obtained from the participants and used to determine trainee satisfaction with the training. Additionally, the training completion reports from the trainers were reviewed to assess the trainee’s reaction. Feedback forms of all participants were reviewed, and feedback was classified into “strengths” and “weaknesses”. Responses were coded and tabulated as seen in Supplemental Table S1 (Supplementary file 4). By synthesizing the training evaluation forms and completion reports, the reaction towards the training emerged into themes including relevance, adequacy of the facilitator’s presentation, skill level, and pace of the program.

KM level 2: Learning

A 20-question evaluation form was administered before and after the completion of the training to determine the change in knowledge attributable to the PEC course. The topics covered are recognition and management of commonly seen emergency conditions, compartment syndrome, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and triage. Paired t-tests were used to assess change before and after the training and disaggregated by cadre.

KM level 3: Behavior and practice skills

Knowledge retention was assessed using the same 20-question as evaluation form as in Level 2, while skill retention was assessed through Objective Structured Clinical Exam (OSCE) stations and structured simulation scenarios at purposively selected sites. Scoring for skill retention was done using a structured checklist to assess completion of proper steps in each demonstrable skill. Participants received a score belonging to either “satisfactory = 3”, “partially satisfactory = 2”, or “unsatisfactory = 1” categories on each evaluated skill set.

KM level 4: Results

Enabling environments of the hospital, as envisioned by the PEC training package, was physically observed and noted. The standard tool used to evaluate the enabling environment of the sampled hospitals was developed based on the PEC training guide and via consultation with the PEC training resource persons. Key Informant interviews (KIIs) and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) with PEC trained, non-PEC trained, and ED in-charges, were done to qualitatively evaluate change in trainee behavior and obstacles faced in attempting to apply training skills in the field.

Analysis

Work status of PEC graduates, knowledge, skills, and environment assessment were obtained using KOBO online survey questionnaire. The data was downloaded in Excel and exported to SPSS software. Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, and mean) were used for demographic, work status, knowledge, and skills of the PEC graduates, and hospital environment assessments. The training evaluation reports were reviewed for participant perception and feedback regarding the training. A paired sample t-test was applied to compare knowledge scores before and after the training, and for the scores obtained by the 48 participants during the follow-up period. One-way ANOVA was done to test for any differences in knowledge and skills between different cadres.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted to understand the experience and perception of PEC graduates regarding the utilization of knowledge and skills gained during the training, the relevance of the training content, and to determine the factors facilitating and hindering skill utilization. Non-PEC trained personnel such as the Medical Superintendent or Head of the ED were interviewed and a separate FGD with other non-PEC medical staff was conducted to evaluate observed changes to the ED as a result of the newly acquired knowledge and skills acquired by the PEC-trained personnel. These recorded interviews and FGDs were transcribed and translated into English. Respondents were assigned unique identification numbers to anonymize transcripts. The study team reviewed interview transcripts, and a codebook was prepared. All the transcripts were coded in Excel separately for KIIs and FGDs. A descriptive report of the analysis was prepared. The verbatims were re-read, reviewed, and a thematic analysis was done for both the KII and FGDs.

Results

Participant characteristics

As of January 2023, 295 PEC graduates had completed the training and 255 (86.4%) responded to the survey at the time of the study. Of respondents, 56.1% were < 30 years of age, 54.1% were male, and belonged to either medical (34.5%), nursing (32.5%) or paramedic (32.9%) cadres. Of the respondents, 69.4% worked in public hospitals and 25.5% of them had more than 2 years of working experience. Approximately 7.8% of the respondents were not working during the survey period, and 3.8% of the working population were not handling emergency cases at the time of the study.

Most participants from the 48 sampled graduates were male (52%), and a majority (52%) fell within the age group of 20–30 years. Cadres were well represented in the sub-sample, with PEC-trained nurses slightly over-represented compared to the entire PEC graduate population. Further, due to the nature of purposive sampling, all the selected 48 PEC-graduates were currently working and utilizing their training, compared to 96.2% and 72.1% of the survey respondents, respectively. The participants represented a total of ten districts from all seven provinces. See Table 2.

Level 1: Reaction

Training evaluation forms for all 15 batches (n = 295) were used to evaluate program relevance, adequacy of the facilitator’s presentation, skill level and pace of the program. The training program was well accepted and thought to be highly relevant by participants. Some of the paramedical staff reported it was difficult to understand certain technical terms, whereas others believed the content was tailored more towards the paramedical staff out of the three groups of trainees. Almost all participants thought the duration of the course should be longer than the allotted six days, with additional time given to the delivery of practical sessions.

The participant feedback compiled from the evaluation forms included suggestions such as providing training material translated into Nepali, the addition of obstetric emergency-related material, and longer training times. They believed the training would have been more effective if the practical sessions on Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability and Exposure (ABCDE) and triage management, among others, could be performed in a real hospital setting. See Supplemental Table S1 (Supplementary file 4) for themes under which, feedback forms have been evaluated.

Level 2: Learning

Among graduates, the pre/post test scores of 271 participants (91.9%) were available for analysis. The mean pretest score was 52.7% ± 19.1% reflecting the wide range in pre-course knowledge. The mean post-test score was 88.6% ± 6.4%, showing a significant 35.8% ± 17.3% (p = 0.001) increase in knowledge as a direct result from the training. The retention of knowledge from post test and at the followup during study remained relatively high but decayed significantly over time. See Table 3; Fig. 2.

Following the training, knowledge scores differed significantly between cadres (p < 0.001), with the medical cadre again scoring highest (91.7% ± 5.5%), followed by nursing (87.9% ± 5.2%) and paramedics (85.8% ± 6.5%). Level 4: Result

Enabling environment

Assessing the enabling environment utilized the survey data of the 255 PEC graduates. Of the survey respondents, a total of 184 (72%) reported utilizing their training and working in the ED. Among the 71 not utilizing their training, 20 (7.8%) reported not working in a clinical setting, with “studying” being frequently cited. Another nine PEC-graduates (3.8%) were not posted in the ED or utilizing their training and 42 (16.5%) mentioned they were not using the training knowledge and skills in their practice. While most participants did not complete all the required fields of the survey, reasons such as “posted in a different department” and “no opportunity” were observed upon evaluating the survey forms of some participants who did not utilize training skills.

Purposively selected health facilities (n = 11) were observed for the availability of physical facilities, equipment, and medicines required to provide emergency services. Most facilities (81.6%) exhibited availability of essential equipment such as ECG machines, AEDs or defibrillators, and oxygen delivery devices. Furthermore, facilities generally had well-defined disaster preparedness plans, resuscitation areas, and triage systems in place, reinforcing their readiness to handle critical situations. However, the availability of certain basic pharmaceuticals, oral medications like aspirin and atorvastatin, and certain medical supplies like Nasopharyngeal Airway (NPA) and Laryngeal Mask Airways (LMAs) were challenges. Perceived improvement in service provision

All study sites reported availability of comprehensive patient management and basic emergency care services while the referral and private hospitals could provide specialized services like cardiac surgery, neurosurgery, uro-surgery, neuropsychiatry for disaster affected cases directly from ER. Participants reported facing short supply of HR, medicines, equipment and space during mass casualty incidents. The PEC graduates reported challenge to work in ER due to a lack of practical skills, but the training proved to be instrumental in enhancing proficiency on prioritizing cases by severity, providing need based care through ABCDE, Basic life support, Chest tube insertion, intubation, and mass casualty management, and performing CPR after the training. Study participants perceived improvement in the service readiness and provision as the trained ones could provide lifesaving services during medical emergencies, snake bite, disaster, and orthopedic emergencies with the skills learnt.

Effectiveness and utilization of PEC training

Graduates reported that the PEC training helped to enhance their knowledge, skills, and confidence in emergency case management ultimately improving patient care. A participant from a referral hospital credited the PEC training as enhancing his effectiveness as a leader and improved decision-making in the ED. Other respondents recommended that the PEC training be provided to all ED staff regardless of their educational background as they perceive it to improve effectiveness of the service delivery from the ER as a coordinated team.

PEC training brings different skills to the emergency department, ultimately leading to better patient care. -PEC Graduate

The staffing improvement in the hospital and training to the juniors or all staff of ER department with PEC then it will help to improve the quality-of-service delivery. - PEC Graduate

The responses from KII and FGD participants had positive alignment that PEC trained ones had higher confidence and better case management in emergencies to cases compared to their own behavior before attending the training.

One of the staff, Reetu (Name changed), after taking the training she takes lots of responsibility. like she does intubation, medications and many. She also teaches us at work. Yeah, I have seen that she has been more responsive than before. – FGD Referral Hospital

Mainly, PEC graduates are confident and can easily manage cases. I find non-attendees of PEC training less confident. KII Non-PEC

The trained ones have shown a change in their behavior. I observe that Dr. Sanju [PEC graduate- Name changed] is providing a lot of time to patients. Having responsibility to handle the ER department also provides Normal OT services. They are working here very efficiently. Patients get service and are satisfied with them. – FGD Province Hospital

Further, participants reported improved coordination among team members, which enhanced ED management. PEC graduates were described as added value to the ED team, enhancing the overall management and quality of emergency services, while other non-PEC trained staff also received an opportunity to learn life-saving skills from their peers while working as a team in ED.

After taking the training [a PEC graduate] takes lots of responsibility– like she does intubation, medications, and many other things. She also teaches us at work. - Non-PEC Healthcare Worker

The attendees have more knowledge on the specific topic and improved skills. And he/she corrects and makes suggestions to other staff during the treatment procedure. - Non-PEC Healthcare Worker

However, certain learned skills, such as ECG reading, remained underutilized due to the division of labor among the nurses and paramedics. A difference in skill utilization existed between participants from government and private facilities. Participants from private hospitals reported an increase in confidence but mentioned many skills remained unused due to the need to follow a pre-existing SOP.

Every attendee of the [PEC] training teaches us one CME after they come back from the training. They transfer skills to other staff for ED management… We have a [texting] group. If someone attends training, they also inform us about new things through that group. - Non-PEC Healthcare Worker

I don’t think there are many differences between before and after the training as we follow the SOP. As we are talking about the remote village then we might see some changes but in a hospital like ours we give the best care they can get. - Non-PEC Healthcare Worker, Private Hospital

Supportive and enabling environments

Challenges faced by participants in implementing skills at their workplace centered around a shortage of clinical and administrative staff, lack of equipment and medicine, and inadequate infrastructure.

Due to insufficient human resources, we could not regularly check the stock level of emergency drugs. Unmanaged handover of drugs must be the other factor for emergency drugs unavailability besides less human manpower. Emergency drugs need to be in stock but sometimes we lack them. This is the problem for us. - ED In charge

There are only two staff members at once for a huge number of patients. Human manpower is an important factor but is limited in this hospital. We could have strengthened ED services as per the requirement of PEC training if adequate staff were available. - KII Province Hospital.

A majority of the participants reported satisfaction at work despite lower wages and numerous challenges. They appreciated support from supervisors, fellow doctors, paramedics, and nursing staff. The heads of the ED were satisfied with the skills and work of the PEC graduates.

Until now I am satisfied. The senior doctors teach and guide us if anything or any procedures are unknown to me. I find this very good in this hospital. -PEC Graduate

There is good coordination among staff. It’s like a family in the emergency department. The working environment is very good if everyone works with their duties. There might be a few who are a bit irresponsible. Overall, the emergency team is good. - FGD Province Hospital

Areas needing improvement

Participants recommended refresher training for graduates, extending the duration of training to 15 days and developing an advanced training module for participants working in referral and provincial hospitals.

It would be better if it was composed of all packages from basic to advanced and running for 15 days. Addition of advanced packages like ventilator training, placing of an intubated patient on type of mode; that is equally helpful in ICU and skills of minor operation would be helpful. - PEC Graduate.

Participants emphasized the need for government coordination and expansion of training facilities to accommodate more, if not all, staff working in EDs. A needs assessment to determine areas of improvement and ensure enabling working environments was recommended. Along with regular support, they highlighted the importance of hospital authorities in continuous monitoring, supervision, and evaluation of trained staff. The role of the government in addressing issues regarding staff shortages and lack of infrastructure was also highlighted.

Regular monitoring and assessment of trained personnel from hospitals is necessary; only giving training is not enough … For example, if someone has taken the Primary Emergency Care training but is posted to work simply doing an ECG then the objective of training is not fulfilled as well as his/her capacity is not utilized. - PEC Graduate

Convergent findings

The findings at all four Kirkpatrick levels provide consistent evidence that PEC training was relevant, effective, and applicable in capacity building at both individual and institutional levels contributing to strengthened emergency service delivery.

Level 1: Reaction

The training participants were largely positive and motivated for the training, perceived it to be highly relevant, enhancing their knowledge and skills in emergency care with shared recommendations to increase time for practical sessions.

Level 2: Learning

Both qualitative and quantitative findings demonstrated significant improvements in knowledge and skills that were largely sustained over time, though participants emphasized the need for regular supervision and refresher training. Convergent evidence indicated the training was particularly beneficial for nursing and paramedic cadres while it enhanced confidence and team-based emergency care for all cadres.

Level 3: Behavior

Quantitative assessment of critical skill performance outcomes aligned with qualitative reports of improved confidence, leadership, decision-making, quality care and teamwork. Though the real world change in behaviour was not measured, participants demonstrated retention and application of key emergency skills during their assessment in the OSCE during the survey at hospitals while the qualitative discussions reported contribution to strengthened emergency management and response capacity within the department after the training.

Level 4: Results

Quantitative findings on follow-up of training utilization and facility readiness aligned with qualitative reports of perceived workplace satisfaction, institutional support and availability of essential equipment. It suggests a positive contribution of PEC training to both individual performance as well as system preparedness for emergency service delivery.

In overall, the quantitative improvements in knowledge, skill performance and service provision shows the convergence with qualitative reports of increased confidence, teamwork and application at the hospital.

Discussion

Hospitals in Nepal, especially in rural areas, struggle to provide quality emergency care services to meet the growing needs of the population, with shortages in human resources and lack of formal training contributing to the widening gap [28]. With only one doctor per 150,000 patients in rural areas, much of Nepal’s emergency care is provided by paramedical and nursing staff [28, 29]. Equipping service providers is essential to ensure timely, effective, and updated emergency care. In response to these changes and to requests from healthcare workers in the field, the Primary Emergency Care (PEC) training was developed to equip different cadres of healthcare workers to provide effective emergency care [21]. The training also aims to fulfill calls made by WHO member states in the 72nd World Health Assembly (WHA) to provide dedicated training in the management of emergency conditions to all relevant service providers [30]. The PEC training is a six-day, off-site, in-person program consisting of five modules addressing management of common emergency room conditions.

Using the Kirkpatrick model, this study evaluated the effectiveness of the PEC training on knowledge, skills, utilization in the field, and the environmental factors enabling the use of the training. We found that participants positively received the training, significantly improving knowledge scores from 52.7% ± 19.1% to 88.6% ± 6.4% by the end of the course (p < 0.001), with less variation between cadres after the training (p < 0.001). Evaluation of pre-test scores showed a variation in baseline level knowledge between healthcare cadres (p < 0.001), This, coupled with the training feedback, suggests the adoption of simpler language and a need to translate materials into Nepali. As medical doctors have shown the highest-baseline knowledge between cadres, and consistent performance during the follow-up, utilizing peer-based teaching in the classroom and learning activities could be considered in improving training delivery [31].

The participants experienced a significant decrease in knowledge (7.2% ± 12.9%) at follow up (p = 0.001), with paramedics scoring significantly lower than other cadres (p = 0.005). Knowledge attrition is a common challenge that could be attributed to lack of an enabling environment, infrequent use of skills, and lack of refresher training. A systematic review on adult advanced life support reported significant decay in knowledge and skills at 6 months to a year, with skills decay occurring at a faster rate compared to knowledge decay [32]. Assessment of skills at follow up including BLS, Basic Airway Management, LPA slab application, and case-based management of dyspnea showed satisfactory performance in most areas, while ventilation rate (70.8%), and performing jaw thrust (66.7%) showed either partially or unsatisfactory performance. Similar research in LMICs shows paramedics trained in pediatric airway management scored only 42% on endotracheal intubation follow-up assessments, with technique deterioration linked to time since training [33]. The absence of equipment and structured simulation practice in LMIC settings supplements these challenges [34]. Our findings highlight the importance of refresher training, and an enabling environment at work as possible strategies to mitigate knowledge and skill attrition.

Out of the 255 survey respondents, 226 (88.6%) were posted in the ED and 184 (72.1%) reported active utilization of training skills in the workplace, showing nearly 30% of participants lacked an opportunity to apply their knowledge and skills. This not only poses an obstacle in properly evaluating the training effectiveness but also highlights a systemic issue. Lack of access to emergency care is a multi-pronged issue that cannot be addressed in silos. Proper workforce governance plays a key role in ensuring deployment of trained professionals to the appropriate departments and allowing for proper utilization of training programs [35].

System-level barriers to skill application were assessed through evaluation of enabling environments and qualitative interviews. Most facilities (81.6%) had the required basic equipment and physical infrastructure, but lacked basic medication such as aspirin, and medical equipment like NPA and LMA mirroring a previous national assessment of Basic Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care in 2021, which found medicine and equipment availability to be 64.5% and 76.8% respectively, with only 2.5% of facilities possessing all essential emergency signal functions [36]. Participants expressed a consistent lack of support in the work from the administration including inadequate human resources, equipment unavailability and obstacles in maintaining privacy during delivery of care resulting in limited utilization of skills gained. One of them expressed, “We could have strengthened ED services as per the requirement of PEC training if adequate staff were available”. Appropriate referral and patient transport are another crucial part of emergency management, but proper referral mechanisms are not being followed by many hospitals. Studies report the need for a systematic referral process where hospitals should be informed about the referral, potentially preventing crucial delays [37]. Maintaining proper records is also equally important after the referral has been made [37, 38]. Thus, any training to address emergency care must be done in conjunction with interventions to address an enabling environment and functional referral systems.

Participants suggested conducting practical sessions such as the ABCDE method and triage in real-time hospital-based settings to assist in addressing organizational, equipment, and systemic challenges encountered in the hospital. In line with our study, Forde et al. reported that emergency care training with real-life, onsite simulation-based training led to sustained and increased confidence among the GPs and primary care clinicians in the management of emergencies, including anaphylaxis, hypoglycemia, convulsions, choking, asthma, croup, chest pain, and cardiac arrest [39].

Although this study was well representative of the PEC trainees, there are several limitations. This study analysed the four level KM model based on the evaluated training program reflections, knowledge and skills gained, behavior changed and the results obtained after the service by the trained participants being posted in the hospitals. Regarding the model application, Level 3 requires demonstrating behavior change in routine clinical practice overtime, assessed through workplace based observation, peer assessments, clinical performance indicators while the Level 4 refers to system or patient service outcomes attributable to training (e.g., care processes, timeliness, referral rates, morbidity/mortality proxies) were not directly measured. The reasons for their measurement through indirect assessment methods included: For Level-3 health workers are deployed in emergency departments and are expected to apply their skills in real-time patient care; it was not feasible to directly observe and evaluate their performance in real-time settings. Particularly in resource-limited settings like Nepal, where there is already shortage of health workers, assigning an additional medical professional solely for observation and evaluation is impractical. Therefore, we used knowledge assessment and simulation-based methods and made efforts to minimize bias by conducting qualitative interviews with trainees’, coworkers and supervisors about the change in trainees’ behavior while the service remained uninterrupted even during the simulation-based assessments of the graduates (i.e. study participants) during the survey. Such measures of assessing Level 3 outcomes by surveying trainees and using their responses as a measure of behavior performance had already been referred to by a few remarkable training evaluation studies that are worth noting [40, 41].

While the analysis of Level 4 was focused on the enabling environment and perceived changes in patient care outcomes rather on the ideal direct measures of the service utilization and patient outcomes. We acknowledge this limitation in analysis of this level though we had considered during the study design phase to include the patient treatment outcomes for analysis of level 4. During the survey, reviewing the service utilization data from hospitals relevant to the PEC training from initial few sites, we observed notable discrepancies between facility service registers and HMIS service reports. The data were grossly incomplete and inconsistent, especially those referencing the patient care outcomes including the referral ins and outs. Due to these data quality issues, we decided not to include this component in the study while opting for the enabling environment and perceived changes in the care outcomes as the result level analysis. Thus, future studies could systematically study levels 3 and 4 in detail.

Although only 11 of the facilities were selected for study, efforts were made to assess a diverse range of health facilities including all provinces, geographies and type of cadres, limiting the follow-up assessment to 48 participants, representing 16.3% of PEC graduates. Further, 100% of selected participants for this study were actively utilizing their training in the ED so skill retention may be overestimated compared to the wider PEC graduate population, where nearly 30% reported not actively utilizing their skills. Additionally, qualitative interviews focused on the healthcare workers, and the study did not explore patient perceptions. While qualitative findings highlighted a transfer and knowledge of skills from trained to non-PEC trained healthcare workers, the recipients of this transferred knowledge were not assessed. Informal training is a frequent way that healthcare workers in low resource environments develop skills, and should be further explored [42].

Conclusions

The study findings suggest that the PEC training significantly improves the knowledge and skills of emergency care teams including medical doctors, nursing and paramedics in emergency service delivery. Participants reported increased confidence in service delivery and strengthened teamwork within the emergency department through horizontal knowledge transfer to other healthcare staff following training. Though the study had limitations in measuring the patient level outcomes, which could have better reflections of training results. The observations of an enabling environment created within the emergency department as well as improved motivation and confidence in managing emergency cases and mass casualties by graduates after the training have broadly reflected the results to which most of the non-PEC and the department heads have reported to have contributed to a more effective service delivery. However, study findings also highlighted the need for regular supervision, evaluation, and refresher training to improve the service quality.

Additionally, strengthening the service recording and reporting systems would enable accurate measurement of patient outcomes and better assess the results of the training program. Recommendations to improve the course include provision of translated training material, extending course duration, and cadre-specific training sessions. The study highlights the importance of an enabling environment at the workplace to ensure effective application of the skills gained. Further research is warranted to assess the service quality, explore patients’ perspectives on service delivery improvements, and evaluate the effectiveness of referral services.
