Determinants of smoking-cessation intentions among female university students in Thailand
Authors: Suparinya Sumpuntharat, Arpaporn Powwattana, Uthumporn Panitanarak, Oi Saeng Hong, Surintorn Kalampakorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmoking, including e-cigarette use, remains a significant global public health issue, with a concerningly high prevalence among female university students in Thailand. Given the unique physiological and psychosocial barriers that female face in cessation, this study aimed to identify determinants of the intention to quit smoking among female university students in Thailand.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in the Bangkok vicinity. Using snowball sampling, 423 female university students aged 18–25 years who were current users of tobacco products were recruited. Data were collected via an online questionnaire and analyzed using logistic regression.ResultsA majority of participants used e-cigarettes (67.8%), and 52.5% reported a high intention to quit. Factors significantly associated with intention to quit included decisional balance (Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) = 3.556; 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 1.852–6.828) and self-efficacy (AOR = 3.371; 95% CI: 1.639–6.932). Other positive correlates were type of smoking (AOR = 3.102; 95% CI: 1.350–7.129), vigorous physical activity (AOR = 2.692; 95% CI: 1.250–5.794), norms and support from peers (AOR = 2.606; 95% CI: 1.124–6.040), and previous quit attempts (AOR = 2.550; 95% CI: 1.194–5.449). Conversely, nicotine dependence (AOR = 0.058; 95% CI: 0.027–0.122), frequency of smoking (AOR = 0.394; 95% CI: 0.202–0.767), and BMI (AOR = 0.500; 95% CI: 0.252–0.992) were inversely associated with intention to quit.ConclusionInterventions must focus on enhancing decisional balance, self-efficacy and providing targeted support to overcome their unique barriers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27637-1.

Background

Smoking remains a critical global health crisis, accounting for over 8 million deaths annually, with approximately 1.51 million occurring among females [1]. Notably, a systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted that the global prevalence of ever smoking and current smoking among females stands at 28% and 17%, respectively [2]. According to a recent survey of Thai youth (15–24 years) in institutions across each region, the prevalence of cigarette use was 4.3%, e-cigarette use was 3.5%, and dual use was 2.4%. Furthermore, e-cigarettes were more popular than conventional cigarettes among female youth, with the highest prevalence found among students in higher education (4.6%) [3]. This upward trend is further evidenced by a 2023 study in central Thailand, where 20.4% of university students had ever used e-cigarettes, and 16.8% of these users were female [4].

The rising prevalence of e-cigarettes among university students, particularly females, is associated with appealing product features such as trendy designs, diverse flavors, pleasant aromas, and ease of use. Additionally, usage correlates with social influences including peer and family smoking behaviors and advertising exposure as well as the miss perception of e-cigarettes as tools for smoking cessation, weight control, and stress management, as well as symbolize gender equality [5–9]. However, these positive perceptions mask the critical reality that, like all forms of smoking, e-cigarettes pose severe health risks for female, including skin, respiratory, cardiovascular, and reproductive issues [10–12].

Female smokers face unique barriers when attempting to quit smoking. These include psychological factors such as emotion and stress [13], as well as biological influences, including hormone fluctuations and faster nicotine metabolism, which can increase withdrawal symptoms and reduce the effectiveness of nicotine replacement therapies (NRTs). Concerns about weight gain and persistent cravings pose significant challenges [14].

Additionally, socioeconomic factors, including cultural stigma, further complicate cessation efforts [15]. Collectively, these gender-specific factors help explain why a standardized, general approach to smoking cessation is often less effective for female.

The intention to quit is a vital first step in cessation [16]. Existing studies among female smokers aged 18 and older have identified multiple factors associated with the intention to quit [17, 18]. Individual and demographic factors, such as higher education and physical activity, correlate positively with these intentions [17]. For instance, while higher education is a significant factor among female call center employees and clinical outpatients [18], its association is less definitive among university students, where academic specialization (such as medical vs. non-medical fields) remains a key source of variation [19]. Psychological factors, such as self-efficacy to quit smoking [18, 20], decisional balance of smoking [21, 22], and the perceived risk of cigarette cravings [18] are significantly associated with the intention to quit. Furthermore, the perceived risk of craving and unmanageable negative affect are critical psychological barriers linked to a lower likelihood of intending to quit [18]. Smoking-related behaviors, including daily frequency, nicotine dependence levels, and the time to the first cigarette, are significantly associated with the intention to quit [18, 19, 23]. Existing studies identify distinct female patterns, such as light and irregular smoking, which serve primarily as coping mechanisms for stress and negative affect [22]. Previous quit attempts remain a robust factor associated with the intention to quit across diverse groups of females, from service employees to clinical outpatients [18, 24]. However, a critical pattern emerges, while high nicotine dependency is associated with more intense cravings, intention remains low unless paired with high abstinence self-efficacy to manage habitual triggers [18]. Furthermore, the irregular or social nature of these smoking patterns may ironically act as a barrier to cessation, as individuals often underestimate their level of addiction [22].

In addition, social, environmental, and policy linked to intention to quit include awareness of anti-smoking messages and cessation policies; conversely, exposure to smoking advertisements is negatively associated with these intentions [24, 25]. Notably, norms and support from peers [18, 26, 27] appear to be more significantly associated with intention to quit among females than males. For example, always-present peer support is associated with over four times the odds of smoking abstinence in females, whereas it shows no significant effect in males [28].

Nevertheless, findings across different studies remain unclear as to which factors strongly determine the intention to quit in the specific context of female university students. Previous studies have focused on general populations, leaving it uncertain whether these same patterns apply to the unique social and behavioral environment of young female students in higher education. Therefore, identifying these key factors associated with intention to quit is essential for developing targeted interventions that effectively support female university students.

Bangkok and the vicinity are experiencing rapid social changes driven by economic growth, and home to large higher education institutions, resulting in a large youth population. A significant public health concern in this region is the high prevalence of substance use among young people, including traditional cigarettes, and e-cigarettes [29]. Notably, the vicinity reports one of the highest national rates of e-cigarette use, with a 2023 survey indicating that 13.2% of youth use e-cigarettes [30]. Furthermore, a 2025 survey indicated that 51.4% of youth who use e-cigarettes are female [31]. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the intention to quit smoking of female university students and its determinants. Gaining a deeper understanding is essential for developing effective, targeted interventions and support strategies tailored to this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design

The study employed a cross-sectional design. The study adhered to the STROBE checklist guidelines.

Study participants and inclusion criteria

Participants were female university students who were current smokers. Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18–25 years old; (2) having used any tobacco product, including e-cigarettes, at least once in the past 30 days; (3) willing to participate in the study; and (4) having access to an internet-enabled device.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was determined using the Cochran’s Formula [32] n=Z2pq/e2, to ensure sufficient statistical power. Based on an estimated proportion (p) of 0.50 [33], a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), q = 1-p = 0.5, and a 5% margin of error (e = 0.05), the calculation resulted in a base requirement of 384 participants. To account for a 10% potential dropout rate in the online survey, the final target was set at 423 participants.

Recruitment

The study recruited participants using a snowball sampling technique, starting at two of the eight universities in Pathum Thani Province. These sites were selected for their large student populations, providing access to a diverse group of female students who smoke. The researchers initially contacted eligible female students at these universities and provided them with access to the survey via a QR code.

Potential participants who scanned the QR code were required to complete screening questions to verify their eligibility (specifically: female student, aged 18–25, and current smoker). After providing digital informed consent, only those who confirmed their eligibility were granted access to the main questionnaire. If these criteria were not confirmed, the individual could not proceed. Once the survey was completed, participants were encouraged to forward the QR code to eligible peers within their own networks. These referred individuals also underwent the same mandatory screening process, allowing the recruitment to expand through referrals until the final sample of 423 was reached.

Data collection procedure

Prior to the data collection, the researcher explained the purpose and objectives of the study. Informed consent forms were distributed in a digital format, ensuring that participants understood their rights and that confidentiality would be maintained. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire developed specifically for this study and not previously published. The full English version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1. The questionnaire was accessed via a link or QR code. The average time taken to complete the questionnaire was approximately 15 min.

The data collection was conducted over a two-month period, from August 1 to September 30, 2025. Following the collection phase, we performed a thorough data screening process; consistency checks and response pattern analysis were utilized to ensure that only valid and reliable data were included in the final analysis.

Study variables and instruments

A Thai online questionnaire developed for this study was used to measure the following variables.

The dependent variable: intention to quit smoking was assessed using a single-item Visual Analogue Scale (VAS): How strong is your intention to quit smoking in the next month? ranging from 0 (no intention) to 10 (strongest intention).

Independent variables: A total of 13 independent variables were used for this study.

Individual factors included age and body mass index (BMI), both collected through fill-in-the-blank responses, alongside academic status (categorized by year of study), field of study, and physical activity, which were assessed via checklists. Physical activity was specifically defined as engaging in at least 30 min of vigorous activity (e.g., jogging/running or lap swimming) on three or more days per week.

Smoking-related factors included type of smoking (traditional cigarettes, e-cigarettes, or dual use) and frequency (number of smoking days per week) were both measured through multiple-choice questions. Nicotine dependence was assessed using the Thai version of the 6-item of the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence FTND questionnaire [34] for traditional cigarette smokers and the Thai version of the 10-item Penn State e-cigarette dependence index questionnaire (PS-ECDI) [35] for e-cigarette users dual users completed both, with higher scores across both scales indicated greater dependence. Additionally, previous quit attempts were measured through a single fill-in-the-blank question, defined as any deliberate effort to stop smoking using any method within the past 30 days.

Psychological and social factors were evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale (1–5) with anchors tailored to each instrument. Researcher-developed 10-item questionnaires, based on literature reviews, were used to assess norms and support from peers regarding social pressure to quit (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Another 10-item scale measured awareness of cessation across various settings, including media, public places, shops, health warnings on cigarette packages, and exposure to advertising stimuli (1 = not at all true to 5 = very true). Regarding psychological constructs, researchers translated the decisional balance for smoking scale and the self-efficacy scale [36] into Thai. The smoking decisional balance scale (20 items) evaluates 10 pros and 10 cons statements (1 = not important at all to 5 = extremely important) by subtracting total cons from pros. Positive scores indicate a perception of more pros. In contrast, negative scores suggest more cons. The self-efficacy scale (20 items) assesses confidence in resisting smoking urges across various situations (1 = not confident at all to 5 = extremely confident), where higher scores reflect greater self-efficacy.

Validity and reliability

Instruments demonstrated content validity through a review by a three-expert panel (a smoking cessation physician, a nursing instructor, and a tobacco control academic). To ensure semantic and cultural equivalence, all scales underwent a multi-step translation and back-translation process, refined by a native English speaker and the expert panel. Construct validity was further established through Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) for the researcher-developed scales. This analysis confirmed that both norms and support from peers and awareness of cessation achieved acceptable factor loadings.

Internal consistency was strong, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.81 to 0.98. All four Likert-based instruments exceeded the 0.90 threshold. Consequently, Corrected Item-Total Correlations were analyzed to evaluate potential item redundancy. The resulting correlations ranged from 0.334 to 0.879 across all scales, confirming that each item contributed unique information without redundant overlap. These values justified the retention of all items to ensure full content coverage.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe study variables. Intention to quit smoking was dichotomized using the sample median (median = 7, IQR = 6) due to its non-normal distribution. Participants were categorized into low intention (< 7) and high intention (≥ 7) groups to ensure a balanced distribution for binary analysis. All of 13 independent variables were first screened using univariate logistic regression with a significance threshold of p < 0.25 [37] to avoid the premature exclusion of potentially important variables that might become significant when adjusted in a multivariable model.

For the primary analysis, factors associated with intention to quit smoking were identified using multiple logistic regression (enter method). Variables with a p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the model; age did not meet this criterion and was therefore excluded. Twelve independent variables were included in the model: academic status, field of study, BMI, vigorous physical activity, type of smoking, frequency of smoking, nicotine dependence, previous attempts, norms and support from peers, decisional balance, self-efficacy in quitting smoking, and awareness of cessation. Model assumptions and fit were rigorously assessed; multicollinearity was ruled out as all Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were within acceptable limits (< 5). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and Nagelkerke R2 were utilized for model evaluation. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 30.

Factors associated with intention to quit

The final multiple logistic regression model included 12 independent variables and was statistically significant (χ2 = 319.31, df = 12, p < 0.001). The model demonstrated a good fit, as indicated by the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ2 = 5.873, df = 8, p = 0.661), and multicollinearity was ruled out with all VIF values ranged from 1.032 to 2.083, well below the threshold of 5. Overall, the model explained 70.7% of the variance in the intention to quit smoking (Nagelkerke R2= 0.707) and achieved an overall classification accuracy of 86.3%. Several factors were significantly associated with the intention to quit. When controlling for other factors, it was found that nicotine dependence (AOR: 0.058, 95% CI: 0.027 − 0.122, p < 0.001), decisional balance of smoking(AOR: 3.556, 95% CI: 1.852 − 6.828, p < 0.001), self-efficacy in quitting smoking (AOR: 3.371, 95% CI: 1.639 − 6.932, p < 0.001), BMI (AOR: 0.500, 95% CI: 0.252 − 0.992, p = 0.047), vigorous physical activity (AOR: 2.692, 95% CI: 1.250 − 5.794, p = 0.011), type of smoking (AOR: 3.102, 95% CI: 1.350 − 7.129, p = 0.008), frequency of smoking (AOR: 0.394, 95% CI: 0.202 − 0.767, p = 0.006), previous quit attempts (AOR: 2.550, 95% CI: 1.194–5.449, p = 0.016), and norms and support from peers (AOR: 2.606, 95% CI: 1.124–6.040, p = 0.026) were associated with intention to quit. Conversely, field of study and awareness of cessation were found to be non-significant. Details are shown in Table 3.

Discussion

E-cigarette use among female university students

The present study demonstrated a high proportion of e-cigarette use among female university students, which is considerably higher than rates reported in previous studies of mixed-gender university students in Bangkok and vicinity, as well as major cities across three regions of Thailand [29]. This disparity reflects the socio-environmental influence of a megacity context, where the increased availability and accessibility of e-cigarettes are associated with a more permissive atmosphere for tobacco adoption among females. In contrast to more conservative regional settings, such as Chanthaburi [38]. These findings reflect contextual differences in accessibility and evolving social norms, both of which likely contribute to the reported variations in e-cigarette use.

Although studies on e-cigarette prevalence among female university students are limited, the findings of this study show a significant demographic shift, identifying female students as a high-risk group with an alarmingly high rate of e-cigarette use that warrants urgent public health intervention. The highest percentage of users was enrolled in Social Sciences and Humanities faculties, supporting previous evidence that students in non-health fields are more likely to use e-cigarettes [39]. This suggests a potential gap in specialized health literacy. Female students whose academic disciplines do not emphasize clinical health risks may perceive e-cigarettes as lifestyle-oriented products rather than harmful nicotine delivery systems. Additionally, the observation that final-year students are the most frequent users identifies this educational level as a critical transition period, likely characterized by heightened academic and career-related stress, which serves as a vital window for targeted intervention.

The behavioral profile observed in this female population defined by low nicotine dependence and infrequent usage aligns with social smoking patterns. These patterns suggest that usage is closely associated with situational and social triggers rather than severe physiological addiction. Furthermore, the prevalence of insufficient vigorous physical activity identifies a potential area for integrating lifestyle modifications within cessation strategies. The high prevalence of female students reporting a strong intention to quit is consistent with prior research indicating readiness for cessation within 30 days [40]. These findings suggest that female university students are highly receptive to cessation efforts. Specifically, interventions appear more appropriate when tailored to address social influences and situational triggers, rather than focusing exclusively on nicotine dependence.

Factors associated with intention to quit smoking among female university students

Decisional balance emerged as a significant associate of quitting intention. Participants who prioritized the negative consequences (cons) of smoking over its perceived benefits (pros) demonstrated a higher readiness to quit. This aligns with the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), which posits that increased awareness of negative outcomes enhances motivation for behavior change [41]. Given that female students tend to place greater emphasis on the disadvantages of smoking [22], smoking cessation programs targeting this demographic should underscore the negative health, social, and personal consequences of smoking to strengthen the intention to quit.

Self-efficacy demonstrated a significant association with the intention to quit smoking. Beyond its direct correlation with quitting intention [22, 41, 42], self-efficacy serves as a critical bridge for translating that intention into actual cessation. This aligns with evidence linking self-efficacy to daily abstinence intention [43], reflecting a student’s belief in their ability to overcome situational temptations and barriers. Therefore, smoking cessation programs tailored for female students should prioritize strategies that enhance self-efficacy to support the transition from intending to quit to successfully maintaining abstinence.

Regarding smoking type, e-cigarette and dual users demonstrated higher odds of intention to quit compared to traditional smokers. This pattern is associated with heightened perceived health risks among female users [44], where concerns often focus on acute respiratory anxieties (e.g., EVALI) and aesthetic issues (e.g., skin and oral health) rather than long-term disease prevention [45, 46]. These perceptions, coupled with Thailand’s restrictive policy environment and legal sanctions [47], appear to reinforce the motivation to quit observed within these groups.

Vigorous physical activity was also identified as a significant factor associated with the intention to quit. This finding is consistent with prior research among adolescents, which identified physical activity as an associate of intention to quit, particularly among females [17]. This relationship appears to be mediated by the subjective reward and enjoyment derived from exercise. When female students experience pleasure through physical activity, they may be less inclined to seek gratification through nicotine, suggesting that exercise functions as an effective alternative reinforcer [48]. Accordingly, cessation strategies that promote physical activity may be more impactful when they specifically target exercise enjoyment to support the intention to quit.

Norms and support from peers were also significantly associated with the intention to quit, aligning with the Theory of Planned Behavior [16]. This framework suggests that subjective norms, or the perceived social pressure to perform or refrain from a behavior, is a key associate of behavioral intention. Among female students, the decision to quit often operates within a framework of social acceptance, where peer support or disapproval acts as a significant external factor. This is supported by diverse international evidence: for instance, social support was identified as a key factor among female workers in South Korea [18] ; similarly, peer disapproval (injunctive norms) was found to be a vital associate of quitting intention in the United Kingdom [49]. Further evidence from Germany also reinforces this relationship, particularly among females who perceive strong social norms within their networks [50]. Collectively, these findings highlight that for females, peer-related social dynamics are essential in promoting the intention to quit.

Previous attempts, frequency of smoking, and nicotine dependence were also significant factors associated with the intention to quit. Previous quit attempts emerged as a positive associate, suggesting that even unsuccessful attempts may be linked to future motivation. This trajectory aligns with findings among female call center workers [18] and working females [25], which demonstrate that a history of quit attempts consistently correlates with higher quitting intentions. Furthermore, research among Korean American females suggests that past-year attempts, when considered alongside attitudes and norms, account for a substantial variance in the intention to quit [51].

In contrast, higher nicotine dependence and greater smoking frequency were inversely associated with the intention to quit. These findings suggest that female students with higher levels of addiction may have lower odds of intending to quit compared to those with lower dependence. This inverse relationship aligns with evidence from mainland China, where higher nicotine dependence was found to significantly decrease the likelihood of intention to quit [52]. Similarly, observations in South Korea indicate that students smoking more frequently, such as more than two days per week, were less likely to intend to quit than occasional smokers [53, 54]. These patterns may be associated with physiological and psychological barriers stemming from nicotine withdrawal symptoms, such as restlessness and irritability, potentially diminishing the intention to quit. Collectively, these findings highlight that frequent or daily smokers consistently demonstrate lower quitting intentions compared to occasional smokers, reflecting the increased intensity of nicotine use.

BMI was associated with the intention to quit, a finding that aligns with prior research suggesting that concerns over post-cessation weight gain association among females [55, 56]. These results suggest that for individuals ready to quit, physical factors such as BMI appear to be uniquely associated with the strength of their intention. However, as weight-related anxiety was not directly assessed in this study, further research is required to confirm this specific psychological mechanism.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that female students demonstrated a high intention to quit smoking. Along with decisional balance and self-efficacy, vigorous physical activity, type of smoking (e-cigarettes and dual use), norms and support from peers, and previous quit attempts were positively associated with the intention to quit. Conversely, nicotine dependence, smoking frequency, and self-reported BMI were inversely associated with the intention to quit. These findings suggest smoking cessation interventions for this group should prioritize enhancing internal motivators, such as decisional balance and self-efficacy, alongside targeted strategies to address physical and physiological barriers, including high nicotine dependence and BMI status.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, this study addresses a gap in tobacco control literature by specifically targeting female university students, whose smoking prevalence is rising but remains understudied compared to the general or male population. Second, we utilized a multi-dimensional framework by integrating selected factors across biological (e.g., body mass index, physical activity), psychological (e.g., self-efficacy, decisional balance), and social (e.g., norms and support from peers) domains. This approach provides an integrative perspective on the determinants of the intention to quit within this specific demographic. Third, the use of online self-administered questionnaires to ensure anonymity, encouraging respondents to provide more honest responses.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design collected data at a single point in time, which allows only for the identification of associations between variables rather than establishing causal relationships. Second, the use of snowball sampling, a non-probability method, may have resulted in a participant enriched with highly motivated smokers. However, this approach was essential for accessing this hard-to-reach group, as female smoking remains a sensitive social issue in Thailand and e-cigarette use is currently illegal. These factors often lead users to conceal their habits, making this targeted method necessary for gaining insights into this specific population. Furthermore, as data collection was centered in the Bangkok vicinity, the findings provide insights into behavioral patterns within this context. Applying these results to other regions or a broader population should account for the specific socio-cultural and environmental factors prevalent in different areas. Third, limitation in the instrument used for data collection, the reliance on self-reported data particularly weight and height are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases, which may result in an underestimation of BMI. To mitigate potential measurement bias from the use of some newly developed scales, we ensured that all items underwent pre-testing before implementation However, further validate the newly developed scales through rigorous testing, including cross-validation in different samplescould enhance precision in future research. Fourth, the use of a median split to dichotomize the dependent variable, while necessary due to its non-normal distribution, may have resulted in some loss of detailed information and potential classification bias. Finally, our analysis focused on selected biological, psychological, and social correlates. We acknowledge that other factors, such as socioeconomic status and mental health, were not included in the current model. Future research could investigate these additional variables to broaden the scope of factors associated with intention to quit in this population.

Recommendations

Smoking cessation programs should be developed to modify decisional balance by highlighting the benefits of quitting smoking, emphasizing activities that build self-efficacy in quitting smoking, and enhancing peer support and social norms. In addition, the programs should also integrate strategies for overcoming barriers that hinder smoking cessation. Furthermore, research should be expanded to universities in different regions to obtain data that is more generalizable.
