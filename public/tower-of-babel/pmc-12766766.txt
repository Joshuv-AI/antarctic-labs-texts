Willingness-to-pay for community-based health insurance among formal and informal doctors in Dhaka, Bangladesh: a comparative cross-sectional study
Authors: Md Fuad Al Fidah, Syeda Sumaiya Efa, Md Ziaul Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHealth insurance enables financial protection and reduces medical burdens. Currently, less than 1% of Bangladeshi citizens have health insurance coverage. We aimed to assess the willingness-to-pay for community-based health insurance and explore associated factors among formal and informal doctors in Bangladesh.MethodsThis comparative cross-sectional study was conducted on Bangladeshi doctors with formal and informal employment. For each group, 116 doctors were selected via purposive sampling, as no national register of doctors was available, indicating their current employment status and purposive selection ensured equal inclusion of formal and informal doctors. Separate binomial logistic regression models were fitted for each independent variable, adjusting for age and sex. The mean willingness-to-pay (95% CI) was measured using the double-bounded dichotomous choice approach of the contingent valuation method.ResultsThe median (IQR) age of the doctors was 34 (31.75–36.0) years, and 53.0% were male. Most (79.7%; 95% CI 74.0% to 84.7%) doctors exhibited willingness-to-pay for community-based health insurance with higher proportion among formal (85.3%) than informal (74.1%) doctors. The overall mean (95% CI) willingness-to-pay was 5.6% (5.1% to 6.2%) of monthly personal income and was higher among informal (5.8%; 5.0% to 6.6%) than formal (5.4%; 4.6% to 6.1%) doctors. Doctors seeking healthcare (adjusted OR (AOR): 0.28; 95% CI 0.08 to 0.99; p value=0.048) and having a middle career (AOR: 0.32; 95% CI 0.11 to 0.90; p value=0.031) were associated with lower odds of being willing-to-pay. Despite having lower mean willingness-to-pay, formal doctors showed higher odds (AOR: 2.02; 95% CI 1.01 to 4.07; p value=0.048) of accepting community-based health insurance.ConclusionsBoth formal and informal doctors showed high willingness-to-pay for community-based health insurance. Formal doctors were likely to contribute, while informal doctors expressed higher mean payments. Communication campaigns, tiered premiums and better healthcare quality could enhance participation. These findings offer insights for designing provider-supported community-based health insurance models that foster trust, include low-income groups and support universal health coverage in Bangladesh.Keywords: economics, Health Personnel, Public Health

Introduction

Individuals in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face greater barriers to healthcare access than wealthier nations. An efficiently planned healthcare system is crucial for protecting the public from illness-related financial distress. However, rapidly rising healthcare costs, due to factors like ageing population and growing chronic diseases, pose a significant challenge.1 Approximately 800 million individuals allocate over 10% of their household income exclusively for medical care. According to the WHO, catastrophic health expenditures (CHE) have forced 100 million people worldwide into acute poverty.2

Access to affordable healthcare remains a profound challenge in Bangladesh. Existing research shows that approximately 26% of households experience CHE during hospitalisation each year.3 Examination into Bangladesh’s healthcare financial landscape makes it evident that the burden of out-of-pocket (OOP) medical costs is considerable. Research from 2015 revealed that Bangladeshi households collectively spend approximately US$1.49 billion annually on OOP medical expenses, accounting for 64.3% of the nation’s total health expenditures.4

Bangladesh has a large informal sector (85.1% of total workforce) that encompasses economic activities occurring outside formal systems.5 Unfortunately, most of the informal sector workers have limited or no access to health insurance (HI), which increases their risk of financial hardships. On the other hand, only less than 1% of the country’s total population, that too primarily those who are working in the formal sector, have the privilege of social health protection.6 In other words, the majority of both the formally and informally employed workforce lacks healthcare coverage and social protection. While the country aims to achieve universal coverage in coming decades, HI currently reaches a small portion of the population. There are two main options—standalone policies or policies bundled with life plans. Around 90 000 total life insurance policies for over 160 million people suggest an extremely low HI penetration.7 A pivotal concept in insurance is the willingness-to-pay (WTP), representing the maximum amount one will spend on a good or service. Health economists argue that WTP effectively develops strong insurance schemes and is an effective approach for developing robust HI schemes.8

The current projections in Bangladesh indicate that the number of doctors will be 125 000 by 2026, exceeding healthcare needs.9 Even now, annual medical graduates far exceed available job openings. Many must take informal jobs with precarious incomes and irregular work, rendering them uniquely vulnerable to financial crises if healthcare is needed. Due to the additional health and financial risks associated with their profession, not to mention the occupational hazards, HI is crucial for doctors to manage emergencies, support families and fulfil patient care duties without financial concerns. Additionally, doctors play a key role in service delivery, and their acceptance and participation are important for establishing any successful HI programme. Theories like Supplier-Induced Demand indicate that patients’ choice is often significantly impacted by the physicians’ views.10 It has also been suggested that healthcare reform prospects can be influenced by the physician’s opinion.11 Given the current extent of insurance coverage in Bangladesh, it is imperative to explore the doctors’ WTP for community-based health insurance (CBHI) to promote its uptake and reform policies. Doctors’ participation in CBHI has implications beyond their own protection. As trusted providers, their acceptance of such schemes can shape patients’ perceptions and willingness to enrol, thereby influencing broader community adoption of HI.12

Although several studies have assessed WTP for HI in Bangladesh, most focused on households, informal workers or mixed employee groups, with none specifically investigating doctors.13,16 Doctors constitute a distinct group due to their medical knowledge, occupational exposure and influence on patients’ health-seeking behaviour. Evidence from LMICs shows wide variation in WTP, largely shaped by income, occupation and healthcare experience. In Ethiopia, only 17% of health workers were WTP for social HI, rising to 29% among formal providers.17 18 By contrast, 54% of informal healthcare providers in Uganda expressed WTP.19 Despite these international findings, provider-level data from Bangladesh remain unavailable. This study, therefore, aimed to quantify doctors’ WTP for CBHI and identify factors associated with it.

Methods

Study design

This was a comparative cross-sectional study conducted on formal and informal doctors in Dhaka city, Bangladesh.

Study setting

This study was conducted in Dhaka city, the capital and largest metropolitan area of Bangladesh. Dhaka is the country’s main hub for medical education and healthcare delivery, hosting government hospitals, private clinics and research centres. Both formally and informally employed doctors practice here across diverse service settings.9 The high density of medical institutions and mixed employment structures made Dhaka an appropriate site to compare doctors’ WTP for CBHI. Data were collected from different areas of the city to capture variation in workplace types and practice arrangements.

Study period, population and sample size

The study period was 1 July 2022 to 30 June 2023. The sample size was calculated using the following formula:20 n= (Zα+Zβ)2 ×[{P11-P1}+{P21-P2}](P2-P1)2.

Here, P1=proportion of formal workers WTP for HI=0.80.15

P2=proportion of informal workers WTP for HI=0.93.16

Considering a 95% CI, 80% power and 10% non-responsive rate, the sample size for each group was 116. So, a total of 232 doctors working formally or informally at private chambers, government and non-government organisations like medical colleges and research centres of Dhaka city were purposively included in the study. Purposive sampling was chosen because doctors constitute a specific and often hard-to-reach professional group. Additionally, there is no up-to-date national register of doctors indicating their current employment status in Bangladesh that could serve as a sampling frame, and purposive selection allowed us to ensure equal representation of both formal and informal doctors.

Data collection

Data were collected using a semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire developed following a comprehensive literature review.13,19 The tool covered socio-demographic, occupational and health-related characteristics, including healthcare use and expenditure. It also included the double-bounded dichotomous choice questions for estimating WTP for CBHI. The questionnaire was pre-tested among 30 doctors (15 formal, 15 informal) to assess clarity and timing. Necessary wording adjustments were made based on feedback. Data collection was performed by the authors between July 2022 and June 2023 after obtaining written informed consent from all participants. The complete questionnaire is provided as ‘online supplemental annexure’.

Outcome variables

Willingness-to-pay for community-based health insurance

WTP for CBHI was the dependent variable. It was measured using the double-bounded dichotomous choice approach within the contingent valuation method. Each participant was asked whether they would pay a proposed percentage of their monthly personal income as a premium for the insurance scheme. A ‘yes’ response was followed by a higher bid, and a ‘no’ response by a lower bid. Four starting bids (6%, 4%, 2% and 1% of monthly income) were selected after a pre-test among 30 doctors to identify realistic payment ranges. The final outcome was categorised as ‘willing’ or ‘not willing’ to pay any positive amount for enrolment. The mean WTP was calculated as a percentage of monthly personal income.

The CBHI model presented to doctors was adapted from studies conducted in Addis Ababa, which identified OOP expenses and limited coverage as major barriers to participation.17 21 In this scheme, participants would pay a monthly premium entitling themselves, their spouses and dependants under 18 years to free healthcare and prescribed medicines. All levels of care would be fully covered through an independently managed health fund.

Independent variables

The independent variables of this study included the type of doctors,22 age (in years), sex, religion, marital status, postgraduate, monthly personal income (MPI) in Bangladeshi Taka (BDT), monthly family income (MFI) in BDT, number of family members, number of under 5 children, number of elderly, service years,23 clinical attachment, workdays per week, work hours per day, history of illness, sought healthcare and healthcare expenditure. A detailed definition of these variables is provided in online supplemental table 1.

Data analysis

The study employed the double-bounded dichotomous choice (DBDC) method within the contingent valuation framework to estimate the mean WTP. Each participant was first asked whether they would pay a proposed percentage of their monthly personal income as a premium for CBHI. A ‘yes’ response triggered a higher follow-up bid, and a ‘no’ response triggered a lower bid, producing two data points per respondent. This method increases statistical efficiency and improves precision compared with single-bounded formats.24 There are four possible outcomes: (1) yes-yes (WTP>second higher bid), (2) yes-no (first bid>WTP>second higher bid), (3) no-yes (first bid<WTP<second lower bid) and (4) no-no (WTP<second lower bid).25 Four initial bids (6%, 4%, 2% and 1% of monthly income) were selected after pre-testing among 30 doctors to identify realistic payment levels. The mean WTP was estimated using the ‘doubleb’ package in Stata.26 Further details of DBDC estimation are available elsewhere.27

All collected data were edited, recorded and analysed using Stata (V.17; College Station, Texas, USA) in alignment with the study objectives and variables. Descriptive statistics included frequencies and percentages for categorical data, and medians with IQRs for quantitative data. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Association between the dependent and independent variables was assessed using the χ² test, Fisher’s exact test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. We fitted separate logistic regression models to examine the association between WTP and each independent variable. To control for potential confounding, all models were adjusted for age and sex. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with the corresponding 95% CI were reported for statistically significant variables.

A post hoc power analysis was performed in Stata (V.17) using the power two proportion command. The analysis assessed whether the sample size provided sufficient power to detect observed differences in WTP proportions. For doctor type, observed proportions were 0.7 for informal doctors and 0.9 for formal doctors, with a total sample of 232 (1:1 ratio). For healthcare-seeking status, observed proportions were 0.7 for ‘No’ (n=35) and 0.9 for ‘Yes’ (n=145), with a ratio of 1:4.1. In both comparisons, the calculated statistical power exceeded 80%, indicating adequate sample precision for detecting group differences.

Results

A total of 232 doctors participated in the study and were equally distributed among the formal and informal groups. The median (IQR) age of the doctors was 34.0 (31.5–36.0) years and 53.0% of them were male. The majority (81.0%) were early career doctors and had clinical attachment (59.9%). The mean WTP (95% CI) for CBHI was 5.6% (5.1% to 6.2%) of MPI. Informal doctors had higher mean WTP than formal doctors (5.8% and 5.4%, respectively). A significant association was found between the type of doctors and age (p value<0.001), number of elderly (p value=0.033), MPI in BDT (p value=0.030), workday per week (p value=0.002), marital status (p value=0.031), service years (p value=0.002) and clinical attachment (p value=0.001). No extreme or implausible WTP values were observed, as responses were constrained within the pre-tested bid range of 1%–6% of monthly personal income (table 1).

Discussion

This study explored doctors’ WTP for CBHI in Bangladesh, focusing on their dual role as beneficiaries and advocates within the system. Doctors, whether working in public or private settings, form a crucial link between health services and patients. Their participation in CBHI does not indicate an expansion of private practice but rather the integration of all practicing doctors into a shared risk-pooling mechanism that strengthens healthcare financing. Understanding doctors’ willingness to participate is important because they influence patients’ treatment choices and can shape the acceptance of insurance programmes.10 11 Given Bangladesh’s limited HI coverage, evidence on provider-level WTP helps identify opportunities to build professional engagement and improve public trust in future health-financing reforms.

Access to affordable healthcare remains a major challenge in Bangladesh, where high OOP spending limits service use. HI could reduce financial barriers and promote equity in access. Experiences from India and other LMICs show that integrating CBHI within primary healthcare systems can expand coverage and financial protection.28 In mixed healthcare systems, private practitioners often bridge gaps where public facilities are overburdened, providing timely access and a wider choice of services.29 Effective collaboration between public and private sectors, supported by inclusive insurance schemes, may therefore accelerate progress toward universal health coverage (UHC) in Bangladesh.

This study found that a substantial proportion of both informal (74.1%) and formal (85.3%) doctors expressed WTP for CBHI, with an overall rate of 79.7%. Informal doctors reported a higher mean WTP than formal doctors, yet the likelihood of being WTP was higher among formally employed doctors. Mid-career doctors and those who had sought healthcare in the previous 6 months showed lower odds of WTP. These findings highlight how employment stability and healthcare experience shape doctors’ participation in insurance schemes.

Although informal doctors reported a higher mean WTP, their overall likelihood of being WTP was lower. This apparent inconsistency may reflect the unstable nature of informal practice, which is often characterised by irregular income and limited job security. Informal doctors may express higher payment levels when able to contribute, but remain uncertain about sustaining regular premiums. In contrast, formal doctors have fixed salaries and institutional benefits, allowing consistent contributions despite slightly lower mean WTP. Similar patterns have been observed in other CBHI studies, where income stability strongly influenced enrolment behaviour.13 30

Formally employed doctors had 2.02 times higher odds of WTP for CBHI than informal doctors. This finding aligns with studies from Ethiopia, where private hospital workers showed 0.7 times lower odds of WTP than government employees,21 and Malaysia, where private sector workers were also less likely to contribute.31 Formal doctors’ stable incomes and job security may support regular contributions, whereas financial uncertainty among informal doctors may limit participation.32

Overall, 79.7% of doctors expressed WTP for CBHI, with higher proportions among formal (85.3%) than informal (74.1%) doctors. Comparable studies reported similar trends. In Bangladesh, reported WTP levels among other groups ranged from 83% to 93%.13 14 16 The mean WTP was 5.6% of monthly income, comparable to Zambia (4.6%)33; however, higher than Ethiopia (2.5–4.0%),34 35 and Bangladesh (1.7%).13 Informal doctors’ higher mean WTP (5.8%) likely reflects lower job security and limited employer benefits.32

Differences in socio-demographic and occupational factors also influenced WTP. Senior doctors, who typically earn higher incomes, may view CBHI as unnecessary because their financial capacity enables them to access private care if needed.30 In addition, higher premium expectations associated with seniority may discourage participation.30 36 This pattern is consistent with studies from Ethiopia and Malaysia, where more experienced participants exhibited lower WTP for HI.30 36 In our study, informal doctors tended to be younger, a trend similar to earlier findings from Bangladesh.14 A higher proportion of informal doctors (70.7%) were clinicians compared with formal doctors (49.1%), and most doctors in both groups were in the early stages of their careers.21

We observed a significant association between healthcare-seeking behaviour and WTP for CBHI. Doctors who had sought healthcare in the past 6 months had lower odds of being WTP. This finding aligns with a systematic review that identified factors such as provider behaviour, distance and healthcare experiences as important determinants of WTP.32 In Bangladesh, declining trust in the health system, evidenced by increasing numbers of patients seeking treatment abroad, has been well documented.37 Such erosion of trust may partly explain the reduced WTP among doctors who recently engaged with healthcare services. This association was only borderline significant and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Regional experiences demonstrate how CBHI can be expanded through tailored policy approaches. In Bangladesh, CBHI operates chiefly through micro-HI initiatives led by microfinance institutions, non-governmental organisations (NGOs) and healthcare providers. These schemes are most commonly linked to microfinance organisations, which introduced insurance products to protect their borrowers. During the late 1990s and early 2000s, microfinance groups began incorporating HI into their service portfolios, offering coverage to members and their families. Organisations such as Gonoshasthaya Kendra, BRAC, Grameen Kalyan and other community-based providers have played a central role in advancing these initiatives.38 In India, state-level schemes such as Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana and Ayushman Bharat have improved financial protection for informal and low-income groups through subsidised premiums and digital enrolment systems.39 In Nepal, the National Health Insurance Programme combines community-level contributions with government co-financing to enhance service access.40 These models show how integrating partial subsidies, simplified enrolment and provider engagement can strengthen CBHI in Bangladesh. Although CBHI schemes primarily target low-income and rural populations, this study examined doctors as potential contributors and advocates. Their participation can enhance scheme credibility and indirectly encourage uptake among primary beneficiary groups by fostering trust and professional endorsement of risk-pooling approaches.

This study is among the first to assess both formal and informal doctors’ WTP for CBHI in Bangladesh. The DBDC method, considered the gold standard in economic evaluation, was used to estimate mean WTP. The comparative design enabled a clear examination of socio-demographic differences between two professional groups. However, several methodological limitations should be considered. The purposive sampling approach limits representativeness, particularly for doctors with weaker professional networks or those practicing outside central urban areas. The cross-sectional design prevents any conclusion about causality. Stated WTP may also be influenced by hypothetical bias, since responses do not reflect actual purchase behaviour. The exclusive focus on Dhaka limits generalisability to settings with different employment structures or service environments. Broader determinants such as equity, community participation and past exposure to insurance were outside the study scope. These limitations should guide the interpretation of the findings.12 41 42 Finally, since the sample included only doctors, the findings reflect provider-level willingness and cannot be generalised to the wider population.

Conclusion

In this observational study, both formal and informal doctors showed substantial WTP for CBHI, though influencing factors differed. Formal doctors were more likely to contribute, while informal doctors expressed higher mean payment levels despite financial uncertainty. Targeted communication campaigns and tiered premium structures based on income or career stage could enhance participation and fairness. Improving healthcare quality and access may further strengthen WTP.

These findings provide actionable guidance for developing context-specific CBHI models in Bangladesh. Engaging doctors in scheme design and advocacy can build public trust and encourage wider enrolment. Their WTP also offers a basis for provider-supported CBHI frameworks that extend coverage to low-income and informal groups, supporting progress toward UHC.
