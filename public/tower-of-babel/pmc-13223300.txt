Ultraviolet radiation exposure and melanoma risk among over 2 million Ontario workers
Authors: Dylan E O’Sullivan, Fanni R Eros, Nathan L DeBono, Paul A Demers, Cheryl E Peters, Jeavana Sritharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine the association between occupational sun exposure and melanoma risk among a large cohort of 2.4 million workers. These workers were identified through accepted compensation claims, which were linked to cancer records to identify melanoma diagnoses from 1983 to 2019 in Ontario, Canada. An existing job exposure matrix, SUNJEM, was used to estimate occupational solar ultraviolet radiation (UVR) exposure as low-intermittent (intermittent/partly shaded exposure), moderate (2 to < 6 h/day), or high (≥6 h/day). The association between occupational solar UVR and melanoma risk was assessed using Cox proportional hazard regression models, with age as the timescale and adjustment for sex and birth cohort. A total of 8,170 incident melanoma cases were identified among workers. Both the low-intermittent (HR = 1.26; 95%CI = 1.17–1.38) and high exposure groups (HR = 1.13; 95%CI = 1.01–1.26) had higher rates of melanoma than indoor workers. The low-intermittent exposure group had elevated melanoma rates on the arms and trunk (body), while the high exposure group had an elevated rate on the head and trunk. High risk occupation groups included truck drivers and farmers. Findings support an association between low-intermittent and high occupational solar UVR exposures and melanoma risk. Introduction

Worldwide, melanoma incidence rates continue to increase, where it is estimated that by 2040, the number of new cases will increase by 50%1. Among World Health Organization (WHO) regions, North America has the third highest incidence rate of melanoma, accounting for nearly one-third of all global cases1. The largest contributor to the development of melanoma is exposure to ultraviolet radiation (UVR)2. Intense and intermittent sun activities, such as sunbathing or outdoor sports, have been consistently associated with an increased risk of melanoma2. In addition, the occurrence of painful and blistering sunburns increases the risk of melanoma in a dose-response manner2.

A previous meta-analysis on chronic exposure to the sun, which included a large number of studies on occupational sun exposure, reported a suggestive protective effect with the relationship being stronger at higher latitudes2. In contrast, a recent meta-analysis performed by the WHO found that high (or any) occupational exposure to solar UVR was associated with a modest increase in the risk of melanoma (16%) compared with no (or low) occupational exposure3. Some studies have found increased risks of melanoma associated with specific occupations, such as farming4–6. The inconsistencies observed for this relationship could partly be due to confounding, where workers with fair skin and at high-risk of melanoma may self-select into indoor jobs7. It is also possible that some of the inconsistencies come from a lack of examination of nuanced patterns of sun exposure.

A large focus of research on occupational sun exposure and the risk of melanoma has been on comparing high exposure to low exposure, but there has been a paucity of studies focusing on occupations with mixed or intermittent occupational exposure. Compared to intermittent exposure, moderate levels of continuous sun exposure may provide protection against melanoma through increased epidermal melanin production, which minimizes the genotoxic effects of future sun exposure8,9. However, there is a threshold to this protective response beyond which sustained sun exposure can cause genotoxic effects in melanocytes, potentially leading to melanoma development10. For prevention of melanoma, it is imperative to understand how different patterns of occupational sun exposure might influence the development of melanoma. Another possible explanation for the inconsistent relationship between outdoor work and melanoma risk is that overall risk estimates may mask site-specific effects. Melanomas on the head, neck, or arms are more likely to be linked to occupational sun exposure, unlike those on less exposed areas such as the trunk11. It is therefore important to examine how different patterns of occupational sun exposure are associated with melanoma risk across various body sites.

This study aimed to characterize patterns of occupational UVR exposure in a large cohort of workers in Ontario, Canada, and to examine their association with melanoma risk overall, and by sex, anatomical location, histologic subtype, and age at diagnosis.

Methods

Study population

The Occupational Disease Surveillance System (ODSS) has been described in detail elsewhere12,13. Briefly, the ODSS was established by linking approximately 2.4 million workers, identified in the Ontario Workplace Safety and Insurance Board (WSIB, 1983–2019) accepted lost-time compensation claims records, to provincial cancer records. For each worker, information on sex, date of birth, and occupation and industry at the time of each claim, were retrieved from the WSIB records. A majority of the WSIB claims were for musculoskeletal-related injuries. Occupation and industry at the time of claim were coded by the WSIB according to the Canadian Classification Dictionary of Occupations (CCDO 1971) and Canadian Standard Industry Classification (SIC 1970). Workers were excluded if they were missing sex or date of birth, had invalid claim dates or missing job codes (n = 17,557). Those with accepted claims for malignant cancers were also excluded as prevalent cases, including 14 workers compensated for melanoma (where cancer site was specified). Workers were then linked to the Registered Persons Database (RPDB) which provides information on death (if applicable), emigration out of Ontario, and a unique health insurance number (HIN) for all Ontarians registered for provincial health insurance. Workers were subsequently linked to the Ontario Cancer Registry (OCR) to identify incident melanoma cases (ICD-O-3 C44) during follow-up. Workers with a melanoma diagnosis date prior to cohort entry were excluded (n = 1,490). Ethics approval was granted by the University of Toronto Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (REB) (#39013). All methods followed Ontario Health guidelines and the approved REB protocol. Informed consent was not required for secondary data use, as permitted under Ontario Health policies and the REB approval (reference 39013).

Exposure to occupational solar ultraviolet radiation

A previously created job exposure matrix, SUNJEM, was used to assign exposure to occupational solar UVR to all workers in the ODSS using CCDO and SIC codes associated with their first claim in ODSS14. The majority of workers in ODSS only have one claim (approximately 67%), and workers with multiple claims tend to stay in the same industry. Exposure to solar UVR was defined as exposure to the sun at work that is likely to exceed typical non-occupational exposure (not including heavy recreational sunbathing). Four exposure categories were utilized including indoor workers (none), low-intermittent (intermittent or partly shaded exposure), moderate (2 to < 6 h) and high (≥ 6 h). To identify jobs in the high-exposure category, SUNJEM used the occupational skin cancer prevention workbook developed by Cancer Council Australia’s SunSmart program, which identifies typical outdoor jobs15 and was adapted for the Canadian context. In addition, the SUNJEM utilized two-career-selection websites that describe tasks by job title and include expected amount of time spent outdoors to identify occupations in the low-intermittent and moderate exposure categories. Some occupation groups include workers with different exposure depending on their specific tasks within the occupation. For these occupations, we utilized industry codes to determine which individuals are exposed and which are not (e.g. a veterinarian is classified as an indoor worker unless in the agriculture industry). When the industry codes did not provide the information required to assign discordant exposure level within an occupation, we assigned exposure to all workers in the occupation if it was estimated that over 75% of workers in the occupation would be exposed, otherwise we assigned the entire occupation as non-exposed, as was done in previous studies using this JEM14. This approach has been previously used to estimate the risk of cancer associated with occupational UVR in Canada14.

Statistical analyses

We utilized Cox proportional hazard regression models using age as the time-scale and adjusted for sex and birth year to determine the association of different patterns of occupational exposure to solar UVR and the rate of melanoma during follow-up. Workers were followed from 1983 or their first WSIB accepted claim to the earliest of the date of primary melanoma diagnosis, emigration from Ontario, death, age 85, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2019). We included an interaction term in the model and conducted stratified analyses to determine if the association of occupational exposure to solar ultraviolet radiation varied by sex. We also performed analyses to assess whether the association between occupational exposure to solar UVR and melanoma risk varied by body site, subtype of melanoma, or age at diagnosis. This was completed by modelling each as a separate outcome and comparing rates between exposure groups. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Among the 2,359,100 workers in the ODSS, there were 1,524,433 male workers and 834,667 female workers. The median age at the start of follow-up was 34.4 (IQR: 25.5–45.6) years. A total of 378,177 (16.0%) workers were assessed as exposed to occupational solar UVR. Among exposed workers, 22.7% were highly exposed, 34.4% had low-intermittent exposure, and 42.9% had moderate exposure. A higher proportion of males were exposed to solar UVR compared to females (22.5% vs. 4.5%).

Table 1 presents characteristics of exposure groups stratified by sex. For males, moderate occupational solar UVR was the most common exposure category (10.0%), followed by low-intermittent exposure (7.7%). The low-intermittent exposure category consisted of a slightly older population than the other exposure categories. Among males, there has been a decrease in occupational exposure to solar UVR from 24.6% for workers born before 1950 to 20.0% for workers born in 1970 or after, with the decrease coming exclusively among the low-intermittent exposure group. For females, the high exposure group was the most common exposure category (1.8%), followed by low-intermittent exposure (1.5%). Among females the non-exposed and low-intermittent exposed groups were slightly older than the moderate and high exposure groups. For females, overall exposure to solar UVR has been fairly consistent over time. For both sexes, the industries with the greatest proportion of their workers in each of the exposure categories were agriculture industry for the high exposure; forestry, fishing, and trapping industry for the moderate exposure; and the transportation, communication and other utilities industry for the low-intermittent exposure.

Table 2 presents the ten occupations with the highest proportion of workers exposed to different levels of solar UVR, stratified by sex. Among males, truck drivers made up most of the low-intermittent exposure group (73.6%). The moderate exposure group primarily included other construction trade (24.3%); carpenters and related (16.6%); and industrial, farm and construction machinery mechanics and repairmen (15.9%). Nearly half of the high exposure group were nursery and related workers (22.8%) and general farm workers (21.0%). Among females, truck drivers (34.1%) and other transport and related equipment operators (33.3%) made up much of the low-intermittent exposure group. The moderate exposure group was largely longshore workers, stevedores and freight handlers (22.7%); and other farming, horticultural and animal-husbandry occupations (17.4%). In the high exposure group, nearly all were general farm workers (33.0%), mail carriers (32.6%), and nursery workers (26.7%).

Over a median follow-up period of 24.5 (IQR: 14.8–32.2) years, a total of 8,170 incident melanoma cases were identified with 5,766 occurring among males and 2,404 occurring among females. Malignant melanoma most commonly occurred on the trunk (i.e., torso) of males and on the legs for females. For both sexes, superficial spreading melanoma was the most frequent histologic subtype, followed by nodular melanoma.

Table 3 presents results for the association of exposure to occupational UVR (compared to indoor workers) and the incidence rate of developing melanoma in the ODSS. Compared to indoor workers, the low-intermittent and high exposure groups had higher incidence rates of melanoma during follow-up, while the moderate exposure group had reduced rates of melanoma. By body site of melanoma, the low-intermittent exposure group had higher rates of melanoma on the arms (43% increased risk) and trunk (21% increased risk), as well as higher rates on the head (19% increased risk) compared to indoor workers. The high exposure group had higher rates of melanoma on the trunk and head. Compared to indoor workers, no occupational exposure groups had higher rates of melanoma on the legs, and rates of melanoma at other body sites in the moderate exposure group were similar to those of indoor workers (Table 3). The low-intermittent exposure group had higher rates of superficial spreading (30% increased risk) and nodular melanoma (43% increased risk), while the high exposure group showed a 19% increased risk of superficial spreading melanoma only (compared to indoor workers). The moderate exposure group had significantly lower rates of lentigo maligna melanoma. Both the low-intermittent and high exposure groups had higher rates of melanoma diagnosed at age 50 or older, but not among those under age 50 compared to indoor workers.

Discussion

We observed that compared to indoor workers, the low-intermittent (intermittent or partly shaded exposure) and high (≥ 6 h/day) exposure groups had significantly higher risks of developing melanoma during follow-up, while the moderate exposure group (2 to < 6 h/day) did not. Additionally, compared to indoor workers, the low-intermittent exposure group had elevated risks of melanoma on the arms and trunk, while the high exposure group had elevated risk on the head and trunk. Interestingly, we observed some sex-based effect modification, where elevated melanoma risk was associated with low-intermittent exposure primarily in males and the risk associated with high exposure was stronger in females. It is important to note that non-differential misclassification introduced through one-time claim ascertainment or the JEM itself would have likely biased the results towards the null and that the associations observed in this study are likely an underestimation of the true relationships. Further, while the lack of control for confounding by skin tone may have introduced confounding bias, we still observed positive associations in the high exposed groups. Based on these results, sun protection measures should target at-risk groups, such as truck drivers (intermittent) and farmers (high exposure), while accounting for sex-based differences in risk.

Previous studies have shown elevated melanoma risk in association with occupational UVR exposure, but these associations may vary by latitude where the intensity of outdoor UVR differs. In a systematic review by Jiang and colleagues, 6 out of the 10 included studies found a significant association between outdoor occupational UVR exposure and melanoma risk16. Two of the studies found that indoor work was significantly associated with melanoma risk, but they hypothesized that this might be attributable to the relationship between higher socioeconomic status and high levels of recreational sun exposure among indoor work workers16. In the Western Canada Melanoma Study, Elwood and colleagues found elevated melanoma risks for superficial spreading and nodular melanoma in association with occupational UVR exposure, with the highest risks observed for moderate exposure (approximately 1–8 h/week)17. This exposure category corresponds somewhat to the low exposure category in our study, where we also observed elevated melanoma risks overall, as well as for these two subtypes, but not lentigo maligna melanoma. In a European multicenter case-control study, Zanetti and colleagues also observed an elevated risk of superficial spreading melanoma among outdoor workers with the highest number of lifetime outdoor working hours18. It is also notable that the majority of the high exposure group were farming and nursery workers, occupational groups that have been identified as high risk for melanoma in previous studies4,5. There is biological plausibility for the differential risk of melanoma by pattern of exposure. Intermittent sun exposure can result in sunburns and DNA damage to melanocytes from short intense UVR exposure, which can lead to melanoma development2,19,20. While many studies have suggested a plateauing of risk with increased sun exposure, chronic high exposure to solar UVR over a long time is likely to produce DNA damage, eventually resulting in melanoma2,9,21,22. Moderate occupational sun exposure, however, was not associated with increased melanoma risk compared with individuals working indoor jobs, and we even observed that it was associated with a lower risk of the lentigo maligna subtype. It is possible that moderate sun exposure results in melanin production in melanocytes and epidermal thickening, protecting the nuclei from future UVR-related damage11,12,23. Further, vitamin D produced in the skin through solar UVR exposure may potentially play a protective role in melanoma development24–26. It is also possible that moderately exposure workers have better sun protection behaviours or lower recreational exposure than indoor workers.

In this study, we observed that the low-intermittent exposure group had elevated risks of melanoma on the arms and trunk, while the high exposure group had elevated risks on the head and trunk. The elevated melanoma risk on the arms in the low-intermittent group is likely due to most workers with low intermittent sun exposure being truck drivers, where the driver’s side arms are frequently exposed to solar UVR. A United States based study had found an increased risk of left arm melanoma, which may predominantly be due to driver’s-side automobile UVR exposure where the driver sits on the left side27. In the same study, the highest melanoma risk was observed in the head, neck, and trunk areas for both high and low-intermittent sun exposure. Similarly, in a pooled analysis of fifteen case-control studies, Chang and colleagues found that higher levels of occupational sun exposure were significantly associated with an increased risk of melanoma on the head or neck, but only at lower latitudes (where UVR is strongest)28. It is possible that the association with head and neck melanomas was not observed in this study because of the higher latitudes of Ontario. Low-intermittent solar UVR exposures also potentially cause head and neck cancers through sunburns2,19,20. On the other hand, a study from Australia did not observe elevated melanoma risk in association with any level of occupational solar UVR at any body site6. The elevated risk of melanoma on the trunk associated with low-intermittent and high occupational UVR exposure in this study is less clear. It is possible that workers in these groups engage in more recreational sun exposure where their trunks are not covered, leading to burns29–31. This study found no association between occupational UVR exposure and leg melanoma, suggesting adequate coverage of the lower extremities for most outdoor workers.

We also observed some evidence of effect modification by sex, where the risk of melanoma was associated with low-intermittent exposure primarily among males, while the risk associated with high exposure was stronger among females. One explanation may be differences in occupation distribution, where there were more male truck drivers (n = 92,834) compared to females (n = 4,762). As a result, it appears that males are more likely to be exposed to low intermittent occupational sun exposure. Furthermore, sex differences in behaviour may also play a role, with females more likely to implement sun protection measures than males32. By limiting exposure at low levels by protective measures, it is possible that only high levels of chronic exposure become a risk for females. However, differences in the biology between the sexes may also contribute to differences in melanoma development. In a recent review, evidence supported the protective role of estrogen against melanoma progression33. Studies have shown that estrogen might enhance the female immune system and protect against tumor cell growth by promoting and expanding tumor-associated antigen-specific cell responses and cytokine interactions33,34. In contrast, male immune systems are enriched for natural killer cells and regulatory T cells, potentially making them more susceptible to melanoma at lower exposures of UVR compared to females33,34. Furthermore, studies suggest an age-specific sex-difference in melanoma risk, where older males and younger females are at higher risk35,36. We also observed elevated melanoma risks among males 50 years or older and slightly elevated risk among younger females at higher levels of UVR exposure. It is also possible that some of these observed sex-differences are due to uncontrolled confounding by skin tone/ethnicity, although the magnitude and direction of this potential confounding on our results is difficult to estimate. In addition, it is possible that some of these differences are chance findings and need to be replicated. Future studies that adjust for skin tone are necessary to confirm these findings. In addition, future studies should also examine recreational UVR exposures among different occupation groups.

With the large sample size and longitudinal nature of the study, we were able to examine the association of different patterns of occupational solar UVR with the risk of melanoma. In addition, we were able to examine how different patterns of occupational solar UVR exposure and melanoma risk varied by sex, body site location, histologic subtype, and age at diagnosis, which is rarely done in studies of this nature. Despite these strengths, this study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, we were unable to adjust for important confounders, such as ethnicity, skin tone, other host characteristics, and recreational UVR exposure, since these factors were not captured in administrative data sources. It is possible that individuals with a very fair photo-type or other melanoma risk factors, are more likely to self-select for indoor jobs, compared to individuals without those characteristics37. Skin colour is an important risk factor for melanoma and could not be addressed in this study. It is also likely that recreational exposure to solar UVR and the associated melanoma risk varies between indoor and outdoor workers. Previous studies have shown an association between indoor work and higher socioeconomic status, as well as more recreational time spent outdoors38. Canadians are more likely to spend time outdoors recreationally leading to increased UV exposure39. Further, we do not have data on sunbed use and this may also impact findings. A lack of adjustment for these characteristics likely underestimated the risk of developing melanoma associated with different levels of occupational solar UVR exposure. Moreover, we don’t have information on individual level sun protection behaviour, such as wearing protective clothing, using sunscreen or seeking shade. These measures might be among the most relevant factors influencing melanoma risk and could also be related to the sex-differences observed in this study40,41. Another limitation is that exposure was assigned based on the workers’ job information at the time of their accepted compensation claim, limiting our ability to assess duration or changes in exposure, in which may lead to exposure misclassification. As a result, exposure misclassification may have contributed to the null results for the moderate exposure group. Although there is a large number of females in the cohort, it only includes those with an accepted lost-time claim, and females make up a smaller proportion of these claims in the province. This further limits analyses by exposure status, specific occupational groups, and sex-specific outcomes.

Overall, we observed elevated melanoma risk among workers in the low-intermittent and high solar UVR groups, whereas no increased risk was observed in the moderate exposure group. Sex-differences may influence findings, with females showing higher risk in the high-exposure group and males showing higher risk in the low-intermittent exposure group. The elevated risk among males may, in part, be attributable to melanoma of the arm in truck drivers. However, because this study did not account for several potential confounders, including skin tone, ethnicity, and recreational outdoor behaviours, the findings should be interpreted with caution and causal conclusions should not be drawn. However, the results are still relevant for surveillance and are informative for identifying high-risk occupational groups that could benefit from targeted prevention efforts, both in the work place and in their recreational activities. Our results highlight the need for further research on sex-differences in melanoma risk related to solar UVR exposure, taking into account factors such as ethnicity, recreational sun exposure, and sun protective behaviours. They also emphasize the importance of protecting workers from occupational solar UVR exposure, even at low-intermittent levels.
