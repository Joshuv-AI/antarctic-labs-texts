Real‐World Effectiveness and 12‐Month Persistence of a Semaglutide‐Supported Digital Weight‐Loss Service: A Retrospective Cohort Study in Germany
Authors: Louis Talay, Jason Hom, Marilyn Tan, Neera Ahuja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the 12‐month effectiveness and patient persistence of a semaglutide‐supported digital weight‐loss service (DWLS) in a real‐world German cohort.MethodsThis retrospective study analysed 4535 patients who initiated the Juniper Germany DWLS. Primary endpoints included 12‐month medication and data submission adherence (receiving ≥ 10 orders) and mean weight‐loss percentage. Effectiveness was assessed via a per‐protocol (PP) analysis of an adherent sub‐cohort (n = 546) and an intention‐to‐treat (ITT) analysis of the full cohort using Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) and a Worst‐Case Scenario (WCS) sensitivity model. Binary logistic and multiple linear regressions identified predictors of attrition (churn) and efficacy.ResultsOf the total cohort (n = 4535), mean 12‐month weight loss was 9.47% (LOCF). However, sensitivity analysis using a WCS model—assuming 0% weight loss for all dropouts—yielded a mean of 2.40%. Among the 12% of patients (n = 546) who remained adherent to both medication and data submission protocols, mean weight loss was 15.13% (±7.1). Program persistence was significantly bolstered by achieving a healthy BMI milestone (OR = 0.29, p < 0.001), encountering a weight‐loss plateau (OR = 0.41, p < 0.001), and higher baseline obesity severity (Obesity Class I–III: p < 0.023). Conversely, high clinical complexity (≥ 3 comorbidities) more than doubled the risk of churn (OR = 2.12, p = 0.002), while previous GLP‐1 RA use also significantly increased churn risk (OR = 1.57, p = 0.001). While moderate early engagement (2–10 logs) was a strong predictor of persistence (p < 0.001), intensive tracking (> 10 logs) did not significantly reduce churn risk (p = 0.393).ConclusionThe population‐level impact of this DWLS is fundamentally limited by high attrition (88%) in an unsubsidised environment. While significant weight loss is possible for a non‐representative adherent minority, the mean effectiveness for the total initiating population (2.40% WCS) highlights that real‐world success is strictly conditional upon long‐term persistence. The study found that both clinical success and setbacks were associated with program adherence. It also observed that patients of high clinical complexity were at greater risk of attrition. Finally, it found that high‐friction manual weight logging serves as a marker of commitment rather than anxiety as had been reported in app‐based DWLSs where tracking has significantly lower friction. These findings suggest that the predictive value of digital engagement is fundamentally shaped by DWLS delivery mode and the behavioural cost of engagement.Keywords: antiobesity drug, cohort study, obesity care, real‐world evidence, semaglutide, weight management

Introduction

Obesity is increasingly being recognised as one of the greatest public health challenges in Germany [1]. Recent data from the Robert Koch Institute (RKI) indicate that approximately 53.5% of the German adult population is living with overweight, with nearly 19.7% affected by obesity [2, 3]. These conditions are intrinsically linked to a heightened risk of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs), most notably type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and several forms of cancer [4]. Beyond clinical pathology, excess weight in the German population exerts a profound negative impact on quality of life, mental health, and social functioning, while contributing to significant economic burdens—estimated to account for approximately 2.6% of Germany's GDP due to direct healthcare expenditures and productivity losses [5, 6].

The complex aetiology of obesity, involving a multifaceted interplay of genetic, sociocultural, and metabolic factors, necessitates a comprehensive and sustained management strategy [7]. Despite the foundational importance of caloric restriction and physical activity, users of traditional lifestyle programs often struggle to maintain clinically significant long‐term weight loss [8]. In response, modern pharmacological agents have emerged as critical tools to augment these efforts. Among these, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs), such as semaglutide, have demonstrated substantial efficacy in landmark clinical trials, providing weight reductions far exceeding previous pharmacological standards [9, 10]. Semaglutide functions by selectively binding to GLP‐1 receptors, stimulating insulin secretion, suppressing appetite through central satiety centers, and delaying gastric emptying [11].

The arrival of these highly effective medications has coincided with the rapid expansion of digital health solutions. Consequently, digital weight‐loss services (DWLSs) have become a prominent avenue for access. These services offer a primary advantage by overcoming geographic and temporal barriers within the German healthcare landscape, reducing the necessity for frequent in‐person clinical visits [12, 13]. Research suggests that patients often exhibit greater comfort in disclosing weight‐related concerns within a virtual environment, which may help mitigate the pervasive weight‐related stigma often encountered in traditional medical settings [13, 14].

However, major health institutions such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in the UK and the German Obesity Society stress that pharmacological intervention must only serve as an adjunct to comprehensive lifestyle therapy under multidisciplinary guidance [15, 16]. Critics have questioned whether some DWLSs function primarily as ‘prescription engines’ lacking the necessary behavioural support and multidisciplinary team (MDT) oversight [16]. Juniper is a multinational DWLS that combines semaglutide prescribing with remote behavioural support and MDT oversight [17]. A recent real‐world study from an Australian Juniper cohort described favourable 12‐month weight‐loss and safety outcomes and emphasised the role of health‐coach contact and digital tracking [18].

Although clinical trials establish semaglutide's efficacy, longitudinal real‐world evidence from the German digital care setting remains limited. Most currently available literature in Germany focuses on short‐term evaluations of app‐based interventions or Digital Health Applications (DiGAs) that lack integration with effective pharmacotherapy. For instance, Gemesi et al. [19] reported a modest 3.2% weight loss over 12 weeks using an app‐only multimodal intervention, while Lautenbach et al. [20] observed a 5.29% reduction at 6 months with a stand‐alone digital therapeutic. Both studies also operated under controlled trial settings rather than real‐world contexts in which everyday adherence problems emerge. A 2024 retrospective analysis of the Juniper program in Germany provided a five‐month snapshot, reporting a mean weight loss of 9.52%, yet it highlighted the need for longer‐term data to understand persistence beyond the initial treatment phase [13].

The present study seeks to address this knowledge gap by assessing the long‐term effectiveness and patient persistence of a semaglutide‐supported DWLS in Germany. Beyond assessing weight loss, this study uniquely evaluates the behavioural thresholds, such as weight tracking frequency and clinical milestones, that govern persistence in an asynchronous care model. The primary aim is to evaluate weight‐loss outcomes and program adherence over a 12‐month period. Additionally, the study aims to identify the demographic, clinical, and early behavioural predictors that govern both clinical success and the risk of program attrition in this real‐world setting.

Methods

Study Design

This study retrospectively analysed the trajectory of all patients who initiated semaglutide‐supported treatment through the Juniper Germany DWLS between the program's inception and February 26, 2025 (12 months before data extraction). Prescribing clinicians followed European Medicines Agency guidelines for semaglutide dosing [21]. All study data were retrieved from the Juniper central data repository using BigQuery SQL and visualised via Metabase. The Stanford University IRB determined that the study did not meet the definition of human subject research as defined in federal regulations 45 CFR 46.102 or 21 CFR 50.3. Investigators followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22]. As a retrospective, single‐arm observational study, this design evaluates the effectiveness of the DWLS in a real‐world setting. Due to the out‐of‐pocket nature of the service, a parallel ‘usual care’ control group was not available for comparison.

Program Overview

The Juniper Germany DWLS is delivered asynchronously through a web‐based platform. Prospective patients complete an online pre‐consultation questionnaire containing over 100 questions regarding their health background. Certified doctors evaluate these responses to determine eligibility. To validate self‐reported comorbidities, clinicians request supplementary evidence—including recent pathology (blood tests), medical imagery, or existing specialist reports—whenever a report is clinically ambiguous or suggests a high‐risk contraindication. Eligibility decisions are governed by the European Medicines Agency guidelines for semaglutide, which specify BMI cut‐off points, contraindications, and drug–drug interactions [21]. The BMI thresholds for inclusion are 27 kg/m2 for individuals with at least one weight‐related health condition (e.g., hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, or symptomatic cardiovascular disease) and 30 kg/m2 for all others.

Contraindications for the program include pancreatitis, hypoglycemia, concomitant insulin use, diabetic retinopathy complications, previous acute kidney injury, multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2, a family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma, or known hypersensitivity to semaglutide. Doctors also exercise discretion regarding the concomitant use of other oral medications that may interact with semaglutide's effect on gastric emptying. Upon eligibility, patients pay an initial monthly subscription fee of EUR 356, which remains constant for subsequent months. Each patient is allocated a multidisciplinary team (MDT) consisting of a doctor, a university‐qualified health coach (nutritionist), and a nurse practitioner. To ensure consistency, all patients are instructed to utilise the same set of scales for every weight measurement provided during the program.

MDTs provide reactive semaglutide guidance and health coaching exclusively via email. At the start of the program, health coaches provide standardised nutritional recommendations organised into modules covering topics such as caloric deficit, portion control, macronutrients, and meal guidelines. Patients may also opt in for personalised meal and exercise plans by completing a lifestyle quiz. While coaches provide personalised responses to patient‐initiated inquiries regarding mindset or movement, they do not proactively contact patients with advice after program initiation. Patients are encouraged to track their weight on a bi‐weekly basis, which they submit manually via the Juniper desktop platform.

To supplement lifestyle interventions, patients receive a monthly supply of semaglutide. Patients receive two reminder emails prior to each automated monthly payment and order fulfilment. Side effects are reported via email with a severity rating (mild, moderate, or severe). Moderate side effects prompt ad hoc consultations with a doctor, while severe adverse events are referred to emergency services. All reported events trigger an automated alarm in the Eucalyptus clinical auditing system to ensure appropriate escalation.

All communication, questionnaire responses, and multimedia files are automatically uploaded to a central data repository on Metabase. To facilitate coordinated care, each MDT member has full access to their patients' health profiles and communications. Data are encrypted and restricted to MDTs and the Eucalyptus data analytics and clinical auditing teams.

Medication Titration and Cost

Physicians follow a standard escalation schedule: 0.25 mg once weekly for 4 weeks, followed by 0.5 mg, 1.0 mg, and potentially up to 2.0 mg, depending on clinical response and tolerability. Monthly fees for the Juniper Germany DWLS cover both the medication and access to the MDT and digital platform. Unlike the subsidised German statutory health insurance (GKV) model for some chronic conditions [23], the Juniper DWLS is currently unsubsidised, requiring patients to pay out‐of‐pocket.

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were the percentage of patients adhering to the program over 12 months and the mean weight‐loss percentage at that point. 12‐Month adherence was defined as receiving a minimum of 10 medication orders within 365 days and submitting a weight log within the 12‐month window (341–379 days post‐initiation). The ≥ 10‐order threshold was selected as a proxy for 12‐month medication persistence to ensure adequate treatment exposure while accounting for patients who may have paused or delayed treatment for short periods (e.g., managing transient side effects). This specific criterion has been utilised in previous 12‐month real‐world evaluations of the Juniper DWLS in the UK and Australia to ensure cross‐cohort comparability [18, 24]. Given the asynchronous nature of the service, adherence is measured via the count of pharmacy orders fulfilled; actual self‐administration (injection) is not directly observed, making order count a necessary pragmatic proxy for medication consumption. Secondary Endpoints included the identification of predictors for 12‐month persistence and weight‐loss efficacy, including the impact of early behavioural engagement (Month 1 tracking), clinical milestones (reaching a ‘Healthy BMI’ ≤ 25), the incidence of weight‐loss plateaus (defined as < 0.5 kg change over a 30–40‐day window) and the number of obesity‐related comorbidities (clinical complexity). The comorbidities captured during the program eligibility questionnaire included hypertension, high cholesterol or high triglycerides, symptomatic cardiovascular disease, obstructive sleep apnea, asthma, gallstones, non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease, osteoarthritis, pre‐diabetes, type 2 diabetes, severe kidney disease, and depression.

Statistical Analysis

Data distribution was evaluated using Shapiro–Wilk tests; due to significant departures from normality (p < 0.05), non‐parametric methods were prioritised. Binary logistic regression was used to calculate Odds Ratios (OR) for predictors of 12‐month churn. Multiple linear regression models identified independent predictors of percentage weight loss (%WL).

To identify the ‘activation threshold’, early tracking was categorised into levels: Baseline (0–1 logs), Moderate (2–5 logs), Consistent (6–10 logs), and Intensive (> 10 logs).

The LOCF method was utilised for the primary ITT analysis to maintain consistency with established real‐world digital health literature, enabling direct comparison with published benchmarks. However, acknowledging that LOCF may introduce optimistic bias by assuming weight stability after dropout, we prioritised a WCS boundary analysis. This conservative approach provides a more transparent estimate by assuming all patients who discontinued treatment returned to their baseline weight, thereby accounting for the non‐random nature of attrition often observed in self‐funded clinical settings.

Investigators selected candidate predictor variables a priori based on clinical relevance and prior Juniper DWLS analyses (age, sex, baseline BMI category, comorbidity count, prior GLP‐1 RA use, early tracking category, side‐effect reporting, and weight‐loss metrics). Variables identified in univariable screening (p < 0.10) were included in exploratory models. We then assessed collinearity with variance inflation factors (VIFs; all VIFs < 5) and evaluated model fit via Hosmer–Lemeshow (logistic) or residual diagnostics (linear).

Sensitivity Analysis

To address the potential optimistic bias of the LOCF method—which assumes weight stability after dropout—a Worst‐Case Scenario (WCS) sensitivity analysis was performed. In the WCS model, all patients who discontinued and had missing 12‐month data were assumed to have returned to their baseline weight (0% weight loss). This ‘Boundary Analysis’ provides a transparent, conservative lower bound of effectiveness, avoiding the potentially biased missing at random (MAR) assumptions of multiple imputation in a real‐world setting where attrition is often linked to cost or non‐response.

Potential Sources of Bias

Socioeconomic selection bias is a primary consideration, as the out‐of‐pocket cost of the service (€356/month) likely selects for individuals with higher disposable income, education levels, and health literacy. Consequently, this cohort is not representative of the broader German population living with obesity, particularly those managed within the GKV system where such costs are currently unsubsidised. This limitation fundamentally constrains the interpretability of our adherence rates as a benchmark for the general population. Information bias is a significant consideration due to the self‐reported nature of weight data. In the absence of Bluetooth‐integrated scales, manual entry is susceptible to social desirability bias, where patients may selectively report weight loss and omit periods of weight gain or plateaus. Finally, the use of the LOCF method to impute missing data in the full cohort introduces a bias skewed toward greater effectiveness, which is discussed further in the limitations section.

Results

Of the 4535 patients who initiated the Juniper Germany DWLS within the study window, 1431 (31.6%) met the medication adherence criterion of receiving at least 10 semaglutide orders. Of the 3104 patients who failed to meet this criterion, 378 achieved a healthy BMI. The final 12‐month study cohort, requiring both medication adherence and the submission of a weight log within the specified 355–375‐day window, consisted of 546 patients, representing a 12% overall adherence rate. A flow chart is presented to visualise patient adherence in Figure 1. Comparison of baseline characteristics between the adherent sub‐cohort (n = 546) and non‐completers (n = 3989) revealed significant differences in age and sex. Patients who completed the 12‐month program were significantly older (45.1 vs. 42.8 years, p < 0.001) and more likely to be female (85.9% vs. 78.3%, p < 0.001). While the difference in baseline BMI was statistically significant (33.59 vs. 34.14 kg/m2, p = 0.014), the effect size (d = 0.10) and absolute difference (0.55 kg/m2) were negligible. No significant difference was found regarding ethnicity (p = 0.86). Table 1 shows demographic characteristics.

Effectiveness and Side Effect Outcomes

Analysis of the total initiating population (n = 4535) represents the broad clinical impact of the service. Using ITT‐LOCF, the mean 12‐month weight loss was 9.47% (±7.4) (Table 3). To address the inherent selection bias of survivors, the WCS sensitivity analysis (Figure 2) indicates a conservative lower boundary of 2.40% (±6.16) for the total population. For the highly selected adherent sub‐cohort (n = 546), weight loss reached 15.13% (±7.1). When assessing the full cohort (n = 4535) via LOCF method to account for attrition, the mean weight loss was 9.47% (±7.4). While 91.2% of the adherent sub‐cohort achieved ≥ 5% weight loss, this figure dropped to 57.2% in the full cohort (ITT‐LOCF). Under the WCS model, the proportion of the total initiating population achieving this milestone was significantly lower, further highlighting that effectiveness is conditional upon persistence. Regarding side effects, 325 (59.5%) patients in the adherent cohort reported at least one side effect versus 2086 (38.4%) in the full cohort with a chi‐square test revealing that this difference was statistically significant (X 2(1, n = 4535) = 48.18, p < 0.001). In both cohorts, roughly 4% of side effects were reported as severe. A WCS sensitivity analysis was performed on the full cohort outcomes to assess the impact of the LOCF imputation. Under the WCS assumption—where all patients with missing final weight data were imputed as having 0% weight loss—the mean 6‐month weight loss for the full cohort was 3.43% (±6.10) (vs. 8.26% using LOCF). For the mean 12‐month weight loss, the mean percentage under WCS was 2.40% (±6.16) (vs. 8.97% using LOCF). These figures represent a conservative lower boundary for the program's overall effectiveness, accounting for potential weight regain in patients who did not persist with the treatment pathway (Figure 2).

Predictors of 12‐Month Persistence

The binary logistic regression model identified several clinical and behavioural predictors significantly associated with the probability of 12‐month program attrition (churn) (SM 1). Reaching a healthy BMI milestone (≤ 25) was the strongest predictor of persistence, significantly reducing the odds of churn (OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.24–0.36, p < 0.001). Similarly, patients who experienced a weight‐loss plateau during the study period exhibited lower odds of attrition (OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.35–0.47, p < 0.001).

Investigators evaluated baseline BMI as a continuous and categorical variable and found no difference in model fit. For clinical interpretability and alignment with guideline thresholds, BMI was subsequently assessed as a categorical variable. Analysis of baseline BMI categories demonstrated a significant protective effect of obesity severity on program persistence up to BMI 45 kg/m2. Compared to the overweight reference group (27–29.99 kg/m2), significantly lower odds of attrition were observed for patients in Obesity Class I (OR = 0.67, p < 0.001), Class II (OR = 0.76, p = 0.023), and Class III (OR = 0.61, p < 0.001). In contrast, patients in the highest BMI category (> 45 kg/m2) did not exhibit a statistically significant difference in attrition risk compared to the reference group (p = 0.140).

Early behavioural engagement demonstrated an activation threshold for persistence; patients who tracked their weight moderately (2–5 logs: OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.58–0.80, p < 0.001) or consistently (6–10 logs: OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.54–0.80, p < 0.001) during the first month had significantly lower odds of churn compared to the baseline tracking group (baseline weight only). Intensive tracking (more than 10 logs) did not provide a statistically significant reduction in churn odds over consistent tracking (OR = 0.85, p = 0.347).

Conversely, a history of previous modern GLP‐1 RA use significantly increased the odds of program attrition (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.24–2.15, p < 0.001). Analysis of clinical complexity revealed a notable threshold effect: while having one (p = 0.131) or two (p = 0.112) comorbidities did not significantly influence attrition compared to those with none, the presence of three or more comorbidities emerged as a primary risk factor, doubling the odds of churn (OR = 2.12, 95% CI: 1.32–3.40, p = 0.005). Age demonstrated a protective effect, where each additional year of age was associated with a slight reduction in the odds of attrition (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p < 0.001). Sex at birth (p = 0.152), ethnicity (p = 0.160), and the reporting of side effects (p = 0.407) were not significant predictors of 12‐month attrition. Factors Associated With Weight Loss

A multiple linear regression model was conducted to identify independent predictors of 12‐month weight‐loss percentage within the adherent sub‐cohort (n = 546). The model significantly predicted weight‐loss outcomes (F(10, 535) = 6.69, p < 0.001); however, the model explained only 11.1% of the variance (Adjusted R 2 = 0.095). This low explanatory power indicates that the majority of variance in real‐world weight loss is driven by factors not captured in this dataset, such as caloric intake, physical activity, and genetic predispositions. Sex at birth was a highly significant predictor, with male patients achieving 5.54% less weight loss than female patients (β = −5.54, p < 0.001); no significant interaction effects were observed between sex and baseline BMI (p = 0.64) or sex and comorbidity count (p = 0.42). High‐intensity early engagement was also associated with superior outcomes, as patients who logged weight more than 10 times in the first month achieved an additional 3.46% weight loss compared to the baseline tracking group (β = 3.46, p = 0.008). Age at consultation demonstrated a small but significant inverse relationship with weight loss outcomes, where each additional year of age was associated with a 0.07% decrease in total weight loss (β = −0.07, p = 0.034). Baseline BMI (p = 0.621), a history of previous modern GLP‐1 RA use (p = 0.236), comorbidity tally (p = 0.339), moderate (2–5 logs, p = 0.921) or consistent (6–10 logs, p = 0.164) early tracking, the experience of a weight‐loss plateau (p = 0.297), and the reporting of side effects (p = 0.326) were not significantly associated with 12‐month weight‐loss percentage in this cohort.

Discussion

This study represents the first large‐scale, 12‐month evaluation of a semaglutide‐supported DWLS in Germany. The primary takeaway of this analysis is that the real‐world clinical impact of the service is fundamentally moderated by high rates of attrition, with only 31.6% of patients maintaining the medication schedule and only 12% satisfying the criteria for the 12‐month per‐protocol analysis. These findings underscore that clinical success within the Juniper DWLS is strictly conditional upon long‐term persistence. While the 15.13% weight loss in the highly unrepresentative PP group aligns with STEP 1 clinical trial results [9], the population‐level outcomes (9.47% LOCF; 2.40% WCS) are comparable to the outcomes observed in recent non‐medicated German DWLS studies of shorter duration, such as the 3.2% reported by Gemesi et al. [19] and the 5.29% by Lautenbach et al. [20]. This suggests that regardless of the pharmacological catalyst provided by semaglutide, the effectiveness of digital weight‐loss interventions remains tethered to the same behavioural and financial barriers that challenge traditional app‐based models.

Selection Bias and the Survivor Cohort

The 12% adherence rate for the final PP analysis necessitates a highly cautious interpretation of effectiveness in this sub‐cohort. The adherent sub‐cohort minority likely represents a ‘positive outlier’ group characterised by higher baseline motivation and fewer socioeconomic barriers. Consequently, the PP mean weight loss (15.13%) should only be viewed as a theoretical ceiling of the intervention for the highly unrepresentative minority who can maintain long‐term persistence. Conversely, the outcomes of the broader initiating population are more accurately reflected in the LOCF and WCS figures.

The substantial divergence between our ITT‐LOCF (9.47%) and WCS (2.40%) estimates highlights the inherent instability of weight‐loss projections when attrition is high. Rather than viewing these as competing figures, we present them as a ‘feasibility range’ that acknowledges the uncertainty of real‐world outcomes. The WCS figure serves as the essential lower bound for population‐level impact, neutralizing the potential MAR fallacies often associated with more complex imputation methods in cohorts with high financial or clinical churn.

This selection bias also extends to external validity. Because the Juniper DWLS operates outside the statutory health insurance (GKV) system, these findings cannot generalise to patients in subsidised settings where financial barriers are removed. In our cohort, persistence may be driven as much by financial capability as by clinical response—a factor that differentiates these real‐world participants from those in controlled, subsidised clinical trials.

The Adherence Challenge and the ‘Boundary of Truth’

The cohort's 31.6% 12‐month medication adherence rate and 12% complete study adherence rate (medication plus final weight data) underscore the significant attrition inherent in unsubsidised, real‐world digital obesity care. This medication adherence rate compares somewhat poorly to the those reported in 12‐month studies of the Juniper Australia and UK DWLSs (34.8% and 40.7%, respectively) [18, 24]. When assessing program adherence success, it could be reasonably argued that these rates should be combined with the percentage of patients who achieve a healthy BMI before reaching their 12‐month medication, given that patients who reach this outcome have a ‘success’ based reason for discontinuation. Under this logic, 39.9% of Juniper Germany patients adhered to their medication schedule or achieved a healthy BMI, versus 46.4% of Juniper UK patients and 42.3% of Juniper Australia patients. The largest gap was observed in the full study adherence rate (medication plus 12‐month weight data submission), with the Juniper Germany cohort recording 12% versus 27% in Juniper UK and 16.1% in the Juniper Australia cohorts, respectively. A likely explanation for this disparity is the mode of DWLS delivery. The UK and Australian programs are delivered via a dedicated smartphone application that integrates seamlessly with Bluetooth‐enabled ‘smart’ scales, allowing for passive, low‐effort data entry [18, 24]. In contrast, the German program currently operates via an asynchronous email‐based platform, requiring patients to manually log into a web portal to enter weights. This additional layer of friction may also manifest as a form of lower general program engagement in the Juniper Germany program and be a factor in its lower medication adherence rate. However, to draw stronger conclusions, future research will need to compare DWLS delivery modes across the same cohort.

The significance of these adherence outcomes is that they define the dependency of clinical success on program persistence. The high efficacy of GLP‐1 RAs in clinical trials can be misleading in a real‐world digital context where the ‘average’ outcome is heavily diluted by attrition. By leading with the WCS analysis, we provide a necessary counter‐perspective to the per‐protocol results, acknowledging the inherent uncertainty of the intervention's population‐level impact. Our use of a WCS sensitivity analysis provides a sobering but necessary counter‐perspective to traditional LOCF reporting. By identifying a conservative lower bound of 2.4% mean weight loss for the total enrolled population, we acknowledge that the high effectiveness of GLP‐1 RAs is inextricably linked to long‐term program persistence. The disparity in weight‐loss outcomes between the adherent cohort, and the LOCF and WCS figures of the full cohort underscores the variability in real world DWLS effectiveness. In the German landscape, where out‐of‐pocket costs are substantial (€356/month), attrition may reflect financial barriers as much as clinical non‐response, a factor that differentiates this cohort from subsidised clinical trial participants. Although investigators did not have access to discontinuation reason data in this study, previous designated adherence studies of the Juniper program have found that program cost accounts for a large proportion of program attrition. This remains a challenge for unsubsidised DWLSs throughout the world and future research should endeavour to identify ways to make continuous obesity care more accessible.

Behavioural Predictors: Early Tracking and Persistence

A central finding of this study is the relationship between early weight‐tracking frequency and program persistence. Our results indicate that moderate (2–5 logs) to consistent (6–10 logs) tracking in the first month significantly reduces the odds of 12‐month churn. Interestingly, this contrasts with recent findings from Juniper's UK and Australian cohorts. In those studies, intensive early tracking was identified as the primary predictor of churn, with authors suggesting that high‐frequency logging may signal ‘weight anxiety’ or unrealistic expectations that lead to early burnout [18, 21].

We hypothesise that this divergence may be influenced by behavioural friction. In the app‐based Juniper UK and AU DWLSs, minimal effort is required for patients to upload weight data—they simply step on their Bluetooth scales and the figure appears on their app in seconds. By contrast, in the high‐friction Juniper Germany DWLS, consistent tracking is likely not a proxy for anxiety, but rather a robust marker of intentional program engagement and commitment to the multidisciplinary process. This distinction is also reflected in the distribution of early weight tracking totals across the two cohorts: whereas less than 5% of patients in the German program tracked more than 10 times in the first month, roughly a quarter of patients in the app‐based Juniper UK program tracked more than 25 times over that period. This suggests that in digital health, the ‘meaning’ of a data point is heavily dependent on the effort required to produce it. In other words, where friction is high, engagement may be a more reliable predictor of long‐term persistence. However, without direct psychological assessment, this interpretation remains speculative and requires dedicated behavioural research.

The only lifestyle‐support measure investigators had access to was health coach message count. Univariable analyses showed no correlation between message count and 12‐month weight loss or persistence, so these measures were not retained in the regression models. Nevertheless, the potential influence of qualitative coaching content and unmeasured lifestyle behaviours cannot be excluded.

Clinical Milestones and Treatment Persistence

The role of the healthy BMI milestone (≤ 25) as the strongest predictor of persistence (OR = 0.29) suggests a powerful psychological feedback loop, a finding mirrored in the Juniper UK 12‐month study [21]. However, this study's data suggests an unexpected observation regarding persistence: both a positive clinical outcome (healthy BMI) and a clinical stall (weight loss plateau; OR = 0.41) were independently associated with a lower risk of churn. A possible explanation for the former observation is that achieving a healthy BMI acts as a positive reinforcement mechanism for these patients. Conversely, plateaus may drive support‐seeking behaviour, leading patients to utilise the MDT as a safety net [25, 26]. It should be stressed, however, that despite adjustment for baseline BMI and comorbidity count, both the healthy BMI and weight‐loss plateau variables are time‐ and engagement‐dependent and therefore may still reflect survivor/time‐in‐program effects. Future DWLS studies might consider soliciting post‐plateau/healthy BMI persistence reason data.

Safety and Tolerability in a Digital Context

The safety profile observed in this study aligns with the established tolerability of semaglutide, though the reporting rates warrant contextualisation. While 59.5% of the adherent cohort reported at least one side effect, the rate was significantly lower in the full cohort (38.4%; X 2 (1, n = 4535) = 48.18, p < 0.001). This disparity likely reflects a ‘surveillance bias’ or engagement effect; adherent patients, by definition, remained in the program longer and interacted more frequently with the digital platform, providing more opportunities to log adverse events. Crucially, the multi‐variable regression confirmed that side effect reporting was not a significant predictor of 12‐month churn (p = 0.407). This suggests that while side effects are common, they are not the primary drivers of program attrition in the German landscape, especially when compared to behavioural and financial factors.

The severity distribution further supports the safety of the digital delivery model. In both cohorts, approximately 96% of reported side effects were classified as mild or moderate. This is consistent with the STEP 1 clinical trial, where gastrointestinal events were frequent but typically transient and manageable during dose titration [9]. The low incidence of severe adverse events (~4%) suggests that the Juniper MDT's reactive guidance and automated clinical auditing effectively manage patient safety asynchronously. However, importantly, these Juniper adverse‐event counts do not constitute comprehensive safety surveillance. Reporting is likely influenced by engagement and survivorship, that is, patients with longer follow‐up have more opportunity to report and disengaged patients may have unreported symptoms. For these reasons the data cannot be used to make definitive claims about the safety of the remote care model. Instead, they describe tolerability as reported within the platform among users who remained engaged.

It is important to emphasise that the Juniper DWLS model relies on remote screening and asynchronous communication, which can limit opportunities for face‐to‐face physical examination, blood‐pressure measurement, and opportunistic clinical assessment. Potential risks include missed or delayed detection of contraindications, suboptimal management of polypharmacy, incomplete assessment of frailty or functional status, and barriers to managing complex comorbid conditions. For these reasons, asynchronous remote models may be less suitable for patients with high clinical complexity, unstable cardiometabolic disease, polypharmacy, or limited digital literacy. It is important to emphasise that the safety data presented here are derived from reactive, patient‐initiated reporting within the digital platform. This methodology is inherently subject to surveillance bias and likely leads to a significant underestimation of the true incidence and severity of adverse events compared to controlled clinical trials. Therefore, the safety profile observed should be interpreted as ‘tolerability among those who remained engaged’ rather than a comprehensive safety assessment of the remote care model. Future research should endeavour to assess whether hybrid care models that integrate intermittent in‐person assessments improve persistence and safety in high‐complexity groups.

Clinical and Demographic Predictors of Persistence

Our analysis of baseline clinical characteristics revealed a significant geographic divergence from previous Juniper cohorts. In the German cohort, we observed a significant protective effect of obesity severity on program persistence. Compared to the overweight reference group, patients in Obesity Class I through III (BMI 30–44.99) were significantly less likely to churn. This trend was not observed in 12‐month Tirzepatide‐supported studies on app‐based Australian and British cohorts, which found that baseline BMI only had a significant (negative) impact on the highest British BMI sub‐group (BMI > 45) [18, 24]. In the higher friction German program, patients with a greater clinical burden of obesity may perceive a greater medical necessity for the service, leading to higher persistence. The trend might reflect the relative scarcity of competitor services; whereas many medicated DWLSs are operating in Australia and the UK, researchers have criticised the obesity treatment access gap in Germany [27].

Furthermore, a complexity ceiling was observed regarding multimorbidity. In our cohort, having three or more comorbidities more than doubled the risk of churn (OR = 2.12, p = 0.002). This is the exact inverse of the Australian study, where patients with ≥ 3 comorbidities exhibited better persistence [18]. This suggests that while severe obesity motivates German patients to stay, high clinical complexity may make the asynchronous, digital‐first model less manageable or insufficient for their multifaceted needs [28].

Demographic disparities also persisted in effectiveness; we observed a significant male weight‐loss gap, with men achieving 5.54% less weight loss than women. However, given that men comprised only 20.8% of the total cohort (and 14.1% (n = 77) the adherent sub‐group), no strong conclusions can be derived from this finding. Future research should, nevertheless, seek to explore the reasons behind low male engagement in DWLSs that has been reported consistently [18, 19, 24]. Furthermore, the increased attrition among prior GLP‐1 RA users (OR = 1.62) and the lack of significance in the most extreme BMI category (> 45, p = 0.140) suggests that “treatment‐experienced” patients and those with the highest clinical hurdles may have lower thresholds for discontinuation if initial trajectories do not meet their complex expectations [29].

It needs to be stressed, however, that these observations are exploratory given the study's retrospective, observational design and should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than causal.

Study Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective, observational nature and the reliance on self‐reported weight data. The investigators acknowledge that the relatively low explanatory power of the regression models limits the interpretability of identified predictors; therefore, observed associations should be viewed as modest correlates rather than strong determinants. While digital self‐reporting is a common pragmatic necessity in real‐world DWLS research, it introduces a specific risk of social desirability bias. Our finding that early tracking frequency predicts 12‐month persistence must be interpreted with caution, as patients with a more favourable early weight‐loss trajectory may be more motivated to log data than those experiencing slower progress. Without automated, objective data capture (e.g., Bluetooth‐connected scales), we cannot definitively conclude that tracking is a causal driver of adherence; it may instead function as a symptom of early treatment response. Crucially, the absence of a parallel control group severely limits causal inference regarding the specific contribution of the DWLS versus the pharmacological effect of semaglutide alone. In a real‐world, self‐funded environment, a simultaneous comparator group is often unavailable as patients self‐select into treatment. Consequently, investigators cannot determine the exact proportion of weight loss attributable to multidisciplinary support versus medication. Furthermore, although the ≥ 10‐order adherence threshold provides a standardised metric for comparison with previous DWLS studies [18, 24], it is a proxy based on medication procurement rather than direct observation of administration. Consequently, we cannot account for ‘primary non‐adherence’ where a patient may have received an order but did not self‐inject the medication. Additionally, the unsubsidised nature of the program introduces selection bias, limiting the generalizability of these findings to the broader German population covered by GKV. The LOCF method, while standard, likely overestimates population‐level success, though our inclusion of the WCS analysis serves to mitigate this. Finally, the unavailability of program discontinuation data and patient income data limits the ability of investigators to accurately assess Juniper Germany's access barriers. Without these data, investigators cannot determine the extent which patient attrition was driven by voluntary discontinuation (e.g., cost, side effects and program dissatisfaction) or involuntary discontinuation (e.g., loss to follow‐up, medical contraindications). This lack of specificity weakens the causal interpretations of our attrition models and highlights the need for prospective ‘exit interview’ data in future real‐world evaluations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that while significant weight loss is achievable for a non‐representative adherent minority, the overall impact of a semaglutide‐supported DWLS in Germany is fundamentally limited by a high program attrition rate. With a population‐level mean weight loss of 2.40% (WCS), clinical success in this model is strictly conditional upon persistence. Thus the key takeaways from this study are the high attrition rates: only 31.6% of patients adhered to the 12‐month medication schedule and only 12% of the full cohort satisfied the medication and data submission criteria for inclusion in the final per‐protocol analysis.

The regression models revealed some interesting persistence trends. Firstly, both clinical success and clinical stalls appeared to serve as powerful, independent drivers of persistence. Investigators tentatively suggested that patients who achieved a healthy BMI and persisted may have viewed the milestone as positive reinforcement whereas those who experienced plateaus may have been more motivated to seek support through their MDT. Furthermore, higher baseline BMI (Obesity Class I–III) was associated with persistence—a finding that contrasts with international cohorts. However, a ‘complexity ceiling’ was observed: while obesity motivated persistence, high multimorbidity (≥ 3 comorbidities) more than doubled the risk of attrition, suggesting that highly complex patients may require more intensive human support than asynchronous digital platforms currently provide.

Finally, this study generated valuable nuance to the question of patient engagement in emerging DWLS literature. Whereas previous studies of the Australian and British cohorts have found that intensive early tracking was the strongest predictor of 12‐month program churn and had a linear relationship, this study found a positive correlation between moderate tracking and persistence. We interpreted this as a product of the different DWLS delivery modes. Whereas in the app‐based AU and UK programs, patients experience very low friction in uploading weight data via Bluetooth scales, Juniper Germany requires patients to open their computers and manually upload weight data. Investigators believe that the higher‐friction nature of the German portal transformed consistent logging into a marker of intentional commitment rather than excessive tracking and progress anxiety. This suggests that in digital health, the predictive value of patient engagement is fundamentally shaped by the friction of the platform and the behavioural cost of participation. Future research should focus on how subsidised access and app‐integrated ecosystems alter the behavioural thresholds required for long‐term clinical success. Investigators should also consider the use of a non‐digital semaglutide‐only comparator group to isolate the effect of the DWLS. The absence of this comparator severely limited causal inference in this study.
