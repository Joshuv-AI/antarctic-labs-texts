Penetrating cervical spine injuries caused by nail guns are uncommon.1,2 In penetrating neck trauma, the apparent external wound may underestimate the true depth and trajectory of injury.3 Associated esophageal injury is particularly important in anterior cervical trauma because delayed recognition may result in major local or deep infectious complications.4,5 We report a case of nail gun penetrating cervical spine injury at C4–5 in which the projectile traversed the disc space, entered the vertebral body, and violated the spinal canal, resulting in right-sided weakness and intraoperatively confirmed esophageal perforation. The patient was successfully treated through an anterior cervical approach with favorable early neurological recovery.

Illustrative Case

A 40-year-old man presented to our hospital approximately 4 hours after sustaining a nail gun injury to the anterior neck. On initial evaluation, he had weakness involving the right upper and lower extremities. Neurological examination demonstrated right-sided hemiparesis, with muscle strength graded as 4/5 in both the right upper and right lower extremities. No sensory deficit was documented. No relevant medical, family, psychosocial, or genetic history was reported.

Cervical radiography and CT were performed shortly after presentation. Preoperative cervical radiography demonstrated a metallic foreign body at the C4–5 level. CT further showed that the nail traversed the C4–5 intervertebral disc space, entered the vertebral body, and extended horizontally into the spinal canal (Fig. 1). The external entry site and CT-defined trajectory were relatively midline and did not appear to extend toward the carotid sheath or vertebral artery region. Therefore, the risk of major vascular injury was judged to be low based on the available clinical and CT findings, and preoperative CT angiography was not obtained. Nevertheless, because no dedicated vascular imaging was performed, occult vascular injury could not be definitively excluded. Because direct intraoperative measurement with a ruler was not performed, the nail dimensions were estimated from preoperative radiographs and CT images. The metallic nail was approximately 31 mm in length and 3 mm in diameter. The commercial gauge could not be confirmed because the original nail gun cartridge information was unavailable. The extracted foreign body consisted of the metallic nail and a separated plastic cap.

A: Preoperative cervical radiograph showing a metallic foreign body at the C4–5 level. B: Preoperative CT image showing that the nail traversed the C4–5 disc space, entered the vertebral body, and extended into the spinal canal.

Approximately 14 hours after presentation, corresponding to approximately 18 hours after injury, anterior cervical exploration was undertaken because of the neurological deficit and the CT-defined trajectory across the C4–5 disc space, vertebral body, and spinal canal. A standard anterior cervical approach to the C4–5 level was used. Preoperative CT images were reviewed to understand the trajectory of the nail before removal. Intraoperatively, special care was taken to avoid blind extraction or excessive manipulation of the nail before adequate exposure, because uncontrolled movement could theoretically worsen spinal canal injury or adjacent soft tissue injury.

The nail was identified in situ under direct visualization (Fig. 2). The surrounding soft tissue was carefully exposed, and the nail was removed slowly under direct visualization, with attention to maintaining the original trajectory as much as possible. The separated plastic cap was also retrieved. After removal, the metallic nail and plastic component were separated, but both components were accounted for. No barbs were identified on the nail. The wound tract and adjacent prevertebral soft tissues were copiously irrigated and inspected after foreign body removal. A traumatic anterior dural defect measuring approximately 2.5 mm was identified, with visible CSF egress. Direct suture repair was not feasible because of the small anterior dural defect and the limited operative corridor. Therefore, the defect was managed with an onlay gelatin sponge and fibrin glue sealant. During the same exploration, a 0.5-cm esophageal perforation was identified. Thoracic surgeons were consulted intraoperatively, and the esophageal defect was repaired with primary suture closure. A surgical drain was left in place postoperatively. Postoperative radiography confirmed successful foreign body removal without retained radiopaque material (Fig. 3), and the extracted metallic nail and separated plastic cap are shown in Fig. 4.

Gross photograph of the extracted metallic nail and separated plastic cap after anterior cervical removal. The nail was estimated on preoperative radiographs and CT images to be 31 mm in length and 3 mm in diameter.

Postoperatively, the patient was allowed nothing by mouth, and a nasogastric tube was maintained for 9 days to protect the esophageal repair and reduce local contamination. Cefuroxime was administered postoperatively according to local perioperative practice in the absence of clinical evidence of established infection at presentation. Given the combined esophageal perforation, disc space and vertebral body violation, spinal canal involvement, and traumatic CSF leakage, the patient was closely monitored for meningitis, deep neck infection, discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, and epidural abscess. Although no infectious complication occurred in this patient, broader-spectrum antimicrobial coverage, including coverage for oral and anaerobic flora, may be considered in similar injuries with esophageal contamination and dural involvement. The surgical drain was removed on postoperative day 3. No obvious postoperative CSF leakage was observed. The patient had no fever, signs of meningeal irritation, wound abnormality, or neurological deterioration. Oral intake was resumed after negative esophagography findings. The postoperative course was otherwise uneventful, with no clinical evidence of cervical wound infection, meningitis, deep neck infection, or recurrent esophageal leakage. The patient’s neurological function improved progressively. At the 1-month follow-up, muscle strength in the right upper and lower extremities had returned to grade 5/5.

Immediate postoperative MRI was not obtained because postoperative radiography confirmed complete foreign body removal, the patient remained neurologically stable, and the patient declined additional MRI. Follow-up MRI was performed at 1 year. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient had no fever, neck pain, dysphagia, wound abnormality, meningeal signs, or neurological deterioration, and his motor strength was grade 5/5 in all extremities. Follow-up cervical MRI demonstrated postoperative changes at C4–5 and a small intramedullary T2-hyperintense focus at the C4 level, consistent with myelomalacia (Fig. 5). There was no clinical or imaging evidence of discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, or deep neck infection.

Discussion

Observations

This case is notable because a low-velocity nail gun projectile traversed the C4–5 disc space, entered the vertebral body, and violated the spinal canal while also causing a clinically occult esophageal perforation. Such injuries are rare, and the coexistence of spinal and esophageal injuries substantially increases diagnostic and therapeutic complexity.1,2,4–7

In this patient, radiography established the level of injury, whereas CT precisely delineated the projectile trajectory across the disc space, vertebral body, and spinal canal, which was critical for operative planning. Prior reports of nail gun spinal injuries have likewise emphasized the importance of careful preoperative imaging to define the position and configuration of the retained foreign body.1,2,6,7 More broadly, penetrating neck trauma should not be judged solely by the appearance of the entry wound, because the true trajectory may be considerably deeper and more complex.3

The anterior cervical approach was appropriate in this patient for several reasons. First, it allowed direct visualization and controlled removal of the penetrating foreign body. Second, it permitted assessment of the injury tract and adjacent soft tissues. Third, the same approach enabled immediate recognition and repair of the esophageal perforation, underscoring the value of multidisciplinary intraoperative management when aerodigestive injury is suspected or encountered.4,5

The combined esophageal perforation and traumatic durotomy with CSF leakage raised concern for contamination of the prevertebral soft tissues, C4–5 disc space, vertebral body, spinal canal, and CSF. In traumatic esophageal injury, early recognition, primary repair when feasible, drainage or decompression when indicated, delayed oral intake, and antibiotic therapy are important management components.4,5 In this case, infection prevention included copious wound irrigation, coverage of the dural defect with gelatin sponge and fibrin glue, primary esophageal repair, postoperative nasogastric decompression, delayed oral intake after negative esophagography findings, surgical drainage, and administration of cefuroxime according to local perioperative practice. Because the nail traversed the esophagus, disc space, vertebral body, spinal canal, and dura, delayed meningitis, discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, and deep neck infection were considered potential complications. At 1 year, the patient had no fever, neck pain, dysphagia, wound abnormality, meningeal signs, or neurological deterioration, and motor strength was grade 5/5 in all extremities. Follow-up MRI showed a small C4 intramedullary T2-hyperintense focus consistent with myelomalacia, without clinical or imaging findings suggestive of delayed infection. Long-term follow-up was planned to include serial neurological examinations; assessment for fever, neck pain, dysphagia, wound changes, recurrent esophageal symptoms, or meningeal signs; laboratory surveillance with inflammatory markers when clinically indicated; and repeat cervical imaging if delayed infection, spinal instability, or neurological deterioration was suspected.

Lessons

Anterior cervical nail gun injury may simultaneously cause spinal canal violation, traumatic CSF leakage, neurological deficit, and occult esophageal perforation. CT-based trajectory assessment is essential because the external wound may underestimate the true depth and complexity of injury. Controlled anterior exploration can allow safe foreign body removal, management of dural and esophageal injuries, and source control when contamination is suspected. When the trajectory involves the esophagus, disc space, vertebral body, spinal canal, and dura, long-term surveillance for delayed meningitis, discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis, epidural abscess, and deep neck infection is warranted.

This report has several limitations. First, although 1-year clinical and MRI follow-up was available, longer follow-up is needed to evaluate very late infectious complications and long-term spinal stability. Second, preoperative CT angiography was not performed. Although the entry site and CT-defined trajectory were relatively midline and did not appear to involve the carotid sheath or vertebral artery region, dedicated vascular imaging would have provided a more definitive evaluation for occult vascular injury. In similar penetrating cervical spine injuries, particularly when the trajectory is close to the vertebral artery or other major cervical vessels, preoperative vascular imaging should be considered.1,7

Nail gun injury to the anterior cervical spine may simultaneously cause spinal canal violation, neurological deficit, traumatic CSF leakage, and occult esophageal perforation.1,2,4,6,7 CT-based trajectory assessment is critical because the depth and complexity of injury may not be predicted from the external wound alone.2,6,7 When the trajectory involves the disc space, vertebral body, spinal canal, dura, and esophagus, controlled anterior exploration with multidisciplinary support can allow safe foreign body removal, management of dural and esophageal injuries, source control for contamination, and postoperative surveillance for delayed infectious complications.4,5
