New Fourth-Order Grayscale Indicator-Based Telegraph Diffusion Model for Image Despeckling Corresponding author: Rajendra K. Ray, rajendra@iitmandi.ac.in
Rajendra K. Ray, Manish Kumar

Abstract
Second-order PDE models have been widely used for suppressing multiplicative noise, but they often introduce blocky artifacts in the early stages of denoising. To resolve this, we propose a fourth-order nonlinear PDE model that integrates diffusion and wave properties. The diffusion process, guided by both the Laplacian and intensity values, reduces noise better than gradient-based methods, while the wave part keeps fine details and textures. The effectiveness of the proposed model is evaluated against two second-order anisotropic diffusion approaches using the Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Mean Structural Similarity Index (MSSIM) for images with available ground truth. For SAR images, where a noise-free reference is unavailable, the Speckle Index (SI) is used to measure noise reduction. Additionally, we extend the proposed model to study color images by applying the denoising process independently to each channel, preserving both structure and color consistency. The same quantitative metrics PSNR and MSSIM are used for performance evaluation, ensuring a fair comparison across grayscale and color images. In all the cases, our computed results produce better results compared to existing models in this genre.



1
Introduction

Image restoration plays a crucial role in image processing by reconstructing original images from noisy observations. In real-world applications, noise can degrade images in additive, multiplicative, or mixed forms, making denoising an essential preprocessing step for tasks like segmentation and pattern recognition. This study focuses on multiplicative speckle noise, a common issue in synthetic aperture radar (SAR) and ultrasound imaging. The multiplicative noise is mathematically represented as:

N=I\eta,

(1)

where
I
is the original image,
\eta
denotes noise, and
N
is the noisy image observed. In the case of multiplicative speckle noise,
\eta
follows a gamma distribution with mean 1 and variance
1/L
, where
L
represents the number of observations:

p(\eta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\Gamma(L)}L^{L}\eta^{L-1}e^{-L\eta},&\eta>0,\\
0,&\eta=0.\end{cases}

(2)

Unlike additive noise, multiplicative noise varies with intensity, making brighter regions more vulnerable to degradation. This complexity makes speckle noise reduction particularly challenging. Over the years, variational and PDE-based methods have been extensively explored to address image denoising

. The AA model by Aubert and Aujol
[

introduced a regularization-based approach, but its non-convex nature posed computational difficulties. To resolve this, Shi and Osher
[
2
]
transformed the multiplicative noise into an additive form using logarithmic functions, leading to a globally convex model. Further advancements, such as the novel total variation model (NTV)
[
9
]
and the convex variational model (CV)
[
11
]
, improved edge preservation and denoising efficiency. In PDE-based methods, Zhou et al.
[
4
]
introduced a Doubly Degenerate (DD) diffusion model, which used gray-level indicators in the diffusion coefficient but lacked well-posedness. Later, Shan et al.
[
17
]
refined this model to ensure stability. Recent approaches have combined diffusion and wave dynamics to better preserve high-frequency details while reducing noise. However, second-order PDEs tend to produce blocky artifacts in denoised images. Majee et al.
[
26
]
have proposed effective methods for color image denoising, addressing both additive Gaussian noise and multiplicative speckle noise. Their approaches demonstrate significant improvements in noise reduction while preserving essential image details. More recently, Roy et al.
[
25
]
developed a Bayesian MAP framework integrating total variation regularization to effectively model speckle noise while preserving edges. Li et al.
[
24
]
proposed a hybrid PDE model, blending total variation filtering with anisotropic diffusion, achieving better despeckling performance in SAR images. Cuomo et al.
[
23

introduced a learned variational model leveraging deep learning to suppress adaptive noise in ultrasound and satellite imaging. Building on these developments, we propose a novel fourth-order hyperbolic-parabolic PDE model designed specifically for multiplicative noise suppression in SAR images. Our approach balances noise removal and texture preservation, retains fine image details while significantly reduces noise.

1.1
Diffusion Models

Perona and Malik
[
16
]
introduced the first anisotropic diffusion model in 1990 to address Gaussian additive noise. The governing equation of the Perona-Malik model is given by:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=\text{div}\left(C(|\nabla I|)\nabla I\right),

(3)

where
I
represents the image,
t
denotes time, and
C(|\nabla I|)
is the diffusion coefficient that depends on the gradient magnitude. This function, which is smooth and non-increasing, satisfies
C(0)=1
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}C(x)=0
. The model effectively preserves edges but may retain or amplify high-frequency noise, limiting its denoising capabilities. To enhance its performance, several extensions have been proposed. Dong et al.
[
11
]
highlighted the significance of image brightness for multiplicative noise reduction. Zhou et al.
[
4
]
introduced a Doubly Degenerate (DD) model that incorporates both grayscale indicators and gradients, improving image denoising compared to purely gradient-based models. The governing equation is:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=\text{div}\left(C(I,|\nabla I|)\nabla I\right),\quad\Omega_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T),

(4)

with the boundary and initial conditions:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{T}=\partial\Omega\times(0,T),

(5)

I(a,0)=f(a),\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(6)

Here,
C(I,|\nabla I|)
incorporates intensity variations,
\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}
denotes the normal derivative, and
f(a)
represents the noisy image. The diffusion coefficient is given by:

C(I,|\nabla I|)=\frac{2|I|^{\alpha}}{M^{\alpha}+|I|^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+|\nabla I|^{2})^{(1-\beta)/2}},

(7)

where
\beta
regulates gradient sensitivity. Zhou et al.
[
19
]
later refined this model with the ZZDB model, written as:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=\text{div}\left(\frac{\nabla I}{1+\left(\frac{|\nabla I_{\xi}|}{K}\right)^{\beta(I)}}\right),\quad\text{in }\Omega_{T},

(8)

where
\beta(I)
dynamically adjusts based on intensity variations:

\beta(I)=2-\frac{2|I|^{\alpha}}{M^{\alpha}+|I|^{\alpha}}.

(9)

subject to the boundary and initial conditions:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{T},

(10)

\displaystyle I(a,0)

\displaystyle=f(a),\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(11)

Shan et al.
[
17
]
introduced a regularized version of the DD model to ensure well-posedness:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(C(I_{\xi},|\nabla I_{\xi}|)\nabla I\right),

(12)

with boundary and initial conditions:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{T},

(13)

I(a,0)=f(a),\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(14)

where
I_{\xi}
is a Gaussian-smoothed image, and the diffusion coefficient is defined as:

C(I_{\xi},|\nabla I_{\xi}|)=\left(\frac{I_{\xi}}{M_{\xi}}\right)^{\alpha}\cdot\frac{1}{1+|\nabla I_{\xi}|^{\nu}}.

(15)

To further enhance despeckling performance, Majee et al.
[
12
]
proposed the TDE model, which combines the diffusion and wave equations. The governing equation for the TDE model is:

\frac{\partial^{2}I}{\partial t^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(C(I_{\xi},|\nabla I_{\xi}|)\nabla I\right),

(16)

where
\gamma
regulates wave behavior. The initial and boundary conditions are:

I(a,0)=f(a),\quad\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}(a,0)=0,\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(17)

\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}=0,\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{T}.

(18)

The diffusion coefficient in the TDE model is expressed as:

C(I_{\xi},|\nabla I_{\xi}|)=\frac{2|I_{\xi}|^{\alpha}}{M_{\xi}^{\alpha}+|I_{\xi}|^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+(|\nabla I_{\xi}|/k)^{2}},

(19)

where
M_{\xi}=\max_{x\in\Omega}|I_{\xi}(x,t)|
,
\alpha
is a parameter that regulates intensity adaptation, and
k
controls the gradient-based diffusion. The inclusion of wave dynamics in the TDE model helps in maintaining fine details and providing improved image restoration results. Higher-order PDEs have been explored to overcome the limitations of second-order models. You and Kaveh
[
18
]
replaced the gradient with the Laplacian, resulting in the fourth-order equation:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=-\Delta\left(C(\Delta I)\Delta I\right),

(20)

with boundary and initial conditions:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}=0,\frac{\partial(\Delta I)}{\partial n}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{T},

(21)

I(a,0)=f(a),\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(22)

where the diffusion coefficient is:

C(|\Delta I|)=\frac{1}{1+(|\Delta I|/k)^{2}}.

(23)

This approach improves noise reduction while preserving edges. To further refine speckle noise removal, an energy-based approach was introduced:

\arg\min E(I)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla^{2}I|\,d\Omega+\lambda\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{I-f}{I}\right)^{2}d\Omega.

(24)

Applying Euler-Lagrange minimization results in the fourth-order PDE:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=-\nabla^{2}\left(C(\nabla^{2}I)\nabla^{2}I\right)-\lambda\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{I-f}{I}\right)^{2},

(25)

subject to boundary and initial conditions:

\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}=0,\frac{\partial(\Delta I)}{\partial n}=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{T},

(26)

I(a,0)=f(a),\quad\text{in }\Omega.

(27)

We propose a novel fourth-order nonlinear PDE model tailored for general multiplicative speckle noise, achieving superior results in real SAR images.

2
Proposed Model

We propose a nonlinear fourth-order model that effectively integrates wave dynamics and diffusion to balance noise reduction with structure preservation. The governing equation is:

\frac{\partial^{2}I}{\partial t^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=-\Delta\left(C(I_{\xi},|\Delta I_{\xi}|)\Delta I\right)-\lambda\left(\frac{I-f}{I}\right)^{2},\quad\forall(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),

(28)

with initial and boundary conditions:

I(a,0)=f(a),\quad I_{t}(a,0)=0,\quad\forall x\in\Omega,

(29)

\frac{\partial I}{\partial n}=0,\quad\frac{\partial(\Delta I)}{\partial n}=0,\quad\forall(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

Here,
f
is the noisy image, and
t
represents time.

Diffusion Coefficient

The diffusion coefficient is defined as:

C(I_{\xi},|\Delta I_{\xi}|)=\frac{2|I_{\xi}|^{\alpha}}{M_{\xi}^{\alpha}+|I_{\xi}|^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+(|\Delta I_{\xi}|/k)^{2}},

(30)

where
M_{\xi}=\max_{x\in\Omega}
|I_{\xi}(x,t)|
represents the maximum intensity, and
\alpha
and
k
are parameters. .The term
\frac{2|I_{\xi}|^{\alpha}}{M_{\xi}^{\alpha}+|I_{\xi}|^{\alpha}}
adapts diffusion to intensity variations, reducing smoothing in regions with significant features to preserve edges. The factor
\frac{1}{1+(|\Delta I_{\xi}|/k)^{2}}
further modulates diffusion based on the Laplacian, slowing diffusion in high-gradient regions. Gaussian smoothing,
I_{\xi}=J_{\xi}\ast I
, stabilizes the model by reducing noise while retaining fine details.

3
Numerical Implementation

3.1 Numerical Scheme

We consider the proposed fourth-order PDE as given in equation (28):

\frac{\partial^{2}I}{\partial t^{2}}+\gamma\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}=-\Delta\left(C(I_{\xi},|\Delta I_{\xi}|)\Delta I\right)-\lambda\left(\frac{I-f}{I}\right)^{2},\quad\forall(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),

(31)

The 2D domain
\Omega
is discretized into a grid with spatial step sizes
\delta x
,
\delta y
, and a temporal step size
\delta t
. Let
I^{n}_{i,j}
represent the numerical approximation of
I
at grid point
(x_{i},y_{j},t_{n})
.

\frac{I^{n+1}_{i,j}-2I^{n}_{i,j}+I^{n-1}_{i,j}}{(\delta t)^{2}}+\gamma\frac{I^{n+1}_{i,j}-I^{n}_{i,j}}{\delta t}=-\Delta\left(C(I^{n}_{\xi,i,j},|\Delta I^{n}_{\xi,i,j}|)\Delta I^{n}_{i,j}\right)-\lambda\left(\frac{I^{n}_{i,j}-f_{i,j}}{I^{n}_{i,j}}\right)^{2}.

Here temporal discretization employs a second-order central difference for the second derivative,

I_{tt}(t_{n})\approx\frac{I^{n+1}_{i,j}-2I^{n}_{i,j}+I^{n-1}_{i,j}}{(\delta t)^{2}},

and a first-order forward difference for the first derivative,

I_{t}(t_{n})\approx\frac{I^{n+1}_{i,j}-I^{n}_{i,j}}{\delta t}.

For spatial derivatives, second-order central differences are used for both the gradient,

\nabla_{x}I^{n}_{i,j}\approx\frac{I^{n}_{i+1,j}-I^{n}_{i-1,j}}{2\delta x},\quad\nabla_{y}I^{n}_{i,j}\approx\frac{I^{n}_{i,j+1}-I^{n}_{i,j-1}}{2\delta y},

and the Laplacian,

\Delta I^{n}_{i,j}\approx\frac{I^{n}_{i+1,j}-2I^{n}_{i,j}+I^{n}_{i-1,j}}{(\delta x)^{2}}+\frac{I^{n}_{i,j+1}-2I^{n}_{i,j}+I^{n}_{i,j-1}}{(\delta y)^{2}}.

The laplacian magnitude(
|\Delta I|
) is computed as:

|\Delta I^{n}_{i,j}|=\sqrt{\left(\Delta_{x}I^{n}_{i,j}\right)^{2}+\left(\Delta_{y}I^{n}_{i,j}\right)^{2}}.

Rearranging terms to solve for
I^{n+1}_{i,j}
, we obtain:

I^{n+1}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{(\delta t)^{2}+\gamma\delta t}\Bigg[2I^{n}_{i,j}-I^{n-1}_{i,j}-(\delta t)^{2}\Delta\left(C(I^{n}_{\xi,i,j},|\Delta I^{n}_{\xi,i,j}|)\Delta I^{n}_{i,j}\right)-(\delta t)^{2}\lambda\left(\frac{I^{n}_{i,j}-f_{i,j}}{I^{n}_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\Bigg].

Here,
\lambda
represents the fidelity term’s weight, ensuring that the solution stays close to the clean image . The discretized fidelity term
\lambda\frac{(I^{n}_{i,j})^{2}-(f_{i,j})^{2}}{(I^{n}_{i,j})^{2}}
gives a balance between preservation of the image’s original features and noise reduction.

For color images, the restoration process is applied separately to each channel (Red, Green, and Blue). Let
I^{n}_{i,j,s}
denote the intensity at pixel
(i,j)
in channel
s
at time step
n
, where
s\in\{R,G,B\}
. The discretized equation for each channel follows:

\frac{I^{n+1}_{i,j,s}-2I^{n}_{i,j,s}+I^{n-1}_{i,j,s}}{(\delta t)^{2}}+\gamma\frac{I^{n+1}_{i,j,s}-I^{n}_{i,j,s}}{\delta t}=-\Delta\left(C(I^{n}_{\xi,i,j,s},|\Delta I^{n}_{\xi,i,j,s}|)\Delta I^{n}_{i,j,s}\right)-\lambda\left(\frac{I^{n}_{i,j,s}-f_{i,j,s}}{I^{n}_{i,j,s}}\right)^{2}.

(32)

Each channel is processed independently using the same numerical scheme as in the grayscale case. Spatial derivatives (
\nabla I,\Delta I
) and the fidelity term are computed separately for each channel. The final restored color image is obtained by merging the individually denoised channels:

I_{\text{denoised}}=\text{cat}(I_{\text{denoised},R},I_{\text{denoised},G},I_{\text{denoised},B}).

(33)

3.2 Numerical Experiments and Performance Evaluation

This section evaluates the proposed model in comparison with existing second-order models using natural grayscale images (Boat, Texture), five images from the BSD68 dataset, and color images (Baboon, Peppers), all corrupted with multiplicative speckle noise at varying intensity levels (
L={1,3,5,10}
). The noise was generated using MATLAB’s
gamrnd
function as
\eta=\text{gamrnd}(L,1/L,A,B)
, where
A\times B
represents the image dimensions.

Experiments were conducted in MATLAB on an Intel Core i7 (3.60 GHz) with 8 GB RAM. The proposed model was evaluated across five images, ensuring consistency by applying the same finite-difference discretization across all methods.

For numerical stability, the explicit scheme adhered to the CFL condition
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
, given by
\tau\leq\frac{h}{\max g(x,t)}
, where
h
is the spatial step size. Time steps of
\tau=0.2
and
\xi=2
were used. The stopping criteria were set to a tolerance of 0.0001 for SAR images, while PSNR served as the convergence criterion for natural images, ensuring optimal restoration quality.

Image Quality Measurement

This section evaluates the proposed model’s performance in noise reduction and edge preservation. For comparison, we use the following test images: a grayscale Boat image of size
292\times 292
and a Texture image of size
512\times 512
(Figure
1
); two color images, namely Baboon of size
500\times 480
and Peppers of size
512\times 512
(Figure
3
); and five images from the BSD68 dataset, each of size
321\times 481
.(Figure
2
) Two widely used metrics, i.e. PSNR and MSSIM, are computed to assess image quality.

For synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images, the Speckle Index is used to measure noise reduction. A higher PSNR and MSSIM indicate better restoration, while a lower SI suggests effective despeckling. The SI is given by:

SI=\frac{\text{S.D.}(I)}{\text{M}(I)},

(34)

where
\text{S.D.}(I)
and
\text{M}(I)
represent the standard deviation and mean of pixel intensities, respectively.

The PSNR is calculated as:

\text{PSNR}=10\cdot\log_{10}\left(\frac{\text{MAX}^{2}}{\text{MSE}}\right),

(35)

where MSE represents the Mean Squared Error, and MAX is the maximum pixel value.

The Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) is defined as:

\text{SSIM}(a,b)=\frac{(2\mu_{a}\mu_{b}+d_{1})(2\sigma_{ab}+d_{2})}{(\mu_{a}^{2}+\mu_{b}^{2}+d_{1})(\sigma_{a}^{2}+\sigma_{b}^{2}+d_{2})},

(36)

where
\mu_{a},\mu_{b}
are mean values,
\sigma_{a}^{2},\sigma_{b}^{2}
are variances, and
\sigma_{ab}
is the covariance. The constants
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
stabilize the calculation. The MSSIM over
N
iterations is:

MSSIM=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}SSIM_{i}.

(37)

For SAR image denoising, we apply specific norm-based criteria to evaluate the convergence of the proposed model. The convergence is generally assessed using the relative error norm and the Speckle Index.
When the ground truth image is unavailable, the relative error norm serves as the stopping criterion. It is given by the formula:

\text{Relative Error}=\frac{\|I^{k+1}-I^{k}\|_{2}}{\|I^{k}\|_{2}}\leq\epsilon,

(38)

where
I^{k}
and
I^{k+1}
are the images in the iterations
k
-th and
(k+1)
-th.
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
\epsilon
is a small positive threshold, often set to
10^{-4}
.This criterion ensures that the algorithm is terminated when the changes between successive iterations become negligibly small. These norm-based criteria ensure the robustness and precision of the proposed model’s performance during the image restoration process.

Conclusion

This work introduces a novel fourth-order telegraph diffusion model designed to address the challenges of multiplicative speckle noise in image denoising. The proposed model effectively balances noise reduction and feature preservation, mitigating common issues such as staircase artifacts that are prevalent in second-order models. Through extensive numerical simulations, the model demonstrates superior performance in maintaining intricate details like contours and textures while efficiently suppressing noise. The results highlight the model’s ability to outperform existing state-of-the-art second order pde based image despeckling, particularly in applications involving noisy SAR images. Future research could focus on developing more efficient numerical methods to reduce computational time without compromising the model’s performance. Overall, the fourth-order PDE model represents a significant advancement in image restoration, offering a robust solution for handling noisy images in various real-world applications.
