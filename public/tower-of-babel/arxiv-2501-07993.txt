The regular singular inverse problem in differential Galois theory
Thomas Serafini, Michael Wibmer

Abstract
We show that every linear algebraic group over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero is the differential Galois group of a regular singular linear differential equation with rational function coefficients. † † footnotetext: Mathematics Subject Classification Codes: 34M50, 12H05. Key words and phrases : Differential Galois theory, regular singular differential equations, Riemann-Hilbert correspondence, free proalgebraic groups.



1
Introduction

Hilbert’s 21st problem, also known as the Riemann-Hilbert problem, asks whether there exists a Fuchsian linear differential equation on the Riemann sphere with prescribed singularities and monodromy group. In general, the answer is no, but J. Plemelj showed that the answer is yes if the Fuchsian requirement is slightly relaxed by allowing one singularity to be regular singular (
[
1
]
). In this article, we generalize Plemelj’s result from the field of complex numbers to an arbitrary algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. As the monodromy group is only defined over the complex numbers, it is replaced with the differential Galois group in our context. This replacement is justified by Schesinger’s density theorem, stating that, for regular singular linear differential equations, the differential Galois group is the Zariski closure of the monodromy group.

Theorem 1.1

(Theorem
2.14
)
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and let
S=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}\}
be a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
with
d+1
elements. Then, for every Zariski closed subgroup
G
of
\textup{GL}_{n,k}
that can be generated by
d
elements (as an algebraic group), there exists an
{n\times n}
-matrix
A
of rational functions over
k
such that the differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
has differential Galois group
G
. Furthermore,
A
can be chosen such that
\partial(y)=Ay
is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
, regular singular at
p_{d+1}
and all poles are contained in
S
.

Recall that
\partial(y)=Ay
is
Fuchsian
at
p\in k
if
A
has at most a pole of order one at
p
and that
\partial(y)=Ay
is
regular singular
at
p
if
\partial(y)=Ay
is Gauge-equivalent to an equation that is Fuchsian at
p
. These definitions extend to the point
p=\infty
via the coordinate transformation
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}
. The differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
is
regular singular
if it is regular singular at every point of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
.

We note that in Theorem
1.1
for
k=\mathbb{C}
, the topological fundamental group of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C})\smallsetminus S
is the free group on
d
generators. Therefore, the monodromy group of
\partial(y)=Ay
can be generated by
d
elements (as an abstract group) and the differential Galois of
\partial(y)=Ay
can be generated by
d
elements (as an algebraic group). Furthermore, for
k=\mathbb{C}
, Theorem
1.1
is well-known. It is essentially the combination of Plemelj’s (weak) solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problem with Schlesinger’s density theorem.

As every linear algebraic group over
k
is the Zariski closure of a finitely generated subgroup and can be embedded into some general linear group, we obtain from Theorem
1.1
:

Corollary 1.2

(Corollary
2.15
)
.

Every linear algebraic group is the differential Galois group of a regular singular differential equation.

Corollary
1.2
generalizes
[
10
, Theorem 4.3]
where it was shown, based on a study of moduli of (scalar) linear differential equations, that Corollary
1.2

holds for a large class of linear algebraic groups, including connected and finite linear algebraic groups and groups whose identity component is semisimple or unipotent.

Corollary
1.2
can be seen as a sharpening of the solution of the inverse problem in differential Galois theory, which states that every linear algebraic group is the differential Galois group of a linear differential equation with rational function coefficients (
[
4
]
). Interestingly, this was first proved for
k=\mathbb{C}
in
[
12
]
via Theorem
1.1
, i.e., by combining Plemelj’s (weak) solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problem and Schlesinger’s density theorem.

Our strategy to prove Theorem
1.1
is to deduce it from the case
k=\mathbb{C}
via a specialization argument, based on a specialization theorem from
[
3
]
. This specialization theorem was used in
[
3
]
to generalize the solution of the inverse problem in a different direction, by showing that the absolute differential Galois group of the rational function field is a free proalgebraic group.

For
k=\mathbb{C}
it is an immediate consequence of the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence that the differential Galois group of the family of all regular singular differential equations with singularities contained in
S
, is the free proalgebraic group on
d
generators (where
|S|=d+1
). It seems natural to expect that this statement is true in full generality, i.e., for any algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic zero. This question was already raised in
[
15
]
and the above theorem can be seen as a small step towards its resolution. Our theorem implies that every linear algebraic group over
k
that is a quotient of the free proalgebraic group on
d
generators also is a quotient of the differential Galois group of the family of all regular singular differential equations with singularities in
S
(Corollary
2.16
).

2
The regular singular inverse problem

Throughout this article
k
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and all fields are assumed to have characteristic zero. All rings are assumed to be commutative. For the sake of clarity, we use
t
for the local variable and
z
for the global variable. In other words, we write
k((t))
for the field of formal Laurent series over
k
and
k(z)
for the field of rational functions over
k
. For brevity, we use the term “algebraic group (over
k
)” for “affine group scheme of finite type (over
k
)”. In other words, all algebraic groups are assumed to be linear.
A “proalgebraic group (over
k
)” is an affine group scheme (over
k
). A “closed subgroup” of a proalgebraic group is a closed subgroup scheme.
For a morphism
R\to R^{\prime}
of rings and a scheme
X
over
R
we denote with
X_{R^{\prime}}
the scheme over
R^{\prime}
obtained from
X
by base change via
R\to R^{\prime}
. For an affine scheme
X
over a ring
R
, we denote with
R[X]
the
R
-algebra representing
X
. In particular, for an algebraic group
G
over
k
, its coordinate ring is denoted with
k[G]
.

In this section we prove the results announced in the introduction. After recalling the basics of differential Galois theory and the theory of regular singular differential equations, we study regular singular differential equations under an extension of the constants and under specialization. These preparatory results are then combined to yield the proof of the main theorem.

2.1
Differential Galois theory

In this section we recall a few key definitions and facts from differential Galois theory. For more background see any of the textbooks
[
13
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
2
]
or
[
9
]
.

Let
(K,\partial)
be a differential field with field of constants
K^{\partial}=\{a\in K|\ \partial(a)=0\}
equal to
k
. A
differential module

(M,\nabla)
over
(K,\partial)
is a finite dimensional
K
-vector space together with an additive map
\nabla\colon M\to M
satisfying
\nabla(am)=\partial(a)m+a\nabla(m)
for
a\in K
and
m\in M
. To a differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
with
A\in K^{n\times n}
we can associate the differential module
(K^{n},\nabla_{A})
, where
\nabla_{A}=\partial-A\colon K^{n}\to K^{n}
is given by
\nabla_{A}(\xi)=\partial(\xi)-A\xi
.

Conversely, if
(M,\nabla)
is a differential module and
\underline{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})
is a
K
-basis of
M
, then
we can write
\nabla(\underline{e})=\underline{e}(-A)
for some matrix
A\in K^{n\times n}
so that

\nabla(\underline{e}\xi)=\underline{e}\partial(\xi)+\nabla(\underline{e})\xi=\underline{e}(\partial(\xi)-A\xi)
for all
\xi\in K^{n}
. Thus, up to the choice of a basis,
(M,\nabla)
is of the form
(K^{n},\nabla_{A})
. The choice of another basis
\underline{e}^{\prime}
, say
\underline{e}=\underline{e}^{\prime}F
with
F\in\textup{GL}_{n}(K)
, leads to another matrix
A^{\prime}\in K^{n\times n}
satisfying

A^{\prime}=FAF^{-1}+\partial(F)F^{-1}

(2.1)

Two matrices
A,A^{\prime}\in K^{n\times n}
are said to be
Gauge equivalent
(over
K
) if there exists a matrix
F\in\textup{GL}_{n}(K)
such that (
2.1
) is satisfied.

Base change for differential modules works as expected: If
(K^{\prime},\partial^{\prime})
is a differential field extension of
(K,\partial)
and
(M,\nabla)
is a differential module over
(K,\partial)
, then
M^{\prime}=M\otimes_{K}K^{\prime}
becomes as differential module over
(K^{\prime},\partial^{\prime})
via
\nabla^{\prime}(m\otimes a)=\nabla(m)\otimes a+m\otimes\partial^{\prime}(a)
for
m\in M
and
a\in K^{\prime}
. If
(M,\nabla)=(K^{n},\nabla_{A})
for some
A\in K^{n\times n}
, then
(M^{\prime},\nabla^{\prime})=(K^{\prime n},\nabla_{A})
. I.e., in terms of differential equations, this construction simply means that a differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
over
K
is considered as a differential equation over
K^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
A\in K^{n\times n}
, a differential
K
-algebra
R
is a
Picard-Vessiot ring
for
\partial(y)=Ay
if

(i)

R
is
\partial
-simple (i.e., it has no
\partial
-stable ideals other than
\{0\}
and
R
),

(ii)

there exists a fundamental solution matrix for
\partial(y)=Ay
in
R
, i.e., there exists
Y\in\textup{GL}_{n}(R)
such that
\partial(Y)=AY
,

(iii)

as a
K
-algebra
R
is generated by the entries of
Y
and the inverse of its determinant, i.e.,
R=K\big[Y_{i,j},\frac{1}{\det(Y)}\big]
.

Given
A\in K^{n\times n}
, there exists a unique (up to
K
-
\partial
-isomorphisms) Picard-Vessiot ring
R/K
for
\partial(y)=Ay
. The
differential Galois group
of
\partial(y)=Ay
or of
R/K
is then defined as the group of differential automorphisms of
R/K
. More formally, we define
\textup{Gal}(R/K)
as the functor, from the category of
k
-algebras to the category of groups, that associates to a
k
-algebra
T
the group of differential automorphisms of
R\otimes_{k}T
over
K\otimes_{k}T
, where
T
is considered as a constant differential ring. The functor
\textup{Gal}(R/K)
is representable by a finitely generated
k
-algebra, i.e.,
\textup{Gal}(R/K)
is an algebraic group (over
k
).

Definition
2.1
and the definition of the differential Galois group can be generalized to (potentially infinite) families
(\partial(y)=A_{i}y)_{i\in I}
of linear differential equations over
K
. For example, for
K=k(z)
and
S
a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
, one can consider the differential Galois group of the family of all regular singular differential equations over
k(z)
with singularities contained in
S
(see Section
2.2
below for precise definitions). In condition (ii) one requires that for every member of the family there exists a fundamental solution matrix in
R
and in condition (iii) one allows all entries and the inverse of the determinant of all fundamental solution matrices.

For families
\textup{Gal}(R/K)
need not be an algebraic group, however, it is still a proalgebraic group (over
k
). Also note that the Picard-Vessiot ring for a family
(\partial(y)=A_{i}y)_{i\in I}
is generated by the Picard-Vessiot rings of its members
\partial(y)=A_{i}y
.

2.2
Regular singular differential equations

In this section we recall the basic definitions regarding regular singular differential equations and observe that the relevant properties are preserved under an extension of the base field
k
. We also establish a key result (Lemma
2.7
) for the proof of our main theorem (Theorem
2.14
): We show that if a matrix
A\in k(t)^{n\times n}
is Gauge equivalent over
k((t))
to a matrix
A^{\prime}
such that
tA^{\prime}\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
, then
A
is already Gauge equivalent over
k(t)
to such a matrix. More background on regular singular differential equations can be found in
[
13
, Sections 5 and 6]
,
[
9
]
or
[
8
, Part I]
.

We first recall the basic definitions regarding formal differential modules, i.e., differential modules over the differential field
(k((t)),\frac{d}{dt})
of formal Laurent series with coefficients in
k
.

A
k[[t]]
-lattice

\Lambda
in a finite dimensional
k((t))
-
vector space
M
is a
k[[t]]
-submodule of the form
\Lambda=k[[t]]e_{1}+\ldots+k[[t]]e_{n}
, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is a
k((t))
-basis of
M
.
A differential module
(M,\nabla)
over
(k((t)),\frac{d}{dt})
is

•

regular
if there exists a
k[[t]]
-lattice
\Lambda
in
M
such that
\nabla(\Lambda)\subseteq\Lambda
;

•

regular singular
if there exists a
k[[t]]
-lattice
\Lambda
in
M
such that
\nabla(\Lambda)\subseteq\frac{1}{t}\Lambda
.

For a matrix
A\in k((t))^{n\times n}
, the differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
is
regular
(or
regular singular
) if the corresponding differential module
(k((t))^{n},\nabla_{A})
is regular (or regular singular). Thus
\partial(y)=Ay
is regular if and only if
A
is Gauge equivalent over
k((t))
to a matrix
A^{\prime}\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
and
\partial(y)=Ay
is regular singular if and only if
A
is Gauge equivalent over
k((t))
to a matrix
A^{\prime}\in k((t))^{n\times n}
such that
tA^{\prime}\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
.

We next discuss our main topic of interest, linear differential equations over
k(z)
, the field of rational functions with coefficients in
k
. Throughout this article we consider
k(z)
as a differential field with respect to the standard derivation
\frac{d}{dz}
.

A differential module
(M,\nabla)
over
k(z)
defines for every
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)=k\cup\{\infty\}
a formal differential module
(M_{p},\nabla_{p})
. In detail: For
p\in k
we set
\widehat{k(z)}^{p}=k((z-p))
. Then, writing
t=z-p
so that
\widehat{k(z)}^{p}=k((t))
, we see that
(k(z),\frac{d}{dz})
is a differential subfield of
(k((t)),\frac{d}{dt})
. We define the differential module
(M_{p},\nabla_{p})
over
\widehat{k(z)}^{p}=k((t))
as the base change of
(M,\nabla)
via the inclusion
k(z)\subseteq\widehat{k(z)}^{p}
.

For
p=\infty
, we set
\widehat{k(z)}^{p}=k((z^{-1}))
. Then, writing
t=z^{-1}
so that
\widehat{k(z)}^{p}=k((t))
, we see that
(k(z),\frac{d}{dz})
is a differential subfield of
(k((t)),-t^{2}\frac{d}{dt})
. Thus, via base change from
(k(z),\frac{d}{dz})
to
(k((t)),-t^{2}\frac{d}{dt})
we obtain from
(M,\nabla)
a differential module
(M_{p},\nabla^{\prime})
over
(k((t)),-t^{2}\frac{d}{dt})
. Set
\nabla_{p}=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}\nabla^{\prime}\colon M_{p}\to M_{p}
. Then
(M_{p},\nabla_{p})
is a differential module over
(k((t)),\frac{d}{dt})
.

A point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is
regular
for
(M,\nabla)
if
(M_{p},\nabla_{p})
is regular. Otherwise it is
singular
. A point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is
regular singular
for
(M,\nabla)
if
(M_{p},\nabla_{p})
is regular singular. Finally,
(M,\nabla)
is
regular singular
if all points
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
are regular singular for
(M,\nabla)
. As regular points are regular singular, this is equivalent to saying that all singular points are regular singular.

For
A\in k(z)^{n\times n}
, a point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is
regular
(or
regular singular
) for
\partial(y)=Ay
if
p
is a regular (or regular singular) point of the corresponding differential module
(k(z)^{n},\nabla_{A})
.
So, explicitly, a point
p\in k
is regular (or regular singular) for
\partial(y)=Ay
if and only if
\partial(y)=A(t+p)y
is regular (or regular singular). On the other hand,
p=\infty
is a regular (or regular singular) point for
\partial(y)=Ay
if and only if
\partial(y)=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})y
is regular (or regular singular).

The
singularities
or
singular points
of
\partial(y)=Ay
are the points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
that are not regular for
\partial(y)=Ay
. A point
p\in k
is a
pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
if it is a pole of one of the entries of
A
. The point
p=\infty
is
a pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
if
t=0
is a pole of
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})
.

If
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is not a pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
, then
A\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
for
t=z-p
if
p\in k
and
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
if
p=\infty
. Therefore,
p
is a regular point for
\partial(y)=Ay
. Thus, the singularities of
\partial(y)=Ay
are poles of
\partial(y)=Ay
. On the other hand, a pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
need not be a singularity of
\partial(y)=Ay
. For example,
p=0
is a regular point for
\partial(y)=\frac{1}{z}y
(with solution
y(z)=z
). Indeed, for
F=\frac{1}{z}
, we have
F\frac{1}{z}F^{-1}+\partial(F)F^{-1}=0
. In the literature, a pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
that is not a singularity of
\partial(y)=Ay
is sometimes referred to as an
apparent singularity
. But note that, according to our terminology, an apparent singularity is not a singularity.

The differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
is
Fuchsian
at
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
if the corresponding matrix has at most a pole of order one, i.e.,
tA(t+p)\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
for
p\in k
or
-\frac{1}{t}A(\frac{1}{t})\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
for
p=\infty
. If
\partial(y)=Ay
is Fuchsian at
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
, then
\partial(y)=Ay
is regular singular at
p
. Conversely, if
\partial(y)=Ay
is regular singular at
p
, there exists a Gauge transformation over
k((t))
leading to a matrix with at most a pole of order one.

The differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
is
regular singular
if all points
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
are regular singular for
\partial(y)=Ay
, i.e., if the differential module
(k(z)^{n},\nabla_{A})
is regular singular.

For
k=\mathbb{C}
, the condition of being regular singular is usually defined via a growth condition on the solutions (see e.g.,
[
9
, Def. 9.11]
or
[
8
, Part I, Def. 1.24]
). However, this growth condition is equivalent to the existence of a local meromorphic gauge transformation yielding a transformed matrix that has at most a pole of order one (
[
9
, Theorem 9.17]
). The existence of such a local meromorphic gauge transformation is equivalent to the existence of a local formal gauge transformation with the same effect (
[
13
, Prop. 3.12 and Lemma 3.42]
). Thus the above definition of a regular singular differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
with
A\in k(z)^{n\times n}
is equivalent to the classical definition for
k=\mathbb{C}
.

The following lemma shows that the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
remains unfazed under an extension of the base field
k
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
k\subseteq k^{\prime}
be an inclusion of algebraically closed fields and
S=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}\}
a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
with
d+1
elements. If
A\in k(z)^{n\times n}
is such that
\partial(y)=Ay
has all poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
, regular singular at
p_{d+1}
and has differential Galois group
G
,
then
\partial(y)=Ay
, considered as a differential equation over
k^{\prime}(z)
, has all poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
, regular singular at
p_{d+1}
and has differential Galois group
G_{k^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

If
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k^{\prime})
is a pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
, then
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
. So the poles of
\partial(y)=Ay
do not depend on whether we consider the equation over
k(z)
or over
k^{\prime}(z)
. If
\partial(y)=Ay
is Fuchsian (or regular singular) at
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
, when considered as a differential equation over
k(x)
, then
\partial(y)=Ay
is also Fuchsian (or regular singular) at
p
, when considered as a differential equation over
k(x)
.

The statement about the differential Galois group follows from
[
3
, Lemma 3.2]
.
∎

Our next goal is to show that if a matrix
A\in k(t)^{n\times n}
is Gauge equivalent over
k((t))
to a matrix
A^{\prime}
such that
tA^{\prime}\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
, then
A
is already Gauge equivalent over
k(t)
to such a matrix.
For the sake of clarity of the exposition, we separate the proof into a series of lemmas.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\Lambda\subseteq k((t))^{n}
is a
k[[t]]
-lattice and
E
is a finite subset of
k((t))^{n}
, then there exists a non-zero
h\in k((t))
such that
he\in\Lambda
for all
e\in E
.

Proof.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be a
k((t))
-basis of
k((t))^{n}
such that
\Lambda=k[[t]]e_{1}+\ldots+k[[t]]e_{n}
. Fix
e\in E
and write
e=h_{1}e_{1}+...+h_{n}e_{n}
with
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in k((t))
. Let
m_{e}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
t^{m_{e}}h_{i}\in k[[t]]
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then
t^{m_{e}}e\in\Lambda
. Let
m
be the maximum of all
m_{e}
’s. Then
h=t^{m}
has the required property.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(M,\nabla)
be a differential module over
(k((t)),\frac{d}{dt})
and
\Lambda\subseteq M
a
k[[t]]
-lattice such that
\nabla(\Lambda)\subseteq\frac{1}{t}\Lambda
. Then
h\Lambda\subseteq M
is a
k[[t]]
-lattice with
\nabla(h\Lambda)\subseteq\frac{1}{t}h\Lambda

for any non-zero
h\in k((t))
.

Proof.

Clearly
h\Lambda
is a
k[[t]]
-lattice in
M
. Furthermore,
\nabla(h\Lambda)\subseteq\frac{d}{dt}(h)\Lambda+h\nabla(\Lambda)\subseteq\frac{d}{dt}(h)\Lambda+\frac{1}{t}h\Lambda
. So it suffices to show that
\frac{d}{dt}(h)\Lambda\subseteq\frac{1}{t}h\Lambda
. But
\frac{t\frac{d}{dt}(h)}{h}\in k[[t]]
because the order of
t\frac{d}{dt}(h)
is the order of
h
unless
h\in k
. Thus
\frac{d}{dt}(h)\Lambda=\frac{h}{t}\frac{t\frac{d}{dt}(h)}{h}\Lambda\subseteq\frac{h}{t}\Lambda=\frac{1}{t}h\Lambda
.
∎

As usual, we denote with
k[t]_{(t)}
the localization of the ring
k[t]
at the prime ideal
(t)
, i.e.,
k[t]_{(t)}=\left\{\frac{h_{1}}{h_{2}}|\ h_{1},h_{2}\in k[t],\ t\text{ does not divide }h_{2}\right\}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
be a
k(t)
-basis of
k(t)^{n}
. For
\Lambda=k[[t]]e_{1}+\ldots+k[[t]]e_{n}\subseteq k((t))^{n}
we have

Proof.

The inclusion

follows from

.
For the reverse inclusion, consider

. As

is a

-basis of

, we have

for uniquely determined

. As

, it follows that

and as

is follows that

Recall that the condition of being regular singular is characterized via local formal gauge transformations. The following lemma shows that in fact it is enough to consider rational gauge transformations. We believe this lemma is well-known but we could not find a suitable reference. For statements of a similar spirit in the analytic context see

Lemma 2.7
.

Let

. Then there

satisfies

to a matrix

is already Gauge equivalent over

to such a matrix.

Proof.

As
\partial(y)=Ay
is regular singular at

-lattice

in

such that
\nabla_{A}(\Lambda)\subseteq\frac{1}{t}\Lambda
, where
\nabla_{A}=\frac{d}{dt}-A
.

Let

be the standard basis of

. By Lemma

there exists a non-zero

such that

for

. Then

is a

-lattice in

with

(Lemma

) and

.
Replacing

Let

be the

generated by all

, and

Recall (e.g. from

, Chapter III, Theorem 7.1]

as a

shows that we can select

2.3
Generating algebraic groups

In this short section we consider algebraic groups generated by finitely many elements and how this property behaves under base change.

Let
G
be an algebraic group over
k
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in G(k)
. The intersection of all closed subgroups
H
of
G
such that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in H(k)
, is a closed subgroup of
G
that also satisfies this property. Thus there exists a smallest closed subgroup of
G
whose
k
-points contain
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
. If this closed subgroup agrees with
G
, we say that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}

generated
G
(as an algebraic group)
.

Let
\Gamma
be the (abstract) subgroup of
G(k)
generated by
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
. As the Zariski closure of
\Gamma
is a subgroup of
G(k)
(
[
11
, Lemma 2.2.4]
), we see that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G
if and only if
\Gamma
is Zariski dense in
G
. This means that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G
if and only if
f(\gamma)=0
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
implies
f=0
for
f\in k[G]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
k\subseteq k^{\prime}
be an inclusion of algebraically closed fields,
G
an algebraic group over
k
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in G(k)
. Then

g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G
if and only if
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G_{k^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be the (abstract) subgroup of
G(k)\subseteq G(k^{\prime})=G_{k^{\prime}}(k^{\prime})
generated by
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
.
First assume that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G
. We have to show that
f^{\prime}(\gamma)=0
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
implies
f^{\prime}=0
for
f^{\prime}\in k^{\prime}[G_{k^{\prime}}]=k[G]\otimes_{k}k^{\prime}
. Let
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}
be
k
-basis of
k^{\prime}
. Then
f^{\prime}
can be written as
f^{\prime}=\sum f_{i}\otimes\lambda_{i}
with
f_{i}\in k[G]
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we have
0=f^{\prime}(\gamma)=\sum f_{i}(\gamma)\lambda_{i}
. As the
\lambda_{i}
are
k
-linearly independent and
f_{i}(\gamma_{i})\in k
, it follows that
f_{i}(\gamma)=0
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and

. Because

generate
G
, this implies that

. So

as desired.

Conversely, assume that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G_{k^{\prime}}
. Then an

that vanishes on all of

must be zero. In particular, this holds for

and therefore
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G
.
∎

The proof strategy for our main result is to deduce it from the already known special case

We begin by recalling some terminology from

the poles in
k
of the differential equation
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
are contained in
S
.

For a non-zero rational function
h\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]_{f}\subseteq k^{\prime}(z)
, when written as
h=\frac{h_{1}}{f^{m}}
, with
h_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]
, the rational function
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}h(\frac{1}{t})\in k^{\prime}(t)
has a pole at
t=0
if and only if
\deg(h_{1})-m(d+e)+2>0
. Thus, if
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}h(\frac{1}{t})
does not have a pole at
t=0
, for any specialization
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{0},k)
, the rational function
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}h^{c}(\frac{1}{t})
does not have a pole at
t=0
. So, as
p=\infty
is not a pole of
\partial(y)=Ay
,

p=\infty
is also not a pole of
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
for any
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{0},k)
. In summary, for any
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{0},k)
, the poles of
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
are contained in
S
.

The differential equation
\partial(y)=A(t+p_{d+1})y
is regular singular. Thus, by Lemma
2.12
, there exists a finitely generated
k
-subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
of
k^{\prime}
and a monic polynomial
f_{1}\in\mathcal{B}[t]
such that
A(t+p_{d+1})\in\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}^{n\times n}
and
\partial(y)=A(t+p_{d+1})^{c}y
is regular singular for all
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B},k)
. Enlarging
\mathcal{B}
if necessary, we may assume that
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. Furthermore, replacing
f_{1}\in\mathcal{B}[t]
by
f_{1}(t)f(t+p_{d+1})\in\mathcal{B}[t]
, we may assume that
f(t+p_{d+1})
divides
f_{1}
in
\mathcal{B}[t]
.
We claim that
\mathcal{B}
and
f\in\mathcal{B}[z]
have the required properties.

Let
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B},k)
. Note that
\mathcal{B}_{0}[t+p_{d+1}]_{f(t+p_{d+1})}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}
and
h(t+p_{d+1})^{c}=h^{c}(t+p_{d+1})
for
h\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]_{f}
. So
A(t+p_{d+1})^{c}=A^{c}(t+p_{d+1})
.
As
\partial(y)=A(t+p_{d+1})^{c}y
is regular singular, this shows that
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
is a regular singular at
p_{d+1}
. As explained in the second paragraph of this proof, the poles of
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
are contained in
S
.
Finally, from
(z-p_{1})\ldots(z_{1}-p_{d})(z-p_{d+1})^{e}A\in\mathcal{B}[z]
, we obtain
(z-p_{1})\ldots(z_{1}-p_{d})(z-p_{d+1})^{e}A^{c}\in k[z]
, showing that
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
.

In case
\infty\in S
, a slight modification of the above proof yields the result. In detail, if
\infty
is among the Fuchsian points, say
p_{1}=\infty
, then, with
f=(z-p_{2})\ldots(z-p_{d})(z-p_{d+1})^{e}\in k[z]
, we can proceed as above, noting that
-\frac{1}{t}A(\frac{1}{t})\in k^{\prime}[[t]]^{n\times n}
implies
-\frac{1}{t}A^{c}(\frac{1}{t})\in k[[t]]^{n\times n}
for any
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{0},k)
(cf. the second paragraph of this proof).

If
\infty
is only regular singular, i.e.,
p_{d+1}=\infty
, then, with
f=(z-p_{1})\ldots(z-p_{d})\in k[z]
we can proceed similarly to the above, but using that, as
\partial(y)=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})y
is regular singular, there exists, by Lemma
2.12
, a finitely generated
k
-subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
of
k^{\prime}
and a monic polynomial
f_{1}\in\mathcal{B}[t]
such that
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})\in\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}^{n\times n}
and
\partial(y)=(-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t}))^{c}y
is regular singular for every
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B},k)
. Enlarging
\mathcal{B}
if necessary, we can assume that
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. Writing
f(\frac{1}{t})=\frac{cf_{2}}{t^{m}}
with
c\in k
non-zero and
f_{2}\in k[t]
monic and replacing
f_{1}
by
f_{1}tf_{2}
, we can assume that
t
and
f_{2}
divide
f_{1}
in
\mathcal{B}[t]
. As
\mathcal{B}_{0}[\frac{1}{t}]\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{0}[t]_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}
and
\frac{1}{f(\frac{1}{t})}=\frac{c^{-1}t^{m}}{f_{2}}\in\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}
, we see that
\mathcal{B}_{0}[\frac{1}{t}]_{f(\frac{1}{t})}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}
. Furthermore, for
h\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]_{f}
, we have
-\frac{1}{t^{2}}h(\frac{1}{t})\in\mathcal{B}[t]_{f_{1}}
and
\left(-\frac{1}{t^{2}}h(\frac{1}{t})\right)^{c}=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}h^{c}(t)
for any
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B},k)
. Thus
\left(-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})\right)^{c}=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})^{c}
and
\partial(y)=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}A(\frac{1}{t})^{c}y
is regular singular, i.e.,
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
is regular singular at
\infty
for every
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k}(\mathcal{B},k)
. It follows that
\mathcal{B}
and
f
have the required properties.
∎

2.5
Proof of the main result

We are now prepared to prove our main theorem.

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
\textup{GL}_{n,k}
that can be generated by
d
elements and let
S=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}\}
be a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
with
d+1
elements. Then there exists a matrix
A\in k(z)^{n\times n}
such that the differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
has all poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
, regular singular at
p_{d+1}
and has differential Galois group
G
.

Proof.

We roughly follow the short solution to the inverse problem from
[
3
, Section 5.2]
. The algebraic group
G
(together with the closed embedding into
\textup{GL}_{n,k}
) descends to a finitely generated field
k_{0}
, i.e., there exist a subfield
k_{0}
of
k
, finitely generated over
\mathbb{Q}
, and a closed subgroup
G_{0}
of
\textup{GL}_{n,k_{0}}
such that
(G_{0})_{k}=G
(as closed subgroups of
\textup{GL}_{n,k}
).

Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in G(k)
generate
G
. Enlarging
k_{0}
if necessary, we can assume that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in G_{0}(k_{0})\subseteq G_{0}(k)=G(k)
and
S\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{1}(k_{0})
.

Let
k_{1}\subseteq k
be the algebraic closure of
k_{0}
and set
G_{1}=(G_{0})_{k_{1}}
. Then
G_{1}
is a closed subgroup of
\textup{GL}_{n,k_{1}}
. As
k_{1}
is countable, there exists an embedding
k_{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
(that we fix).

Because
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\in G_{0}(k_{0})\subseteq G_{0}(k_{1})=G_{1}(k_{1})
generate
G=(G_{1})_{k}
, it follows from Lemma
2.8
that
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
generate
G_{1}
. The same lemma, but applied in the reverse direction, shows that
(G_{1})_{\mathbb{C}}
is generated by
g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
. In particular,
(G_{1})_{\mathbb{C}}
is a closed subgroup of
\textup{GL}_{n,\mathbb{C}}
that can be generated by
d
elements.

As noted in the introduction, Theorem
2.14
holds for
k=\mathbb{C}
as a consequence of the (weak) solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problem (see
[
1
, Theorem 3.2.1]
,
[
9
, Theorem 12.4]
or
[
8
, Part I, Theorem 4.49]
) and Schlesinger’s density theorem (see e.g.,
[
8
, Part I, Theorem 2.28]
).
Thus, there exists a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}(z)^{n\times n}
such that
\partial(y)=Ay
has poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
, regular singular at
p_{d+1}
and has differential Galois group
(G_{1})_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Applying Lemma
2.13
to the inclusion
k_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and the differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
yields a finitely generated
k_{1}
-subalgebra
\mathcal{B}_{0}
of
\mathbb{C}
and a monic
f_{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]
with
A\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]_{f_{0}}^{n\times n}
such that
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
has all poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
and regular singular at
p_{d+1}
for all
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k_{1}}(\mathcal{B}_{0},k_{1})
. By Lemma
2.11
, there exists a finitely generated
k_{1}
-subalgebra
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{C}
containing
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, a monic polynomial
f\in\mathcal{B}[z]
such that
f_{0}
divides
f
, and a differential
(G_{1})_{\mathcal{B}}
-
torsor
\mathcal{R}/\mathcal{B}[z]_{f}
for
\partial(y)=Ay
such that
\mathcal{R}
is flat over
\mathcal{B}[z]_{f}
and
\mathcal{R}\otimes_{\mathcal{B}[z]_{f}}K(z)
is
\partial
-simple, where
K
is the algebraic closure of the field of fractions of
\mathcal{B}
.
By Theorem
2.10
, there exists a
c\in\textup{Hom}_{k_{1}}(\mathcal{B},k_{1})
such that
R^{c}=\mathcal{R}\otimes_{\mathcal{B}[z]_{f}}k_{1}(z)
is a Picard-Vessiot ring for
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
(over
k_{1}(z)
) with differential Galois group
((G_{1})_{\mathcal{B}})_{k_{1}}=G_{1}
.
By construction of
\mathcal{B}_{0}
and
f_{0}
, and using that
A\in\mathcal{B}_{0}[z]_{f_{0}}^{n\times n}\subseteq\mathcal{B}[z]_{f}^{n\times n}
, we see that
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
has all poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
and regular singular at
p_{d+1}
.

Finally, by Lemma
2.2
, the equation
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
, considered as a differential equation over
k(z)
, has the required properties, i.e.,
\partial(y)=A^{c}y
(over
k(z)
) has all poles contained in
S
, is Fuchsian at
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}
, regular singular at
p_{d+1}
and has differential Galois group
(G_{1})_{k}=G
. ∎

Corollary 2.15
.

Any algebraic group over
k
is the differential Galois group of a regular singular differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
over
k(z)
.

Proof.

As any (affine) algebraic group is isomorphic to a closed subgroup of some general linear group (
[
7
, Cor. 4.10]
) and every algebraic group (in characteristic zero) is finitely generated (
[
13
]
, Lemma 5.13), this follows from Theorem
2.14
.
∎

2.6
An interpretation in terms of proalgebraic groups

In this final section we offer an interpretation of our main result (Theorem
2.14
) in terms of proalgebraic groups.

Recall that the
free proalgebraic group

\Gamma_{d}
on
d
generators (over
k
) is characterized by the following universal property of the map
\iota\colon X\to\Gamma_{d}(k)
from a
d
-element set
X
into the
k
-points of
\Gamma_{d}
: For every proalgebraic group
G
and every map
\varphi\colon X\to G(k)
there exists a unique morphism
\phi\colon\Gamma_{d}\to G
of proalgebraic groups such that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma_{d}(k)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G(k)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

commutes. The proalgebraic group
\Gamma_{d}
can be constructed as the fundamental group of the neutral tannakian category of all (finite dimensional,
k
-linear) representations of the (abstract) free group
F_{d}
on
d
generators. In other words,
\Gamma_{d}
is the proalgebraic completion (or proalgebraic hull) of
F_{d}
. (See
[
14
]
for more background on free proalgebraic groups.)

Let
S
be a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
with
d+1
elements and let
\Gamma_{S}
be the differential Galois group of the family of all regular singular differential equations over
k(z)
with singularities contained in
S
.
For
k=\mathbb{C}
, it is an immediate corollary of the Riemann-Hilbert correspondence (
[
13
, Theorem 6.15]
) that
\Gamma_{S}
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{d}
. It therefore seems natural to expect that
\Gamma_{S}
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{d}
in general. This question was already raised in
[
15
, Section 4.1]
. While we are far from being able to show that indeed
\Gamma_{S}
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{d}
, Theorem
2.14
can be seen as a small step into the right direction. It implies that every algebraic quotient of
\Gamma_{d}
is also a quotient of
\Gamma_{S}
.

Corollary 2.16
.

Let
G
be an algebraic group over
k
. If
G
is a quotient of
\Gamma_{d}
, then
G
is also a quotient of
\Gamma_{S}
.

Proof.

If
G
is a quotient of
\Gamma_{d}
, then
G
can be generated by
d
elements (
[
14
, Lemma 2.16]
). After embedding
G
into some
\textup{GL}_{n,k}
, we can apply Theorem
2.14
to find a regular singular differential equation
\partial(y)=Ay
with differential Galois group
G
. The Picard-Vessiot ring
R
of
\partial(y)=Ay
is canonically embedded in the Picard-Vessiot ring
R_{S}
of the family of all regular singular differential equations with singularities in
S
. By the second fundamental theorem of differential Galois theory (see e.g.,
[
AmanoMasuokaTakeuchi
, Theroem 2.11]
), the differential Galois group of
R
is a quotient of the differential Galois group of
R_{S}
, i.e.,
G
is a quotient of
\Gamma_{S}
as desired.
∎
