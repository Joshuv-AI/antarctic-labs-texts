vWD, von Willebrand disease.

Central Message

Cardiac surgery in inherited von Willebrand disease requires individualized perioperative hemostatic management guided by factor replacement and real-time coagulation monitoring.

Perspective

Current evidence on cardiac surgery in von Willebrand disease is limited and heterogeneous. This review supports individualized, factor-guided management strategies and highlights the need for prospective data to establish standardized perioperative protocols in this high-risk population.

First described in 1926 by Erik von Willebrand as hereditary pseudohemophilia,1 von Willebrand disease (vWD) is now recognized as the most common inherited bleeding disorder worldwide,2 with an estimated prevalence of 25.6 per million population.3 Despite advances in diagnostic phenotyping and targeted therapy, perioperative management remains challenging in cardiac surgery, where cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) introduces multiple hemostatic stressors including hemodilution, platelet dysfunction, inflammatory activation, and loss of high-molecular-weight Von Willebrand factor (vWF) multimers that may increase bleeding risk.4 vWF plays a dual role in hemostasis by mediating platelet adhesion and stabilizing factor VIII (FVIII), protecting it from premature degradation. A deficiency of vWF leads to reduced FVIII levels and impairment of both primary and secondary hemostasis, an effect exacerbated under high shear conditions and during CPB.5,6

Inherited vWD is traditionally classified into quantitative and qualitative variants. Type 1 represents a partial quantitative deficiency of vWF and is the most common form, whereas type 3 is characterized by severe or near-complete deficiency of vWF. Type 2 vWD includes qualitative defects of vWF function and is further subdivided into several variants (2A, 2B, 2M, and 2N) on the basis of specific functional abnormalities.6

Current international guidelines (American Society of Hematology/International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis/National Hemophilia Foundation/World Federation of Hemophilia, 2021) provide recommendations for perioperative management, including target factor levels and replacement strategies.7,8 However, evidence specific to cardiac surgery remains limited to case reports and small series, restricting standardization of care and comparative assessment of available approaches.9 This scoping review aims to map the existing evidence on perioperative hemostatic management in patients with inherited vWD undergoing cardiac surgery, summarize key management patterns, and identify clinically relevant knowledge gaps.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting Framework

This scoping review was conducted to explore the existing literature on perioperative hemostatic management in patients with inherited vWD undergoing cardiac surgery. The review methodology followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.10 Ethical approval was not required for this study, because it is a scoping review based exclusively on previously published data and does not involve human participants or identifiable personal data.

Protocol and Registration

An a priori review protocol was developed by the authors to guide the search strategy, eligibility criteria, and data-charting process. Formal registration was not performed because registration is not mandatory for scoping reviews and is not currently supported by international registries for this study design.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) adult or adolescent patients with a laboratory-confirmed diagnosis of inherited vWD (any subtype); (2) performance of cardiac surgery with or without CPB; and (3) original patient-level perioperative data regarding hemostatic management and/or bleeding-related outcomes. Eligible study designs included case reports, case series, and observational studies reporting original patient-level data. Narrative and systematic reviews were screened for reference mining but were not included in the final synthesis.

Studies were excluded if they involved noncardiac surgery, minor surgical or dental procedures, pediatric-only populations without relevance to adult cardiac surgery, experimental laboratory studies, or publications lacking sufficient clinical or perioperative data. Publications focused exclusively on acquired von Willebrand syndrome were excluded because inclusion of these reports would introduce substantial biological and clinical heterogeneity related to distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, particularly in patients with valvular heart disease.

Information Sources and Online Search

A comprehensive literature search was performed across the following electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search covered all available publications from database inception to January 2026. Search terms included combinations of Medical Subject Headings and free-text key words related to vWD and cardiac surgery, such as “von Willebrand disease,” “cardiac surgery,” “cardiopulmonary bypass,” “valve surgery,” and “coronary artery bypass grafting.” Reference lists of all included articles were screened for additional relevant studies.

Selection and Inclusion of Articles

All records found through the database search were imported into a reference-management system, and duplicate publications were removed before screening. Titles and abstracts were screened independently by 2 reviewers to assess potential eligibility. Full-text articles were reviewed independently by 2 coauthors. Discrepancies regarding eligibility were resolved through discussion and consensus among the investigators.

Data-Charting Process and Data Items

Data were extracted using a standardized data charting form developed for this review. The extracted variables included author and year of publication; study design; patient demographics; subtype of vWD; type of cardiac surgical procedure; use of CPB; preoperative laboratory parameters of vWF and FVIII; perioperative hemostatic therapy; intraoperative hemostatic monitoring strategies; bleeding complications; transfusion requirements; and postoperative outcomes. The data-charting process was iterative, allowing refinement of extracted variables as familiarity with the literature increased. Classification of bleeding was performed descriptively rather than according to formal validated bleeding scales. “Severe bleeding” generally referred to reports describing major transfusion requirements, re-exploration for bleeding, hemodynamic instability, or uncontrolled diffuse hemorrhage. “Moderate” or “diffuse” bleeding referred to clinically relevant bleeding requiring additional hemostatic interventions or transfusion support without reoperation or catastrophic instability. “No bleeding” indicated absence of clinically significant hemorrhagic complications as described by the original authors. Cases reported as “minimal bleeding” were categorized separately when explicitly stated in the source publication.

Critical Appraisal of Individual Sources of Evidence

Consistent with the methodologic principles of scoping reviews, formal assessment of methodologic quality or risk of bias was not performed. The primary objective of this review was to map the scope of existing evidence and identify gaps in current knowledge, while descriptively exploring associations between perioperative management strategies and reported bleeding-related outcomes, rather than to formally quantify the effectiveness of specific therapeutic interventions.

Synthesis of Results

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, patient populations, and reported outcomes, findings were summarized descriptively and presented in structured tables and narrative form. Results were analyzed to identify recurring management strategies, patterns of hemostatic monitoring, surgical characteristics, and reported clinical outcomes.

Results

The literature search identified 378 records, of which 301 remained after duplicate removal and underwent title and abstract screening. After full-text assessment, 11 publications met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Perioperative Management

Preoperative vWF-containing factor replacement was administered in 11 of 12 patients (91.7%; S1-S10), most commonly using plasma-derived FVIII/vWF concentrates. Reported loading doses ranged from 50 to 107 IU/kg, with a median dose of approximately 60 IU/kg. Desmopressin was used in 4 patients (33.3%; S2, S6, S7, S12), primarily in those with type 1 vWD or milder phenotypes, either alone or as adjunctive therapy. Fresh-frozen plasma and/or cryoprecipitate were administered in 5 patients (41.7%; S1, S6, S7, S10, S11), mainly in earlier publications or in settings in which specific concentrates were unavailable. Baseline laboratory evaluation before surgery was reported in all patients (S1-S11), typically including vWF antigen, vWF activity, and FVIII levels.

Conventional coagulation tests, including activated clotting time and/or activated partial thromboplastin time, were used in all patients (100%; S1-S11). Viscoelastic assays, often using heparinase-modified protocols, were applied in 4 patients (33.3%; S1, S2, S7, S10). Platelet function testing using Multiplate (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) or PFA-100 (Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Marburg, Germany) was reported in 2 patients (16.7%; S2, S5), primarily in cases characterized by diffuse microvascular bleeding or suspected platelet dysfunction.

Intraoperative supplementation with vWF/FVIII concentrates was required in 6 patients (50.0%; S1, S2, S3, S7, S8, S9) and was administered either as repeated bolus doses or continuous infusions. Reported FVIII doses ranged from 1000 to 3000 IU, depending on surgical duration and the severity of intraoperative bleeding.

Postoperative continuation of vWF-containing concentrates was reported in 9 patients (75.0%; S1, S3-S5, S7-S11), most commonly for a duration of 3 to 7 days. Daily monitoring of FVIII and vWF levels was described in 5 cases (41.7%; S3, S4, S5, S8, S9), enabling dose adjustments on the basis of laboratory trends and clinical assessment of bleeding. Blood product transfusion was required in 8 patients (66.7%; S1, S2, S4, S6, S7, S9-S11), including red blood cell transfusions in 7 patients (58.3%; S1, S2, S6, S7, S9-S11), platelet transfusions in 5 patients (41.7%; S2, S6, S7, S10, S11), and administration of fresh-frozen plasma and/or cryoprecipitate in 6 patients (50.0%; S1, S6, S7, S9-S11).

Discussion

Despite advances in perioperative hemostatic management and cardiac surgical techniques, evidence regarding inherited vWD in cardiac surgery remains limited to isolated case reports and small case series. This reflects both the rarity of the condition and the challenges of conducting prospective studies in this complex population. The available evidence suggests that several baseline clinical and laboratory characteristics may be associated with increased perioperative bleeding risk, particularly when preventive hemostatic strategies are insufficient or delayed. Severe baseline deficiency of vWF and/or FVIII, a clinically significant bleeding phenotype, exposure to CPB, and complex or redo cardiac surgical procedures appear to represent the most important risk-enhancing factors identified across the included reports. In addition, delayed initiation or inadequate intensity of perioperative replacement therapy may further aggravate bleeding-related complications in these high-risk settings.

Bleeding complications were observed across different vWD phenotypes, suggesting that perioperative bleeding risk reflects a combination of disease severity, surgical complexity, CPB exposure, and perioperative hemostatic management rather than a single vWD subtype alone. Despite the inherited bleeding disorder and frequent CPB use, transfusion rates appeared broadly comparable with those reported in contemporary high-risk cardiac surgical populations, suggesting that satisfactory hemostatic control may be achievable in selected patients with individualized management and targeted replacement strategies.

Effective perioperative management requires multidisciplinary planning and comprehensive preoperative assessment. Bleeding risk stratification using tools such as the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis Bleeding Assessment Tool may help identify high-risk patients.22 Baseline evaluation should include vWF antigen, vWF activity (preferably ristocetin cofactor), and FVIII levels.23,24

Cardiac surgery introduces unique hemostatic stressors, including high shear forces, hemodilution, platelet dysfunction, and loss of high-molecular-weight vWF multimers.25,E1 These factors likely explain the limited efficacy of desmopressin alone in several cases and support the use of plasma-derived vWF/FVIII concentrates as first-line therapy. CPB may act as a secondary hemostatic insult, inducing a transient acquired von Willebrand–like state even in patients with pre-existing disease.6 Therefore, management should be dynamic, with repeated reassessment of vWF and FVIII levels rather than reliance on static preoperative correction.5 In the included reports, administration of vWF-containing concentrates was generally guided by baseline vWF/FVIII deficiency, bleeding phenotype, procedural risk, and perioperative hemostatic assessment. Preoperative replacement was most commonly performed before CPB initiation, targeting vWF activity >50 IU/dL and FVIII levels >80 to 100 IU/dL. Additional supplementation was triggered by diffuse bleeding, persistent coagulopathy, transfusion requirements, inadequate laboratory correction, or abnormal viscoelastic findings. More recent reports emphasized serial reassessment of vWF and FVIII levels to guide therapy and avoid excessive postoperative FVIII accumulation. However, replacement strategies remained largely individualized and nonstandardized across studies.

Real-time hemostatic monitoring is critical during CPB.4 Laboratory assays confirm correction of vWF/FVIII deficiency, whereas viscoelastic testing provides rapid functional assessment of clot formation. A combined strategy using serial factor measurements and viscoelastic monitoring appears most clinically useful. However, viscoelastic abnormalities are nonspecific and should be interpreted as complementary rather than standalone markers of vWF-related coagulopathy.E2

Reporting of patient blood management strategies was limited, particularly in historical reports. Information regarding anemia correction, iron supplementation, erythropoietin use, nutritional optimization, and transfusion thresholds was rarely available. Nevertheless, modern blood-conservation approaches, including restrictive transfusion strategies and intraoperative cell salvage, may help reduce perioperative bleeding risk and allogeneic transfusion requirements in this population.E3

The available evidence is limited to case reports and small case series, precluding causal inference and quantitative synthesis. Publication bias toward successful or complex cases is likely, although uncomplicated outcomes may be underreported. Variability in surgical procedures, monitoring strategies, bleeding definitions, and perioperative management limited direct comparison between studies. In addition, the 5-decade time span of the included reports introduced substantial historical variability related to advances in CPB technology, coagulation monitoring, transfusion practices, and vWF-replacement therapy. Consequently, the findings of this review should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive synthesis of the available clinical experience rather than definitive management guidance.

Unlike previous narrative reviews addressing both inherited and acquired von Willebrand disorders in cardiac surgery, the present review specifically analyzed patient-level perioperative management data in patients with laboratory-confirmed inherited vWD undergoing cardiac surgery.9 In addition to replacement strategies and bleeding outcomes, the review focused on CPB-related hemostatic alterations and contemporary coagulation monitoring approaches.

Conclusions

Available evidence suggests that cardiac surgery may be successfully performed in selected patients with inherited vWD when individualized multidisciplinary perioperative hemostatic management is applied. CPB represents an additional hemostatic challenge requiring dynamic perioperative reassessment of vWF/FVIII levels and targeted replacement strategies. These findings support a structured multidisciplinary approach and highlight the need for prospective studies to establish standardized perioperative management protocols.
