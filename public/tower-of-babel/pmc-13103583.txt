The role of ovarian transposition prior to pelvic radiation: A review
Authors: Lauren M. Lim, Joseph Lafferty, Mitchell Kamrava, Margaret I. Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Ovarian transposition can preserve ovarian function prior to pelvic radiation.•Success of ovarian transposition can vary widely (15–100%).•Radiation type and dose, chemotherapy, and position of ovaries impact success.•Ovarian transposition is safe in well-selected patients with low risk of ovarian metastasis.

Introduction

Pelvic radiation therapy is a common treatment modality for various pelvic cancers, although it confers significant risk to ovarian tissue and therefore may profoundly impact pre-menopausal women. Originally developed in the late 1950 s, ovarian transposition (OT) is a procedure that aims to maintain ovarian function in the setting of pelvic cancer treatment. OT has been subsequently used prior to pelvic radiation in the setting of cervical cancer, Hodgkin’s lymphoma, soft tissue cancers, and anorectal cancer (Hoekman et al., 2019). Despite advances in vaccination and screening, cervical cancer continues to be a common gynecologic malignancy with most patients diagnosed between ages 35–44 (Cancer of the cervix uteri - cancer stat facts, 2025). Additionally, the incidence of rectal cancer is rising among adults aged 20–40 (Sioulas et al., 2017). With many pre-menopausal women affected by these cancers, OT is an important consideration in their course of treatment.

The goal of OT is to reposition one or both ovaries outside of the field of radiation that would otherwise damage their functionality. For women under age 40, it is estimated that a radiation dose of <2 Gy may lead to degradation of 50% of oocytes and a dose of 14 Gy may lead to complete ovarian failure (Wallace et al., 2005). Untreated primary ovarian insufficiency (POI) puts young women at risk for infertility, decreased sexual function, decreased bone density, and increased cardiovascular morbidity leading to reduced life expectancy (Hoekman et al., 2019, Buonomo et al., 2021). Success rates for ovarian function preservation following OT can vary based on the type of cancer, radiation therapy fields and dosages, use of adjuvant chemotherapy, and patient factors such as age, body mass index (BMI), and baseline ovarian function. OT is a feasible procedure with minimal risks for complications that can provide pre-menopausal women with an option to maintain the natural benefits of ovarian function and certain options for fertility preservation (Hoekman et al., 2019, Buonomo et al., 2021, Husseinzadeh et al., 1984).

Indications for ovarian transposition

OT is indicated for pre-menopausal women undergoing pelvic radiation when the ovaries do not need to be removed for treatment or staging purposes. OT is generally recommended for women aged 40 or younger due to reduced ovarian function after this age (Sioulas et al., 2017, Hwang et al., 2012, Hoekman et al., 2018, Morice et al., 1998). The ovaries should appear grossly normal on preoperative imaging and intraoperative visualization, and patients should have no history of breast cancer or family history of ovarian cancer (Yamamoto et al., 2001). Patients who may be at increased risk for ovarian metastasis should be carefully counseled prior to OT.

Ovarian metastasis is rare in patients with early-stage cervical cancer. A recent meta-analysis reported the incidence of ovarian metastasis of 1.46% with squamous cell carcinoma and 3.61% with adenocarcinoma of the cervix (Fan et al., 2020). The NCCN guidelines for early-stage cervical cancer support that ovaries can be spared at the time of radical hysterectomy (Abu-Rustum et al., 2020). However, some studies suggest that OT should only be performed if the initial cervical tumor size is <2 cm9. Rectal and anal cancers carry a higher risk of ovarian metastases than cervical cancer, with rates ranging from 1 to 8%, and about 5% in pre-menopausal women (Van der Meer et al., 2022). In pre-menopausal women with locally advanced rectal and anal cancer, the risks may outweigh the benefits of OT, though there are no reports of ovarian metastases after transposition in this subgroup (Fish, 2022).

Procedure

OT has been described using open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches (Hoekman et al., 2019, Iavazzo et al., 2013). Unilateral or bilateral OT can be performed at the time of radical hysterectomy or as a separate procedure. The goal of OT is to displace the ovaries outside the field of radiation where they would otherwise be compromised. The location is patient-specific given unique radiation fields determined by radiation oncologists, though it is recommended to place the ovaries at least 4 cm from the radiation field or 1.5 cm above the iliac crest (Buonomo et al., 2021, Hwang et al., 2012, Lv et al., 2019, Gay et al., 2022).

The first step of OT is isolating the ovaries from the uterus by ligating the utero-ovarian ligament. Next, the peritoneum is incised parallel to the infundibulopelvic ligament, and the ovarian vasculature is carefully dissected cephalad. Some reports suggest dissecting to the level of the aortic bifurcation, whereas others describe dissecting far enough to allow displacement of ovaries to the costal margin without placing the vascular pedicle on tension (Morice et al., 1998, Huang et al., 2007, Turkgeldi et al., 2019). The ovaries are then secured, typically to the peritoneum, with non-absorbable sutures. To reduce the risk of ovarian torsion, the ovary should be secured at three points (Buonomo et al., 2021). Commonly, ovaries are positioned superiorly and laterally in the paracolic gutters (Sioulas et al., 2017, Buonomo et al., 2021, Morice et al., 1998, Yoon et al., 2015). The inferior margin of the ovaries should be tagged with radiopaque surgical clips for future identification on CT imaging (Buonomo et al., 2021).

Several variations of OT have also been described. The ovaries can be secured to the anterior abdominal wall medially or laterally 3–4 cm above the umbilical line, or transposed superiorly to the hepatic and splenic flexure (Huang et al., 2007, Swift et al., 2018, Turkgeldi et al., 2019). With extensive retroperitoneal dissection, the ovaries and their vasculature can be placed through a fascial incision and subsequently positioned in subcutaneous breast or upper abdominal tissue in order to be accessible for examination of recurrence (Fujiwara et al., 1997). Graeer et al. described a unique technique for OT in rectal cancer and Hodgkin’s lymphoma patients in which the ovary is isolated from the utero-ovarian ligament, placed on the anterior abdominal wall, and a percutaneous straight needle is inserted through the skin and used to secure the ovary to the abdominal wall (Gareer et al., 2011).

In contrast to OT, ovariopexy can be performed by maintaining the utero-ovarian ligament and infundibulopelvic ligament and securing one or both ovaries outside of the radiation field while still within the pelvis. This can be achieved by suturing the ovary to the medial umbilical ligament, round ligament, posterior uterine wall or uterosacral ligament. Placing the ovaries posterior to the uterus allows the uterus to act as a radiation shield (Yazdani et al., 2024, Grabenbauer et al., 1991, Genovese et al., 2024). This method is primarily performed in the setting of Hodgkin’s lymphoma.

If the uterus remains in place, the fallopian tubes may also be removed. Opportunistic bilateral salpingectomy can reduce the risk of ovarian cancer by up to 65% and should be considered at the time of ovarian transposition (Sioulas et al., 2017, Falconer et al., 2015, Opportunistic salpingectomy as a strategy for epithelial ovarian cancer prevention, 2025). Ovarian transposition reversal has also been reported in the literature to restore the ovaries to their original position after radiation treatment is completed (Turkgeldi et al., 2019).

Outcomes

Successful preservation of ovarian function occurs in approximately 15%–100% of patients who undergo OT followed by radiotherapy without adjuvant chemotherapy (Hoekman et al., 2019). This wide range reflects variability in treatment modalities, patient demographics, and definitions of ovarian function (Buonomo et al., 2021, Moawad et al., 2017). Different measurements and markers have been used for determining ovarian function, including menopausal symptoms, return of menses, laboratory values (i.e., estradiol, follicle stimulating hormone, and testosterone levels), and fertility. Studies are summarized in Table 1. A systematic review by Hoekman et al. of 765 patients with various pelvic cancers who underwent OT, including ovariopexy, found that ovarian function was more likely to be preserved with brachytherapy alone (63.6%–100%) when compared to external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) with or without brachytherapy (20–100%). Additional chemotherapy with radiation yielded successful ovarian preservation in 0–69.2% of patients (Hoekman et al., 2019). Another systematic review by Gubbala et. al. included 892 women who underwent OT. Ninety percent of patients who had OT alone with no pelvic radiation (95% CI 82–99, n = 428) and 90% of patients who underwent OT with brachytherapy (95% CI 79–111, n = 143) maintained ovarian function (Gubbala et al., 2014). In the group who underwent EBRT with or without brachytherapy, there was significantly decreased ovarian function (65%, 95% CI 56–74, n = 321). While brachytherapy alone shows less radiation impact on the ovaries, the more common post-operative radiation treatment for cervical cancer will also include EBRT. A 2021 systematic review showed that 61.7% (n = 699) of patients with cervical cancer who underwent OT prior to radiotherapy maintained ovarian function. In this systematic review, preserved ovarian function was 85.7% after brachytherapy alone, 54.8% after EBRT with brachytherapy, and 51.1% after chemoradiation (Buonomo et al., 2021). A recent study suggests that for patients undergoing treatment for Hodgkin’s lymphoma with pelvic radiotherapy and adjuvant chemotherapy, there was no significant association between OT and median age of primary ovarian insufficiency (Fernandez-Pineda et al., 2018). This study reviewed 90 patients with Hodgkin’s lymphoma, 49 of whom underwent OT prior to pelvic radiotherapy. The age of primary ovarian insufficiency was not statistically different between the group with and without OT (22.7 and 17.9 years, respectively). The authors noted a possible bias that patients who underwent OT were more likely to be treated with higher doses of radiotherapy that may have contributed to increased risk of ovarian damage. While the radiation doses for Hodgkin's lymphoma vary (typically in the 30 Gy range), they tend to be much lower than the doses used for gynecologic cancers (typically in the 45–50 Gy range for postoperative cases and closer to 85 Gy in locally advanced cervical cancer treatments).

While the ovaries may be repositioned in several locations, placing them furthest from the radiation field and specifically above the iliac crests may yield better outcomes. In a retrospective analysis of 18 patients (31 ovaries) who underwent OT followed by pelvic radiation, CT imaging showed most ovaries were relocated outside of the planning target volume (20/31) though below the iliac crest. Thirty of 31 ovaries that were transposed still received radiation doses >5 Gy, a gonadotoxic dose (Gay et al., 2022). There was a significant inverse correlation between maximum radiation dose and distance above the iliac crest. Of the 6 patients with post-treatment FSH levels reported, all showed FSH levels >40mIUL/mL. Another study showed a similar inverse relationship between the distance the ovaries were transposed from the radiation field and serum FSH, though this negative correlation was not significant (Yoon et al., 2015). Some reports suggest that lateral compared to medial ovariopexy may be superior. Four of 7 patients with Hodgkin’s lymphoma who underwent lateral ovariopexy had preserved ovarian function, but only 1 of 4 patients with medial ovariopexy had preserved ovarian function, suggesting a higher radiation dose to medially fixed ovaries (Grabenbauer et al., 1991).

Higher doses of radiation are associated with increased risk of ovarian failure (Fernandez-Pineda et al., 2018). Most studies have found that age < 35 years old is associated with better ovarian function outcomes compared to age >35 years old (Hwang et al., 2012, Hoekman et al., 2018, Salih et al., 2009, Mossa et al., 2015). It is still disputed whether BMI plays a role in ovarian function after OT. While one study showed that a higher pre-treatment BMI correlated with lower FSH levels post-treatment, this finding was not replicated in a different study that evaluated the association of BMI with ovarian function (Hwang et al., 2012, Yoon et al., 2015). It is important to note that ovarian function may take time to return to normal levels following treatment. One study on cervical cancer patients showed that FSH was transiently elevated 3 months following to chemoradiation, though there was a return to normal FSH levels 6 months after treatment (Salih et al., 2015).

Fertility is another measure of ovarian function. While fewer studies report fertility outcomes, successful pregnancies and live births following OT have occurred. Spontaneous conception can occur when OT is reversed or in the setting of medial ovariopexy with intact fallopian tubes. Egg retrieval can potentially be performed transabdominally for transposed ovaries (Jang et al., 2019). Gestational carrier after egg retrieval and in vitro fertilization (IVF) from transposed ovaries is another fertility option after OT and pelvic radiation (Hoekman et al., 2019). Uterine transposition has been reported in case reports as an option for fertility preservation in pelvic cancers but is outside of the scope of this review (Ribeiro et al., 2024). The vast majority of successful pregnancies following OT and pelvic radiation occur in patients with Hodgkins’s lymphoma with a specific inverted Y field radiation protocol with lead shield to the uterus (Hoekman et al., 2019) In one retrospective review of 34 patients who underwent OT or ovariopexy, 5 patients conceived spontaneously, and 4 of the 5 patients had a transposition reversal surgery (Turkgeldi et al., 2019). In the single patient who did not have reversal surgery, imaging confirmed her ovaries remained in her abdomen at the time of conception, though this pregnancy ended in miscarriage. Three of the patients had live births. Medial ovariopexy has favorable fertility outcomes compared to lateral ovariopexy and was associated with higher rates of pregnancy and live birth (OR 7.04, P = 0.001 and OR 5.29, P = 0.003, respectively) (Genovese et al., 2024).

Risks and complications

There are very few complications reported with OT, with some small studies reporting no intraoperative or immediate postoperative complications (Morice et al., 1998, Huang et al., 2007). Patients may develop abdominal pain or pain at the site of transposition (3.4%), possibly related to ovulation pain or scarring (Buonomo et al., 2021). Ovarian cysts are a more common occurrence after ovarian transposition, ranging from 11% to as high as 94.9% (Hoekman et al., 2019, Buonomo et al., 2021, Winarto et al., 2013, Laios et al., 2022). In a meta-analysis of women with cervical cancer divided into groups who received OT surgery alone, OT with brachytherapy, and OT with brachytherapy and EBRT, there was no difference between groups for development of ovarian cysts; across all groups combined, 89% of patients did not develop ovarian cysts (Laios et al., 2022). Importantly, not all ovarian cysts are symptomatic or require additional management. One retrospective study specifically identified an increased risk for symptomatic ovarian cysts in those who underwent OT compared to the general population. Furthermore, in this study, surgical management was performed in only 21.6% of symptomatic cysts; medical management with oral contraceptive pills and analgesics was used in 3% of symptomatic cysts (Buonomo et al., 2021). Two cases of ovarian torsion following OT have been published (Moawad et al., 2017). There is a small risk of pelvic hematoma, fallopian tube infarction, small bowel obstruction, and chronic pelvic pain (Buonomo et al., 2021, Yazdani et al., 2024). There is also risk that the ovaries may migrate back to the pelvis and become damaged by radiotherapy (Yazdani et al., 2024).

The most serious complication of OT is ovarian metastasis. Ovarian metastasis is extremely rare, and many studies report no evidence of metastasis (Hwang et al., 2012). Buonomo et al. found that ovarian metastases occurred in 5/1377 patients (0.36%) with cervical cancer who underwent OT. In the systematic review by Hoekman et al., 5 patients out of 872 were found to have ovarian metastasis; all 5 patients had cervical cancer (Hoekman et al., 2019). Laios et al. reported that of 1,160 women with cervical cancer that underwent OT, there was no evidence of ovarian metastases in 99% of patients; for those who developed recurrence, 6/292 had recurrence in the transposed ovaries (Laios et al., 2022). There is a single report of abdominal wall metastasis at the trocar site after ovarian transposition, though this may have been attributable to initial microscopic cancer spread rather than the transposition surgery itself (Moawad et al., 2017).

Conclusion

OT is a well-established procedure that may benefit pre-menopausal women undergoing pelvic radiation therapy for pelvic cancers, such as cervical cancer. Ovarian function preservation rates vary widely, ranging from 15% to 100% following radiotherapy. There are few reports of pregnancies and live births after OT and pelvic radiation, and gestational carrier may be an option for patients whose uterus is affected by radiation (Hoekman et al., 2019). For young women, OT conveys benefits for quality of life related to avoiding menopause, cardiovascular, and bone health. It is a low-risk procedure with no evidence of ovarian metastasis in up to 99% of patients without clinical suspicion for ovarian involvement (Laios et al., 2022). While there are various techniques for OT, in general, ovaries should be relocated as far from the radiation field as feasible which is generally at least 4 cm from the radiation field or 1.5 cm above the iliac crest to avoid gonadotoxic radiation doses. Many studies on OT have small sample sizes that make it challenging to ascertain significance and external validity. In systemic reviews, there are high levels of heterogeneity attributable to the wide variations in study design and definitions of ovarian function (Hoekman et al., 2019, Buonomo et al., 2021). OT should be offered to pre-menopausal women age 35–40 or younger who plan on undergoing pelvic radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy if they have no increased risk for ovarian cancer or ovarian metastasis. There is an increased risk of ovarian cysts compared to the general population, but these can frequently be managed medically without surgery. It is important to counsel patients that results may vary, and they may still require hormonal replacement therapy for treatment of primary ovarian insufficiency or assisted reproductive technology for fertility.
