Stability study of topotecan in ophthalmic prefilled syringes for intravitreal delivery
Authors: William Bello, Camille Hosotte, Camille Stampfli, Antoine Pierrot, Francis L Munier, Markoulina Berger-Gryllaki, Julian Pezzatti, Laurent Carrez, Farshid Sadeghipour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravitreal and intracameral administration of melphalan and topotecan (TPT) has shown its efficacy in the treatment of retinoblastoma over the last few years. Due to rapid hydrolysis, melphalan must be administered within the hour following reconstitution. With improved stability at room temperature and reduced ocular toxicity, TPT seems to be a promising candidate for production of prefilled syringes in terms of safety and efficiency of preparation and treatment administration. Due to the lack of TPT stability data, the stability of TPT at 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL in prefilled syringes was evaluated in three different storage conditions.MethodsThe stability of TPT in prefilled syringes was assessed via reversed-phase liquid chromatography coupled to a diode array detector analytical platform. The physicochemical stability of TPT in prefilled syringes in both concentrations, stored at 30±2°C with a relative humidity (RH) of 65±5%, 5±3°C, and −20±5°C, was evaluated over 12 months including visual inspection and pH measurement.ResultsTPT solution in syringes at 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL was stable at 5±3°C and −20±5°C for up to 12 months, with no precipitate and no significant change in pH and concentration. A TPT-related degradant, 8-methoxy-TPT, was detected at <0.5% only in 30±2°C (65±5% RH) after 3 months.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that TPT solutions at 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL conditioned in BBraun Omnifix syringes could be stored at 5±3°C for 12 months for safe and secure administration to patients.Keywords: Topotecan, retinoblastoma, prefilled syringes, stability indicating method, UHPLC-UV, intravitreal administration

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Intravitreal and intracameral administration of melphalan and topotecan (TPT) has shown efficacy in the treatment of retinoblastoma. However, TPT appears to have a more favourable safety profile for producing prefilled syringes due to its improved stability at room temperature and reduced ocular toxicity compared with melphalan. TPT is generally used at 20 µg/mL for intracameral injections and 200 µg/mL for intravitreal injections by caregivers.

Introduction

Retinoblastoma, described as the most prevalent paediatric intraocular cancer, is lethal by metastasis if left untreated.1,3 The primary goal of retinoblastoma treatment is to prevent metastatic disease and preserve life. Eye conservative treatment using chemotherapy aims to prevent enucleation and preserve vision in the affected eye with minimal side effects to the patient and impact on their quality of life.2 4 Intravitreal and intracameral injections allow for direct exposure of the avascular vitreous and aqueous humours to high concentrations of chemotherapy by bypassing the blood–retinal barrier. This also results in low plasma exposure thus minimising systemic side effects.5 6

Intravitreal melphalan therapy emerged in 2012 as a promising administration technique for active recurrent or persistent vitreous seeds.7 8 In 2017, intracameral melphalan therapy was introduced for aqueous seeds, transforming the eye conservation rate from 0% with all other available therapies to 85% in the largest series.9,11 However, melphalan's retinal toxicity after administration of clinical doses and its low stability once reconstituted make its use and production process more complicated.78 12,14

Recently, topotecan (TPT) has shown promising results in the treatment of retinoblastoma not only by intravitreal injection as monotherapy or in combination with melphalan, but also by intracameral injection.15 Compared with melphalan, TPT also showed a lack of ocular and systemic toxicity in animal and human studies as well as favourable pharmacokinetics with prolonged exposure in the vitreous of preclinical models.5 Even if some reports have shown good results for the combined use of intravitreal melphalan and TPT, more recently intravitreal TPT monotherapy has been shown to effectively cause total seed regression and eradication of vitreous seeds at clinical doses ranging from 20 µg to 30 µg while causing no retinal toxicity in patients.5 7 12 16 However, stability data for TPT in ready-to-administer prefilled syringes are lacking in the scientific literature.

Even though TPT may appear to be promising for employment in monotherapy against retinoblastoma, it is always ideal to consider combining different drugs with different mechanisms of action to promote optimal efficacy against retinoblastoma tumours. Even though exclusive TPT monotherapy is sufficiently efficient to salvage eyes, most patients would receive intravitreal melphalan at some point during their treatment course.8 12

This article describes the development of an analytical method and its validation for TPT absolute quantification conducted in accordance with International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) and French Society of Pharmaceutical Sciences and Techniques (Société Française des Sciences et Techniques Pharmaceutiques, SFSTP) requirements. Subsequently, TPT’s stability in prefilled syringes at three distinct temperature storage conditions, namely 30±2°C with a relative humidity (RH) of 65±5%, 5±3°C and −20°C±5°C, are assessed. The conducted stability study will enable hospital pharmacies to improve patient safety and optimise production of TPT in prefilled syringes as well as facilitate their distribution process to hospital healthcare units.

Experimental section

Reagents and materials

TPT hydrochloride was purchased from the United States Pharmacopoeia (USP) Convention (India), Hycamtin from Novartis (Rotkreuz, Switzerland), Omnifix-F syringe 1 mL from B. Braun Medical SA (Melsungen, Germany), NaCl 0.9% Ecobag 50 mL intravenous (IV) bags from B. Braun Medical SA (Crissier, Switzerland) and syringe caps from B. Braun Medical SA (Sempach, Switzerland).

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade acetonitrile and water were procured from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), ammonium bicarbonate Bio-Ultra ≥99.5% and anhydrous acetic acid ≥99% European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur.) grade from Sigma-Aldrich (Buchs, Switzerland) to be used for the preparation of the mobile phase. Tartaric acid ACS (American Chemical Society) reagent ≥99.5% from Sigma-Aldrich, mannitol Ph. Eur. from Hänseler (Herisau, Switzerland) and NaCl 0.9% Ecoflac 50 mL from B. Braun (Crissier, Switzerland) were purchased to be used for the matrix and the saline solution for sample dilution. A reference standard containing Topotecan Related Compound A or 8-methoxy-TPT was procured from the USP reference store (Rockville, MD, USA) via Sigma-Aldrich.

Instrumental section

Liquid chromatography-ultraviolet method for product stability evaluation

A Thermo Scientific ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) Dionex UltiMate 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to an ultraviolet diode array detector (UV-DAD) was used to analyse TPT (lactone form) concentration in the samples. A Waters Acquity BEH C18 analytical column (100×2.1 mm, 1.7 µm) (Milford, MA, USA) and the corresponding VanGuard pre-column were used. Chromeleon 7.2 SR4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to acquire and process the data.

The isocratic mobile phase consisted of 85% ammonium bicarbonate 12.6 mM buffered with acetic acid 25% (v/v) to pH 6.5, and 15% HPLC grade acetonitrile pumped at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The pH of the aqueous phase was checked for stability for 14 days at room temperature. The injection volume of the samples was 5 µL in a column maintained at 25°C. The UV detection wavelength used was 228 nm. The total run time was 10 min to enable peak identification of TPT in carboxyl form and TPT in lactone form.

Standards and solution preparation

A replicate of the Hycamtin matrix was prepared using 12 mg/mL mannitol and 5 mg/mL tartaric acid in NaCl 0.9% mixed in a 1:1 ratio with the buffered ammonium bicarbonate and acetonitrile mobile phase (85:15). This matrix was also used for the blank solution.

Standard solutions used for the calibration curve were prepared by dissolving TPT USP in the previously prepared matrix. The five concentrations used were 10 µg/mL, 20 µg/mL, 50 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL. The solutions were stored at −20°C±5°C until use.

TPT control standards were prepared by reconstitution of Hycamtin 1 mg vials with 1.1 mL NaCl 0.9%. The solution was further diluted using NaCl 0.9% to obtain 20 µg/mL and 100 µg/mL solutions.

Preparation of prefilled syringes

Nine batches each of TPT 20 µg/mL and TPT 200 µg/mL prefilled 1 mL syringes were prepared in an isolator GMP Class A cleanroom dedicated to the production of chemotherapeutic treatments located in a GMP Class C cleanroom. This isolator is monitored weekly in accordance with good manufacturing practice (GMP) to prove its sterility. To produce the 20 µg/mL syringes, one vial of Hycamtin 1 mg was reconstituted using NaCl 0.9% to obtain a primary solution of 1 mg/mL. The content of the vial was then transferred into a 50 mL NaCl 0.9% IV bag with a volume adjusted to 49 mL to produce a final concentration of 20 µg/mL TPT. The final solution was aliquoted into 40 Omnifix-F 1 mL syringes which were closed using sterile syringe caps. This process was repeated eight times to produce a total of nine batches of 40 syringes of 20 µg/mL TPT.

The same method was used to produce the 200 µg/mL TPT syringes; however, two vials of Hycamtin 4 mg were each reconstituted with 4 mL NaCl 0.9% and transferred into a 50 mL NaCl 0.9% IV bag filled at 32 mL to obtain a final concentration of 200 µg/mL.

Sample preparation

The 20 µg/mL samples were analysed pure whereas the 200 µg/mL samples were diluted 1:1 with NaCl 0.9% before analysis. All samples were left to reach room temperature before being analysed.

Stability study

The study was conducted at three temperature conditions, protected from light: intermediate storage condition at 30±2°C (65±5% RH) in a Rumed climatic chamber (Laatzen, Germany), refrigerator at 5±3°C and freezer at −20±5°C (all monitored using temperature probes). Three batches of both concentrations were stored at each temperature condition.

Three syringes from each batch were analysed for TPT (lactone form) content on the day they were produced to be used as the reference concentration for the study (ie, concentration at time zero). Three syringes from each batch were then analysed for TPT content on days 1, 2 and 14 and then at 1, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. The stability of TPT was assessed by determining the percentage of the initial concentration remaining at each analysis time point. The initial TPT concentration was defined as 100%. Solutions were considered stable if the concentration remained within ±10% of the initial TPT concentration and within ±10% of the indicated label’s concentration.17

Physical assessment

Once the sample had reached room temperature, each sample was vigorously shaken using a vortex and visually inspected for particulate matter, clarity and colour change by direct sample observation.

pH determination

The pH at every sampling time point was determined for each syringe assayed using pH paper (MQuant pH-indicator strips; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). A pH meter was not used on account of the limited volume of sample present in the syringes (1 mL) and also because of the toxic nature of TPT and the complexity of cleaning the electrode after use.

Characterisation of 8-methoxy-TPT

A Thermo Scientific UHPLC Dionex UltiMate 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a single quadrupole was used to confirm the identity of 8-methoxy-TPT in the samples. The detector was linked to the same HPLC instrument via split configuration with the DAD detector. The same method, sample column, data acquisition and processing software were used as the stability-indicating method (see section on ‘Liquid chromatography-ultraviolet method for product stability evaluation’).

Results and discussion

Development, forced degradation and method validation

Batch drug compounding of TPT was commissioned at the Lausanne University Hospital Pharmacy by the Lausanne Jules-Gonin Ophthalmic Hospital as a means of treating retinoblastoma in paediatric patients. Therefore, method development was performed to bring about a stability-indicating method via HPLC-UV. Before performing a method validation, a forced degradation was done to investigate the chemical stability of the drug product by applying stressful physical and chemical conditions such as pH, temperatures and oxidations according to ICH Q1A (R2).18 19

An analytical method validation was conducted in accordance with ICH and SFSTP requirements before analysing the TPT prefilled syringes at 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL.20 21 Validation was carried out with six sets of two replicates of five calibration standards and validation standards, over a calibration range of 10 to 200 µg/mL (R2=0.9996). The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated at 0.2650 µg/mL and the limit of quantification (LOQ) at 10 µg/mL. The acceptance limit of the method was ±6% with an accuracy profile (β-tolerance of 0.95), precision (relative intermediate) <2.2% and repeatability (relative standard deviation, RSD) <1.7%. The assay of TPT solutions was within the acceptance range. Thanks to the validation results, it can thereby be used to study the stability of TPT solutions.18 An accuracy profile is presented in figure 1, describing the closeness of agreement between the observed results and the true values. A chromatogram, obtained by means of the validated method, is presented in online supplemental figure S1).

Stability study

The stability study was designed to establish shelf life and assess ideal storage conditions for this compounded drug product at two concentrations, 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL. The maximum time period for a stability study in hospital pharmacy is usually 12 months according to the Pharmaceutical Inspection Convention and Pharmaceutical Inspection Co-operation Scheme (PIC/S) GMP and Pharmacopoeia Helvetica.1722,24 The parameters in terms of time points and conditions of storage were set using usual industrial practices.25

After 12 months of storage at 30±2°C (65±5% RH), 5±3°C and −20±5°C, the initial concentration of TPT percentages remaining in the 20 µg/mL prefilled syringes were, respectively, 85.46±0.001% (IC95%), 100.78±0.016% and 95.39±0.005%. The syringes stored at 30±2°C (65±5% RH) showed a decrease of more than 10% below the initial concentration at 9 months (89.04±0.007%) as shown in figure 2. One batch stored at −20°C had an initial concentration of TPT percentage remaining of 87.28±0.031% at 6 months and 88.98±0.068% at 9 months but 93.67±0.042% at 12 months. For the 200 µg/mL samples, the assays were 95.13±0.019%, 96.52±0.011% and 101.93±0.006% at 30±2°C (65±5% RH), 5±3°C and −20±5°C, respectively, after 12 months’ storage. All sample concentrations stayed within the ±10% range throughout the study as shown in figure 3.

No significant change in pH was observed during the entire stability study. The pH of the 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL syringes were 4 and 3, respectively. No change in colour or formation of precipitate was observed throughout the stability study. The solutions remained pale yellow-green and clear. Although osmolality is an important chemical parameter to consider in a compounded drug product, it was not included in the monitoring of this stability study. However, a final evaluation of the osmolality of the product was performed, this being in the recommended interval for the drug product as per the provider’s technical datasheet. These data will be included as a quality control reference as per GMP standards.

The stability profile of TPT prefilled syringes at 20 µg/mL presented in figure 2 demonstrates stability in absence of light for 6 months at 30±2°C (65±5% RH) and 12 months at 5±3°C and −20±5°C. The stability profile of TPT prefilled syringes at 200 µg/mL, presented in figure 3, highlights stability in all three storage conditions assessed for up to 12 months. These results are consistent with previously published studies. Few studies have addressed the stability of TPT, and those that do indicate a limited shelf life, depending on the primary packaging. This article will undoubtedly be valuable for hospital pharmacists in compounding units, particularly since it demonstrates stability for both concentrations for up to a year when stored at refrigerated temperatures.4 26 27

The stability of TPT is to be expected, being known to be more stable than other anticancer compounds for this indication in the market, especially melphalan. Although there is limited published information concerning the stability of this compound, its molecular structure proves its stability, possessing aromatic structures and enabling it to have many resonance hybrid forms. In resonance structures the electrons delocalise to help stabilise the molecule.28

Concerning the stability influenced by temperature, as observed the ideal storage condition temperature for TPT is between 2°C and 8°C. It is apparently the same situation for −20°C, but there were signs of slight degradation for both concentrations which could be due to possible crystallisation. As for 30±2°C (65±5% RH), there were signs of further degradation, especially for the lower concentration TPT than the one with the higher concentration. Higher temperature together with higher humidity could possibly promote more chemical reactions such as hydrolysis, thus inducing more degradation. A TPT-related degradant was detected in possibly low concentrations and this is discussed further in detail in the next section (see section on ‘Development, forced degradation and method validation’).

The conducted stability study of TPT prefilled syringes at 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL enables hospital pharmacies’ production units to produce batches to be stored for up to 6 months at room temperature for direct transfer to ophthalmic hospitals or hospital wards in a ready-to-administer form for use by surgeons. Alternatively, it is possible to store TPT syringes in refrigerator conditions for up to 12 months at a cost to ensure complete traceability of temperature monitoring (ie, including during transportation) if the product is stored in hospital wards. The available data and recommendations enable the delay between the prescription of the treatment and its administration to be decreased. An additional positive impact on the ward and the hospital pharmacy production unit is the possibility to better plan the workload and decrease the number of preparations needed to be produced urgently, thus reducing the probability of making preparation errors. Moreover, as TPT syringes can be produced in batches, mandatory quality controls carried out will ensure better drug product quality.

Degradation of TPT

The degradation product 8-methoxy-TPT (chemical structure shown in figure 4) was detected at <0.5% in the 30±2°C (65±5% RH) conditions after 3 months only in the 20 µg/mL concentration. However, this compound was not present in other storage conditions (ie, 5°C and −20°C). A chromatogram, showing the peaks of 8-methoxy-TPT and TPT, is presented in online supplemental figure S1).

Chemical structure of 8-methoxy-topotecan.

This byproduct is also known as Topotecan Related Compound A according to the USP, which means that this compound could be a synthesis-related impurity (figure 4).29 However, since this compound was only present in a particular storage condition involving a higher temperature and RH, it could also possibly be a degradant. This compound could be induced by oxidation or hydrolysis involving the excipients in the aqueous solution. It was identified during the development of the TPT analytical method and was observed during forced degradation studies, with a retention time of approximately 6 min. The degradant was confirmed by a single quadrupole mass spectrometer and the use of an analytical standard identified at 452 [M+H]+ via a reference standard procured from the USP store.18 29 The mass spectrum of the compound concerned is shown in online supplemental file 2). Throughout the stability study it was uniquely detected at 30±2°C (65±5% RH) in the 20 µg/mL concentration and not in the 200 µg/mL concentration. A ratio was calculated by comparing the peak area of 8-methoxy-TPT and the peak area of TPT. It was noted that this ratio increased from 0.4% after 3 months to 1.2% after 12 months as illustrated in figure 5. Interestingly, it was observed in the less concentrated solution rather than the more concentrated one. As an assumption, the lower concentration of TPT in the solution could allow interactions with excipient components as well as the diluent (ie, the saline solution).

Histogram illustrating the increase in ratio of 8-methoxy-topotecan (TPT) to TPT for the 20 µg/mL concentration at 30±2°C (65±5% RH) from 3 to 12 months, performed using triplicates.

According to ICH Q3A guidelines, the impurity level should generally not exceed 0.1% of the primary compound of interest.30 However, as shown in figure 5, the impurity level rose to 1.2% after 12 months of storage, significantly surpassing the recommended limit. This finding highlights the importance of cold storage conditions for maintaining stability, particularly for customers in tropical regions, where careful attention to temperature control is essential. Notably, under storage conditions of 5°C and –20°C, no detectable levels of 8-methoxy-TPT were observed in either concentration, marking a critical observation for ensuring product quality.

Conclusions

The method developed allows rapid identification of TPT and 8-methoxy-TPT and enables quantification of TPT in prefilled syringes. The study highlighted that TPT solutions at concentrations of 20 µg/mL and 200 µg/mL were stable at 30±2°C (65±5% RH), 5±3°C and −20±5°C for 12 months, except for TPT at 20 µg/mL 30±2°C (65±5% RH), which showed an optimal stability of up to 6 months.

A TPT-related impurity, known as 8-methoxy-TPT, was exclusively identified under accelerated conditions of 30±2°C (65±5% RH), specifically in 20 µg/mL prefilled syringes. This observation led to the hypothesis that the less concentrated solution was indeed less stable.
