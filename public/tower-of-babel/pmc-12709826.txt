Background

Isolated retinal astrocytic hamartoma (IRAH) is a rare, benign glial tumor of the retinal nerve fiber layer that arises from retinal astrocytes. RAH most commonly accompanies tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) but may occur with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) or in isolation [1]. Astrocytic hamartomas usually present as flat, round, and transparent lesions that subsequently grow into calcified nodular structures. Hard exudates may also be formed around the lesions. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) reveals dome- or plateau-shaped lesions with moderate to high reflectivity, accompanied by choroidal shadows [1]. Fundus autofluorescence imaging reveals hyperautofluorescence, which depends on the calcium content [2]. Fluorescein angiography (FA) shows early blockage in the choroidal phase and a gradual increase in hyperfluorescence in the arterial phase. Leakage can also occur in the late venous phase [2]. OCT angiography clearly demonstrates well-defined hyperreflective lesions and finely branched tumor vessels [3]. Differential diagnoses for RAH include retinoblastoma, myelinated nerve fibers, and massive retinal gliosis [4], complicating the clinical diagnosis of RAH.

Laser speckle fluorography (LSFG) is a noninvasive blood flow imaging system that uses laser scattering to visualize blood flow within the eye in two dimensions. This technology enables the detailed observation of fundus circulation in various retinal and choroidal lesions. To date, we have analyzed the blood flow dynamics in various retinochoroidal lesions, including metastatic choroidal tumors [5], choroidal macrovessels [6], sclerochoroidal calcification [7], choroidal lymphoma [8], leukemic retinopathy [9], retinal metastasis [10], and radiation retinopathy in choroidal melanoma [11]. Additionally, we conducted LSFG analyses of choroidal blood flow and retinal circulation in juxtapapillary tumors, including retinal capillary hemangioblastoma [12] and optic disc melanocytoma [13]. However, the details of IRAH blood flow remain poorly understood.

Herein, we report two patients with IRAH in the juxtapapillary region and evaluate their multimodal imaging findings.

Case report

Patient 1

A 71-year-old woman complained of blurred vision in her right eye (OD) and was referred to our hospital due to a retinal mass. She had a history of sinister amblyopia oculus (OS), hypertension, and dyslipidemia. At the initial examination, her best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.4 OD and 0.3 OS with myopic astigmatism and normal intraocular pressure. Slit-lamp microscopy detected mild cataracts in uterque (OU). No distinct findings are observed in the left fundus. Color fundus photography (CFP) of the right fundus revealed a retinal mass adjacent to the superior optic disc (Fig. 1A, arrowhead). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed a hyperreflective tumor with small optically empty spaces in the inner retinal layer (Fig. 1B). FA revealed hypofluorescence in the tumor from the early to late stages (Fig.s 1C and D). Indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) revealed hypofluorescence in the tumor from the early to late stages (Fig.s 1E and F). LSFG demonstrated a tumor with a relatively cold color signal, indicating poor blood flow within the tumor (Fig. 1G). The lesion was isolated with no other ocular findings, and physical examination ruled out TSC or NF1. She was diagnosed with IRAH and showed no marked changes over the course of 3 years.

Patient 2

A 37-year-old Japanese woman with eye floater OS was referred to our hospital because of a retinal mass lesion. She had a history of Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada (VKH) disease at the age of 17 years; however, the presence of a peripapillary lesion at that time was uncertain. At the initial examination, her BCVA was 1.0 OU with myopia with normal intraocular pressure. Slit-lamp microscopy revealed no abnormalities in the OU. No distinct findings were observed in the right fundus. In the left fundus, CFP revealed a retinal mass in the nasal optic nerve head (Fig. 2A, arrowhead). OCT revealed a preserved inner retinal layer with high reflectance, compression of the outer retinal layers, and posterior optical shadowing (Fig. 2B). Ultrasound B-mode echography showed an intraretinal hyperechoic lesion indicating calcification near the optic papilla, although this finding is atypical of such tumors (Fig. 2C). FA detected hypofluorescence in the early stage, hyperfluorescence in the optic disc, and pooling surrounding the optic disc in the late stage, suggesting the possibility of exudation around the tumor. In addition, window defects and mild leakage suggestive of retinal pigment epithelial atrophy were observed, likely due to a past history of VKH disease (Fig 2D and E). LSFG revealed a tumor with a cold-colored signal, suggestive of poor blood flow within the tumor (Fig. 2F). The lesion was isolated with no other ocular findings, and physical examination ruled out TSC or NF1. She was diagnosed with IRAH and showed no marked changes over the course of 1 year.

Discussion

We encountered two patients with IRAH in the juxtapapillary region and evaluated their multimodal imaging findings. Compared with that in Case 2, the tumor in Case 1 had unclear margins and was slightly shorter in length. The whiter appearance and more sharply defined borders observed in Case 2 may indicate calcification. In Case 1, a single retinal vessel was adjacent to the tumor, and both FA and ICGA demonstrated consistent blood flow. LSFG showed a warm-colored vascular portion, whereas the tumor as a whole was cold-colored. In contrast, the tumor in Case 2 exhibited clear margins and was slightly longer. It showed hyperfluorescence on FA and appeared cool-colored on LSFG.

Four types of RAH have been identified [2]. In the present cases, OCT revealed elevated lesions ( > 500 μm) with calcification in the inner retina and “mulberry-like” calcification, consistent with type 3. Type 3 is common in patients with TSC, often accompanied by subependymal giant cell astrocytic hamartoma [2]; however, no TSC comorbidities were observed in this study.

Aggressive RAH associated with TSC represents a rare variant of this typically benign tumor. In aggressive cases, the lesion gradually enlarges and may lead to complications including exudative retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, and neovascular glaucoma [14–16]. In cases of aggressive RAH, FA typically demonstrates marked leakage from neovascular vessels [16]. In the present study, FA of Patient 2 revealed hyperfluorescence, unlike that of Patient 1; however, the absence of evident neovascularization and tumor growth during follow-up excluded the possibility of aggressive RAH. The poor blood flow observed on LSFG further supported its non-aggressive nature.

Optic disc drusen, which are considered IRAH mimics, consist of calcified refractive nodules derived from mucoproteins and mucopolysaccharides within the optic disc. These are characterized by increased reflectivity of the optic disc on echography, early fluorescence, and well-demarcated late hyperfluorescence without leakage on FA [17]. LSFG typically demonstrates reduced microcirculation in the optic nerve head [18]. In the present cases, the IRAH differed in location and did not involve the optic nerve. While multimodal imaging provides support for distinguishing between these entities, clinical expertise remains essential for accurate diagnosis.

Histopathological findings of RAH typically reveal fibrous glial cells (mainly astrocytes) with small, uniform nuclei and intertwined cytoplasmic processes [19]. Additional histological features include dystrophic calcification foci and large pleomorphic gemistocytes [3]. Reported histopathological findings of RAH generally involve large lesions requiring enucleation. Although the vascular distribution within these tumors has not been fully characterized, all four cases reported by Shields et al. exhibited profuse iris neovascularization [16]. Further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between tumor vasculature and angiographic features observed on FA and LSFG.

Our previous analyses of multi-modal imaging, including LSFG and FA, have characterized ocular circulation in various retinochoroidal tumors [5–13]. In juxtapapillary retinal capillary hemangioblastoma [12], LSFG showed markedly warm colors, reflecting rapid blood flow within the tumor, and FA demonstrated early hyperfluorescence with pronounced late-phase dye leakage. In contrast, LSFG findings of retinal metastasis [10] and optic disc melanocytoma [13] displayed cold colors, the latter likely associated with pigmentary changes. FA in retinal metastasis typically shows early hyperfluorescence with prominent late leakage, whereas optic disk melanocytoma exhibits persistent hypofluorescence throughout all phases [10, 13]. In the present cases, FA displayed a distinct pattern; however, LSFG findings showed a cold color, indicating the slow, non-progressive, benign circulation characteristic of IRAH. The combination of LSFG and FA, both are clinical modalities, may provide valuable diagnostic insights for differentiating retinochoroidal tumors.

LSFG is a noninvasive technique that offers additional clinical information on tumor circulation and diagnosis, although its use remains limited to certain countries and institutions. LSFG is safe and advantageous for patients with juxtapiapillary tumors who are allergic to dye injection. The authors hope that this modality will become more widely accessible and routinely used worldwide in the near future.

In conclusion, multimodal imaging, including LSFG, contributed to the clinical diagnosis of juxtapapillary IRAH, revealing slow and stable tumor blood circulation consistent with its benign nature.
