Parallel Branch Model Predictive Control on GPUs
Luyao Zhang, Chenghuai Lin, Sergio Grammatico

Abstract
We present a parallel GPU-accelerated solver for branch Model Predictive Control problems. Based on iterative LQR methods, our solver exploits the tree-sparse structure and implements temporal parallelism using the parallel scan algorithm. Consequently, the proposed solver enables parallelism across both the prediction horizon and the scenarios. In addition, we utilize an augmented Lagrangian method to handle general inequality constraints. We compare our solver with state-of-the-art numerical solvers in two automated driving applications. The numerical results demonstrate that, compared to CPU-based solvers, our solver achieves competitive performance for problems with short horizons and small-scale trees, while outperforming other solvers on large-scale problems.



I
Introduction

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a state-of-the-art planning method that numerically solves optimal control problems in a receding horizon fashion.
MPC has gained popularity in practice due to its ability to handle constraints and adapt to dynamic environments.
In recent years, this approach has achieved significant success in its application to mobile robots
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Nominal MPC does not explicitly account for uncertainty, often resulting in undesirable planning performance.
For example, when an automated vehicle attempts an unprotected left turn, the surrounding vehicle needs to decide whether to yield to it or not.
If the automated vehicle misinterprets the surrounding vehicle’s driving intention, it may need to make abrupt braking maneuvers to ensure safety, resulting in an uncomfortable ride.

By modeling uncertainty via a discrete distribution, Branch Model Predictive Control (BMPC)
[
5
]
, also known as contingency planning in
[
6
,
7
]
, scenario tree planning in
[
8
]
, or multistage MPC in
[
9
,
10
]
, has been proposed in recent years.
In the context of automated driving, an advantage of BMPC planners is their ability to leverage multi-modal predictions generated by motion prediction modules.
Specifically, BMPC plans a trajectory tree with multiple branches, each corresponding to a different uncertainty realization.
In contrast, traditional robust motion planners
[
11
,
12
]
attempt to generate a single motion plan that accommodates all predicted scenarios.
As a result, BMPC can avoid overly conservative motion plans.
Furthermore, recent research has successfully integrated large language models with BMPC, enhancing the trustworthiness of the system
[
13
]
.

While BMPC demonstrates strong potential for modeling uncertainty in real-world applications, its widespread deployment is hindered by the significant computational time required to solve the BMPC problem with a long planning horizon and numerous predicted scenarios.
Therefore, it is necessary to develop customized BMPC solvers that fully exploit the inherent tree structure.
A common approach is to decompose the trajectory tree into distinct scenarios that can be processed in parallel on multi-core CPUs
[
14
,
10
]
and GPUs
[
15
]
.
The coupling between scenarios is established by enforcing the causality constraints, ensuring that the control input does not anticipate the realization of uncertainty.
However, the use of Lagrangian relaxation in these methods increases the number of iterations required for convergence, making them unsuitable for real-time applications
[
14
]
.
Instead, similarly to
[
9
]
, in this paper, we perform a modified Riccati recursion directly on the tree, which implicitly respects the causality constraints at each iteration.

While the aforementioned methods carefully consider the inherent tree structure in BMPC, they fail to achieve temporal parallelism.
This type of parallelism is well-suited for applications requiring a long planning horizon, such as autonomous parking
[
16
]
and planning for low-thrust spacecraft
[
17
]
.
Several parallel approaches have been proposed for the linear quadratic (LQ) problem.
One category follows the idea of “divide and conquer”, breaking the problem into smaller subproblems that can be solved independently before combining the results.
In
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, the subproblem can only be solved approximately because it relies on information from the previous iteration.
This methodology may fail if the trajectories between iterations diverge significantly. Consequently, some research efforts have focused on developing exact parallel algorithms.
Wright
[
21
]
introduces two parallel algorithms, PDP and PRI, which differ in how they solve the linear systems associated with the LQ problem and how they parameterize the subproblems.
Nielsen and Axehill
[
22
]
treat the terminal cost in the subproblem as the unknown part and introduce an additional control input term to compensate for its influence.
This approach shares a concept similar to that of PDP, but with the difference that the consensus problem here remains an optimal control problem.
Compared to
[
21
]
, Laine and Tomlin
[
23
]
parameterize the optimal state and input trajectories, as well as the multipliers for the subproblem (fixed-end LQR), using only the initial and terminal states.
A recent work
[
24
]
, closely related to PRI, interprets the LQ problem as a cumulative sum problem and takes advantage of the parallel scan algorithm
[
25
]
to solve it efficiently on GPUs.
In
[
26
]
, similarly to
[
24
]
, the authors formulate the consensus problem as a min-max problem. They approach the subproblem through parametric optimization and perform a parametric generalized Riccati recursion to derive the parameters for the consensus problem.

In this paper, we introduce a parallel BMPC solver that exploits the tree-sparse structure to achieve parallelism in both the planning horizon and the predicted scenarios.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We extend the LQR solver proposed in
[
24
]
to develop two parallel LQR-Tree solvers capable of handling a wide variety of tree structures.

•

Building upon the parallel LQR-Tree, we introduce a multiple-shooting iterative LQR-Tree (MSiLQR-Tree) solver for BMPC problems.
This approach draws inspiration from the MSiLQR solvers proposed in
[
27
,
28
]
.
We further optimize its performance on GPUs and incorporate constraint handling using an augmented Lagrangian method.

•

We benchmark the proposed solver against other iLQR solvers, including
Trajax

[
29
]
and MSiLQR
[
27
]
, on different computing platforms: CPU and GPU.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
, we review the parallel scan and the BMPC formulation.
Sections
III
and
IV
discuss methods for parallelizing sparse and dense LQR problems, respectively.
In Section
V
, we introduce two parallel methods for solving LQR problems with tree structures and extend them to address constrained nonlinear BMPC problems.
Next, we compare the computational performance of the proposed methods with other state-of-the-art iLQR solvers in automated driving applications in Section
VI
.
Finally, we summarize the results and discuss future research directions in Section
VII
.

Parallel Scan

We first present the core parallel algorithm for LQR problems through a simple example.
The LQR problem serves as a fundamental building block for nonlinear optimal control problems.
We start by considering a problem where the input is an array of
N
elements,

[a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{N-1}],

(1)

and the objective is to output the array

[a_{0},(a_{0}\oplus a_{1}),\dots,(a_{0}\oplus a_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus a_{N-1})],

(2)

where each element is the “cumulative sum” of the previous elements in the input array.
The symbol
\oplus
denotes an associative operator, such as addition or multiplication.
Additionally, we consider a similar output array, but in reverse order:
[(a_{0}\oplus a_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus a_{N-1}),\dots,(a_{0}\oplus a_{1}),a_{0}]
.
This “cumulative sum” problem can be solved sequentially with a time complexity of
O(N)
.
To reduce the complexity to
O(\log N)
, the so-called parallel scan is proposed in
[
25
]
, which involves two phases: an up-sweep and a down-sweep.
Figure
1
illustrates the parallel scan applied to an array of 8 elements.
We process the array using a balanced binary tree.
During the up-sweep phase, a reduction operation is performed, resulting in the value
a_{0}\oplus a_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus a_{N-1}
.
Next, a down-sweep is executed, starting from the root and moving toward the leaves. At each level of the tree, the value of each node is copied to its right child, while its value, updated by adding the right child’s value in the up-sweep phase, is sent to the left child.
In Section
III
, we establish the connection between the parallel scan and the LQR problem by precisely defining the array elements and the corresponding associative operator.

III-A

Connection to Parallel Scan

We now fit an LQR problem
\mathcal{P}_{1}^{i}
into the parallel scan framework to achieve temporal parallelism.
Let us consider a time-varying finite horizon LQR problem without tree structures:

\begin{split}\min_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u}}&\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\begin{bmatrix}q_{k}\\
r_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\
u_{k}\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\
u_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{k}&M_{k}^{\top}\\
M_{k}&R_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\
u_{k}\end{bmatrix}\\
&\quad\quad+q_{N}^{\top}x_{N}+\frac{1}{2}x_{N}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{N}\\
\text{s.t.}&\;x_{k+1}=A_{k}x_{k}+B_{k}u_{k}+c_{k},\;\forall k\in[0,N-1],\end{split}

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{x}:=(x_{k})_{k\in[0,N]}
,
\boldsymbol{u}:=(u_{k})_{k\in[0,N-1]}
, and
N
is the horizon length.
We denote the optimal state and input sequences by
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{*}
, respectively.

III-A
1
Backward Pass

We recursively solve the Bellman equations, starting from the final step and working backward to the initial step.
The Bellman equation is expressed as:

\begin{split}V_{k}(x_{k})=\min_{u_{k}}&\;\;\ell_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})+V_{k+1}(x_{k+1})\\
\text{s.t.}&\;\;x_{k+1}=A_{k}x_{k}+B_{k}u_{k}+c_{k},\end{split}

with
V_{N}(x_{N})=q_{N}^{\top}x_{N}+\frac{1}{2}x_{N}^{\top}Q_{N}x_{N}
. It is known that the value function
V_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}
has a quadratic form:

V_{k}(x_{k})=z+p_{k}^{\top}x_{k}+\frac{1}{2}x_{k}^{\top}P_{k}x_{k},

(5)

where
z
denotes an arbitrary constant.
Using the value functions, we can compute the optimal control laws and subsequently recover the optimal state and input trajectories through the forward rollout.
This dynamic programming (DP)-based algorithm is an inherently sequential approach, since the value function at the next step
V_{k+1}
needs to be computed before
V_{k}
can be determined.
To parallelize the DP process, the entire prediction horizon is divided into smaller segments that can be processed in parallel.
This motivates us to define the conditional value function
[
24
]
,
V_{k\to i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}
, which represents the cost of the optimal trajectory starting from
x_{k}
and ending at
x_{i}
.
The conditional value function is related to the fixed-end LQR problem, which is formulated as

\begin{split}\min_{u_{k:i-1}}&\sum_{n=k}^{i-1}\ell_{n}(x_{n},u_{n}):=V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})\\
\text{s.t.}&\;x_{n+1}=A_{n}x_{n}+B_{n}u_{n}+c_{n},\;\forall n\in[k,i-1].\end{split}

(6)

If the state
x_{i}
is not reachable from
x_{k}
, indicating an infeasible problem, then the corresponding conditional value function becomes infinite.

Next, we decompose the computation of
V_{k\to i}
into two subproblems:
V_{k\to j}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}
and
V_{j\to i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The optimal trajectories for the two subproblems are linked by the intermediate state
x_{j}
. To recover
V_{k\to i}
, as derived in
[
24
]
, we minimize the sum of two conditional value functions over the intermediate state:

\displaystyle V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})

\displaystyle=V_{k\to j}(x_{k},x_{j})\oplus V_{j\to i}(x_{j},x_{i})

(7)

\displaystyle=\min_{x_{j}}\;V_{k\to j}(x_{k},x_{j})+V_{j\to i}(x_{j},x_{i}),

(8)

\displaystyle\;\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad k<j<i\leq N.

Similarly, the value function decomposition is expressed as

\displaystyle V_{k}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})\oplus V_{i}(x_{i})

(9)

\displaystyle=\min_{x_{i}}\;V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})+V_{i}(x_{i}),\;k<i\leq N.

(10)

In the case of LQR problems, the closed-form solutions to (
8
) and (
10
) can be derived, which is critical for efficient computation.
For clarity, we now focus on the connection between the parallel scan and the LQR problem, postponing the discussion of the analytical forms for later.
In an LQR problem, we can interpret the one-step conditional value function
V_{k\to k+1}
as the input array element
a_{k}
in (
1
).
In this setting, the input to the parallel scan algorithm is the array of the one-step conditional value functions,
\left[V_{0\to 1},V_{1\to 2},\dots,V_{N}\right]
, and the corresponding output is the collection of the value functions,
\left[V_{0},V_{1},\dots,V_{N}\right]
.
The associative operator
\oplus
used to combine two elements is defined by equations (
8
) and (
10
).
After completing the parallel scan, we can determine the affine control law in parallel, with a time complexity of
O(1)
:

\displaystyle K_{k}

\displaystyle=-\left(R_{k}+B_{k}^{\top}P_{k+1}B_{k}\right)^{-1}\left(M_{k}+B_{k}^{\top}P_{k+1}A_{k}\right),

(11)

\displaystyle k_{k}

\displaystyle=-\left(R_{k}+B_{k}^{\top}P_{k+1}B_{k}\right)^{-1}\left[r_{k}+B_{k}^{\top}\left(p_{k+1}+P_{k+1}c_{k}\right)\right].

III-A
2
Forward Pass

We conduct a linear rollout to obtain the optimal state and input trajectories using all the feedback gains and affine terms derived in (
11
):

\displaystyle u_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle=K_{k}x_{k}^{*}+k_{k},

\displaystyle x_{k+1}^{*}

\displaystyle=A_{k}x_{k}^{*}+B_{k}u_{k}^{*}+c_{k}

\displaystyle=(A_{k}+B_{k}K_{k})x_{k}^{*}+c_{k}+B_{k}k_{k}

\displaystyle=\tilde{A}_{k}x_{k}^{*}+\tilde{c}_{k}:=f_{k}^{\text{lin}}(x_{k}^{*}),

where
\tilde{A}_{k}=A_{k}+B_{k}K_{k}
and
\tilde{c}_{k}=c_{k}+B_{k}k_{k}
.
Due to the affine structure, the parallel scan can also be applied to accelerate this process.
Let us figure out the combination rule.
We express
x_{i}^{*}
in terms of
x_{k}^{*}
for
i>k
, yielding
x_{i}^{*}=f_{k\to i}^{\text{lin}}(x_{k}^{*})=\tilde{A}_{k,i}x_{k}^{*}+\tilde{c}_{k,i}
.
The computation of
\tilde{A}_{k,i}
and
\tilde{c}_{k,i}
can be split into two parts, forming the combination rule for the forward pass:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=f_{k\to j}^{\text{lin}}(x_{k}^{*})\oplus f_{j\to i}^{\text{lin}}(x_{j}^{*})

(12)

\displaystyle=\tilde{A}_{j,i}(\tilde{A}_{k,j}x_{k}^{*}+\tilde{c}_{k,j})+\tilde{c}_{j,i},

\displaystyle\implies\tilde{A}_{k,i}

\displaystyle=\tilde{A}_{j,i}\tilde{A}_{k,j},\;\tilde{c}_{k,i}=\tilde{A}_{j,i}\tilde{c}_{k,j}+\tilde{c}_{j,i}.

(13)

Next, we define the input array for the parallel scan using the one-step dynamics
f_{k\to k+1}^{\text{lin}}(\cdot)
:

\left[f_{0\to 1}^{\text{lin}},f_{1\to 2}^{\text{lin}},\dots,f_{N-1\to N}^{\text{lin}}\right],

(14)

where for
k\in[0,N-1]
, the dynamics matrices in
f_{k\to k+1}^{\text{lin}}
are

\tilde{A}_{k\to k+1}=\tilde{A}_{k},\;\tilde{c}_{k\to k+1}=\tilde{c}_{k}.

(15)

The output array, resulting from the parallel scan, is then expressed as

[f_{0\to 1}^{\text{lin}},f_{0\to 2}^{\text{lin}},\dots,f_{0\to N}^{\text{lin}}],

(16)

where each element is given by:

\displaystyle f_{0\to i}^{\text{lin}}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{0\to 1}^{\text{lin}}\oplus f_{1\to 2}^{\text{lin}}\dots\oplus f_{i-1\to i}^{\text{lin}}=\tilde{A}_{0,i}x+\tilde{c}_{0,i}.

(17)

Finally, the optimal state trajectory
[x^{*}_{1},x^{*}_{2},\dots,x^{*}_{N}]
can be obtained by evaluating (

) at the initial state
x_{0}
.

III-B

Analytic Conditional Value Function

The remaining ingredients are the analytic forms of the conditional value function and the combination rules.
To derive them, we start by reformulating (
6

\boldsymbol{u}_{k}=(u_{n})_{n\in[k,i-1]}

The optimization problem is then

\displaystyle V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})

(18)

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}g_{q,k}\\

\displaystyle g_{k\to i}(\lambda;x_{k},x_{i})=z

\displaystyle=\min_{x_{j}}\,\left\{\max_{\lambda_{1}}\,g_{k\to j}(\lambda_{1};x_{k},x_{j})+\max_{\lambda_{2}}\,g_{j\to i}(\lambda_{2};x_{j},x_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}}\min_{x_{j}}g_{k\to j}(\lambda_{1};x_{k},x_{j})+g_{j\to i}(\lambda_{2};x_{j},x_{i})

\displaystyle=\max_{\lambda_{2}}\underbrace{\left\{\max_{\lambda_{1}}\min_{x_{j}}g_{k\to j}(\lambda_{1};x_{k},x_{j})+g_{j\to i}(\lambda_{2};x_{j},x_{i})\right\}}_{g_{k\to i}(\lambda_{2};x_{k},x_{i})}

(21)

We then derive the closed-form solution to the min-max problem within the curly brackets in (
21
) and align the resulting matrices with those in (
20
).
We present the full derivation in Appendix
-B
and summarize the resulting relationships below:

\begin{split}A_{k,i}&=-A_{j,i}(I+C_{k,j}P_{j,i})^{-1}A_{k,j},\\
c_{k,i}&=A_{j,i}(I+C_{k,j}P_{j,i})^{-1}(-c_{k,j}+C_{k,j}p_{j,i})+c_{j,i},\\
C_{k,i}&=A_{j,i}(I+C_{k,j}P_{j,i})^{-1}C_{k,j}A_{j,i}^{\top}+C_{j,i},\\
p_{k,i}&=A_{k,j}^{\top}(I+P_{j,i}C_{k,j})^{-1}(p_{j,i}+P_{j,i}b_{k,j})+p_{k,j},\\
P_{k,i}&=A_{k,j}^{\top}(I+P_{j,i}C_{k,j})^{-1}P_{j,i}A_{k,j}+P_{k,j}.\end{split}

(22)

We note that
(I+C_{k,j}P_{j,i})^{-1}
is the transpose of
(I+P_{j,i}C_{k,j})^{-1}
, meaning that only a single LU factorization is required to compute the matrices in (
22
).
Moreover,
(I+C_{k,j}P_{j,i})^{-1}
is nonsingular, as both
C_{k,j}
and
P_{j,i}
are positive definite.
Next, we discuss the one-step conditional value function
V_{k\to k+1}
, which represents the smallest subproblem and serves as an element in the input array for the parallel scan.
By considering the dual problem in (
19
) again, we can express the matrices in
V_{k\to k+1}
as

\displaystyle P_{k,k+1}

\displaystyle=Q_{k}-M_{k}R_{k}^{-1}M_{k}^{\top},

(23)

\displaystyle p_{k,k+1}

\displaystyle=q_{k}-M_{k}R_{k}^{-1}r_{k},

\displaystyle C_{k,k+1}

\displaystyle=B_{k}R_{k}^{-1}B_{k},

\displaystyle A_{k,k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{k}-B_{k}R_{k}^{-1}M_{k}^{\top},

\displaystyle c_{k,k+1}

\displaystyle=c_{k}-B_{k}R_{k}^{-1}r_{k}.

Since the cost weight matrix
R_{k}
is typically diagonal and positive definite, its inverse can be easily computed.

IV
Parallel Dense LQR

The parallel method described in the previous section is well-suited for long-horizon problems, such as
P_{1}
and
P_{1}^{i}
, but may incur significant parallelization overhead in short-horizon cases, such as
P_{2}
.
Therefore, we can alternatively solve the dense form of (
4
) obtained by eliminating the state variables.
Since we aim to solve multiple LQR problems, the condensing procedure must be performed online, unlike in
[
34
,
35
]
.
The major computational overhead consists of the condensing procedure and the factorization of the linear system.
As noted in
[
35
]
,
cuSolver

[
32
]
efficiently performs dense matrix factorization.
Therefore, the main challenge lies in the condensing process.
To optimize this process for GPU execution, we parallelize it over time.

We now delve into technical details.
The state sequence
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)n_{x}}
can be expressed in terms of the input sequence
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{u}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\Phi_{\text{p}}x_{0}+S_{\text{p}}\boldsymbol{u}+F_{\text{p}}\boldsymbol{c}

(24)

where the prediction matrices
\Phi_{\text{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)n_{x}\times n_{x}}
,
S_{\text{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)n_{x}\times Nn_{x}}
and
S_{\text{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)n_{x}\times Nn_{u}}
are derived as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}I\\
A_{0}\\
A_{1}A_{0}\\
\vdots\\
\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{0}A_{k}\end{bmatrix},F_{\text{p}}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0\\
I&0&\cdots&0\\
A_{1}&I&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{1}A_{k}&\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{2}A_{k}&\cdots&I\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle S_{\text{p}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0\\
B_{0}&0&\cdots&0\\
A_{1}B_{0}&B_{1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{1}A_{k}B_{0}&\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{2}A_{k}B_{1}&\cdots&B_{N-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Since generating these prediction matrices resembles executing a linear rollout, we can apply the parallel scan again.
To generate
\Phi_{\text{p}}
, we consider the input array
\left[I,A_{0},A_{1},\cdots,A_{N-1}\right]
,
with the combination rule defined as

A_{i}\oplus A_{j}=A_{j}A_{i}
.
For
F_{\text{p}}
, we first introduce the following auxiliary matrices:

\displaystyle F_{\text{p},0}:=\begin{bmatrix}I&I&\cdots&I\\
I&I&\cdots&I\\
A_{1}&I&\cdots&I\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&I\\
A_{1}&A_{2}&\cdots&I\end{bmatrix},F_{\text{p},\text{c}}:=\begin{bmatrix}-I&-I&\cdots&-I\\
0&-I&\cdots&-I\\
0&0&\cdots&-I\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&-I\\
0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}.

We then apply the reverse parallel scan to each block-wise row of
F_{\text{p},0}
, resulting in

\displaystyle F_{\text{p},1}:=\begin{bmatrix}I&I&\cdots&I\\
I&I&\cdots&I\\
A_{1}&I&\cdots&I\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&I\\
\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{1}A_{k}&\prod\limits_{k=N-1}^{2}A_{k}&\cdots&I\end{bmatrix}.

Note that the computation of each row is independent and can be performed in parallel.
Finally, we obtain
F_{\text{p}}
by adding
F_{\text{p},1}
and
F_{\text{p},\text{c}}
.
Subsequently,
S_{\text{p}}
is generated from
F_{\text{p}}
by multiplying the
i\text{-th}
block-wise column of
F_{\text{p}}
with
B_{i}
. Next, we let
\bar{Q}
,
\bar{R}
and
\bar{M}
denote the block-diagonal cost matrices in (
4
).
By substituting (
24
) into (
4
), we obtain the dense LQR formulation:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}}\;\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}H\boldsymbol{u}+h^{\top}\boldsymbol{u},

(25)

where
H\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{u}\times Nn_{u}}:=S_{\text{p}}^{\top}\bar{Q}S_{\text{p}}+\bar{R}+2\bar{M}S_{\text{p}}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn_{u}}:=S_{\text{p}}^{\top}\bar{q}+(\bar{M}+S_{\text{p}}^{\top}\bar{Q})F_{\text{p}}\bar{c}+\bar{r}+\left(\bar{M}\Phi_{\text{p}}+S_{\text{p}}^{\top}\bar{Q}\Phi_{\text{p}}\right)x_{0}
.
Moreover, these matrix multiplications can be optimized by exploiting the sparse structure of the cost matrices.

V
Parallel Branch MPC

In this section, we combine the methods presented in Sections
III
and
IV
to develop an efficient BMPC solver for (
3
).
We adopt an iLQR-based method, where the original problem in (
3
) is addressed by iteratively solving a sequence of LQR-Tree problems constructed by approximating the original problem around the trajectory trees
(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{u}})
obtained from the previous iteration:

\displaystyle\min_{\delta\boldsymbol{x},\delta\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}}w_{i}\left(q_{i}^{\top}\delta x_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\delta x_{i}^{\top}Q_{i}\delta x_{i}\right)+

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{L}}w_{i}\left(\begin{bmatrix}q_{i}\\
r_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\delta x_{i}\\
\delta u_{i}\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\delta x_{i}\\
\delta u_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{i}&M_{i}^{\top}\\
M_{i}&R_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta x_{i}\\
\delta u_{i}\end{bmatrix}\right)

(26a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\delta x_{i}=A_{\text{p}(i)}\delta x_{\text{p}(i)}+B_{\text{p}(i)}\delta u_{\text{p}(i)}+c_{\text{p}(i)},\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\{0\},

(26b)

where
\delta x_{i}
and
\delta u_{i}
represent the deviations from the nominal trajectory trees.
The matrices
A_{i}=\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}
and
B_{i}=\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial u_{i}}
describe the linearized system dynamics, while
c_{\text{p}(i)}=f_{\text{p}(i)}(\bar{x}_{\text{p}(i)},\bar{u}_{\text{p}(i)})-\bar{x}_{i}
denotes the dynamics defect.
The cost matrices are the gradients and Hessian matrices of the Lagrangian, evaluated along the nominal trajectory trees:

\displaystyle Q_{i}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{i}^{2}},\,R_{i}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{i}^{2}},\,M_{i}=\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{i}\partial x_{i}},\,q_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{i}},\,r_{i}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{i}},

where
\mathcal{L}
is the Lagrangian function expressed as:

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}}w_{i}\ell_{f,i}\left(x_{i}\right)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{L}}w_{i}\ell_{i}\left(x_{i},u_{i}\right)\\
+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\{0\}}\lambda_{i}^{\top}\left(x_{i}-f_{\text{p}(i)}(x_{\text{p}(i)},u_{\text{p}(i)})\right),

with
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
representing the Lagrange multiplier for the dynamics constraint.
Each LQR-tree problem can be efficiently solved through the Riccati recursion, progressing backward from the leaf nodes to the root, followed by a forward pass to recover the optimal trajectory trees.
To enhance computational efficiency, we employ the so-called parallel scan to enable temporal parallelism in both the backward and forward passes, as detailed in the following sections.

V-A

Backward Pass

We split the backward pass into two segments,
\mathcal{P}_{1}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, according to their levels of parallelism, as shown in Figure
3
.
Let
N_{b}
denote the last branching time step.

\mathcal{P}_{1}
represents the portion of the backward pass beginning from the leaf nodes and proceeding to the nodes at time step
N_{b}+1
.

\mathcal{P}_{1}
is highly parallelizable, as the parallel scan can be applied to each branch independently. In other words, the backward passes for each branch can be executed in parallel.

Once the value functions at time step
N_{b}+1
are obtained, the remaining segment
\mathcal{P}_{2}
can be solved via two approaches introduced in Sections
III
and
IV
.
First, we can apply the sequential Riccati recursion to the initial portion of the trajectory tree.
During this process, we parallelize the computation across different branches without achieving temporal parallelism.

Alternatively, we can solve the condensed LQR-Tree problem, similar to (
25
), with the Hessian
H_{\text{tree}}
and the gradient
h_{\text{tree}}
adapted to the tree structure.
We develop a condensing process that enables parallelism across both time steps and scenarios.
Specifically, we flatten the tree in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, obtaining
|\mathcal{L}|
tree paths, and independently condense the LQR problem for each tree path, producing
H^{i}
and
h^{i}
.
We then define
\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{i}:=(\delta u^{i}_{k})_{k\in[0,N_{b}]}
to represent the input sequence for
i
-th tree path, while
\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}:=(\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{i})_{i\in[1,|\mathcal{L}|]}
denotes the stacked input sequence.
The relation between the input tree
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
and the stacked input sequence
\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}
is expressed as

\delta\boldsymbol{u}=\Gamma\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},

where
\Gamma
is used to eliminate redundant variables associated with shared branches in
\delta\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}
.
Using the Hessian matrices
H^{i}
and the gradient vectors
h^{i}
for each tree path
i
, we finally derive
H_{\text{tree}}
and
h_{\text{tree}}
as follows:

\displaystyle H_{\text{tree}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{\top}\mathrm{diag}\left(\left[H^{i}\right]_{i\in[1,|\mathcal{L}|]}\right)\Gamma

\displaystyle h_{\text{tree}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{\top}\mathrm{diag}\left(\left[h^{i}\right]_{i\in[1,|\mathcal{L}|]}\right),

(27)

where
\mathrm{diag}(\,)
is a function that creates a block diagonal matrix from provided matrices.

V-B

Forward Pass

In an iLQR-based solver, a nonlinear rollout is commonly used
[
36
,
37
]
.
However, running a nonlinear rollout on GPUs is time-consuming since it cannot be parallelized easily (see Section
VI
).
To optimize computational efficiency, we employ the parallel linear rollout (see Section
III-A2
) to compute the optimal perturbations of the state and input trees,
(\delta\boldsymbol{x}^{l,k},\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{l,k})
, where the superscript
k
is the iteration index.
Our method is classified as a multi-shooting (MS) approach due to the use of the linear rollout.
After the rollout, we execute a line search to determine the best step size
\alpha\in(0,1]
.
As the linear rollout cannot guarantee the feasibility of the dynamics, we introduce a
L_{1}
merit function to balance cost reduction and constraint violation
[
27
]

[
38
, Section 18]
:

M(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u};\mu)=J(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})+\mu\lVert\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{1},

(28)

where
J(\cdot,\cdot)
is the objective function of (
3
),

\mu>0
is a penalty weight and
\boldsymbol{d}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a vector containing all the dynamics defects.
We decide if the trial step
(\alpha\delta\boldsymbol{x}^{l,k},\alpha\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{l,k})
can be accepted based on the following sufficient decrease condition:

M(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime};\mu)\leq M(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{u}^{k};\mu)+\underbrace{\beta\left(\text{EC}(\alpha)-\alpha\mu\lVert\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{u}^{k})\rVert_{1}\right)}_{\Delta M}

(29)

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}^{k}+\alpha\delta\boldsymbol{x}^{l,k}
,
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{u}^{k}+\alpha\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{l,k}
, and
\beta\in(0,1)
is a tuning parameter.
EC(
\alpha
) represents the expected cost change, which is calculated by substituting
(\delta\boldsymbol{x}^{l,k},\delta\boldsymbol{u}^{l,k})
into (
26a
).
Following
[
27
]
, we adopt an adaptive update method for
\mu
to ensure that the reduction
\text{EC}(\alpha)-\alpha\mu\lVert\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{u}^{k})\rVert_{1}
is sufficient.
The corresponding update rule is given by:

\displaystyle\mu^{k}_{\text{trial}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\text{EC}(1)}{(1-\gamma)\lVert\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{u}^{k})\rVert_{1}}+\mu_{0}\quad&\text{if}\;\lVert\boldsymbol{d}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{u}^{k})\rVert_{1}>\epsilon,\\
\mu^{k-1}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(30)

\displaystyle\mu^{k}

\displaystyle=\max(\mu^{k}_{\text{trial}},\mu^{k-1}),

(31)

where
\epsilon>0
is a small number,
\mu_{0}>0
sets a safety margin, and
\gamma\in(0,1)
is a tuning parameter.
The computation of trial steps is performed in parallel.
We then find all feasible step sizes and select the largest one following the backtracking approach in
[

Augmented Lagrangian (AL) MSiLQR-Tree

We handle the stage-wise inequality constraints in (

[

]

\rho\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

The AL MSiLQR-Tree method utilizes a double-loop structure.
The penalty weight and Lagrange multipliers are updated in the outer loop to reinforce constraint satisfaction.

VII
Conclusion

We have developed a GPU-based trajectory optimization solver tailored for a variety of BMPC problems.
This solver leverages the parallel scan algorithm to achieve temporal parallelism, while scenario-based parallelism is realized via a careful
JAX
implementation.
Our solver showcases exceptional performance in handling large-scale problems in two typical case studies of automated driving.
One limitation of the current method stems from the initial section of the trajectory tree, where parallelization is significantly constrained.
We leave the development of an efficient GPU-based method for trees with medium horizon lengths and multiple leaf nodes to future work.
In addition, we plan to implement the method in CUDA C++ to fully harness the computational power of GPUs.

The optimization problem (

\displaystyle V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})

\displaystyle=\min_{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}\max_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}g_{q,k}\\
g_{r,k}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k}\\

\boldsymbol{u}_{k}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+\lambda^{\top}\left(x_{i}-\bar{A}_{k}x_{k}-\bar{B}_{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-\bar{c}_{k}\right).

By applying the duality theorem, we interchange the order of the max and min operators, resulting in

\displaystyle V_{k\to i}(x_{k},x_{i})=\max_{\lambda}\;g_{k\rightarrow i}(\lambda;x_{k},x_{i}),

where the inner optimization is expressed as

\displaystyle g_{k\rightarrow i}(\lambda;x_{k},x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}^{\top}H_{R,k}\boldsymbol{u}_{k}-(\lambda^{\top}\bar{B}_{k}-g_{r,k}^{\top}-x_{k}^{\top}H_{M,k}^{\top})\boldsymbol{u}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}\;\mathcal{E}(\lambda,u_{k},x_{k},x_{i})

By setting
\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}_{k}}=0
, we obtain the optimal control input

\displaystyle g_{k\rightarrow i}(\lambda;x_{k},x_{i})

(37)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\chi_{k}^{\top}H_{R,k}^{-1}\chi_{k}+\lambda^{\top}(x_{i}-\bar{A}_{k}x_{k}-\bar{c}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}x_{k}^{\top}\left(H_{Q,k}-H_{M,k}^{\top}H_{R,k}^{-1}H_{M,k}\right)x_{k}

\displaystyle+\left(g_{q,k}^{\top}-g_{r,k}^{\top}H_{R,k}^{-1}H_{M,k}\right)x_{k}

We express the combination of the two conditional value functions,
