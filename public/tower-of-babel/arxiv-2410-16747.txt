Quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy revisited
Zhe Wang

Abstract
. We quantize Hamiltonian structures with hydrodynamic leading terms using the Heisenberg vertex algebra. As an application, we construct the quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy via a non-associative Weyl quantization and compute the corresponding eigenvalue problem.



1.
Introduction

Since the proof of the Witten-Kontsevich theorem
[
16
,
24
]
, integrable hierarchies have provided important tools for studying various problems in mathematical physics. For example, in 2d topological field theories, the partition functions are controlled by integrable hierarchies of evolutionary Hamiltonian PDEs
[
2
,
7
]
. In symplectic field theories
[
8
]
, the partition functions satisfy a family of Schrödinger type equations. The quantum Hamiltonians involved in these Schrödinger equations
mutually commute, and therefore can be viewed as quantum integrable hierarchies. While Hamiltonian integrable hierarchies as well as their relationships to 2d topological field theories have been studied in great detail during the last three decades, the corresponding quantum counterparts are still under investigation.

An important advancement toward a general theory of quantum integrable hierarchies was achieved by Buryak and Rossi
[
3
]
, where a quantization framework is established for arbitrary cohomological field theories using the geometry of double ramification cycles. In their constructions, quantum Hamiltonian structures are given by the canonical quantization of the classical Hamiltonian structures of hydrodynamic types, and quantum Hamiltonians are defined through the intersection theory which is specified by the given cohomological field theory. In particular, their work revealed very deep relationships between quantum integrable hierarchies and geometries of the moduli space of stable curves.

This paper approaches quantum integrable hierarchies in terms of vertex algebras by studying the quantization of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy. The quantization of such a particular integrable hierarchy was addressed both in the framework of symplectic field theory
[
8
,
21
]
and of the double ramification cycle theory
[
3
]
, and its properties were studied in a series of works
[
6
,
11
,
12
,
22
]
. In the present paper, however, a quantization framework different from above-mentioned ones is established. In particular, we realize a quantum Hamiltonian structure of hydrodynamic type as the Lie algebra canonically attached to the Heisenberg vertex algebra, and apply it to study the quantization of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy. As a result, we construct an Abelian Lie subalgebra consisting of certain states in the Heisenberg vertex algebra, and classical limits of these states coincide with classical Hamiltonian densities of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy. These states provide a quantization of the classical hierarchy, and their properties are studied in detail.

It should be emphasized that the quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy constructed in this paper looks different from the one studied in the above-mentioned papers. Both the quantum Hamiltonian structure and the quantum Hamiltonians are different. However,
despite these differences, the quantization framework based on vertex algebras shares great similarities with the framework based on geometries of moduli spaces. A comparison between these two constructions is given in Sect.
4
.

Let us illustrate the basic concepts regarding quantization of Hamiltonian integrable hierarchies and present main results of this paper. We begin by introducing necessary notations. Define the ring of differential polynomial

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}\left[v,v^{(1)},v^{(2)},\dots\right],

and denote by

\partial_{x}=\sum_{n\geq 0}v^{(n+1)}\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{(n)}},\quad v^{(0)}:=v,

a derivation on
\mathcal{A}
. It follows that we can formally view
v
as a function
v(x)
with the independent variable
x
, and we have
v^{(n)}=\partial_{x}^{n}v(x)
. Furthermore, we introduce the quotient space
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}/\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}
whose elements are called local functionals. For an element
f\in\mathcal{A}
, denote by
F=\int f
its class in
\mathcal{F}
, and we call
f
a density of
F
. Using these notions, the (classical) dispersionless KdV hierarchy can be presented by

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t_{n}}=\frac{v^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\delta H_{n+1}^{c}}{\delta v}\right),\quad H_{n}^{c}=\int\frac{v^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},\quad n\geq 0,

where the variational derivative is defined by

\frac{\delta F}{\delta v}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-\partial_{x})^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{(n)}},\quad F=\int f\in\mathcal{F}.

Note that the variational derivative of a local functional is independent of the choice of its density.

One of the most important properties of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy is that it is Hamiltonian. We define the classical Hamiltonian structure of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy to be the Lie algebra structure on
\mathcal{F}
specified by

(1.1)

\left\{F,G\right\}=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta v}\partial_{x}\frac{\delta G}{\delta v}.

For a given local functional
F
, this Lie bracket induces a derivation on
\mathcal{A}
given by

\{-,F\}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A},\quad\{g,F\}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\partial g}{\partial v^{(n)}}\partial_{x}^{n+1}\frac{\delta F}{\delta v},\quad g\in\mathcal{A}.

It follows from the definition that for any
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
and any density
g\in\mathcal{A}
of
G
, we have

\int\{g,F\}=\{G,F\},

hence we abuse the notation and use the same bracket to denote both the Lie bracket on
\mathcal{F}
and its induced action on
\mathcal{A}
. Using these notations, the dispersionless KdV hierarchy can be represented by the following Hamiltonian form:

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t_{n}}=\{v,H_{n}^{c}\}.

We call
H_{n}^{c}
the classical Hamiltonians of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy. They are in involution, namely they span an Abelian Lie subalgebra of
\mathcal{F}
:

\{H_{n}^{c},H_{m}^{c}\}=0,\quad n,m\geq 0.

The Hamiltonian property of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy enables us to formulate its deformation quantization as follows:

(1)

Construct a Lie algebra bracket
[-,-]
on
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
which deforms the classical Hamiltonian structure in the sense that

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{1}{\hbar}[F,G]=\{F,G\},\quad F,G\in\mathcal{F}.

This Lie algebra structure is called the quantum Hamiltonian structure.

(2)

Deform the classical Hamiltonians
H_{n}^{c}
into quantum Hamiltonians
H_{n}\in\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
such that

[H_{n},H_{m}]=0,\quad\lim_{\hbar\to 0}H_{n}=H_{n}^{c},\quad n,m\geq 0.

We call the quantum Hamiltonians
H_{n}
as well as the quantum Hamiltonian structure a quantization of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy.

(3)

Solve the corresponding Schrödinger equations, i.e., determine a representation of the Lie algebra
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
and study the eigenvalue problem of
H_{n}
.

In this paper, we study all these three procedures in a unified framework based on the theory of vertex algebras.
Let us denote by
V
the state space of the Heisenberg vertex algebra generated by the state
b\in V
, whose precise definition and related properties will be given in Sect.
2.1
(or see Chapter 2 of
[
9
]
). Associated with
b
, there is a formal power series of operators forming a basis of the Heisenberg Lie algebra:

Y(b,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{(n)}z^{-n-1},\quad b_{(n)}\in\mathrm{End}(V),\quad\left[b_{(n)},b_{(m)}\right]=\hbar\,n\,\delta_{m+n,0},

here the Lie bracket is the commutator on
\mathrm{End}(V)
.
Denote by
|0\rangle\in V
the vacuum state, then the state space
V
is a free
\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
-module spanned by states of the form

b_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle,\quad k_{i}\geq 0,\quad n\geq 0.

For each state
a\in V
, it follows from the definition of a vertex algebra that we have a corresponding field
Y(a,z)
given by

Y(a,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(n)}z^{-n-1},\quad a_{(n)}\in\mathrm{End}(V),

where
a_{(n)}
is called the
n
-th mode of
a
. In particular, the
n
-th mode of the state
b
is exactly
b_{(n)}
.
The general formula for
a_{(n)}
in terms of modes of
b
is given in Prop.
2.2
. We view the unknown function
v(x)
of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy as a classical field, and we treat
Y(b,z)
as the corresponding quantum field. It follows that there is a natural
\hbar
-linear isomorphism of vector spaces

\varphi:\mathcal{A}[\hbar]\to V,\quad v^{(k_{1})}\dots v^{(k_{n})}\mapsto k_{1}!\dots k_{n}!\,b_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle.

Let
T\in\mathrm{End}(V)
be the infinitesimal translation operator of the Heisenberg vertex algebra, which is characterized by

T|0\rangle=0,\quad\left[T,b_{(n)}\right]=-nb_{(n-1)},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Hence, it follows that

\varphi\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\partial_{x}=T\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi,

where the action of
\partial_{x}
is extended to
\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
by
\partial_{x}\hbar=0
. As a result, the isomorphism
\varphi
induces a natural isomorphism between the quotient spaces
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
and
V/TV
which we still denote by
\varphi
.

Using the above notations, we present here the main results of the paper. First, we construct a quantum Hamiltonian structure based on the vertex algebra structure.

Theorem 1.1
.

The Lie bracket

(1.2)

[F,G]=\varphi^{-1}\left(\varphi(G)_{(0)}\varphi(F)\right),\quad F,G\in\mathcal{F}[\hbar],

defines a deformation quantization of the Hamiltonian structure (
1.1
), here
\varphi(G)_{(0)}
is the zeroth mode of the state
\varphi(G)
. In terms of differential polynomials, this bracket is of the form

\displaystyle[F,G]=\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\hbar^{m}}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\geq 0\\
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 0\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{m}f}{\partial v^{(s_{1})}\dots\partial v^{(s_{m})}}\frac{(-1)^{\sum r_{i}}\prod(r_{i}+s_{i}+1)!}{(2m-1+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i})!}

\displaystyle\cdot\partial_{x}^{2m-1+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}}\frac{\partial^{m}g}{\partial v^{(r_{1})}\dots\partial v^{(r_{m})}},\quad F,G\in\mathcal{F},

here
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
are arbitrary densities of
F
and
G
, respectively.

Next, we construct the quantum Hamiltonians
H_{n}
for the quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy. Let us denote by
h_{n}\in\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
the differential polynomials determined by the recursion relation

h_{0}=\varphi^{-1}\left(b_{(-1)}|0\rangle\right),\quad h_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\binom{n+1}{k+1}}\varphi^{-1}\left(\varphi(h_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(h_{n-k-1})\right),\quad n\geq 1.

We call this a non-associative Weyl quantization. Very roughly speaking,
h_{n}
is obtained by averaging the non-associativity of the Heisenberg vertex algebra. See Sect.
3.1
for details and a combinatorial way for constructing
h_{n}
. Though these quantum Hamiltonians are defined via the Heisenberg vertex algebra, it turns out that they can be computed effectively.

Proposition 1.2
.

We have
h_{0}=v
and for
n\geq 1

(1.3)

\displaystyle h_{n}=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{s,t\geq 0}\frac{(s+t+1)!}{s!t!}v^{(s)}v^{(t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{(s+t)}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\hbar\sum_{s,t\geq 0}\frac{(s+1)!(t+1)!}{(s+t+2)!}v^{(s+t+2)}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial v^{(s)}\partial v^{(t)}}\right)h_{n-1}.

It follows that differential polynomials
h_{n}
indeed define quantum Hamiltonians, and hence we construct a quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy.

Theorem 1.3
.

Denote by
H_{n}=\int h_{n}
, then

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}H_{n}=H_{n}^{c},\quad\left[H_{n},H_{m}\right]=0,\quad n,m\geq 0,

here the bracket is the quantum Hamiltonian structure defined in (
1.2
).

Finally, we consider the eigenvalue problem. As discussed before, we need to fix a representation of the quantum Hamiltonian structure. One of the motivations for constructing quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy using a vertex algebra is the observation that any vertex algebra has a natural representation on its state space. Indeed, for any state
a\in V
, their modes
a_{(n)}
are already endomorphisms on
V
. Since the state space is identified with the space
\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
of differential polynomials, it follows that there is a family of actions on
\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
for any given
f\in\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
, as described by the following proposition.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
be a differential polynomial. Then for
n\geq 0
, the
n
-th mode of the state
\varphi(f)
induces an endomorphism on
\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
which reads

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi(f)_{(n)}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi=\sum_{m\geq 1}

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{m}}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\geq 0\\
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 0\\
2m-1+\sum r_{i}+s_{i}\geq n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\sum r_{i}}\prod(r_{i}+s_{i}+1)!}{(2m-1-n+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i})!}

\displaystyle\cdot\partial_{x}^{2m-1-n+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial^{m}f}{\partial v^{(r_{1})}\dots\partial v^{(r_{m})}}\right)\frac{\partial^{m}}{\partial v^{(s_{1})}\dots\partial v^{(s_{m})}}.

It follows that we can associate a family of operators for each
h_{n}
. To consider the eigenvalue problem it is essential to choose operators

\hat{H}_{n}=\varphi^{-1}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi(h_{n})_{(n)}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi\in\mathrm{End}(\mathcal{A})[\hbar]

corresponding to the
n
-th mode of the state
\varphi(h_{n})
for
n\geq 0
, and the reason for this specific choice is explained in Sect.
3.3.3
. We show that operators
\hat{H}_{n}
mutually commute with each other, and their common eigenvectors are given by Schur polynomials, which are defined by

S_{\lambda}=\det{\left({\bf{e}}_{\lambda_{i}-i+j}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell(\lambda)}}\in\mathcal{A}[\sqrt{\hbar}],

here
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)})
with
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}>0
is an integer partition of length
\ell(\lambda)
, and
{\bf{e}}_{n}\in\mathcal{A}[\sqrt{\hbar}]
is determined by the generating series

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}{\bf{e}}_{n}z^{n}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\hbar^{k/2}v^{(k)}}{(k+1)!}z^{k+1}\right).

Theorem 1.5
.

Schur polynomials
S_{\lambda}
, where
\lambda
runs over all integer partitions, serve as a complete set of common eigenvectors of operators
\hat{H}_{n}
. More precisely, we have

\hat{H}_{n}S_{\lambda}=\frac{\hbar^{\frac{n+1}{2}}}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n+1}{k}\left(\binom{-\ell(\lambda)}{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell(\lambda)}\binom{\lambda_{i}-i}{k}\right)S_{\lambda},\quad\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)}).

In studying these properties, we also arrive at the following explicit formula relating
h_{n}
to Schur polynomials:

Proposition 1.6
.

We have

h_{n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}S_{(n-k+1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k})}.

2.
Heisenberg vertex algebra and quantum Hamiltonian structure

2.1.
Basics of vertex algebra

Let us review some basic facts about vertex algebras, and we refer to
[
9
]
for details. The paper
[
5
]
also serves as a reference for the topic.

A vertex algebra is given by the data
\left(V,|0\rangle,T,Y(-,z)\right)
, where
V
is a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
called the state space,
|0\rangle\in V
is a special vector called the vacuum vector,
T\in\mathrm{End}(V)
is a linear operator called the infinitesimal translation operator and
Y(-,z)\colon V\to\mathrm{End}(V)[[z^{\pm 1}]]
is a linear morphism called the state-field correspondence. The state-field correspondence associates to each state
a\in V
operators
a_{(n)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
given by

Y(a,z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{(n)}z^{-n-1},\quad a_{(n)}\in\mathrm{End}(V).

We call
a_{(n)}
the
n
-th mode of
a
. The data
\left(V,|0\rangle,T,Y(-,z)\right)
of a vertex algebra are required to satisfy the following axioms:

•

|0\rangle_{(n)}=\delta_{n,-1}\mathrm{Id}_{V}
and for any
a\in V
, the action of
a_{(n)}
on the vacuum vector is given by

a_{(n)}|0\rangle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{for }n\geq 0,\\
\delta_{n,-1}a,&\text{for }n\leq-1.\end{cases}

•

T|0\rangle=0
and for any
a\in V
,
\left[T,a_{(n)}\right]=-na_{(n-1)}
and
Y(Ta,z)=\partial_{z}Y(a,z)
.

•

For any
a,b\in V
, there exists a positive integer
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that

(z-w)^{N}[Y(a,z),Y(b,w)]=0.

A convenient tool for actual computation of vertex algebras is the so-called
\lambda
-bracket formalism (
[
4
,
15
]
). For
a,b\in V
, we denote

[a_{\lambda}b]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}a_{(n)}b.

It can be proved that the
\lambda
-brackets are polynomials in
\lambda
with coefficients being states in
V
. They satisfy the following properties, where many useful identities for vertex algebras are encoded:

Sesquilinearity:

[Ta_{\lambda}b]=-\lambda[a_{\lambda}b]
, and
[a_{\lambda}Tb]=(T+\lambda)[a_{\lambda}b]
.

Skewsymmetry:

[b_{\lambda}a]=-[a_{-\lambda-T}b]
.

Jacobi identity:

[a_{\lambda}[b_{\mu}c]]=[b_{\mu}[a_{\lambda}c]]+[[a_{\lambda}b]_{\lambda+\mu}c]
.

For
a,b\in V
, we denote by
:ab:
the normal order product, which is defined to be the state
a_{(-1)}b
. For general vertex algebras, the normal order product is neither commutative nor associative. It satisfies the following quasi-commutativity and quasi-associativity (
[
5
]
):

(2.1)

\displaystyle:ab:-:ba:=\int_{-T}^{0}d\lambda\,[a_{\lambda}b],

(2.2)

\displaystyle::ab:c:-:a:bc::=:\left(\int_{0}^{T}d\lambda\,a\right)[b_{\lambda}c]:+:\left(\int_{0}^{T}d\lambda\,b\right)[a_{\lambda}c]:.

To compute the right-hand sides of the above formulae, we must put
\lambda
on the left under the sign of integral, and act the operators obtained from the definite integral to the right. For example, we have

\int_{-T}^{0}d\lambda\,[a_{\lambda}b]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\int_{-T}^{0}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}d\lambda\right)\left(a_{(n)}b\right)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{n}T^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\left(a_{(n)}b\right).

When computing the
\lambda
-bracket of normal order product, we can use the following useful formula:

(2.3)

[a_{\lambda}:bc:]=:[a_{\lambda}b]c:+:b[a_{\lambda}c]:+\int_{0}^{\lambda}[[a_{\lambda}b]_{\mu}c]d\mu.

Another standard fact about the normal order product is that

(2.4)

Y(:ab:,z)=Y(a,z)_{+}Y(b,z)+Y(b,z)Y(a,z)_{-},

where we set

Y(a,z)_{+}=\sum_{n<0}a_{(n)}z^{-n-1},\quad Y(a,z)_{-}=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{(n)}z^{-n-1}.

In this paper, we will only consider the Heisenberg vertex algebra which we define now. Recall that a Heisenberg Lie algebra of rank
N
is a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space spanned by
b^{\alpha}_{(n)}
with
\alpha=1,\dots,N
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, whose Lie bracket is specified by

(2.5)

\left[b^{\alpha}_{(n)},b^{\beta}_{(m)}\right]=\hbar\,\eta^{\alpha\beta}\,n\,\delta_{m+n,0},\quad\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N,\quad n,m\in\mathbb{Z},

where
(\eta^{\alpha\beta})
is a constant non-degenerate symmetric matrix and
\hbar
is a formal parameter.

Remark 2.1
.

Strictly speaking, the underlying space of the Heisenberg Lie algebra we define here is a free
\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
-module rather than a vector space. The only reason for the introduction of the parameter
\hbar
is to make sense of classical limits by taking
\hbar\to 0
. In particular, all the definitions and axioms of vertex algebras make sense if the state space is a free
\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
-module and all endomorphisms are required to be
\hbar
-linear.

We proceed to define the Heisenberg vertex algebra. First define the state space
V
to be the free
\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
-module spanned by states of the form

b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle,\quad n,k_{i}\geq 0,\quad\alpha_{i}=1,\dots,N.

In particular, we have the vacuum vector
|0\rangle\in V
, and we view
b^{\alpha}_{(n)}
as endomorphisms on
V
whose actions are determined by the commutation relation (
2.5
) and
b^{\alpha}_{(n)}|0\rangle=0
for
n\geq 0
. As an example, we have

b^{\alpha}_{(n)}\left(b^{\beta}_{(-k-1)}|0\rangle\right)=\begin{cases}b^{\alpha}_{(n)}b^{\beta}_{(-k-1)}|0\rangle,&n<0\\
\hbar\eta^{\alpha\beta}n\delta_{n-k-1,0}|0\rangle,&n\geq 0.\end{cases}

Note that we have
b^{\alpha}_{(0)}=0
. We define the infinitesimal translation operator
T
similarly by specifying its action, which is determined by
T|0\rangle=0
and the commutation relation
\left[T,b^{\alpha}_{(n)}\right]=-nb^{\alpha}_{(n-1)}
. Finally, we define
the state-field correspondence
Y(-,z)
by specifying the modes of each state. For a state
b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle
, its modes are defined recursively by induction on
n

[
1
]
:

\displaystyle|0\rangle_{(i)}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i,-1}\mathrm{Id}_{V},

\displaystyle(b^{\alpha}_{(i)}a)_{(j)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}(-1)^{\ell}\binom{i}{\ell}\left(b^{\alpha}_{(i-\ell)}a_{(j+\ell)}-(-1)^{i}a_{(i+j-\ell)}b^{\alpha}_{(\ell)}\right),\quad i,j\in\mathbb{Z}.

From now on, we will denote by
b^{\alpha}
the state
b^{\alpha}_{(-1)}|0\rangle
, and it follows that

Y(b^{\alpha},z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}b^{\alpha}_{(n)}z^{-n-1}.

Lemma 2.1
.

The date
(V,|0\rangle,T,Y(-,z))
described above define a vertex algebra called the Heisenberg vertex algebra of rank
N
.

Proof.

We refer to Chapter 2 of
[
9
]
for a proof.
∎

For the Heisenberg vertex algebra, the modes of states admit particularly nice formulae in terms of the normal ordering which we shall define now. We call
b^{\alpha}_{(n)}
an annihilator if
n\geq 0
and a creator if
n<0
. Then, the normal ordering is defined for a sequence of modes of
b^{\alpha}
, which is denoted by
:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(k_{n})}:
, and its action is given by placing all annihilators on the right of all creators. It is important to note that the normal ordering is well-defined because creators commute with each other, so do annihilators. Now we proceed to give an explicit formula for the state-field correspondence of the Heisenberg vertex algebra.

Proposition 2.2

(Remark 2.2.6 of
[
9
]
)
.

For
n\geq 0
,
k_{1},\dots k_{n}\geq 0
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}=1,\dots,N
, let us denote

a=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle\in V,

then we have

Y\left(a,z\right)=(-1)^{\sum k_{i}}\sum_{m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}}\binom{m_{1}}{k_{1}}\dots\binom{m_{n}}{k_{n}}:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:z^{-n-\sum m_{i}}.

Proof.

Let us prove by induction on
n
. The case
n=0
trivially holds true. Assume that we have proved the proposition for some
n\geq 0
, and let us consider the state

a=b^{\alpha}_{(-k-1)}b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle\in V

for some
k\geq 0
. First, it follows from
Y(T^{k}b^{\alpha},z)=\partial^{k}_{z}Y(b^{\alpha},z)
that

b^{\alpha}_{(-k-1)}=\frac{1}{k!}(T^{k}b^{\alpha})_{(-1)},

which implies that

a=\frac{1}{k!}:(T^{k}b^{\alpha})\left(b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle\right):.

The field corresponding to
a
is then computed by using the identity (
2.4
), and we arrive at the following expression for
Y(a,z)
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}(-1)^{k+\sum k_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m<0\end{subarray}}&\binom{m}{k}\binom{m_{1}}{k_{1}}\dots\binom{m_{n}}{k_{n}}\\
&\cdot b^{\alpha}_{(m-k)}\left(:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:\right)z^{-n-\sum m_{i}-m-1}\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}+(-1)^{k+\sum k_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
m\geq 0\end{subarray}}&\binom{m}{k}\binom{m_{1}}{k_{1}}\dots\binom{m_{n}}{k_{n}}\\
&\cdot\left(:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:\right)b^{\alpha}_{(m-k)}z^{-n-\sum m_{i}-m-1}.\end{split}

In the first summation, since we have
m-k<0
, it follows from the definition of the normal ordering that

b^{\alpha}_{(m-k)}\left(:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:\right)\,=\,:b^{\alpha}_{(m-k)}b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:,\quad m<0.

In the second summation, terms for
0\leq m\leq k-1
vanish due to the fact that the binomial coefficient
\binom{m}{k}=0
, and for
m\geq k
we have

\left(:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:\right)b^{\alpha}_{(m-k)}=:b^{\alpha}_{(m-k)}b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:.

Therefore, we conclude that
Y(a,z)
is indeed of the desired form and the proposition is proved.
∎

Using the above proposition as well as the definition of the normal ordering, we arrive at the following description of modes.

Corollary 2.3
.

Fix a state
a=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle
for some
n\geq 0
and
k_{i}\geq 0
, its modes are given by

a_{(m)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}\leq\dots\leq j_{n}\\
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}\end{subarray}}C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}}b^{\beta_{1}}_{(j_{1})}\dots b^{\beta_{n}}_{(j_{n})}\in\mathrm{End}(V),\quad m\in\mathbb{Z},

here
C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}}
are some integers depending on both the state
a
and
m
, where the indices
\beta_{i}
run over
1,\dots,N
and
j_{i}
run over
\mathbb{Z}
.

Finally, let us recall the conformal degree of the Heisenberg vertex algebra. For a state

a=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle\in V,

we call the integer

\Delta_{a}=n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}

the conformal weight of
a
. Let us denote by
V^{\Delta}
the subspace of
V
consisting of homogeneous states of conformal weight
\Delta
, and we set
V^{\Delta}=0
if
\Delta<0
. An endomorphism is called of conformal degree
d
if it maps
V^{\Delta}
to
V^{\Delta+d}
for any
\Delta\geq 0
. For example, it is easy to see that the mode
b^{\alpha}_{(n)}
is of conformal degree
-n
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
a\in V^{\Delta}
, then
a_{(n)}
is of conformal degree
\Delta-n-1
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In particular, if
a\in V^{\Delta_{1}}
and
b\in V^{\Delta_{2}}
, then
:ab:\in V^{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

2.2.
Quantum Hamiltonian structure

In this section, we construct a deformation quantization of Hamiltonian structures of hydrodynamic types using the Heisenberg vertex algebra. To this end, we first recall the notion of a (classical) Hamiltonian structure, and we refer to
[
17
]
for an introduction to the general theory of Hamiltonian structures.

Let
M
be an
N
-dimensional smooth manifold, and let us denote by
\mathcal{A}
the ring of differential polynomials of the infinite jet bundle
J^{\infty}M
. Locally, if we choose a coordinate system
(v^{1},\dots,v^{N})
of
M
, then

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}\left[v^{\alpha,p}\colon\alpha=1,\dots,N;\,p\geq 0\right].

From now on, we will fix such a local coordinate system. Define a derivation
\partial_{x}
of
\mathcal{A}
by

\partial_{x}=\sum_{p\geq 0}v^{\alpha,p+1}\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{\alpha,p}},

here and henceforth we assume the summation over repeated lower and upper Greek indices. Then we denote by
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}/\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}
the space of local functionals, and for
f\in\mathcal{A}
, we denote by
\int f
its class in
\mathcal{F}
. The ring
\mathcal{A}
is graded by the differential degree defined by

\deg_{\partial_{x}}v^{\alpha,p}=p,

and we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{d}
the space of homogeneous differential polynomials of differential degree
d
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the literature, the ring of differential polynomials are defined by a certain completion of
\mathcal{A}
. For our purpose, this completion is not needed.

A Hamiltonian structure is a Lie algebra structure on
\mathcal{F}
whose Lie bracket is of the following special form:

\{F,G\}=\int\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\delta F}{\delta v^{\alpha}}P^{\alpha\beta}_{k}\partial_{x}^{k}\frac{\delta G}{\delta v^{\beta}},\quad P^{\alpha\beta}_{k}\in\mathcal{A},\quad\forall F,G\in\mathcal{F},

here the variational derivatives are defined by

\frac{\delta F}{\delta v^{\alpha}}=\sum_{p\geq 0}(-\partial_{x})^{p}\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{\alpha,p}},\quad F=\int f.

Let us consider the following special Hamiltonian structure which is called the Hamiltonian structure of hydrodynamic type:

(2.6)

\{F,G\}=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta v^{\alpha}}\eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{x}\frac{\delta G}{\delta v^{\beta}},

where
(\eta^{\alpha\beta})
is a non-degenerate symmetric constant matrix. It is proved
[
10
]
that the bracket (
2.6
) serves as a universal local model for Hamiltonian structures with hydrodynamic leading terms. Such Hamiltonian structures are ubiquitous in the theory of integrable hierarchies. For example, all Dubrovin-Zhang hierarchies
[
7
]
and all double ramification hierarchies
[
2
]
possess Hamiltonian structures of these kinds.

Let us fix a Hamiltonian structure of the form (
2.6
) and let
V
be the state space of the Heisenberg vertex algebra of rank
N
as described in Sect.
2.1
. Recall that we have states
b^{1},\dots,b^{N}\in V
whose modes satisfy the commutation relation

\left[b^{\alpha}_{(n)},b^{\beta}_{(m)}\right]=\hbar\,\eta^{\alpha\beta}\,n\,\delta_{m+n,0},\quad\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,N,\quad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

The key observation is that we can identify the state space
V
and the space of differential polynomials
\mathcal{A}
via the
\hbar
-linear map given by

(2.7)

\varphi:\mathcal{A}[\hbar]\to V\colon v^{\alpha_{1},k_{1}}\dots v^{\alpha_{n},k_{n}}\mapsto k_{1}!\dots k_{n}!b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle.

Lemma 2.5
.

The map
\varphi
defined in (
2.7
) is an isomorphism between vector spaces. For any
f\in\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
, we have

(2.8)

\varphi\left(\partial_{x}f\right)=T\varphi\left(f\right),

and for any
p\geq 0
, we have

(2.9)

\varphi\left(v^{\alpha,p}f\right)=p!\,b^{\alpha}_{(-p-1)}\varphi(f),\quad\hbar\,\eta^{\alpha\beta}\varphi\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial v^{\beta,p}}\right)=\frac{1}{(p+1)!}b^{\alpha}_{(p+1)}\varphi(f).

Proof.

These facts are straightforward to verify, and we omit the details.
∎

It follows from (
2.8
) that
\varphi
induces an isomorphism between quotient spaces
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
and
V/TV
, and we will still denote this isomorphism by
\varphi
. It is well-known that
V/TV
admits a natural Lie algebra structure given by
0
-th modes.

Lemma 2.6

(Theorem 4.1.2 of
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\pi\colon V\to V/TV
be the projection map. Then the bracket

(2.10)

[A,B]=\pi\left(\pi^{-1}(B)_{(0)}\pi^{-1}(A)\right),\quad A,B\in V/TV

is well-defined, and defines a Lie algebra structure on
V/TV
.

Via the isomorphism
\varphi
, the above bracket on
V/TV
can be identified to give a Lie bracket on
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
. Our first goal is to compute this bracket in terms of differential polynomials explicitly and hence to prove that this bracket gives a deformation quantization of the Hamiltonian structure (
2.6
). For this purpose, let us compute the modes
\varphi(f)_{(n)}
for
f\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\geq 0
.

Recall that
V
is a free
\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
-module, hence we can decompose

V=\bigoplus_{n\geq 0}V_{n},\quad V_{n}=\hbar^{n}V_{0},

where
V_{0}
is the
\mathbb{C}
-vector space spanned by states of the form
b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle
. Then it is clear that for
a=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle\in V_{0}
, we have
(\hbar^{m}a)_{(n)}=\hbar^{m}\cdot a_{(n)}
, and furthermore the modes of
a
can be decomposed as

a_{(n)}=\sum_{i\geq 0}a_{(n)}^{[i]},\quad a_{(n)}^{[i]}\colon V_{0}\to V_{i},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

It follows from Corollary
2.3
that these modes are of the forms

a_{(n)}=\sum_{j_{1}\leq\dots\leq j_{n}}C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}}b^{\beta_{1}}_{(j_{1})}\dots b^{\beta_{n}}_{(j_{n})},

here the coefficients
C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}}
are constant numbers independent of
\hbar
. Then the
\hbar
-dependence of modes is given by the commutation relation (
2.5
), hence we arrive at

(2.11)

a_{(n)}^{[i]}=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in J^{i}}C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}}b^{\beta_{1}}_{(j_{1})}\dots b^{\beta_{n}}_{(j_{n})},

where we use
J^{i}
to denote the index set

\{(j_{1},\dots,j_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\colon j_{1}\leq\dots\leq j_{n-i}\leq 0\leq j_{n-i+1}\leq\dots\leq j_{n}\}.

Lemma 2.7
.

Fix a state
a=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}|0\rangle\in V_{0}
for some
n\geq 0
and
k_{i}\geq 0
. Then we have

a_{(-1)}^{[0]}=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
2.2
that

a_{(-1)}=(-1)^{\sum k_{i}}\sum_{m_{1}+\dots+m_{n}=-n}\binom{m_{1}}{k_{1}}\dots\binom{m_{n}}{k_{n}}:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:,

and by further using (
2.11
), we arrive at

a_{(-1)}^{[0]}=(-1)^{\sum k_{i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}+\dots+m_{n}=-n,\\
m_{i}\leq k_{i}\end{subarray}}\binom{m_{1}}{k_{1}}\dots\binom{m_{n}}{k_{n}}:b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(m_{1}-k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(m_{n}-k_{n})}:.

Since
b^{\alpha_{i}}_{(0)}=0
, it follows that the non-vanishing summand is with indices
m_{i}\leq-1
. Therefore, the only non-vanishing summand is given by the term with indices
m_{1}=\dots=m_{n}=-1
, namely,

a_{(-1)}^{[0]}=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{n}}_{(-k_{n}-1)}.

The lemma is proved.
∎

We have the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 2.8
.

For
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
, we have

\varphi(f)_{(-1)}^{[0]}\varphi(g)=\varphi(fg).

Proof.

This follows directly from the definition of
\varphi
. The corollary is proved.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
be two differential polynomials. Then for
n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}\left(\varphi(f)_{(n)}\varphi(g)\right)=\sum_{m\geq 1}

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{m}}{m!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\geq 0\\
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 0\\
2m-1+\sum r_{i}+s_{i}\geq n\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\sum r_{i}}\prod(r_{i}+s_{i}+1)!\eta^{\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}}{(2m-1-n+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i})!}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{\partial^{m}g}{v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}}}\partial_{x}^{2m-1-n+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial^{m}f}{\partial v^{\alpha_{1},r_{1}}\dots\partial v^{\alpha_{m},r_{m}}}\right).

Proof.

By a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
2.7
, we see that
\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[0]}=0
for
n\geq 0
. Therefore, we only need to consider
\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}
for
m\geq 1
. Without loss of generality, we simply assume

\varphi(f)=b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(-k_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\alpha_{\ell}}_{(-k_{\ell}-1)}|0\rangle

for some
\ell\geq 0
and
k_{i}\geq 0
.
Using (
2.11
), we may write

\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}=\sum_{(j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell})\in J^{m}}C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\ell}}b^{\beta_{1}}_{(j_{1})}\dots b^{\beta_{\ell}}_{(j_{\ell})},\quad C^{j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell}}_{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{\ell}}\in\mathbb{Z}.

It is more convenient to
separate annihilators and creators in the above expression, and rewrite it into the form

\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}=\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}>0}A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}b^{\alpha_{1}}_{(k_{1})}\dots b^{\alpha_{m}}_{(k_{m})},\quad A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\in\mathrm{End}(V),

here
A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}
are operators given by linear combinations of creators of the form

b^{\beta_{1}}_{(-j_{1}-1)}\dots b^{\beta_{\ell-m}}_{(-j_{\ell-m}-1)},\quad j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell-m}\geq 0.

Since annihilators commute with each other, we may and do fix each operator
A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}
uniquely by requiring that it is symmetric with respect to indices
(\alpha_{1},k_{1}),\dots,(\alpha_{m},k_{m})
.
Therefore, by using (
2.9
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}\varphi(g)=\hbar^{m}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}>0}

\displaystyle k_{1}!\dots k_{m}!\,\eta^{\alpha_{1}\gamma_{1}}\dots\eta^{\alpha_{m}\gamma_{m}}

\displaystyle\cdot A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\varphi\left(\frac{\partial^{m}g}{\partial v^{\gamma_{1},k_{1}-1}\dots\partial v^{\gamma_{m},k_{m}-1}}\right).

In particular, it follows that

\displaystyle\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}\varphi(v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}-1}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}-1})=

\displaystyle\hbar^{m}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}>0}k_{1}!\dots k_{m}!\,\eta^{\alpha_{1}\gamma_{1}}\dots\eta^{\alpha_{m}\gamma_{m}}

\displaystyle\cdot A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\delta^{\beta_{\sigma(i)}}_{\gamma_{i}}\delta_{k_{i},s_{\sigma(i)}}|0\rangle

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\hbar^{m}m!\,s_{1}!\dots s_{m}!\,\eta^{\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}}\dots\eta^{\alpha_{m}\beta_{m}}A^{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}|0\rangle.

Note that we have used the fact that
A^{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}}_{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}}
is symmetric with respect to indices. Hence, by using Lemma
2.7
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}\varphi(g)=\frac{\hbar^{m}}{m!}\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 0}

\displaystyle\left(\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}\varphi(v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}})\right)_{(-1)}^{[0]}

\displaystyle\cdot\varphi\left(\frac{\partial^{m}g}{\partial v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots\partial v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}}}\right).

It follows from the skew-symmetry property of the
\lambda
-bracket that for general
A,B\in V
,

A_{(n)}B=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{k+n+1}}{k!}T^{k}\left(B_{(k+n)}A\right),

hence to further simplify the above expression for
\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}\varphi(g)
we only need to compute

\varphi(v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}})_{(k+n)}^{[m]}\varphi(f),\quad k\geq 0.

By using the definition (
2.7
) of
\varphi
and Proposition
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi(

\displaystyle v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}})_{(k+n)}^{[m]}=(-1)^{\sum s_{i}}s_{1}!\dots s_{m}!

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\geq 0\\
\sum n_{i}+\sum s_{i}+2m=k+n+1\end{subarray}}\binom{n_{1}+s_{1}+1}{s_{1}}\dots\binom{n_{m}+s_{m}+1}{s_{m}}b^{\beta_{1}}_{(n_{1}+1)}\dots b^{\beta_{m}}_{(n_{m}+1)}.

Then after a straightforward computation, we arrive at

\displaystyle\varphi(f)_{(n)}^{[m]}\varphi(v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\geq 0\\
\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}+2m\geq n+1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\sum r_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(r_{i}+s_{i}+1)!\eta^{\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}}{\left(\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}+2m-n-1\right)!}

\displaystyle\cdot T^{\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}+2m-n-1}\left(\varphi\left(\frac{\partial^{m}f}{\partial v^{\alpha_{1},r_{1}}\dots\partial v^{\alpha_{m},r_{m}}}\right)\right).

Finally, we arrive at the desired formula by using the identity (
2.8
) and Corollary
2.8
. The theorem is proved.
∎

Recall that we have a Lie bracket on
V/TV
defined by the formula (
2.10
). By identifying
V/TV
with
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
via
\varphi
, it follows from the above theorem that we have a Lie bracket on
\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
defined by

\displaystyle[F,G]=\int\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\hbar^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\geq 0\\
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\sum r_{i}}\prod(r_{i}+s_{i}+1)!\eta^{\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}}}{(2m-1+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i})!}

\displaystyle\cdot\frac{\partial^{m}f}{v^{\beta_{1},s_{1}}\dots v^{\beta_{m},s_{m}}}\partial_{x}^{2m-1+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial^{m}g}{\partial v^{\alpha_{1},r_{1}}\dots\partial v^{\alpha_{m},r_{m}}}\right),

where
f,g\in\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
are arbitrary choices of densities of
F
and
G
, respectively. This Lie bracket defines a deformation quantization of the Hamiltonian structure (
2.6
) in the sense that

(2.12)

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}\frac{1}{\hbar}[F,G]=\{F,G\},\quad F,G\in\mathcal{F}.

In particular, we have proved Theorem
1.1
and Proposition
1.4
as special cases of Theorem
2.9
for
N=1
and
\eta=1
.

As an application of the above formula, let us prove the following interesting fact which will be used later.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
a\in V
be a state with
a_{(0)}=0
. Then there exist polynomials
B_{\alpha}(\hbar),C(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
such that

a=B_{\alpha}(\hbar)b^{\alpha}+C(\hbar)|0\rangle+TV.

Proof.

Let us denote by
f=\varphi^{-1}(a)\in\mathcal{A}[\hbar]
, and we decompose

f=\sum_{n\geq 0}\hbar^{n}f_{n},\quad f_{n}\in\mathcal{A}.

The assumption
a_{(0)}=0
implies that for any
g\in\mathcal{A}
,
\varphi(f)_{(0)}\varphi(g)=0
.
Then by taking the
\hbar
-coefficient of
\varphi(f)_{(0)}\varphi(g)
, we arrive at

(2.13)

\sum_{r,s\geq 0}\eta^{\alpha\beta}(-1)^{r}\frac{\partial g}{\partial v^{\beta,s}}\partial_{x}^{r+s+1}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial v^{\alpha,r}}=0.

In particular, we have

\{G,F_{0}\}=\int\frac{\delta G}{\delta v^{\alpha}}\eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{x}\frac{\delta F_{0}}{\delta v^{\beta}}=0,\quad F_{0}=\int f_{0},\quad G=\int g.

Then it follows from Lemma 2.1.7 of
[
18
]
that there exist constants
A
and
B^{\alpha}
such that

\eta^{\alpha\beta}\frac{\delta f_{0}}{\delta v^{\beta}}=Av^{\alpha}+B^{\alpha},

which implies that there exists another constant
C
such that

f_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\alpha\beta}Av^{\alpha}v^{\beta}+\eta_{\alpha\beta}B^{\alpha}v^{\beta}+C+\partial_{x}\mathcal{A},

here we denote by
\eta_{\alpha\beta}
the inverse of
\eta^{\alpha\beta}
. Due to (
2.13
), we see that
A=0
, therefore we conclude that

f_{0}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}B^{\alpha}v^{\beta}+C+\partial_{x}\mathcal{A},

which implies that actually
\varphi(f_{0})_{(0)}=0
. Hence, we replace
f
by
(f-f_{0})/\hbar
, and the same argument shows that
f_{1}
is of the same form as
f_{0}
. In this way, we prove that actually each
f_{n}
has the same form of
f_{0}
, namely, there exist polynomials
B_{\alpha}(\hbar),C(\hbar)\in\mathbb{C}[\hbar]
such that

f=B_{\alpha}(\hbar)v^{\alpha}+C(\hbar)+\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}[\hbar].

The proposition is proved.
∎

3.
Weyl quantization of dispersionless KdV hierarchy

In this section, let us consider the quantization of the dispersionless KdV hierarchy. To state the problem in a precise way, recall that the classical dispersionless KdV hierarchy is a family of compatible PDEs

\frac{\partial v}{\partial t_{n}}=\frac{v^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial v}{\partial x},\quad n\geq 0

for the unknown function
v
. This system admits a Hamiltonian structure given by

\{F,G\}=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta v}\partial_{x}\frac{\delta G}{\delta v},\quad F,G\in\mathcal{F},

and each flow
\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{n}}
is the Hamiltonian vector filed with Hamiltonian
H_{n+1}^{c}
, where

H_{n}^{c}=\int\frac{v^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},\quad n\geq 0.

It follows that these Hamiltonians are in involution:

\{H_{n}^{c},H_{m}^{c}\}=0,\quad n,m\geq 0.

To consider the quantization problem, we replace the Hamiltonian structure by the deformed bracket. Then the classical Hamiltonians do not commute anymore. For example, we have

[H_{2}^{c},H_{3}^{c}]=\int\frac{\hbar}{4}v^{4}v_{x}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{12}(3vv_{x}v_{xx}+v^{2}v_{xxx})+\frac{\hbar^{3}}{720}v^{(5)}\neq 0.

The quantization problem is then to find quantum Hamiltonians
H_{n}\in\mathcal{F}[\hbar]
such that

[H_{n},H_{m}]=0,\quad\lim_{\hbar\to 0}H_{n}=H_{n}^{c}.

3.1.
Construction of quantum Hamiltonians

From now on, we will consider the Heisenberg vertex algebra of rank 1 with
\eta=1
. Our idea to construct the quantum Hamiltonians is as follows. The ring
\mathcal{A}
of differential polynomials is a commutative algebra, and we can view the normal order product on

as a deformation of this algebra structure, in the sense that

\lim_{\hbar\to 0}:\varphi(f)\varphi(g):=\varphi(fg),\quad f,g\in\mathcal{A},

which follows from Corollary

. Roughly speaking, in Weyl quantization, a quantum Hamiltonian is obtained from a classical Hamiltonian by averaging all the possible ways to compose all the quantum operators appearing in Hamiltonian. For example, let

be a pair of conjugated variables, then we have the following Weyl quantization:

(xp)^=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x}),\quad(xp^{2})^=\frac{1}{3}(\hat{x}\hat{p}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{x}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{p}\hat{x}).

In our case, we quantize the Hamiltonians
H_{n}^{c}
by averaging all the possible ways to associate the state

using the normal order product. For example, we should define

h_{2}=\frac{1}{12}\varphi^{-1}(:b:bb::+::bb:b:),\quad H_{2}=\int h_{2}.

3.2.
Another expression for quantum Hamiltonians

As we have already seen, it is not easy to compute each
h_{n}
using the definition. In what follows, we give another expression of

, which simplifies computations and more importantly, enables us to prove various properties of quantum Hamiltonians. From now on, for simplicity, we will set
\hbar=1
, and the dependence of
\hbar

We start by defining states
p^{(n)}\in V
for
n\geq 0

p^{(0)}=b,\quad p^{(n+1)}=\frac{1}{n+2}\left(Tp^{(n)}+:bp^{(n)}:\right),\quad n\geq 0.

Denote by

, then by using relations (

satisfies the recursion relation

This recursion relation makes it easy to compute each

, and in particular, it follows that

are given by Schur polynomials. Recall that for an integer partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)})
, we associate it with a differential polynomial

, called the Schur polynomial associated with the partition

where

is given by

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}{\bf{e}}_{n}z^{n}=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{v^{(k)}}{(k+1)!}z^{k+1}\right).

Lemma 3.3
.

We have
g_{n}=S_{(n+1)}
for
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let us denote by

G(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}g_{n}z^{n}

the generating function of

, then it follows from the recursion relation of
g_{n}
that this function is the unique formal series solution of the equation

z\frac{\partial G}{\partial z}=z(\partial_{x}G+vG)-G+v,\quad G(0)=v.

Therefore, it suffices to show that the formal power series

\frac{W(z)}{z}-\frac{1}{z}

solves the above equation, with

W(z)=\exp\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{v^{(k)}}{(k+1)!}z^{k+1}\right).

Indeed, it follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\left(\frac{W}{z}-\frac{1}{z}\right)=\frac{1}{z}\sum_{k\geq 0}v^{(k+1)}\frac{\partial W}{\partial v^{(k)}}=\frac{W}{z}\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{v^{(k+1)}}{(k+1)!}z^{k+1}\right),

\displaystyle z\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{W}{z}-\frac{1}{z}\right)=\frac{\partial W}{\partial z}-\frac{W}{z}+\frac{1}{z}=vW+\partial_{x}W-\frac{W}{z}+\frac{1}{z}.

Then the lemma is proved by a straightforward verification.
∎

Remark 3.2

We will give another proof of the above lemma in the next subsection based on the definition of Hall-Littlewood polynomials.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k+1}\sum_{a=n-k}^{n}(-1)^{a-n}\binom{a}{n-k}S_{(a+1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{n-a})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}S_{(n-k+1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k})},

here we use the following binomial identity proved in Corollary 2.2 of

In this way, we prove Proposition

\tilde{h}_{n}

3.3.2.
Proposition

Let us proceed to prove that differential polynomials

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{s,t\geq 0}\frac{(s+t+1)!}{s!t!}v^{(s)}v^{(t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial v^{(s+t)}}+\frac{(s+1)!(t+1)!}{(s+t+2)!}v^{(s+t+2)}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial v^{(s)}\partial v^{(t)}}.

\mathcal{D}=P^{(1)}_{-1}+2P^{(2)}_{-1},

and we need to prove that

The actions of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{\lambda_{i}-m-i}{k}S_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{i}-m,\dots,\lambda_{\ell})}+\delta_{m,0}\binom{-\ell}{k+1}S_{\lambda}

\displaystyle+\delta_{m<0}\sum_{i=1}^{-m}(-1)^{m-i}\binom{i-\ell-1}{k}S_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{\ell},i,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{-m-i})},

Then it follows that

P^{(1)}_{-1}S_{(n-k+1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k})}=(n-k+1)S_{(n-k+2,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k})}-(k+1)S_{(n-k+1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k+1})},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\tilde{h}_{n})=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\mathcal{D}(S_{(n-k+1,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{k})})

By applying the well-known identity

3.3.5.
Proof of Theorem (
3.4
)

Finally, we are to prove
\tilde{h}_{n}=h_{n}
. Since we already have
\tilde{h}_{0}=h_{0}=v
, it suffices to prove the validity of the recursion relation

\varphi(\tilde{h}_{n+1})=\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\binom{n+2}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{n-k}),\quad n\geq 0.

Let us verify the above relation by induction on
n
. The case
n=0
can be proved directly and we assume that we have proved this for
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
for some
N\geq 1
. Now let us consider the case
n=N
. Firstly, it follows from the recursion

\mathcal{D}(\tilde{h}_{N})=(N+1)(N+2)\tilde{h}_{N+1}

that

\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N+1})=\frac{2}{(N+1)(N+2)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N}).

Therefore, by using the induction hypothesis, we arrive at

\displaystyle\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(N+1)(N+2)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\binom{N+1}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-1-k}).

By combining (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we arrive at

\left[\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)},\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\right]=\left(\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})\right)_{(-1)}.

Finally, we can compute straightforwardly as

\displaystyle\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N+1})=\,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\binom{N+1}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-1-k})

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\binom{N+1}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-1-k})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\binom{N+1}{k+1}}\left(\varphi(\tilde{h}_{2})_{(1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})\right)_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-1-k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\frac{1}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(N-k)(N-k+1)}{\binom{N+1}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-k})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N(N+1)(N+2)}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{\binom{N+1}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k+1})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-1-k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{1}{\binom{N+2}{k+1}}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{k})_{(-1)}\varphi(\tilde{h}_{N-k}),

and we conclude that
\tilde{h}_{n}=h_{n}
.

4.
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the relationship between vertex algebras and quantum integrable hierarchies. This idea is quite natural, indeed, Barakat, De Sole, Kac and their collaborators developed a theory to describe Hamiltonian integrable hierarchies via Poisson vertex algebras (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
), and these algebras, in some cases, can be viewed as a classical limit of vertex algebras. In this sense, the vertex algebras should provide suitable tools to study quantum integrable hierarchies, and we investigate this possibility in this paper via the example of quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy. An explicit deformation quantization of Hamiltonian structures of hydrodynamic type is constructed, and the corresponding quantum Hamiltonians are constructed for this hierarchy. All these constructions are based on the structure theory of the Heisenberg vertex algebra.

Let us compare the results of the present paper to those obtained in the papers
[
3
,
6
]
. First, we briefly recall their construction of quantum Hamiltonian structures and quantum Hamiltonians for quantum dispersionless KdV hierarchy. The classical Hamiltonian structure (
1.1
) can be equivalently represented by

(4.1)

\{v(x),v(y)\}=\delta^{\prime}(x-y).

This Poisson bracket admits a canonical quantization by expanding the field
v(x)
into Fourier modes

v(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}v_{k}e^{ikx},

and rewriting the Poisson bracket (
4.1
) into the form

\{v_{m},v_{n}\}=i\,m\,\delta_{m+n,0}.

Then, the Fourier modes
v_{n}
are quantized to creators
\hat{q}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
and to annihilators
\hat{p}_{-n}
for
n\leq-1
, and the mode
v_{0}
is quantized to 0 for simplicity. These quantum operators satisfy the commutating relation

(4.2)

[\hat{p}_{n},\hat{q}_{m}]=\hbar\,n\,\delta_{m,n},\quad[\hat{p}_{n},\hat{p}_{m}]=[\hat{q}_{n},\hat{q}_{m}]=0,\quad n,m\geq 1,

where
\hbar
is a formal parameter. An explicit formula of the above quantum Hamiltonian structure in terms of differential polynomials is given in
[
3
]
, and it reads

\displaystyle[F,G]=\int\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\hbar^{m}}{m!}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\dots,r_{m}\geq 0\\
s_{1},\dots,s_{m}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial^{m}f}{v^{(s_{1})}\dots v^{(s_{m})}}(-1)^{\sum r_{i}}

\displaystyle\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{2m-1+\sum r_{i}+\sum s_{i}}C_{j}^{r_{1}+s_{1}+1,\dots,r_{m}+s_{m}+1}\partial_{x}^{j}\left(\frac{\partial^{m}g}{\partial v^{(r_{1})}\dots\partial v^{(r_{m})}}\right),

where
F,G\in\mathcal{F}
with
f,g\in\mathcal{A}
a choice of their densities, and
C_{j}^{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}}
are certain constants. In particular, it is proved that

C^{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}}_{m-1+\sum a_{i}}=\frac{a_{1}!\dots a_{m}!}{(m-1+\sum a_{i})!}.

We compare this bracket to the one we obtained in Theorem
1.1
, and conclude that the above bracket can be viewed as a further deformation of the quantum Hamiltonian structure constructed in the present paper.

The quantum Hamiltonians constructed and studied in
[
3
,
6
]
are also different from those presented in this paper. Denote by
H_{n}^{BR}=\int h_{n}^{BR}
the quantum Hamiltonians constructed by Buryak and Rossi, then it is given by the following generating series
[
6
,
21
]
:

h^{BR}(z)=1+\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}^{BR}z^{n+1}=\exp\left(zs(\sqrt{\hbar}z\partial_{x})v(x)\right),

where the function

s(t)=\frac{e^{t/2}-e^{-t/2}}{t}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{t^{2n}}{4^{n}(2n+1)!}.

Here we slightly modify the original choices of densities presented in
[
3
,
6
]
for a clearer comparison. Indeed,
we compare them with the quantum Hamiltonian densities constructed in the present paper, and they read

\displaystyle h_{0}=h_{0}^{BR}=v;

\displaystyle h_{1}=h_{1}^{BR}=\frac{1}{2}v^{2};

\displaystyle h_{2}=\frac{1}{6}v^{3}+\frac{\hbar}{12}v_{xx},\quad h_{2}^{BR}=\frac{1}{6}v^{3}+\frac{\hbar}{24}v_{xx};

\displaystyle h_{3}=\frac{v^{4}}{24}+\frac{\hbar}{24}\left(v_{x}^{2}+2vv_{xx}\right),\quad h_{3}^{BR}=\frac{v^{4}}{24}+\frac{\hbar}{24}vv_{xx}.

The choices of quantum densities are different, however, we note that as local functionals,
H_{n}=H_{n}^{BR}
in the above examples. Actually, this can be checked for more examples, and we conjecture this to hold true for any
n
. This implies a close relation between two different ways of quantizations.

Another difference is that, in the construction of
[
3
,
6
]
, what matters is the quantum Hamiltonians, instead of quantum Hamiltonian densities. Indeed, the eigenvalue problem considered in
[
6
]
only depends on the deformed Hamiltonians as local functionals, and does not depend on the choice of densities. However, in our construction, the densities
h_{n}
are important since we are considering operators

\varphi^{-1}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi(h_{n})_{(n)}\mathchoice{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\scriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}{\mathrel{\raisebox{0.43057pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\circ$}}}\varphi,

and we cannot add arbitrary terms in
\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}
to
h_{n}
.

Despite these differences, our results also share similarity to the known results. Besides the conjectural relation
H_{n}=H_{n}^{BR}
, the common eigenvectors of
H_{n}^{BR}
computed in
[
6
]
are also given by Schur polynomials, and further properties of quantum KdV hierarchy studied in
[
11
,
12
,
22
]
seem to be applicable to the present setting. Therefore, it makes sense to conjecture that our formulation is equivalent to the formulation presented in
[
3
,
6
]
in some sense. For example, can we actually recover the results in
[
6
]
using results of the present paper? Is the bracket constructed in
[
3
]
related to the bracket presented in this paper by a certain transformation? These problems are still under investigation.
