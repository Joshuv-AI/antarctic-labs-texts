Associations between carotenoids and rheumatoid arthritis risk: evidence from two population-based studies
Authors: Xinzhe Jing, Meiqun Lv, Ying Chen, Xuanli Zhao, Jiayu Li, Xiaohui Sun, Zhixing He, Yingying Mao, Ding Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarotenoids are common natural anti-inflammatory antioxidants produced by plants and some microorganisms. However, there are limited reports on the association between carotenoids and risk of rheumatoid arthritis (RA).MethodsWe analyzed data of participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and UK Biobank (UKB). In the NHANES, a total of 40,410 and 13,440 participants were included to evaluate the association of dietary carotenoid intake and serum carotenoid levels with RA, respectively. Among 179,171 participants in the UKB, 2,156 individuals developed RA over a median follow-up of 7.74 years. A weighted multivariable logistic regression model and quantile G-Computation (QGC) model were established to investigate single and joint effect of carotenoid levels (alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, beta-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, lutein and zeaxanthin) on RA in NHANES by cross-sectional study design. Furthermore, Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to validate the potential association in UKB cohort, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to estimate the possible nonlinear association.ResultsCompared to the lowest quartile (Q1), dietary intake level of alpha-carotene in Q3 were consistently associated with a decreased risk of RA in NHANES (odds ratio [OR]: 0.771, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.628 to 0.946, P-value = 0.013) and UKB (hazards ratio [HR]: 0.875, 95% CI: 0.775 to 0.987, P-value = 0.030). At serum level, all kinds of carotenoids in Q4 were significantly inversely associated with RA when compared with Q1. In QGC model, each one-quartile increase in the intake (OR: 0.886, 95% CI: 0.841 to 0.933, P-value < 0.001) or serum levels (OR: 0.795, 95% CI: 0.732 to 0.864, P-value < 0.001) of the six carotenoids showed a negative correlation against of RA in NHANES. RCS analysis indicated that dietary intake of total-carotene (P-value = 0.0020 for nonlinear), alpha-carotene (P-value = 0.0175 for nonlinear), and beta-carotene (P-value = 0.0021 for nonlinear) exhibited a nonlinear relationship with the risk of RA in UKB. The protective effects against RA were found within the ranges of log natural transformation for total-carotene (5.28 to 8.69 µg/day), and beta-carotene (5.18 to 8.61 µg/day).ConclusionBoth cross-sectional and prospective studies indicate that carotenoids, as natural and effective anti-inflammatory antioxidants, were negatively associated with RA. Daily intake of an appropriate dose can significantly reduce the risk of RA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-026-01333-7.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic inflammatory autoimmune disease characterized by diffuse polyarthritis and the infiltration of pro-inflammatory cytokines [1–3]. The main clinical features of RA include joint warmth, swelling, pain, the formation of vascular pannus, cartilage degeneration, and bone erosion [3]. Currently, RA represents a growing global public health issue, with the latest global burden of disease study indicating 17.6 million prevalent cases in 2020. The age-standardized global prevalence rate of RA was 208.8 cases per 100,000 population, representing a 14.1% increase since 1990. It is projected that the number of individuals affected worldwide will reach 31.7 million by 2050 [4]. Therefore, it is of significant importance to actively explore effective measure for preventing and controlling RA to improve the health status.

Diet has received considerable attention as a potential factor contributing to the etiology of RA and other chronic diseases [5]. Studies have shown that carotenoids are common natural antioxidants that can reduce reactive oxygen species-induced damage, prevent lipid peroxidation, and participate in the regulation of apoptosis signaling pathways to reduce inflammatory damage in the body [6–12]. Harari et al. found that dietary intake of beta-carotene is converted into vitamin A in the body, which can improve levels of inflammation associated with atherosclerosis [13]. In addition, two population-based studies have shown that appropriate carotenoids can reduce osteoarthritis inflammation and uric acid levels, reduce the risk of osteoarthritis and gout [14, 15]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials demonstrated that beta-cryptoxanthin and lutein/zeaxanthin significantly reduce CRP levels, and lycopene significantly lowers IL-6 levels. Overall, the findings support the role of carotenoids in reducing inflammatory biomarkers [16]. However, there are limited reports on the association between the carotenoids and RA. Thus, exploring the association between the carotenoids and RA risk is of great significance.

In this study, we utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the UK Biobank to analyze and validate the association mutually. First, we employed multivariable logistic regression to investigate the association between carotenoids (alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, beta-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, lutein and zeaxanthin) and RA using cross-sectional data from NHANES. Subsequently, we conducted Cox regression analysis to further explore the causal relationship between carotenoids (total-carotene, alpha-carotene and beta-carotene, beta-cryptoxanthin) and RA utilizing prospective cohort data from the UK Biobank.

Methods

Study population

In the NHANES

NHANES is a nationally representative, population-based cross-sectional survey designed to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The study protocol has been approved by the research ethics review board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in the survey [17, 18]. Our study covered NHANES cycles from 2001 to 2023.

The specific inclusion and exclusion processes of study population in NHANES are illustrated in Fig. 1A. In the dietary carotenoid intake study, of the initial 109,590 participants, 69,180 individuals were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) 4,439 participants with missing RA diagnostic information; (2) 17,059 participants with missing dietary visit records; (3) 5,042 participants with missing two-day dietary weight data; (4) participants with missing data on age (n = 0), sex (n = 0), race (n = 2,855), marital status (n = 26,455), education (n = 4,606), PIR (n = 4,196), smoking (n = 26), drinking (n = 3,213), BMI (n = 535), physical activity (n = 628), history of diabetes (n = 12) or hypertension (n = 112), energy (n = 0), and dietary quality (n = 2) were excluded, resulting in the exclusion of 42,640 participants. In the serum carotenoid study, of the initial 40,763 participants, 27,323 individuals were excluded according to the following criteria: (1) 1,929 participants with missing RA diagnostic information; (2) 11,435 participants with missing serum carotenoid measurement records; (3) participants with missing sample weight (n = 0); and (4) 13,959 participants with missing data on age (n = 0), sex (n = 0), race (n = 1,605), marital status (n = 4,700), education (n = 4,447), PIR (n = 1,199), smoking (n = 8), drinking (n = 1,302), BMI (n = 287), physical activity (n = 2), history of diabetes (n = 4) or hypertension (n = 85), energy (n = 320), and dietary quality (n = 0) were excluded.

Ultimately, 40,410 participants were included in the dietary carotenoid intake analysis, of whom 2,121 were diagnosed with RA and 38,289 were non-RA participants. In the serum carotenoid analysis, 13,440 participants were included, including 730 individuals diagnosed with RA and 12,710 participants without reported RA in NHANES (Fig. 1A; Table 1).

In the UK Biobank

The UK Biobank is a large prospective cohort study that recruited approximately 500,000 individuals from England, Wales, and Scotland between 2006 and 2010 [19]. During the recruitment process, participants provided blood samples along with information on socio-demographics, diet, medical conditions, medications, and anthropometric measurements [20]. The application number for this study in the UK Biobank is 113,769.

As shown in Fig. 1B, out of the initial 502,355 participants, 323,184 individuals were excluded from the UK Biobank study based on the following criteria: (1) 224 participants who had withdrawn from this cohort study; (2) 291,289 participants who had completed none of the 24-hour dietary recalls; (3) 800 participants who had prevalent RA at baseline; (4) 30,871 participants with missing data on age (n = 0), sex (n = 0), race (n = 961), education (n = 912), household income (n = 20,908), smoking (n = 332), drinking (n = 52), BMI (n = 413), physical activity (n = 0), history of diabetes (n = 261) or hypertension (n = 7,031), energy (n = 0), and dietary quality (n = 1) were excluded. A total of 179,171 participants were included in our primary analysis, including 2,156 patients with RA with a median follow-up of 7.74 years, and 177,015 non-RA participants with a median follow-up of 13.24 years (Fig. 1B; Table 1).

Measurement of the carotenoids

In the NHANES cross-sectional study, dietary intake of carotenoids (2001 to 2002, 2003 to 2004, 2005 to 2006, 2007 to 2008, 2009 to 2010, 2011 to 2012, 2013 to 2014, 2015 to 2016, 2017 to 2020, and 2021 to 2023 cycles), and serum carotenoids levels (2001 to 2002, 2003 to 2004, 2005 to 2006, and 2017 to 2018 cycles) were selected as exposures, covering alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, beta-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, lutein and zeaxanthin.

The dietary interview component of the NHANES was conducted through a collaboration between the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the department of health and human services, supervised by the NCHS, which manages sample design and data collection process of the survey. Data on carotenoid intake were obtained through 24-hour dietary recall interviews [21]. The initial dietary recall was conducted in person during the visit, followed by a subsequent interview via telephone within 3 to 10 days. Detailed information on the dietary data collection process can be found on the NHANES website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). In this study, carotenoids intake were derived from the average value of dietary recall data collected on the first and second days from the total nutrient intake data set. The unit of daily intake for each type of carotenoids was µg/day.

The NHANES database measures the serum concentrations of carotenoids using a modified high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) method coupled with photodiode array detection [22]. The specific carotenoids measured include alpha-carotene, trans-beta-carotene, cis-beta-carotene, alpha-cryptoxanthin, beta-cryptoxanthin, the lutein and zeaxanthin combination, trans-lycopene, and total lycopene. Only trans-lycopene (but not total lycopene) was measured in the 2001–2002 cycle, while both trans-beta-carotene and cis-beta-carotene were measured in the 2001–2006 and 2017–2018 cycles. Therefore, the serum beta-carotene concentration was expressed by summing the concentrations of trans-beta-carotene and cis-beta-carotene in this study. The unit of serum concentration for each type of carotenoid was µg/dL. Furthermore, to retain the maximum sample size for this study, the measured trans-lycopene was used to represent the total lycopene content in the 2001–2002 survey data. For participants with values below the limit of detection (LOD), the official NHANES database applied LOD imputation for the 2001–2004 data, whereas values were defaulted to missing for the 2005–2006 and 2017–2018 cycles. Detailed laboratory procedures and quality control protocols can be accessed on the official NHANES website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx) by navigating to the corresponding survey cycle.

In the UK Biobank cohort study, total-carotene (the total sum of all carotene components, not limited to alpha and beta carotene), alpha-carotene, beta-carotene and beta-cryptoxanthin were included for analysis. Dietary consumption was measured using the Oxford WebQ questionnaire, a 24-hour online dietary assessment tool that collected information on up to 206 foods and 32 beverages consumed in the previous day [23]. Participants recruited at baseline were invited to complete the first 24-hour dietary assessment. Subsequently, from February 2011 to April 2012, those with valid email addresses were invited to repeat the survey four times [24, 25]. The average dietary carotenoid intake was calculated from multiple measurements and was used as the exposure variable in this study. The unit of daily intake for each type of carotenoid was µg/day.

Outcomes

For the NHANES cross-sectional study, the diagnosis of RA was obtained through self-reported questionnaires. Participants were asked two questions regarding RA: (1) Has a doctor ever told you that you have arthritis? (2) What type of arthritis do you have? If participants answered affirmatively to the first question and specified “rheumatoid arthritis” for the second, they were classified as RA patients. Otherwise, they were considered non-RA individuals. Previous studies have shown substantial consistency between self-reported arthritis and clinically diagnosed arthritis [26].

For the UK Biobank cohort study, the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (M05, M06, M08.0) were used to identify incident cases of RA [27].

Covariates

Demographic characteristics comprised baseline age, sex, race, education level, and household income or poverty income ratio (PIR) [28]. In NHANES, race was categorized as White (non-Hispanic White) and other (non-Hispanic Black, Asian, multi-racial, Mexican American and other Hispanic). Education level was classified as no diploma (including Less than 9th grade and 9-11th grade), high school (high school graduate/GED or equivalent), and college graduate (including some college or associates (AA) degree and college graduate or above). PIR was utilized to assess household income, categorized as ≤ 1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, and > 3.5. In the UK Biobank, race was divided into White and Non-White. The White group included British, Irish, and other White backgrounds. Non-White group included Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, Chinese, Mixed, or other ethnicities. Education level was categorized as higher, intermediate, and other. Household income was classified as less than £18,000, £18,000 to £100,000, and greater than £100,000.

Lifestyle factors included body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity [28]. In both UK Biobank and NHANES, BMI was categorized as normal weight (BMI < 25 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 25.0 to 29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2). For the NHANES study, smoking status was classified as < 100 cigarettes and ≥ 100 cigarettes in lifetime. Alcohol consumption was divided into < 12 alcohol drinks/year and ≥ 12 alcohol drinks/year. Physical activity was categorized as “Yes” or “No”. In the UK Biobank study, smoking status was classified as never smoker, former smoker, and current smoker. Alcohol consumption was categorized as never drinker and drinker (including special occasions only, 1–3 times/month, 1–2 times/week, 3–4 times/week, and daily or almost daily). Physical activity was classified as “Yes” or “No”. Medical history included diabetes and hypertension, both categorized as “Yes” or “No”.

Energy intake was treated as a continuous variable, and the unit was Kcal/day and KJ/day in NHANES and UKB, respectively. In terms of dietary quality, we adopted the latest definition outlined in the U.S. Dietary Guidelines [29, 30], which included seven relevant components. The specific details were provided in Supplementary Table 1. The dietary quality was defined as healthy if it meets 4 to 7 of the criteria; otherwise, it was considered an unhealthy diet.

Statistical analysis

First, continuous variables were expressed as means (standard deviation [SD]) or medians (interquartile range [IQR]), while categorical variables were expressed as n (%). Values of dietary carotenoid intake were log-transformed to approximate normality (Supplementary Fig. 1–3). To perform logarithmic transformation in the presence of zero values, we applied log(x + 1) transformation to dietary intake data from NHANES and UK Biobank, where x represents the original dietary intake level. Chi-square tests and t-tests were used to compare categorical and continuous variables between different groups.

Second, in the NHANES cross-sectional study, weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to establish three models investigating the association between carotenoids (dietary intake and serum levels) and RA. We incorporated the survey weights, clustering, and stratification using the svydesign () function in R to account for the complex sampling structure of the NHANES design. Model 1 was adjusted for age (continuous), gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, PIR, and cycle, Model 2 was further adjusted for BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity, and Model 3 added adjustments of diabetes and hypertension based on Model 2. In the UK Biobank cohort study, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate the relationship between carotenoid levels and RA risk. Model 1 was adjusted for age (continuous), gender, race/ethnicity, education level, household income, Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, and physical activity, and Model 3 was a fully adjusted model, which further added diabetes, hypertension, energy and dietary quality. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to explore the non-linear relationship between carotenoid and RA risk. Given the large sample size, long follow-up period, and high level of epidemiological evidence available in the UKB cohort, RCS were exclusively applied in this cohort to robustly examine potential non-linear dose-response relationships. Since carotenoids are consumed in combination rather than individually, quantile G-Computation (QGC) model (‘qgcomp’ package) was used to estimate the joint effects of six carotenoids from NHANES, which were transformed into quartiles and included in a linear model [31]. The UKB dataset includes only three carotenoid species (alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, and beta-cryptoxanthin), which is insufficient for a comprehensive mixture analysis by QGC. In addition, QGC is designed for cross-sectional binary outcomes and OR estimation, whereas the UKB cohort was analyzed as a prospective time-to-event study with hazards ratio (HR) as the effect measure, for which no validated QGC framework is available. Therefore, QGC was not applied in the UKB cohort.

Third, sensitivity analyses were performed in both NHANES and UK Biobank to test the stability of the results, involving using adjusted dietary intake based on energy intake using the residual method [32], and excluding participants with extreme energy intake (Male: 3347 to 17,573 kJ/d or 800 to 4200 Kcal/d; Female: 2092 to 14,644 kJ/d or 500 to 3500 Kcal/d) [33]. Additionally, the NHANES population, a part of participants had carotenoids intake not only from daily diets but also from dietary supplements. Additionally, a subset of patients was taking medications for RA treatment, such as methotrexate and hydroxychloroquine. These factors may affect the accuracy of dietary questionnaires and serum carotenoids measurements. Therefore, in the study examining the relationships of dietary carotenoids intake and serum carotenoids levels with RA, participants using dietary supplements and RA treatment medications were excluded separately. For serum carotenoids measurement, participants whose detected levels were below the LOD published on the NHANES official website were excluded. In the UKB population, three sensitivity analyses also were performed to verify the robustness of our results, considering the following factors: RA is a long-term chronic disease; the initial disease diagnosis was based solely on hospital medical records; and some participants had limited dietary follow-up data. Specifically, three sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) excluding patients with RA within two years of follow-up; (2) using the first occurrences data; and (3) only participants with at least two dietary follow-up records were included.

Subgroups were created based on age groups (< 60 years, and ≥ 60 years), gender, race/ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, and dietary quality. Stratified analyses were used to study the relationship between carotenoid levels and RA in Model 3.

Statistical analysis was performed using R (version 4.1.3, http://www.r-project.org), with all statistical tests being two-tailed and p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

In the QGC analysis, the significantly negative mixed effect of dietary carotenoid components on RA was primarily attributed to beta-carotene (34.60%), followed by lycopene (27.20%), beta-cryptoxanthin (25.80%) and alpha-carotene (12.30%). Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

After conducting multiple sensitivity analyses, the associations of dietary carotenoid intake, serum carotenoid levels, with RA in NHANES were largely consistent with the main analysis. In the sensitivity analysis of dietary carotenoids intake, after excluding participants receiving treatment for RA (Sensitivity analysis 1), those with at least two dietary follow-up records (Sensitivity analysis 3), using the residual method based on energy intake to adjust dietary intake (Sensitivity analysis 4), and excluding subjects with extreme energy intake (Sensitivity analysis 5), the dietary intake of alpha-carotene in Q3, lycopene in Q2, Q3, and Q4 were negatively correlated with RA compared to Q1. However, this result showed slight fluctuations after excluding participants using dietary supplements (Sensitivity analysis 2) (Supplementary Table 2). In the sensitivity analysis of serum carotenoids, after excluding those receiving treatment for RA (Sensitivity analysis 1) and users of dietary supplements (Sensitivity analysis 2), and excluding subjects with extreme energy intake (Sensitivity analysis 4), the levels of alpha-carotene, beta-carotene, beta-cryptoxanthin, and lutein and zeaxanthin in Q4 all showed a negative correlation with RA (P-value < 0.050 or P-value < 0.100) compared with Q1. The continuous carotenoids also exhibited a significant and stable a negative correlation with RA (P-value < 0.050). After excluding participants with values below the detection limit (Sensitivity analysis 3), slight fluctuations in these results were observed (Supplementary Table 3). These findings generally supported the robustness of our study results.

Subgroup analyses showed that dietary intake of carotenoids was significantly protective against RA in over 60 years old, physically active individuals, and those without diabetes or hypertension. Lycopene showed a significant negative correlation with RA in women, while beta-carotene, lutein, and zeaxanthin demonstrated a more pronounced negative correlation with RA in men. The subgroup analysis of serum carotenoid levels was generally consistent with this finding, except that in the age subgroup, the negative correlation with carotenoid levels against RA was more pronounced among individuals aged under 60 years. To be noted, results from subgroup analysis exhibited slight fluctuations across different classes of carotenoids (Supplementary Fig. 4–5, Supplementary Tables 4–13).

UKB results

Association between dietary carotenoids intake and RA

The results of the Cox regression analysis for log-transformed quartiles and continuous carotenoids in relation to RA in the UK Biobank are presented in Table 3. Compared with Q1, beta-cryptoxanthin was not significantly associated with the risk of RA. Restricted cubic spline analysis

We found that alpha-carotene (P-value = 0.0175 for nonlinear), beta-carotene (P-value = 0.0021 for nonlinear) and total-carotene (P-value = 0.0020 for nonlinear) exhibited a nonlinear relationship with RA, with the RCS curves displaying a positive U-shaped pattern (Fig. 4A-C). Although the RCS results for beta-cryptoxanthin also exhibited a U-shaped pattern, the curve did not conform to nonlinearity (P = 0.1020 for nonlinear) (Fig. 4D). Specifically, the dietary intake of total-carotene of 5.28 to 8.69 µg/day was associated with a protective effect against the risk of developing RA. Conversely, deficient intakes of total-carotene that below 5.28 µg/day was identified as risk factor for RA. Deficient intakes of alpha-carotene below 3.42 µg/day and intakes above 7.65 µg/day were identified as risk factors for RA. For beta-carotene, dietary intake below 5.81 µg/day was found to increase the risk of RA, while the intake between 5.81 and 8.61 µg/day was associated with a decreased risk of RA.

Sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis

The sensitivity analysis in UKB showed that, adjusting dietary intake based on the residual method for energy intake (Sensitivity analysis 1), excluding participants with extreme energy intake (Sensitivity analysis 2), and after excluding RA patients within the two-year follow-up period (Sensitivity analysis 3), compared to Q1, the intake of alpha-carotene at Q2 and Q3 levels were significantly or marginally significantly associated with reduced risk of developing RA (P-value < 0.050 or P-value < 0.100). Compared with Q1, beta-cryptoxanthin was not significantly associated with a reduced risk of RA. These findings were consistent with the main results. The results showed some fluctuation in the sensitivity analyses for multiple sources of outcome diagnosis (Sensitivity analysis 4) and least two dietary follow-up records (Sensitivity analysis 5) (Supplementary Table 4).

Subgroup analysis shows that dietary intake of carotenoids can significantly reduce the risk of RA in populations who were women, without diabetes, and without hypertension (Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Tables 14–17). However, such results varied somewhat across different carotenoid species.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the association between carotenoids and RA using both cross-sectional and cohort study design. In the NHANES and UK Biobank, we observed a significant negative correlation between carotenoids levels and the risk of RA in the general population. This association exhibited consistency across different study designs, further supporting the potential protective role of carotenoids against RA. The U-shaped RCS curve in the UK Biobank study indicates a non-linear relationship between carotenoids intake levels and the risk of developing RA.

Previous studies have demonstrated that carotenoids were important anti-inflammatory and antioxidant substances [34]. As a precursor to vitamin A, beta-carotene may further contribute to the regulation of immune tolerance through its conversion to retinoic acid [35]. Dietary carotenoid intake that was negatively correlated with the risk of fractures, and beta-carotene had been shown to improve bone density and reduce the risk of osteoporosis and fractures [36]. Research by Yi et al. indicates that carotenoids intake can lower the risk of various adverse health outcomes [37]. Additionally, previous studies have found that dietary intake of carotenoids, such as lutein/zeaxanthin, can effectively slow telomere shortening, thereby reducing inflammation levels [38, 39]. A study published in Nature journal indicates that with advancing age, telomeres progressively shorten, and when telomeres become critically short, they interact with mitochondria, triggering an inflammatory response in the body [40]. The Mendelian randomization meta-analysis indicates that genetically determined longer telomere length is robustly associated with a reduced risk of RA, supporting telomere shortening as a causal risk factor for RA [41]. These findings indirectly suggest a protective role of carotenoids against RA.

There was a significant negative association between serum carotenoid levels and RA, and the protective threshold ranges observed in this study were consistent with the findings of multiple previous studies [42–44]. For example, Zhu et al. [43]. reported that the serum protective thresholds of α-carotene, β-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin, lycopene, and lutein + zeaxanthin against osteoarthritis were 0.41 to 4.46 µg/dL, 2.34 to 6.08 µg/dL, 1.43 to 5.02 µg/dL, 2.65 to 4.39 µg/dL, and 2.55 to 2.81 µg/dL, the values that are basically consistent with the corresponding thresholds after log transformation in our analysis (1.030 µg/dL, 3.182 µg/dL, 1.548 to µg/dL, 3.813 µg/dL, and 2.715 µg/dL). Furthermore, the protective thresholds in our study are consistent with the reference ranges recommended by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (alpha-carotene: 0.72 to 20 µg/dL, beta-carotene: 2 to 74 µg/dL, beta-cryptoxanthin: 2 to 31 µg/dL, lycopene: 11 to 91 µg/dL, lutein and zeaxanthin: 5 to 39 µg/dL) [45], which supports the reliability of the protective effect of serum carotenoids against RA observed in this study and provides a basis for further exploring the complex association between serum carotenoid and RA.

The results from dietary intake and serum levels of carotenoids complement each other. Volker et al. reported a review on the significant correlation between carotenoid intake and serum concentrations [46]. In a dose-escalating study, daily intake of 2.5, 5, or 10 mg of lutein for 6 months resulted in final serum concentrations of 620, 1040, and 1180 nmol/L, respectively. The higher the dietary intake of carotenoids, the higher the corresponding serum concentrations [47]. The consistency of the findings on the effect of dietary carotenoids intake and serum carotenoids further strengthens the evidence that carotenoids have a protective effect on RA. However, it is important to note that a universal consensus on the normal reference ranges for human dietary carotenoids intake and serum carotenoids has not yet been established, so the clinical implications of the protective thresholds identified in this study should be interpreted with caution.

Our application of QGC adds unique value to the current literature by addressing critical limitations of conventional single-exposure analyses. Unlike standard regression models that treat carotenoids in isolation, QGC quantifies the joint effect of the six correlated carotenoid components, confirming that their combined action exerts a significant negative correlation with RA. Furthermore, QGC decomposes the overall effect to identify component-specific contributions. For dietary carotenoids, beta-carotene (34.60%) and lycopene (27.20%) were the primary drivers, while for serum levels, alpha-carotene (34.70%) and lutein/zeaxanthin (22.54%) dominated. This granularity helps prioritize carotenoids for future mechanistic and clinical research, and advances understanding of how synergistic interactions between carotenoids may contribute to RA protection.

Notably, restricted cubic spline analysis in the UKB population suggested a possible dose-response relationship between dietary carotenoids and RA risk, indicating that the protective effects were most pronounced within specific intake ranges: approximately 5.28 to 8.69 µg/day for total carotene, 5.18 to 8.61 µg/day for beta-carotene after log transformation. This finding suggests a potential optimization threshold for dietary intake, which is similar to the previous findings of Volker Böhm et al. The study suggests that the recommended dietary intake of total-carotene did not exceed 11.8 mg per day (9.375 µg/day after log transformation) and beta-carotene did not exceed 4.1 mg/day (8.32 µg/day after log transformation) [46]. At the same time, the threshold range is largely consistent with the recommendations of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, which emphasize the importance of consuming carotenoid-rich foods, such as vegetables and fruits, as part of a healthy diet [30]. This finding complements the limitations of previous cross-sectional studies regarding reasonable dietary intake levels and provides valuable reference for exploring recommended dietary intake of carotenoids.

Therefore, carotenoids, as lipid-soluble antioxidants, exhibit a typical concentration-dependent effect. Within an appropriate dosage range, carotenoids can scavenge free radicals, directly neutralize reactive oxygen species, inhibit the activation of NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasome, and reduce the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 [48]. However, excessive intake may lead to the oxidation of unabsorbed carotenoids in the intestine, potentially inducing local inflammation [49]. Moreover, adipose tissue serves as the primary reservoir for carotenoids, excessive amounts of carotenoids may cause lipid peroxidation, releasing pro-inflammatory mediators and even leading to toxicity from fat-soluble vitamin A [50]. However, the external applicability of this threshold range should be interpreted with caution, taking factors such as regional populations into account. It is essential to regulate carotenoid intake based on individual health conditions.

Subgroup of physically active, without diabetes, and without hypertension were consistently significant in both dietary carotenoid and serum carotenoid. Physical activity can indirectly enhance the bioavailability of carotenoids by increasing skeletal muscle mitochondrial biogenesis, improving insulin sensitivity, and promoting lipid metabolism, thereby amplifying the protective effects of carotenoids [51, 52]. Additionally, in patients with diabetes or hypertension, chronic hyperglycemia or angiotensin II can exacerbate mitochondrial ROS production by activating NADPH oxidase, creating a vicious cycle of “oxidative stress-inflammation” [53]. In such pathological conditions, the endogenous antioxidant system may be in a state of compensatory exhaustion, significantly raising the protective threshold for exogenous carotenoids, making simple dietary intake insufficient to highlight their protective effects against RA. In contrast, individuals without diabetes or hypertension have a protective threshold for exogenous carotenoids that aligns with their physiological state, making the protective effects more apparent. However, due to variability in the subgroup analysis results across different types of carotenoids, these findings should be interpreted with caution in future discussions.

The strengths of this study lie in its inclusion of both cross-sectional and prospective cohort data, and the effective control of confounding factors such as age, gender, BMI, and smoking status through multivariable models, which enhances the robustness of causal inferences. However, caution is warranted in interpreting the results due to potential limitations. First, dietary intake data relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall bias or measurement error. Second, despite adjusting for various confounding factors, residual confounding (such as unmeasured genetic or environmental factors) may still affect the results. Additionally, the UK Biobank cohort is predominantly composed of individuals of European descent, so the generalizability of the conclusions needs to be validated in diverse racial populations. Third, the diagnosis of RA in NHANES was based on participant self-report, which may lead to misclassification bias and limits the accuracy of RA case identification in this study. Furthermore, the types of carotenoids retrieved from the NHANES and UKB databases in this study are not entirely consistent, and future research should expand to a broader spectrum of antioxidants. Finally, the study lacks an exploration of the mechanisms by which carotenoids may influence RA.

Conclusion

Both cross-sectional and prospective studies indicate that carotenoids, as natural and effective anti-inflammatory antioxidants, were negatively associated with RA. The U-shaped RCS curve showed that this negative association was more pronounced and significant with moderate daily intake of carotenoids. Further exploration of the mechanisms by which different types of carotenoid supplements may aid in the prevention or adjunctive treatment of RA is warranted.
