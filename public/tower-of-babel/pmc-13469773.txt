Congenital Pulmonary Airway Malformation With Pectus Carinatum: A Novel Case Report

Introduction

Congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM) is an uncommon developmental anomaly of the lower respiratory tract, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 25,000-35,000 live births. 1 The clinical presentation ranges from severe neonatal respiratory distress to incidental detection in asymptomatic older children and adults. 2 Surgical resection remains the accepted standard of care; however, controversy persists regarding the optimal timing of intervention and the extent of pulmonary resection required for the best outcomes. 3

We report an unusual case of a large, localized CPAM in an asymptomatic 11-year-old boy, successfully managed with parenchyma-sparing wedge resection. The novelty of this case lies in two key aspects: (1) the first documented association between CPAM and pectus carinatum (PC), thereby expanding the phenotypic spectrum of CPAM, and (2) the successful use of parenchyma-sparing wedge resection for a large Stocker type I lesion in an asymptomatic older child, challenging the prevailing paradigm of routine lobectomy.

Case Presentation

An 11-year-old boy (BMI 15.6 kg/m2) was prenatally diagnosed with a right lung cystic lesion via ultrasonography at 36 weeks of gestation. Postnatal computed tomography (CT) at one month of age confirmed the diagnosis of CPAM (Figure 1A). Given its limited size and absence of symptoms, the patient was managed conservatively with regular follow-up. Growth and development were appropriate for their ages, and immunizations were complete. There was no relevant medical or family histories.

(A) Computed Tomography (CT) coronal view at one month of age showing multiple cystic lesions in the right lower lobe (B) A male patient with symmetrical pectus carinatum deformity (C) Chest x-ray posterior anterior (PA) view showing right basal pneumothorax (D) Computed tomography (CT) coronal view showing multiple cystic lesions of varying sizes in the right lower lobe

At the time of the current evaluation for the first time, the patient remained asymptomatic. On examination, he was hemodynamically stable (pulse 93/min, blood pressure 110/65 mmHg, respiratory rate 21/min, oxygen saturation 100% on room air, and temperature 98.4 °F). A symmetric pectus carinatum deformity (PC) with a chest circumference of 66cm was present, but cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were otherwise unremarkable, with normal breath sounds and no signs of respiratory distress (Figure 1B).

Chest radiography revealed a right basal pneumothorax (Figure 1C). High-resolution chest CT revealed a large, well-lobulated, multiseptated, thin-walled, air-filled cystic lesion involving the right lower lobe, measuring 10 × 8.7 × 9 cm (craniocaudal × anteroposterior × transverse) (Figure 1D). The lesion replaced the basal segments, causing a mild mass effect and displacement of adjacent peripheral vessels, with features consistent with Stocker-type I CPAM. Haematological and renal function tests were within normal limits, although liver function tests were mildly deranged. Two-dimensional echocardiography revealed a normal cardiac anatomy and function. Pulmonary function testing (PFT) revealed a mild restrictive pattern, with a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 70%, forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) of 63%, and FEV1/FVC ratio of 90%.

Given the lesion’s large size, localized distribution, and absence of infection, the patient underwent right posterolateral thoracotomy under single-lung ventilation. Intraoperatively, the cystic lesion was confined to the basal segments sparing the superior aspect, while the remainder of the lung was normal, with relative hypoplasia of the upper and middle lobes with no suspicion of malignancy (Figure 2A and B, respectively). Wedge resection was performed using endoscopic GIA staplers, achieving well-demarcated margins with well-inflated lobes, and a single chest drain was placed (Figure 2C). Gross examination of the resected specimen revealed multiple cystic spaces with thickened walls (0.1-0.3 cm) and focal hemorrhage (Figure 2D). Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of CPAM (Figure 3A).

Haematoxylin and Eosin stain (20× magnification) shows cystic spaces lined by ciliated to cuboidal epithelium, with dilated alveoli, disrupted septa, and chronic inflammatory infiltrates (B) Chest x-ray PA view at 44 weeks follow-up (C) Orthotic brace resulting in favorable response

The postoperative course was uneventful. The drain was removed on postoperative day 4, and the patient was discharged the next day. At follow-up visits at 1, 4, 8, 12, and 24, 44 weeks, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of recurrence or complications (Figure 3B). The associated PC deformity showed a favorable response to orthotic therapy (Gpad-Pectus Carinatum Brace, Türkiye) initiated after 12 weeks, with a serial improvement in chest circumference to 63 cm over 44 weeks with optimal PFT -FEV1 of 69%, FVC of 75% and FEV1/FVC at 91% (Figure 3C).

Discussion

The management of CPAM remains controversial, particularly regarding the timing of surgery and the extent of resection.2,3 Early elective resection during infancy is commonly advocated to prevent complications, such as infection, pneumothorax, and potential malignant transformation. 4 Furthermore, the rapid alveolar multiplication phase in early life may facilitate compensatory lung growth following early intervention. 1 However, our case demonstrates that a delayed intervention may be appropriate in carefully selected asymptomatic patients. The patient presented to us in the late phase as the first presentation, remained clinically stable since childhood, and the pneumothorax at presentation was secondary to the longstanding cystic lesion rather than a primary event or the principal indication for surgery. The delayed intervention reflects late presentation rather than deferred management, and this case supports a more individualized, risk-stratified approach rather than routine early resection in all patients.

Histopathological examination confirmed Type I CPAM according to the Stocker classification, consistent with the radiological and intraoperative findings. Although lobectomy is widely regarded as the standard surgical procedure for CPAM, asymptomatic patients who undergo lobectomy generally achieve good long-term survival and maintain satisfactory pulmonary function due to compensatory lung growth.4,5 However, most asymptomatic patients eventually develop symptoms that prompt surgery, reinforcing the need for early surgical intervention to prevent morbidity. 5 In the present case, the intraoperative findings revealed hypoplasia of the upper and middle lobes, significantly limiting the functional lung tissue. Performing lobectomy would likely have resulted in a disproportionate loss of pulmonary reserve and an increased risk of postoperative complications, including space-related sequelae and persistent air leaks. Thus, given that the lesion was non-malignant in appearance and confined to the basal segments, a wedge resection was performed as a lung-sparing alternative. 6

Emerging evidence supports parenchyma-sparing procedures, such as wedge resection or segmentectomy, for peripheral, well-circumscribed lesions, with outcomes comparable to lobectomy in selected patients. 7 In our case, the postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the pulmonary function remained satisfactory, supporting the short-term safety and feasibility of this approach. Although the risk of malignancy, including mucinous adenocarcinoma arising within CPAM, remains a recognized concern, it is relatively rare and must be balanced against the risk of excessive parenchymal loss. 8 In this context, the absence of symptoms, long-term lesion stability, and lack of intraoperative suspicion of malignancy justified a conservative surgical strategy that did not compromise oncological safety.

Long-term follow-up after CPAM resection is essential because clinical and functional respiratory complications may persist or develop over time. Previous studies have reported a substantial incidence of long-term morbidity, including impaired pulmonary function, irrespective of the surgical approach or symptom status at presentation.8,9 Accordingly, the 24-week follow-up in our case was insufficient to evaluate long-term outcomes, such as recurrence, pulmonary function trajectory, and risk of malignant transformation. Nevertheless, the absence of early postoperative complications supports the validity of reporting short-term outcomes, while emphasizing the need for continued surveillance using serial imaging and PFT. 7

Another notable aspect of this case is the coexistence of PC. 10 While chest wall deformities, particularly pectus excavatum with limited data, have been sporadically associated with CPAM, an association with PC has not been previously reported, to the best of our knowledge, despite an extensive literature search across major databases (PubMed, Medline, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, EBSCO, Google Scholar, and arXiv till 30/04/2026) with key words such as “Congenital Pulmonary Airway Malformation,” “CPAM,” “Congenital cystic adenomatoid malformation,” “pectus excavatum,” “pectus carinatum,” “pectus arcuatum,” “thoracic deformities,” and “chest wall abnormalities dated 2/05/2026. 11 The pathogenesis of PC is likely multifactorial and involves abnormalities in cartilage growth and extracellular matrix composition.11,12 Genetic contributions have been implicated, including collagen-related genes (e.g., COL5A1, COL5A2, COL1A1, and COL27A1), cartilage matrix components (ACAN), and signaling pathways such as TGF-β/BMP (e.g., SMAD4), along with developmental regulators such as the SIX family of transcription factors.12,13 These mechanisms influence thoracic wall development; however, a direct causal relationship between CPAM and PC cannot be established in this case and may represent a coincidental association. The deformity showed objective improvement with orthotic therapy, as monitored through serial chest circumference measurements. Although the deformity in this patient responded well to orthotic therapy, this report is limited by its single-case design and short follow-up, which restrict generalizability and long-term outcome assessment.

The patient’s parents reported satisfaction with the treatment provided. They noted improvement in the child’s symptoms and expressed acceptance of the orthotic management and follow-upplan.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that wedge resection can be a safe and effective alternative to lobectomy in selected older children with localized CPAM. Although short-term follow-up validates early postoperative complications, long-term surveillance with PFT and imaging is necessary. This novel association with PC expands the recognized spectrum of CPAM-related anomalies and emphasizes the importance of individualized and comprehensive evaluation.

Take Away Lesson

This case highlights that parenchyma-sparing wedge resection can be a safe and effective alternative to lobectomy in carefully selected older children with localized congenital pulmonary airway malformation, emphasizing individualized surgical decision-making. It also highlights the importance of multidisciplinary management and long-term follow-up, particularly in the presence of associated anomalies such as pectus carinatum.
