Coexisting adrenal hemangioma and aldosterone-producing micronodule presented as adrenal incidentaloma
Authors: Du Hee Jung, Hyunjin Kim, Seung Eun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdrenal hemangiomas are rare benign vascular tumors, an uncommon cause of adrenal incidentalomas. We report a patient with coexisting adrenal hemangioma and aldosterone-producing micronodules within the same adrenal gland. A 67-year-old man was referred for an incidental left adrenal mass. Noncontrast computed tomography revealed a 1.6 × 1.4 cm lesion with an unenhanced attenuation value of 40 Hounsfield units. Initial hormonal evaluation was unremarkable except for an elevated aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) of 115.6. On follow-up evaluation, the ARR decreased to 52.8, with a relatively low aldosterone concentration of 9.5 ng/dL (264 pmol/L; reference range, 2.52-39.2 ng/dL [69-1087 pmol/L]), yielding equivocal findings for primary aldosteronism. Serial imaging revealed growth to 3.4 × 2.8 cm over 3 years. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a T2-hyperintense lesion with peripheral nodular enhancement and delayed centripetal filling, suggestive of adrenal hemangioma. Owing to continued growth, laparoscopic adrenalectomy was performed. Histopathological examination confirmed cavernous hemangioma. Given the marked postoperative reduction in ARR to 9.7, additional pathological review identified an adjacent CYP11B2-positive aldosterone-producing micronodule. This report illustrates that adrenal incidentalomas may have dual pathologies. An integrated assessment of imaging, biochemical findings, clinical course, and histopathology, together with close clinicopathological collaboration, is essential for accurate diagnosis and management.

Introduction

Adrenal incidentalomas are frequently encountered in clinical practice because of the widespread use of imaging techniques [1]. Although most tumors are benign and nonfunctional, a subset can represent significant disease, including functional tumors or cancers. Adrenal hemangiomas are rare benign vascular tumors that account for only a small percentage (∼0.6%) of all adrenal tumors [2]. Primary aldosteronism, characterized by excessive aldosterone production, is a common cause of secondary hypertension and is often associated with aldosterone-producing adenomas or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia [3]. This report describes a unique case of an adrenal incidentaloma presenting as an adrenal hemangioma with a coexisting aldosterone-producing micronodule, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and importance of a comprehensive evaluation.

Case presentation

A 67-year-old man with a history of hypertension and atrial fibrillation was referred to our clinic for the evaluation of an incidentally detected left adrenal mass. Noncontrast computed tomography (CT) revealed a 1.6 × 1.4 cm lesion with an unenhanced attenuation of 40 Hounsfield units (HU) in the left adrenal gland (Fig. 1A).

Baseline unenhanced computed tomography (CT) and follow-up unenhanced CT. (A) Initial and (B) follow-up unenhanced CT images demonstrating a left adrenal incidentaloma (arrow). The lesion increased in size from 1.6 × 1.4 cm at baseline to 2.1 × 1.5 cm on the follow-up imaging.

Diagnostic assessment

Initial evaluation revealed normal levels of cortisol, plasma metanephrine, adrenocorticotropic hormone, and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) (Table 1). In contrast, plasma aldosterone was elevated at 18.5 ng/dL (513 pmol/L; reference range, 2.52-39.2 ng/dL [69-1087 pmol/L]), with suppressed renin at 0.16 ng/mL/h (reference range, 0.17-5.38 ng/mL/h). The aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR), calculated as plasma aldosterone concentration divided by plasma renin activity, was 115.6. The initial elevated ARR was reevaluated because the biochemical findings were not concordant with the adrenal imaging characteristics. At the follow-up examination, the aldosterone level decreased to 9.5 ng/dL (264 pmol/L), with a corresponding reduction in the ARR to 52.8.

During the first year of follow-up, hormone levels and imaging were followed regularly and ARR remained stable between 52.8 and 58.9; however, over this 1-year interval, imaging revealed gradual growth from 1.6 × 1.4 cm to 2.1 × 1.5 cm (Fig. 1B). Hormonal evaluation for potential adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) included measurement of DHEA-S, estradiol, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP), all of which were within the respective reference ranges: DHEA-S, 49.4 µg/dL (1.34 µmol/L; reference range, 33.6-249.0 µg/dL [0.91-6.76 µmol/L]); estradiol, 17.10 pg/mL (62.78 pmol/L; reference range, 8.95-39.12 pg/mL [32.86-143.62 pmol/L]); and 17-OHP, 1.35 ng/mL (4.09 nmol/L; reference range, 0.59-3.44 ng/mL [1.79-10.41 nmol/L]).To further characterize the adrenal incidentaloma, adrenal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. The MRI demonstrated high signal intensity on T2-weighted images (Fig. 2A and 2B), and a dynamic study showed peripheral nodular enhancement with delayed centripetal filling (Fig. 2C and 2D), suggestive of an adrenal hemangioma [4].

Treatment

Given the relatively small size of the lesion, periodic radiological follow-ups were planned. In the next 2 years, the mass gradually increased to 3.4 × 2.8 cm. Owing to the high likelihood of hemangioma on imaging and continued growth, the patient underwent left laparoscopic adrenalectomy.

Outcome and follow-up

The resected adrenal gland measured 5.2 × 2.5 × 2 cm and weighed 66 g. Periadrenal adipose tissue was attached without evidence of adhesion or fibrosis. Serial section of the specimen revealed an ovoid hemorrhagic lesion, measuring 3.1 × 2.3 × 1.8 cm. The cut surface was purplish tan and soft with multifocal hemorrhage. On histopathological examination, dilated vascular channels were lined by a single layer of bland endothelial cells. This case was diagnosed as a cavernous hemangioma with organizing hematoma (Fig. 3A and 3B). On postoperative day 2, renin activity was 0.31 ng/mL/h, and aldosterone was 3.0 ng/dL (0.083 nmol/L), with an ARR of 9.7, markedly lower than preoperative values (Table 1). Based on the biochemical responses, additional pathology consultations and slide reviews were conducted to investigate the possible presence of aldosterone-producing nodules in the resected adrenal gland. CYP11B2 immunohistochemical analysis was performed, which revealed multifocal positivity that was indistinguishable on hematoxylin and eosin slides (Fig. 3C and 3D). As suggested by the International Histopathology Consensus for Unilateral Primary Aldosteronism (HISTALDO), the CYP11B2-positive lesions were defined as aldosterone-producing micronodules since the lesions were <10 mm in size, located beneath the adrenal capsule, and were not histologically different from the nearby adrenocortical cells [5].

There were no notable fluctuations or adverse changes in kidney function or electrolyte balance between the pre- and postsurgery (Table 1). At the 6-month postsurgical follow-up, the antihypertensive therapy was reduced from 3 agents (amlodipine, 5 mg; losartan, 50 mg; chlorthalidone, 12.5 mg) to monotherapy (telmisartan, 20 mg), and the blood pressure remained well-controlled.

Discussion

This report presents a rare case of coexisting adrenal hemangioma and an aldosterone-producing micronodule within a single adrenal incidentaloma. Adrenal incidentalomas often pose diagnostic dilemmas, requiring careful evaluation to differentiate between benign and nonfunctional lesions and those with clinical significance.

Adrenal hemangiomas are rare entities that account for approximately 0.6% of adrenal incidentalomas [2]. Preoperative diagnosis is challenging because of its rarity and imaging features that overlap with those of other adrenal tumors. Previous studies have reported that 42.5% of patients were initially misdiagnosed with pheochromocytoma [2]. Recently, Duan et al analyzed 21 patients with adrenal hemangioma and described their corresponding CT and MRI characteristics [4]. Features suggestive of adrenal hemangioma include a T2-hyperintense signal with an inner hypointense fibrotic scar and peripheral discontinuous nodular enhancement, findings consistent with those observed in our patient. However, our patient lacked calcification, which is another characteristic radiological feature of adrenal hemangioma [4]. Our patient differed from the typical demographic profile of adrenal hemangiomas, which have been reported to occur more than 2 times as often in women as in men [2].

Current guidelines recommend assessing the malignant potential of adrenal incidentalomas primarily based on tumor size and unenhanced CT attenuation values [6]. However, a substantial proportion of lesions fall into the indeterminate category, particularly those with attenuation values greater than 10 HU and/or borderline size characteristics [7], which poses a diagnostic challenge in clinical practice. Lesions in the indeterminate category encompass differential diagnoses, including pheochromocytoma, ACC, and metastatic disease. In our patient, the adrenal lesion was considered indeterminate because of its high unenhanced CT attenuation value (40 HU), despite its relatively small size (1.6 cm). In this clinical setting, careful integration of biochemical, clinical, and radiological findings is essential to refine the differential diagnosis and increase clinical suspicion for adrenal hemangioma.

In our patient, ACC was considered less likely in the absence of biochemical evidence of cortisol overproduction and with normal levels of sex steroids and steroid precursors, including DHEA-S, estradiol, and 17-OHP. Additionally, the lesion was relatively small, further supported a benign etiology rather than ACC [8]. Pheochromocytoma was biochemically excluded, and the likelihood of metastatic disease was low, given the absence of a prior history of cancer or other clinical findings suggestive of an extraadrenal primary tumor. Furthermore, characteristic imaging findings, including a T2-hyperintense signal and peripheral nodular enhancement with delayed centripetal filling, increased clinical suspicion of adrenal hemangioma. Accurate preoperative recognition of these features facilitates appropriate surgical planning and avoids unnecessary preoperative preparation for pheochromocytoma.

To date, several cases of adrenal hemangiomas associated with hormonal hypersecretion have been reported [9-11]. However, none of these studies performed CYP11B2 immunohistochemical staining. Consequently, direct evidence of aldosterone production by the adrenal lesions is lacking.

Notably, the elevated aldosterone level was mitigated during the follow-up examination in our case. While this reduction could theoretically suggest an enlarging adrenal hemangioma compressing the adjacent nodule, several factors argue against this hypothesis. First, hematoxylin and eosin staining confirmed the aldosterone-producing micronodule was morphologically unaffected, with no signs of mechanical compression. Second, the first follow-up was conducted after a mere 1-week interval, which is far too short for a hemangioma to enlarge. Instead, this fluctuation likely reflects the inherent physiological variability of aldosterone, as Yozamp et al previously reported a substantial mean intraindividual coefficient of variation of 31% (range, 0%-81%) [12]. Ultimately, the presence of CYP11B2-positive micronodules, combined with the postadrenalectomy resolution of hypertension and reduced medication needs, confirms the functional nature of the nodule and the successful resolution of primary aldosteronism.

In this case, further studies, including an aldosterone suppression test and adrenal venous sampling, were not performed because the primary indication for surgery was the enlarging adrenal hemangioma rather than primary aldosteronism, and the results of these tests were unlikely to alter the decision for unilateral adrenalectomy. However, if the aldosterone-producing lesion had been located in the contralateral adrenal gland, unilateral adrenalectomy would not have resulted in resolution of primary aldosteronism, and postoperative treatment with a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist might have been required.

This report highlights the importance of an integrated assessment of adrenal incidentalomas, encompassing detailed imaging, comprehensive hormonal evaluation, and meticulous histopathological examination. The presence of multiple pathologies within a single adrenal mass can lead to complex clinical scenarios in which a multidisciplinary approach is crucial for accurate diagnosis and optimal patient management. Without a comprehensive pathological review with immunohistochemical analyses, aldosterone-producing micronodules might have been overlooked, and the coexistence of primary aldosteronism may have remained unrecognized.

In conclusion, this report illustrates that adrenal incidentalomas may have dual pathologies. An integrated assessment of imaging findings, hormonal evaluation, clinical course, and histopathological examination is essential for an accurate diagnosis, and serial follow-ups may provide additional diagnostic clues in selected patients. Furthermore, close collaboration between clinicians and pathologists is crucial for the recognition of clinically relevant coexisting lesions and the optimization of patient management.
