A longitudinal study of the effects of risk perception and response efficacy on adaptation of elderly individuals and individuals with chronic health conditions to heat waves
Authors: Kaddour Mehiriz, Sara Cherqaoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn this study, protection motivation theory (PMT) was used to understand the drivers of adaptation of elder individuals and individuals with chronic health conditions to heatwaves. More particularly, it examines the influence of the perception of heatwave risks and of the effectiveness of five heat protection actions commonly recommended by health authorities on the adoption of these actions.MethodsThe data for this study were collected using three surveys on a sample of residents of Longueuil city - Canada. This longitudinal design allowed the use of individual and time fixed effects model to test the effects of risk perception and response efficacy on adaptation to heatwaves. In addition, the random assignment of a subset of study participants to a group that was exposed to heat warning (treatment group) and a group that was not exposed (control group) enabled the use of instrumental variable method to assess the robustness of the panel fixed effect model findings.ResultsA positive association was found between the perception of the effectiveness of heat protection actions and the likelihood of adopting these actions. However, risk perception and its interaction with the perceived effectiveness of protective actions show no significant effects.ConclusionThis study suggests that improving the perception of the effectiveness of heat prevention actions increases their adoption. However, it questions the relevance of raising risk awareness to achieve this objective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25348-7.Keywords: Heatwave adaptation, Risk perception, Response efficacy, Protection motivation theory

Introduction

Heatwaves, marked by unexpected temperature spikes, can disrupt the body’s ability to regulate itself, leading to health issues ranging from mild conditions to severe cases like heatstroke [1]. Studies have shown increased ambulance usage and emergency room visits during heatwave events [2–4], hospital admissions and deaths [5].Vulnerable groups such as seniors and individuals with heart and respiratory diseases are particularly affected [6].

Adaptive behaviors are crucial in helping communities to cope with hot weather, potentially increasing preparedness and reducing harm [7]. This explains why public health authorities rely on disseminating heatwave warnings and advice to improve risk awareness and prompt protective actions [8]. These communication strategies are devised based on theoretical models such as the health belief model and protection motivation theory (PMT) and, consequently, their effectiveness depends to a large extent on the capacity of these models to explain adaptation to heatwaves.

This study investigates the effects of the perception of heatwaves threats and efficacy of heat protection actions on the adoption of these actions. Prior research generally indicates a positive link between these variables [9–12], but most evidence is based on ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions using cross-sectional data, a situation that raises serious questions on the validity of their findings [13]. More robust approaches therefore are needed to fully understand these influences.

The interest in this study resides in using fixed effects panel models and instrumental variable techniques, overcoming therefore methodological limitations of previous studies [14–16]. Data were collected through three surveys, enabling the implementation of panel fixed effects model to test the effect of risk perception and response efficacy on the adoption of five heatwave protection behaviors. Moreover, the random assignment of study participants to a treatment and a control group was used as an instrument to test the effect of response efficacy on heat protection behaviors. The results of this study show that response efficacy has a positive effect on the adoption of heat protection behaviors. However, no significant association was found between risk perception and the outcome variables and only weak evidence was found supporting the assumption that people showing high risk perception and response efficacy are more likely to adapt to heatwaves than other individuals. These findings, therefore, highlight the importance of the perceived efficacy of heat protection actions over mere awareness of heat risks in driving behavioral change.

Theoretical framework

The Protection Motivation Theory (PMT), established by Rogers in 1975, forms the theoretical background of this study. PMT provides insights into why individuals choose to engage in preventive behaviors to avoid negative consequences. As indicated by its name, PMT posits that individuals’ behaviors are largely motivated by the desire to protect themselves from the threats of their surrounding environment and therefore, protective behavioral changes are driven by the perceived consequences and probability of exposure to the threats, as well as the effectiveness of defensive actions [17]. PMT identifies two principal cognitive appraisal mechanisms that underpin the adoption of protection actions: threat appraisal and coping appraisal. The threat appraisal process involves evaluating the severity of the threat and one’s vulnerability to it. In the literature on health adaptation to extreme weather events, this involves in practice assessing the intensity and probability of exposure to weather events and consequences of this exposure on individual’s safety and health. Coping appraisal, on the other hand, involves evaluating the effectiveness of the adaptive response to avert the threat (response efficacy), confidence in one’s ability to carry out the response (self-efficacy), and the perceived costs of its implementation [17]. The threat appraisal and coping appraisal are thus combined to provide the motivation for the adoption of protection behaviors. More particularly, the theory posits that when a significant threat is perceived, and there’s a robust conviction in the efficacy and practicability of protective actions, the likelihood of adopting such precautions increases [18]. This sequential process of decision-making is important in the theory; it implies that the absence of threat perception hinders the coping evaluation process, thereby reducing the chance of taking protective actions.

The objective of this study is to test the effect of risk perception and response efficacy and their interactions on the adoption of heat protection behaviors1. The majority of studies on this topic show that higher heat risk perception positively influences heat-protective behaviors. In fact, studies have demonstrated that individuals who perceive higher heat risks are more likely to adopt protective behaviors such as using air conditioning, seeking cooler environments, and reducing physical activity [19–24]. Other studies have however found no significant effects of heat risk perception on the adoption of protective behaviors [25, 26]. For response efficacy, studies have found that individuals are more likely to adopt protective behaviors when they believe that these actions effectively mitigate the threat [13, 19, 27]. This aligns with PMT’s assertion that belief in the efficacy of recommended responses encourages their adoption.

Our review of the literature on adaptation to heatwaves did not identify empirical studies that have directly tested the effect of the interaction between risk perception and response efficacy, suggesting that this assumption of the PMT remains untested by research in this field. Given this gap, it is valuable to look at research conducted on other extreme weather events, such as floods, where PMT has been applied. The results of the few studies on this topic are mixed. While one study found a positive effect of the interaction between risk perception and response efficacy on the adoption of air pollution protection actions [28], other studies did not find empirical support for this assumption [29, 30].

The results of previous studies, often limited by cross-sectional data, may not adequately control for confounding factors, leading to biased estimates of the effects of risk perceptions and response efficacy on adaptation to heatwaves. Therefore, there is a need for more robust designs to improve the validity of the evidence that can be used to understand and enhance public health preparedness and resilience against heatwaves, as highlighted by previous studies [19, 31, 32].This study seeks to overcome these methodological issues by using designs known for their strong capacity to deal with unobserved confounding factors, as explained in the methods section.

Data and methodology

Study variables

Heat protection behaviors: this study investigates the effects of risk perception and response efficacy on 5 health protection behaviours. These are drinking water, reducing physical efforts, taking cool showers and baths, frequenting cool or air-conditioned places, and using air conditioner. Data on the adoption of heat protection behaviours were collected immediately after the two heatwaves of the summer 2015. As survey was used to collect data of this study, study participants were asked to indicate the frequency of drinking water and exerting physical effort during the heatwave (1 = much less often than usual to 5 = much more often than usual). To preserve the coherence of interpreting regression coefficients, responses to exerting physical efforts were inverted in the analysis so that its values range between 1 (much more often than usual) to 5 (much less often than usual). This means that higher values indicate better adaptation to heatwaves, as is the case of the other variables.

For cool showers and baths, frequenting cool or air-conditioned places, and using air conditioners, the respondents were first asked about whether they had adopted each behaviour. Those who answered yes were asked to indicate on a five-point scale the frequency of adopting these actions (1 = much less often than usual to 5 = much more often than usual). A new variable for each protection action was then created by merging the answers to these two questions that took the value of 0 if the respondent had not adopted the behaviour at all and 5 if the respondent had adopted the behaviour much more often than usual2. Moreover, as the study is about the short-term responses to heatwaves and not on the long-term adaptation to this hazard, questions on the use of the AC were asked to individuals who indicated that they have this system installed in their homes (80% of the respondents) and thus it was considered irrelevant for the remaining 20% of survey respondents.

Risk perception: participants were asked to indicate to what extent extreme heat is dangerous to their health. Responses were recorded on a five levels scale ranging from 1 (not at all dangerous) to 5 (extremely dangerous).

Response efficacy: to measure this variable, respondents were requested to indicate the best ways to protect their health from the heatwaves, and they had the possibility to mention more than one strategy to this open-ended question. The actions mentioned by the respondents were therefore considered to be perceived as effective and were attributed the value of one. The actions that have not been mentioned were assigned the value of 0. Compared to studies that have used ordinal scales [33], our approach is admittedly less sensitive to within and between individuals variations, but it has the advantage of differentiating between those who hold strong beliefs regarding the efficacy of the heat protection in question and those who did not.

Heatwave awareness: this variable was coded one for respondents who indicated that they were aware of the heatwaves that hit their region and zero for those who indicated they were not.

Sampling and data collection

This study uses data that was collected in a research project that was conducted between 2015 and 2017 in Longueuil city, Canada. The main objective of this project was to use experimental and quasi experimental designs to test the effects of heatwave warning systems on individuals vulnerable to heatwaves. The recruitment of study participants was conducted between 23 March 2015 and 19 June 2015. A call for participation in this study was published in local newspapers, websites and social media and advertised in municipal facilities. Participants could register in person, by phone or email or online. All registrants signed an informed consent to participate in the study form as required by the research ethics committee. At the end of this process, a non-random sample of 1811 individuals meeting the eligibility criteria was formed.

Survey methods were used to collect data on study participants. The first draft of the questionnaire was prepared based on a review of the relevant literature on the issue [34, 35] and was reviewed by 5 public health experts. The revised version was then pretested on 22 individuals who met the eligibility criteria.

The data used in this study were collected by three successive phone surveys. The first survey took place in 2015 from June 25 to July 14 and collected data on the socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of the study participants. A response rate of 79.4% was obtained. The second survey was conducted immediately after the first 2015 heatwave, which hit the city of Longueuil between 27 and 29 July. Its objective was to collect data on the adoption of heat protection behaviors by study participants who responded to the first survey. In addition to heat protection behaviors, data on the three constructs of interest in this study were also collected, that is awareness of the heatwave, perception of the health risk of heatwave and perception of the efficacy of heat protection actions. A response rate of 82.6% was achieved. The third survey was conducted after the second heatwave wave that occurred in the region of Laval between 16 and 19 August 2015, and as was the case of the second survey, its objective was to collect data on awareness, risk perception, response efficacy and heat protection behaviors. A response rate of 72.3% was achieved. A rich database on a sample of persons vulnerable to heat hazards was therefore built that contained two repeated measures of heat protection behaviors, heatwave awareness, perception of heatwave risks and perception of heat protection actions. This panel data provided an opportunity to use the panel fixed effects model to test the effect of risk perception and response efficacy on heat protection behaviors. Moreover, the fact that a subsample of study participants was randomly assigned to treatment and control group made it possible to test the robustness of the findings using instrumental variable design as discussed in the identification strategy section.

Sample attrition is an important issue in longitudinal studies using survey data. In this study, the attrition between the three waves of survey was reduced thanks to the high survey response rates achieved, as mentioned above. Moreover, we tested the effect of the variables listed in Table 1 and of the treatment or control group membership on the likelihood of responding to the second and third waves of the survey, using logit models (see Table 2 in Supplementary material). Compared to the first survey respondents, individuals with chronic health conditions were more likely to respond to the second and third surveys than those who do not suffer from this condition. In absolute values, this corresponds to an 8% difference in the dropout rate between the two groups. The coefficients of the other variables were not significant. While we cannot completely rule out the existence of an attrition bias, this analysis suggests that there are no significant and systematic observable differences between the characteristics of individuals who completed all the survey waves and those who dropped out. Therefore, no other measures were taken to address the attrition bias in the data analysis. Likewise, observations with missing values were dropped from the analysis.

Compared to the cross sectional model, the fixed effects model has the advantage of testing the effects of risk perception and response efficacy on the adoption of heat protection behaviors while controlling for time invariant characteristics of study participants such as sex and ethnicity. It is thus assumed to yield more consistent estimates than cross sectional designs.

Instrumental variable model

To test the robustness of the panel fixed effects model, instrumental variable method was used to assess the relationship between response efficacy and adaptation to heat waves [28]. Instrumental variable method is commonly used to deal with three forms of endogeneity threatening the validity of causal inference studies: omitted variable bias, reverse causality, and measurement errors [36]. This method consists in using a variable that meets the relevance, exogeneity and exclusivity conditions as an instrument to estimate the effect of an endogenous variable on outcome variables [37].

In this study, the random assignment of a subset of study participants (n = 1328) to treatment and control groups was used as an instrument to test the effect of response efficacy on adaptation to heatwaves. The treatment group received a warning message on their landline just before the onset of the second heatwave, in which recipients were informed about the heatwave and advised to adopt the five heat protection actions analyzed in this study. As the warning message has positive effect on the perception of the efficacy of three protective actions (Table 6), it fills the relevance condition. Moreover, the random assignment of participants to treatment and control groups ensures that exposure to the heatwave warning message is independent of the participants’ adaptation behaviors, thereby satisfying the exogeneity condition of the instrument [38].

In addition to the relevance and exogeneity conditions, the validity of the instrument depends on the assumption that after controlling for the confounding factors, the effect of the warning message on respondents’ behavior is exclusively channeled through response efficacy (exclusivity restriction). The exclusivity restriction assumption cannot be verified empirically in all instrumental variable designs, but in the relatively controlled character of this experiment, this assumption is highly plausible. In fact, the heat warning message was designed to influence the behaviors of the experimental group members through two mechanisms. The first mechanism consists in informing the participants of the occurrence of the heatwave and its health consequences in order to improve their risk awareness. The second mechanism is recommending the five heat protection behaviors mentioned above to increase the perception of their efficacy. It is thus unlikely that the experiment influenced participants’ behavior by other means than those included in the heat warning message. Therefore, to make the restriction assumption more plausible, we controlled in the statistical model for the risk perception and awareness of the heatwave that can be affected by the exposure to the heat warning. In addition, we tested the direct effect of the instrument on adaptation behaviors while controlling response efficacy, the endogenous variable, and the other covariates, as suggested by some studies to gauge the plausibility of the exclusivity restriction assumption [39]. We found that the coefficient of the instrument was not statistically significant across the five health protection behaviors, suggesting that its effect on the outcome variables is totally channeled through the three constructs of the PMT considered in this study. In sum, while the exclusion restriction cannot be tested directly, the validity of this study’s instrument is reinforced by the randomized assignment of the participants in the experiment and inclusion of awareness and perceived risk as control variables. These steps help isolate the effect of response efficacy on heat protection behaviors.

The use of the instrumental variable method complements the fixed effect model in two ways. First, it reduces the risk of omitted variable bias caused by the difficulty of including as controls all the relevant and time-sensitive variables in the two way fixed effects model. Second, the method deals adequately with the problem of reverse causality. In this study, the problem of reverse causality is present when respondents, based on their experience during the heatwave, were amenable to reevaluate their vulnerability to this hazard and the effectiveness of the recommended protection actions. The regression coefficients in the panel fixed effect model might thus reflect the net effect of this mutual influence between the dependent and explanatory variables. As the exposure to the warning message was decided by a random process and thus it is not influenced by the adoption of heat protection actions, it is an exogenous source of variation of the perception of response efficacy that is used to estimate the effect of this variable on heat protection behaviors. The regression coefficient thus expresses the effect of response efficacy on the outcome variables and not the other way around, provided that the exclusivity restriction assumption is valid, as discussed before.

The instrumental variable method was implemented using STATA 18’s eoprobit command, as eoprobit is suitable for ordinal outcome variables. In this model, the instrument is the treatment group. It takes the value of one for study participants who were randomly selected to receive the heat warning message and zero for the members of the control group. As the instrument (Treatment Group) is a dummy variable, a binary logit model was used in the first stage regression to estimate the likelihood that a respondent cites the protective action in question among the most effective ways of heat protection measures. In the second equation, an ordinal logit model was used to test the effect of response efficacy on the five behaviors that were measured on ordinal scales.

Due to their strong capacity to control for unobserved characteristics compared to multiple regression analysis using cross sectional data that predominates research on adaptation to heatwaves [13], these methods are assumed to yield results that are more consistent than those of most previous studies on this topic.

Perception of response efficacy

Data on risk perception indicate that 50% of survey respondents consider that heatwave is very dangerous or extremely dangerous to their health, 27% as somewhat dangerous and 23% as slightly or not at all dangerous. For heatwave awareness, 97% of respondents knew that their region was exposed to heatwave and among them 63% were informed about this heat episode before its onset. Panel fixed effects model

Table 4 presents the results of testing the association between risk perception and response efficacy with heat protection behaviors using the ordinal fixed effect panel model3. The analysis shows that there are positive and statistically significant associations between the perception of the response efficacy and heat protection behaviors for the outcomes drinking water (β = 0.475; p-value = 0.037), reducing physical efforts (β = 0.664; p-value = 0.02) and taking cool baths and showers (β = 0.791; p-value = 0.010). By contrast, the coefficient of the perceived risk of heatwave is not statistically significant in the five models. The analysis thus suggests that the perceived effectiveness of heat protection actions increases the likelihood of adopting these actions. In contrast, risk perception does not significantly influence these behaviors. This emphasizes the importance of response efficacy perception over risk perception in motivating adaptive behaviors during heatwaves.

Instrumental variable

At the beginning of this section, it should be noted that the instrumental variable (IV) method was used to check the robustness of the effect of response efficacy, but not for risk perception. This is because the instrument, the random assignment to the treatment group, does not exhibit a statistically significant association with risk perception in the first stage regression. This violates the relevance condition, a key requirement for a valid instrument as discussed in the methodology section. As risk perception could not be instrumented, it was instead included as a control variable, similar to the awareness of the heatwave variable.

Table 6 presents the results of using the random assignment of study participants to treatment and control groups as an instrument to test the effect of response efficacy on the adoption of the recommended behaviors. The results of the first stage regression indicate that the instrument is significant in three models: drinking water, reducing physical efforts, and visiting cool or air-conditioned places. It thus meets the relevance conditions for these models and, accordingly, we focus our attention on the interpretation of their results.

The results of the second stage regression indicate the existence of positive and significant associations between response efficacy and drinking water (β = 1.679), reducing physical efforts (β = 1.79) and visiting cool places (β = 1.488), confirming therefore the findings of the fixed effect model. Table 6 also shows the existence of strong correlations between the error terms of the first and second stage regressions, suggesting the endogeneity of the response efficacy and thus the bias of cross-sectional designs that fail to account for relevant unobserved differences between study participants.

In summary, this study suggests that the perception of response efficacy has positive and significant effects on the adoption of heat protection actions. By contrast, risk perception and its interaction with response efficacy were not found to affect adaptation to heat waves.

Discussion

This study investigates the roles of perceived risk and response efficacy in influencing individual adaptation to the increasing frequency and severity of heatwaves driven by climate change. Drawing on the Protection Motivation Theory, the research was conducted in the city of Longueuil, Canada between 2015 and 2017, targeting a social group vulnerable to hot weather and composed of elders and individuals with chronic health conditions. Employing advanced statistical methods, including fixed effects panel models and instrumental variable models, this study contributes to the existing literature by providing a more robust analysis of the factors shaping heatwave adaptation behaviors, thereby addressing significant methodological limitations identified in previous studies [14–16].

The fixed effects model, which controls for unobserved heterogeneity by allowing individuals to have their own intercepts, reveals that the perceived effectiveness of drinking water, reducing physical efforts, taking showers, visiting cool places, and using air conditioner significantly increases the frequency of using these heat protection actions. These results are confirmed by the use of the instrumental variable method. In contrast, risk perception does not significantly influence these behaviors. The analysis also indicated that the interaction between risk perception and response efficacy does not augment the frequency of adapting to heatwaves. The results of this study thus lend partial support to the PMT. While the effect of response efficacy is as it was expected, the insignificant effect of risk perception is not aligned with the predictions of the theory. Regarding the available evidence, the majority of studies found a positive effect of risk perception on the adoption of heat protection behaviors [19, 21, 22] and that only few studies did not find a significant association between these variables [25, 26]. A possible explanation of the divergence of our results from the findings of the majority of studies on this topic is related to the methodological differences: the fixed-effects and IV models of this study account for unobserved heterogeneity, whereas many prior studies rely on cross-sectional ordinary least squares (OLS) models that may overstate associations due to omitted variable bias. This is likely the case because when we reanalyze the data of this study using cross-sectional OLS models or panel random effect models that do not control for unobserved heterogeneity, we find, like these studies, a positive association between risk perception and heat protection behaviors. Equally important, the significant correlations between the error terms of the first stage and second stage regressions of the instrumental models that are reported in Table 6 suggest that the estimates of the OLS models are biased. The sensitivity of the results to causal inference methods thus reinforces the need to re-evaluate commonly accepted assumptions using more robust designs.

For the interaction between risk perception and response efficacy, the current study’s findings reveal a significant association for visiting cool places, but not for other protective behaviors, offering limited support for the hypothesized positive relationship across behaviors. Studies on other health protection behaviors using fine grained distinctions between the categories of respondents based on the extended parallel process model [33] found that the interaction is significant only for individuals showing high levels of risk perception and response efficacy [28, 40]. Studies using similar approach would thus improve our understanding of the intricate relationships between risk perception, response efficacy and adoption of heat protection actions.

Regarding the policy implications, insights from this study can contribute to the development of heatwave warning campaigns and safety guidelines specifically designed to protect vulnerable groups, such as seniors and individuals with heart and respiratory diseases, from heatwave exposure. More specifically, it suggests that efforts need to be invested in finding ways of improving the perception of the efficacy of the heat protection actions commonly recommended by health experts and authorities. This study also suggests that information on the occurrence of heatwaves is not an issue as almost all participants knew that their region was exposed to this hazard. This is a stark contrast with smog as indicated by a study on the same region that found that half of the respondents to the survey were not aware of the smog episode [41].

As mentioned above, this study uses a strong design to test the effects of risk perception and response efficacy and their interaction on the adoption of heat protection behaviors, contributing therefore to the accumulation of reliable knowledge on this topic. However, we did not test in this study the effect of perceived self-efficacy, an important component of the PMT, on the adoption of heat protection actions. This is an important issue because the adaptive behaviors examined in this study are not uniform in their ease of implementation or resource demands. Some behaviors, such as increasing water intake or reducing physical effort, can in most situations be enacted immediately and independently. Others, like using air conditioning or accessing cool public spaces, may require financial means, physical mobility, or prior infrastructure. The existence of differences between individuals in terms of their capacity to implement these actions contributes thus to the heterogeneity of responses to heatwaves [13, 42]. Therefore, future studies should focus on investigating the role of self-efficacy in greater detail to gain a clearer understanding of how it affects people’s choices to adopt protective behaviors during heatwaves. This could lead to valuable insights for developing more effective intervention strategies. Likewise, the data of this study was collected in 2015 and thus it may not represent adequately the current responses to heatwaves, threatening therefore the validity of studies whose objective is to measure the present level of responses to heatwaves. The age of research data, in contrast, is less preoccupying in the case of this studies that test the effects of risk perception and response efficacy on adaptation to heatwaves, provided that the causal relationships between the constructs of the PMT are relatively stable over time, as assumed by the theory.

Conclusion

In this study, panel fixed effect model and instrumental variable methods were used to test the effects of risk perception and response efficacy on the adaptation of elderly people and individuals with chronic health conditions to heatwaves, overcoming therefore the methodological limitations of cross sectional designs commonly used to study this topic. The findings of this study indicate that a positive perception of the effectiveness of heat protection actions increases the likelihood of the adoption of these actions. However, risk perception and its interaction with the perceived effectiveness show no significant effects. This study therefore suggests that improving the perception of the effectiveness of heat prevention actions increases their adoption. However, it questions the relevance of raising risk awareness to achieve this objective. Compared to most studies on this topic, it thus provides relevant and reliable evidence on the drivers of adaptation to heatwaves that can be used to guide policies and practices to mitigate the adverse effect of this weather hazard.
