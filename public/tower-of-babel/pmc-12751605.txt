Exploring doctors experiences of transitioning to full time research and back to clinical practice: a qualitative study
Authors: Maria Farren, Cathal Darby, Shahad Al Tikriti, Mary Higgins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the growing interest in research degrees among clinicians, little is knowns about doctors’ lived experiences transitioning between clinical practice and full-time research. Understanding these transitions is essential for developing appropriate support structure and optimising career pathways. This study aimed to explore the experience for doctors of transitioning to research and back to clinical work.MethodsQualitative study using interpretative phenomenological analysis and the conceptual framework of Multiple and Multidimensional Transitions (MMT). Doctors undertaking or having completed full-time research participated in semi-structured interviews, analysed using template analysis.ResultsTen doctors (one man, nine women) took part in the research. Six were undertaking an doctorate of medicine (MD), three a doctorate of philosophy (PhD) and one a full time Masters (MSc). Six themes emerged: pre research phase, decision to undertake research, initial honeymoon, later reality, drive to complete and back to clinical practice.Participants chose research for career progression, teaching experience or a “break” from full time clinical practice. Initially there was a sense of freedom from the clinical weekly roster, enjoying the flexibility that research afforded them, and then frustration and disbelief over how long everything took to happen or come to fruition. Support was mainly from peers, with those who were part of a research group finding the transition easiest. After the initial novelty the reality of perhaps more tedious aspects of full time research became apparent. Experience with proposal writing, project execution, data analysis and writing was invaluable for future projects and medical literature interpretation. For those who had returned to full time clinical work, there was increased confidence as they had gained skills during their research years that made aspects of the clinical job easier. Maintaining clinical skills during research was essential for successful transition back to practice.ConclusionThis study reveals challenges in the research transition from clinical practice to research that were common to participants but often experienced in isolation. Findings highlight the need for enhanced peer support networks, realistic expectation-setting, and structured mentorship programmes to optimise the research training experience and facilitate successful return to clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08174-8.Keywords: Transition, Clinical training, Research, Doctorate, PhD

Background

The medical education continuum encompasses the structured pathway from medical school through speciality training to consultant practice, characterised by multiple transition points that require adaptation of professional identity, skills, and responsibilities. At first glance, progression through this continuum can appear linear, with smooth stepping from one phase – student, to doctor in training, to trained doctor – to another. In reality, the continuum provides multiple transitions and decision points, allowing numerous opportunities and experiences. These transitions, can have an impact and effect personally, professionally and inter-professionally [1, 2] and may affect both professional and personal lives, including changes in geography, responsibility, finances, team and culture amongst others [3]. Transition theory defines transitions as passages between life phases that require adaptation of roles, relationships, and behaviours [4]. Four key factors identified in literature affecting transition success: the situation, self, support, and strategies [5]. In healthcare education, research has revealed that successful transitions require adequate preparation, realistic expectations, and appropriate support structures [6–8]. To make the process as smooth as possible may mean re-adapting, re-evaluating and re-learning coping mechanisms [6]. Transitions can present opportunities for change and improvement [7], for professional growth and, for many an opportunity for a much needed change [9]. The person transitioning may be naïve about the differences, with little appreciation of the challenges and stresses ahead. Conversely, hidden and unforeseen difficulties and challenges may emerge [9].

The transition from pre-clinical to clinical undergraduate, from undergraduate to postgraduate and from trainee to trained doctor has been well described [10, 11]. Research has largely focused on these linear educational transitions (student to doctor, trainee to consultant) [10–14] rather than the bidirectional clinical-research-clinical pathway that is increasingly common in modern medical careers. There is minimal research in the area of transitions to researcher and back to clinical practice in doctors. Of the research to date, most has focused on medical students completing MD (Doctorate of Medicine, here in context as medical student)/PhD (Doctorate of Philosophy) programmes where students take eight years to complete a medical school programme and a research project to PhD standard. For these, the focus is largely on the transition back to clinical medical student practice [15] or opinions of or goals after the programme [16].

There are numerous advantages of medical doctors completing formal research programmes. Research experience may add value to career progression, given the transferable skills developed as part of informal and, more so, formal research programmes [17]. There is an ongoing need for clinician academics to devise and lead research, for “research able” clinicians to undertake local research and coordinate projects, and all clinicians need to be able to appraise and apply research literature [18]. For the individual, multiple skills can be acquired including management, communication, presentation, statistical analysis and dissemination of findings [19]. Residency applicants are more likely to be successful in matching to a speciality with increased research experience [17]. Many medical doctors have expressed a wish for more research experience [18], and nearly one in ten have a formal doctorate by research [18]. The benefits of research do not lie just with the clinicians. Patients attending research active hospitals have multiple benefits, with studies showing improvement in patient care [20] including improved communication and confidence in staff [21] and even reductions in post operative mortality and increased five year survival compared to research poor hospitals [22].

Understanding these transitions is essential for several reasons. First, the growing emphasis on clinicians pursuing higher research degrees means more doctors will experience these transitions. General PhD support structures may require some changes to meet the needs of clinicians (and, indeed, others who have been in the working population before returning to university); if inadequate this may result in stress, attrition, and suboptimal outcomes. Second, unsuccessful research transitions represent significant resource waste for healthcare systems and lost opportunities for medical advancement. Third, without understanding the lived experience of these transitions, medical education programmes cannot develop evidence-based support interventions. This study addresses a critical gap by exploring the stages of the bidirectional transition experience, providing insights essential for optimising research training pathways and supporting the next generation of clinician- scientists.

We aimed to explore the experiences of doctors who undertook clinical research posts. We wanted to better understand their transition from full time clinical practice within their specialty to clinical researcher and back again to clinician. We were also curious to identify what advice participants would give on how to improve the experience for others.

Methods

Context

This is a qualitative study of doctors who were working/had worked in a research post while in clinical training, including a Masters in Science (MSc), doctorate in Medicine (MD, in Ireland this is a research degree qualification, rather than a qualification to practice medicine) or doctorate in philosophy (PhD).

Research question and paradigm

The research question was: What are the experiences of doctors as they transition to full time research posts and then back to clinical practice? For this research question the most appropriate research design was qualitative research, using an interactive research design and inductive approach [23], and a constructivist, post-positivist lens [24]. Phenomenology [25] specifically Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) was most appropriate this helps study how participants make sense of their personal and social world [26], with theoretical foundations of phenomenology, hermeneutics and ideography [27]. The conceptual framework was Multiple and Multidimensional Transitions (MMT), a framework originally described for different life transitions [28, 29] and has more recently been used to explore healthcare transitions [6, 14, 30, 31]. MMT reflects that transitions are often not linear, but that multiple domains may be identified, including physical, cultural, psychological, and social [6, 32].

Population and participant recruitment

In January 2023 we invited doctors who were registered as full time MSc, MD or PhD candidates, or who had completed one of these research degrees to participate in the study. Posters were displayed in workplaces and via social media and trainees messaging groups inviting doctors to participate. Those who were interested in participating contacted the researcher by email. Further information was provided and time given to review the participant information leaflets. Written informed consent was obtained.

Data collection

For this qualitative analysis study, semi structured interviews were conducted by one researcher (MF) at a time convenient to the participants. At the time of conducting the interviews, MF was still in a training role, similar to all participants. Some of the participants had been former colleagues of members of the research team, which may have been beneficial in the interview due to a mutual established trust and relationship. These participants knew and had consented that transcripts would be anonymised and the remaining members of the research team would be not aware of their specific participation. For participants who were unknown to MF, time was taken to establish a rapport to allow time to reflect on their experiences. As interviews progressed questions were adapted to reflect new areas developed (supplementary file). Interviews were conducted virtually using a secure virtual communication tool (Zoom) using a university account and were recorded and transcribed.

Data analysis

Once transcribed and anonymised, all interviews were coded independently by the researchers and then analysed using template analysis (TA). This is a form of is structured, flexible and adaptive to the study needs. It involves six steps: becoming familiar with the data, preliminary coding on a subset of data, organising themes (hierarchical and/or lateral), defining an initial coding template, applying and modifying and “finalising” the template [33]. As IPA methodology is more of a “bottom up” approach, TA was adapted not to use an a priori template [33]. Each researcher read the anonymised interviews coded and analysed the data then met to compare themes and subthemes. Any differences were reconciled by discussion.

Rigour was assured by sufficiency of data (rather than saturation) based on information power, which was determined by the aims, the sample participants interviewed, quality of interviews and the methodologies used [34]. Quotes are used to provide evidence of the developed themes of the pathway, with participants referred by number and course of study (e.g. Participant 2, P2, PhD student; Participant 5, P5, MD student).

Reflexivity and reflection

Reflexivity and reflection were practiced during this study. Personal characteristics that may influence the research include that two researchers had completed full time research projects to doctorate standard (MH, MF), and may have assumptions based on their own personal experience. MH has supervised students to complete MD/PhD degrees, and MF was working as a Fellow in Advanced Medical Education. CD was a medical student with minimal research experience, with perhaps less assumptions on the experiences of this bidirectional transition than the others. Finally SA-T was a medical doctor starting a full-time research study (SA-T) on the topic of transitions within formalised PhD programmes, while herself also experiencing the transition from clinical to research work. The three doctors work in Obstetrics and Gynaecology. MF had prior relationships with some but not all of the participants; the other three researchers were unaware of the identify of participants and only had access to anonymised transcripts.

Research ethics

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the School of Medicine in University College Dublin (LS-C-22–195-Farren); this study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

There were 10 participants, one man and nine women. Six were undertaking an doctorate of medicine (MD), three a doctorate in philosophy (PhD) and one a full time Masters (MSc). Eight participants were in training in Obstetrics and Gynaecology and two from Medicine. Four had already returned to full time clinical while six were still pursuing their higher degree. Out of the ten projects, three were clinical based research, two were lab based and five were mixed clinical and lab projects. The median interview length was 56 min (range 42 to 75 min).

After analysis the most appropriate way we believed to map the overarching themes was to the timeline or journey of research. This includes the pre-research phase and the decision to undertake research, the initial honeymoon phase, the later reality, the drive to complete and finally the transition back to full time clinical work for those who had completed their time in full time research.

Pre-research phase and decision to do research

When exploring the time preceding the start of research, participants reflected on why they undertook research. Decisions were based on career progression or “currency”, meaning that research would give a professional edge in competing for a substantive post. While this was not the sole motivation for research, it was a strong theme for motivation. Full time research also afforded the opportunity to gain teaching experience, for example also working in tutor roles during the time spent in research. Another reason for research was the need for a “break” from full time clinical practice.

“I was barely hanging on as it is….so I said to her, ‘is there any way I can do a PhD in July, I need to get off this’….the PhD was a life belt, as a way of staying in O&G (Obstetrics and Gynaecology) while also doing research. I told them.

I am taking a break, so I am doing a PhD”. (P1, PhD student).

Burn out and exhaustion were also cited as the reason for switching to research, or a need to be in the same physical location as family, either children or parents or both. There was a perception that life in research would be easier, though this was proven to not be the case. Expectations included that the foundations of the project would already be in place; this was not always possible, with a description of how a project was a vague idea, without any proposal or plan for how the project would be carried out.

“It was given to me in a very bare bones sort of way. And I sort of had to figure out what exactly we would do. It was sort of like ‘here’s an idea, and see what you can do.’’ (P5, MD student).

Participants were surprised if they had to build the project from the ground up, write proposals, apply for ethical approval or apply for funding.

“ethics was done. But in terms of anything else I had to start from scratch” (P9, PhD student).

This could be a positive experience, especially if they were to consider research in the future

“I still do no regret doing it and since [time] I am really enjoying it” (P3, MD Student).

For those that already had a project in place, even if it was a broad topic, there were participants that reported that they struggled more to be enthusiastic around a topic that was of limited interest to them.

Initial honeymoon

Initially there was a universal sense of freedom from the clinical weekly roster. Participants described enjoying the flexibility that research afforded them.

“Initially, yes, it was great. The flexibility. It was July, the weather was good, I could finish [for the day] when I wanted”.

When asked about the physical environment, there were designated places to work, albeit small and overcrowded in most instances. While some participants were well furnished with laptop, computer facilities, desk spaces and lab access if needed, material, laptops and resources for others had to be self-sourced and self-funded. Overall, this was not a source of stress in the early stages of research.

The financial transition was varied across the study group, with reports of little change in salary, small differences or dramatic difference that necessitated taking on additional work to top up income.

“The way it worked was that I got my stipend…that was a big drop in salary. You did your [paid] on calls [clinical work] to bring that up”.

Of those who did receive a comparative salary, they felt that this was a factor in facilitating them being able to take time out for a full time research post.

“No, I was lucky I had a normal salary. It wasn’t completely like for like with my SpR [Specialist Registrar, or senior resident] point, but it wasn’t far off” (PA5, MD student).

A strong theme was the change in pace compared to the clinical world. The transition from short term to medium-long term goals resulted in frustration and disbelief over how long everything took to happen or come to fruition.

“It was very different in that you still had lots to do but that it happened much slower. research was ‘here’s a task, it might take you weeks.’’ (P5, MD student).

Participants described a feeling of being lost and experiencing a loss of professional identity.

“.then you go to a role where you’re probably the most junior, You feel very insecure again and probably less supported” (PA7, MD student).

They had left a world where they were practising at a senior level and were now finding themselves in a new world where they were relatively junior. There was a new skill set to learn, new challenges and a sense of not knowing what to do. This change could be unsettling and participants cited the support around them, or lack thereof, as being impactful.

Support in transition to research

Support was mainly from peers rather than supervisors or institutions. Those who worked in research teams or groups found the transition easiest, compared to those who predominantly worked alone.

“Embodiment of girl power, everyone helped everyone, they told me how to register, all the things I needed to do about referencing, software, easy modules to do to get started.” (P1, PhD student).

Peers also supported them during difficult phases.

“Trauma is very bonding” (P1, PhD student).

Later reality

After the initial novelty of the flexibility in hours and a change in work/life balance, the reality of the more tedious aspects of full time research became apparent.

“You just have to figure everything out a little bit, especially with that programming and coding stuff. It was definitely really hard” (P2, PhD student).

The slow pace of progress became frustrating and resulted in a lack of job satisfaction. The “hidden work” of full time research became a struggle. This hidden work included more clinical commitments than they had thought, with some only having a half day or one full day per week for research. This ultimately resulted in stress for the participant and necessitated work outside of work hours, often late at night. Those who worked in units where their research time was fully protected and additional work was for their own personal experience described rarely having to work outside of core hours, unless they had deadlines to meet.

“anything I did have to do (outside hours) was just if I thought I had not been efficient during the day and I would do more coming up to deadlines” (PA 8, MD student).

Participants spoke of the contrasting “always on” feeling that research brought.

“It quickly started to eat into my evenings and weekends. It becomes a bit of a monster, always on your shoulder…this all-consuming beast”. (P1, PhD student).

After the initial novelty of flexibility, it became apparent that the work involved, in their absence, simply didn’t get done. This influenced whether or not they took annual leave or study leave. Even while on leave there was still a feeling of being connected to work.

“in ignoring emails you are only hurting yourself” (PA2 PhD student).

Drive to complete

Supervisors were described as being very much on the periphery, with the supervisor role having little effect day to day. Participants described frustration at the absence of their supervisor but then when meetings occurred the assumption that task would be done or more progress occurred but without any specific guidance.

“Initially I felt like I wanted much more direction than she was expecting to give, And I think they even commented on it at one point which was very embarrassing” (PA3, PhD student).

This was not a universal experience but a notable theme. For those who worked in larger groups, the absence of a supervisor had less impact, as they drew on the surrounding team for ongoing help. They also felt that by having the team structure allowed the supervisor more freedom and a degree of separation from the researchers

“but I think the support structure being there meant they didn’t have to be as involved. It was all really well thought out by them” (PA8, MD student).

The process of having transitioned from clinical to research and in some cases back to clinical work again was useful in “demystifying” the process. They initially embarked on a “great unknown” that at times was unfulfilling and left them with a change in professional identity. They cited the experience of working with universities, writing proposals, executing projects, analysing data and writing papers as being invaluable, not just for future projects but for the analysing and interpretation of medical literature.

Meaning and significance of their work

Participants reported a sense of insignificance to their project. What started out with high numbers, after various exclusions, the final numbers, to them, seemed small.

"…thinking God, I’m not achieving anything, this is terrible" (PA 9, PhD student).

For some, the opposite was true. They had encountered a topic so wide, that it was proving difficult to narrow down specific aspects.

End in sight

There was also perhaps a sense of burn out in itself as the end of the project neared. Near the end they spoke of the constant presence of the unfinished thesis.

“My thesis that isn’t submitted, maybe it will never be” (P5, MD student).

Others were nervous about returning to responsibility.

“. .feeling of having let your clinical skill set sit on the shelf for a while… Will I be able to do a laparoscopy when I go back….that fear that ‘she’s useless, she sat upstairs doing research for three years, what sort of consultant shell be’ ”. (P1, PhD student).

This was especially difficult for those who may not have had significant clinical experience before research

"That was the most challenging part of it all (more challenging than doing a PhD)..going back to training meant that I was starting from scratch"

(P6, PhD student).

Return back to clinical practice

For those who had not submitted their thesis before returning to clinical practice, there was pressure to submit while also working long hours clinically.

“…it’s almost like task is too enormous and I don’t know where to start…I can’t even entertain it and it’s so daunting.” (P5, MD student).

One had completed their research viva, leaving only minor corrections to the thesis when returning to clinical practice.

“I did manage to get this out of the way before I went back to clinical work… that chapter was nearly closed… It would have been a lot more complicated if I went back to clinical work, all the time it takes and the commitment it takes to try and write things up and prepare for the viva” (P6, PhD student).

For those who had returned to full time clinical work, they spoke of an increased confidence. They had gained skills during their research years that made aspects of the job easier. It was essential to maintain some clinical skills whilst in full time research and without this, the transition back would be much more challenging. They struggled was the sense of having to once again conform to a rigid rota and the loss of the being able to control their own time. This contrasted with the pleasure being able to “leave work at work”, knowing someone else will take care of whatever arises. There was a strong theme that research was useful and gave a greater understanding of medical literature. Opinion was divided on whether every clinician should have to do research or not. There was a perception of a hidden goal that research is considered almost essential for career progression. They noted that research would not be appropriate for everyone. There was strong advice that alternatives to research should also be valued in career progression for those who chose not to embark on research projects.

There was a satisfaction of short term projects in clinical practice.

“I would say that clinical work is in a way easier, you are busy, running around putting out fires. But there’s a much more instant gratification to clinical work and as I said before, the clinic starts, the clinic has to end.” (P5, MD student).

Discussion

Research to date on the experiences of PhD students and graduates have highlighted may themes that were reiterated in this study, including the challenges in shift in role and work practice, changes in work style, emotional and psychological challenges, new relationships, personal developments, work life balances and financial stress [35–38]. This study provides important insights that both support and extend existing literature on professional transitions and medical education. Our findings that doctors enter research seeking respite from clinical burnout, rather than academic ambition, contrasts with assumptions in existing research training literature and has significant implications for how research programmes are designed and supported. The general findings were in many ways unsurprising and also met experiences of other healthcare and general transitions. There was a sense of uncertainty as routines change, skills are learned and professional identity evolves [3]. We had expected that the participants would highlight the pressure to publish, the challenges of submitting a thesis and difficulties around data analysis. However, this did not bear out to be the case. What was surprising was the reasons for doing research including for career progression and for a change from what was described as a gruelling training process. Some saw it as an opportunity to spend time in one location, allowing them some certainty during years when families were young or parents were in need of care, perhaps because most were women with different domestic or caregiving needs to male researchers [39]. Many were exhausted in clinical practice and looked to research as a break, including the poignant comment that “I was so burnt out I chose to do a PhD”. The idea of making a difference or a plan to be an academic was not a reason reported that initiated the transition to research.

The start of full time research was for many initially freeing, moving from clinical rosters to self-directed work with medium to long term deadlines. Support was mostly from other peers, and those who worked in research teams found the transition easier. As the research journey progressed, the initial freedom changed into day to day work to move research forward, sometimes tedious or challenging, and sometimes spilling into personal life. For some there was a challenge to move from the comfort of familiarity with clinical practice to becoming a research novice and building new skills; all highlighted the importance exposure to some clinical work to maintain confidence, competence in clinical skills and remind themselves of their primary role as clinicians and make the transition back easier. Given that only four participants had returned to clinical practice after completion, challenges of submission of theses, assessment by Viva or publications of research were not as evident as the experience of being a researcher. Despite not calling themselves academics, most felt the experience they gained would be of use for “future projects”.

The universal experience of professional identity disruption identified in our study supports theoretical work [40, 41] on professional identity formation being fluid and context-dependent. Having a well-formed professional identify allows “individuals to develop a meaningful professional self-understanding that facilitates their transition to and sustainability in professional work”. The work to develop this professional identity includes “becoming” (learning, developing, strengthening and feeling like a professional), “aligning” (with values, behaviours and expectations) and “exploring” (different aspects of the role career paths or approaches) [42]. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating how identity loss specifically affects research motivation and performance, particularly when combined with perceived inadequate institutional support. There is a call to make professional identify formation training explicit in medicine. Including by establishing identify formation as an educational objective, engaging students in the development of their own identifies, providing a welcoming community and offering faculty development that includes professional identity as an educational objective. These arguments for medicine also hold for academic medicine and research; they are often implicitly included in training but could perhaps be more explicit.

Our participants’ emphasis on peer support as most valuable aligns with transition literature emphasizing social support during career changes [5]. However, the frequent perception of absence or peripheral role of formal supervisors contrasts with expectations in research training programs and suggests significant gaps between intended and actual support structures.

The predominance of female participants (90%) and their citation of caring responsibilities as research drivers aligns with literature documenting gender disparities in academic medicine career patterns [39, 43]. Our finding that women viewed research as offering better work-life balance, only to discover it becoming “all-consuming,” suggests misaligned expectations that may impact women’s career progression in academic medicine. It is also worth noting that we did not ask if participants had children or the age of their children. Research has shown that women with children aged < 5 years of age during the pandemic had reduced academic productivity compared to those with no children or children aged over six years of age [44]. Academic productivity in this paper related to first, corresponding, co-author submissions, peer review assignments and funding panel meetings.

Many of the participants were specialising in Obstetrics and Gynaecology. It is interesting to note that this speciality has an inherent oxymoron on research capability. While female trainees in Obstetrics and Gynaecology report that they are less likely to consider academic careers than male trainees (13% females compared to 27% male trainees) [45] this is also a speciality with one of the highest increases in female first and senior authors [46].

These findings have several important implications for medical education policy and practice. First, research training programs could address possible unrealistic expectations about work-life balance and provide structured support for professional identity transitions. Second, supervisor training should include awareness of transition challenges and skills in supporting professional identity development. Third, peer mentorship programs could be formalized, given participants’ identification of peer support as most valuable. Finally, institution and training bodies could consider how to maintain clinical skills during research training to facilitate smoother transitions back to practice.

As with any qualitative study, the findings from this study are limited by context. All of the participants were medical doctors completing thesis research, and given the time of interviews (2023–2024) many had worked in healthcare during the COVID pandemic. It may be that the intensity of clinical work during this time could have contributed to the burnout or changes in priorities of clinical staff [47–49]resulting in a wish to transition to other areas of healthcare education and training. In addition, four of the ten participants had returned back to clinical practice and completed the bi-directional transition. We deliberately included those at all stages in the transition but recognise that this may be a limitation in that six of the participants were still working in research.

Other limitations may include the gender differences (with only one male participant) and focus on one speciality in medicine (Obstetrics and Gynaecology).

Conclusion

While research training ultimately builds valuable skills and confidence, as supported by existing literature on clinician-scientist outcomes [50], our study reveals significant gaps between long-term benefits and immediate transition challenges. These findings highlight the need for evidence-based support interventions during the transition process itself.

An element of the study that surprised the researchers was the emotion demonstrated by those who participated. The transitions itself, the struggles encountered all in the context of what was occurring external to the research world were sources of deep emotion for many. It would be our belief that those embarking on research have greater support from research teams, supervisors and colleges. There needs to be an awareness of the universal challenges experienced when embarking on a full time research programme. Despite these being universal, the perception by the individual is that they are alone in their feelings. Greater supports to ease the transitions along the way would serve to reduce the sense of loneliness and prevent any future researchers from experiencing the struggles of those who have gone before them. Peers were identified as being particularly useful to support new colleagues and allow them space to reflect on the transitions. Future research projects by our group will include a similar qualitative study exploring the experiences of clinicians transitioning to research within a formalised clinician academic structure, the Irish Clinician Academic Training (ICAT) programme, and development and validation of a questionnaire exploring this transition more widely.
