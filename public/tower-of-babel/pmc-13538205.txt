Impact of car-wash wastewater discharge on urban streams: a case study from Jimma city, southwestern Ethiopia
Authors: Mahmud Ahmednur, Derartu Nasir, Samson Ayele, Higemengist Astatkie, Megersa Worku Tesso, Dessalegn Dadi Olani, Kasahun Eba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Freshwater ecosystems in rapidly urbanizing regions are increasingly threatened by effluent discharges. Car-wash stations are a significant source of pollution, releasing effluents rich in detergents, oils, and organic matter directly into streams. Despite global evidence demonstrating the effects of car-wash effluent on receiving water bodies, it remains overlooked in Ethiopia. This study assessed the impact of car-wash effluent on water quality and habitat condition of urban streams in Jimma city, Ethiopia. The study was conducted at seven car-wash stations in April 2023. Forty-two water samples were collected from upstream, discharge, and downstream sites during weekdays and weekends and analyzed for different physicochemical quality parameters. Habitat integrity was evaluated using the USEPA rapid bioassessment protocol. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to identify water quality variation among stream sites. This study revealed deteriorated water quality at discharge points, with significantly higher mean values for turbidity (483.3 ± 193.5 NTU), total suspended solids (TSS) (561.0 ± 460.9 mg/L), and 5-day biochemical oxygen demand (BOD₅) (333.6 ± 104.5 mg/L) and with lower dissolved oxygen (DO) record (4.7 ± 1.5 mg/L) compared to upstream sites (p < 0.05). Higher BOD₅, orthophosphate, nitrate, and water temperature were recorded during weekends compared to weekdays. PCA results showed that discharge points were associated with elevated turbidity, TSS, BOD₅, and orthophosphate, which are inversely correlated with DO. Habitat scores classified most discharge points as poor and several downstream sites as marginal, suggesting ecological stress consistent with car-wash effluent impacts. These findings indicate a need for targeted monitoring and regulatory attention to uncontrolled car-wash wastewater discharge.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-026-15870-5.

Introduction

Water is one of the most critical natural resources on Earth, essential for sustaining life, supporting ecosystems, and driving economic development. Despite its importance, freshwater systems around the world are increasingly threatened by pollution, particularly in developing countries where rapid urbanization, population growth, and industrial expansion have outpaced environmental management efforts (Musie & Gonfa, 2023). Globally, the wastewater generation and its poor handling have emerged as major challenges. In low-income nations, only about 8% of industrial and municipal wastewater is treated, largely due to limited public awareness, the high costs of treatment infrastructure, and insufficient policy attention (Angello et al., 2021; Rai et al., 2019; UNHSP, 2003).

Car-washing stations are a significant and growing contributor to urban water pollution (Firmansyah et al., 2025; Nyirandayisabye et al., 2022). These facilities consume large volumes of water and discharge effluents containing petroleum-based substances such as diesel, gasoline, engine oil, greases, and lubricants (Mansour et al., 2024; USEPA, 1999). A single car-wash station can generate between 150 and 350 L of wastewater daily (Millinga et al., 2020), often laden with cleaning agents and chemical pollutants that pose serious risks to both surface and groundwater sources (Haimene, 2016). Effluent pollutants, such as dirt, brake dust, oils, greases, and detergents have also been linked to adverse health outcomes including renal dysfunction, increased cancer risk, and circulatory system disorders (Lin et al., 2022; Phungula, 2016).

Car-wash effluent is characteristically greasy, oily, and turbid. When discharged into aquatic ecosystems, oil and grease reduce sunlight penetration, inhibit photosynthesis, and impair oxygen replenishment. These changes elevate water temperature, pH, and biological and chemical oxygen demand (BOD and COD), leading to habitat degradation, reduced productivity, and biodiversity loss (Rai et al., 2020; Susaj et al., 2023). In many developing countries, including Zimbabwe, Ghana, and Ethiopia, car washing is often conducted directly in rivers or along riverbanks, exacerbating the pollution problem. The proliferation of informal and unregulated car-wash stations has intensified threats to water resources and public health (Andama et al., 2022; Chukwu et al., 2008; Kazembeigi et al., 2023).

Ethiopia, often referred to as the “water tower of Africa” due to rivers originating in its highlands, faces significant challenges in water quality management (Awoke et al., 2016). Despite its abundant water resources, recent studies have documented high levels of surface water pollution, particularly in urban centers (Ahmednur et al., 2024), intensively cultivated agricultural zones, and coffee-producing regions (Awoke et al., 2016; EAS & EPCC, 2015). Most industries in Ethiopia discharge their effluents directly into freshwater systems without treatment, with urban streams being especially vulnerable (Assegide et al., 2022; Gebre et al., 2016; Hailu et al., 2024). Among these threats, car-wash effluent remains an overlooked yet potentially significant source of pollution, including a complex mix of pollutants including heavy metals, salts, organic matter, sand, and microorganisms that may contribute to water quality degradation and could pose health risks to downstream communities if left unmanaged (Chukwu et al., 2008).

The pollution load from car-wash facilities is further compounded by temporal demand fluctuations. Tian et al. (2019) found that car-wash demand peaks on Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays, suggesting that car-wash usage closely tied to vehicle use patterns similarly surges on weekends, thereby intensifying pollutant discharge into nearby water bodies during these periods (Tian et al., 2019). Rozenes and Cohen (2018) further corroborated this pattern, documenting significant differences in demand between workdays and weekend days across service sectors, implying that wastewater generation from car-wash operations is not uniform but episodic, with peak loads likely overwhelming any informal drainage or disposal infrastructure during high-use periods (Cohen & Rozenes, 2018). However, temporal variation in effluent loading is poorly studied; weekday-only monitoring may systematically underestimate peak pollution.

Despite evidence from other countries demonstrating the effects of habitat alteration caused by car-wash activities on water quality (Chukwu et al., 2008), no such studies have been conducted in Ethiopian streams. Given the distinct climatic and physiographic conditions of the region, urban stream responses to wastewater inputs may differ considerably, highlighting the need for locally grounded research. In Jimma city, southwestern Ethiopia, the problem is particularly acute, as most car-wash stations are located adjacent to streams and rivers, where the station workers extract water and discharge untreated effluents directly back into the receiving water bodies. Despite valuable contributions from previous studies on urban streams in Jimma (Astatkie et al., 2021; Haddis et al., 2014; Leta & Dibaba, 2019) regarding wastewater impacts on stream water chemistry, none has specifically assessed the effect of car-wash effluents. As a result, the environmental impacts and potential health risks remain poorly understood, leading to limited public awareness, minimal regulatory attention, and inadequate mitigation strategies.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the impact of car-wash station effluent on urban streams and to examine whether weekday versus weekend differences in car-wash activity translate to measurable variation in effluent pollutant loading, with a particular focus on Jimma city, southwestern Ethiopia, by evaluating key physicochemical parameters and in-stream habitat conditions.

Materials and methods

Study area, design, and period

The study was conducted on urban streams in Jimma city, located in the southwestern part of Ethiopia. The area lies approximately 355 km southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Geographically, the city extends between 7°37′ and 7°43′ latitude and 36°40′ and 36°60′ longitude (Fig. 1). According to the City Municipality in 2022, the city has a total population of 207,573, with a population density of 4151.46 persons per km2. Administratively, the city is divided into 17 urban kebeles (smallest administrative division) and covers a total area of 106.34 km2. Two major rivers flow through the city: the Awetu river, which divides the city into two parts, and the Kito river, which flows along its western edge. In addition, several smaller rivers including Dipo, Seto, Kochi, Aramaic, and Dololo are found in the eastern part of the city. These rivers converge and ultimately drain into the Boye wetland, a large water body characterized by dense aquatic vegetation. A cross-sectional study was conducted in dry season (April 2023), prior to the onset of wet season to avoid the influence of stormwater runoff and isolate the effect of car-wash effluent. Sampling site selection and design

According to data from the Jimma City Environmental Protection, Forest and Climate Change Authority, there are 22 car-wash stations operating in the city. Of these, seven stations were purposively selected based on their use of stream water as a source. These selected stations discharge untreated wastewater directly into nearby streams. The distance of upstream sampling points from the effluent discharge point was set to 15–20 m. Because the streams are so narrow, 0.5 to 2 m wide, there is no need to go far up. This distance is far enough to avoid any backwater mixing or eddy currents that might push car-wash water slightly upstream. The downstream sampling points were placed 30–50 m below the outfall. Complete lateral mixing occurs at roughly 30 times the width of the stream channel (Cooke et al., 2010).

Sampling technique

A total of 42 water samples were collected across the seven stations, with each station contributing three distinct sampling points: upstream control sites, discharge points, and downstream sites. These samples were collected and analyzed separately for each station as individual data points. To assess the variation between day types (weekdays and weekends), 21 samples were taken in each case. Weekends were expected to have higher car-wash activity based on local vehicle use patterns and informal personal observation. At each sampling site, composite samples (morning and afternoon) were collected and pooled in polyethylene bottles and transported to the laboratory following standard preservation and handling protocols (APHA et al., 2017).

Water sampling and analysis

The water quality parameters were assessed at each of the seven purposively selected car-wash stations. Field measurements of pH, temperature, electrical conductivity (EC), and dissolved oxygen (DO) were taken at the day type using single input a multiparameter digital probe (HQd40). Turbidity was also measured onsite with a turbidimeter (EUTECH TN). Water samples were collected and transported to the laboratory for further physicochemical quality analysis. Samples were preserved at 4 °C in a refrigerator until analysis. Collected water samples were analyzed following the APHA standard for BOD₅, nitrate, orthophosphate, and total suspended solids (TSS) by using Winkler, phenoldisulfonic acid, stannous chloride, and gravimetric methods, respectively (APHA et al., 2017).

Habitat survey

Habitat condition assessments were carried out in parallel with the measurement of the water quality metrics. The scoring was done using the USEPA’s rapid bioassessment protocol habitat assessment procedure (Barbour, 1999; Fitzpatrick, 1998). Ten habitat parameters, substrate/available cover, embeddedness, velocity/depth regime, sediment deposition, channel flow condition, channel change, frequency of riffles, bank stability, vegetative protection, and riparian vegetative, were the habitat condition assessment parameters. All the parameters were evaluated and rated on a numerical scale from 0 to 20 for each sampling site, and the rated total number was used as a score to classify the habitat condition of respective site as either excellent (160–200), suboptimal (100–159), marginal (60–99), or poor (< 60).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum) were computed to summarize water quality parameters. As the data did not satisfy normality assumptions, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to evaluate overall streams water chemistry differences among stream sites (upstream, discharge point, and downstream). When significant variation was detected, the Mann–Whitney U test was used as a pairwise comparison test. Likewise, the water quality difference between weekday and weekend at discharge points was also assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. A multivariate analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), was used to see the variation among the three sampling site categories. Habitat condition scores across sites were compared using box-and-whisker plot.

Quality assurance

To ensure data quality, water samples were collected in precleaned polyethylene bottles, labeled in the field, and handled with care to prevent contamination. Samples were stored at 4 °C until analysis. A composite sample (morning and afternoon) was collected at each sampling site and during each day type to represent the variability in water quality within a day and between day types. Besides, due to resource constraints, field and laboratory duplicates were not considered. However, standard operating procedures were followed throughout, and blank tests using deionized water were conducted alongside actual samples to detect potential contamination from containers, reagents, or instruments, thereby validating the integrity of the analytical process.

Physicochemical water quality by stream site categories

The findings of this study revealed variation in water quality among the three categories of stream sites. The Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Mann–Whitney U pairwise comparisons, revealed a significant difference (p < 0.01) in turbidity, BOD₅, temperature, and TSS among the other measured quality parameters (Table 2). For instance, the water pH values were slightly alkaline across all sites, but the mean value was significantly higher at the discharge points (8.79 ± 0.23) as compared to both upstream (8.35 ± 0.19) and downstream sites (8.57 ± 0.22). Notably, among all parameters, only pH showed a significant difference between upstream and downstream sites (p = 0.004). Discussion

Consistent with the study hypothesis, findings suggest that informal car-wash stations are likely contributing as point sources of pollution, associated with markedly poorer physicochemical conditions and habitat scores at discharge points relative to upstream controls. These results indicate that untreated car-wash effluent is a potentially significant and previously underquantified stressor in this rapidly urbanizing catchment. This aligns with a growing body of literature from sub-Saharan Africa and other developing regions, where informal vehicle washing is a pervasive threat to urban water quality (Andama et al., 2022; Kazembeigi et al., 2023; Nyirandayisabye et al., 2022). However, given the cross-sectional design, the contribution of other co-occurring urban pollution sources cannot be fully excluded, and the observed differences should be interpreted as associations rather than direct causal evidence.

These findings are also consistent with previous research in similar urban contexts, where car-wash effluents have been identified as concentrated sources of surfactants, hydrocarbons, heavy metals, and suspended solids (Ogbonaya et al., 2008; Rai et al., 2019). In Jimma, this direct discharge into streams often lacking robust riparian buffers creates localized zones of ecological stress evidenced by our significantly degraded habitat scores at discharge points.

The major physical change caused by the streams is evident in the high turbidity (800 NTU) and total suspended sediments (TSS > 560 mg/L) recorded at the discharge points. These levels exceed the guideline threshold for the conservation of aquatic species (< 50 mg/L for TSS (EPA & UNIDO, 2003)) and are consistent with severe levels of sediment pollution. Based on established ecological thresholds, such concentrations would be expected to contribute to degradation of benthic habitats through the choking of substrate, reduction of interstitial areas with critical macroinvertebrate substrate, and clogging of fish gills (Allan et al., 1997; Jones et al., 2012). That the major loss of habitat scores is observed at these locations, consistently classified as “poor,” is consistent with physical habitat impairment attributable, at least in part, to car-wash effluent discharge. Though dilution and sedimentation lower the levels of TSS downstream, any elevation suggests continuing transport of sediments, implying that damage is not confined to the point of release but finds established trends elsewhere in effluent-damaged streams (Adesina et al., 2018; Matthaei et al., 2010).

Meanwhile, the effluent presented a high organic pollution load, while mean BOD₅ in the discharge points reached 333.6 mg/L, a value that is suggestive of intensive microbial activity. This elevated organic load is likely a contributing factor to the low dissolved oxygen concentrations recorded, though other oxygen-demanding inputs in this urban catchment cannot be excluded which have been widely identified to cause physiological stress, reduced growth, and higher mortality among aquatic organisms (Ali et al., 2022; Ebsa & Kabeta, 2022). There exists hypoxia at the point of discharge, a common feature of raw organic waste in car-wash discharges, as commonly reported throughout the literature (Chukwu et al., 2008; Rai et al., 2020). This oxygen depletion is likely exacerbated by hydrocarbons and oils from the effluent, which form surface films that impede atmospheric oxygen reaeration a mechanism developed in studies relating to petroleum-derived wastewater (Aikins & Boakye, 2015; Mansour et al., 2024). Partial DO recovery downstream indicated that oxygen demand was protracted, with the suboptimal conditions extending along a stretch that can fragment the habitat of oxygen-sensitive species and alter the community structure (Cuput et al., 2024; Ghaly et al., 2021; Rai et al., 2020).

The chemical signature of car-wash effluent was unique, with high pH—up to 9.2—and orthophosphate. This alkaline shift is consistent with the application of synthetic detergents in car washing (Ghaly et al., 2021), though other alkalinity sources in the urban stream environment cannot be ruled out. Although not acutely toxic, a prolonged elevated pH may act to stress acid-sensitive biota, modify the bioavailability and toxicity of ammonia and metals, and disrupt osmoregulation in aquatic organisms (Wurts, 2003). More critically, the elevated orthophosphate—likely from phosphate-based cleaners—introduces a key limiting nutrient into these streams. Phosphorus inputs are a well-established contributor to eutrophication risk in freshwater systems, and the elevated orthophosphate concentrations observed here suggest a potential for accelerated algal growth if concentrations persist. Subsequent bloom decay consumes oxygen, creating a negative feedback loop that further exacerbates hypoxic conditions initiated by high BOD₅ (Ji et al., 2024; Li et al., 2021). This interplay between organic matter and nutrient enrichment suggests the synergistic potential of car-wash effluent to disrupt multiple ecological processes simultaneously.

In this study, water quality at discharge points revealed a significant degradation during the weekend. Significantly higher BOD₅, nutrients, and conductivity were observed on weekends, a pattern may reflect increased car-wash activity and associated effluent generation, as weekends are typically associated with higher vehicle washing demand. This might be attributed to greater volume of detergent and phosphate-based cleaning agents applied, more engine oil, grease, and hydrocarbon residues washed off vehicle surfaces, and larger cumulative wastewater discharge volumes entering the stream. However, the number of cars washed and discharge volume were not directly measured; thus, this explanation remains inferential. Elevated organic and nutrient loading intensifies microbial decomposition activity explaining the BOD₅ increase while simultaneous phosphate inputs from cleaning agents account for the orthophosphate elevation. The resulting pattern of discharge is therefore not a steady-state background process but a series of acute, episodic pollutant pulses, which are potentially more detrimental to aquatic ecosystems than chronic exposures (Rolls et al., 2012). This is a critical difference that has major implications: It means that environmental monitoring efforts that only occur on weekdays would yield systematically lower pollutant discharges. This is important for effective management regulations that must account for peak periods of discharge, such as inspections or water recycling on peak days. However, since car-wash traffic volumes were not directly measured in this study, this interpretation remains inferential.

The combined physicochemical stressors observed—elevated turbidity, altered pH, high organic load, and reduced DO—are consistent with a multiple-stressor environment likely to compromise stream integrity. Based on established ecological principles and comparable studies, such conditions would be expected to reduce biodiversity, favoring pollution-tolerant generalist species over sensitive, specialized taxa (Barbour, 1999). Within the context of Jimma and other Ethiopian cities where urban streams may serve as water sources for domestic and agricultural uses downstream, this pollution poses indirect public health risks through degradation of a common-pool resource (Awoke et al., 2016). The findings highlight how often-overlooked informal economic activities in urban planning can have disproportionate environmental impacts and represent a critical gap in current water governance frameworks in rapidly developing cities.

This study has identified physicochemical and habitat conditions associated with car-wash effluent in dry season; however, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting its findings. First, the study’s cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal attribution. Second, the seven stations varied in their surrounding land use, stream characteristics, and proximity to other pollution sources, introducing site heterogeneity that may confound comparisons and limit the generalizability of mean values reported across stations. Third, the seasonal scope is restricted to dry season, and wet season dynamics including dilution effects and increased nonpoint source loads remain uncharacterized. Future work should incorporate longitudinal sampling, biological indicators such as macroinvertebrates, and source apportionment analyses including heavy metals and surfactants to strengthen causal interpretation and ecological assessment. Direct sampling of car-wash effluent prior to its discharge into the stream could further enhance source attribution by yielding a more distinct pollutant fingerprint. However, this approach was not undertaken in the current study due to concerns and apprehension expressed by the business owner, making it unfeasible. Future studies are encouraged to consider incorporating this method where possible.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that untreated effluent from car-wash stations is strongly associated with physicochemical water quality degradation and habitat loss in the urban streams of Jimma, Ethiopia, suggesting that these facilities represent an important and previously overlooked pollution pressure in this setting. The study showed that the discharge points serve as point-source pollution hotspots, characterized by markedly elevated physical (turbidity and TSS), chemical (pH and nutrients), and organic (BOD5) pollutant loads together with extremely reduced DO. These conditions correspond to local zones of ecological impairment where habitat quality is rated as “poor,” with evidence of continued water quality alteration at downstream sites.

This study also determined the magnitude of the impact relative to established water quality guidelines and identified a significant weekend effect, wherein pollutant loads increased due to heightened activity. These findings underscore the need for targeted monitoring and regulatory strategy for car-wash effluent discharge, including predischarge treatment requirements, enforceable regulatory frameworks, and periodic water quality assessment, particularly during weekends as it might be a peak-activity period.
