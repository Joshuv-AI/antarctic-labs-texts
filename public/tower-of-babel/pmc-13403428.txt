Post-PCI outcomes in women with acute coronary syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis of risk factors
Authors: Qian Jiang, Tingting Zhang, Hu Geng, Tongtong Guo, Hongyan Xia, Dingdan Shen, Yueping Chi, Huiqin Zhong, Huaqing Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute coronary syndrome (ACS) is a serious clinical condition linked to considerable morbidity and mortality. While percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the mainstay of treatment, the occurrence of adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) following the procedure remains a significant challenge. Although numerous studies have explored risk factors for MACE in female ACS patients after PCI, inconsistent results exist due to sample size and regional variations. The purpose of this meta-analysis and systematic review is to evaluate these risk factors to obtain higher-level evidence in ACS patients.MethodsComputerized searches were conducted in CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, China Biology Medicine disc, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases to identify literature on risk factors for MACE in female patients with ACS after PCI. The search period spanned from database inception to September 2025. Based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, articles were screened by reviewing titles, abstracts, and full texts. Data extraction was performed, and quality assessment was conducted independently by two researchers using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.4 software and Stata 14.0.ResultsA total of 22 studies comprising 16,500 female patients were included. All studies were cohort studies and reported adjusted odds ratios from multivariable analyses. The meta-analysis identified eight significant risk factors for MACE in female ACS patients after PCI: advanced age (OR = 1.08, 95%CI: 1.00–1.15), decreased LVEF (OR = 1.77, 95%CI: 1.19–2.62), hypertension (OR = 1.73, 95%CI: 1.40–2.15), diabetes mellitus (OR = 1.69, 95%CI: 1.51–1.88), smoking (OR = 2.43, 95%CI: 1.74–3.39), multivessel coronary artery disease (OR = 1.69, 95%CI: 1.43–1.99), history of cerebrovascular disease (OR = 2.18, 95%CI: 1.55–3.05), and symptom-to-balloon time delay (OR = 1.33, 95%CI: 1.24–1.42) were identified as risk factors for MACE in female ACS patients with PCI management. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent effect sizes across geographic regions, publication years, sample sizes, and study quality levels. Meta-regression analyses identified no significant sources of heterogeneity for advanced age (I2 = 91%), suggesting that clinical rather than methodological factors account for this variability. Insufficient reporting in the original studies prevented a meta-analysis of female-specific risk factors, including premature menopause, gestational diabetes, and PCOS, which constitutes a critical gap in the literature.DiscussionThe incidence of MACE is elevated in female ACS patients following PCI. Healthcare professionals should monitor patients' clinical status promptly, develop personalized treatment strategies. Perioperative management must be enhanced, particularly for patients with comorbidities such as advanced age and diabetes, to mitigate the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-06020-3.Keywords: Acute coronary syndrome, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Female, MACE, Adverse cardiovascular events, Risk factors

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of death globally, with a rising prevalence. Notably, women exhibit distinct vulnerability in both the prevalence trends and prognostic outcomes of these disease. In 2020, an estimated 19.05 million people worldwide died from CVD, representing an 18.71% increase from 2010 [1]. According to estimates, the prevalence of CVD has reached 330 million in China, with female patients accounting for 3.8 million [2]. Surveys have indicated that the incidence of CVD in women significantly increases with age and fluctuations in hormone levels, ultimately exceeding that in men [3]. Among numerous cardiovascular emergencies, Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) has garnered widespread attention due to its rapid onset and high fatality rate. Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI) serves as the primary treatment modality for ACS [4]. Although PCI significantly improves patients' prognosis, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) postoperatively occur in 14% to 22.5% of cases [5]. Notably, women demonstrate a higher probability of MACE than men at any age. This discrepancy may be associated with factors such as smaller coronary diameter in female patients, which predisposes them to coronary plaque rupture, endothelial injury, and acute thrombosis formation [6]. The occurrence of MACE after PCI increases patient morbidity and mortality, while also imposing a significant financial burden on households. Therefore, identifying the risk factors for MACE in female ACS patients following PCI is essential. Although relevant studies exist, no consensus has been reached, particularly regarding factors that may be underestimated or hold unique significance in the female population. Our study aims to quantitatively assess these risk factors through a systematic review and meta-analysis, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation for the development of gender-specific risk prediction models.

Materials and methods

The present study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Statement guideline and registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration number: CRD420251242167).

Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Articles were enrolled if they fulfilled the criteria: (1) study subjects were female patients with ACS who underwent PCI, with the criteria for procedural success referencing the American and Chinese PCI guidelines [7]; (2) study types were observational studies, including cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and case–control studies; (3) study content focused on risk factors for MACE in female ACS patients after PCI; and (4) articles were published in Chinese or English. Exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) literature with incomplete content or inaccessible full text; (2) duplicate publications (the most comprehensive version was retained); (3) literature types including reviews, conference proceedings, or case reports; (4) studies without control groups, those with incomplete outcome data, or low-quality literature; and (5) literature from which valid data could not be extracted. MACE were defined as encompassing all-cause death, cardiac death, recurrent myocardial infarction, target vessel revascularization, and stroke.

Retrieval strategy

This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in multiple electronic databases—CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), PubMed, and Embase—from their inception until September 2025. The search strategy was structured according to the PICOS framework, with terms categorized into the five core concepts: Population (P), Intervention (I), Comparison (C), Outcome (O), and Study type (S). Synonyms within each concept were linked using the Boolean operator "OR", while different concepts were combined using "AND". The search employed the following subject terms: “Female and women”; “Acute Coronary Syndrome, Myocardial Infarction, Unstable Angina”; “Percutaneous Coronary, coronary revascularization, stent implantation”; and “Risk factors, influencing factors, associated factors, relevant factors”, along with their combinations. Literature screening and dataextraction

Duplicate records were removed using EndNote X9 software. Two reviewers screened the titles and abstracts independently, after which the full texts of potentially eligible articles were independently assessed by the same reviewers. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion. If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted. Inter-reviewer agreement for study selection was excellent, with a Kappa coefficient of 0.776. Data were extracted on the following: first author's name, publication year, study type, sample size, influencing factors, and study region. The adjusted odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) from multivariate regression analysis were prioritized. If studies reporting both univariate and multivariate analysis, only the multivariate analysis results were extracted to reduce confounding in the pooled effect size. As a result, all 22 studies included in this meta-analysis reported multivariate regression estimates, ensuring that every pooled OR represents an adjusted effect.

Literature qualityassessment

The quality of cohort studies and case–control studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [8]. The evaluation was structured around three domains: selection of study participants (4 items, maximum score 4 points), comparability (1 item, maximum score 2 points), and assessment of exposure or outcome (3 items, maximum score 3 points). Studies were categorized as low-quality, medium-quality, or high-quality based on their total scores, with scores of 0–3, 4–6, and 7–9 points, respectively.

Statistical methods

Evidence was synthesised in RevMan 5.4 and Stata 14.0 software. Odds ratios OR with 95%CI for risk factors of MACE in female ACS patients PCI were calculated. Q test and I2 test were used to test for heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was considered meaningless when P > 0.1 and I2 < 50%. The potential causes of heterogeneity were analyzed by subgroup analysis and meta regression analysis. In the study, we used a random effects model. The stability and reliability of the comprehensive results of each meta-analysis in this study was evaluated by sensitivity analysis, which was completed by changing effect model and eliminating one study at a time. For the assessment of publication bias in outcomes with 7 or more articles, Egger's tests were performed using Stata 14.0, with a P < 0.05 considered indicative of statistically significant publication bias.

Study outcomes

Based on the included studies, the outcome measures were classified as primary or secondary. The primary outcome was the composite MACE, encompassing all-cause death, cardiac death, recurrent myocardial infarction, and target vessel revascularization. All 22 studies reported this composite endpoint, ensuring strong comparability for the primary analysis. Secondary outcomes consisted of the individual components of MACE when reported separately. In studies reporting both the composite and its components, the composite MACE was prioritized for the primary analysis.

Meta-analysis results

Advanced age

The impact of advanced age on MACE in female ACS patients after PCI was reported in 12 studies [9, 11, 14, 16, 18–21, 23–25, 27], with significant heterogeneity observed across the study results (I2 = 91%). Subgroup analyses conducted for this factor revealed persistently high heterogeneity across all subgroups. For region of publication, heterogeneity was high in both China (I2 = 81.30%, OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 1.02–1.05) and other countries (I2 = 92.50%, OR = 1.04, 95%CI: 1.02–1.06). For year of publication, studies published before 2020 showed an I2 = 84.90% (OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 1.00–1.06), while those published after 2020 showed an I2 = 94.50% (OR = 1.04, 95%CI: 1.0–1.05). For sample size, studies with fewer than 500 participants exhibited an I2 = 80.40% (OR = 1.04, 95%CI: 1.02–1.06), whereas those with 500 or more participants exhibited an I2 = 96.50% (OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 1.02–1.05). For literature quality, studies with a quality score of ≤ 7 showed an I2 = 73.60% (OR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.72–0.84), while those with a score of ≥ 7 showed an I2 = 95.40% (OR = 1.05, 95%CI: 1.04–1.06). Further meta-regression analysis confirmed that none of these factors significantly explained the heterogeneity observed for advanced age: region of publication (β = 0.467, 95%CI: −0.327–1.262, P = 0.22), year of publication (β = 0.107, 95%CI: −0.699–0.914, P = 0.77), sample size (β = −0.372, 95%CI: −1.100–0.356, P = 0.28), and literature quality (β = 0.347, 95%CI: −0.780–1.173, P = 0.37). Accordingly, a random-effects model was selected for the meta-analysis of age. The pooled results demonstrated that advanced age is a significant risk factor for MACE in female ACS patients after PCI (OR = 1.08, 95%CI: 1.00–1.15, P < 0.05), indicating that advanced age confers a 1.08-fold increased risk of MACE following PCI in this population. Sensitivity analysis

To ensure the robustness and reliability of the meta-analysis, it was first examined whether the pooled effect size was altered when the statistical effect model was modified. Individual sensitivity analyses for each risk factor yielded results consistent with prior findings (Table 3). Additionally, sensitivity analysis excluding low-quality studies (NOS < 7 points) identified two low-quality studies among the 22 included (NOS = 6 points). Taking advanced age as an example, the original meta-analysis included 12 studies, yielding a pooled effect size of OR = 1.08 (95%CI: 1.00–1.15), with I2 = 91%. After the exclusion of one low-quality study, the pooled effect size for the remaining 11 high-quality studies was OR = 1.11 (95%CI: 1.05–1.18), with I2 = 87%. The effect sizes remained directionally consistent before and after exclusion, with OR values falling within each other's confidence intervals, indicating that study quality did not significantly impact the pooled results for advanced age. Sensitivity analysis was performed individually for all eight statistically significant risk factors. The results showed that excluding low-quality studies did not alter the direction of the pooled effect sizes for any factor, and the findings remained substantially consistent with the original analysis (Table 4). Sensitivity analysis through alteration of effect measures and exclusion of low-quality studies demonstrated robust findings in this research.

Publication bias assessment

As recommended by the Cochrane Handbook, funnel plot symmetry evaluation and Egger's test are typically conducted when ≥ 7 studies are included. Publication bias assessment for the risk factors advanced age and diabetes mellitus revealed generally symmetrical funnel plots (Figs. 13 and 14). Egger's tests for advanced age (t = 1.03, P = 0.329) (Fig. 13) and diabetes mellitus (t = −0.67, P = 0.532) (Fig. 14) indicated absence of publication bias among included studies, demonstrating result stability. Funnel plot analysis was not performed for other risk factors with fewer than seven included studies due to insufficient statistical power with a low number of studies, potentially leading to unreliable results.

Discussion

Advanced age

It was indicated in our study that female ACS patients of advanced age face a higher risk of MACE following percutaneous coronary intervention (OR = 1.08, 95%CI: 1.00–1.15, P < 0.05). However, significant heterogeneity was observed in the pooled effect size. Subgroup analysis and meta-regression failed to identify the source of this heterogeneity, suggesting that it primarily arises from clinical variations—specifically, inconsistent definitions of ' advanced age' across studies. Among the 12 included studies, some defined advanced age as ≥ 60 years while others used ≥ 65 or ≥ 70 years as thresholds. This heterogeneity in cutoff values may lead to variation in effect sizes. In younger women, estrogen secretion exerts protective effects on the vasculature [31, 32], conversely, the loss of this estrogen-mediated protection during the postmenopausal period represents a physiological alteration unique to females. The synergistic effect of age-related diminished vascular regenerative capacity, combined with smaller coronary artery diameter in females, predisposes them to more diffuse and severe coronary lesions compared to male counterparts of the same age [33]. Additionally, female ACS patients of advanced age often present with atypical symptoms such as fatigue, weakness, and dyspnea as initial manifestations, which may lead to delay medical consultation and adversely affect prognostic outcomes. Wenqi et al. [34] reported that the incidence of MACE post-PCI in acute myocardial infarction patients over 60 years was 1.212 times higher than in those under 60 years. Accordingly, when managing elderly female patients, healthcare professionals should not only focus on traditional cardiovascular disease risk factors but also maintain a high level of vigilance for atypical symptoms. Early risk assessment should be performed, and tailored prevention, monitoring, and therapeutic strategies should be formulated based on patients' actual physical conditions to improve prognostic outcomes.

Symptom-to-balloon time delay

According to the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of myocardial infarction [35], early restoration of myocardial blood flow and reduction of ischemic injury to myocardial cells are critical determinants of prognostic outcomes in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. The guidelines recommend total ischemic time be kept within 2 h, and ideally within 1 h. Myocardial damage resulting from short-term ischemia is reversible, and timely restoration of blood flow can normalize myocardial structure. However, when myocardial fiber destruction is extensive and the duration of ischemia exceeds myocardial tolerance, the number of recoverable cells is markedly reduced. Under such circumstances, subsequent restoration of blood flow may paradoxically exacerbate the injury [36]. Bessonov et al. [37] reported that prolonged myocardial ischemia time was significantly associated with the occurrence of MACE after PCI, with female sex identified as a primary predictor of increased total ischemia time–a finding consistent with the results of the present study (OR = 1.33, 95%CI: 1.24–1.42, P < 0.05). Due to inconspicuous clinical presentations, women are more likely to have their initial symptoms attributed to non-cardiac causes, such as fatigue, lethargy, and dyspepsia. This phenomenon is associated not only with insufficient public awareness of cardiovascular disease in women but also with inadequate assessment of female patients during initial hospital evaluation. Therefore, awareness of atypical symptoms should be enhanced through public health lectures and multimedia platforms. In addition, the establishment of multidisciplinary teams, the development of barrier-free pathways within and outside the hospital, and the coordination of multiple departments to form specialized teams may comprehensively improve rescue efficiency and reduce delays in transport and treatment.

Decreased LVEF and multivessel coronary artery disease

Previous studies have indicated that decreased LVEF and multivessel coronary artery disease significantly influence MACE occurrence after PCI [38, 39], findings that are consistent with those of the present study (for decreased LVEF: OR = 1.77, 95%CI: 1.19–2.62, P < 0.05; for multivessel disease: OR = 1.69, 95%CI: 1.43–1.99, P < 0.05). Compared to patients with single-vessel disease, those with multivessel disease undergo more complex procedures, experience more severe myocardial ischemia, and have poorer prognostic outcomes [40]. In female patients, the prognostic significance of multivessel disease is more pronounced. Women with multivessel disease are more likely to present with plaque erosion rather than plaque rupture, and a higher prevalence of microvascular dysfunction has been observed in this population [41]. This may also explain why female patients with comorbid multivessel disease face higher risks of secondary heart failure and adverse events. LVEF is recognized as a critical indicator of ventricular remodeling [42], and decreased LVEF is closely associated with poor prognostic outcomes. Gender differences may exist in LVEF recovery following myocardial infarction. However, some studies demonstrate that when timely reperfusion therapy is administered, women exhibit greater myocardial recovery capacity, although they may experience more severe diastolic dysfunction [43]. This finding may be attributable to the protective effects of estrogen accounted for in the study. These observations suggest that assessment should not rely solely on LVEF values; rather, functional status and heart failure symptoms must also be evaluated to comprehensively assess cardiac reserve function in female patients. Furthermore, LVEF serves as an early reversible indicator. Post-discharge cardiac rehabilitation guided through internet platforms, combined with physical therapies such as enhanced external counter pulsation, can improve cardiac function and reduce the risk of MACE.

Hypertension and diabetes mellitus

Previous studies have demonstrated that myocardial infarction patients with comorbid hypertension exhibit significantly increased postoperative MACE [44, 45], a finding consistent with the results of the present study (OR = 1.73, 95%CI: 1.40–2.15, P < 0.05). Sex-based differences have been identified in the pathogenesis of hypertension. In postmenopausal women, the protective effect of estrogen on blood vessels is lost, and blood pressure rises more rapidly than in men. Long-term hypertension increases women's susceptibility to MACE. Additionally, decreased estrogen levels have been shown to heighten vulnerability to systemic hypertension in this population [46]. Simultaneously, elevated blood pressure increases systemic oxidative stress levels and reduces the production of vasodilatory substances, thereby exacerbating endothelial dysfunction [47] and further impacting disease progression. Previous research has also demonstrated that diabetes mellitus is a particularly significant risk factor [48], consistent with the findings of this study (OR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.51–1.88, P < 0.05). The incidence of MACE in female patients with comorbid diabetes has been reported to be 1- to threefold higher than in males [49], a difference that may be associated with decreased metabolic capacity, greater insulin sensitivity, and increased fat accumulation in this population [50]. Therefore, for female patients with combined hypertension and diabetes mellitus, close follow-up should be conducted to enhance medication adherence. In addition, guidance should be provided on reasonable dietary adjustments, increased physical exercise, and the maintenance of physical and mental well-being to help achieve management goals.

Smoking and history of cerebrovascular disease

In our study, female ACS patients with a history of smoking were found to have a higher risk of MACE after PCI (OR = 2.43, 95%CI: 1.74–3.39, P < 0.05). Yang et al. [28] noted that smoking exerts a far greater impact on prognostic outcomes in women than in men, and that women find it more difficult to quit smoking. Smoking induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, counteracts the protective effects of estrogen, increases blood viscosity, promotes the aggregation of blood components within the vasculature, and accelerates thrombus formation [51, 52]. Additionally, when combined with other risk factors, smoking has been shown to exert a greater detrimental synergistic effect in women. A history of cerebrovascular disease was identified as a significant factor contributing to mortality among acute myocardial infarction patients (OR = 2.18, 95%CI: 1.55–3.05, P < 0.05). Lv et al. [53] reported that smoking is significantly associated with adverse prognostic outcomes in ACS patients with a comorbid history of cerebrovascular disease. Therefore, comprehensive assessment of patient history should be conducted in clinical practice to facilitate the development of personalized treatment and management plans. This necessitates enhanced follow-up, the provision of guidance on smoking cessation and alcohol restriction to promote healthy lifestyles, and attention to the interrelationship between cerebrovascular and cardiovascular disease. A patient-centered holistic management strategy should be implemented, rather than treating each disease in isolation.

Other risk factors

In our study, Killip classification and elevated leukocyte count were not found to have significant predictive value, which differs from the conclusions of some previous studies [10, 13, 16, 17, 22, 26]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including the reduced discriminatory efficacy of Killip classification in female patients, inconsistencies in the timing of leukocyte measurement across studies, and additional influences arising from the limited number of included studies, heterogeneity of sample sources, and variations in study designs. Therefore, high-quality, large-sample, multicenter stratified studies should be prioritized in future research to further elucidate the influencing factors and underlying mechanisms of postoperative MACE in female patients with acute coronary syndrome ACS.

Female-specific risk factors and research gaps

While this study identified traditional risk factors such as advanced age, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, these factors were present in both sexes. Genuine female-specific risk factors—including premature menopause, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and autoimmune diseases—were seldom reported or analyzed in the reviewed literature. This gap constitutes a limitation of the present work and highlights a critical avenue for future investigation. Women who experience menopause before age 45, for example, face a 20% higher risk of mortality following a cardiovascular event than those aged 45 or older [54]. Premature loss of estrogen accelerates the progression of atherosclerosis and impairs vascular endothelial function. However, among the 22 studies included in this review, only one study [19] reported the duration of menopause, and none separately analyzed the impact of premature menopause. Pregnancy-related complications, including gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and hypertensive disorders such as preeclampsia, are established female-specific precursors of cardiovascular risk. A history of GDM or hypertensive disorders of pregnancy confers a 2- to threefold [55] and 1.7- to twofold [56] increased risk of future cardiovascular disease, respectively. However, obstetric history is seldom collected or reported in existing studies of post-PCI prognosis among female ACS patients, representing a critical knowledge gap. An approximately two-fold elevated cardiovascular risk is also observed in women with PCOS [57]. Additionally, autoimmune diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which exhibit a strong female predominance, are recognized as independent cardiovascular risk factors [30]. This study indicates that prognostic research for female ACS patients continues to emphasize traditional risk factors, while female-specific determinants remain significantly understudied. Nursing personnel should therefore routinely inquire about female-specific health issues when obtaining medical histories from women with cardiovascular disease.

Conclusion

In our meta-analysis, key risk factors influencing prognostic outcomes following percutaneous coronary intervention were identified among female patients with ACS. In clinical practice, intensified monitoring and personalized rehabilitation plans should be implemented for female patients presenting with high-risk characteristics, including advanced age, multivessel disease, and delay in symptom-to-balloon time. Future efforts should be directed toward the integration of these factors to develop and validate specialized risk scoring tools, construct risk prediction models, conduct high-quality prospective studies, and implement targeted interventional research aimed at improving long-term outcomes in this population.

Limitations: This study revealed high heterogeneity for the advanced age factor. We performed subgroup analysis and meta-regression, both of which failed to identify the source of heterogeneity, which may be related to inconsistent operational definitions of " advanced age" across studies. Future studies are recommended to adopt standardized definitions to facilitate further exploration. Additionally, funnel plots and Egger’s test were carried out for advanced age and diabetes mellitus and the results were not significant. However, we cannot completely rule out potential publication bias or the effect of small-scale studies because the number of studies included in this study was relatively small (for example, only 7 were related to diabetes). Moreover, the majority of studies included in this research were cohort studies, a design that can introduce measurement and selection biases. Our literature search was limited to publications in Chinese and English and did not encompass grey literature. This limitation may affect the comprehensiveness and representativeness of the findings. Due to insufficient reporting of female-specific risk factors in the original studies, a meta-analysis of these factors could not be conducted. This limitation reflects a systematic gap in the current literature, which future high-quality prospective studies must address.
