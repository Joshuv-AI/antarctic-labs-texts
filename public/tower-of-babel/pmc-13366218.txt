24-hour movement behaviors of physical activity, screen time, and sleep in youth with down syndrome compared to typically developing peers
Authors: Azeem Hussain, Bethany Forseth, Amy Bodde, Paula Papanek, Michele Polfuss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Youth with Down syndrome (DS) tend to have a higher prevalence of obesity compared to their peers with typical development (TD). Physical activity, sleep and screen time are modifiable behaviors which can influence childhood obesity. To better understand an individual’s activity, these behaviors should be examined concurrently versus independently over a 24-h period.Objective:To pilot the examination and comparison of physical activity, sleep, and screen and non-screen sedentary behaviors concurrently in a 24-h period, and determine if 24-h guidelines were met in a sample of youth with DS and peers with TD.Methods:This secondary data-analysis from a cross-sectional study included youth with DS and peers with TD. Parent reported 24-h activity logs were completed for 6 days. Total minutes spent in each behavior were calculated and the 24-h movement guideline recommendations were compared between groups using independent t-tests and chi-square analyses.Results:Youth with DS (n = 8) and youth with TD (n = 9) spent significantly different time in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (t(15) = 2.334, p = 0.034). Light physical activity, non-screen and screen time sedentary behaviors, and sleep were comparable. Both groups met recommended guidelines for moderate-to-vigorous activity and sleep, however, screen time guidelines were only met by 50% of youth with DS and 22% of youth with TD.Conclusions:Youth with DS met two of the three recommended guidelines for the 24-h movement behaviors. By studying each behavior concurrently over a 24-h period, an increased understanding of activity is present and targeted changes can be made.Keywords: Down syndrome, 24-hour movement behaviors, Physical activity, Sleep, Screen time

Introduction

Youth with Down syndrome (DS) have a high prevalence of obesity, which can lead to an increased risk of developing chronic health issues (Forseth et al., 2019; Ptomey, Oreskovic, Hendrix, Nichols, & Agiovlasitis, 2023). Within the United States, the prevalence of obesity for children with DS is reported as high as 37% compared to 19.7% for their peers with typical development (TD) (Forseth et al., 2022; Childhood Obesity Facts | Overweight & Obesity | CDC, 2022). Physical activity, screen time, and sleep are three modifiable behaviors that can improve physical and psychological health and play an important role in preventing and managing childhood obesity (Healy, Foley, & Haegele, 2020). To better understand the role of and interactions between physical activity, screen time, and sleep, recommendations have been made to examine and report all three behaviors concurrently in a 24-h period (Children & Youth 5–17 Years – 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, n.d.). To support this approach, the Canadian 24-h movement guidelines were developed by leading Canadian public health and research agencies and national and international stakeholders for youth 5–17 years of age (Children & Youth 5–17 Years – 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, n.d.).

These evidence based guidelines were made using a robust process, including four systematic reviews, and while the guidelines were not made specifically for youth with disabilities the authors note that the “guidelines may be appropriate for children and youth with a disability or medical condition; however, a health professional should be consulted for additional guidance.” (Children & Youth 5–17 Years – 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, n.d.; Tremblay, Carson, & Chaput, 2016) These widely-adopted guidelines recommend that youth participate in several hours of light intensity physical activity (LPA), a minimum of 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA), limited time in sedentary behaviors (less than 2 h of recreational screen time and limiting extended periods of sitting) and uninterrupted sleep of 9–11 h for ages 5–13 and 8–10 h for ages 14–17 years in a 24-h period (Children & Youth 5–17 Years – 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, n.d.; Physical activity, n.d.; Jenco, 2016).

The rationale for examining movement over 24 h is the inherent interdependence of physical activity, screen time, and sleep. The 24-h period enables quantification of behaviors and the identification of patterns among them. There is an association with improved social, physical, and mental health when the recommended guidelines are met in youth (Healy et al., 2020; Sampasa-Kanyinga, Lien, Hamilton, & Chaput, 2022). Most studies examine one or two behaviors (physical activity, screen time, or sleep) in a 24-h period. Emerging research is increasingly examining trends across all three behaviors in relation to the 24-h movement guidelines among youth with disabilities. Findings have suggested that children with disabilities are achieving a varying degree of 1–2 behaviors, however a very low number of children are meeting all of the recommended behavior guidelines (3.7% – 10.1%) (Arbour-Nicitopoulos et al., 2021; Arbour-Nicitopoulos et al., 2025; Hao, Zhou, Razman, Peng, & Ahmad, 2024; Hou et al., 2024). Research examining the three behaviors specifically for youth with DS is limited. When it has been examined, findings support that youth with disabilities (including DS) have had a recent decline in achieving 24-h movement guidelines, and that a significant percentage of these individuals did not meet the recommendations (Hao et al., 2024; Healy et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2024). A recent ACSM expert consensus statement emphasizes that, for individuals with intellectual disabilities, a “move more” recommendation may be more beneficial than traditional recommendations targeting MVPA. Incorporating light PA and healthy sleep into the 24-h day may be a more practical and feasible health promotion strategy for this population considering the well-documented barriers to MVPA participation (Ptomey et al., 2025). This nuanced understanding of activity behaviors can be used to inform future interventions to modify these behaviors and to prevent or treat obesity in youth.

Findings from previous reports of activity behavior in youth with DS are mixed. When assessing physical activity, Izquierdo-Gomez, et al. (n = 100) and Pitchford, et al. (n = 39) reported that youth with DS (ages 11–18) are not achieving the guideline recommendations for 60 min of MVPA per day, with reported MVPA ranging from 28 to 57 min per day (Izquierdo-Gomez et al., 2014; Pitchford, Adkins, Hasson, Hornyak, & Ulrich, 2018). Whereas a study by Yamanaka, et al. (n = 93) suggested that youth with DS (ages 7–12) met the 60 min of MVPA per day guideline, with youth in first and second grade spending 65 to 85 min per day and youth in fifth and sixth grade spending 52 to 64 min in MVPA per day (Yamanaka, Inayama, Ohkawara, Okazaki, & Kita, 2020). For sedentary behavior specific to screen time, Amatoria, et al. (n = 57) reported that youth with DS (mean age of 21.4 ± 7.7) spent an average of over 3 h per day in screen time (Amatori et al., 2022). Similar to their peers with TD, youth with DS (ages 6–18) did not meet the sleep guidelines for their age with averages ranging from 7.1 h to 8.4 h per day (Ashworth, Hill, Karmiloff-Smith, & Dimitriou, 2013; Brooks et al., 2015; Lovos et al., 2021). Hou, et al. and Hao, et al. examined all three behaviors in samples of children with disabilities that included DS (Hao et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2024). Recommendations for the three behaviors were met by 8.4% and 7.2% while 19.9% and 20% respectively did not achieve any of the recommended behavior guidelines. To our knowledge, only one study examined all three behaviors concurrently in a U.S. national sample of youth with chronic health conditions that included 51 youth and reported behaviors specific to youth with DS, but outcomes only indicated if a guideline was met (Healy et al., 2020). Findings indicated that 9.6% of youth with DS met 0 guidelines, 60.0% met 1 guideline, 30.4% met 2 guidelines, and 0% met all 3 guidelines. Within this study, the guideline most commonly met for youth with DS was sleep (89.1%), followed by screen time (16.0%) and MVPA for 60 min (15.7%). While there are no specific guidelines based on youth with intellectual disabilities, examining behaviors in a 24-h period can provide a foundational understanding of the behavior of youth with DS over the course of the day, similar to that of children with TD. The purpose of this paper is to assess physical activity, sedentary behaviors (including screen time), and sleep concurrently in a sample of youth with DS and youth with TD, and to describe and compare the total time spent in each behavior while identifying if the 24-h guidelines were achieved.

Methods

Design, setting, and participants

Data for this secondary data-analysis were collected from a cross-sectional study that examined body composition and physical activity in youth (5–17 years) with intellectual and developmental delays (including DS) and those with TD (Polfuss et al., 2018). The study protocol was approved by the local Institutional Review Board and is published elsewhere (Polfuss et al., 2018). Participants were recruited through a Midwestern Children’s hospital in the United States. Prior to data collection, participant consent (parents and caregivers – hereby referred to as parent) and/or assent (youth) was obtained as appropriate. Inclusion criteria for this secondary analysis included the youth being 5–17 years of age and diagnosed with DS or TD.

Measures

Activity Logs were completed by parents to reflect their child’s activity over a 24-h period for 6 days over a 2-week period with a minimum of two days being weekend days. The logs listed 20 activities and provided time increments of 15-min intervals for the 24-h day. The parent was asked to mark if and when their child engaged in the activity and total time was summed to determine time spent in each respective activity. School time was accounted for with parent-report of the child’s school day schedule and parent reviewed and confirmed the data with their child to the best of their ability. Previous literature accounts for the development of these logs and their application to the parent study of this secondary analysis (Forseth et al., 2019; Bringolf-Isler et al., 2009).

The study team categorized the 20 activities based on the WHO criteria of the metabolic equivalence (MET) values with vigorous PA > 6 METs, moderate PA 3–6 METs, LPA <4 METs, sedentary ≤1.5 METS and assigned them to one of the 24-h movement behaviors (PA broken down by LPA and MVPA and sedentary behaviors broken up by sitting, screen time, and sleep) (World Health Organization, 2019). When the range of METS crossed over categories, such as sedentary to PA, the category was chosen based on the conceptual level of movement associated with the activity. Once complete, ten activities met MVPA classification, three were considered LPA, and four were sedentary. Screen time included 2 activities: watching TV, videos or DVD and computer games/play station/internet. Finally, sleep was listed as its own activity. See Table 1 for a listing of activities. For an activity log to be included in the analysis, ≥4 days with a minimum of one weekend day with data capture documented of at least 80% or 1152 min of activity for each day were needed (Forseth et al., 2019).

Analysis

Descriptive analyses (means, standard deviation, frequency and percentages) were conducted for participant demographics and categories of 24-h movement behavior data. Total time (minutes or hours) spent in each behavior was averaged per day and reported as well as total number of participants meeting each behavior per the guidelines. Criteria to meet a guideline included engaging in ≥60 min of MVPA, ≤2 h of screen time, and ≥ 9 or ≥ 8 h of sleep for children 5–13 years and 14–18 years, respectively. Current guidelines do not specify ideal time spent in LPA and non-screen sedentary time, so these behaviors were not analyzed as guidelines to meet but are descriptively included in the results. Total minutes in each behavior was compared between youth with DS and those with TD using two-sided independent t-tests and prevalence of meeting 24-h movement behavior categories was tested using chi-square (χ2) analyses (Campbell, 2007). Each diagnosis group was analyzed following the guidelines with all ages combined for physical activity and screen time and two age categories for sleep. Due to the exploratory nature of this preliminary study, Bonferroni correction factor was not applied.

Discussion

An individual’s engagement in activity behaviors plays an important role in their overall health. This study concurrently assessed physical activity, screen time, and sleep data for a sample of youth with DS and TD by describing time spent in each behavior, determining if 24-h activity guidelines were met, and comparing this data between the two groups. Results indicated that both groups met the criteria of MVPA for a minimum of 60 min per day, as well as achieved sleep guidelines per their age groups. Only half of the group with DS and even fewer of the TD group met the screen time guidelines. Compared to the Healy, et al. study that reported on all three behaviors (physical activity, screen time, and sleep), this study had a higher percentage of youth with DS achieving 1, 2, or 3 guidelines and 0% achieving 0 guidelines (Healy et al., 2020). Both Healy et al. and this study utilized parent report of the child’s activity, but the present study has a smaller sample size of 17 as compared to Healy et al. having 51 (Healy et al., 2020). While the chi-square values did not show significance, the results were comparable between diagnosis groups and their prevalence of meeting each 24-h movement behavior guideline. Having studies measure and report on activity behaviors across a 24-h period will result in data that reflects a more accurate daily representation, confirm if guidelines are being met, and allow patterns or trends to become evident. This can be particularly useful for youth with DS as we currently do not have a strong understanding of their daily behaviors. Once there is a better understanding of how their activity is spent and patterns identified over a 24-h period, interventions or suggestions to modify behaviors can be employed.

All participants of this study met the physical activity guidelines for 60 min per day of MVPA. Furthermore, the amount of time that youth with DS spent in MVPA was at least twice that of the 60 min recommended in the guideline and higher than previous studies done with this population (7 to 20 years of age) reflecting 136 min per day versus 28 to 85 min per day (Izquierdo-Gomez et al., 2014; Pitchford et al., 2018; Yamanaka et al., 2020). While a positive finding, these other studies utilized accelerometer data instead of parent report, which could explain part of the discrepancy. This discrepancy may be due to the parent-report tool, which logged activities in 15-min increments. When a parent recorded an activity, the entire 15-min block was assigned the highest intensity level that occurred during that period, potentially overestimating the time children spent in MVPA. While parents and their children double-checked their activity reporting, there is always the risk of social desirability bias. In the context of this study, there is also a chance of error when parents discussed the school day with their children, despite there being a structured schedule for school. Additional issues to be mindful of when using self-report include error in perception or a further complication due to adolescents who have increased independence, resulting in a risk of parents not being aware of their activities to the exact 15-min increment.

In this study, when the two groups were compared, the average time spent in MVPA for children with DS was significantly less than that of their peers with TD. These results are similar to previous studies reporting a discrepancy in MVPA of youth with DS as compared to their peers with TD (Fox, Moffett, Kinnison, Brooks, & Case, 2019; Izquierdo-Gomez et al., 2014; Pitchford et al., 2018; Shields, Dodd, & Abblitt, 2009). It is notable that none of these studies included concurrent reports of the three activity behaviors. Explanations for decreased engagement in MVPA for youth with DS may be attributed to having fewer opportunities (e.g., organized sports or activities that require a higher level of coordination such as skateboarding) available to them. Additionally, the DS group had data primarily collected during the school year, while the TD group was collected primarily during the summer holiday. This could account for an increase in MVPA for the TD group due to inclusion in sports/other activities and no school to limit their participation. This may be further influenced by seasonal climate changes that may impact opportunities available to the youth (Atkin, Sharp, Harrison, Brage, & Van Sluijs, 2016). Additional studies including objective measurement of activity with larger sample sizes could provide a more accurate description of the amount of time spent in and patterns of MVPA over a 24-h period for youth with DS.

Although this analysis did not show a significant difference in LPA, there was a higher mean time spent engaging in LPA for youth with DS compared to the group with TD. This is supported by a few studies that examined LPA, which also reported that youth with DS spent more time in LPA than their peers with TD (Fox et al., 2019; Shields et al., 2009). The DS group in this present study spent more time in LPA than reported by Izquierdo-Gomez, et al., but not compared to the Pitchford, et al. sample (Izquierdo-Gomez et al., 2014; Pitchford et al., 2018). Similar to MVPA, this could be due to the use of parent report versus accelerometer measured data used in the other studies. Understanding the amount of time spent in LPA is useful as it may be targeted when attempting to modify other behaviors. For example, if the goal is to move more by increasing LPA, then targeting ways to reduce time spent sitting by engaging less with screen time/non-screen time sedentary behaviors may be a viable goal for the child.

In this study, youth with DS had higher time engaging in non-screen sedentary behaviors compared to youth with TD. While this difference was trending towards statistical significance, it did not achieve a significant difference in this small sample. Even though non-screen sedentary activities such as eating, reading books, lessons, and doing homework are not negative activities, these are often completed while seated. When considering 24-h movement behaviors, any activities that require prolonged period of sitting can present an opportunity to break up this sedentary behavior with movement. Even though more research needs to be done to understand the impact of breaking up prolonged sitting in youth with DS, current literature suggests that this adjustment in behavior can help improve time spent engaging in PA and MVPA (Amor-Barbosa, Ortega-Martínez, Carrasco-Uribarren, & Bagur-Calafat, 2022; Carrasco-Uribarren et al., 2023). While this preliminary study assessed total time spent in non-screen and total sedentary behaviors, we did not identify individual bouts of time spent engaging in these behaviors. Future studies would benefit from identifying periods of prolonged sedentary activity and introduce ways to break up these behaviors with any level of activity.

The youth with DS in this study spent less time on average (138.60 ± 104.40 min/day) engaging in screen time compared to the group with TD (183.60 ± 125.40 min/day). These findings are lower when compared to Amatori, et al., where parents reported youth with DS spent an average of 186 min per day participating in screen time (Amatori et al., 2022). Even with the reduced time spent in screen time, only 50% of the DS group met the guidelines for ≤2 h per day. This was similar to Amatori, et al. where only 30–55% met screen time guidelines over three timepoints (Amatori et al., 2022). Despite half the DS group meeting the suggested screen time guidelines, the average screen time was still higher than the 2 h recommendation.

Within this study, both the groups with DS and TD met guideline recommendations for sleep, but youth with DS ages 14–18 received ~1 h less sleep than their peers with TD. Previously stated research reported that youth with DS and their peers with TD tend to have similar hours of sleep (approximately 7 h per day), less than the guideline recommendations of 8 h per day (Ashworth et al., 2013; Brooks et al., 2015; Lovos et al., 2021). These studies utilized different methods of measurement (e.g., actigraphy, polysomnography, questionnaires and multiple sleep latency testing) which may contribute to the variation in findings (Ashworth et al., 2013; Brooks et al., 2015; Lovos et al., 2021). This is not unexpected as the prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea for youth with DS ranges from 45 to 76%, which is associated with sleep restlessness and fragmentation (which would affect total sleep time) (Maris, Verhulst, Wojciechowski, Van De Heyning, & Boudewyns, 2016; Nerfeldt & Sundelin, 2020). This study did not examine sleep quality and recognizes that quantity of sleep by parent self-report is not comparable. In addition, by aligning with the age categories of the 24-h guidelines, the sample size of the 14–18 years groups was limited to 3 individuals for the DS group and 2 for TD group. Due to such a small sample size for this subgroup, the time difference should be taken anecdotally and no meaningful statistical conclusion should be drawn. Future studies should include an objective method of sleep assessment and address quality along with quantity due to the increased prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea occurring in youth with DS.

Limitations of this study include having a small sample size and use of parent-reported subjective measures which may have inflated guidelines met (potentially impacted by recall and social desirability bias) instead of using an activity monitor that is automated. Additionally, the increased challenge on parents reporting on their child’s self-perceived intensity level could lead to under/over-reporting PA activities, which may impact the confidence of PA data reported. Another limitation is that a subset of the data was collected over the school year while the remainder was collected over the summer break, which may contribute to different patterns of behaviors. The MVPA differences between both groups should be interpreted with caution due to the seasonal differences in data collection. One more limitation to consider is the lack of racial and ethnic diversity in the sample, which may limit external validity towards more diverse populations of youth with DS. A final limitation to consider is not comparing behaviors between weekdays/weekend or in-school/holidays. While routines may differ in these scenarios, this study did not analyze these differences due to the small sample size. Despite these limitations, the strengths of this study involve the ability to compare youth with DS to participants with TD as well as being able to capture ≥85% of daily activity of the child. To the author’s knowledge, very few studies report time spent across the 24-h movement behaviors for youth with DS (Brown, McPhee, Kwan, & Timmons, 2021; Hao et al., 2024; Healy et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2023). An additional strength of this study is how sedentary behaviors were reported in multiple ways: non-screen sedentary time, screen time, and total sedentary time. This insight into sedentary activity type that the individual is engaged in can allow for targeted change to occur. It would be recommended for future studies to continue to report this information and examine patterns of activity and sedentary behaviors to emphasize where interventions could better break up long periods of sitting.

Recommendations for future research includes concurrently measuring each behavior (activity, sleep and sedentary behaviors) objectively over a 24-h period that will allow assessment of time spent in each activity as well understanding daily patterns and trends for these behaviors. Having comparisons between the school year, holidays, weekdays, and weekends can also give additional insight towards how routines can differ. This can contribute evidence towards future adjustment of guidelines for children and youth with DS. Based on the results reported with youth meeting guidelines for MVPA and sleep, it would support that future studies include nutrition and metabolism as other primary factors that warrant further investigation related to the development of obesity in youth with DS. Lastly, future work could benefit from a larger, more representative sample as well as more inclusion from youth of other disability groups to better allow for cross-disability comparisons of 24-h movement behavior patterns.

Conclusion

There is limited research observing all three behaviors (physical activity, sleep, and sedentary behaviors) of the 24-h movement guidelines for youth with DS. Encouraging the assessment of the three behaviors concurrently in a 24-h period will provide a better understanding of how youth, particularly those with DS, engage in activity and where modifications can be made. This approach will be beneficial for understanding each individual behavior as well as informing prevention and treatment strategies of weight management in this at-risk population.
