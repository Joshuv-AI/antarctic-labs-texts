Utilizing deep learning from mobile phone photos for early detection of horizontal strabismus: a screening approach
Authors: Rathachai Chawuthai, Anawat Sermswan, Chatree Boonnithititikul, Kiatthida Hokierti, Wasawat Sermsripong, Piyaphat Jaruniphakul, Thammanoon Surachatkumtonekul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To develop and validate an artificial intelligence pipeline for binary screening of horizontal strabismus versus orthotropia using smartphone-acquired facial images and geometric landmark analysis. This two-stage system combines Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) to localize nine ocular landmarks per eye across three gaze directions (left, center, right), and supervised machine learning classifiers. A feature set of five biometric ratios was derived from coordinates including the canthi, limbi, and corneal light reflexes. The model was trained on facial images from 150 participants (96 with strabismus and 54 controls). To address class imbalance and improve generalizability, Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) and 4-fold cross-validation were applied. RT-DETR achieved an intersection over union of 0.62 and a mean center-point error of 6.52 pixels in landmark localization. The Random Forest classifier achieved an accuracy of 0.95, sensitivity of 0.96, specificity of 0.94, positive predictive value of 0.97, and negative predictive value of 0.92. This study demonstrates the feasibility of combining transformer-based landmark detection with geometric ratios for strabismus screening. The framework shows high performance under controlled conditions. While the use of biometric ratios allows for feature-level inspection, further research is required to establish full clinical interpretability and performance in uncontrolled environments.

Introduction

Strabismus, defined as ocular misalignment during fixation, is a common ophthalmic disorder affecting approximately 1–3.9% of the population, including both children and adults1,2. Depending on the direction of deviation, strabismus is classified into four types: esotropia (inward), exotropia (outward), hypertropia (upward), and hypotropia (downward). Persistent strabismus can lead to amblyopia (commonly known as “lazy eye”), consequently impairing binocular vision and depth perception. Beyond visual consequences, both children and adults with visible strabismus often experience psychosocial challenges, including stigmatization, anxiety, and depression3,4.

Recognition of the psychosocial effects of strabismus underscores the importance of counseling for patients and their families. Early diagnosis and treatment of strabismus are essential to lessen these adverse outcomes and prevent long-term visual impairment. However, diagnosis of strabismus typically requires direct examination by a pediatric ophthalmologist and the use of specialized tests, such as the Krimsky test, cover–uncover test, or alternate prism cover test, which require significant patient cooperation. This dependence presents a substantial barrier to effective screening in the general population, especially in rural or underserved regions where ophthalmologic services are limited5.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have shown promise in automating ophthalmic diagnostics, including strabismus detection6–8. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and other deep learning techniques have demonstrated efficacy in identifying ocular misalignments through image analysis7,8. Nonetheless, many of these models depend on structured clinical images or red-reflex photorefraction devices, which may not be consistently available in remote areas with limited access to specialized ophthalmic centers9. Furthermore, few existing systems simultaneously achieve high accuracy and methodological transparency, particularly when handling images captured under specific practical constraints, such as subtle variations in patient positioning or inconsistent gaze direction, even within a controlled environment10.

To address these limitations, we propose a hybrid framework that combines transformer-based landmark detection with human-readable biometric ratios and supervised machine learning classifiers, thereby providing explicit geometric features that align with clinical parameters. Specifically, we utilize the Real-time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) model to localize key ocular landmarks from smartphone-acquired images captured at three gaze angles—left, center, and right. From these landmarks, we derive five geometric ratios for each eye, which are then used as input features for established machine learning classifiers, such as Random Forest11. This modular approach was designed to support screening while preserving feature-level inspectability and facilitating potential mobile deployment.

This study represents a proof-of-concept application of RT-DETR in horizontal strabismus screening. By integrating landmark detection with feature-based classification, we aimed to evaluate whether this multistage framework could support binary screening of strabismus versus orthotropia from smartphone-acquired images under controlled acquisition conditions. Future external validation will be needed before broader deployment in less controlled settings can be considered.

Literature reviews

The following review includes both related work and technological background to support the theoretical and practical aspects of this study.

Related works

Currently, deep learning has been widely applied in medical image analysis12. Several studies have focused on ophthalmology, particularly retinal fundus imaging13–16, demonstrating that deep learning is a promising approach for enhancing clinical diagnostic support.

In the paper “Anomaly Detection in Red Reflex Images Using Deep Learning Approaches”6, a deep learning model for identifying amblyopia from red reflex images was proposed. The authors emphasized the high prevalence of pediatric eye disorders, with amblyopia being a leading cause of vision loss. Their framework consisted of three components: amblyopia classification, pupil localization, and data preparation. The model combined Mask R-CNN and Multi-task Cascaded Convolutional Neural Networks (MTCNN) to segment and detect pupil and iris regions, followed by a CNN for classification. The study highlighted the importance of early amblyopia detection and reviewed prior work, including smartphone-based photorefraction approaches. Overall, the results demonstrated the potential of deep learning techniques for amblyopia screening.

A second study by Hamid (2022)7 further investigated deep learning approaches for detecting amblyopia in red reflex images. The authors emphasized early detection and the applicability of CNN-based models. In addition, the study discussed the potential of deep learning methods for automatic strabismus detection, citing prior work by Almeida et al. and Valente et al.8,17, which reported high accuracy. The paper also incorporated eye region segmentation using the Viola–Jones algorithm as part of the detection pipeline. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of automated systems to improve strabismus diagnosis and clinical outcomes.

Technological reviews

This study employs several techniques, including object detection using RT-DETR, multiple machine learning classifiers, and oversampling using the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE).

Object detections

RT-DETR is a transformer-based object detector designed for real-time performance. It consists of a backbone, a hybrid encoder, and a transformer decoder with auxiliary prediction heads. The model efficiently processes multi-scale features by decoupling intra-scale interaction and cross-scale fusion. Its Intersection over Union (IoU)–aware query selection improves the quality of initial object queries, while the decoder iteratively refines them to generate bounding boxes and confidence scores.

RT-DETR has demonstrated strong performance in both speed and accuracy. For example, RT-DETR-L achieves 53.0% mean average precision (mAP) on COCO val2017 and 114 frames per second (FPS) on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU, while RT-DETR-X achieves 54.8% AP, outperforming existing YOLO-based detectors18.

The training objective combines three loss functions: Focal loss, Generalized IoU (GIoU) loss, and L1 loss. Focal loss addresses class imbalance by down-weighting easy negatives, while GIoU loss measures overlap and spatial alignment between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes. L1 loss is used for bounding box regression. The relative contributions of these losses are controlled by weighting coefficients during training, enabling robust localization across varying object sizes.

In addition, IoU-aware query selection initializes decoder queries using encoder features from regions with high predicted IoU scores. The process includes:

Oversampling

SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) is an oversampling method used to address class imbalance. It was introduced by Chawla et al. (2002)19,20. This method operates by selecting instances that are proximate in the feature space, establishing a connection between these instances, and generating a new sample along this connection. Initially, a random instance from the minority class is selected, followed by the identification of its k nearest neighbors (usually k = 5). From these neighbors, another instance is randomly chosen, and a synthetic sample is generated at a randomly selected position between the original instance and its neighbor in the feature space. The above algorithm is shown in Fig. 1. SMOTE has been effectively applied across various domains, including software quality modeling and defect prediction.

Methods

This study was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University Panel 3 (COA no. SI 775/2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians for participation in the study and for the publication of any potentially identifiable images or information in an open-access journal. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Images and demographic data were securely stored in password-protected files, accessible only to the project leader and authorized researchers. Personal identifiers (e.g., names, hospital numbers) were excluded and replaced with anonymized codes. A note documenting participation in the AI-based Eye Disease Screening Project was added to each subject’s medical records.

Between January 2023 and June 2024, 150 participants were recruited from a tertiary hospital, comprising 96 individuals with horizontal strabismus (54 exotropia, 42 esotropia) and 54 controls with normal ocular alignment. Inclusion for the strabismus group required a confirmed diagnosis with a deviation angle of ≥ 10 prism diopters. Participants of all ages were eligible for both the strabismus and control groups, with the latter confirmed to have no ocular misalignment through comprehensive examination. Exclusion criteria consisted of inability to cooperate with clinical examination and image acquisition, coexisting ocular surface pathologies (including pterygium and pinguecula), and any congenital craniofacial anomalies.

A single researcher performed the eye landmark annotation using a custom-developed desktop application designed to ensure consistency. Image patches centered on each landmark were then cropped with a 10-pixel margin for training. Subsequently, a two-stage analysis pipeline was implemented: first, the RT-DETR deep learning algorithm was employed to automate landmark localization; second, seven machine learning classifiers were evaluated for strabismus detection. These classifiers utilized 30 predefined geometric ratios derived from the localized landmark coordinates as input features for binary classification.

Landmark annotation

Landmark annotation and data formatting

As illustrated in Fig. 2(B), nine ocular landmarks per image were manually annotated using a custom OpenCV interface21, with the annotator blinded to strabismus status. The landmarks (Fig. 3(B)) were numbered in a clockwise spiral from the outer margins to the central light reflex: medial canthus (P1), superonasal cornea (P2), superotemporal cornea (P3), temporal canthus (P4), inferotemporal cornea (P5), inferonasal cornea (P6), medial limbus (P7), temporal limbus (P8), and central corneal light reflex (P9). Annotations were exported in YOLO format for object detection training, mapped onto the image in Fig. 4(B). Each image included attributes such as participant and image identifiers, gaze direction, eye side, confidence scores, landmark indices, and Cartesian coordinates (x, y). For example, landmark P9 of the left eye in a middle-gaze image (‘img0001’) was recorded at coordinates (492, 419) (Fig. 4(C)).

Data partitioning and experimental setup

Participants who met the exclusion criteria were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 150 participants (54 exotropia, 42 esotropia and 54 orthotropia). This resulted in 450 raw facial images (three per participant), 900 eye crops (two per image), and a total of 8,100 landmark instances.

For eye landmark detection, a deep learning model was evaluated using 4-fold cross-validation. Although nine landmark types were defined, 18 classes were implemented to distinguish between left and right eyes. As the dataset size was not evenly divisible by four, each fold contained 37–38 participants. In each iteration, three folds were used for model development (80% training, 20% validation), and one-fold was reserved for testing. This resulted in approximate splits of 90:23:37 or 90:22:38 (train: validation: test). In a representative fold: training 90 participants (270 images; 4,860 landmarks), validation 23 participants (69 images; 1,242 landmarks), and test 37 participants (111 images; 1,998 landmarks).

For strabismus classification, seven machine learning models were trained using 30 features derived from landmark-based geometric ratios. A 4-fold cross-validation strategy was applied at the participant level, with development and test splits of either 113:37 or 112:38. In a representative fold: training 113 participants (72 strabismus, 41 orthotropia) augmented to 400 samples via SMOTE, and test 37 participants (24 strabismus, 13 orthotropia).

The decoder in RT-DETR used IoU-aware query selection and iterative refinement to produce final bounding boxes and confidence scores for each predicted landmark. Moreover, the flipping parameter was set to zero in order to prevent inadvertent image flipping and ensure the integrity of the eye images throughout the training process.

In this step, the backbone extracted multi-scale features from the input images. These were then processed by a hybrid encoder, which used intra-scale interaction and cross-scale fusion to combine them into a refined sequence. Then, an IoU-aware query selection mechanism identified the most precise features to serve as initial object queries for the decoder. After that, the decoder refined the object queries to generate candidate bounding boxes and confidence scores.

Training process

In the eye landmark detection phase using RT-DETR, a 4-fold cross-validation approach was employed to separate the dataset according to participants. In each of the 4 iterations, each fold (n = 37–38) was reserved as an independent test set (unseen data). The remaining three folds (n = 112–113) were used for model development, further internal split into a training set (80%) and a validation set (20%) to optimize the RT-DETR model. The eye landmark coordinates predicted from the test set of each fold were saved for use in the next step.

Eye landmark feature engineering

In the step of feature engineering, as performed in Fig. 2(D), five geometric ratios (RT1–RT5) were computed using selected landmarks in the horizontal line.

According to the clinical expertise of the ophthalmologists in our research team, the pupil and corneal light reflex are approximately centered in orthotropic eyes during primary gaze, with relatively balanced visible scleral exposure on the nasal and temporal sides. Horizontal strabismus changes this relationship by shifting the corneal light reflex and altering the relative scleral show across gaze positions. On that basis, the selected landmarks were intended to capture three complementary geometric domains: overall eye width for normalization, nasal and temporal exposed scleral segments, and the horizontal position of the corneal light reflex relative to the limbal boundaries. We therefore used horizontal distances between selected landmarks as key features, specifically the medial canthus (P1), temporal canthus (P4), medial limbus (P7), temporal limbus (P8), and central corneal light reflex (P9). These points created geometric ratios (RT1–RT5) as expressed in Fig. 3(B). All distances were normalized to eye width.

Based on prior ophthalmic knowledge within the research team, orthotropic eyes were expected to show relatively balanced nasal-versus-temporal relationships in primary gaze and predictable directional changes during lateral gaze. Accordingly, RT1 and RT4 were designed to reflect nasal and temporal scleral exposure, RT2 and RT3 to reflect the position of the corneal light reflex within the horizontal corneal span, and RT5 to reflect visible iris width as an internal reference during gaze change. In the primary position, RT1–RT4 were expected to remain relatively symmetric, particularly across the RT1:RT4 and RT2:RT3 pairs. During lateral gaze, directional shifts in these parameters were expected according to the side of fixation, with reciprocal changes between nasal and temporal measurements in the two eyes. RT5 was also expected to decrease during lateral gaze relative to the primary position, likely reflecting the effect of ocular curvature and changing visibility of the limbal boundaries during horizontal rotation. Some variation between eyes was anticipated because of inter-individual differences in eyelid configuration and gaze-dependent changes in visible scleral exposure.

Result of eye landmark detections

For the eye landmark detection task, the RT-DETR model was employed and trained on a dataset of eye landmark annotations. The training process spanned 100 epochs per fold, utilizing a multi-part loss function consisting of Generalized Intersection-over-Union (GIoU) loss, classification (Cls) loss, and L1 loss. Model settings

Based on experimental results, the settings were adjusted for optimizing the performance of the model. The resulting configurations for the models are summarized below.

For the RT-DETR hyperparameters, the Loss functions are considered by the costs and weights: weight_loss_bbox (L1 weight) is set to 5.0, weight_loss_giou (GIoU weight) is set to 2.0, and focal_loss_alpha (0.75) and focal_loss_gamma (2.0) are for the Focal Loss used for classification, controlled by use_focal_loss=True. The IoU-aware query selection mechanism uses a fixed number of num_queries = 300 and incorporates denoising with num_denoising = 100, label_noise_ratio = 0.5, and box_noise_scale = 1.0. Hyperparameters for the Transformer model include a shared d_model = 256, and specific settings for the hybrid encoder (e.g., encoder_layers = 1, encoder_ffn_dim = 1024, encoder_attention_heads = 8) and the RT-DETR Transformer decoder (e.g., decoder_layers = 6, decoder_ffn_dim = 1024, decoder_attention_heads = 8), with a standard activation_function="silu” for the model and encoder_activation_function="gelu” for the encoder. Essential training hyperparameters include the dropout rate dropout = 0.1 and attention_dropout = 0.1, the classification penalty eos_coefficient=1e-4, and the option with_box_refine=True. Other parameters set precision and stability features like initializer_range = 0.01, layer_norm_eps=1e-5, and batch_norm_eps=1e-5, while the backbone is configured with use_pretrained_backbone=False and freeze_backbone_batch_norms=True.

In addition, the tuned hyperparameter for a Random Forest contains 30 estimators or trees to be constructed, where each base decision tree will utilize the Gini impurity to evaluate the quality of a split. In addition, the feature subset size considered at each split is set to the square root of the maximum number of features via max_features=’sqrt’. For the SMOTE, the number of neighbors was set to 5, and the sample strategy of True: False cases was up to 200:200.

Implementation

The StrabSeen prototype was developed as a proof-of-concept mobile application to demonstrate the clinical feasibility of the proposed AI pipeline that returns a binary screening output and associated probability score for horizontal strabismus, as depicted in Fig. 6. The AI model is stored on a server with an API for access. A mobile application has been developed with Flutter and deployed on the Android platform to demonstrate the technical feasibility.

The application guides the user to capture three images corresponding to the required gaze directions (center, left, and right). To ensure data privacy, the server was designed as a dedicated processing unit that performs real-time inference without persistent image storage.

During the experimental phase, the inference was hosted on a server environment with 2 vCPUs and 4 GB of RAM. The end-to-end processing time, including image transmission and deep learning inference, averaged 15–20 s per case. While this latency is acceptable for a prototype, further optimization—such as utilizing GPU acceleration or migrating to on-device inference—could enhance scalability and reduce dependency on network connectivity in high-traffic or resource-limited settings.

Discussion

This study presented a two-stage, AI framework for binary screening of horizontal strabismus versus orthotropia using smartphone-acquired images under controlled conditions—an approach that combines deep-learning-based landmark localization with geometric feature extraction that provides feature-level transparency by utilizing human-readable geometric descriptors, rather than offering a mechanistic explanation of the underlying disease pathology. This section discusses our work from the following perspectives.

Interpretation of results

By employing RT-DETR for landmark localization and biometric ratio–based classification via traditional ML models, we demonstrated promising screening performance under controlled image acquisition conditions.

Across the RT-DETR models trained for each gaze, landmark detection reached acceptable reliability with IoU = 0.62 and mean center-point error of 6.52 pixels, despite the absence of clinical-grade imaging. These results match or exceed prior ocular landmark detection systems. For instance, Simoes et al.17 used U-Net and ResNet to detect ocular alignment in digital images, achieving strong detection performance but limited generalizability beyond controlled settings. Our model expanded on this by using RT-DETR, which enabled real-time inference and robust performance across three gaze directions.

The use of biometric landmark ratios provided a more interpretable representation for strabismus classification than an end-to-end image classifier. Similar to earlier work by Mengash et al.22, who applied rule-based and CNN methods to video analysis for detecting horizontal strabismus22, our method demonstrated comparable screening performance—accuracy of 0.95, sensitivity of 0.96, and specificity of 0.94—using only still images from smartphones. In contrast, Seo et al.9 reported 0.93 accuracy with an infrared video–based system that required specialized hardware. Our proof-of-concept framework used smartphone-acquired images collected under controlled conditions and required three static photographs for binary screening of strabismus versus orthotropia.

Additionally, Valente et al.8 employed a cover-test–based automated diagnosis with digital video, reporting accuracy of 0.94, sensitivity of 0.97, and specificity = 0.73. While they achieved slightly higher sensitivity, our approach showed higher specificity. High specificity is clinically advantageous in screening contexts to minimize unnecessary referrals and parental anxiety resulting from false positives. Furthermore, their system relied on motion cues, whereas ours relied on still images and landmark geometry, thus reducing environmental constraints and allowing asynchronous screening.

From an architectural perspective, prior mobile-based AI attempts typically used CNNs without landmark explanation6. In contrast, our framework uses human-readable input features (RT1–RT5) derived from ocular geometry, which may facilitate clinical inspection of model inputs and improve transparency relative to end-to-end image classifiers. Nevertheless, feature importance and rule extraction from a Random Forest should be interpreted as providing partial interpretability of how the model separates classes within this dataset, rather than a clinically exhaustive or directly causal explanation of ocular misalignment.

In summary, geometric eye landmark ratios are used to classify strabismus in this study and the classification of Random Forest model offers a methodological alternative that utilizes explicit geometric features, which are more accessible for clinical review than the latent representations in ‘black-box’ deep learning models. Instead of complex motions or specialized hardware, images from smartphones performed well in our proposed screening approach. This modular design represents a proof-of-concept framework that may support future development of telehealth and community screening tools.

Technical advantages of the proposed methodology

Our technical approach consists of two main components: Eye Landmark Detection using RT-DETR and Strabismus Classification using Random Forest. Additionally, the oversampling technique using SMOTE is utilized to aid in the training process.

Eye landmark detection using RT-DETR

We chose to use eye landmark detection using the deep learning model of RT-DETR due to the following key factors:

The RT-DETR utilizes a mix of loss functions to enhance several facets of the detection process for assessing ocular alignment in strabismus, which depends on a precise detection of points surrounding the eye, the pupil, and the light reflex. The Focal loss is crucial for reducing class imbalance, ensuring the model does not neglect small, significant landmark points within the picture. The GIoU loss provides correct localization by removing poor bounding box overlap and spatial misalignment, which is essential for accurately framing minor features such as the corneal reflex. Finally, the L1 loss facilitates precise coordinate refinement with steady gradients, enhancing the predicted positions for optimal spatial accuracy. Overall, these losses allow the RT-DETR to be able to identify tiny eye landmarks in the picture of patients.

The IoU-aware query selection mechanism in RT-DETR has an advantage in eye landmark identification, which requires precise localization of small areas. The system selects decoder queries based on expected IoU scores, thereby prioritizing high-confidence regions and suppressing suboptimal predictions. Focused selection reduces decoding noise and enhances convergence efficiency, making eye corners, pupils, and eyelid curves easier to recognize. IoU-aware query selection improves detection accuracy in complex, high-density localization applications, such as eye landmark identification.

Classification using random forest

Among its practical advantages, Random Forest (RF) offers a relatively transparent tabular classification framework for clinical screening compared with fully end-to-end deep learning classifiers. The model can summarize how combinations of input features contribute to class separation through feature-importance ranking and tree-based decision paths, which may enhance confidence and facilitate result inspection by domain experts. However, our pipeline remains dependent on deep learning to identify eye landmarks, and the derived rules should be viewed as approximations of model behavior in this dataset rather than direct statements of pathophysiology. Aligned with the goals of interpretable machine learning, our approach leverages the transparency of a feature-based classifier with the effectiveness of deep learning for landmark extraction, thereby supporting more transparent screening outputs without claiming full explainability.

To provide the interpretation of the final model, the entire population was analyzed to derive rule-based insights from the Random Forest. This analysis highlights which features contributed most strongly to class separation in the final model, but these patterns should be interpreted as dataset-specific model behavior rather than validated clinical rules. According to our feature importance analysis, the variables ranked by their contribution to the model are: LR_rt1, RL_rt3, MR_rt3, RL_rt1, RL_rt5, LR_rt4, LL_rt2, LL_rt3, RR_rt4, LR_rt5, RR_rt3, MR_rt2, LR_rt2, RR_rt2, LR_rt3, ML_rt5, LL_rt1, ML_rt4, MR_rt5, MR_rt4, RL_rt4, ML_rt1, ML_rt2, LL_rt5, ML_rt3, MR_rt1, LL_rt4, RL_rt2, RR_rt1, and RR_rt5. The box plots in Fig. 7 illustrate the distribution of these feature strabismus condition. These visualizations highlight distributional shifts in metrics such as LR_rt1 and RL_rt3 between the True and False groups. These differences in medians and ranges provide the empirical basis for the classification rules used in the Random Forest model.

To facilitate interpretation, the structure of the Random Forest model can be partially expressed through if–then rules derived from representative trees (see Table 4). These rules highlight several influential features, including LR_rt1, RL_rt3, MR_rt3, and RL_rt1. For instance, LR_rt1—representing the medial scleral area of the right eye—is expected to increase during leftward gaze. In this context, Rule 1 indicates that lower values are linked to strabismus, whereas Rule 2 reflects the alternative condition. Furthermore, features with suffixes _rt2 and _rt3 correspond to corneal light reflex positions, which relate to visual axis alignment. For example, Rule 4 suggests that an imbalance between MR_rt2 and MR_rt3 in the right eye during primary gaze is predictive of strabismus.

More specifically, the model captures patterns related to medial scleral exposure during lateral gaze. Rules 1 and 3 indicate that reduced medial scleral area in the right eye during leftward gaze (LR_rt1 ≤ 0.07), in combination with increased temporal corneal light reflex distance in the left eye (LL_rt3 > 0.45), is consistent with strabismus, potentially reflecting reduced adduction of the right eye. Similarly, Rule 3 shows that increased temporal light reflex in the left eye during rightward gaze (RL_rt3 > 0.08) serves as a classifier for strabismus, whereas lower values are more consistent with orthotropia.

In addition, the temporal positioning of corneal light reflex serves as a critical feature for strabismus classification. For example, Rule 5 indicates that a discrepancy between MR_rt2 and MR_rt3 (MR_rt2 < 0.16 and MR_rt3 > 0.20) is associated with strabismus, whereas more balanced values are observed in orthotropic cases during primary gaze. The same rule also suggests that limited temporal exposure during rightward gaze (RR_rt3 ≤ 0.08) may further indicate strabismic deviation. Furthermore, Rule 9 indicates that a wider iris width in primary gaze (MR_rt5 > 0.44), combined with reduced medial light reflex (ML_rt2 ≤ 0.28), is linked to strabismus, which may reflect restricted abduction.

This geometric-feature approach provides greater interpretability than end-to-end black-box deep learning methods. However, establishing simple, fully comprehensible rules for all cases remains challenging. If straightforward classification were possible, a basic Decision Tree would likely achieve high accuracy. Due to the complexity and high dimensionality of features across multiple gaze positions necessitate the use of an ensemble method such as Random Forest—comprising multiple trees that vote on the final classification. Each rule captures specific patterns identified during training, and the ensemble aggregates these partial signals to achieve a level of performance that isolated, interpretable rules cannot provide. From a clinical perspective, these rules should be interpreted with caution. They do not constitute definitive diagnostic criteria nor isolate independent parameters for each eye. Rather, they are broadly consistent with established domain knowledge while also reflecting dataset-specific patterns learned by the model, and therefore should not be considered comprehensive clinical explanations.

Oversampling technique using SMOTE

In this study, SMOTE was employed to address class imbalance in a dataset of limited size, which is crucial for improving model training stability and representation of minority classes. However, in some cases where many outliers are found, the synthetic data generated from noise may impact the overall results of the predictive model. To mitigate the issue of noise amplification, we conducted analyses of the synthetic samples by comparing the results against Over-sampling using SMOTE and cleaning using Edited Nearest Neighbors (SMOTE-ENN)23, which is a variant of SMOTE reducing synthesis noise and borderline data. Our prediction approach with the replacing SMOTE with SMOT-EEN resulted in an accuracy of 0.95, which is close to the accuracy of the original experiment using SMOTE. Therefore, the results suggest that the data synthesis was not negatively impacted by noise presented in the processed data.

In addition, the dataset was first divided into training and testing sets prior to oversampling, ensuring that SMOTE was applied to the training data and thus avoiding any data leakage into the test set. This design maintains the validity of performance evaluation. Thus, the application of SMOTE under these controlled conditions provides an advantage in enhancing model generalization in small or imbalanced datasets while minimizing the risk of overfitting.

Theoretical and clinical implications

This study contributes to the ongoing convergence between explainable AI, real-time computer vision, and mobile health. It aligns with broader efforts to build AI systems in healthcare that prioritize methodological transparency and feature-level inspectability, while acknowledging that interpretability exists on a spectrum rather than as an all-or-none property2425. By reducing the reliance on specialized hardware and clinician-guided tests, our system offers a proof-of-concept framework that serves as a foundation for future screening applications, pending further external validation in broader and less controlled settings.

If validated in broader settings, the proposed system could be used by general practitioners, general ophthalmologists, or trained nurses. The model is intended to support screening rather than provide a definitive diagnosis. In practice, a positive result may help identify individuals who should be referred for confirmatory ophthalmic examination. A negative result, however, should still be interpreted with caution because screening tools have inherent limitations and do not replace clinical examination.

To maximize the sensitivity of the screening process and minimize the risk of missed diagnoses, one aim of our study is to minimize false negatives (FN). While Random Forest performed well, as the confusion matrix presented in Table 3, eliminating all FN remains a significant challenge given the inherent variability in image data. Future work should aim to further reduce false-negative results while maintaining acceptable specificity.

Limitations and critical evaluation

Despite promising results, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the dataset was collected from a single center and may not reflect real-world variability in age, ethnicity, lighting, or device quality. Second, landmark annotation was performed manually; inter-rater reliability was not formally assessed. One ophthalmologist provided the strabismus labels, and landmark annotation was performed by a researcher blinded to the clinical diagnoses. Third, although we used SMOTE and cross-validation to address imbalance, additional external datasets are needed to confirm generalizability.

Moreover, we recognize that ratio-based classification—while interpretable—may have limitations in more complex forms of strabismus involving torsion or vertical components. Future work could explore deep feature fusion between landmark geometry and convolutional texture information to address mixed-type presentations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, using Real-Time Detection Transformer (RT-DETR) for high-precision eye landmark detection across three gaze positions—straight, left, and right—this study created an automated framework for binary screening of horizontal strabismus versus orthotropia. The geometric eye landmark ratios (RT1–5), which are the proportional distances between key ocular landmarks, such as the medial canthus, medial limbus, central corneal light reflex, and temporal canthus, were the basis of our proposed feature set. A Random Forest model, which showed high performance with an accuracy of 0.95, sensitivity of 0.96, and specificity of 0.94, was then used to classify these features. In addition, internal analysis suggested that several learned feature patterns exhibited alignment with established ophthalmic principles, especially with regard to the off-center displacement of the central corneal light reflex in strabismus patients and the visible sclera area during various gazes. However, these findings should be interpreted as model-level associations rather than direct causal explanations. Although an exploratory three-class analysis was also performed, further validation is required before reliable subtype classification can be claimed.

Future research should focus on external validation of the proposed framework under more diverse and realistic image acquisition conditions, including low-light settings, variable facial orientations, and different smartphone cameras. Additional multicenter studies are needed to evaluate performance and generalizability across broader patient populations. Further methodological development may also explore video-based analysis to better capture ocular motility. Finally, lightweight on-device models should be investigated to reduce reliance on server-side processing and to improve the practical feasibility of real-time screening.
