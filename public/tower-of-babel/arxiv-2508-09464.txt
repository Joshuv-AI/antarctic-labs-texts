Sequential Non-Bayesian Persuasion
Yaron Azrieli, Rachana Das

Abstract
We study a model of persuasion in which the receiver is a ‘conservative Bayesian’ whose updated belief is a convex combination of the prior and the correct Bayesian posterior. While in the classic Bayesian case providing information sequentially is never valuable, we show that the sender gains from sequential persuasion in many of the environments considered in the literature on strategic information transmission. We also consider the case in which the sender and receiver are both biased and prove that the maximal expected payoff for the sender under sequential persuasion is the same as in the case where neither of them is biased.



1
Introduction

Following the work of
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
, Bayesian Persuasion (BP) has become a focus of economic theory research. As its name suggests, a pillar of this literature is Bayes rule: Upon observing new information agents ‘rationally’ update beliefs as Bayes rule prescribes. While this is a normatively appealing assumption, there is ample evidence that people regularly deviate from this rule and are influenced by various types of biases
(
Benjamin 2019
;
Ortoleva 2024
)
. In a recent paper,
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
study BP while relaxing the assumption that the receiver follows Bayes rule; instead, they assume that the receiver is characterized by a systematic bias which determines their posterior as a function of the correct Bayesian posterior; among other things, they show that the concavification method used to solve the standard BP problem still applies in this setting.

In this paper we revisit the framework of
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
but expand the set of persuasion strategies to include sequential information provision. It has been shown experimentally that releasing information over time and repetition of information influences decisions in various contexts
(
Carlson et al. 2006
;
Schulz-Hardt et al. 2016
)
, but such persuasion strategies are redundant if decision makers are Bayesian: Any sequential strategy can be replaced by a single information structure that induces the same distribution over posteriors and hence the same expected payoffs for the sender and the receiver
(
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2009
)
. As we argue below, a departure from Bayesianism can explain why sequential persuasion is more effective than a one-shot release of information.

To make our point we focus on a particular type of biased updating that reflects ‘prior-stickiness’, i.e., a tendency to cautiously update beliefs less than prescribed by Bayes rule. Specifically, we assume that the receiver is a “conservative Bayesian”: Upon observing a signal their posterior is given by

\alpha\mu_{0}+(1-\alpha)\mu,

where
\mu_{0}
is the prior,
\mu
is the Bayesian posterior, and
\alpha\in[0,1)
is a parameter that captures the strength of the bias.

1

This is a special case of the class of updating rules considered in
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
.
Under-updating of beliefs in light of new information is well-documented in the experimental literature
(
Benjamin 2019
, Section 8)
and can explain anomalies in the field
(
Ortoleva 2024
, Section 3)
. Furthermore, the specific form of bias we consider is relatively easy to work with, has axiomatic foundations
(
Epstein 2006
;
Kovach 2021
)
, and it preserves the martingale property of Bayesian updating which is known to be crucial in BP models.

When signals arrive over time, one should specify how the bias is applied to reach the final posterior. For most of the paper we assume that the receiver uses an updating procedure that we refer to as
Period-by-Period bias relative to New prior (PbP-N)
. This procedure treats the (biased) posterior obtained after the
n
th signal as the prior for the
(n+1)
th signal, and uses this prior both for the calculation of the Bayesian posterior
\mu
and as the anchor for the bias
\mu_{0}
. In other words, after each signal the receiver fully embraces the updated belief and uses it as their prior when additional information is provided; the original prior from the beginning of the process has no special significance.

2

2

In Section
5
we discuss how our results would change with a couple of alternative updating procedures.

Our main results compare the maximal attainable payoff for the sender with a single period of persuasion to their maximal payoff with an unlimited (but finite) number of signals. A first simple observation is that with multiple signals we can no longer describe the bias of the receiver as a function of the correct Bayesian posterior: There are different combinations of signals that lead to the same Bayesian posterior (held by the sender) but to different biased posteriors for the receiver. As a result, the concavification approach is not applicable once sequential persuasion is allowed. Consequentially, it is typically challenging to calculate the optimal payoff for the sender with sequential persuasion even in simple environments where concavification can be used to characterize sender’s payoff with one-shot persuasion as in
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
.

Nevertheless, we show that in many of the classic environments considered in the literature sequential persuasion is valuable. We say that
the sender gains from sequential persuasion
if there exists a finite sequence of experiments
3
3

3

More precisely, in a sequential persuasion strategy the sender can condition the experiment chosen in the
n
th period on the history of signal realizations in all previous
n-1
periods. The sender can also decide whether to stop or provide additional information as a function of the history.
that yields a strictly higher expected payoff to the sender than the maximal expected payoff attainable with one experiment. We show that under mild conditions the sender gains from sequential persuasion when sender and receiver have identical preferences (Proposition
1
), in
Crawford and Sobel 1982
type of environments (Proposition
2
), when sender’s payoff is linear in the receiver’s belief (Proposition
3
), and when there are only two actions as in the prosecutor example of
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
(Proposition
4
).

There are various intuitive reasons why sequential persuasion outperforms static persuasion in environments such as the above. First, suppose that a certain action
a
yields a high payoff to the sender (either in a particular state or in all states), but for the receiver to choose this action they need to hold a relatively extreme belief. With only one signal receiver’s posterior cannot be too far from the prior due to the bias, so
a
will not be chosen, but by repeating the information multiple times receiver’s belief can drift further away to a point where
a
is selected. Second, even if a belief that induces
a
can be achieved with a single signal, the sender may be able to increase the probability that
a
is chosen by providing additional information when the initial signal realization induces other actions.
4
4

4

To illustrate, consider the famous prosecutor example from
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2009
. Even if the first realized signal indicates that the defendant is innocent, the belief of the judge remains interior, and the prosecutor can induce conviction with positive probability by providing additional information.
Third, with a single experiment the distribution over receiver’s biased posteriors is Bayes plausible, but with multiple rounds this is no longer the case due to the disagreement that forms between the sender and receiver. This implies that new action distributions may be achieved which are preferable to the sender. Propositions
1
-
4
exemplify these forces in economic-relevant setups.

The results described up to this point assume that the sender updates beliefs in the proper Bayesian manner. As pointed out in
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
, this is a natural benchmark that reflects the effort that the sender exerts to persuade. However, BP can also be used to model the interaction between multiple selves of the same agent whose preferences change between the time of information acquisition and the time of decision. Under such interpretation it is suitable to assume that sender and receiver are equally biased. It turns out that in this case characterizing the optimal expected payoff of the sender is much simpler: We show in Proposition
5
that it is equal to the payoff in the standard unbiased BP problem, regardless of the value of the bias parameter
\alpha
.
5
5

5

This result requires that the standard BP problem has an interior solution, or that the value function
\hat{v}
is continuous on the boundary of the simplex.
This result implies that checking whether the sender gains from sequential persuasion amounts to comparing the values of two standard concavification programs.

1.1
Related literature

This paper is at the intersection of two strands of the BP literature. The first consists of models that introduce frictions to the receiver’s interpretation and processing of information. As already mentioned above, our work builds on the results in
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
, which studies the static BP problem with a receiver that systematically distorts beliefs. Perhaps the closest work to ours is
Levy et al. 2022
who consider a receiver that suffers from correlation neglect when obtaining multiple signals; the paper shows that the sender can take advantage of this bias and obtain any state-contingent posterior distribution by sending sufficiently many signals. In contrast, a sender interacting with a conservative Bayesian has limited persuasion power even with arbitrarily long sequence of experiments. In
Augias and Barreto 2020
the receiver is a wishful thinker that biases their belief (at a cost) whenever this increases their expected utility.
Galperti 2019
studies the BP problem with a receiver that ex-ante assigns zero probability to some states and updates in a non-Bayesian manner upon observing unexpected news. In
Eliaz et al. 2021
the sender adds ambiguity to their message and as a result the receiver fail to properly apply Bayes rule. Finally,
Bloedel and Segal 2018
,
Wei 2021
, and
Dall’Ara 2024
assume that the receiver is rationally inattentive and incurs a cost for processing the signal sent by the sender. We note that all these papers consider a single period of information transmission, while our focus is on the potential benefits of sequential persuasion with non-Bayesian updating.

The second strand contains papers in which information revelation is sequential. This is by now a large literature and we only mention a few of the more closely related works. In
Su et al. 2021
and
Ni et al. 2023
the sender is restricted to a limited set of experiments and can therefore benefit from sending multiple signals.
Zhang 2024
studies a model in which the receiver is rationally inattentive, showing that sequential persuasion can endogenously arise in this case.
Le Treust and Tomala 2019
and
Escudé and Sinander 2023
consider a sender that sequentially provides information to a receiver subject to an exogenous constraint on the rate of information transmission. Several papers
(
Li and Norman 2021
;
Koessler et al. 2022
;
Wu 2023
)
study the equilibrium of BP games with multiple senders who move sequentially. These papers and the rest of the literature restrict attention to Bayesian agents.

Organization

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section

The model

2.1
Environment

We start with some notation. For any finite set
Y
let
\Delta(Y)
be the set of probability distributions on
Y
. Let
\Omega
be the (finite) set of states. A typical element of
\Delta(\Omega)
is denoted
\mu
and is viewed as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
whose non-negative coordinates sum up to 1. If
Y
is a set in some linear space then
Conv(Y)
denotes the convex hull of
Y
. Finite-support distributions over
\Delta(\Omega)
will usually be denoted by
\tau
and their support by
T
; we say that
\tau
is interior if its support
T
is contained in the relative interior of
\Delta(\Omega)
.

As in
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
, we consider a game between a sender called S and a receiver called R. In the classic version of this game S moves first choosing an information structure, a signal is realized with distribution that depends on the true state
\omega\in\Omega
and on the choice of S, and finally R chooses an action
a
from some set
A
. The utility function for R is
u(\omega,a)
and for S is
v(\omega,a)
. The action that maximizes the expected utility of R at belief
\mu
is denoted
\hat{a}(\mu)
, and as usual we assume that a maximizer exists and that R chooses an S-optimal action when indifferent. Thus, the expected payoff of S given that both R and S hold belief
\mu
is
\hat{v}(\mu):=\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu}[v(\omega,\hat{a}(\mu))]
.

Throughout we assume that S and R have a full-support common prior
\mu_{0}
. We let
V(\mu_{0})
be the value of the concave closure of
\hat{v}
at
\mu_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle V(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\tau}~~\sum_{\mu\in T}\tau(\mu)\hat{v}(\mu)

(1)

\displaystyle\textit{s.t. }\sum_{\mu\in T}\tau(\mu)\mu=\mu_{0}.

It has been shown in
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2011
that
V(\mu_{0})
is the maximal equilibrium expected utility for S in this game.

2.2
Biased updating

We deviate from the standard theory by relaxing the assumption that R updates beliefs in a Bayesian way. Instead, we focus on conservative Bayesianism: R is characterized by a bias parameter
\alpha\in[0,1)
, so that given a prior belief
\mu_{0}
and a properly updated (Bayesian) posterior
\mu
, their (biased) belief is

D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})=\alpha\mu_{0}+(1-\alpha)\mu.

(2)

It is worth pointing out a couple of simple properties of the updating rule (
2
). First, given
\mu_{0}
and
\alpha
, for a biased agent to hold some belief
\mu^{\prime}
after updating there must exist some belief
\mu
such that
\mu^{\prime}=\alpha\mu_{0}+(1-\alpha)\mu
. Equivalently, we need that
\mu=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}[\mu^{\prime}-\alpha\mu_{0}]\in\Delta(\Omega)
, which is satisfied if and only if
\mu^{\prime}(\omega)\geq\alpha\mu_{0}(\omega)
for every
\omega\in\Omega
.

Second, if
\tau
is any posterior distribution that satisfies the Bayes plausibility constraint
\sum_{\mu\in T}\tau(\mu)\mu=\mu_{0}
, then after applying the bias to each belief in
T
the resulting posterior distribution would still satisfy this constraint. The next lemma immediately follows from these properties.

Lemma 1
.

Fix
\mu_{0}
and
\alpha
. A (biased) posterior distribution
\tau^{\prime}
can be induced by some information structure if and only if (i)
\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}}\tau^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{0}
; and (ii)
\mu^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mu_{0}
for every
\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
.

Note that as
\alpha
increases the set of feasible posterior distributions shrinks, and that this lemma reduces to the standard Bayes plausibility constraint under Bayesian updating, i.e., when
\alpha=0
.

2.3
Sequential persuasion

It is well-known that S cannot increase their payoff by revealing information sequentially in the standard Bayesian case. Indeed, under Bayesian updating observing a sequence of signal realizations leads to the same posterior as observing them all at once. In other words, every sequence of information structures chosen by S can be replaced by a one-shot information structure that induces the same posterior distribution and hence the same payoffs to S and R
(
Kamenica and Gentzkow 2009
)
. However, as we will show below, under biased updating S would often be able to do better with a sequence of signals than with single one.

To formalize this, we define a
a sequential persuasion strategy
recursively as follows. Let
X
be a finite set of signals and let
\Sigma_{X}=\{P:\Omega\to\Delta(X)\}
be the set of information structures with signals in
X
.

When information arrives sequentially, we need to specify how R updates their belief to reach a final posterior. For most of the paper we focus on an updating procedure that we refer to as

Example 1

Suppose that
\Omega=\{\omega_{0},\omega_{1}\}
and denote
\mu=\mu(\omega_{1})\in[0,1]
the belief that the state is
\omega_{1}
. Let the prior be
\mu_{0}=0.5
and fix the bias parameter
\alpha=0.5

x^{0}
or
x^{1}
and the probability that the signal matches the state is
\beta\in[0.5,1]
. Note that in this persuasion strategy the information structure in the second period does not depend on the realized signal in the first period.

Suppose first that the signal
x^{1}

\mu=\beta

\mu_{1}(x^{1})=0.5*\mu_{0}+0.5*\beta=0.25+0.5\beta

\mu_{2}(x^{1},x^{1})=0.5*\mu_{1}(x^{1})+0.5*\frac{(0.25+0.5\beta)*\beta}{(0.25+0.5\beta)*\beta+(0.75-0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)}.

If instead

\mu=\frac{(0.25+0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)}{(0.25+0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)+(0.75-0.5\beta)*\beta}

\mu_{2}(x^{1},x^{0})=0.5*\mu_{1}+0.5*\frac{(0.25+0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)}{(0.25+0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)+(0.75-0.5\beta)*\beta}.

3
One-shot versus sequential persuasion

This section contains the main results of the paper – we show that in many of the classic environments studied in the literature sequential persuasion outperforms single-stage persuasion. We start by recalling the characterization of the highest achievable payoff for S with one-shot persuasion from
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
. As mentioned above, an important observation of that paper is that the concavification method applies even when R is biased. Indeed, the maximization problem for S can be written in a similar way to (
1
) as follows:

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\tau}~~\sum_{\mu\in T}\tau(\mu)\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu}[v(\omega,\hat{a}(D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})))]

(3)

\displaystyle\textit{s.t. }\sum_{\mu\in T}\tau(\mu)\mu=\mu_{0}.

Therefore, if we define
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
by
\hat{v}_{\alpha}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu}[v(\omega,\hat{a}(D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})))]
then the problem for S becomes just like a standard persuasion problem with the function
\hat{v}
replaced by
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
.

The key to the applicability of concavification as in (
3
) is that R’s bias ‘systematically distorts updated beliefs’. Namely, the Bayesian posterior
\mu
held by S uniquely pins down the biased belief
D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})
held by R, and hence also pins down the action that R takes at this belief. In contrast, with sequential persuasion this is no longer true, as there can be different sequences of signals that lead to the same Bayesian posterior
\mu
for S but to different biased posteriors for R. We have already seen that in Example
1
: The sequences of signals
(x^{1},x^{0})
and
(x^{0},x^{1})
both result in the same Bayesian posterior of
\mu=0.5
, but R’s beliefs differ after these two realizations (see Figure
2
).
8
8

8

Hoffman et al. 2011
experimentally show that posteriors change depending on the order in which signals are presented.
If R’s optimal action at the posterior induced by
(x^{1},x^{0})
is different than at the posterior induced by
(x^{0},x^{1})
, then we wouldn’t be able to neatly characterize the optimal payoff for S as in (
3
). We document this observation in the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

With sequential persuasion there can be different sequences of signal realizations that lead to the same Bayesian posterior
\mu
for S but to different biased posteriors for R. Thus, R’s bias does not systematically distort updated beliefs.

The failure of systematic beliefs distortion makes it challenging to find optimal sequential persuasion strategies. Nevertheless, by focusing on specific classes of environments we can show that the ability to send multiple signals is often beneficial to S. For a given prior
\mu_{0}
and bias parameter
\alpha
, we say that
S gains from sequential persuasion
if there exists a sequential persuasion strategy
\sigma
that yields a strictly higher payoff for S than
V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})
given in (
3
).

3.1
Common preferences

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that S and R have the same preferences,
u(\omega,a)=v(\omega,a)
for all
\omega
and
a
. Then S gains from sequential persuasion if and only if

V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})<\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}\mu_{0}(\omega)\max_{a}\{v(\omega,a)\}.

(4)

The right-hand side of (
4
) is the expected payoff for S (and R) under full information; by repeatedly sending fully revealing signals S can achieve a payoff arbitrarily close to full information, which implies that (
4
) is sufficient for S to gain from sequential persuasion. Conversely, it is not hard to show that the payoff from any sequential persuasion strategy is bounded above by the full information payoff, so this inequality is also necessary.

While Proposition
1
provides a characterization, checking whether (
4
) holds requires solving for
V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})
, which may not be easy. The next corollary provides a simple sufficient condition for S to gain from sequential persuasion. Let
F(\mu_{0},\alpha)=\{\mu^{\prime}\in\Delta(\Omega):\mu^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mu_{0}\}
be the set of feasible biased posteriors given prior
\mu_{0}
and bias
\alpha
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose that S and R have the same preferences. If there exists a state
\bar{\omega}
such that for all
\mu\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)

\argmax_{a}u(\bar{\omega},a)\bigcap\argmax_{a}\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu}[u(\omega,a)]=\emptyset,

(5)

then S gains from sequential persuasion. If
|\Omega|=2
then (
5
) is also necessary for S to gain from sequential persuasion.

3.2
Crawford-Sobel preferences

The next proposition concerns the classic environment analyzed by
Crawford and Sobel 1982
.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
A=\mathbb{R}
,
u(\omega,a)=-(a-\omega)^{2}
, and
v(\omega,a)=-(a-(\omega+b))^{2}
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then S gains from sequential persuasion.

In the proof of this result we first show that the function
\hat{v}_{\alpha}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu}[v(\omega,\hat{a}(D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})))]
used to find the optimal one-shot persuasion strategy is convex, and hence that it is optimal to fully reveal the state.
9
9

9

Kamenica and Gentzkow 2009
consider the same environment without the bias and show that
\hat{v}(\mu)
is convex.
If the bias
b
is positive, and if the state turns out to be high, then S can increase their payoff by sending a second fully revealing signal that further pushes up R’s posterior and induces a higher action; for a negative
b
the same argument applies when the state is low.

3.3
Transparent motives

Following
Lipnowski and Ravid 2020
, we say that S has transparent motives if
v
depends only on the action taken by R and not on the state. With transparent motives we write
v(a)
for the utility of S when R takes action
a
. Notice that in this case the objective in (
3
) is simplified to
\sum_{\mu\in T}\tau(\mu)v(\hat{a}(D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})))
. Furthermore, by a ‘change of variable’
\mu^{\prime}=D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})
, and using Lemma
1
, we can rewrite the maximization problem (
3
) as the problem of choosing a distribution of
biased
posteriors:

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\tau^{\prime}}~~\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}}\tau^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})v(\hat{a}(\mu^{\prime}))

(6)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}}\tau^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{0},

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mu_{0}~~~\forall\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}.

Since the feasible set in (
6
) decreases (in the sense of inclusion) as
\alpha
increases, we immediately get the following.

Corollary 2
.

With transparent motives
V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})
weakly decreases in
\alpha
; in particular, when R is Bayesian (
\alpha=0
) the payoff for S is weakly higher than when R is biased (
\alpha>0
).

The key to Corollary
2
is that conservative Bayesianism preserves the martingale property of Bayesian updating, so that any distribution over actions that can be induced with a biased receiver can also be induced with an unbiased one. However, with sequential persuasion S and R would typically hold different beliefs after the first period, and this may allow S to achieve action distributions that were not feasible in the Bayesian case and potentially increase their payoff. The following example illustrates this point and therefore shows that Corollary
2
doesn’t hold under sequential persuasion.

Example 2
.

Let
\Omega=\{0,1\}
and
A=[0,1]
. The payoff for R is
u(\omega,a)=-(a-\omega)^{2}
, and the payoff for S is
v(a)=a
. Let
\mu
denote the belief that the state is
\omega=1
and suppose the common prior is
\mu_{0}=0.5
.

The optimal action for R given belief
\mu
is
a=\mu
. Therefore, in the standard Bayesian persuasion problem (
1
), the value function for S is given by
\hat{v}(\mu)=\mu
. Since
\hat{v}
is linear, S can’t benefit from persuasion; in fact, the expected payoff for S is always 0.5 regardless of what information they provide.

Suppose now that R is biased with
\alpha=0.5
. Note that, with a single period, S can’t benefit from persuasion (as we know from Corollary
2
). Consider the following 2-period sequential strategy: In the first period the information structure fully reveals the state; in the second period, if the state was revealed to be
\omega=0
then no additional information is provided; and if the state was revealed to be
\omega=1
then another fully revealing signal is sent.

Let us calculate the distribution over posterior beliefs of R from the point of view of S. With probability 0.5 the state is initially revealed to be
\omega=0
, so the posterior of R is
\mu=1/4
and the process stops; and with probability 0.5 the state is initially revealed to be
\omega=1
, which leads to a posterior of
\mu=3/4
for R after the first period; then, in the second period, the state will for sure be revealed to be
\omega=1
again, so the posterior of R would be
\mu=7/8
. Overall, the posterior distribution is uniform on the points
\mu=1/4
and
\mu=7/8
. The payoff of S is
0.5*1/4+0.5*7/8>0.5
.
10
10

10

Of course S can continue to send fully revealing signals when the state is
\omega=1
, and the posterior of R would converge to
\mu=1
. The limit payoff for S is
0.5*1/4+0.5*1=5/8
. It turns out that this is not the optimal sequential persuasion strategy and S can achieve an even higher payoff (we do not know what is the best possible payoff).

∎

Besides showing that S would rather interact with a biased receiver than with a Bayesian one, in Example
2
it is also the case that S gains from sequential persuasion. The following proposition shows that this is generally true in this type of environments.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
for some
k\geq 1
and that
Conv(\Omega)
is a set of full dimension. Suppose further that
A=\mathbb{R}^{k}
and that
\hat{a}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim\mu}[\omega]
for every
\mu\in\Delta(\Omega)
. If S has transparent motives and
v(a)=\beta\cdot a
for some non-zero vector
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
then S gains from sequential persuasion.

Finally, another type of environments with transparent motives that received much attention is when there are two actions and S needs to provide information in order to persuade R to take their preferred one. The next result concerns such environments.

Proposition 4
.

Consider an environment with two actions
A=\{a,a^{\prime}\}
and where S has transparent motives with
v(a)>v(a^{\prime})
. If
\hat{a}(\mu_{0})=a^{\prime}
, and if there is some interior
\mu
such that
\hat{a}(\mu)=a
, then S gains from sequential persuasion.

4
Biased sender

So far we have assumed that S properly updates beliefs in a Bayesian manner while R is biased. In this section we consider the case where S and R are both biased in the same way, and moreover use the same sequential updating procedure when receiving multiple signals. Our motivation to consider this scenario is that S and R may in fact be the same agent whose preferences change between the time of information choice and the time of action choice. For example, certain models of rational inattention can be viewed as a Bayesian persuasion game in which sender’s preferences are affected by the cost of information and are hence different than those of the receiver.
11
11

11

See for example the discussion in
Caplin et al. 2019
.
Under such interpretation, it is natural that S and R share the same updating rule but have different maximizing objectives.

Using Lemma
1
, the value for (the biased) S with one period of persuasion can be characterized by considering all possible distributions over biased posteriors induced by a single information structure:

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}^{Biased}(\mu_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\max_{\tau^{\prime}}~~\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}}\tau^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})\hat{v}(\mu^{\prime})

(7)

s.t.

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}}\tau^{\prime}(\mu^{\prime})\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{0},

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mu_{0}~~~\forall\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}.

Notice that the above program is identical to the classic version in (
1
), except that here we have the additional feasibility constraint
\mu^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mu_{0}
for the biased posteriors in the support
T^{\prime}
.

Next, let us denote by
V_{\alpha}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})
the supremum of the set of achievable expected payoffs for S when using sequential persuasion strategies
\sigma
(of any finite length). Since the Bayes plausibility constraint is satisfied at any stage of the persuasion process, it follows that the final distribution of posteriors induced by any
\sigma
is Bayes plausible. Therefore, it is clear that
V_{\alpha}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})\leq V(\mu_{0})
, i.e., the expected utility of S is bounded above by their expected utility in the standard case where both agents are Bayesian; the following result shows that typically these two numbers are in fact equal.

Proposition 5
.

Fix
\mu_{0}
and suppose that there is an interior
\tau^{*}
that solves (
1
). Then for every
\alpha\in[0,1)
we have that

V_{\alpha}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})=V(\mu_{0}).

The proof of Proposition
5
explicitly constructs a sequential persuasion policy whose value converges to
V(\mu_{0})
as the number of periods increases. The assumption that there exists an interior solution
\tau^{*}
to the Bayesian problem is necessary for the result to hold in general, but is redundant if
\hat{v}
is continuous on the boundary of
\Delta(\Omega)
. The proposition implies that the concavification of
\hat{v}
is still useful in calculating the optimal value for S and the ultimate posterior distribution. It also implies that we should expect to see sequential information provision whenever the addition of the constraints
\mu^{\prime}\geq\alpha\mu_{0}
makes the classic Bayesian persuasion solution infeasible.

5
Discussion

5.1
Other sequential updating procedures

Our view is that the PbP-N procedure assumed thus far is best in capturing the spirit of biased updating and of the fact that information arrives sequentially over time: The prior of R at the beginning of each period reflects the information accumulated in previous periods, and this prior is the only statistic needed to calculate the next posterior. However, there are other natural ways to extend a biased updating rule from static to dynamic setups; in this section we briefly discuss two such alternatives.

Consider first the following procedure which we refer to as
Last-Period bias (LP)
: R updates their belief in a Bayesian manner along the entire sequence of signals; if the final Bayesian posterior is
\mu
, then their biased posterior is
D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})
. To illustrate, in the environment of Example
1
, if the signal
x^{1}
is observed in both periods then the Bayesian posterior is
\mu=\frac{\beta^{2}}{\beta^{2}+(1-\beta)^{2}}
; under LP the biased posterior is therefore

0.5*0.5+0.5*\frac{\beta^{2}}{\beta^{2}+(1-\beta)^{2}}.

Since with Bayesian updating the set of feasible posterior distributions under sequential persuasion is the same as under one-shot persuasion, it immediately follows that with the LP procedure S never gains from sequential persuasion; the LP procedure essentially ignores the dynamic nature of sender’s persuasion strategy and hence makes it irrelevant. It follows that the maximal payoff for S can be calculated using concavification as in (
3
) and is equal to
V_{\alpha}(\mu_{0})
.

Second, consider the
Period-by-Period bias relative to Original prior (PbP-O)
procedure: The bias is applied in every period, but the anchor for the bias is the original prior
\mu_{0}
from the beginning of the process rather than the last period posterior. Formally,
\mu_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})
, where
\mu
is the Bayesian posterior calculated from the prior
\mu_{n-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
and the realized signal
x_{n}
. Thus, this procedure assigns greater prominence to the initial belief
\mu_{0}
and makes it harder for S to induce extreme posteriors. Note that if in a certain period the realized signal keeps the (Bayesian) belief unchanged, the bias would cause the posterior to drift back towards
\mu_{0}
.

Going back to Example
1
, and again assuming that
x^{1}
is observed in both periods, we have that the biased posterior after the first period is

\mu_{1}(x^{1})=0.5*\mu_{0}+0.5*\beta=0.25+0.5\beta.

Therefore, the Bayesian posterior after the second signal is
\mu=\frac{(0.25+0.5\beta)*\beta}{(0.25+0.5\beta)*\beta+(0.75-0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)}
, and the final biased posterior is now

\mu_{2}(x^{1},x^{1})=0.5*\mu_{0}+0.5*\frac{(0.25+0.5\beta)*\beta}{(0.25+0.5\beta)*\beta+(0.75-0.5\beta)*(1-\beta)}.

It is straightforward to check that under PbP-O the conclusions of Lemma
2
remain true: There are different sequences of signal realizations that lead to the same Bayesian posterior but to different biased posteriors, so the bias does not systematically distort updated beliefs. Hence, the concavification method is not applicable here as well.

Under PbP-O, Proposition
1
(and Corollary
1
) are no longer true since even if fully revealing signals are sent repeatedly the posterior of R does not converge to the vertices of
\Delta(\Omega)
. Thus, in general S cannot achieve the full-information payoff under common preferences. Propositions
2
and
4
also don’t hold as stated, as the ability of S to gain from sequential persuasion depends on the specifics of these environments. Proposition
3
is true, although the sequential persuasion strategy in its proof needs to be modified. Overall, the power of sequential persuasion is often reduced with the PbP-O procedure relative to PbP-N.

Finally, suppose as in Section
4
above that S is biased in the same way as R and that both use the PbP-O procedure. Let
V_{\alpha,PbP-O}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})
be the supremum of the set of achievable expected payoffs for S when using sequential persuasion strategies. The next result is the analogue to Proposition
5
. Recall that
F(\mu_{0},\alpha)=\{\mu\in\Delta(\Omega):\mu\geq\alpha\mu_{0}\}
.

Proposition 6
.

For any
\mu_{0}
and
\alpha\in[0,1)
,

V_{\alpha,PbP-O}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})=\sup_{\mu\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)}\{\hat{v}(\mu)\}

The set
F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
contains all those beliefs that can be reached from the prior
\mu_{0}
by some signal after the bias is applied. Since after each period of persuasion the posterior is pulled back towards
\mu_{0}
, it is clear that the belief can never leave this set and thus that
\sup_{\mu\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)}\{\hat{v}(\mu)\}
is an upper bound on the utility of S. The proposition claims that S can achieve (or get arbitrarily close to) this upper bound. The key difference between PbP-O and PbP-N when S is biased is that under PbP-N the random posterior is a martingale throughout the persuasion process, while under PbP-O it is not. Indeed, if the realized posterior after the first signal is
\mu
, then the average posterior after the second signal would be
\alpha\mu_{0}+(1-\alpha)\mu
. It is therefore possible that
V_{\alpha,PbP-O}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})>V(\mu_{0})
(see, e.g., Example
2
). The converse inequality
V_{\alpha,PbP-O}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})<V(\mu_{0})
is also possible, for instance this would be the case whenever
\hat{v}
is strictly convex and symmetric.

5.2
Other biases

A natural direction for future work is to explore other types of updating biases to understand whether and when S gains from sequential persuasion. In a recent paper,
Whitmeyer 2024
proves that any distortion of Bayes rule (within the class of biases considered by
De Clippel and Zhang 2022
) causes violations of monotonicity with respect to the Blackwell order in some decision problem.
12
12

12

Thus, even when S and R have identical preferences they will sometime disagree on the ranking of experiments due to the difference in updating.
It would be interesting to know whether a similar characterization of Bayes rule can be obtained using sequential persuasion: Are there any updating rules, besides proper Bayesianism, for which S never (in any persuasion environment) gains from sequential persuasion?

∎

We can now prove the proposition. Let
\tau^{\prime}
be an optimal distribution over biased posteriors that S can induce with one period of information, i.e.,
\tau^{\prime}
maximizes the probability that
a
is chosen. We construct a sequential strategy
\sigma
that achieves a strictly higher payoff for S than the payoff under
\tau^{\prime}
: In the first period
\tau^{\prime}
is induced; if the realized posterior
\mu^{\prime}
satisfies
\hat{a}(\mu^{\prime})=a
then the strategy terminates; and if the realized posterior
\mu^{\prime}
satisfies
\hat{a}(\mu^{\prime})=a^{\prime}
then we apply the strategy constructed in Lemma
4
.

To show that
\sigma
improves the payoff for S it is enough to argue that
\hat{a}(\mu^{\prime})=a^{\prime}
for some
\mu^{\prime}\in T^{\prime}
(the support of
\tau^{\prime}
). But this is an immediate consequence from our assumption that
\hat{a}(\mu_{0})=a^{\prime}
, from the fact that the set of beliefs at which
a
is optimal is convex, and from the Bayes plausibility of
\tau^{\prime}
(recall Lemma
1
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
5

Let
\tau^{*}
be an interior solution to (
1
). Denote the support of
\tau^{*}
by
T^{*}=\{\mu_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mu_{M}^{*}\}
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\{\mu_{1}^{*},\ldots,\mu_{M}^{*}\}
are affinely independent (e.g.,
Lipnowski and Mathevet 2017
). Thus, each
\mu\in Conv(T^{*})
can be uniquely represented as a convex combination of elements in
T^{*}
, namely
\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}(\mu)\mu_{i}^{*}
.

Given some
\mu\in Conv(T^{*})
, let
R(\mu)=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}:\mu_{i}^{*}\geq\alpha\mu\}
and
UR(\mu)=\{1,\ldots,M\}\setminus R(\mu)
. In words, we divide the beliefs in
T^{*}
to those that are Reachable from
\mu
(the set
R(\mu)
) and those that are UnReachable (
UR(\mu)
).

We construct a sequential persuasion strategy under which the expected payoff of S converges to
V(\mu_{0})
as
N
grows. The strategy is ‘Markovian’ in the sense that in each period the information structure depends only on the current belief and not on how they arrived at this belief. Furthermore, the posterior never leaves the set
Conv(T^{*})
. Thus, we describe the strategy by specifying a Markov chain on
Conv(T^{*})
.

Given the current period belief
\mu\in Conv(T^{*})
, the belief in the next round is determined as follows. If
\mu\in T^{*}
then the strategy stops; otherwise, one of the indices from
\{1,\ldots,M\}
is randomly drawn, where the probability of each
i
is given by

\displaystyle Pr(i)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(\mu)}{1-\alpha\sum_{j\in R(\mu)}\beta_{j}(\mu)}&\textit{ if }~~i\in R(\mu),\\
\frac{\beta_{i}(\mu)}{1-\alpha\sum_{j\in R(\mu)}\beta_{j}(\mu)}&\textit{ if }~~i\in UR(\mu).\end{array}\right.

If the realized index
i
is in
R(\mu)
then the next posterior is
\mu_{i}^{*}
; and if
i\in UR(\mu)
then the posterior is
\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}^{*}
.

We first check using Lemma
1
that the resulting posterior distribution can be induced by some information structure. For condition (i) in the lemma we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in R(\mu)}\frac{(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(\mu)}{1-\alpha\sum_{j\in R(\mu)}\beta_{j}(\mu)}\mu_{i}^{*}+\sum_{i\in UR(\mu)}\frac{\beta_{i}(\mu)}{1-\alpha\sum_{j\in R(\mu)}\beta_{j}(\mu)}(\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}^{*})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\alpha\sum_{j\in R(\mu)}\beta_{j}(\mu)}\left[(1-\alpha)\sum_{i\in R(\mu)}\beta_{i}(\mu)\mu_{i}^{*}+\alpha\left(\sum_{i\in UR(\mu)}\beta_{i}(\mu)\right)\mu+(1-\alpha)\sum_{i\in UR(\mu)}\beta_{i}(\mu)\mu_{i}^{*}\right]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\alpha\sum_{j\in R(\mu)}\beta_{j}(\mu)}\left[(1-\alpha)+\alpha\left(\sum_{i\in UR(\mu)}\beta_{i}(\mu)\right)\right]\mu=\mu,

as needed. For condition (ii), if
i\in R(\mu)
then by definition
\mu_{i}^{*}\geq\alpha\mu
, and if
i\in UR(\mu)
then clearly
\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}^{*}\geq\alpha\mu
.

Next, notice that the above transition rule indeed maps points in
Conv(T^{*})
to distributions over
Conv(T^{*})
, since for any current belief
\mu
the next period belief is either in
T^{*}
or it is a convex combination of
\mu
and a point in
T^{*}
.

Let
\tilde{\mu}_{n}
be the (random) posterior of R after the
n
th period. The heart of the proof is the following lemma, which claims that the probability of reaching the absorbing set
T^{*}
within a fixed number of periods is uniformly bounded from below.

Lemma 5
.

There is a positive integer
\bar{k}
and a
\bar{\delta}>0
such that
Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{n+\bar{k}}\in T^{*}|\tilde{\mu}_{n}=\mu)\geq\bar{\delta}
for every
\mu\in Conv(T^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
C=\min_{\omega\in\Omega}\min_{i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}}\mu_{i}^{*}(\omega)
. Our assumption that
T^{*}
is interior guarantees that
C>0
. Let
\bar{k}
be sufficiently large so that
\frac{\alpha^{\bar{k}}}{1-\alpha+\alpha^{\bar{k}}}<C
. We claim that for any starting belief
\mu\in Conv(T^{*})
, if some index
i
is selected
\bar{k}
times consecutively then the posterior would be at the absorbing belief
\mu_{i}^{*}
after these
\bar{k}
periods. Indeed, if
i
is selected
k>0
times and
\mu_{i}^{*}
is UnReachable in all these periods then the final posterior is
\alpha^{k}\mu+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu_{i}^{*}
. For
\mu_{i}^{*}
to be Reachable from this posterior we need that
\mu_{i}^{*}\geq\alpha[\alpha^{k}\mu+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu_{i}^{*}]
, which is the same as
\frac{\mu_{i}^{*}(\omega)}{\mu(\omega)}\geq\frac{\alpha^{k+1}}{1-\alpha+\alpha^{k+1}}
for every
\omega\in\Omega
. Since by definition
\frac{\mu_{i}^{*}(\omega)}{\mu(\omega)}\geq\mu_{i}^{*}(\omega)\geq C
, after at most
\bar{k}-1
periods
\mu_{i}^{*}
would be Reachable; if
i
is selected again then
\mu_{i}^{*}
would be reached.

Thus, to prove the lemma it is enough to show that there is
\bar{\delta}>0
such that for any initial
\mu
the probability that some
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
is selected
\bar{k}
times consecutively is at least
\bar{\delta}
. Note that the probability that
i
is selected at
\mu
is at least
(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(\mu)
. Moreover, if
i\in UR(\mu)
and
i
is selected then in the next period we would have
\beta_{i}(\alpha\mu+(1-\alpha)\mu_{i}^{*})=\alpha\beta_{i}(\mu)+(1-\alpha)\geq\beta_{i}(\mu)
, so the probability that
i
would be selected in the next period is again at least
(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(\mu)
. By applying this argument to
\bar{k}
consecutive periods we have that the probability that
i
is selected in all of them starting from
\mu
is at least
[(1-\alpha)\beta_{i}(\mu)]^{\bar{k}}
. Finally, since for any
\mu
the numbers
\{\beta_{i}(\mu)\}_{i}
are non-negative and sum up to one, there exists an index
i
such that
\beta_{i}(\mu)\geq\frac{1}{M}
. Setting
\bar{\delta}=\left[\frac{1-\alpha}{M}\right]^{\bar{k}}
completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We can now complete the proof of the proposition. It follows from Lemma
5
that by following the above strategy for
N
periods guarantees that the probability of not reaching
T^{*}
is at most
Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}\notin T^{*})\leq(1-\bar{\delta})^{\left\lfloor{\frac{N}{\bar{k}}}\right\rfloor}
. In addition, we have that

\displaystyle\mu_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mu}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}=\mu)\mu=

\displaystyle Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}\in T^{*})\sum_{i=1}^{M}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}=\mu_{i}^{*}~|~\tilde{\mu}_{N}\in T^{*})\mu_{i}^{*}+Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}\notin T^{*})\sum_{\mu\notin T^{*}}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}=\mu~|~\tilde{\mu}_{N}\notin T^{*})\mu,

Taking the limit as
N\to\infty
gives

\mu_{0}=\lim_{N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}=\mu_{i}^{*}~|~\tilde{\mu}_{N}\in T^{*})\mu_{i}^{*}.

Since the mapping
\mu\mapsto(\beta_{1}(\mu),\ldots,\beta_{M}(\mu))
is continuous, it follows that for each
i=1,\ldots,M

\lim_{N}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}=\mu_{i}^{*}~|~\tilde{\mu}_{N}\in T^{*})=\beta_{i}(\mu_{0})=\tau^{*}(\mu_{i}^{*}).

Hence,

\lim_{N}\sum_{i=1}^{M}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}=\mu_{i}^{*}~|~\tilde{\mu}_{N}\in T^{*})\hat{v}(\mu_{i}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tau^{*}(\mu_{i}^{*})\hat{v}(\mu_{i}^{*})=V(\mu_{0}).

Combined with
\lim_{N}Pr(\tilde{\mu}_{N}\in T^{*})=1
, the last equation completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
6

Fix
\mu_{0}
and
\alpha
. For any
\mu\in\Delta(\Omega)
we have that
D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})=\alpha\mu_{0}+(1-\alpha)\mu\geq\alpha\mu_{0}
, implying that
D_{\alpha}(\mu;\mu_{0})\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
. Therefore, regardless of what sequence of signals R observes, their final posterior would be in the set
F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
. This shows that
V_{\alpha,PbP-O}^{Biased-Seq}(\mu_{0})\leq\sup_{\mu\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)}\hat{v}(\mu)
.

Next, fix some
\mu^{*}\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
and
\epsilon>0
. We construct a sequential persuasion strategy which gives S a payoff of at least
\hat{v}(\mu^{*})-\epsilon
. Since
\mu^{*}\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
and
\epsilon>0
are arbitrary this would suffice to prove the theorem.

If
\mu^{*}=\mu_{0}
then not sending any information achieves
\hat{v}(\mu^{*})
as needed. Suppose then that
\mu^{*}\neq\mu_{0}
. Let
\bar{\gamma}>1
be such that
\mu_{*}:=\bar{\gamma}\mu_{0}+(1-\bar{\gamma})\mu^{*}\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
; since
\mu_{0}
is in the interior of
F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
such
\bar{\gamma}
exists. Define
t_{1}=\frac{\bar{\gamma}-1}{\bar{\gamma}}
and
t_{2}=\alpha t_{1}
. It is immediate to check that
t_{1},t_{2}\in(0,1)
and that

\displaystyle t_{1}\mu^{*}+(1-t_{1})\mu_{*}=\mu_{0}\,,

(10a)

\displaystyle t_{2}\mu^{*}+(1-t_{2})\mu_{*}=\alpha\mu_{0}+(1-\alpha)\mu_{*}\,.

(10b)

In the first period S induces the posterior distribution that assigns probability
t_{1}
to
\mu^{*}
and probability
1-t_{1}
to
\mu_{*}
. Since
\mu^{*},\mu_{*}\in F(\mu_{0},\alpha)
and satisfy (
10a
) it follows from Lemma
1
that this is a feasible posterior distribution.

In any subsequent period, if the realized posterior is
\mu^{*}
then the strategy terminates; if the realized posterior is
\mu_{*}
then additional signal is sent that induces posterior
\mu^{*}
with probability
t_{2}
and the posterior
\mu_{*}
with probability
1-t_{2}
; Equation (
10b
) implies that this is a feasible distribution.

Under the above sequential persuasion strategy the probability that the posterior is NOT
\mu^{*}
after
N
periods is
(1-t_{1})(1-t_{2})^{N-1}
. Therefore, the expected utility of S after
N
periods of persuasion is

(1-(1-t_{1})(1-t_{2})^{N-1})\hat{v}(\mu^{*})+(1-t_{1})(1-t_{2})^{N-1}\hat{v}(\mu_{*}),

and for sufficiently large
N
this is greater than
\hat{v}(\mu^{*})-\epsilon
.
∎
