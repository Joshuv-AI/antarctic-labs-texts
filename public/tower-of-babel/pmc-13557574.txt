Potential remote measurement of C-peptide using a minimally invasive and convenient sampling technique
Authors: Kathryn Duschean, Nina Vujovic, Emad Dumar, Lili Yu, Fedelis Mutiso, Allison B. Goldfine, Arkady Gusev, Dmitri Mikhailov, Arvind Kinhikar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn clinical research, alternatives to venipuncture can reduce participant burden and increase accessibility and sampling opportunities for biomarkers. Residual pancreatic beta-cell function is assessed in clinical research and in diabetes care using fasting and stimulated C-peptide measurements, and changes are assessed over time. Operational, technical, and acceptability considerations of utilizing Tasso+ SST devices for C-peptide collection compared to venipuncture-derived serum samples were investigated.MethodologySamples were collected from prospectively enrolled healthy participants via Third Generation Tasso+ SST micro-sampling devices and phlebotomy. Eleven fasted Tasso+ samples collected on-site or remote underwent alternative handling conditions and were evaluated for consistency, quality, and stability. Participants responded to device acceptability questionnaires.ResultsThe Tasso+ SST micro-sampling device showed excellent concordance with simultaneous venipuncture-derived fasted C-Peptide, with concentration being largely conserved in healthy volunteers despite alternative sample handling. Delayed centrifugation over 72 hours caused no consequential degradation of C-peptide when kept at 4°C. Questionnaire responses indicate acceptable device usability and associated comfort levels.ConclusionFasting C-peptide levels can be accurately collected using micro-sampling collection techniques. Tasso+ C-peptide stability is seen when kept at 4°C. Micro-sampling devices can be used with shipping mechanisms (without centrifugation) to decrease participant burden, expand trial populations, and aid biomarker measurements.

Introduction

Collection of blood is an important component of biomedical research, including clinical trial design and execution. Laboratory assessments from potential trial participants inform inclusion/exclusion selection for trial involvement, and ongoing collections provide key data for safety, tolerability, and efficacy of novel drug therapies. Clinical research has expanded to include more complex and higher total numbers of endpoints and procedures, which often translates to increased blood volume requirements. Such incorporations create complex clinical research studies, which tend to suffer lower willingness to participate and insufficient recruitment/retention rates, negatively impacting data integrity, and statistical power [1]. Trials with high participant burden may also have decreased referral rates, narrower eligibility requirements, and more frequent amendments [1] and 6–29% of clinical trials are terminated due to insufficient recruitment, slowing medical progress [2]. This rise in participant burden exposes the critical need for remote sampling options and/or decentralized trial capabilities.

C-peptide as biomarker of interest

C-peptide (connecting peptide) levels have been used to assess pancreatic beta-cell function and differentiate endogenous insulin production from exogenous insulin administered in those who utilize insulin therapy. The hormone insulin is transcribed in the pancreatic beta-cell as a preprohormone from a single chain mRNA precursor. The preprohormone undergoes folding, formation of disulfide bridges, and is then cleaved in the endoplasmic reticulum to form α and β chains of proinsulin, and the subsequent cleavage product; C-peptide [3]. Once formed, C-peptide is secreted from pancreatic beta-cells in the insulin granule in a 1:1 molar ratio with insulin. This secretion in equimolar concentration of C-peptide and insulin occurs following stimulation of the pancreatic beta cells by glucose and other metabolic secretagogues. C-peptide does not undergo extensive first pass hepatic extraction and is primarily metabolized renally. This maintains a slower elimination half-life of approximately 30 minutes, compared to approximately 5 minutes for insulin [4], and levels are more similar in the portal and systemic circulations as opposed to insulin which is higher in the portal space. Thus, C-peptide is regularly favored over insulin as a surrogate diabetes-related biomarker, and it enables the validation of residual insulin production in beta-cells, including patients utilizing exogenous insulin.

The physiological concentration of C-peptide in a healthy individual in fasted state ranges from 0.9 to 1.8 ng/mL (0.3 nmol/L to 0.6 nmol/L) and increases to 3 to 9 ng/mL (1 nmol/L to 3 nmol/L) post-prandially [3]. High C-peptide levels have been observed in defined conditions including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, kidney disease, and (rarely) insulinoma. Low levels, particularly below 0.6 ng/mL (or 0.2 nmol/L), support the diagnosis of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) [3]. As such, C-peptide has become a relevant analyte for testing, diagnosis of type of diabetes, and monitoring of residual beta-cell function.

C-peptide assessment after an 8–10 hour fast is preferred in clinical practice, but it may also be measured under non-fasting conditions. The value associated with fasting C-peptide is often used to assess basal beta-cell function, particularly in patients utilizing exogenous insulin. Additional endocrine conditions that may mimic or contain components of C-peptide/insulin resistance can also be investigated using this process and marker. The monitoring of this residual beta-cell function over time can require frequent or repeated testing.

C-peptide can also be measured following stimulation, such as post-prandially, as seen during an oral glucose or mixed meal tolerance test (OGTT or MMTT). The multiple samples and subsequent values obtained following a nutrient load regularly require an intravenous catheter to avoid multiple short-timed venipuncture collections. In each of these clinical situations, the repeated testing required is often challenging, especially in pediatric or other specialty populations. Thus, capillary micro-sampling may improve patient experience for the multiple basal and stimulated sample collections required for one or repeat proactive testing.

Utility of decentralized sampling

Patients with diabetes, or related conditions, would benefit from minimally invasive, patient-centric sampling techniques usable for remote sample collection and clinical research involvement. These patients may readily accept micro-sampling approaches, as they are essentially an additional method of capillary testing with which this population is already familiar.

Furthermore, the addition of capillary micro-sampling into clinical trials may lower trial budget via improved enrollment efficiency and reduced clinic visit frequency, leading to the retention of those participants who best meet the criteria earlier on in the recruitment process [5]. The use of novel micro-sampling devices has been well established and offers several advantages. These advantages include pain mitigation, lowering fear, and/or limiting discomfort associated with traditional phlebotomy. All of this while achieving required blood volumes for trial inclusion or maintenance testing, minimization of certain risks, and expansion of patient eligibility.

Limitations to transdermal collections do exist, such as risk of inadequate sample collection/device failure, challenges with integration into analytical lab workflow, costs of device kits/shipping, and tight limits on blood volume/number of tests that can be run on each sample. These limitations may be offset by the advantage of fewer clinic visits, and decreased burden for participants or patients with complex medical issues, such as pediatrics/geriatrics, patients with bleeding disorders, or those with multiple comorbid conditions. The advancement of newer generations of capillary micro-sampling collection devices may address many of the above limitations. This makes the use of such devices appealing to multiple medical conditions and areas of research.

Micro-sampling device

A micro-sampling capillary device currently being tested is the 510k Class II Lancet-Cleared and CE-marked Tasso + Device, with attached Serum Separating Tube (SST) and manufactured by Tasso Inc. (Seattle, WA, USA.) The Tasso+ is a self-contained transdermal blood collection device and kit that can collect up to 600 µL of capillary blood from a donor in a single application. These single-use, sterile devices include easy step-by-step instructions that can be utilized in remote conditions by laypeople with minimal training and allow for relatively painless blood draws. Henceforth, this paper shall refer to the Tasso+ device as “TMD” (transdermal micro-sampling device), unless otherwise required in context.

At the time of the experiment described, Tasso Inc. had two forms of collection: a liquid blood collection device (Tasso+) and a dry blood collection device (Tasso M20). The Tasso+ device for liquid blood collection has since been updated to TassoOne Plus. This paper specifically focuses on the Tasso+ device and its liquid whole-blood collection capabilities. Previous work with dried blood collection for C-peptide has been reported by others [6] and both Tasso devices have been FDA approved, and CE marked [7]. The collection, transportation, storage, and analysis of C-peptide in healthy volunteers serve as the basis for utilizing the Tasso+ device for prospective use in clinical research, trials, and patient monitoring.

Participants & methods

Study design

This study was a single-site, prospective investigation using the Tasso+ SST (Tasso Inc., WA, USA), in future use of this paper to be called TMD, to assess the utility and efficiency of serum separated (SST) C-peptide obtained via capillary micro-sampling collection for clinical research and trial capabilities, with a focus on remote testing capabilities and feasibility. This study focused on potential for device utilization to drive efficiency of participation and limiting unnecessary study participant burden. The study protocol, volunteer solicitation flyer, and informed consent were approved by the WIRB-Copernicus Group, Institutional Review Board (MA, USA) prior to enrollment.

Comparing C-peptide measurements from a TMD device (Tasso+) versus venous samples

This study focused on evaluating concordance of C-peptide measurements coming from a TMD (Tasso+) and venipuncture-derived samples, including evaluation of inter-device variability. To test this, 11 consented participants were instructed to fast overnight before each of two morning in-clinic visits, occurring between 7:00 and 10:00 am. During each visit, a site nurse obtained verbal confirmation from each participant that fasting instructions were observed prior to collections.

During Visit 1, understanding the correlation between a TMD (Tasso+) and venous collected samples was the primary objective. Furthermore, inter-device variability within the same participant at any given time was evaluated. For Visit 1, following confirmation of fasting, three TMDs (Tasso+) and one venipuncture sample were collected from all participants. The TMD (Tasso+) samples collected included 1 sample on the upper right arm, 1 sample on the lower right, and 1 sample on the upper left. All TMD (Tasso+) and the single venipuncture samples were collected within 2 to 12 minutes of each other. Of the three TMD (Tasso+) derived samples, all were processed the same way; samples were left upright, allowing for at least 30 minutes to clot (as confirmed by visual inspection by site personnel). Successful clotting was followed by centrifugation within 120 minutes of collection. Any samples not yet clotted by 30 minutes were monitored by site personnel, and spun once successful clotting was reached. All TMD (Tasso+) samples then underwent separation, storage at 4°C for 72 hours, and subsequent aliquoting of serum supernatant into 80 µL aliquots. Venipuncture samples also underwent centrifugation within 120 minutes of collection (and following at least 30 minutes to allow for clotting), separation, and subsequent aliquoting into 100 µL aliquots. This processing was consistent between Visit 1 and Visit 2. All aliquots were stored frozen at −80°C until they underwent C-peptide quantitation on the Abbott ARCHITECT C-peptide assay following manufacturer’s recommendations (Figure 1). Understanding sample stability

Stability analysis using visit 2 samples was performed only after confirmation from visit 1 sample analysis that there was no TMD (Tasso+) inter-device variability. Visit 2 was designed to help simulate remote collection capabilities, shipping, and storage conditions. As mentioned, visit 2 collected a standard venipuncture sample, along with three TMD (Tasso+) samples, with devices applied in the same array as visit 1.

Once collected, the TMD (Tasso+) devices were subjected to three alternative handling procedures and named based on their subsequent handling procedure. TMD (Tasso+) tube 1 was processed the same as the venipuncture Gold Standard sample: tubes were left upright and allowed to clot for a minimum of 30 minutes. Centrifugation then occurred within 120 minutes of collection, followed by aliquoting and storage at −80°C. This sample was denoted as T0h. TMD (Tasso+) tube 2 underwent centrifugation within 120 minutes of collection (following at least 30 minutes for clotting), then was stored at 4°C for 72 hours prior to aliquoting of serum for subsequent storage at −80°C. This second sample was denoted T72S. TMD (Tasso+) tube 3 was stored at 4°C for 72 h after collection, then centrifuged, followed by being aliquoted for storage at −80°C. Sample 3 was labeled as T72B.

All TMD (Tasso+) derived serum samples were aliquoted into 80 µL aliquots, while all venipuncture samples were aliquoted into 100 µL aliquots, with any remaining volume going into a final aliquot. All aliquots were stored at −80°C until undergoing C-peptide quantitation (Figure 2). Both visits required a participant questionnaire to be completed assessing device usability and comfortability of device use following collections/device applications by subjects. For further testing of remote collection feasibility, a single, off-site, remote collection was performed with 10 unfasted participants to validate shipping procedures and remote collection capabilities. Participants applied a single device to the back of an arm for sample collection, without medical assistance or oversight. Neither arm preference nor fasting requirement was specified. These participants also scheduled sample pick-up via pre-specified overnight courier and completed the participant questionnaire, providing feedback on use. Return address labels and instructions for shipping were provided to these participants, along with all TMD (Tasso+) kit components. Upon receipt from the participant, remotely collected samples were processed via centrifugation, then aliquoted into 80 µL aliquots and stored at −80°C until analysis.

It has been established that C-peptide in serum can remain stable for 24 hours at ambient temperature, and up to 5 days when refrigerated, following separation [8]. This knowledge helped to inform the design of this experiment. Additionally, typical transit time for remote collections takes 15–36 hours. The delayed centrifugation period of 72 hours was chosen in this setting to mimic unfavorable shipping conditions and timelines.

Sample collection

TMD (Tasso+) devices were supplied sterile, with manufacture-authored instructions, a heating pack, alcohol pad, and a bandage. The TMD (Tasso+) SST kit also included a resealable pouch for return, and the capabilities of attaching a return label, should a collection occur remotely or require shipment to an analytical facility, as applicable in the remote feasibility component of this experiment.

Prior to application and collection, the upper arm of each participant was warmed for 5 minutes with a heating pack. An alcohol pad was used for sterilization of the application site, and the skin was allowed to dry before a device was applied. Primary device applications in the two on-site visits occurred on the back of both upper arms; two devices applied to the right arm, approximately a few inches apart, one upper and one lower, and a single device applied to the left upper arm. Remote feasibility was assessed via a single device placed on the back of an unspecified upper arm. The TMD (Tasso+) device was applied following the removal of adhesive backing surrounding the device lancet on the back of the device. The device was activated via a firm press on the actuator, a prominent red button on the top of the TMD (Tasso+) device that was held until an audible click was heard, typically within 5 seconds. This click indicates the release of the microneedle into the skin to begin the collection process.

Following the depression of the button and subsequent microneedle deployment, a vacuum is created within the device, facilitating drawing of capillary blood into the attached SST tube [9]. Gravity assists in the flow of blood into the tube on the bottom of the device [9]. Once collection was completed, as indicated by sufficient volume, or a time of 5 minutes, whichever came first, the device was removed from the arm, and a bandage was applied to the area of collection. The SST tube was removed from the device, allowed to clot (for at least 30 minutes or until successful clotting was achieved by visual inspection), and processed using one of the three approaches outlined previously, depending on the visit.

A successful collection on the TMD (Tasso+) device was determined by an approximate volume of 400 µL of whole blood (or greater) collected over a maximum of 5 minutes. If any volume collected was below the minimum required value for analysis of C-peptide (320 μL), but non-negligible, the sample was preserved, and no further collection attempts were made. Anything below 320 µL was considered a failure. The minimum successful volume is indicated on the device collection tube with a graduated mark and provides 2 aliquots of 80 µL serum for a primary and a backup sample. If a device collected less than 320 µL in either experiment 1 or 2, site staff were instructed to attempt collection with a single additional device at a site on the arm approximately 1 inch away from original application site. No such placement requirements were specified outside of upper arm for the remote feasibility collection, and all samples were returned with minimum required volume or better.

C-peptide quantification

The Abbott ARCHITECT analytical platform used in this experiment is a Gold Standard instrument in the quantification of C-peptide. This instrument can quantitatively measure C-peptide in serum, plasma, and urine, with this team utilizing serum as the primary sample matrix.

The in-house Abbott ARCHITECT used for this experiment followed manufacturer’s recommendations for quantification of C-peptide. The ARCHITECT has a lower detection limit for C-peptide of 0.01 ng/mL, sufficient for a clinical diagnosis of insulin insufficiency as seen in T1DM (LLOQ = 0.06 ng/mL), and an upper limit of 30.00 ng/mL in serum or plasma.

In these experiments, comparison was made between the three separate TMD (Tasso+) devices and standard venipuncture. To appropriately compare the samples, standard acceptance criteria of +/-20% were applied. This study evaluated all information and analyzed all samples collected, even those considered outside of standard variability range, to better understand the overall impact of sample concordance and potential confounding factors.

Remote collection necessitates testing sample stability under certain conditions that mimic remote sample collection, especially the time between collection and receipt at an analytical laboratory for measurement. For example, samples collected remotely would either be centrifuged quickly for serum preparation and compared to standard venous collection, or samples would be tested for stability over a period of 72 hours with and without centrifugation. Thus, TMD (Tasso+) tube #1 was centrifuged relatively quickly (once clotting was successfully seen, usually 30 minutes), and aliquoted after collection to allow comparison with venous processing, whereas TMD (Tasso+) tube #2 was centrifuged in the same way, but kept as such for a period of 72 hours, then aliquoted, and TMD (Tasso+) tube #3 was kept for 72 hours refrigerated and later centrifuged to process for serum. The two alternative samples collected during this visit were subjected to alternative processing, one centrifuged “quickly” and another after 72 hours, which simulated conditions where samples move from remote collection to receipt at a laboratory facility for further processing, and 72 hours was assumed to be enough time for a remote sample to reach the laboratory.

Participant questionnaires

After collection completion at any given visit, remote or on-site, participants completed a questionnaire designed to collect feedback from device utilization as compared to venipuncture and to ascertain participant experience related to device use. Questionnaires included Likert scales on pain level associated with device utilization, clarity of instructions provided, ease of application, and overall participant preference with device use compared to standard venipuncture collection. Questionnaires also contained feedback sections related to potential associated side effects or adverse events, and a free-text feedback field. On-site medical staff were given a similar questionnaire that they were to answer once per visit. Participant responses to critical questions can be found in Figure 4(a,b).

Results/Discussion

Success rate of TMD (Tasso+) device use

During Visit 1, there were 31 adequate collections out of a total of 33 attempted: an overall success rate of 93.9%. One device failure occurred, and a single sample had low volume as previously defined above. During Visit 2, there were 33 adequate collections out of a total of 33 attempted (100% success rate). All participants were able to provide the minimum required volume of blood for analysis of C-peptide. Thus, only one collection out of the total 66 showed device failure and was reattempted successfully. Remote collections were successful for all 10 participants with single devices.

Correlation of C-peptide levels collected with TMD (Tasso+) and standard venous collection

Visit 1 results showed that the majority (29/33) of TMD (Tasso+) samples, irrespective of sampling area (Upper Right Arm, Lower Right Arm and Upper Left Arm) origin, had strong C-peptide level correlation within 20% of venous sample (Table 2). Only 3 out of 33 TMD (Tasso+) collections deviated from the 20% standard range, with slightly higher levels (up to 23%) compared to venipuncture C-peptide levels (Table 2). For the remote collection of 10 individuals, all C-peptide levels fell within the standard unfasted range of healthy participants. Overall, this experiment gives confidence that C-peptide can be measured reliably via capillary micro-sampling, and the processing of such a sample obtained remotely does not require the need to centrifuge rapidly following collection.

Device variability and C-peptide

Evaluating device variability was also a critical component to determine remote collection feasibility, as a goal of this experiment included participant comfortability with placements of the device. This was primarily addressed in tandem with the device location question in Visit 1, where three devices were placed on a participant within the space of a few seconds-minutes: two on the upper right arm, and one on the upper left with consistent processing being maintained between all 3 devices.

Each device across all collections obtained capillary blood within 5 minutes of actuation and was analyzed against venipuncture collection. The venipuncture collection was also completed during the visit window for both Visit 1 and Visit 2. As seen in Table 3, device variability of C-peptide was minimal, allowing for variation of device placement in future settings with minimal impact on C-peptide level.

Stability of C-peptide in samples simulating remote collection

Visit 2 was designed to determine C-peptide stability in various potential conditions relating to sample centrifugation, storage, and temperature (Figure 2), mimicking what may occur in remote collection environments. Stability analysis using Visit 2 samples was performed only after confirmation from Visit 1 sample analysis that there was no TMD (Tasso+) inter-device variability (Figure 2). All three alternative processing techniques (T0h-Gold Standard processing and storage, T72S-immediate processing and 72-hour 4°C storage and T72B-Storage at 4°C with delayed processing) provided similar results when compared against venipuncture C-peptide levels. Of the 33 TMD (Tasso+) samples, 31 samples were well within the predetermined 20% correlation of venous collected C-peptide. The remaining two collections varied slightly (up to 35%) as seen in Table 3. Based on the results of the simulated remote collections, it is indicated that collections from a TMD (Tasso+) device kept at 4°C and processed within 72 hours provide reliable C-peptide levels compared to venous collection with standard processing. It appears that C-peptide is stable in such conditions and can be successfully measured.

Participant survey/questionnaire results

During on-site collections, questionnaire data indicated that all participants preferred TMD (Tasso+) micro-sampling devices over standard venipuncture, with varying preference intensity (quantified via Likert scale). To further elaborate, 12 of 24 participants strongly agreed with the preference of TMD (Tasso+) (50%), 11 of 24 agreed with the preference (45.8%), and 1 somewhat agreed with the preference (4.2%). No one preferred venipuncture over the TMD (Tasso+) collection (0 out of 24: 0%), as shown in Figure 4(a,b). All participants indicated that the pain, or lack thereof, from collection with the capillary sampling device was acceptable, and noted that the most associated side effect related to collection with the TMD (Tasso+) included a visible mark on the skin, followed by a few noting mild pain with application, but still preferred TMD (Tasso+) application and overall pain level comparatively. All participants (100%) felt various levels of comfortability utilizing the TMD (Tasso+) device on-site, ranging from somewhat comfortable to completely comfortable. Responses indicated that 0 out of 24 (0%) participants reported “feeling uncomfortable” with device use. Based on both the provider facing questionnaire data and overall low rate of device failure during onsite collections, it would indicate that healthcare professionals became proficient in device use quickly, following hands-on training, practice, and review of the TMD (Tasso+) provided instructions.

There were additional participant comments on using the capillary sampling device as a future option: “The Tasso+ device was safe and easy to use … having 3 Tasso+ devices applied at the same time was quick, convenient and I didn’t mind at all.” Participants also commented that “Only a small visible mark was left where each of the devices pierced my skin and these marks heeled quickly.” For remote collections, one participant did note shipping concerns with an external vendor, and a second participant experienced a device failure, where “it was unclear why the device did not fill after 5 minutes.” No reported adverse events noted by participants were deemed device-related by the PI; however, the investigator reported one case of contact dermatitis at the site of the post-collection bandage. Contact dermatitis is highly suspected to be caused by adhesive in the bandage, as this event was not seen by the participants on their second visit, following a change of bandage brand.

Discussion

While the collection of blood-based samples remains a crucial component of clinical research, the ability to minimize patient/participant burden and increase participation remains a vital component of advancing research capacities. Through successful demonstration of use done in this study, the TMD (Tasso+) SST micro-sampling device has emerged as a feasible option for use by participants in remote and clinic-based settings for sample collection and subsequent analysis of C-peptide, though there were some limitations that should be noted.

The study population utilized in this study includes only healthy volunteers with above‑average health literacy and may not be representative of the disease population intended for future prospective research. Furthermore, the demonstration of TMD (Tasso+) measurements for this experiment was done in a narrow population range (fasted healthy individuals), which may not capture the full clinical range of C-peptide concentrations for all potential future applications. It should be noted that although fasting levels of C-peptide in healthy individuals can be quite low, in this study the device was not tested at the LLOQ for C-peptide, which is relevant for future conversations in the utilization for T1DM.

It is also important to note that potential research participants diagnosed with T1DM can have unique cutoffs, values, and requirements due to the autoimmunity associated with their condition, while patients with T2DM may benefit from understanding treatment thresholds and trajectory with a faster response analyte compared to something like A1C. Other endocrine conditions can also benefit from this information.

Additionally, a small sample size potentially contributed to the error range. Although the majority (47/65 of the total collections across both visits, excluding a single device failure, 72.3%) of our TMD (Tasso+) derived samples were within 10% concordance to Gold Standard venous collections, a larger number of participants and readings are required to make inferences about underlying reasons why some sample pairs did not reach this concordance level. A single participant experienced outlying values far beyond the 10% concordance. This could be potentially due to an unknown medical condition not discussed during enrollment. The associated values were relayed to the attending medical practitioner, and future applications could include further diagnostic testing following collection.

Over the rollout of TMD (Tasso+) devices, it has been shown to be a very acceptable collection technique and alternative for standard venipuncture collection, across multiple age groups, populations, disease states, and analytes. Such examples include [10], who evaluated high‑risk transplant patients for potential of self-collection and subsequent analysis of CMV, and [11], who evaluated the potential for remote collection of HcG testing in early pregnancy as an alternative to frequent necessary venipuncture collections.

Plentiful opportunities exist for use in research of diabetes, such as prescreening, alternative collections mid-trial, dose response collections, and more. Future potential uses of TMD (Tasso+) based C-peptide measurements can also be scaled to include field-based MMTT’s/OGTT’s, point-of-care testing in disease management, and therapeutic validation applications. C-peptide AUC (area under the curve) in these assessments would allow for quantification of residual pancreatic beta-cell function and monitoring outside required clinic visits. This would also allow monitoring of pancreatic function during clinical trials and/or use of this as a criterion for entry into a study, or a specific cohort or stage thereof. Repeating samples over a short period of time may have a different level of success and acceptance than the single timepoint collection shown here. The specific appropriateness of utilization of TMD (Tasso+) SST devices during MMTT’s/OGTT’s or monitoring techniques following the diagnosis of diabetes, one must consider the number of “pokes” required. If blood drawings can be limited via minimally invasive capillary micro-sampling, these individuals can benefit from increased clinical research involvement and overall management of their condition.

While this team utilized a single clinical site, with the same personnel, who are familiar with the device, being present for each visit, a multi-center clinical study involving diverse site personnel could help to further assess the consistency and generalizability of protocol implementation. Expanding recruitment to multiple sites would allow for evaluation of inter-site variability, enhance external validity, and provide more robust insights into how standardized procedures perform across different settings and teams. The opportunity to allow for collection under supervision by a trained personnel prior to remote collection may also be investigated in future.

Conclusion

Certain additional use contexts where the overall reduction of blood volume is advantageous can also be evaluated in future. Examples of this include pediatrics, geriatrics, and additional conditions where there is a concern for multiple blood draws or overall volume restrictions. Limiting volume for multiple timepoints, repeat collections, longitudinal study conditions, and studies that include response curves are additional trial criteria that would be beneficial to evaluate these devices in a broader setting.

This experiment focused primarily on liquid blood collection through TMD (Tasso+) SST, and subsequently controlled shipping conditions, with potential for more robust remote procedures to be developed. Other techniques can be investigated in future that may mitigate cost and efficiency issues. This can include evaluation of ambient shipping, which would require insulated shipping containers, and future clarification on temperature impact on C-peptide in serum. Another future direction to explore is the utilization of remote micro-sampling using dried blood sampling, including the Tasso M20 device, now re-engineered and labeled as the Tasso Tile-t20, or other related dried blood collection devices, as it has been shown that C-peptide analysis is possible using this collection approach [12]. This approach would allow even more flexibility on temperature, timing, and shipping logistics.

This study establishes feasibility and acceptability in measuring C-peptide via capillary sampling. Future work is needed to establish analytical validation and clinical utility using a larger and more heterogenous population. As clinical research becomes more elaborate, expensive, and cumbersome, with a high focus on patient/participant centricity, incorporation of capillary micro-sampling may be key to clinical trial success and progression in future. High blood volumes, frequent measurements, restrictive conditions, and travel requirements are often clinical research factors that limit potential involvement, especially in populations that require frequent monitoring. The utility of capillary micro-sampling has been previously established and demonstrated, with examples in finger prick technologies, heel sticks, and swabs, but there is now a generation of devices that can be utilized by individuals to collect samples with decreased associated burden. These devices, such as the Tasso+ SST device, are a pivotal addition to the expansion of capabilities for clinical research. Despite the limitations of this study, including small participant size, health status, and on-site location, the conclusions drawn remain pertinent for discussion and evaluation, considering the strong correlation seen between venipuncture and serum capillary micro-samples. This study indicates that the collection and measurement of C-peptide using TMD (Tasso+) SST devices can be an efficient and dependable technique for remote sampling and viable alternative to venipuncture in patients with diabetes, and other related endocrine conditions. The TMD (Tasso+) reliably collected sufficient blood volume for use on the Gold Standard analytical platform for C-peptide quantification, Abbott ARCHITECT, and received questionnaire feedback indicating it was well accepted and tolerated by study staff and participants. This study also demonstrates that C-peptide is conserved in the peripheral blood, and that centrifugation is not required immediately upon collection to ensure sample stability.

This study establishes feasibility and acceptability in measuring C-peptide via capillary sampling. Future work is needed to establish analytical validation and clinical utility using a larger and more heterogenous population.

This study took place in the United States; thus, our user acceptance data and end-to-end process may not be generalizable to the rest of the world. Alternative blood collection techniques may face different regulatory, ethical, and logistical requirements elsewhere. In this study, we did not evaluate all aspects of end-to-end processing for traditional clinical trial rollout, including, but not limited to, kit provisioning, shelf life, chain of custody for samples, additional analyte‑specific shipping logistics, cold chain requirements with these devices, and regulatory requirements in these spaces have also not been evaluated by this team.

In conclusion, this study shows that capillary micro-sampling and subsequent processing into serum for C-peptide measurement can be done successfully and with very good correlation to Gold Standard blood sampling methods. The micro-sampling capabilities and minimal processing methods used enabled maintenance of analyte integrity, and the usability of capillary blood for reliable C-peptide measurement without many complications associated with collection and subsequent analysis. Assuming a patient or participant population with a general base-level knowledge and comfortability with capillary self-collection, micro-sampling is an attractive option for remote C-peptide collection and can serve to facilitate involvement in clinical research, disease management, and progression.
