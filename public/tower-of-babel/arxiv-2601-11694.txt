Anisotropic Tensor Deconvolution of Hyperspectral Images
Xinjue Wang, Xiuheng Wang, Esa Ollila, Sergiy A. Vorobyov

Abstract
Hyperspectral image (HSI) deconvolution is a challenging ill-posed inverse problem, made difficult by the data’s high dimensionality. We propose a parameter-parsimonious framework based on a low-rank Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (CPD) of the entire latent HSI 𝓧 ∈ ℝ P × Q × N \mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{X}$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q\times N} . This approach recasts the problem from recovering a large-scale image with P ​ Q ​ N PQN variables to estimating the CPD factors with ( P + Q + N ) ​ R (P+Q+N)R variables. This model also enables a structure-aware, anisotropic Total Variation (TV) regularization applied only to the spatial factors, preserving the smooth spectral signatures. An efficient algorithm based on the Proximal Alternating Linearized Minimization (PALM) framework is developed to solve the resulting non-convex optimization problem. Experiments confirm the model’s efficiency, showing a numerous parameter reduction of over two orders of magnitude and a compelling trade-off between model compactness and reconstruction accuracy.

Introduction

High-dimensional data, such as hyperspectral images (HSIs) are often represented as tensors
[
14
,
19
]
.
In practical acquisition systems, the signals are usually degraded by a convolution process (blurring) and corrupted by additive noise
[
9
,
17
,
18
]
.
The task of
tensor deconvolution
is to recover the latent clean signal
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{X}$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q\times N}
from its degraded observation
\mathcal{Y}
is a fundamental ill-posed inverse problem.

Different prior information on the latent images has been considered to regularize the solution in the HSI deconvolution task. For example, the fast hyperspectral restoration algorithm in
[
9
]
performs deconvolution under positivity constraints
while accounting for spatial and spectral correlation. In
[
15
]
, an online deconvolution algorithm is devised by considering sequentially
collected data by push-broom devices. 3D fractional total variation (3DFTV)
[
8
]
leverages non-local smoothness in all dimensions to better preserve textures.
More recently, learning-based regularization methods, such as plug-and-play
[
17
,
18
]
and deep unrolling
[
7
]
frameworks, have shown superior performance in reconstruction accuracy.

However, these are full-rank models that operate on the entire tensor, and thus must solve a high-dimensional optimization problem without addressing the data’s intrinsic low-rank structure.
A different line of work leverages the low-rankness of the latent images.
For example, the weighted low-rank tensor recovery method (WLRTR)
[
5
]
leverages the non-local self-similarity within the HSI by searching for similar 3D patches and assuming that the resulting patch-group forms a low-rank tensor.
However, this patch-based strategy retains the full-rank tensor as its optimization variable and relies on computationally expensive patch processing that neglects the scene’s global structure.

Contributions.

In this work, we propose a novel tensor deconvolution framework for HSIs.
Our framework is composed of three key components:
(i)
a low-rank CPD model that recasts the recovery problem from a large-scale image recovery problem to a small-scale parameter estimation problem;
(ii)
a structure-aware, anisotropic Total Variation regularization applied to the spatial factors, preserving the smooth signatures; and
(iii)
an efficient algorithm based on the PALM framework
[
1
,
6
]
to solve the resulting non-convex problem.
The source code is available at
https://github.com/xnnjw/TensorDeconv_release
.

Problem Formulation

2.1
Tensor Imaging Model

We consider the problem of constructing a clear HSI
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{X}$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q\times N}
from its degraded observation
\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{Y}$}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q\times N}
, where
P,Q
, and
N
are the number of rows, columns, and spectral bands.
The ill-posed nature of the inverse problem necessitates the use of effective priors. A powerful prior is the low-rankness of the latent images, which has been widely used in the literature
[
16
,
5
,
2
]
.
Based on this prior information, we consider modeling
\mathcal{X}
directly as a single, global, low-rank tensor via the Canonical Polyadic Decomposition (CPD)
[
3
]
, also known as the Kruskal model
[
12
]
.
The CPD factorizes the entire tensor into a sum of
R
rank-one components, where
R
is the rank for CPD.
This factorization is defined by factor matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times R}
,
{\boldsymbol{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{Q\times R}
, and
{\boldsymbol{C}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times R}
as

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\mathcal{X}$}\approx\llbracket{\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}}\rrbracket=\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\boldsymbol{A}}(\cdot,r)\circ{\boldsymbol{B}}(\cdot,r)\circ{\boldsymbol{C}}(\cdot,r),

(1)

where
\circ
denotes the vector outer product,
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
correspond to spatial abundances, and
{\boldsymbol{C}}
corresponds to spectral signatures.
Furthermore, it is parameter-parsimonious, reducing the number of variables to estimate from
PQN
for the full tensor to
(P+Q+N)R
for the factor matrices.
The deconvolution is thus reformulated from a high-dimensional image recovery problem into a lower-dimensional parameter estimation problem.

The degradation process is modeled on a slice-by-slice basis, i.e., the 3D tensor
\mathcal{X}
is processed by its frontal slices
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q}
.
Each observed slice
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q}
is assumed to be a 2D convolution of the corresponding clean slice
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}
with a blurring kernel
{\boldsymbol{H}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q}
, corrupted by additive independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian noise
{\boldsymbol{N}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P\times Q}
as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{i}={\boldsymbol{H}}_{i}\star{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{N}}_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(2)

where
\star
denotes the 2D convolution.
The clean slice
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}
in the degradation model (
2
) is thus constructed from the CPD factor matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}}
,
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{C}}
as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{X}}_{i}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}{\boldsymbol{C}}(i,r){\boldsymbol{A}}(\cdot,r){\boldsymbol{B}}(\cdot,r)^{\top}.

(3)

The goal of the deconvolution is to recover the factor matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}}
from the observations
\mathcal{Y}
.

2.2
The optimization problem

Based on the degradation model (
2
) and the CPD representation (
3
), the restoration of the latent tensor
\mathcal{X}
is equivalent to estimating its factor matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}}
from the observations
\mathcal{Y}
. Thus, we can formulate the tensor deconvolution of HSIs as the following constrained optimization problem:

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{minimize}_{{\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}}}

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}})+g_{A}({\boldsymbol{A}})+g_{B}({\boldsymbol{B}}),

(4)

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{subject\ to}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}}\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{B}}\geq 0,{\boldsymbol{C}}\geq 0.

The objective function consists of a smooth data fidelity term,
f({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}})
, and non-smooth regularization terms
g_{A}({\boldsymbol{A}})
,
g_{B}({\boldsymbol{B}})
which encode prior information of the latent tensor
\mathcal{X}
.
The non-negative constraints are imposed to reflect physical constraints, such as material abundances and spectral signatures being non-negative.
The data fidelity term
f({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}})
is defined as

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{A}},{\boldsymbol{B}},{\boldsymbol{C}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{H}}_{i}\star({\boldsymbol{A}}\operatorname{diag}({\boldsymbol{C}}(i,:)){\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top})\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}^{2}+\lambda_{3}\|{\boldsymbol{C}}\|_{{\mathsf{F}}}^{2},

(5)

which combines the least-square (LS) loss with zero-order Tikhonov (Frobenius-norm) penalties on the factors to improve conditioning and numerical stability.
Here,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
are non-negative hyperparameters.
The non-smooth terms regularize the spatial factors using the TV norm
[
13
]
, defined as

\displaystyle g_{A}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\lambda_{A}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|{\boldsymbol{A}}_{r}\|_{\mathsf{TV}},\ g_{B}({\boldsymbol{B}})=\lambda_{B}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|{\boldsymbol{B}}_{r}\|_{\mathsf{TV}},

(6)

where
\lambda_{A}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{B}\geq 0
control the strength of the regularization, with the TV norm for a vector
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{P}
defined as

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}=\sum_{p=1}^{P-1}|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{p+1}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{p}|.

(7)

This encourages piecewise constant patterns, preserving sharp transitions between regions
[
13
]
.

2.3
PALM Framework

The PALM algorithm
[
1
]
is an effective framework for problems of the form:

\displaystyle\min_{x_{1},\dots,x_{p}}F(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})=\sum_{i=1}^{p}g_{i}(x_{i})+f(x_{1},\dots,x_{p}),

where each
g_{i}(x_{i})
is a (possibly non-convex) non-smooth function, and
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})
is a smooth coupling function.

The core of PALM is a block coordinate descent scheme that sequentially minimizes a surrogate function for each variable block.
At iteration
k
, to update block
x_{i}
, PALM linearizes the smooth term
f
at the current iterate and adds a quadratic proximal term.
This leads to the update rule

\displaystyle x_{i}^{k+1}\in\arg\min_{u}\ g_{i}(u)+\langle\nabla_{i}f,u-x_{i}^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2c_{i,k}}\|u-x_{i}^{k}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\nabla_{i}f
is the partial gradient of
f
with respect to
x_{i}
at
x_{i}^{k}
, and
c_{i,k}>0
is a step size.
This update can be written more compactly as a proximal gradient step.
The step size
c_{i,k}
is crucial for convergence and can be found using a backtracking line search method.

3
Proposed method

Solving the optimization problem (
4
) involves the proximal operators of
g_{A}
and
g_{B}
(
6
).
A key property of these operators is their separability: since the regularization is a sum of TV norms applied independently to each column, the overall proximal operator can be computed by applying the one-dimensional (1D) TV proximal operator to each column of the input matrix.
For an input matrix
{\boldsymbol{U}}=[{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{R}]
, this separability is expressed as

\begin{split}&\operatornamewithlimits{minimize}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{R}}\quad\sum_{r=1}^{R}\left(\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{r}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{r}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{r}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\\
&\equiv\operatornamewithlimits{minimize}_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{R}}\quad\sum_{r=1}^{R}\|{\boldsymbol{v}}_{r}\|_{\mathsf{TV}}+\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{V}}-{\boldsymbol{U}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(8)

This separability is computationally advantageous. The core task of computing the 1D proximal operator
\operatorname{prox}_{\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{TV}}}(\cdot)
can be efficiently solved
[
4
]
.

Our choice of regularizers is tailored to the distinct physical meaning of each factor matrix in the CPD model.
For the factor matrices
{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}
, which represent piecewise-constant spatial abundance maps, TV is a proper regularization for preserving their sharp edges.
Conversely, the columns of matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}
represent smooth spectral signatures, for which TV regularization may introduce artifacts.
This tailored TV regularization, combined with Frobenius norm penalties for numerical stability and non-negativity constraints to preserve physical meaning, allows our model to better reflect the intrinsic properties of HSI.

The update steps in Algorithm

5
Conclusion

In this paper, a novel framework for HSI deconvolution was proposed based on a low-rank CPD model.
By leveraging the physical interpretation of the CPD factors, we designed an anisotropic Total Variation regularization scheme that targets only the spatial modes of the HSI.
Experimental results demonstrated that our method is extremely parameter-efficient, reducing model complexity by over two orders of magnitude while maintaining competitive reconstruction performance.
