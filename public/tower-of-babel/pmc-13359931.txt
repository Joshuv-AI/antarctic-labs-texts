Teaching communication competencies across the undergraduate medical curriculum: a qualitative exploratory case study
Authors: Elena María Trujillo Maza, Valentina Navarro Palau, Mariana Lema-Vélez, Sofía Gnecco González, Andrés Cuervo Landinez, Andrés Camilo Cardozo Alarcón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunication skills are essential for ethical, person-centered care and interprofessional collaboration. Although their importance in medical education is well established, there is limited evidence on how they are integrated across undergraduate curricula, particularly in Latin American. This study explored how communication skills are represented and taught within the formal Competency–Based Medical Education (CBME) curriculum of an undergraduate medical program in Colombia.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory case study through documentary content analysis of course syllabi from Universidad de los Andes, representing 19.52% of the total academic credits of the undergraduate medical curriculum. Coding followed the School of Medicine communication competencies framework. The analysis explored the ways in which Communication competencies are represented and taught across the 16 undergraduate medical courses. Descriptive frequency counts were used to highlight patterns in how these competencies are incorporated through the curriculum.ResultsContent analysis identified variations in communication competencies across themes and disciplines. Communication and teamwork were most frequently addressed, while emotionally sensitive communication and interpersonal relationship skills appeared less often. Although several courses included communication outcomes, there was limited incorporation of systematic, longitudinal development—particularly in patient-centered communication, safe communication protocols, and emotional competencies.ConclusionsThis study highlights the need for meaningful integration of communication competencies within the curriculum, fostering their longitudinal development and contextualization across areas. It also emphasizes encouraging student engagement and addressing the perception of communication skills as innate traits. Findings provide a foundation for curricular planning and future research to strengthen communication training in undergraduate medical education within Latin American settings.Keywords: Communication skills, Professional Competence, Curriculum analysis, Undergraduate Medical Education, Qualitative Research

Introduction

Effective communication skills are fundamental to high-quality medical care and patient safety. They enable clinicians to establish trust, exchange information accurately, and support shared decision-making across diverse patient populations [1]. In the physician–patient relationship, these skills are essential for ethical and effective care [2]. Communication occurs through both verbal channels, such as speech and active listening, and non-verbal channels, including gestures, eye contact, and body language, which together shape clinical interactions [1]. Traditionally, communication was considered an innate trait or something acquired implicitly through clinical observation; however, evidence highlights the necessity of structured and formal training, even for those with strong interpersonal abilities [3].

The impact of proficient communication extends across multiple dimensions of healthcare delivery. Effective interprofessional communication fosters teamwork, empathy, and shared decision-making, thereby improving care coordination [4]. Clinician communication has been directly linked to improved outcomes such as better treatment adherence [5], patient satisfaction [6], and even reduced malpractice claims, often related to the perception of empathy and clarity of information provided [7]. Conversely, poor communication is a recognized contributor to diagnostic errors, adverse events, and diminished trust in the medical profession [7, 8]. These findings reinforce the imperative of embedding communication skills education into medical curricula as a cornerstone of professional development [3].

Recognizing this, medical schools worldwide have increasingly acknowledged the importance of communication skills, the biopsychosocial model, and person-centered care in effective practice [6]. Frameworks such as Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) have been adopted to integrate communication competencies intentionally and longitudinally within training programs [9]. International guidelines reinforce this approach: for example, the Association of American Medical Colleges [13] emphasizes communication competencies in its recommendations for undergraduate medical education. Similarly, the UK General Medical Council underscores direct observation of students, followed by standardized feedback from experienced tutors, as the most effective strategy for developing communication skills [10]. However, while these global benchmarks provide a robust foundation, their integration within the regional Latin American context, and specifically within the Colombian regulatory framework, requires a detailed analysis to understand how these competencies are formally embedded in the curricula.

Despite these frameworks, there is still no universally accepted definition of satisfactory physician–patient communication, and a gap remains in how these competencies are explicitly and systematically embedded within undergraduate medical curricula [11]. Recent qualitative research in North America continues to explore these challenges [12], yet little comparable evidence exists for regional Latin American contexts. Addressing this gap is critical to align medical education with CBME principles and ensure graduates acquire the skills necessary for patient-centered care. In Latin America, where evidence remains scarce, the need for systematic documentation and curricular mapping of communication competencies is particularly pressing. This study is part of a broader effort to comprehensively understand how communication competencies are represented and taught in medical education. Specifically, it examines how these competencies are incorporated and described within the undergraduate medical curriculum at Universidad de los Andes, Colombia. Guided by a documentary review of course programs and teaching strategies across different curricular areas, the study addresses the following research question: How are communication competencies represented and taught across the 16 courses of the undergraduate medical curriculum?

Methods

Context

This study was conducted at Universidad de los Andes School of Medicine, a private, accredited institution in Bogotá, Colombia, which operates under national guidelines while maintaining autonomy in curricular design. The six-year competency-based program incorporates seven communication competencies adapted from the AAMC Recommendations for Clinical Skills Curricula for Undergraduate Medical Education (Table 1) [13, 14], distributed across six curricular areas: Knowledge Management and Research, Biomedical Sciences, Public Health, Clinical Practice, Communication, Human Development and Professionalism, and Integrated Systems. Each semester includes courses intended to foster communication skills, from introductory modules to advanced clinical rotations. Two concentration courses (Communication Skills I and Communication Skills II) provide foundational and advanced training through lectures, workshops, and standardized patient practice [14].

Study design

This research represents the first phase of a four-phase project aimed at comprehensively mapping the teaching and learning of communication competencies throughout the undergraduate medical curriculum. This initial phase focused exclusively on the formal curriculum through documentary analysis of course syllabi to examine how communication competencies were represented and taught in institutional documents. Perspectives of students and educators, as well as the analysis of concentration courses, will be addressed in later stages of the project, providing a more comprehensive and layered understanding of how communication training is implemented, experienced, and evaluated. The overall project was developed sequentially across phases over time; accordingly, the interval between data collection and manuscript submission reflects the staged progression of the broader study.

Course selection

To ensure diverse representation across curricular areas and academic years, 16 courses were purposively selected representing 19.52% of the total academic credits of the undergraduate medical curriculum (Table 2). Selection prioritized coverage of multiple disciplines, inclusion of both early and advanced stages of the program, and representation of areas where communication competencies were likely to be emphasized. Final-year clerkships were excluded due to variability in clinical sites and documentation, which are often externally managed. Similarly, the concentration courses (Communication Skills I and II) were intentionally excluded in this phase, aligning with the sequential design of the research project. These courses will be examined in subsequent phases alongside qualitative data from students and faculty.

Analysis

A thematic analysis was conducted in two stages, primarily by two authors (EMTM and VNP, referring to the authors’ initials), with analytical support and discussion from other members of the research team (ACCA, AMCL, and SGG). The first stage involved the a priori development of a detailed codebook containing five themes and their corresponding categories (see Table 3), derived from the communication competencies defined in the undergraduate curriculum (Table 1).

The second stage encompassed familiarization with the documents, which VNP and EMTM carefully and systematically reviewed to ensure a deep understanding of their content. This involved repeated readings to grasp the meaning of the material. During this process, the authors categorized relevant information according to the established codebook, with particular attention to alignment between information and themes. Targeted focus was paid to how the information aligned with each category, allowing for nuanced interpretation and accurate classification. Ambiguous cases were discussed until consensus was achieved, ensuring consistency and analytical rigor. In this study, a “quote” refers to a segment of text within the syllabus (e.g., learning objectives, teaching activities, or assessment statements) that explicitly referred to communication competencies. Longer passages were segmented only when they contained distinct meaning units corresponding to different competencies or categories. Repeated text across syllabi was treated as a separate coded instance only when it appeared in a different course document.

To enhance coherence, the research team consolidated three closely related competencies—communication with peers, teamwork, and professional collaboration—into a single theme. This decision reflected the predominance of empirical literature addressing these competencies in postgraduate medical education internationally, alongside the more limited evidence regarding their consistent implementation at the undergraduate level, particularly in Latin America. The resulting thematic structure preserved the intent of the AAMC framework and the institutional competencies while supporting a context-sensitive and analytically approach.

Elements of qualitative content analysis were used to support the systematic organization and comparison of coded material across documents [16], providing a structured examination of curricular narratives based on predefined understandings of communication competencies drawn from institutional documents and relevant literature. This method draws on established principles to produce valid and replicable inferences by quantifying how often specific elements such as words, codes, or themes occur within the dataset [17]. Consistent with the study’s objective, the analysis focused on examining how communication competencies were represented across the syllabi. Quantifying coded quotes within each theme and category provided a descriptive overview of the relationship between communication competency themes and their representation across documents, supporting pattern identification and comparability. These frequencies were summarized and displayed in a graphical representation (Fig. 1) to highlight trends and facilitate interpretation of the findings. The frequency counts were used for descriptive purposes only, as complementary support for the thematic analysis, illustrating how communication competencies were represented across course documents.

Research team and reflexivity

The project was designed and conducted by a team composed of physicians, specialized physicians, and undergraduate medical students, all of whom have participated as students, teachers, or both in the concentration courses. They were supported in the larger project by other health care professionals, including a speech therapist, a psychologist, and more specialized physicians. The research team was composed of six members; three of the authors (EMTM, ACCA and MLV) hold postgraduate degrees in fields such as education, epidemiology, public health, bioethics, and medical anthropology.

The research team was part of the academic community of the School of Medicine at Universidad de los Andes, where the study was conducted. Furthermore, their interest in communication competencies may have sensitized them to notice subtle cues or place greater emphasis on references to communication. Conversely, it might also have led them to apply overly critical judgments when analyzing the empirical data and the way they framed communication skills. Finally, the teachers who voluntarily provided their course documents for analysis held collegial relationships with most of the research team members. It is noteworthy that these teachers might have felt vulnerable, considering that their course documents could be used as an evaluation of their performance.

Results

A total of 467 quotes related to communication competencies were identified across 16 syllabi. These were distributed among five themes aligned with the competencies defined in the undergraduate curriculum (Table 1). Their frequency and depth of articulation varied, offering a nuanced view of how communication skills are integrated into the curriculum. Figure 1 displays content analysis frequencies with color-coded themes and categories providing a visual overview of curricular emphasis: yellow (1), green (2), orange (3), blue (4), and gray (5).

Theme 2 “Communicates appropriately and in a timely manner with all personnel involved in patient care and working effectively as a member or leader of a health care or other professional group” was the most frequent, comprising 46.9% of all identified quotes (n = 219). This reflects the emphasis on collaborative practice, leadership, and responsibility across various stages of training. Categories such as interprofessional relations, teamwork, and leadership (n = 73) and self-knowledge and responsibility (n = 52) were especially prominent. These expectations were explicit in both introductory and clinical courses. For example, Introduction to the Medicine (C4-1) states, “Students will be encouraged to develop responsibility for their own health and that of those around them through group work and discussions based on real cases or situations.” Similarly, Pharmacology (C3-2) reinforces group accountability by noting that “All students in a group are expected to be prepared to correctly answer evaluations. A student’s grade may be used to calculate the group score in an exam or quiz.” Clinical courses such as Emergency Medicine (C2-5) make interprofessional communication explicit: “[The student is] able to communicate appropriately with colleagues, emergency physicians, interns, surgeons, internists, family medicine residents, nurses, and other emergency team personnel”. Although teamwork and leadership were strongly integrated, references to structured communication for patient safety were less frequent (n = 42), suggesting a potential area for strengthening.

Theme 1 “Communicates adequately and in a timely manner with patients, families, and communities” accounted for 23.13% of the total quotes (n = 108), underscoring the value placed on patient-centered communication. The most common category was participation in interactions that allow understanding of patients’ and families’ circumstances (n = 39). Courses like Emergency Medicine (C2-5) expect students to “listen carefully, gather and synthesize relevant information about the patient’s issues, and comprehend its content”, while Semiology I (C2-1) emphasizes overcoming barriers: “[The student is] capable of overcoming communication barriers caused by cultural differences or physical and mental impairments”. Health education is also evident in system-based courses such as Urinary and Renal System (C6-1): “Educate in health and inform patients and their families about renal disease timely and effectively.” However, references to patient communication were rare in Public Health or research-oriented courses, pointing to opportunities to reinforce these skills in non-clinical settings.

Theme 3 “Prepares the clinical history and other medical records in a clear, truthful, and concrete manner” was reflected in 16.27% of all quotes (n = 76) and particularly emphasized in Semiology I (C2-1), Psychiatry (C2-6), and Urinary and Renal System (C6-1). Documentation of patient information (n = 45) and follow-up planning (n = 31) were central categories. In Semiology I (C2-1), students are introduced to the dual function of medical records: “An introduction to the preparation of medical histories and the fundamental elements of these documents as medical and legal records will be provided.” As students advance, expectations become more complex. In Psychiatry (C2-6), students must “organize clinical data from psychiatric interviews and mental examinations to make reasonable diagnostic hypotheses” and “efficiently document clinical information through medical records, including precise and complete notes and patient case records.” Additionally, in Urinary and Renal System (C6-1), students are expected to “properly develop a follow-up plan.” These findings suggest a longitudinal progression in documentation skills, although this theme was not represented in some areas, such as Knowledge Management and Research.

Theme 5 “Being aware of human emotions, understanding them and considering them in the management of interpersonal relationships” comprised 7.28% of quotes (n = 34) and included key categories such as empathy and therapeutic relationships (n = 16), and confidentiality and respect (n = 18). These elements were concentrated in clinical courses. For example, Emergency Medicine (C2-5) encourages students to “listen attentively, obtain and synthesize pertinent information about the patient’s problems, and understand the content of such information.” Similarly, Psychiatry (C2-6) reinforces ethical responsibility: “Maintain and respect the confidentiality of the information provided by patients.” This competency was absent in Biomedical Sciences and Knowledge Management, indicating limited integration outside clinical contexts.

Theme 4 “Demonstrating sensitivity, honesty, and compassion regarding end-of-life dilemmas, adverse events, bad news, and other sensitive topics” was the least frequently represented theme, with 6.42% of quotes (n = 30). Still, the presence of this competency was meaningful. Introduction to the Medicine (C4-1) promotes ethical reflection, stating that “[Learners] will address ethical dilemmas as students or professionals and encounter the most frequent ethical dilemmas in modern medical practice.” In Pharmacology (C3-2), students are expected to “recognize in a timely manner the occurrence of adverse events associated with medications and appropriately modify therapeutic plans.” However, no explicit references to this competency were found in Communication, Human Development, and Professionalism or Knowledge Management and Research areas, reflecting its underdevelopment outside clinical contexts.

Curricular area analysis supports these observations (Table 4). Most of the quotes were associated with the Clinical Practice area (n = 156), a result potentially influenced by the number of courses selected and the inherently communicative nature of clinical education. Within this area, themes 2 and 3 were more frequently represented than the remaining themes. Sixty-five quotes were linked to the Integrative Systems and Disease area, where both team-based communication and patient education were also observed. Forty-four quotes were connected to the Communication, Human Development and Professionalism area, particularly to themes 1 and 5. In contrast, a small number of quotes were associated with the remaining curricular areas: Knowledge Management and Research (n = 21), Biomedical Sciences (n = 12), and Public Health (n = 6), especially in themes 3, 4, and 5. While most of the quotes were unsurprisingly linked to the Clinical Practice area, fewer references were identified between quotes representing communication skills and foundational areas like Biomedical Sciences and Knowledge Management and Research, particularly skills requiring ethical communication or emotional sensitivity. These findings should be interpreted as an exploration of how communication competencies are represented across curricular areas within the formal curriculum, rather than as a direct comparison between courses with different purposes and content.

Discussion

This study represents the first phase of a broader project mapping the integration of communication competencies in the undergraduate medical curriculum at Universidad de los Andes. The purpose was to identify salient elements within curricular narratives to generate insights for curriculum development. Documents from sixteen courses were analyzed, representing 16.7% of total academic credits and covering academic, clinical, and interdisciplinary areas. Findings reveal not only the presence but also the curricular intention behind integrating communication competencies throughout various stages of undergraduate training. Communication-related learning outcomes appeared in every syllabus analyzed, reflecting institutional and pedagogical interest in promoting these skills. Rather than suggesting fragmentation, the variation observed across themes and curricular areas indicates that some competencies are already strong anchors, while others represent opportunities to design targeted interventions. Making these variations explicitly can guide educators in planning authentic and progressive learning experiences, ensuring that communication is cultivated transversally and longitudinally across the curriculum. As an initial exploratory phase, this study offers a foundation for subsequent curriculum review and for future efforts to strengthen communication as a transversal competency.

Theme 2 “Communicating appropriately and in a timely manner with all personnel involved in patient care and working effectively as a member or leader of a health care or other professional group” was the most represented, reflecting a strong institutional commitment to promoting professional collaboration, particularly in clinical practice and integrative systems courses. This approach aligns with global trends, where interprofessional education (IPE) has been shown to strengthen attitudes, knowledge, and skills for teamwork [18]. Within this theme, the lower frequency of references to “safe communication” protocols (n = 42) highlights an opportunity to intentionally reinforce this area in undergraduate training, consistent with evidence that structured tools such as I-PASS [19] and SBAR [20] reduce failures in information transfer. While their integration has often been limited and considered an advanced competency [21], positioning them as foundational skills to be introduced early could foster a stronger culture of patient safety. This aligns with global efforts to view communication as a patient-safety competency rather than an optional add-on. Likewise, the category “interprofessional relations, teamwork, and leadership” played a prominent role, providing a solid foundation for further strengthening IPE. Across the Latin American region, evidence from Brazil [22] suggests that expanding the scope of IPE beyond isolated clinical experiences can enhance its impact. Similarly, pilot initiatives in Colombia using ICT [23] have proven to be a promising starting point for promoting early interprofessional learning, suggesting a strong foundation for longitudinal training strategies. Overall, the curriculum demonstrates a strong commitment to collaboration, while also offering opportunities to expand structured and longitudinal approaches.

Theme 1 “Communicating adequately and in a timely manner with patients, families, and communities” was the second most frequent competency, appearing in clinical and professionalism-oriented courses. This emphasis reflects the institutional commitment to patient-centered communication and empathetic interactions, recognized as essential for building therapeutic relationships and improving health outcomes [24]. The category “participates in communicative interactions that allow understanding of the person’s and family’s circumstances” (n = 39) demonstrates an interest in empathy, although there is room for further development through strategies such as experiential learning and the skill of noticing [25]. While these initiatives highlight institutional commitment, Rieffestahl [26] emphasize that without consistent assessment and structured feedback, their impact may remain limited. Embedding systematic evaluation alongside experiential methods could progressively reinforce empathy as a measurable and teachable skill, rather than leaving it to be implicitly acquired. Our findings are consistent with previous research in the Colombian context. For instance, Sánchez-Angarita [27] highlights the value of problem-based learning for enhancing communication skills and fostering more effective, humanized doctor–patient relationships. Although concentration courses on communication were not included in this analysis, they represent a strategic opportunity to intentionally integrate methodologies that strengthen these competencies within the broader regional curriculum.

Theme 3 “Preparing clinical histories and medical records clearly, truthfully, and concretely” was addressed across several course documents, showing a progression from introductory instruction during early semesters to more advanced applications during clinical practice. This curricular trajectory aims to gradually build students’ ability to produce accurate, ethical, and legally sound medical records, a crucial skill for patient safety, continuity of care, and healthcare system operations. Quotes related to this theme were found in course documents from the Clinical Practice and Integrated Systems areas. However, multiple studies highlight persistent gaps in how documentation skills are formally taught. The American Medical Association [13] has pointed out that many medical students lack consistent exposure to electronic health records (EHR) and receive limited structured training in documentation practices during their undergraduate education. Further research supports the role of practical interventions in improving documentation skills. A Korean study found that providing students with real-time assessment and feedback on their medical records increased their skills and confidence in medical record writing [28]. Additionally, it showed that near-peer teaching approaches, where senior students coach juniors in documentation workshops, can reinforce skills acquisition more effectively than passive instruction alone [28]. Despite these advances, in many contexts, clinical documentation is still perceived as a competency naturally acquired through clinical exposure rather than a skill requiring deliberate and progressive curricular development [29]. Addressing this perception through structured, longitudinal training could better equip future physicians to document patient information precisely, ethically, and in ways that directly contribute to patient safety and care quality [30].

Theme 4, “Demonstrating sensitivity, honesty, and compassion regarding end-of-life dilemmas, adverse events, bad news, and other sensitive topics” and theme 5, “being aware of human emotions, understanding them, and considering them in the management of interpersonal relationships” were the least represented competencies. Although they were present in clinical courses addressing empathy, confidentiality, and ethical conversations, they lacked systematic, longitudinal development. Consistent with global findings, this reflects a fragmented approach to socioemotional skills training [31]. Overcoming the perception that these abilities are innate rather than teachable could open opportunities for curricular innovation, such as structured reflective practice or faculty development programs [32]. Such approaches would reinforce not only student competencies but also faculty capacity to model and guide socioemotional learning. Within the regional Latin American context, specifically in frameworks developed in Chile [32] there is a strong advocacy for faculty development frameworks that incorporate empathy and socioemotional teaching as core elements, further reinforcing the need for intentional integration.

This first phase, focused on general curriculum courses, underscores the strategic role of Communication Skills I and II—to be examined in subsequent phases—as anchor points for reinforcing competencies less frequently addressed elsewhere. Future stages will incorporate student and faculty perspectives to complement documentary analysis and provide a more comprehensive understanding of how these skills evolve throughout undergraduate education. This aligns with previous report [26], noting that while communication curricula are common, they often lack consistent instructional strategies and systematic evaluation, highlighting the need for longitudinal approaches.

This study offers a foundation to reflect on how communication competencies are conceptualized, prioritized, and developed throughout the curriculum. First, it provides a clearer view of the distribution and emphasis of these competencies across different stages of training. Second, it underscores the role of communication as a principal component of professional identity, essential not only in clinical practice but throughout all medical education. Third, it identifies how the development of these competencies is currently supported and where it could be more intentionally reinforced along with the educational continuum.

Building these insights, the study can inform targeted strategies to strengthen communication competencies across the curriculum. Rather than being confined to specific courses, communication appears as a competency developed across multiple curricular areas, in alignment with CBME principles and a longitudinal approach to training. This integrative perspective underscores communication not only as a transversal competency but also as a defining element of professional identity. The presence of related competencies across multiple courses suggests a longitudinal curricular intention, while allowing for different emphases across curricular areas according to their disciplinary and clinical relevance. Global evidence further supports this perspective, as early and longitudinal curricular integration has been associated with improved self-assessment of professional competencies and greater readiness for practice [33]. Beyond offering insights from a Latin American context, this study also contributes to broader regional and international conversations on communication training by highlighting the value of context-sensitive curricular exploration. Such efforts reinforce that communication is central to medical professionalism, patient safety, and collaborative care, and that its deliberate integration helps prepare graduates to be not only competent clinicians but also empathetic and reflective professionals.

Conclusions

This study reveals how communication competencies are represented within the formal undergraduate medical curriculum at Universidad de los Andes. Analysis of course syllabi shows that communication is embedded across academic, clinical, and interdisciplinary courses, positioning it as a core component of undergraduate medical training. The findings identify domains with stronger curricular presence as well as areas that may benefit from further alignment and reinforcement. As the first phase of a broader research program, this work provides a baseline for future studies incorporating student and faculty perspectives and supports informed curriculum development aimed at strengthening longitudinal communication competencies training.
