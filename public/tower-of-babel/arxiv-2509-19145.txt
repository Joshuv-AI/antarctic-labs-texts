We prove Theorems

1.2

\lambda\geq 0

u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
,
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega

\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)=\inf_{\text{dim}V=p}\max_{v\in V\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx},

p

k

\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(1.7)

-\Delta\varphi-\lambda\varphi=\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

This is proven in Proposition
2.2

1.2

be a solution of (
1.2

\tilde{v}=\frac{v}{\|v\|_{2^{*}}}

(1.8)

-\Delta\tilde{v}-\lambda\tilde{v}=\mu|\tilde{v}|^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{v}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

1.2
. Most of the analytical difficulties that we face in this paper arise from the non-coercive assumption
\lambda\geq\Lambda_{1}
and are related to the min-max definition of generalised eigenvalues and to their lack of differentiability in
u
.

Property (
1.9
) is reminiscent of the celebrated minimisation approach of
[
13
]
. This is not surprising: when
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})
we prove (Appendix
A
below) that

\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\big)dx}{\big(\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2^{*}}dx\big)^{\frac{n-2}{n}}},

so that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is precisely the minimisation problem of
[
13
]
. When
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
for some
i\geq 1
,
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
with
k=N(i)+1
should thus be understood as the natural generalisation, in the higher-frequency setting
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
, of the minimisation problem of
[
13
]
. The motivation for defining
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
comes from conformal geometry:
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is the euclidean transposition of eigenvalue-optimisation problems in conformal geometry in dimension
3
and higher that have attracted renewed attention in very recent years (see for instance
[
2
,
3
,
32
,
37
,
46
,
53
]
). The problem
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
that we introduce in this paper therefore follows the original philosophy of
[
13
]
, which transposed in the euclidean setting ideas of Aubin
[
6
]
for the Yamabe problem. The new variational framework that we develop in this paper is robust and may be applied to other nonlinear critical problems, such as polyharmonic equations, to produce least-energy solutions (we refer for this to
[
37
]
where polyharmonic problems on manifolds are discussed). An adaptation of the arguments in this paper also gives an analogue of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
for radial solutions of (
1.2
) on the unit ball, but we will not pursue this here.

1.3.
Structure of the paper

The organisation of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the definition of generalised eigenvalues, investigate their properties and prove the existence of generalised eigenfunctions. We prove in particular Proposition
2.3
, where we establish the connection between non-zero solutions of (
1.2
) and generalised eigenvectors. In Section
3
we prove that the sign of generalised eigenvalues only depends on the position of
\lambda
with respect to
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
(Proposition
3.1
) and we develop a variational theory for the generalised eigenvalues functionals
u\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
(Proposition
3.2
) to overcome their lack of differentiability. Section
4
contains the proof of the right implication in (
1.9
) (Theorem
4.1
). We introduce a family of relaxed problems
(\mu_{k,\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
that approximate
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and that penalise the vanishing locus of
u
(see (
4.11
) below). We prove that each relaxed problem admits a minimiser
u_{\varepsilon}
and that the family
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
defines a minimising sequence for
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
that converges in a strong enough sense to a minimiser of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. In the course of the proof we rely on the variational theory of Section
3
and we prove in particular that if
u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega),u>0
a.e. attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
,
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is simple and any normalised generalised eigenvector
\varphi
satisfies

u=|\varphi|\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

The latter is the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
and together with (
1.7
) it implies that
\varphi
is a solution of (
1.2
) of energy
\int_{\Omega}|\varphi|^{2^{*}}\,dx=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)^{\frac{n}{2}}
. That this
\varphi
is a least-energy solution then follows from (
1.8
). In Section
5
we prove (Theorem
5.1
) by test-function computations that the strict inequality in (
1.9
) is satisfied under the assumptions of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. We carefully estimate, for a well-chosen family of weights and of
k
-dimensional test subspaces, their contribution to
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. In the non-coercive case, the min-max nature of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
forces us to work with a higher degree of precision. In Section
6
we develop a second-order variational theory for
u\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
: we first expand
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}
to second order along interior variations and use the latter to prove a stability inequality of sorts at minimisers (Proposition
6.2
), from which we deduce the left implication in (
1.9
).
This is the first time, to our knowledge, that a second variation for generalised eigenvalue functionals is computed. Section
7
contains the proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Appendix
A
contains the proof that, in the case
i=0
,
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is exactly the celebrated minimisation problem of
[
13
]
. Appendix
B
proves a compactness result for sign-changing solutions of (
1.2
) that is used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. And finally, Appendix
C
contains a detailed study of the mass function
x\in\Omega\mapsto m_{\lambda}(x)
in the non-coercive case
\lambda\geq\Lambda_{1},\lambda\not\in{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
.

2.
Generalised eigenvalues and their properties

We introduce in this section the notion of generalized eigenvalues that we investigate in this paper. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 3
be any connected open set. In the rest of the paper if
p\in[1,+\infty]
and
u\in L^{p}(\Omega)
we will denote its
L^{p}
norm in
\Omega
by
\|u\|_{p}
. For
u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
,
u\geq 0
a.e., where
2^{*}=\frac{2n}{n-2}
, we let

K_{u}=\Big\{v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable such that }u^{\frac{2}{n-2}}v=0\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\Big\}

and

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega,u^{2^{*}-2}dx)

\displaystyle=\Big\{v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\text{ measurable such that }\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}dx<+\infty\Big\}.

Recall that
2^{*}-2=\frac{4}{n-2}
. We define
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
as

\displaystyle L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)=L^{2}(\Omega,u^{2^{*}-2}dx)/K_{u},

and we endow it with the quotient norm.
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
endowed with the scalar product
(v,w)_{L^{2}_{u}}=\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}vwdx
and the associated norm
\|v\|_{L^{2}_{u}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}dx
is thus a Hilbert space. Denote by
D^{1,2}(\Omega)
the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
for the norm
\|\nabla\cdot\|_{2}
. If
\Omega
is bounded,
D^{1,2}(\Omega)=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Hölder and Sobolev inequalities imply that
D^{1,2}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
and
L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
,
u\geq 0
a.e.,
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, and let
V\subseteq D^{1,2}(\Omega)
be a linear subspace. We say that

\dim_{u}V=k

if there exist
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in D^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
V=\text{Span}(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
and such that the family
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
is free in
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
.

Equivalently,
\dim_{u}V=k
if and only if there exist
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in D^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
V=\text{Span}(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
and
(u^{\frac{2}{n-2}}v_{1},\dots,u^{\frac{2}{n-2}}v_{k})
is free in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and in this case
\dim_{u}V
is also the real dimension of
V
as a vector subspace of
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
, which we denote by
\dim V
. We also define

(2.1)

\displaystyle L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\big\{u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\},u>0\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\big\}.

For
V\subset D^{1,2}(\Omega)
and
u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
,
u\geq 0
a.e., we have

\dim_{u}V\leq\dim V

and it is easily seen that equality holds when
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
.

From now on and until the end of this paper we will assume that
\Omega
is a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
. We recall that for
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
we let
\|u\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=\|\nabla u\|_{2}
, that
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}\leq\cdots
are the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity, of
-\Delta
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and that
\Lambda_{k}
, for
k\geq 1
, are the eigenvalues counted without multiplicity. Recall the convention
\Lambda_{0}=0
, so that
0=\Lambda_{0}<\Lambda_{1}<\cdots
. For
i\geq 1
we denote by
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
the eigenspace associated to
\Lambda_{i}
. We let
N
be the multiplicity map: it is the increasing map
N:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that
N(0)=0,N(1)=1
and, for any
k\geq 0
,

(2.2)

\lambda_{N(k)+1}=\cdots=\lambda_{N(k+1)}=\Lambda_{k+1},

so that the number of eigenvalues equal to
\Lambda_{k+1}
is
N(k+1)-N(k)\geq 1
. Let
\lambda\geq 0
,
k\geq 1
be an integer and
u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
,
u\geq 0
a.e. We define:

(2.3)

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=\inf_{\text{dim}_{u}V=k}\max_{v\in V\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx},

where the infimum is taken over all linear subspaces
V\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
satisfying
\dim_{u}V=k
in the sense of Definition
2.1
. We allow this infimum to be equal to
-\infty
. We also let, for
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

(2.4)

Q_{u}^{\lambda}(v)=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx}.

This Section and the next one are devoted to investigating the properties of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. When
u\equiv 1
, (
2.3
) is the classical min-max definition of the eigenvalues of
-\Delta-\lambda
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. When
u\not\equiv 1
,
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
generalises this definition over the measure space
L^{2}_{u}
. We call it a
generalised eigenvalue of weight
u
. In this section we prove that most of the well-known properties of eigenvalues of
-\Delta
remain true for generalised eigenvalues, up to some small adaptations. We first prove that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
is always finite provided
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, in which case
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
is also simple:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be bounded and smooth. Let
\lambda\geq 0
and
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, where
L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
is as in (
2.1
). Then:

(1)

There exists
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
that attains
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
and satisfies
\|\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\|_{L^{2}_{u}}=1
. It satisfies, in a weak sense,

(2.5)

\big(-\Delta-\lambda\big)\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)=\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

(2)

Denote by
E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\subseteq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
the vector space of solutions of (
2.5
). Then
\dim E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)=\dim_{u}E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)=1
and if
\varphi\in E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\backslash\{0\}
we either have
\varphi>0
a.e. or
\varphi<0
a.e in
\Omega
.

We call
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
the first generalised eigenfunction and
E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
the associated generalised eigenspace.

Proof.

A first important observation is that, by (
2.3
),
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
rewrites as

(2.6)

\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx}.

We first prove that when
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
,
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)>-\infty
. We follow for this the arguments in
[
25
, Proposition 2.2]
. Assume by contradiction that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)=-\infty
and let
(v_{i})_{i\geq 0}
be a minimising sequence for
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
normalised such that
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{i}^{2}dx=1
. By (
2.6
) we then have
\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v_{i}|^{2}-\lambda v_{i}^{2}\big)dx\to-\infty
and thus
\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\to+\infty
as
i\to+\infty
. We let, for
i\geq 1
,
\tilde{v}_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}
. Up to passing to a subsequence there exists
\tilde{v}_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
\tilde{v}_{i}\rightharpoonup\tilde{v}_{0}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\tilde{v}_{i}\to\tilde{v}_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. By the normalisation of
v_{i}
we have

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{v}_{i}^{2}dx=\frac{1}{\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}}\to 0

as
i\to+\infty
, so that Fatou’s lemma yields
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{v}_{0}^{2}dx\leq 0
. Since
u>0
a.e. in
\Omega
, this shows that
\tilde{v}_{0}=0
p.p. in
\Omega
. But for
i
large enough we have
\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v_{i}|^{2}-\lambda v_{i}^{2}\big)dx\leq 0
, so dividing by
\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}
and using that
\tilde{v}_{0}=0
yields
\|\tilde{v}_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}\leq o(1)
as
i\to+\infty
, a contradiction with
\|\tilde{v}_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1
. Hence
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)>-\infty
.

We now prove that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
is attained. Let
(v_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a minimising sequence normalised such that
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{i}^{2}dx=1
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
v_{i}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
. We first claim that
(v_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Assume by contradiction that
\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\to+\infty
as
i\to+\infty
and let
\tilde{v}_{i}=\frac{v_{i}}{\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}
. Then, up to a subsequence,
\tilde{v}_{i}\rightharpoonup\tilde{v}_{0}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\tilde{v}_{i}\to\tilde{v}_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. As before,

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{v}_{i}^{2}dx=\frac{1}{\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}}\to 0

as
i\to+\infty
so that, as before,
\tilde{v}_{0}=0
a.e. in
\Omega
. For
i
large enough we then have

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tilde{v}_{i}|^{2}dx=\lambda\int_{\Omega}\tilde{v}_{i}^{2}dx+\frac{\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)+o(1)}{\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}}=o(1)

as
i\to+\infty
, a contradiction with
\|\tilde{v}_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1
. Hence
(v_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Denote by
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
its weak limit in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. We have
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
and we claim that, up to a subsequence, the following holds:

(2.7)

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}(v_{i}-\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx\to 0\quad\text{ as }i\to+\infty.

We prove (
2.7
). Let
R>0
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}(v_{i}-\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\{u\leq R\}}u^{2^{*}-2}(v_{i}-\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{\{u>R\}}u^{2^{*}-2}(v_{i}-\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx.

Up to passing to a subsequence
v_{i}\to\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, so the first integral in the right-hand side converges to
0
. Since the embedding
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\subset L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
is continuous and
\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}
is bounded we obtain, by Hölder’s inequality,

\limsup_{i\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}(v_{i}-\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx\leq C\Big(\int_{\{u>R\}}u^{2^{*}}dx\Big)^{\frac{2^{*}-2}{2^{*}}}

for some
C>0
independent of
R
. Letting
R\to+\infty
proves (
2.7
). The limiting function
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
thus satisfies
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}(\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx=1
and so
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\neq 0
. By weak lower semicontinuity of the
H^{1}_{0}
norm,
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
attains
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
. That
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
satisfies (
2.5
) follows form simple variational arguments. This proves point
(1)
of Proposition
2.1
.

We now prove point (2), and we adapt here an argument from
[
32
, Proposition 2.4]
. We first prove that every solution of (
2.5
) has constant sign and vanishes on a set of measure zero. Let
\varphi\in E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
be such that
\varphi_{+}=\max(\varphi,0)\neq 0
. We will prove that
|\{\varphi_{+}=0\}|=0
, which will imply that
\varphi\geq 0
a.e. and
|\{\varphi=0\}|=0
. By replacing
\varphi
by
-\varphi
if needed the same argument shows that if
\min(\varphi,0)\neq 0
then
\varphi\leq 0
a.e. and
|\{\varphi=0\}|=0
. Let thus
\varphi
be a solution of (
2.5
) with
\varphi_{+}\neq 0
and let
\psi=\varphi_{+}
. Integrating (
2.5
) against
\psi
shows that
\psi
still attains
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
. Simple variational considerations thus show that
\psi
still satisfies (
2.5
). For
\varepsilon>0
we let
\psi_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+\psi^{2}}
. Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
r>0
be such that
B(x_{0},2r)\subset\subset\Omega
and let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B(x_{0},2r))
be such that
\eta\equiv 1
in
B(x_{0},r)
and
\|\nabla\eta\|_{\infty}\leq 2r^{-1}
. We have

(2.8)

\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\psi}{\psi_{\varepsilon}}\nabla\psi\quad\text{ and }\quad|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\frac{\psi^{2}}{\psi_{\varepsilon}^{2}}|\nabla\psi|^{2}.

Integrating (
2.5
) against
\eta^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}
and using Young’s inequality shows that

(2.9)

\int_{\Omega}\eta^{2}\Bigg(\frac{|\nabla\psi|^{2}}{\psi_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\frac{\psi}{\psi_{\varepsilon}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\nabla\psi|^{2}}{\psi_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\Bigg)dx\leq-\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\int_{\Omega}\eta^{2}u^{2^{*}-2}\frac{\psi}{\psi_{\varepsilon}}dx+2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\eta|^{2}dx.

Since
\psi\leq\psi_{\varepsilon}
by definition, the right-hand side is easily estimated as

\Big|-\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\int_{\Omega}\eta^{2}u^{2^{*}-2}\frac{\psi}{\psi_{\varepsilon}}dx+2\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\eta|^{2}dx\Big|\leq C(1+\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2})r^{n-2},

where
C>0
is independent of
u
and
r
. Since
\eta^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{-2}
and
\eta^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{-3}
are integrable for every
\varepsilon>0
and non-increasing in
\varepsilon
the monotone convergence theorem applies separately to the two integrands in the left-hand side of (
2.9
): passing (
2.9
) to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
then gives

\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\frac{|\nabla\psi|^{2}}{\psi^{2}}dx\leq C(n,u)r^{n-2}.

By (
2.8
) and since
\psi_{\varepsilon}\geq\psi
we have
|\nabla\ln\psi_{\varepsilon}|=\frac{|\nabla\psi_{\varepsilon}|}{\psi_{\varepsilon}}\leq\frac{|\nabla\psi|}{\psi}

a.e. in
\Omega
. The latter inequalities then shows that for any
\varepsilon
small enough we have

\int_{B(x_{0},r)}|\nabla\ln\psi_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\leq C(n,u)r^{n-2},

where
C(n,u)
is independent of
\varepsilon
. With Hölder’s inequality we then get

\int_{B(x_{0},s)}|\nabla\ln\psi_{\varepsilon}|dx\leq Cs^{n-1}\quad\text{ for all }s\leq r.

John-Nirenberg’s inequality (see e.g.
[
31
, Theorem
7.21
]
) shows the existence of some
p>0
and
C
, independent of
\varepsilon
and depending only on
n,u,r
, such that

\int_{B(x_{0},s)}e^{p|w_{\varepsilon}|}dx\leq C

for all
s\leq r
, where we have let
w_{\varepsilon}=\ln\psi_{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{|B(x_{0},s)|}\int_{B(x_{0},s)}\ln\psi_{\varepsilon}dx
. Simple arguments then show that

\Big(\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{p}dx\Big)\Big(\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\psi_{\varepsilon}^{-p}dx\Big)\leq C(n,u,r).

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
by Fatou’s lemma and monotone convergence then yields

\Big(\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\psi^{p}dx\Big)\Big(\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\psi^{-p}dx\Big)\leq C(n,u,r).

Since
\psi\neq 0
by assumption this shows that
\int_{B(x_{0},r)}\psi^{-p}dx<+\infty
. Hence
\{\psi=0\}\cap B(x_{0},r)
has measure zero for every ball
B(x_{0},2r)
compactly supported in
\Omega
and thus
\{\psi=0\}
has measure zero, which shows that
\varphi>0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

We finally show that
E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
is one-dimensional. Let
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be two solutions of (
2.5
) satisfying
\int_{\Omega}\psi_{1}dx=\int_{\Omega}\psi_{2}dx=1
. In particular
\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}
still solves (
2.5
). Assume first that
(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})_{+}\neq 0
. The previous argument shows that
\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}>0
a.e. which is impossible since
\int_{\Omega}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})dx=0
. Hence
\psi_{1}-\psi_{2}\leq 0
. The same argument for
(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})_{-}
shows that
\psi_{1}=\psi_{2}
a.e. in
\Omega
.
∎

We now consider generalised eigenvalues of higher order. We prove that for
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
all the
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
,
k\geq 1
, are attained, we construct generalised eigenvectors and prove an equivalent characterisation of the
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be bounded and smooth. Let
\lambda\geq 0
and
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
. Then

(1)

For any
k\geq 1
,
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)>-\infty
and it is attained at some generalised eigenfunction
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
that satisfies weakly

(2.10)

\big(-\Delta-\lambda\big)\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

The functions
(\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u))_{k\geq 1}
are normalised to satisfy

(2.11)

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{i}^{\lambda}(u)\varphi_{j}^{\lambda}(u)dx=\delta_{ij}\quad\text{ for }i\neq j,

and for
k\geq 2
each
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is sign-changing.

(2)

The following equivalent characterisation of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
holds: for any
k\geq 1
,

(2.12)

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=\inf_{v\in S_{k-1}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx}

where

\displaystyle S_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\Big\{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}\text{ such that }\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{i}^{\lambda}(u)vdx=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq k-1\Big\}.

(3)

For
k\geq 1
we denote by
E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
the vector space of solutions of (
2.10
). Then
\dim E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)<+\infty
.

(4)

We have
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\leq\mu_{k+1}^{\lambda}(u)
for any
k\geq 1
and there exists
\gamma=\gamma(u)>0
such that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)+\gamma\leq\mu_{2}^{\lambda}(u)
.

(5)

We have
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\to+\infty
as
k\to+\infty
.

(6)

If there exist
\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\big(-\triangle-\lambda\big)\varphi=\mu u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi
in
\Omega
then
\mu=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
for some
k\geq 1
and
\varphi\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
.

When we say that for
k\geq 2
each
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is sign-changing we mean that

|\{\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)>0\}|\cdot|\{\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)<0\}|>0.

Proof.

Points (1) and (2) for
k=1
have been proven in Proposition
2.1
. We prove (1) and (2) by induction: assume that (1) and (2) are true for some integer
k-1\geq 1
, i.e. that generalised eigenvectors
(\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u),\dots,\varphi_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u))
attaining
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u),\dots,\mu_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u)
, satisfying (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) have been constructed, and that (
2.12
) holds for
\mu_{\ell}^{\lambda}(u)
for all
\ell\leq k-1
. We first prove that (
2.12
) holds for
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. We denote by
\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
the right-hand side in (
2.12
). Let
v_{0}\in S_{k-1}
. Then, by (
2.11
),

V:=\text{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u),\dots,\varphi_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u),v_{0}\Big\}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

satisfies
\dim_{u}V=k
. Up to multiplying
v_{0}
by a constant we can assume that
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{0}^{2}dx=1
. The induction property shows that
\tilde{\mu}_{i}^{\lambda}(u)=\mu_{i}^{\lambda}(u)
for any
1\leq i\leq k-1
, so that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\leq\cdots\leq\mu_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u)
by (
2.12
). Since
v_{0}\in S_{k-1}
and
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{0}^{2}dx=1
we have, by definition of
\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
,

(2.13)

\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v_{0}|^{2}-\lambda v_{0}^{2}\right)\,dx\geq\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\geq\mu_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u).

Since
\dim_{u}V=k
it is admissible in the definition of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. Using (
2.3
), (
2.10
), (
2.11
), the normalisation of
v_{0}
and (
2.13
) we get

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\leq\max_{v\in V\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx}=\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v_{0}|^{2}-\lambda v_{0}^{2}\right)dx.

Since
v_{0}\in S_{k-1}
is arbitrary this proves
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\leq\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. For the reverse inequality we let
V\subseteq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be such that
\dim_{u}V=k
. Then

\text{Span}(\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u),\dots,\varphi_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u))^{\perp_{u}}\cap V\neq\{0\}\quad\text{ in }L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)

by simple dimensional considerations, where
\perp_{u}
denotes the orthogonal complement in
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
for
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)}
. We can thus let
v_{0}\in V\cap S_{k-1}
. We then have

\displaystyle\max_{v\in V\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v_{0}|^{2}-\lambda v_{0}^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{0}^{2}\,dx}\geq\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u),

where the second inequality follows from the definition of
\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
in (
2.12
). Taking the infimum over subspaces
V
finally yields
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\geq\tilde{\mu}_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
and proves (
2.12
).

Once (
2.12
) has been established the existence of
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
follows again from a standard minimisation procedure. Assume indeed that
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u),\dots,\varphi_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u)
have been constructed. We use (
2.12
) and let
(v_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a minimising sequence for
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
in
S_{k-1}
normalised as
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{i}^{2}dx=1
. We first observe that
(v_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, which follows by a straightforward adaptation of the proof of Proposition
2.1
in the
k=1
case. We denote by
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
the weak limit of
(v_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence. Then
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}(\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx=1
holds, and this is proven by mimicking the proof of (
2.7
). Also,
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\in S_{k-1}
by weak convergence, which yields (
2.11
). The lower semi-continuity of the
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
norm then shows that
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. The proof of (
2.10
) again follows from standard variational arguments. That every
k\geq 2
is sign-changing follows from (
2.11
) and the fact that
|\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)|>0
a.e. in
\Omega
by Proposition
2.1
. This proves points (1) and (2) of Proposition
2.2
for any
k\geq 1
.

We now prove (3). In Proposition
2.1
we already proved that
\dim E_{1}^{\lambda}(u)=1
. Let
k\geq 1
and let
(\varphi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
be a sequence of solutions of (
2.10
) associated to
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
satisfying
\|\varphi_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1
. We can assume, up to passing to a subsequence, that
\varphi_{m}\rightharpoonup\varphi_{0}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
as
m\to+\infty
. Since
\varphi_{m}\to\varphi_{0}
strongly in
L^{\frac{n}{n-2}}(\Omega)
it is easily seen that
\varphi_{0}\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. We now claim that

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{0})^{2}dx\to 0\quad\text{ as }m\to+\infty.

The proof is identical to the proof of (
2.7
) and we omit it here. Since
\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{0}
still satisfies (
2.10
) integrating it against
\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{0}
and using the latter yields

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\varphi_{m}-\varphi_{0})|^{2}dx=o(1)\quad\text{ as }m\to+\infty,

so that
\varphi_{m}\to\varphi_{0}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and hence the unit ball of
E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
for
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{0}}
is compact. This concludes the proof of (3).

The first part of point (4) easily follows from the equivalent characterisation (
2.12
). That
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)<\mu_{2}^{\lambda}(u)
for any
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
follows from point (2) of Proposition
2.1
and from (
2.10
) and (
2.11
).

We now prove point (5). We proceed by contradiction and assume that the sequence
(\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u))_{k\geq 1}
is bounded. We claim that, as a consequence,
E=\overline{\bigoplus_{k\geq 1}E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)}
is finite-dimensional, where the closure is taken in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
for the
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}_{0}}
norm. This will be an obvious contradiction since the family
(\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u))_{k\geq 1}
is free in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
by (
2.11
). To prove that
E
is finite-dimensional, we let
(\varphi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
be a sequence of functions in the unit ball of
E
. For any
m\geq 0
there exists in particular
\mu_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\varphi_{m}
satisfies

\big(-\Delta-\lambda\big)\varphi_{m}=\mu_{m}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Since
\|\varphi_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1
and
(\mu_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is bounded, the same arguments as in the proof of point (3) show that
(\varphi_{m})_{m\geq 0}
has a strongly convergent subsequence as
m\to+\infty
, and hence that the unit ball of
E
is compact, which is a contradiction.

We finally prove point (6). Assume that there exists
\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\big(-\triangle-\lambda\big)\varphi=\mu u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi
in
\Omega
and assume that
\mu\not\in(\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u))_{k\geq 1}
. Let
k\geq 1
be such that
\mu_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u)<\mu<\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
(where we have let
\mu_{0}^{\lambda}(u)=-\infty
by convention) and let

V=\text{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u),\dots,\varphi_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u),\varphi\Big\}.

By (
2.10
) we have
(\varphi,\varphi_{i}^{\lambda}(u))_{L^{2}_{u}}=0
for all
1\leq i\leq k-1
. As a consequence, the variational characterisation (
2.3
) of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
shows that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\leq\mu
, which is a contradiction. Hence
\mu=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
for some
k\geq 1
. This also proves that for all
k\geq 1
we have

E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=\text{Span}\Big\{\varphi_{i}^{\lambda}(u);i\geq 1,\mu_{i}^{\lambda}(u)=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\Big\},

and concludes the proof of (6) and of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

We conclude this section by proving that every solution of (
1.2
) is a generalised eigenvector. This property is the main observation that will allow us to construct least-energy solutions of (
1.2
) via
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
(see Theorem
4.1
below).

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}
and let
\tilde{u}=\frac{u}{\|u\|_{2^{*}}}
. Then
u
is a solution of (
1.2
) if and only if there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(|\tilde{u}|)=\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
and
\tilde{u}\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(|\tilde{u}|)
.

In other words, up to a scaling factor,
u
solves (
1.2
) if and only if it is a generalised eigenvector, with positive generalised eigenvalue, associated to the weight
|u|
.

Proof.

Assume first that
u
satisfies (
1.2
). It is then easily seen that
\tilde{u}
satisfies

-\triangle\tilde{u}-\lambda\tilde{u}=\mu|\tilde{u}|^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{u}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

where
\mu=\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
. Point (6) of Proposition
2.2
applies and shows that there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(|\tilde{u}|)=\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
and such that
\tilde{u}\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(|\tilde{u}|)
. Assume now that there exists
k\geq 1
such that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(|\tilde{u}|)=\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
and
\tilde{u}\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(|\tilde{u}|)
. By (
2.10
)
\tilde{u}
then satisfies

\big(-\triangle-\lambda\big)\tilde{u}=\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}|\tilde{u}|^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{u}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

which is equivalent to saying that
u
satisfies (
1.2
).
∎

3.
Variational theory for generalised eigenvalues

In this section we develop a variational theory for generalised eigenvalues. We first investigate their sign:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be bounded and smooth and let
\lambda\geq 0
. Let
i\geq 0
be such that
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
k=N(i)+1\geq 1
. We have

(3.1)

\inf_{u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}>0

and, for any
1\leq p\leq k-1
,

(3.2)

\sup_{u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)}\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}\leq 0.

We recall that
L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
is defined in (
2.1
) and the multiplicity function
N
is defined in (
2.2
). The normalisation factor
\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
is required so that the quantities appearing in (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) are scale-invariant, as is easily seen by (
2.3
). Proposition
3.1
shows in particular that if
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
k=N(i)+1
, for any
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
we have

\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)\leq 0\text{ for all }p\leq k-1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)>0\text{ for all }p\geq k.

When
\lambda
belongs to a fixed set
[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
between two consecutive eigenvalues the generalised eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
, for
k=N(i)+1
, is thus the least positive one, regardless of
u
. We will call it the
principal
generalised eigenvalue in this paper.

Proof.

Let
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
and let
(\varphi_{p}^{\lambda}(u))_{p\geq 1}
be the eigenvectors given by Proposition
2.2
. Let
i\geq 0
be such that
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. We first prove that
\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)\geq 0
for all
p\geq k
, where
k=N(i)+1
. Consider first the case
u\equiv 1
: by (
2.3
),

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(1)=\lambda_{k}-\lambda=\Lambda_{i+1}-\lambda>0

by definition of
N
in (
2.2
). Let
V\subseteq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be such that
\dim_{u}V=\dim V=k
. It is also admissible in the definition (
2.3
) for
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(1)
: since
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(1)>0
there thus exists
v_{0}\in V\backslash\{0\}
such that
\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v_{0}|^{2}-\lambda v_{0}^{2}\big)dx>0
. Since
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{0}^{2}dx>0
we then have

\max_{v\in V\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}\,dx}\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v_{0}|^{2}-\lambda v_{0}^{2}\right)\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v_{0}^{2}\,dx}>0.

This holds true for any
V
with
\dim_{u}V=k
and thus
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\geq 0
by (
2.3
). Similar arguments show that
\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)\leq 0
for every
p\leq k-1
, which proves (
3.2
).

We now claim that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)>0
for all
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
. Assume by contradiction that there exists
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
such that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=0
and let
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
be the
k
-th eigenfunction associated to
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
by Proposition
2.2
. It satisfies
-\Delta\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)-\lambda\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=0
in
\Omega
. If
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
, so that
\lambda\not\in{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
, this is an obvious contradiction. If
\lambda=\Lambda_{i}
,
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\in E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
, where we recall that
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
is the
i
-th eigenspace for
-\Delta
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and thus satisfies
-\Delta\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)-\Lambda_{i}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=0
in
\Omega
. Let
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k-1}
be the eigenvectors of
-\Delta
associated to
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k-1}
. Integrating the latter against
\varphi_{\ell}
shows that
\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{\ell}\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)dx=0
for all
1\leq\ell\leq k-1
, but this would then imply that

\dim\Big(\bigoplus_{p=1..i}E_{\Lambda_{p}}(\Omega)\Big)\geq k=N(i)+1,

which contradicts the definition of
N
in (
2.2
). Hence
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)>0
.

We now prove (
3.1
). We proceed by contradiction and assume that there is a sequence
(u_{m})_{m\geq 1}
in
L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
,
\|u_{m}\|_{2^{*}}=1
, such that
\mu_{k,m}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})\to 0
as
m\to+\infty
. Let
\varphi_{k,m}
be a generalized eigenvector associated to
\mu_{k,m}
by Proposition
2.2
and normalised by
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx=1
. It satisfies:

(3.3)

-\Delta\varphi_{k,m}-\lambda\varphi_{k,m}=\mu_{k,m}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Assume first that
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
, so that
\lambda\not\in{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
. Then
-\Delta-\lambda
is invertible and thus there exists
C=C_{\lambda}
such that

\|\varphi_{k,m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq C\mu_{k,m}\|u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\|_{\frac{2n}{n+2}}.

Hölder’s inequality shows that

(3.4)

\|u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\|_{\frac{2n}{n+2}}^{\frac{2n}{n+2}}\leq\Big(\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}}dx\Big)^{\frac{2}{n+2}}\Big(\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx\Big)^{\frac{n}{n+2}}=1,

and since
\mu_{k,m}\to 0
we thus obtain that
\|\varphi_{k,m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=o(1)
as
m\to+\infty
. Since
\|u_{m}\|_{2^{*}}=1
this contradicts the normalisation condition
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx=1
. Assume now that
\lambda=\Lambda_{i}
. We split
\varphi_{k,m}
as

\varphi_{k,m}=v_{m}+z_{m},

where
z_{m}\in E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
and
v_{m}
is
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-orthogonal to
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
. Since
-\Delta z_{m}-\Lambda_{i}z_{m}=0
, and by (
3.3
),
v_{m}
satisfies

(3.5)

-\Delta v_{m}-\Lambda_{i}v_{m}=\mu_{k,m}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

By definition
-\Delta-\Lambda_{i}
is invertible in
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)^{\perp}
and the same argument as above shows that
\|v_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=o(1)
as
m\to+\infty
. Thus
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}v_{m}^{2}dx=o(1)
and, by Cauchy-Schwarz’s inequality,
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}v_{m}dx=o(1)
as
m\to+\infty
. Integrating (
3.5
) against
z_{m}
thus yields, since
\mu_{k,m}>0
for all
m\geq 0
,

0=\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}z_{m}=\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx-\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}v_{m}dx=1+o(1),

a contradiction. This proves (
3.1
).
∎

One of the main difficulties that we face is that the functionals
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)\mapsto\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)
are not even differentiable in general. In the next Proposition we prove however that we can compute directional derivatives of the principal eigenvalue
u\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
along interior variations of sorts at a given weight
u
:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be bounded and smooth and let
\lambda\geq\Lambda_{1}
. Let
i\geq 1
be such that
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
k=N(i)+1
. Let
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
,
h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let

u_{t}=(1+th)u.

Then the left and right derivatives of
t\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
at
0
exist and are given by

(3.6)

\frac{d}{dt}_{|t=0_{+}}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})=\inf_{\varphi\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\backslash\{0\}}\Bigg(-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\frac{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi^{2}dx}\Bigg)

and

(3.7)

\frac{d}{dt}_{|t=0_{-}}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})=\sup_{\varphi\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\backslash\{0\}}\Bigg(-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\frac{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi^{2}dx}\Bigg).

We point out that, when
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
for some
i\geq 1
, Proposition
3.2
only holds for the specific value
k=N(i)+1
, that is for the principal generalised eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. The reason for this is as follows: left and right differentiability of
t\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
art
0
relies on a strict spectral gap between
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
and all the
\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
,
p\leq k-1
, that needs to be uniform in
t
for
t
small enough (see (
3.9
) below). When
k=N(i)+1
this gap easily follows from Proposition
3.1
. Note that we do
not
assume a priori the simplicity of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
in Proposition
3.2
. For a generalised eigenvalue
\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)
with
p\geq k
it is possible to compute directional derivatives, but the expressions are in general more complicated than (
3.6
) and (
3.7
). See for instance

4.
Minimising the principal generalised eigenvalue

4.1.
Minimisation of
\mu_{k,\lambda}(u)
under a strict inequality

Let
\lambda\geq 0
and let
i\geq 0
such that
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. Throughout this section and until the end of this paper we will always let

k=N(i)+1

where
N
is the multiplicity function for
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
defined in (
2.2
). As we proved in Proposition
3.1

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is, regardless of
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, the least positive generalised eigenvalue associated to
u
. We investigate in this section the following variational problem which consists in minimising
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
over all weights
u
:

(4.1)

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\inf_{u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}.

The quantity in the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is invariant if
u
is scaled by a constant, and it is easy to see that we also have

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\inf_{u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega),\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u).

When
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})
, i.e. when
i=0
, we have
k=1
and simple arguments show that

\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\big)dx}{\big(\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2^{*}}dx\big)^{\frac{n-2}{n}}},

and that both these quantities are simultaneously attained (see Lemma
A.1
below). By the celebrated results in
[
13
]
, minimising
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
when
0\leq\lambda\leq\Lambda_{1}
(when possible) yields a least-energy positive solution of (
1.2
). We will prove in this section that, for any
i\geq 1
, minimising
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
with
k=N(i)+1
similarly yields a least-energy sign-changing solution of (
1.2
).

A first property of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be bounded and smooth, let
i\geq 0
and let
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. Then

(4.2)

0<\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\leq K_{n}^{-2},

where
K_{n}
is as in (
1.1
).

Proof.

That
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)>0
follows from Proposition
3.1
, in particular from (
3.1
). We thus only have to prove that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\leq K_{n}^{-2}
. We define

(4.3)

U_{0}(x)=\left(1+\frac{|x|^{2}}{n(n-2)}\right)^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It is well-known (see
[
7
,
65
]
) that
U_{0}
satisfies

(4.4)

-\Delta U_{0}=\,U_{0}^{2^{*}-1}\hbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

(4.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla U_{0}|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}U_{0}^{2^{*}}dx=K_{n}^{-n}.

In particular
U_{0}
attains the infimum in (
1.1
). We let
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k-1}
be eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, normalised by
\|\varphi_{i}\|_{2}=1
, respectively associated to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k-1}
. For
1\leq\ell,p,q\leq k-1
we thus have

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\ell}\varphi_{\ell}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla\varphi_{p},\nabla\varphi_{q}\rangle dx

\displaystyle=\lambda_{p}\delta_{pq}\text{ and }\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{p}\varphi_{q}dx=\delta_{pq}.

Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
be fixed. For
\varepsilon>0
we define, for
x\in\Omega
,
B_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}U_{0}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)
and we let
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the unique solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}&=B_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-1}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}&=0\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

It is easily seen by the maximum principle that
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}>0
in
\Omega
and, since
x_{0}\in\Omega
is fixed, that
\|\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}-B_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We let in what follows

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}=\frac{\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}}{\|\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}},

so that
u_{\varepsilon}>0
in
\Omega
. Straightforward computations show that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}dx=K_{n}^{-n}+o(1),\quad\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}dx=o(1),

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-1}\varphi_{\ell}\,dx=o(1),\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{\ell}u_{\varepsilon}dx=o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
(see for instance
[
13
]
). We let

V_{\varepsilon}=\text{Span}\big(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k-1},u_{\varepsilon}\big),

which is a subset of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. We first observe that
\dim V_{\varepsilon}=k
: this follows from (
4.6
) and the fact that the Gram determinant of
(\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{k-1},u_{\varepsilon})
for the
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
scalar product converges to
\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{k-1}K_{n}^{-2}>0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. As a consequence,
V_{\varepsilon}
is an admissible subspace to compute
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
for every
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
. Let
\eta>0
be fixed. For any
\varepsilon>0
we let
v_{\varepsilon}\in V_{\varepsilon}\backslash\{0\}
be such that

Q_{u_{\varepsilon}+\eta}^{\lambda}(v_{\varepsilon})=\max_{v\in V_{\varepsilon}\backslash\{0\}}Q_{u_{\varepsilon}+\eta}^{\lambda}(v),

where
Q_{u_{\varepsilon}+\eta}^{\lambda}
is as in (
2.4
). We let
\alpha_{\varepsilon}=(\alpha_{1,\varepsilon},\cdots,\alpha_{k,\varepsilon})\in\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
be such that
v_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{\ell,\varepsilon}\varphi_{\ell}+\alpha_{k,\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}
. Straightforward computations using (
4.6
) show that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla v_{\varepsilon}|^{2}-\lambda v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}(\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda)\alpha_{\ell,\varepsilon}^{2}+\alpha_{k,\varepsilon}^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|^{2}dx+o(1)

and that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big(u_{\varepsilon}+\eta\big)^{2^{*}-2}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{k,\varepsilon}^{2}+\eta^{2^{*}-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{\ell,\varepsilon}^{2}+o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Assume first that, up to a subsequence,
\alpha_{k,\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, so that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{\ell,\varepsilon}^{2}\to 1
. Since
\lambda\geq\lambda_{\ell}
for all
1\leq\ell\leq k-1
, (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) show that
\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}Q_{u_{\varepsilon}+\eta}^{\lambda}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq 0
, and hence that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\leq 0
. We may thus assume that, up to a subsequence,
\alpha_{k,\varepsilon}\not\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Equation (
4.8
) then shows that

\int_{\Omega}\big(u_{\varepsilon}+\eta\big)^{2^{*}-2}v_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dx\geq(1+o(1))\alpha_{k,\varepsilon}^{2},

so that combining the latter with (
4.7
), using again (
4.6
) and since
\lambda\geq\lambda_{\ell}
for all
1\leq\ell\leq k-1
, we have

Q_{u_{\varepsilon}+\eta}^{\lambda}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq K_{n}^{-2}+o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
\|u_{\varepsilon}+\eta\|_{2^{*}}\leq 1+\eta|\Omega|^{\frac{n-2}{2n}}
we obtain in the end that

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\leq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon}+\eta)\|u_{\varepsilon}+\eta\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}\leq K_{n}^{-2}\big(1+\eta|\Omega|^{\frac{n-2}{2n}}\big)^{\frac{4}{n-2}}+o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Letting first
\varepsilon\to 0
then
\eta\to 0
concludes the proof of Proposition
4.1
.
∎

We now turn to the main result of this section. It states that (
4.1
) is achieved provided the second inequality in (
4.2
) is also strict:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
, be bounded and smooth. Let
i\geq 0
,
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
k=N(i)+1
. Assume that

(4.9)

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)<K_{n}^{-2}

holds. Then
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained, ie there exists
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
such that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)>0
. Furthermore:

(1)

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is simple, i.e.
\dim E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=1
. In particular there is a unique generalised eigenvector
\varphi=\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
associated to
u
by Proposition
2.2
and normalised by
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi^{2}dx=1
, which changes sign if
i\geq 1
.

(2)

We have
u=|\varphi|
. As a consequence,
\psi:={\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)}^{\frac{n-2}{4}}\varphi
satisfies
\int_{\Omega}|\psi|^{2^{*}}dx=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)^{\frac{n}{2}}
and is a non-zero solution of (
1.2
):

-\Delta\psi-\lambda\psi=|\psi|^{2^{*}-2}\psi\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Also,
\psi\in C^{3,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
0<\alpha<1
, and if
i\geq 1

\psi
changes sign.

(3)

The solution
\psi
constructed in (2) is a least-energy solution of (
1.2
), in the sense that

\int_{\Omega}|\psi|^{2^{*}}dx=\inf_{w\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}\backslash\{0\}}\int_{\Omega}|w|^{2^{*}}dx

where
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is the set of all
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
solutions of (
1.2
).

(4)

\psi
has at most
k=N(i)+1
nodal domains. If
i=1
,
\psi
has exactly two nodal domains and if
i=0
it can be assumed to be positive in
\Omega
.

Theorem
4.1
shows that the minimisation problem (
4.1
) is the direct counterpart, in the case
\lambda\geq\Lambda_{1}
, of the celebrated Brézis-Nirenberg
[
13
]
minimisation problem in the coercive case
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1}
). When
i\geq 1
Theorem
4.1
shows that achieving
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
yields a non-zero solution of (
1.2
) of least energy which changes sign. A crucial feature of Theorem
4.1
is that if
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
then
E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is one-dimensional. We prove this in the course of the proof and we do not assume it
a priori
. We also prove that
\dim E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=1
implies the fundamental relation

(4.10)

u=|\varphi|,

where
\varphi
is, up to
\pm 1
, the unique normalised eigenvector in
E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
. Relation (
4.10
) has to be understood as the Euler-Lagrange equation associated to the non-differentiable minimisation problem (
4.1
). That
\dim E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)=1
follows from the uniform spectral gap of Proposition
3.1
. For this reason Theorem
4.1
only applies to the
principal
generalised eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
, with
k=N(i)+1
. The problem of minimising
u\mapsto\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
for some
p>k
can also be considered, and Euler-Lagrange equations in this setting are in general more complicated: see
[
37
]
.

We briefly explain the strategy of proof of Theorem
4.1
. Unlike in the coercive case
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})
, and due to the variational characterisation of generalised eigenvalues, a control on
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
does not immediately ensure a strong
L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
control on a sequence of minimising weights. To prove Theorem
4.1
we therefore introduce a family of relaxations of (
4.1
): we construct minimising weights for each perturbed variational problem and prove that these weights, together with their associated families of generalised eigenfunctions, converge strongly enough. The relaxation is as follows: for any
\varepsilon>0
we let

X_{\varepsilon}=\Big\{u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)\text{ such that }\int_{\Omega}u^{-\varepsilon}dx<+\infty\Big\}.

For
u\in X_{\varepsilon}
we introduce the following functional:

(4.11)

F_{k,\varepsilon}(u)=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}+\varepsilon\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{-\varepsilon}dx\Big)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}

and consider the associated minimisation problem:

(4.12)

\mu_{k,\varepsilon}=\inf_{u\in X_{\varepsilon},\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1}F_{k,\varepsilon}(u).

In Lemma
4.2
below we first prove that, under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
,
\mu_{k,\varepsilon}
is attained, i.e. there is
u_{\varepsilon}\in X_{\varepsilon}
with
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}=1
such that
F_{k,\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\mu_{k,\varepsilon}
. The minimality of
u_{\varepsilon}
then allows us to show, in Lemma
4.3
below, that the eigenspace
E_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})
associated to
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})
is one-dimensional, which we then use in Lemma
4.4
to obtain an Euler-Lagrange equation for
u_{\varepsilon}
. This equation relates
u_{\varepsilon}
and its normalised eigenvector
\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})
(see (
4.28
) below) and although it is more involved than (
4.10
) it allows us to prove that
u_{\varepsilon}
converges towards a minimiser of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Assumption (
4.9
) is used at every step in the proof, both to construct
u_{\varepsilon}
and to prove the compactness of families of eigenfunctions. The functional
F_{k,\varepsilon}
in (
4.11
) is an adaptation of the relaxation introduced in
[
32
]
. The correction term
\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u^{-\varepsilon}dx
has two main advantages: it breaks the scale invariance of problem (
4.1
) and ensures that minimisers of
F_{k,\varepsilon}
are in
L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
(which is not closed under strong convergence in
L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
). Knowing that
u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
is crucial to apply Proposition
3.2
and to obtain the Euler-Lagrange equation (
4.28
) for
u_{\varepsilon}
. Our analysis here is more involved than the one in
[
32
]
since we consider positive eigenvalues and our eigenvalues equations are thus energy-critical with focusing sign. Observe that as soon as
k\geq 2
(ie
i\geq 1
) it is highly unlikely that
every
minimising sequence for eigenvalue-optimisation problems like
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
converges, and this is why we have to define a relaxation of (
4.1
). For problem (
4.1
) this explains why the non-coercive case
\lambda\geq\Lambda_{1}
is considerably more difficult than the coercive case. Let us finally mention that other relaxations of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
may be considered: we refer for instance to
[
37
]
where energy-subcritical relaxations are considered.

4.2.
Minimisers of the relaxed problem

We first prove that (
4.9
) remains true for
F_{k,\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon
small enough:

Lemma 4.1
.

We have:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{k,\varepsilon}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega).

As a consequence, for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we have

(4.13)

\mu_{k,\varepsilon}<K_{n}^{-2}.

Proof.

Let
\eta>0
and
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),u_{0}>0
in
\overline{\Omega}
, with
\|u_{0}\|_{2^{*}}=1
and
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{0})\leq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+\eta
. By dominated convergence we have
\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{-\varepsilon}dx\to|\Omega|
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Thus

\mu_{k,\varepsilon}\leq F_{k,\varepsilon}(u_{0})\leq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+\eta+o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
, which then gives
\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{k,\varepsilon}\leq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+\eta
. Letting
\eta\to 0
proves that
\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{k,\varepsilon}\leq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. For the reverse inequality, remark that, by (
4.11
),
F_{k,\varepsilon}(u)\geq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
for any
u\in X_{\varepsilon}
. Taking the infimum over
u\in X_{\varepsilon}
and then the
\liminf
as
\varepsilon\to 0
yields
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mu_{k,\varepsilon}\geq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
, which proves the first part of the Lemma. Estimate (
4.13
) then follows from (
4.9
).
∎

We then prove that
\mu_{k,\varepsilon}
is attained:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\varepsilon_{0}>0
be such that, for every
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, (
4.13
) is satisfied. Then, for all
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
\mu_{k,\varepsilon}
is attained at some function
u_{\varepsilon}\in X_{\varepsilon}
with
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}=1
.

Proof.

We assume that
\varepsilon>0
is fixed small enough so that (
4.13
) is satisfied. We let
(u_{m})_{m\geq 1}
be a minimising sequence for
\mu_{k,\varepsilon}
:
u_{m}\in X_{\varepsilon}
,
\|u_{m}\|_{2^{*}}=1
and

(4.14)

F_{k,\varepsilon}(u_{m})=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{-\varepsilon}dx\to\mu_{k,\varepsilon}

as
m\to+\infty
. For every
m\geq 1
,
u_{m}\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, so by Proposition
2.2
a generalised eigenfunction
\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})
exists. We will let for simplicity in what follows
\varphi_{k,m}=\varphi_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})
and
\mu_{k,m}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})
. This function satisfies
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx=1
and

(4.15)

-\Delta\varphi_{k,m}-\lambda\varphi_{k,m}=\mu_{k,m}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Let in what follows
w_{m}=u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}
. Then
(w_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
and there exists
w_{\varepsilon}\in L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
,
w_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
a.e., such that
w_{m}\rightharpoonup w_{\varepsilon}
weakly in
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence, as
m\to+\infty
. Let
u_{\varepsilon}=w_{\varepsilon}^{\frac{1}{2^{*}-2}}\geq 0
a.e. By lower semi-continuity of the
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
norm we have
\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}}dx\leq 1
. Since
t\mapsto t^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{*}-2}}
is convex in
(0,+\infty)
,
u\mapsto\int_{\Omega}u^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{*}-2}}dx
is weakly lower semi-continuous over
\{w\in L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega),w\geq 0\text{ a.e.}\}
. As a consequence of the weak convergence and of (
4.14
) we get

(4.16)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}dx=\int_{\Omega}{w_{\varepsilon}}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{*}-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{m\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}w_{m}^{-\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{*}-2}}dx

\displaystyle=\liminf_{m\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{-\varepsilon}dx\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mu_{k,\varepsilon}<+\infty.

This shows that
u_{\varepsilon}\in X_{\varepsilon}
and hence that
u_{\varepsilon}\neq 0
. By (
4.14
) the sequence
(\mu_{k,m})_{m\geq 1}
is bounded. We claim that there exists
C>0
such that

(4.17)

\|\varphi_{k,m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq C

for all
m\geq 1
. First, if
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
, that is if
\lambda\not\in{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
,
-\Delta-\lambda
is invertible and (
4.17
) follows from (
4.15
) since the right-hand side of (
4.15
) is uniformly bounded in
L^{\frac{2n}{n+2}}(\Omega)
(this is the same argument that we used in (
3.4
)). We may thus assume that
\lambda=\Lambda_{i}
. We proceed as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
: We split
\varphi_{k,m}
as

\varphi_{k,m}=v_{m}+z_{m},

where
z_{m}\in E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
and
v_{m}
is
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-orthogonal to
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
. Since
-\Delta z_{m}-\Lambda_{i}z_{m}=0
, by (
4.15
)
v_{m}
satisfies

-\Delta v_{m}-\Lambda_{i}v_{m}=\mu_{k,m}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

By definition
-\Delta-\Lambda_{i}
is invertible in
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)^{\perp}
and the same argument as above shows that
\|v_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq C
for some
C>0
and for all
m\geq 0
. To prove (
4.17
) we thus proceed by contradiction and assume that
\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\to+\infty
as
m\to+\infty
, and we let
\tilde{\varphi}_{k,m}=\frac{\varphi_{k,m}}{\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}
. Since
(v_{m})_{m\geq 0}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
we have

\tilde{\varphi}_{k,m}=\frac{z_{m}}{\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}+\frac{v_{m}}{\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}=\frac{z_{m}}{\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}+o(1)\quad\text{ in }H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

The function
\tilde{z}_{m}:=\frac{z_{m}}{\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and thus weakly converges to some
Z\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Since
\tilde{z}_{m}\in E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
for every
m\geq 0
and since
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
is finite-dimensional,
\tilde{z}_{m}
strongly converges to
Z
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, and we have
\|Z\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1
and
Z\in E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
. Thus
\tilde{\varphi}_{k,m}
strongly converges to
Z
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
as
m\to+\infty
. The normalisation condition
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx=1
now becomes:

(4.18)

\frac{1}{\|z_{m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}}=\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\varphi}_{k,m}^{2}dx=\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}Z^{2}dx+o(1).

Since
u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\rightharpoonup u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}
in
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
we can pass (
4.18
) to the limit and we obtain

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}Z^{2}dx=0.

Since
u_{\varepsilon}\in X_{\varepsilon}
we have in particular
u_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, which implies that
Z=0
a.e. in
\Omega
. But since
Z\in E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
standard elliptic theory shows that it is smooth, and hence
Z=0
, which contradict
\|Z\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1
. This proves (
4.17
).

We denote by
\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}
the weak limit in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence, of
(\varphi_{k,m})_{m\geq 1}
, and we let
\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\mu_{k,m}
. Since
\varphi_{k,m}
strongly converges to
\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}
in
L^{\frac{n}{n-2}}(\Omega)
by Sobolev’s embedding and since
w_{m}
weakly converges to
w_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}
in
L^{\frac{n}{2}}(\Omega)
we get that
\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}
weakly satisfies

(4.19)

-\Delta\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}-\lambda\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}=\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

We claim that
\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}\neq 0
. Assume otherwise: then
\varphi_{k,m}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
up to a subsequence as
m\to+\infty
. Integrating (
4.15
) against
\varphi_{k,m}
and using Hölder and Sobolev inequalities yields, since
\|u_{m}\|_{2^{*}}=1
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi_{k,m}|^{2}dx+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{k,m}\big(\int_{\Omega}|\varphi_{k,m}|^{2^{*}}dx\big)^{\frac{n-2}{n}}\leq\mu_{k,m}K_{n}^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi_{k,m}|^{2}dx,

where
K_{n}
is the optimal constant in Sobolev’s inequality given by (
1.1
). By (
4.13
) we have
\limsup_{m\to+\infty}\mu_{k,m}<K_{n}^{-2}
, so the latter yields
\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi_{k,m}|^{2}dx=o(1)
. But this is a contradiction with
\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx=1
, hence
\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}\neq 0
.

We now remark that
\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}>0
. Indeed, we have
\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\mu_{k,m}
(up to a subsequence), and
\mu_{k,m}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})\geq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)>0
, where we used Proposition
3.1
. By Proposition
3.1
, and since
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
, the
N(i)
first generalised eigenvalues
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon}),\dots,\mu_{N(i)}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})
are nonpositive. Since
\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}>0
, (
4.19
) implies that

\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}\varphi_{p}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})dx=0\quad\text{ for all }1\leq p\leq k-1.

Thus
V=\text{Span}\big(\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon}),\dots,\varphi_{k-1}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon}),\varphi_{k,\varepsilon}\big)
satisfies
\dim_{u_{\varepsilon}}V=k
and is an admissible subspace in the definition of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})
: (
2.3
) and (
4.19
) then show that

(4.20)

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}.

It remains to let
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}=\frac{u_{\varepsilon}}{\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}}
, so that
\|\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}=1
. Since
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}\leq 1
we have, using (
4.14
), (
4.16
), (
4.20
) for
\varepsilon
small enough:

\displaystyle F_{k,\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon\Big(\int_{\Omega}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}dx\Big)

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}+\varepsilon\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}^{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}dx

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}^{\varepsilon}\Big(\hat{\mu}_{k,\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}dx\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}^{\varepsilon}\liminf_{m\to+\infty}\Big(\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{m})+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u_{m}^{-\varepsilon}dx\Big)=\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}^{\varepsilon}\mu_{k,\varepsilon}.

By (
4.12
) this shows at once that
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}=1
and that
u_{\varepsilon}
attains
\mu_{k,\varepsilon}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

We have
\dim_{u_{\varepsilon}}E_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})=1
.

Proof.

Let
h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. For
|t|<\|h\|_{\infty}^{-1}
we let
u_{\varepsilon,t}=(1+th)u_{\varepsilon}
and

\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon,t}=\frac{(1+th)u_{\varepsilon}}{\|(1+th)u_{\varepsilon}\|_{2^{*}}}.

First, direct computations show that

\frac{d}{dt}_{|t=0}\|u_{\varepsilon,t}\|_{2^{*}}=\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}}hdx\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{d}{dt}_{|t=0}\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon,t}^{-\varepsilon}dx=-\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}hdx.

We now apply Proposition
3.2
: with the latter we obtain that left and right derivatives of
t\mapsto F_{k,\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon,t})
at
t=0
exist and are given by

(4.21)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}_{|t=0_{+}}F_{k,\varepsilon}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon,t})=\inf_{\varphi\in E_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})\backslash\{0\}}\Bigg(-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})\frac{\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi^{2}dx}\Bigg)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\Big[\Big(\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}dx\Big)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}}hdx\Big)-\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{-\varepsilon}hdx\Big]+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{\varepsilon})\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2^{*}}hdx

and

(4.22)

\varepsilon\to 0
:

\text{ either }\quad\frac{|\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}|}{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}\to+\infty\quad\text{ or }\quad|\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}|=O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}).

We let
0\leq L\leq k-1
be the number of indices
\ell\in\{1,\dots,k-1\}
satisfying the first case, which can be assumed to be constant up to passing to a subsequence. Up to relabeling the indices we may assume that
\beta_{1,\varepsilon},\dots,\beta_{L,\varepsilon}
satisfy the first case and we may thus write that

\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}|\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}|\to+\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{\ell=L+1}^{k-1}|\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}|=O(\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}})

Young’s inequality then shows that

\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}|\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}|=o\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{L}\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}^{2}\Big)+O(\varepsilon),

so that (
5.19
) becomes

\displaystyle F(u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\big(\lambda_{\ell}-\lambda+o(1)\big)\beta_{\ell,\varepsilon}^{2}+J_{\lambda}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})+o(\varepsilon)\leq J_{\lambda}(\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon})+o(\varepsilon),

where we again used that
\lambda>\Lambda_{i}
. Using (
5.9
) we finally obtain that

\displaystyle F(u_{\varepsilon},v_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leq K_{3}^{-2}-C_{3}m_{\lambda}(x_{0})\varepsilon+o(\varepsilon)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since
m_{\lambda}(x_{0})>0
by assumption this proves (
5.12
) up to choosing
\varepsilon
small enough and concludes the proof of Theorem
5.1
.
∎

6.
Second-order expansion of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
at a global minimum point

In this section we perform a second-order expansion of the generalised principal eigenvalue at a global minimum point. We obtain a stability inequality of sorts that we use to prove that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
cannot be attained while being equal to
K_{n}^{-2}
. We recall that if
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
, Proposition
4.2
shows that
E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is one-dimensional and spanned by a nonzero function
\varphi_{k}
that satisfies
u=|\varphi_{k}|
and
\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}\,dx=1
. We let in the following
E_{k,0}=E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
and, for any
h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
fixed, we let
\tilde{\Psi}\in E_{k,0}^{\perp_{u}}
, where
\perp_{u}
denotes the orthogonal complement in
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
, be the unique solution in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
of

(6.1)

\displaystyle\big(-\Delta-\lambda-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\big)\tilde{\Psi}

\displaystyle=-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi_{k}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

\tilde{\Psi}
is well-defined since the r.h.s of (
6.1
) integrates to
0
against
\varphi_{k}
. For simplicity we omit the dependence of
\tilde{\Psi}
in
h
in what follows and simply write
\tilde{\Psi}
. The first result that we prove is the following improvement of Proposition
3.2
:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i\geq 0
,
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
k=N(i)+1
where
N
is given by (
2.2
). Consider
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
defined by (
4.1
) and assume that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained at some
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
. Let
h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and, for
t\geq 0
small, let
u_{t}=(1+th)u
and
\mu_{k,t}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
. Then, as
t\to 0
, we have

(6.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{k,t}}{\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=1-(2^{*}-2)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}hdx\Big)t

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\Bigg[(2^{*}-2)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}hdx\Big)^{2}-\frac{2^{*}-3}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}dx

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\Psi}\varphi_{k}h\,dx\Bigg]t^{2}+o(t^{2}),

where
\tilde{\Psi}
is given by (
6.1
).

Proposition
6.1
requires
u
to be a global minimum of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}
, since it heavily relies on the property that
E_{k,0}
is a line.

Proof.

The proof of (
6.2
) follows from the same arguments in the proof of (
3.19
), except that we push the expansions one order further. We use the notations in the proof of Proposition
3.2
. Let
(t_{m})_{m\geq 1},t_{m}>0
be a sequence such that
t_{m}\to 0
as
m\to+\infty
. For
m\geq 1
we will let
\mu_{k,t_{m}}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t_{m}})
. Let, for any
m\geq 1
,
\varphi_{k,m}\in E_{k,t_{m}}
be such that
\int_{\Omega}u_{t_{m}}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}dx=1
. It satisfies

-\Delta\varphi_{k,m}-\lambda\varphi_{k,m}=\mu_{k,t_{m}}u_{t_{m}}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Step
2
of Proposition
3.2
shows that
(\varphi_{k,m})_{m\geq 0}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Using (
3.8
) and since
u_{t_{m}}\to u
strongly in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
E_{k,0}
is spanned by
\varphi_{k}
we may assume that
\varphi_{k,m}\to\varphi_{k}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence and up to sign. For
m\geq 1
we let

\Psi_{1,m}=\varphi_{k,m}-\Pi_{k,0}(\varphi_{k,m}).

Using Proposition
4.2
it is easily seen that
\Psi_{1,m}
satisfies

(6.3)

\displaystyle\big(-\Delta

\displaystyle-\lambda-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\big)\Psi_{1,m}

\displaystyle=-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}\cdot t_{m}

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi_{k}\cdot t_{m}+t_{m}\eta_{m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

where
\|\eta_{m}\|_{\infty}=o(1)
as
m\to+\infty
. Since
u\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, standard elliptic theory shows that there exists
C>0
such that for any function
f\in E_{k,0}^{\perp_{u}}
we have

(6.4)

\|f\|_{H^{1}_{0}}\leq C\|\big(-\Delta-\lambda-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\big)f\|_{H^{-1}}.

Since
\Psi_{1,m}\in E_{k,0}^{\perp_{u}}
for all
m\geq 1
and by (
6.3
) we thus obtain first that
\|\Psi_{1,m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=O(t_{m})
and then that

\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}:=\frac{\Psi_{1,m}}{t_{m}}
weakly converges in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, up to a subsequence, to the unique solution
\tilde{\Psi}
of (
6.1
). By (
6.1
) and (
6.3
),
\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}-\tilde{\Psi}
satisfies

\big(-\Delta-\lambda-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\big)(\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}-\tilde{\Psi})=\eta_{m}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

so that using again (
6.4
) we also obtain that

(6.5)

\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}\to\tilde{\Psi}\quad\text{ in }H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\quad\text{ as }m\to+\infty.

Independently, since
E_{k,0}
is one-dimensional by Proposition
4.2
and since
\varphi_{k,m}\to\varphi_{k}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence
(\varepsilon_{m})_{m\geq 1}
, with
\lim_{m\to+\infty}\varepsilon_{m}=0
, such that

\Pi_{k,0}(\varphi_{k,m})-\varphi_{k}=\varepsilon_{m}\varphi_{k}.

We claim that

(6.6)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{m}=O(t_{m})\quad\text{ as }m\to+\infty.

Indeed, using the definition of
\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}
and
\varepsilon_{m}
, we may write

(6.7)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k,m}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{k,m}-\Pi_{k,0}(\varphi_{k,m})+\Pi_{k,0}(\varphi_{k,m})-\varphi_{k}+\varphi_{k}

\displaystyle=t_{m}\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}+(1+\varepsilon_{m})\varphi_{k}.

Since
\|u_{t_{m}}-u\|_{\infty}=O(t_{m})
and by (
6.7
) we may expand the condition
\int_{\Omega}u_{t_{m}}^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}^{2}\,dx=1
which gives
1=(1+\varepsilon_{m})^{2}+O(t_{m})
, from which (
6.6
) follows. For
m\geq 1
we let

\displaystyle\alpha_{m}

\displaystyle=t_{m}\big\|\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}-\tilde{\Psi}\big\|_{H^{1}_{0}}+t_{m}^{2}+t_{m}|\varepsilon_{m}|

\displaystyle+\Big|\mu_{k,t_{m}}-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)\cdot t_{m}\Big|

and

\tilde{\Psi}_{2,m}=\frac{t_{m}}{\alpha_{m}}\Big(\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}-\tilde{\Psi}\Big)\in E_{k,0}^{\perp_{u}}.

Using the equation satisfied by
\varphi_{k,m}
, (
6.1
) and (
6.7
) shows that
\tilde{\Psi}_{2,m}
satisfies

(6.8)

\displaystyle\big(-\Delta

\displaystyle-\lambda-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\big)\tilde{\Psi}_{2,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}\Bigg[\mu_{k,t_{m}}-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)t_{m}\Bigg]u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k,m}

\displaystyle-(2^{*}-2)^{2}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)\frac{t_{m}^{2}}{\alpha_{m}}u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi_{k,m}

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)\frac{t_{m}}{\alpha_{m}}u^{2^{*}-2}\big(-t_{m}\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}-\varepsilon_{m}\varphi_{k}\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{(2^{*}-2)(2^{*}-3)}{2}\frac{t_{m}^{2}}{\alpha_{m}}u^{2^{*}-2}h^{2}\varphi_{k,m}

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\frac{t_{m}}{\alpha_{m}}u^{2^{*}-2}h\big(t_{m}\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}+\varepsilon_{m}\varphi_{k}\big)+\eta_{m}

in
\Omega
, where again
\eta_{m}
satisfies
\|\eta_{m}\|_{\infty}=o(1)
as
m\to+\infty
. By definition
(\tilde{\Psi}_{2,m})_{m\geq 1}
is bounded in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and thus weakly converges to some
\tilde{\Psi}_{2}\in E_{k,0}^{\perp_{u}}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Passing (
6.8
) to the limit shows that
\tilde{\Psi}_{2}
satisfies

(6.9)

\displaystyle\big(-\Delta-\lambda-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\big)\tilde{\Psi}_{2}=\Gamma_{1}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Gamma_{2}\Bigg[\frac{2^{*}-3}{2}u^{2^{*}-2}h^{2}\varphi_{k}-(2^{*}-2)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi_{k}\Bigg]

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Gamma_{3}\Bigg[u^{2^{*}-2}h\varphi_{k}-\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}\Bigg]

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\Gamma_{2}\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Bigg[u^{2^{*}-2}h\tilde{\Psi}-\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\Psi}\Bigg],

in
\Omega
, where
\tilde{\Psi}
is as in (
6.1
) and where we have let, up to a subsequence,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\frac{\mu_{k,t_{m}}-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)t_{m}}{\alpha_{m}},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\frac{t_{m}^{2}}{\alpha_{m}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Gamma_{3}=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\frac{t_{m}\varepsilon_{m}}{\alpha_{m}}.

Substracting (
6.8
) and (
6.9
) and using (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) we conclude as before that
\tilde{\Psi}_{2,m}
strongly converges to
\tilde{\Psi}_{2}
in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Integrating (
6.9
) against
\varphi_{k}
shows, since
u=|\varphi_{k}|
,
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
and since
\varphi_{k}
and
\tilde{\Psi}
are orthogonal in
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
, that

(6.10)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=

\displaystyle-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Gamma_{2}\Bigg[\frac{2^{*}-3}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx-(2^{*}-2)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)^{2}\Bigg]

\displaystyle-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Gamma_{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\Psi}\varphi_{k}h\,dx.

We first assume that
\Gamma_{2}=0
. By definition of
\Gamma_{2}
this means that
t_{m}^{2}=o(\alpha_{m})
as
m\to+\infty
, and thus implies by (
6.6
) that we also have
\Gamma_{3}=0
and then, by (
6.10
), that
\Gamma_{1}=0
. Coming back to the definition of
\alpha_{m}
we thus have

\alpha_{m}=(1+o(1))t_{m}\big\|\tilde{\Psi}_{1,m}-\tilde{\Psi}\big\|_{H^{1}_{0}}

as
m\to+\infty
, which implies that
\|\tilde{\Psi}_{2,m}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=1+o(1)
. The strong convergence towards
\tilde{\Psi}_{2}
then implies that
\tilde{\Psi}_{2}\neq 0
. However, since
\Gamma_{i}=0
for
i=1,2,3
, (
6.9
) now shows that
\tilde{\Psi}_{2}\in E_{k,0}
which is a contradiction. Therefore
\Gamma_{2}\neq 0
and by (
6.10
)

(6.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma_{1}}{\Gamma_{2}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to+\infty}\frac{\mu_{k,t_{m}}-\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\Big)t_{m}}{t_{m}^{2}}

\displaystyle=(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\Bigg[(2^{*}-2)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}hdx\Big)^{2}-\frac{2^{*}-3}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}dx

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\Psi}\varphi_{k}h\,dx\Bigg].

Since
(t_{m})_{m\geq 0}
was any sequence of positive numbers converging to
0
and since the right-hand side of (
6.11
) is independent of the choice of
(t_{m})_{m\geq 0}
this shows that

t\mapsto\frac{1}{t^{2}}\Bigg[\frac{\mu_{k,t}}{\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)}-1+(2^{*}-2)\Big(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}hdx\Big)t\Bigg]

has a limit as
t\to 0
which is given by the right-hand side of (
6.11
). This concludes the proof of (
6.2
).
∎

As a consequence we obtain the following result:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
i\geq 0
,
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
k=N(i)+1
where
N
is given by (
2.2
). Assume that
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
, attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. Then, for any
h\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have

(6.12)

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx-\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\right)^{2}\geq\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\Psi}\varphi_{k}h\,dx,

where
\tilde{\Psi}
is as in (
6.1
).

Proof.

Let as before
u_{t}=u(1+th)
. Straightforward computations show that

(6.13)

\displaystyle\|u_{t}\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}

\displaystyle=1+(2^{*}-2)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dxt

\displaystyle+\Bigg[\frac{(2^{*}-1)(2^{*}-2)}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx-(2^{*}-2)\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\right)^{2}\Bigg]t^{2}+o(t^{2})

as
t\to 0
. Since
u
attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
we have

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}\leq\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})\|u_{t}\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}

for any
t
small enough. We may now expand the right-hand side of the latter inequality using (
6.2
) and (
6.13
): straightforward computations show that the
0
-th and first-order terms in
t
vanish. Dividing by
t^{2}
and letting
t\to 0
yields (
6.12
).
∎

The following result establishes a stability inequality of sorts for the functional
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}
at a minimiser:

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
i\geq 0
,
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
k=N(i)+1
where
N
is given by (
2.2
). Assume that
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
, attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. Then, for any
h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, we have

(6.14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(2^{*}-2)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx.

Proposition
6.2
is, to the best of our knowledge, the first instance of a stability inequality for second variations of generalised eigenvalue functionals
u\mapsto\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
.

Proof.

Assume that
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
,
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
, attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. Let
h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. For
|t|<\|h\|_{\infty}^{-1}
we let as before
u_{t}=u(1+th)
. Then
u_{t}\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
,
\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
is well-defined for any
p\geq 1
and by Proposition
3.1
we have
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(u_{t})>0
. We again adopt the notation
\mu_{p,t}=\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
and
E_{p,t}=E_{p}^{\lambda}(u_{t})
for any
p\geq 1
. For
1\leq p\leq k-1
we will denote by
\Pi_{p,t}
the orthogonal projection over
E_{p,t}
in
L^{2}_{u_{t}}(\Omega)
, and we will denote by
\Pi_{t}
the orthogonal projection over
\oplus_{i=1}^{k-1}E_{i,t}
in
L^{2}_{u_{t}}(\Omega)
, so that
\Pi_{t}=\sum_{p=1}^{k-1}\Pi_{p,t}
. Using (
3.13
) we have

\|\Pi_{t}(\varphi_{k})\|_{L^{2}_{u_{t}}}=O(t)

as
t\to 0
.
We let, for any
p\geq 1
,
\varphi_{p}=\varphi_{p}^{\lambda}(u)
be the
L^{2}_{u}(\Omega)
-orthonormal family of generalised eigenfunctions associated to
u
and given by Proposition
2.2
. Recall that
\mu_{p}^{\lambda}(u)\leq 0
for
p\leq k-1
. By Proposition
4.2
,
E_{k,0}=E_{k}^{\lambda}(u)
is one-dimensional, is spanned by
\varphi_{k}
and
u=|\varphi_{k}|
. In what follows we let, for
t
small enough,

\Psi_{k,t}=\frac{(1+th)\varphi_{k}}{\|(1+th)\varphi_{k}\|_{L^{2}_{u_{t}}}}.

We define inductively, for
2\leq i\leq k
and
t
small enough,

\Psi_{i-1,t}=\varphi_{i-1}-\sum_{i-1\leq\ell\leq k-1}\big(\varphi_{\ell},\Psi_{\ell+1,t}\big)_{L^{2}_{u_{t}}}\Psi_{\ell,t}.

The family
(\Psi_{1,t},\dots,\Psi_{k,t})
obtained in this way is therefore
L^{2}_{u_{t}}
-orthonormal and

V_{t}=\text{Span}\big(\Psi_{1,t},\dots\Psi_{k,t}\big)

satisfies
\dim_{u_{t}}V_{t}=k
. By construction of the family
(\Psi_{i,t})_{1\leq i\leq k}
it is easily proven that, for all
1\leq i\leq k
, we have
\|\Psi_{i,t}-\varphi_{i}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=O(t)
as
t\to 0
. As a consequence

Q_{u_{t}}^{\lambda}(\Psi_{i,t})=Q_{u}^{\lambda}(\varphi_{i})+O(t)=\mu_{i}^{\lambda}(u)+O(t)\leq O(t)\quad\text{ as }t\to 0

for
1\leq i\leq k-1
, where
Q_{u_{t}}^{\lambda}
is as in (
2.4
), and

Q_{u_{t}}^{\lambda}(\Psi_{k,t})=Q_{u}^{\lambda}(\varphi_{k})+O(t)=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+O(t)\quad\text{ as }t\to 0.

Since the family
(\Psi_{1,t},\dots,\Psi_{k,t})
is
L^{2}_{u_{t}}
-orthonormal we have

\max_{v\in V_{t}}Q_{u_{t}}^{\lambda}(v)=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}Q_{u_{t}}^{\lambda}(\Psi_{i,t})=Q_{u_{t}}^{\lambda}(\Psi_{k,t}),

and by the definition (
2.3
) of
\mu_{k,t}
and the expression of
\Psi_{k,t}
we finally obtain

(6.15)

\mu_{k,t}\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega}\big|\nabla\big((1+th)\varphi_{k}\big)\big|^{2}-\lambda(1+th)^{2}\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx}{\int_{\Omega}(1+th)^{2^{*}}u^{2^{*}}\,dx},

where we again used that
u=|\varphi_{k}|
. We easily have

(6.16)

\int_{\Omega}(1+th)^{2^{*}}u^{2^{*}}\,dx=1+2^{*}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\cdot t+\frac{2^{*}(2^{*}-1)}{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx\cdot t^{2}+o(t^{2})

as
t\to 0
. Straightforward computations show that

(6.17)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big|\nabla\big((1+th)\varphi_{k}\big)\big|^{2}-\lambda(1+th)^{2}\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)+\Bigg[2\int_{\Omega}h|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}\,dx-2\lambda\int_{\Omega}h\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx+2\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\langle\nabla h,\nabla\varphi_{k}\rangle\,dx\Bigg]\cdot t

\displaystyle+\Bigg[\int_{\Omega}u^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\,dx+2\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}h\langle\nabla h,\nabla\varphi_{k}\rangle\,dx+\int_{\Omega}h^{2}|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle-\lambda\int_{\Omega}h^{2}\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx\Bigg]\cdot t^{2}.

Integrating the equation
-\Delta\varphi_{k}-\lambda\varphi_{k}=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{k}
against
h\varphi_{k}
and
h^{2}\varphi_{k}
gives, respectively,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}\,dx-\lambda\int_{\Omega}h\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\langle\nabla h,\nabla\varphi_{k}\rangle\,dx

and

\displaystyle\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h^{2}|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}\,dx+2\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}h\langle\nabla h,\nabla\varphi_{k}\rangle\,dx-\lambda\int_{\Omega}h^{2}\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx.

Plugging the latter in (
6.17
) finally gives

(6.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big|\nabla\big((1+th)\varphi_{k}\big)\big|^{2}-\lambda(1+th)^{2}\varphi_{k}^{2}\,dx=\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)

\displaystyle+2\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\cdot t+\Bigg[\int_{\Omega}u^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\,dx+\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx\Bigg]t^{2}.

We now combine (
6.2
), (
6.15
), (
6.16
) and (
6.18
). It is easily seen that the
0
-th and first-order terms in
t
in (
6.15
) cancel. By dividing the result by
t^{2}
and letting
t\to 0
we find, after direct computations, that

(6.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\,dx+2(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx-(2^{*}-2)\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\tilde{\Psi}h\varphi_{k}\,dx

holds true. Using (
6.12
) finally concludes the proof of (
6.14
).
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
6.2
we finally prove that
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
cannot be simultaneously attained and equal to
K_{n}^{-2}
or, in other words, that (
4.9
) is a necessary condition for the attainment of
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
:

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
i\geq 0
,
\lambda\in[\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
k=N(i)+1
where
N
is given by (
2.2
). Then
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained if an only if
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)<K_{n}^{-2}
.

Proof.

The right implication is given by Theorem
4.1
and we only prove the left implication. Since
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\leq K_{n}^{-2}
by Proposition
4.1
we only have to prove that if
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=K_{n}^{-2}
then
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is not attained. We proceed by contradiction and assume that there exists
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
, which attains
\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=K_{n}^{-2}
. Proposition
6.2
then applies and (
6.14
) shows that, for any
h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, we have

(6.20)

\displaystyle K_{n}^{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}|\nabla h|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+(2^{*}-2)\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h\,dx\right)^{2}\geq(2^{*}-2)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}h^{2}\,dx.

To obtain a contradiction we adapt an argument of
[
23
]
. For
(t,y)\in(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define

Z^{0}_{t,y}(x)=\frac{1-t^{2}|x-y|^{2}}{1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad Z^{i}_{t,y}(x)=\frac{2t(x_{i}-y_{i})}{1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2}},i=1\dots n.

It is easily seen that, for any
x\in\Omega
, we have

\sum_{i=0}^{n}(Z^{i}_{t,y}(x))^{2}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left|\nabla Z^{i}_{t,y}(x)\right|^{2}=\frac{4nt^{2}}{(1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2})^{2}}.

The arguments in
[
23
]
show that there exist
(t,y)\in(0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(6.21)

\int_{\Omega}Z^{i}_{t,y}u^{2^{*}}\,dx=0\quad\text{ for all }i=0\dots n.

Applying (
6.20
) and (
6.21
) to each
Z^{i}_{t,y}
,
i=0\dots n
, and summing over
i
then yields

(2^{*}-2)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}\,dx\leq K_{n}^{2}\int_{\Omega}\frac{4nt^{2}}{(1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2})^{2}}u^{2}\,dx.

By Hölder’s inequality and since
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
we obtain that

(6.22)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq n(n-2)K_{n}^{2}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{t^{n}}{(1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2})^{n}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{2}{n}}

\displaystyle<n(n-2)K_{n}^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{t^{n}}{(1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2})^{n}}\,dx\right)^{\frac{2}{n}},

where the strict inequality follows since
\Omega
is bounded. A simple change of variables using (
4.5
) finally shows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{t^{n}}{(1+t^{2}|x-y|^{2})^{n}}\,dx=\big(n(n-2)\big)^{-\frac{n}{2}}K_{n}^{-n},

which contradicts (
6.22
).
∎

Proof of the main results

We prove in this section Theorems

Proof of Theorem

Let

We now consider the three-dimensional case and prove Theorem

.

. Throughout the proof we will let
k=N(i)+1
where

\lambda_{i,*}

\max_{\Omega}m_{\lambda}>0

We now prove (3) and (4). We assume from now on and until the end of this proof that
\bar{\lambda}_{i}\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. We first claim that the following holds:

(7.1)

\bar{\lambda}_{i}=\lambda_{i,*}.

In view of the previous paragraph, we only have to prove that
\bar{\lambda}_{i}\geq\lambda_{i,*}
. By Proposition
4.4
and by definition of
\bar{\lambda}_{i}
, for any

\mu_{k}^{\lambda}(\Omega)

), denoted by

We first claim that

\|u_{\lambda}\|_{\infty}\to+\infty

as

from above. Indeed, if

were bounded in

) and standard elliptic theory would show that

converges in

Let us conclude with a few remarks on Theorem

and on the values of

and

. Estimating them for

: in the non-coercive case

for

displays richer properties than in the coercive case. We prove in Proposition

below, for instance, that the asymptotic behavior of

as

from the right is related to the geometry of the nodal sets of eigenfunctions of

and to the mass of the unique Green’s function of

, both of which are not easily understood. In particular, Proposition

below hints at the possibility of having

for some

and some smooth bounded domain

, something that would be in striking contrast with the coercive case. Concerning

, the rearrangement argument used in
[

to show that

turns out to be ineffective when

would require new techniques to obtain non-existence results for sign-changing solutions of (

) when

. In Proposition

below we could only prove that in the specific case

we have

(see (

) below), but without being able to estimate

.

We conclude this section by proving the following result which establishes that, as

from the left, the sign-changing least-energy solutions of (

) bifurcate from

:

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

. Let

be fixed and let

, for
\lambda
close enough to
\Lambda_{i+1}
, be a solution of (

) attaining
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}(\Omega)
. Then
\frac{u_{\lambda}}{\|u_{\lambda}\|_{\infty}}
converges, up to a subsequence, to a non-zero element of

as

By Theorems
1.1
and

,
\int_{\Omega}|u_{\lambda}|^{2^{*}}dx\to 0
as

from the left. Since

solves (

), we also obtain that

-compactness result of Struwe

then shows that

∎

Proposition

thus hints that least-energy solutions of (

) belong to one of the bifurcating families in constructed in Cerami-Fortunato-Struwe
[
16
]
.

Appendix A
Minimisation of
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
in the coercive case

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 3
be an open bounded set. For
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1}]
we define

(A.1)

I_{\lambda}(\Omega)=\inf_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla v|^{2}-\lambda v^{2}\big)dx}{\big(\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2^{*}}dx\big)^{\frac{n-2}{n}}}.

In the celebrated paper
[
13
]
, based on previous work by Aubin
[
6
]
, Brézis and Nirenberg investigated when
I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained. We consider the following minimisation problem, which is exactly (
4.1
) in the coercive case
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})
, that is when
k=1
:

(A.2)

\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=\inf_{u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)}\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}

where
L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
is as in (
2.1
) and
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
is as in (
2.3
). Since
-\Delta-\lambda
is coercive for
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})
we have
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega),I_{\lambda}(\Omega)\geq 0
. We claim that (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) are equivalent:

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a smooth open bounded set of
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 3
. For any
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})

\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)=I_{\lambda}(\Omega)

holds. In addition
I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained if and only if
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is.

Proof.

We assume that
\lambda\in[0,\Lambda_{1})
. Let
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
, with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
. We use the equivalent caracterisation (
2.6
) for
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
. By Hölder’s inequality, for any
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, we have

\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}v^{2}dx\leq\Big(\int_{\Omega}|v|^{2^{*}}dx\Big)^{\frac{2}{2^{*}}},

and thus
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\geq I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
, which shows that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\geq I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
. Independently, by choosing
v=u
in (
2.6
) we obtain
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\|u\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}\leq I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
and hence
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\leq I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
.

Assume first that
I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained at some function
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, with
u>0
in
\Omega
and
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
. Standard variational arguments show that

-\triangle u-\lambda u=I_{\lambda}(\Omega)u^{2^{*}-1}\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Choosing
u
and
v=u
in (
2.6
) shows that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\leq I_{\lambda}(\Omega)=\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
, and hence
u
attains
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
. Assume now that
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
is attained at some function
u\in L^{2^{*}}_{>}(\Omega)
with
\|u\|_{2^{*}}=1
. We let
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
be the first normalised eigenvector given by Proposition
2.1
. Choosing
u=v=\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)
in the definition of
\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
gives

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)|^{2}-\lambda(\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2}dx}{\int_{\Omega}(\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u))^{2^{*}}dx}\|\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)\|_{2^{*}}^{2^{*}-2}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}-2}\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u)^{2}\,dx\leq\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)

where the last inequality follows from Hölder’s inequality. The equality case in Hölder’s inequality then shows that
u_{0}=c\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u_{0})
a. e. for some
c>0
. The normalisation of
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u_{0})
then implies
c=\pm 1
, and hence
\varphi=\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u_{0})\neq 0
solves

-\triangle\varphi-\lambda\varphi=\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)|\varphi|^{2^{*}-2}\varphi\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Since
I_{\lambda}(\Omega)=\mu_{1}^{\lambda}(\Omega)
,
\varphi_{1}^{\lambda}(u_{0})
then attains
I_{\lambda}(\Omega)
.
∎

Appendix B
Asymptotic analysis for sign-changing solutions of (
1.2
) at the least energy level

In this section we prove an asymptotic analysis result that was used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Let
n\geq 3
. For
z\in\Omega
and
\mu>0
we let, for
x\in\Omega

B_{z,\mu}(x)=\frac{\mu^{\frac{n-2}{2}}}{\left(\mu^{2}+\frac{|x-z|^{2}}{n(n-2)}\right)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}}=\mu^{-\frac{n-2}{2}}U_{0}\Big(\frac{x-z}{\mu}\Big),

where
U_{0}
is as in (
4.3
). For

and
\mu>0

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta PB_{z,\mu}&=B_{z,\mu}^{2^{*}-1}\quad\text{ in }\Omega\\
PB_{z,\mu}&=0\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

By the maximum principe we have

(B.2)

0<PB_{z,\mu}(x)\leq B_{z,\mu}(x)

for every

. We will let in the following

(B.3)

\displaystyle T_{z,\mu}

\displaystyle=\text{Span}\Big\{PB_{z,\mu},\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}PB_{z,\mu},\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{i}}PB_{z,\mu},1=i\cdots n\Big\}

and we will let
T_{z,\mu}^{\perp}
be the orthogonal complement with respect to the
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
scalar product. We then have the following result:

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be smooth open bounded. Let
i\geq 0
, let
\bar{\lambda}\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and let
(u_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
, for
\lambda
close to
\bar{\lambda}

for all
\lambda
and
\|u_{\lambda}\|_{\infty}\to+\infty
as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
. Then, up to a subsequence as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
, there exist families
(x_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of points of
\Omega
,
(\mu_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of positive real numbers and
(\alpha_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of real numbers such that
\mu_{\lambda}\to 0
,
\alpha_{\lambda}\to 1
,

in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and such that

with

.

This result is a variation, for sign-changing solutions, of similar and by now classical results that have been proven for positive solutions (see for instance
[
23
,
29
,
54
]
) or for almost-minimisers of (

) in the coercive case
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda_{1})
(see e.g.
[
28
]
). A generalisation of Proposition

, for sign-changing solutions that may have energy higher than

, was recently given in
[
17
]
.

Proof.

We let
u_{\lambda}
be as in the statement of Proposition
B.1
. Since
u_{\lambda}
solves (
1.2
), Struwe’s celebrated
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-compactness result
[
61
, Chap. III, Theorem 3.1]
shows that there exist families
(\tilde{x}_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of points of
\Omega
and
(\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of positive real numbers converging to
0
such that
\frac{d(\tilde{x}_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)}{\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda}}\to+\infty
and

(B.4)

u_{\lambda}=\tilde{B}_{\lambda}+o(1)\quad\text{ in }H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
, where we have let
\tilde{B}_{\lambda}=B_{\tilde{x}_{\lambda},\tilde{\mu}_{\lambda}}
. By (
B.4
) and by assumption we thus have
\lim_{\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}}E(u_{\lambda})=K_{3}^{-3}
from below. Throughout this proof all the limits are taken up to a subsequence a
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
. We let
x_{0}=\lim_{\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}}\tilde{x}_{\lambda}\in\overline{\Omega}
.

Step
1
:
we first claim that the following holds:

(B.5)

x_{0}\in\Omega.

We adapt a simple argument in
[
28
, Proposition 4.1]
, initially written for positive solutions, that immediately extends to sign-changing solutions. We only sketch it here. First, a classical argument à la
[
8
, Proposition 7]
shows, up to changing
u_{\lambda}
in
-u_{\lambda}
, that there exist families
(x_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of points of
\Omega
,
(\mu_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of positive real numbers converging to
0
and
(\alpha_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
of real numbers such that

(B.6)

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\lambda}\big(PB_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}+s_{\lambda}\big),\quad\frac{d(x_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)}{\mu_{\lambda}}\to+\infty,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\to 1,\quad s_{\lambda}\in T_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}^{\perp}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H_{0}^{1}}=o(1)

as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
, and where
T_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}
is as in (
B.3
). As explained in
[
8
, Proposition 7]
we have
|x_{\lambda}-\tilde{x}_{\lambda}|\to 0
as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
, so that we still have
\lim_{\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}}x_{\lambda}=x_{0}
. Using (
B.6
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{\lambda}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla u_{\lambda}|^{2}-\lambda u_{\lambda}^{2}\,\big)dx=K_{3}^{-3}+C_{0}m_{0}(x_{\lambda})\mu_{\lambda}+\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla s_{\lambda}|^{2}-\lambda u_{\lambda}^{2}\big)\,dx

\displaystyle+o(\mu_{\lambda})+O(\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}})+o\Big(\frac{\mu_{\lambda}}{d(x_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)}\Big)+o(\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2})

for some positive constant
C_{0}
independent of
\lambda
, and that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{\lambda}^{6}}\int_{\Omega}u_{\lambda}^{6}\,dx=K_{3}^{-3}+2C_{0}m_{0}(x_{\lambda})\mu_{\lambda}+15\int_{\Omega}PB_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}^{4}s_{\lambda}^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle+o\Big(\frac{\mu_{\lambda}}{d(x_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)}\Big)+o(\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}),

where
m_{0}(x_{\lambda})
is defined as in (
5.2
). For the details, see
[
28
, Proposition 4.1]
. Since
u_{\lambda}
solves (
1.2
), we have

\frac{\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla u_{\lambda}|^{2}-\lambda u_{\lambda}^{2}\big)\,dx}{\left(\int_{\Omega}|u_{\lambda}|^{6}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u_{\lambda}|^{6}\,dx\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\leq K_{3}^{-2}.

Using the previous expansions to expand the left-hand side of the latter yields

(B.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big[|\nabla s_{\lambda}|^{2}-\lambda u_{\lambda}^{2}\,-15PB_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}^{4}s_{\lambda}^{2}\Big]\,dx-C_{0}m_{0}(x_{\lambda})\mu_{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq o(\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2})+o(\mu_{\lambda})+O(\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}})+o\Big(\frac{\mu_{\lambda}}{d(x_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)}\Big)

as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
. It is well-known that there exists
\eta>0
such that for any
\lambda
small enough,

\int_{\Omega}\Big[|\nabla s|^{2}-\lambda s^{2}\,-15PB_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}^{4}s^{2}\Big]\,dx\geq\eta\|s\|^{2}\quad\text{ holds for all }s\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

(see e.g.
[
54
, Equation (D.1)]
). It was also proven in
[
54
, Equation (2.8)]
that
\sup_{y\in\Omega}d(y,\partial\Omega)m_{0}(y)<0
. Combining these observations in (
B.7
) then shows that there exists
C>0
independent of
\lambda
such that

\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{\lambda}}{d(x_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)}\leq C\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}+o(\mu_{\lambda})\quad\text{ as }\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}.

The latter first implies that
\|s_{\lambda}\|_{H^{1}_{0}}=O(\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}})
and then that
d(x_{\lambda},\partial\Omega)\not\to 0
as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
, which proves (
B.5
).

Step
2
: improved pointwise estimates.
For simplicity we will let
B_{\lambda}=B_{x_{\lambda},\mu_{\lambda}}
. We claim that there is a constant
C>0
such that for every
\lambda
we have

(B.8)

|u_{\lambda}(x)|\leq CB_{\lambda}(x)\quad\text{ for every }x\in\Omega.

Pointwise estimates like (
B.8
), originally developed for positive solutions of critical elliptic equations (see e.g.
[
23
,
24
]
), have recently been generalised to sign-changing solutions that exhibit bubbling profiles that may not be non-degenerate: see e.g.
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
. The proof of (
B.8
) is essentially contained in the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
(up to assuming
v_{\infty}=0
, with the notations of
[
17
]
). We simplify the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 2.1]
here using (
B.5
). First, an adaptation of the arguments in
[
17
, Proposition 2.2]
shows that

(B.9)

\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}(\mu_{\lambda}+|x-x_{\lambda}|)^{\frac{n-2}{2}}|u_{\lambda}(x)-PB_{\lambda}(x)|\to 0

as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
. For
\rho>0
small enough, we define

\eta_{\lambda}(\rho):=\sup_{\Omega\backslash B(x_{0},\rho)}\big|u_{\lambda}(x)\big|.

Thanks to (
B.4
) and standard elliptic theory we obtain that, for any fixed
\rho>0
,

(B.10)

\lim_{\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}}\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)=0.

We now claim that for any
\theta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
there exists
R>0
,
\rho>0
, and
C>0
such that

(B.11)

\big|u_{\lambda}(x)\big|\leq C\left(\frac{\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{n-2}{2}-\theta(n-2)}}{|x-x_{\lambda}|^{(n-2)(1-\theta)}}+\frac{\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)}{|x-x_{\lambda}|^{(n-2)\theta}}\right)

holds for all
\lambda
and for all
x\in\Omega\backslash B(x_{\lambda},R\mu_{\lambda})
. We prove (
B.11
): we let
G_{0}
be the Green’s function of
-\Delta
in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Since
x_{0}\in\Omega
, it follows from
[
55
]
that there exists
c,C>0
such that

(B.12)

c|x_{\lambda}-x|^{-1}\leq G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)\leq C|x_{\lambda}-x|^{-1}\hbox{ for all }x\in\overline{\Omega}\backslash\{x_{\lambda}\}

and

(B.13)

\frac{|\nabla G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)|}{G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)}\geq c|x_{\lambda}-x|^{-1},

for all
x\in B(x_{\lambda},\rho)\backslash\{x_{\lambda}\}\subset\Omega
. We define

\psi_{\theta,\lambda}(x):=\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{n-2}{2}-\theta(n-2)}G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)^{1-\theta}+\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)^{\theta},

which is a smooth positive function in
\Omega\backslash\{x_{\lambda}\}
, and we let
L_{\lambda}=-\Delta-\lambda-u_{\lambda}^{4}
. Straightforward computations show that

\frac{L_{\lambda}\psi_{\theta,\lambda}}{\psi_{\theta,\lambda}}=-\lambda-u_{\lambda}^{4}+\theta(1-\theta)\frac{|\nabla G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)|^{2}}{G_{0}(x_{\lambda},x)^{2}}

for any
x\in B(x_{\lambda},\rho)\backslash B(x_{\lambda},R\mu_{\lambda})
. Using (
B.2
) and (
B.9
) we obtain that

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\limsup_{\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}}\sup_{\Omega\backslash B(x_{\lambda},R\mu_{\lambda})}|x_{\lambda}-x|^{2}u_{\lambda}(x)^{4}=0.

Combining the latter with (
B.13
) now shows that for
R
large enough and
\rho
small enough (depending on
\theta
, but both fixed) we have, for any
\lambda
close enough to
\bar{\lambda}
,
L_{\lambda}\psi_{\theta,\lambda}>0
in

. By (

) and the definition of
\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)
there is a constant
C>0
such that
u_{\lambda}\leq C\psi_{\theta,\lambda}
in
\partial\big(B(x_{\lambda},\rho)\backslash B(x_{\lambda},R\mu_{\lambda})\big)
. Since independently we have

by (

) the maximum principle shows that
u_{\lambda}\leq C\psi_{\lambda,\theta}
in
B(x_{\lambda},\rho)\backslash B(x_{\lambda},R\mu_{\lambda})
. Together with the definition of

this proves (
B.11
).

We now prove (
B.8
). First, arguing as in the proof of
[
17
, Proposition 2.4]
, a representation formula with (

) shows that there exists
\rho>0
such that

(B.14)

|u_{\lambda}(x)|\leq C\big(B_{\lambda}(x)+\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)\big)

for every
x\in\Omega
and
\lambda
close to
\bar{\lambda}
, for some
C>0
. We proceed by contradiction and assume that, up to a subsequence as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
, we have
\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)>>\mu_{\lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}
. We let
U_{\lambda}=\frac{u_{\lambda}}{\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)}
. For any
\lambda
we let
z_{\lambda}\in\Omega\backslash B(x_{\lambda},\rho)
be such that
|u_{\lambda}(z_{\lambda})|=\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)
. By (
B.14
) we see that for any
\delta>0
fixed we have
|U_{\lambda}(z_{\lambda})|=1
and

|U_{\lambda}(x)|\leq C+o(1)\quad\text{ for }x\in\Omega\backslash B(x_{\lambda},\delta).

in
\Omega
. Since
\eta_{\lambda}(\rho)\to 0
by (
B.10
), standard elliptic theory shows that
U_{\lambda}\to U_{\infty}
in
C^{2}_{loc}(\overline{\Omega}\backslash\{x_{0}\}
as

, where
U_{\infty}
satisfies
|U_{\infty}(x)|\leq C
for any

,

and

. In particular, the singularity of

Appendix C
Properties of the mass functions

In this last section we assume that
n=3
. We let
\lambda\not\in{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
and
G_{\lambda}
be defined as in (
5.1
). For any
x\in\Omega
and
y\neq x
we let

H_{\lambda}(x,y)=G_{\lambda}(x,y)-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}.

By (
5.1
) it is easily see that
H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
satisfies

(C.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)-\lambda H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi|x-y|}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Standard elliptic theory and (
C.1
) then show that, for any
x\in\Omega
fixed,
H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. Using the expansion (
5.2
) it is then easily seen that

m_{\lambda}(x)=H_{\lambda}(x,x).

Since
G_{\lambda}
is symmetric in
x,y
then so is
H_{\lambda}
. In this section we investigate several properties of the mass function. This function has been thoroughly studied in the coercive case
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda_{1})
(see e.g.
[
21
]
) but little is known in the non-coercive case
\lambda\geq\Lambda_{1}
. We prove several properties of
m_{\lambda}
and relate its asymptotic behavior as
\lambda
approaches
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
to the nodal sets of eigenfunctions of
-\Delta
in
\Omega
. We first prove that the mass function is increasing as
\lambda
varies between two consecutive eigenvalues:

Lemma C.1
.

Let
i\geq 0
and let
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. Let
x\in\Omega
be fixed. Then

(C.2)

\frac{d}{d\lambda}m_{\lambda}(x)=\int_{\Omega}G_{\lambda}(x,y)^{2}\,dy>0.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
x\in\Omega
be fixed and let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough. By definition of
G_{\lambda}
we have

-\Delta\big(G_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,\cdot)-G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\big)-(\lambda+\varepsilon)(G_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,\cdot)-G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot))=\varepsilon G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot).

Since
G_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,\cdot)-G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
vanishes in
\partial\Omega
and by (
5.3
), standard elliptic theory shows that there exists
C>0
independent of
x
such that

(C.3)

\|G_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,\cdot)-G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\varepsilon.

A representation formula also gives, for
y\in\Omega
,

H_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,y)-H_{\lambda}(x,y)=G_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,y)-G_{\lambda}(x,y)=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}G_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(y,z)G_{\lambda}(x,z)\,dz.

Expanding the integral in the right-hand side with (
C.3
) then yields

H_{\lambda+\varepsilon}(x,y)-H_{\lambda}(x,y)=\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}G_{\lambda}(y,z)G_{\lambda}(x,z)\,dz+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

and applying the latter at
y=x
yields (
C.2
).
∎

Note that in the coercive case
\lambda\in(0,\Lambda_{1})
the monotony of
\lambda\mapsto m_{\lambda}(x)
easily follows from the maximum principle (see e.g.
[
21
]
). We now investigate the behavior of the mass function as
\lambda
approaches
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
. We first prove that interior points of positive mass always exist provided
\lambda
approaches
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
from the left:

Proposition C.1
.

Let
i\geq 0
and let
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. Let
K\subset\subset\Omega
be a compact set of positive measure. Then

\max_{K}m_{\lambda}\to+\infty\quad\text{ as }\lambda\underset{<}{\to}\Lambda_{i+1}.

Proof.

Let
E_{\Lambda_{i+1}}(\Omega)
denote the eigenspace associated to
\Lambda_{i+1}
and let
d=\dim E_{\Lambda_{i+1}}(\Omega)
, with the convention that
d=0
if
i=0
. We let
(\varphi_{1},\cdots,\varphi_{d})
be a
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthonormal basis of
E_{\Lambda_{i+1}}(\Omega)
. Let
K
be a compact subspace of
\Omega
of positive measure and let
x\in K
. We decompose
H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
as

H_{\lambda}(x,y)=S_{\lambda}(x,y)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}^{\lambda}(x)\varphi_{j}(y),

where
S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
satisfies
\int_{\Omega}S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\varphi_{j}\,dy=0
for all
1\leq j\leq d
and where
a_{j}^{\lambda}(x)=\int_{\Omega}H_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\varphi_{j}dy
. First, a representation formula for
\varphi_{j}
at
x
shows that

(C.4)

\displaystyle\varphi_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=(\Lambda_{i+1}-\lambda)\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}\varphi_{j}(y)dy+(\Lambda_{i+1}-\lambda)a_{j}^{\lambda}(x).

Using (
C.1
) it is easily seen that
S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)-\lambda S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi|x-y|}-(\Lambda_{i+1}-\lambda)\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}^{\lambda}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}\text{ in }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Since
x\in K
, standard elliptic theory then shows that
\|S_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})}=O(1)
as
\lambda\underset{<}{\to}\Lambda_{i+1}
. Thus, using (
C.4
), we have

(C.5)

\displaystyle m_{\lambda}(x)=S_{\lambda}(x,x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}^{\lambda}(x)\varphi_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{i+1}-\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varphi_{j}(x)^{2}+O(1)

as
\lambda\underset{<}{\to}\Lambda_{i+1}
. By the unique continuation results in
[
34
]
, each
\varphi_{j}
vanishes on a set of measure zero in
\Omega
. Since
|K|>0
, we may thus assume that
x\in K
is chosen so that
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\varphi_{j}(x)^{2}>0
, and (
C.5
) shows that
m_{\lambda}(x)\to+\infty
as
\lambda\underset{<}{\to}\Lambda_{i+1}
.
∎

We now investigate the behavior of
m_{\lambda}
as
\lambda
approaches
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
from the right. The situation in this case turns out to be surprisingly more involved and the nodal sets of the eigenfunctions associated to
\Lambda_{i}
arise in the expansion of the mass. We first prove that if the nodal sets of the eigenfunctions of
\Lambda_{i}
have no common point in
\Omega
the mass goes to
-\infty
everywhere in
\Omega
as
\lambda
approaches
{\rm Sp}(-\Delta)
from the right. As before we let
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
be the eigenspace associated to
\Lambda_{i}
, we let
d_{i}=\dim E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
and we let
(\varphi_{1},\cdots,\varphi_{d_{i}})
be a
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthonormal basis of
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
.

Proposition C.2
.

Let
i\geq 1
. We assume that
\cap_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\{\varphi_{j}=0\}=\emptyset
. Then

\max_{\Omega}m_{\lambda}\to-\infty\quad\text{ as }\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. For any
x\in\Omega
fixed we decompose
G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
as

G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)=\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)+\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}a_{j}^{\lambda}(x)\varphi_{j}(y),

where
\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
satisfies
\int_{\Omega}\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\varphi_{j}\,dy=0
for all
1\leq j\leq d_{i}
. Since, for all
1\leq j\leq d_{i}
, we have
\varphi_{j}=\frac{1}{\Lambda_{i}-\lambda}(-\Delta-\lambda)\varphi_{j}
, a representation formula for
\varphi_{j}
yields

(C.6)

\displaystyle a_{j}^{\lambda}(x)=-\frac{1}{\lambda-\Lambda_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x).

By definition of
G_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
and with (
C.6
) it is easily seen that
\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
satisfies

(C.7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)-\lambda\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=\delta_{x}-\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=0\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

for any
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
. We observe that
\delta_{x}=\text{div}F_{x}
in the distributional sense, where
F_{x}(y)=\frac{x-y}{|x-y|^{3}}
for
y\in\Omega
. For a fixed
1\leq p<\frac{3}{2}
,
F_{x}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. Since, for every
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
x\in\Omega
,
\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
is orthogonal to
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
for the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-scalar product, classical arguments from standard elliptic theory (see for instance
[
30
, Theorem 7.1, Theorem 7.4]
) show that for any
1\leq p<\frac{3}{2}
fixed there exists
C=C(\Omega,p)>0
such that, for every
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
x\in\Omega
, we have

\|\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\|_{W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C.

If we choose
p\geq\frac{6}{5}
, Sobolev’s embeddings then show that there exists
C>0
such that, for every
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
x\in\Omega

(C.8)

\|\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C.

We now let, for
x\neq y
in
\Omega
,

\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,y)=\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,y)-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}.

The definition of
m_{\lambda}
and (
C.6
) then yield, for any
x\in\Omega
and
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
,

(C.9)

\displaystyle m_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,x)-\frac{1}{\lambda-\Lambda_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x)^{2}.

Independently, using (
C.7
) it is easily seen that
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
satisfies

(C.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)-\lambda\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi|x-y|}-\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)&=-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

and standard elliptic theory shows that, for any
x\in\Omega
fixed,
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. Let now
G_{0}
denote the Green’s function of
-\Delta
in
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary conditions, which solves (
5.1
) with
\lambda=0
. Standard estimates (see again
[
55
]
) show that
0\leq G_{0}(x,y)\leq C|x-y|^{-1}
for
x\neq y
in
\Omega
. Using (
C.8
) we have
\int_{\Omega}G_{0}(x,z)\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,z)dx=O(1)
as
\lambda\to\bar{\lambda}
. With the latter, a representation formula for
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
shows that there exists
C>0
such that, for every
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
and
x\in\Omega
, we have

(C.11)

\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,x)\leq C+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}G_{0}(x,y)\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}d\sigma(y).

We claim that

(C.12)

\limsup_{d(x,\partial\Omega)\to 0}\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}G_{0}(x,y)\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}d\sigma(y)=-\infty.

Assume for the moment that (
C.12
) holds true. For
\delta>0
we let in the following
\Omega_{\delta}=\{x\in\Omega,d(x,\partial\Omega)<\delta\}
. For any
L>0
fixed, by (
C.9
), (
C.11
) and (
C.12
) we may choose
\delta
small enough such that for every
\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1})
,

(C.13)

\max_{\Omega_{\delta}}m_{\lambda}(x)\leq-L.

Independently, since every
\varphi_{j}
,
1\leq j\leq d_{i}
is smooth in
\overline{\Omega}
, the assumption
\cap_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\{\varphi_{j}=0\}=\emptyset
implies that
\min_{x\in\Omega\backslash\Omega_{\delta}}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x)^{2}>0
. Using (
C.9
) we thus have

(C.14)

\lim_{\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}}\max_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{\delta}}m_{\lambda}(x)=-\infty.

Combining (
C.13
) and (
C.14
) finally shows that for every
L>0
fixed,

\limsup_{\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}}\max_{\Omega}m_{\lambda}\leq-L

holds. Letting
L\to+\infty
concludes the proof of Proposition
C.2
. We thus only have to prove (
C.12
). Let, for
z\in\Omega
,
u(z)=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}G_{0}(z,y)\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}d\sigma(y)
. It is easily seen that
u
is the unique solution of
-\Delta u=0
in
\Omega
with
u=-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-\cdot|}
in
\partial\Omega
. As a consequence, and by (
C.1
), we thus have
u(z)=H_{0}(x,z)
and thus

\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}G_{0}(x,y)\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}d\sigma(y)=H_{0}(x,x)=m_{0}(x).

It is well-known that
m_{0}(x)\sim\frac{-1}{8\pi d(x,\partial\Omega)}\to-\infty
as
d(x,\partial\Omega)\to 0
(see e.g.
[
54
, Equation (2.8)]
), and this concludes the proof of (
C.12
).
∎

For
i\geq 0
we recall the definition of
\lambda_{i,*}
introduced in (
1.4
):

\lambda_{i,*}=\inf\big\{\lambda\in(\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{i+1}),\,\,\max_{\Omega}m_{\lambda}>0\big\},

where we used the convention
\Lambda_{0}=0
. Proposition
C.1
shows that
\lambda_{i,*}<\Lambda_{i+1}
. It is well-known that
\lambda_{0,*}\in(0,\Lambda_{1})
(see
[
13
, Equation (1.51)]
). When
i=1
, since
E_{\Lambda_{1}}(\Omega)
is spanned by a positive eigenvector
\varphi_{1}
, Proposition
C.2
shows that we similarly have

(C.15)

\lambda_{1,*}\in(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}).

When
i\geq 2
and on a general smooth bounded domain
\Omega
, however, we only have
\lambda_{i,*}\geq\Lambda_{i}
. By Proposition
C.2
the strict inequality holds when the elements in
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
have no common zero, but this condition is complicated to verify. For a general
i\geq 2
, suprisingly, the asymptotic behavior of the mass function
x\mapsto m_{\lambda}(x)
as
\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}
is related to the behavior of the nodal lines of the eigenvectors in
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
. To illustrate this fact we conclude this section by proving a generalisation of Proposition
C.2
. If
i\geq 2
we let
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}
be the Green’s function of
-\Delta-\Lambda_{i}
, that is the unique function
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\Omega)
such that for all
x\in\Omega
,
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)
is
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonal to
E_{\Lambda_{i}}(\Omega)
and satisfies

(C.16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)-\Lambda_{i}\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)&=\delta_{x}-\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x)\varphi_{j}(y)\quad\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)&=0\quad\text{ in }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

Again by standard elliptic theory, for any fixed
x\in\Omega
we may expand
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)
as

\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi|x-y|}+\bar{m}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x)+O(|x-y|)\quad\text{ as }y\to x.

We then have the following result:

Proposition C.3
.

Let
i\geq 2
and
x\in\Omega
be fixed.

•

Assume that
x\not\in\cap_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\{\varphi_{j}=0\}
. Then

m_{\lambda}(x)\to-\infty\quad\text{ as }\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}.

•

Assume that
x\in\cap_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\{\varphi_{j}=0\}
. Then

m_{\lambda}(x)\to\bar{m}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x)\quad\text{ as }\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}.

Proof.

We use the notations in the proof of Proposition
C.2
. Estimate (
C.8
) together with (
C.7
) shows that, for every fixed
x\in\Omega
,
\bar{G}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
weakly converges to
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)
in
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
for some
\frac{6}{5}\leq p<\frac{3}{2}
, up to a subsequence as
\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}
. Again (
C.8
) together with (
C.10
) and standard elliptic theory shows that there is
0<\alpha<1
such that
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,\cdot)
strongly converges in
C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
to
\bar{G}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x,\cdot)-\frac{1}{4\pi|x-\cdot|}
. As a consequence,
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,x)\to\bar{m}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x)
as
\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}
. If
x\in\cap_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\{\varphi_{j}=0\}
, (
C.9
) shows that
\bar{H}_{\lambda}(x,x)=m_{\lambda}(x)
and the conclusion follows. If
x\not\in\cap_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\{\varphi_{j}=0\}
then
\sum_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\varphi_{j}(x)^{2}>0
and
again by (
C.9
) we have
m_{\lambda}(x)\to-\infty
as
\lambda\underset{>}{\to}\Lambda_{i}
.
∎

Proposition
C.3
thus shows that determining whether the strict inequality
\lambda_{i,*}>\Lambda_{i}
holds reduces to investigating the sign of
x\in\Omega\mapsto\bar{m}_{\Lambda_{i}}(x)
. This seems to be a complicated question in general and it may happen that
\lambda_{i,*}=\Lambda_{i}
for some
i\geq 2
, although no examples of domains
\Omega
where this holds are known.
