Know Your Users! Estimating User Domain Knowledge in Conversational Recommenders
Ivica Kostric, Ujwal Gadiraju, Krisztian Balog

Abstract
The ideal conversational recommender system (CRS) acts like a savvy salesperson, adapting its language and suggestions to each user’s level of expertise. However, most current systems treat all users as experts, leading to frustrating and inefficient interactions when users are unfamiliar with a domain. Systems that can adapt their conversational strategies to a user’s knowledge level stand to offer a much more natural and effective experience. To make a step toward such adaptive systems, we introduce a new task: estimating user domain knowledge from conversations, enabling a CRS to better understand user needs and personalize interactions. A key obstacle to developing such adaptive systems is the lack of suitable data; to our knowledge, no existing dataset captures the conversational behaviors of users with varying levels of domain knowledge. Furthermore, in most dialogue collection protocols, users are free to express their own preferences, which tends to concentrate on popular items and well-known features, offering little insight into how novices explore or learn about unfamiliar features. To address this, we design a game-based data collection protocol that elicits varied expressions of knowledge, release the resulting dataset, and provide an initial analysis to highlight its potential for future work on user-knowledge-aware CRS.

Introduction

Helping users find relevant items in vast catalogs is a key challenge that conversational recommender systems (CRS) address by engaging them in interactive dialogues, typically involving preference elicitation based on item attributes

7
]
. The effectiveness of CRS relies heavily on accurately capturing user preferences and interpreting their expressions about desired attributes

However, most current CRS approaches typically assume that users understand item attributes clearly and can directly map them to their preferences
[
21

14

20

. In contrast,
11

In isolation, both approaches can lead to limited and inefficient interactions, as users differ significantly in domain knowledge, vocabulary, and preferred interaction style. As illustrated in the example in Figure

This forces users to adapt to the system’s language, even though recent work shows that task performance and user satisfaction improve significantly when a CRS adapts its interaction style to the user’s domain knowledge

To bridge this gap and create more adaptive systems, we introduce the novel task of estimating user domain knowledge from conversations, enabling a CRS to tailor its interaction strategy—from preference elicitation to the presentation of recommendations and explanations. Following prior work on modeling knowledge, we adopt a discretized representation, where users are grouped into a small number of categories
[
2
]
. Specifically, we categorize users into three relative levels of knowledge:
low
,
medium
, and
high
. Low domain knowledge is characterized by infrequent and superficial interactions with the domain, where users have limited exposure and may lack a precise technical vocabulary or jargon. In contrast, high domain knowledge corresponds to frequent, hobby-like engagement, where users are more familiar with detailed attributes and exhibit a refined understanding. Medium knowledge falls between these extremes, indicating occasional but shallow interactions.

A major obstacle in advancing this task is the lack of datasets that capture user domain knowledge within a conversational context. Existing CRS datasets rely on open-ended protocols in which participants discuss only the attributes they are comfortable with
[
8
,
16
,
13
]
. This approach leads to conversations focused primarily on well-known aspects of the domain, seldom encouraging users to explore areas outside their knowledge. As a result, these setups fail to capture the dynamic exchanges that naturally emerge when users encounter unfamiliar aspects in real-world scenarios. Consequently, systems trained on such data might fail to generalize or adapt to less common attribute mentions, leading to misunderstandings and lower satisfaction.

Given these challenges, a critical question is:
RQ1:
How can we design a data collection protocol that elicits signals of domain knowledge through engaging and natural conversations?
We address this by implementing a gamified data collection protocol where participants are assigned a specific target item, presented as a concise background narrative. During the interaction, participants must guide the CRS to recommend the target item by providing preference statements, responding to elicitation prompts, and discussing the system’s recommendations.

Our second question concerns the differences in interaction patterns between users. Specifically,
RQ2:
How can we characterize users with varying domain knowledge in their interactions with a CRS?
To answer this, we analyze the collected conversations for behavioral patterns and utterance characteristics such as length, vocabulary complexity, and the frequency of attribute mentions. We hypothesize that knowledgeable users use more precise language and more often reference specific attributes, while users with lower domain knowledge might use broader, less technical descriptions.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a novel task of adapting conversational recommender systems to user domain knowledge by predicting users’ familiarity with the domain during interactions.

•

We design a dialogue collection protocol,
RecQuest
, that incorporates gamification, using background narratives and attribute clues to elicit natural conversations containing domain knowledge signals.

•

We collect and release a novel dataset of CRS dialogues that captures these domain-specific interactions, offering a valuable resource for examining the role of knowledge in recommendation scenarios.

Related Work

Our work is situated at the intersection of two key research areas. We first review the main paradigms of CRS, as they provide the architectural context for the system we developed for our data collection. We then critically examine existing dialogue collection methods, highlighting the gap our protocol directly addresses.

Conversational Recommender Systems.

Research on CRS is broadly categorized into two main groups.
Attribute-based CRS
cast the dialogue as a slot-filling exercise
[
12
,
4
]
, eliciting preferences from users about items or item attributes with fixed templates.

Generation-based CRS
, in contrast, generate free-form responses while recommending items, generally utilizing an end-to-end architecture that integrates the conversation and the recommendation components
[
22
]
.
Our work is relevant to both paradigms, as estimating user knowledge can, among others, help tailor the elicitation strategy and the explanatory language.
Our work employs a hybrid CRS that combines attribute-based preference elicitation with natural language generation for recommendations and explanations. The system maintains an internal representation of user preferences and supports interactions entirely in natural language.

3
Dialogue Collection Method

This section outlines the data collection protocol for a recommendation game designed to expose domain knowledge effects in CRS dialogues.

3.1
Task Overview

To elicit domain-knowledge signals in a controlled way, we design a recommendation game called
RecQuest
. Participants are given a short scenario satisfied by a single target item from a predefined collection. The goal is a natural, goal-directed discussion of items and features, allowing differences in expertise to become apparent in their free-form articulation.
To cover a range of consumer product types, we focus on five domains: bicycles, laptops, digital cameras, running shoes, and smartwatches. These categories were deliberately chosen to represent both everyday items and more specialized products, enabling us to examine how interaction patterns differ across domains and various types of consumer goods.

Item Collection.

For each domain, we curated an item subset from the Amazon Review Dataset (2023)
[
6
]
. We extracted metadata, structured attributes, and product descriptions, then removed duplicates and near-identical items using cosine similarity over vector representations of their descriptions. Each resulting collection contains diverse but distinguishable products with clearly defined attributes.
Each item retains only non-identifying metadata (e.g., name, category, key features), ensuring all data is publicly available and suitable for research.

3.2
Game Mechanics

Figure 2:
Screenshot of the game in action, demonstrating a user who obtained several recommendations during the conversation and is ready to make a final selection.

Game Start.

At the start, participants see a chat interface and a brief background story setting up a use scenario (Fig.
2
). The story is generated from a specific item in the curated domain collection to anchor the conversation context without revealing the item itself. Each target item has two background-story variants: a concise (5 feature constraints, 80–100 words) and a more detailed version (10 feature constraints, 150–200 words). This enables us to investigate whether the richness of contextual cues affects how participants formulate preferences or describe product features.
Participants are instructed to find the item that best fits the scenario by engaging in a turn-based dialogue with the CRS.

Participant Turns.

Participant turns are open-ended, allowing various conversational strategies.
Participants may state preferences, answer/ask questions, or critique recommendations.
The ability to ask about an item’s properties allows them to probe which constraints or features are satisfied—much like in real-world interactions between customers and salespeople. Feedback on recommendations is optional. Each recommendation includes an image and a brief system-generated explanation of its fit to the participant’s stated needs. Participants may ask follow-up questions, refine their needs, or ignore the suggestion, using their own vocabulary.

A session requires that CRS has produced at least two recommendations, which remain visible and can be selected at any point. Selecting an item triggers a confirmation prompt, asking whether they are certain of their choice, followed by a request for a brief feature-based justification. Participants are incentivized to identify the correct item by a monetary reward, fostering realistic behavior and increasing the external validity of the data thus gathered.

CRS Turns.

On each system turn, the CRS performs one of three possible actions: asking an elicitation question, answering a participant’s question, or providing a recommendation. All actions are generated by a target-aware conversational agent guided by a mix of rule-based heuristics and LLM output.

•

Elicitation.

When a participant expresses several preferences at once, the CRS restricts them to at most three constraints. If more are listed, the system asks which ones are most important before continuing. This keeps early turns focused and prevents participants from giving the entire need description in a single turn.
Elicitation questions are open-ended but specific to attributes present in the domain’s schema (e.g.,
“Is weight or range more important for your use?”
). Thus, they maintain engagement through incremental exchanges.

•

Answering questions.

When the participant asks about item attributes or domain facts, the CRS responds directly. All factual answers are LLM-generated and framed relative to the target item when applicable, so that information indirectly guides the user toward it without revealing the item’s identity.

•

Recommendation.

A recommendation turn presents a single item from the domain’s curated collection together with an image and a short natural-language explanation. The explanation is generated through a two-step LLM procedure: (i) the system identifies key differences between the candidate item and the hidden target, and (ii) produces an explanation that highlights how those differences relate to the stated preferences (example in Fig.
2
). The system may suggest a previously recommended item if new preferences align with it.

Game End.

A session ends when the participant confirms a selection.
The session length is a configurable parameter (limited to 15 minutes in our work). If the time expires, a soft grace period begins, allowing finalizing a selection from the recommendations already shown.
On confirmation, participants provide a brief, feature-based justification, after which the dialogue concludes.

3.3
CRS Architecture

Figure 3:
CRS Architecture.

Figure
3
shows the CRS architecture and data flow between its core components. Upon each participant’s turn, the system processes the input, updates its internal state, and determines the next intent before producing a response.

Preference Summarizer.
The CRS maintains a structured JSON-like representation of atomic preference statements. After each turn, an LLM-based preference summarizer updates this record by merging newly mentioned constraints with the existing ones, ensuring all active needs are captured concisely.

Item Retrieval.
A retrieval step is triggered when the preference record contains at least two statements or when new ones are added. Dense vector representations of items are pre-computed and held in memory. Current preferences are encoded to retrieve relevant candidates. If
\geq 3
preferences appear in a single turn, the system defers retrieval and asks the participant to prioritize which constraints matter most before continuing. This mechanism is designed to promote multi-turn interaction, encouraging gradual refinements of needs through dialogue rather than trying to complete the task in a single turn.

Decision Module.
After preference updating and retrieval, the Decision Module, implemented as an LLM augmented with heuristics, selects the next conversational intent based on the preference list, available recommendations, and last message. It chooses one of four intents: redirect, answer, recommend, or elicit.
Redirects
occur when the dialogue drifts outside the designated domain.
Answer
intents are triggered when the user asks factual or conversational questions.
Recommendation
intents occur when sufficient and new preference information is available, while
elicitation
intents are used otherwise to gather additional constraints or clarify vague statements.
Once the intent is selected, the system generates a response through specialized LLM modules.

Response Generation.

Recommendation
turns involve a multi-step process: an LLM selects the best candidate from the top-10 retrieved items given the current preferences and the latest exchange.
A second model performs
target grounding
by comparing the selected candidate to the hidden target item, identifying which differences are relevant to the participant’s stated needs. This ensures explanations focus on meaningful contrasts. A third model generates the final natural language response that presents the recommended item and integrates the contrastive explanation. The
Answering
intent also uses target grounding, particularly for follow-up questions about recommendeds.
Redirection
and
Elicitation
use a single generation step to produce a contextually appropriate response.

4
Study Design and Setup

RecQuest
is designed to engage participants while simultaneously functioning as an effective mechanism for eliciting and measuring domain knowledge.

4.1
Study Flow

Our study consists of four sequential stages: a screening questionnaire, a domain knowledge assessment, the main task, and an exit questionnaire. This flow is designed to balance participants across domains and expertise levels while collecting both self-reported and measured indicators of domain knowledge. The self-reported knowledge categories used in our study offer structure, noting that the boundaries between them are soft, with many participants falling in the middle of the spectrum.

Screening.

Participants first complete a brief self-assessment of their familiarity with each of the five domains used in this work: bicycles, laptops, digital cameras, running shoes, and smartwatches. For each domain, they classify themselves into one of three categories:
novice
,
intermediate
, or
expert
. After the screening, they are assigned a domain with the least number of participants for their expertise level at the time of choosing. Thus, the self-assessment provides a coarse estimate of knowledge, which is used to balance participation, ensuring that each expertise level is represented approximately equally across domains.

Domain knowledge questionnaire.

Following the self-assessment, participants complete a short factual knowledge test in the domain to which they are assigned. This step provides us with the ground-truth estimate of their true expertise. The questionnaire adopts the certainty-in-knowledge format proposed by
19
. Each item presents a factual statement about the domain, and participants rate its correctness using one of five options:
Definitely True
,
Probably True
,
I don’t know
,
Probably False
, or
Definitely False
. This captures both accuracy and confidence, and is more informative than binary correctness.

Main Task.

Participants are then presented with the instructions for
RecQuest
and perform the main task presented in Section
3
. The assigned domain determines the item pool from which each participant is randomly assigned one of 3 target items and its corresponding background story. Each session lasts up to fifteen minutes, with the interaction process and system behavior governed by the game mechanics and dialogue flow previously outlined.

Exit questionnaire.

After completing the main task, participants fill out an exit questionnaire in which they rate the quality of the interaction and provide open-ended feedback on their experience. These responses offer a subjective account of interaction satisfaction and help identify areas for improvement in both the system and the overall study design.

4.2
Participants

Participants were recruited from the Prolific platform,
2
2

2

https://www.prolific.com/
, who were from English-speaking countries and had a
\geq 95\%
approval rate and
\geq 1000
previous submissions. Each participant could take part in only one task to avoid repeated exposure to the experimental setup. Participants completed the study fully online. After reading the task instructions, they proceeded through the screening questionnaire, domain knowledge test, main task, and exit questionnaire. A complete session lasted for 18 minutes on average. Participants received an hourly wage of 8£, in line with the platform’s fair-wage guidelines, with a 1£ bonus awarded to those who successfully identified the target items.

4.3
Generating Game States

Item collection.
From the Amazon dataset, we extracted all items with categories related to the five chosen domains. These resulting collections ranged from 1k to 200k and included multiple (near-)duplicates differing only in brand. To clean this data and maintain sufficient item differentiation, we narrowed down the original sets to a curated collection of 50 items per domain. We achieved this by embedding the item descriptions using
Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B
and running k-means clustering on the embeddings. We chose
k=50
and picked the items nearest to the cluster centroids. The same embedding model was also used for retrieval during gameplay.

Target Items.

We prepared a total of 30 background stories.
To do this, we selected three unique items from the curated collection for each domain, and for each created two versions:
long
and
short
stories. Each story was designed to have a fixed number of feature constraints (ten for the long version and five for the short) that would uniquely identify the target item. Leveraging ChatGPT-5, we converted these constraints into a narration without explicitly mentioning the corresponding item features (see Fig.
2
for an example).

LLMs.
The CRS pipeline relies on multiple calls to an LLM, with different prompts and inputs for each component. For all LLM calls we employed
gpt-4.1-mini
, for which early tests indicated a good balance between speed and quality.

5.2
Characterizing Interaction Patterns by Domain Knowledge

To answer RQ2, we must first establish a reliable ground-truth measure for user knowledge. We then use this measure to analyze differences in interaction patterns, focusing on dialogue acts, task outcomes, and specific language use.

Assessing Domain Knowledge.

As explained in detail in Section
4
, to obtain ground-truth labels for the conversations, we conducted two types of assessment: self-assessment and a knowledge questionnaire.
Figure
4
(Left) shows the distribution of questions by the fraction of participants that correctly answered them. We see that all questions were answered correctly by at least 15% of participants, and there are only a handful of easy questions answered correctly by almost everyone. Most questions were answered correctly by 40% to 80% of participants. On the right, we have a distribution of assessed knowledge split by the self-assessed groups. It is clear that self-assessment is a noisy signal; it is therefore essential not to rely on self-assessment alone, but to include an unbiased knowledge questionnaire. In our study, the score from the knowledge questionnaire serves as the ground-truth label for user knowledge. In the following sections, we consider novices to be under the 20th percentile on the knowledge assessment and experts above the 80th percentile.

Figure 5:
(Left) Dialogue intent progression. (Right) Distribution of dialogue acts.

Dialogue Patterns and Task Outcomes.
The dialogue intent progression (Fig.
5
) shows that
Recommendation
dominates early interactions, peaking around the third turn, then gradually declines as
Answer About Recommendation
intents increase over time.
While the overall progression follows a similar pattern for all groups, we observe subtle differences: experts receive slightly more recommendations, consistent with more frequent preference updates and refinements, while novices engage in more question-asking, suggesting a greater need for clarification and guidance.
Beyond the flow of conversation, we also analyze
success rate
: the percentage of participants who correctly identified the target item. This rate varied, from 21.8% (running shoes) to 60.8% (bicycles), with an overall success rate of about one-third. Experts (36.3%) outperformed novices (31.9%), suggesting that greater domain knowledge helps users guide the system effectively.

Linguistic Markers of Expertise.
To further answer RQ2, we first analyze basic linguistic features, such as the number of turns and utterance length. Experts engaged in slightly longer dialogues (10.68 messages on average) and used longer utterances (17.3 words) than novices (9.87 messages, 14.0 words).

Next, we compute domain-specific TF-IDF scores to quantify differences in keyword usage between the novice and expert groups. For each domain, we construct joint TF-IDF models over all documents to ensure a shared vocabulary and comparable scaling. Then, we average TF-IDF values within each group. The resulting normalized differences indicate how strongly each keyword is associated with experts versus novices (ranging from -1 to +1).

Across domains, novices tend to use more general or evaluative terms (e.g., good, recommend, comfort), often reflecting subjective impressions or purchase intentions. Experts, in contrast, rely more on technical or domain-specific vocabulary (e.g., brake, autofocus, performance), focusing on detailed specifications and functional attributes. This pattern suggests that expert interactions with the CRS emphasize analytical comparison, while novices communicate in broader, more experience-based terms.

This TF-IDF analysis provides preliminary evidence and helps formulate a clear hypothesis:
experts tend to communicate using specific product attributes, while novices tend to communicate using intended usage or scenarios
.
To validate this hypothesis systematically, we designed an LLM-based annotation pipeline. We prompted
gpt-4.1-mini
to analyze each user utterance and extract phrases corresponding to two distinct categories: (i)
Specific Attributes
: Mentions of technical features, components, or specifications (e.g., “disc brakes”).
(ii)
Intended Usage
: Descriptions of goals, contexts, or scenarios (e.g., “for daily rides to work”).
This annotation allows us to move beyond simple keyword counts and quantify the method of communication on a per-utterance basis.
The results confirmed this hypothesis, especially at the conversation level. We found that experts mentioned significantly more specific attributes on average (9.01 per conversation) than novices (6.80). While experts also provided more usage-based context (5.38 vs. 4.59), their overall linguistic preference still leaned more heavily toward attributes (62.6% of expressions) compared to novices (59.8%).

6
Discussion

Before conducting the study, we considered collecting data only from clear novices and clear experts to simplify data collection and sharpen distinctions. In fact, narrowing the expertise range to users below the 10th percentile for novices and above the 90th percentile for experts illustrates how much sharper those distinctions can appear: experts average 10.1 attribute mentions per conversation, while novices do only 5.9 (compared to 9.0 vs. 6.8 under the 20th/80th percentile split). Also, attribute mentions rise to 64.1% versus 58.5% (compared to 62.6% vs. 59.8%). However, relying only on such extreme thresholds would have sacrificed ecological validity. Real users are rarely at the tails, and excluding the middle would remove the very cases that make knowledge estimation challenging and realistic. Additionally, because there is no single correct expertise threshold, we aim to cover the full spectrum and leave it to future researchers to define and determine the thresholds appropriate to their investigations.

While we see clear distinctions between knowledge levels and interaction styles, another layer of difficulty comes from the fact that experts correspond to a broader spectrum of interaction behavior. This can be due to their ability to transition between conversational styles, and how they express or articulate themselves.
Experts can freely shift between technical attributes and usage-based descriptions, depending on what feels most natural in the moment. As shown in Figure
6
, both groups begin with high usage-based interaction rates, but they diverge in later turns, with lower-expertise users maintaining consistently high usage while higher-expertise users alternate between usage and attribute expressions. For illustration, consider an exchange from a high-expertise laptop seeker. The user first states a usage-oriented goal:
“I want something that is good for editing highlight reels and gaming videos for streamers.”
They then specify a concrete technical constraint:
“It needs to have multiple HDMI ports.”
After the system proposes an option that only partially satisfies their needs, the user shifts back to a usage framing:
“I also need something that is good for gaming and not bog down with demanding tasks.”
The turn concludes with an explicit attribute requirement:
“I need a more powerful GPU.”
This flexibility weakens any assumption that expert users will consistently use an attribute-driven interaction and further emphasizes the need for a CRS that adapts to both a user’s knowledge level and their preferred way of interacting, rather than relying on a single elicitation strategy.

7
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this work, we introduced the novel task of adapting conversational recommender systems to user domain knowledge. To facilitate research in this area, we designed
RecQuest
, a gamified data collection protocol, and released the resulting dataset. Our analysis confirms the effectiveness of the protocol at capturing knowledge-based interactions in an engaging setting, revealing distinct behavioral and linguistic patterns.

Our new dataset opens several avenues for future work, providing a critical resource for benchmarking models that estimate user domain knowledge directly from dialogue. Furthermore, the data collection protocol provides a scalable framework, enabling the collection of additional, larger datasets in new domains. These resources pave the way for truly adaptive CRS that can personalize their interaction strategies—such as switching between attribute-based elicitation and usage-based guidance—to better support both novices and experts in their decision-making processes.
