Estimating quantile treatment effect on the original scale of the outcome variable: a case study of common cold treatments
Authors: Harri Hemilä, Matti Pirinen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffects of treatments on continuous outcomes are commonly estimated using the mean difference (in units of measurement) or the ratio of means (percentages), each providing a single average effect across the study population. Quantile treatment effect (QTE) analysis is more informative as it estimates the effect of treatment across the whole population. A limitation of the standard QTE is its presentation over control group quantiles, which can hinder interpretability. Presentation of the effect over the measurement units would often be more informative.MethodsWe introduce a method to estimate back-transformed QTE (BQTE), which presents QTEs as a function of the original outcome values in the control group. This approach uses a bootstrap algorithm to estimate both the BQTE curve and its uncertainty. We further derive informative bounds for the average treatment effect at the upper and lower tails of the distribution. The method was applied to 3 datasets on the treatment of the common cold: zinc gluconate lozenges, zinc acetate lozenges, and nasal carrageenan.ResultsAcross all 3 datasets, BQTE revealed substantial heterogeneity in treatment effects on the units of measurement scale (days). Specifically, shorter colds showed smaller average effects than longer colds, indicating that the assumption of a constant mean difference across the distribution may be inappropriate. In all cases, the relative scale provided a better summary of the BQTE distribution than the mean difference.ConclusionsThe BQTE method enhances the interpretability of QTEs by presenting results on the outcome’s original scale. It provides a nuanced understanding of how the average treatment effect varies across the distribution. BQTE is particularly suited for analyzing continuous clinical outcomes such as illness duration or hospital stay and offers a valuable complement to the standard effect size measures in individual-patient data meta-analysis and clinical trial reporting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13063-025-09265-z.

Introduction

In meta-analyses of continuous outcomes, treatment effects are often estimated using the standardized mean difference (SMD) scale [1]. The SMD approach expresses the difference between treatment and control groups in units of standard deviation (SD) such that one unit equals the SD of that trial. This standardization allows for combining studies that use different measurement scales. However, interpreting SMDs can be difficult. For instance, few medical readers can easily assess whether a 0.97 SD unit effect of zinc on common cold duration [2] is clinically meaningful. Unsurprisingly, a recent survey of physicians found that SMDs—despite being widely used—were poorly understood and considered the least useful presentation format by physicians [3].

Another common approach is to present continuous outcomes on the average treatment effect (ATE) scale, also called the mean difference scale [1], which is based on the units of the empirical observations. This format is often easier to interpret. For example, stating that colds are, on average, 1.03 days shorter in the zinc groups is straightforward [4]. However, many continuous outcomes have wide distributions. In the case of colds—which can last for less than 1 day to over 2 weeks—a 1-day cold cannot be shortened by more than 1 day. Thus, the ATE may not adequately represent effects across the distribution, especially at the lower tail.

A third method for estimating the effect on continuous outcomes is to use the relative scale, calculating the ratio of means (RoM) [5]. In this approach, the control group mean is used as a reference, and the RoM expresses the treatment effect relative to it. While the use of the relative scale has been very common over decades in the analysis of binary outcomes, it has rarely been used in the analysis of continuous outcomes. Yet, there are strong arguments that continuous outcomes, such as the duration of illness, should also be analyzed on the relative scale since it adjusts for baseline variation between trials, and appears to better capture within-trial effects [5, 6]. RoM estimates can also be expressed as percentages, which are familiar and accessible to most readers.

Each of these three methods uses a single value to describe the average effect of the medical intervention on the population. A fourth approach, the quantile treatment effect (QTE), describes the effect of the treatment by comparing the entire outcome distributions between the treatment and control groups [7–14]. QTE plots show the difference between the treatment and control groups on the vertical axis, with the horizontal axis showing the quantiles of the outcome distribution in the control group [8, 12, 13]. The QTE function describes the effect of the treatment on the population in more detail than the three average effects described above. With the added assumption that the ordering of the outcomes of the treated individuals was the same as their ordering would have been, had they not been treated [15], the QTE gives an estimate of the treatment effect for each quantile level of the untreated population. However, interpreting QTEs can be challenging since quantiles in the untreated control group are not as intuitive as the units of the original outcome variable. For example, in the Mossad et al. (1996) trial [16], the QTE of zinc gluconate lozenges was 8 days at the 80th percentile [13, 14], but it is unclear what control group duration that percentile represents.

In this paper we propose an approach to estimate the back-transformed quantile treatment effect (BQTE) function together with an estimate of its uncertainty. The BQTE presents the QTE on the empirical outcome scale making interpretation more intuitive. While mathematically our BQTE function corresponds to an earlier definition of a “shift function” by Doksum [7], we use the term BQTE to highlight its direct connection to the familiar QTE function. The methodological novelties in our work include a bootstrap algorithm to estimate the BQTE function and its uncertainty at any given outcome value in the control group and a derivation of informative bounds on the average treatment effects at the upper and lower tails of the population. We provide a freely available implementation of these methods. To support interpretation of our results, we explain in detail the relationship between BQTE and the theoretical concept of individual-level treatment effects. Finally, we apply the methodology to trial data on the duration of the common cold from a zinc gluconate lozenge trial [16], pooled data from three zinc acetate lozenge trials [17–19], and pooled data from two nasal iota-carrageenan trials [20, 21]. Through these analyses, we demonstrate how our approach enables an informative comparison of whether the original scale or the relative scale better captures the observed effect of a treatment in a specific data set.

Connection between the BQTE distribution and the individual-level treatment effect

The BQTE function is a population-level measure of the effect of the treatment. We could also ask how the treatment would have affected a particular individual whose outcome value without the treatment is known. As we explain in Supplementary Material, such an individual-level treatment effect can be estimated by the BQTE function only when we assume that the treatment preserves the order of outcome values in the study population. However, even without such an assumption, the BQTE function can be used to provide upper and lower bounds for the ATE at the tails of the control distribution. We define the upper tail back-transformed quantile treatment effect (UTBQTE) and the lower tail back-transformed quantile treatment effect (LTBQTE) for any outcome value x in the control population as differences in the expected values of the outcome between the corresponding upper and lower tails of the treatment and control populations. In Supplementary Material, we give definitions of these measures also for discrete distributions and we show that UTBQTE gives an upper bound for the ATE of the upper tail and LTBQTE gives a lower bound for the ATE of the lower tail. Our implementation of the BQTE function [22] includes the computation of these bounds together with their confidence intervals estimated by the bootstrap method.

Validation of BQTE approach

We validated our methodology and implementation using a simulation study reported in detail in Supplementary Material. First, we assessed which were the suitable quantile levels as a function of sample sizes where our estimator performed reliably, that is, bias was small and the confidence intervals were well-calibrated. Second, we assessed how K, the number of the cut points used in the piecewise linear interpolation, affected the results. Finally, we compared three versions of our estimator for the BQTE function (linear interpolation with and without bootstrap averaging (bagging) and the natural spline model) with the previously introduced Doksum estimator [7].

As a summary of the validation results reported in the Supplementary Material, we observed reliable results by restricting the estimation of the BQTE and QTE functions between the quantile levels of 10/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{\text{min}}$$\end{document}Nmin and 1–10/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N}_{\text{min}}$$\end{document}Nmin (Fig. S2). Our default choice for the number of cutoff points is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K={N}_{\text{C}}$$\end{document}K=NC based on the results reported in Fig. S3. In Fig. S4, we compared the root-mean-square-error (RMSE) of Doksum’s estimator [7] and the three versions of our estimator, and we found smaller RMSE for our estimators. Additionally, we observed that the linear interpolation with bagging performed at least as well as the more complex spline approach, and both performed clearly better than the linear interpolation without bagging. In Fig. S5, we show that there are clear differences between the BQTE and QTE approaches with respect to interpretability of the results and the variance of the estimators. We also derive and validate asymptotic formulas for the variance of the BQTE and QTE estimators in Supplementary Material.

Common cold trials

The contexts of the four common cold trials with zinc lozenges, and the characteristics of the patients are described in the trial reports, and summarized in previous analyses [6, 24, 25]. In brief, all trials were randomized, double-blind and placebo controlled. In the zinc gluconate trial, Mossad et al. (1996) studied employees of the Cleveland Clinic (mean age 38 y) [16]. In the zinc acetate lozenge trials, Petrus et al. (1998) recruited volunteers from the University of Texas (mean 26 y) [17], and Prasad et al. (2000, 2008) recruited volunteers from Detroit Medical Center (mean 35–37 y) [18, 19]. All trials recruited patients with natural common colds acquired in the community, and all trials tested the treatment effect of zinc lozenges.

Mossad et al. (1996) published the findings of their trial on zinc gluconate lozenges as survival curves [16]. The numbers of common cold patients recovering each day were measured from the curves [25].

The data sets of the three randomized trials on zinc acetate lozenges [17–19] were provided by the authors of the trials and were used in previous individual-patient data meta-analyses of common cold duration [24] and recovery rate [25]. In our analysis of the zinc acetate lozenge trials, we combined the three data sets. The zinc lozenge data sets are available [22].

In the Mossad (1996) trial [16], there were eight censored observations (8% of the total). In the placebo group there were two on day 7, one on day 15, one on day 16, and two on day 19; and in the zinc treatment group there was one on day 9 and one on day 11. We imputed the common cold duration as the day of censoring. Four of the censored observations were beyond the 93rd percentile and this imputation has minimal influence on our analysis. There were no censored observations in the three zinc acetate lozenge trials.

The characteristics of the patients and the contexts of the two nasal iota-carrageenan trials are described in the trial reports [20, 21], and were summarized in the previous meta-analyses [26, 27]. In brief, the Ludvig et al. (2013) trial with adults (mean age 33 y) [20] and the Fazekas et al. (2012) trial with children (mean 5 y) [21] were randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trials, carried out in Vienna, Austria. For enrollment, participants were required to have mild to moderate common cold symptoms of natural origin. The numbers of common cold patients recovering each day were measured from the published curves [27]. In these two trials, there were 6 censored observations in the carrageenan groups and 21 in the placebo groups, all on day 20. We imputed the common cold duration as the day of censoring. In our bootstrap approach, right-hand censoring in the control group typically has little influence on the estimation of BQTE on days prior to censoring. In a sensitivity analysis, we imputed 30 days for the 27 censored observations but observed no notable difference from our original results (see Supplementary Material Fig. S6). The carrageenan data set is available [22].

Discussion

Quantile regression is a well-established statistical method, which has been widely used in econometrics [28–34]. So far, it has been little used in clinical medicine though its use has been encouraged by several authors [35–39].

The QTE approach is a special case of quantile regression and can be particularly useful in the analysis of randomized trials and other controlled studies since it enables investigation of how the treatment affects the whole distribution of a continuous outcome of untreated patients [7–14, 27, 40–43]. The QTE approach can be used to examine treatment effects on the duration of various illnesses, the duration of hospital stay and ICU stay, and on comparable continuous outcomes. These outcomes usually have asymmetric distributions with fat tails, and censored observations at the right-hand tail are not uncommon, yet the QTE analysis allows for all these features.

QTE values are usually shown on the vertical axis as a function of the quantiles of the outcome in the untreated population on the horizontal axis [8, 12, 13]. An attractive interpretation of QTE is as the average benefit from the treatment for patients in a particular quantile of the untreated distribution, for example, in the first decile or in the last decile. Conceptually, however, this interpretation requires that the treatment preserves the relative order of the outcome value among the treated compared to the hypothetical case where they were not treated [15]; see Supplementary Material for a detailed discussion; and [14]. Since there is no way to confirm that the treatment operates in this way, strictly speaking, QTE is a quantity related to populations rather than to any single individual. The same is true for our BQTE values that transform the QTE function back to the original observation scale. However, using the BQTE function, we are able to derive general upper and lower bounds on the average treatment effect of the individuals from the upper or lower tails of the untreated population without making any assumptions about how the treatment operates, i.e. whether order is preserved (see Supplementary Material). This information provides an informative extension of the standard ATE approach that uses a single value to describe the average treatment effect on the whole population.

In medicine, often a clinically more relevant question is how large the effect is on patients with a particular duration of disease rather than on patients in a particular quantile of the disease duration. Furthermore, the impact of treatment on a disease of long duration is often much more relevant than slightly shortening an already short disease. In calculating the overall ATE, the latter may mask substantially larger effects on the former. By comparing the entire distributions of disease durations, our approach captures more information about the effect of the treatment than a single estimate of ATE. It can be applied to single RCTs as in Fig. 2, and to individual-patient data (IPD) meta-analyses as in Figs. 3 and 4. Our approach applies to IPD but even when only a limited number of controlled studies included in a meta-analysis have IPD available, our approach can be used to test whether the RoM or the ATE approach gives an estimate that better describes the full BQTE distribution. Thereafter the study-level findings can be combined using the more appropriate scale.

Conceptually, our analyses are important in illustrating that the ATE estimates, which are widely used in meta-analyses and in analyses of individual trials, appear to seldom describe properly the whole BQTE distribution. In all three data sets, there was a strong discrepancy between the ATE estimates and the BQTE estimates at the extreme ends of the outcome distribution of the untreated group (Figs. 2A, 3A and 4A). As a separate approach, we used the UTBQTE and LTBQTE functions to calculate bounds for the average treatment effects in the tails, and we showed that the ATEs at the tails deviate from the overall ATE estimate no matter how the treatment operates, i.e. without any assumptions about order preservation between the control and treatment groups (Figs. 2B, 3B and 4B). In general, the discrepancy between the BQTE function and the ATE estimate is the smallest when the treatment preserves order whereas the variance of the treatment effect increases when the treatment deviates more from maintaining the order (see Supplementary Material and [14]).

In both zinc lozenge data sets, the relative scale estimates of BQTE were consistent with a constant value throughout the distribution (Figs. 2C and 3C). This gives further support for a wider use of the relative scale in the analysis of continuous outcomes [5, 6, 44]. However, our findings should not be interpreted as an indication that in all cases the relative BQTE is necessarily constant over the whole range of the outcome value in the control group. Rather, our approach provides an easy tool to assess whether the relative BQTE is constant over the control outcome distribution in a particular case. BQTE of carrageenan on the relative scale does not appear to be constant but rather seems greater at long colds, although the confidence intervals for the effects are wide and a firm conclusion cannot be drawn (Fig. 4C). Our comparisons of the BQTE approach with the ATE and RoM estimates suggest that often the latter is preferable.

We do not propose that our approach should replace the standard plotting of QTEs by the quantiles of the outcome distribution in the control group. Both ways of presenting data have their strengths, and it might be most informative to present results by both approaches.
