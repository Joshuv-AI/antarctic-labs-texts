Normative feeling: socially patterned affective mechanisms
Stavros Anagnou, Daniel Polani, Christoph Salge

Abstract
Norms and the normative processes that enforce them such as social maintenance are considered fundamental building blocks of human societies, shaping many aspects of our cognition. However, emerging work argues that the building blocks of normativity emerged much earlier in evolution than previously considered. In light of this, we argue that normative processes must be taken into account to consider the evolution of even ancient processes such as affect. We show through an agent-based model (with an evolvable model of affect) that different affective dispositions emerge when taking into account social maintenance. In this paper we demonstrate that social maintenance results in the emergence of a minimal population regulation mechanism in a dynamic environment, without the need to predict the state of the environment or reason about the mental state of others. We use a cultural interpretation of our model to derive a new definition of norm emergence which distinguishes between indirect and direct social maintenance. Indirect social maintenance tends to one equilibrium (similar to environmental scaffolding) and the richer direct social maintenance results in many possible equilibria in behaviour, capturing an important aspect of normative behaviour in that it bears a certain degree of arbitrariness. We also distinguish between single-variable and mechanistic normative regularities. A mechanistic regularity rather than a particular behaviour specified by one value e.g. walking speed, its a collection of values that specify a culturally patterned version of a psychological mechanism e.g. a disposition. This is how culture reprograms entire cognitive and physiological systems.

1
Introduction

Social norms are said to be maintained through various processes known as social maintenance
[
37
]
. This can come in the form of positive reinforcement of a compliant behaviour (rewards) or negative deterrents to discourage non compliant behaviour (punishments). These processes shape our norms and can occur both on an institutional level through laws and regulations but also on the level of individuals through direct peer to peer social maintenance. Normative processes involved in maintaining norms, such as punishment, occur not only in higher level organisms like humans but across phyla
[
27
]
, even in simple organisms like insects
[
36
]
and bacteria
[
17
]
. For example in Bacillus velezensis SQR9 have bacterial free riders who benefit from the collective biofilm but do not contribute to it, and so are punished by toxins released by the producers of the biofilm
[
17
]
, this also occurs in other species through punishment of cheating individuals by cooperator-produced antibiotics
[
35
,
34
]
.

Despite this high degree of conservation of punishment across phylogeny, its effect on the evolution of such organisms, specifically mechanisms that developed early in evolution, isn’t very well studied. Including, most computational accounts of affect (i.e. mood, emotion, hormonal systems) focus on individuals and not taking into account normative processes like punishment
[
9
,
30
]
.
We propose that in order to have a fuller understanding of affective mechanisms we must go beyond individualist accounts and consider how normative processes such as social maintenance shaped those mechanisms from very early on in their evolution. We build a multi agent simulation with an evolvable model of affect to investigate this.

Another point we want to address is how social maintenance pattern affect on a cultural time scale. The dynamics of behavioural norms and how they arise in populations has been widely studied
[
37
,
19
]
. For example, food sharing and which side of the road to drive on
[
19
]
. However, social norms are not only behavioural but said to be intertwined within us, patterning our emotions. This is evidenced by how humans emotionally react to various situations differs between cultures
[
23
,
26
]
. Despite this empirical evidence, a computational model specifying mechanistically how social maintenance gives rise to cultural variation in emotion is lacking. Our model specifies a mechanism of how cultural variations of affect may emerge in different populations. In doing so we study a richer conception of norms: norms as systems of parameters that specify a mechanism rather than a single variable to be optimised. That is to say, how social maintenance can reprogram an agents cognitive apparatus and the way they make decisions rather than a single variable or binary uptake of a behaviour.

1.1
Related studies and relevant concepts

1.1.1
Normative regularities

We take Westra and Andrews’
[
37
]
account of normative regularities as a starting point for defining social norms. They define them as
socially maintained patterns of behavioural conformity within a community
. Where patterns of behavioural conformity correspond to readily discernible empirical behavioural regularities that support robust predictions and generalisations, whose existence does not depend upon us knowing the underlying mechanisms that produce them.
And where social maintenance is some form of positive reinforcement of the regularity and punishment for breaking away from the regularity. This criterion is needed since not all sources of behavioural conformity are normative. For example, if a group of animals all congregate at the same water source in an otherwise dry landscape, this needn’t imply that drinking at that water source is a social norm
[
38
]
. For it to be considered a social norm there needs to be social pressure or encouragement from the group, otherwise it is purely a consequence of the environment. It should be noted that in many cases both processes are at play. For example, out of bounds areas in a ski resort are really hard to ski on and will dissuade most but you may also get banned from the ski resort for not obeying the rules (social maintenance)
[
37
]
. We expand on this definition, by operationalising it into an agent based model and adding extra distinctions.

1.1.2
Direct social maintenance and the evolution of affect

We assert that normative processes such as social maintenance arrived early in evolution, since they are ubiquitous across phyla, and therefore they must be considered in an evolutionary account of affect. For example social maintenance processes such as punishment, are involved in maintaining norms occur not only in higher level organisms like humans but across phyla
[
27
]
, even in simple organisms like insects
[
36
]
and bacteria
[
17
]
.

To clarify the effect of social maintenance on affect, which is missing in current evolutionary models of affect, we define the concept of
direct
social maintenance (extending
[
38
]
). It is a fundamentally different from non normative forms of social interaction such as competition. Direct social maintenance is defined as the explicit regulation based on some criteria i.e. selfishness or stealing to effect another agent’s evolutionary success and therefore the propagation for their genetic or cultural strategy. For example, it could be by decreasing their chances of survival or ability to reproduce (so they do not propagate genetically and therefore also culturally), or their ability to spread their cultural profile such as diminishing their status so they are not copied by others. This allows us to also examine the effect of social maintenance on genetic evolution as well as on cultural norms.

Further, we emphasise direct social maintenance as defined here is an explicit and direct form of social interaction, since it is based on some explicit criteria. This is then used by the agent to determine whether they punish or encourage a behaviour in another agent rather than indirectly affecting the others survival such as through competition.

We assert that punishment across the phyla that we reviewed conforms to our definition of direct social maintenance as the fitness of cheating individuals is reduced and therefore their behaviour is less likely to continue in the population. We can now assert that direct social maintenance, according to our definition, is present in many species and is ergo widely conserved in evolution. Therefore its influence must be considered in an evolutionary account of affect.

1.1.3
Social preferences

Our attempt to model the normative influence on the evolution/patterning of affect is closely related to several other lines of research. For example with regard to the emergence of social preferences: Frank
[
10
]
proposes that in social scenarios such as the Prisoner’s Dilemma some emotions described as "moral sentiments," commit a person to act contrary to their immediate self-interest
[
10
]
. For example, the predisposition to feel guilt commits a person to be altruistic, even if cheating were in their material interest. A person with the predisposition to get outraged after having been cheated is committed to punish the cheater, even if it is costly in material terms. Thereby, emotions such as guilt and anger act as "commitment devices" that alter the material incentives. Even so, there should be a material gain from having these emotions, otherwise they would not have evolved in the first place.

Computational studies have investigated how social preferences affect behaviour. Namely by incorporating social preferences by hard coding them into the agent model to see their effect on adaptive group behaviour e.g normative concern motivation
[
1
]
, equity motivation
[
18
]
. Although these studies illuminate how social preferences may affect behaviour in multi-agent settings, they do not explain the emergence of social preferences from purely material concerns. Other studies exist that do address the preconditions of internalising social preferences but assume trivial mappings between genome and behaviour not based on biological mechanisms
[
5
,
12
]
. Cimpeanu et al
[
6
]
study the material incentives that may lead to the evolution of guilt but uses a mechanism of guilt that is already functionally pre-specified and linked trivially to a binary genetic character instead of letting the functional relations of the emotion evolve by itself. Another study shows how preferences may be internalised in a psychologically realistic mechanism but this finding relies on high level cognitive capacities found only in humans
[
3
]
.

In contrast to these other examples, we are interested in how social preference "emerges" and is encoded in our
evolvable
model of affect out of purely material concerns with the constraints of a
simple
biologically plausible mechanism that in a similar approach to how other research looks at how social dynamics influence the evolution of affect e.g. how predator-pray interactions affect the neuroevolution of affect
[
13
,
16
]
. However instead of prey-predator interactions, we want to clarify the effect of social maintenance on the evolution of affect.

1.2
Contributions

Models of the evolution of emotion do not take into account normative processes. We argue that processes such as social maintenance were present very early on in evolution, as evidenced by direct social maintenance’s conservation across phyla and therefore must be taken into account to have a full theory of the evolution of affect. We decided to address this with an agent based model in which agents have an affective mechanism whose vital parameters can be evolved, therefore allowing us to study the effect of different aspects of the physical and social environment (specifically social maintenance) on these mechanism’s evolution in an artificial setting.

1. In the context of a resource dilemma, we demonstrate how social preferences might be encoded into affect through social maintenance and evolution, encoding social preferences into the mechanism in a distributed fashion from purely material concerns. We therefore urge for normative processes to be taken into account when considering the evolutionary origins of affect.

2. Show specifically how social maintenance results in a counter-intuitive sustainable (and therefore moral) strategy for a dynamic environment which, in turn, provides a strategy capable of regulating growth without the need of more cognitively demanding mechanisms of foresight or Theory of Mind, therefore highlighting the importance of normative considerations even for very simple affective systems across living beings.

3. We also extend a known definition of norms
[
37
]
and operationalise it in a continuous norm context. This allows us, given a cultural interpretation of our model, to study how social maintenance may culturally pattern mood on a cultural time scale and how our extended definition allows a more fine grained understanding of norms. Namely, being able to distinguish between the richer, direct social maintenance and the less rich, indirect social maintenance which tend towards many or one equilibria across populations respectively.

4. Further, our simulation gives an example of a mechanistic norm, where several parameters which are part of an (affective) mechanism define behavioural tendencies under different stimuli. This is a richer conception of a norm which goes beyond considering norms as single parameters or binary uptake of a behaviour.

2
Methods

2.1
Normative regularities extended

We extend Westra and Andrews
[
37
]
definition of both social maintenance and behavioural conformity. We define social maintenance as the increasing or lessening of another’s evolutionary success and therefore the propagation of either their biological or cultural strategy in the near future. For example it could be by decreasing their chances of survival or ability to reproduce (so they do not propagate genetically and therefore also culturally); or their ability to spread their cultural profile, such as diminishing their status so they are not copied by others. This allows us to also examine the effect of social maintenance on genetic evolution as well as on cultural norms.

We specifically implement social maintenance as normative punishment in our model (see section 2.8). We also distinguish between direct and indirect social maintenance.

We also extend Westra and Andrews
[
37
]
definition of behavioural conformity and provide two criteria 1. behavioural convergence, e.g. a decrease in variance in behaviour; and 2. a level of arbitrariness to the behaviour. We show why these criteria are important by distinguishing behaviours that may be due to environmental scaffolding and not to social maintenance and discuss various examples in our model.
This extension also allows us to distinguish between two types of social maintenance: direct social maintenance which produces many behavioural equilibria by changing someone’s behaviour based on explicit criteria, such as sanctions’ and indirect social maintenance that tends towards one equilibrium, e.g. interacting through the environment through competition.
We assert that the former is more truly social as its factors within the group that determine the outcome which leads to many equilibria. Whereas indirect social maintenance such as competition tends to one equilibria, so factors within the group do not affect the outcome. An assumption in this distinction is that the group is sharing the same niche; since competition can produce multiple equilibria by creating new niches.

Although this is a minimal definition of social norms we believe this permissive definition allows to appreciate the basic building blocks of normative cognition that may be excluded by more stringent definitions, therefore allowing us to trace the evolution of normative cognition across phyla in a bottom-up fashion
[
37
,
7
,
38
]
. Further, this normative framework is agnostic to the psychological mechanisms involved in normativity, therefore avoiding any bias that may mean we miss relevant phenomena because they lack human psychological capacities.

2.2
Model overview

We use an evolutionary multi-agent simulation with agents who have evolvable models of affect (which we call disposition) that modulate their behaviour. Agents (N=100) take turns to consume a dynamically replenishing resource (eat step) and punish (sanction step) a random subset of the agents by subtracting from their energy. The level of energy determines if the agent type will become more common in the population. We let these systems evolve with social maintenance and without social maintenance (i.e. agents being allowed to punish and not allowed to punish each other), to see its effect on the affective mechanism that emerges. We will term the current affective state of the agent as mood. This is a space-less simulation with no topology. This was done in order to focus on the effects of differing conditions on the evolution of affect. After initialisation, the simulation proceeds in rounds. Each round has a different, randomised order of all agents, and each of the following steps is performed in the following order for 2,000 time steps.

2.3
Eat

On their turn each agent consumes resources based on
\mu_{eat}
, which is their internal Bite Size (
B
) model modified by their affective state. This value is added to their internal energy and removed from the global resource level. If there are no resources left, the agent gets no energy. If all agents eat at a higher Bite Size, the environment will not be able to support as many agents; thereby, exhibiting ’tragedy of the commons’ dynamics. A higher value is therefore considered more greedy or selfish.

[
14
]
.

2.4
Sanction

During their turn, each agent observes the actually consumed resources of the 10 previous agents. Each agent checks if the previous agents ate more than its own internal Sanctions Threshold (
S
) modified into
\mu_{sanction}
based on their disposition state (see below); if so, it sanctions them. In other words, S is the amount of resource taken by another agent that an agent tolerates before punishing that agent, i.e. what an agent finds acceptable in terms of other agents acquiring resources. Sanctioning means the agent reduces the other agent’s internal energy by its own Sanction Damage (
D
) which is set to 0.6 for all runs, and it pays a sanctioning cost of
0.1\cdot D
, which is subtracted from its own energy level. This behaviour is a type of social maintenance we will use in our simulation.

2.5
Metabolise

Each agent has their energy level reduced by
0.1
during each round.

2.6
Death and Reproduction

After those steps, the simulation checks if any agents have an energy level lower than 0.0, in which case they are removed from the simulation. Then any agent with an energy level larger than 10 gets to reproduce. Reproduction means that we generate a copy of the agent with the same traits, mutated with a
0.1
chance (this is a global parameter that applies to all traits being mutated) . Mutations are adding gaussian noise with
0
mean and
1
variance to each of the agents parameters. The energy of the child and parent are both set at half the parent’s prior energy level.
Further, to ensure evolution does not stop when populations are stable, we assume at each simulation step that each agent has a 1/100 chance of dying. Therefore, this means that each agent has on average a 100 time-step life span instead of being immortal, allowing for other agents to be born, and therefore allowing variation through mutation.

2.7
Environment

The environment comprises of a resource that replenishes dynamically with on average 10 units per time-step and is distributed across time in a sinusoidal manner with a peak of 20 units and a trough of 0 units.

2.8
Model of affect: dispositional layer to modulate behaviour

We define
affect
as the underlying experience of feeling, emotion, attachment, or mood. It is a catch all terms a wide range of emotional states and can be positive (e.g., happiness, joy, excitement) or negative (e.g., sadness, anger, fear, disgust). We use it here to reference these phenomena in general and use
mood
to refer to the fluctuating (positive or negative) affective state in our model
[
28
]
.
Disposition
can mean the tendency of an individual toward something i.e. psychological inclination, and it can mean the environment’s power of disposing the individual to certain modes of interaction
[
24
]
. Inspired by this we define how mood is affected by stimuli (environment) and how mood in turn affects behaviour as disposition.

We take inspiration for our model of mood from the empirically verified model of mood
[
8
,
9
]
and add the ability for evolution to sculpt it. We don’t specify how each of these factors effect mood and, consequently, behaviour and let evolution specify the disposition of the agents.

We define mood as a state that fluctuates over an agents lifetime. Mood is affected due to perception of stimuli and also alters behavioural tendencies (Figure 1). Mood in this case affects baseline behaviours by either increasing them or decreasing them. More specifically, Eldar et al
[
9
]
describe mood state as a scalar summary of "how well things are going": an average of recent reward error (positive or negative) over recent experiences in their case. We define it as average energy gain or loss in our case (its our model’s equivalent of reward, see D array stimuli). We choose this model as it is a reification, capturing many aspects of affective cognition instead of focusing on any particular emotion. Those features being: stimuli (both internal and external) influencing a fluctuating mood state that in turn modulates behavioural and perceptual tendencies.

We also add other stimuli as those chosen in
[
9
]
. We chose these stimuli based on the sensory information available to our agents that we believe to be important regarding their survival in the simulated environment (both physical and social) For example, hunger, being punished, seeing others violating a norm, seeing others die. This list is not exhaustive and in theory you could add more (hence the dotted lines between
Stimulus_{2}
and
Stimulus_{n}
). We randomly initialise the importance of these stimuli and the direction of their effect on mood as a way of seeing when evolution (if at all) utilises them. In other words, we let our evolutionary process specify the functional relationship between its senses and its mood and in turn its mood and its behaviour. This functional specification is what we call disposition. This allows us to see different kinds of disposition emerging without presupposing a mechanism.

2.12
Notes on evolution and model of mood

Our model of evolution is not a complete model of evolution (big E Evolution) but merely attempts to show how optimisation propagates the type strategies that climb the adaptive gradient.

We are not interested in any particular affective phenomenon i.e. a specific emotion or hormonal mechanism. We model affect as an aggregate level phenomena of affect and assume a particular reified model and not a specific one; many hormones play a role in affect but we are interested in the aggregate effect of these mechanisms here. Also, we take a model that has been empirically verified as a starting point
[
8
,
9
]
. Further, we evolve multiple stimuli and let our evolution process specify each stimuli’s role in behaviour instead of presupposing it. This allows us to examine how evolution couples mechanism to the various aspects of the social and environmental niche.

The model lends itself both cultural and genetic interpretations, since more successful strategies (attaining more resource) become more common in the population. In the genetic interpretation, genes determining behaviour are passed down with mutation and are more likely to be passed down if they are successful at attaining a resource. In the cultural interpretation it can be seen as strategies that are passed down culturally with a transmission error, with more successful strategies being imitated more to become the most common. The cultural interpretation allows for us to investigate the effect of normative punishment on norm emergence and the genetic interpretation which allows us to examine the effect of social maintenance on the evolution of affect.

3
Results

3.1
Norm emergence with social maintenance

In this section we will use our norm definition to compare the emergence of norma across conditions with and without social maintenance(normative punishment).
Following Westra and Andrews
[
37
]
on norm emergence we take a normative regularity stance to defining a norm, which they define as
socially maintained patterns of behavioural conformity within a community
. Where patterns of behavioural conformity refers to behavioural practices that are common in their local group and social maintenance refers to either positive or negative encouragement of a behaviour as to conform to a behavioural regularity.

We extend that definition in a continuous context
[
2
]
to provide our own definition of norms, taking after
socially maintained patterns of behavioural conformity within a community
. with the term "patterns of behavioural conformity", in our definition consisting of two criteria:

•

1. The behaviour converges and stabilises: Do traits decrease in terms of their variance across the population from where they began, and do the average behaviours stabilise? This would be indicated by a decreased value for the variance (compared to random distribution) of a given behaviour across the agent population.

•

2. The behaviour the population stabilises at is arbitrary across runs to a certain extent: This criterion ensures the resultant behaviour is not fully due to environmental scaffolding, meaning when the behaviour is the only rational/viable action given the environmental constraints, e.g. the population converges on one watering whole in an otherwise dry landscape
[
38
]
. This would be indicated by repeated simulations stabilising at different average values i.e. there is a high variance of the means of each population.

We use these criteria above to distinguish between two types of social maintenance. Which we will compare in the experiments below (Figure 2).

•

1. Indirect social maintenance e.g. competition between individuals which usually tends to one equilibrium e.g. nash equilibrium in prisoners dilemma/race to the bottom. This is the type we see in the no social maintenance condition.

•

2. Direct social maintenance (explicit regulation of another’s behaviour based on some criteria) that allows for many equilibria, and therefore a much richer diversity in behaviour, which is consistent with the arbitrariness seen in some social norms. This is the one we see in the social maintenance condition.

Figure 2:
Flowchart that shows steps to distinguish whether a given behaviour is a norm or not according to the criteria set out in our definition. The criteria being: whether 1. behavioural regularity has been established. 2. if a regularity is established, is it arbitrary across different simulation runs? If not it is likely due to an environmental variable or indirect social processes such as competition that the regularity exists. In an empirical context you can asses 2 by looking at very similar environments and measuring extent of arbitrariness of behavioural regularities across them.
If 1 and 2 are both satisfied, then a norm has been established. We then distinguish between two types of norm: single variable norms that relate to just one variable e.g. walking speed and mechanistic norms that are mechanisms composed of systems of variables that all converge on a norm through direct social maintenance e.g. a disposition.

Now that we have articulated our definition we will use it to interpret our simulation results, comparing the values of different traits after evolution, with and without social maintenance. In the social maintenance condition: Agents can punish each other according to their trait
\mu_{sanction}
which determines what
\mu_{eat}
from another agent triggers a punishment. In the no social maintenance condition: Agents only have
\mu_{eat}
and socially interact indirectly through the shared resource.
Overall we see in Table 1 that social maintenance shows stronger norm emergence across values, satisfying both conditions 1 and 2. This includes the weights which are internal norms that dictate how mood is influenced by stimuli and how stimuli influence behaviour. We assert that this is, therefore, a mechanistic normative regularity going beyond the behavioural definition of a norm.

3.3
Mood injection to causally confirm effect of disposition on behaviour

Although the correlation matrix analysis sheds some light on how behaviour is organised, sometimes correlations can be spurious. So we decided to causally probe the simulation.

To do this, we artificially injected positive mood into the agents to see if there was a net effect on the behaviour. We did this every 500 time steps, injecting 200 units of positive mood for 200 time steps into each agent and these graphs show how this affected behaviour.
We confirm that agents in the social maintenance condition increase their
\mu_{eat}
when they are in a good mood and decrease it when they are in low mood, and that they punish more in a good mood and punish less in a bad mood. We see that, especially in the
\mu_{sanction}
graph that not all populations respond in the same way when positive disposition is injected e.g. some decrease their amount of punishment. However, we can see that on average that injecting disposition has the effect described above (eat more and punish more in a good mood, eat less and punish less in a bad mood (Figure 6). Therefore we can conclude that social maintenance does indeed cause our model of affect to become patterned as seen here.

3.4
Social maintenance causes emergence of population regulation mechanism

In the previous section we established that including social maintenance results in a different dispositional mechanism to evolve; however, is this mechanism useful?
Here we argue that social maintenance and evolution allow beneficial coordination between sanction and eat behaviours resulting in the emergence of a counter intuitive heuristic that allows population regulation without more complex cognition.
An issue that these populations run into is that, due to the evolutionary incentive to reproduce, they overproduce themselves in plentiful times and, therefore, die out when there are fewer resources.
We assert that in these scenarios, that the strategy resulting from social maintenance may actually be beneficial, i.e. eat more and punish more when you are in a good mood and vice versa when you are in a bad mood. Although this behaviour seems counter intuitive: 1. It allows agents to eat only when things are going well (gaining energy) and food is available. This prevents a "race to the bottom" where agents are constantly trying to out compete each other when there are not many resources and allows the resource time to recuperate. 2. The sanctioning behaviour (punishing more in a good mood and punishing less in a bad mood) is rational in this context as it puts on break to stop others from increasing their eating too much during a good period and prevents too much punishment happening when agents are in a bad mood and therefore saves energy. This dynamic regulates population without the need for more complex costly cognition like Theory of Mind (the ability to ascribe mental states and predict others behaviour
[
11
]
or forward projection, the ability to simulate and predict future states of the environment
[
32
]
.

To confirm if this mechanism did indeed avoid over growth we plotted the populations that consumed the least amount of energy and managed to regulate their growth, and compared them to that of populations that consumed a lot of energy and didn’t manage to regulate their growth. This allows us to see if the strategy we are talking about is indeed beneficial for the environment and the agents (Figure 8).

The most sustainable populations (least energy drain) seem to have the strategy described above in a higher concentration (0.79 vs 0.65 correlation for D_array_avg_list vs eat_Mu_weight_avg_list, and -0.54 vs -0.29 correlation for sanction_Mu_weight_avg_list vs D_array_weight_avg_list) than the full subset of the population and is less concentrated/breaks down in cases where energy variance and population fluctuation is larger (0.61 vs 0.65 for D_array_avg_list vs eat_Mu_weight_avg_list and -0.18 vs -0.29 for D_array_weight_avg_list vs sanction_Mu_weight_avg_list ). These strategies manage to maintain small populations which are more efficient, with less energy drain (Figure 7 e, f) and does not fall into the short sighted oscillating trap of the less sustainable populations (Figure 7 g, h).
Sustainable populations also have more stable average well being during periods of low resource (Figure 7 a,b) and no hunger i.e. they always manage to quench their bite size compared to the inefficient populations (Figure 7 c, d).

A drawback is that the damage from sanctions is larger in the sustainable populations (Figure 7 g,h), so although relatively stable, they remain quite small because they lose so much energy to sanctions. In summary, social maintenance produces strategies that result in more sustainable and smaller societies due to managing to regulate their growth. They manage to restrain themselves from growing too big and, therefore, maintain a more or less constant population. However, a drawback is that the damage from the punishment that is so great (Figure 7 i, j) that it also results in populations with the population regulation strategy being less likely to survive 36 populations vs 151 populations. This population regulation mechanism emerges to differing extents for all levels of sanction damage D (0.1 - 1) and in all cases comes with the cost of smaller populations due to sanction damage. Therefore, our results hold qualitatively across all levels of D (see Table 3 in appendix for more details).

4
Discussion

Social maintenance is replete across phyla yet many accounts of affect remain individualist. We decided to address this with an agent based model in which agents have a mood mechanism whose vital parameters could be evolved, therefore allowing us to study the effect of different aspects of the physical and social environment (specifically normative punishment) on affects evolution in an artificial setting.

4.1
Summary of results

1. We show that what emerges in the two different contexts (with social maintenance and without social maintenance) is different, which is the first result, taking into account social maintenance gives us a different picture of affect emergence in evolution.

2. Social maintenance and affect are enough for a mechanism to emerge that regulates population growth, suggesting minimal cognition is enough to regulate population growth in a dynamic environment (no Theory of Mind or forward projection needed). Although, the downside is a lot of energy is lost to punishment to control others behaviour.

3. We propose a refinement of Westra and Andrews definition of social maintenance that distinguishes between indirect social maintenance e.g. competition between individuals which usually tends to one equilibria i.e. Nash equilibrium/race to the bottom and a richer direct social maintenance (explicit regulation of another’s behaviour based on some criteria) that allows for many equilibria. Further we also distinguish between simple single variable norms e.g. walking speed and mechanistic norms, multiple variables that specify a behavioural mechanism, converging at the population level e.g. a culturally patterned disposition.

4.2
Discussion of results

a) We show that considering social maintenance in the evolution of affect had a strong effect, producing a qualitative different behaviour. Social maintenance being, as we define it, the explicit regulation based on some criteria e.g. selfishness/food intake to effect someone else’s evolutionary success and therefore the propagation for their genetic or cultural strategy. The face that normative punishment is widely conserved across phyla
[
27
]
, even in simple organisms like insects
[
36
]
and bacteria
[
17
]
, should urge us to consider the effects of social maintenance on the evolution even in ancient simple mechanisms such as affect.

b) Using an evolvable model of affect we show that a social preference can be encoded in the agent through evolution and social maintenance alone can result in the encoding of a social preference. This takes into account the social consequences of taking up too many resources and is encoded across the weights of the disposition mechanism. One could argue that this is a simple form of social preference encoded into the model. Social preferences (non-material agent motivations such as concern for how others will react to an individual breaking a social norm), are instantiated by emotions and are invoked to explain why organisms act in an altruistic manner
[
10
]
.
In most models these preferences are often hardcoded into models (e.g normative concern/ guilt
[
1
,
6
]
and explanations about how they emerge evolutionary from purely material concerns are thin on the ground and assume trivial mappings between genes and behaviour that omit mechanism
[
5
,
12
]
. We demonstrate that this is a simple way moral preferences can be encoded in a distributed manner in mood with social maintenance via normative punishment as the mechanism for agents to evolve social preferences. This provides a possible path to seeing how social motivations/sentiments can evolve from purely material incentives encoded in simple mechanisms present in many phyla.

The above mechanism allows multiple concerns to be encoded in one i.e. mood reflecting both lack of food but also level of punishment. One could start thinking of mood as funnelling certain goals into one. One may feel bad when they don not get what they are individually interested in but also when socially shamed (i.e. ones mood depends on what others think of you). If an agent maximises for feeling "good" then they are implicitly capturing multiple goals in one "utility" to maximise. This can be the basis for brains or in this case simple affective mechanisms becoming socially entangled and therefore socially rational
[
15
]
.

c) We show how counter-intuitive strategy (i.e. eating only when things are going well (gaining energy) and vice versa and punishing only when things are going well) result in a sustainable behaviour in a dynamic environment that manages to regulate growth. This sustainable behaviour emerges from these simple ingredients of social maintenance and affect without the need for something more complicated like ToM and forward projection. This comes at the cost of a lot of energy being lost to punishment and therefore, despite being able to evolve, it results in maladaptive features like lesser likelihood of survival(non-adaptive norm emerging in many cases). Although this is interesting in itself, how normative processes can drive agents away from their own benefit it is also creates an issue with processes like punishment being able to engender cooperation at the cost of resulting in unstable populations, we argue in our extensions section (section 4.5) how evolution may be able to avoid this pitfall.

d) We extend a known definition of norm emergence ,aka normative regularities
[
38
]
, and provide an operationalisation of this in a model in a continuous norm context:
1. The behaviour converges and stabilises, and 2. the behaviour the population stabilises at is arbitrary across runs to a certain extent, which enable us to distinguish cases of norm emergence through social maintenance and environmental scaffolding and provide a further refinement by distinguishing between indirect and direct social maintenance. Indirect social maintenance leads to one equilibrium (similar to environmental scaffolding) and the richer direct social maintenance results in many possible equilibria in behaviour. This difference is due to differing number of motivations affecting behaviour. In the indirect case, there is only one motivation (maximising resource intake) that governs an agents success. Therefore, in this case, strategies will tend to the maximum value as they compete against each other for survival. In the direct case, there are two competing motivations that an agent must balance, maximising resource intake but also not being punished, this results in a continuum of viable strategies that balance these motivations. This results in direct social maintenance leading to many equilibria, and we believe it captures an important aspect of normative behaviour in that it bears a certain degree of arbitrariness.

We then use our definition to extend norms to the mechanistic realm, to propose a mechanistic regularity. A mechanistic regularity rather than a particular behaviour specified by one value (e.g. selfishness), is a collection of values that specify a culturally patterned version of a psychological mechanism, in this case affect. This then defines certain behavioural tendencies in certain situations and changes over time. This opens up considering how even very simple systems become culturally patterned and offers a richer conception of norms that takes into account the underlying mechanism producing the behaviour and not only the behaviour itself. This is similar to the approach taken by the Evolutionary Game Theory community in the context of the genetic evolution of behaviour, studying the evolutionary dynamics mechanisms variables in an equation, that specify some sort of mechanism (e.g. Rescorla Wagner learning) rather than a single variable that defines a strategy
[
22
,
30
,
21
]
.

4.3
Advantage of our model vs other models

Our model does not use discrete motivations as assumed in other models i.e.
[
1
,
20
]
but allows them to be encoded in a distributed manner across a network. On the other hand, different from traditional neural network models which, while more abstract and with fewer assumptions, are harder to relate to the real world due to their opaqueness. Our model can relate back to real motivations through the use of affective language while still seeing how social preferences (not wanting to be punished and food preference are coupled together by disposition are “smeared” across a network instead of presupposing discrete motivations). Therefore our model is a nice middle ground between these approaches.

4.4
Limitations

Given that we are using the decrease in variance as one of our norm metrics, smaller populations will appear to have stronger norm emergence due to the reduction in population. To remedy this, we could run a constant sized population with a replacement evolutionary paradigm, although this will preclude us from studying the effects of social maintenance on the population dynamics. Further, we have only included negative social maintenance in our model. But, we also know that positive social maintenance exists, whose inclusion may allow for richer dynamics to emerge.
Moreover, we have a single dimensional view of mood and there exist different models that have at least two dimensions other than valance and magnitude
[
25
,
33
]
. In the future, we can expand our model to see the effects of social maintenance on these models of affect.

4.5
Future work

An issue that arose is, that despite social maintenance allowing frugal populations to evolve, it results in sub optimal survival and small populations due to the energy lost to punishment, suggesting that the medicine in some ways might be worse than the disease. A proposed way out of this is agents signalling to each other to change their behaviour with the threat of punishment
[
4
]
i.e. direct effect on mood through negative signals (yelling at someone). This would mean that you could get the desired population regulation effect without the damage from the sanctions.
This is possible because including punishment in the first place has resulted in social entanglement i.e. alignment of the way disposition mechanisms work also means agents are socially entangled and have a common emotional response; As we see in figure 4, a coordinated response emerges when we artificially inject mood into the system. This means that they can in turn use emotional (positive and negative) signals instead of physical punishment to influence each other, thereby solving the issue of punishment costing energy and, subsequently, making this system even more efficient.
This is a good future direction, especially to examine the conditions under which signalling will evolve and the possibility of emotional free-riders i.e. agents that manage to "unlearn" their response to the punishment signal to regulate themselves and start acting selfishly again.
