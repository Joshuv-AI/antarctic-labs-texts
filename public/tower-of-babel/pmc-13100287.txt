Meckel’s diverticulum presenting as massive gastrointestinal bleeding in a 2-year-old child: A case report
Authors: Salma Alaoui Ismaili, Zineb Moudafia, Taha Iken, Bouchra Dahmani, Omar Dalero, Zaineb Ayoubi, Mariem Chraibi, Aziz El Madi, Siham Alaoui Rachidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Massive lower gastrointestinal bleeding in young children is a rare but potentially life-threatening emergency requiring rapid localization of the bleeding source. We report the case of a 2-year-old child who presented with abrupt onset of profuse rectal bleeding, profound anemia (hemoglobin 4.6 g/dL), and hemodynamic instability. Emergency computed tomography angiography demonstrated active intraluminal contrast extravasation within the small bowel, associated with imaging signs of hypovolemic shock. No target vessel amenable to endovascular treatment was identified, and urgent surgical exploration was performed. Resection of an ileal lesion confirmed Meckel’s diverticulum with ectopic gastric mucosa as the source of bleeding. This case highlights the value of CT angiography for early diagnosis and management of massive gastrointestinal bleeding in pediatric patients.

Introduction

Massive gastrointestinal bleeding in children is a rare but potentially fatal emergency requiring rapid localization of the bleeding source and prompt therapeutic intervention [1]. Diagnostic evaluation may be particularly challenging in hemodynamically unstable pediatric patients, in whom conventional endoscopic approaches are often limited [2].

Meckel’s diverticulum is the most common congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal tract, with a reported prevalence ranging from 0.3% to 2.9% in the general population [3]. Although frequently asymptomatic, symptomatic cases occur predominantly in children, and gastrointestinal bleeding represents the most common clinical presentation in this age group [4,5].

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) has emerged as a rapid and non-invasive imaging modality capable of localizing active bleeding and guiding urgent management decisions in unstable patients in whom endoscopy is not feasible [6].

Case presentation

A 2-year-old female child with no prior medical history was brought to the emergency department of a regional hospital for acute onset of significant rectal bleeding. The bleeding was profuse and associated with signs of hemodynamic instability, including tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, and altered general condition. There was no history of trauma, medication intake, fever, or previous gastrointestinal symptoms. Physical examination revealed signs of hypovolemic shock, with no abdominal distension or palpable mass.

On admission, laboratory investigations demonstrated acute anemia with a hemoglobin level of 4.6 g/dL, consistent with active blood loss. Given the severity of the clinical presentation and the patient’s unstable hemodynamic status, resuscitation was initiated with intravenous fluid replacement and packed red blood cell transfusion, and the patient was subsequently transferred to a university hospital for further management. Endoscopic evaluation was not considered an appropriate first-line diagnostic modality.

Emergency contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography of the abdomen was performed. Arterial-phase images demonstrated active endoluminal contrast extravasation within the small bowel, consistent with ongoing gastrointestinal hemorrhage (Fig. 1). Despite the presence of active contrast extravasation, no definite feeding artery or clearly identifiable target vessel could be delineated on CT angiography, limiting the feasibility of endovascular treatment. Interventional radiology was therefore not pursued.

Based on the clinical severity and imaging findings, urgent surgical exploration was performed. Intraoperatively, an ileal lesion corresponding to the bleeding site identified on CT angiography was discovered and surgically resected. Histopathological examination of the resected small bowel specimen confirmed the diagnosis of Meckel’s diverticulum. These histopathological findings explained the source of the massive gastrointestinal bleeding.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient resumed oral feeds progressively and was discharged home on postoperative day 3 with a 7-day course of oral amoxicillin-clavulanate. At the 15-day follow-up appointment, the patient was clinically well with no recurrence of gastrointestinal bleeding.

Discussion

Gastrointestinal bleeding in pediatric patients represents a wide range of etiologies that vary according to age, bleeding severity, and anatomical location. In infants and young children, lower gastrointestinal bleeding is most commonly due to anal fissures, infectious colitis, allergic or eosinophilic proctocolitis, intussusception, vascular malformations, juvenile polyps, inflammatory bowel disease, and Meckel’s diverticulum [1]. Although most of these conditions cause mild or self-limited bleeding, massive hemorrhage associated with hemodynamic instability is uncommon and should prompt urgent evaluation for a potentially life-threatening cause [2].

Clinical presentation alone is often insufficient to accurately localize the source of lower gastrointestinal bleeding in pediatric patients, particularly in cases of severe hemorrhage associated with rapid intestinal transit [2,7]. In children, Meckel’s diverticulum classically presents with a painless, abrupt onset of large-volume rectal bleeding, most frequently in early childhood [4]. Recognition of this clinical pattern is critical, as delayed diagnosis may result in significant morbidity, including hypovolemic shock and intestinal hypoperfusion [5].

Endoscopic evaluation plays a central role in the diagnostic work-up of gastrointestinal bleeding in stable pediatric patients. However, its utility is limited in hemodynamically unstable children due to the need for bowel preparation, sedation, and the risk of clinical deterioration [8]. In addition, conventional upper endoscopy and colonoscopy may fail to identify bleeding sources originating from the small bowel, a well-recognized limitation in pediatric clinical practice [9]. This limitation often requires alternative diagnostic approaches in the acute setting.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography (CTA) has emerged as a valuable imaging modality for the evaluation of acute gastrointestinal bleeding. CTA enables rapid, non-invasive assessment of the entire gastrointestinal tract and can directly demonstrate active intraluminal contrast extravasation, allowing accurate localization of the bleeding source, and has been shown to be particularly useful in unstable patients when endoscopy is contraindicated or inconclusive [6]. In addition, CTA may depict systemic imaging signs of hypovolemic shock, including vascular collapse and bowel wall hypoperfusion, which can directly influence therapeutic decision-making [10].

In the present case, CTA proved to be the pivotal diagnostic tool. Given the patient’s degree of hemodynamic compromise on admission, endoscopic evaluation was not feasible, and CTA was performed as the first-line imaging modality. It not only localized the active bleeding to the small bowel but also revealed the absence of a clearly identifiable target vessel, which directly precluded endovascular management and led to the decision for urgent surgical exploration. Furthermore, the associated imaging signs of hypovolemic shock provided critical information about the severity of blood loss, reinforcing the urgency of surgical intervention. This case illustrates how CTA can serve as a comprehensive, actionable diagnostic tool in the hemodynamically unstable pediatric patient, guiding both the diagnosis and the therapeutic strategy in a single examination.

The pathophysiology of bleeding in Meckel’s diverticulum is strongly associated with the presence of ectopic gastric mucosa, which is frequently identified in symptomatic cases and is particularly prevalent in patients presenting with hemorrhage. Acid secretion from ectopic gastric tissue leads to ulceration of the adjacent ileal mucosa, resulting in painless but potentially massive bleeding. When complicated by active bleeding, inflammation, or ischemia, CT angiography may demonstrate active intraluminal contrast extravasation and associated bowel abnormalities, facilitating prompt diagnosis in emergency situations [5,11]. Although technetium-99m pertechnetate scintigraphy remains a well-established diagnostic tool for detecting ectopic gastric mucosa, false-negative results may occur due to insufficient ectopic gastric tissue, rapid bleeding with dilution effects, or local vascular compromise [12]. In this case, given the degree of hemodynamic instability, scintigraphy was not a viable option, further underscoring the value of CTA as first-line imaging in this setting.

Therapeutic management of bleeding Meckel’s diverticulum depends on clinical severity and diagnostic findings. Although angiography with possible embolization has been reported in selected adult cases, surgical resection remains the definitive treatment for symptomatic Meckel’s diverticulum, particularly in cases of massive bleeding [13]. Pediatric surgical series report favorable outcomes following timely surgical intervention, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and appropriate management [4]. In the present case, urgent resection resulted in complete resolution of bleeding and an uneventful postoperative course, consistent with these findings.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the diagnostic challenge of massive lower gastrointestinal bleeding in young children and highlights the value of CT angiography in the acute setting. Early localization of the bleeding source guided prompt surgical management and resulted in a favorable outcome. Meckel’s diverticulum should be considered in cases of life-threatening lower gastrointestinal bleeding in infants and young children, and CT angiography should be regarded as the imaging modality of choice when conventional endoscopy is not feasible.
