Semi analytical solution strategy for fractional Fornberg Whitham equation using Temimi Ansari method
Authors: Anas A M Arafa, Essam M Elsaid, Sameh E Ahmed, Sameh A Hussein, A A Al Qarni, Mohamed R Eid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents semi-analytical solutions for the fractional Fornberg–Whitham (FFW) equation using the Fractional Temimi–Ansari Method (FTAM). Fractional derivatives are defined in the senses of Atangana–Baleanu–Caputo (ABC) and Caputo. The existence and uniqueness of solutions are rigorously examined. Furthermore, a detailed discussion of the FTAM framework, including convergence analysis is provided. The accuracy of the obtained solutions is validated through comparisons with exact solutions when the fractional order is . Another key objective of the study is to compare the two fractional derivative definitions to assess the extent to which each captures memory effects from the past. Owing to its Mittag-Leffler-type kernel, the ABC derivative enhances memory effects and promotes faster stabilization.Keywords: Fractional derivatives, Fornberg–Whitham equation, FTAM, Convergence analysis, Numerical resultsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

It is quite clear from the framework of our study of natural phenomena and sophisticated dynamic systems that mathematical modeling is the fundamental and most effective tool for understanding, analyzing, and controlling the multitude of behaviors exhibited by these systems. Among the most well-known and powerful mathematical instruments for describing a wide range of physical and technical phenomena are nonlinear differential equations, owing to their ability to provide precise explanations for numerous practical applications. Within this class of equations, a distinctive subset—nonlinear wave equations—stands out due to their capacity to capture wave behavior propagating in multiple directions. One notable application of these equations is in the transmission of electrical signals. Fractional differential equations show their relevance for improved knowledge of systems with non-local characteristics1,2. Compared to classical differential equations, fractional differential equations exhibit greater flexibility in accounting for past events, a property reflected in their inherent memory effect. This characteristic underscores how the integration of both types of equations provides a robust theoretical framework that enables the development of highly accurate models capable of mimicking natural phenomena observed in the real world. Indeed, it is now evident that combining these two distinct classes of equations yields a theoretical foundation from which numerous precise models can be constructed to replicate natural processes found in nature. In several disciplines, including engineering and mathematical physics, nonlinear waves are classes of nonlinear partial differential equations3,4. The Fornberg-Whitham (FW) equation, which depicts the specific behavior of breaking of the wave, is one of these crucial nonlinear equations5–8. In recent decades, numerous natural phenomena—such as anomalous diffusion, viscoelastic behavior, wave propagation, and population dynamics—arising in biology, chemistry, mechanics, engineering, and physics have been successfully modeled using fractional differential equations9–13. The reason for using this type of equation as a substitute for classical differential equations is that they incorporate memory effects, which allow us to obtain more information about these systems. The FW equation is useful in engineering design and safety in contexts involving nonlinear, steepening waves—such as coastal structures, hydraulic channels, porous media interactions, flood risk management, and wave energy systems. Its fractional form extends its applicability to more realistic scenarios involving damping, porosity, and viscoelastic environments.

There are numerous approaches to defining fractional derivatives, such as the Grünwald–Letnikov, Riemann–Liouville, Caputo, Caputo–Fabrizio (CF), and Atangana–Baleanu (AB) derivatives. The Riemann–Liouville fractional derivative has not been widely adopted by some researchers for solving real-world physical problems because it is incompatible with standard initial conditions typically encountered in such contexts. Therefore, Caputo14 made a new definition that can solve this problem and use the initial conditions, which enabled him to solve many fractional-order physics problems15,16. Recently, Caputo and Fabrizio proposed a new definition called the Caputo–Fabrizio (CF) fractional derivative17. They showed that the derivative is not based on a singular kernel, and thus it enabled more accurate solutions to many fractional differential equations across various fields compared to previous definitions18–20. However, some authors have published papers criticizing this definition when applied to certain models involving a kernel that is both non-singular and non-local. To address this limitation, Atangana and Baleanu (AB) introduced a novel definition of fractional differentiation that incorporates a non-singular and non-local kernel21. This definition was named the Atangana–Baleanu (AB) fractional derivative and is based on the generalized Mittag-Leffler function, which serves as a non-singular and non-local kernel and fulfills the essential properties required of fractional derivatives. This definition has been applied very usefully to some mathematical models in more than one field22–24. It has been demonstrated that this definition offers greater accuracy and clarity compared to earlier fractional derivatives, thereby facilitating a more insightful analysis of solution characteristics. Many researchers have employed integral transforms to simplify the methods used for solving complex fractional differential equations25–27. Fractional calculus-based models have been resolved utilizing a diversity of numerical and analytical procedures. Apart from these, one of the central methods, FTAM, is adjusted to raise TAM’s effectiveness and precision28–30.

The primary objective of this study is to develop and validate semi-analytical solutions for the fractional Fornberg–Whitham (FFW) equation using the fractional Temimi–Ansari Method (FTAM) with both the Atangana–Baleanu–Caputo (ABC) and Caputo fractional derivatives. The main motivation is to compare the ABC derivative with the classical Caputo derivative. To achieve this, we consider two illustrative examples—each formulated under a single initial condition but employing one of the two derivative definitions. The Caputo derivative is widely used due to its compatibility with classical models and standard initial conditions, whereas the ABC derivative offers enhanced modeling capabilities for complex systems through its generalized, non-singular memory kernel. This study aims to investigate the convergence, existence, and uniqueness of the obtained solutions, highlight the advantages of the FTAM in handling nonlinear terms without requiring linearization, perturbation, or restrictive assumptions, and demonstrate the improved memory effects and numerical stability afforded by the ABC derivative.

Findings

In this study, we propose a novel investigation of the fractional Fornberg–Whitham (FFW) equation using the Atangana–Baleanu–Caputo (ABC) and Caputo fractional operators. The existence and uniqueness of solutions to these fractional problems are rigorously established. To address fractional-time dynamics, the Fractional Temimi–Ansari Method (FTAM) proves to be a highly efficient approach. It not only yields more accurate and physically consistent solutions, but also provides a robust framework for modeling complex phenomena with enhanced memory effects and improved convergence properties.

The study concludes by highlighting significant potential for future research in this area. It is anticipated that further investigations will lead to more substantial theoretical insights and compelling numerical results. In particular, other fractional derivative definitions could be explored, and the proposed methodology could be extended to higher-dimensional systems.
