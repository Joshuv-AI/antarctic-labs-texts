Sempreverdità (evergreen vitality): a resilience-based, trajectory-oriented extension of healthy ageing
Authors: Davide Maria Andreucci, Virginia Boccardi
Published: 2026

Population ageing represents one of the most consequential transformations in modern medicine. Although life expectancy has increased substantially over the past decades, these gains have not been paralleled by equivalent improvements in healthspan. Increasingly, individuals live longer while experiencing extended periods of vulnerability, multimorbidity, and functional decline. This growing discrepancy has prompted a necessary shift from disease-centered paradigms toward models focused on function, resilience, and adaptive capacity. The concept of healthy ageing, as defined by the World Health Organization, marked a critical step in this transition by conceptualizing ageing as the process of developing and maintaining functional ability through the interaction between intrinsic capacity and environmental factors [1]. Intrinsic capacity integrates locomotor, cognitive, psychological, sensory, and metabolic domains, offering a clinically meaningful framework. However, despite its operational strength, this model remains largely descriptive. It defines the goal—functional preservation—but does not fully capture the dynamic, adaptive, and experiential nature of ageing trajectories [2, 3].

Emerging evidence from geroscience challenges the traditional view of ageing as a linear and inevitable decline. Instead, ageing appears as a heterogeneous and, at least in part, modifiable process shaped by resilience—the capacity of biological systems to respond to, recover from, and adapt to stressors over time [4]. Within this framework, intrinsic capacity may be interpreted as functional reserve, while resilience reflects the temporal dimension of recovery and adaptation, linking biological mechanisms to clinical outcomes. Building on this perspective, we propose the concept of Sempreverdità—a novel Italian term (not present in the vocabulary) that may be translated as evergreen vitality—as a conceptual and translational evolution of healthy ageing.

Sempreverdità does not replace healthy ageing; rather, it extends and deepens it. It shifts the focus from preserving function to expressing functional potential, even in the presence of biological ageing. In this sense, ageing is no longer viewed as a purely defensive process aimed at preventing decline, but as a dynamic trajectory in which the organism continues to generate, adapt, and express capacity within changing constraints. Healthspan has traditionally been interpreted as a period free from disease. Yet, real-life ageing is inherently non-linear, characterized by phases of vulnerability, recovery, and adaptation. Sempreverdità explicitly embraces this complexity by integrating longitudinal trajectories of intrinsic capacity with perturbation–recovery cycles and the potential reversibility of early decline. Ageing thus becomes a trajectory to be shaped rather than a condition to be endured. A further, essential dimension introduced by Sempreverdità is the integration of meaning and perception. Ageing is not solely a biological process but also a lived experience. Psychosocial factors—such as purpose, engagement, and social participation—have been shown to modulate inflammatory pathways, neuroendocrine regulation, and even survival. In this light, Sempreverdità bridges biological resilience with behavioral adaptation and existential engagement, transforming ageing into an interpretable and meaningful process.

From a translational standpoint, Sempreverdità (evergreen vitality) can be defined as a dynamic state characterized by the sustained capacity to express functional, adaptive, and meaningful potential across the life course through the integration of intrinsic capacity, biological resilience, and behavioral-contextual modulation. This perspective calls for a shift from static assessments toward longitudinal and multidimensional evaluation, incorporating functional domains, resilience metrics, metabolic and inflammatory regulation, and lifestyle determinants. These elements converge within the immune–endocrine–autonomic axis, which governs adaptive capacity and recovery dynamics [5]. Interventions, therefore, should no longer target isolated disease endpoints but rather aim to modulate trajectories. Nutritional patterns rich in high-quality protein and plant-based components, resistance and multicomponent exercise, circadian alignment, and cognitive and social engagement represent not separate strategies but interconnected levers acting on the same biological network of resilience [6–8]. Adopting this framework has implications that extend beyond clinical practice It requires a shift in measurement—from static biomarkers to dynamic indicators of adaptability—a shift in care—from disease treatment to trajectory modulation—and a shift in societal perspective—from ageing as a burden to ageing as a resource [7].

At a deeper level, Sempreverdità invites a cultural transformation. Ageing, maybe, does not begin at 65; it begins at birth. Every moment represents an opportunity to influence future trajectories. The present is not disconnected from the future; it is its biological foundation. Risk factors for many age-related diseases are not sudden events, but the cumulative result of behaviors, exposures, and decisions made over time. Prevention, therefore, is not an alternative to care—it is its earliest and most powerful form. Modern society often fears ageing, associating it with loss, decline, and irrelevance [8]. This perception generates anxiety, disengagement, or compensatory excess. In contrast, reframing ageing as a phase of continued potential expression may mitigate this fear. The goal is not to maintain the performance of a 20-year-old body in later life, but to express the highest possible potential at each stage of life, relative to one’s own biological and experiential context. In this sense, ageing resembles the growth of a tree. With time, the trunk becomes stronger, the structure more complex, and the identity more defined. The leaves may change in tone, but they remain present. What matters is not their brightness, but their persistence.

The central question is no longer whether we can live longer, but whether we can continue to express our biological and functional potential throughout the lifespan. We propose that ageing trajectories should be interpreted as “adaptive landscapes”, where biological systems continuously reorganize in response to internal and external perturbations. Importantly, ageing is not only a biological trajectory but also a perceived and interpreted experience, where meaning, engagement, and expectation actively modulate physiological pathways. In this context, prevention should no longer be considered separate from care, but rather its earliest and most effective form (Fig. 1). Future research should move beyond cross-sectional measures of function and incorporate longitudinal assessments of recovery dynamics, resilience biomarkers, and trajectory-based outcomes.

Conceptual framework of “Sempreverdità” (Evergreen Vitality) across the lifespan

Ageing is no longer a process to endure, but a trajectory to design

This editorial stems from a teaching experience in physiotherapy, where the reflection of a student, now first author, sparked a broader scientific reconsideration of ageing. What began as a conceptual insight within an educational setting evolved into a multidisciplinary perspective, bridging clinical knowledge, geroscience, and lived experience, and ultimately leading to the proposal of an expanded framework for understanding ageing.

The figure illustrates ageing as a dynamic, trajectory-based process in which biological foundations and lived experience are continuously integrated. The tree represents the life course, with the trunk embedding the genomic substrate and the canopy symbolizing the expression of functional capacity over time. The root system depicts the underlying determinants of metabolic resilience, including skeletal muscle function, cognitive integrity, circadian regulation, nutritional patterns, and immune–protective mechanisms, all interconnected within a complex adaptive network. The central circular element reflects the cyclical interaction between day–night rhythms and temporal adaptation, emphasizing the role of chronobiology in ageing trajectories. Human figures illustrate functional expression, social engagement, and autonomy across later life. Overall, the model integrates intrinsic capacity, biological resilience, and behavioral–environmental modulation, positioning ageing not as a linear decline but as a modifiable, adaptive process aimed at sustaining functional and meaningful potential throughout the lifespan.
