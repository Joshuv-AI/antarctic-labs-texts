Frequency-specific alterations in low-frequency functional connectivity in children with ADHD
Authors: Ahmed Ameen Fateh, Hassan Muhammed, Adam A. Q. Mohammed, Abla Smahi, Abdulqawi Alarefi, Luning Zhang, Feng Yue, Hongwu Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis investigation seeks to elucidate frequency-specific alterations in resting-state functional connectivity (FC) among pediatric subjects with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). The study focuses on three distinct sub-bands of slow blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) oscillations: Slow3 (0.073–0.198 Hz), Slow4 (0.027–0.073 Hz), and Slow5 (0.010–0.027 Hz). We assess the discriminative capacity of these sub-bands as potential neural biomarkers through the application of machine learning classification techniques. This frequency-resolved methodology addresses a significant lacuna in extant ADHD FC research, which has predominantly treated low-frequency fluctuations as a homogeneous band. By parsing these oscillations into discrete frequency ranges, we aim to provide a more nuanced understanding of the neurophysiological underpinnings of ADHD.MethodsThis study explores the frequency-specific functional connectivity (FC) differences between (ADHD = 40) and healthy control (HC = 45) children, focusing on the Slow3 (0.073–0.198 Hz), Slow4 (0.027–0.073 Hz), and Slow5 (0.010–0.027 Hz) frequency bands. A two-sample t-test was used to identify significant functional connectivity (FC) differences between the two groups. followed by machine learning techniques to evaluate the classification accuracy of these features.ResultsSignificant differences in FC were found between ADHD and HC in specific brain regions. In the Slow3 band, ADHD subjects showed increased connectivity in the right precentral gyrus, which is associated with motor-related neural activity. In the Slow4 and Slow5 bands, higher connectivity was observed in the right inferior frontal orbital region in ADHD compared to HC. Given the identical FC patterns in Slow4 and Slow5, these bands were combined into a single feature for further analysis. Machine learning classification using FC features from Slow3 and the combined Slow4/Slow5 bands demonstrated notable accuracy, with 79% accuracy for ADHD and 82% for HC in the Slow3 band, and 85% accuracy for ADHD and 80% for HC in the combined Slow4/Slow5 band. ROC curve analysis further supported these findings with AUC values of 0.7550 and 0.7830, respectively.ConclusionThese findings suggest that frequency-specific FC patterns may serve as potential biomarkers for ADHD, offering insights into the disorder’s neural mechanisms and aiding in more accurate diagnosis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is one of the most common childhood onset disorders and is characterized by developmentally inappropriate inattentiveness, impulsivity and hyperactivity. ADHD is a common neurodevelopmental condition that affects between 5% and 7% of children world [1, 2] wide, with a significant number of cases persisting into adulthood. Despite extensive research, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying ADHD remain incompletely understood.

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) has been widely applied to explore the functional abnormalities of ADHD. Many rs-fMRI studies have reported abnormal FC of certain brain regions and networks in patients with ADHD and Rs-fMRI has emerged as a valuable tool for investigating the intrinsic FC of the brain, offering insights into the neural networks associated with various cognitive and behavioral functions [3–7]. In ADHD research, rs-fMRI has been used to explore alterations in brain connectivity patterns, particularly within networks involved in attention, executive function, and motor control; traditional rs-fMRI studies have primarily focused on broad frequency bands; however, recent evidence suggests that frequency-specific FC analysis can reveal distinct neural dynamics that are not captured in aggregate measures [4, 5, 7].

Most prior rs-fMRI studies have primarily investigated low-frequency oscillations (0.01–0.1 Hz), which have been linked to distinct mechanisms and are associated with various brain states [8]. Reviews have highlighted that understanding the physiological basis of self-emerging oscillations not only enhances insight into their roles but also aids in diagnosing and treating neurological disorders [4, 9]. However, recent findings suggest a more complex picture. These oscillations also represent spontaneous neural activity. For instance, Malinen et al. [10] proposed that fluctuations within the range of 0.12 to 0.25 Hz might reflect altered autonomic nervous system activity in individuals with chronic pain.

Additionally, we divided the low-frequency range into four sub-bands: slow5 (0.010–0.027 Hz), slow4 (0.027–0.073 Hz), slow3 (0.073–0.198 Hz), and slow2 (0.198–0.25 Hz) as previous studies defined [11–14]. Prior studies show distinct contributions of slow5 and slow4 to low-frequency oscillation amplitudes in regions including the basal ganglia, thalamus, and posterior cingulate cortex (PCu) in healthy participants [14, 15]. The global signal (GS) is the average BOLD time series across gray-matter voxels. GS correlation is the Pearson correlation between each voxel’s time series and the GS (Fisher-z transformed), yielding a whole-brain map for each subject; we use GS topography to denote the spatial distribution of these correlations, where higher values indicate stronger co-fluctuation with whole-brain activity [14, 16]. Because GS coupling can vary by frequency, we examine it within Slow3/Slow4/Slow5. In particular, the Slow3 (0.073–0.198 Hz), Slow4 (0.027–0.073 Hz), and Slow5 (0.01–0.027 Hz) bands have been implicated in long-range connectivity and integrative brain functions [17]. Previous studies have identified abnormalities in these frequency bands in individuals with ADHD, suggesting that altered FC in specific frequency ranges may contribute to the clinical symptoms of the disorder, for example, increased connectivity in motor-related regions such as the right supplementary motor area has been associated with hyperactivity symptoms in ADHD [17–19].

Consistent with these findings, the observed increase in functional connectivity (FC) within regions implicated in emotional regulation, such as the right supplementary motor area, may reflect an over-reliance on these areas as compensatory mechanisms for deficits in other brain regions. This interpretation aligns with the behavioral symptoms commonly associated with ADHD, including impulsivity and emotional dysregulation [20–22]. Notably, research has demonstrated that the Slow4 and Slow5 frequency bands are particularly sensitive to disruptions in emotional regulation and decision-making processes core domains impacted in ADHD [23–25]. These findings underscore the importance of analyzing multiple frequency bands, rather than relying exclusively on broad-spectrum analyses, which may mask critical differences in neural dynamics. A frequency-specific approach facilitates a more nuanced.

Despite its prevalence, ADHD remains a diagnostically and therapeutically challenging condition due to its behavioral heterogeneity [26], symptom overlap with comorbid disorders, and lack of reliable biological markers. These challenges contribute to real-world consequences, including misdiagnosis, pharmacological overtreatment, and off-label use of antipsychotics in youth, as highlighted by recent population studies, and adolescents with ADHD show high rates of psychiatric comorbidities, including depression, conduct problems, and substance use, which complicate management and increase long-term risk [27, 28]. These findings underscore the urgent need for objective, neurobiologically grounded tools that can aid in diagnostic precision, risk stratification, and treatment planning. Frequency-specific FC analysis, by revealing hidden neural dynamics linked to key behavioral domains such as impulsivity, motor control, and emotional regulation, offers a promising pathway toward such tools. Identifying robust neural signatures of these traits may help clinicians move beyond symptom checklists and tailor interventions more effectively to individual neurobiological profiles.

Building on this motivation, we investigate frequency-specific alterations in GS topography among individuals with ADHD by examining functional connectivity (FC) patterns across three low-frequency BOLD sub-bands: Slow3 (0.073–0.198 Hz), Slow4 (0.027–0.073 Hz), and Slow5 (0.010–0.027 Hz). We hypothesize that individuals with ADHD exhibit distinct FC alterations within these frequency bands compared to healthy controls, and that these alterations are regionally specific particularly within motor and prefrontal circuits implicated in ADHD symptomatology. Furthermore, we hypothesize that frequency-specific FC features can be used to accurately classify ADHD and healthy participants using machine learning. To test these hypotheses, we performed group-level comparisons using two-sample t-tests and assessed classification performance using support vector machines. Our results reveal significant FC differences between groups and demonstrate that these features yield promising diagnostic accuracy. These findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the neural connectivity patterns associated with ADHD and underscore the value of frequency-resolved resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) analysis in biomarker discovery.

Materials and methods

Participants and measures

This study was conducted between June 2019 and May 2021 at Shenzhen Children’s Hospital in Shenzhen, China. This study involved 40 children diagnosed with ADHD, aged 7 to 10 years (mean age: 10.6 ± 0.85), who were recruited from the Hospital, along with a control group of 45 healthy children (HCs) matched by age (mean age: 9.1 ± 0.52), as outlined in Table 1. The sample size was determined based on power calculations using G*Power software, targeting a medium effect size (d = 0.5), a power of 0.8, and a significance level of 0.05 for independent sample t-tests. This yielded a minimum requirement of 35 participants per group. Our final sample sizes exceeded this minimum to ensure sufficient power and account for potential exclusions due to head motion or incomplete data. Prior to their participation, both the children and their parents completed a clinical interview, which was further supported by the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children - Present and Lifetime Version (K-SADS-PL) [29], to confirm ADHD diagnoses or rule out other psychiatric disorders, adhering to the DSM-V criteria [30]. Inclusion criteria for the ADHD group specified that participants must be between 7 and 10 years old, currently enrolled in either public or private schooling, and have a verified ADHD diagnosis. The same age and educational criteria were applied to the HC group. Exclusion criteria for all participants included any history of head trauma resulting in loss of consciousness, significant physical or neurological disorders, substance abuse, a full-scale IQ below 70, as assessed by the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Chinese Children-IV (WISC-IV), long-term use of prescription medications for ADHD or other conditions, and the presence of comorbid conduct disorder or Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD). Only right-handed participants with no visible abnormalities on MRI scans or a history of claustrophobia were selected for the study. ADHD participants displayed six or more symptoms of inattention and six or more symptoms of hyperactivity/impulsivity, with the severity of these symptoms being used as indicators in subsequent statistical analyses. Informed consent was obtained from parents, and assent was given by the children, with the study receiving approval from the Hospital’s Ethics Committee.

Resting-state fMRI data acquisition

Resting-state fMRI scans for all participants were obtained using a 3.0-T Siemens Magnetom Skyra scanner located in the Radiology Department of the Hospital. The imaging was conducted using an echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following settings: repetition time (TR) of 2000ms, echo time (TE) of 30 ms, a flip angle of 90º, and a matrix size of 64 × 64. The scans included 32 axial slices with a field of view (FOV) of 24 × 24 cm² and a slice thickness of 3 mm, without any gap. Additionally, a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) T1-weighted sequence was captured with these parameters: TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.26 ms, an average count of 1.0, a slice thickness of 1.0 mm, and an FOV of 256 mm.

Data pre-processing

Pre-processing of the data was performed using the DPARSF toolkit [31] which operates on the SPM12 platform (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12). To compensate for initial MRI signal fluctuations and allow participants to acclimate to the experimental setup, the first 10 volumes were excluded. The subsequent 230 volumes were then realigned to correct for head movement and adjusted for slice timing. ADHD participants were required to have head motion translation under 3 mm and rotation under 3º in any direction. Afterward, normalization and resampling were conducted, producing voxel sizes of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³. Nuisance covariates, including global signal, white matter signal, cerebrospinal fluid signal, and Friston-24 head motion parameters, were removed from each voxel’s time series.

The resultant brain scans were linearly detrended and subjected to filtering with three bandpass, including the slow3, slow4, and slow5 bands (0.073–0.198, 0.027–0.073, and 0.010–0.027 Hz, respectively) [32]. In order to evaluate the comparability of head movement among groups, the mean frame-wise displacement was computed, as resting-state functional connectivity is susceptible to modest head movements. Following filtering, the brain was parcellated using the AAL atlas. For each participant and frequency band, the mean BOLD signal was extracted from each region of interest (ROI). Functional connectivity (FC) was then calculated by computing Pearson correlation coefficients between the time series of all possible ROI pairs. The resulting correlation matrices were Fisher’s Z-transformed to improve normality and were used in subsequent statistical comparisons and classification analyses.

Head motion

Head motion analysis was conducted by calculating the mean frame-wise displacement (FD) using Jenkinson’s method to compare voxel-wise motion differences between the ADHD and HC groups. The results showed no significant differences in mean FD between the two groups (p > 0.6).

Brain regions showing significant group differences were designated as regions of interest (ROIs) for subsequent analyses, defined by 4 mm radius spheres centered on the peak statistical differences.

Model training and classification

To evaluate the classification performance of our model, we employed a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier with a linear kernel, utilizing functional connectivity features from the Slow3, Slow4, and Slow5 frequency bands. The selection of SVM was drivenby its capacity to efficiently handle high-dimensional data, a crucial consideration given the extensive number of connectivity features extracted fom fMRl. Moreover, SVM offers advantages for smaller datasets, as its margin-maximizing approach helps mitigate overfitting. This characteristic makes it especially appropriate when the number of features greatly exceeds the number of samples, a circumstance commonly encountered in neuroimaging research.The dataset was split into training and testing sets using a 70/30 ratio to ensure that the model was evaluated on unseen data. A confusion matrix was constructed to assess the classification results, containing True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives (FP), and False Negatives (FN). From this matrix, we derived key performance metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. To further evaluate the model’s discriminatory power, we generated a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve by plotting the True Positive Rate (sensitivity) against the False Positive Rate (1-specificity) across various thresholds. The AUC was calculated to quantify the model’s overall performance, where an AUC of 1.0 indicates perfect classification ability, and an AUC of 0.5 suggests no better than random guessing. Both the ROC curve and AUC were computed using the scikit-learn library in Python. The statistical significance of the classification results was validated through permutation testing, with the p-value determined by comparing the observed AUC to a null distribution generated from 1000 permutations. This comprehensive evaluation approach ensured robust assessment of our model’s ability to distinguish between ADHD and healthy control groups.

Correlation with clinical symptoms

We did not observe any significant correlations between changes in functional connectivity and clinical symptom scores within the ADHD group. Specifically, although individuals with ADHD showed significantly higher anxiety levels compared to HCs, no meaningful associations were found between anxiety scores and functional connectivity measures. Therefore, anxiety was not included as a covariate in the main analysis.

Model training and classification

The results of our analysis reveal distinct FC patterns between patients with ADHD and HC across different frequency bands. The Slow3 band, with an Area Under the AUC of 0.7550, demonstrated moderate discriminatory power, as evidenced by the confusion matrix, which showed 82% accuracy in correctly identifying HC and 79% accuracy for ADHD. This suggests that FC in the Slow3 band provides a reasonable ability to distinguish between the two groups, albeit with some overlap. In contrast, the combined Slow4 and Slow5 bands exhibited slightly better performance, with an AUC of 0.7830, indicating a stronger discriminatory capacity. The confusion matrix for this combined feature showed 80% accuracy for HC and 85% for ADHD, highlighting that these frequency bands together offer more robust classification accuracy. These results suggest that while each band individually contributes to the differentiation between ADHD and HC, the combined analysis of Slow4 and Slow5 enhances the overall classification performance, making it a valuable approach for identifying neural markers associated with ADHD (Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

The current study investigates frequency-specific FC patterns in children with ADHD compared to HC, focusing on the Slow3, Slow4, and Slow5 frequency bands. Our findings revealed significant differences in FC between the two groups, particularly in the right precentral gyrus and the right inferior frontal orbital region, which are known to be involved in motor control, decision-making, and emotional regulation. These results offer additional insight into ADHD neurobiology and indicate that frequency-resolved FC features are preliminary candidate markers rather than clinically actionable biomarkers.

The right precentral gyrus, which showed increased connectivity in the ADHD group in the Slow3 band, is a region primarily associated with motor control and planning (M1). This region is part of the primary motor cortex and plays a critical role in the execution of voluntary movements [33]. The observed increase in FC in this area could be related to the hyperactivity symptoms commonly observed in ADHD, as the disorder has been associated with motor restlessness and difficulties in motor coordination [34]. Previous studies have linked abnormalities in motor-related brain areas to ADHD symptoms, suggesting that dysfunctions in these regions might underlie the motor hyperactivity and impulsivity characteristic of the disorder [35]. The findings from the present study align with these earlier observations, indicating that the altered FC in the right precentral gyrus may contribute to the motor symptoms of ADHD. This provides further evidence for the involvement of the motor network in the pathophysiology of ADHD and supports the use of frequency-specific FC analysis to uncover these neural alterations.

The right inferior frontal orbital region exhibited increased connectivity in both the Slow4 and Slow5 bands in the ADHD group compared to HC. This region is part of the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), which is critically involved in decision-making, impulse control, and emotional regulation [36]. The OFC integrates sensory input with affective states, allowing for appropriate behavioral responses to environmental stimuli [37]. Dysfunctions in this area have been consistently implicated in ADHD, particularly in relation to the impulsivity and emotional dysregulation observed in affected individuals [38]. The consistency of FC findings across the Slow4 and Slow5 bands suggests that the right inferior frontal orbital region is a key node in the neural circuitry affected by ADHD. Increased connectivity in this area could reflect compensatory mechanisms or maladaptive neural patterns associated with the disorder. For instance, the heightened FC might indicate an over-reliance on this region for cognitive control processes, which could lead to the characteristic impulsivity and difficulties in regulating behavior seen in ADHD [39]. These findings underscore the importance of the orbitofrontal cortex in ADHD and highlight the value of investigating FC across multiple frequency bands to capture the full spectrum of neural alterations in the disorder.

However, it is important to note that our findings differ from those of [17], who reported reduced activity in the same region using fractional ALFF and ReHo metrics. The discrepancy between our finding of increased OFC functional connectivity (FC) in the Slow5 band and the decreased intrinsic activity reported by [17] may arise from fundamental differences in the underlying neural signals captured by the respective methods. Our study used static FC based on inter-regional correlations, which are sensitive to stable, long-range functional coordination over time. In contrast, Chen et al. used both static and dynamic amplitude-based metrics (fALFF, ReHo), which quantify local spontaneous activity and regional synchrony, respectively, and are more sensitive to short-term temporal fluctuations [40, 41]. Anatomically, the OFC is a highly dynamic hub involved in emotion regulation, reward processing, and decision-making [42], and its activity can vary significantly based on moment-to-moment internal states (e.g., arousal, mood), which may be better captured by dynamic metrics. Furthermore, dynamic measures may be more susceptible to non-stationarities in BOLD signals, especially in children with ADHD, whose neural activity is often more variabl [43]. Thus, reduced fALFF may index diminished local amplitude, whereas increased FC reflects stronger network coupling; these patterns are not mutually exclusive. These findings underscore the importance of integrating both connectivity and amplitude-based measures in future frequency-resolved ADHD research.

The regions of interest (ROIs) identified in this study, including the right precentral gyrus and the right inferior frontal orbital region, are pivotal areas involved in motor control, decision-making, and emotional regulation all of which are significantly impacted in individuals with ADHD. The observed increase in functional connectivity (FC) within these regions may reflect underlying neural mechanisms contributing to the core symptoms of ADHD, such as hyperactivity, impulsivity, and challenges in emotional and behavioral regulation.

Specifically, the right precentral gyrus, a critical region for motor control and the execution of voluntary movements, may exhibit hyperconnectivity in ADHD, potentially explaining the characteristic motor hyperactivity associated with the disorder [32]. Similarly, the right inferior frontal orbital region, which plays a central role in decision-making and emotional regulation, may display increased connectivity as a compensatory response to deficits in cognitive control and emotional regulation hallmark features of ADHD [35, 36]. These findings suggest that the altered functional connectivity in these regions could provide valuable insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying ADHD.

Beyond their neuroscientific relevance, these findings also address critical challenges in ADHD management. The observed frequency-specific FC alterations particularly in orbitofrontal regions linked to impulse control and affect regulation align with behavioral domains that often drive treatment escalation, off-label antipsychotic prescribing, and comorbidity development, as highlighted in recent population-level studies [27, 28]. For example, youth with ADHD who display high levels of impulsivity or emotional dysregulation are more likely to receive antipsychotics, often inappropriately, and are at higher risk for developing additional psychiatric disorders [27, 28]. Our results suggest that Slow4 and Slow5 band hyperconnectivity in the right inferior frontal orbital region may serve as an early neural marker of these risk traits, helping clinicians identify patients who may require specialized behavioral support, emotion-focused interventions, or closer psychiatric monitoring. In this way, frequency-resolved FC analysis may hold potential to inform future risk stratification, but the present findings are preliminary and not yet clinically actionable.

Such insights support the development of targeted, mechanism-based interventions. For example, neurofeedback [44] or cognitive training could be personalized to modulate activity in brain circuits showing frequency-specific dysregulation. These interventions may offer novel therapeutic pathways for alleviating ADHD symptoms and reducing reliance on non-specific pharmacological strategies. Moreover, frequency-specific functional connectivity analysis may provide a refined tool for identifying individuals most likely to benefit from specific interventions, thereby improving treatment efficacy and reducing trial-and-error approaches in clinical management.

The identification of distinct FC patterns in the Slow3, Slow4, and Slow5 bands contributes to our understanding of the complex neurobiological mechanisms underlying ADHD. The involvement of the motor network, as evidenced by altered FC in the right precentral gyrus, aligns with the well-documented motor symptoms of the disorder. Meanwhile, the consistent findings in the right inferior frontal orbital region across multiple frequency bands underscore the orbitofrontal cortex’s role in cognitive and emotional dysregulation in ADHD. These results suggest that frequency-specific FC analysis can yield meaningful neural biomarkers for ADHD, potentially leading to more accurate diagnosis, earlier identification, and tailored treatment planning.

Future studies should evaluate whether these FC markers can predict treatment trajectories, emergence of comorbidities, or escalation to antipsychotic or complex pharmacological regimens, as described in Sultan et al. [27, 28]. Establishing such links would position these frequency-specific connectivity patterns as clinically actionable tools for guiding treatment decisions and improving long-term outcomes in youth with ADHD.

The absence of significant correlations between functional connectivity alterations and clinical symptom scores in the ADHD group may reflect several interacting factors. ADHD is a highly heterogeneous condition, with symptom expression varying widely across individuals, which can dilute linear associations between brain connectivity and behavioral ratings, especially in modestly sized samples [45]. Moreover, brain–behavior relationships in ADHD may follow non-linear trajectories, such that connectivity changes do not scale proportionally with symptom severity. Parent- and teacher-reported questionnaires, while widely used, may also lack the sensitivity to detect subtle gradations in behavior that align with neural alterations. Another possibility is that the observed FC differences represent relatively stable neurodevelopmental traits or compensatory mechanisms, rather than state-dependent markers of current symptom expression, and thus would not be expected to correlate directly with momentary symptom severity [46]. Furthermore, although anxiety levels were elevated in the ADHD group, it is possible that the neural circuits contributing to FC changes in our analysis are not primarily those involved in anxiety regulation [47]. inally, state-related fluctuations (e.g., attentional engagement, fatigue, arousal during scanning) and measurement variability may further obscure meaningful associations [48]. Together, these considerations highlight the complexity of linking neurobiological measures with clinical symptoms and underscore the need for larger, multimodal, and longitudinal studies to disentangle trait- versus state-related neural markers in ADHD.

Limitations and future directions

While the findings are promising, there are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples are needed to confirm these findings and to explore potential subgroup differences within ADHD. Second, the study focused on specific frequency bands, but other bands, such as Alpha and Beta, may also be relevant to ADHD and should be investigated in future research. Additionally, while the study employed machine learning techniques to evaluate the diagnostic potential of the identified FC patterns, further validation using independent datasets and more advanced algorithms is needed. Third, ADHD participants had a history of psychostimulant medication use, including methylphenidate and atomoxetine. Although these participants discontinued medication for at least 48 h prior to scanning, residual effects cannot be entirely ruled out and may have influenced the functional connectivity results. Moreover, depressive symptoms were not assessed in this study. Given the high comorbidity of depression with ADHD and its potential influence on brain connectivity, this represents an additional limitation. Future studies should include standardized assessments of both anxiety and depression to more thoroughly account for their potential impact on functional neuroimaging findings. Another limitation of this study is the potential influence of psychostimulant medication history in some ADHD participants. While participants with ADHD were instructed to discontinue their medication (including methylphenidate and atomoxetine) for at least 48 h prior to scanning, we cannot completely rule out residual effects that may have influenced the functional connectivity results. The 48-hour washout period may not have been sufficient to fully eliminate the impact of long-term medication use on brain function. Future studies should consider implementing longer washout periods or including a subgroup analysis comparing medication-naive participants with those who have a history of medication use. This approach would help to better isolate the effects of ADHD itself from the potential confounding effects of psychostimulant medications on functional connectivity patterns. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to draw conclusions about the causal relationship between FC alterations and ADHD symptoms. Longitudinal studies are necessary to determine whether these FC patterns can prpedict the onset or progression of the disorder.

Conclusion

The findings from this study contribute significantly to the growing body of literature supporting the role of frequency-specific FC analysis in uncovering neural alterations associated with ADHD. By demonstrating distinct FC patterns in the Slow3, Slow4, and Slow5 bands, this research underscores the importance of investigating multiple frequency bands to fully capture the spectrum of neural alterations in ADHD. These frequency-specific FC patterns not only enhance our understanding of ADHD’s neurobiological basis but also should be regarded as preliminary candidate markers that may hold potential to aid future risk stratification and individualized intervention after replication and prospective validation.

Our results highlight the Right Precentral Gyrus, Left Inferior Temporal Gyrus, and Right Middle Frontal Gyrus as key regions showing altered connectivity across different frequency bands in ADHD. These findings provide a more nuanced understanding of the disorder’s neural underpinnings and suggest that a multi-band approach to FC analysis may help refine future tools and inform targeted interventions once stability and generalizability are established.

Future research should aim to validate these frequency-specific FC patterns in larger, diverse populations and explore their potential as predictive markers for ADHD subtypes or treatment responses. This approach may pave the way for more personalized and effective management strategies for individuals with ADHD.
