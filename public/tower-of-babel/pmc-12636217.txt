Optimal cutoff score for the Malay version of parenting and child tooth brushing assessment (M-PACTA)
Authors: Yu Jie Chin, Jennifer Geraldine Doss, Sabri Musa, Danaee Mahmoud, Shani Ann Mani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRoutine effective tooth brushing with fluoridated toothpaste has a substantial impact on preventing dental caries. Parent and child roles in establishing routine tooth brushing habits in preschool children encounter numerous challenges. The Malay version of the Parenting and Child Tooth Brushing Assessment (M-PACTA) is a comprehensive tool with four scales that assesses various aspects of parent-child behaviour during tooth brushing. However, it lacks a clear threshold score for each scale to distinguish between favourable and unfavourable outcomes. Therefore, it is imperative to develop a reliable cutoff score for each scale of the M-PACTA.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the minimum cutoff score for child’s behaviour, parental strategies, attitude, and knowledge scales of the M-PACTA using the modified Angoff method.MethodsA panel of nine experts, including paediatric and community dental specialists, independently assessed 77 items in M-PACTA. The experts were required to indicate good and bad outcomes of each item of the M-PACTA. Cronbach’s alpha was used to evaluate inter-rater agreement, and the final scores for each scale were determined by summing up the mean score for each item.ResultsThe cutoff scores for child behaviour, parental strategies, attitude, and knowledge scales was determined to be 37, 98, 44, and 41, respectively. The interrater agreement yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.958.ConclusionsIn conclusion, the cutoff score for each scale in the M-PACTA questionnaire- child’s behaviour, parental strategy, attitude, and knowledge was determined for the Malaysian population.Keywords: M-PACTA, Cutoff score, Angoff method, Tooth brushing, Children

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) is a global problem with a combined prevalence of 48%, with the highest levels found in Asian countries [1]. ECC has a detrimental effect on preschool children, including their development, appearance, function and their families’ quality of life [2, 3]. The oral hygiene habits developed during early childhood have a substantial impact on preventing ECC [4]. Instilling and sustaining effective tooth brushing practices in children is a crucial responsibility of parents, who serve as the primary carers [5, 6]. The most significant determinants of tooth brushing frequency were oral health awareness and the applications of routines, while the most significant predictor of the perceived difficulty during regular tooth brushing was found to be the resistive child behaviour [5].

Tooth brushing in preschool children should be a team effort between parents and children for a number of reasons [7]. Family dynamics can influence tooth brushing behaviour and oral health of pre-school children [8]. Preschool children are yet to develop fine motor skills necessary for tooth brushing and hence parents are required to guide their child’s hand movements and teach correct techniques to ensure effective tooth brushing. Parents who set up regular tooth brushing times, make it a routine habit in children establishing it for a lifetime [9]. Considering tooth brushing can feel like a chore to young children, parents play a crucial role in making it enjoyable and engaging [10]. Parents are also effective role models who set a positive example by brushing their teeth alongside their child and making it a family activity [10].

The Parenting and Child Tooth Brushing Assessment (PACTA) is a reliable tool for evaluating parent-child associated behaviours that facilitate and hinder effective tooth brushing among preschool children in Australia [11]. PACTA was translated into Malay and culturally adapted for Malaysian preschool children (M-PACTA) [12]. The M-PACTA demonstrated good construct validity, internal consistency reliability, and test-retest reliability and comprised 77 items with four independent scales: child behaviour, parental strategies, attitude, and knowledge [12]. While each scale measures the construct, no definitive cutoff score for each scale in M-PACTA distinguishes between favourable and unfavourable outcomes.

Establishing cut-off scores for questionnaires are not always essential [13]. They are particularly beneficial for tools that assess specific conditions, guide diagnosis or make decisions. Cutoff scores help ensure consistent interpretation and provide clarity to objective assessments [13]. Cutoff scores for the M-PACTA will assist the clinician in identifying caries-risk categories, conveying customised advice to the parents to improve tooth brushing behaviour, monitoring improvement in tooth brushing behaviour or identifying the need for additional support. Additionally, researchers can effectively identify at-risk populations and tailor interventions accordingly. Consequently, it is crucial to construct a reliable cutoff score for the M-PACTA to make it a more effective tool.

Thus, the current study aimed to determine the minimum cutoff score for each scale of the M-PACTA, namely the child’s behaviour, parental knowledge, attitude, and knowledge, using the modified Angoff method.

Materials and methods

Background of the M-PACTA

The four independent scales within the M-PACTA are child behaviour, parental strategies, attitude, and knowledge. The child behaviour scale measures the frequency of problematic behaviour among the children in the previous week and comprised 21 items, using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always). Scores range from 21 to 105, with higher scores indicating poorer child’s behaviour. Parental strategies during tooth brushing sessions were elicited using 27 items, each rated on a 5-point scale. Higher scores implied the implementation of more effective parental strategies and scores range from 27 to 135. The parental attitude scale has 18 items ranging from 18 to 90 using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), where greater scores indicate more favourable parenting attitudes [12]. The parental knowledge scale consists of 11 items, each assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, with a total score ranging from 11 to 55 [12]. Higher scores corresponded to better knowledge among parents.

Conduct of study

Ethics approval was obtained from the institution for the conduct of the study [Ethical approval number: (DF CD2318/0039 (P)]. A panel of 10 content experts, consisting of five specialists in Paediatric Dentistry and five specialists in Community Dental Health were identified and invited to participate. The experts were affiliated with local dental schools, the private sector, or the Malaysian Ministry of Health. The selection process complied with the recommendation of having a minimum of five experts [14]. Informed consent to participate was obtained from the participants prior to data collection. The experts were contacted via email and provided with a detailed explanation of the objectives and protocol of the study, including details on their expected roles and procedures involved in the evaluation process. User-friendly evaluation forms were also provided to record the favourable and less favourable outcome for effective tooth brushing across each of the 77 items in the child’s behaviour, parental strategies, attitude, and knowledge scales. Each participant was required to independently determine the minimum desired score for each item on a scoring form that was emailed to the experts [15]. The form included all items in M-PACTA with five scoring cells representing each item’s five Likert scale responses. The experts were required to shade cells that signified the minimum favourable outcome for each item, while the cells representing less favourable outcomes were to remain empty.

Data analysis

The experts’ recommended scores for each item were documented in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Cronbach’s alpha was employed to evaluate inter-rater agreement using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 29.0 for Mac. Cronbach’s alpha was chosen to determine the inter-rater agreement where the correlations among the raters were evaluated [16]. The mean of each item was computed by summing the ratings given by all experts and dividing by the total number of experts. The score of each item was subsequently aggregated for each subscale and rounded to the nearest whole number to produce the final score.

The inter-rater agreement for the child’s behaviour scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.961 for the avoidance behaviour subscale and 0.957 for the non-compliance subscale. Within the parental strategies scale, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.947 (uncommon positive method), 0.957 (negative method) and 0.952 (routine positive method), respectively. For the parental attitude scale, the inter-rate agreement was 0.960 for the lack of concern subscale and 0.969 for the attitude of care subscale. In addition, the general knowledge and awareness of tooth brushing care subscales showed Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.959 and 0.960, respectively. Overall, the inter-rater agreement among all nine experts yielded an average Cronbach’s apha of 0.958, indicating a high agreement among the experts.

Discussion

The original PACTA did not provide cutoff scores for each scale; instead, it presented score ranges indicating the quality of children’s behaviour, the effectiveness of parental strategies, and the levels of parental attitudes and knowledge [11]. The cutoff score for the M-PACTA questionnaire is essential in providing clear and objective acceptable measures of tooth brushing behaviours, parental strategies, attitude and knowledge [17]. This standardisation ensures consistent assessment among Malaysian populations, aids in identifying at-risk groups, facilitates ongoing monitoring, and supports policy development aimed at improving oral health outcomes in preschool children.

The Angoff method is a well-established approach in education, medical and psychological assessment for establishing cutoff scores through expert judgement on the minimal passing level (MPL) [18]. The modified Angoff approach has been increasingly adopted in recent years, refining this process by focusing on the minimal passing level of an individual rather than a group [18]. This study employed the modified Angoff method due to its precision in item-level analysis, allowing experts to evaluate whether each behaviour meets the minimum level of expectation within the Malaysian context [19]. The experts involved in determining cutoff scores were evenly distributed among teaching institutes, and the Malaysian Ministry of Health, and they were able to decide a cutoff score that reflects the expert consensus on local expectations and regulations. Although some experts were not routinely engaged in clinical practice, they were regularly involved in dental public health activities and/or development of clinical practice guidelines. Given Malaysia’s cultural and racially diversity, the racially homogenous experts reduced the potential biases, thereby improving the reliability of determined cutoff scores.

During the consensus-building process among nine experts, a strict criterion was applied to several items on the child’s behaviour scale. Specifically, the refusal of tooth brushing before bedtime and refusal of tooth brushing in the morning were considered crucial, with more than half of the experts expecting these behaviours to never occur during tooth brushing sessions. This aligns with the recommendation from the evidenced-based toolkit for prevention, which suggests that tooth brushing should occur twice daily, with one session before bedtime and another at different time during the day [20]. Therefore, it is expected that these two items were considered the most crucial behaviours to uphold, given the significance of maintaining the appropriate tooth brushing frequency and timing among preschool children. Experts viewed avoidance behaviours, such as tantrums, crying, talking, and refusal to open the mouth during tooth brushing as unacceptable. The majority of experts leaned towards the opinion that these behaviours should never occur during tooth brushing sessions. The findings of a short study conducted by Agarwal et al. [21] demonstrated that children who exhibited tantrums and obstinacy were more likely to have poorer oral hygiene. Consequently, such behaviours were deemed unacceptable by the experts.

All nine experts unanimously agreed that establishing morning and evening routines for children, using alarms as reminders, offering positive reinforcement for good behaviour, and making tooth brushing a fun activity should be frequently employed by parents. This finding is consistent with a recent study by de Jong-Lenters et al. [10], which demonstrated that enhancing parental strategies for tooth brushing is an effective approach to preventing dental caries in children. Contrarily, most experts support the occasional application of negative parental strategies, asserting that infrequent methods like physical punishment can be beneficial. This is because sporadic physical punishment may help manage a child’s short-term behaviour and reinforce parental authority [22]. On the other hand, the cultural differences in parenting styles may explain why other studies suggests that physical punishment and verbal scolding would make tooth brushing sessions more stressful for children, which, in turn, would increase the difficulty of establishing tooth brushing habits [10]. Hence cut-off scores for the same scale may vary between cultures depending on the predominant parenting styles of the region.

Most experts concur that items in the attitude of care subscale reflect a positive parental attitude. This includes parents feeling anxious and guilty if their children did not brush their teeth, as well as feeling shame and worry if their children developed new cavities. All experts agreed that the parents taking the initiative to find ways to make their child cooperative during tooth brushing reflects a positive parental attitude, indicating that parents are aware of the consequences of inadequate tooth brushing [23]. Conversely, all experts agreed that items falling into the lack of concern subscale indicate a negative parental attitude. Attitudes such as not brushing their child’s teeth if the child did not want to and believing that brushing once daily is sufficient would exacerbate the undesirable behaviour during tooth brushing [10].

All experts agreed that children younger than eight years old need parental assistance to brush their teeth effectively. They also concurred that commencing tooth brushing with fluoridated toothpaste from six months of age, along with the use of an age-appropriate amount, is crucial for preventing tooth decay. These views align with recommendations from Public Health England [20] and Scottish Dental Clinical Effectiveness Programme [24]. Additionally, experts indicated that tooth brushing should last at least 2 min and that using a toothbrush with a small head and soft bristles is more effective for children [20]. Moreover, all experts acknowledged the importance of not rinsing after tooth brushing, which aligns with the guideline from Public Health England [20]. In contrast, all experts disagreed with the idea that routine brushing should only begin once the permanent teeth erupt and that it is safe for children to swallow toothpaste daily. This corresponds to the recommendation that brushing should begin as soon as the first primary tooth emerges and that parents should supervise tooth brushing to prevent the habit of licking toothpaste [20].

The established cutoff scores provide practical benchmarks for identifying levels of parental strategies, knowledge, attitudes and children’s tooth brushing behaviours that may warrant intervention. In clinical and community oral health settings, these thresholds can assist practitioners in recognising families at risk of suboptimal oral health practices or attitudes, allowing for tailored educational or behavioural support. Moreover, the cutoff scores can serve as reference points for evaluating the effectiveness of health promotion programs and preventive interventions aimed at improving tooth brushing behaviours. However, this study is not without limitations. The inclusion of experts in child behaviour, parenting strategies and behaviour change would have further validated the results of the study. Secondly, the determined cutoff scores are influenced by cultural context and, therefore, may not be reliable for other populations. Thirdly, the validity of the cutoff score can only be assessed when further applied to a Malaysian population. Lastly, Malaysia being a multiracial society, parental practices and attitudes may differ between individual races.

Conclusions

In this study, the optimal cutoff score for each scale- child’s behaviour, parental strategy, attitude, and knowledge in the M-PACTA questionnaire was successfully determined for the Malaysian population using the modified Angoff method.
