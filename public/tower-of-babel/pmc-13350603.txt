Safety of BNT162b2 COVID-19 Vaccine in Pregnancy: A Systematic Review
Authors: Elizabeth Thoburn, Vassilis G. Giannakoulis, Tianyan Hu, Henry Goebe, Sheetal Raina, Mary M. Moran, Benedicte Perreault, Luis Val-Maranes, Evelyn Barron Millar, Isabelle Whittle, Fiona Pearson, Santiago M. C. Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSince COVID-19 vaccine introduction in December 2020, > 13 billion doses have been distributed globally, including > 5 billion Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) doses. Real-world evidence in pregnancy remains heterogenous in design and outcome definitions, with limited analysis by dose timing or variant period. Most reviews evaluated COVID-19 vaccines as a class without focus on individual products, limiting product-specific interpretation. Given expanding evidence and public concern around safety of vaccines during pregnancy, an updated, pregnancy-focused systematic review of BNT162b2 safety is warranted. This study aimed to review and synthesize evidence on the safety of Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine in pregnant women, including pregnancy-related outcomes.MethodsA systematic literature review (PROSPERO registered) was conducted in accordance with Cochrane methodology and reported following PRISMA guidelines. Medline and Embase were searched from December 2020 to June 2025, supplemented by regulatory reports and conference searches. Eligible randomized and observational studies reporting obstetric or neonatal outcomes after ≥ 1 dose of BNT162b2 were included. Quality appraisal was conducted using NICE checklists. Results were narratively synthesized and reported following SWiM guidance.ResultsAcross 25 studies, comprising > 450,000 BNT162b2-vaccinated pregnant women, no increased risk was observed for miscarriage, congenital anomalies, preterm birth, hypertensive disorders, small for gestational age, low birth weight, or neonatal death, with effect estimates broadly consistent and centered around the null across available trimester-stratified analyses, dose numbers, and study design. However, trimester-specific data, particularly for first-trimester exposure, were limited.ConclusionThe cumulative evidence demonstrates a reassuring safety profile for Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) COVID-19 vaccination with no indication of increased pregnancy-related or neonatal risk. The evidence is mainly observational and heterogeneous, with limited precision for rare outcomes, but the results align with findings from international registry, surveillance, and population-based evidence and support BNT162b2 vaccination in pregnancy as an effective and safe means to reduce preventable maternal morbidity, although evidence remains limited for first-trimester-specific exposure.Trial RegistrationThe review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO [CRD420251080193] [1].Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01378-5.

Introduction

Rationale and Background

Since the Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) of COVID-19 vaccines in late 2020, more than 13 billion doses have been distributed worldwide, including over 5 billion doses of the Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) mRNA vaccine [2, 3]. Despite widespread availability and recommendations from international health agencies, vaccination coverage among pregnant women continues to lag behind that of the general population [4, 5]. Pregnant women face substantially higher risks of severe outcomes from COVID-19 and intensive care admission compared with non-pregnant adults alongside risk of adverse obstetric outcomes [6, 7]. Nevertheless, concerns about safety remain key drivers of vaccine hesitancy [8, 9].

Knowledge Gap

The evidence base on mRNA COVID-19 vaccine safety has expanded rapidly, but the literature is marked by substantial heterogeneity. Studies vary widely in design, analytic approaches, population definitions, exposure windows, and the terminology used for key obstetric and neonatal outcomes such as spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, congenital anomalies, and preterm birth [10]. This heterogeneity makes individual studies difficult to interpret [11].

National and international surveillance-systems, including the UK MHRA Yellow Card scheme, the US Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) and Vaccine Safety Datalink, and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) EudraVigilance database, have consistently reported no concerning patterns of adverse pregnancy or neonatal outcomes following mRNA vaccination. However, findings remain dispersed across systems that differ in reporting structures, case definition, and denominator accuracy, limiting their utility for guiding clinical decision-making during pregnancy [12, 13].

Recent Evidence Context

High-quality observational studies have recently strengthened confidence in the safety of mRNA vaccination during pregnancy. For example, Bernard et al., in a nationwide French cohort of more than 527,000 live births, found no increased risk of major congenital malformations following first trimester mRNA COVID-19 vaccination [adjusted odds ratio 0.98 (95% CI 0.93–1.04)] [14]. These findings complement a growing body of registry, surveillance, and population-based evidence, reinforcing the need for an updated synthesis focused specifically on BNT162b2, including variant-adapted formulae.

Given the variability in study design, exposure definition, and outcome reporting across mRNA COVID-19 vaccines, it was decided a priori to understand product specific safety signals for the single product BNT162b2, rather than across the mRNA class, to ensure homogeneity in mechanism of action, vaccine formulation, and dosing schedules. This allowed for a more consistent and interpretable synthesis across studies with comparable exposure definitions, dosing schedules, and follow-up periods.

Study Objective

A systematic review was conducted to evaluate maternal, fetal, and neonatal safety outcomes associated with Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) COVID-19 vaccines across all trimesters of pregnancy. The review aimed to synthesize the rapidly expanding and heterogenous evidence base, to analyze inconsistencies across data sources, and to provide clinicians, policymakers, and public health communicators with an up-to-date, pregnancy-specific assessment of vaccine safety.

Materials and Methods

Design and Registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported following PRISMA 2020 guidelines [15]. The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251080193) [1], and the systematic review focused exclusively on the maternal, fetal, and neonatal safety of BNT162b2 COVID-19 vaccines. This study is based on existing literature, and the study did not involve collection of novel data relating to human participants or animals.

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed as outlined in the approved protocol. Searches were performed in MEDLINE and Embase (via Ovid; Supplementary Table 1), covering December 2020 (the point of initial vaccine authorization) through to 20 June 2025. Search strings combined controlled vocabulary and keywords related to COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2, mRNA vaccination, Pfizer-BioNTech formulae (including lineage-adapted variants, pregnancy, and safety outcomes). The Embase strategy included coronavirus terms, Pfizer-BioNTech vaccine identifiers (e.g., BNT162b2, Comirnaty, tozinameran) and filters for randomized and observational study designs. Search terms were translated for use in MEDLINE.

To mitigate publication bias, supplementary searching was undertaken across major regulatory and national public-health bodies with established COVID-19 vaccine safety programs (e.g., MHRA, CDC/ACIP, EMA, FDA, Health Canada). Gray literature was captured though targeted searches of relevant 2024–2025 infectious disease conference proceedings (ESCMID, ID Week, ESPID) when not indexed in Embase. Citation searching was performed using forward and backward screening of relevant systematic reviews.

All returned records were exported to a reference management system, deduplicated, and screened using the eligibility criteria defined below.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility was determined using the PICOTS framework (Supplementary Table 2). Randomized controlled trials, cohort, case–control, pharmacovigilance, and large registry analyses published from 1 December 2020 were considered. The inclusion criteria for the target population comprised pregnant women of any gestational age receiving at least one dose of the BNT162b2 vaccine. Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccines (BNT162b2) included the original WuHan-Hu-1 strain formulae and Omicron adapted formulae (e.g., BA.4/BA.5, XBB.1.5, JN.1, KP.2). Studies evaluating heterologous vaccination were included only if outcomes were stratified by BNT162b2 exposure. Acceptable comparators included unvaccinated pregnant women or pregnant women receiving variable timing of dose or variable doses of BNT162b2. Eligible studies had to report at least one maternal, fetal, or neonatal safety outcome, including spontaneous abortion, miscarriage, stillbirth, preterm birth, hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, postpartum hemorrhage, chorioamnionitis, congenital malformations, small for gestational age (SGA), low birth weight, and neonatal death.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Initial screening was completed by two reviewers (IW and LVM) using dual-reviewer calibration followed by single-reviewer screening with independent verification of all exclusions by a third reviewer (FP). Full text screening utilized dual-reviewer concordance checks followed by single-reviewer screening with second-reviewer verification of excluded full texts. Conflicts were resolved by consensus or third-reviewer adjudication. Data extraction was conducted using a structured and piloted extraction form based on the protocol. One reviewer extracted all relevant information (LVM and EBM), which was independently checked by a second reviewer (IW). Extracted items included study design, source population, country and study period, sample size, participant characteristics such as maternal age, comorbidities, gestational age, and vaccination. Exposure details included vaccine formulae, number of doses, and timing within pregnancy. Definition and methods of ascertaining safety outcomes were extracted including available effect estimates. Regulatory surveillance findings were linked to full-text analyses where possible.

Quality Assessment

Risk of bias for each included study was assessed using the appropriate National Institute for health and Care Excellence (NICE) methodology checklist for its design [16]. Appraisals were completed by one reviewer with verification by a second. Studies judged to have high risk of bias in one or more core domains, including selection, performance, detection, or attrition bias were excluded from synthesis. Risk of bias findings were documented during the data extraction process and are summarized descriptively in the results. Studies assessed as high risk of bias were excluded from the primary synthesis to maintain internal validity, remove any systematically distorted results, and prevent misinterpretation of evidence.

Outcome Harmonization

Outcome terminology was harmonized to improve comparability across studies. Related outcomes were grouped using clinically coherent categories, consistent with approaches used in prior vaccine safety syntheses [17]. Variations in terminology, such as ‘spontaneous abortion’ versus ‘miscarriage’, were resolved by mapping to standard categories. Exposure timing was harmonized by assigning each study to the relevant trimester or dose window based on gestational age at vaccination.

Narrative Synthesis and Analysis

Given the expected diversity of study designs, populations, surveillance systems, and outcome definitions, a meta-analysis was not planned. Instead, findings were synthesized narratively and are reported following SWiM guidance [18]. Evidence was organized by maternal, pregnancy-related, fetal, and neonatal outcomes. Patterns were examined in relation to timing of exposure, dose number, comparator type, and putative effect modifiers, such as maternal age, comorbid conditions, and high-risk pregnancy status where data permitted. Consistency was defined as concordance in the direction of effect estimates across studies, irrespective of statistical significance. Findings were considered ‘centered around the null’ where effect estimates approximated unity (e.g., odds ratios near 1.0) and confidence intervals included the null value, with no systematic deviation observed across studies. Conflicting findings were examined in the context of methodological differences, population heterogeneity, and risk of bias assessments.

Studies were tabulated in structured tables, showing study design categories, outcome category, vaccination dose, and operational definitions and effect estimates for pregnancy outcomes. For each study, key characteristics and effect estimates with confidence intervals were presented. To enhance methodological rigor, all stages of the review, including study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment, were conducted with independent verification by a second reviewer following the pre-specified protocol. Given inconsistent reporting of dose number across studies, results were not stratified or pooled by dose category. Consistency in the direction and magnitude of effect estimates was assessed within and across studies where dose-specific data were available, and findings were interpreted in the context of overall evidence and summarized narratively.

Results

Study Identification and Selection

The database searches yielded 6659 records after de-duplication. Following title and abstract screening, 1513 articles were retrieved for full-text assessment. Of these, 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. Supplementary searches of national regulatory bodies, conference proceedings, and citation tracking yielded an additional 366 records; of those not already identified though database searches, two met the inclusion criteria. Identified systematic reviews were used solely for citation tracking to identify additional relevant primary studies. No pooled or study-level data were extracted from these reviews, and all included studies were assessed independently to avoid duplication.

A total of 25 studies were included in the review. The most common reasons for full-text exclusion were non-pregnant cohorts (population) and lack of stratification into BNT162b2-specific analyses (outcomes). A PRISMA flow diagram summarizes the study identification and selection process (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 25 studies were included, representing 454,765 women who received at least one dose of BNT162b2 COVID-19 vaccine during pregnancy or within a clinically relevant pre-conception window as deemed by the study authors. Study designs comprised cross-sectional studies (n = 1), prospective cohort studies (n = 8), case–control studies (n = 2), pharmacovigilance analyses (n = 2) and retrospective cohort studies (n = 12), reporting pregnancy-specific safety outcomes. A summary of the characteristics of included studies is presented in Supplementary Table 3. The included studies spanned 11 countries, with study periods predominantly covering the early vaccine rollout in 2020–2022, alongside more recent analyses from 2023 onwards. Sample sizes ranged from a small prospective cohort with fewer than 162 participants to a national registry-based study including over 205,263 women.

Across studies, exposure was most often defined as receipt of one or more doses of BNT162b2 during pregnancy, with varying granularity on timing. Two studies provided trimester-specific analyses [19, 20], and one study reported exposure windows spanning multiple gestational periods [21]. Most studies (n = 17) reported three or more pregnancy safety outcomes, with outcome coverage summarized in Supplementary Table 4. Dose numbers (first/second/booster) were inconsistently reported, though most contemporary datasets included at least the primary series. Where dose-specific estimates were available, the direction and magnitude were assessed descriptively within and across studies. Studies without dose stratification were interpreted within the overall evidence context. Variations in exposure definitions, including the number of BNT162b2 doses received and whether booster doses were administered, are presented in Supplementary Table 5.

Quality Assessment

Risk of bias appraisal using the NICE methodology checklists indicated a predominantly low risk of bias across the included evidence base (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). Four publications were excluded from the synthesis due to high risk of bias, primarily reflecting limitations with passive pharmacovigilance designs, including lack of denominator data, inconsistent case definitions, and potential reporting bias. As the population of interest had received COVID-19 vaccination, performance bias and maintenance of blinding to treatment allocation, judgement of unclear or high risk on this basis was noted, but did not impact overall assessment. Most study designs were retrospective reviews of medical or insurance claims databases, therefore selection bias was of particular relevance, and included studies were closely reviewed for comparability and balancing of major confounders at baseline. Common limitations included incomplete adjustment for confounding, limited granularity in exposure timing, and reliance on passive surveillance for rare events.

Outcome Harmonisation

Outcome definitions were broadly aligned with national reporting or clinical coding standards, but variation was observed across studies in gestational thresholds, diagnostic terminology, and ascertainment methods. Supplementary Table 8 summarizes these areas of consistency and variability, providing a cross-study definition matrix for all pregnancy, fetal, and neonatal outcomes included in this review.

Operational definitions and associated effect estimates (where reported) are presented in Supplementary Table 9, enabling comparison of how each study defined and measured key outcomes such as spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, stillbirth, congenital anomalies, and neonatal complications. Together, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5 provide context for interpreting directionality and consistency of the results.

Pregnancy Outcomes

Across eight studies reporting early pregnancy loss, encompassing more than 100,000 BNT162b2-vaccinated pregnant women, effect estimates for spontaneous abortion and miscarriage were consistently close to the null across studies, with no directional increase in risk observed following BNT162b2 vaccination [19, 21–27]. Adjusted analyses were consistent across subgroups showing no observed association between BNT162b2 vaccination and the outcomes evaluated, with odds ratios clustering close to the null (0.93–0.95, 95% CI 0.83–1.07) and an additional estimate of odds ratio of 1.05 (95% CI 0.78–1.40; p = 0.77) also demonstrating no association. Differences in operational definitions did not alter overall interpretation (see Supplementary Table 8).

Preterm birth was reported by 15 studies [19, 20, 22, 23, 26–36], representing more than 300,000 exposures during pregnancy. Across these studies, no evidence of increased preterm birth (< 37 weeks) was observed following BNT162b2 vaccination. Reported effect estimates for overall preterm birth consistently centered around the null, with confidence intervals across subgroups ranging from 0.67 to 1.46 for preterm birth < 37 weeks. When early and late preterm births were examined separately, findings remained stable: < 32 weeks and 32–36 weeks with no subgroup showing a directional increase in risk.

The observed lack of association between BNT162b2 vaccination and preterm birth was consistent across weighted and unweighted analyses, gestational age-specific vaccination windows, and populations stratified by maternal COVID-19 infection status. For overall preterm birth (< 37 weeks), both inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)-adjusted and unadjusted analyses showed no evidence of increased risk [23]. Maternal COVID-19 infection stratified comparisons showed no elevated risk with < 37 weeks and early preterm birth (< 32 weeks) [20]. Similar findings were observed across gestational age strata, including early preterm (< 32 weeks), moderate preterm (32–36 weeks), and overall preterm birth [20]. Although definitions of preterm birth and analytic approaches varied across studies (summarized in Supplementary Table 8), this heterogeneity did not affect overall interpretation. Subpopulation-level coverage of preterm birth outcomes is presented in Supplementary Table 4.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were reported in seven studies [26, 28, 31–33, 37, 38], encompassing over 200,000 exposures during pregnancy. Across all analyzed subpopulations, including pre- and post-IPTW vaccinated and unvaccinated groups, first-trimester vaccination cohorts, women with and without documented SARS-CoV-2 infection, and large registry-based national populations, findings for hypertensive disorders were variable across studies, but showed no consistent directional pattern suggestive of increased risk of gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia following BNT162b2 vaccination [26, 28, 31–33, 37, 38]. Reported effect estimates for hypertensive disorders demonstrated no consistent directional shift in risk estimates, with odds ratios ranging from 1.30 to 3.13 (95% CI 0.45–21.7, p values 0.13–0.30), with no statistically significant associations.

Gestational diabetes outcomes were reported across nine studies, representing more than 150,000 exposures during pregnancy [19, 26–29, 31–33, 39]. Incidence of gestational diabetes findings were variable across subpopulations. Within the Rottenstreich cohort, some subgroups showed elevated adjusted odds for gestational diabetes, including ‘received booster’ and ‘unvaccinated’ subgroups, with adjusted odds ratios of 2.88 (95% CI 1.75–4.76, p < 0.01) and 3.69 (95% CI 2.15–6.31, p < 0.01), respectively [26]. The significant result in both vaccinated and unvaccinated subgroups from the same study suggests that these findings may be influenced by differences in baseline metabolic risk, ascertainment, or residual confounding rather than a consistent vaccine-related signal across the broader evidence base. Variation in how gestational diabetes was defined and captured across studies (summarized in Supplementary Table 8) may contribute to this heterogeneity, necessitating a cautious interpretation.

Within univariate analyses, Rottenstreich reported comparable incidence of chorioamnionitis among pregnant women following receipt of ≥ 1 dose of BNT162b2 vaccine versus unvaccinated controls (p values ranged from 0.5 to 0.97), consistent across primary series and additional doses [26, 33].

Postpartum hemorrhage was author-defined across the five reporting studies [19, 26, 31, 33, 39], limiting direct comparability across datasets. However, no evidence of increased risk was observed following BNT162b2 vaccination. The only reported effect estimate, from Wainstock, showed no difference by timing of vaccination, with an unadjusted odds ratio of 1.28 (95% CI: 0.62–2.62) with BNT162b2 vaccine receipt during second or third trimester [39].

Fetal and Neonatal Outcomes

Definition of birth defects and congenital malformations varied across the three reporting studies, which included large registry and population-based datasets capturing a range of structural, chromosomal, and polymalformative conditions [25, 27, 40]. Only Rowe directly compared outcomes by vaccination status, reporting a prevalence rate of 155.2 structural birth defects per 10,000 live births among women who received ≥ 1 BNT162b2 vaccine dose versus 160.6 per 10,000 live births among unvaccinated pregnant women, consistent with rates typically reported in general obstetric populations [40].

Reported effect estimates for low birth weight, from more than 350,000 exposures during pregnancy, consistently indicated no observed association between BNT162b2 vaccination and low birth weight (95% confidence intervals ranging from 0.78 to 1.18) [20, 26, 33, 35]. Definitions of low birth weight varied across studies, with absolute birth weight thresholds (≥ 2500– < 4000 g) and percentile-based definitions (< 10th percentile, referencing the International Fetal Growth Standards) most commonly applied [19, 29, 30, 34, 41].

Risk of low birth weight remained comparable across subgroups analyzed, including when stratified into first trimester vaccination, maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection pre-birth, and booster or primary series vaccine dose [20, 33].

Across 12 studies assessing impact of vaccination on SGA, seven reported effect estimates and showed no observed association with SGA risk across adjusted analyses for ≥ 1 BNT162b2 vaccine dose versus unvaccinated pregnant women (95% confidence intervals ranging from 0.40 to 2.64) [20, 30–32, 34, 35, 39]. SGA was typically defined as birth weight < 10th percentile for gestational age, although some studies did not state a definition or clinical codes. SGA outcomes were included within synthesis when referred to as intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR). Only one study, Peretz-Machluf, reported IUGR as a distinct outcome, comparing booster dose receipt with primary series vaccination, rather than with an unvaccinated or general population comparator, and found no difference in adjusted risk (adjusted odds ratio: 0.63, 95% CI 0.127–3.10; p = 0.569) [31].

Denoble and Suseeladevi reported effect estimates for neonatal mortality, both concluding comparable risk following receipt of BNT162b2 vaccine and among unvaccinated controls in adjusted analyses [34, 42]. No significant difference (p value range 0.17–0.68) in intrauterine fetal death between BNT162b2 vaccinated and unvaccinated pregnant women was reported across univariate analyses including a total of 3362 recipients of ≥ 1 BNT162b2 dose and 2890 unvaccinated comparators [19, 26, 29, 33].

Summary of Evidence Trends

Across all outcomes, findings were consistent, with effect estimates clustering around the null and no systematic pattern of increased risk of adverse outcomes identified following BNT162b2 COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy. Reported rates of spontaneous abortion, preterm birth, hypertensive disorder, congenital malformations, low birth weight, SGA, and neonatal mortality were comparable to expected background rates, with no pattern suggesting dose-, trimester-, or formula-specific risk. Although heterogeneity in outcome definitions and exposure windows was noted, this variability did not alter the overall interpretation across subgroups, including first-trimester vaccination, maternal infection status, and primary-series versus booster dosing. Taken together, the findings converge toward a consistent and reassuring safety profile, with no emerging safety signals related to BNT162b2 COVID-19 vaccination identified across maternal fetal or neonatal outcomes.

Discussion

Principal Findings

This systematic review synthesized pregnancy, fetal, and neonatal outcomes following BNT162b2 COVID-19 vaccination across 25 studies encompassing more than 450,000 exposures during pregnancy, across diverse populations and healthcare systems with reporting on all major outcomes of interest. Across all major outcomes, no consistent evidence of increased risk was identified following vaccination. The directions and magnitude of effect estimates were consistent across heterogenous study designs, including national registry analyses, prospective cohorts, and retrospective database studies.

Importantly, no signal of increased risk emerged for outcomes of heightened public concern, including spontaneous abortion, congenital anomalies, preterm birth, SGA, stillbirth, or neonatal death. Maternal outcomes such as hypertensive disorders, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, and postpartum complications similarly showed no vaccine-associated increase, with variation between individual datasets largely attributable to differences in baseline population characteristics, exposure timing granularity, or outcome ascertainment rather than directional risk patterns.

Findings were stable across subpopulations, including women vaccinated in the first trimester, those with or without documented SARS-CoV-2 infection, and cohorts receiving booster doses compared with primary series vaccinations. Studies reporting variant-adapted formulae did not identify meaningful deviations in effect direction. Together, the consistency of findings across trimesters, doses, formulae, and health-system contexts provide a coherent evidence base supporting the safety of BNT162b2 vaccination during pregnancy, with no new or emerging signals detected across all evaluated outcomes.

Context Within Global Literature

The findings of this review are consistent with earlier systematic reviews conducted during the initial COVID-19 vaccine rollout, which reported no increased risk of adverse pregnancy or neonatal outcomes following mRNA COVID-19 vaccination, despite limitations related to short follow-up and incomplete trimester coverage [43, 44]. More recent syntheses in the general population have further reinforced the overall favorable safety profile of mRNA vaccine and have not yet identified biologically plausible mechanisms for gestational toxicity [11, 17].

National surveillance programs have similarly reported no safety signals for miscarriage, congenital anomalies, stillbirth, or neonatal complications among vaccinated pregnant women, despite extensive monitoring [45]. The consistency of findings across systems with different reporting structures strengthens the observed findings in this review.

Following the pre-specified review methods in our registered protocol [1], two pharmacovigilance studies were deemed at high risk of bias (as assessed using the NICE checklist) [22, 46]. Passive surveillance system data, such as that captured within VAERS and EudraVigilance, are useful for exploratory signal detection but are limited by their descriptive nature, lack of corresponding denominator information, inconsistent outcome/case definition, and high chance of self-reporting bias [47]. As such, any detected signals require external validation within active surveillance systems or well-designed population-based cohort or case control studies to more robustly estimate risk with adjustment for confounding. A strength of this systematic review was that included analyses were restricted to effect estimates presented by population-based cohort or case control studies and did not incorporate descriptive data. Review findings correspond with findings reported in literature also based on high-quality analytic evidence.

Long-standing under-representation of pregnant women in clinical trials has historically limited available safety data, but the accumulation of robust observational evidence following COVID-19 vaccine rollout represents a substantial improvement [48]. The present review builds on this developing evidence base and demonstrates a consistent absence of increased risk across maternal, pregnancy-related, and neonatal outcomes.

Benefit Contextualization

These results must also be interpreted in the context of the well-established risks associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection, including higher rates of severe disease, intensive-care admission and obstetric complication such as hypertensive disorders and preterm birth [6, 7]. Given the persistent public concerns regarding miscarriage, congenital anomalies, and long-term infant outcomes, and hesitancy documented among pregnant women [8, 9, 49], consolidating evidence from high-quality observational data demonstrating no increased risk is essential for informed counseling.

Finally, the pattern of results aligns with broader observations that pregnant women have had limited representation in clinical trials, with much of the available safety evidence derived from high-quality observational studies [48]. The large, diverse, and methodologically varied evidence base synthesized in this review represents a substantial strengthening of pregnancy-specific data. Taken together, surveillance findings and consistency across outcomes support the continued recommendation of BNT162b2 vaccination during pregnancy to mitigate the considerable risks posed by SARS-CoV-2.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this review include a comprehensive global search strategy across bibliographic databases, national surveillance systems, and conference proceedings, ensuring broad capture of relevant evidence across multiple stages of the pandemic. Dual-reviewer screening, independent verification of exclusions, and structured data extraction enhanced methodological rigor, as did the prospective registration of the protocol (CRD420251080193) [1], and decision a priori to focus on BNT162b2 rather than across the mRNA class to ensure homogeneity in vaccine mechanism of action, formulation, and dosing schedules. The inclusion of regulatory surveillance data from agencies with established COVID-19 vaccine monitoring programs provided additional reassurance and supported interpretation of rare outcomes [45]. Adherence to PRISMA and SWiM standards ensured transparency in reporting [50, 51], and risk of bias assessment using the NICE methodology checklists offered a structured approach suitable for the predominantly observational evidence base [16].

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. As expected for a rapidly evolving evidence landscape, the included studies demonstrated heterogeneity in outcome definitions, exposure windows, and analytical approaches, which may limit direct comparability across datasets. Trimester-specific data were inconsistently reported across studies, with particularly limited availability of first-trimester vaccination-specific analyses. The available evidence was underpowered for extremely rare events, such as specific congenital anomaly subtypes or very early pregnancy losses. This is important, given that early pregnancy represents a critical window for these outcomes and limits the precision of conclusions for these endpoints. Still, and reassuringly, large nationwide studies with a broader focus on mRNA COVID-19 vaccines have not identified any safety issues regarding exposure during the first trimester and adverse outcomes [14, 24, 52, 53]. While the use of observational design is appropriate for pregnancy safety research, residual confounding (particularly in datasets with limited adjustment for baseline risk) cannot be fully excluded [43, 54]. Passive surveillance systems may underestimate non-serious events, and although gray literature searches were undertaken, publication bias remains a potential limitation. As with all observational research, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, exposure misclassification (e.g., incomplete capture of vaccination timing or dose) and differences in healthcare utilization may influence observed associations.

This study used the NICE methodology checklist to assess risk of bias; a tool widely applied to observational research and suitable for evaluating the design features most relevant to vaccine safety studies. The direction of findings in excluded high-risk studies was broadly consistent with those included, suggesting their exclusion is unlikely to have materially influenced overall conclusions. While some commentary has questioned the fit of existing tools to vaccine effectiveness studies, these concerns are less applicable to pregnancy safety studies [55]. Nevertheless, certain challenges, such as heterogenous outcome definitions and residual confounding, are not fully captured by any single tool, therefore the NICE framework provided a transparent and structured approach for appraisal while acknowledging these inherent limitations [56]. Although the evidence base is now substantially larger than in early reviews, continued accumulation of data on variant adapted COVID-19 formulae and longer-term infant outcomes will be important for future assessments.

Public Health and Clinical Implications

The findings of this review have clear implications for clinical practice and public health communication. The consistent absence of increased risk across maternal, pregnancy-related, fetal, and neonatal outcomes supports recommendations for BNT162b2 vaccination during pregnancy, particularly given the substantially higher rates of severe COVID-19, intensive-care admission, and obstetric complications observed in unvaccinated pregnant women [6, 7]. These data reinforce that vaccination provides meaningful maternal protection without introducing detectable safety concerns for the pregnancy or infant.

Improving vaccine uptake during pregnancy remains a priority given persistent inequalities and misinformation, as highlighted in prior work [48, 57]. The evidence synthesized here can inform antenatal care pathways, enabling clinicians to provide clear, risk-balanced guidance and address common misconceptions. At a population level, high vaccination coverage in pregnancy is likely to reduce maternal morbidity and protect infants in the early postnatal period.

Overall, this synthesis provides a robust foundation for public health messaging and decision-making, supporting prioritization of COVID-19 vaccination in pregnancy as an effective and safe strategy to reduce preventable maternal and neonatal harms.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesized global evidence on safety of BNT162b2 COVID-19 vaccine during pregnancy. Across more than 450,000 exposures during pregnancy, no increased risk was observed for maternal, pregnancy-related, fetal, or neonatal outcomes, and findings were consistent across trimesters, dose numbers, vaccine formulae, and healthcare settings. These results align with international registry, surveillance and population-based evidence, and prior systematic reviews [43, 45, 54].

Given the substantial risks associated with SARS-CoV-2 infections in pregnancy, including increased maternal morbidity and adverse obstetric events these findings support recommendations for COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy as a safe and effective strategy to reduce preventable maternal and neonatal harms [6, 7]. Ongoing surveillance and harmonized data collection will be essential as variant-adapted formulae continue to be introduced, but the evidence to date provides a strong and consistent safety profile to support clinical counseling and public health messaging.

Future Research

Despite the substantial expansion of evidence since vaccine rollout, several areas warrant further investigation. Continued surveillance of variant-adapted BNT162b2 formulae is essential, particularly as updated vaccines are introduced in response to evolving SARS-CoV-2 strains. Larger datasets with consistent, trimester-specific exposure timing, especially for first-trimester vaccination, will strengthen assessment of early developmental outcomes, where data remain comparatively limited.

Future studies should prioritize harmonized outcome definitions, standardized gestational thresholds, and clearer reporting of exposure windows to improve comparability across jurisdictions. Linkage of maternal and infant health records will be important for capturing less common neonatal outcomes and for enabling follow-up into early childhood. Enhanced analytic approaches that address residual confounding, including high-resolution adjustment for baseline risk and infection status, will further refine interpretation of observational data. These efforts will ensure that safety assessments remain responsive to emerging variant adapted vaccines.
