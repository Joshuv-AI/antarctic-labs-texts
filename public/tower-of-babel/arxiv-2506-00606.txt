Heisenberg-limited Hamiltonian learning continuous variable systems via engineered dissipation
Tim Möbus, Andreas Bluhm, Tuvia Gefen, Yu Tong, Albert H. Werner, Cambyse Rouzé

Abstract
Discrete and continuous variables oftentimes require different treatments in many learning tasks. Identifying the Hamiltonian governing the evolution of a quantum system is a fundamental task in quantum learning theory. While previous works mostly focused on quantum spin systems, where quantum states can be seen as superpositions of discrete bit-strings, relatively little is known about Hamiltonian learning for continuous-variable quantum systems. In this work we focus on learning the Hamiltonian of a bosonic quantum system, a common type of continuous-variable quantum system. This learning task involves an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space and unbounded operators, making mathematically rigorous treatments challenging. We introduce an analytic framework to study the effects of strong dissipation in such systems, enabling a rigorous analysis of cat qubit stabilization via engineered dissipation. This framework also supports the development of Heisenberg-limited algorithms for learning general bosonic Hamiltonians with higher-order terms of the creation and annihilation operators. Notably, our scheme requires a total Hamiltonian evolution time that scales only logarithmically with the number of modes and inversely with the precision of the reconstructed coefficients. On a theoretical level, we derive a new quantitative adiabatic approximation estimate for general Lindbladian evolutions with unbounded generators. Finally, we discuss possible experimental implementations.

2
Main results

The main contribution of this article is to show that there exist Heisenberg-limited algorithms to learn bosonic Hamiltonians of bounded degree, with the help of engineered dissipation.

Setting

Bosonic systems with
\mathfrak{k}
bosonic modes are described in terms of creation and annihilation operators
b^{\dagger}_{i}
and
b_{i}
, one for each mode.
A bosonic mode
i
is in a state
\ket{k}_{i}
, indexed by a non-negative integer
k
, or their superposition. The annihilation operator
b_{i}
annihilates an excitation on the
i
th mode by
b_{i}\ket{k}_{i}=\sqrt{k}\ket{k-1}_{i}
, while the creation operator
b_{i}^{\dagger}
has the opposite effect, with
b_{i}^{\dagger}\ket{k}_{i}=\sqrt{k+1}\ket{k+1}_{i}
.
We start by defining the considered Hamiltonian as follows:

Definition 2.1

(Low-intersection bosonic Hamiltonian)
.

A low-intersection bosonic Hamiltonian acting on
m
modes is a Hamiltonian that takes the following form:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{a=1}^{M}E_{a}\,,

(1)

where, labeling the sites in
\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})
as
1,\ldots,\mathfrak{k}
, each
E_{a}
is an
\mathfrak{k}
-mode interaction of the form

\displaystyle E_{a}=\sum_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathfrak{k}}\,:\,\|\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{j}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq d}h^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{b}^{\dagger})^{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}\,.

We assume that at least one of
\mathbf{j}\neq 0
or
\mathbf{j}^{\prime}\neq 0
holds. More generally, given a set
C\subseteq[m]
, we denote by
H_{C}
the sum of all interactions supported in
C
. Here, we use the multi-index notation
\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{j}}=\prod_{\ell\in\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})}b_{\ell}^{j_{\ell}}
. Additionally, we assume that
|h^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}|\leq 1
. Since
E_{a}
is self-adjoint, we have
\overline{h^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}}=h^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j}^{\prime},\mathbf{j}}
. We assume that each
E_{a}
acts on at most
\mathfrak{k}=\mathcal{O}(1)
modes and that each
E_{a}
overlaps with at most
\mathfrak{d}=\mathcal{O}(1)
other interactions
E_{b}
.

Our learning protocol generates estimates
\hat{h}^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}
such that

\max_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}|\hat{h}^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}-{h}^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}|\leq\epsilon\text{ with probability at least }1-\delta.

(2)

Heisenberg-limited learning of low-intersection bosonic Hamiltonians

Our main result is that a Hamiltonian as in Definition
2.1
can be learned by a Heisenberg-limited algorithm. Our core insight is that by adding sufficiently strong engineered dissipation, we can restrict the time evolution to a subspace of our choosing, which allows us to extract the coefficients of
H
from the time evolution under an effective Hamiltonian. The following is an informal version of Theorem
B.4
:

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists an algorithm which makes use of dissipation with strength
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(m^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\log^{2d+1/2}(1/\epsilon))
as introduced below, that can estimate all coefficients of
H
to precision
\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
. It requires

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}((1/\epsilon)\log(m/\delta))

total evolution time, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(\log(1/\epsilon)/\epsilon)\log(m/\delta))

experiments.

Thus, the learning results in this article can be seen as combining the best aspects of previous works like
[
18
]
and
[
24
]
: While
[
18
]
gives learning algorithms for any bosonic Hamiltonian of bounded degree using engineered dissipation, it does not attain Heisenberg scaling, whereas
[
24
]
gives learning algorithms with Heisenberg scaling, but is limited to Hamiltonians of Bose-Hubbard type.

The generator
\mathcal{L}
of the dissipation consists of single-mode terms
\mathcal{L}_{i}
. On each mode
i
, for
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
we consider a dissipation in the Lindbladian form
\mathcal{L}_{i}:=\mathcal{L}[L_{1,\alpha_{i}}]+\mathcal{L}[L_{r_{i},\alpha_{i}}]
, where
\mathcal{L}[L]=L\cdot L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\cdot\}
and

L_{r,\alpha}=b^{r}(b-\alpha)

are the jump operators of the Lindblad operators
\mathcal{L}[L_{r,\alpha}]
. Here,
r_{i}
has to be chosen sufficiently large as a function of
d
. We then run a time evolution defined by

\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=-i[H,\rho(t)]+\gamma\mathcal{L}(\rho(t)),\qquad\rho(0)=\rho_{0}\,.

(3)

Above, we used the standard notations for the (formal) commutator
[A,B]=AB-BA
and anticommutator
\{A,B\}=AB+BA
. Motivated by the bosonic cat codes
[
61
,
62
,
42
]
, which are based on the jump operators
L=b^{r}-\alpha^{r}
defining higher-order photon losses, we use a modified photon dissipation with the jump operators
L_{r,\alpha}
. In the case of
r=2
, the photon dissipation of the bosonic cat code was experimentally realized, for example, in
[
49
]
. Following the construction of the photon dissipation, we discuss a possible implementation of the modified photon dissipation in Section
5
. As in
[
18
]
, one can use Trotterization to alternate between Hamiltonian evolution and dissipation for short times.

Adiabatic approximation for general Lindbladian evolutions

To prove soundness of our learning scheme, our main technical contribution is to provide precise and explicit bounds on the convergence of the engineered dissipation to the time evolution generated by the effective Hamiltonian. For that, we derive a new quantitative adiabatic approximation estimate for general Lindbladian evolutions with unbounded generators. As we will show at the end of this section, it has applications beyond the setting of Hamiltonian learning and is therefore of independent interest. The following theorem is a simplified version of Theorem
A.1
.

Theorem 2.3

(Informal)
.

Let
H
be a self-adjoint operator defined on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
, and let
\mathcal{L}
be a Lindbladian over the state space on
\mathscr{H}
. Let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto the intersection of the finite-dimensional kernels of the jump operators defining
\mathcal{L}
. Then, under some reasonable technical conditions on
H
and
\mathcal{L}
, for any state
\rho=P\rho P
and
\gamma>0
,

\left\|e^{t(\gamma\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{H})}(\rho)-e^{t\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{proj}}}(\rho)\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{tC}{\gamma}+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\gamma}\,,

where
C
and
C^{\prime}
are constants. Here,
\mathcal{H}=-i[H,\cdot]
and
\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{proj}}
is an effective version of
\mathcal{H}
restricted to the image of
P
.

The theorem states that if the dissipation strength
\gamma
is large enough, the time evolution generated by the Hamiltonian
H
is effectively restricted to the invariant subspace of the dissipative evolution generated by
\mathcal{L}
.

Application to cat codes

Another interesting and valuable application of our adiabatic Theorem
2.3
is in the context of bosonic cat codes.
This class of continuous-time error correction codes relies on the use of
r
-photon driven dissipation, which takes the form

\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}[L_{r}](\rho(t))\qquad\text{with}\qquad L_{r}\coloneqq b^{r}-\alpha^{r}\,.

One fundamental property of the above dissipation is that for large times, the evolution drives any state exponentially fast to the finite-dimensional code space

\mathcal{C}_{r}(\alpha)\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\left\{\ket{\alpha_{1}}\bra{\alpha_{2}}:\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\left\{\alpha e^{\frac{i2\pi j}{r}}:j\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}\right\}\right\}

in the following sense
[
40
]
:

\mathrm{tr}[L_{r}e^{t\mathcal{L}[L_{r}]}(\rho)L_{r}^{\dagger}]\leq e^{-{tr}!}\mathrm{tr}[L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}]\,.

We do not only extend the above weighted convergence rate to the convergence of the quantum dissipative evolution in the natural trace norm (see Proposition
A.2
), but we also prove an adiabatic limit for bounded-degree Hamiltonians, which is stated informally below (see Proposition
A.3
):

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
H
be as in (
1
) for
m=1
,
d/2\leq r
, and let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{C}_{r}(\alpha)
. Then, for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho\in\mathcal{C}_{r}(\alpha)
,

\|e^{-it[H,\cdot]+t\gamma\mathcal{L}[b^{r}-\alpha^{r}]}(\rho)-e^{-it[PHP,P\cdot P]}(\rho)\|_{1}\leq\frac{tC}{\gamma}+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\gamma}\,

for constants
C,C^{\prime}\geq 0
.

Full details of our results on cat codes can be found in Appendix
A.2
. Thus, we have demonstrated that the
r
-photon driven dissipation of sufficient strength leads to an effective time evolution on the code space for any bounded-degree bosonic Hamiltonian. For
r=2
, this specifically provides explicit convergence bounds for implementing rotations around the
x
-axis on the code space, which has been experimentally realized in
[
49
]
.

3
Adiabatic evolutions

Quantum dynamical time-evolutions are usually characterized by the initial value problem

\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}(\rho(t))\,,\qquad\rho(0)=\rho_{0}

(4)

given by a possibly unbounded operator
\mathcal{L}
defined on a subspace (its domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{L})
) of trace-class operators
T_{1}(\mathscr{H})
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
(see
[
63
, Sec. 3.6]
for more details). Note that the trace-class operators are equipped with the trace norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, the Hilbert space with the induced norm
\|\cdot\|
, and the bounded operators
\mathcal{B}(\mathscr{H})
with the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. Due to the quantum mechanical nature of the generated evolution, it is assumed that the initial value is a quantum state, i.e., a positive semidefinite operator
\rho_{0}\geq 0
with unit trace
\mathrm{tr}[\rho_{0}]=1
.

Assume that the initial value problem (
4
) admits a unique, trace-preserving, and completely positive solution, called a quantum dynamical semigroup, for which we write
\rho(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}
(see
[
64
, Sec. 3.3]
for more details). Motivated by the GKSL-representation theorem
[
65
,
66
]
, we consider unbounded generators — Lindbladians — of the form

\mathcal{L}(X):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}L_{i}XL_{i}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\big\{L_{i}^{\dagger}L_{i},X\big\}

(5)

defined by a finite set of jump operators
\{L_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, i.e., for some closed, densely defined linear operators
(L_{i},\operatorname{dom}(L_{i}))
on
\mathscr{H}
. Note that
L_{i}^{\dagger}
denotes the adjoint operator of
L_{i}
(see
[
63
, Ch. 7.1]
).

The two main problems we need to overcome to prove Theorem
2.3
are, first, that we are dealing with unbounded generators, meaning that in every step where we use tools like Duhamel’s formula, operator norm bounds are cannot be used—for example, to approximate the difference to the effective time evolution. Second, we are dealing with Lindbladians, which describe non-reversible quantum evolutions. In addition to having no quantum mechanical inverse, they do not have a mathematical inverse time representation either due to the unboundedness of the generator. The reasonable technical conditions mentioned in Theorem
2.3
guarantee that we can still control the generators even though they are unbounded. Concretely, we have to impose that

1.

the jump operators
L_{i}
defining
\mathcal{L}
have an energy gap,

2.

H
is relatively bounded with respect to the jumps
L_{i}
,

3.

different products of jump operators and the Hamiltonian are bounded on the image of
P
.

To use the adiabatic approximation estimate in Theorem
2.3
, we thus need to verify that these technical conditions are met. This way, we obtain an explicit dependence of
C
and
C^{\prime}
on the degree of the Hamiltonian
d
and the parameter
\alpha
in the dissipation. For the proof of Theorem
2.3
and more applications, we refer the reader to Appendix
A
.

We will illustrate this for the single-mode setting, i.e., where the Hamiltonian is as in (
1
) with
m=1
. The dissipation is defined by
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}[L_{1,\alpha}]+\mathcal{L}[L_{r,\alpha}]
. It is easy to see in this case that
P
is the projection onto the kernel of
L_{1,\alpha}
, since the latter is included into that of
L_{r,\alpha}
. Then, we prove the following adiabatic theorem:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
H
be as defined as in (
1
) with
m=1
,
r=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil-1
,
\alpha\in\mathbbm{C}
, and let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathrm{span}(\ket{0},\ket{\alpha})
. Then, for all
t\geq 0
and
\rho=P\rho P
,

\|e^{t\mathcal{H}+t\gamma\mathcal{L}[b^{r}(b-\alpha)]+t\gamma\mathcal{L}[b(b-\alpha)]}(\rho)-e^{-it[PHP,P\cdot P]}(\rho)\|_{1}\leq\frac{tC}{\gamma}+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\gamma}\,

for constants
C=C_{d}(|\alpha|^{4d+2}+1)
and
C^{\prime}=C_{d}^{\prime}(|\alpha|^{d}+1)
for
C_{d}
and
C_{d}^{\prime}
depending on
d
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
A.3.1
. It consists of verifying the assumptions needed to apply Theorem
2.3
.

4
Learning a single-mode Hamiltonian

In this section we will describe our Hamiltonian learning protocol in the single-mode case, i.e.,
m=1
. At the end of this section, we will sketch how to obtain Theorem
2.2
from this.

We consider learning a single mode Hamiltonian, which is the Hamiltonian (
1
) for
m=1
:

H=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{d-j}h_{j,j^{\prime}}(b^{{\dagger}})^{j}b^{j^{\prime}}.

(6)

To remove the global phase we let
h_{0,0}=0
. We also assume that
|h_{j,j^{\prime}}|\leq 1
. Because
H
is self-adjoint, we have
\overline{h}_{j,j^{\prime}}=h_{j^{\prime},j}
. Our learning protocol will generate estimates
\hat{h}_{j,j^{\prime}}
such that

\max_{j,j^{\prime}}|\hat{h}_{j,j^{\prime}}-h_{j,j^{\prime}}|\leq\epsilon\text{ with probability at least }1-\delta.

(7)

A pseudocode for the protocol is provided in Algorithm
1
in the appendix and an illustration of it is given in Fig.
1(a)
.

4.1
The effective Hamiltonian

We apply strong dissipation with jump operators
L_{1,\alpha}=b(b-\alpha)
and
L_{r,\alpha}=b^{r}(b-\alpha)
where
r=\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil-1
to constrain the dynamics to the subspace spanned by
\ket{0}
and
\ket{\alpha}
as well as to regularize it. Here,
\ket{\alpha}
is the coherent state defined by
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
as
e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{n\geq 0}\alpha^{n}/\sqrt{n!}\ket{n}
. In particular, it holds that
b\ket{\alpha}=\alpha\ket{\alpha}
. The effective Hamiltonian is then

H^{\mathrm{proj}}=\Pi H\Pi,

(8)

where
\Pi=\ket{\Psi_{0}}\bra{\Psi_{0}}+\ket{\Psi_{1}}\bra{\Psi_{1}},

with

\ket{\Psi_{0}}=\ket{0},\quad\ket{\Psi_{1}}=\frac{\ket{\alpha}-\ket{0}\braket{0|\alpha}}{\|\ket{\alpha}-\ket{0}\braket{0|\alpha}\|}.

(9)

In the above
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
and
\ket{\Psi_{1}}
are obtained by the Gram-Schmidt process, and they form an orthonormal basis for the subspace. One can compute that
\braket{0|\alpha}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}
, and
\braket{\alpha|\Psi_{1}}=\sqrt{1-e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}}
.
From this we can see that
\ket{\Psi_{1}}
is in fact very close to
\ket{\alpha}
if
|\alpha|
is large:

\|\ket{\Psi_{1}}-\ket{\alpha}\|=\mathcal{O}(e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}).

(10)

Relative to the basis
\ket{\Psi_{0}}
and
\ket{\Psi_{1}}
, the effective Hamiltonian is represented by

\begin{pmatrix}\braket{\Psi_{0}|H|\Psi_{0}}&\braket{\Psi_{0}|H|\Psi_{1}}\\
\braket{\Psi_{1}|H|\Psi_{0}}&\braket{\Psi_{1}|H|\Psi_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\approx\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}\end{pmatrix}.

Direct calculation shows that the entry-wise error in the above approximation is at most
\mathcal{O}(|\alpha|^{d}d^{2}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2})
.
Moreover, we have

\|\ket{\Psi_{1}}\bra{\Psi_{1}}-\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}\|\leq 2\|\ket{\alpha}-\ket{\Psi_{1}}\|.

Therefore we define

\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}=\ket{\alpha}\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}\bra{\alpha},

(11)

which is a good approximation of the effective Hamiltonian but is of a simpler form:

\|\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}-H^{\mathrm{proj}}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(|\alpha|^{d}d^{2}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}).

(12)

4.2
Experimental setup

We will run phase estimation experiments that are to be described in this section, to extract the eigenvalues of
H^{\mathrm{proj}}
, from which we will extract the coefficients
h_{j,j^{\prime}}
. For this, we need to introduce an ancilla qubit. In each experiment, we initialize the system in the state
\ket{+}\ket{0}
, apply controlled displacement
D(\alpha)
to obtain the state
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\ket{0}+\ket{1}\ket{\alpha})
, and then let the system evolve under the effective Hamiltonian. We then apply the controlled displacement
D(-\alpha)
, and measure the ancilla qubit in either the
X
or
Y
basis.
This protocol is illustrated with a quantum circuit in Fig.
1(b)
.
Mathematically, the procedure can be described as follows:

\displaystyle\ket{+}\ket{0}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{\operatorname{controlled}-D(\alpha)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}\ket{0}+\ket{1}\ket{\alpha})

(13)

\displaystyle\xmapsto{e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}\ket{0}+\ket{1}e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}\ket{\alpha})

\displaystyle\xmapsto{\operatorname{controlled}-D(-\alpha)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}\ket{0}+\ket{1}D(-\alpha)e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}\ket{\alpha}).

Measuring the ancilla qubit in the
X
and
Y
bases, we have

\braket{X\otimes I}=\operatorname{Re}\braket{0|e^{iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}D(-\alpha)e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}|\alpha},\quad\braket{Y\otimes I}=\operatorname{Im}\braket{0|e^{iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}D(-\alpha)e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}|\alpha}.

(14)

From (
10
) and (
12
), we have

\displaystyle\braket{0|e^{iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}D(-\alpha)e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}|\alpha}

\displaystyle=\braket{0|e^{i\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}t}D(-\alpha)e^{-i\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}t}|\alpha}+\mathcal{O}(|\alpha|^{d}d^{2}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}t).

(15)

Moreover

\displaystyle\braket{0|e^{i\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}t}D(-\alpha)e^{-i\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}t}|\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t}\braket{0|e^{i\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}t}D(-\alpha)|\alpha}

(16)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t}\braket{0|e^{i\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}t}|0}

4.3
Robust frequency estimation

In this section we introduce the robust frequency estimation protocol used in
[
24
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\theta\in[-\Phi,\Phi]
.
Let
X(t)
and
Y(t)
be independent random variables satisfying

\displaystyle|X(t)-\cos(\theta t)|<1/\sqrt{8},\text{ with probability at least }2/3,

\displaystyle|Y(t)-\sin(\theta t)|<1/\sqrt{8},\text{ with probability at least }2/3.

Then with independent samples
X(t_{1}),X(t_{2}),\cdots,X(t_{\Gamma})
and
Y(t_{1}),Y(t_{2}),\cdots,Y(t_{\Gamma})
, with

{\Gamma}=\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(\Phi/\epsilon)),\quad T=t_{1}+t_{2}+\cdots+t_{\Gamma}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon),\quad\max_{j}t_{j}=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon),

(19)

and
t_{j}\geq 0
, we can construct an estimator
\hat{\theta}
such that

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\theta}-\theta|^{2}]}\leq\epsilon.

(20)

The proof can be found in Appendix
B.1
. The proof is given by describing the algorithm to achieve the above scalings. The algorithm works by turning the estimation problem into a sequence of decision problems. Each decision problem refines our knowledge of the unknown parameter
\theta
, and can be correctly solved with high probability using a few rounds of majority voting. This results in an algorithm that is robust against noise and achieves the
1/\epsilon
total evolution time (i.e.,
T
) scaling.

4.4
Recovering the coefficients

In Section
4.2
we introduced an experiment that, for any
t>0
, allows us to obtain in expectation
\cos(\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t)
and
\sin(\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t)
by measuring in the
X
and
Y
bases respectively on an ancilla qubit (see (
18
)). We note that this is exactly what is needed in the robust frequency estimation procedure in Theorem
4.1
. Using the fact that

|\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{d-{j}}h_{j,j^{\prime}}|(\alpha^{*})^{j}\alpha^{j^{\prime}}|=\mathcal{O}(d^{2}|\alpha|^{d}),

we know from Theorem
4.1
that
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
can be estimated to precision
\epsilon_{1}
with total evolution time
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon_{1})
and
\mathcal{O}(\log^{{2}}(d^{2}|\alpha|^{{d}}/\epsilon_{1}))
samples.

With
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
we will be able to recover the coefficients
h_{j,j^{\prime}}
by carefully choosing
\alpha
. Letting
\alpha=Ae^{i\theta}
, we have

\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{d-{j}}h_{j,j^{\prime}}A^{j+j^{\prime}}e^{i(j^{\prime}-j)\theta}=\sum_{l=1}^{d}A^{l}e^{i\theta l}\sum_{j=0}^{l}h_{j,l-j}e^{-2ij\theta}.

(21)

We note that this is a polynomial of
A
and proceed to estimate the coefficients

h_{l}(\theta)=e^{i\theta l}\sum_{j=0}^{l}h_{j,l-j}e^{-2ij\theta}.

(22)

Fixing
\theta
, we first estimate
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
for
\alpha=Ae^{i\theta}
and
A=A_{\mu}
,
\mu=1,2,\cdots,d+1
,
where

A_{\mu}=\frac{A_{-}+A_{+}}{2}+\frac{A_{+}-A_{-}}{2}\cos\left(\frac{(2\mu-1)\pi}{2d+2}\right).

(23)

We choose
0<A_{-}<A_{+}
such that
A_{+}-A_{-}\geq 4A_{-}
.
For each
A_{\mu}
we estimate
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
to precision
\epsilon_{1}
.
We then perform Chebyshev interpolation on the interval
[A_{-},A_{+}]
using these data points to get a set of coefficients
\hat{h}_{l}
for
l=0,1,\cdots,d
.
From Corollary
D.3
in Appendix
D
, we know that

|h_{l}(\theta)-\hat{h}_{l}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{l}\epsilon_{1}}{(A_{+}-A_{-})^{l}}\right).

(24)

Further requiring
A_{+}-A_{-}=\Omega(1)
we have
|h_{l}(\theta)-\hat{h}_{l}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{1})
.

Having estimated all polynomial coefficients

, we then choose a set of
\theta
to recover the coefficients

are in fact the Fourier coefficients of

via a Fourier transform.

Choosing

(25)

and therefore

(26)

From this we can see that after we evaluate

to precision
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{1})

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{1})

In the above we need to estimate

for

From Proposition

From (

4.5
Extensions to few-mode and multi-mode Heisenberg-limited learning

The great generality of Theorem
2.3
allows us to prove that our single mode algorithm can be adapted to the few-mode and multi-mode setting. In both settings, we consider a low-intersection bosonic Hamiltonian as in Definition
2.1
. The difference between the few-mode and the multi-mode setting is that while the few-mode setting directly generalizes the single-mode setting, the multi-mode setting uses decoupling to reduce the global Hamiltonian to a sum of local few-mode Hamiltonians: First, we introduce a coloring that defines the choice of single-mode dissipation. To decouple a color from its neighboring color, we choose the dissipation
\mathcal{L}^{c}_{\mathrm{dec}}
on the interacting/ boundary modes such that it projects to the vacuum state. This directly erases all interactions in the adiabatic limit. For terms within a color, we act in exactly the same way as in the few-mode setting and use the same learning scheme. This guarantees that the total evolution time and the number of experiments scale only logarithmically with the system size, i.e., the number of modes
m
, and that dissipation strength is only required to scale quadratically in
m
. In the few-mode case, we consider
m=\mathcal{O}(1)
, for which Theorem
B.3
in Appendix
B.2
shows that the few-mode setting has essentially the same requirements as the single-mode setting. The few-mode setting can then be extended via decoupling to the multi-mode setting, which gives Theorem
2.2
. For the details, we refer to Appendix
B.3
.

5
Possible experimental realization schemes

The modified photon-dissipation master equation, i.e.
L_{r,\alpha}=b^{r}\left(b-\alpha\right)
, can be obtained using a scheme similar to the one used for engineering the cat code dissipation
[
61
,
62
,
42
]
.
Cat code dissipation,
L=b^{2}-\alpha^{2},
is obtained by introducing an ancillary mode, denoted as
a
, and engineering a dynamics of:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho\left(t\right)=-ig\left[a^{\dagger}\left(b^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right)+h.c.,\rho\right]+\kappa_{a}\mathcal{D}\left[a\right]\rho.

The probe mode is thus coupled to a strongly decaying ancilla through
H=ga^{\dagger}\left(b^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right)+h.c.

In the limit of
g\ll\kappa_{a}
we can adiabatically eliminate the ancilla, leading to an effective dynamics of
\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{s}=4\frac{g^{2}}{\kappa_{a}}\mathcal{D}\left[b^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right]\left(\rho_{s}\right)
of the probe’s density matrix
\rho_{s}
.
In our case, we similarly need to engineer a coupling of
H=ga^{\dagger}b^{r}\left(b-\alpha\right)+h.c.
between the probe and the ancilla. Using the same derivation as in
[
42
]
, we can adiabatically eliminate the ancilla in the limit of
g\ll\kappa_{a}
leading to
\mathcal{L}\left[b^{r}\left(b-\alpha\right)\right]
.
A coupling of the form of
H=ga^{\dagger}b^{r}\left(b-\alpha\right)+h.c.
can be engineered using transmons or asymmetrically threaded SQUID (ATS)
[
61
,
62
]
. Relevant Hamiltonian engineering schemes are discussed in Appendix
F
.

6
Outlook

In this work, we present the first efficient Hamiltonian learning scheme for a broad class of local Hamiltonians governing

m
-mode continuous variable systems, expressed as polynomials in the creation and annihilation operators. Our approach achieves a Heisenberg-limited scaling of

\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1}\log(m))
in the total Hamiltonian evolution time required to estimate each coefficient to precision

\epsilon
. A key technical contribution enabling this optimal scaling is a new adiabatic theorem for unbounded generators of quantum dynamical semigroups, which we anticipate will have broader applications. As a demonstration, we rigorously establish the emergence of effective finite-dimensional unitary dynamics in multi-photon driven dissipation processes, relevant to quantum computing with cat codes.

An important open question is the optimal strength
\gamma
required to confine the effective evolutions within the finite-dimensional subspace where the learning protocol is conducted. While our analysis establishes convergence from a quadratic dependence on the number of modes
m
, we anticipate that a more refined approach could yield a scaling independent of
m
. A natural first step in this direction would be to incorporate the physically relevant assumption that the unitary dynamics generated by
H
does not lead to an unbounded increase in the system’s energy, as measured by the total photon number.

Finally,
It would be interesting to further explore feasible experimental realizations, in particular finding experimental schemes for the required photon dissipation,
L_{r,\alpha},
for r>1. It would be also interesting to explore the robustness of this protocol to realistic noises.

A.1

In this appendix, we will prove our main technical tool, the adiabatic approximation for general Lindbladian evolutions, of which Theorem

for large

(see Section

(i)

L^{\prime}\geq\eta\,(I-P)

(ii)

such that, for any

(iii)

\iota^{\prime}_{1},\iota^{\prime}_{2}\geq 0

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\Big\|K_{j}\big(e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}X_{t-s}\big)K_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\big\{K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j},e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}X_{t-s}\big\}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\Big\|K_{j}\big(e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}X_{t-s}\big)K_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\big\{K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j},e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}X_{t-s}\big\}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\Big\|K_{j}\big(X_{t-s}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}\big)K_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\big\{K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j},X_{t-s}^{\dagger}e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}\big\}\Big\|_{1}

where we also used Assumptions (ii) and (iv) above. Similarly, each of the summands in the above two sums can be controlled as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\Big\|K_{j}\big(e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}X_{t-s}\big)K_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\big\{K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j},e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}X_{t-s}\big\}\Big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\,\leq\sum_{j}\left(\|K_{j}P\|_{\infty}\Big\|K_{j}e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}B\Big\|_{\infty}+\Big\|K_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j}e^{-\frac{\gamma u}{2}L^{\prime}}B\Big\|_{\infty}\!\!\!\!+(C_{H}+|\mathcal{J}|C_{K})e^{-\frac{\gamma\eta u}{2}}\|PK_{j}^{\dagger}K_{j}\|_{\infty}\right)

where we used Assumptions (iii) and (iv) again. Combining the above bounds and using that

, we get

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{K})e^{-\frac{u\gamma}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(X_{t-s}+X^{\dagger}_{t-s})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\frac{\gamma\eta u}{2}}\Bigl(\iota_{1}C_{L}+(\iota_{2}+C_{H}+|\mathcal{J}|C_{K})(C_{H}+|\mathcal{J}|C_{K})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\big(C_{K}+1\big)\Big(\iota_{1}^{\prime}C_{L}\!\!+(C_{H}+|\mathcal{J}|C_{K})\iota_{2}^{\prime}\Big)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\eqcolon\frac{\eta C}{2}e^{-\frac{\gamma\eta u}{2}}\,.

We can now integrate over

and

in (

) to get

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\,e^{(s-u)(\gamma\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{K})}(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{K})e^{-\frac{u\gamma}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(X_{t-s}+X^{\dagger}_{t-s})\,duds\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}\,\|(\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{K})e^{-\frac{u\gamma}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(X_{t-s}+X^{\dagger}_{t-s})\|_{1}\,duds

A.2
Bosonic cat code

Next, we briefly discuss the bosonic cat code as introduced in
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
and others. For that, we define the annihilation operator
b
and the creation operator
b^{\dagger}
on the separable Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}\coloneqq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of square-integrable functions with orthonormal basis denoted by
\{\ket{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots\}
, known as the Fock basis. The annihilation and creation operators are characterized by

b\ket{n}=\sqrt{n}\ket{n-1},\qquad b^{\dagger}\ket{n}=\sqrt{n+1}\ket{n+1}\,.

In particular, they obey the canonical commutation relation:

[b,b^{\dagger}]=I.

(35)

Furthermore, we define the number operator as
N\coloneqq b^{\dagger}b
and refer for more details to
[
68
, Sec. 12]
. The bosonic cat code is constructed on these bosonic systems and is introduced in the following discussion. The cat code relies on
r
-photon driven dissipation, defined by the master equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\rho(t)=\mathcal{L}[L_{r}](\rho(t))\qquad\text{with}\qquad L_{r}\coloneqq b^{r}-\alpha^{r},

(36)

with
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{L}[L](\rho)=L\rho L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,\rho\}
. Motivated by a driven damped harmonic oscillator, the
r
-photon driven dissipation is theoretically constructed using a harmonic oscillator with multi-photon drive and dissipation. A more detailed description can be found in Section
5
and Appendix
F
.
For
r=2
, a detailed construction of the above dynamics can be found in
[
42
, Sec. 3]
. These master equations admit unique solution operators, as established in
[
40
,
69
]
. One of the major properties of the
r
-photon driven dissipation is that it admits the following invariant subspace — the code space:

\mathcal{C}_{r}(\alpha)\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\left\{\ket{\alpha_{1}}\bra{\alpha_{2}}:\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\left\{\alpha e^{\frac{i2\pi j}{r}}:j\in\{0,\ldots,r-1\}\right\}\right\}.

(37)

The property that the
r
-photon dissipation rapidly converges to the code space is fundamental for continuous-time error correction codes. However, this was proven only in the following sense
[
40
]
:

\mathrm{tr}\big[L_{r}e^{t\mathcal{L}[L_{r}]}(\rho)L_{r}^{\dagger}\big]\leq e^{-tr!}\mathrm{tr}[L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}]\,.

Using a similar approach to Theorem
A.1
, we prove the following convergence in trace norm:

Proposition A.2
.

Let
r\geq 0
,
L_{r}
be the jump operator defining the
r
-photon dissipation
\mathcal{L}[L_{r}]
(
36
) and let
P
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{C}_{r}(\alpha)
(
37
). Then, for all
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\|(e^{t\mathcal{L}[L_{r}]}-\mathcal{P})(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!}\Bigl(\frac{2}{r!}\|L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}\|_{1}+\|\rho\|_{1}\Bigr),

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathrm{dom}(L\cdot L^{\dagger}),

\displaystyle\leq 6e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!}\Bigl((1+\frac{|\alpha|^{r}}{\sqrt{r!}})^{2}\|\widetilde{N}^{r/2}\rho\widetilde{N}^{r/2}\|_{1}+\|\rho\|_{1}\Bigr),

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathrm{dom}(\widetilde{N}^{r/2}\cdot\widetilde{N}^{r/2}),

where
\widetilde{N}=N+I
and
\mathcal{P}=P\cdot P
.

Proof.

As stated in the result, we assume that
\rho\in\mathrm{dom}(L\cdot L^{\dagger})
or
\rho\in\mathrm{dom}(\widetilde{N}^{r/2}\cdot\widetilde{N}^{r/2})
. By
[
67
, Prop. 3.18 and 6.14]
, the operator
-L^{\prime}\coloneqq(I-P)L_{r}^{\dagger}L_{r}(I-P)
generates a contractive semigroup on
\mathscr{H}
, so that

\displaystyle\|(e^{t\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{P})(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{t\mathcal{L}}(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|(I-\mathcal{P})\circ(e^{t\mathcal{L}}-e^{-\frac{t}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}})(\rho)\|_{1}+\|(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{-\frac{t}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq t\bigl\|\int_{0}^{1}(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{-\tau\frac{t}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}\Bigl(L_{r}(e^{(1-\tau)t\mathcal{L}}(\rho))L_{r}^{\dagger}\Bigr)d\tau\bigr\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\|(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{-\frac{t}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq t\int_{0}^{1}\|(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{-\tau\frac{t}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}\|L_{r}(e^{(1-\tau)t\mathcal{L}}(\rho))L_{r}^{\dagger}\|_{1}d\tau

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\|(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{-\frac{t}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}(\rho)\|_{1}\,,

where we used that
\mathcal{L}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}=L_{r}\cdot L^{\dagger}_{r}
. In the next step, we use the following two inequalities: First, for every
X\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{P})\circ e^{-\frac{s}{2}\{L^{\prime},\cdot\}}(X)\|_{1\rightarrow 1}

\displaystyle=\|Pe^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}Xe^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}(I-P)+(I-P)e^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}Xe^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}P+(I-P)e^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}Xe^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}(I-P)\|_{1\rightarrow 1}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigl(\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}(I-P)\|_{\infty}+\|(I-P)e^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}+\|e^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}(I-P)\|_{\infty}\|(I-P)e^{-\frac{s}{2}L^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\Bigr)\|X\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-\frac{s}{2}\eta}\,,

where we used assumption
(i)
, which is satisfied due to Lemma
E.5
combined with Lemma
E.11
with
L^{\prime}\geq\eta(I-P)
and
\eta\geq r!
. Second, we use the bound given in
[
40
]
, which is

\mathrm{tr}\big[L_{r}e^{t\mathcal{L}[L_{r}]}(\rho)L_{r}^{\dagger}\big]\leq e^{-tr!}\mathrm{tr}[L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}]\,.

Together these two bounds show

\displaystyle\|(e^{t\mathcal{L}}-\mathcal{P})(\rho)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 3t\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\frac{\tau t}{2}r!}e^{-(1-\tau)tr!}d\tau\,\|L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}\|_{1}d\tau+3e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!}\|\rho\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!}\Bigl(t\int_{0}^{1}e^{-(1-\tau)t\frac{r!}{2}}d\tau\|L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}\|_{1}+\|\rho\|_{1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!}\Bigl(\frac{2}{r!}(1-e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!})\|L_{r}\rho L_{r}^{\dagger}\|_{1}+\|\rho\|_{1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq 3e^{-\frac{t}{2}r!}\Bigl(\frac{2}{r!}(\sqrt{r!}+|\alpha|^{r})^{2}\|\widetilde{N}^{r/2}\rho\widetilde{N}^{r/2}\|_{1}+\|\rho\|_{1}\Bigr).

In the last line, we used Hölder’s inequality combined with the relative boundedness

\|\widetilde{N}^{-r/2}L_{r}\|_{\infty}=\|L_{r}^{\dagger}\widetilde{N}^{-r/2}\|_{\infty}\leq(1+|\alpha|^{r})

which is proven by following the strategy in
[
69
, Lemma B.1]
, i.e.

\displaystyle\|b^{r\dagger}\widetilde{N}^{-r/2}\ket{\varphi}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bra{\varphi}(N+I)^{-r/2}b^{r}b^{r\dagger}(N+I)^{-r/2}\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\bra{\varphi}(N+I)^{-r}(N+I)\cdots(N+r)\ket{\varphi}

\displaystyle\leq r!\|\varphi\|^{2}

so that
\|L_{r}^{\dagger}\widetilde{N}^{-r/2}\|_{\infty}\leq\|b^{r\dagger}\widetilde{N}^{-r/2}\|_{\infty}+|\alpha|^{r}\leq\sqrt{r!}+|\alpha|^{r}
, which completes the proof.
∎

A.3
Modified photon-dissipation and decoupling

In the following appendix, we consider a modified family of photon dissipation processes. Here, the invariant subspace is adjusted in such a way that the effective dynamics of a Hamiltonian still retain all the information needed to characterize the original Hamiltonian. The method for extracting this information is presented in Section
4
and Appendix

\mathcal{L}[L_{r,\alpha}](\cdot)=L_{r,\alpha}\cdot L_{r,\alpha}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{r,\alpha}^{\dagger}L_{r,\alpha},\cdot\}\qquad\text{with}\qquad L_{r,\alpha}=b^{r}(b-\alpha),

(41)

for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and

. The above single-mode case naturally extends to the multi-mode case by summing over all modes. However, we first consider the single-mode case.

A.3.1
Single-mode dynamics

In this appendix, we prove Proposition
3.1
, restated as Proposition
A.4
below for convenience. In what follows, we make the notation less cumbersome by writing

Proposition A.4
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
H
be as defined in (

. For

A.3.3
Multi-mode decoupling

In this appendix, we adapt the result in Proposition
A.5
such that the limit is a product of decoupled, local, Hamiltonian evolutions. Again, we consider Hamiltonian of the form introduced in Definition
2.1
given by

\displaystyle H=\sum_{a=1}^{M}E_{a}\qquad\text{with}\qquad E_{a}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathfrak{k}}\\
\|\mathbf{j}+\mathbf{j}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq d\end{subarray}}h^{(a)}_{\mathbf{j},\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{b}^{\dagger})^{\mathbf{j}}\,\mathbf{b}^{\mathbf{j}^{\prime}}\,,

To describe the interaction pattern of the above Hamiltonian, we define the interacting cluster in the following:

Definition A.6

(Interacting cluster)
.

For each
a
, let
\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})
be the support of
E_{a}
, that is, the collection of modes in which
E_{a}
acts non-trivially. From the set
\{\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})\}
, we remove all
\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})
such that
\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})\subset\operatorname{supp}(E_{b})
for some

. The remaining
\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})

\mathcal{V}
. Each element of
\mathcal{V}
we call an interacting cluster. Clearly
|\mathcal{V}|\leq M
.

Definition A.7

(Cluster interaction graph)
.

The cluster interaction graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
has interacting clusters as its vertices. The set of edges
\mathcal{E}
is defined as follows: for each pair of interacting clusters
C
and
C^{\prime}
(
C\neq C^{\prime}
) in
\mathcal{V}
,

if
C\cap C^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
or if there exists
C^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
such that
C\cap C^{\prime\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
C^{\prime}\cap C^{\prime\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

For a low-intersecting Hamiltonian, the degree of
\mathcal{G}
,
\operatorname{deg}(\mathcal{G})
, is upper bounded by a constant that is independent of the system size
m
. More precisely,
\operatorname{deg}(\mathcal{G})\leq\mathfrak{d}^{2}
. In order to optimize the sample complexity, we aim at estimating different interacting clusters in parallel. For this, we need to color the graph
\mathcal{G}
so that adjacent vertices have different colors. The number of colors

, known as chromatic number of the graph, satisfies

. Hence,
\mathcal{V}
can be divided into a disjoint union

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\bigsqcup_{c=1}^{\chi}\mathcal{V}_{c}\,,

(50)

where no two adjacent vertices are in the same

C
and

in

, and for any

C\cap C^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
or
C^{\prime}\cap C^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
. Next, given a color

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{c}=\bigsqcup_{C\in\mathcal{V}_{c}}C\,.

Next, we fix a color

and we consider two dissipative generators, one supported on

for decoupling interaction clusters of color

from other modes,

, and the other supported on
\mathcal{A}_{c}
which serves as a means to locally project to an effective finite dimensional Hamiltonian on each cluster,

. More precisely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{c}_{\operatorname{dec}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\ell\in[m]\backslash\mathcal{A}_{c}}\mathcal{L}_{\ell}

(51)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{c}_{\operatorname{proj}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{V}_{c}}\mathcal{L}^{c}_{C}

where each evolution

and

with

for all

\ell\in\operatorname{supp}(E_{a})
. On the other site

acts on modes in cluster

and has Lindblad operators

described in Appendix

46

d\in\mathbb{N}
,
H

2.1

\mathcal{L}
the sum over single-mode modified
r

51

T=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}t_{l}.

(59)

The above is the objective that we want to minimize. The constraint is imposed by the precision that we want to achieve, which we compute below.

With probability

(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})\cdots(1-\delta_{l-1})\delta_{l},

the
l
-th iteration is the first one to fail. The resulting error is no larger than
2\Phi(2/3)^{l-2}
. Therefore the mean squared error (MSE) is at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\theta}-\theta|^{2}]

(60)

\displaystyle=4\Phi^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})\cdots(1-\delta_{l-1})\delta_{l}(4/9)^{l-2}+(1-\delta_{1})(1-\delta_{2})\cdots(1-\delta_{L})(\epsilon/3)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\Phi^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\delta_{l}(4/9)^{l-2}+\epsilon^{2}/9.

Now we choose
\delta_{l}
so that

\delta_{l}(4/9)^{l-2}\leq\frac{\epsilon^{2}/9}{2^{L-l}\Phi^{2}},

which ensures
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\theta}-\theta|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
.

We therefore only need to choose

\delta_{l}\leq\left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{2}{9}\right)^{L-l}\leq(9/4)^{l-2}\frac{\epsilon^{2}/9}{2^{L-l}\Phi^{2}},

where we have used
\epsilon/(3\Phi)\geq(2/3)^{L}
.
For the above choice of
\delta_{l}
, it suffices to choose
m_{l}=c_{3}+c_{4}(L-l)
for some universal constants
c_{3},c_{4}
.
Therefore we can compute

{\Gamma}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}(c_{3}+c_{4}(L-l))\leq\mathcal{O}(L^{2})=\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}(3\Phi/\epsilon)),

(61)

and

T=\sum_{l=1}^{L}m_{l}t_{l}=\frac{\pi}{2\Phi}\sum_{l=1}^{L}(c_{3}+c_{4}(L-l))\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{l-1}=\mathcal{O}((3/2)^{L}/\Phi)=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon),

(62)

where we have used the elementary equality that

\sum_{l=1}^{L}x^{l-1}(L-l)=\frac{x^{L}-1}{(x-1)^{2}}-\frac{L}{x-1}.

∎

B.2
Learning a few-mode Hamiltonian

The protocol for learning a Hamiltonian on
\mathfrak{k}
bosonic modes for
\mathfrak{k}=\mathcal{O}(1)
follows largely the same idea as the single-mode case. The Hamiltonian takes the form

H=\sum_{l_{1}=0}^{d_{1}}\cdots\sum_{l_{\mathfrak{k}}=0}^{d_{\mathfrak{k}}}\sum_{j_{1}+j_{1}^{\prime}=l_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{\mathfrak{k}}+j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}=l_{\mathfrak{k}}}h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}(b_{1}^{\dagger})^{j_{1}}b_{1}^{j_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots(b_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\dagger})^{j_{\mathfrak{k}}}b_{\mathfrak{k}}^{j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}.

(63)

for coefficients
h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}
with
h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}=\overline{h_{j_{1}^{\prime}j_{1}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}j_{\mathfrak{k}}}}
and
|h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}|\leq 1
. We also denote
d=\sum_{q}d_{q}
as in Definition
2.1
. Our learning protocol will generate estimates
\hat{h}_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}
such that

\max_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}|\hat{h}_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}-{h}_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}|\leq\epsilon\text{ with probability at least }1-\delta.

(64)

B.2.1
Experimental setup

The experimental setup is similar to the one described in Section
4.2
.
There are two differences: On each mode
q
we apply dissipation with jump operators
b_{q}(b_{q}-\alpha_{q})
and
b_{q}^{r}(b_{q}-\alpha_{q})
, where
r=\lceil d/2\rceil-1
, so that the dynamics is stabilized within a
2^{\mathfrak{k}}
dimensional subspace spanned by
\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\mathfrak{k}}\{\ket{0},\ket{\alpha_{q}}\}
. Also, instead of applying the controlled displacement on one bosonic mode we apply it on
\mathfrak{k}
modes, and the displacement is described by a vector
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{\mathfrak{k}})
. The experiment allows us to measure
X
and
Y
observables whose expectation values are approximately
\cos(\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t)
and
\sin(\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t)
respectively. We will see that by analyzing the effective Hamiltonian, with the same robust frequency estimation procedure described in Section
4.3
, we can again estimate the value of
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
with Heisenberg-limited scaling.

B.2.2
The effective Hamiltonian

We let
|\alpha_{q}|=A_{q}
. and throughout this section we assume
A_{q}\geq 1
.
Similar to the single-mode case, we introduce

\ket{\Psi_{0}^{q}}=\ket{0},\quad\ket{\Psi_{1}^{q}}=\frac{\ket{\alpha_{q}}-\ket{0}\braket{0|\alpha_{q}}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-A_{q}^{2}}}}.

(65)

Then we define, for a bitstring
b=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{\mathfrak{k}})\in\{0,1\}^{\mathfrak{k}}
,

\ket{\Psi_{b}}=\bigotimes_{q=1}^{\mathfrak{k}}\ket{\Psi_{b_{q}}^{q}}.

(66)

We therefore have a basis for the subspace stabilized by the dissipation. The effective Hamiltonian is

H^{\mathrm{proj}}=\Pi H\Pi,

(67)

where

\Pi=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{\mathfrak{k}}}\ket{\Psi_{b}}\bra{\Psi_{b}}.

(68)

We want to show that this effective Hamiltonian can be well-approximated by

\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{\mathfrak{k}}}\ket{\Psi_{b}}\bra{\Psi_{b}}H\ket{\Psi_{b}}\bra{\Psi_{b}}.

(69)

In other words, we want to show that the off-diagonal elements can be ignored. This can be done by computing an upper bound for the off-diagonal elements. We first describe the effect of the annihilation operator on
\ket{\Psi_{1}^{q}}
:

b_{q}^{j}\ket{\Psi_{1}^{q}}=\begin{cases}\ket{\Psi_{1}^{q}},&\text{if }j=0\\
\frac{\alpha_{q}^{j}\ket{\alpha_{q}}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-A_{q}^{2}}}},&\text{if }j\geq 1\,.\end{cases}

(70)

Using this we can compute
\braket{\Psi_{0}^{q}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j}b_{q}^{j^{\prime}}|\Psi_{1}^{q}}
. Note that if
j\neq 0
then
\braket{\Psi_{0}^{q}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j}b_{q}^{j^{\prime}}|\Psi_{1}^{q}}=0
. Therefore we only need to focus on the case with
j=0
, and using (
70
) we have

|\braket{\Psi_{0}^{q}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j}b_{q}^{j^{\prime}}|\Psi_{1}^{q}}|=\mathcal{O}(A_{q}^{j^{\prime}}e^{-A_{q}^{2}}).

(71)

Similarly we can bound
\braket{\Psi_{1}^{q}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j}b_{q}^{j^{\prime}}|\Psi_{1}^{q}}
by examining all cases of
j=0
or
j\neq 0
and
j^{\prime}=0
or
j^{\prime}\neq 0
. We can obtain a bound

|\braket{\Psi_{1}^{q}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j}b_{q}^{j^{\prime}}|\Psi_{1}^{q}}|=\mathcal{O}(A_{q}^{j+j^{\prime}}).

(72)

We also know that
|\braket{\Psi_{0}^{q}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j}b_{q}^{j^{\prime}}|\Psi_{0}^{q}}|\leq 1
.
Based on these observations, we have, for any
b\neq b^{\prime}

\braket{\Psi_{b}|(b_{1}^{\dagger})^{j_{1}}b_{1}^{j_{1}^{\prime}}\cdots(b_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\dagger})^{j_{\mathfrak{k}}}b_{\mathfrak{k}}^{j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}|\Psi_{b^{\prime}}}=\prod_{q=1}^{\mathfrak{k}}\braket{\Psi^{q}_{b_{q}}|(b_{q}^{\dagger})^{j_{q}}b_{q}^{j_{q}^{\prime}}|\Psi^{q}_{b^{\prime}_{q}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}A_{q}^{j_{q}+j_{q}^{\prime}}\right).

(73)

Therefore

|\braket{\Psi_{b}|H|\Psi_{b^{\prime}}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right),

(74)

which gives us

\|H^{\mathrm{proj}}-\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}\|_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}\left(4^{\mathfrak{k}}e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right).

(75)

We then analyze the effect of the non-orthogonality of coherent states on the phase estimation experiment. Note that

\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}\ket{\alpha}=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{b}}\ket{\Psi_{b}}\braket{\Psi_{b}|H|\Psi_{b}}\braket{\Psi_{b}|\alpha}.

(76)

Similar to (
74
) we can derive a bound

|\braket{\Psi_{b}|H|\Psi_{b}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right).

(77)

Also observe that

|\braket{\Psi_{b}|\alpha}|=\mathcal{O}(e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}})

(78)

if
b\neq(1,1,\cdots,1)
, we have

\widetilde{H}^{\mathrm{proj}}\ket{\alpha}=\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}\ket{\alpha}+\mathcal{O}\left(2^{\mathfrak{k}}e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right),

(79)

where we have also used

|\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}-\braket{\Psi_{(1,1,\cdots,1)}|H|\Psi_{(1,1,\cdots,1)}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathfrak{k}e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right).

(80)

The latter follows in turn from
\|H\ket{\Psi_{(1,1,\cdots,1)}}\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right)
,
\|H\ket{\alpha}\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right)
(again similar to (
74
)) and
\|\ket{\alpha}-\ket{\Psi_{(1,1,\cdots,1)}}\|=\mathcal{O}(\mathfrak{k}e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}})
by (
10
).

Combining the above with (
75
), we have

H^{\mathrm{proj}}\ket{\alpha}=\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}\ket{\alpha}+\mathcal{O}\left(4^{\mathfrak{k}}e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}\right).

(81)

From this we have

\|(e^{-iH^{\mathrm{proj}}t}-e^{-i\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}t})\ket{\alpha}\|=\mathcal{O}\left(4^{\mathfrak{k}}e^{-\min\{A_{q}^{2}\}}\prod_{q}(A_{q}+1)^{d_{q}}t\right).

(82)

B.2.3
Recovering the coefficients

Next, we briefly discuss how to recover the coefficients from
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
for different values of
\alpha
, and postpone the proofs of the results used in this appendix to Appendix
D
. Let
\alpha_{q}=A_{q}e^{i\theta_{q}}
for
q=1,2,\cdots,\mathfrak{k}
. Then

\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}=\sum_{l_{1}=0}^{d_{1}}\cdots\sum_{l_{k}=0}^{d_{\mathfrak{k}}}h_{l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}(\theta)A_{1}^{l_{1}}\cdots A_{\mathfrak{k}}^{l_{\mathfrak{k}}},

(83)

where
\theta=(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{\mathfrak{k}})
, and

h_{l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}(\theta)=\sum_{j_{1}+j_{1}^{\prime}=l_{1}}\cdots\sum_{j_{\mathfrak{k}}+j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}=l_{\mathfrak{k}}}h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}e^{-i\theta(j_{1}-j_{1}^{\prime})}\cdots e^{-i\theta(j_{\mathfrak{k}}-j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime})}.

(84)

From the above we can see that
\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
is a polynomial of
A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{q}
, whose coefficients
h_{l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}(\theta)
are Fourier sums of the angles
\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{\mathfrak{k}}
. Therefore we first use polynomial interpolation to estimate the polynomial coefficients
h_{l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}(\theta)
for any given
\theta
, and from that estimate the Hamiltonian coefficients
h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}
through a Fourier transform.

More concretely, for a given
\theta
we denote
p_{\theta}(A)=\braket{\alpha|H|\alpha}
, where
A=(A_{1},\cdots,A_{\mathfrak{k}})
, and
\alpha=(A_{1}e^{i\theta_{1}},\cdots,A_{\mathfrak{k}}e^{i\theta_{\mathfrak{k}}})
.
p_{\theta}(A)
is therefore a polynomial of
A
where the degrees for
A_{1},\cdots,A_{\mathfrak{k}}
are
d_{1},\cdots,d_{\mathfrak{k}}
respectively. We define a set of
(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1)\cdots(d_{\mathfrak{k}}+1)
nodes
A_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{\mathfrak{k}}}=(A^{1}_{\mu_{1}},A^{2}_{\mu_{2}},\cdots,A^{\mathfrak{k}}_{\mu_{\mathfrak{k}}})
, where each
A^{q}_{\mu_{q}}
is

A^{q}_{\mu_{q}}=\frac{a_{q}+b_{1}}{2}+\frac{b_{q}-a_{q}}{2}\cos\left(\frac{(2\mu_{q}-1)\pi}{2(d_{q}+1)}\right),

(85)

as defined in Appendix
D
’s equation (
110
) with some change of notation, on a hypercube
[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{\mathfrak{k}},b_{\mathfrak{k}}]
, so that the coefficients of
p_{\theta}(A)
can be uniquely determined from
\{p_{\theta}(A_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{\mathfrak{k}}})\}
. From experiments, we can generate estimates
\{\hat{p}_{\theta,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{\mathfrak{k}}}\}
satisfying

|p_{\theta}(A_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{\mathfrak{k}}})-\hat{p}_{\theta,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\cdots,\mu_{\mathfrak{k}}}|\leq\epsilon_{1}

(86)

for all
\theta
and
(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{\mathfrak{k}})
and with high probability. Lemma
D.4
in Appendix
D
then tells us that as long as

b_{q}-a_{q}\geq 2,b_{q}\geq 2a_{q}>0,

(87)

for all
q=1,2,\cdots,\mathfrak{k}
, we can guarantee that the recovered coefficients
\hat{h}_{\theta,l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}
are also accurate

|h_{l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}(\theta)-\hat{h}_{\theta,l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}|\leq C_{d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{\mathfrak{k}}}\epsilon_{1},

(88)

where
C_{d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{\mathfrak{k}}}
is a constant that depends only on
d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{\mathfrak{k}}
.
In estimating
h_{l_{1}\cdots l_{\mathfrak{k}}}(\theta)
we are essentially estimating the high-order derivatives of
p_{\theta}(A)
around
A=0
. Because we cannot obtain the value of
p_{\theta}(A)
for small
\|A\|
(the states
\ket{\alpha}
and
\ket{0}
would have large overlap in this case), the above procedure is essentially an extrapolation from the hypercube
[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{\mathfrak{k}},b_{\mathfrak{k}}]
to
0
. This is the reason why we require (
87
) so that the distance we extrapolate is not too large compared to the size of the interval in which we have the needed information
p_{\theta}(A)
.

From the above we can see that we can estimate each
h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{k}j_{k}^{\prime}}
to precision
\epsilon
if we can estimate each
p_{\theta_{u}}(A_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{\mathfrak{k}}})
to precision
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, where

\theta_{u}=(\theta_{u_{1}}^{1},\theta_{u_{2}}^{2},\cdots,\theta_{u_{\mathfrak{k}}}^{\mathfrak{k}})=\left(\frac{u_{1}\pi}{l_{1}+1},\frac{u_{2}\pi}{l_{2}+1},\cdots,\frac{u_{\mathfrak{k}}\pi}{l_{\mathfrak{k}}+1}\right)

(89)

is chosen so that we can recover the coefficients
h_{j_{1}j_{1}^{\prime}\cdots j_{\mathfrak{k}}j_{\mathfrak{k}}^{\prime}}
using the Fourier transform.
Each
p_{\theta}(A)
is obtained through the phase estimation experiment introduced in Section
B.2.1
, and can be estimated to precision
\epsilon_{1}
with
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon_{1})
total evolution time when
A_{q}=\Omega(\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon_{1})})
using (
82
) and the robust frequency estimation procedure in Section
4.3
.
We therefore have the following theorem:

Running the experiments in Section
B.2.1

Appendix C
Relative boundedness by the (modified) photon dissipation

\mathbf{j}\neq 0

E_{a}

E_{a}
overlaps with at most
\mathfrak{d}=\mathcal{O}(1)

. This can be further simplified by

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle(y-2(\ell+1))r_{2(\ell-1)}(y)+(\ell+1)^{2}(p_{\ell}(y)+2\ell y^{2\ell})

\displaystyle=(y^{2}-2y(\ell+1)+(\ell+1)^{2})r_{2(\ell-1)}(y)+(\ell+1)^{2}(y^{2\ell}-(\ell+1)\ell y^{2\ell-1}+2\ell y^{2\ell})

\displaystyle=(y-(\ell+1))^{2}r_{2(\ell-1)}(y)+y^{2\ell-1}(\ell+1)^{2}((2\ell+1)y-(\ell+1)\ell)

\displaystyle\geq 0

by assumption
y\geq\ell
and
r_{2(\ell-1)}\geq 0
. This proves

\displaystyle y^{2\ell}-(\ell+1)\ell y^{2\ell-1}\leq p_{\ell}(y)

for all
y\geq\ell
. Due to the inequality

y^{2\ell}\leq(\ell+1)\ell y^{2\ell-1}

and
p_{\ell}(y)=0
for
1\leq y\leq\ell-1
and
y\in\mathbbm{N}
, the inequality extends to all
\ell\geq 1
and shoes the following inequality via its spectral decomposition in the Fock basis:

\displaystyle N[-r+1:0]^{2}\geq(N+I)^{2r}-(r+1)r(N+I)^{2r-1}\,.

(98)

∎

Appendix D
Polynomial regression bounds

In the above, we need to regard the expectation values as polynomials in
|\alpha|
, and estimate the coefficients of this polynomial to learn the coefficients in the Hamiltonian. To this end, we will study the following problem: given a polynomial
p(x)
of degree
d
that satisfies
|p(x)|\leq\epsilon
for
x\in[a,b]
, how large can its coefficients be?

Lemma D.1
.

Let
p(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}p_{n}x^{n}
. If
|p(x)|\leq\epsilon
for
x\in[a,b]
and
|b-a|>2|a|
, then

|p_{n}|\leq\frac{d(2d-2)!!}{n!}\left|\frac{2}{b-a}\right|^{n}\frac{\epsilon}{1-2|a|/|b-a|}.

(99)

Proof.

Note that
p_{n}=p^{(n)}(0)/n!
, and therefore we will first focus on upper bounding
|p^{(n)}(0)|
.
The following derivation is almost identical derivation to
[
9
, Lemma E.2]
, but we still include it here for completeness. By Markov brothers’ inequality, we have

|p^{(k)}(a)|\leq\left|\frac{2}{b-a}\right|^{k}C_{M}(d,k)\epsilon,

(100)

where

C_{M}(d,k)=\frac{d^{2}(d^{2}-1^{2})(d^{2}-2^{2})\cdots(d^{2}-(k-1)^{2})}{(2k-1)!!}\leq d((2d-2)!!),

(101)

where the last inequality comes from
C_{M}(d,k)\leq C_{M}(d,d)=d(2d-2)!!
(one can prove
C_{M}(d,k)\leq C_{M}(d,k+1)
by direct computation).
Performing Taylor expansion at
x=a
, we have

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{d}p^{(k)}(a)\frac{(x-a)^{k}}{k!}.

Therefore

p^{(n)}(0)=\sum_{k=n}^{d}\frac{1}{(k-n)!}p^{(k)}(a)(-a)^{k-n}.

Consequently,

|p^{(n)}(0)|\leq\sum_{k=n}^{d}\frac{1}{(k-n)!}\left|\frac{2}{b-a}\right|^{k}C_{M}(d,k)|a|^{k-n}\epsilon.

(102)

Using
(k-n)!\geq 1
and
C_{M}(d,k)\leq d(2d-2)!!
, we have

|p^{(n)}(0)|\leq d(2d-2)!!\sum_{k=n}^{d}\left|\frac{2}{b-a}\right|^{k}|a|^{k-n}\epsilon\leq d(2d-2)!!\left|\frac{2}{b-a}\right|^{n}\frac{\epsilon}{1-2|a|/|b-a|},

(103)

if
|b-a|>2|a|
as assumed in the lemma.
Therefore we have the inequality we want to prove by observing that
p_{n}=p^{(n)}(0)/n!
.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

Let
f(x)
be a polynomial with degree at most
d
, and let
x_{i}=\cos\left(\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{2(d+1)}\right)
,
i=1,2,\cdots,d+1
, be the roots of the
(d+1)
th Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind. Then for any
x\in[-1,1]
,

|f(x)|\leq\frac{2d+1}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}|f(x_{i})|.

Proof.

Because the Chebyshev polynomials
T_{0}(x),T_{1}(x),\cdots,T_{d}(x)
form an orthogonal basis of the vector space of polynomials with degree at most
d
, we have

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{2T_{n}(x)}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f(y)T_{n}(y)}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f(y)}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\mathrm{d}y.

(104)

Because Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature with
q
quadrature points is exact for polynomials up to degree
2q-1
, and
f(y)T_{n}(y)
has degree at most
2d
, by choosing
q=d+1
we have

\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f(y)T_{n}(y)}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\pi}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}f(x_{i})T_{n}(x_{i}),

(105)

for
n=0,1,\cdots,d
. Therefore

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{2T_{n}(x)}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}f(x_{i})T_{n}(x_{i})+\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}f(x_{i}).

(106)

Consequently

|f(x)|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{d}\frac{2}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}|f(x_{i})|+\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}|f(x_{i})|=\frac{2d+1}{d+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}|f(x_{i})|,

(107)

where we have used the fact that
|T_{n}(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in[-1,1]
.
∎

We remark that the above lemma holds for arbitrary interval
[a,b]
instead of just for
[-1,1]
. We only need to redefine

x_{i}=\frac{a+b}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}\cos\left(\frac{(2i-1)\pi}{2(d+1)}\right),

(108)

for
i=1,2,\cdots,d+1
.

Combining the above lemmas, we have the following corollary:

Corollary D.3
.

Let
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}p_{n}x^{n}
be a polynomial with degree at most
d=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Assume
|f(x_{i})|\leq\epsilon
for
x_{i}
,
i=1,2,\cdots,d+1
, defined in (
108
) in which
(b-a)>2a>0
, then

|p_{n}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{(b-a)^{n}}\frac{\epsilon}{1-2a/(b-a)}\right).

We next look at the multivariate case. Instead of going through a detailed calculation as we did for the single variable case, we will use the norm equivalence in finite dimensional vectors spaces to establish a similar bound without specifying the constant. We only need to focus on the dependence on
a_{1},\cdots,a_{k}
, which needs to be tuned according to the required precision.

First we define the Chebyshev quadrature nodes
y_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}=(y_{i_{1}}^{1},\cdots,y_{i_{k}}^{k})
:

y_{i_{\nu}}^{\nu}=\cos\left(\frac{(2i_{\nu}-1)\pi}{2(d_{\nu}+1)}\right)

(109)

for
i_{\nu}=1,2,\cdots,d_{\nu}+1
,
\nu=1,2,\cdots,k
. The Chebyshev quadrature nodes in the domain
[a_{1},b_{1}]\times\cdots\times[a_{k},b_{k}]
is then

r_{i_{\nu}}^{\nu}=\frac{a_{\nu}+b_{\nu}}{2}+\frac{b_{\nu}-a_{\nu}}{2}y_{i_{\nu}}^{\nu},

(110)

Lemma D.4
.

Let
r_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}=(r_{i_{1}}^{1},r_{i_{2}}^{2},\cdots,r_{i_{k}}^{k})
be as defined in (
110
). Let
p
be a polynomial of the form

p(x)=\sum_{l_{1}=0}^{d_{1}}\cdots\sum_{l_{k}=0}^{d_{k}}p_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}x^{l_{1}}\cdots x^{l_{k}}.

If
b_{\nu}-a_{\nu}\geq 2
and
b_{\nu}\geq 2a_{\nu}>0
for all
\nu=1,2,\cdots,k
, then there exists a constant
C_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}>0
that depends only on
d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}
such that

\max_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}|p_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}|\leq C_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\max_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}|p(r_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}})|.

(111)

Proof.

We shift and rescale the domain by defining

\varphi(x)=\left(\frac{2x_{1}-(a_{1}+b_{1})}{b_{1}-a_{1}},\cdots,\frac{2x_{k}-(a_{k}+b_{k})}{b_{k}-a_{k}}\right),

and

q(y)=p(\varphi^{-1}(y)).

(112)

Then
p(r_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}})=q(y_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}})
.

Because all polynomials with degrees
d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k}
for variables
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}
form a complex vector space of dimension
(d_{1}+1)(d_{2}+1)\cdots(d_{k}+1)
, it is easy to check that

\|q\|_{\mathrm{nodes}}:=\max_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}|q(y_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}})|

(113)

is a norm for this vector space. Similarly,

\|q\|_{\max}:=\max_{y\in[-3,3]^{k}}\max_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}\left|\frac{\partial^{l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}}}{\partial_{y_{1}}^{l_{1}}\cdots\partial_{y_{k}}^{l_{k}}}q(y)\right|

(114)

also defines a norm.
Here, we take the maximum over
[-3,3]^{k}
because
b_{\nu}\geq 2a_{\nu}>0
ensures
\varphi(0)\in[-3,3]^{k}
.
By the equivalence between norms for finite-dimensional vector spaces, we then have a constant
C_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}
that depends only on
d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}
such that

\|q\|_{\max}\leq C_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\|q\|_{\mathrm{nodes}}.

(115)

We then relate
\|q\|_{\max}
to the coefficients of
p(x)
. Note that

p_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}=\frac{1}{l_{1}!\cdots l_{k}!}\frac{\partial^{l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}}}{\partial_{x_{1}}^{l_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{k}}^{l_{k}}}p(x)\Big|_{x=0}=\prod_{\nu=1}^{k}\frac{1}{l_{\nu}!}\left(\frac{2}{b_{\nu}-a_{\nu}}\right)^{l_{\nu}}\frac{\partial^{l_{1}+\cdots+l_{k}}}{\partial_{x_{1}}^{l_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{k}}^{l_{k}}}q(y)\Big|_{y=\varphi(0)}.

Since
b_{\nu}-a_{\nu}\geq 2
for all
\nu
, and
\varphi(0)\in[-3,3]^{k}
, which is ensured by
b_{\nu}\geq 2a_{\nu}>0
, we have

\max_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}|p_{l_{1},\cdots,l_{k}}|\leq\|q\|_{\max}.

Further we have

\|q\|_{\max}\leq C_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\|q\|_{\mathrm{nodes}}=C_{d_{1},\cdots,d_{k}}\max_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}}|p(r_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{k}})|.

Therefore we have (
111
).
∎

Appendix E
Pseudoinverses of unbounded operators

We consider a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Let
A
and
B
be possibly unbounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and we want to provide a lower bound for
\eta
satisfying

\braket{\psi|(I-P)B^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}AB(I-P)|\psi}\geq\eta\braket{\psi|I-P|\psi},

(116)

where
P
is the projection operator onto
\ker(AB)
.

To prove this, we will need the notion of the pseudoinverse. While pseudoinverses are well-known for matrices, they have been extended to unbounded operators on Hilbert spaces
[
70
]
.

Definition E.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two complex Hilbert spaces. Let
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
be a linear operator. Let
P_{1}
be the orthogonal projection onto
\overline{\operatorname{ran}(T)}
and
P_{2}
the orthogonal projection onto
\overline{\operatorname{ran}(T^{+})}
. Then, the operator
T^{+}
is a pseudoinverse of
T
if
\overline{\operatorname{dom}(T^{+})}=\mathcal{H}_{2}
and

\displaystyle\operatorname{ran}(T)\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T^{+})\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\operatorname{ran}(T^{+})\subseteq\operatorname{dom}(T)\,,

\displaystyle TT^{+}=P_{1}\,,

\displaystyle\qquad T^{+}T=P_{2}\,.

The following theorem follows from
[
71
, Theorem A]
(see also
[
70
, Theorem 6]
):

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two complex Hilbert spaces. Let
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
be a densely defined closed linear operator. Then,
T
has a unique closed pseudoinverse
T^{+}
.

A priori,
T^{+}
might be unbounded, but if
\operatorname{ran}(T)
is closed, we can say more:

Lemma E.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two complex Hilbert spaces. Let
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to H_{2}
be a densely defined closed linear operator. Then, the pseudoinverse
T^{+}
is a bounded operator if and only if
\operatorname{ran}(T)
is closed.

In order to calculate
T^{+}
explicitly, Theorem 3.2
[
72
]
provides a series expansion:

Lemma E.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be two complex Hilbert spaces. Let
T:\mathcal{H}_{1}\to\mathcal{H}_{2}
be a densely defined closed linear operator with closed range. Then,

T^{+}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}T^{\dagger}(I_{\mathcal{H}_{2}}+TT^{\dagger})^{-k}

in the uniform operator topology.

In the following, we use the above results to prove rigorous lower bounds on
\eta
in (
116
). The idea is to find a lower bound in terms of the norms of the pseudoinverse of
A
and
B
on
\eta
, which is proven in the following result:

Lemma E.5
.

Let
A,B
be densely defined closed operators in Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with closed range. We assume that
\ker(A)\subset\operatorname{ran}(B)
. Then

\braket{\psi|(I-P)B^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}AB(I-P)|\psi}\geq\eta\braket{\psi|I-P|\psi}

holds with
\eta=\|B^{+}\|^{-2}\|A^{+}\|^{-2}
, where
P
is the orthogonal projection operator into
\ker(AB)=B^{+}\ker(A)+\ker(B)
.

Proof.

Note that in (
116
), we only need to consider
\ket{\psi}
such that
P\ket{\psi}=0
and therefore we make this assumption without loss of generality. We denote
\ket{\phi}=AB\ket{\psi}
, and the goal is to relate
\|\ket{\phi}\|
to
\|\ket{\psi}\|
. We assume that
A
and
B
are densely defined closed operators with closed range such that they have bounded pseudoinverses by Lemma
E.3
. From
\ket{\phi}=AB\ket{\psi}
, we have

B\ket{\psi}=A^{+}AB\ket{\psi}+(I-A^{+}A)B\ket{\psi}\eqqcolon A^{+}\ket{\phi}+\ket{r_{1}},

where
\ket{r_{1}}\in\ker(A)
. Similarly, we achieve

\ket{\psi}=B^{+}B\ket{\psi}+(I-B^{+}B)\ket{\psi}\eqqcolon B^{+}A^{+}\ket{\phi}+B^{+}\ket{r_{1}}+\ket{r_{2}},

(117)

where
\ket{r_{2}}\in\ker(B)
. Note that
AB\ket{r_{2}}=0
, and therefore
\ket{r_{2}}\in\ker(AB)
. Thus, as a result
(I-P)\ket{r_{2}}=0
. Definition
E.1
states that
BB^{+}=P_{B}
, where
P_{B}
is the projection operator onto
\operatorname{ran}(B)
. Therefore, if we further assume that
\ker(A)\subset\operatorname{ran}(B)
, then

ABB^{+}\ket{r_{1}}=AP_{B}\ket{r_{1}}=A\ket{r_{1}}=0\,.

Therefore,
B^{+}\ket{r_{1}}\in\operatorname{ker}(AB)
and
(I-P)B^{+}\ket{r_{1}}=0
. Now multiplying
I-P
to both sides of (
117
), we have

\ket{\psi}=(I-P)\ket{\psi}=(I-P)B^{+}A^{+}\ket{\phi}+(I-P)(B^{+}\ket{r_{1}}+\ket{r_{2}})=(I-P)B^{+}A^{+}\ket{\phi}.

(118)

Therefore

\|\ket{\psi}\|=\|(I-P)B^{+}A^{+}\ket{\phi}\|\leq\|B^{+}\|\|A^{+}\|\|\ket{\phi}\|
, which finishes the proof.
∎

In the context of the bosonic systems introduced in Appendix
A.3
the jump operator
L=b^{k}(b-\alpha)
split into
A=b-\alpha
and
B=b^{k}
. For
k=1
, we can easily verify that
\operatorname{ran}(B)=\mathcal{H}
,
\ker(A)=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{\alpha}\}
, and
\ker(B)=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{0}\}
. Therefore, the assumptions of Lemma
E.5
are satisfied. For arbitrary
k\in\mathbbm{N}_{\geq 1}
, we need that the closed operators
b^{k}
have closed range:

Lemma E.6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 1
. Then,
\operatorname{ran}(b^{k})=\mathcal{H}
is closed.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{H}
is a separable Hilbert space, for every
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
, there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\ket{\psi}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\psi_{n}\ket{n}
. We then define the sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by

\phi_{n}=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }n\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n[k-1:0]}}\psi_{n+k}&\text{for }n\in\{k,k+1,\ldots\}\end{cases}

The sequence
\ket{\phi_{m}}=\sum_{n=0}^{m}\phi_{n}\ket{n}
is a Cauchy sequence by definition and converges to a state
\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}
. Moreover,
b^{k}\ket{\phi_{n}}=\ket{\psi_{n}}
converges by definition to
\ket{\psi}
. Since
b^{k}
is a closed operator, because it is the adjoint of
(b^{\dagger})^{k}
, which is densely defined
[
73
, Theorem VIII.1]
, we have
b^{k}\ket{\phi}=\ket{\psi}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Next, we show that, for example,
AB=b(b-\alpha)
satisfies (
116
) with
\eta=1
.

Lemma E.7
.

Let
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
A=(b-\alpha)^{j}
,
B=b^{j}
. Then,
\|A^{+}\|_{\infty}
,
\|B^{+}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
E.6
, we know that
b^{j}
has closed range. By Lemma
E.4
and the identity
b^{j}(b^{j})^{\dagger}=(N+I)\cdot\ldots\cdot(N+jI)=:N[1:j]
, we can represent the pseudoinverse of
B
by

(b^{j})^{+}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(b^{\dagger})^{j}(I+N[1:j])^{-k}\,.

For
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\psi_{n}\ket{n}
, we can estimate the norm using Tonelli’s theorem:

\displaystyle\|B^{+}\ket{\psi}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{n[1:j]}(n[1:j]+1)^{-k}\psi_{n}\ket{n+j}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{n[1:j]}{(1+n[1:j])^{2k}}|\psi_{n}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2+n[1:j]}|\psi_{n}|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{j!}\,,

so that
\|B^{+}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}
. The operators
A
and
B
are related through
B=D(\alpha)AD(\alpha)^{\dagger}
, where
D(\alpha)=e^{\alpha b^{\dagger}-\overline{\alpha}b}
is the bosonic displacement operator. Since the displacement operator in unitary,
\|A^{+}\|_{\infty}=\|B^{+}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{j!}}
.
∎

Lemma E.8
.

Let
A=(b-\alpha)
,
B=b
. Then,
B^{+}\ker(A)
is spanned by
B^{+}\ket{\alpha}=\frac{1}{\alpha}(\ket{\alpha}-e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\ket{0})
. Therefore
\ker(AB)=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{0},\ket{\alpha}\}
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
\ker(A)
is spanned by
\ket{\alpha}
, since
A=b-\alpha
. Moreover, the continuity of the map
\bra{\phi}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{C}
, along with Lemma
E.4
, shows that
\bra{0}B^{+}\ket{\alpha}=0
. In a similar vein, we can compute for
n\in\mathbb{N}
:

\displaystyle\bra{n}B^{+}\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bra{n}b^{\dagger}(2I+N)^{-k}\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(n+1)^{-k}\braket{n|\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\braket{n|\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\,.

Using the definition of the coherent state, the first assertion follows.
For the second assertion, we observe from Lemma
E.5
that
\ker(AB)=B^{+}\ker(A)+\ker(B)
. Since
\ker(B)
is spanned by
\ket{0}
, the second assertion follows from the first.
∎

Lemma E.9
.

Let
L=b^{k}-\alpha^{k}I
. Then,
[L,L^{\dagger}]\geq k!I
.

Proof.

This result follows essentially from
[
40
]
. There, they prove that

[L,L^{\dagger}]=(N+I)(N+2I)\ldots(N+kI)-N(N-I)_{+}\ldots(N-(k-1)I)_{+}\,.

Here, for
\mu\in\mathbb{N}
, the operator
(N-\mu I)_{+}
denotes the positive part of
N-\mu I
, i.e.,

(N-\mu I)_{+}\ket{n}=\begin{cases}0&\text{for }n\in\{0,\ldots,\mu\},\\
(n-\mu)\ket{n}&\text{for }n>\mu,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\,.\end{cases}

(119)

We can rewrite this as

\displaystyle[L,L^{\dagger}]=

\displaystyle N(N+2I)(N+3I)\ldots(N+kI)+(N+2I)(N+3I)\ldots(N+kI)

\displaystyle-N(N-I)_{+}\ldots(N-(k-1)I)_{+}\,.

We observe that
(N+2I)(N+3I)\ldots(N+kI)\geq k!I
. Moreover, by applying the spectral decomposition, we find that

\displaystyle N(N+2I)(N+3I)\ldots(N+kI)-N(N-I)_{+}\ldots(N-(k-1)I)_{+}

\displaystyle=N\left[(N+2I)(N+3I)\ldots(N+kI)-(N-I)_{+}(N-2I)_{+}\ldots(N-(k-1)I)_{+}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 0\,.

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma E.10
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
k\geq 1
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
L=b^{k}-\alpha^{k}I
. Then,
L
is closed and has closed range, where
\operatorname{dom}(L)=\operatorname{dom}(b^{k})
.

Proof.

We know that
b^{k}
is a closed operator because it is the adjoint of
(b^{\dagger})^{k}
, which is densely defined
[
73
, Theorem VIII.1]
. Moreover,
-\alpha^{k}I
is a bounded operator. Thus,
L
is closed by Problem III.5.6 of
[
74
]
. To prove that
L
also has closed range, we consider the reduced minimum modulus of
L^{\dagger}
(see
[
74
, Section III.5.1]
), defined for some closed operator
T
as

\gamma(T):=\sup\{\gamma\geq 0\mid\|Tu\|\geq\gamma\|\tilde{u}\|\quad\forall u\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\}\,,

where
\|\tilde{u}\|=\inf_{z\in\ker(T)}\|u-z\|
. Using Lemma
E.9
and the fact that
\ker(L^{\dagger})=\{0\}
, we obtain

\|L^{\dagger}u\|^{2}=\bra{u}LL^{\dagger}\ket{u}\geq\bra{u}[L,L^{\dagger}]\ket{u}\geq k!\|u\|^{2}\,.

It follows that
\gamma(L^{\dagger})\geq\sqrt{k!}
. By Theorem IV.5.2 of
[
74
]
,
\gamma(L^{\dagger})>0
implies that
\operatorname{ran}(L^{\dagger})
is closed. By Theorem IV.5.13 of
[
74
]
, this further implies that
\operatorname{ran}(L)
is closed as well.
∎

Lemma E.11
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha\in C
and
L=b^{k}-\alpha^{k}I
. Then,
\|L^{+}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}
.

Proof.

We find by Lemma
E.10
that
L
has closed range. We aim to apply Lemma
E.4
for the pseudoinverse of
L^{\dagger}
. Let
\ket{\psi}\in\operatorname{dom}(L)
. Then, by
[
75
, Theorem 5.1.9(iii)]
, it holds that

L(I+L^{\dagger}L)^{-1}\ket{\psi}=(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-1}L\ket{\psi}

(see also the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
[
72
]
for a direct argument). Now define

A_{n}:=\sum_{r=1}^{n}(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-r}L\,,

which is a bounded operator. We claim that
\|A_{n}\ket{\psi}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}\|\ket{\psi}\|
for all
\ket{\psi}\in\operatorname{dom}(L)
. Assuming this claim for now, since
\operatorname{dom}(L)
is dense, the BLT theorem implies that
\|A_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Lemma
E.4
states that the operators
A_{n}
converge to
(L^{\dagger})^{+}
in norm, thus also
\|(L^{\dagger})^{+}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{k!}}
. Since
(L^{\dagger})^{+}=(L^{+})^{\dagger}
(see
[
70
, Theorem 8(g)]
, which is attributed to
[
71
]
), the assertion follows. It remains to prove the claim. We start by bounding

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-r}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{n}(1+k!)^{-r}I

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(1+k!)^{-r}I=\frac{1}{k!}I

(120)

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where we have used Lemma
E.9
in the first inequality since
I+LL^{\dagger}\geq I+[L,L^{\dagger}]
. Next, we consider
\ket{\psi}\in\operatorname{dom}(L)
again and find that

\displaystyle\bra{\psi}A_{n}^{\dagger}A_{n}\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}L^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{n}(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-r}\right)^{2}L\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k!}\bra{\psi}L^{\dagger}\sum_{r=1}^{n}(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-r}L\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k!}\bra{\psi}\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}L^{\dagger}(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-r}L\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{k!}\bra{\psi}P\ket{\psi}\leq\frac{1}{k!}\,.

In the first inequality, we applied (
120
). In the third line, we used that
(I+LL^{\dagger})^{-r}
is positive for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
. In the final line, we used the definition of the pseudoinverse of
L
from Lemma
E.4
and the fact that
L^{+}L=P
, where
P
is the projection onto the closure of
\operatorname{ran}(L^{+})
(see
[
70
, Eq. (26)]
).
∎

Appendix F
Discussion on possible experimental realizations

The required dissipation
L_{r,\alpha}=b^{r}\left(b-\alpha\right)
can be obtained by engineering a coupling Hamiltonian
H=ga^{\dagger}b^{r}\left(b-\alpha\right)+h.c.

between the probe and a fast decaying ancilla.
Here, we discuss possible platforms and protocols to obtain this Hamiltonian.
We study the case of
r=1,
i.e.

L_{1,\alpha}=b\left(b-\alpha\right),

where a more general and detailed discussion is left for future work.

The standard two-photon dissipation used for cat code is typically obtained by coupling the two resonators, the probe (referred to as the storage mode) and the ancilla (buffer mode),
to asymmetrically threaded SQUID (ATS).
The ATS consists of two Josephson junctions with energies
E_{j_{1}},E_{j_{2}}
that are connected in parallel and split in the middle by a linear inductor with inductance
L_{b}
which forms two loops (see Refs
[
61
,
62
,
42
]
for more details and illustrations).
The loops have magnetic fluxes of
\varphi_{\text{ext},1},

\varphi_{\text{ext},2}.

The potential energy of this system is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&U_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}E_{L,b}\varphi^{2}-2E_{j_{1}}\cos\left(\varphi_{\text{ext},1}+\varphi_{1}\right)-2E_{j_{2}}\cos\left(\varphi_{\text{ext},2}+\varphi_{2}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}E_{L,b}\varphi^{2}-2E_{J}\cos\left(\varphi_{\Sigma}\right)\cos\left(\varphi+\varphi_{\Delta}\right)-2\Delta E_{J}\sin\left(\varphi_{\Sigma}\right)\sin\left(\varphi+\varphi_{\Delta}\right),\end{split}

(121)

where
\varphi
is the operator of the phase difference across the ATS,

E_{J}=\frac{E_{j_{1}}+E_{j_{2}}}{2},\Delta E_{J}=\frac{E_{j_{1}}-E_{j_{2}}}{2},

and
\varphi_{\Sigma}=\frac{\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}}{2},

\varphi_{\Delta}=\frac{\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}}{2}.

Taking
\varphi_{\Delta}=\pi/2,

\varphi_{\Sigma}=\pi/2+\epsilon\left(t\right)
where
\epsilon\left(t\right)=\epsilon_{0}\cos\left(\omega_{p}t\right)
:

\displaystyle U_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}E_{L,b}\varphi^{2}+2E_{J}\epsilon_{1}\left(t\right)\cos\left(\varphi+\varphi_{\Delta}\right)-2\Delta E_{J}\sin\left(\varphi+\varphi_{\Delta}\right).

Taking
\Delta E_{J}=0
we get
U_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}E_{L,b}\varphi^{2}-2E_{J}\epsilon\left(t\right)\sin\left(\varphi\right),
the full Hamiltonian is then

\displaystyle H=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b-2E_{J}\epsilon\left(t\right)\sin\left(\varphi_{a}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(b+b^{\dagger}\right)\right)

Expanding the sine up to third order yields

\displaystyle H=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b-2E_{J}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left[\varphi_{a}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(b+b^{\dagger}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{E_{J}}{3}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left[\varphi_{a}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(b+b^{\dagger}\right)\right]^{3}.

In the standard two-photon dissipation a drive of
\left(\epsilon_{d}e^{-i\omega_{d}t}+\epsilon_{d}^{*}e^{i\omega_{d}t}\right)\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)
is introduced, in our case we need to introduce instead a time-dependent “beam-splitter” interaction between the modes
\left(\epsilon_{d}e^{-i\omega_{d}t}+\epsilon_{d}^{*}e^{i\omega_{d}t}\right)\left(a^{\dagger}b+h.c.\right).

Moving to the interaction frame with respect to
\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b
we get

\displaystyle H_{\text{rot}}=-2E_{J}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left[\varphi_{a}\left(ae^{-i\omega_{a}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{a}t}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(be^{-i\omega_{b}t}+b^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{b}t}\right)\right]+

\displaystyle\frac{E_{J}}{3}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left[\varphi_{a}\left(ae^{-i\omega_{a}t}+a^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{a}t}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(be^{-i\omega_{b}t}+b^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{b}t}\right)\right]^{3}+

\displaystyle\left(\epsilon_{d}e^{-i\omega_{d}t}+\epsilon_{d}^{*}e^{i\omega_{d}t}\right)\left(a^{\dagger}be^{i\left(\omega_{a}-\omega_{b}\right)t}+h.c.\right).

By taking
\omega_{p}=2\omega_{b}-\omega_{a}
we get that the
b^{\dagger 2}a+h.c.
are non-rotating, and by taking
\omega_{d}=\omega_{b}-\omega_{a}
we obtain a non-rotating
a^{\dagger}b+h.c.
.
Given that
\omega_{b}\neq\omega_{a}
it can be seen that all other terms are fast rotating,
hence the rotating wave approximation leaves us with

\displaystyle H_{\text{RWA}}=g_{2}\left(b^{2}a^{\dagger}+h.c.\right)+g_{1}\left(ba^{\dagger}+h.c.\right)=g_{2}\left[b\left(b+\frac{g_{1}}{g_{2}}\right)a^{\dagger}+h.c.\right]

where
g_{2}=\frac{E_{J}\epsilon_{0}}{2}\varphi_{b}^{2}\varphi_{a}
,
g_{1}=\epsilon_{d}\varphi_{b}\varphi_{a}
.

This protocol requires replacing the drive
\left(\epsilon_{d}e^{-i\omega_{d}t}+\epsilon_{d}^{*}e^{i\omega_{d}t}\right)\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)

with a time dependent coupling
\left(\epsilon_{d}e^{-i\omega_{d}t}+\epsilon_{d}^{*}e^{i\omega_{d}t}\right)\left(a^{\dagger}b+h.c.\right)
which may not be feasible experimentally. We therefore propose another approach with this platform that may be more realistic.
In (
121
) we can choose the following parameters
\varphi_{\Delta}=0,

\varphi_{\Sigma}=\pi/4+\epsilon\left(t\right),\epsilon\left(t\right)\ll 1

and also
\Delta E_{J}>0.
The potential energy, in leading order of
\epsilon\left(t\right),
is then:

\displaystyle U_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{2}E_{L,b}\varphi^{2}-2E_{J}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\epsilon\left(t\right)\right)\cos\left(\varphi\right)-2\Delta E_{J}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1+\epsilon\left(t\right)\right)\sin\left(\varphi\right),

and the Hamiltonian is thus:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b-\sqrt{2}\left(E_{J}\cos\left(\varphi\right)+\Delta E_{J}\sin\left(\varphi\right)\right)+\sqrt{2}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left(E_{J}\cos\left(\varphi\right)-\Delta E_{J}\sin\left(\varphi\right)\right)

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{1}+H_{\epsilon},

where
H_{0}=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b,

H_{1}=-\sqrt{2}\left(E_{J}\cos\left(\varphi\right)+\Delta E_{J}\sin\left(\varphi\right)\right),

and
H_{\epsilon}=\sqrt{2}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left(E_{J}\cos\left(\varphi\right)-\Delta E_{J}\sin\left(\varphi\right)\right).

Let us focus on
H_{\epsilon}
and expand it up to the third order:

\displaystyle H_{\epsilon}=-\sqrt{2}\Delta E_{J}\epsilon\left(t\right)\left[\varphi_{a}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(b+b^{\dagger}\right)-\frac{1}{6}\left[\varphi_{a}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(b+b^{\dagger}\right)\right]^{3}\right]

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\epsilon\left(t\right)E_{J}\frac{\left(\varphi_{a}\left(a+a^{\dagger}\right)+\varphi_{b}\left(b+b^{\dagger}\right)\right)^{2}}{2}.

Note that the since
\cos\left(\varphi\right)
contains
a^{\dagger}a
and
b^{\dagger}b
terms the frequencies
\omega_{a},\omega_{b}
are changed to
\bar{\omega}_{a}=\omega_{a}-\sqrt{2}E_{J}\varphi_{a}^{2}
,
\bar{\omega}_{b}=\omega_{b}-\sqrt{2}E_{J}\varphi_{b}^{2}
.
Moving to the interaction frame with respect to
\bar{\omega}_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\bar{\omega}_{b}b^{\dagger}b
we get that
\epsilon\left(t\right)
is resonant with
\left(a^{2}b^{\dagger}e^{-i\left(2\bar{\omega}_{a}-\bar{\omega}_{b}\right)t}+h.c.\right)
if
\epsilon\left(t\right)
has a frequency of
2\bar{\omega}_{a}-\bar{\omega}_{b}.

In addition,
\epsilon\left(t\right)
is resonant with
\left(ab^{\dagger}e^{-i\left(\bar{\omega}_{a}-\bar{\omega}_{b}\right)t}+h.c.\right)
if
\epsilon\left(t\right)
has a frequency of
\bar{\omega}_{a}-\bar{\omega}_{b}.

We can therefore use
\epsilon\left(t\right)
that has two frequencies:
\epsilon\left(t\right)=\epsilon_{1}\cos\left(\omega_{p,1}t\right)+\epsilon_{2}\cos\left(\omega_{p,2}t\right)
with
\omega_{p,1}=2\bar{\omega}_{a}-\bar{\omega}_{b}
,
\omega_{p,2}=\bar{\omega}_{a}-\bar{\omega}_{b}.

It should be checked now that all other terms are fast rotating and can be therefore neglected.
In
H_{\epsilon}
all other terms rotate as
\bar{\omega}_{a/b},2\bar{\omega}_{a/b},3\bar{\omega}_{a/b}
hence
\epsilon\left(t\right)
is not resonant with any of these terms.
H_{1}
does not contain any
\epsilon\left(t\right)
hence all its terms are fast rotating. We therefore obtain
H_{\text{RWA}}=g_{2}\left(b^{2}a^{\dagger}+h.c.\right)+g_{1}\left(ba^{\dagger}+h.c.\right)
with
g_{2}=\Delta E_{J}\epsilon_{1}\frac{\varphi_{b}^{2}\varphi_{a}}{\sqrt{2}}
,

g_{1}=\sqrt{2}E_{J}\epsilon_{2}\varphi_{a}\varphi_{b}
.

For
r>1
higher-orders of
\sin\left(\varphi\right),\cos\left(\varphi\right)
should be used with suitable frequency matching conditions.
