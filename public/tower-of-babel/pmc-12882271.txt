Effectiveness and user satisfaction of an online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform on blood smear diagnostic skills
Authors: Peichao Zhu, Yingbo Miao, Wei Zhou, Qiang Zhao, Huijing Wu, Chong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness and user satisfaction of an online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform designed to enhance the blood smear morphology diagnostic skills of hospital laboratory technicians.MethodsWe developed an online platform incorporating machine learning algorithms for automated recognition and classification of blood cell morphology. The platform allows users to assess cell types and provides real-time feedback. We conducted a multi-center study involving 1,034 registered users from various hospitals. Introduction

Blood smear examination is a fundamental diagnostic tool in hematology, providing valuable information for the diagnosis and management of various blood disorders [1]. It involves the microscopic evaluation of stained blood films to assess the morphology of red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. Accurate interpretation of blood smear morphology requires extensive training and experience, as well as continuous education to maintain competency [2].

Despite its importance, blood smear examination is often challenging due to the complexity and variability of cell morphology, particularly in the presence of pathological changes. The manual nature of the task also makes it time-consuming and prone to inter-observer variability [3]. These limitations have led to the development of automated morphology recognition systems using machine learning techniques, such as support vector machines (SVMs), random forests (RFs), and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [4, 5]. These systems aim to improve the efficiency, consistency, and accuracy of blood smear analysis by automatically classifying cells and detecting morphological abnormalities.

However, the adoption of automated systems in clinical practice has been hindered by several factors, including concerns about their reliability, interpretability, and potential impact on the diagnostic skills of laboratory professionals [6]. Moreover, the lack of standardized datasets and evaluation protocols makes it difficult to compare the performance of different systems and assess their generalizability [7]. To address this, the online platform integrates a multicenter, expert-validated dataset with standardized annotations and employs uniform evaluation protocols (e.g., pre/post-test case sets and a benchmark dataset), enabling objective comparison of system performance and generalizability assessment.

To address these challenges, there has been growing interest in developing online educational platforms for blood cell morphology [8, 9]. These platforms leverage digital technologies to provide accessible, interactive, and personalized learning resources for laboratory professionals. They often include features such as virtual microscopy, case-based learning, and self-assessment tools to facilitate knowledge acquisition and skills development.

Despite the potential benefits of online morphology education platforms, their effectiveness in improving the diagnostic skills of hospital laboratory technicians and their impact on user satisfaction remain largely unexplored. Furthermore, there is a need to compare the performance of these platforms with that of automated morphology recognition systems to assess their relative strengths and limitations.

In this study, we developed an online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform incorporating machine learning algorithms for automated recognition and classification of blood cell morphology. We aimed to evaluate the platform’s effectiveness in improving users’ diagnostic accuracy and assess user satisfaction through a multi-center study involving hospital laboratory technicians. We also compared the platform’s performance with other state-of-the-art automated morphology recognition systems using a benchmark dataset.

Methods

The online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform was developed using a machine learning-based approach for automated recognition and classification of blood cell morphology. The platform’s architecture consists of three main components: image processing, feature extraction, and classification.

In this study, the term “Online platform” refers to a comprehensive system that integrates multiple machine learning algorithms (SVM, Random Forest, Deep Neural Network, and InceptionV3) with an interactive educational interface. This integrated approach combines automated cell morphology recognition with a structured learning environment, allowing users to not only analyze blood smear images but also improve their diagnostic skills through interactive features.

The image processing module involves several steps to enhance the quality and consistency of blood smear images. First, color normalization is applied to reduce variations in staining intensity and illuminate conditions across different samples. This is achieved by converting the images from the RGB color space to the LAB color space and applying a global contrast stretching algorithm [10]. Next, the images are segmented to isolate individual cells from the background using a combination of thresholding, morphological operations, and watershed transform [11]. The segmented cells are then cropped and resized to a uniform size for further analysis.

The feature extraction module aims to capture the relevant morphological characteristics of the segmented cells. It employs a hybrid approach combining hand-crafted features and deep learning-based features. The hand-crafted features include geometric parameters (e.g., area, perimeter, circularity), color-based features (e.g., mean intensity, standard deviation), and texture features (e.g., local binary patterns, Haralick features) [12]. These features are computed using traditional image processing techniques and provide a comprehensive representation of the cell morphology.

In addition to the hand-crafted features, deep learning-based features are extracted using a pre-trained CNN model. Specifically, we used the InceptionV3 model, chosen for its superior performance in medical image classification, efficient computational architecture, and ability to learn hierarchical features from cellular images, which has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art performance on various image classification tasks [13]. The model was pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset and fine-tuned on a large dataset of blood cell images. The output of the last global average pooling layer is used as a feature vector, capturing high-level semantic information about the cell morphology.

The classification module takes the extracted features as input and predicts the cell type and the presence of morphological abnormalities. It employs an ensemble of three machine learning algorithms: SVM, RF, and DNN, selected for their complementary strengths: SVM excels in high-dimensional feature spaces with interpretability, RF is robust to noise and provides feature importance rankings, and DNN captures complex patterns from deep features. The SVM classifier uses a radial basis function (RBF) kernel and is trained using a one-versus-one strategy for multi-class classification [14]. The RF classifier consists of an ensemble of decision trees, each trained on a random subset of features and samples [15]. The DNN classifier is a multilayer perceptron with three hidden layers and a softmax output layer [16]. The ensemble predictions are obtained by majority voting, which has been shown to improve the robustness and generalizability of the classification results [17].

The platform’s user interface is designed to provide an intuitive and engaging learning experience. It allows users to view blood smear images, assess cell types, and receive real-time feedback on their performance. The system also provides educational resources, including tutorials, case studies, and quizzes, to facilitate self-directed learning. The user’s progress and performance are tracked over time, enabling personalized recommendations and adaptive learning features (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Study design and participants

We conducted a multi-center prospective study to evaluate the effectiveness and user satisfaction of the online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the participating hospitals, and all participants provided informed consent.

The study participants were recruited from the clinical hematology laboratories of 12 tertiary hospitals in China. The inclusion criteria were: (1) working as a full-time laboratory technician in the hematology department, (2) having at least one year of experience in blood smear examination, and (3) willing to use the online platform for self-study and self-assessment for a period of three months. The exclusion criteria were: (1) having received specific training in blood cell morphology within the past six months, and (2) currently using other online morphology education platforms.

A total of 1,034 users participated in the study, recruited from diverse healthcare settings: 226 (21.8%) from tertiary hospitals, 214 (20.7%) from secondary hospitals, 356 (34.4%) from private laboratories/clinics, and 238 (23.1%) from primary hospitals. Their experience was stratified as novice (< 5 years, 39.8%, mostly from primary/private settings), intermediate (5–10 years, 37.4%), and expert (> 10 years, 22.8%, mostly from tertiary hospitals), ensuring representation of varying practice environments and skill levels. They were provided with access to the online platform and instructed to use it for self-study and self-assessment at their own pace. The participants were encouraged to complete at least 20 cases per week and to participate in the discussion forums and quizzes. They were also asked to complete a set of 50 assessment cases at the beginning and end of the study period to evaluate their diagnostic accuracy.

Data collection

We collected the following data during the study period:

Baseline characteristics: The participants’ demographic information, educational background, and years of experience in hematology laboratory were obtained through a self-administered questionnaire (Supplementary File 1).

Platform usage: The platform automatically recorded the frequency and duration of each participant’s use, as well as the number of cases completed, quizzes attempted, and discussion posts made.

Diagnostic accuracy: The participants’ accuracy in classifying cells and identifying morphological abnormalities was assessed using a set of 50 validated cases at baseline and after three months of platform use. The cases were selected from a 500-case database of blood smear images annotated by hematopathologists (≥ 10 years of experience) from 12 hospitals. The database includes 300 normal and 200 pathological cases (e.g., NSTEMI), categorized into easy (≥ 85% historical accuracy), intermediate (60–85%), and hard (< 60%) levels based on WHO-aligned criteria. During the study, 50 unique cases were used for baseline and post-intervention assessments, with 100–200 new cases added annually to expand the dataset. The case database was developed through collaboration between hematopathologists from 12 participating tertiary hospitals and the research team. Hematopathologists with at least 10 years of clinical experience annotated blood smear images and clinical data to establish a standardized dataset. The annotation process included cell type labeling, morphological feature description, and clinical correlation (e.g., linking abnormal cell morphology to specific hematological disorders such as non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI)). The participants’ responses were compared with the ground truth labels (the gold standard annotations for cell classification and morphological abnormality detection, established through a rigorous expert consensus process) to calculate the overall accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity for each cell type and abnormality.

User satisfaction: At the end of the study period, the participants were asked to complete a user satisfaction survey using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The survey consisted of 20 items covering various aspects of the platform, including ease of use, content quality, interactivity, feedback, and perceived impact on knowledge and skills. The participants were also asked to provide free-text comments and suggestions for improvement.

Comparison with automated systems: To compare the performance of the online platform with that of other automated morphology recognition systems, we used a pre-validated benchmark dataset of 1,000 blood smear images (previously validated in References [18–21]), annotated by a panel of hematopathologists and verified across clinical sites to ensure diagnostic accuracy. The dataset consisted of equal numbers of normal and abnormal cases, with a variety of cell types and morphological features. We applied our platform’s classification module to the dataset and computed the overall accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. We then compared these metrics with the reported performance of other state-of-the-art systems using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc pairwise comparisons.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics, platform usage, and user satisfaction of the study participants. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

The diagnostic accuracy of the participants was analyzed using paired t-tests to compare the overall accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity between baseline and post-intervention. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the impact of participant characteristics (e.g., years of experience, educational background) on the improvement in diagnostic accuracy.

The platform’s performance on the benchmark dataset was compared with that of other automated systems using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Platform usage

During the three-month study period, the participants used the platform for an average of 2.5 ± 1.2 h per week and completed a median of 38 (IQR: 25–52) cases. The most frequently accessed educational resources were tutorials on cell identification (85%), case studies (72%), and quizzes (68%). The participants posted a total of 1,526 comments and questions in the discussion forums, with an average of 1.5 ± 2.3 posts per participant. Subgroup analyses showed no significant differences in platform usage frequency or resource engagement across educational backgrounds (associate/bachelor’s/master’s degree) or experience levels (years of practice). However, participants with less than 5 years of experience demonstrated a significantly greater improvement in diagnostic accuracy (92.1% vs. 87.3% for those with ≥ 5 years, p = 0.02), likely reflecting their increased utilization of basic tutorials and structured quizzes. The platform’s adaptive design dynamically adjusted case difficulty and learning paths based on user performance, ensuring equitable access to resources across all subgroups.

Diagnostic accuracy

The participants’ diagnostic accuracy for classifying cells and identifying morphological abnormalities significantly improved after using the platform for three months (Table 2). The overall accuracy increased from 65.3% at baseline to 89.6% at post-intervention (p < 0.001). The sensitivity and specificity also improved significantly for all cell types and abnormalities (p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that an online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform incorporating machine learning algorithms can effectively improve the blood smear diagnostic skills of hospital laboratory technicians. The platform’s interactive features, real-time feedback, and comprehensive educational content contributed to its high user satisfaction and significant impact on participants’ diagnostic accuracy.

The significant improvement in diagnostic accuracy after using the platform for three months highlights its effectiveness as a learning tool. The platform’s ability to provide immediate feedback and explanations, along with the variety and relevance of the cases, likely played a key role in this improvement. The subgroup analysis showing a greater improvement in accuracy among less experienced participants suggests that the platform may be particularly beneficial for novice technicians or those with limited exposure to diverse cases [22]. Additionally, accuracy improvement correlated with case completion volume (r = 0.31), and discussion forum engagement showed a trend toward enhanced learning, highlighting the value of practice and peer interaction.

The high user satisfaction scores reflect the platform’s user-centric design and engaging learning experiences. The participants appreciated the flexibility of self-paced learning, the interactivity of the platform, and the quality of the educational content. The suggestions for improvement, such as including more challenging cases and gamification elements, underscore the importance of continuously updating and adapting the platform to meet the evolving needs and preferences of the users.

The comparison of the platform’s performance with that of other automated morphology recognition systems on the benchmark dataset supports its validity and potential for clinical application. The platform’s accuracy was comparable to that of deep learning-based systems, which are considered the state-of-the-art in this field [23]. This finding suggests that the platform’s machine learning algorithms, which combine hand-crafted features and deep learning-based features, can achieve a level of performance similar to that of more complex and computationally intensive systems [12].

However, it is important to note that automated systems, including the online platform, should be used as assistive tools rather than replacements for human expertise. The interpretation of blood smear morphology requires the integration of clinical context, patient history, and other laboratory findings, which may not be fully captured by machine learning algorithms. Therefore, the platform should be used in conjunction with traditional teaching methods, expert consultation, and clinical correlation to optimize learning outcomes and patient care.

The strengths of this study include the large sample size, the multi-center design, and the comprehensive evaluation of the platform’s effectiveness, user satisfaction, and performance compared with other automated systems. The use of a validated benchmark dataset for the comparison with other systems enhances the reliability and generalizability of the findings.

However, the study also has some limitations. First, the lack of a control group limits the ability to attribute the improvement in diagnostic accuracy solely to the use of the platform. Second, the study did not assess the long-term retention of the acquired skills or the impact of the platform on clinical outcomes. Third, the study was conducted in a single country, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings and populations.

While some individual deep learning models showed marginally higher accuracy in benchmark tests, our integrated platform offers several practical advantages. First, the ensemble approach provides more robust and consistent performance across diverse real-world samples. Second, the platform’s architecture allows for better interpretability of results, which is crucial for educational purposes. Third, the computational requirements are more suitable for online deployment and real-time feedback. Most importantly, the platform’s performance in actual clinical settings has shown remarkable stability and reliability, with user feedback confirming its effectiveness as a learning tool.

Future studies should employ randomized controlled designs with longer follow-up periods to evaluate the platform’s effectiveness compared with traditional teaching methods and its impact on patient outcomes. The platform’s performance should also be validated in different clinical settings and geographic regions to assess its generalizability. Furthermore, qualitative studies exploring the users’ experiences and perceptions of the platform could provide valuable insights for its continuous improvement and optimization.

Platform availability and accessibility

The online cell morphology self-study platform developed in this study is designed to be accessible to medical institutions and educational organizations. Interested parties can contact the corresponding authors for detailed information about platform licensing, implementation, and customization. We are committed to working with healthcare institutions to adapt the platform to their specific training needs. Moreover, we plan to make a basic version of the platform available through an open-access repository, which will facilitate wider adoption and further research in medical education technology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that an online cell morphology self-study and self-test platform incorporating machine learning algorithms is an effective and well-accepted tool for improving the blood smear diagnostic skills of hospital laboratory technicians. The platform’s high user satisfaction, significant impact on diagnostic accuracy, and comparable performance to state-of-the-art automated systems support its potential for widespread adoption in clinical practice. The integration of this platform into the training and continuing education of laboratory professionals, along with traditional teaching methods and expert consultation, could enhance the quality and efficiency of blood smear morphology assessment and ultimately improve patient care.
