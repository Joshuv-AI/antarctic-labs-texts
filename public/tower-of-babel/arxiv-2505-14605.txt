On the Mathematical Theory of Quantum Stochastic Filtering Equations for Mixed States Thanks: The paper was supported by RSF research grant (project no. 24-11-00123)
Vassili N. Kolokoltsov

Abstract
Quantum filtering equations for mixed states were developed in 80th of the last century. Since then the problem of building a rigorous mathematical theory for these equations in the basic infinite-dimensional settings has been a challenging open mathematical problem. In a previous paper, the author developed the theory of these equations in the case of bounded coupling operators, including a new version that arises as the law of large numbers for interacting particles under continuous observation and thus leading to the theory of quantum mean field games. In this paper, the main body of these results is extended to the basic cases of unbounded coupling operators.



Keywords:
quantum stochastic master equation, stochastic Lindblad equation,
quantum stochastic filtering, Belavkin’s equations,
quantum interacting particle systems, singular SDEs in Banach spaces.

MSC classification:
60H15, 60K35, 81P15, 81Q93, 93E11

1.2
Main objects of analysis

In this paper, the letters
H
and
L=(L^{1},\cdots,L^{n})
denote linear operators in a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where
H
is self-adjoint and is referred to as
the Hamiltonian. The vector-valued
L
is closed and densely defined with densely defined adjoint
L^{*}
. It describes the coupling of a quantum system with a measurement device.
We shall assume also that the operator
\sum_{k}(L^{k})^{*}L^{k}
is densely defined and closed
(and consequently symmetric).

We use the notations

L_{S}=(L+L^{*})/2=(L^{S1},\cdots,L^{Sn}),\quad L_{A}=(L-L^{*})/2i=(L^{A1},\cdots,L^{An}).

We write
\,Re\,z=z_{R}
and
Im\,z=z_{I}
for the real and imaginary parts of a complex number or a vector,
write
Dom(A)
for the domain of an operator
A
, use the brackets
[A,B]

and
\{A,B\}
to denote the commutator and anti-commutator of operators
A,B
.
By
\|A\|
we denote
the standard operator norm of a bounded operator
A
in
\mathcal{H}
. The norm of an operator
A
from a Banach space
B_{1}
to a Banach space
B_{2}
will be denoted
\|A\|_{B_{1}\to B_{2}}

and simply
\|A\|_{B}
for operators
B\to B
. The letter
\mathbf{E}
is used to denote the expectation, and we write
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}
to stress that the expectation is taken with respect to the measure
\mathbf{P}
.

Remark 1
.

Most of our results have an extension to the case of
time dependent families
L(t)
, but we do not give details here.

The quantum filtering equation describing
the stochastic evolution of pure states
(vectors in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
) under continuous measurement
of a diffusive type can be written in two versions:

(i) as the
linear Belavkin quantum filtering SDE (stochastic differential equation)
for a non-normalized state:

d\chi(t)=-[iH\chi(t)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(L^{j})^{*}L^{j}\chi(t)]\,dt+\sum_{j}L^{j}\chi(t)dY_{j}(t),

(1)

where
\chi(t)\in\mathcal{H}
and
Y(t)=(Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n})(t)
is an
n
-dimensional Brownian motion (BM) referred to as the
output process
;

(ii) as the
nonlinear Belavkin quantum filtering SDE

for the normalized state
\phi(t)
:

d\phi(t)=\sum_{j}(L^{j}-(\phi(t),L^{Sj}\phi(t)))\phi(t)\,dB_{j}(t)

-[i(H-\sum_{j}(\phi(t),L^{Sj}\phi(t))L^{Aj})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(L^{j}-(\phi(t),L^{Sj}\phi(t)))^{*}(L^{j}-(\phi(t),L^{Sj}\phi(t)))]\phi(t)\,dt,

(2)

where
B(t)=(B_{1},\cdots,B_{n})(t)
is an
n
-dimensional Brownian motion
(BM) referred to as the
innovation process
.

Of course the rigorous form of these equations is the integral one. Say, equation (
1
) with initial vector
\chi_{0}
writes down as

\chi(t)=\chi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(-iH-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(L^{j})^{*}L^{j})\chi(s)ds+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}L^{j}\chi(s)\,dY_{j}(s).

(3)

Remark 2
.

In the literature one finds also a version of these equations with an infinite number of
L^{j}
. We decided here to work with finite
n
to make our exposition more transparent (avoiding just additional technical complications).

Equation (
1
) is clearly well posed in the case of bounded
H
and
L
. Moreover, one checks by direct application of Ito’s formula that if
\chi
satisfies (
1
), then

d\|\chi(t)\|^{2}=2\sum_{j}(\chi(t),L^{Sj}\chi(t))dY_{j}(t),

(4)

and the normalized vectors

\phi(t)=\chi(t)/\|\chi(t)\|
satisfy (
2
) with

dB_{j}(t)=dY_{j}(t)-2(\phi(t),L^{Sj}\phi(t))\,dt.

(5)

Thus, by Girsanov’s theorem, if
Y(t)
is a BM on a certain stochastic basis

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{t},\mathbf{P})
, then
B(t)
is a BM on the basis
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{t},\mathbf{Q})
s.t.

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}\xi=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}(\|\chi(t)\|^{2}\xi)

(6)

for
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable
\xi
.

If one agrees to understand all products of operator expressions
as appropriate inner products
(sum over available indices), for instance, writing
L^{*}L
instead of
\sum_{j}(L^{j})^{*}L^{j}
,
and
L\chi\,dY(t)
instead of
\sum_{j}L^{j}\chi\,dY_{j}(t)
,
one can write all equations above in a simpler form,
say the main equations (
1
) and (
2
) will look like

d\chi(t)=-[iH\chi(t)+\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L\chi(t)]\,dt+L\chi(t)dY(t),

(7)

and, respectively,

d\phi(t)=-[i(H-(\phi(t),L_{S}\phi(t))L_{A})+\frac{1}{2}(L-(\phi(t),L_{S}\phi(t)))^{*}(L-(\phi(t),L_{S}\phi(t)))]\phi(t)\,dt

+(L-(\phi(t),L_{S}\phi(t)))\phi(t)\,dB(t).

(8)

We will mostly use this short way of writing having in mind more detailed versions above.

The derivation of (
8
) from the linear equation, given above,
suggests that it is meant to describe evolutions of vectors with a unit norm.
It is often convenient to include it in a more general class of norm-preserving evolutions, the simplest version being

d\phi(t)=-[i(H-\langle L_{S}\rangle_{\phi(t)}L_{A})+\frac{1}{2}(L-\langle L_{S}\rangle_{\phi(t)})^{*}(L-\langle L_{S}\rangle_{\phi(t)})]\phi(t)\,dt

+(L-\langle L_{S}\rangle_{\phi(t)})\phi(t)\,dB(t),

(9)

where we introduced the (rather standard) notation for the value
of an operator
A
in a pure state
\phi
:

\langle A\rangle_{\phi}=\frac{(\phi,A\phi)}{(\phi,\phi)}.

Clearly for
\phi(t)
of unit norm solutions to equations (
8
)
and (
9
) coincide, but (
9
) is explicitly norm-preserving for arbitrary
\phi(t)
, which is not the case for equation (
8
).

Equation (
7
) simplifies essentially in the most important case of self-adjoint
L
, as it takes the form

d\phi(t)=-[iH+\frac{1}{2}(L-\langle L\rangle_{\phi(t)})^{2}]\phi(t)\,dt+(L-\langle L\rangle_{\phi(t)})\phi(t)\,dB(t).

(10)

It is also insightful to write down equation for
\chi

in terms of the innovation process
B
:

d\chi(t)=-[iH\chi(t)+\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L\chi(t)]\,dt+L\chi(t)\,(dB(t)+\langle L+L^{*}\rangle_{\chi(t)}\,dt).

(11)

For unbounded
H,L
, the meaning of all these equations is of course not obvious.

Recall that the density matrix or density operator
\gamma
corresponding to a unit vector

\chi\in\mathcal{H}
is defined as the orthogonal projection operator on
\chi
.
This operator is usually expressed either as the tensor product

\gamma=\chi\otimes\bar{\chi}
(with the usual identification of
H\otimes H

with the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators in
\mathcal{H}
) or in the most common
for physics bra-ket Dirac’s notation as
\gamma=|\chi\rangle\langle\chi|
.

As one checks by direct application of Ito’s formula, (i) if
\chi(t)\in\mathcal{H}

satisfies (
7
), then the corresponding operator
\gamma=\chi\otimes\bar{\chi}

satisfies the
linear stochastic quantum master equation
or
linear Belavkin’s quantum filtering equation for mixed states

d\gamma(t)=-i[H,\gamma(t)]\,dt+\mathcal{L}_{L}\gamma(t)\,dt+(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*})dY(t),

(12)

with

\mathcal{L}_{L}\gamma=L\gamma L^{*}-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\gamma L^{*}L=L\gamma L^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{*}L,\gamma\};

and (ii) if
\phi(t)
satisfies (
2
), then the corresponding matrices

\rho=\phi\otimes\bar{\phi}
satisfies the
nonlinear stochastic quantum master equation

or
nonlinear Belavkin’s quantum filtering equation

d\rho(t)=-i[H,\rho(t)]\,dt+\mathcal{L}_{L}\rho(t)\,dt+[L\rho(t)+\rho(t)L^{*}-\rho(t)\,{\rm{tr}}\,(L\rho(t)+\rho(t)L^{*})]dB(t).

(13)

The well posedness of these master equations (not necessarily for solutions of form
\chi\otimes\bar{\chi}
) and of their nonlinear extensions for interacting particle systems were proved in
[
28
]
in case of bounded
L
. The objective of the present paper is to extend the main body of these results to (practically important) cases of unbounded
L
. Notice that the arguments of the present paper use the ideas from
[
28
]
, but the results are essentially independent, because the assumption of boundedness of
L
allows for certain specific estimates that are absent in the unbounded case.

As an important property of the stochastic equations above, let us observe that taking expectation from both sides of (
12
) shows (at least heuristically in infinite dimensional case) that
\hat{\gamma}(t)=\mathbf{E}\gamma(t)
satisfies the
quantum master equation

or
Lindblad equation

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\gamma}(t)=-i[H,\hat{\gamma}(t)]+\mathcal{L}_{L}\hat{\gamma}(t).

(14)

Solutions to these equations generating quantum dynamic semigroups were attentively studied in the literature, see
[
32
]
and numerous references therein.

1.3
Content

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
we first recall some known facts
on the quantum filtering SDEs (stochastic differential equations) for pure states, often providing simplified proofs. We discuss two approaches, a more abstract one and another based on some explicit solutions, the latter giving more insight in the behavior of the stochastic dynamics. Finally we prove our first result concerning the well-posedness of the nonlinear Belavkin’s equation for pure states in the strong (probabilistic) sense, while previously only probabilistically weak solutions were developed.

In Section
3
we build the theory of the linear quantum filtering SDE (
12
). We follow the approach from
[
28
]
considering these equations in the Hilbert space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators. This approach leads necessarily to SDEs with singular coefficients, but allows one to avoid working with the inconvenient (from the point of view of stochastic calculus) Banach space of trace-class operators. By passing we note that the well known result on the well-posedness and conservativity of dynamic quantum semigroups given by equation (
14
), is a direct consequence of our present theory on the corresponding quantum stochastic equations.

In Section
4
, armed with the results of the previous sections, we address the most nontrivial object of this paper: nonlinear quantum filtering equations
for mixed states (
13
), and obtain the full well-posedness result for these equations, first in the weak (probabilistically) and then in the strong sense.

2
Dynamics of pure states

2.1
Linear equation: general results

In this section we collect some mostly known facts about
the linear quantum filtering equations for pure states. We shall present the well posedness of the linear Belavkin equation with unbounded coefficients in two settings: (1) the most general one due to Mora-Rebolledo based on the introduction of the so called control operator and with ideas going back to Holevo and Fagnola-Chebotarev) and (2) a more specific one arising from explicit Green functions of the standard stochastic Schrödinger equation describing continuously observed momentum or position.

Starting with the first approach we introduce the following
Hypothesis due to Mora-Rebolledo, though in a slightly reduced (and more intuitive) form,
which appears in all applications.

Hypothesis MR
. Suppose there exists a (strictly) positive self-adjoint linear operator
C:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
with discrete spectrum
0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
, the corresponding orthonormal basis of eigenvectors
\{e_{m}\}
, subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{m}
generated by the first
m
eigenvectors and orthogonal projections
P_{m}
on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
. On the domain
Dom(C)
of
C
one can define the corresponding
C
-norm

\|x\|_{C}^{2}=(\|x\|^{2}+\|Cx\|^{2})^{1/2}
. It is then assumed that
Dom(C)\subset Dom(H)\cap Dom(L)\cap Dom(L^{*}L)
and therefore (according to the closed graph theorem) there exists a constant
K
such that

\|Hx\|^{2}\leq K\|x\|^{2}_{C},\quad\|L^{*}Lx\|^{2}\leq K\|x\|_{C}^{2},

(15)

for all
x\in Dom(C)
, and consequently

\|Lx\|^{2}\leq\sqrt{K}\|x\|\,\|x\|_{C}.

(16)

Finally, the following generalized
dissipativity with respect to

C
is assumed: either

-2Re\,(Cx,iCP_{m}Hx)-Re\,(Cx,CP_{m}L^{*}Lx)+\|CP_{m}Lx\|^{2}\leq\alpha(\|x\|_{C}^{2}+\beta),

(17)

or (this second version is noted bypassing as Remark 21 in
[
33
]
)

-2Re\,(Cx,iCP_{m}Hx)-Re\,(Cx,CP_{m}L^{*}P_{m}Lx)+\|CP_{m}Lx\|^{2}\leq\alpha(\|x\|_{C}^{2}+\beta),

(18)

hold for any
m
,
x\in\mathcal{H}_{m}
and some constants
\alpha,\beta>0
.

Let us stress again that we use our reduced notations with the summation tacitly assumed,
so that, e.g.,
L^{*}L
means
\sum_{k}(L^{k})^{*}L^{k}
and
\|Lx\|^{2}=\sum_{k}\|L^{k}x\|^{2}
.

Conditions (
17
) and (
18
) are similar and often hold simultaneously. However, (
17
) is usually easier to check, because
it is equivalent (under all other conditions) to the inequality

-2Re\,(Cx,iCHx)-Re\,(Cx,CL^{*}Lx)+\|CLx\|^{2}\leq\alpha(\|x\|_{C}^{2}+\beta),

(19)

holding for all
x\in\cup_{m}\mathcal{H}_{m}
(not involving any projections). In fact,
(
19
) follows from (
17
) passing to the limit as
m\to\infty
, and
(
19
) implies (
17
), because

-2Re\,(Cx,iCP_{m}Hx)-Re\,(Cx,CP_{m}L^{*}Lx)

=-2Re\,(Cx,iCHx)-Re\,(Cx,CL^{*}Lx),\quad x\in\mathcal{H}_{m}.

On the other hand, (
18
) is more convenient for working with nonlinear equations,
as will be seen later on.

Given BM
Y(t)
on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{t},\mathbf{P})
,
a
C
-
strong solution
of equation (
3
) on a time-interval
[0,T]
is defined as an adapted continuous
\mathcal{H}
-valued process
\chi(t)
s.t. (i) its norm is non-increasing, that is,
\mathbf{E}\|\chi(t)\|^{2}\leq\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}
, (ii)
\chi(t)\in Dom(C)
a.s. and

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbf{E}\|C\chi(t)\|^{2}<\infty,

(iii) equation (
3
) holds.

Remark 3
.

To be more precise, one introduces the mapping
\pi_{C}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
s.t.
\pi_{C}(x)
equals
x
or
0
for
x\in Dom(C)
and otherwise, respectively, and one writes
\pi_{C}(\chi(t))
instead of
\chi(t)
everywhere in the equation. We shall assume that this is done whenever necessary, but we shall not explicitly use this
\pi_{C}
in our notation.

The following result is the combination of Theorems 4 and 17 from
[
33
]

(given here under some simplifying assumption, e.g. assuming only a finite number of
L_{j}
).

Theorem 2.1
.

(Mora-Rebolledo) Under Hypothesis MR, for any
\chi_{0}\in Dom(C)
there exists a unique
C
-strong solution
\chi(t)
to equation (
3
), and moreover, this solution satisfies the estimate

\mathbf{E}\|C\chi(t)\|^{2}\leq e^{\alpha t}[\|C\chi_{0}\|^{2}+\alpha t(\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}+\beta)],

(20)

and is conservative in the sense that
\mathbf{E}\|\chi(t)\|^{2}=\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}
,
so that
\|\chi(t)\|^{2}
is a positive martingale.

Remark 4
.

Theorem
2.1
extends several previous contributions by other authors (see e.g.
[
5
]
,
[
21
]
and references therein), some of them being inspired by the works on the conservativity of quantum dynamic semigroups from
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
.
For other classes of stochastic Schrödinger equation we can refer to
[
2
]
and references therein.

Paper
[
33
]
contains a detailed non-trivial and elegant proof. We shall sketch here an essentially simplified argument assuming an additional condition that usually holds in examples.

Hypothesis A
: (a) There exists
l\in\mathbf{N}
such that
L:\mathcal{H}_{m}\to\mathcal{H}_{m+l}
,

H:\mathcal{H}_{m}\to\mathcal{H}_{m+l}
for all
m
and (b)
Dom(\sqrt{C})\subset Dom(H)
, and therefore (according to the closed graph theorem) there exists a constant
K^{\prime}
such that

\|Hx\|^{2}\leq K^{\prime}\|x\|^{2}_{\sqrt{C}}\leq 2K^{\prime}\|x\|\,\|x\|_{C},

(21)

Proof.

First of all, uniqueness of solution is more or less straightforward under Hypothesis MR:
one writes down the equation for the difference
\Delta(t)
of two solutions and observes that
\|\Delta(t)\|^{2}
is a positive local martingale (here is an important detail to note: it is not seen apriori that it is a martingale), then one introduces the stopping time
\tau_{n}
when
\|\Delta(t)\|
reaches the level
n
and concludes that
\Delta_{t\wedge\tau_{n}}=0
for all
n
, and then finally that
\Delta(t)=0
.

The main point is really the existence, which is proved via approximations. Namely, under (
17
), one considers the solutions to the equation

\chi_{m}(t)=P_{m}\chi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}P_{m}(-iH-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L)\chi_{m}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}P_{m}L\chi_{m}(s)\,dY(s),

(22)

and, under (
18
), one considers the solutions to the equation

\chi_{m}(t)=P_{m}\chi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}P_{m}(-iH-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}P_{m}L)\chi_{m}(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}P_{m}L\chi_{m}(s)\,dY(s),

(23)

in
\mathcal{H}_{m}
. Everything is well-posed in this finite-dimensional setting. Moreover,
writing down the equations for
C\chi_{m}
(say, under (
23
)) one obtains

d\,\mathbf{E}\,\|C\chi_{m}\|^{2}=2\,Re\,(CP_{m}(-iH-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}P_{m}L)\chi_{m},CP_{m}\chi_{m})\,dt+(CP_{m}L\chi_{m},CP_{m}L\chi_{m})\,dt.

By Hypothesis MR and Gronwall’s Lemma, it follows
that
\chi_{m}
satisfies (
20
) for all
m
:

\mathbf{E}\|C\chi_{m}(t)\|^{2}\leq e^{\alpha t}[\|C\chi_{0}\|^{2}+\alpha t(\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}+\beta)].

(24)

So the main point in the proof is to show that
\chi_{m}
(or its subsequence) converges (in some sense), as
m\to\infty
, and that the limit is the solution required.

Remark 5
.

The proof of
[
33
]
goes as follows. First it is shown that there exists a weakly converging subsequence
\chi_{m_{k}}
(in the Hilbert space of square integrable
\mathcal{H}
-valued random variables), then by accurate consideration of duality it is shown that the limit satisfies equation (
3
), and then rather elaborate stopping arguments (which are improved versions of the arguments from
[
21
]
) allows one to show that this solution is conservative, and finally one concludes that subsequence
\chi_{m_{k}}
converges strongly. We shall prove here directly the strong convergence of
\chi_{m}
, even including the rates of convergence, though assuming additionally Hypothesis A.

We shall work with approximations (
23
). This equation, considered in space
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, coincides with the basic equation
(
3
), if one chooses as operators
H
and
L
their finite-dimensional approximations
H_{m}=P_{m}HP_{m}
and
L_{m}=P_{m}LP_{m}
, respectively. As was mentioned, finite-dimensional equations of this kind are well understood and it is known that they are conservative:
\mathbf{E}\|\chi_{m}(t)\|^{2}=\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}
. In fact, to see that this is the case, one writes down the equation for
\|\chi\|^{2}
and observes that it is a martingale.

Subtracting equations for
m
and
k
we obtain (omitting argument
t
for brevity)

d(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m})=-[i(H_{k}-H_{m})+\frac{1}{2}(L_{k}^{*}L_{k}-L_{m}^{*}L_{m})]\chi_{k}\,dt+(L_{k}-L_{m})\chi_{k}\,dY(t)

-(iH_{m}+\frac{1}{2}L_{m}^{*}L_{m})(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m})\,dt+L_{m}(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m})\,dY(t).

And therefore

d\,\mathbf{E}\,(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m})^{2}=-2\,\mathbf{E}\,(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m},i(H_{k}-H_{m})\chi_{k})_{R}\,dt

+\mathbf{E}\,(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m},(L_{k}^{*}L_{k}-L_{m}^{*}L_{m})\chi_{k})_{R}\,dt+\mathbf{E}\,\|(L_{k}-L_{m})\chi_{k}\|^{2}\,dt

+2\,\mathbf{E}\,((L_{k}-L_{m})\chi_{k},L_{m}(\chi_{k}-\chi_{m}))_{R}\,dt,

(25)

because the terms not containing differences
L_{k}-L_{m}
or
H_{k}-H_{m}
nicely cancel out.

To estimate the right hand side we need the following observation. If
C
is as assumed in Hypothesis MR and
A
is an operator such that
A:\mathcal{H}_{m}\to\mathcal{H}_{m+l}
for all
m
and
\|Ax\|^{2}\leq R\|x\|\,\|x\|_{C}
for
x\in Dom(C)
and a constant
R
, then

\|(A_{k}-A_{m})x\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2R}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m-l+1}}}\|x\|_{C},\quad\|(A-A_{m})x\|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2R}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m-l+1}}}\|x\|_{C},

(26)

for
k>m>l
, where
A_{q}=P_{q}AP_{q}
. In fact, say, dealing with the second estimate,

\|(A-A_{m})x\|^{2}=\|(A-A_{m})(1-P_{m-l})x\|^{2}\leq 2R\|x\|_{C}\,\|(1-P_{m-l})x\|\leq\frac{2R}{\lambda_{m-l+1}}\|x\|_{C}^{2}.

Applying this result to the operators
L
and
H
that satisfy
(
26
) with
R=\max(K^{\prime},\sqrt{K})
, writing

L_{k}^{*}L_{k}-L_{m}^{*}L_{m}=(L_{k}^{*}-L_{m}^{*})L_{k}+L_{m}^{*}(L_{k}-L_{m}),

and assuming

for definiteness,
we derive from (
25
) (taking into account (
24
)) that, for
m
so large that
\lambda_{m-l+1}>1
,

\mathbf{E}\|\chi_{k}(t)-\chi_{m}(t)\|^{2}\leq\|(P_{k}-P_{m})\chi_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{R}{\sqrt{\lambda_{m-l+1}}}e^{\alpha t}[\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}_{C}+\alpha t(\|\chi_{0}\|^{2}+\beta)].

Consequently, the sequence of continuous
\mathcal{H}
-valued processes
\chi_{m}(t)
is Cauchy and hence it converges to some continuous process
\chi(t)
. Since all
\chi_{m}(t)
are conservative, it follows that
\chi(t)
is conservative implying that
\|\chi(t)\|^{2}
is a positive martingale. Uniform estimate (
24
) implies the corresponding estimate (
20
) for the limiting curve
\chi(t)
. In fact, it implies that

CP_{k}\chi_{m}(t)\to CP_{k}\chi(t)
, as
m\to\infty
and any

, and hence
\|P_{k}C\chi(t)\|
satisfies (
20
), and thus
\|C\chi(t)\|
has the same bound.

It remains to prove that
\chi(t)
satisfies equation (
3
). To this end we rewrite (

)
(with our usual reduced way of writing with
the appropriate summation assumed) as

\chi_{m}(t)=P_{m}\chi_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(-iH-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L)\chi_{m}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}L\chi_{m}(s)\,dY(s)+I_{m}(t),

(27)

with

I_{m}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}[-i(H_{m}-H)-\frac{1}{2}(L_{m}^{*}L_{m}-L^{*}L)]\chi_{m}(s)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}(L_{m}-L)\chi_{m}(s)\,dY(s).

Then

tends to zero in the mean square sense, by (
26
).
Thus passing to the limit in (

) we get (
3
).
∎

To this end we develop an easy verified criterion
for the condition of

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose the estimates

\|[C,H]x\|\leq\frac{\alpha}{4}\|x\|_{C},\quad\sum_{k}|([L^{k},C^{2}]\}x,L^{k}x)|\|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\|x\|_{C}^{2}

(28)

hold. Then both (

) and (

Proof.

We have that

-2Re\,(Cx,C(iH)x)-Re\,(Cx,CL^{*}Lx)+\|CLx\|^{2}

=-2\,Re\,(Cx,[C,iH]x)-Re\,(C^{2}Lx,Lx)-Re\,([L,C^{2}]x,Lx)+\|CLx\|^{2}

=-2\,Re\,(Cx,[C,iH]x)-Re\,([L,C^{2}]x,Lx).

This implies (

). Similarly, for

-2Re\,(Cx,CP_{m}(iH)x)-Re\,(Cx,CP_{m}L^{*}P_{m}Lx)+\|CP_{m}Lx\|^{2}

2.2
Linear equation: explicit calculations

Let us now outline an alternative, more constructive, approach (developed essentially in
[
11
]
and
[
22
]
) to the equations arising at the continuous observation of momentum and position of a standard quantum system considered above in Proposition
2.2
.

In this approach one solves explicitly the corresponding filtering equation with quadratic
V
and linear
A
, and more general (sufficiently regular) potentials are treated via perturbation technique. Let us consider here only the case with position measurement, because including momentum just makes the calculations more lengthy (not requiring any additional ideas). For vanishing potential the corresponding filtering equation (
7
) takes the form

d\chi(t)=\frac{1}{2}(ih\Delta-\alpha^{2}x^{2})\chi(t)\,dt+\alpha x\chi(t)dY(t),

(30)

where
\chi(t)\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
Y(t)=(Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{d})(t)
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion (BM),
\alpha
real and
h
positive constants.

It was proved in
[
22
]
(and can be checked by direct calculations) that this equation has the Green function (fundamental solution for the Cauchy problem)
expressed in the explicit Gaussian form:

u_{G}(t,x,y)=\exp\{-\frac{\omega}{2}(x^{2}+y^{2})+\beta xy-ax-by-\gamma\}

(31)

where

\omega=\sigma\coth(\sigma Gt),\quad\beta=\sigma(\sinh(\sigma Gt))^{-1},\quad C=\sqrt{\beta/(2\pi)}.

a=\alpha(\sinh(\sigma Gt))^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\sinh(\sigma Gs)dB(s)

b=\sigma G\int_{0}^{t}\frac{a(s)}{\sinh(\sigma Gs)}ds,\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a^{2}(s)ds

with

\sigma=\sqrt{2\alpha^{2}/ih}=\sqrt{2\alpha^{2}/h}\exp\{-i\pi/4\}
.

It follows, that for small
t
,

\omega=\frac{1}{iht}+\frac{2}{3}\alpha^{2}t+O(t^{3}),\quad\beta=\frac{1}{iht}-\frac{1}{3}\alpha^{2}+O(t^{3}).

and

a\sim\frac{\alpha}{t}\xi(t),\quad\xi(t)=\int_{0}^{t}s\,dB(s),\quad b\sim\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\xi(s)}{s^{2}}ds.

(32)

Let us introduce
the Hilbert space
L^{2}_{R}=L^{2}_{R}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
of functions from
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
a finite norm squared

\|f\|^{2}_{R}=\|f\|^{2}+\sum_{j}\|x_{j}f\|^{2}+\sum_{j}\|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}f\|^{2}.

Proposition 2.3
.

The resolving operator
U_{t}
for the Cauchy problem to equation (
30
) with the integral kernel (
31
) takes
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}_{R}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for any
t>0
and moreover, for
t\in[0,T]
with any
T
, and any
p\in[1,2)

\mathbf{E}\|U_{t}\|^{p}\leq C,\quad\mathbf{E}\|U_{t}\|^{p}_{L^{2}_{R}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}\leq C,

(33)

with a constant
C
depending on
T
and
p
. The norm squared
\|U_{t}\|^{2}
has no finite expectation.

Remark 7
.

Note that neither of these estimates follow from the general Theorem

2.1
. For instance, the conservation property of the norm squared of the solution from this theorem implies only that
\mathbf{E}\|U_{t}\|^{2}\geq 1
. The fact that
p=2
is
the exact boundary between the existence and nonexistence of the expectation of

\|U_{t}\|^{p}
comes out as the result of some not obvious explicit calculations. It leads to the natural questions: (i) what is the intuition behind this fact? (ii) Is it a casual fact about a particular equation, or is it a performance of some general effect?

Proof.

Step 1. The first statement is evident from the form
of the Green function.

Let us prove that the second estimate in (
33
)
follows from the first one. To this end we observe that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)=-\omega x+\beta y-a,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)=-\omega y+\beta x-b,

so that

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)=(\beta-\omega)(y+x)-a-b.

Hence,

\int x_{j}u_{G}(t,x,y)f(y)dy=\frac{1}{\beta}\int\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)f(y)dy+\int u_{G}(t,x,y)\frac{\omega y+b}{\beta}f(y)dy

=-\frac{1}{\beta}\int u_{G}(t,x,y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}f(y)dy+\int u_{G}(t,x,y)\frac{\omega y+b}{\beta}f(y)dy

And thus

\|\hat{x}_{j}U_{t}f\|\leq\frac{1}{|\beta|}\|U_{t}\|\,\|p_{j}f\|+\frac{|\omega|}{|\beta|}\|U_{t}\|\,\|x_{j}f\|+\frac{|b|}{|\beta|}\|U_{t}f\|.

Similarly

\int\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)f(y)dy=-\int\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}u_{G}(t,x,y)f(y)dy+\int[(\beta-\omega)(y+x)-(a+b)]u_{G}t,(x,y)f(y)dy,

so that

\|\hat{p}_{j}U_{t}f\|\leq\|U_{t}\|\,\|p_{j}f\|+(\beta-\omega)\|U_{t}\|\,\|\hat{x}_{j}f\|+|a+b|\|U_{t}f\|+(\beta-\omega)\|x_{j}U_{t}f(y)\|.

Therefore,

\mathbf{E}\|\hat{x}_{j}U_{t}f\|\leq\left(\frac{1+|\omega|}{|\beta|}\mathbf{E}\|U_{t}\|+\mathbf{E}\frac{|b|}{|\beta|}\|U_{t}f\|\right)\|f\|_{L^{2}_{R}(\mathbf{R}^{d})},

(34)

\mathbf{E}\|\hat{p}_{j}U_{t}f\|\leq\left((1+\beta-\omega)\mathbf{E}\|U_{t}\|+\mathbf{E}|a+b|\|U_{t}\|\right)\|f\|_{L^{2}_{R}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+(\beta-\omega)\mathbf{E}\|x_{j}U_{t}f(y)\|.

(35)

Taking into account that
\beta-\omega=O(t)
,
|\beta|^{-1}=O(t)
and that
a,b
are small for small
t
, we can in fact conclude that
the second estimate in (
33
)
follows from the first one.

Step. 2.
The norm squared of
U_{t}
is the norm of the operator
U_{t}U_{t}^{*}
with the kernel

|C|^{2m}(\pi/Re\,\omega)^{m/2}\exp\bigl\{-\frac{1}{2}(Re\,\omega-\frac{Re(\beta^{2})}{2\,Re\,\omega})(x^{2}+y^{2})+\frac{|\beta_{G}|^{2}}{2\,Re\,\omega_{G}}xy

-(x+y)(a_{R}+\frac{\beta_{R}b_{R}}{\omega_{R}})-(2\gamma_{R}-\frac{b_{R}^{2}}{\omega_{R}})\bigr\}.

By shifting
x,y
on

\xi=\frac{\omega_{R}(a_{R}+\frac{\beta_{R}b_{R}}{\omega_{R}})}{\omega_{R}^{2}-\beta_{R}^{2}},

we find that the norm of this operator is the same as that of
the operator

|C|^{2m}(\pi/Re\,\omega)^{m/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(Re\,\omega-\frac{Re(\beta^{2})}{2\,Re\,\omega})(x^{2}+y^{2})+\frac{|\beta|^{2}}{2\,Re\,\omega}xy\right\}

\times\exp\left\{\frac{\omega_{R}(a_{R}+\frac{\beta_{R}b_{R}}{\omega_{R}})^{2}}{\omega_{R}^{2}-\beta_{R}^{2}}-(2\gamma_{R}-\frac{b_{R}^{2}}{\omega_{R}})\right\}.

Comparing with the resolving operator of quantum oscillator and using small time asymptotics of
\omega,\beta
we find that the deterministic part of the kernel gives the norm
1+O(t)
, so that

\mathbf{E}\|U_{t}\|^{2}=(1+O(t))\mathbf{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\omega_{R}(a_{R}+\frac{\beta_{R}b_{R}}{\omega_{R}})^{2}}{\omega_{R}^{2}-\beta_{R}^{2}}-(2\gamma_{R}-\frac{b_{R}^{2}}{\omega_{R}})\right\}.

Since
\gamma_{R}
is seen to be small, we really need to estimate the expectation

\mathbf{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\omega_{R}(a_{R}+\frac{\beta_{R}b_{R}}{\omega_{R}})^{2}}{\omega_{R}^{2}-\beta_{R}^{2}}+\frac{b_{R}^{2}}{\omega_{R}}\right\},

which up to terms of lower order rewrites as

\mathbf{E}\exp\left\{\frac{\omega_{R}}{\omega_{R}^{2}-\beta_{R}^{2}}(a_{R}^{2}-a_{R}b_{R}+b_{R}^{2})\right\}\sim\mathbf{E}\exp\left\{\frac{2}{\alpha^{2}t}(a_{R}^{2}-a_{R}b_{R}+b_{R}^{2})\right\}.

(36)

Similarly, for any
p\geq 1
,

\mathbf{E}\,\|U_{t}\|^{p}\sim\mathbf{E}\exp\left\{\frac{p}{\alpha^{2}t}(a_{R}^{2}-a_{R}b_{R}+b_{R}^{2})\right\}.

(37)

Step 3.
By (
32
), we see that asymptotically (for small
t
)

Var(a_{R})=Var(b_{R})=2\,Cov\,(a_{R},b_{R})=\alpha^{2}t/3.

Consequently,

a_{R}^{2}-a_{R}b_{R}+b_{R}^{2}=(a_{R}-\frac{1}{2}b_{R})^{2}+\frac{3}{4}b_{R}^{2},

with

Var(a_{R}-\frac{1}{2}b_{R})=Var(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}b_{R})=\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}t,\quad Cov\,(a_{R}-\frac{1}{2}b_{R},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}b_{R})=0.

Consequently, up to a multiplier of type
(1+O(t))
we see that

\mathbf{E}\,\|U_{t}\|^{p}=\mathbf{E}\exp\{\frac{p}{4}(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2})\},

with
z_{1},z_{2}
independent standard normal, so that
z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}
has the
standard
\chi^{2}
distribution of degree 2. This distribution has the MGF

(1-2t)^{-1}
for
t<1/2
. Therefore,
\mathbf{E}\,\|U_{t}\|^{p}
is finite exactly when
p<2
.
∎

This Proposition implies the following result.

), the equation being satisfied generally for all

, and, for the initial condition from

.

(38)

with

) with sufficiently regular

.

and

2.3
Nonlinear equation: weak solutions

In the setting of Theorem
2.1
, solutions to the corresponding nonlinear equation have only been built in the weak (probabilistic) sense.
Namely, a
C
-weak solution of equation (
2
) with initial condition
\phi_{0}
on a time interval

is the pair

\{\phi(t),B(t)\}
of adapted continuous processes defined on some filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}_{t},\mathbf{Q})
(satisfying the usual conditions), where
\phi(t)
is
\mathcal{H}
-valued,

-dimensional standard BM, s.t. (i)

and

for all

The following result is a simplified version of Theorem 1 from

:

Theorem 2.2
.

(Mora-Rebolledo) Under Hypothesis MR (with either (
17
) or (
18
)), for any
\phi_{0}\in Dom(C)
with
\|\phi_{0}\|=1
there exists a unique in law (that is, all solutions have identical finite-dimensional distributions)

C
-weak solution
\{\phi(t),B(t)\}
to equation (
2
), and moreover, this solution satisfies the estimate

\mathbf{E}\|C\phi(t)\|^{2}\leq e^{\alpha t}[\|C\phi_{0}\|^{2}+\alpha t(\|\phi_{0}\|^{2}+\beta)].

(39)

Proof.

We sketch here a simplified argument. First of all, the existence of a solution is mostly straightforward from Theorem
2.1
and the remark above (see (
5
)) that, if
\chi(t)
solves (
1
), then
\phi(t)=\chi(t)/\|\chi(t)\|
solves (
2
). The estimate (
39
) follows from (
6
) and
(
20
). The difficult part of the proof of
[

]
concerns uniqueness. It is proved there independently of the link with the linear equation. However, this link can be used to obtain a simpler proof, as was noted in
[
28
]
.
Namely: let
\phi(t)
have unit norms for all

and satisfy the

2
). Define

as the solution
(with the initial condition equal to

) to the equation

Nonlinear equation: strong solutions

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume that Hypothesis A holds and Hypothesis MR holds with the second alternative (

Proof.

To shorten formulas we perform our argument only for self-adjoint operators

.
Generally one just has to work in the same way with equation (

Step 1.
Recall that equation (

for all

Applying Girsanov’s theorem and (

)

Step 2.
Following the line of arguments used for the linear equation, we aim to show that the sequence

Subtracting equations for

And therefore

+2\,\mathbf{E}\,Re\left((L_{k}-L_{m})\langle L_{k}\rangle_{\phi_{k}}\phi_{k}+L_{m}(\langle L_{k}\rangle_{\phi_{k}}-\langle L_{m}\rangle_{\phi_{m}})\phi_{k}+L_{m}\langle L_{m}\rangle_{\phi_{m}}(\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}),\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}\right)\,dt

-\mathbf{E}\,Re\,\left((\langle L_{k}\rangle_{\phi_{k}}^{2}-\langle L_{m}\rangle_{\phi_{m}}^{2})\phi_{k},\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}\right)\,dt+\mathbf{E}\,\|(\langle L_{k}\rangle_{\phi_{k}}-\langle L_{m}\rangle_{\phi_{m}})\phi_{k}\|^{2}\,dt.

The first four terms on the right hand side are the same as in linear case and are dealt with as in the liner case:
they are bounded by terms tending to zero, as
m,k\to\infty
.
The same concerns other terms containing
L_{k}-L_{m}
, in particular, those arising
by writing

\langle L_{k}\rangle_{\phi_{k}}-\langle L_{m}\rangle_{\phi_{m}}=(L_{k}\phi_{k},\phi_{k}-\phi_{m})+(L_{k}(\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}),\phi_{m})+((L_{k}-L_{m})\phi_{m},\phi_{m}).

However further we meet difficulties not present in the linear case: the terms of type

(L_{m}(\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}),\phi_{k}-\phi_{m})
. In case of bounded
L
, these terms would be bounded
by
\|\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}\|^{2}
and direct application of Gronwall’s inequality would complete a proof that the sequence
\phi_{k}
is Cauchy. Here we need an additional argument.

Step 3. Let us introduce the stopping times

\tau_{n}=\inf\{t\leq T:\sup_{k}\|\phi_{k}(t)\|_{C}\geq n\}

(or
\tau_{n}=T
, if
\sup_{k}\|\phi_{k}(t)\|_{C}<n
for all
t\leq T
).
Then we get from (
44
) and (
26
)
(and using Doob’s optional sampling theorem) that

\mathbf{E}\,\|\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}\|^{2}(t\wedge\tau_{n})\leq R\bigl(\|(P_{k}-P_{m})\phi(0)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{m-l+1}}+n^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{E}\,\|\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}\|^{2}(s\wedge\tau_{n})\,ds\bigr),

with a constant
R
(depending on
T
). Consequently, by Gronwall’s lemma,

\mathbf{E}\,\|\phi_{k}-\phi_{m}\|^{2}(t\wedge\tau_{n})\leq R\,e^{tRn^{2}}\bigl(\|(P_{k}-P_{m})\phi(0)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{m-l+1}}\bigr).

Therefore the processes
\phi_{k}(t\wedge n)
have a limit in the mean square sense. Since these limits are clearly consistent, we can denote all these limits by a single letter
\phi(t\wedge\tau_{n})
and to conclude that these processes satisfy the integral version of equation (
2
), where all upper bounds for the integrals are taken as
t\wedge\tau_{n}
instead of
t
.

It remains to observe that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau_{n}=T
a.s. In fact, otherwise, we would have a set of positive measure, where
\tau_{n}\geq K
for all
n
with some
K<T
, so that there
\sup_{k}\max_{t}\|\phi_{k}(t)\|_{C}=\infty
. Consequently, there would exist
k
such that

\max_{t}\|\phi_{k}(t)\|_{C}=\infty
, which contradicts the continuity and boundedness of
\phi_{k}
.

Therefore, passing to the limit
n\to\infty
we can conclude that
\phi(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\phi(t\wedge\tau_{n})
satisfies (
2
).

Step 4. Finally, the uniqueness of solution is as easy as in the linear case. One writes down the equation for
\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}\,\|\phi^{1}(t)-\phi^{2}(t)\|^{2}
of two solutions with the same initial condition, which is similar to (
44
), but is much simpler,
because
H,L
are fixed:

\frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbf{E}\,\|\phi^{1}-\phi^{2}\|^{2}=2\,\mathbf{E}\,Re\left((\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{1}}-\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{2}})L\phi^{1}+\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{2}}L(\phi^{1}-\phi^{2}),\phi^{1}-\phi^{2}\right)

-\mathbf{E}\,Re\,\left((\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{1}}^{2}-\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{2}}^{2})\phi^{1},\phi^{1}-\phi^{2}\right)+\mathbf{E}\,\|(\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{1}}-\langle L\rangle_{\phi^{2}})\phi^{1}\|^{2}.

As above, we introduce stopping times

\tau_{n}=\min\{t\leq T:\max(\|\phi^{1}(t)\|_{C},\|\phi^{2}(t)\|_{C})\geq n\}.

Applying Gronwall’s lemma we then conclude that

\phi^{1}(t\wedge\tau_{n})=\phi^{2}(t\wedge\tau_{n})
. Passing to the limit
n\to\infty

yields the equation
\phi^{1}(t)=\phi^{2}(t)
for all
t
, as required.
∎

3
Stochastic Lindblad (or quantum master) equations: linear version

The first mathematical problem with equation (
12
),
that is, equation

d\gamma(t)=-i[H,\gamma(t)]\,dt+\mathcal{L}_{L}\gamma(t)\,dt+(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*})dY(t),

with

\mathcal{L}_{L}\gamma=L\gamma L^{*}-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L\gamma-\frac{1}{2}\gamma L^{*}L=L\gamma L^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{*}L,\gamma\},

is that it is not obvious, in which space it should be considered.
Since we are interested in trace-class operators, the most natural
space from physical point of view would be the Banach space
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{S}
of self-adjoint
trace-class operators in
\mathcal{H}
. However, the classes of Banach spaces,
for which a satisfactory extension of Ito stochastic calculus was developed,
namely the so-called UMD spaces, spaces of martingale type 2 and spaces with a smooth norm
(see review
[
36
]
) do not include
\mathcal{H}^{1}
.
In
[
28
]
in was suggested to work with this equation in a larger Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}^{2}
of Hilbert-Schmidt operators or more precisely in its closed subspace
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{S}
of self-adjoint operators. We shall follow this idea here, having in mind the additional difficulty arising for unbounded
L
: solution-processes may not be square integrable in the Hilbert-Schmidt class.
In fact, even for factorized solutions of type
\gamma(t)=\chi(t)\otimes\bar{\chi}(t)
with
\chi(t)
solving the pure state linear filtering equation,

\mathbf{E}\,{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)^{2}=\mathbf{E}\|\chi(t)\|^{4}
, which may not be finite generally.

A natural class for well-posedness for equation (
12
) in the setting of Hypothesis MR is the cone
\mathcal{T}_{C}^{S+}(\mathcal{H})
introduced in

[
17
]
:
\mathcal{T}^{S+}_{C}(\mathcal{H})
is the cone of trace-class positive operators
\gamma
in
\mathcal{H}
such that one of three equivalent properties holds: (i)
C\sqrt{\gamma}\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
(including the statement that the image of
\sqrt{\gamma}
belongs to the domain of
C
), (ii)
\sqrt{\gamma}C\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
,
(iii)
C\gamma C
(more precisely, its unique extension by continuity) is of trace class. The easiest way to see that these properties are equivalent
is to use the spectral basis of
C
, where it is diagonal with eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
on the diagonal, since then

{\rm{tr}}\,C\gamma C=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{2}\gamma_{jj}=\sum_{j,k}\lambda_{j}^{2}|\sqrt{\gamma}_{jk}|^{2}=\|C\sqrt{\gamma}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}=\|\sqrt{\gamma}C\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}.

The closed linear span
\mathcal{T}_{C}^{S}(\mathcal{H})
of the cone
\mathcal{T}_{C}^{S+}(\mathcal{H})
is a Banach space when equipped with the norm
\|\gamma\|_{C}={\rm{tr}}\,(C|\gamma|C)
.

We shall call a continuous
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-valued process
\gamma(t)
a
C
-strong solution of (
12
), if it is adapted to the filtration generated by
Y(t)
, satisfies (
12
) in
\mathcal{H}^{2}
and enjoys the bound

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbf{E}\,\|\gamma(t)\|_{C}<\infty.

Let us say that such a solution with an initial condition
\gamma(0)

respects finite-dimensional approximations

if it is an
L^{2}
-limit (in the space of
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-valued random variables),
as
m\to\infty
, of finite dimensional approximations
\gamma_{m}(t)
defined as the solutions to the equations of type (
12
) in
\mathcal{H}_{m}
, where the operators

L_{m}=P_{m}LP_{m}
and
H_{m}=P_{m}HP_{m}
are taken instead of
L
and
H
,

d\gamma_{m}(t)=-i[H_{m},\gamma_{m}(t)]\,dt+\mathcal{L}_{L_{m}}\gamma_{m}(t)\,dt+(L_{m}\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L_{m}^{*})\,dY(t),

(45)

considered with the initial condition
\gamma_{m}(0)=P_{m}\gamma(0)P_{m}
.

Remark 8
.

For the stochastic differential (and its integral) on the r.h.s. of (
12
) to be well-defined it is needed that the integral

\int_{0}^{t}\|L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\,dt=\int_{0}^{t}{\rm{tr}}(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*})^{2}\,dt

(46)

is finite a.s. One can see that this condition is fulfilled under the assumptions of the definition of
C
-strong solution. In fact,
under these assumptions

is an a.s. continuous curve both in
\mathcal{H}^{2}
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{S}
(because
\gamma(t)
is continuous and

is a continuous operator from
\mathcal{T}_{C}^{S+}(\mathcal{H})
to
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, which holds by the closed graph theorem), hence its square norm is continuous a.s. and therefore integrable. For unbounded

one can hardly expect to have a finite expectation of expression (

), which complicates the arguments of analysis.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Hypothesis MR, for any

Remark 9

Since all reasonable solutions are built from finite-dimensional approximations, our condition that solutions respect finite dimensional approximation seems to be satisfactory
for any application. Moreover, it implies the Markov property for the solution and the semigroup (propagator) property for resolving operators.
Aesthetically however, it would be nice to prove uniqueness without this restriction. That is, what we will do in the next theorem under additional Hypothesis A.

Proof.

Step 1.

Since finite-dimensional equations are well-posed (see e.g.

Clearly, if

so that the series converges also in

From the discussion above, it is clear that it is sufficient to prove the convergence of finite-dimensional approximations for decomposable initial conditions

Stochastic Lindblad (or quantum master) equations: normalized version

d\frac{1}{{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)}=-\frac{1}{({\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t))^{2}}\,{\rm{tr}}\,(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*})dY_{t}+\frac{1}{({\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t))^{3}}[{\rm{tr}}\,(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*})]^{2}\,dt.

Hence by Ito’s product rule we check that the normalised density operator

\rho(t)=\gamma(t)/{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)
satisfies the equation

d\rho=-i[H,\rho(t)]\,dt+\mathcal{L}_{L}\rho(t)\,dt

+(L\rho(t)+\rho(t)L^{*}-\rho(t)\,{\rm{tr}}\,(L\rho(t)+\rho(t)L^{*}))[dY_{t}-{\rm{tr}}\,(L\rho(t)+\rho(t)L^{*})dt].

(52)

Therefore, in terms of the
innovation process

B(t)=Y(t)-\int_{0}^{t}{\rm{tr}}\,(L\rho(s)+\rho(s)L^{*})\,ds

(53)

the equation for the inverse trace rewrites as

d\frac{1}{{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)}=-\frac{1}{{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)}\,{\rm{tr}}\,(L\rho(t)+\rho(t)L^{*})dB(t)

(54)

and the equation for the normalized density operator (
52
) rewrites in the
standard form (
13
) of the nonlinear filtering equation. Of course, these manipulations being straightforward in finite-dimensional case, may not be well justified in infinite-dimensions. However, the results of Theorem
3.1
make them all rigorous for the class of
C
-solutions. Notice that, for positive solutions,
{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)
is a positive martingale that specifies the density between the measures
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
that make
Y(t)
and
B(t)
Brownian motions, respectively:

\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{Q}}\xi=\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{P}}(\xi\,{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t))

for
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-adapted
\xi
. And therefore estimate (
47
)
for the solutions
\gamma(t)
of a linear equation with an initial condition of unit trace rewrites as the estimate

\mathbf{E}_{Q}\|\rho(t)\|_{C}\leq e^{\alpha t}[\|\gamma_{0}\|_{C}+\alpha t(1+\beta)],

(55)

for the solution
\rho(t)=\gamma(t)/{\rm{tr}}\,\gamma(t)
of the nonlinear equation.
Therefore, Theorem
3.1
implies the existence of
C
-weak solutions to nonlinear equation (
13
).

Moreover, let a continuous pair of processes
(\rho(t),B(t))
be a

\rho_{0}
and with

.

Assume Hypothesis MR and A hold, and let

.

Remark 10

Assuming only Hypothesis MR we get, by Theorem

To analyze the strong solutions of (
13
), we shall use
the key observation made in
[
28
]
that one can rewrite stochastic master
equation for mixed states in the equivalent form that coincides with the corresponding equation for pure states in an appropriately chosen Hilbert space.

The following result is proved in the same way as
the corresponding result from
[
28
]
devoted to bounded operators
L
. We sketch the proof for completeness.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume Hypothesis MR and A hold, and let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{T}_{C}^{S+}(\mathcal{H})
have unit trace.
Then, for a given BM
B(t)
, there exists a unique
C
-strong solution of equation (
13
) in
\mathcal{H}^{2}
with the initial data
\rho_{0}
.

Proof.

According to calculations performed above,
\rho(t)
solves (
13
)
if and only if
\gamma(t)=T(t)\rho(t)
solves the equation

d\gamma(t)=-i[H,\gamma(t)]dt+\mathcal{L}_{L}\gamma(t)dt+(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*})[dB(t)+\pi(t)\,dt],

(56)

with

\pi(t)=T^{-1}(t)\,{\rm{tr}}\,(L\gamma(t)+\gamma(t)L^{*}).

Expanding
\rho_{0}=\gamma_{0}
in a series

\gamma_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}e_{k}\otimes\bar{e}_{k}

with a non-negative sequence
\{p_{k}\}
summing up to one
and an orthonormal basis
\{e_{k}\}
,
we represent
\gamma(t)
as the convergence series of pure states

\gamma(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}e_{k}(t)\otimes\bar{e}_{k}(t),

with
e_{k}(t)
solving the linear filtering equation for
pure states (
7
):

de_{k}(t)=(-iHe_{k}(t)-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}Le_{k}(t))\,dt+Le_{k}(t)[dB(t)+\pi(t)\,dt],

(57)

where

\pi(t)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}(e_{k}(t),(L+L^{*})e_{k}(t))}{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\|e_{k}(t)\|^{2}}.

And now we observe that system (
57
) can be equivalently written as a single SDE
with values in the Hilbert space
l^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(\{p_{k}\})
consisting of infinite sequences

\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},\cdots)
of vectors from
\mathcal{H}
and equipped
with the norm

\|\mathbf{e}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}(e_{k},e_{k}).

The controlling operator
C
and other operators involved (like
H
and
L
)
extend naturally (acting identically on each coordinate)
to operators in
l^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(\{p_{k}\})
.
In this notation system (
57
) writes down as the SDE

d\mathbf{e}(t)=(-iH\mathbf{e}(t)-\frac{1}{2}L^{*}L\mathbf{e}(t))\,dt+L\mathbf{e}(t)\left[dB(t)+\frac{(\mathbf{e},(L+L^{*})\mathbf{e})}{(\mathbf{e},\mathbf{e})}\,dt\right].

(58)

This equation is the same as (
11
) (though written in an enhanced Hilbert space). Hence the well-posedness for this equation follows from the well-posedness of the quantum filtering equation for pure states, that is Theorems
2.1

and
2.3
.
