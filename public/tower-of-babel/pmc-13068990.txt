Transitioning From Oral to Intravenous Treprostinil in Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension: A Case Report
Authors: Abigail M Cummings, Samuel B Konkol, Andrew D Mihalek, Mary E Roth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe optimal approach to transitioning from oral treprostinil to intravenous treprostinil in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) has been poorly characterized in published literature. We present a case involving a 21‐year‐old male with PAH experiencing worsening symptoms despite treatment with high doses of oral treprostinil (36.75 mg three times daily), ambrisentan, and tadalafil. The patient was admitted to our institution for transition from oral to intravenous treprostinil due to severe PAH as demonstrated on right heart catheterization (RHC). The transition process involved cross‐tapering intravenous and oral treprostinil over 11 days with the aid of central venous pressure (CVP) measurements and noninvasive blood pressure monitoring. The patient remained clinically stable throughout the transition, and a repeat RHC upon completion demonstrated hemodynamic stability on a final dose of intravenous treprostinil 100 ng/kg/min. This case demonstrates that it is possible to safely transition from oral to intravenous treprostinil more rapidly than recommended by product labeling.Keywords: drug tapering, hypertension, infusions, intravenous, pharmacologic therapy, pulmonary, pulmonary heart disease

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive pulmonary vasculopathy characterized by elevated pulmonary artery pressure (PAP), pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), and eventual right‐ventricular failure with advanced disease. The prostacyclin pathway is one of multiple dysregulated pathways in PAH. Prostacyclin analogs act as potent vasodilators that target this dysregulation and serve as one of the main treatment modalities for PAH. The most recent pulmonary hypertension guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology and European Respiratory Society recommend the use of intravenous prostacyclin analogs, treprostinil and epoprostinil, as initial therapy in patients at high risk of mortality or as add‐on therapy in those who experience disease progression while on other therapies, including oral treprostinil [1]. However, the optimal approach to transition from oral treprostinil to intravenous treprostinil has been poorly characterized in published literature. We present the case of a patient who safely transitioned from a high dose of oral treprostinil to intravenous treprostinil using a rapid taper.

Case Report

Case History and Examination

A 21‐year‐old male with PAH secondary to bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2 mutation presented with poorly controlled disease despite treatment with ambrisentan 10 mg daily, tadalafil 40 mg daily, and oral treprostinil 36.75 mg three times daily, a dose approximately three times higher than the maximum studied dose. The patient reported increased shortness of breath, fatigue, and multiple syncopal episodes over the preceding months. His oxygen requirement at presentation was 3 L/min, used only at night. A transthoracic echocardiogram 1 month prior to presentation demonstrated a severely dilated right atrium, severely reduced right ventricular ejection fraction, and evidence of mild regurgitation of the mitral, tricuspid, and pulmonic valves. His past medical history was otherwise unremarkable. A right heart catheterization (RHC) on admission revealed severe PAH with the following hemodynamic measurements: right atrial pressure (RAP) 8 mmHg, PAP 103/59 (mean 72) mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) 9 mmHg, cardiac index (CI) (thermodilution) 3.56 L/min/m2, CI (indirect Fick) 3.55 L/min/m2, and PVR 9.35 Wood units. Given disease progression despite escalation to such high doses of oral treprostinil, the patient was promptly admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit for transition to intravenous treprostinil.

Intervention

A pulmonary artery catheter was placed at the time of RHC, but due to poor positioning it was removed and therefore not used to assist with the transition. As such, central venous pressure (CVP) measurements, symptoms, oxygen requirements, and noninvasive blood pressure monitoring were used to assess tolerance of the cross‐tapering process. The intravenous treprostinil was started at 2 ng/kg/min (dosing weight 75 kg). Initial plans were to increase intravenous treprostinil by 2 ng/kg/min every 4 to 6 h and begin decreasing the oral treprostinil dose once the patient developed prostacyclin‐related symptoms. If hypotension occurred, low dose norepinephrine was permitted to prevent interruption or delay of the cross‐titration process. The patient developed a headache and nausea on Day 2 of the cross‐taper, when the intravenous treprostinil dose was 4 ng/kg/min. At this time, the oral treprostinil dose was decreased by 25% to 27.5 mg three times daily. Subsequently, the patient was monitored every 4 h for prostacyclin‐related symptoms. If such symptoms developed, the dose of intravenous treprostinil was not escalated. Conversely, if the patient developed increased oxygen requirements, oral treprostinil was not reduced. Oral treprostinil dosing strategies were limited by the patient's provided home medication supply as this product was not on our institutional formulary. The patient's symptoms during the titration included flushing, headaches, and nausea, and were adequately controlled with acetaminophen, dronabinol, ondansetron, and lorazepam given as needed. His oxygen requirements increased from baseline, peaking on Day 8 with a maximum of 6 L/min needed to maintain oxygen saturation above 90%, before trending back to a baseline of 2 L/min required only at night. The patient remained clinically stable throughout the titration and did not require vasopressor support at any point.

Results

The cross‐tapering process was completed on Day 11. A repeat RHC on Day 12 demonstrated stable hemodynamics (RAP 6 mmHg, PAP 102/49 [mean 67] mmHg, PCWP 15 mmHg, CI [thermodilution] 2.46 L/min/m2, CI [indirect Fick] 2.81 L/min/m2, PVR 9.5 Wood units). The patient's oxygen requirements had returned to baseline and he tolerated exercise with physical therapy without desaturation events or presyncope. The patient was discharged 13 days after admission on intravenous treprostinil 100 ng/kg/min administered via home continuous infusion pump, ambrisentan 10 mg daily, and tadalafil 40 mg daily. After discharge, his intravenous treprostinil was uptitrated in the outpatient setting, reaching a dose of 145 and 189 ng/kg/min at 1 and 2 years, respectively.

Discussion

Current published guidance recommends initiation of intravenous prostacyclin analogs, treprostinil and epoprostinil, in those who experience disease progression while on other therapies, including oral treprostinil [1]. While oral treprostinil labeling does not suggest a maximum effective dose, literature evaluating the efficacy of this product beyond 12 mg three times a day is limited. Studies evaluating the effectiveness of de novo oral treprostinil used significantly lower doses than those administered to our patient. In the randomized controlled trial by White et al., the oral treprostinil dose was capped at 12 mg three times daily [2]. Patients achieved a median total daily dose of 10.86 mg at Week 24, and a mean total daily dose of 18.3 mg at Week 48 of an open‐label extension [2, 3]. A more recent real‐world study by Lachant et al. reported a mean total daily dose of 12.3 mg at 12 months for patients on de novo oral treprostinil [4]. As our patient demonstrated disease progression despite being on a total daily dose of 110.25 mg of oral treprostinil, a much higher dose than evaluated in these studies, it was deemed appropriate to transition to intravenous treprostinil.

There is limited published guidance on the optimal transition from oral to intravenous treprostinil. Chakinala et al. demonstrated that 1 mg of oral treprostinil three times a day was approximately equivalent to 6 ng/kg/min of intravenous administration. The study enrolled 33 patients, of which 23 were transitioned from parenteral treprostinil (median baseline dose 60 ng/kg/min) to oral treprostinil three times daily (median total daily dose of 30.0 mg at day 4 and 43.9 mg at week 24) [5]. Other studies evaluating intravenous to oral treprostinil transition include lower total daily oral doses of 2–24 mg [4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Oral treprostinil product labeling states that the bioavailability is approximately 17% and provides the following equation to estimate oral to intravenous equivalence: total daily oral dose (mg) = 0.0072 × intravenous dose (ng/kg/min) × weight (kg) [11]. Product labeling further suggests transitioning from intravenous to oral treprostinil by cross tapering with a maximum reduction of 30 ng/kg/min per day of intravenous and maximum increase of 6 mg per day (2 mg three times daily) of oral [11]. It does not specify recommendations for the inverse transition process.

In the most comparable case report to this, the referenced patient was taking 4 mg three times daily of oral treprostinil prior to the transition (estimated equivalent intravenous dose of 32 ng/kg/min). Oral treprostinil was reduced by 1 mg three times daily every 24 h, and the transition was completed in 4 days to a parenteral dose of 24 ng/kg/min at discharge and 40 ng/kg/min at 2 months [12]. In contrast, our patient was taking a much larger dose of oral treprostinil, 36.75 mg three times daily, with an estimated intravenous equivalent of 204 ng/kg/min. Given our patient's large baseline dose, limiting oral adjustments to the manufacturer recommendations would result in a prolonged titration of at least 19 days. Considering this, as well as the absence of published guidance on the ideal transition method, we elected to complete a more rapid conversion guided by symptoms and clinical data. Oral doses were reduced by 25%–33% every 1–2 days, with a maximum daily dose reduction of 27.75 mg.

To our knowledge, this case represents the highest dose of oral treprostinil involved in a parenteral conversion reported in the literature. Despite the patient's high baseline oral treprostinil dose, he developed prostacyclin‐associated symptoms early after the initiation of intravenous treprostinil. The patient's final intravenous treprostinil dose, at which he demonstrated baseline symptoms, oxygen requirements, and RHC parameters, was much lower than predicted by oral equivalence estimates (approximately 50% of predicted at discharge and 75% of predicted at 1 year). One hypothesis to explain the lower equivalent intravenous dose is that treprostinil may have diminishing oral bioavailability when used at doses exceeding those initially studied. However, this would require further research to elucidate. The expedited transition process was completed in 11 days, a 42% reduction from the duration according to manufacturer guidance, saving the patient several additional days in the hospital. This was accomplished with close monitoring and using the patient's symptoms and clinical picture to guide individual dose adjustments.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates that with careful monitoring, it is possible to safely transition from high dose oral treprostinil to intravenous treprostinil, even when completed more rapidly than existing manufacturer recommendations. In appropriately selected patients, a more rapid cross titration could allow for a shorter hospitalization while still maintaining clinical stability.
