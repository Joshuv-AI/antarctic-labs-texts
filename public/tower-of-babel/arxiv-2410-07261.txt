On the Average Resistance of n n -circuits
Mehdi Nikopour Deilami, Bohdan Zhelyabovskyy

Abstract
n n -circuits are series-parallel networks composed of exactly n n unit resistors. This paper is focused on evaluating the mean resistance of all n n -circuits, M n M_{n} , establishing that it lies between 1 1 and 4.3954 4.3954 for all n n . We ultimately conjecture that M n M_{n} converges to 1.25 1.25 as n n grows, based on computational analysis and other intuitive arguments. Although the number of n n -circuits has been explored quite thoroughly, this paper also provides complete proofs of some important results.

Introduction

The enumeration of series-parallel networks was first investigated by MacMahon more than a century ago — see
MacMahon (1890)
and
MacMahon (1892)
. Since then, a few papers have been published on the topic, mainly concerned with enumeration and relations with Graph Theory. For example, there is a bijection between
n
-circuits and unlabeled trees with
n
leaves in which no node has exactly one child — see
(
OEIS Foundation Inc., 2024
, A000084)
. What has yet to be fully investigated, however, is the main physical property: the resistances of such circuits.

It is well known that the total resistance of series circuits can be determined by summing the individual resistances of their components, whereas for parallel circuits this is achieved by reciprocating the sum of the reciprocals. The observation that series and parallel connections can be used to increase and decrease the total resistance of a circuit, respectively, naturally gives rise to the question:

“What is the average resistance of all circuits

consisting of a fixed number of identical resistors?”

What even further motivates this exploration is that were one willing to hand-calculate the first 6 values, they would notice an unpredictable behavior. We concentrate our investigation on
n
-circuits, which are series-parallel networks composed of exactly
n
unit resistors. The graph readily suggests that the lower bound of the average resistance of all
n
-circuits,
M_{n}
, is
1
, a result that will be proven later in the paper.

Quantifying Circuits

Let
Q_{n}
denote the number of distinct
n
-circuits.
To begin counting the
n
-circuits, one must come up with a way to uniquely represent them. We will be using Ordered Pairs of Multisets (OPoM) to do so. Let
(\{\},\{\})
represent the unit circuit, which is a single resistor and is considered to be both series and parallel. The series connection of a multiset of parallel circuits
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\cdots,\alpha_{m}\}
is represented by
\alpha=(\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\cdots,\alpha_{m}\},\{\})
, and the parallel connection of a multiset of series circuits
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\cdots,\beta_{n}\}
is represented by
\beta=(\{\},\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\cdots,\beta_{n}\})
. Not every ordered pair produces a circuit: a circuit
\gamma=(\{m_{1}\},\{m_{2}\})
represents a series or a parallel circuit if and only if
m_{1}=\{\}
or
m_{2}=\{\}
, respectively. This motivates the use of
\accentset{\leftrightsquigarrow}{\gamma}
to denote the non-empty multiset of sub-circuits of
\gamma
. The sub-circuits of any circuit are of its opposite connection type.

Theorem 1.1

(Partition-based
n
-circuit Generation)
.

The number of circuits generated by a partition
p
,
N(p)
can be recursively counted by:

N(p)=\prod_{i\in p^{\times}}\sum_{t\vdash v_{p}(i)}{{q_{i}}\choose{|t|}}|t|!\prod_{j\in t^{\times}}\frac{1}{v_{t}(j)!}

where

Q_{n}=2q_{n}-\delta_{n1}=\sum_{p\vdash n}N(p)

counts the number of
n
-circuits
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

In order to construct a series
n
-circuit
\gamma=(\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\cdots,\gamma_{k}\},\{\})
, one should first choose the number of resistors in each
\gamma_{i}
, namely an unordered
k
-tuple of numbers adding up to
n
. Thus, one should start by considering a partition
p
of
n
. For any unique element

in

,
v_{p}(i)
parallel

-circuits have to be chosen. However, these

-circuits are not necessarily unique, therefore, all the different ways for those circuits to be equivalent should be considered. Mathematically, partitions of

. Now any permutation of

Inverses

The inverse of a circuit is defined as the circuit obtained by switching the order of every pair in its OPoM notation. This reveals a bijection between the series and parallel
n
-circuits, which is a property that will be used extensively.

Theorem 2.1

(Circuit-Multiplicative Inversion)
.

For any circuit
\gamma
,

r(\gamma)r(\gamma^{*})=1

Proof.

For any circuit
\gamma
, define its depth
\mathcal{D}(\gamma)
to be the maximum nesting of its resistors. We will prove the theorem by strong induction on
\mathcal{D}(\gamma)
.

•

Base Case
:
\mathcal{D}(\gamma)=0\Rightarrow\gamma
is unit
\Rightarrow r(\gamma^{*})=r(\gamma)=1\Rightarrow r(\gamma^{*})r(\gamma)=1
.

•

Induction Hypothesis
:
r(\gamma)r(\gamma^{*})=1,\ \forall\mathcal{D}(\gamma)<n
.

•

Induction Step
:

Let
\mathcal{D}(\gamma)=n
. If
\gamma
is a series circuit, let
\accentset{\leftrightsquigarrow}{\gamma}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\cdots,\gamma_{k}\}
, where
\mathcal{D}(\gamma_{i})<\mathcal{D}(\gamma)=n,\ \forall i
. Then:

\gamma^{*}=(\{\},\{\gamma^{*}_{1},\gamma^{*}_{2},\gamma^{*}_{3},\cdots,\gamma^{*}_{k}\})\Rightarrow r(\gamma^{*})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{r(\gamma_{i}^{*})}}

By the induction hypothesis:

r(\gamma_{i}^{*})=\frac{1}{r(\gamma_{i})},\ \forall i\Rightarrow r(\gamma^{*})=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r(\gamma_{i})}=\frac{1}{r(\gamma)}.

The parallel case can be proven similarly.

∎

Corollary 2.1.1

(The Lower Bound)
.

Let
M_{n}
be the average resistance of all
n
-circuits. Then,
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
:

M_{n}\geq 1

Proof.

GM(\{r(\gamma):\gamma\in\Gamma_{n}\})=\left(\prod_{\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{n}}r(\gamma)r(\gamma^{*})\right)^{\frac{1}{Q_{n}}}=\left(\prod_{\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{n}}1\right)^{\frac{1}{Q_{n}}}=1

Since the arithmetic mean is always greater than or equal to the geometric mean, this gives the lower bound
M_{n}\geq 1
— equality only occurs at
n=1
.
∎

Theorem 3.1

(Omnicircuit Meta-Counting Property)
.

C_{i}(n)
and
Q_{n}
are related by the following expression:

C_{i}(n)=C_{i}(n-i)+Q_{n-i}

where
i,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\leq n
.

Proof.

Any parallel
i
-circuit appears in
\mathcal{O}_{n}
either as a sub-circuit of or connected to an
(n-i)
-circuit. The first case, by definition, happens
C_{i}(n-i)
times, and the second case happens
Q_{n-i}
times, once for each
n
-circuit.
∎

Corollary 3.1.1
.

The observed behavior of
C_{i}(n)
can be expressed as:

Proof.

i>\frac{n}{2}\Rightarrow i>n-i\Rightarrow C_{i}(n-i)=0
. By
3.1
,
C_{i}(n)=C_{i}(n-i)+Q_{n-i}=Q_{n-i}
.
∎

Corollary 3.1.2
.

C_{i}(n)
can be fully expressed in terms of
Q
by:

C_{i}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}Q_{n-ki}

with special case:

C_{i}(ni)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}Q_{ki}

Proof.

Recursively apply
3.1
to get the general form. Replace
n
with
ni
and re-index to get the special case.
∎

Remark
.

Setting
i=1
,
C_{1}(n)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}Q_{i}
, which is the cumulative sum of
Q_{n}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

By double-counting the number of resistors in
\mathcal{O}_{n}
,

nQ_{n}=2C_{1}(n)-\delta_{1n}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}iQ_{i}C_{i}(n),\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}

Proof.

There are
q_{i}
parallel
i
-circuits, each containing
i
resistors and appearing
C_{i}(n)
times in
\mathcal{O}_{n}
. On the other hand, there are
Q_{n}
circuits in
\mathcal{O}_{n}
, each containing
n
resistors. Thus,
nQ_{n}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}iq_{i}C_{i}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(2q_{i})iC_{i}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{i(Q_{i}+\delta_{1i})C_{i}(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}{iQ_{i}C_{i}(n)}+C_{1}(n)=2C_{1}(n)-\delta_{1n}+\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n-1}{iQ_{i}C_{i}(n)}
.
∎

4
The Generating Function of
Q_{n}

The fundamental difference in the nature of the relationships found between
C_{i}(n)
and
Q_{n}
in the previous section allows for a non-trivial recursive expression of
Q_{n}
. By
3.1.2
and
3.2
:

nQ_{n}=2C_{1}(n)-\delta_{1n}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}iQ_{i}C_{i}(n)=-\delta_{1n}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Q_{i}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}iQ_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}Q_{n-ij}

\Rightarrow Q_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\left(-\delta_{1n}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Q_{i}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}{iQ_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}Q_{n-ij}}\right)

(2)

This allows for
O(n^{2})
computation of
Q_{n}
and
O(n^{3})
computation of all
Q_{i}
up to
i=n
, improving the complexity from exponential time — using brute force or
N(p)
— to polynomial.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the generating function of
Q_{n}
,
\mathcal{Q}(x)
be defined as:

\mathcal{Q}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}Q_{n}x^{n}

Then,
\mathcal{Q}(x)
satisfies:

\mathcal{Q}(x)=\exp{\left(\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\frac{\mathcal{Q}(x^{n})+x^{n}-1}{2n}}\right)}

(3)

and equivalently,

\mathcal{Q}(x)=\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{(1-x^{n})^{-q_{n}}}

(4)

Proof.

This proof is quite technical and can be skipped; nonetheless, it is included for the sake of completeness.

Applying (
2
) inside the generating function gives:

\begin{split}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{nQ_{n}x^{n}}=&\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\left(-\delta_{1n}+2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}Q_{i}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}{iQ_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}Q_{n-ij}}\right)x^{n}}\\
\Rightarrow x\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{Q_{n}\left(nx^{n-1}\right)}=&-x+2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}{Q_{i}x^{n}}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}{iQ_{i}Q_{n-ij}x^{n}}}}\end{split}

By re-indexing and introducing
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(x)
, one can get the following differential equation:

\begin{split}x\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(x)=&-x+2\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{\sum_{n=i+1}^{\infty}{Q_{i}x^{n}}}+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{\sum_{n=i+1}^{\infty}{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}{iQ_{i}Q_{n-ij}x^{n}}}}\\
=&2\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{Q_{i}\frac{x^{i+1}}{1-x}}+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\sum_{n=ij}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}Q_{n-ij}x^{n}}}}-\left(x+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}Q_{0}x^{i}}\right)\\
=&\frac{2x\mathcal{Q}(x)}{1-x}+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{Q_{n}x^{n+ij}}}}-\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}x^{i}}\\
\Rightarrow 2x\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(x)=&\frac{2x\mathcal{Q}(x)}{1-x}+\mathcal{Q}(x)\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{x^{ij}}}\end{split}

Trying to simplify
\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{x^{ij}}}
using geometric series gives the Lambert Series of
Q_{n}
. Instead, one can introduce
\mathcal{Q}(x^{j})
:

\begin{split}2\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(x)}{\mathcal{Q}(x)}=&\frac{2}{1-x}+\frac{1}{x}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}{iQ_{i}\left(x^{j}\right)^{i}}}\\
=&\frac{1}{1-x}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{x^{j-1}iQ_{i}\left(x^{j}\right)^{i-1}}}\\
=&\frac{1}{1-x}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{1}{j}\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{Q}(x^{j})}\end{split}

Then, integrating and using
\ln(1-x)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\frac{x^{n}}{n}}
:

\int_{0}^{x}{\frac{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(t)}{\mathcal{Q}(t)}\ dt}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}{\frac{1}{1-t}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{1}{j}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{Q}(t^{j})}\ dt}

\begin{split}\Rightarrow\ln\mathcal{Q}(x)=&\frac{1}{2}\left(-\ln(1-x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{1}{j}\left(\mathcal{Q}(x^{j})-\mathcal{Q}(0)\right)}\right)\\
=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{\mathcal{Q}(x^{j})+x^{j}-1}{2j}}\\
\Rightarrow\mathcal{Q}(x)=&\exp{\left(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{\mathcal{Q}(x^{j})+x^{j}-1}{2j}}\right)}\end{split}

Finally, the equivalence between (
3
) and (
4
) can be shown as follows:

\begin{split}\ln\mathcal{Q}(x)=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{\mathcal{Q}(x^{j})+x^{j}-1}{2j}}\\
=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{1}{2j}\left(1+x^{j}+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}{Q_{k}x^{jk}}+x^{j}-1\right)}\\
=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}{\left(\frac{x^{j}}{j}+\frac{1}{2j}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}{Q_{k}x^{jk}}\right)}\\
\Leftrightarrow\ln\mathcal{Q}(x)+\ln(1-x)=&\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}{\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}{Q_{k}\frac{x^{jk}}{j}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}{Q_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\frac{x^{jk}}{j}}}\\
=&\frac{-1}{2}\sum\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}{Q_{k}\ln(1-x^{k})}\\
\Leftrightarrow(1-x)\mathcal{Q}(x)=&\prod\limits_{k=2}^{\infty}{(1-x^{k})^{-\frac{Q_{k}}{2}}}\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{Q}(x)=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}{(1-x^{k})^{-q_{k}}}\end{split}

∎

— (

(5)

where

is the reciprocal of a root of

.

Theorem 4.2
.

The asymptotic behavior of
C_{i}(n)
relative to
Q_{n}
is:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{i}(n)}{Q_{n}}=\frac{1}{d^{i}-1},\text{ }\forall i\in\mathbb{N}

Proof.

Using
3.1.2
and (
5
):

\begin{split}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{i}(n)}{Q_{n}}&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}{Q_{n-ij}}}{Q_{n}}\\
&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}{cd^{n-ij}(n-ij)^{-\frac{3}{2}}}}{cd^{n}n^{-\frac{3}{2}}}\\
&=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}{d^{-ij}\left(\frac{n}{n-ij}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\\
\end{split}

Considering a constant
j
, as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
d^{-ij}
does not change and
\left(\frac{n}{n-ij}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}
approaches
1
. The terms that are added due to
n
increasing — generally when
j=an
with
0<a\leq 1
— are of the form
d^{-ani}\left(\frac{n}{n(1-ai)}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}
and will clearly approach
0
. Therefore, it is safe to assume that removing
\left(\frac{n}{n-ij}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}
from the sum will not change the limit:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{C_{i}(n)}{Q_{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor\frac{n}{i}\rfloor}{d^{-ij}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{d^{-ij}}=\frac{d^{-i}}{1-d^{-i}}=\frac{1}{d^{i}-1}

∎

5
Resistance: Circuits & Biscuits

Let
\widetilde{\Omega}_{n}=\{r(\gamma):\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{n}\}
and
\overline{\overline{\Omega}}_{n}=\{r(\gamma):\gamma\in\overline{\overline{\Gamma}}_{n}\}
be the sets of resistances of all series and parallel
n
-circuits, respectively. We begin investigating
R_{n}
by exploring the ranges of these two sets.

Theorem 5.1

(Resistance Range)
.

The ranges of
\widetilde{\Omega}_{n}
and
\overline{\overline{\Omega}}_{n}
,
\forall n>1
can be expressed as:

\frac{4}{n}\leq r(\gamma)\leq n,\ \forall\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{n},\ \ \ \ \frac{1}{n}\leq r(\gamma)\leq\frac{n}{4},\ \forall\gamma\in\overline{\overline{\Gamma}}_{n}.

Proof.

It is clear that
r(\gamma)\leq n,\ \forall\gamma\in\widetilde{\Gamma}_{n}
. It immediately follows from
2.1
that
\frac{1}{n}\leq r(\gamma),\ \forall\gamma\in\overline{\overline{\Gamma}}_{n}
. Thus, if
\widetilde{\gamma}_{min}=(\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3},\cdots,\gamma_{k}\},\{\})
is the series
n
-circuit with the least resistance, then
r(\gamma_{i})=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}
,
\forall i
, where
\alpha_{i}
is the number of resistors in
\gamma_{i}
. This gives:

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}=n,\ \ \ \ \ \ r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}

r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})
can be found by analyzing its partial derivatives with respect to
\alpha_{i}
:

\begin{split}r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})&=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq k,j\neq i}\frac{1}{\left(n-\sum\limits_{1\leq l\leq k,l\neq i,j}\alpha_{l}\right)-\alpha_{i}}\\
\Rightarrow\frac{\partial r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})}{\partial\alpha_{i}}&=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}^{2}}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq k,j\neq i}-\frac{-1}{\left(\left(n-\sum\limits_{1\leq l\leq k,l\neq i,j}\alpha_{l}\right)-\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}}\\
&=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq k,j\neq i}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}=-\frac{2}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}\end{split}

The minimum occurs when
\frac{\partial r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})}{\partial\alpha_{i}}=0,\ \forall i
. In which case:

\begin{split}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}=\frac{2}{\alpha_{i}^{2}},\ \forall i&\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{2}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}}\\
\Rightarrow\sum_{j=1}^{k}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{2}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}}&\Rightarrow k\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}^{2}}=2\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}}\\
\Rightarrow k&=2\end{split}

Thus,
\widetilde{\gamma}_{min}=(\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\},\{\})
and
r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})=\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}
, with
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=n
. Note:

\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}=\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}=\frac{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}{\frac{1}{4}\left((\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})^{2}-(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{2}\right)}=\frac{4n}{n^{2}-(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})^{2}}

Thus
r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})
is minimized when
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are as close to each other as possible. For even
n
, this happens when
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\frac{n}{2}
, and for odd
n
when
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}=\{\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2}\}
. Because
\frac{4n}{n^{2}-1}>\frac{4n}{n^{2}}\geq\frac{4}{n}
, in either case
r(\widetilde{\gamma}_{min})\geq\frac{4}{n}
. By
2.1
,
r(\overline{\overline{\gamma}}_{max})\leq\frac{n}{4}
as well.
∎

Theorem 5.2

(Biscuit Injection and Inversion)
.

Let
\widetilde{\varphi}:\Psi_{n}\rightarrow\widetilde{\Psi}_{n+1}
and
\overline{\overline{\varphi}}:\Psi_{n}\rightarrow\overline{\overline{\Psi}}_{n+1}
represent adding a resistor in series and parallel, respectively. Then, they are both injections. Furthermore,

\widetilde{\varphi}\left(r\right)\overline{\overline{\varphi}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)=1

Proof.

First note that the two functions can be expressed as follows:

\begin{split}\widetilde{\varphi}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)&=\frac{a}{b}+1=\frac{a+b}{b}\\
\overline{\overline{\varphi}}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)&=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\frac{a}{b}}+\frac{1}{1}}=\frac{a}{a+b}\end{split}

So
\widetilde{\varphi}\left(r\right)\overline{\overline{\varphi}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)=1
follows immediately. By way of contradiction assume that
\exists r_{1},r_{2}\in\Psi_{n}.r_{1}\neq r_{2},\widetilde{\varphi}(r_{1})=\widetilde{\varphi}(r_{2})
. Writing
r_{1}=\frac{a}{b}
and
r_{2}=\frac{c}{d}
,

\frac{a+b}{b}=\frac{c+d}{d}\Rightarrow\frac{a}{b}=\frac{c}{d}

which is a contradiction. A similar argument can be used for the injectivity of
\overline{\overline{\varphi}}_{n}
. Furthermore, a bijection is revealed, analogous to
2.1
.
∎

Theorem 5.3

(Biscuit Means)
.

The average resistance of all
n
-biscuits is given by:

\mathfrak{M}_{n}=\frac{3}{2}-2^{-n},\text{ }\forall n\in\mathbb{N}

Moreover ,

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n}=\frac{5}{2}-2^{1-n},\text{ }\overline{\overline{\mathfrak{M}}}_{n}=\frac{1}{2},\text{ }\forall n>1

Proof.

As
\overline{\overline{\Psi}}_{n}=\overline{\overline{\varphi}}(\Psi_{n-1})=\overline{\overline{\varphi}}(\widetilde{\Psi}_{n-1})\cup\overline{\overline{\varphi}}(\overline{\overline{\Psi}}_{n-1}),\ \forall n>1
, by
5.2
one can write:

\begin{split}\overline{\overline{\mathfrak{R}}}_{n}&=\sum_{r\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{n-1}}{\overline{\overline{\varphi}}(r)}+\sum_{r\in\overline{\overline{\Psi}}_{n-1}}{\overline{\overline{\varphi}}(r)}\\
&=\sum_{r\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{n-1}}{\overline{\overline{\varphi}}\left(\frac{r}{1}\right)+\overline{\overline{\varphi}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\right)}\\
&=\sum_{r\in\widetilde{\Psi}_{n-1}}{\frac{r}{r+1}+\frac{1}{r+1}}\\
&=\mathfrak{q}_{n-1}\\
\end{split}

Therefore:

\overline{\overline{\mathfrak{M}}}_{n}=\frac{\overline{\overline{\mathfrak{R}}}_{n}}{\mathfrak{q}_{n}}=\frac{\mathfrak{q}_{n-1}}{\mathfrak{q}_{n}}=\frac{1}{2}

Using this result, a recurrence relation for the average resistance of series
n
-biscuits can be derived as follows:

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}_{n}&=\mathfrak{R}_{n-1}+\mathfrak{Q}_{n-1}\\
&=\widetilde{\mathfrak{R}}_{n-1}+\overline{\overline{\mathfrak{R}}}_{n-1}+2\mathfrak{q}_{n-1}\\
\Rightarrow 2\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n}&=\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-1}+\frac{1}{2}+2\\
\Rightarrow\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n+1}&=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n}+\frac{5}{4},\text{ }\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}=2\end{split}

Solving this recurrence relation results in:

\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n}=\frac{5}{2}-2^{1-n},\text{ }\forall n>1

Finally, averaging over means gives:

\mathfrak{M}_{n}=\frac{\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n}+\overline{\overline{\mathfrak{M}}}_{n}}{2}=\frac{3}{2}-2^{-n},\text{ }\forall n>1

Coincidentally, the expression is also satisfied for
n=1
as
\mathfrak{M}_{1}=1=\frac{3}{2}-2^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

For any two positive sequences
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
, the following inequality holds:

\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{a_{i}}+\frac{1}{b_{j}}}}}}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}}}+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{b_{i}}}}

Proof.

Start by considering the
AM-HM
inequality for the sequence
\{a_{i}+b_{j}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, where
j
is constant:

\frac{m}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{1}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}+b_{j}}

This inequality can be modified to get the result as follows:

\begin{split}\frac{m}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{1}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}}&\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}+b_{j}}\\
\Rightarrow\frac{m}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{1}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}\\
\Rightarrow m-\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{b_{j}}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}&\leq m-\frac{b_{j}m}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}\\
\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(1-\frac{b_{j}}{a_{i}+b_{j}}\right)}&\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{(a_{i}+b_{j})}-b_{j}m}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}\\
\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{a_{i}}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}&\leq\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}}}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}\\
\Rightarrow\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{a_{i}b_{j}}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}&\leq\frac{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}b_{j}}}{\frac{1}{m}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}}\right)+b_{j}}\\
\Rightarrow\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{a_{i}}+\frac{1}{b_{j}}}}}&\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}}}+\frac{1}{b_{j}}}\end{split}

Clearly, the symmetric version of this inequality also holds. Namely:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{b_{j}}+\frac{1}{a_{i}}}}}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{b_{j}}}+\frac{1}{a_{i}}}

The result follows immediately:

\frac{1}{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{a_{i}}+\frac{1}{b_{j}}}}}}\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{b_{j}}}+\frac{1}{a_{i}}}}\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{a_{i}}}+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{b_{i}}}}

∎

We seek to establish a connection between circuits and biscuits that will later allow us to finally bound
M_{n}
. Define rearranging an
n
-biscuit as combining its single resistors in one or more depths to create sub-circuits without modifying the non-unit sub-circuits. Then:

Theorem 5.5

(The Connection)
.

Rearranging series and parallel
n
-biscuits, on average, decreases and increases their resistance respectively.

Proof.

Begin by noting that any
n
-biscuit has at most one non-unit sub-circuit; this is because, by definition, the construction of an
n
-biscuit is invertible. Hence, if one assumes by way of contradiction that there exists an
n
-biscuit with more than one non-unit sub-circuit, after reverting the construction of all of its unit sub-circuits, one can remove neither a series nor a parallel connection to a unit circuit, reaching a contradiction.

Now consider a parallel
n
-biscuit
\beta
with
k
unit sub-circuits and a non-unit sub-circuit that is a series
(n-k)
-biscuit. As by
5.1
the connection of the
k
unit circuits in parallel is the smallest possible value for the resistance of a
k
-circuit, any first-depth rearrangement of this biscuit will result in a positive change in its total resistance. Inversely, any such rearrangement in a series biscuit will result in a negative change; this corresponds to a second-depth rearrangement of
\beta
. This argument can be recursively repeated to show that any odd-depth rearrangement of parallel
n
-biscuits results in a positive change in total resistance and any even-depth rearrangement in a negative; the converse is true for series circuits. It can be shown, using calculus, that the magnitude of change decreases as depth increases, resulting in the statement of the theorem. The rigorous proof is left as an exercise for the reader.
∎

We now have the required tools to return to the main topic of investigation, the average resistance of
n
-circuits.

Theorem 5.6
.

The average resistance of parallel
n
-circuits is bounded by

\frac{1}{2}<\overline{\overline{M}}_{n}<\frac{5}{2}

Proof.

Any parallel
n
-circuit can be thought of as a rearrangement of a parallel
n
-biscuit, and
5.5
shows that the average resistance of these rearrangements is higher than that of parallel
n
-biscuits, resulting in the lower bound.

Using
5.4
and setting
\{a_{i}\}
and
\{b_{i}\}
to
\Omega_{k}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}_{n-k}
for any
1<k<n
, one can get:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{Q_{k}\mathfrak{q}_{n-k}}\sum_{i=1}^{Q_{k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{q}_{n-k}}{{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{j}}}}}}&\leq\frac{1}{\frac{1}{M_{k}}+\frac{1}{\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}}=\frac{M_{k}\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}{M_{k}+\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}\\
\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{Q_{k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{q}_{n-k}}{{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\gamma_{i}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{j}}}}}}&\leq\frac{Q_{k}\mathfrak{q}_{n-k}M_{k}\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}{M_{k}+\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}\end{split}

Summing the left hand side over all
k
gives the total resistance of all first-depth rearrangements of all parallel
n
-biscuits except for the parallel connection of
n
resistors which can be neglected. Note that this includes all parallel
n
-circuits except for the ones that require higher-depth rearrangements. By
5.5
, however, we know that the weighted average of these terms should be larger than the true average of parallel
n
-circuits. Thus:

\begin{split}\overline{\overline{M}}_{n}&\leq\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{Q_{k}\mathfrak{q}_{n-k}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{Q_{k}\mathfrak{q}_{n-k}M_{k}\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}{M_{k}+\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{Q_{k}2^{-k}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{2^{-k}Q_{k}M_{k}\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}{M_{k}+\widetilde{\mathfrak{M}}_{n-k}}\\
&<\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{5M_{k}}{2M_{k}+5}Q_{k}2^{-k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{Q_{k}2^{-k}}}\leq\max{\left\{\frac{5M_{k}}{2M_{k}+5}\right\}}<\frac{5}{2}\end{split}

∎

.

The average resistance of

Proof.

-parallel connection of a multiset of

-series circuits

-resistance of

. As

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}{r_{k}(\gamma_{i})^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(\frac{1}{r_{k}(\gamma_{i})}\right)^{-k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\frac{1}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left(\frac{1}{r_{k}(\gamma_{i})}\right)^{-k}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{k}}},

the main property of the system,

Theorem 6.2
.

A more general form of the remark on

\sum_{k=1}^{i}C_{i}(n+k)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}Q_{k}

\begin{split}&\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}C_{i}(n+k)=\\

\end{split}
