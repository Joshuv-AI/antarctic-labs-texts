Abstract
. We introduce the notions of Fatou and Julia sets in the context of word maps on complex Lie groups and polynomial maps on finite-dimensional associative ℂ \mathbb{C} -algebras. For the group-theoretic question, we investigate the dynamics of the power map x ↦ x M x\mapsto x^{M} on the Lie group GL n ​ ( ℂ ) \mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}) , where M ≥ 2 M\geq 2 is an integer. For the algebra-related question, we study polynomial self-maps of M n ​ ( ℂ ) \mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}) induced by monic polynomials in one variable. In both cases, we pin down the explicit description of the Fatou and Julia sets. We also show that there does not exist any wandering Fatou component of the pair ( p , M n ​ ( ℂ ) ) (p,\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})) where p ∈ ℂ ⁡ [ z ] p\in\mathbb{C}[z] is a monic polynomial of degree ≥ 2 \geq 2 .

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Complex dynamics

The modern study of discrete complex dynamical systems dates back to the early twentieth century, beginning with the seminal work of P. Fatou and G. Julia on the iteration of rational maps
\varphi(z)\in\mathbb{C}(z)
on the Riemann sphere; see, for instance,
[
14
,
8
]
.
A natural point of departure is the analysis of fixed points—those
z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
\varphi(z)=z
. These are classified by the value of
\varphi^{\prime}(z)
as attracting, repelling, or neutral, reflecting whether nearby points approach or recede from the fixed point under iteration; periodic and preperiodic points are also studied in the same spirit.

More subtle features of the dynamics appear in the Fatou and Julia sets. The Fatou set consists of points where the iterates of
\varphi
vary stably, in the sense that small perturbations remain small under iteration. Its complement, the Julia set, is the locus of chaotic behaviour, where arbitrarily small errors can be amplified indefinitely.
Towards the end of the twentieth century, this theory was extended to several complex variables in foundational work by Bedford–Smillie, Fornæss–Sibony, and others; see
[
2
,
3
,
11
,
9
]
.

1.2.
Word maps on groups and polynomial maps on algebras

Let
\mathbf{C}
be the category of groups (or of
k
-algebras for some field
k
).
Let
w\in\mathbf{F}_{r}
, where
\mathbf{F}_{r}\in\mathbf{C}
is the free group (resp. free algebra) on
r
generators.
For any object
X\in\mathbf{C}
, one obtains a map

\displaystyle\widetilde{w}:X^{r}\longrightarrow X,

defined by substitution.
When
X
is a group (resp. an algebra), the map
\widetilde{w}
is called a
word map
(resp. a
polynomial map
).
By abuse of notation, we denote the map
\widetilde{w}
by
w
itself.

The study of word maps dates back at least to 1951, to work of Øystein Ore, who proved that every element of the alternating group
A_{n}
is a commutator and conjectured that the same holds for all finite non-abelian simple groups; see
[
21
]
.
This conjecture was resolved in full generality through the efforts of many mathematicians; see
[
19
]
and the references therein.
In a related direction, a striking result was later established: for every finite non-abelian simple group
G
of sufficiently large order, and every nontrivial word
w
, one has
w(G)^{2}=G
; see
[
18
]
.
However
2
can not be reduced further; for example squaring map on
A_{5}
is not surjective.
In this article we study the
power map

x\mapsto x^{M}
for an integer
M\geq 2
.
Such maps have been investigated extensively: by Chatterjee and Steinberg, independently, in the context of algebraic groups
[
6
,
33
]
; for some finite groups of Lie type in
[
17
,
28
]
, for general linear groups over finite principal ideal local rings of length two in
[
25
]
; see also
[
30
]
and the references therein.
Power maps exhibit a number of interesting features (see
[
23
]
), and they also provide a useful tool for studying the number of real conjugacy classes
[
29
]
.

On the other hand, the study of polynomial maps has a long history.
For example, Shoda proved in 1937 that over a field of characteristic zero, every trace-zero matrix is a commutator; see
[
32
]
.
Kaplansky and Lv́ov later conjectured that the image of a multilinear polynomial on
\mathrm{M}_{n}(k)
, the full matrix algebra over an infinite field
k
, is always a vector space.
Several positive results supporting this conjecture are known–see, for instance,
[
15
,
16
]
–but the conjecture remains wide open in the cases
n\geq 4
.
Brěsar in 2020 proved that
[
5
]
if
C
is a commutative unital algebra over a field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
0
,
A=\mathrm{M}_{n}(C)
, and the polynomial
f
is neither an identity nor a central polynomial of
A
, then every commutator in
A
can be written as a difference of two elements, each of which is a sum of
7788
elements from
f(A)
; for a stronger result see
[
4
]
.
Polynomial maps have also been investigated in other algebraic settings: for instance, upper triangular matrix algebras were studied in
[
24
]
, and Octonion algebras in
[
27
]
; see also
[
26
]
for a more general formulation of this problem.

1.3.
Motivation and the statement of the result

Motivated by the study of several complex variables, we aim to study Fatou and Julia sets in the context of word maps on complex Lie groups and polynomial maps on
\mathbb{C}
-algebras.
Since a word map is a map
w:X^{r}\longrightarrow X
, it does not make sense to iterate; that is why we need to work with a tuple of maps, see
Section

2
.
However, when one considers the free object on one generator
\mathbf{F}_{1}\in\mathbf{C}
, then a relevant
w\in\mathbf{F}_{1}
induces a map
w:X\longrightarrow X
.
With this in mind, we investigate the dynamics for the power map on the group
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and the polynomial map in one variable on the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Our main result is the following:

1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{C}[x]
be a monic polynomial of degree
\geq 2
and let

X\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
X\in\mathscr{F}_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if and only if
\sigma(X)\subseteq\mathscr{F}_{p}(\mathbb{C})
, where
\sigma(X)
denotes the spectrum of
X
. Furthermore
\mathscr{F}_{x^{M}}(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))=\mathscr{F}_{x^{M}}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))\cap\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

This can be seen as a generalisation of results obtained in
[
22
]
.
After providing necessary definitions and discussing some examples in
Section

2
, we prove the main theorem in
Section

3
.

2.
Preliminary definitions and known results

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a complex Lie group and
w\in\mathscr{F}_{r}
be an element from the free group on
r
generators, say
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}
.
This defines a
word map on
G
,

(g_{1},g_{2},\cdots,g_{r})\mapsto w(g_{1},g_{2},\cdots,g_{r})
, by plugging
g_{i}
in place of
x_{i}
.
Given
r
words
w_{1},\cdots,w_{r}
one gets a map
\underline{w}:G^{r}\longrightarrow G^{r}
by defining:

\underline{g}=(g_{1},g_{2},\cdots,g_{r})\mapsto({w_{1}(\underline{g}),\cdots,w_{r}(\underline{g})}).

The
Fatou set of the pair
(G,\underline{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r}))
consists of the set of points
x\in G^{r}
which have a neighborhood
U
such that
(\underline{w}^{n}|_{U})_{n}
is normal.
Here
\underline{w}^{1}(x)=\underline{w}(x)
,
\underline{w}^{2}(x)=\underline{w}(\underline{w}(x))
and more generally
\underline{w}^{n}(x)=\underline{w}(\underline{w}^{n-1}(x))
.

With necessary modification, for a topological
\mathbb{C}
-algebra
A
and
f_{1},\cdots,f_{r}\in\mathbb{C}\langle x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r}\rangle
the
Fatou set of the pair
(A,\underline{f}=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{r}))
consists of the set of points
x\in A^{r}
which have a neighborhood
U
such that
(\underline{f}^{n}|_{U})_{n}
is normal.

The
Julia set
of the pair is defined to be the set-theoretic complement of the Fatou set.

The Julia set for the pair
(G,w)
, of a group and a word, is denoted by
\mathscr{J}_{w}(G)
, and the Fatou set is denoted by
\mathscr{F}_{w}(G)
; similar notation is followed for the pair
(A,f)
, of an algebra and a polynomial.
Our definitions are motivated by the theory of several complex variables.
In this article, we compute the Fatou set (consequently Julia set) for the pairs
(\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),x^{M})
and
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),x^{M}+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{M-1}a_{i}x^{i})
, where
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is considered to be a topological group and
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is considered to be a topological algebra; here
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,M-1
.

In the spirit of the theory of a single complex variable, we define the
filled Julia set
for the pair
(G,\underline{w})
to be

\displaystyle K_{\underline{w}}(G)=\left\{x\in G^{r}:\left(\underline{w}^{n}(x)\right)_{n\geq 0}\text{ is a bounded sequence}\right\}.

For a polynomial function (of one variable)
p
on
\mathbb{C}
, one has the Julia set
J_{p}(\mathbb{C})=\partial K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
; see
[
20
, Lemma 9.4]
.
However, this need not continue to hold in general, as we see in later sections.
So one needs to be careful while dealing with the case of several variables.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
G
be a complex Lie group (resp.
A
be a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra). Given a word map (resp. polynomial map)
\chi
, the associated Fatou set is the disjoint union of its connected components, known as
Fatou components
.
The Fatou components are permuted by
\chi
.
For a Fatou component
U\subseteq G^{r}
(resp.
\subseteq A^{r}
), it is (a)
periodic
if
\chi^{p}(U)=U
for some
p>0
, (b)
preperiodic
if
\chi^{k}(U)
is periodic, and (c)
wandering Fatou component
if the sets
\left\{\chi^{k}(U)\right\}_{k\geq 0}
are pairwise disjoint.

Let us now see some examples.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the group
\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
with
r=2
.
Take the words
w_{1}=x_{2}
and
w_{2}=x_{1}^{2}x_{2}\in\mathbf{F}_{2}
, and the pair of matrices

\displaystyle(A,B)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\right).

Then the induced map is

\displaystyle W(X,Y)=(Y,\,X^{2}Y).

For instance,

\displaystyle W^{3}(A,B)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\
7&5\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}89&62\\
33&23\end{pmatrix}\right).

Moreover,

\displaystyle W^{6}(A,B)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{pmatrix}69210849&48219134\\
25665025&17880799\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1557268252466751&1084947340259330\\
3792477575677951&2642215592726081\end{pmatrix}\right).

Example 2.4
.

Consider the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
with
r=2
.
Let
w_{1}=x_{2}
and
w_{2}=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}+x_{2}\in\mathbf{F}_{2}
, and take

\displaystyle(A,B)=\left(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

Then

For later use, we tally the Sullivan classification of Fatou Components on the Riemann sphere.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
20
, Theorem 16.1]

Let
f
be a rational function on the Riemann sphere.
If
f
maps the Fatou component
U
onto itself,
then there are just four possibilities, as follows: Either
U
is the
immediate basin for an attracting fixed point or for one petal of a parabolic fixed point which has multiplier
\lambda=1
or else
U
is a Siegel disk or Herman ring.

Further by
[
20
, Lemma 9.4]
, all the bounded components of the Fatou set of a polynomial of degree
\geq 2
are simply connected.
Thus while analyzing the elements of the Fatou components of the polynomial
f
, one gets exactly three possibilities; Herman ring does not occur (as it is conformally isomorphic to some annulus).
To check whether the family
\{w^{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
is normal or not, we use the criterion developed by Gerado and Krantz which applies to complex manifold.
For future reference we note it down here.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
1
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a complex manifold. Let
N
be a complete
complex Hermitian manifold of dimension
k
.
Let
\mathscr{F}=\left\{f_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\in A}\subseteq\mathrm{Hol}(\mathscr{M},N)
.
The family
\mathscr{F}
is not normal if and only if there exist (a) a compact set
K\subseteq\subseteq\mathscr{M}
, (b) a sequence
\{p_{j}\}\subseteq K
, (c) a sequence
\{f_{j}\}\subseteq\mathscr{F}
, (d) a sequence of reals
\{\rho_{j}\}
satisfying
\rho_{j}>0
and
\rho_{j}\longrightarrow 0^{+}
and (e) a sequence of Euclidean vectors
\{\varepsilon_{j}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle g_{j}(\zeta)=f_{j}(p_{j}+\rho_{j}\varepsilon_{j}\zeta),\,\,\,\zeta\in\mathbb{C},

converges uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
to a
nonconstant
entire function
g
.

3.
Polynomial maps on matrix rings and power maps on general linear group

We start with the following lemma, which reduces the problem of candidacy of
A
in
\mathscr{J}_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
(equivalently
\mathscr{F}_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
) to that of the Jordan canonical form of
A
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{C}[z]
. For a matrix
X\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, it is an element of the Julia set
\mathscr{J}_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
if and only if for any
Y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the matrix
YXY^{-1}
is in the Julia set
\mathscr{J}_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proof.

For any matrix
Y\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
p(z)=z^{n}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{n-i}z^{n-i}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
,

\displaystyle p(Y^{-1}XY)

\displaystyle=(Y^{-1}XY)^{n}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{n-i}(Y^{-1}XY)^{n-i}

\displaystyle=Y^{-1}X^{n}Y+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{n-i}Y^{-1}X^{n-i}Y=Y^{-1}XY.

Since conjugation by
Y
is a homeomorphism of the algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the result follows.
∎

Consider the map
w(x)=x^{M}
for the polynomial algebra
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.
Let
g\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be regular semisimple; hence there exists a
Q\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
QgQ^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
with
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for all
i\neq j
.
Then
w^{m}(g)=g^{M^{m}}
; which is equal to
Q^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{1}^{M^{m}},\cdots,\alpha_{n}^{M^{m}})Q
.
Recall that the spectral radius of
g
is by definition
\rho(g)=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}|\alpha_{i}|
.
Let
g\in\mathscr{J}_{w}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
such that
\rho(g)<1
, we get that
w^{n}(g)\longrightarrow 0
as
n\longrightarrow\infty
.
Since
g
is regular semisimple, and the set of regular semisimple elements forms an open set of
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
w
is a holomorphic map of full rank.
Hence, by
[
10
, Proposition 2.4]
, the Julia set
\mathscr{J}_{w}
satisfies
w(\mathscr{J}_{w})\subseteq\mathscr{J}_{w}
, which would imply that
0\in\mathscr{J}_{w}
.

Let us calculate the Jacobian of
w
at
g
.
Note that up to conjugation
g=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
.
Then for the standard matrix units
E_{ij}
, we have

d(w^{m})_{g}(E_{ij})=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{M^{m}-1}\alpha_{i}^{\,k}\alpha_{j}^{M^{m}-1-k}\right)E_{ij}.

Hence each
E_{ij}
is an eigenvector of
d(w^{m})_{g}
, with corresponding eigenvalue

\mu_{ij}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\alpha_{i}^{M^{m}}-\alpha_{j}^{M^{m}}}{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}},&i\neq j,\\
M^{m}\alpha_{i}^{M^{m}-1},&i=j.\end{cases}

Moreover, since
|\alpha_{i}|<1
, as
n\longrightarrow\infty
, the Jacobian tends to
0
, which implies that the zero matrix is an attracting point corresponding to the pair
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),x^{M})
.
This contradicts the assertion that
0\in\mathscr{J}_{w}
.
Therefore, for any regular semisimple element
g
with
\rho(g)<1
, we have
g\not\in\mathscr{J}_{w}
.

Next, if
\rho(g)>1
, the sequence
{w^{m}}
diverges uniformly to infinity in a small neighborhood of
g
, since this neighborhood consists entirely of regular semisimple elements whose eigenvalues remain close to those of
g
.
We note the whole discussion in the following remark.

Remark 3.2
.

For the pair
(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}),x^{M})
, the inclusion of a regular semisimple element
g\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
in the Julia set implies that
\rho(g)=1
.

Let us point out that it is not true that for a matrix
A\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
the sequence
\{p^{m}(A)\}
is bounded if and only if the eigenvalues of
A
are in
K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
.
Consider the power map
p(z)=z^{2}
and the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\
&1&1\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{M}_{3}(\mathbb{C})
.
Obviously
1\in K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
, but

\displaystyle p^{m}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}1&2^{m}&2^{m-1}\\
&1&2^{m}\\
&&1\end{pmatrix}

implies that the sequence
\{p^{m}(A)\}_{m\geq 0}
is unbounded.

Now we determine some elements
g\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
g\in K_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
, where
p\in\mathbb{C}[x]
is a monic polynomial.
Recall the Jordan–Chevalley decomposition of a linear operator
g=g_{s}+g_{u}\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, where
g_{s}
is semisimple and
g_{u}
is nilpotent. (In the literature, the nilpotent part is usually denoted by
g_{n}
; however, to avoid confusion with the dimension parameter
n
, we adopt the notation
g_{u}
.)
We have the following result;

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\deg p\geq 2
and
g\in\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. The following statements hold.

(1)

if
g
is semisimple and the eigenvalues of
g
are in
K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
, then
g\in K_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
,

(2)

if
g_{u}\neq 0
, and the eigenvalues of
g_{s}
are in
\mathrm{Int}K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
(the interior of
K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
), then
g\in K_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proof.

(1) Let
g
be semisimple.
Then there exists
Q\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that

Q^{-1}gQ=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})
for some
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.
Then

\displaystyle p^{m}(g)=Q\begin{pmatrix}p^{m}(\alpha_{1})&&&\\
&p^{m}(\alpha_{2})&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&p^{m}(\alpha_{n})\end{pmatrix}Q^{-1}.

Since conjugation by an element of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a homeomorphism of
\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the sequence
\left\{p^{m}(g)\right\}_{m\geq 0}
is bounded if each of the sequence
\left\{p^{m}(\alpha_{i})\right\}_{m\geq 0}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

(2) Since
\mathbb{C}
is algebraically closed, there exists
Q\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that

Q^{-1}gQ=\bigoplus\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}J_{\alpha_{i},m_{i}}
,
where
J_{\alpha_{i},m_{i}}\in\mathrm{M}_{m_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
is the matrix

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{i}&1&&&&\\
&\alpha_{i}&1&&&\\
&&\alpha_{i}&&&\\
&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&\alpha_{i}&1\\
&&&&&\alpha_{i}\\
\end{pmatrix};

here,
\alpha_{i}
s can be the same for two distinct
i
.
Whether this element belongs to
K_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
is equivalent to check whether the elements
J_{\alpha_{i},m_{i}}
are member of
K_{p}(\mathrm{M}_{m_{i}}(\mathbb{C}))
.
Without loss of generality, thus we may assume that
g
is of the form
J_{\alpha,n}
. By induction, we have

\displaystyle(p^{m})(J_{\alpha,n})=\begin{pmatrix}(p^{m})(\alpha)&(p^{m})^{(1)}(\alpha)&\cdots&(p^{m})^{(n-1)}(\alpha)\\
&(p^{m})(\alpha)&\cdots&(p^{m})^{(n-2)}(\alpha)\\
&&\ddots&(p^{m})^{(1)}(\alpha)\\
&&&(p^{m})(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}.

Given
\alpha\in\mathrm{Int}K_{p}(\mathbb{C})
, it must belong to one bounded component of the Julia set which can be of three types, see
Lemma

2.5
.
We study each case separately, starting with the case when
\alpha
lies in an attracting basin.

Case 1:

Suppose
U
is the basin of an attracting periodic cycle of period
N
, and let

z\in U
. Then the sequence
\{p^{m}(z)\}
is bounded by definition and we want to show that for every
k\geq 1

the sequence
\{(p^{m})^{(k)}(z)\}
is bounded.

It is enough to prove the statement for the
p^{N}
and the derivatives
(p^{N})^{(m)}
, where
p^{N}(\alpha)=\alpha
; here
\alpha
is an attracting fixed point of the map
q:=p^{N}
,
|q^{\prime}(\alpha)|<1
.
By continuity of
q^{\prime}
at
\alpha
we can choose
r>0
and
0<\rho<1
such that

\displaystyle\overline{D(\alpha,r)}\subset U\text{ and }\sup_{w\in D(\alpha,r)}|q^{\prime}(w)|\leq\rho<1.

which implies

\displaystyle|q(w)-q(\alpha)|=|q(w)-\alpha|\leq\rho\,|w-\alpha|\leq\rho r,

since
\overline{D(\alpha,r)}
is compact.
Hence
q(D(\alpha,r))\subset D(\alpha,\rho r)\subset D(\alpha,r)
; on iteration one gets,

q^{m}(D(\alpha,r))\subset D(\alpha,r)
for all
m\geq 0.

Fix any
0<r_{0}<r
and any point
y\in D(\alpha,r_{0})
. Because
q^{m}
maps

\overline{D(\alpha,r)}
into itself for every
m
, one gets the uniform bound

\sup\limits_{w\in D(\alpha,r)}|q^{m}(w)|\leq M:=|\alpha|+r,

independent of
m
.
Applying the Cauchy integral formula on the disk
D(\alpha,r)
: for any
k\geq 1
and any

y\in D(\alpha,r_{0})
with
r_{0}<r
,

\displaystyle(q^{m})^{(k)}(y)=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{|\zeta-\alpha|=r}\frac{q^{m}(\zeta)}{(\zeta-y)^{k+1}}\,d\zeta.

Using
|\zeta-y|\geq r-r_{0}
on the circle, we get that

\displaystyle\big|(q^{m})^{(k)}(y)\big|\leq\frac{k!}{(r-r_{0})^{k}}\,\sup_{|\zeta-\alpha|=r}|q^{m}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{k!\,M}{(r-r_{0})^{k}},

which is independent of
m
. Thus for every fixed
k
and every
y\in D(\alpha,r_{0})

the sequence
\{(q^{m})^{(k)}(y)\}_{m\geq 0}
is uniformly bounded.
For arbitrary
\ell
, we may find
r
and
\ell^{\prime}
such that
\ell=Nr+\ell^{\prime}
with
0\leq\ell^{\prime}<N
. Then one observes that

and applies the chain rule.

Case 2:
Let
\alpha\in U
where

is an immediate attracting petal for a parabolic
periodic point of
p
.
As in the previous case, we may assume that
\alpha
is a fixed point, keeping the notation

.
The iterates
q^{m}
converge uniformly on compact subsets of the
immediate petal
U
to the parabolic fixed point
\alpha
.
Fix the given

and choose

r>0
with
\overline{D(z,r)}\subseteq U
. Uniform convergence on the compact set

\overline{D(z,r)}
implies the family
\{q^{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
is uniformly bounded there, and hence there
exists
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 0\\
\zeta\in\overline{D(z,r)}\end{subarray}}|q^{m}(\zeta)|\leq M.

Now, for every
k\geq 1
and every

m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle(q^{m})^{(k)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{k!}{2\pi i}\int\limits_{|\zeta-z|=r}\frac{q^{m}(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^{k+1}}\,d\zeta,

and therefore,

\displaystyle\big|(q^{m})^{(k)}(z)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k!}{r^{k}}\,\sup_{|\zeta-z|=r}|q^{m}(\zeta)|\leq\frac{k!\,M}{r^{k}},

which is independent of
m
. Thus for each fixed
k
the sequence
\{(q^{m})^{(k)}(z)\}
is
uniformly bounded.

Case 3:

Lastly let
\alpha\in U
such that
U
is a Siegel disk for
p
, i.e. there
exists a conformal map
\phi:\mathrm{D}_{r}\to U
from some disk
\mathrm{D}_{r}=\{w\in\mathbb{C}:|w|<r\}
and an irrational
\theta
such that

\displaystyle\phi(e^{2\pi i\theta}w)=p(\phi(w))\qquad\text{for }w\in\mathrm{D}_{R}.

By the conjugacy relation
\phi\circ R=\;p\circ\phi
with
R(w)=e^{2\pi i\theta}w
we have
for every
m\geq 0

\displaystyle p^{m}=\phi\circ R^{m}\circ\phi^{-1}.

Set
w_{m}:=R^{m}(w_{0})=e^{2\pi im\theta}w_{0}
. Since
|w_{m}|=|w_{0}|<r
for all
m
, the points

\{w_{m}\}
lie in a compact subset of
\mathrm{D}_{r}
, and hence the maps
\phi
and its inverse
\phi^{-1}
have all their derivatives to be uniformly bounded on the relevant compact sets.

For the first derivative,

\displaystyle(p^{m})^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=\phi^{\prime}(w_{m})\cdot(R^{m})^{\prime}(w_{0})\cdot(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}(z)=\phi^{\prime}(w_{m})\,e^{2\pi im\theta}\,(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}(z).

Hence

\displaystyle\big|(p^{m})^{\prime}(z)\big|=|\phi^{\prime}(w_{m})|\;|(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}(z)|,

and the right-hand side is uniformly bounded in
m
because
\{\phi^{\prime}(w_{m})\}_{m}
is bounded
(on the compact set containing the
w_{m}
) while
(\phi^{-1})^{\prime}(z)
is a fixed finite number.

For higher derivatives, firstly note that
R^{m}(z)=e^{2\pi mi}z
, and hence all the higher (
\geq 2
) derivatives vanish.
Further, for each fixed
k
, there exist
constants
C_{j,\ell}
(depending only on
k
) such that

\displaystyle(p^{m})^{(k)}(z)=\sum_{1\leq j\leq k,\;0\leq\ell\leq k}C_{j,\ell}\;\phi^{(j)}(w_{m})\;(\phi^{-1})^{(\ell)}(z),

by the chain rule.
Because

remains in a compact subset of
\mathrm{D}_{r}
, the values
\phi^{(j)}(w_{m})
are
uniformly bounded in
m
for every fixed

, and the finitely many derivatives

(\phi^{-1})^{(\ell)}(z)
are fixed numbers. Therefore each
(p^{m})^{(k)}(z)
is uniformly
bounded in
m
.
∎

Corollary 3.6

Let
C
be a Fatou component of the pair
(p,\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
.

m

.

The map

\displaystyle\chi:\mathrm{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n},

\displaystyle A
