All Borel Group Extensions of Finite-Dimensional Real Space Are Trivial
Linus Richter

Abstract
. For N ≥ 2 N\geq 2 , we study the structure of definable abelian group extensions of the additive group ( ℝ N , + ) (\mathbb{R}^{N},+) by countable abelian (Borel) groups G G . Given an extension H H of ( ℝ N , + ) (\mathbb{R}^{N},+) by G G , we measure the definability of H H by investigating its complexity as a Borel set. We do this by combining homological algebra and descriptive set theory, and hence study the Borel complexity of those functions inducing H H , the abelian cocycles. We prove that, for every N ≥ 2 N\geq 2 , there are no non-trivial Borel definable abelian cocycles coding group extensions of ( ℝ N , + ) (\mathbb{R}^{N},+) by a countable abelian group G G , and hence show that no non-trivial such group extensions exist. This completes the picture first investigated by Kanovei and Reeken in 2000, who proved the case N = 1 N=1 , and whose techniques we adapt in this work.

Background

The theory of group extensions of abelian groups by abelian groups can be expressed in two ways:
category-theoretically
(i.e. in terms of short exact sequences, as indicated in

group cohomology
, which captures the relationship between certain functions between groups:

[

Let

be a Borel group. A function

is an

(or just

\displaystyle C(x,y)=C(y,x)\text{ \; and \; }C(x,y)+C(x+y,z)=C(x,z)+C(x+z,y)

if there exists a map

such that

C(x,y)=C_{h}(x,y)=h(x)+h(y)-h(x+y).

The quotient group of cocycles modulo coboundaries yields the
cohomology group

H^{2}(A,G)

[
35
, 9.2]
, which measures complexity: the more complicated
H^{2}(A,G)
is as a group, the more complicated is the structure of group extensions of
A
by
G
. It is clear that two cocycles are
equivalent
if their difference is a coboundary

Remark
.

The definition of coboundaries hints at the relationship between the theory of group extensions and the aforementioned Hyers-Ulam-Rassias-stability framework of almost homomorphisms: in the sense of Ulam, coboundaries are trivial objects (well-behaved almost homomorphisms), just as they are trivial objects in the study of group cohomology (by definition of the quotient group).

To explain our theorems, we need to introduce descriptive set theory. A topological space is

if it is separable and completely metrisable. The

[

which codes the construction of

2.1.
Algebraic properties of abelian cocycles

In the subsequent arguments, we generally separate
algebraic properties
of abelian cocycles from
non-algebraic
properties.

As the name suggests, algebraic properties hold for all abelian cocycles; the underlying space and its topology are inconsequential. On the contrary, non-algebraic properties depend on the underlying topological and order-theoretical properties. For instance, the reals
\mathbb{R}
are ordered, while
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is not. Hence, any proof of
Theorem

1.2

\mathbb{R}^{2}

The difficulties of extending
Theorem

1.2
reduce to proving topological lemmas which permit the application of algebraic properties. This is our task in the following section. In this section, we collect useful algebraic properties of abelian cocycles—all can be found in
[
22
]

and a group

C\colon X^{2}\rightarrow G
be an abelian cocycle.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in X
we have

C(x_{1},x_{2})=C(x_{1},x_{3})+C(x_{1}+x_{3},x_{2})-C(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{3}).

Cocycles can be extended naturally to accept more arguments: let

\displaystyle C(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

\displaystyle=C(x_{1},x_{2})+C(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{3})

and hence recursively define

\displaystyle C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+C(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n},x_{n+1}).

Various useful identities can be deduced from this recursive definition, such as the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{m}\in X
, let
x=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}
and
x^{\prime}=x_{n+1}+\ldots+x_{m}
. Then

C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})+C(x_{n+1},\ldots,x_{m})+C(x,x^{\prime}).

Recall that a cocycle
C
is a coboundary if there exists a map
h\colon X\rightarrow G
for which

C(x_{1},x_{2})=h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})-h(x_{1}+x_{2}).

A simple induction proves:

Lemma 2.4
.

C_{h}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=h(x_{1})+\ldots+h(x_{n})-h(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n})

Further, since
H^{2}(X,G)
is a group it immediately follows that:

Lemma 2.5
.

If
C_{h}
and
C_{k}
are coboundaries, then
C_{h}+C_{k}=C_{h+k}
, where

and

are Borel coboundaries (i.e.

.

Proposition

\sum_{j\leq n}(\pi_{1}(y_{i_{j}})-\pi_{1}(x_{i_{j}}))\geq\pi_{1}(x_{1})-\pi_{1}(y_{1})

\pi_{1}(y_{i_{j}})-\pi_{1}(x_{i_{j}})>0

. We now build sequences

(a)

Suppose there exist

\displaystyle x_{1}^{(\ell+1)}=x_{1}^{(\ell)}-(0,e^{(\ell)})\;

\displaystyle,\;x_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell+1)}=x_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}+(0,e^{(\ell)})

\displaystyle y_{1}^{(\ell+1)}=y_{1}^{(\ell)}+(0,d^{(\ell)})\;

\displaystyle,\;y_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell+1)}=y_{i_{\ell}}^{(\ell)}-(0,d^{(\ell)})

Claim 1
.

C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})=C(x_{1}^{(\ell)},\ldots,x_{k+1}^{(\ell)})

3.2.
Trivialising in stages

The previous proposition shows that
C
is well-behaved under equal sums of sequences of equal length. In order to fully trivialise
C
to a Borel coboundary, we require more, both algebraically and combinatorially. Most of the arguments in this subsection are of algebraic type, and hence carry over from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
; in this subsection, we reproduce lemmas from
[
22
]
with additional detail, and follow their general proof structure.

Accordingly, we introduce some notation: given a sequence
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, denote it by
(\bar{x})_{n}
, and hence let
\sum(\bar{x})_{n}=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n}
. We also write
(\bar{x})_{n}\in p^{*}
if
x_{i}\in p^{*}
for all
i\leq n
, and
C((\bar{x})_{n})=C(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. When the length of
(\bar{x})_{n}
is clear, we also simply write
\bar{x}
in its place. Further, if
k<\omega
and
g\in G
, write
kg=g+\ldots+g
containing
k
-many terms.

The following two combinatorial lemmas are due to
[
22
]
. As results in this subsection, as can be seen from their proofs, hold for all
\mathbb{R}^{N}
; we state some without proof and refer to
[
22
]
for details.

Lemma 3.5

(
[
22
]
, Lemma 53)
.

Suppose
(\bar{x})_{n},(\bar{y})_{m},(\bar{x}^{\prime})_{n^{\prime}},(\bar{y}^{\prime})_{m^{\prime}}\in p^{*}
where
1\leq m<n
and
1\leq m^{\prime}<n^{\prime}
. Suppose
\sum(\bar{x})_{n}=\sum(\bar{y})_{m}=s
and
\sum(\bar{x}^{\prime})_{n^{\prime}}=\sum(\bar{y}^{\prime})_{m^{\prime}}=s^{\prime}
. Then

(n^{\prime}-m^{\prime})(C(\bar{x})-C(\bar{y}))=(n-m)(C(\bar{x}^{\prime})-C(\bar{y}^{\prime})).

Lemma 3.6
.

There exist
\tilde{x},\tilde{y}\in p^{*}
and
n<\omega
such that
(n+1)\tilde{x}=n\tilde{y}
.

Proof.

Fix any
x\in p^{*}
and consider the straight line
L
containing the origin and

. Since
p
is open, there exists
n<\omega
for which
\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)x\in p^{*}\cap L
, which yields the desired

.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

The pair of sequences
(\bar{x})_{(n+1)}
and
(\bar{y})_{n}
given by
x_{i}=\tilde{x}
and
y_{i}=\tilde{y}
for all

is the

, denoted by
(\lambda,\mu)
. The

is given by

\tilde{g}=C(\lambda)-C(\mu).

Lemma 3.8
.

For any

with

, we have

.

Proof.

.
∎

Following

]
, we define

given by

By induction, we have
C_{1}((\bar{x})_{(n+1)})=C((\bar{x})_{(n+1)})-n\tilde{g}

(

]

3.3.
Trivialising on a closed set

From the difference in complexity between our

and Lemma 52 in

We begin by identifying a well-behaved subset of

\tilde{z}
. Observe that
\tilde{z}\in T
. By construction, we may choose
\tilde{z}
to not be contained in
p
, which we assume from now on. Hence
T\cap p=\emptyset
.

Lemma 3.12
.

With
T
defined as above, the following hold:

(a)

T
is non-empty and closed (hence in particular Borel).

(b)

L^{\tilde{z}}\subset T
.

(c)

T
is closed under addition.

(d)

The interior of
T
has infinite diameter: for every
K<\omega
there exists
y\in T
such that the open ball
B_{K}(y)
of radius
K
and centre
y
is contained in
T
.

Proof.

These are all routine. It is clear from the construction that (a)
T
is non-empty and closed (it is the closure of the area bounded by
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
in the direction of
(\infty,\infty)
), and similarly (b) is immediate. The fact (c) that
T
is closed under addition follows easily from the construction of
T
and by using polar coordinates. Since
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
are not parallel, the interior of
T
must have infinite diameter, proving (d).
∎

Importantly, every real in
T
can be expressed as a sum of generics:

Lemma 3.13
.

If
z\in T
then there exists a sequence of generics
(\bar{x})_{n}
for which
z=\sum\bar{x}
.

Proof.

Let
z=2x
where
x\in p\setminus p^{*}
. We find
x_{1},x_{2}\in p^{*}
for which
z=x_{1}+x_{2}
. Let
r<p
contain
x
, and let
\epsilon
be
M
-generic and sufficiently small so that
x_{1}=x+\epsilon
and
x_{2}=x-\epsilon
are elements of
r^{*}
. Now
z=x_{1}+x_{2}
as required, and it follows by induction that every
z\in k\cdot p
(for any
k\geq 2
) can be written in the required form. Since
T\subset p^{+}
, the lemma is proven.
∎

We begin to trivialise cocycles to coboundaries. As in
[
22
]
,
Lemma

3.9
shows that the map
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow G
defined by

F(x)=\begin{cases}C_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})&\text{ if $x=\sum(\bar{x})_{n}\in T$}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

for
(\bar{x})_{n}\in p^{*}
is well-defined. Further, as
C_{1}
is Borel, the graph of
F
is clearly analytic; hence
F
is a Borel function and
C_{F}
is a Borel coboundary. Now define

C_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=C_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})+C_{F}(x_{1},x_{2}).

Lemma 3.14
.

If
(\bar{x})_{n}\in p^{*}
and
\sum(\bar{x})_{n}\in T
then
C_{2}(\bar{x})=0
.

Proof.

By induction. For
n=2
, it is easily seen that
C_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=F(x_{1})+F(x_{2})=0
by definition of
F
and since
T\cap p=\emptyset
. Thus,
Lemma

2.4
implies

\displaystyle C_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=C_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})+C_{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})

\displaystyle=C_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})-F(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{n+1}).\qed

Importantly, the invariance of
C_{2}
on generics in fact extends to all of
T
, which follows from the combinatorial properties of abelian cocycles.

Corollary 3.15

(
[
22
]
Lemma 55)
.

If
x,y\in T
then
C_{2}(x,y)=0
.

Proof.

Suppose
x=\sum(\bar{x})_{n}
and
y=\sum(\bar{y})_{m}
, all generics in
p
(this is possible by
Lemma

3.13
). By the algebraic properties of
C_{2}
and the previous lemma we have

\displaystyle C_{2}(\bar{x},\bar{y})-C_{2}(\bar{x})-C_{2}(\bar{y})

\displaystyle=C_{2}(x,y)

where all terms on the left-hand side equal
0
.
∎

We are now ready to define the aforementioned class of families
t(x)
.

Definition 3.16
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
t(x)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be defined by

t(x)=\{\,y\in T\,\mid\,x+y\in T\,\}.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
x,x^{\prime},w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

(a)

t(x)\neq\emptyset

(b)

t(x)\cap t(x^{\prime})\neq\emptyset

(c)

If
x,x^{\prime}\in T
then there exist
z,z^{\prime}\in t(x)\cap t(x^{\prime})\cap t(w)
for which we have
x+z=x^{\prime}+z^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For (a), let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be given. If
x\in T
then
x+x\in T
, thus
x\in t(x)
. Similarly, if
x\in L\setminus T
(with
L
defined as in
Lemma

3.10
), take a sufficiently large
y\in L^{\tilde{z}}\subset T
satisfying
x+y\in T
. Hence suppose
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(T\cup L)
. Recall that
T
has infinite diameter (
Lemma

3.12

(d)
). Hence let
z\in T
be such that
B_{|x|+1}(z)\subset T
. Then
x+z\in B_{|x|+1}(z)\subset T
. Part (b) follows easily; in fact, the result holds for all finite intersections by a similar argument.

For part (c), let
T_{x}=\{\,x+y\,\mid\,y\in T\,\}
be the shift of
T
to
x
. Since
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
(as defined in
Lemma

3.10
) are not parallel, it is easily seen that if
x,x^{\prime}\in T
then
T_{x}\cap T_{x^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, and in fact also has infinite diameter. As in (a) above, let
v\in T
be such that
B_{|w|+1}(v)\subset T_{x}\cap T_{x^{\prime}}
. Then any element in
B_{|w|+1}(v)
can be decomposed as desired.
∎

Since the set
T
is closed by construction, the following is immediate.

Corollary 3.18
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the set
t(x)
is Borel.

Define
J(x)=C_{2}(x,\tilde{x})
where
\tilde{x}\in t(x)
. By our construction, the choice of
\tilde{x}
is inconsequential, highlighting the
C_{2}
-invariance on
T
. To see this, first recall that

C_{2}(x,y)=C_{1}(x,y)-F(x+y)=C(x,y)-\tilde{g}-F(x+y)=C_{2}(y,x)

since
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
G
are abelian, and since
C
is an abelian cocycle.

Lemma 3.19
.

J
is well-defined and a Borel function.

Proof.

By
Lemma

2.2
,

J(x)=C_{2}(x,\tilde{x})=C_{2}(\tilde{x},x)=C_{2}(\tilde{x},z)+C_{2}(\tilde{x}+z,x)-C_{2}(\tilde{x}+x,z)

for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence if
z\in t(x)
then
Lemma

3.12

(c)
and
Corollary

3.15
imply
J(x)=C_{2}(\tilde{x}+z,x)
. Now suppose
\tilde{x},\tilde{x}^{\prime}\in t(x)
, and, using
Lemma

3.17

(c)
, let
z,z^{\prime}\in t(x)\cap t(\tilde{x})\cap t(\tilde{x}^{\prime})
be such that
\tilde{x}+z=\tilde{x}^{\prime}+z^{\prime}.
Then

C_{2}(\tilde{x},x)=C_{2}(\tilde{x}+z,x)=C_{2}(\tilde{x}^{\prime}+z^{\prime},x)=C_{2}(\tilde{x}^{\prime},x).

To see that
J
is Borel as a map, first note that
C_{2}
is Borel by construction. Hence it suffices to show that the set
\{\,t(x)\,\mid\,x\in X\,\}
has a Borel choice (or
uniformisation
) function. To that end, let
P\subset(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{2}
be the relation

(x,y)\in P\iff y\in t(x).

Clearly,
P
is a Borel subset of
(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{2}
and, fortunately, every section
P_{x}
has a canonical witness, which we identify as follows: fix
x
and travel along
L
starting at
\tilde{z}
towards the point at infinity
(\infty,\infty)
. Then the first
y\in L^{\tilde{z}}
for which a sufficiently large closed ball centred at
y
is contained in
T
does the trick (recalling that
T
is closed shows that this is well-defined).

To formalise this, consider
L^{\tilde{z}}
and define the function
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
by

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\min_{|y|}\left\{y\in L^{\tilde{z}}\,\middle|\,\overline{B}_{|x|+1}(y)\subset T\right\}

where
\overline{B}_{r}(x)
denotes the closed ball of radius
r
and centre
x
. It follows from the properties of
T
from
Lemma

3.12
that
\rho(x)
is well-defined and as desired. To see that
\rho
is Borel, recall that a function is Borel if and only if the pre-image of every basic open set is Borel
[
23
, 11.C]
. Fix a condition
q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
; we show
\rho^{-1}[q]
is Borel using
Proposition

2.1
. We may assume that
q\cap L^{\tilde{z}}\neq\emptyset
since otherwise
\rho^{-1}[q]=\emptyset
. By definition of
\rho
we know that
\tilde{z}\not\in\ran(\rho)
; hence
q\cap L^{\tilde{z}}
is homeomorphic to the open interval
(0,1)
. Let

b=(\tilde{z}\oplus\tilde{q})\oplus(\varphi\oplus\theta_{1}\oplus\theta_{2})

where
\tilde{q}
codes the limit points of
q\cap L^{\tilde{z}}
; we use this as an oracle to apply
Proposition

2.1
. Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, first compute
|x|+1
. From the limit points
c_{1},c_{2}\in L^{\tilde{z}}
of
q\cap L^{\tilde{z}}
(which are coded into
\tilde{q}
) compute the largest radii
r_{1},r_{2}
such that
\overline{B}_{r_{1}}(c_{1})\subset T
and
\overline{B}_{r_{2}}(c_{2})\subset T
, which requires finitely-many jumps from
b
(recall that
|c_{1}|<|c_{2}|
). By construction,
r_{1}<r_{2}
, and if
r_{1}<|x|+1<r_{2}
then
x\in\rho^{-1}[q]
, which proves the result by
Proposition

2.1
.
∎

To complete the proof of
Theorem

3.21
, it suffices to show that
C_{2}
is a Borel coboundary. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
let

\tilde{x}=\rho(x)\in t(x)

which we call the
canonical witness for
x
.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If
z\in t(x)\cap t(x+y)
then
z+x\in t(y)
.

Proof.

By definition,
z\in t(x)
implies
z+x\in T
. Since
z\in t(x+y)
we also know that
z+x+y\in T
. Hence
z+x\in t(y)
.
∎

Suppose
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and choose
z\in t(x)\cap t(y)\cap t(x+y)
. Then

\displaystyle C_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=C_{2}(x,z)+C_{2}(x+z,y)-C_{2}(x+y,z)

\displaystyle=C_{2}(x,\tilde{x})+C_{2}(x+z,y)-C_{2}(x+y,\widetilde{x+y})

\displaystyle=J(x)+C_{2}(x+z,y)-J(x+y).

Finally, notice that
z\in t(x)\cap t(x+y)
implies
x+z\in t(y)
by
Lemma

3.20
. Hence

C_{2}(x+z,y)=C_{2}(y,x+z)=C_{2}(y,\tilde{y})=J(y)

and so
C_{2}(x,y)=C_{J}(x,y)
. Now,

C_{J}(x,y)=C_{2}(x,y)=C_{1}(x,y)+C_{F}(x,y)=C(x,y)+C_{B}(x,y)+C_{F}(x,y)

and thus
C(x,y)=C_{J}(x,y)-C_{B}(x,y)-C_{F}(x,y)
, a sum of Borel coboundaries. This suffices by
Lemma

2.5
. Hence we conclude:

Theorem 3.21
.

For every group
G
we have
H^{2}_{{\textsf{Bor}}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{2},G\right)=0
.

4.
The Case
N>2

Let
N>2
and
G
be a group. Suppose
C\colon\left(\mathbb{R}^{N}\right)^{2}\rightarrow G
is an abelian Borel cocycle with Borel code
z
and let
M
be a countable transitive model of
\mathsf{ZFC}
containing
z
and
G
as elements. As in
section

2.2
, we assume there exist
N
-dimensional conditions
p=I_{1}\times\ldots\times I_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
q=J_{1}\times\ldots\times J_{N}
satisfying that each
I_{i}\subset(0,\infty)
(so
p
lies in the
N
-dimensional “first” quadrant, octant, etc.) and so that there exists
\tilde{g}\in G
for which
C(x)=\tilde{g}
for every
x\in(p\times q)^{*}
. Further, we may of course assume
D(p)<D(q)
(cf. after
Lemma

2.8
). We also stipulate that
|I_{i}|\neq|I_{j}|
for all
i\neq j
, which is needed in
Proposition

4.2
below.

We retrace the arguments from
section

3
. In order to prove the theorem along our arguments in the previous section, we need to prove higher dimensional versions of our topological lemmas. We begin with a high-dimensional version of
Proposition

3.3
:

Proposition 4.1
.

If
(\bar{x})_{n},(\bar{y})_{n}\in p^{*}
and
\sum\bar{x}=\sum\bar{y}
, then
C(\bar{x})=C(\bar{y})
.

Proof.

As before, the
special case
is combinatorial and hence applies: if there exists
\ell\leq n
for which
x_{\ell}=y_{\ell}
then the lemma follows immediately. If
n\in\{2,3\}
, then we are also done: if
n=2
, the combinatorial argument in
[
22
]
applies; if
n=3
, then the vectors
(y_{1}-x_{1}),(y_{2}-x_{2}),(y_{3}-x_{3})
bound a triangle
\Delta
. Since
p
is open, the same argument as in
Lemma

3.2
holds, considering
\Delta
in its induced plane inside
p
. Hence, assume
n>3
and that there exists no pair
(i,j)
for which
x_{i}=y_{j}
. Note that the arguments in
Lemma

3.4
and the recursion in the proof of
Proposition

3.3
do not depend on the number of dimensions in the ambient space; they can be carried out identically in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
for any
N>2
.
∎

Recall that the results in
section

3.2
are purely combinatorial, hence apply immediately in the case
N>2
. The remaining argument reduces to translating
section

3.3
to
N
. In fact, the only obstacle is to correctly define
T
; all subsequent arguments are combinatorial.

Recall the case
N=2
and denote the set
T
defined in
section

3
by
T(2)
, to highlight its topological dimension. We defined two auxiliary lines
L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2}^{\prime}
which induce
T(2)
: it is the space bounded by
L_{1}^{\prime},L_{2}^{\prime}
in the direction of the limit point
(\infty,\infty)
. Note that
T(2)
is homeomorphic to the closed upper half plane, which we denote by

\displaystyle U^{2}

\displaystyle=\{\,(x_{1},x_{2})\,\mid\,x_{2}\geq 0\,\}.

For each
N>2
, we define a set
T(N)
homeomorphic to
U^{N}
, the
N
-dimensional closed upper half plane given by

\displaystyle U^{N}

\displaystyle=\{\,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\,\mid\,x_{N}\geq 0\,\}.

With the condition
p\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
fixed as before, it is easily seen that there exists
M<\omega
for which
(M\cdot p)\cap((M+1)\cdot p)\neq\emptyset
(cf.
Lemma

3.10
). Indeed, consider
p
as an
N
-dimensional hypercube in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. By assumption, all
2^{N}
-many vertices of
p
are positive. The existence of
M<\omega
is derived exactly as in the two-dimensional case: the shortest side of
p
gives a sufficient bound for
M
just as in the proof of
Lemma

3.10
. Hence, fix such an
M
, and consider the induced set
p^{+}
.

From now on, we write
X\cong Y
to mean that
X
and
Y
are homeomorphic. The set of straight lines containing the origin
O
is given by
\mathcal{L}_{O}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

There exists a subspace
T(N)\subset p^{+}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, homeomorphic to
U^{N}
, which shares the properties of
T(2)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Before we give the proof, we require a technical lemma. Let
R_{M}
denote the
N
-hypercube given by the intersection
(M\cdot p)\cap((M+1)\cdot p)
, and define
R_{k}
for
k\geq M
similarly. We assume here that
R_{M}
is homeomorphic to
D^{N}
, the closed disc of
N
dimensions.
To define
T(N)
, consider auxiliary lines
L^{\prime}_{1},L^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{O}
which bound each
R_{k}
for
k\geq M
; we make this precise below. The induced subspace will serve as our space
T(N)
.

To define the relevant notions, we need the following lemma first. Denote the set of vertices of
R_{M}
by
V(R_{M})
, and define
V(k\cdot p)
similarly.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
k\geq M
. Then
V(R_{k})\cap V(k\cdot p)
and
V(R_{k})\cap V((k+1)\cdot p)
are distinct singletons.

Proof.

By shifting and shrinking, w.l.o.g., express the set
V(M\cdot p)
as the set of
\{0,2\}
-words of length
2^{N}
, and similarly,
V((M+1)\cdot p)
as the set of
\{1,3\}
-words of length
2^{N}
. Now, a vertex
v\in V(M\cdot p)
is an element of
(M+1)\cdot p
if and only if each of its coordinates lies in the interval
[1,3]
. Clearly, only the vertex
(2,2,\ldots,2)
satisfies this. By an identical argument, the only vertex
v\in V((M+1)\cdot p)
in
M\cdot p
is
(1,1,\ldots,1)
.
∎

For a subset
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, let its interior be denoted by
A^{\circ}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists a line
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{O}
such that, for every
k\geq M
we have
R^{\circ}_{k}\cap L^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be as provided by
Lemma

4.3
, where

v_{k}\in V(R_{k})\cap V((k+1)\cdot p).

By definition of
k\cdot p
, there exists
v^{-}\in V(p)
for which
v_{k}=(k+1)v^{-}
. Note that
v_{k}
is the vertex of
V((k+1)\cdot p)
closest to
O
. Since the set
\{\,v_{i}\,\mid\,i<\omega\,\}
lies on a straight line containing
O
, defining
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{O}
to be the unique line containing
v_{1}
suffices.
∎

As provided by the previous lemma, fix the straight line
L
that passes through every
R_{k}
. Let
B_{M}
be the
N
-dimensional open ball contained in
R^{\circ}_{M}
with centre on
L
and of maximal radius so that
\partial B_{M}\cap\partial R_{M}\neq\emptyset
. Define a sequence
(B_{k})_{k\geq M}
such that:

(1)

\partial B_{M}\cap\partial R_{M}\neq\emptyset

(2)

if
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{O}
and
L^{\prime}
is tangent to
B_{M}
then
L^{\prime}
is tangent to
B_{k}
for every
k\geq M
.

Since all sides of
R_{k}
grow by a constant as
k
increases, a straightforward geometrical argument shows that the latter condition can be satisfied. This also shows that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\diam(B_{k})=\infty.

The following corollary is now immediate.

Corollary 4.5
.

The sequence
(B_{k})_{k\geq M}
induces an
N
-dimensional hypercone
\mathcal{H}
. In particular,
\mathcal{H}
has infinite
N
-dimensional diameter.

Since all sides of
(k+1)\cdot p
are larger than their respective sides of
B_{k}
, we see that, once
\mathcal{H}
enters
p^{+}
, it does not escape: the radius of
B_{k}
is bounded above by the shortest side length of
R_{k}
, which is bounded above by the shortest side length of
k\cdot p
. By construction, all bounds are strict.

Proof of
Proposition

4.2
.

With
p\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
fixed, consider the hypercone
\mathcal{H}
(induced by the
N
-sphere
B_{M}
) as given by lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
. Isolate its
positive filled part
, defined by

\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\ldots,

\displaystyle x_{N})\in\mathcal{H}^{+}\iff

\displaystyle(\exists L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{O})(x\in L^{\prime}\land(\forall m\leq N)(x_{m}\geq 0)\land L^{\prime}\cap\partial B_{M}\neq\emptyset).

It is easily seen that

\partial\mathcal{H}^{+}=\bigcup\{\,L\in\mathcal{L}_{O}\,\mid\,|L\cap\partial B_{M}|=1\,\}

and hence
\partial\mathcal{H}^{+}\cong\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
. And since
\mathcal{H}^{+}
is clearly convex, it has trivial homology for every
k>0

[
7
, 10.8]
. By taking the stereographic projection we hence see that
\mathcal{H}\cong U^{N}
.
To define
T(N)
, fix some
\tilde{z}\in L\cap p^{+}
(as before, we may assume
\tilde{z}\not\in p
) and take the shift
\mathcal{H}^{+}+\tilde{z}
defined by

\mathcal{H}^{+}+\tilde{z}=\{\,x+\tilde{z}\,\mid\,x\in\mathcal{H}^{+}\,\}.

Since
\mathcal{H}^{+}+\tilde{z}\subset p^{+}
, we define
T(N)=\mathcal{H}^{+}+\tilde{z}
. The important properties of
T(2)
from lemma
3.12
carry over to
T(N)
since
\mathcal{H}^{+}
is closed, has infinite
N
-dimensional diameter and
T(N)\cong U^{N}
.
∎

To complete the proof, note that the family
\left\{t(x)\,\middle|\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\right\}
can be defined as in
Definition

3.16
, and shares the properties of
Lemma

3.17
. The map
J
(as defined on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
) is Borel by the same argument as in
Lemma

3.19
. Since all remaining lemmas in
section

3.3
are algebraic, we conclude:

Theorem 4.6
.

H^{2}_{{\textsf{Bor}}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{N},G\right)=0
for every
N<\omega
and every group
G
.

Proof.

The case
N=1
is
[
22
, Theorem 49]
;
N=2
is
Theorem

3.21
. The remaining cases were covered in
section

4
. Hence the theorem is proved.
∎

5.
Open Questions

In the present work we have focussed on the special cases of Borel definable group extensions of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by a
countable
group
G
. If
G
is uncountable Borel (recall
Definition

1.1
:
G
is a Borel subset of some standard Borel space, and the group operation is a Borel function), the forcing arguments in
section

3
do not apply as Cohen forcing has the c.c.c.
[
17
]
.

This obstruction is fundamental
[
22
, p. 265]
: based on an idea of Greg Hjorth, one can construct a
Borel subgroup
G\leq\mathbb{R}
and a Borel cocycle
C
such that
C
is a Borel coboundary in
H^{2}_{{\textsf{Bor}}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})

but not in
H^{2}_{{\textsf{Bor}}}(\mathbb{R},G)
