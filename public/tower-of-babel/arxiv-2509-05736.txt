Stabilizing RED using the Koopman Operator
Shraddha Chavan, Kunal N. Chaudhury

Abstract
The widely used RED (Regularization-by-Denoising) framework uses pretrained denoisers as implicit regularizers for model-based reconstruction. Although RED generally yields high-fidelity reconstructions, the use of black-box denoisers can sometimes lead to instability. In this letter, we propose a data-driven mechanism to stabilize RED using the Koopman operator, a classical tool for analyzing dynamical systems. Specifically, we use the operator to capture the local dynamics of RED in a low-dimensional feature space, and its spectral radius is used to detect instability and formulate an adaptive step-size rule that is model-agnostic, has modest overhead, and requires no retraining. We test this with several pretrained denoisers to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed Koopman stabilization.

Introduction

The goal in image reconstruction is to recover a clean image
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from measurements

\boldsymbol{b}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the forward operator and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is additive noise
[
1
]
. This formulation subsumes inverse problems such as deblurring, superresolution, MRI, and tomography. Image reconstruction is an extensively studied problem, with a rich literature encompassing variational approaches
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
, deep-learning models
[

,

[

In model-based reconstruction, one typically considers the optimization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\rVert^{2}

(2)

where
f
enforces data fidelity and the regularizer
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty]
promotes image properties
[
4
,
1
]
. Traditional regularizers like total variational and wavelet sparsity
[
4
]
often fail to capture rich structures in natural images. The key insight in
[
11
,
13
]
was to replace hand-crafted regularizers with powerful off-the-shelf denoisers within classical iterative algorithms. This paper focuses on Regularization-by-Denoising (RED)
[
13
]
, in which the iterations are given by

\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-\gamma\Big(\nabla\!f(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\lambda\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-D(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})\big)\Big),

(3)

where
\gamma
is the step size,
\lambda
is the regularization weight, and
D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a denoising operator. As shown in
[
14
]
, under suitable assumptions on
D
, the update in (
3
) can be interpreted as gradient descent on an objective of the form (
2
).

Recent studies have shown that substantial improvements over traditional regularizers can be achieved when
D
is a pretrained denoiser
[
17
,
12
]
. The key advantage of RED lies in the decoupling of the forward model and the denoiser in (
3
), which allows a single pretrained denoiser to be applied across a wide range of imaging problems without retraining
[
13
]
. This flexibility, however, comes at the cost of theoretical guarantees—deep denoisers can lead to instability and degraded reconstructions
[
14
,
18
,
19
]
. To address this issue, convergence-preserving denoisers have been proposed
[
12
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. A common approach is to interpret RED as a dynamical system,

\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1}=T_{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}),\quad T_{\gamma}=I-\gamma\big(\nabla f+\lambda(I-D)\big),

(4)

and to establish convergence using properties of the operator
T_{\gamma}
, such as contractivity and averagedness
[
23
,
17
,
19
]
. However, deep denoisers such as DnCNN and DRUNet do not satisfy the nonexpansiveness property
[
14
,
19
]
, which is necessary for establishing convergence guarantees. While structural constraints can enforce nonexpansiveness
[
23
,
20
,
24
,
25
]
, they can also degrade the regularization capacity.

2.2
Koopman Operator

The Koopman operator was introduced to bring Hilbert space and spectral analysis techniques to the study of dynamical systems
[
27
]
. Koopman’s seminal insight was to identify a nonlinear evolution operator
T
with a linear operator
\mathcal{K}
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of
observables

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
. Specifically, the operator is defined as

(\mathcal{K}\psi)(\boldsymbol{x})=\psi(T(\boldsymbol{x}))\qquad(\psi\in\mathcal{H}).

(5)

In other words,
\mathcal{K}
maps an observable
\psi
to
\mathcal{K}\psi
, whose value at
\boldsymbol{x}
equals
\psi
at the next state
T(\boldsymbol{x})
under the dynamics. The key observation is that although
T
may be highly nonlinear, its Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
is always linear. This connection makes it possible to leverage the spectral theory of operators on Hilbert spaces
[
31
]
.

The Koopman operator offers a principled framework for analyzing nonlinear dynamics, enabling insights into mixing, stability, and transients
[
32
,
28
,
29
,
30
]
. Its practical utility was advanced by dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
[
33
]
, which approximates the infinite-dimensional operator with a finite linear model learned from trajectory data. This forms the basis of our stabilization mechanism.

Classical RED algorithms—RED-SD, RED-ADMM, RED-FP
[
13
]
—and their extensions, RED-APG, RED-DPG, RED-PRO
[
14
,
17
]
, use a fixed, manually-tuned step size
\gamma
. For instance, RED-SD, which we refer to as
vanilla
RED in this paper, uses a fixed
\gamma
derived from technical assumptions on the denoiser. However, it was observed in
[
14
]
that deep denoisers tend to violate these conditions. As shown in Fig.
6
, reconstruction quality suffers when
\gamma
is too small or too large. Notably, a large
\gamma
can lead to instability after a few iterations.

We will show that data-driven step size schedules can significantly improve the stability of RED. Our approach, called SKOOP (
S
tabilization using the
Ko
opman
Op
erator), adjusts the step size dynamically based on the spectral radius of the Koopman operator. This is outlined in Algorithm
1
.

The step size correction in SKOOP-RED is performed at checkpoints
\Omega\subset\mathbb{N}
, balancing computational efficiency with effective monitoring. For each checkpoint
t\in\Omega
, we consider a history of past samples:

X_{t}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{x}_{t}:t-w+1\leqslant\tau\leqslant t\Big\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We project
X_{t}
into a low-dimensional space using a feature map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,d\ll n
, analogous to an observable in the Koopman formulation (
5
). We next learn a linear operator
\mathbf{K}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that for
t-w+1\leqslant\tau\leqslant t
,

\mathbf{K}_{t}\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau})=\psi(T(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau}))=\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau+1}).

(6)

Thus, similar to (
5
),
\mathbf{K}_{t}
progresses the dynamical system (in the feature space) by one time step. However, such an operator need not always exist. Therefore, following
[
33
,
29
]
, we estimate
\mathbf{K}_{t}
using linear regression:

\mathbf{K}_{t}=\underset{\mathbf{K}}{\arg\min}\sum_{\tau=t-w+1}^{t-1}\,\big\|\mathbf{K}\,\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau})-\psi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\tau+1})\big\|^{2}.

(7)

This is solvable using efficient numerical techniques
[
34
,
35
]
.

The spectral radius
\varrho(\mathbf{K}_{t})
is used as an indicator for local stability in the feature space:
\varrho(\mathbf{K}_{t})<1
implies decaying dynamics in the feature space, while
\varrho(\mathbf{K}_{t})\geqslant 1
indicates potential instability. In the latter case, we shrink the step size by a factor

\eta_{t}=\exp\left(-\beta(\varrho(\mathbf{K}_{t})-1)\right),

(8)

where
\beta>0
controls the rate of decay. We set
\beta=2
based on empirical validation, as this choice consistently provided a favorable balance between stability and efficiency across all tested scenarios. Larger values of
\beta
result in rapid decay in the step size, hindering progress, while smaller values are less effective at mitigating instability, particularly in more challenging cases. This completes the description of SKOOP-RED. We note that the operator
T_{\gamma}
in Algorithm
1
is the Vanilla RED update (
4
).

2.4
Implementation

We explored various feature maps
\boldsymbol{\psi}
to balance reliability and efficiency, and ultimately selected a
d=66
dimensional descriptor combining per-channel global statistics,
4{\times}4
spatial pooling, and four low-frequency DCT coefficients. Snapshots were collected over a window
w\in[30,40]
with checkpoints at
\Omega=\{w+tr:t\geqslant 1\}
, using a stride
r=10
. The above choices were validated through sensitivity studies and generalized well across architectures and forward models (see Section
A
).

We compute
\mathbf{K}_{t}
using the technique in
[
33
]
, and evaluate its spectral radius
\varrho(\mathbf{K}_{t})
using an efficient linear algebra routine. For the configuration
d=66,w=30
, and
r=10
, our implementation requires approximately
42
milliseconds per iteration to perform motion deblurring on a
256\times 256
color image using the DiffUNet denoiser. This is an overhead of
11\%
compared to Vanilla RED (
38.35
ms). Of this total runtime, denoising accounted for
6\%
, feature extraction for
4.4\%
, and the Koopman operator and spectral radius computation for less than
0.1\%
. The overall runtime overhead relative to Vanilla RED remains consistently below
20\%
.

4
Conclusion

We demonstrated that the Koopman operator provides a simple yet effective data-driven mechanism for stabilizing RED with trained deep denoisers—a setting where obtaining convergence guarantees under verifiable assumptions remains elusive. In principle, the framework extends beyond RED-GD to other variants and shows promise for broader classes of iterative reconstruction methods
[
11
,
23
,
15
]
. The Koopman viewpoint offers a fresh perspective on stabilization that should be of interest to the community.

Feature map design for SKOOP-RED:

The RED iterate
x_{t}
is turned into a 66-dimensional feature vector at each Koopman checkpoint. This gives a compact but informative state description, allowing stable and efficient monitoring even when using black-box deep denoisers.

•

Step-by-step construction:

(a)

For each color channel:

–

Compute the mean and standard deviation of all pixel values (2 features).

–

Divide the channel into a non-overlapping
4\times 4
grid, and compute the mean in each cell (16 features).

–

Apply the 2D discrete cosine transform (DCT) to the channel and extract the four lowest-frequency coefficients (top-left
2\times 2
block, 4 features).

(b)

Concatenate the 22 features from each channel to obtain
22\times 3=66
features for the image.

•

This design provides a compact summary for robust, data-driven instability detection in RED. Empirically, global statistics capture large-scale deviations, spatial pooling detects local anomalies, and DCT features signal emerging instability, forming an efficient basis for stability monitoring.

•

The modular feature map enables Koopman-based real-time stability control and can be readily adapted to new observables, imaging tasks, or more advanced dynamics (see codebase for details).

Practical guide: checkpointing and computational tradeoffs

•

Koopman updates use only the
w
most recent iterates, taken at regular intervals (
r
iterations). This checkpointing strategy reduces both computational and memory costs compared to continuous updates.

•

Sensitivity Analysis:
Small
r
(frequent checks) improves instability detection but increases overhead; large
r
reduces cost but can delay intervention. Larger
w
increases noise robustness with little runtime impact.

•

Recommended default:

w=30
and
r=10
provide an effective balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency (see Fig.
S2
), with performance robust across a range of values.

•

Parameters are user-tunable to suit different hardware or application needs. For example, using a higher
r
(less frequent updates) reduces runtime overhead, which is useful for embedded or real-time imaging systems. In contrast, a lower
r
(more frequent updates), especially when combined with a larger
w
(longer history), enables finer-grained instability detection in applications where reconstruction reliability is critical, such as medical imaging or scientific analysis.

•

In all tested scenarios, the added cost of stability monitoring (feature extraction and Koopman update at checkpoints) is negligible compared to the dominant cost of denoising and forward model computation.
