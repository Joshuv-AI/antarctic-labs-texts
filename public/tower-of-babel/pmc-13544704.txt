Late femoral button migration following autograft all-epiphyseal anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction in a skeletally immature child: a case report
Authors: Kevin Z. Kwan, Garrett Flynn, John A. Schlechter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Late femoral button migration following an all-epiphyseal ACL reconstruction utilizing a semitendinosus autograft is uncommon.Materials and methods:We report a case of a 17-year-old male patient with bilateral anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstructions using semitendinosus autografts who presented with late migration of the femoral button in the right knee nearly 5 years after the index procedure.Results:A physeal-sparing, all-epiphyseal, all-inside technique with semitendinosus autograft and adjustable-loop suspensory fixation was utilized for the initial procedure. Subsequent radiographs and magnetic resonance imaging were obtained, demonstrating the late migration of the femoral button into the joint, as well as confirming the incorporation and integrity of the ACL graft. The femoral button was removed arthroscopically under general anesthesia. After removal of the femoral button from the right knee, the patient returned to pain-free activity and recovered full range of motion.Conclusions:This case report serves to highlight late failure of a pediatric all-epiphyseal ACLR technique with an intact graft. Surgeons utilizing all-epiphyseal techniques with adjustable-loop suspensory fixation in skeletally immature ACLR cases should be aware of this potential late complication.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are common within the pediatric population and continue to rise[1,2]. With the increasing number of ACL injuries within the skeletally immature population, there has been a commensurate development of physeal-sparing and physeal-respecting techniques to mitigate complications associated with iatrogenic physeal arrests, leading to potential limb-length discrepancy and angular deformity[3–5].

The decision regarding the ideal technique for this susceptible population is often influenced by bone age, height, Tanner staging, and a surgeon’s training and experience. Four primary approaches have emerged: (1) over-the-top extra-articular iliotibial band reconstruction, (2) transphyseal reconstruction, (3) all-epiphyseal reconstruction, and (4) hybrid techniques using one of the aforementioned techniques for the femur and a different one for the tibia[6].

Prior literature has demonstrated improved arthrometric stability and fewer graft ruptures associated with suspensory fixation[7]. Although misplaced femoral-sided fixation is the most common complication associated with suspensory fixation, the literature has demonstrated failure of femoral-sided fixation secondary to infection or graft rupture[8–10]. Additionally, the lone documented case of button migration that used an all-epiphyseal technique was observed in the immediate postoperative period, and failure occurred at the tibial fixation[11].

We present a case of late femoral button migration with an intact graft after a pediatric all-epiphyseal ACLR with adjustable-loop suspensory fixation.

Case presentation

As a single-patient case report, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval. Written informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient. This case report was reported in accordance with the SCARE checklist[12].

Medical history

We report the case of a 17-year-old male with bilateral ACLRs with semitendinosus autografts who presented with new complaints of persistent pain and swelling of the right knee after playing soccer without a discrete traumatic injury, 5 years after his initial ACLR, which had been performed at age 12. The right knee ACLR was performed using an all-epiphyseal technique with an 8.5-mm semitendinosus autograft. Femoral fixation was achieved with an ACL TightRope RT adjustable-loop suspensory fixation construct, and tibial fixation was achieved with a TightRope attachable button (Arthrex, Naples, FL). Initial radiographs performed within 1 month before the index ACLR did not reveal any mechanical axis malalignment, with a bone age of approximately 12.17 years according to the Greulich and Pyle method (Figure 1)[13]. Preoperative examination demonstrated a Lachman grade 2B and pivot shift grade 1+. Two years later, the left ACLR was completed with a hybrid physeal-respecting technique. Intraoperatively, fluoroscopy demonstrated appropriate positioning of the implants, with the button appropriately seated on the lateral femoral cortex. He otherwise had an uneventful recovery. At final follow-up, range of motion had returned to baseline, strength and functional testing were symmetric, and the patient returned to competitive sports without recurrent instability or need for revision surgery. Physical examination

On examination, the surgical incisions over the right knee had healed well. There were no signs of erythema or drainage, and there was a mild effusion. He demonstrated −10 degrees of hyperextension to 125 degrees of flexion, limited by pain at terminal flexion. There was no varus or valgus laxity and no neurovascular deficits.

Imaging

Radiographs obtained nearly 4 years following the index ACLR of the right knee (1 year prior to the current presentation) demonstrated appropriately positioned adjustable-loop suspensory fixation at the femur and tibia. Subsequent radiographs obtained following symptom onset demonstrated migration of the femoral button, with the tibial button found to be intact. Additional magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated that the autograft was intact and that the femoral button had migrated within the right knee joint. Intervention

It was recommended to undergo arthroscopic removal of the button because arthroscopy allowed for minimally invasive retrieval of the button that appeared to be localized within the joint. Diagnostic arthroscopy of the right knee demonstrated no additional chondral, meniscal, or ligamentous pathology. There was no gross cartilage wear, synovitis, or meniscal damage attributed to the migrated button. Additionally, the semitendinosus autograft appeared to be intact and stable to probing. The femoral button was visualized within the submeniscal recess under the medial meniscus. A monofilament polydioxanone suture was used to apply traction to the meniscus and retract it anteriorly. This allowed for the use of a probe to free the metal button, which then floated into the posteromedial aspect of the joint. A modified Gillquist maneuver was used to visualize the button. A posteromedial arthroscopic portal was then created under direct visualization, which allowed for removal of the button with a hemostat (Figure 3). Fluoroscopic images were obtained, confirming the removal of the femoral button. No retained suture material was seen attached to the migrated femoral button or within the joint, and no gross deformation of the button was appreciated. No postoperative immobilization was used, and the patient was discharged home with weight bearing as tolerated. The patient returned for follow-up 10 days postoperatively. The surgical incisions were clean, dry, and intact, without signs of infection. He denied pain and demonstrated full range of motion. At 59 days postoperatively, the patient returned without any interval changes from his initial postoperative visit. He reported actively participating in physical therapy and had returned to school. Discussion

Implant migration into the knee following ACLR is a rare but known complication associated with fixation devices[8,10,11]. There is a paucity of literature on late femoral button migration following a pediatric all-epiphyseal ACLR with adjustable-loop suspensory fixation. There have been reports of suspensory fixation failure; however, three of the cases involved first-generation cortical buttons (Endobutton, Acufex Microsurgical, Mansfield, MA), one of which was performed with two Endobuttons in a double-bundle semitendinosus reconstruction[9,14,15]. In the case involving the double-bundle reconstruction, the proposed mechanism of failure was attributed to repetitive impingement and shearing across the suture loop over the anterior button[14]. In the other two cases, the failures of the Endobuttons were attributed to loosening of the knot tied over the button[15] and anterior malpositioning of the femoral button[9]. Malpositioning of the graft was hypothesized to have resulted in a more vertically oriented tunnel, leading to increased stress on the graft. Additionally, persistent bathing of the site in synovial fluid could have led to an osteoclastic response, leading to tissue damage and delayed incorporation. It is unlikely that these were contributing factors in this case, given the late migration of the button, the appropriate position of the femoral tunnel, and maintained graft integrity.

Williams et al. reported a case involving adjustable-loop suspensory fixation with a tibialis anterior allograft and subsequent migration of the femoral button 2 years following the index ACLR. In their case, the proposed mechanism of failure was attributed to micromotion between the button and the lateral cortex of the femur[10]. The case currently reported did not involve any infectious process or underlying inflammatory process that could be observed clinically.

Additionally, in the lone report regarding button migration involving an all-epiphyseal ACLR, failure was noted in the immediate postoperative period, and surprisingly, failure occurred on the tibial side[11]. In this case, Koukoulias et al hypothesized that the tibial button migrated into the joint due to the pliable cortical bone and periosteum, and that the button was able to flip and plastically deform the cortical bone. They eventually revised the reconstruction by retrieving the quadriceps tendon autograft and converting their epiphyseal tibial tunnel into a transphyseal tibial tunnel.

Notable differences from previously reported cases of button migration involving an all-epiphyseal technique include the use of a quadriceps tendon autograft and the acuity of implant failure. In this case, the graft likely remained stable because biologic incorporation had already occurred within the femoral and tibial tunnels prior to late button migration, and the femoral button was found to have migrated nearly 5 years postoperatively. Moreover, intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed appropriate femoral button seating, which was corroborated on subsequent follow-up radiographs. The proposed mode of failure in this case may have been multifactorial. The button had migrated in the presence of an intact graft, without evidence of gross cartilage or meniscal damage, retained suture material, or button deformation. We hypothesize that the thickened periosteum associated with skeletal immaturity and the overriding iliotibial band in an athletic individual may have led to attritional wear and late failure. This process may have been further promoted by micromotion or microfriction, cortical or tunnel remodeling, repetitive iliotibial band friction, occult soft-tissue interposition, or subtle malposition not apparent on radiographs.

Conclusion

This case report highlights late failure of a pediatric all-epiphyseal ACLR technique with an intact graft. Surgeons using all-epiphyseal techniques with adjustable-loop suspensory fixation in skeletally immature ACLR cases should be aware of this potential late complication.
