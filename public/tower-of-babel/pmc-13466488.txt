Construction and validation of interpersonal violence in terms of the hot weather scale for university students
Authors: Saba Ghayas, Simone Varrasi, Giuseppe Alessio Platania, Claudia Savia Guerrera, Francesco Maria Boccaccio, Vittoria Torre, Concetta Pirrone, Emanuele Coco, Sabrina Castellano, Francesca Ferraioli, Simona Massimino, Amama Mahar, Rashida Akmal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development and validation of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather scale is the goal of the current study. The initial pool of items was developed by reviewing the literature and taking into account the results of semi-structured interviews (inductive & deductive approach). After expert opinion and ensuring content validity, 25 Items were finalized for further analysis. To run an exploratory factor analysis, data were collected from 1,000 university students. EFA revealed a unidimensional factor structure with 16 retained items, explaining 80.2% of the variance. The scale’s 0.98 Cronbach’s alpha demonstrates a good level of internal consistency. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed a unidimensional factor structure with 16 items retained on an independent sample of 350 university students. Convergent and divergent validity of the scale was assessed through an independent sample (n = 440). The results showed that there was a strong positive relationship of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather, with aggression, and a non-significant relationship with eating behavior. The scale can be regarded as a psychometrically reliable tool to assess interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04929-0.Keywords: Interpersonal violence, Hot weather, Scale development, Exploratory factor analysis, Confirmatory factor analysis, Psychometric properties

Introduction

The Earth’s atmosphere has experienced multiple changes since the planet’s birth 4.5 billion years ago. With increased population and technological advancements, the environment is under more strain [1]. Sustained overuse of resources from nature has resulted in unparalleled levels of environmental destruction, including the loss of ecosystems and species [2].

The growing usage of fossil fuels and greenhouse gases emitted by land use are causing Earth’s temperature to rise more quickly than it has ever done. It is found that human emissions of radiating gases have already warmed the Earth by over 2 °F since the start of the industrial era [3]. Earth’s ability to provide sustenance, pure water, and support human health and well-being is being altered due to the changing atmosphere [4]. Atmospheric change, which has been called “the greatest worldwide health catastrophe of the 21st Century,” puts the lives and well-being of billions of people at greater risk. It has been widely established that elevated temperatures increase mortality on a global scale [5]. According to reports, the expected effects of climate change pose an unacceptable high and perhaps catastrophic risk to human health [6]. The threat to human health increases due to climate change, which also causes weather-related events like heat waves, hurricanes, tornadoes, and wildfires to become more severe [7]. This raises the possibility that current health hazards may intensify and new public health issues could materialize on a global scale [8]. Although a change in atmosphere is a global phenomenon, its primary and secondary health effects are local and have an immense effect on the physical, social, and emotional health of communities [9].

Temperature and humidity increases can exacerbate asthma, allergens, and other respiratory conditions. Poor air quality can cause asthma episodes and other respiratory and cardiovascular health problems, which are made worse by rising temperatures and shifting weather trends Weather change is linked to a variety of psychiatric diseases, including anxiety, schizophrenia, mood disorders, and sadness, as well as suicide and violent conduct [10].

Understanding the psychological effects of global atmosphere variability requires comprehending the intricate and multiple meanings attached to the acute or traumatic effects of extreme weather events and a changed environment, threats to emotional well-being based on observation of impacts and worry or uncertainty about future risks, chronic social and community effects of heat, drought, migrations, and climate-related conflicts, and post-disaster adjustment [11]. It may not be incorrect to state that after a few years, behavior may change at a higher level because, as the environment changes over time, so does behavior [12].

One major outcome of climate change is an increase in temperature. Summertime temperatures above long-term norms and specific heat anomalies (like heatwaves) are linked to increased mortality and crime rate [13].

Two major hypotheses can shed light on the correlation of crime and violence rate with increase in temperature. Interpersonal violence trigger due to change in weather because it makes people more irritable, impulsive, and uncomfortable. His theory cannot account for rises in violent offenses in places where the temperature has gone from frigid to mild [14, 15]. The temperature-aggression theory holds that warm weather encourages violent motivation and hostile behavior, while increases in homicides throughout the summer may be explained by changes to daily routines. The heat theory, which specifically refers to the hot aspect of it, is the version of this concept that has been the subject of the most study [16]. The relationship between environmental stressors and violent behavior can be understood through established frameworks such as the General Aggression Model [17], which posits that situational inputs, including heat, influence internal states (e.g., affect, cognition, and arousal), thereby increasing the likelihood of aggressive behavior. Similarly, Routine Activity Theory [18] suggests that environmental conditions such as temperature can alter daily routines and social interactions, increasing opportunities for interpersonal conflict and violence. Empirical research supports these frameworks, demonstrating that higher temperatures are associated with increased rates of violent crime, including assault and homicide [19, 20]. These findings highlight the importance of examining interpersonal violence within the context of increased temperature.

Violence is defined as “the deliberate application of force or authority, whether actual or potential, against oneself, another person, or against a group or community that either results in or have a high likelihood of resulting in injury, death, psychological harm, mal-development, or deprivation” [21, 22]. This explanation covers all forms of violence, including interpersonal, self-directed, and group-based aggression. Hitting, striking, biting, shoving, teasing, bullying, and screaming at another person are all examples of violent behavior. Interpersonal violence has long-lasting effects that affect families, towns, and entire nations. In a huge area of the world, it is the top cause of mortality for teens and young adults. Interpersonal aggressiveness increases a person’s risk of experiencing a variety of mental, behavioral, and physical health issues throughout their lifetime. Interpersonal violence, self-directed violence, including suicide, and collective violence, including war, terrorism, and state-perpetrated violence in the form of genocide or maltreatment, are the three main types of violence experienced on a global scale [23].

According to the WHO’s global health estimates, the three main types of violence killed close to 1.4 million people in 2011. 504,587 people died as a result of interpersonal violence, or 35.3% of all fatalities. Intimate partners frequently abuse one another emotionally and sexually. According to a nationwide survey between 2012 and 2015 in Japan, the number of ambulance transports for assault increased linearly as daily temperatures increased [24].

According to GBD, low- and middle-income (LMIC) countries account for 83% of all violent fatalities and 91.4% of deaths from interpersonal violence [24]. The psychological effects associated with climate change and extreme heat are huge, and they can pose a greater risk of mental health disorders and emotional distress [25]. Shows that climate change is a form of psychosocial stressor that intensifies emotional intimate partner violence and the mechanisms of action involve such things as isolation of the house, male jealousy, and controlling behaviors. According to [26], older adults, children, and people with severe mental illness are overrepresented by extreme heat, which intensifies psychiatric symptoms and psychological distress and makes them susceptible to extreme heat events [27]. Demonstrate that as the ambient temperature increases, mental health-related mortality and morbidity increase, and older adults and those who live in tropical locations are the most susceptible segments of the population. According to [28], mood disorders are more likely to be hospitalized and experience an emergency visit under hot weather, particularly in adolescents, women, and older adults, and their symptoms are exacerbated by humidity and extreme cold [29]. Reported that hot weather increases depression, PTSD, sleep disturbances, suicidal ideation, aggression, and low academic performance among adolescents. As [30] point out, hot weather raises stress and anxiety, and cold weather elevates life satisfaction, which proves that temperature extremity has a direct impact on mental health [31]. Developed the idea of climate change as a threat multiplier to the social, economic, and political vulnerabilities and amplifies PTSD, depression, anxiety, suicidality, and substance use.

The risk of interpersonal is also increased in the case of extreme temperatures [32]. Indicates that in the world, rising temperatures and emission of greenhouse gases are associated with more suicides and homicides, and structural vulnerabilities like unemployment and urbanization contribute to an increase in risk. According to [33] self-harm and interpersonal violence are more common in the setting of high temperatures, especially among young men [34]. Prove that 10 degC rise in short-term mean temperature increases the risk of violent crime by 9%, and the largest impact can be seen in the crimes driven by disorganized emotional control and impulse. Another aspect identified by [25] is that cohabiting and separated women are especially subject to emotional IPV in extremely hot weather, and high social support alleviates such effects. Together, the findings depict that climate change and temperature extremes are not only threatening to mental health but also intensifying violence and require specific adaptation, mental health-supporting, and violence-preventing methods.

It is reported that there is an increase in rapes, assaults, and interpersonal violence as the temperature rises [19]. The study found that violent offences tend to happen more frequently during the summer or other warm seasons. The hot season can extend up to six or seven months in Pakistan. The normal maximum and minimum daily temperatures in July are 33 and 23 degrees Celsius, respectively. Many epidemiological studies have found a connection between ambient temperature and outcomes in terms of sickness or mortality [35].

Another study collected time-series daily data on mortality, meteorological variables, and air pollution indicators from 384 locations in 13 countries in order to account for the complicated and varied link between temperature and mortality. This study employed statistical modelling to do so. The findings of the study demonstrated that temperature significantly affects mortality, accounting for 7–71% of mortality in the selected nations [36].

The aggression rates in hotter and cooler settings were compared in a field investigation, which provides additional support for the heat hypothesis. In the United States, for example, there are roughly 2.6% more murders and assaults during the summer months than during other times of the year; hot summers are more violent than cool summers; and violence rates are higher in hotter years than in cooler years even when several statistical controls are taken into account [37]. A link between temperature and aggression among the general population is being suggested by a growing body of evidence. Interpersonal violence may consequently rise as the temperature rises if appropriate mitigating measures are not taken. In order to study the, additional research is required in numerous communities and nations.

The literature supported the impact of hot weather on violent offenses. There are several scales for measuring violence, such as those for measuring physical violence and domestic violence, but none of them assesses interpersonal violence associated with high temperatures. Keeping in view the climate change and its drastic effects, it is important to develop a tool to measure the tendency of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather among university students. Interpersonal violence is usually confused with aggression, and heat discomfort. All these variables share some similarities, but there are differences among these constructs. Interpersonal violence refers to the intentional use of physical force or power against another person that results in or has a high likelihood of resulting in injury, harm, or deprivation [38]. It is important to distinguish interpersonal violence from aggression, which is a broader psychological construct encompassing hostile thoughts, emotions, and behavioral tendencies that may or may not result in actual violence [17]. In contrast, exposure to high ambient temperatures or heat discomfort represents an environmental stressor that can influence affective and physiological states, such as irritability and arousal, thereby increasing the likelihood of aggressive responses [39].

Interpersonal violence is a major public health concern, especially for teenagers and young adults. It is reported that there is a significant impact on the health and well-being of individuals, families, and communities. Studies on university students reflect the high chance of violence because embarking on a new environment with the least parental control and shifting towards new lifestyles increase the risk of violence among university students [40]. Violence entails high social and economic costs for victims, their families, and societies, including intergenerational cycles of violence, lower academic performance, and reduced productivity, and consequently a decrease in well-being [41, 42]. Previously conducted studies supported that interpersonal violence decreases the indices of mental health and well-being of university students [43, 44].

Materials and methods

In order to meet the objectives of current research, three studies were conducted.

In Study I, an item pool will be generated, and exploratory factor analysis will be carried out to provide factorial validity of the scale through SPSS. In Study II, Confirmatory factor analysis will be carried out to confirm the obtained factor structure through AMOS. In Study III, correlation analysis will be carried out to test the hypothesis related to the convergent and divergent validity of the study.

Study I: development of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather

Three phases make up Study I.

Phase I: generation of initial item pool

To produce the original pool of items, various techniques were applied. The literature review was taken into consideration for the Interpersonal Violence in Terms of Hot Weather. Interpersonal violence is a topic that has given rise to a vast range of concepts and ideas, which have all been examined in the pertinent literature. The researchers also conducted semi-structured interviews to deepen the understanding of interpersonal violence and get subjective perspectives. The following steps were used to generate the item pool:

Literature-based: Several interpersonal violence domains were investigated to uncover components that might be used in a single psychometric assessment of interpersonal violence and might provide a numerical score for interpersonal violence as a whole.

In-depth, unstructured interviews were done with 15 university students to conceptualize interpersonal violence and develop items for the measure. Students admitted on disability and sports quota were not recruited for interviews. There were seven girls and 8 boys in the sample. The interview tool is approximately 15 to 25 min. Furthermore, ten professors from various departments were also interviewed. The reason behind the interviews from professors was their experience or their knowledge about the violence of students due to the hot weather. It was made clear to interviewees that any information they shared would be kept confidential. The interview protocol was particularly developed for the current study. The sample of 15 students and 10 faculty members is consistent with previous research. Interviews were conducted until saturation was reached, evidenced by repetition of themes and the absence of new information in the final stages of data collection. Therefore, the sample size was deemed sufficient to achieve the objectives of the study. At the end of all interviews, the transcripts were transcribed, and particular themes were extracted related to the interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather.

Phase II: psychometric cleaning of items

The first item pool, derived from the literature review and the interviews, contained 37 items. After the initial item pool was created, experts (n = 10) examined each question and provided their evaluations of its suitability for the Interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather. These expert evaluations ensured the products’ suitability and relevance for the target construct. The scale’s objectives were explained to the experts, and they provided recommendations for scale item additions or removals based on their own assessments of the items’ cultural and contextual suitability. Twenty-five items were finalized in response to the experts’ recommendations since they were the most appropriate for the literature, context, and target population.

A Likert-type system with a 1–5 scale was used to assess the final scale of twenty-five items, where 1 represents strongly disagree, and 5 represents strongly agree. The five-point response structure was employed to strike a compromise between the two extremes and provide respondents more freedom to choose the choice that most accurately expressed their thoughts [45].

Phase III: exploratory factor analysis and psychometric properties

One thousand students (500 male and 500 female) from various departments made up the sample for the study, which was chosen using a convenient sampling technique. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 32 (M = 22.6, SD = 4.7). Data was collected with the permission of heads of departments, and students were contacted in their classrooms and labs. The university record was verified to exclude students with any record of misconduct or disciplinary rule violations. Moreover, students registered on the basis of sports quota were also not included in the sample.

Results

In order to obtain the factor structure and provide evidence of construct validity, Exploratory factor analysis is considered the best choice for analysis. After testing all assumptions of EFA, the analysis was carried out. The Kaiser-Meier-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were employed to evaluate the sample’s suitability [42]. Claimed that the KMO value of 0.97 showed perfect sample sufficiency and adequacy. The results of Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were significant, which revealed that the correlation matrix is not the identity matrix, and it demonstrated that the items are significantly related and that the sample is appropriate for further examination. All of the commonalities were very high, suggesting that factor analysis might continue, and each variable was picked for additional research.

The principal axis factoring with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 led to a one-factor solution from the EFA. Only one factor was discernible and accounted for a significant percentage of the variance (80.2%). The final one-factor structure was well defined, comprehensible, and pertinent to the construct. The 16 items that came after them all had one component exclusively loaded. Items with a low level of loadings were excluded. The scale’s Cronbach’s alpha calculation yielded a result of 0.98, which demonstrated excellent internal consistency and very high reliability.

Study II

In Study II, Confirmatory factor analysis was carried out to confirm the factor structure. According to Gregory (2020), it is recommended that the CFA should be carried out on a sample other than the sample of EFA. Therefore, another sample was selected, and CFA was carried out through AMOA [46].

Sample

A sample of 443 university students (211 males and 232 females) was recruited through convenience sampling. After careful analysis, a sample of 350 participants (179 females, 171 males) was found to be suitable for further data analysis.

Measurement model of the interpersonal violence scale in terms of hot weather

The model developed by EFA was examined using CFA based on the results of the original criterion (item loading > 0.50). Initially, the model fit appeared to have poor fit indices, with RMR and RAMSEA above 0.05. As per the modification indices, covariances were drawn, and the model was revealed to be excellent with RMR and RAMSEA below 0.05. The factor structure obtained demonstrated a good fit with the data. A good model fit is indicated by the goodness of fit (GFI) value, comparative fit index (CFI) values being relatively close to one, and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) being considerably close to zero, respectively. The 16 items that made up the final model, derived using CFA, had a satisfactory model fit (See Table 2). Study III: convergent and discriminant validation of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather

The objective of Study III was to confirm the validity of the scale of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather. To provide evidence of convergent validity, aggression was selected as the most suitable construct because of its overlap with interpersonal violence. Eating Attitude Scale was selected to provide the discriminant validity of the scale because of having discriminant nature of this construct with relevance to interpersonal violence.H1: Convergent validity would be demonstrated by a positive relationship between aggression and interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather.

H1: Discriminant validity would be demonstrated by non-significant relationship of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather with eating behavior.

Sample

Another sample comprised of four hundred university students was collected for the purpose of providing convergent and discriminant validity evidence of study.

Instrument

Interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather scale

In terms of hot weather, the Interpersonal Violence Scale is a 16-item self-report questionnaire designed to quantify the amount of violence experienced by individuals over the summer. The response scale has a 5-point Likert-type structure (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree). Interpersonal violence is measured on a scale, with a high score signifying a high level and a low score signifying a low level.

Eating Attitude Test (EAT)

Garner and Garfinkel created the EAT-26 eating attitude exam in [47]. A total of 26 items are condensed to measure eating attitude. It uses a 6-point Likert scale, with 1 denoting “always” and 6 denoting “never”. It comprises three subscales: the dieting scale (range: 2, 5, 8, 13, 15, 19, and 20), the bulimia and food fixation scale (range: 3, 4, 9, 18, and 25), and the dieting scale (range: 1, 6, 7, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 22, 23, and 26). A score of less than 20 is used to indicate a disturbed eating attitude. For the original EAT-20, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.89.

Aggression scale

This scale rates behaviors that can cause physical or psychological harm to other students. It comprises eleven items and receives a score between 0 and 7 (1 = 0 times to 7 = six). High scores on this scale denote high aggressiveness levels, whereas low numbers imply low aggression levels. With a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.98, this scale has exceptionally high internal consistency and no reverse-scored [48].

Discussion

Although the physiological effects of climate change are becoming more widely acknowledged, comparatively less focus has been on how rising temperatures might influence potential behavioral changes that have a detrimental effect on society and health, such as violence. Therefore, current research was aimed at the development of a scale to measure interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather for university students.

Inductive and deductive approaches were used to generate an item pool for the purpose of the current study. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with the university students to get their behavior indicators, emotions, and cognitions related to the violence that they experience due to the hot weather. After testing all assumptions of Exploratory factor analysis, analysis was carried out, and results revealed a unidimensional factor structure with excellent cumulative variance. Reliability indices also supported the excellent psychometric properties of the scale. The Interpersonal Violence Scale and the general violence scale are largely unidimensional, as they are designed to capture a single underlying construct rather than multiple distinct factors. In this study, interpersonal violence was also understood in a context-specific manner, meaning that its expression may vary across situational conditions (e.g., environmental or contextual influences such as “hot weather” or similar stress-inducing contexts). Therefore, measuring these variables within a context-specific framework enhances their interpretability and relevance, while maintaining the conceptual consistency of their unidimensional structure.

In order to provide evidence of validity, it is suggested to run a Confirmatory analysis on another sample. Keeping in view the standard guidelines of psychometry, another independent sample was collected. Results demonstrated that the factor structure of the scale is replicable across different samples. The final scale consisted of 16 items with excellent reliability indices.

In order to provide validity evidence, convergent and discriminant validity methods were used. Findings of the study revealed that there was a significant positive relationship between interpersonal violence and aggression. These results are in line with the findings of a previous study [49] and provide evidence of the convergent validity of the scale. According to [50], violence and aggression both share neurological differences and similarities as well. It was also found that violent crimes, property crime, and physical assault and aggression were all positively correlated with the yearly average temperature, using the average regional temperature in the United States from 1950 to 1999 as a predictor. The strong association with aggression is theoretically expected, as interpersonal violence represents a behavioral expression closely aligned with aggressive tendencies, particularly under environmental stress such as heat. The high correlation may therefore reflect shared variance rather than measurement redundancy alone. Nevertheless, the current evidence for discriminant validity is limited, and future studies should include additional theoretically adjacent constructs such as hostility, anger, and emotional regulation to more rigorously test construct distinctiveness.

Findings of the current study revealed non-significant relationship of interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather with eating attitude. These findings support the discriminant validity of the scale. These results can be justified on logical and empirical grounds as eating attitude is not linked with interpersonal violence and these variables are reported to be irrelevant, and so are the findings.

Findings of current study provided factorial validity, structural stability and convergent and discriminant validity of scale.

Limitations and suggestions

There are certain limitations and suggestions of the current research. Firstly, sample is taken from Punjab, Pakistan. The temperature of different regions of Pakistan varies in nature. The temperature of Punjab is hotter than compared of KPK, Gilgit-Baltistan, and even some areas of Baluchistan as well. It is suggested that this scale must be validated on a more diverse sample taken from various provinces of Pakistan. Secondly only a few variables are taken as evidence of validity in this study; it is suggested to test a more diverse nomological network of this construct. The sample of the current study consisted of only university students, and this is a limitation of the current study. Therefore, it is suggested that further studies must be conducted to validate the scale on young adults other than university students.

No validation is conducted against records of violent campus incidents. In correlational research, this is a limitation because the study focuses on identifying relationships between variables in naturally occurring data rather than verifying individual events through official records. The investigation into the links between temperature and violence is still in its infancy. There may be more unrecorded elements that influence different violent behaviors and change temperature connections. A significant association between temperature and homicide, for instance, has been found in US cities with high population density, young populations, a low number of men and White people, and large wealth disparity. A study is needed to look at potential moderating factors such as age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, cultural perspectives, legal system, and gun control laws. This study solely relies upon self-report measures, and it can cause a social desirability element; therefore, it is suggested that further research should be conducted to validate the newly constructed scales with the help of measures other than self-report measures.

Practical implications

The development of a scale to measure interpersonal violence in terms of hot weather will help university administrations. University management can apply this tool to assess the level of interpersonal violence and then they can im protective and proactive measures. For instance, universities located in areas which are having more intense weather should offer increased campus security, conflict management training programs, proper campus cooling system. Some awareness programs must be launched for the students so that they may understand that hot weather makes them more vulnerable to violence. Universities should improve the infrastructure and bring some improvements to the environment of the campus so that the chances of violence can be decreased.
