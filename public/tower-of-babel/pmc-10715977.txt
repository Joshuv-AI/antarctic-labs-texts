Successful Management of Fetal Torsades de Pointes and Long QT Syndrome by a Cardio-Obstetrical Team
Authors: Manesha Putra, Yee Ming Lee, Emily Bucholz, Emma L Ross, Henry Galan, Nicholas Behrendt, Kestutis Micke, Franklin S Chow, Michael V Zaretsky, Bettina F Cuneo
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 32-week fetus with tachycardia and bradycardia, diagnosed with torsades de pointes, atrioventricular block, and sinus bradycardia due to a de novo KCNH2 mutation was successfully managed by a cardio-obstetrical team. Maternal/fetal pharmacogenomic testing resulted in appropriate drug dosing without toxicity and delivery of a term infant in sinus rhythm.Key Words: fetal arrhythmia, genetic testing, long QT syndrome, pharmacogenomics

Case presentation

A healthy 32-year-old G2P1001 pregnant subject with an unremarkable past medical history was referred at 32 weeks because of fetal hydrops and a complex fetal arrhythmia consisting of tachycardia (170-200 beats/min) and bradycardia with 2 distinct rates of 50 beats/min and 69 to 107 beats/min.

Question 1: what is the differential diagnosis of this complex rhythm?

The combination of tachycardia and bradycardia can be seen in anti-Ro/SSA antibody–mediated fetal cardiac disease and long QT syndrome (LQTS). If anti-Ro/SSA antibody titers are positive in the pregnant mother, the most likely fetal rhythms are junctional ectopic tachycardia, second-degree or third-degree atrioventricular (AV) block, and sinus bradycardia due to antibody-mediated damage to the sinoatrial and AV nodes and the conduction system below the bundle of HIS. If anti-Ro/SSA antibody titers are negative, the fetus is likely to have LQTS and an inherited or de novo pathogenic variant in KCNH2 or a de novo variant in SCN5A.1 Another possible diagnosis is a dual-ion channelopathy.

The family history was negative for symptoms of inherited arrhythmia syndromes and the parent’s electrocardiograms were normal (QTc <470 ms). A fetal echocardiogram was performed.

Question 2: if the fetus carried a pathogenic LQTS variant, what do you expect to see on the fetal echocardiogram?

Spectral Doppler would show second-degree AV block with 2:1 conduction when the fetal heart rate is 50 beats/min, 1:1 AV conduction during fetal heart rates of 96 to 107 beats/min consistent with sinus bradycardia, and variable A-V and V-V intervals with AV dissociation during tachycardia, consistent with ventricular tachycardia (VT). The findings of inconsistent V-V intervals during tachycardia and fluctuating ventricular outflow Doppler amplitudes suggest a severe form of LQTS (most likely torsades de pointes [TdP]) (Figure 1). During VT, cardiac function would be severely depressed (Video 1, Figure 2). Lastly, one would also expect a prolonged normalized isovolumic relaxation time, reflecting abnormal repolarization.2 All the above were found on the fetal echocardiogram.

The pregnant subject was admitted to the labor and delivery ward of a fetal care center and managed by a cardio-obstetrical team consisting of maternal-fetal medicine specialists, fetal cardiologists, geneticist/genetic counselor, pharmacologist, and a pharmacogeneticist.

Question 3: how does this multidisciplinary approach optimize care of the maternal/fetal dyad?

Multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics teams provide care and expertise around conception planning, pregnancy and peripartum management, and long-term education and risk reduction. Regular meetings between team members and the patient facilitate the development of concrete management strategies that focus on optimizing outcomes for both the mother and fetus. The involvement of a genetic specialist can be particularly helpful in guiding appropriate prenatal genetic testing and counseling patients on risk of recurrence in future pregnancies. Pharmacologist and pharmacogeneticist provide invaluable expertise when determining appropriate dosing of transplacental medications while also limiting toxicities in the mother and fetus as well as accounting for maternal and fetal pharmacogenomic status.

Maternal blood was sent for pharmacogenomic testing of anticipated transplacental LQTS medications (OneOme) and amniotic fluid was sent for cardiac arrhythmia panel (Prevention Genetics) and pharmacogenomic testing (Fulgent Genetics).

Question 4: what is the advantage of pharmacogenomic testing in this pregnancy?

Pharmacogenomics is the study of genetic variations that confer differences in drug metabolism and thus drug response between individuals. Individual phenotypes vary from ultra-rapid to poor drug metabolizers. Using pharmacogenomics to treat fetal conditions in utero can maximize drug efficacy and drug safety before delivery. We chose to evaluate fetal pharmacogenomics because we were already performing an amniocentesis for a genetic diagnosis and because fetal pharmacogenetics could not be predicted solely from maternal results.

Furthermore, pharmacogenomic testing is especially important in utero because fetal drug toxicity cannot be detected by ultrasound or fetal heart rate monitoring. This fetus is at risk for a poor outcome from proarrhythmic drug toxicities and in utero demise from the underlying condition (LQTS).

Question 5: what are the next steps in management of this pregnancy?

The mother and fetus should be evaluated and managed by a multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team as outlined above.3 Medications that prolong the QT interval such as ondansetron and oxytocin should be avoided. Maternal electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, and 25-OH vitamin D levels should be optimized. Vitamin D correction was recommended because higher rates of AV block occur at times of the year when vitamin D deficiency is most prevelent.4 Although it is not recommended to treat fetal sinus bradycardia or second-degree AV block, treatment is recommended for suspected fetal LQTS with intermittent or sustained TdP.5 Initial treatment consists of a loading dose followed by a continuous infusion of magnesium sulfate. Maintenance therapies include a β-blocking agent (most commonly propranolol), and/or mexiletine, a late sodium channel blocker.3 Both propranolol and mexiletine shorten the QT interval while mexiletine also inhibits early afterdepolarizations. Pharmacogenomic testing should be considered to inform dosing of medications in both the mother and fetus.

The pregnant subject was given intravenous magnesium sulfate (bolus of 5 g followed by a continuous infusion of 2 g/h). Within 24 hours, sinus rhythm was restored. Low-dose, short-acting propranolol (40 mg orally every 6 hours) and mexiletine (150 mg orally every 8 hours) were started and fetal cardiac arrhythmia panel and pharmacogenomic testing was sent. We chose a lower dose prior to results from the pharmacogenomic data to reduce maternal drug side effects while maintaining fetal sinus rhythm. The turnaround time for the pharmacogenomic testing was 1 week for maternal and 3 weeks for fetal studies. Both the mother and the fetus were found to be poor metabolizers of cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6), the enzymatic pathway responsible for metabolizing propranolol and mexiletine, by pharmacogenomic testing (Table 1). Therefore, we increased the propranolol dose to only 60 mg and mexiletine to 150 mg, each given orally every 6 hours. Because the pregnant patient was a CYP2D6 poor metabolizer, this finding may explain the lack of a propranolol dose-response relationship in our pregnant patient (Figure 3). It is difficult to know how propranolol was metabolized in the fetus because CYP2D6 activity is negligible in utero.6 However, the fetal pharmacogenomic data were proven useful in assessing postnatal dosing given that CYP2D6 activity reaches adult levels as early as several weeks after delivery.7 The poor metabolizer status of CYP2D6 in the neonate might explain the successful treatment of the infant with propranolol at a relatively low dose (3 mg/kg/d vs a maximum of 5 mg/kg/d.8 Without a proper prospective study, it is difficult to assess how effective pharmacogenomic testing is on drug dosing for the maternal/fetal dyad. However, knowing pharmacogenomic status supports the clinical gestalt of medication dosing, for example, to begin treatment at a lower dose and slowly increase the dose in CYP2D6 poor metabolizer or treat with a higher dose in CYP2D6 rapid metabolizers. The fetus was found to harbor a pathogenic de novo variant in KCNH2 c.1898A>G p. Asn633Ser, a missense variant in the pore region of the potassium channel gene that is highly conserved across species. This mutation results in reduced protein expression, abnormal trafficking, and enhanced channel inactivation. Serum and buccal cell testing in both parents were negative for the infant’s variant.

Question 6: what other testing would have been helpful in the diagnosis and management of this fetus?

Fetal magnetocardiography (fMCG) would have been useful in monitoring drug effects, such as changes in QTc and other cardiac intervals signifying drug effect or toxicity. Continuous fMCG monitoring of the fetal rhythm to evaluate the duration of VT, second-degree AV block, and sinus rhythm, as well as the type of VT (monomorphic or TdP) and any changes in the VT rate or morphology may have helped inform prognosis and risk of stillbirth.9,10 Unfortunately, fMCG is only available in research centers at this time.

A 2.9-kg female infant was delivered after 5 weeks of treatment at 37 2/7 weeks with APGAR scores of 7 (1 minute) and 9 (5 minutes). She had a brief run of TdP and multiple premature ventricular contractions in the delivery room which resolved after lidocaine (1 mg/kg) and magnesium (43 mg/kg intravenous) infusions. Her immediate postnatal QTc was 630 ms. After birth she continued propranolol (3 mg/kg/d) and mexiletine (6 mg/kg/d) with no recurrence of TdP. Electrocardiogram at 10 days after birth showed prolonged QTc of 546 ms, with new findings of intermittent accelerated idioventricular rhythm with a left bundle branch morphology and a normal QRS axis (Figure 4). After much discussion, we placed an internal cardiac defibrillator rather than a pacemaker with a single ventricular lead at 3 months of age, and shortly thereafter she was discharged to home on propranolol and mexiletine.

Conclusions

This is the first report of a clinically actionable pharmacogenomic-guided prenatal drug therapy and drug dosing for a maternal/fetal dyad in a pregnancy complicated by a severe fetal arrhythmia secondary to a de novo channelopathy. Collaboration by a multidisciplinary cardio-obstetrics team allowed for selection of appropriate prenatal therapy, titration of medication dosing to both the mother and fetus, and delivery of a term infant in sinus rhythm. Such “personalized medicine” before birth can reduce drug toxicity and improve drug efficacy not only for fetuses with arrhythmias, but for fetuses exposed to drugs used to treat maternal conditions, as long as the negligible risk of amniocentesis is less than the benefit of knowledge to be gained.
