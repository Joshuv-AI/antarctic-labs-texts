SCOUT MD™ for breast and axillary localisation: a narrative review toward a single radar-based platform
Authors: Umar Wazir, Kefah Mokbel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveWire-free localisation technologies have transformed the management of non-palpable breast lesions and pathological axillary lymph nodes. Radar-based localisation using the SAVI SCOUT® system is supported by extensive prospective clinical validation. SCOUT MD™ represents a next-generation radar platform incorporating miniaturised reflectors with unique signal identification designed to address recognised limitations of single-reflector systems. This narrative review aimed to examine the technical characteristics, regulatory status, clinical rationale and available evidence relating to SCOUT MD™, and to contextualise the platform within the broader landscape of wireless localisation technologies.MethodsA narrative review of literature published between January 2016 and January 2026 was undertaken using PubMed-indexed articles, regulatory documentation, manufacturer technical reports and reference lists from key studies of radar-based localisation systems.Key Content and FindingsSCOUT MD™ incorporates miniaturised 5-mm reflectors with up to four uniquely identifiable radar signatures enabling multi-target localisation. The reflectors can be discriminated individually by their unique signal signatures, without signal overlap. The system supports ultrasound, stereotactic mammography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT)-guided deployment. Clinical evidence currently consists primarily of early technical reports and a single case report.ConclusionsSCOUT MD™ addresses recognised technical limitations of single-reflector radar localisation systems, particularly in multi-target localisation and tight bracketing scenarios. However, independent clinical evidence remains limited and prospective clinical studies are required to establish its comparative effectiveness.

Introduction

Wire-free localisation technologies have transformed the management of non-palpable breast lesions by improving patient comfort, scheduling flexibility, and operative workflow compared with traditional wire-guided localisation. Among these systems, radar‑based localisation using the SAVI SCOUT® platform has achieved widespread international adoption supported by prospective studies and pooled analyses demonstrating high localisation success rates and favourable oncological outcomes (1-3).

Alternative wireless localisation technologies include magnetic seed localisation (Magseed®) (4), radiofrequency identification (RFID) systems (LOCalizer®) (5), and electromagnetic navigation systems such as EnVisio™. Each technology has distinct advantages and limitations relating to detection depth, imaging artefact and intra-operative workflow.

SCOUT MD™ represents a miniaturised, multi-reflector evolution of this established radar-based technology, designed to address specific limitations of single-reflector systems rather than replace their validated core indications. Its development reflects the growing need for improved localisation in complex clinical scenarios, including tight bracketing, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-only lesions, and localisation of small pathological lymph nodes. The aim of this narrative review is to critically appraise the technical specifications, regulatory status, and potential clinical applications of SCOUT MD™ relative to the established SAVI SCOUT® platform. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://gs.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/gs-2026-1-0031/rc).

Methods

This narrative review synthesised publicly available information on SCOUT MD™ published between January 2016 and January 2026. Sources included PubMed-indexed peer-reviewed publications, U.S. FDA regulatory documentation, manufacturer technical disclosures, and reference lists from key SAVI SCOUT® studies. Search terms included “SCOUT MD”, “SAVI SCOUT”, “radar localisation”, “breast localisation”, “axillary localisation”, “bracketing”, and “MRI artefact”. The search was performed by the senior author and cross-checked against reference lists of key publications.

Evidence was categorised as: (I) independent peer-reviewed clinical evidence; (II) manufacturer technical documentation or regulatory submission data; and (III) early clinical reports. Where performance characteristics of SCOUT MD™ derive from manufacturer documentation or regulatory submissions, this is explicitly identified in the text, as such data are not subject to peer review and may not reflect independent real-world performance. Given the limited peer-reviewed SCOUT MD™ clinical evidence, a formal systematic review was not feasible. No new patient-level data were generated. Findings

Limitations of the standard SAVI SCOUT® platform

Despite extensive clinical validation, the standard SAVI SCOUT® platform has recognised limitations. Outside the United States, MRI-guided deployment for lesions visible only on MRI is not routinely feasible because MRI-compatible delivery instruments are not universally available. Although the SCOUT Bx™ Delivery System is MRI compatible in the USA, this limitation persists in many international settings, necessitating delayed localisation under mammographic or ultrasound guidance.

Tight bracketing or localisation of multiple lesions in close proximity is also challenging because standard SAVI SCOUT® reflectors generate identical radar signals that cannot be distinguished intraoperatively, leading to signal overlap when reflectors are placed close together (1-3). In addition, the 12-mm reflector length, although advantageous for detectability, may be suboptimal for small axillary lymph nodes, particularly following neoadjuvant systemic therapy (NST), where nodes frequently measure less than 10 mm (6).

Signal attenuation has been reported when reflectors are deployed within biopsy-related haematomas, although this can be mitigated with experience, strategic reflector placement, and incision planning (1-3). Reflector inactivation due to electrocautery or mechanical injury is uncommon and has been further reduced through design refinements incorporating additional capacitors (1-3).

Technical and regulatory evolution from SAVI SCOUT® to SCOUT MD™

SCOUT MD™ represents a next-generation evolution of radar-based localisation technology. Its defining innovation is the ability to deploy up to four uniquely shaped reflectors, each generating a distinct radar signature that can be visually identified on mammography and audibly differentiated intraoperatively (Figures 1,2) (7). The reflectors can be discriminated individually by their unique signal signatures, with no minimum distance required to prevent signal overlap. Each reflector provides individualised depth and distance feedback, displayed in real time on a touchscreen console, allowing surgeons to toggle between or isolate specific signals intraoperatively (7-9).

SCOUT MD™ is compatible with ultrasound, stereotactic mammography, MRI, and CT-guided placement, enabling reflector deployment at the time of biopsy in the diagnostic pathway (7-9). Miniaturised reflectors of approximately 5 mm facilitate placement in anatomically constrained locations, including small breast lesions and pathological axillary lymph nodes compared with approximately 12 mm standard SAVI SCOUT® reflectors (7-9). MRI artefact associated with these reflectors has been reported to be consistently less than 2 mm, improving post-NST imaging interpretation (7-9).

FDA regulatory status

The U.S. FDA has granted SCOUT MD™ 510(k) clearance based on substantial equivalence to the SAVI SCOUT® system rather than on prospective comparative clinical outcomes (9). At the time of writing, CE marking in Europe remains under review.

Console compatibility and capital cost

The SCOUT MD™ multi-channel console (Figure 2) is not backward compatible with the standard SAVI SCOUT® console. Centres transitioning from SAVI SCOUT® will require new capital investment. Commercial pricing for SCOUT MD™ had not been disclosed by Merit Medical Systems at the time of writing; formal health-economic analysis will only be possible once pricing data are available. Learning curve

Experience with first-generation SAVI SCOUT® demonstrates a short and predictable learning curve for radiologists and surgeons, with rapid attainment of technical proficiency and workflow integration (1-3). In centres already using SAVI SCOUT®, transition to SCOUT MD™ is expected to require minimal additional training because the core localisation principles, console interface, and intraoperative workflow are preserved (7-9). The incremental complexity of multi-reflector differentiation is intuitive and unlikely to represent a barrier to implementation.

Pragmatic implementation through structured audit

Given FDA clearance (9) and extensive experience with the first-generation SAVI SCOUT® system (1-3), a pragmatic implementation pathway may be considered. Early-adopting centres could introduce SCOUT MD™ within a prospective, registry-linked clinical audit framework. A sample size of approximately 70 consecutive cases may provide adequate power to detect clinically meaningful performance differences compared with the centre’s established localisation method. The audit should capture successful localisation rate (primary endpoint), operative time, margin positivity and re-excision rates, technical failures, and patient experience. If non-inferior performance is demonstrated, centres could consider broader routine use.

Clinical rationale for multi-reflector localisation

The principal rationale underpinning SCOUT MD™ is device miniaturisation combined with multi-target versatility, particularly in scenarios requiring tight bracketing or localisation of extensive disease exceeding 30 mm (Figure 3). Although approximately 10% of lesions currently undergo bracketing (10,11), this proportion is expected to rise. Increasing adoption of oncoplastic techniques (12), increasing utility of breast MRI together with a growing preference for breast-conserving surgery for larger and multifocal disease to avoid mastectomy, are supported by contemporary evidence demonstrating an overall survival advantage for breast-conserving surgery plus radiotherapy compared with mastectomy in appropriately selected patients (13). In parallel, wider availability of effective wire-free localisation platforms may reduce technical barriers to complex localisation.

In targeted axillary dissection, lymph nodes following NST are often small, fibrotic, or partially regressed, making secondary localisation challenging (6). Localisation of pathological lymph nodes at the time of diagnostic biopsy has been advocated to improve surgical accuracy (6,14). In this context, the smaller reflector size and distinct signal identity of SCOUT MD™ may offer advantages over standard single-reflector systems, pending prospective validation (7-9).

Similarly, tight bracketing in patients with small breast volumes or multifocal disease may benefit from uniquely identifiable reflectors that minimise signal overlap, a recognised limitation when standard SAVI SCOUT® reflectors are placed in close proximity (1-3). MRI-only lesions represent another potential application; approximately 1.5% of breast cancers detected in supplemental screening are visible only on MRI (15), and SCOUT MD™ enables wire-free MRI-guided localisation of such lesions (7-9,15).

Bracketing and multi-target surgery

For most lesions measuring 30 mm or less, a single centrally placed standard SAVI SCOUT® reflector achieves excellent oncological outcomes, with reported re-excision rates below 5% (4). Routine bracketing in this group is therefore neither necessary nor cost-effective (4,10,11).

In contrast, lesions exceeding 30 mm, multifocal disease, or cases in which cosmetic preservation is paramount may benefit from tighter circumferential bracketing enabled by SCOUT MD™ (Figure 3) (7,10-12). This approach may reduce unnecessary excision of normal tissue and improve aesthetic outcomes, although confirmation in prospective clinical studies is required.

In extensive ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), disease extent often exceeds the distribution of mammographic microcalcifications, and non-mass MRI enhancement does not reliably predict the true extent of low-grade DCIS (15,16). In such settings, even advanced localisation technologies cannot fully compensate for biological and imaging limitations (15,16).

Replacing the standard SAVI SCOUT® system with SCOUT MD™ for single-reflector localisation could permit platform consolidation, but this must be balanced against higher capital costs and the absence of comparative clinical outcome data.

The clinical need for advanced radar-based localisation systems such as SCOUT MD™ is substantial and may expand. Bracketing is required in approximately 10% of localisation procedures (10,11). However, there are no standardised indications for bracketing of breast tumours, and practice varies considerably between centres, with a range of 15 to 95 mm (10). The average lesion size in published studies has demonstrated a considerable variation. Civil et al. reported an average tumour size of 24 mm, Liberman and colleagues reported an average size of 35 mm and Lyons et al. reported an average tumour size of 50 mm (10,11,17). Bracketed localisation has most commonly been used for tumours with a large disease span, multifocality, and those with a DCIS or invasive lobular carcinoma component. Although these features are typically associated with higher rates of positive margins, the reported rates of additional surgery following bracketed localisation are comparable to the national average for all breast-conserving surgery procedures.

It should also be highlighted that the relative size of the breast is a significant contributing factor to the indication for bracketing. In patients with relatively small breasts, tight bracketing can be particularly beneficial for smaller disease footprints than 30 mm to ensure accurate excision of the disease with minimal removal of surrounding healthy breast tissue, thereby helping to maintain an optimal aesthetic outcome.

Extrapolated nationally, assuming preoperative localisation is performed in approximately 30% of the 1 million breast biopsies undertaken annually in the United States, an estimated 30,000 women per year would require bracketing localisation (10,11). This highlights a clear unmet need for technologies capable of accurate multi-target localisation, for which SCOUT MD™ is specifically designed (7-9).

For most patients requiring localisation of a single non-palpable lesion measuring less than 30 mm, the standard SAVI SCOUT® system remains highly effective, cost-efficient, and supported by extensive clinical validation (1-3). Routine adoption of SCOUT MD™ in this population would increase costs without demonstrable clinical benefit (10,11).

SCOUT MD™ may offer advantages in selected scenarios, including multifocal disease or extensive in-situ carcinoma exceeding 30 mm requiring tight bracketing (7,10-12), MRI-only lesions (7-9,15), and targeted axillary dissection after NST where nodes are small or fibrotic (6-9). However, higher capital and consumable costs and increased workflow complexity necessitate careful patient selection and institutional cost-effectiveness analysis (7-9).

In targeted axillary dissection (TAD), nodes following NST are often small, fibrotic or partially regressed, making localisation challenging (6). Localisation of pathological nodes at diagnostic biopsy has been advocated to improve surgical accuracy (14). In most cases, first-generation reflectors perform reliably; the potential advantage of SCOUT MD™ miniaturisation may be limited to nodes adjacent to major vascular structures (6). For MRI-only lesions (as discussed above), SCOUT MD™ may enable wire-free MRI-guided localisation at the time of diagnostic biopsy according to manufacturer documentation (7-9).

Until prospective studies demonstrate reliable clinical performance, SCOUT MD™ should be considered complementary rather than a replacement technology (7-9). Assuming comparable clinical performance between the single SCOUT MD reflector and the first-generation SAVI SCOUT system, and a similar cost of consumables, it is plausible that breast centres currently using the SAVI SCOUT® platform may transition to SCOUT MD™ if comparable single-reflector performance and cost are confirmed. This would enhance clinical performance and expand capabilities in scenarios such as bracketing of extensive and multifocal disease, more precise localisation of pathological lymph nodes, and the selective use of multiple reflectors for bracketed localisation, as determined through multidisciplinary team discussion. Institutions adopting SCOUT MD™ should implement standardised selection criteria, prospectively monitor outcomes, and contribute to registry-based data collection.

Radiologist’s perspective: deployment, ergonomics, and migration

From a radiologist standpoint, wireless localisation systems differ in deployment ergonomics, imaging compatibility and workflow integration. Radar reflectors are generally straightforward to deploy and demonstrate minimal migration (1-3). Puente-Monserrat et al. report a 100% localisation success rate, a re-excision rate of 9.3%, and no device-related adverse events in the course of a prospective study of the use of radar-based localisation in 247 patients (18). Magnetic seed systems may interfere with surgical instruments (4), while RFID systems provide unique tag identification but may produce larger imaging artefacts (5). Electromagnetic navigation systems such as EnVisio™ enable realtime spatial navigation of surgical instruments relative to implanted markers (19).

With regards to SAVI SCOUT® and SCOUT MD™, several practical considerations warrant attention. Miniaturisation from approximately 12 to 5 mm may reduce ultrasound visibility and the echogenic signature of the implanted reflector, potentially making post-deployment sonographic confirmation more challenging; no published data comparing ultrasound visibility between the two systems are currently available, and this represents a specific evidence gap for early adoption programmes.

Lim et al. reported mechanical difficulty retracting the introducer deployment button with the first-generation SAVI SCOUT® system, which could affect deployment accuracy (20). Whether this has been redesigned in SCOUT MD™ is not stated in available manufacturer documentation; early-adopting centres should prospectively document deployment ergonomics. Migration rates for SAVI SCOUT® have been consistently low (≤1–2%) across prospective series (1-3); migration data for SCOUT MD™ are not yet available.

The ability to deploy SCOUT MD™ reflectors at the time of diagnostic biopsy under MRI, computed tomography (CT) or ultrasound guidance (manufacturer data) offers a potential workflow advantage by eliminating a separate pre-operative localisation procedure, although the economic and logistical implications require prospective evaluation.

Safety considerations

Nickel allergy

Standard SAVI SCOUT® reflectors contain nitinol (nickel-titanium alloy), and nickel hypersensitivity has been documented as a contraindication in prior publications (20-22). The alloy composition of SCOUT MD™ reflectors is not stated in available manufacturer documentation. Clinicians should verify material composition directly with the manufacturer before use in patients with known nickel allergy, and should not assume the contraindication profile is identical to SAVI SCOUT® until formally published.

Electrocautery resistance

Electrocautery-related inactivation was a recognised early limitation of SAVI SCOUT® that was substantially mitigated through incorporation of additional capacitors in later design iterations (1-3). Manufacturer documentation for SCOUT MD™ states that updated electronic components are incorporated; however, no independently published data directly comparing electrocautery resistance between SCOUT MD™ and SAVI SCOUT® are available. Prospective studies should document intraoperative inactivation events.

Haematoma signal attenuation

Signal attenuation due to post-biopsy haematoma has been described for SAVI SCOUT® (1-3). Whether SCOUT MD™ is more or less susceptible to this phenomenon is unknown. Clinicians should apply the same mitigation strategies established for SAVI SCOUT® (strategic placement, avoidance of direct haematoma implantation, incision planning) until comparative data are available.

Conclusions

SCOUT MD™ serves as both a complementary technology for complex scenarios and a replacement platform enabling consolidation for centres currently using SAVI SCOUT®. Assuming comparable single-reflector performance and consumable cost, SCOUT MD™ offers clear technical advantages for selected indications—particularly tight bracketing, MRI-only lesions, and small-node axillary localisation—while preserving overall cost-effectiveness through judicious, MDT-led deployment of multiple reflectors. Until prospective comparative data are available, adoption within structured audit and registry-based evaluation is appropriate to define its true clinical value and optimal patient selection criteria.

The economic impact of SCOUT MD™ is therefore largely dependent on selective multi-reflector use for tight bracketing. When deployed as a single-reflector platform, SCOUT MD™ is expected to demonstrate equivalent performance and comparable consumable costs to SAVI SCOUT®, enabling platform consolidation without financial penalty. Formal health-economic modelling is now required to define the cost-utility threshold at which multi-reflector deployment becomes clinically and financially justified.
