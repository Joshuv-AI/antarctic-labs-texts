Short-term effects of air pollutants on asthma hospitalizations in a megacity in North China (2016–2021): a time-series study
Authors: Wanyu Niu, Junjun Huang, Yan Hu, Guiqian Tang, Guangfa Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma remains a major global health challenge, with air pollution triggering severe exacerbations. Current studies have reported inconsistent results due to limited pollutant coverage, small sample size, or focus on mild cases. Beijing, with extreme air pollution, clear seasons, and dense population, provides an ideal setting to examine pollution-hospitalization relationships for asthma.MethodsUsing 2016–2021 data from 12 Beijing monitoring stations, we analyzed daily particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm (PM₂.₅), particulate matter ≤ 10 μm (PM₁₀), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) and carbon monoxide (CO) levels. A generalized additive time-series model with a Poisson distribution was used to investigate relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for asthma hospitalizations, stratified by sex, age, atopic status and season.ResultsAll five air pollutants were significantly associated with the risk of asthma hospitalization, and each exhibited lagged effects. Per interquartile range (IQR) increase in pollutant concentration, RRs were: PM₂.₅ 1.018 (95% CI: 1.005–1.031), PM₁₀ 1.012 (1.003–1.021), SO₂ 1.019 (1.010–1.028), NO₂ 1.059 (1.041–1.077), and CO 1.025 (1.014–1.036). Strongest associations occurred in winter. Females, the elderly, and non-atopic individuals were most vulnerable.ConclusionShort-term exposure to air pollutants increased asthma hospitalization risks, with amplified effects in females, the elderly, and non-atopic individuals. These findings highlight critical exposure windows and vulnerable populations, guiding targeted interventions. Background

Asthma is a serious global health problem. Epidemiological studies reveal a rising prevalence of asthma, which affects approximately 300 million people worldwide and causes around 1,000 deaths per day [1]. Recurrent asthma exacerbations accelerate disease progression and pose a major challenge for asthma management. Patients with asthma exacerbations requiring hospitalization have higher healthcare costs and worse outcomes, demanding greater attention [2]. Factors triggering asthma exacerbations include infections, exercise, allergens, air pollution, meteorological factors, and emotional stress [3, 4]. Among these triggers, air pollution is predictable and monitorable, enabling the development of proactive asthma management strategies and forming the foundation for protective health services. Previous studies have explored the association between air pollution and asthma exacerbations [5–9]. However, their findings are inconsistent and have notable limitations, including incomplete pollutant coverage, narrow temporal scales, small study sample sizes, and insufficient research depth. Additionally, some studies focus solely on outpatient and emergency department visits, rather than on the more severe patient group who are hospitalized for asthma.

China’s large population means even marginal increases in air pollutant concentrations can impose substantial public health burdens. Regional disparities in air pollution exist across China, with northern regions experiencing higher pollution levels due to an arid climate and heavy reliance on coal combustion for winter heating, which exacerbates associated health risks. Rapid industrialization and urbanization have also driven shifts in the epidemiology of asthma, with the national prevalence estimated at 2.20% [10]. Beijing has a temperate monsoon climate, featuring hot and rainy summers, cold and dry winters, four distinct seasons, and a relatively large annual temperature range, making it particularly suitable for investigating the combined effects of air pollutants under different climatic conditions. As a major metropolis in northern China, Beijing has severe air pollution and a population of over ten million. A large-scale, severe air pollution event occurred in Beijing in 2013, with an annual average PM₂.₅ concentration of 89.5 µg/m³, more than eight times the WHO guideline value of 10 µg/m³ [11]. Beijing’s unique combination of high population density, complex emission sources, and well-documented extreme air pollution events renders it highly significant and representative for environmental health research.

Therefore, this study investigated the short-term effects of ambient air pollutants, including particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm (PM₂.₅), particulate matter ≤ 10 μm (PM₁₀), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), and carbon monoxide (CO), on asthma hospitalizations in Beijing from 2016 to 2021 by analyzing atmospheric pollutant monitoring data and asthma hospitalization records, with the aim of evaluating the health effects of air pollutants and providing a scientific basis for future disease prevention and control. Given that asthma is a heterogeneous disease, identifying populations susceptible to air pollution-induced asthma hospitalization is highly necessary. To identify such susceptible populations and high-risk seasons, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, atopic status, and season. To our knowledge, no previous study in Beijing has simultaneously examined the short-term effects of multiple air pollutants on asthma hospitalizations with a comparable longitudinal duration and population-wide coverage to our investigation.

Method

Study setting and exposure data

PM₁₀ (µg/m³), PM₂.₅ (µg/m³), SO₂ (µg/m³), NO₂ (µg/m³) and CO (mg/m³) were measured at 12 national-level ground air quality monitoring stations across Beijing. The monitoring network is operated by the Beijing Municipal Ecological and Environmental Monitoring Center (https://zx.bjmemc.com.cn/). Hourly measurements were available for at least 20 h per day at each station to calculate 24-hour mean concentrations. Daily city-wide average concentrations for each pollutant were derived from the arithmetic mean of readings at all 12 stations. We collected these daily averaged data from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2021 (2192 days in total), and all data passed quality control verification.

During the 2192-day study period, the missing data rates for the five pollutants were as follows: PM₂.₅ (3.787%), PM₁₀ (13.504%), SO₂ (3.741%), NO₂ (3.741%), and CO (3.741%). PM₁₀ exhibited the highest missing rate among these pollutants. To reconstruct the missing PM₁₀ data, we used 24-h average concentrations for PM₂.₅ on the same date multiplied by the ratio between annual mean concentration for PM₁₀ and annual mean concentration for PM₂.₅ derived for that year [12]. The annual ratios derived were 1.407 for 2016, 1.515 for 2017, 1.620 for 2018, 1.552 for 2019, 1.477 for 2020 and 1.967 for 2021, which were similar to previously reported results [12]. After processing, the missing rate of PM₁₀ data was 3.741%.

Hospital data

We obtained data on asthma-related hospitalizations in Beijing from 2016 to 2021 from the Beijing Municipal Healthcare Security Administration, which includes front-page medical record information from both employee and resident medical insurance databases. This database has been widely used in epidemiological research due to its broad coverage and strong representativeness [13–15]. Each record contains age, sex, admission and discharge dates, discharge diagnoses, and corresponding International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, which were collected following standard procedures. From January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2021, a total of 163,039 records were obtained from Beijing Municipal Medical Insurance Center, where the primary discharge diagnoses or any secondary diagnoses contained the ICD-10 codes about asthma (J45 and J46). To identify asthma-related hospitalizations, we included all patients with an asthma-related diagnosis as the primary discharge diagnosis. Additionally, we included those whose primary discharge diagnosis was respiratory infection, respiratory failure, or cor pulmonale, provided that asthma was documented as either the first or second secondary discharge diagnosis. Patients who were administered for regular biologic agent injections were excluded. A total of 30,888 hospitalization records were included in the final analysis. The above screening process was independently verified by two researchers in a back-to-back manner. Before analysis, researchers de-identified the data, ensuring that patients cannot be recognized at the individual level.

Statistical analyses

Time series analysis is widely applied in environmental epidemiology and is the most commonly used approach to investigating the acute effects of air pollution and meteorological factors on human health [16]. Generalized additive models (GAM) are the most widely used time series models; they can simultaneously assess linear and nonlinear relationships with the dependent variable while adjusting for a variety of confounders [17]. For the total study population, asthma hospitalizations constitute a rare event, which can be approximated by a Poisson distribution. Therefore, in this study, a time series analysis of the relationship between air pollutants and daily asthma hospitalization counts was conducted using a Poisson-based GAM, with adjustment for long-term trends, meteorological factors, heating seasons, and public holidays (including weekends).

where t is the day lag; E(Yt) represents the expected number of asthma hospitalizations on day t; Zt is the city-averaged ambient air pollutant concentration on day t; β represents the log-relative risk (RR) of asthma hospitalizations associated with one unit increase in the pollutant concentration; ns ( ) indicates a natural cubic spline function. These functions were incorporated to control for long-term temporal trends, seasonal patterns, as well as the nonlinear effects of meteorological factors on daily asthma hospitalizations; TEM is the daily mean temperature (°C); and RHU is the daily mean relative humidity (%). Public holidays and heating seasons were included as categorical variables. Degrees of freedom (df) for calendar time, temperature, and relative humidity were selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) analysis and the parameter values used in previous studies [12, 18–20]. A linear concentration-response relationship was assumed based on previous epidemiological literature [11, 12, 18, 20–25]. The results are ultimately presented as the relative risk (RR) of asthma hospitalization per interquartile range (IQR) increase in pollutant concentration, along with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI).

Apart from single-pollutant models, we also performed two-pollutant models for each pollutant to adjust for potential co-pollutant confounding. Only pairs with Spearman correlation coefficients < 0.7 were included in two-pollutant models to avoid unstable estimates due to multicollinearity [12, 26]. Considering the lagged health effects of air pollution, and based on prior research indicating that asthma exhibits an acute and short-lived response to environmental factors, this study analyzed the single-day lag effects of air pollutants from lag 0 to lag 7 and the cumulative lag effects from lag 01 to lag 07 [17]. Specifically, lag 0 denotes the air pollutant concentration on the exposure day, lag 1 represents the concentration one day prior to exposure, and so forth up to lag 7. Cumulative lag effects were calculated as the moving average of pollutant concentrations on the exposure day and preceding days; for example, lag 02 reflects the three-day average of lag 0, lag 1, and lag 2 concentrations.

To investigate the impact of air pollution on the asthma hospitalizations in different populations and seasons, subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, age, atopic status and season with the Z-test used to compare effect estimates across subgroups. Patients with atopic discharge diagnoses were defined as atopic individuals, and these patients were considered to have a greater allergic predisposition than non-atopic patients.

To evaluate whether the COVID-19 pandemic modified the association between air pollutants and asthma hospitalizations, we introduced a binary indicator to distinguish the pre-pandemic period (2016–2019) from the pandemic period (2020–2021). We tested the interaction between each pollutant and the pandemic period using an interaction model with the same covariates as the main model: time, temperature, relative humidity, holiday, and heating season. No significant interactions were detected for any pollutant (Supplementary Material 1), so we retained the main model with the full study period (2016–2021) for all primary and subgroup analyses to ensure model simplicity, interpretability, statistical power, and estimation stability.

We performed several sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings. First, we varied the df for the temporal natural spline (4–10 per year) and for meteorological variables (3–5) to evaluate the robustness of our results to different model specifications for long-term trends, seasonality, and nonlinear weather-related confounding. Second, to validate the PM₁₀ reconstruction method based on PM₂.₅ and the annual ratio between PM₁₀ and PM₂.₅, we repeated the main analyses after excluding the imputed PM₁₀ data. Given that PM₁₀ may exhibit seasonal variations, we further performed a seasonal correlation analysis between PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ using all available observed data to validate the scientific rigor of our imputation method. The appropriateness of our model with a linear pollutant concentration- response relationship, compared with a nonlinear counterpart, was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests, AIC, and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Descriptive statistics including mean, standard deviation (SD), twenty-fifth percentile (P₂₅), fiftieth percentile (P₅₀), seventy-fifth percentile (P₇₅), IQR, minimum (Min), and maximum (Max) for air pollutants and meteorological data were calculated to characterize the dataset. Statistical analyses above were all performed using R 4.2.1 (P < 0.05).

Results

Characteristics of air pollution, meteorology and asthma hospitalizations

During the study period from 2016 to 2021, a total of 30,888 hospital admissions for asthma exacerbations were recorded, with a mean of 14.18 admissions per day and a standard deviation of 7.84. The study population included 41.32% males and 58.68% females. For age groups, 3.61% were aged < 18 years, 41.28% were aged 18–60 years, and 55.11% were aged > 60 years. Atopy was documented in 29.33% of patients, while 70.67% were classified as non-atopy. Seasonal distribution revealed 7,485 cases (24.23%) in spring (March to May), 7,071 cases (22.89%) in summer (June to August), 8,135 cases (26.34%) in autumn (September to November), and 8,197 cases (26.54%) in winter (December to February) (Table 1). Impact of air pollutants on asthma hospitalizations across single-day lag models

In single-day lag models, all assessed pollutants were associated with an increased risk of asthma hospitalization (Fig. 2A). PM₂.₅ demonstrated significant lag effects at lag 2 and lag 3, with the strongest association at lag 2 [RR: 1.018 (95% CI: 1.005–1.031)], representing a 1.77% (95% CI: 0.50%–3.06%) increase in asthma hospitalization risk per IQR increment. For PM₁₀, significant effects were observed from lag 0 to lag 7 except lag 4, with the maximum effect at lag 2 [RR: 1.012 (95% CI: 1.003–1.021)], reflecting a 1.20% (95% CI: 0.29%–2.11%) increase in asthma hospitalization risk per IQR increase. SO₂ showed statistically significant associations from lag 0 to lag 7 except lag 1, with the most pronounced effect at lag 2 [RR: 1.019 (95% CI: 1.010–1.028)], indicating a 1.89% (95% CI: 0.97%–2.80%) increase in risk per IQR increase. The single-day lag effect of NO₂ was observed from lag 0 to lag 7, with the strongest association at lag 7 [RR: 1.059 (95% CI: 1.041–1.077)], corresponding to a 5.89% (95% CI: 4.11%–7.70%) increase in asthma hospitalization risk per IQR increase. CO exhibited significant associations across the same lag periods as NO₂, with the maximum effect at lag 2 [RR: 1.025 (95% CI: 1.014–1.036)], representing a 2.48% (95% CI: 1.39%–3.58%) increase in asthma hospitalization risk per IQR increase. The specific RRs and 95% CIs are detailed in Supplementary Material 2.

Impact of air pollutants on asthma hospitalizations across cumulative lag models

The cumulative lag effects of PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, and CO were all significant from lag 01 to lag 07, showing a gradually increasing trend, with the largest effect at lag 07 [RR: 1.044 (95% CI: 1.020–1.068), 1.044 (95% CI: 1.026–1.062), 1.125 (95% CI: 1.087–1.164), and 1.035 (95% CI: 1.015–1.055), respectively]. The cumulative lag effects of PM₂.₅ were not significant from lag 01 to lag 07. The corresponding increases in asthma hospitalization risk per IQR rise were 4.35% (95% CI: 2.01%–6.75%), 4.42% (95% CI: 2.63%–6.23%), 12.48% (95% CI: 8.70%–16.39%), and 3.49% (95% CI: 1.49%–5.53%), respectively. Furthermore, the cumulative effect of all air pollutants was stronger than their single-day effect, except for PM₂.₅. Subgroup analyses stratifying by sex, age, atopic status and season

We performed cumulative lag analyses for each pollutant in subgroups stratified by sex, age, atopic status, and season.

However, no statistically significant difference by sex was observed for the association between SO₂ exposure and asthma hospitalization. PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, and CO were generally associated with an increased hospitalization risk among females across most lag periods, whereas no significant associations were observed in males. Sensitivity analyses

Effect estimates remained robust with varying df from 3 to 5 for meteorological factors (Supplementary Material 9). Sensitivity analyses using df values from 4 to 10 per year for the temporal natural spline showed that effect estimates for all pollutants were stable when df was set between 4 and 7. However, at higher df values 10 (8–10), some effect estimates were moderately attenuated, which may reflect over-adjustment for long-term and seasonal trends. In contrast, the effects of NO₂ and SO₂ were robust across the full df range (4–10) (Supplementary Material 9). These findings suggest that 6 df per year offers a reasonable balance between controlling for temporal confounding and avoiding overfitting, supporting the robustness of our main results.

Seasonal correlation analysis between PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ using all available observed data revealed very strong positive correlations across all seasons (r = 0.822–0.924, all P < 0.001) (Supplementary Material 10), supporting the use of PM₂.₅ as a reliable predictor for imputing missing PM₁₀ values. Analyses using unimputed PM₁₀ data showed a consistent and statistically significant positive association between PM₁₀ and asthma hospitalization risk, confirming the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Material 11).

Results comparing linear and nonlinear models using likelihood ratio tests, AIC, and BIC (Supplementary Material 12) showed that the linear assumption was statistically supported for PM₂.₅ and CO. For PM₁₀, SO₂, and NO₂, although likelihood ratio tests indicated significant nonlinearity, the more conservative BIC suggested that the improvement in fit from nonlinear models may not justify the increased complexity. This supports the overall rationality of our model with liner concentration-response assumption.

Discussion

Our study assessed the short-term effects of air pollutants on asthma hospitalizations among residents of Beijing, China from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2021. We found that short-term exposure to PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, and CO was associated with an increased risk of asthma hospitalization. The peak lag effects for single-day models were predominantly observed at lag 2. Previous studies are consistent with the findings. A meta-analysis including 87 studies reported that air pollutants were significantly associated with elevated risks of asthma hospitalization, with effects typically occurring at lag 2 or later [27]. A similar pattern was observed in a study conducted in an Asian country at a latitude comparable to Beijing, which found peak effects of PM₁₀, NO₂, and CO at lag 2 or lag 3 [25]. Another study performed in heavily industrialized cities in Northeast China reported that the maximum RR associated with exposure to PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, NO₂, and SO₂ occurred at lag 1 [28]. The observed differences were partly driven by the heterogeneous spatial distribution of air pollutants, as well as regional variations in climate and environmental conditions across study areas.

In single-day lag models, both PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ significantly increased the risk of asthma hospitalization. However, in cumulative lag models, PM₂.₅ did not show a significant effect. This discrepancy may be explained by the complex biological responses to PM₂.₅ exposure. Evidence indicates that the effects induced by PM₂.₅ are not simply linearly cumulative, but involve complex interactions between pro-inflammatory mechanisms and endogenous protective feedback responses [29, 30]. The intricate interplay of multiple regulatory pathways may thus reduce the net effect observed in cumulative exposure models. Previous epidemiological studies have shown that in cumulative exposure models, PM₂.₅ increases the risk of asthma outpatient visits [23, 31] and emergency department visits [21], but not asthma hospitalizations [21]. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in study populations. Asthma outpatient visits, emergency department visits, and hospitalizations represent distinct clinical severity levels, with hospitalization indicating severe asthma exacerbations. Due to the exclusion of outpatient and emergency visit data, our assessment of PM₂.₅’s association with acute asthma exacerbations might be less comprehensive. Additionally, other confounding factors such as hospital bed availability could have influenced the results.

Our findings demonstrated that gaseous air pollutants including SO₂, NO₂ and CO significantly increased asthma hospitalization risks. Similar results have also been reported in studies from other regions of China [31–34]. The observed health effects are mechanistically plausible. SO₂, primarily emitted from industrial and energy sectors in China, exacerbates asthma by increasing airway inflammation, eosinophil infiltration, bronchospasm, and airflow obstruction [35]. NO₂ derived from traffic emissions and cooking gas combustion triggers airway inflammation via oxidative stress and upregulates pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby exacerbating asthma pathogenesis [36, 37]. Across all two-pollutant models, the results showed that the effect of NO₂ remained stable and statistically significant after adjusting for other pollutants, suggesting that NO₂ may be an independent risk factor for acute asthma hospitalization. When adjusted for NO₂, the effects of PM₁₀ and SO₂ lost statistical significance, suggesting that part of the effects of PM₁₀ and SO₂ was mediated by NO₂. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ningbo [23], Guangdong [38] and Australia [39]. CO is nonirritating and easily passes into the bloodstream, where it competes with oxygen for hemoglobin binding, leading to tissue hypoxia [40]. It is not typically considered a potent respiratory irritant. The association between CO and asthma may primarily reflect its role as an indicator of traffic-related air pollution or combustion sources, rather than exerting a direct effect [41, 42]. This interpretation is supported by the observation that the significant effect of CO in single-pollutant models was substantially attenuated and became statistically non-significant after adjustment for other pollutants in two-pollutant models. Currently, China prioritizes particulate matter (PM) reduction as the primary target for air pollution control [43]. However, our study demonstrated that gaseous pollutants pose non-negligible health risks, suggesting that future policies should address the complex mixture of pollutants affecting respiratory health and adopt an integrated approach for comprehensive air quality management.

Our study revealed significantly stronger effects of air pollutants on asthma hospitalization risks during winter than in other seasons. Existing studies indicate that extreme temperatures and air pollutants have synergistic effects in inducing asthma exacerbations [44–46]. Some studies have demonstrated more pronounced effects during cold seasons, such as in Olsztyn [47], a city with comparable winter temperatures to Beijing, and in other heavily polluted Chinese metropolises [9, 22, 32]. Potential mechanisms may include cold-induced reflex bronchoconstriction [48, 49] and aggravated airway inflammation leading to impaired immune defense against respiratory infections [50], all of which could increase the susceptibility of asthma patients to the adverse effects of air pollution. Notably, spring and autumn also showed significant pollutant-asthma associations, potentially linked to elevated levels of aeroallergens such as pollen. These findings provide an evidence base for seasonally targeted interventions and population-specific protective strategies.

Stratified analyses by sex, age, and atopic status identified females, the elderly, and non-atopic individuals as susceptible subgroups to air pollution, which highlights the need for more targeted and personalized public health strategies. This sex-specific susceptibility was supported by studies from Beijing and other cities with similar pollution profiles [9, 24, 34]. This may be explained by females’ narrower airways and weaker lung defense mechanisms against pollutants. Among the elderly, pre-existing airway comorbidities and age-related airway changes enhance pollution-induced inflammation, leading to progressive airway remodeling and clinical deterioration [27]. Consistent findings were reported in an Asian population-based study [51]. However, some studies found that children were more vulnerable to air pollution [9, 31, 34, 46]. This discrepancy may be attributable to the limited sample size of pediatric patients in our study, which limited statistical power to detect significant associations. Some studies indicated that outdoor air pollution was more strongly associated with non-allergic asthma than with allergic asthma [22, 52]. A California cohort study that stratified children by allergic symptoms also found stronger effects in children without such symptoms [53]. Differences in susceptibility to air pollution between atopic and non-atopic individuals may reflect distinct pathophysiological responses across asthma subtypes, stemming from differing mechanistic pathways of pollutant interaction with subtypes. Current evidence suggests that eosinophilic airway inflammation accounts for no more than 50% of asthma cases, while non-eosinophilic asthma may primarily involve neutrophil-dominated inflammatory pathways [54]. Importantly, exposure to air pollutants appears to play a significant role in triggering neutrophilic airway inflammation [55], which may be a key mechanism for asthma exacerbations, especially in non-atopic individuals. Further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify how pollutants interact with different inflammatory pathways in asthma subtypes. In addition, atopic individuals may proactively avoid allergens, which could also reduce their exposure to air pollutants and contribute to the observed difference. However, atopic status in our study was based on discharge diagnoses. In administrative claims data, the non-atopic group may include patients with untested or unrecorded atopy, rather than confirmed non-atopic individuals. This may lead to non-differential misclassification.

We acknowledge that changes in healthcare-seeking behavior during the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced the observed number of asthma hospitalizations. However, interaction analyses in our study indicated that the short-term associations between air pollutants and asthma hospitalizations remained stable. The baseline shift in hospitalizations was adequately controlled by the temporal spline in the model.

The selection of df for the temporal spline is a key methodological choice in time-series studies. In this study, we selected 6 df per year based on AIC. This choice is consistent with common practice in environmental epidemiology, where 4–10 df per year are typically used for multi-year data [12, 18–20, 23, 31]. Sensitivity analyses further supported this selection. Effect estimates remained stable at df = 4, 5, and 7 but attenuated at higher df values (8–10), indicating potential over-adjustment. Excessively high df may dilute the true exposure effect. Therefore, 6 df per year provides a reasonable balance between controlling for temporal trends and preserving the estimated pollutant effect.

Our study has several limitations. First, due to data availability constraints, ozone exposure data were not included in this study. This prevents us from examining the independent effect of ozone on asthma hospitalizations or adjusting for ozone in multi-pollutant models. Second, hospitalization practices in China can be influenced by multiple non-clinical factors, including hospital bed availability, physician scheduling, and health insurance status. Third, while linear models provide an interpretable summary of the average association, we acknowledge that this approach may not fully capture the true exposure-response relationship for all pollutants. Moreover, although the average daily count of 14.18 asthma hospitalizations may raise concerns regarding statistical power, the narrow CIs observed in the main analysis suggest high precision of the estimates, and the long study period provided sufficient variability in exposure levels. However, we acknowledge that the smaller sample sizes in certain subgroups may have limited statistical power and could affect the interpretation of the subgroup findings. Finally, several limitations related to exposure assessment should be acknowledged. Assigning the same city-wide average exposure to individuals living in different areas could introduce Berkson error. Although more advanced spatial methods could theoretically provide more refined exposure estimates, their implementation in this study was constrained by incomplete address information with a substantial missing rate. Furthermore, the medical records only provided residential addresses (or household registration location), which may not reflect individuals’ actual activity spaces, particularly in a megacity where work and residence locations often differ. Additionally, the absence of individual time-activity patterns and indoor sources would preclude refined personal exposure assessment. Thus, some degree of exposure misclassification was unavoidable. These systemic limitations could potentially attenuate or bias the observed exposure-response relationships.

Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, no previous study in Beijing has simultaneously examined the short-term effects of multiple air pollutants on asthma hospitalizations with a comparable longitudinal duration and population-wide coverage as our investigation. Our findings provide novel insights into the effects of air pollutants on asthma exacerbations, offering empirical evidence to guide precision public health interventions and optimize air quality control policies.

Conclusion

This study found that short-term exposure to PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂ and CO was associated with an increased risk of acute asthma hospitalization, with distinct lag patterns. The adverse effects of air pollution were more pronounced in females, the elderly, non-atopic individuals, and during winter. Reducing exposure to PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, and CO by one IQR could prevent approximately 538, 1,289, 1,306, 3,427, and 1,041 hospitalizations for acute asthma exacerbations, respectively. This study provides novel insights into the effects of air pollutants on asthma exacerbations, identifies high-risk seasons and vulnerable populations, and offers a scientific basis for developing refined air quality management strategies and targeted health protection measures.
