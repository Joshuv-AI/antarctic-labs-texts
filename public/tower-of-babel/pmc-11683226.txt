Protocol for whole-mount preparation, clearing, and visualization of the adult zebrafish spinal cord structures
Authors: Mariana Rebocho da Costa, Ana Ribeiro, Raquel Quitéria, Dalila Neves-Silva, Leonor Saúde
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe vascular repair, a pivotal element of the spinal cord (SC) injury response, has been notably neglected in zebrafish research. Here, we detail the extraction and dissection of whole-mount zebrafish SCs and the optimized whole-mount immunofluorescence staining and clearing protocols for the visualization of SC structures, such as the vascular network. Additional downstream applications of these samples, from gelatin embedding to cryosectioning and tissue staining, can be done after clearing reversion and are also described in this work.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Ribeiro et al.1Subject areas: Cell Biology, Microscopy, Model Organisms, Neuroscience

Before you begin

Certification and permission to work with animals need to be granted by the competent entities and by the institution where the work will be performed. Both male and female AB wild-type strain zebrafish (Danio rerio), aged between 3 to 9 months old, were used in this protocol. It is necessary to train SC extractions and dissections in this model to ensure sample integrity.

All room temperature (RT) incubations used in this protocol were performed in a lab where the temperature varies between 22°C–25°C. Overnight incubations done in this protocol refer to time ranges between of 16–18 h. Whenever shaking or rotation is necessary, it is specifically indicated in the step.

Step-by-step method details

Sample preparation | spinal cord extraction & dissection

Timing: 4 h to 1 day

In developing zebrafish, the SC is easily visualized due to the animal’s transparency. Contrary to this, in the adult stage, the SC must be removed from the animal to ensure the best whole-mount processing and visualization. The protocol below details all the material and steps needed to ensure sample integrity, including SC extraction and dissection from the vertebral column (VC).

Note: All the extractions and dissections were performed by right-handed users. Tool positioning and sample orientation might need to be adjusted for left-handed people.

1.Sacrifice an adult zebrafish by anesthetic overdose (15 mM Tricaine) followed by decapitation.

Sacrifice an adult zebrafish by anesthetic overdose (15 mM Tricaine) followed by decapitation.

Note: The euthanasia method used is in accordance with the methods authorized by local authorities and may change depending on regional laws.

2.Under a stereoscope, expose VC of the fish (Figure 1A):a.Transfer the animal to a petri dish lid, with its rostral part to the right and right side upwards.Optional: For inexperienced users, this protocol can be done with the petri dish on top of a malleable cold pack (normally used to ship samples) to avoid sample deterioration.Note: For extraction, we place the animal right side up since, in SC injured animals, the surgical procedure is done with the animal in the opposite position (left side up). By placing it this way, we prevent unwanted damage to that SC area when opening the fish for extraction.b.Secure the animal by the tail, using the left forceps. Remove the scales of the area to be cut, in a caudal to rostral motion along the body of fish, using the 22.5° angle micro knife.c.With the knife, puncture the skin along the first yellow stripe of the animal.Note: The VC, in most animals, will be directly below this stripe. In animals with different pigmentation patterns, the top limit of the opercular opening can be used as the closest anatomical landmark.d.Using the scissors, cut along the punctured path, starting caudally.e.Remove all the muscle covering the VC by cutting along the column’s length, with the scissors parallel to the vertebra. The VC should be exposed as seen in Figure 1Ai.Note: The zebrafish SC has pigmented cells (melanophores) on the dorsal side (except in mutants with pigmentation defects, such as the casper line9), which facilitates its identification and orientation.

Sample clearing and visualization | Scale solutions

Timing: 4 days

Despite the small girth of the zebrafish SC, in comparison to rodent models, its opaqueness hinders the imaging of structures that reside within the tissue, like blood vessels. To visualize the full vascular network of the zebrafish SC, we adapted a clearing protocol using Scale solutions,2,3 which transparentizes the tissue in less than a week, while keeping its integrity. This protocol describes an additional optimization of the protocol used in Ribeiro et al.1 and can be started right away after SC dissection or following whole-mount tissue staining.

Note: For all incubations and washes, sample tubes samples were wrapped in aluminum foil while in the incubator, to protect them from light. Use of a roller during the incubations is optional and, in that case, the samples should be stored in a cylindrical opaque container while on the roller.

CRITICAL: Samples can be hidden inside the cap of the 1.5 mL tubes or can be aspirated while switching the solutions. Ensure that the samples are submerged and that tube caps are well closed before starting incubations.

Expected outcomes

Different methods have been developed and used in order to transparentize different kinds of organs and tissues, especially the Central Nervous System. The Scale protocols2,3 use aqueous-based solutions that are easy to make, with reagents that are relatively inexpensive and present in every lab. Generally, protocols using these solutions might take longer to achieve total sample clearing when compared with protocols that use organic solvent-based protocols, like DISCO variants,12,13 (1–2 weeks vs. days to 1 week, depending on the size of the sample). However, this aqueous-based approach is often less destructive to the tissue, maintaining its overall size and integrity, preserving transgenic fluorescent proteins and displaying high levels of biosafety.14 Additionally, its solutions are compatible with the light sheet systems commonly available for whole-mount imaging, whereas organic solvent-based protocols require specialized immersion objectives that most equipment do not have.

With this optimized version of our clearing protocol, in less than a week, it is possible to prepare fully transparent adult zebrafish SCs that can be imaged using light sheet fluorescence microscopy (Figure 2). This allows for acquisition of the full vascular network of the samples, using kdrl transgenic lines,4,5,6 as seen in Figure 2 and in Ribeiro et al.1 For vascular quantification,1,15 the method described is more informative than the use of histological sections, as it allows the acquisition of the whole vascular network of the sample and not just representative sections of the vasculature. Additionally, whole-mount processing is often less laborious in terms of sample preparation and acquisition time, making it the preferred method.

For transgenic lines where fluorophore signals are affected after 4% PFA fixation, we developed a whole-mount immunofluorescence staining protocol for transgenic signal amplification (Figure 3). In this case, intracellular EGFP from a Tg(mpeg1:EGFP) line,7 labeling microglia in uninjured SCs, is amplified after immunostaining with an anti-GFP antibody. In the unstained SC, less intense and fewer mpeg1+ cells are observed in the tissue and blood autofluorescence is also detected in the green channel, with comparable signal intensity to some mpeg1+ cells. After immunofluorescence staining, both mpeg1high and mpeg1low cells are observed in the tissue with an overall increase of the signal intensity, and blood autofluorescence signal is not detected in the green channel without an increase in image brightness. Additionally, we optimized a method for whole-mount nuclear staining using TO-PRO-3, which can be done independently (Video S1) or added in the final step of the immunofluorescent staining protocol.

We show that it is also possible to retrieve further data from these samples by reusing them in immunofluorescence staining protocols, after sample cryosectioning. This allows for the reduction of the number of animals, since the same samples can be used in more than one type of experiment. An example where this may be useful is for staining axonal and glial bridges/compartments (Figure 4), often evaluated in zebrafish SC regeneration projects.16,17 Both antibodies work and recognize the targeted proteins after clearing reversion, in both transversal and longitudinal sections. Despite this, protocol optimizations might be needed for the transversal sections to ensure a homogeneous distribution of the staining throughout the SC section (e.g., more permeabilization and/or increase antibody concentration), as observed in the longitudinal ones. We also show that native fluorophore signal expressed in blood vessels, in this case nuclear mCherry,6 survives the clearing protocol, its reversion and the cryosectioning protocol. This makes possible the visualization of these transgenic-labeled cells/structures in downstream applications.

Limitations

The method of injury used might influence the sample preparation steps.11,18 For SC compressions, the tissue can be removed from the vertebrae and kept intact as early as 1 day-post-injury (dpi). For transections, at earlier time points (< 7 dpi), it might not be possible to dissect the intact cord from the vertebral column. In this case, the sample must be maintained in the vertebrae for downstream applications and the muscle surrounding the sample/vertebrae removed thoroughly to not hinder the imaging process. The clearing protocol using Scale solutions has been tested in non-dissected sample in Ribeiro et al.1

This whole-mount protocol was only tested in a Light sheet microscopy system with a 20x clearing objective. No other conditions were tested and, thus, the protocol might have to be refined if there is an interest in using higher resolution objectives or to observe smaller structures.

Downstream applications and reusage of samples might be restricted to protein-based techniques. While immunostaining can be done in cryosections of cleared samples, like observed in Figure 4, the efficacy of common stains like H&E is affected after the clearing technique described in this protocol (Figure 5). This limitation can be observed by the altered staining of white matter (outside tissue), which has a darker color when compared to that of naïve samples.11 This might occur since the acid/base characteristics of the tissue/cells are changed. Other types of staining classically used in nervous tissue (FluoroMyelin/Luxol Fast, Prussian Blue, etc.) were not tested but we do not exclude the possibility that the sample clearing protocol might affect their outcome. Additionally, it is important to note that, although the two antibodies above mentioned work in cleared reverted samples, we cannot guarantee the same for other types of antibodies. As always, these tools should be working or should be optimized in naïve samples as positive controls, tested in reverted samples and then both compared to ensure that reverted samples recapitulate the original staining.

For downstream processing of the whole-mount images acquired, a strong processing unit must be available to do vascular quantification, like those done in Kugler et al. and Ribeiro et al., since image files acquired using light sheet microscopy can easily reach sizes of Gigabytes.1,15
