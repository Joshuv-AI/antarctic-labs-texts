Sex-specific cypermethrin-induced hippocampal neurotoxicity is associated with alterations in signaling molecules for antioxidant defense, membrane integrity, apoptosis, and GABAergic integrity
Authors: Aminu Imam, Maryam Busari, Christianah Oyegbola, Balogun Faith Moyinoluwa, Owoeye Faith, Ajayi P G Samuel, Ibrahim Bolaji Ibrahim, Ghazal T Atiyatul-Hanan, Ajibola M Iyiola, Omamuyovwi M Ijomone, Moyosore S Ajao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCypermethrin(CP), a widely used pesticide, is recognized for its neurotoxic potential. The hippocampus is particularly susceptible to such damage, and sex may influence susceptibility. We investigated sex-specific hippocampal neurotoxicity following short-term CP exposure in rats, focusing on oxidative stress, apoptosis, membrane integrity, and GABAergic interneuron function. Adult male and female Wistar rats (n = 8/group/sex) were orally exposed to corn oil (vehicle control), low (6.25 mg/kg), or high (12.5 mg/kg) doses of CP for 14 days. High-dose CP induced significant weight loss in both males (p = 0.0055) and females (p = 0.0025). CP caused dose-dependent and sex-dependent alterations: females showed greater vulnerability in Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and COX-2 suppression (treatment × sex interaction, p < 0.05), while males exhibited reduced Nrf2 expression, indicating impaired antioxidant response. Apoptotic markers revealed a distinct pattern; females showed robust Caspase-3 activation alongside increased BCL-2, whereas males displayed a net reduction in BCL-2. Furthermore, CP disrupted parvalbumin-positive GABAergic interneurons, with males showing greater susceptibility to this effect. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CP induces sex-dependent hippocampal neurotoxicity, with males more vulnerable to oxidative stress and neuronal loss, and females to apoptotic signaling and GABAergic disruption. Understanding such sex-specific vulnerabilities is crucial for risk assessment.Keywords: Cypermethrin, Oxidative stress, Apoptosis, GABAergic interneurons, Hippocampus

Introduction

Cypermethrin, a type II synthetic pyrethroid, is extensively used in agriculture and domestic settings due to its high insecticidal potency [1, 2]. However, evidence links cypermethrin exposure to mammalian neurotoxicity, raising public health concerns [3]. The brain, particularly regions critical for cognition like the hippocampus, appears highly vulnerable to pyrethroid-induced injury [4, 5].

The hippocampus is a key target for environmental neurotoxicants because of its high metabolic demand and central role in memory. Pesticide exposure, including to cypermethrin, can disrupt hippocampal integrity through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, apoptosis, and dysregulation of inhibitory neurotransmission [6–9]. Oxidative stress is a primary mechanism, where reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation leads to lipid peroxidation, membrane destabilization, and impaired function of key enzymes like Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase [10, 11]. Compromised antioxidant defense, involving the transcription factor Nrf2, exacerbates neuronal vulnerability. In parallel, apoptotic signaling cascades, including activation of caspase-3 and downregulation of BCL-2, contribute to neuronal degeneration [8, 10]. These processes converge in the hippocampus, where selective neuronal populations in the CA1 and CA3 subfields are particularly sensitive [8].

Alterations in γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) signaling are also highly relevant. Cypermethrin acts on voltage-gated sodium channels, leading to neuronal hyperexcitation, and can indirectly perturb GABAergic interneurons, tipping the balance between excitation and inhibition [12, 13]. This disruption of inhibitory control is critical for hippocampal function.

Notably, sex has emerged as a biological variable influencing susceptibility to neurotoxicants. Male and female brains differ in hormonal milieu, neuroinflammatory responses, and antioxidant capacity, which can shape vulnerability to oxidative injury and apoptosis [14]. Despite growing awareness, the sex-dependent effects of cypermethrin on hippocampal structure and function, particularly across multiple mechanistic pathways, remain poorly characterized. Addressing this gap is crucial for precise risk assessments.

The present study investigates hippocampal neurotoxicity following short-term exposure to cypermethrin in male and female rats. We focused on markers of oxidative stress (Nrf2), membrane integrity (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), apoptosis (Caspase-3, BCL-2), and GABAergic interneurons (parvalbumin). By combining biochemical assays with histology and immunohistochemistry, we aimed to delineate sex-specific differences in cypermethrin-induced hippocampal injury. We hypothesized that CP would induce hippocampal damage through interconnected pathways of oxidative stress, apoptosis, and GABAergic disruption, and that the vulnerability and response across these pathways would differ significantly between males and females.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethical considerations

Forty-eight (48) adult male and female Wistar rats (8–10 weeks old, 180–220 g) were obtained from the institutional animal facility. The animals were randomly divided into three groups for each sex (n = 8 rats/group/sex, total N = 48). G*Power 3.1 software (effect size f = 0.4, α = 0.05, power = 0.8) was used to determine the sample size [15]. The rats were housed under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle, 50–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to standard pellet diet and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the University of Ilorin, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval no. UIL/UERC/11/46KA072) and conformed to international guidelines for the ethical use of laboratory animals.

Experimental design and treatments

The animals were randomly divided into three groups for each sex (n = 8/group) as below:

· Control: received Corn oil (1 ml/kg.bw orally), the vehicle for CP.

· Low CP: received oral ingestion of CP (6.25 mg/kg.bw in corn oil) daily.

· High CP: received oral ingestion of CP (12.5 mg/kg.bw in corn oil) daily.

The doses (2.5% and 5% of the reported oral LD50 of 250 mg/kg in rats) were selected to represent sublethal, occupationally-relevant exposure levels based on previous neurotoxicity studies [16, 17]. The rats were treated for 14 consecutive days.

Daily body weight measurements

The body weight of the rats was measured and recorded before the commencement of acclimatization. After the administration began, their weights were measured and recorded daily throughout the duration of the experiment. Their brain weight was also measured using a digital scale and recorded immediately after removal.

Animal sacrifice and tissue collections

Twenty-four hours after the last exposure, rats were deeply anesthetized with intramuscular ketamine (100 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused. For biochemical assays, brains from five randomly selected rats per group were rapidly removed, and the hippocampi were dissected, weighed, and homogenized in sucrose solution. For histology/immunohistochemistry (IHC), three rats per group were perfused with normal saline followed by 10% buffered formalin. Brains were post-fixed in the same fixative.

Biochemical assays

Hippocampal tissues were homogenized and centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min.Supernatants were collected for analyses. All markers were quantified using commercial kits according to manufacturer protocols, with all assays performed in technical triplicates and normalized to total protein content (BCA assay). The following kits were used: GnRH (Sandwich ELISA, Kit #MBS269200, MyBioSource), Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (Colorimetric, Kit #E-BC-K022-M, Elabscience), PGE2 (EIA Kit #ADI-900-001, Enzo Life Sciences), COX-2 (ELISA, Kit #MBS7254655, MyBioSource).

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) staining

Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through descending ethanol grades, stained with hematoxylin (5 min), differentiated in 1% HCl-ethanol, blued in ammonia water, and counterstained with eosin (10 min). Sections were dehydrated in ascending ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with DPX for evaluation of general morphology.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), and blocked for endogenous peroxidase and non-specific protein. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Nrf2 (1:100, Abcam, ab137550), cleaved Caspase-3 (1:200, Cell Signaling, #9661), BCL2 (1:150, Santa Cruz, sc-7382), and parvalbumin (1:1000, Novus, NBP1-05162). Detection was performed using ImmPRESS™ HRP polymer reagents (Vector Labs), developed with DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Microscopy, photomicrography and image analysis

Sections were examined under an AmScope microscope.For each stain, approximately 165 images (27 per group) were captured at 400X magnification from 9 sections per group. All IHC processing and analysis were performed with the investigator blinded to the treatment groups. Images were analyzed using ImageJ software. For quantification, images were converted to grayscale, and the mean gray value (optical density) of immunoreactivity in the CA1 and CA3 regions was measured after thresholding to exclude background.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism® (Version 8.4.2) via two-way ANOVA (factors: Sex and Treatment) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Full statistical reporting includes F-values, degrees of freedom, and p-values for significant effects. Effect sizes (η²) are reported for key findings. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Changes in body weight after cypermethrin exposure

Analysis of body weight change (final-initial) by two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of treatment (F(2, 42) = 25.34, p < 0.0001, η² = 0.52) but no main effect of sex or interaction. Tukey’s test revealed significant weight loss in the 12.5 mg/kg group in both males (p = 0.0055) and females (p = 0.0025) compared to controls. A non-significant trend of weight loss was observed in the 6.25 mg/kg group (Fig. 1).

Effects of cypermethrin on biochemical biomarkers in the brain

Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity was significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2B). Two-way ANOVA showed significant main effects of treatment (F(2, 24) = 18.95, p < 0.001, η² = 0.59) and sex (F(1, 24) = 5.12, p < 0.05, η² = 0.10), and a significant treatment × sex interaction (F(2, 24) = 4.01, p < 0.05, η² = 0.13). Post-hoc analysis confirmed females showed greater reductions at both doses compared to male counterparts.

Asterisk (*) indicates significant (P < 0.05) weight loss. Bars are mean ± SD

COX-2 and PGE₂ levels were significantly decreased in CP groups(Fig. 2C, D), with two-way ANOVA indicating stronger suppression in females (significant main effect of sex for COX-2: F(1, 24) = 6.88, p < 0.05, η² = 0.19). GnRH levels were modestly reduced at high doses in both sexes but without a significant sex interaction (Fig. 2A).

Neuronal integrity in the hippocampal CA1 and CA3 regions

H&E-stained sections revealed normal cytoarchitecture in controls (Fig. 3). Low-dose CP induced mild neuronal shrinkage, while high-dose exposure caused marked neuronal loss, vacuolation, and pyknosis. Neuronal density counts confirmed significant reductions in high-dose groups (p < 0.01 vs. control), with males exhibiting greater loss than females in CA3 (p < 0.05; treatment × sex interaction F(2, 12) = 5.11, p < 0.05).

Expression of Nrf2 in the hippocampus

Nrf2 immunoreactivity was significantly altered in a sex-specific manner (Fig. 4). Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant treatment × sex interaction in CA1 (F(2, 12) = 7.45, p < 0.01). Post-hoc tests showed Nrf2 was significantly reduced in high-dose males (p < 0.01 vs. control) but was upregulated in females at both doses (p < 0.05 for high dose).

Expression of cleaved caspase-3 in the hippocampus

Caspase-3 expression was markedly elevated in female hippocampi at both doses, especially in CA1 (p < 0.001 vs. control; main effect of sex F(1, 12) = 28.34, p < 0.001, η² = 0.68), while males showed a modest increase only at the high dose (Fig. 5).

Expression of BCL-2 in the hippocampus

An examination of Bcl-2 immunoreactivity revealed a contrasting sex-specific anti-apoptotic response (Fig. 6). Females showed a significant upregulation of BCL-2 at the high dose (p < 0.05), whereas males exhibited a significant suppression of BCL-2 at both low and high doses (p < 0.05), evidenced by a significant treatment × sex interaction (F(2, 12) = 9.01, p < 0.01).

Parvalbumin-positive GABAergic interneurons in the hippocampus

CP exposure induced degenerative changes in PV + interneurons, including reduced cell size and retracted processes (Fig. 7). These effects were dose-dependent. Quantitative analysis showed a significant reduction in PV immunoreactivity, with a more pronounced effect in males at the high dose (p < 0.01; main effect of sex F(1, 12) = 8.90, p < 0.05, η² = 0.38).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that short-term CP exposure induces hippocampal neurotoxicity through distinct, sex-dependent pathological pathways. While both sexes were affected, males exhibited greater vulnerability to oxidative imbalance and overall neuronal loss, whereas females showed enhanced apoptotic signaling and a unique dual activation of pro- and anti-apoptotic mechanisms. Furthermore, CP disrupted GABAergic interneurons, with males appearing more susceptible to this effect. Our findings provide novel insight into sex-specific susceptibilities by directly comparing multiple injury pathways within the same experiment.

Consistent with systemic toxicity, CP reduced body weight [18]. The observed reduction in Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity confirms membrane destabilization, a recognized effect of pyrethroids [19]. The greater suppression in females suggests a stronger impairment of ionic homeostasis. A key finding was the sexually dimorphic Nrf2 response. Males exhibited a blunted Nrf2 response, indicating a failure to mount an adequate antioxidant defense against CP-induced oxidative stress. In contrast, females showed Nrf2 upregulation, potentially an adaptive, estrogen-mediated response to oxidative challenge [20, 21]. The lack of direct oxidative markers (e.g., MDA, SOD) is a limitation, but the Nrf2 data strongly imply a critical sex difference in managing oxidative insult.

The apoptotic cascade revealed a complex sex-specific pattern.Females displayed robust Caspase-3 activation, indicating a strong engagement of the apoptotic pathway. However, this was concurrent with a significant upregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2. This dual response may reflect heightened cellular stress engaging both death and survival pathways simultaneously, potentially modulated by estrogen’s known role in regulating mitochondrial apoptosis and promoting BCL-2 expression [22, 23]. Conversely, males showed a net pro-apoptotic environment with modest Caspase-3 increase and a significant downregulation of BCL-2, favoring cell death.

The observed decrease in COX-2 and PGE2 is noteworthy, as neurotoxicity is often associated with neuroinflammation and upregulation of these mediators. This suppression may indicate a specific toxicity to cells expressing COX-2 or a failure of compensatory neuroprotective signaling, as prostaglandins can also have cytoprotective functions [24]. This compromised prostaglandin synthesis may contribute to neuronal vulnerability and synaptic dysfunction, indirectly affecting GABAergic interneuron survival.

Our data reveal significant sex-dependent alterations in parvalbumin-positive GABAergic interneurons, crucial for hippocampal inhibitory tone [25]. The greater PV loss in males suggests a particular vulnerability that may be linked to their poorer antioxidant response and greater overall neuronal loss in CA3. The disruption of these interneurons could predispose the hippocampus to hyperexcitability and cognitive deficits [13, 26]. The link between the observed COX-2/PGE2 suppression and GABAergic disruption warrants further investigation, as inflammatory signaling can modulate neuronal and synaptic resilience.

In conclusion, CP induces hippocampal neurotoxicity via interconnected mechanisms of oxidative stress, apoptosis, and GABAergic disruption, with clear sex-specificity. Males are more vulnerable to oxidative damage and interneuron loss, while females show a heightened, albeit complex, apoptotic response. These findings underscore the critical importance of including sex as a biological variable in toxicological risk assessment and highlight the need for sex-specific neuroprotective strategies.

Conclusion

Cypermethrin exerts sex-specific hippocampal toxicity, predominantly through oxidative stress and interneuron disruption in males, and through pronounced apoptotic signaling in females. Recognition of these divergent pathways is essential for refining pesticide safety guidelines and developing targeted interventions.
