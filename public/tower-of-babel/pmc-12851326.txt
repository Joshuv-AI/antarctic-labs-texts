A qualitative study of what motivates, facilitates, and hinders climate-engaged healthcare trainees to advance healthcare sustainability
Authors: Owen Dan Luo, Sumara Stroshein, Yasmeen Razvi, Alanna Jane, Zahra Taboun, Laurence Robert, Omar Taboun, Nicole Simms
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThere is a critical need for low-carbon, environmentally-sustainable health systems in the climate crisis. Healthcare trainees can play a vital role in this process, and we have aimed to explore how they can be motivated and supported by faculties of medicine and health systems to pursue this ambition by conducting an exploratory, qualitative descriptive study of Canadian healthcare trainees engaged in healthcare sustainability initiatives.Materials and methodsTranscripts from individual in-depth interviews were analyzed to identify themes related to the actions that healthcare trainees can take to promote sustainable healthcare, as well as the motivators, barriers and facilitators of healthcare trainee engagement in sustainable healthcare.ResultsParticipants (N = 17) engaged in a spectrum of healthcare sustainability initiatives, including education, quality improvement and advocacy. They were motivated to advance healthcare sustainability through positive role models, the health impacts of climate change, observation of unsustainable healthcare practices, and a sense of social responsibility. Participants articulated that supportive networks, access to resources and funding, and having a growth mindset were facilitators to their engagement. In contrast, the lack of institutional prioritization of healthcare sustainability, limitations of the trainee role, challenges finding allies, and the perceived futility of their individual actions were characterized as barriers.DiscussionHealthcare trainees could support healthcare decarbonization efforts if they are adequately supported by their learning environments. The study's findings can guide educational innovations and health systems transformations to motivate and empower healthcare trainees to reduce the climate impact of healthcare throughout their careers.

Introduction

Climate change is impacting human health and exacerbating psychological distress through increasing the severity and frequency of extreme weather events including heatwaves, floods, and wildfires, prolonging allergy season, and expanding the range of infectious diseases [[1], [2], [3]]. While the healthcare sector grapples with the health impacts of climate stressors, it has itself been identified as a significant contributor to climate change. Healthcare was responsible for 4.6 % of global emissions in 2020 [4]. Thus, there is a critical need for healthcare to fulfill its mandate to do no harm by mitigating its environmental impacts. Healthcare providers have important roles to play in advancing this ambition by reducing the carbon footprint of their practices and advocating for environmentally-sustainable healthcare. The next generation of healthcare professionals, healthcare trainees, are increasingly interested in climate action; however, their training programs do not adequately prepare them to advance healthcare sustainability in their future roles [5]. Numerous surveys of healthcare trainees have demonstrated a vast disconnect between the increasing perceived importance of climate change and sustainability amongst healthcare trainees and the amount of teaching they receive about these topics [[6], [7], [8]].

Previous work has explored the facilitators and barriers to healthcare professional engagement in climate action [9,10]. However, there is little known regarding the trainee experience with healthcare sustainability advocacy and action. The Project Green Healthcare/Projet Vert la Santé (PGH/PVLS) program was launched by the Canadian Federation of Medical Students Health and Environment Adaptive Response Taskforce (CFMS HEART) in October 2020 to empower medical students to take leadership in healthcare sustainability. Since the launch of the program, PGH/PVLS has partnered 19 Canadian medical student teams with mentors from the Canadian Association of Physicians for the Environment (CAPE) and the Canadian Coalition of Green Health Care (CCGHC) to implement sustainability quality improvement projects in their local healthcare systems [11,12]. This qualitative study aims to interview PGH/PVLS participants to characterize the actions that healthcare trainees can take to promote healthcare sustainability, and the motivators, facilitators and barriers to their engagement in this area.

Methods

Aim and design

The aims of this study are to establish the actions that healthcare trainees can take to promote healthcare sustainability as well as the motivators, barriers and facilitators to trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability. The study was conducted with an exploratory, qualitative, descriptive design and involved individual in-depth interviews with climate-engaged healthcare trainees who have participated in the PGH/PVLS program. The theory of planned behaviour developed by Ajzen [13] is the theoretical framework underlying the study design and data analysis. The theory of planned behaviour suggests that human behaviour is determined by the degree to which the performance of the behaviour is valued (behavioural beliefs), the normative expectations of others (normative beliefs), and the perceived difficulty of the performance of the behaviour (control beliefs). This theoretical framework posits that favourable attitudes, subjective norms and perceived control increase a person's intention to perform a behaviour of interest, which in the case of this study is healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability. The study was reviewed and approved by the University of Toronto Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (approval number 43688).

Participants

Current and previous healthcare trainee participants of the PGH/PVLS program were eligible to participate in this study. Participants were recruited by distributing a recruitment email in May 2023 through the PGH/PVLS email list of all the 91 current and previous PGH/PVLS program participants. Of the 17 participants who responded to our recruitment emails (response rate of 18.7 %), none were lost to follow-up, and all 17 interviewed. Participants were scheduled for semi-structured virtual interviews on a rolling basis as they responded to our recruitment emails. All interviews were completed between June 2023 and February 2024.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured virtual interviews, with an interview guide guiding discussion that was adapted from a previous qualitative study exploring the experiences of Canadian climate-engaged physicians conducted by this study group (Table S1; [9]). The interview guide was piloted on a convenience sample of Canadian medical students (n = 4) and demonstrated the capacity to elicit rich conversation regarding the motivators, facilitators and barriers of healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability. Each participant was interviewed in their preferred language of English or French through a virtual video call of around 30–60 min in duration with one of the medical student or resident physician members of the research team (ODL, SS, YR, AJ, OT, ZT, LR).

Demographic data was collected verbally at the beginning of each interview. Field notes were taken during the interview to document the interviewer observations. Interviews were video-recorded and transcribed verbatim. All interviews conducted in French were translated in entirety into English prior to data analysis. No repeat interviews were conducted. To protect participant confidentiality, identifiable information was immediately de-identified and categorical data (such as participant city/setting of medical training) was coded for each interview.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was performed on the interview transcripts through a previously described six-phase approach, which includes becoming familiar with the data, generating codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report [14]. Seven members of the research team (ODL, SS, YR, AJ, OT, ZT, LR) with experience in qualitative data analysis independently annotated the interview transcripts and then, through consensus in pairs, codes were generated regarding the general ideas shared by the participants. The study authors did not use digital software for data analysis to maximize researcher interaction with the transcripts to support data contextualization and reflection. The research team then iteratively met to discuss the codes and arranged them into data-driven themes through induction. The emerging themes were then re-reviewed through deduction in the context of the theory of planned behaviour [13]. The motivators were analyzed through the lens of the behavioural beliefs, and the barriers and facilitators were analyzed through the lens of normative and control beliefs of the theoretical framework.

Motivations for healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability

Healthcare trainees were motivated to engage in healthcare sustainability by: positive role models, the health impacts of climate change, observation of unsustainable healthcare practices, and a sense of social responsibility.

In the first theme, participants cited positive role models as a motivating factor to engage in climate action. These positive role models included their parents and healthcare professionals who championed healthcare sustainability.“I think for me personally, a lot of it stems from my experiences growing up with my family and our values. Both my parents are very motivated to improve sustainability.”Participant 9“I was helping to coordinate a health themed radio show where we would invite speakers to talk about various health topics and we had a really engaging interviewee … who was advocating for environmental sustainability as a physician… it all started with that interview with the physician.”Participant 15

“I think for me personally, a lot of it stems from my experiences growing up with my family and our values. Both my parents are very motivated to improve sustainability.”

“I was helping to coordinate a health themed radio show where we would invite speakers to talk about various health topics and we had a really engaging interviewee … who was advocating for environmental sustainability as a physician… it all started with that interview with the physician.”

In the second theme, participants highlighted how observing the health effects of climate change motivated them to become engaged in climate action.“Climate action is really important, knowing that it's the biggest threat to health in the 21st century, as we always see [in our training].”Participant 9

“Climate action is really important, knowing that it's the biggest threat to health in the 21st century, as we always see [in our training].”

In the third theme, observing unsustainable healthcare practices during their training motivated them to advance healthcare sustainability.“I'd say my “lightbulb alarming moment” was when I entered clerkship and I saw truly how much waste was in the hospital … Once I was in the clinical setting, I realized “Oh my goodness, it's actually true, and now I'm seeing it in practice.”Participant 2

“I'd say my “lightbulb alarming moment” was when I entered clerkship and I saw truly how much waste was in the hospital … Once I was in the clinical setting, I realized “Oh my goodness, it's actually true, and now I'm seeing it in practice.”

In the fourth theme, healthcare trainees also identified that their motivation stemmed from a sense of professional responsibilities as future healthcare providers.“I think that as a [future] healthcare professional, it's really important to get involved because it's going to have a major impact on the health of our patients, and especially the most vulnerable populations. So for me, it's really important to take action on this.”Participant 13

“I think that as a [future] healthcare professional, it's really important to get involved because it's going to have a major impact on the health of our patients, and especially the most vulnerable populations. So for me, it's really important to take action on this.”

Facilitators of healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability

The participants identified supportive networks, access to resources and funding, and having a growth mindset as facilitators for healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability.

Facilitating normative beliefs

In the first theme, participants identified supportive networks of faculty and healthcare administrators as a facilitator to their engagement in healthcare sustainability.“Two faculty members through the college … have been very supportive. I think having those like-minded faculty members who are eager and receptive has made a really big difference in … the success of the projects that we've brought forward.”Participant 16“I think it's important to find the right allies. We had the [healthcare system's] sustainable development manager on our side … and he always supported the projects we'd done.”Participant 13

“Two faculty members through the college … have been very supportive. I think having those like-minded faculty members who are eager and receptive has made a really big difference in … the success of the projects that we've brought forward.”

“I think it's important to find the right allies. We had the [healthcare system's] sustainable development manager on our side … and he always supported the projects we'd done.”

Facilitating control beliefs

In the second theme, healthcare trainees identified resources and funding as a facilitator to their healthcare sustainability initiatives.“The fact that Project Green Healthcare existed and we were able to get funding… [was] very helpful. Our project wouldn't have happened if we didn't have Project Green Healthcare because we wouldn't have had the funding…”Participant 7

“The fact that Project Green Healthcare existed and we were able to get funding… [was] very helpful. Our project wouldn't have happened if we didn't have Project Green Healthcare because we wouldn't have had the funding…”

Lastly, in the third theme, participants underscore the importance of a growth mindset; that is, the notion that intellectual abilities and skills can be developed through time and effort [15]. Progress in climate action can often be slow, but participants persisted by establishing reasonable expectations for themselves and their projects.“I just think of doing … one small step at a time. And that when we have ideas, … to take small goals, to set goals that are achievable so that we always feel like we're making progress… If we set the goal of greening the entire healthcare system, that's not an achievable goal. But if we have small objectives, that's fine.”Participant 12

“I just think of doing … one small step at a time. And that when we have ideas, … to take small goals, to set goals that are achievable so that we always feel like we're making progress… If we set the goal of greening the entire healthcare system, that's not an achievable goal. But if we have small objectives, that's fine.”

Barriers to healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability

Lack of institutional prioritization, limitations of the trainee role, challenges finding allies, and perceived futility of their individual actions were identified as barriers that healthcare trainees experienced in their healthcare sustainability engagements.

Limiting normative beliefs

In the first theme, most participants discussed how the lack of institutional prioritization of healthcare sustainability was a barrier to their work and advocacy.“At the end of the day, sustainability is not the first thing that will come to mind within a healthcare organization. The first few things that come to mind: “is this medically effective,” “is this medically safe,” “is this financially suitable and cost effective for us?” and then down the list you get into sustainability aspects.”Participant 17

“At the end of the day, sustainability is not the first thing that will come to mind within a healthcare organization. The first few things that come to mind: “is this medically effective,” “is this medically safe,” “is this financially suitable and cost effective for us?” and then down the list you get into sustainability aspects.”

This lack of institutional prioritization demands individual initiative to reduce healthcare's environmental impacts; however, the participants emphasized that most healthcare professionals do not have the bandwidth nor knowledge to take action.“I think like people definitely agree for the most part when they hear “Oh yeah it's unfortunate there's so much waste” or “it would be nice if there wasn't as much waste” but I never have really encountered anyone yet who feels strongly enough to do something or change that.”Participant 9“I think it's that people don't know enough about it and don't know how to make actionable change.”Participant 14

“I think like people definitely agree for the most part when they hear “Oh yeah it's unfortunate there's so much waste” or “it would be nice if there wasn't as much waste” but I never have really encountered anyone yet who feels strongly enough to do something or change that.”

“I think it's that people don't know enough about it and don't know how to make actionable change.”

The participants cited that dedicated sustainability directors or ‘green teams’ with mandates to implement healthcare sustainability initiatives would alleviate the individual onus of action on healthcare providers, and facilitate connections between healthcare stakeholders to enact change.“The Sustainability Director would be really nice. Like just designated people in the hospital where their job is doing this kind of stuff so that they can have more information and they can dedicate more time to it rather than the healthcare professionals who are already busy and they might be interested, but they're not able to spend as much time on helping us out with the projects because that's not their main job.”Participant 5“I felt like with the hospital, things were just so slow moving and it was hard for us to breakthrough and figure out who were going to be the people [we could work with] because our hospitals don't have a green team, so it was hard for us to find the people who could help us.”Participant 7

“The Sustainability Director would be really nice. Like just designated people in the hospital where their job is doing this kind of stuff so that they can have more information and they can dedicate more time to it rather than the healthcare professionals who are already busy and they might be interested, but they're not able to spend as much time on helping us out with the projects because that's not their main job.”

“I felt like with the hospital, things were just so slow moving and it was hard for us to breakthrough and figure out who were going to be the people [we could work with] because our hospitals don't have a green team, so it was hard for us to find the people who could help us.”

Limiting control beliefs

Limitations of their trainee role, including a lack of credibility, fear of ruffling feathers, un- or underdeveloped connections, and competing academic demands, was cited by nearly all participants as a second barrier to their engagement in healthcare sustainability. They felt that their trainee role reduced their credibility and thereby the receptiveness of healthcare decision-makers to their ideas.“I think there may sometimes be a credibility issue in the sense that, since we're students, since we're not always in a position of power, maybe our voices aren't always heard, we don't always manage to get in front of the people who really have decision-making power. I'd say it's perhaps the lack of leverage that's the biggest barrier.”Participant 13

“I think there may sometimes be a credibility issue in the sense that, since we're students, since we're not always in a position of power, maybe our voices aren't always heard, we don't always manage to get in front of the people who really have decision-making power. I'd say it's perhaps the lack of leverage that's the biggest barrier.”

Participants suggested that their reliance on their physician preceptors for career advancement discouraged them from speaking freely about their perspectives on healthcare sustainability –particularly if their preceptors thought differently.“It did impact my ability because I'm a student and I'm trying to make a good impression on them, if their perspective [on sustainability] is something else, I can't force my perspective onto them. Given that I am trying to impress them for possible letters of reference or good feedback, because at the end of the day I am applying for residency and I am trying to get into their program.”Participant 17

“It did impact my ability because I'm a student and I'm trying to make a good impression on them, if their perspective [on sustainability] is something else, I can't force my perspective onto them. Given that I am trying to impress them for possible letters of reference or good feedback, because at the end of the day I am applying for residency and I am trying to get into their program.”

Participants also suggested challenges associated with their un- or under-developed connections with the healthcare systems. This disconnect was greatest for participants in their first few years of medical school, when most of their learning occurs outside of healthcare settings. However, senior trainees continue to experience challenges developing the necessary longitudinal relationships with local healthcare institutions to facilitate enduring change due to frequently changing sites and services through the clerkship rotation system.“We were trying to figure out where we were going to put the [recycling] bins specifically based on floor plans of the hospital … [However], before any of us had the chance to actually be working in the hospitals, it is hard to visualize.”Participant 9“I wish [that I was able to change a preceptor's practice to be more sustainable], but unfortunately as medical students, we don't have that longitudinal-like relationship with a lot of physicians, a lot of them I've worked with for a day and then never see again.”Participant 2

“We were trying to figure out where we were going to put the [recycling] bins specifically based on floor plans of the hospital … [However], before any of us had the chance to actually be working in the hospitals, it is hard to visualize.”

“I wish [that I was able to change a preceptor's practice to be more sustainable], but unfortunately as medical students, we don't have that longitudinal-like relationship with a lot of physicians, a lot of them I've worked with for a day and then never see again.”

Many participants also outlined the challenges of balancing their primary learning responsibilities as healthcare trainees with their work in healthcare sustainability.“I also felt like sometimes I don't have time … to continuously … work on the project. I had to … take breaks … [to] prepare for other stuff that was more pressing and felt more impactful on my career in the short term. Let's say if I focus on the [healthcare sustainability] project rather than studying for this exam, which one has a more severe impact on me right now?”Participant 5

“I also felt like sometimes I don't have time … to continuously … work on the project. I had to … take breaks … [to] prepare for other stuff that was more pressing and felt more impactful on my career in the short term. Let's say if I focus on the [healthcare sustainability] project rather than studying for this exam, which one has a more severe impact on me right now?”

Participants highlighted challenges finding allies as a third barrier to their sustainability projects. This included having trouble identifying physician mentors and the other key stakeholders required to develop and launch their healthcare sustainability projects.“Especially as a medical student, especially as a pre clerk, which is when you sort of have … the most time to do your advocacy work, it's really hard to find the staff who are interested in [sustainability], especially if your hospital doesn't have a green team.”Participant 7“And then, even as a clerkship student, when I speak to physicians, they themselves do not know who to go to create these changes… It's just a bit overwhelming as medical students to find the people who make these decisions, because a lot of times they're not people who are visible or contactable by medical students and physicians.”Participant 2

“Especially as a medical student, especially as a pre clerk, which is when you sort of have … the most time to do your advocacy work, it's really hard to find the staff who are interested in [sustainability], especially if your hospital doesn't have a green team.”

“And then, even as a clerkship student, when I speak to physicians, they themselves do not know who to go to create these changes… It's just a bit overwhelming as medical students to find the people who make these decisions, because a lot of times they're not people who are visible or contactable by medical students and physicians.”

A fourth theme raised by participants is the sensation of futility of their local healthcare sustainability project. Participants suggest that their individual actions feel negligible, particularly while juxtaposed with the inaction of world leaders on climate change and the significant proportion of healthcare emissions that are outside of the control of healthcare professionals.“A big issue that working in this field presents … is that ultimately, individual contributions matter some bit, but the bigger issues are the supply chain. And where our waste goes and how it's disposed of and all those things that are generally not actually within control of the physician on a daily basis.”Participant 14“If you look at the global picture, if things such as COP 24 and COP 28 can't make a change, you as an individual feel you can't really make a change. If people at that level can't do anything impactful, you wonder “what can we actually do?” and that de-incentivizes people to move forward.”Participant 17

Discussion

This qualitative study explored the experiences of Canadian healthcare trainees engaged in healthcare sustainability (see Fig. 1 for a graphical summary of the elicited themes). Study participants undertook diverse actions to promote healthcare sustainability, spanning education, quality improvement and advocacy including launching educational modules, waste reduction programs and green teams. The diversity of actions taken amongst participants suggests that healthcare trainees could support healthcare decarbonization efforts if they are empowered with the necessary supports.Fig. 1Graphical summary of the study's elicited themes characterizing the motivators, barriers and facilitators of healthcare trainee engagement in environmentally sustainable healthcare.Fig 1

The motivators of climate-engaged medical trainees have the potential to be harnessed to promote trainee interest in healthcare sustainability. Healthcare trainees were motivated to engage in healthcare sustainability by positive role models, the health impacts of climate change, observation of unsustainable healthcare practices, and social responsibility. These motivators aligned with the literature, with an interview study of climate-engaged Canadian physicians and survey studies of nursing students in Spain and Sweden finding that they were concerned by healthcare waste and the health impacts of climate change, and have a sense of social responsibility to contribute to sustainable healthcare [9,16,17]. This study also identified the important motivating factor of positive role models in their family and learning environments, which highlights the important ripple effects of nurturing environmental action in all spheres of society to motivate future generations to engage in climate action.

Participants highlighted supportive networks and the lack of institutional prioritization as facilitating and hindering normative beliefs, respectively. These findings suggest that the existing motivated pockets of motivated healthcare providers and administrators are insufficient to support healthcare trainees until healthcare systems begin to prioritize action on sustainability. This finding is in accordance with a survey that identified surgeons in the United Kingdom perceived a lack of leadership from health systems and health professional societies as the greatest barrier to healthcare sustainability [18]. Institutions can demonstrate leadership by embedding decarbonization into their strategic plans, and appointing designated sustainability leads and green teams to provide healthcare trainees a point of access into these complex systems, direct them to pertinent stakeholders, and support them in the implementation of their healthcare sustainability projects [19,20]. Institutional leadership from healthcare and academic health institutions is also critical to ensure that the burden of responsibility for climate action in healthcare is not disproportionately borne by trainees that have contributed least to healthcare emissions.

Healthcare trainees identified access to resources and funding as a facilitating control belief and limitations of their role and challenges finding allies as limiting control beliefs. These findings reflect the importance of programs like PGH/PVLS that support trainees to execute their healthcare sustainability projects by connecting them with allies, and providing resources and funding [12]. Partnership and funding are particularly important for healthcare trainees hoping to engage in healthcare sustainability initiatives, due to the power differential with their precepting staff, lack of longitudinal contact with the healthcare systems that they are training in, and the negative impacts of their training role on their credibility and influence. Challenges finding allies can be addressed by mainstreaming planetary health into continuing professional development to equip existing healthcare professionals with actionable knowledge about healthcare sustainability to support and encourage the growing interest amongst trainees [10,21]. It has been demonstrated that planetary health and healthcare sustainability education motivates healthcare professionals to engage in sustainability efforts and directs them towards higher-impact, evidence-based sustainability approaches [[22], [23], [24]]. In addition, many health professional educational programs include experiential learning opportunities in quality improvement. Expanding the scope of the existing quality improvement educational infrastructure would give interested trainees protected curricular time to engage in sustainability quality improvement under faculty supervision and facilitate trainee recognition of environmental sustainability as an important quality metric in healthcare, similar to efficiency and cost-savings [25]. A workshop on healthcare sustainability quality improvement piloted in the United Kingdom significantly improved participant knowledge about healthcare sustainability as well as their confidence in and likelihood of undertaking a sustainability quality improvement project [26].

In addition, having a growth mindset and a sensation of futility of their individual actions as the institutions in which they are learning continue to make little to no progress on healthcare sustainability were identified by participants as facilitating and hindering control beliefs, respectively. This provides additional impetus for trainees to be offered opportunities to join communities of practice of other engaged learners and healthcare professionals in this work. These communities could insulate trainees from feelings of loneliness, isolation, and eco-anxiety, giving them spaces to support each other's climate-related mental wellness and help each other navigate through shared feelings of frustration with slow progress.

In summary, this study identified factors that motivate, facilitate and hinder healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability, which can be harnessed to encourage and empower healthcare trainees to advance low-carbon healthcare throughout their careers. The participants highlighted that a just transition in healthcare would nurture healthcare trainee engagement in healthcare sustainability without expecting them to disproportionately shoulder the burden of climate action as trainees are disproportionately impacted by climate change and have contributed the least to it. First, academic health institutions need to provide more healthcare sustainability teaching in health professions education, and embed curricular opportunities for healthcare trainees to engage in quality improvement in healthcare sustainability. Second, healthcare institutions and systems need to establish time-limited decarbonization goals and involve trainee voices in the infrastructure required to achieve those ambitions including sustainability directors, green teams, and communities of practice. Third, healthcare professional societies should provide continuing professional development opportunities in healthcare sustainability for healthcare professionals to prepare them to supervise and support trainees to engage in efforts to decarbonize healthcare. Lastly, trainees should be incentivized to engage in healthcare sustainability efforts through funding and career advancement opportunities.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted in view of a few limitations. First, the study participants included medical students and resident physicians, and their experiences engaging with healthcare sustainability initiatives may not be generalizable to trainees from other healthcare disciplines. In addition, the small sample size, and voluntary participation with a low recruitment response rate of 18.7 % may introduce potential selection bias. Further research with a larger cohort of climate-engaged healthcare trainees is necessary to validate these results and explore pertinent regional and institutional differences in trainee experiences with healthcare sustainability engagement.
