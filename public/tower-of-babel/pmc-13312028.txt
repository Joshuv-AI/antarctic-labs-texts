Elastic functional Cox regression model with shape predictors
Authors: Yi Tang Chen, Sebastian Kurtek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhase variability in functional data can obscure prominent geometric features, e.g. local extrema, and deteriorate predictive performance of regression models with functional predictors. We propose an elastic functional Cox regression model (EFCRM) that captures the association between a time-to-event outcome and the shape of a functional predictor. As such, EFCRM accounts for phase variation in the functional predictor via registration, which is supervised by the survival outcome. The model is fit using an iterative algorithm that alternates between removal of phase variation and estimation of the regression coefficient function. To improve convergence of the estimation procedure, we use a small number of separate gradient-based updates at each iteration for the two steps. The model is assessed based on estimation accuracy and predictive performance using a simulation study. We additionally apply the proposed framework to two real-world datasets. Overall, we show that the EFCRM is robust to misspecification of the number and type of basis functions used to approximate the coefficient function and results in comparable or better estimation accuracy and predictive ability than competitors.Keywords: Functional Cox regression, phase variability, function registration, elastic framework2020 MATHEMATICS SUBJECT CLASSIFICATIONS: 62N02, 62R10

Introduction

Functional data analysis (FDA) is a growing research area in statistics where data are represented and analyzed as functions [10,13,17,27,30,31,36]. Biological signals, growth evolutions and monthly temperature measurements are common examples of functional data as they are measured continuously over a time interval or intermittently at discrete, but dense, time points. An important and unique characteristic of functional data is phase variability, or the variation in the timing of prominent function features, e.g. local extrema, in a sample of functions. For instance, growth velocities from birth to adulthood generally exhibit a common pattern of local extrema with maxima occurring during growth spurts. However, the timing of these growth spurts can vary across individuals. Ignoring such phase variation in the data can result in summary statistics that poorly describe these important growth events [36, Chapter 4]. Furthermore, in the linear scalar-on-function regression setting, Ahn et al. [2] show that not accounting for noise introduced by random phase changes in functional predictors generally deteriorates predictive performance of the model.

Let ℱ denote the space of absolutely continuous functions with domain [0, 1]. To account for phase variability, Srivastava and Klassen [36] consider metric-based registration, which is a process that aligns functions’ important geometric features via domain warping. A warping function γ is an element of Γ={γ:[0,1]→[0,1]∣γ(0)=0,γ(1)=1,0<γ˙<1} (γ˙ is the time derivative of γ), and domain warping of a function f∈ℱ via the value-preserving action of Γ on ℱ is given by composition: f∘γ. Registration separates overall variation in functional data into two distinct components: (i) phase or timing of prominent features, which is captured by optimal warping γ*, and (ii) amplitude or magnitude of the prominent features, which is captured by the registered function f∘γ*. In this framework, optimality of the warping function is determined via the extended Fisher-Rao (eFR) Riemannian metric, which has desirable mathematical properties for the registration problem, e.g. isometry under the value-preserving action of Γ on ℱ [36,37]. While direct computation under the eFR metric is quite complex, a convenient simplification is given by the square-root velocity function (SRVF) transformation defined as q=f˙/|f˙|(f˙ is the time derivative of f), which simplifies the eFR metric to the L2 metric. The value-preserving action of Γ on ℱ then results in the norm-preserving action of Γ on the space of SRVFs (a subset of L2) given by (q∘γ)γ˙, i.e. ‖(q∘γ)γ˙‖=‖q‖, where ‖⋅‖ is the L2 norm. This registration framework is commonly referred to as elastic. We note that literature on functional data registration is quite extensive. Other approaches are based on landmarks (e.g. [15,25]) or other objective functions such as correlation measures (e.g. [32,40]) or the L2 metric (e.g. [31,38], Chapter 5). In general, landmarks can be difficult to identify in the presence of noise. Further, other objective functions do not satisfy the desirable isometry property under the value-preserving warping action and require regularization to avoid the well-known pinching effect [27].

Ahn et al. [2] propose elastic functional regression models (EFRMs) that adopt the above-described elastic framework to account for noise due to phase variation in functional predictors by incorporating registration. The EFRM that uses the value-preserving action of Γ results in improved predictive performance when compared to functional regression models that do not account for phase variation in the predictors. They further show that predictive performance can be enhanced when the norm-preserving action of Γ is directly applied to functional predictors without the SRVF transformation. In this case, the model uses the predictors’ shape (rather than amplitude) information. Let ℱ now denote the space of square-integrable functions. Then, given scalar outcomes Yi∈R,i=1,…,n, and corresponding functional predictors fi∈ℱ,i=1,…,n, the shape-based regression model defined in [2] is given by Yi=α+supγi∈Γβ,fi∘γiγ‾i+ϵi,i=1,…,n, where ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ is the L2 inner product, α∈R is a scalar intercept, β∈ℱ is a coefficient function and ϵi are i.i.d. errors. This model is an extension of the standard scalar-on-function regression model given by Yi=α+β,fi+ϵi, which does not account for phase variation. Popular estimation methods for β in this setting include penalization and functional principal component analysis (FPCA), see e.g. [31, Chapter 15], [5,7,8,18,44].

However, none of the aforementioned regression models apply to time-to-event outcomes, which are subject to censoring. Thus, we propose an elastic functional Cox regression model (EFCRM), where the outcome is survival time subject to censoring, and predictors are shapes of square-integrable functions. The survival outcome is used to inform registration of given functional predictors to account for noise induced by phase variation. Our model builds on the EFRM [2] and the standard functional Cox proportional hazards model [16,26]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to account for phase variation in the functional Cox proportional hazards model.

Motivating applications

In [16], the functional Cox proportional hazards model was motivated by the Improving Care of Acute Lung Injury Patients (ICAP) study. The data collected as part of this study consisted of post-hospital and in-hospital survival outcomes as well as Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores that were measured daily for each patient during their stay in the intensive care unit (ICU). Since each patient’s ICU stay duration was different, the resulting SOFA score trajectories, i.e. functional predictors, had different domains, posing a challenge for modeling such data using the functional Cox proportional hazards model. To overcome this challenge, Gellar et al. [16] standardized the SOFA trajectory domains to [0, 1] for all patients prior to model fitting. However, this pre-processing step induces nuisance phase variation in the predictor functions that must be accounted for. This motivates the proposed EFCRM, which allows the survival outcome to supervise removal of nuisance phase variation in the functional predictors via registration. The second dataset considered in this manuscript, Medical Information Mart for Intensity Care (MIMIC), poses similar modeling challenges as the ICAP SOFA data. In particular, the functional predictors are Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) trajectories, which were measured repeatedly during patients’ ICU stays. More detailed descriptions of the ICAP SOFA and the MIMIC III DEMO GCS datasets are provided in Section 4.

The rest of this manuscript is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the elastic functional Cox regression model and discusses an estimation procedure for the coefficient function and nuisance parameters (warping functions). In Section 3, we report simulation results, while in Section 4, we report results of applying the proposed model to the ICAP SOFA and the MIMIC III DEMO GCS datasets. We close with a brief discussion in Section 5.

Gellar et al. [16] express the coefficient function β in model (1) as a linear combination of B-spline basis functions and associated (unknown) coefficients. The coefficients are then estimated by maximizing the penalized partial log-likelihood. The parameters include the coefficient vector c∈RJ, the parameter of interest, and the nuisance parameters γi∈Γ,i=1,…,n. We propose an alternating iterative algorithm to estimate these parameters. Briefly, at each iteration, we (i) update each yi via a small number of gradient descent steps [36, Chapter 5] given the current estimate of the coefficient function βˆ(s)=∑j=1Jcˆjbj(s), and (ii) update c using a single gradient step defined using the partial log-likelihood (4) given current estimates of the nuisance parameters γˆi. Thus, taking small steps at each iteration ensures better convergence of the overall procedure. We use ck and γik to denote the estimates of c and γi at the kth iteration, respectively. Also, we use |⋅| and |⋅|2 to denote the absolute value and the Euclidean norm in RJ, respectively. The detailed algorithm is presented as Algorithm 1.

The algorithm parameters ϵ3,δ1,δ2,m1,m2 help ensure convergence of estimates of both, the nuisance parameters yi and the parameter of interest c. In the beginning, the algorithm updates each γi using a few small gradient descent steps m1 of size δ1 (line 2) to allow more focus on estimation of c via an update with a small step size δ2 (line 3). Then, if the change in the estimate of c is smaller than ϵ3, more focus is placed on removing phase variability in the functional predictors using more steps m>m1 (line 8). Meanwhile, if estimation of the nuisance parameters results in unstable updates of c, indicated by a change in the estimate of c larger than ϵ3, the number of steps m for upadating γi is reduced (line 10). Joint convergence of estimates of both γi,1=1,…,n, and c is signaled by a small change in the partial log-likelihood and maximum focus on removing phase variation in the functional predictors, i.e. when m=m2. Uncertainty in the final estimate of β in model (2) can be assessed via a pointwise 95% confidence interval generated using the bootstrap.

Standard approaches for estimating β under the baseline model (1) include penalization [16], as discussed eariler, and FPCA [26]. While penalization and FPCA can be incorporated in Algorithm 1, we leave this as future work. We do note, however, that removal of nuisance phase variation can be viewed as a form of regularization in the EFCRM. In subsequent sections, we consider a priori specifications of the basis B for the proposed model. Simulations in Section 3 explore the effects of using different basis systems and different numbers of basis functions on estimation accuracy and predictive performance of the EFCRM. In particular, we show that Algorithm 1 results in stable estimates of β irrespective of such choices.

Parameter estimation for the EFCRM

Simulation

We conduct a simulation study to assess the proposed estimation procedure (Algorithm 1) and predictive performance of the EFCRM. We compare predictive performance to two competing approaches: (i) the baseline model (1) that does not account for nuisance phase variability using the estimation procedure proposed by Gellar et al. [16], and (ii) a two-step procedure that first removes phase variability by pre-aligning the functional predictors (without use of the survival outcome) and then fits model (1) using the aligned predictors, where the coefficient function is again estimated using the penalized method of [16]. For (i), estimation is performed using the pfr() function in the R package refund [42]. For (ii), estimation is carried out in the same manner, but with pre-aligned predictors f~i, obtained using time_warping() in the R package fdasrvf [39].

The final dataset thereby contains functional predictors fi, observation times T~i=minTi,Ci, and event indicators Di=ITi≤Ci, for i=1,…,n.

For each combination of sample size (n=20,50,100,500) and censoring rate (10%, 50%), we generate 100 simulated datasets. For each simulated dataset, we fit the proposed model (3) using a truncated basis system B=b1(s),…,bJ(s) to estimate β. We consider (i) J=13 cubic B-splines with nine equally spaced interior knots, (ii) J=6 or J=12 Fourier basis functions (without the constant function b0(s)=1), and (iii) J=8 or J=14 modified Fourier basis functions. For J=8, the modified Fourier basis is constructed via Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization of J=6 Fourier basis functions with the additional basis functions b7(s)=s and b8(s)=1-s; for J=14, the modified Fourier basis is obtained similarly. We consider the modified Fourier basis because it allows for different endpoints in the estimated coefficient function, which is not the case for the standard Fourier basis.

Model assessment

To assess estimation accuracy, we calculate the relative root squared error (RRSE) defined as RRSE=βˆ-β0β0. To assess predictive performance, we calculate Harrell’s concordance index or C-index [19,20] given by C=∑i,jIηi<ηjIT~i>T~jDj∑i,jIT~i>T~jDj, where ηi is the estimated linear predictor or risk score. The C-index is used to estimate the concordance probability Prηi<ηj∣Ti>Tj, with larger C-indices indicating better predictive performance. Squared error and Harrel’s C-index are standard metrics to assess estimation accuracy and predictive performance, respectively, in the context of functional Cox regression models; they were recently used for this purpose by Cui et al. [11] and Sezer and Beyaztaş [34].

For each simulated dataset, we calculate the RRSE for βˆ computed using the entire dataset. We then randomly select 80% of the data as the training set, and the remaining 20% serves as the test set. We perform estimation using the training set and compute the C-index using the test set. We report the means and standard deviations of the RRSEs and test C-indices over the 100 simulated datasets.

Applications to real data

We fit the proposed EFCRM to the two motivating datasets briefly described in Section 1.1: (i) ICAP SOFA and (ii) MIMIC III DEMO GCS. We first apply slight pre-processing to given data to obtain functional predictors; this involves smoothing and rescaling of the domain to [0, 1]. We do not provide details of pre-processing here for brevity. For each dataset, we compare predictive performance of the proposed model to the baseline and pre-aligned models via the test C-index.

Data description

ICAP SOFA

We use part of the data from the Improving Care of Acute Lung Injury Patients (ICAP) study [28], which is available in the R package refund [42]. ICAP is a prospective cohort study that aims to investigate the long-term outcomes of patients who suffer from acute lung injury/acute respiratory distress syndrome (ALI/ARDS), which is a severe lung condition characterized by inflammation of the lung tissue. In the ICAP study, the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, which measures a patient’s overall organ function status, was collected daily while in the ICU. The SOFA score is a composite score that ranges from 0 to 24, accumulating six physiological component subscores (each measured on a scale of 0–4 with higher scores indicating poorer organ function): respiratory, cardiovascular, coagulation, liver, renal and neurological [41].

Past research showed that SOFA provides valuable prognostic information for in-hospital mortality [12,14,24,29]. Yet, most studies used SOFA at a single time point, e.g. 48 h after hospital admission, or summary statistics, e.g. mean or maximum SOFA. One exception is [43] who used dynamic time warping (DTW) for pairwise registration of SOFA trajectories. Subsequently, they used hierarchical clustering to identify four clusters with distinct SOFA patterns: delayed improving, rapidly improving, delayed worsening and rapidly worsening. Finally, they discovered that survival profiles differ significantly across the four clusters with distinct SOFA patterns. The findings in [43] suggest considerable nuisance phase variation in the SOFA trajectories. Further, as mentioned earlier, since different patients can have different lengths of stay in the ICU, standardization of the domains of SOFA trajectories to a common domain of [0, 1] induces noise phase variation. Thus, our aim is to understand whether such nuisance phase variation can be effectively accounted for using the EFCRM. For this data, the standardized domain [0, 1] represents the proportion of the duration of stay in the ICU, and n=491.

MIMIC III DEMO GCS

Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) is a large database that contains deidentified health-related data from over 40,000 patients who were admitted to the ICU of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2001 and 2012 [23]. The MIMIC III DEMO data is limited to 100 patients randomly selected from the subset of patients in the dataset who eventually died either during the ICU stay or after hospital discharge [22]. In our study, we focus on patients from this smaller database who had survived up to the hospital discharge, and investigate whether the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) that was repeatedly measured during the patients’ ICU stays is predictive of post-hospital mortality. GCS is used to measure the level of consciousness, ranging from 3 to 15 (higher score indicates higher consciousness), and is aggregated from three component scores that assess aspects of responsiveness: eye-opening, and motor and verbal responses.

Several past studies have shown that GCS measured at a single time point, e.g. 24 h after ICU admission, is a significant prognostic factor for in-hospital mortality [1,3,33]. However, we are not aware of any work that examined the predictive ability of GCS trajectories during an ICU stay on post-hospital mortality. As in the ICAP SOFA data, standardization of the domains of GCS trajectories to a common domain of [0, 1] induces noise phase variation. For this data, the standardized domain [0, 1] represents the proportion of the duration of stay in the ICU, and n=79.

Analysis plan

To assess predictive performance, we fit the proposed EFCRM, and baseline and pre-aligned models, to the two datasets; for the EFCRM, we consider 13 B-spline basis functions, and 8, 14 or 20 modified Fourier basis functions for estimation. As in the simulation, we randomly select 80% of the data as the training set and use the remaining 20% as the test set. Models are fit using the training set and the C-index is evaluated using the test set, i.e. we estimate the coefficient function using the training set only. We repeat this procedure 100 times using different splits of the data into training and test sets. Predictive performance is evaluated using the mean and standard deviation of 100 test C-indices computed using different data splits.

We additionally estimate the coefficient function, using the three aforementioned models and number/type of basis functions for the EFCRM, based on the complete datasets. For the baseline and pre-aligned models, we construct pointwise 95% confidence intervals for the coefficient function using the approach of [16]. For the EFCRM, we construct pointwise 95% confidence intervals based on 100 bootstrap resamples of the data.

For both datasets, it appears that using the shapes of functional predictors based on the proposed approach outperforms the pre-aligned model in terms of predictive ability, regardless of the basis functions chosen a priori for estimation. This suggests that the hazard of death can be better predicted when the removal of nuisance phase variability is informed by the outcome variable. Predictive performance of the baseline model is the same for the ICAP SOFA dataset and slightly worse for the MIMIC III DEMO GCS dataset. These finding suggest that phase variation in the functional predictors is truly nuisance for these two data applications. However, further investigation needs to be carried out to confirm this conjecture. In future work, we will develop a model that can jointly estimate the effects of the shape and phase components of the functional predictors, which will allow us to assess predictive ability of these two distinct features.

Figure 4 displays the estimated coefficient functions using the baseline and pre-aligned models, and the EFCRM (using 20 modified Fourier basis functions), with their respective pointwise 95% confidence intervals (top: ICAP SOFA; bottom: MIMIC III DEMO GCS). Based on the ICAP SOFA dataset, the estimated coefficient function resulting from the proposed model (panel (c)) conveys prognostic importance of the shape of SOFA trajectories on in-hospital mortality. The estimated coefficient function is (approximately) an increasing function that begins with a negative value and crosses zero at around s=0.3 (corresponding to a proportion of stay in the ICU of 0.3). The corresponding pointwise 95% confidence interval does not cover the zero reference line after around s=0.6. This suggests that on average, toward the end of the ICU stay, patients with decreasing SOFA trajectories implying improving organ function are less likely to die compared to patients with increasing SOFA trajectories implying worsening organ function. A similar interpretation can be concluded from the baseline model. However, the coefficient function from the baseline model appears overly simple, i.e. a linear function, due to the underlying penalization in the estimation approach. The interpretation of the estimated coefficient function is less clear from the pre-aligned model due to periodicity. For the MIMIC DEMO GCS dataset, since pointwise 95% confidence intervals mostly cover the zero reference line across the entire domain for all three models, we conclude that GCS trajectories are not significantly predictive of the post-hospital mortality rate; this is consistent with the test C-indices in Table 5.

Discussion and future work

The proposed EFCRM allows for registration of functional predictors to be informed by the survival outcome to extract shape information. The registration process is fully integrated into the modeling framework and plays a considerable role in estimation of the regression coefficient function. As a consequence, phase variability which is treated as nuisance is removed, and the shape of the functional predictor is used for predicting the hazard of the event. We evaluated the proposed model using simulations and two real data examples. In simulations, we found that the EFCRM outperforms competitors in terms of estimation accuracy and predictive performance. In real data examples, predictive performance of the EFCRM was comparable to that of competitors. Empirically, we found that registration of functional predictors, supervised by the survival outcome, guards against overfitting during estimation of the underlying coefficient function. As such, one can view removal of nuisance phase variation as a form of automatic regularization.

We have identified multiple avenues for future research. First, we will incorporate penalization or FPCA into the estimation procedure for the proposed model to further improve estimation accuracy and predictive performance. Second, we will develop methods for uncertainty quantification for all parameters in the model, which is complex for the EFCRM due to the role that the nuisance parameters γi play in the estimation procedure. To remedy this, we will use a Bayesian specification of the model to estimate both β and γi, and assess their uncertainty via the posterior distribution. Third, we will explore extensions of this model to settings where the functional predictors are (size-and-shapes of) higher-dimensional curves and surfaces. In this context, the main challenge is computational in nature due to more complex nuisance variation in the predictors. For curves, we will leverage tools from elastic shape analysis [35,36], while for surfaces we will rely on elastic methods introduced in [21]. Finally, in some data scenarios, phase variability can be an important predictor of the outcome. Nonetheless, fitting a functional Cox regression model to the raw predictors, i.e. without accounting for the fact that phase variation is present, would result in suboptimal estimation and predictive performance since the predictor shape and phase variations are confounded. In such a case, a better approach is to formulate a model that incorporates shape and phase predictors separately. However, care must be taken since the phase predictors are constrained (they are monotone functions mapping the domain to itself).
