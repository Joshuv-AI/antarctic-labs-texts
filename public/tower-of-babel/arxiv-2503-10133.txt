Towards Manufacturing-Friendly Shapes in Discrete Topology Optimization
Vojtech Neuman, Miloslav Capek, Lukas Jelinek, Jan Spacil

Abstract
This paper addresses shape irregularity issues in discrete topology optimization algorithms where the design is created through the automated distribution of material within the design region. Graph theory is employed to derive appropriate regularity measures for any discrete optimization algorithm. Shape regularity is quantified by a set of scalar parameters ready to evaluate design choices in the form of Pareto-frontiers. Developed parameters address information regarding material usage, problematic distribution, and features that complicate manufacturing. The theory is verified by several examples demonstrating the treatment of isolated islands of materials, point connections between material segments, or material homogeneity.

III
Topology Representation by Graph Theory

This section introduces the fundamental theoretical background through an example of the design region discretized into polygons.

The discretization elements constitute a set of vertices
\mathscr{V}
. A polygon edge shared by two adjacent polygons
v_{n},v_{m}\in\mathscr{V}
forms graph edge
e_{j}\in\mathscr{E}
, and both sets form graph
G=\left(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E}\right)
. Figure
7
depicts the situation for a rectangle discretized into arbitrary polygons and visualizes the transition process from the original continuous object to graph
G
.

Fig. 7:
Graph representation of the optimization region. (a) Arbitrary region
\varOmega
to be discretized, (b) discretization of
\varOmega
into a set of arbitrary elements, (c) highlighted connections between important sets, (d) graph representation of
\varOmega
.

Material manipulation in discrete topology optimization directly translates into enabling or disabling graph vertices and their corresponding edges. Encoding the presence of discretization elements in binary values defines a logical vector

t_{m}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\,1&\text{if the $m$-th vertex is present},\\
\displaystyle\,0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4)

Similarly, the presence of graph edges between vertices establishes a logical vector

g_{n}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\,1&\text{if the $n$-th edge is present},\\
\displaystyle\,0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5)

Vectors
\mathbf{t}
and
\mathbf{g}
are not independent. The presence of vertices and edges is related to the incidence matrix
\mathbf{M}
, see Appendix
B
, and the relation is defined as

\mathbf{t}={\mathcal{B}}\left\{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{g}\right\},

(6)

with the element-wise Boolean rounding operator

{\mathcal{B}}\left\{x\right\}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\,0&x=0,\\
\displaystyle\,1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

which maintains all values of both vectors binary

1

1

Since matrix
\mathbf{M}
is rank-deficient, several vectors
\mathbf{g}
can produce the same vector
\mathbf{t}
.
. To further illustrate the meaning of the incidence matrix, Fig.

Visualization of graph vertices

\mathbf{M}

Vectors
\mathbf{g}
and
\mathbf{t}

IV
Regularity Parameters

The problems with manufacturability described in Section
II
are quantified by a set of so-called regularity parameters
r
.

The first shape parameter refers to the isolated islands and is quantified by relative area
r_{\mathrm{area}}
. Adding this parameter to the goal function forces the optimizer to omit parts that do not significantly contribute to the optimized metric.

The treatment of slots and point connections is based on identifying them in the topology and minimizing their number. This is quantified by the parameters
r_{\mathrm{slot}}
, respectively
r_{\mathrm{point}}
, which utilize Boolean operations for detecting these defects and acquiring their count.

Lastly, the frequent changes in design variable are addressed by the homogeneity parameter
r_{\mathrm{hom}}
. Homogeneity is evaluated by checking the neighborhood of each element and calculating the average state of its adjacent optimization variables.

All four parameters
r_{\mathrm{area}}
,
r_{\mathrm{slot}}
,
r_{\mathrm{point}}
, and
r_{\mathrm{hom}}
are normalized to a span interval
\left[0,1\right]
and increase when the phenomenon they are describing increases. For example, a high value of
r_{\mathrm{area}}
represents a structure primarily filled with material.

The formal definitions of the aforementioned parameters are detailed in the following subsections. In the rest of the article, the discretization elements are triangles, and
RWG
basis functions serve as optimization variables. However, the theory can be applied to an arbitrary scheme where design optimization can be represented as a graph.

IV-A

Conducting Area

The relative area is defined as

r_{\mathrm{area}}(\mathbf{g})=\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathcal{B}}\left\{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{g}\right\}}{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathcal{B}}\left\{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{g}_{0}\right\}}=\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{t}}{\left\|\mathbf{a}\right\|_{1}},

(8)

where
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times 1}
is a vector containing the surface area of each discretization element (triangle),
\mathbf{g}_{0}
is the vector of the design region fully spanned by material, and the denominator is the total area of the optimization region. A similar idea has already been adopted in
[
38
]
for a regular grid.

IV-B

Infinitesimal Slots

The infinitesimal slot arises when two adjacent triangles are enabled while the basis function connecting them is disabled. This leads to a definition of the slot parameter

r_{\mathrm{slot}}\left(\mathbf{g}\right)=\frac{\left\|\mathbf{g}\oplus\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}\right\|_{1}}{B-\left\lfloor\dfrac{T}{2}\right\rfloor},

(9)

where
\oplus
represents the XOR operation.

Relation (
9
) utilizes the properties of graph matrices by calculating the modified gene

\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}=\neg{\mathcal{B}}\left\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}}{\mathcal{B}}\left\{\mathbf{M}\mathbf{g}\right\}-2\mathbf{g}_{0}\right\},

(10)

where all infinitesimal slots are removed. The expression proceeds by first converting vector
\mathbf{g}
to vector
\mathbf{t}
by means of relation (
6
), resulting in active triangles. Multiplying the triangle gene with the transposed matrix
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}}
returns a vector containing information on how many active triangles are adjacent to a given basis function. As enabled basis functions must always consist of two active triangles, subtracting the expression
2\mathbf{g}_{0}
and subsequently applying the Boolean rounding operator returns the logically inverted gene with slots filled. The final application of Boolean negation
\neg
inverts all binary values to the correct level. Comparing the original and reconstructed vectors leads to the conclusion that

\text{number of slots}=\left\|\mathbf{g}\oplus\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}\right\|_{1}.

(11)

The vector inside the norm is a gene that contains only enabled basis functions located exactly at the positions of the slots.

Normalization in (
9
) is provided by the maximum number of slots in a given topology. This can be done with graph matching
[
65
]
, see Appendix
C
. Perfect matching
[
65
]
has a cardinality (
i.e.
, a number of active edges)

\left|\mathscr{M}\right|=\frac{T}{2}.

(12)

Using this knowledge, the upper bound on the number of slots can be estimated as

\max_{\mathbf{g}}{\left\|\mathbf{g}\oplus\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}\right\|_{1}}\approx B-\left\lfloor\frac{T}{2}\right\rfloor,

(13)

where the floor operator solves the nonexistence of a perfect matching for an odd number of triangles. Putting both parts together constitutes the slot metric (
9
).

IV-C

Point Connections

A point connection exists when two triangles share only one common point
[
40
]
, and
MoM
(
MoM
), based on
RWG
basis functions
[
60
]
, does not consider these two triangles to be conductively connected. The occurrence of point connections is quantified by

r_{\mathrm{point}}\left(\mathbf{g}\right)=\frac{\left\|{\mathcal{H}}\left\{\mathbf{p}-2\mathbf{p}_{0}\right\}\right\|_{1}}{N-\left\|{\mathcal{H}}\left\{-\mathbf{p}\right\}\right\|_{1}}.

(14)

Evaluation of (
14
) requires vector
\mathbf{p}
defined as

\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{nt}}\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{nb}}\widetilde{\mathbf{g}},

(15)

where
\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}
is a gene defined by relation (
10
), and where
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{nt}}
and
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{nb}}
are additional incidence matrices defined in Appendix
B
, which relate the triangles and basis functions to their incident nodes. Notice that vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{g}}
can be reused when both (
9
) and (
14
) are evaluated.

The meaning of vector
\mathbf{p}
is shown in Fig.
10
for the
n
-th point. The number
p_{n}
is evaluated by counting the enabled incident triangles and subtracting the enabled incident basis functions. It is always greater than or equal to zero. Cases (a) and (c) are undesired, and the corresponding value
p_{n}
is greater than one.

Fig. 10:
Evaluation of the
n
-th component of point connection vector
\mathbf{p}
in different situations. The investigated node lies in the center. Number
p_{n}
is equal to the number of red basis functions subtracted from the yellow triangles.

Using this knowledge, it is possible to calculate the number of problematic points (numerator of (
14
))

\text{number of problematic nodes}=\left\|{\mathcal{H}}\left\{\mathbf{p}-2\mathbf{p}_{0}\right\}\right\|_{1},

(16)

where
\mathbf{p}_{0}
is a vector full of ones (
p_{n}=1,\forall n
) and
{\mathcal{H}}\left\{-\right\}
is an element-wise Heaviside operator

{\mathcal{H}}\left\{x\right\}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1&x\geq 0,\\
\displaystyle 0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(17)

The point connection metric could be normalized to the total number of nodes
N
, however, it is convenient to subtract all nodes for which
p_{n}=0
. Such nodes are fully encircled by metal or a vacuum. The number of such nodes is equal to
\left\|{\mathcal{H}}\left\{-\mathbf{p}\right\}\right\|_{1}
. This reduced number is used as the denominator in (
14
).

IV-D

Metal Distribution

The homogeneity of the design is quantified using

r_{\mathrm{hom}}\left(\mathbf{g}\right)=\frac{B-\left\|2\mathbf{H}\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{0}\right\|_{1}}{B-\sum\limits_{n}\min\limits_{\mathbf{g}}\left|2\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{g}-1\right|},

(18)

where
\mathbf{H}
is the homogeneity matrix defined in Appendix
B
and
\mathbf{g}_{0}
is a basis function vector corresponding to a fully-filled design region (
g_{n}=1,\forall n
). Figure
11
shows the value of (
18
) for two different topologies. It is observed that rapid changes in design variables lead to high values of
r_{\mathrm{hom}}
.

Fig. 11:

18
) for two designs.

A key component of parameter (
18
) is the investigation of the neighborhood of each element, which is provided by the value

\left\|2\mathbf{H}\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{0}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{n}\left|2\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{g}-1\right|,

(19)

where
\mathbf{H}_{n}
is the
n
-th row of the homogeneity matrix. The effect of relation (
19
) is depicted for an
n
-th summation member in Fig.
12
, showing several different material distributions in elements neighboring the
n
-th basis function. Figure
12
(a) and Fig.
12
(d) show the monotonous distribution, and the value of expression (
19
) is maximal. In contrast, Fig.
12
(b) and Fig.
12
(c) show situations where the neighborhood of the
n
-th element is not homogeneous.

The normalization of the homogeneity parameter is obtained using

\min_{\mathbf{g}}\left\|2\mathbf{H}\mathbf{g}-\mathbf{g}_{0}\right\|_{1}\leq\sum_{n}\min_{\mathbf{g}}\left|2\mathbf{H}_{n}\mathbf{g}-1\right|,

(20)

where the right side is significantly easier to evaluate.

Properties and Applications

VI
Conclusion

This article introduces a novel regularity concept for discrete topology optimization of radiating structures. The regularity parameters mitigate known manufacturing issues, such as isolated islands, point connections, or checkerboard problems. The discretized optimization region is expressed as a graph, which leads to a faster evaluation of graph-based sparse matrices. These matrices can be used to constrain structural artifacts during the optimization process. The evaluation of regularity parameters is computationally cheap and easy to incorporate into arbitrary discrete optimization schemes. The examples illustrate the trade-offs that arise from incorporating regularity parameters into the optimization. It is observed that easier-to-manufacture designs tend to exhibit lower physical performance.

The presented approach can be adapted to introduce additional graph-based parameters that assist with inverse antenna design, incorporating prescribed structural or topological features. For example, detecting loops, finding the longest current path within the structure, or minimizing electrical size. The theory can also be expanded for three-dimensional discretization elements.

Appendix B
Graph Matrices

Considering graph
G=(\mathscr{V},\mathscr{E})
with
\mathscr{V}
denoting the set of nodes and
\mathscr{E}
standing for the edges, several matrices can be defined
[
65
]
. Incidence matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{B}^{V\times E}
reads

M_{mn}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\,1&\text{the $m$-th vertex is incident to $n$-th edge},\\
\displaystyle\,0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(27)

adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{B}^{V\times V}
reads

A_{mn}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1&\text{the $m$-th vertex is adjacent to the $n$-th vertex},\\
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(28)

and diagonal degree matrix
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is obtained through relation

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}}-\mathbf{A},

(29)

where elements are equal to the vertices’ degrees
[
65
]
.

The matrix used for the investigation of neighborhood homogeneity (
18
) is homogeneity matrix
\mathbf{H}
, defined as

\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}={\mathcal{B}}\left\{\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{M}\right\}

(30)

where
{\mathcal{B}}\left\{-\right\}
is the Boolean rounding operator (
7
), and
\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}
is the auxiliary matrix used for defining the homogeneity matrix

\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{1}}{\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{1}\right\|_{1}}&\ldots&\dfrac{\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{N}}{\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{H}}_{N}\right\|_{1}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(31)

Focusing on the
m
-th row of matrix
\mathbf{H}
, if the considered basis function has in total
k
adjacent basis functions, then the
m
-th row has exactly
k+1
non-zero equal elements and its norm reads
\left\|\mathbf{H}_{m}\right\|_{1}=1
.

In the general case, when the optimization variable consists of discretization elements instead of the shared edges between them, the homogeneity matrix can be defined with the above matrices as

\mathbf{H}=\left(\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{E}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{E}\right),

(32)

where
\mathbf{E}
is the identity matrix of corresponding size.

To find the point connections, we introduce two additional incidence matrices, starting with

M_{\mathrm{nt},mn}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1&\text{the $m$-th node is incident to the $n$-th triangle},\\
\displaystyle 0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(33)

assigning connections between nodes and triangles, and the incidence matrix

M_{\mathrm{nb},mn}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle 1&\text{the $m$-th node is incident to the $n$-th edge},\\
\displaystyle 0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(34)

which establishes the relation between discretization edges and nodes. These two matrices directly consider nodes in the discretized structure.

All matrices introduced in this appendix are sparse, implying fast numeric manipulation and operations when used.
