Magnetically active MnFe2O4@hydrochar composite as a high-performance adsorbent for amoxicillin removal from water
Authors: Amna Islam, Zia Ul Haq Khan, Amjad Khan, Mahmood M. S. Abdullah, Muhammad Idrees, Fida Ullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Water pollution caused by antibiotics is an emerging issue for aquatic ecosystems and public health due to their stability and biological activity. Due to low removal rates of pharmaceuticals from water, conventional wastewater treatment cannot effectively remove antibiotics. Amoxicillin (AMX) is among the most frequently utilized β-lactam antibiotics and can often be detected in water sources, causing antibiotic resistance and environmental threats. Therefore, there is an urgent need for developing effective and economical materials that can be used to remove AMX from water sources. The synthesis of a hybrid MnFe2O4@hydrochar nanocomposite was carried out using an in situ coprecipitation technique, and the nanocomposite was characterized, demonstrating its ability to remove AMX from aqueous media. The presence of crystalline phases of MnFe2O4 nanoparticles within the hydrochar matrix was evidenced by XRD. SEM and TEM were used to examine nanoparticle dispersions and morphological features. FTIR analysis of the nanocomposite showed peaks indicating the presence of functional groups containing oxygen with metal–oxygen bonds. PSA indicates a broad hydrodynamic particle size distribution, with an average intensity-based diameter of 8487.4 nm, reflecting aggregation of the particles in suspension. This further confirmed the successful synthesis of MnFe2O4@hydrochar with abundant functional groups and magnetic properties, which are suitable for efficient adsorption of amoxicillin from aqueous environments. Batch adsorption experiments demonstrated a maximum AMX removal efficiency of 94.7% under the optimized conditions of pH 7. Kinetic analysis, evaluated through the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order kinetic models, showed that the pseudo-first-order model gave a better fit with higher adjusted R2 values (0.95513–0.99665) and lower χ2, SSE and RMSE values. The equilibrium adsorption data were best described by the Langmuir isotherm model with a maximum adsorption capacity (qmax) of 4.99 mg g−1 (R = 0.9846). Furthermore, the MnFe2O4@hydrochar nanocomposite was reused for five regeneration cycles and was easily recovered using an external magnet, which demonstrated that it is a stable, reusable, and environmentally sustainable adsorbent for the removal of antibiotics from contaminated wastewater.

Introduction

In recent years, pharmaceuticals have received much attention as contaminants in the environment.1 Antibiotic use has increased dramatically to treat infections in humans and animals.2 These are biologically resistant organic compounds that are incompletely degraded by human metabolism, with the excess being excreted into the environment.3 Moreover, the discharge of antibiotics in the aqueous phase, even at lower concentrations, may lead to adverse health effects on aquatic life, such as oxidative stress, histopathological alterations, hematological, metabolic, and reproductive abnormalities, immunosuppression, and genotoxicity.4 Global waste generation, encompassing both solid and liquid refuse, is undergoing a significant surge. Further estimations show that the amount produced will rise from 2.01 billion to 3.40 billion tonnes by 2050.5 AMX, a common antibiotic all around the world, is used to treat bovine mastitis, which is caused by bacteria.6,7 AMX is a β-lactam antibiotic and belongs to the penicillin group of antibiotics. It is a potent antibiotic that works against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. It is most prescribed for the treatment of respiratory, gastrointestinal, urinary tract, and skin infections. The pharmacokinetic and pharmacological characteristics of the compound make it efficient to use against a range of bacterial infections.8,9 This antibiotic is not limited to being used by humans; it is extensively used for prevention and treatment of diseases as well as for growth promotion in various domestic animals, including mammalian, aquatic, and avian species, which leads to its prevalence in the environment.9,10 Therefore, efficient, safe, and cost-effective treatment technologies are required to remove AMX from aquatic environments and minimize its ecological impact. Studies reveal that more than 80% of AMX is eliminated from the body through urine within 2 hours of its ingestion. Its speedy elimination process, along with wide usage, has made it one of the most frequently identified antibiotics in wastewaters.11,12 With regard to its possible negative environmental effects, several removal methodologies have been considered for the elimination of AMX from aqueous solutions,13,14 including membrane separation,15 advanced oxidation, ion exchange, adsorption, and photocatalytic degradation.16,17 However, each of these conventional techniques suffers from several drawbacks, including inadequate pollutant degradation, the formation of toxic by-products, use of hazardous oxidants, high operational costs, and sludge production.18,19 Adsorption is a relatively environmentally friendly process. However, although it can achieve high pollutant removal efficiencies, the spent adsorbents require regeneration or safe disposal, which remains a practical limitation.20,21 It should be noted that the effectiveness of adsorption depends on the adsorbent's characteristics and type, adsorbate nature, and waste composition.5,22 A variety of materials, including activated carbon, black cumin seeds, graphene, bentonite, and ion-exchange resins, have been utilized for pollutant removal. Among these, nanomaterials have gained significant attention due to their unique physicochemical properties. These characteristics can be further optimized through the development of nanocomposites to achieve superior adsorption capacities.23,24 In this study, a MnFe2O4@hydrochar hybrid nanocomposite was synthesized via an in situ coprecipitation method and evaluated for the adsorption of AMX from aqueous solution. This novel configuration is designed to break through the established capacity limits of standard adsorbents, providing a high-efficiency solution for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment.1,24 In order to overcome the problems associated with charge recombination and to make use of wider bands of sunlight, many strategies have been developed by introducing extrinsic dopants, including carbonaceous substrates (hydrochar, biochar, carbon nanotubes, graphene oxide and activated carbon) as well as non-metallic elements (N, S, etc.).25 Hydrochar (HC), which is one of the carbonaceous adsorbents, is obtained by applying a hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) method to biomass resources. In HTC, biomass is transformed into hydrochar, which is rich in carbon content under the temperatures and pressures of 180–250 °C and 2–20 MPa with water.26 Hydrochar has some interesting qualities, such as high porosity, a large variety of surface functional groups, hydrophobic nature, and chemical and mechanical stability. Interestingly, hydrochars have proven to be effective for removing many types of water and wastewater pollutants. They include heavy metals, organic compounds, pharmaceutical residues, dyes, and nutrients, among others.27 Another type of nanocomposite that contains ferrite nanoparticles (with the general formula MFe2O4, where M is Mn2+, Co2+, Zn2+, etc.) is termed spinel ferrites. These nanoparticles are extensively used in catalysts28,29 and in biomedical and electronic applications because of their unique chemical stability, magnetic properties, and rapid adsorption kinetics, which have been exploited for the adsorption of different pollutants present in wastewaters.30 Some of the unique qualities of spinel ferrites, including their magnetic properties, depend upon the nature of the divalent cation M and its distribution, along with Fe3+ cations, at the sites of octahedral and tetrahedral symmetry. Spinel ferrites (MFe2O4) possess excellent magnetic properties and chemical stability, making them attractive adsorbents for wastewater treatment. Among these, MnFe2O4 has attracted considerable attention because of its magnetically separable nature and abundant active sites for adsorption.30–32

Recent studies have demonstrated that MnFe2O4-based magnetic nanocomposites are effective adsorbents for AMX removal because of their high adsorption capacity, easy magnetic recovery, and good reusability. However, further improvement in adsorption performance and structural stability is still required. This research aims to develop and utilize MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposites for efficient adsorption of pharmaceutical pollutants in the environment. In this work, MnFe2O4, a magnetic spinel ferrite possessing high surface activity and easy magnetic recovery, was synthesized through the successful combination with hydrochar from organic matter. Hydrochar, on the other hand, is characterized by the presence of porous and carbonaceous matrices together with functional groups on the surface. These two materials were used together to improve the adsorption efficacy and structural stability. Characterization methods, such as XRD, FTIR, SEM, TEM, EDX, UV-vis, UV-DRS and potentiodynamic polarization curve analyses, were used to characterize the morphology, structure, and surface characteristics of the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposites. The adsorption efficiency of the nanocomposites was systematically evaluated for removing pharmaceutical contaminants based on several operating conditions, such as contact time, pH, adsorbent dosage and temperature. Isotherms and kinetics models were adopted to investigate the adsorption mechanisms and rate control processes, while thermodynamic parameters were determined to analyze the type of adsorption. Additionally, studies involving reusability and stability tests were conducted to determine the practicability of MnFe2O4@HC as a pollutant adsorbent. The results showed that the nanocomposite can effectively and sustainably remove pharmaceuticals from the environment due to its facile separation using magnets.

Chemicals and reagents

Amoxicillin trihydrate (C16H19N3O5S·3H2O), iron(iii) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O), manganese(ii) chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), hydrochloric acid (HCl), ethanol (C2H5OH), distilled water and biochar were of analytical grade and were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Methodology

Preparation of hydrochar (HC)

Quina (Cinchona) tree-derived biomass was rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, and the solid residue was collected by filtration. The biomass was oven-dried for 8 h at 120 °C. Subsequently, 15 g of dried biomass was treated with a NaOH solution that was obtained by dissolving 4 g of sodium hydroxide pellets in 100 mL of distilled water. Initially, the biomass was treated with 50 mL of the prepared NaOH solution, and the mixture was filtered before being transferred to an autoclave for hydrothermal treatment for 6 h at 200 °C. The resulting material was immersed overnight in the remaining 50 mL of NaOH solution. Finally, the material was separated by filtration and thoroughly rinsed several times with distilled water, followed by oven-drying for 6 h at 140 °C to obtain hydrochar, as shown in Fig. 1, which was used for the AMX removal process.

Synthesis of hydrochar.

Preparation of MnFe2O4

MnFe2O4 nanoparticles were prepared using a standard co-precipitation method.32 FeCl3·6H2O and MnCl2·4H2O were mixed in a Fe : Mn molar ratio of 2 : 1 and dispersed in 50 mL of distilled water. The solution was continuously stirred at 500 rpm and heated to 50 °C for 30 minutes. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was brought to pH 10 by the gradual addition of NaOH solution. The mixture was then maintained at 70 °C while stirring continuously for 45 min. The precipitate was separated by centrifugation (10 000, 10 min), repeatedly rinsed with distilled water to achieve neutral pH, and dried at 80 °C. After drying, the obtained product was calcined at 450 °C for 2 hours to improve its crystallinity and structural integrity.33

Preparation of the MnFe2O4@hydrochar nanocomposite

The MnFe2O4@HC hybrid nanocomposite was prepared using an in situ co-precipitation route. Initially, 0.5 g of hydrochar was mixed in 50 mL of distilled water and magnetically stirred for 10 min to obtain a homogeneous suspension (solution A). In a second step, 1.26 g of MnCl2·4H2O and 4.00 g of FeCl3·6H2O were separately dissolved using 10 mL of distilled water to prepare the metal precursor solutions. These two precursor solutions were mixed and stirred for 30 min at 50 °C (solution B). Afterwards, solution B was gradually added to solution A while maintaining constant stirring to facilitate the in situ anchoring of Mn and Fe species onto the hydrochar surface. A pre-heated 1 M NaOH solution (maintained in a hot water bath) was introduced dropwise into the reaction mixture until precipitation was complete. The temperature was kept constant at 70 °C, and the mixture was stirred for another 45 min to accommodate the formation of MnFe2O4 nanoparticles on the hydrochar matrix. The product was obtained as a dark precipitate after centrifugation (10 000 rpm, 0 min) and successive washing with distilled water followed by ethanol to eliminate the residual ions and impurities. The obtained product was oven-dried at 80 °C to yield the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite. Finally, calcination for 2 h at 450 °C of the dried composite was carried out to improve the crystalline nature and structural integrity.33 The synthesis process is shown in Fig. 2.

Preparation of the MnFe2O4@hydrochar nanocomposite.

Regeneration and reusability of MnFe2O4@hydrochar

After each adsorption experiment, the spent MnFe2O4@HC adsorbent was recovered magnetically from the AMX solution.32 The recovered adsorbent was immersed in 50 mL of ethanol and stirred continuously for 4 h to desorb the adsorbed AMX. The adsorbent was then separated by filtration, repeatedly rinsed with distilled water until the residual ethanol was removed, and oven-dried at 80 °C until completely dry. The regenerated adsorbent was reused in subsequent adsorption experiments, and the regeneration process was repeated for five consecutive cycles.33

Material characterization

The hydrochar, MnFe2O4, and MnFe2O4@HC composite were characterized through a range of physicochemical techniques to examine the structural, functional, morphological, and thermal properties of the prepared materials. FTIR spectroscopy in the range 400–4000 cm−1 was used to determine the functional groups in the hydrochar and confirm metal–oxygen (Fe–O and Mn–O) vibrations in MnFe2O4 and MnFe2O4@HC. XRD analysis was carried out to investigate the crystalline structure of the MnFe2O4 nanoparticles and the MnFe2O4@HC composite. Further, the enhancement in crystallinity owing to the calcination step at 450 °C for 2 h was also evaluated. SEM images were recorded to investigate the surface morphology and texture of the hydrochar and the synthesized composites to observe MnFe2O4 nanoparticle deposition on the hydrochar matrix. TEM analysis was carried out with MnFe2O4 and MnFe2O4@HC to determine their particle shape, size, and nanostructure. Energy-dispersive X-ray analysis was performed to confirm the elemental composition and distribution, demonstrating that the nanocomposite consisted of Fe, Mn, O and C. The point of zero charge was identified to understand the surface charge behavior at different pH environments. By employing these comprehensive characterization methods, a detailed understanding of the characteristics and functionality of the produced nanocomposite materials was obtained.

Batch adsorption experiments

A stock solution of AMX (1 g L−1) was prepared by weighing a precise amount of AMX using ultrapure Milli-Q water. The 1 g L−1 AMX stock solution was further used to prepare solutions of 10–100 mg L−1 of AMX for the adsorption experiments. The sorption experiments were conducted by adding 50 mg of synthesized sorbent to 100 mL of AMX solution under optimized conditions. Different factors, such as initial AMX solution concentration (10–100 mg L−1), contact time (0–120 min), solution pH (4–11), and temperature (25–40 °C), were investigated to optimize sorption. XRD

The crystallinity and phase composition of the hydrochar, MnFe2O4 and MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite were investigated by advanced X-ray diffraction analysis, as depicted in Fig. 4. The XRD spectrum of hydrochar exhibits a broad diffraction peak around 2θ ∼ 20°–25°, indicating its low crystallinity and poor structural order. Upon the incorporation of MnFe2O4 nanoparticles in the hydrochar, several sharp and intense diffraction peaks appeared in the XRD pattern of MnFe2O4@HC, confirming the formation of a crystalline MnFe2O4 phase. For the MnFe2O4@HC composite, the characteristic diffraction peaks of MnFe2O4 were still observed, although their intensity was slightly reduced and some peak broadening occurred because of the amorphous hydrochar support. The striking diffraction peaks at about 27.3°, 30.8°, 35.3°, 44.0°, 56.82°, and 62.84° (2θ) can be assigned to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystallographic planes, respectively.38,39 The coexistence of these crystalline reflections with the broad diffraction band of hydrochar suggests that MnFe2O4 nanoparticles were successfully incorporated onto the hydrochar matrix. The slight differences in peak intensity between pure MnFe2O4 and MnFe2O4@HC are ascribed to the presence of amorphous carbon support and the interaction between the two components.40,41

SEM

SEM images of the hydrochar, MnFe2O4 nanoparticles, and MnFe2O4@HC composite are depicted in Fig. 5. The morphological characteristics of the synthesized material were examined using SEM. The hydrochar image (Fig. 5(a)) demonstrated the extent to which a porous carbonaceous material was formed during the hydrothermal carbonization step, resulting in a highly porous adsorbent matrix. However, the SEM image of MnFe2O4 (Fig. 5(b)) revealed surface heterogeneity, with both regular and irregular patterns due to the formation and agglomeration of spinel ferrite nanoparticles. The MnFe2O4 nanoparticles were further stabilized when incorporated onto the hydrochar surface, resulting in the final MnFe2O4@HC morphology (Fig. 5(c)). On the composite surface, bright white aggregates were visible, corresponding to MnFe2O4 nanoparticles well-dispersed across the hydrochar matrix. All images showed carbonaceous residues originating from the biomass, along with ferrite accumulations appearing in brighter contrast due to the higher atomic mass of Mn and Fe. Overall, MnFe2O4 loading caused the distribution of the nanoparticles to be very uniform on the surface of the hydrochar, as obtained from SEM images where particles corresponding to MnFe2O4 appeared as small bright spots.37

EDX was used to investigate the elemental composition of the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite, and the representative EDX spectrum is shown in Fig. 6. As shown in Fig. 6, strong signals are observed for carbon and oxygen, with contributions from the hydrochar matrix, thus confirming that the support prepared from biomass is carbon-rich. Furthermore, the characteristic peaks of manganese and iron were distinctly detected and demonstrated the successful deposition of MnFe2O4 nanoparticles on the hydrochar surface. Minor inorganic elements, such as Si, Ca, and Mg, indicate the intrinsic mineral content of the original biomass. These results together prove the successful synthesis of the MnFe2O4@HC composite, which is representative of proper metal oxide loading and concurrent strong interaction between the ferrite nanoparticles and the carbon framework.16,42,43 EDX spectra of hydrochar and MnFe2O4 (Fig. S1 and S2), and the elemental composition data of MnFe2O4 and MnFe2O4@HC, are presented in Tables S1 and S2.

Particle size analysis (PSA)

PSA assists in measuring the average scale of a group of similar particles. The results indicate three major distributions based on intensity, volume, and number,44 as depicted in Fig. 8(a–d). For the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite, DLS (dynamic light scattering) analysis was used for the particle size determination. The intensity distribution had two distinct peaks: one at approximately 559.2 nm (std. dev. 122.6 nm), and another at about 11 503.5 nm (std. dev. 1185.8 nm). Such a trend is an indication of a combination of both submicron particles and larger aggregates, which are primarily responsible for light scattering. A similar trend was observed in the volume distribution, which had two distinct population peaks at 486.0 nm (std. dev. 104.7 nm) and 11 138.1 nm (std. dev 1158.2 nm), indicating that both small particles and larger chunks have significantly accounted for the total particle volume. The number distribution had a similar trend, with two distinct population peaks at 432.5 nm (std. dev. 81.7 nm) and 10 797.3 nm (std. dev. 1086.5 nm), where smaller particles were dominant in the dispersion. The intensity autocorrelation function had multiple relaxation modes instead of a single decay mode, which provides evidence supporting the heterogeneous system. It is noted that DLS measures the hydrodynamic diameter of dispersed particles or particle aggregates in suspension rather than particle size. Therefore, the broad and bimodal size distributions observed in the PSA analysis mainly reflect particle agglomeration in the aqueous medium. This interpretation is consistent with the SEM and TEM results, which reveal nanosized primary MnFe2O4 particles with partial aggregation on the hydrochar surface. Accordingly, the PSA results represent the hydrodynamic size of aggregates and should not be interpreted as the actual nanoparticle size.

Zeta potential

Zeta potential analysis is carried out to measure the electrostatic charge at the surface of nanoparticles, which acts as one of the most important parameters that help to evaluate the surface charge of adsorbent and its electrostatic interaction with adsorbate during the adsorption process. Nanoparticles having zeta potentials between +10 mV and −10 mV are unstable because their surface charge is almost neutral and hence there is no electrostatic repulsion, resulting in aggregation of nanoparticles. However, nanoparticles having zeta potential beyond ±30 mV have enough electrostatic repulsion for stability.41,45 Zeta potential analysis was thus conducted to determine the electrochemical stability of hydrochar, MnFe2O4, MnFe2O4@HC in water, as shown in Fig. 9. The zeta potential value between −30 mV and −10 mV indicates moderate stability of the nanoparticles because the electrostatic repulsion between the particles does not allow them to clump together.45 This can be observed in Fig. 9(a) as the zeta potential of hydrochar was approximately −17 mV, indicating a negatively charged surface that can influence the interaction between the adsorbent and AMX molecules. In comparison, MnFe2O4 showed a zeta potential of −27 mV (Fig. 9(b)), while the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite showed a zeta potential of −27.20 mV, confirming the presence of negatively charged surface functional groups. The more negative zeta potential value in the nanocomposite shows enhanced surface charge, which can lead to electrostatic interaction with AMX under specific conditions of solution pH, and the obtained zeta potential results show that the surface charge of the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite is very important for its adsorption capacity, as seen in Fig. 9(c).

UV-DRS analysis

The ultraviolet-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-DRS) spectrum of the prepared MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite in the UV-vis range is presented in Fig. 8(a). The spectrum shows a slow increase in absorption from the near-infrared region to the visible region, followed by a broad absorption peak in the wavelength range of 350–380 nm. This broad absorption peak demonstrates the ability of the composite to effectively absorb visible light. This can be attributed to the existence of MnFe2O4 particles along with the carbon-based hydrochar matrix. The absence of any sharp absorption peak demonstrated that there is no crystalline species of the compound formed; rather, the broad spectral response indicates electronic interaction between the MnFe2O4 and hydrochar, possibly facilitating charge transfer and enhancing the optical performance of the material.46–48

Potentiodynamic polarization curve

The polarization curves obtained from potentiodynamic polarization for the MnFe2O4@HC material are shown in Fig. 10(b). The polarization behavior was studied at scan rates of 1000, 2000, and 3000 mV s−1.49 The polarization properties depend on the scan rate and thus indicate that the electrochemical activity of the material depends on scan potential. With a lower scan rate (1000 mV s−1), there was a wide range of variation of potential as the current density increased. However, with faster scanning speeds (2000 and 3000 mV s−1), smaller potential variations were observed. The results indicate that the electrochemical response of the MnFe2O4@HC composite is stable at the examined scan rates and has reproducible polarization characteristics.50,51

Current–time (I–t) response

The I–t graph for the MnFe2O4@HC material is shown in Fig. 10(c). At the beginning of the experiment, there was a sharp rise in the value of current, which stabilized after a rapid decrease. Subsequently, a series of current transients with similar shape and peak intensities was measured throughout the process. The reproducibility of these current responses in successive experiments indicates that the electrochemical behavior of the prepared material is stable over multiple polarization cycles. Moreover, there is no significant decline in the current signal in each successive cycle, suggesting that the electrochemical properties of the MnFe2O4@HC remain stable under the experimental conditions. The consistent and stable nature of the current response suggests that the synthesized material is reliable.52,53

Adsorption study

Effect of solution pH

MnFe2O4@HC was evaluated for AMX removal at pH 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11, as shown in Fig. 11(a). After 120 minutes, the corresponding removal efficiency percentiles were ∼65%, 75%, 92%, 55%, and 40%, respectively. The adsorption efficiency was significantly affected by the pH of the solution. The maximum AMX removal (∼92%) was achieved at pH 7, whereas minimum removal (∼40%) was observed at pH 11. At acidic pH, AMX exists predominantly in its protonated form, resulting in moderate electrostatic interactions with the adsorbent surface. At near-neutral pH, favorable electrostatic interactions between AMX and the MnFe2O4@HC surface enhanced the adsorption efficiency. In contrast, under strongly alkaline conditions, both the adsorbent surface and AMX acquire a negative charge, leading to electrostatic repulsion and consequently lower adsorption efficiency.54,55

(f) Recyclability study of the synthesized MnFe2O4@HC.

Effect of MnFe2O4@HC dose

The performance of adsorption capacity was further tested by varying the amount of adsorbent from 20 to 80 mg, as illustrated in Fig. 11(b). Removal of AMX was enhanced with increasing dosages from 20 to 60 mg. The adsorption increased by about 45% at a dosage of 20 mg, approximately 65% at a dosage of 40 mg, and approximately 75% at a dosage of 60 mg. As the dosages were increased to 80 mg, an increase in removal to approximately 82% was achieved. This remarkable increment can be attributed to an increased number of sites available for adsorption, increasing the chances of removing more AMX molecules.37,56

Effect of amoxicillin concentration

The adsorption of AMX was also studied at concentrations of 10–75 mg L−1 (Fig. 11(c)). A distinct decrease in removal efficiency with increasing concentration can be observed. At a concentration of 10 mg L−1, the removal efficiency stood at approximately 78%, at 25 mg L−1 it was approximately 70%, at 50 mg L−1 it was approximately 62%, and at 75 mg L−1, it approached 42%. As can be noted, with an increase in AMX concentration, the available active sites become saturated. At lower concentrations, an adequate number of adsorption sites remains available for efficient interaction, but higher concentrations quickly saturate these sites, leading to fewer available adsorption sites and hindered diffusion pathways.57,58

Effect of temperature

Reaction temperature is one of the key parameters that controls the adsorption behavior of AMX and has a great influence on the kinetics of the process. As shown in Fig. 11(d), increasing the temperature from 25 °C to 45 °C increased the removal efficiency from approximately 62% to 85%. This trend suggests an endothermic adsorption process, where increasing the temperature improves the mobility of AMX molecules, increases the possibility of their interaction with MnFe2O4@HC, and activates more surface sites.59 Besides, high temperatures accelerate electron transfer processes on the surface of the adsorbent, further contributing to better removal performance.

Comparison between MnFe2O4@HC, MnFe2O4, and hydrochar

To assess the adsorption performance of MnFe2O4-based materials, a comparison was carried out under the optimum conditions for maximum removal: pH 7, adsorbent dosage of 80 mg L−1, AMX concentration of 10 mg L−1, and a temperature of 45 °C. According to Fig. 11(e), hydrochar exhibited the lowest removal efficiency, and MnFe2O4 nanoparticles achieved a moderate removal efficiency. However, the removal efficiency for the MnFe2O4@HC composite was the highest under the same conditions. The enhanced performance of MnFe2O4@HC is likely due to the synergistic interaction between the magnetic MnFe2O4 nanoparticles and the hydrochar matrix. The hydrochar is rich in surface functional groups that enable interactions with AMX, while the MnFe2O4 nanoparticles contribute to the adsorption process through complex formation and electrostatic interactions. Thus, the combination of both materials provides better adsorption efficiency than either MnFe2O4 or hydrochar alone, making the MnFe2O4@HC composite an effective adsorbent for AMX removal.60,61

Stability and recyclability

In terms of practical applications, the stability and reusability of an adsorbent are critical parameters for its sustained operation. Synthesis and performance of MnFe2O4@HC were performed in five subsequent adsorption cycles after the simple washing and drying of the recovered adsorbent. As shown in Fig. 11(f), a slight decrease in removal efficiency during each regeneration is observed. The first cycle exhibited a maximum AMX removal of ∼97.6%, which gradually declined to ∼94.7% by the fifth cycle. This minor reduction in performance indicates that the composite maintains high stability but loses active sites over repeated use. The efficiency decline is due to partial coverage of the surface by adsorbed molecules and some adsorbent loss during the regeneration process.62,63

Kinetic modeling

Adsorption kinetics of AMX onto MnFe2O4@HC was investigated based on the pseudo-first- and pseudo-second-order models, as presented in Fig. 12(a and b) and Table 1. Since the two models are expressed in different linearized forms, the fitting statistics obtained from these transformations may not provide a reliable basis for directly comparing their relative performance.64,65 Therefore, the kinetic fitting was further evaluated using the experimental and model-predicted adsorption capacities (qt) on the same adsorption capacity scale. In addition to the adjusted R2, χ2, sum of squared errors (SSE), and root mean square error (RMSE) were used as goodness-of-fit criteria. This approach provides a more appropriate assessment of kinetic model performance than relying primarily on differently linearized equations.64,66,67 As shown in Table 1, the PFO model exhibited lower χ2, SSE and RMSE values than the PSO model at all investigated initial AMX concentrations. At 10 mg L−1, the PFO model gave χ2, SSE and RMSE values of 0.02089, 0.12536 and 0.1583, respectively, compared with 0.05788, 0.34727 and 0.2635 for the PSO model. The same trend was observed at 15, 20 and 25 mg L−1, where the PFO model consistently showed lower values of all three error criteria. The adjusted R2 values were also higher for PFO than PSO at each concentration. Thus, the revised comparison based on the experimental and model-predicted qt values indicates that the PFO model provides a better description of the adsorption kinetics of AMX onto MnFe2O4@HC under the investigated conditions. However, the better statistical fit of the PFO model should not be considered definitive evidence of a specific adsorption mechanism.66,68

Adsorption mechanisms governing the removal of pharmaceutical contaminants

Adsorption of AMX depends on several factors, including the physicochemical properties of the adsorbent, the characteristics of the pharmaceutical, and the operating conditions, such as temperature, ionic strength and pH, which influence the adsorbent–adsorbate interaction.73 The proposed adsorption mechanism involves hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, π–π stacking, ion exchange, surface complexation and magnetic recovery, as illustrated in Fig. 14. These interactions contribute to the adsorption of AMX onto the MnFe2O4@HC composite.74 Initially, electrostatic attraction takes place among the charged functional groups of AMX and the negatively charged surface of the composite. Hydrogen bonding is then established between the –COOH and –OH functional groups of the hydrochar and the functional groups of AMX. In addition, π–π stacking occurs between the aromatic ring of AMX and the carbonaceous domains of the hydrochar. Surface complexation also contributes to adsorption through coordination between the functional groups of AMX and the Mn/Fe active sites on the composite surface. Finally, after adsorption, the magnetic nature of the MnFe2O4@HC composite enables its easy recovery from the solution using an external magnet, allowing efficient separation and potential reuse of the adsorbent.75–77

Conclusion

A MnFe2O4@hydrochar nanocomposite was synthesized via an in situ co-precipitation method and characterized extensively to assess its performance for AMX removal from aqueous media. From structure characterizations, crystalline MnFe2O4 could be effectively incorporated in the hydrochar matrix, while the surface characterization indicated that abundant oxygen-containing functional groups and a porous carbonaceous framework could be obtained. The magnetic nanoparticles were uniformly dispersed over the surface of the hydrochar and enhanced the presence of active sites for adsorption, increasing the overall composite stability. The UV-vis analysis confirmed nanoparticle formation, while the overall characterization results demonstrated the successful synthesis of the MnFe2O4@HC composite with favorable structural and surface properties for AMX adsorption. The potentiodynamic polarization curve illustrates the electrochemical activity of the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite and exhibits stable polarization behavior within the applied potential. In addition to that, the current–time (I–t) response also reveals that there is a stable current response during the measurement, demonstrating electrochemical stability and charge transfer behavior. Overall, the synthesized MnFe2O4@HC composite demonstrated promising potential for AMX removal from aqueous solutions due to its effective adsorption performance, magnetic separability, and reusability. Batch adsorption experiments demonstrated that a maximum AMX removal efficiency of 94.7% was attained at the optimized conditions of pH 7. Under neutral conditions (pH 7), maximum adsorption occurred, while less adsorption occurred under strongly alkaline conditions because of the electrostatic repulsive force between the negatively charged surface of the adsorbent and the AMX molecules. Adsorption kinetic studies indicated that the adsorption mechanism followed the pseudo-first-order model as compared to the pseudo-second-order, which was supported by higher adjusted R2 values (0.95513–0.99665) and lower χ2 (0.02089–0.96205), SSE and RMSE values. This behavior indicates that chemisorption is the main controlling factor of AMX uptake. In this regard, the Langmuir isotherm best fitted the equilibrium adsorption data of AMX, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 4.99 mg g−1 with R2 = 0.9846, indicating its monolayer adsorption on the relatively homogeneous surface, with energetically equivalent active sites. Furthermore, the MnFe2O4@HC nanocomposite exhibited a removal efficiency of 94.7% after five adsorption–desorption cycles and was easily recovered using an external magnet, demonstrating its structural stability and reusability. Synergetic effects of surface functional groups, a porous hydrochar structure, and well-dispersed magnetic nanoparticles collectively enhanced the adsorption performance of the nanocomposite. In general, the study has confirmed MnFe2O4@HC as an effective, stable, and reusable adsorbent for the elimination of AMX from contaminated water. The combined advantages of high adsorption efficiency, favorable kinetics and isotherm behavior, magnetic separability, and reusability highlight its strong potential for practical applications in antibiotic wastewater treatment systems.
