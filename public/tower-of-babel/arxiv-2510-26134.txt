Variance vs. range for linear extensions, and balancing extensions in posets of bounded width.
Max Aires, Jeff Kahn

Abstract
. An old conjecture of Kahn and Saks says, roughly, that any poset P P of large enough width contains elements x , y x,y which are “balanced” in the sense that the probability that x x precedes y y in a uniformly random linear extension of P P is close to 1 / 2 1/2 . We show this implies the seemingly stronger statement that the same conclusion holds if, instead of large width, we assume only that, for some x x , the number, π ⁡ ( x ) \pi(x) , of elements of P P incomparable to x x is large. The implication follows from our two main results: first, that if π ⁡ ( P ) := max ⁡ π ⁡ ( x ) \pi(P):=\max\pi(x) is large then P P has large variance, i.e. there is a y y whose position in a uniform extension of P P has large variance; and second, that the conclusion of the Kahn-Saks Conjecture holds for P P with large variance and bounded width. These two assertions also yield an easy proof of a (not easy) result of Chan, Pak and Panova on “sorting probabilities” for Young diagrams, together with its natural generalization to higher dimensions.

Introduction

Our main results are Theorems

Some basics are recalled at the end of this section.

Following a standard abuse we identify a partially ordered set (poset)
P
with its ground set.
A

of an
n

P

.
We use

for the set of linear extensions of
P
,
and
\prec

for a uniform member of

,
according to whether we are thinking of an ordering or a bijection.
Of central interest here are the “balancing constants,”

\delta_{xy}:=\min\{\mathbb{P}(x\prec y),\mathbb{P}(y\prec x)\}
,

\delta_{x}:=\max_{y\neq x}\delta_{xy}
,
and

\delta(P):=\max\delta_{xy}
(the max over distinct

).

The primary motivation for
[
2
]
was a pair of old

Conjecture 1.1
.

If the finite poset P is not a chain then

\delta(P)\geq 1/3.

This was proposed by Kislitsyn
[
15
]

and again by Fredman (circa 1975) and Linial
[
18
]
,
all motivated by questions about sorting.
From the algorithmic standpoint what’s wanted is existence of

universal
\delta>0

such that
\delta(P)\geq\delta
whenever
P
is not a chain.
The first such bound, with
\delta=3/11
, was given in

[
13
]
,
and the current best value
is
(5-\sqrt{5})/10
, due to
Brightwell, Felsner and Trotter
[
3
]
.
(See also
[
18
]
,
[
14
]
and
[
12
]
.)

While Conjecture
1.1
is tight,
it seems likely that one can usually do better, as, for example,
in the following natural guess from
[
13
]
,
now usually called the

.
Recall that the

, is the size of a largest antichain in

Conjecture 1.2
.

If
w(P)\rightarrow\infty
, then

(1)

\delta(P)\rightarrow 1/2.

Here and throughout, assertions like the one in Conjecture

there is a

Prior to

]

This was based on a lovely probabilistic result that is again crucial for the parts of

2
]

.
See also e.g.
[

]

Much of

2
]
is concerned with understanding relations between a few

.

As in
[
2
]
, we use

For the parameters above,

On the other hand,

]

.

One motivating idea for

2.
Variance and Balance

Before turning to Theorem
1.5
, we recall a pair of basic results, the first of which is Shepp’s
“XYZ Inequality”
[
20
]
:

Theorem 2.1
.

For
x\in P
and
Y\subseteq P-x
,

\mathbb{P}(x\succ Y)\geq\prod_{y\in Y}\mathbb{P}(x\succ y).

(The statement in
[
20
]
is for
|Y|=2
, but implies Theorem
2.1

via a straightforward induction.)

The next result, due to Stanley
[
21
]
,
is used here and again in the proof of Theorem
1.3
(see Section
4
).

Theorem 2.2
.

For any P and x, the sequence
\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k)
is log-concave.

(That is,
\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k)^{2}\geq\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k-1)\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k+1)
for all
k
.)

We use this with
the following standard observation
(for which we unfortunately don’t know a reference).

Proposition 2.3
.

Any
integer-valued random variable
f
with
a log-concave distribution has

{\rm Var}(f)=O((\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k))^{-2}).

With

q(x):=\max_{k}\mathbb{P}(f(x)=k)
, we have the following important consequence.

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
P
and
x
,
\sigma(x)=\Theta(1/q(x))
.

Proof.

That
\sigma(x)=\Omega(1/q(x))
is obvious (even without log-concavity), and
\sigma(x)=O(1/q(x))

is given by Theorem
2.2
and Proposition
2.3
.
∎

In view of Corollary
2.4
we may rewrite Theorem
1.5
as

Theorem 2.5
.

For fixed
w
,
P
of width at most
w
, and
x\in P
,

\delta_{x}\rightarrow 1/2
as
q(x)\rightarrow 0
.

Remark.

When
w(P)=2
, Theorem
2.5
is easy:
Let
a=|\{y:y\leq x\}|
and
\Pi(x)=\{y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{r}\}
.
Since each of
\mathbb{P}(x\prec y_{1})
(
=\mathbb{P}(f(x)=a)
and
\mathbb{P}(x\succ y_{r})
(
=\mathbb{P}(f(x)=a+r)
)
is at most
q(x)
, we have

k:=\max\{i:\mathbb{P}(x\succ y_{i})>1/2\}\in[r-1],

and

1/2<\mathbb{P}(x\succ y_{k})=\mathbb{P}(x\succ y_{k+1})+\mathbb{P}(f(x)=a+k)\leq 1/2+q(x).

For general (fixed)
w
, the main point for the proof of Theorem
2.5

is the following statement, which we think is interesting in its own right.

Theorem 2.6
.

Suppose
P
is the disjoint union of
\{x\}
,
D
and
U
, with
D
an ideal and
U
a filter;
that
A:=\max(C)
and
B:=\min(U)
satisfy
|A|,|B|\leq w
; and that

\mathbb{P}(a\prec x\prec b)\geq\varepsilon\,
for all
\,a\in A
and
b\in B
.

Then
\mathbb{P}(A\prec x\prec B)\geq\varepsilon^{w^{2}}
.

For Theorem
2.5
(or
1.5
), notice that the assumptions of
Theorem
2.6
hold if
w(P)\leq w
and
\delta_{x}\leq 1/2-\varepsilon

(with
D=\{y:\mathbb{P}(x\succ y)\geq 1/2+\varepsilon\}
,
U=\{y:\mathbb{P}(x\prec y)\geq 1/2+\varepsilon\}
, and

2\varepsilon
in place of
\varepsilon
in (
4
)).
Thus
\mathbb{P}(f(x)=|A|+1)\geq(2\varepsilon)^{w^{2}}
, which is
\Omega(1)
if
w
and
\varepsilon>0
are fixed.
(So if
w(P)=O(1)
and

, then
\varepsilon
must be

.)

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

According to (
4
)
(with

and

always in
A
and

respectively),

\mathbb{P}(x\prec b|a\prec x)\geq\varepsilon/\mathbb{P}(a\prec x)\,\,\,\forall a,b,

and Theorem

gives, for any

,

So

, and a second application of

∎

3.
Incomparable Pairs and Average Variance

We now turn to Theorem
1.3
. Since the theorem is not true “locally”
(that is,
\pi(x)
large doesn’t imply
\sigma(x)
large; see the paragraph preceding the
statement of the theorem),
a basic difficulty is understanding where,
given only that some
\pi(x)
is large,
one should look to find elements of
P
with
large
\sigma
.

For disjoint

, call the pair
(A,B)

if
a\nsim b
for all

,
and call an incomparable

\mu

—if
|A||B|\geq\mu|C||D|
for

(C,D)
.
Theorem

follows from the next assertion.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all
\mu>0
and

there is a
K
such that
if
(A,B)
is a maximal

\mu

|B|\geq\max\{\mu|A|,K\}

\sum_{x\in A}\sigma^{2}(x)\geq L|A|.

Thus the conditions on

In the situation of Theorem

, we may

: choose

incomparable with

|A||B|
maximum (so

) and

.

In the rest of our discussion we will usually be working

P=X\sqcup Y
with

and

Y=\{y_{1}<\cdots<y_{n}\}
.

We call such a

.

We next derive Theorem

from the following variant, which is proved in

Theorem 3.2
.

For all
\mu>0
and

) and unidirectional,

is a
\mu
-pair with

,

\sum_{x\in A}\sigma^{2}(x)\geq L|A|.

Proof of Theorem

assuming Theorem

.

Let
K
be as in Theorem
3.2
with

in place of

and
\mu/4
in place of

,
and let

Set

.

.

) when

Since the statement in Theorem

But if

, and thus a

We will not use this formulation—or attempt translations—here, but a reader may (or may not)

For the rest of our discussion

Lemma 4.3

(a)

(b)

Proof.
(a) It is enough to show this conditioned on

Lemma 4.4
