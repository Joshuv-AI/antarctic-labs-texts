Lipschitz stability for an inverse problem of a fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equation
Bin Wu, Xu Zhu, Wenwen Zhou, Zewen Wang

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate a discrete inverse problem of determining three unknowns, i.e. initial displacement, initial velocity and random source term, in a fully-discrete approximation of one-dimensional stochastic hyperbolic equation. We firstly prove a new Carleman estimate for the fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equation. Based on this Carleman estimate, we then establish a Lipschitz stability for this discrete inverse problem by the discrete spatial derivative data at the left endpoint and the measurements of the solution and its time derivative at the final time. Owing to the discrete setting, an extra term with respect to mesh size arises in the right-hand side of the stability estimate. Keywords: Discrete Carleman estimate, full-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equation, inverse problems, Lipschitz stability.

It is well known that the Carleman estimate is a fundamental tool for studying inverse problems associated with stochastic partial differential equations [
12
,
20
,
23
,
25
,
31
]. For the inverse problem of stochastic hyperbolic equation, Lü [
22
] introduced a special weight function to place the unknown random source term on the left-hand side of the Carleman estimate, thereby establishing uniqueness in determining three unknowns. This approach was subsequently extended to the stochastic dynamic Euler–Bernoulli beam equation [
29
] and the damped stochastic plate equation [
27
]. We also refer to [
19
] for the inverse source problem and [
28
] for the initial data determination problem, both concerning stochastic parabolic equations. Regarding numerical methods for stochastic inverse problems, we mention [
12
] for the ill-posed Cauchy problem of stochastic parabolic equation, [
21
] for the inverse source problem of semilinear stochastic hyperbolic equation, and [
2
] for recovering a random source in quantifying the elastic modulus of nanomaterials. All the above stochastic inverse problems have been studied within a continuous framework.

Let us briefly review relevant studies on discrete Carleman estimates and their applications. Extensive literature exists for deterministic partial differential equations: see [
4
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
17
] for spatial semi-discrete Carleman estimates, and [
6
,
13
,
30
] for time semi-discrete estimates. In the fully discrete setting, in [
9
] the authors established a Carleman estimate for implicit finite-difference approximations of one-dimensional parabolic operators, which was then applied to derive relaxed controllability results for fully discrete linear and semilinear parabolic equations. This line of research was subsequently extended to fully discrete parabolic operators with dynamic boundary conditions [
16
] and to fully discrete Korteweg–de Vries equations [
15
]. However, discrete Carleman estimates for stochastic partial differential equations remain relatively scarce. Recently, spatial semi-discrete Carleman estimates have been derived for stochastic parabolic equations [
26
,
32
] and successfully applied to controllability and inverse problems. Moreover, a new global Carleman estimate for spatial semi-discrete backward stochastic fourth-order parabolic operators has been used to establish relaxed null controllability for a fourth-order semi-discrete parabolic equation [
24
].

Relatively few works have focused on discrete inverse problems. For deterministic equations, in [
3
] the authors established a stability result for the discrete inverse problem of determining the potential in spatial semi-discrete hyperbolic equations. In [
14
], ill-posed problems and coefficient inverse problems for deterministic parabolic equations were investigated within a time finite-difference framework. Stability analyses and globally convergent numerical methods for such discrete inverse problems have also been developed. As for discrete stochastic inverse problems, we refer to [
26
] for results on spatial semi-discrete stochastic parabolic equations.

However, to the best of our knowledge, no existing work has addressed discrete Carleman estimates together with discrete inverse problems for fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equations, not even for their fully discrete deterministic counterparts. In this paper, we first establish a Carleman estimate for fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equations. As an application, this estimate is then employed to study the stability of the associated discrete inverse problem. Although the overall structure of the proof resembles that of the continuous framework, the discrete setting introduces many additional terms that cannot be directly absorbed on the left-hand side of the Carleman estimate. Moreover, compared with deterministic discrete Carleman estimates, further terms arising from stochastic effects must also be handled. To place the unknown random source on the left-hand side and to control the discretization errors associated with it, we modify the temporal part of the Carleman weight function by replacing
(t-T)^{2}
in [
22
] with
(t-(T+1))^{2}
. Under this choice of weight function, we are able to obtain a stability result for the discrete inverse problem, rather than merely a uniqueness result. The trade-off is the need for additional time-derivative information at the final time. Nevertheless, the stability result is particularly valuable for error analysis in numerical implementations.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the discrete setting. In Section 3, we establish a new Carleman estimate for the fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equation. Section 4 is devoted to deriving a Lipschitz stability result for the corresponding discrete inverse problem.

Discrete settings

In this section, we introduce fundamental concepts and formulas of discrete calculus, including discrete function spaces, some useful discrete identities, integration by parts for discrete operators and discrete calculus results on the Carleman weight function, which will be utilized in the proofs of the main results.

2.3
Weight function and its discrete calculus

We first introduce a weight function which will be used in our discrete Carleman estimate. Let
s
and
\lambda

be two large parameters. For some
x^{*}>1
and a small positive parameter
\beta
, we define the regular weight function by

r=e^{l(x,t)},\quad l(x,t)=s\varphi(x,t),\quad\varphi(x,t)=e^{\lambda\phi(x,t)},\quad(x,t)\in Q

(2.15)

with

\phi(x,t)=|x-x^{*}|^{2}-\beta|t-(T+1)|^{2}+M,\quad(x,t)\in Q,

(2.16)

where
M
is a large positive constant such that
\phi(x,t)>0
for all
(x,t)\in Q
. We also set
\rho=r^{-1}
.

Remark 2.1.
The temporal weight factor
|t-(T+1)|^{2}
in our discrete Carleman estimate is different from the continuous analogue
|t-T|^{2}
employed in [
22
]. In discrete settings, the errors associated with
g
, i.e.
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta t)\mathbb{E}\iint_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}r^{2}|g|^{2}

need to be absorbed by
\mathbb{E}\iint_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi\partial_{t}\phi r^{2}|g|^{2}
. However, in [
22
] the term related to random source
g
is
\iint_{Q}s\lambda\varphi(T-t)r^{2}|g|^{2}
, which can not control any arbitrary small errors associated with
g
.
Consequently, we substitute
|t-(T+1)|^{2}
for
|t-T|^{2}
to guarantee
\partial_{t}\phi>0
in
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}
.

Next we give some discrete calculus results on the Carleman weight function. We present these results here without
proofs and refer to [
6
,
7
] and [
16
] for a complete discussion. In contrast to the weight function used for discrete parabolic operator in Carleman estimates, the corresponding weight for hyperbolic equation has sufficiently smoothness in time variable

{\bf A}_{t}

{\bf D}_{t}

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\alpha,k,l,m,n\in\mathbb{N}

Provided
\max\{{s\Delta x},s\Delta t\}\leq 1
, we have

\displaystyle r{\bf A}_{\xi}^{m}{\bf D}_{\xi}^{n}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}\rho=r\partial_{\xi}^{n+\alpha}\rho+s^{n+\alpha}{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda}\left((s\Delta\xi)^{2}\right)=s^{n+\alpha}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1),

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{\xi}^{k}{\bf D}_{\xi}^{l}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}(r{\bf A}_{\xi}^{m}{\bf D}_{\xi}^{n}\rho)=\partial_{\xi}^{l+\alpha}(r\partial_{\xi}^{n}\rho)+s^{n}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((s\Delta\xi)^{2}\right)=s^{n}{\mathcal{O}}_{\lambda}(1),\quad\xi\in\{x,t\}.

We also need the following asymptotic expressions of mixed difference operators.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Provided
\max\{{s\Delta x},s\Delta t\}\leq 1
and
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(\Delta x)
, we have

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{t}^{m}{\bf D}_{t}^{n}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho)=\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x),\quad{\bf A}_{x}^{m}{\bf D}_{x}^{n}(r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho)=\partial_{x}^{n}(r\partial_{t}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x),

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{t}^{m}{\bf D}_{t}^{n}(r{\bf D}_{x}^{2}\rho)=\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}^{2}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{3}\Delta x),\quad{\bf A}_{x}^{m}{\bf D}_{x}^{n}(r{\bf D}_{t}\rho)=\partial_{x}^{n}(r\partial_{t}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x).

Proof.
We prove only the first result here. The remaining results follow by similar arguments. Obviously, we have

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho={\bf A}_{x}(\partial_{x}\rho+R_{{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho))=\partial_{x}\rho+R_{{\bf A}_{x}}(\partial_{x}\rho)+R_{{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)+R_{{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho).

(2.17)

Then we obtain from Proposition 4.1 in [
17
] that

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{t}^{n}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}\left(r\partial_{x}\rho+rR_{{\bf A}_{x}}(\partial_{x}\rho)+rR_{{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)+rR_{{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)\right)\right),

(2.18)

where

\displaystyle R_{{\bf D}_{\xi}^{k}}(f)=(\Delta\xi)^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}(-1)^{l}\left(\frac{k-2l}{2}\right)^{k+2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-\sigma)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}f^{(k+2)}(\xi+(k-2l)\sigma\Delta\xi/2){\rm d}\sigma,

\displaystyle R_{{\bf A}_{\xi}^{k}}(f)=\frac{1}{2^{k+2}}(\Delta\xi)^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}(k-2l)^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\sigma)f^{(2)}(\xi+(k-2l)\sigma\Delta\xi/2){\rm d}\sigma,\quad\xi\in\{x,t\}

and
R_{{\bf A}_{\xi}^{m}{\bf D}_{\xi}^{n}}=R_{{\bf A}_{\xi}^{m}}\circ R_{{\bf D}_{\xi}^{n}}
.

Let
f_{\sigma,\tau}(x,t)=f(x+\sigma\Delta x/2,t+\tau\Delta t/2)
. We easily see that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}(r\partial_{x}\rho)\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle(\Delta t)^{2}\sum_{l=0}^{n-k}\binom{n-k}{l}(-1)^{l}\left(\frac{n-k-2l}{2}\right)^{n-k+2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-\tau)^{n-k+1}}{(n-k+1)!}\partial_{t}^{n+2}(r\partial_{x}\rho)_{0,(n-k-2l)\tau}{\rm d}\tau,

together with
\partial_{t}^{n+2}(r\partial_{x}\rho)=\partial_{t}^{n+2}(-s\lambda\varphi\partial_{x}\phi)=s\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(1)
, which implies

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}(r\partial_{x}\rho)\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta t)^{2}\right),&k=0,1,2,\cdots,n-1,\\
\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho),&k=n.\end{array}\right.

Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}(rR_{{\bf A}_{x}}(\partial_{x}\rho))\right)=s^{3}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta x\Delta t)^{2}\right),\\
\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}\left(rR_{{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)\right)\right)=s^{3}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta x\Delta t)^{2}\right),\\
\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}\left(rR_{{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)\right)\right)=s^{5}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta x)^{4}(\Delta t)^{2}\right)\end{array}\right.

for
k=0,1,2,\cdots,n-1
and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}(rR_{{\bf A}_{x}}(\partial_{x}\rho))\right)=s^{3}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta x)^{2}\right),\\
\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}\left(rR_{{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)\right)\right)=s^{3}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta x)^{2}\right),\\
\partial_{t}^{k}\left(R_{{\bf D}_{t}^{n-k}}\left(rR_{{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}}(\rho)\right)\right)=s^{5}\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}\left((\Delta x)^{4}\right)\end{array}\right.

for
k=n
.
Then substituting (
2.3
)-(
2.3
) into (
2.3
) yields

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{t}^{n}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho)=\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x).

Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{t}^{m}{\bf D}_{t}^{n}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho)=\

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho)+R_{{\bf A}_{t}^{m}}(\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho))+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s(\Delta t)^{2})+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{n}(r\partial_{x}\rho)+\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}(s^{2}\Delta x).

The last three results can follow from similar arguments.
\Box

3
Discrete Carleman estimates

This section is devoted to proving Carleman estimate for the fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\bf d}_{t}z-{\bf D}_{x}^{2}y\Delta t=f\Delta t+g{\bf t}^{+}({\bf d}_{t}B),&(x,t)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N},\\
z={\bf D}_{t}y,&(x,t)\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N},\\
y(0,t)=y(L,t)=0,&t\in{\mathcal{N}},\\
y(x,0)=y_{0},\quad{\bf t}^{+}(z)(x,0)=y_{1},&x\in{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}.\end{array}\right.

The proof of our Carleman estimate builds upon the fundamental framework established for the continuous setting [
22
,
31
]. However, discretization in both space and time gives rise to additional terms. In particular, the treatment of discrete computations involving random terms introduces specific technical challenges. To overcome these obstacles, we adapt techniques from existing works on semi-discrete and fully-discrete partial differential operators [
6
,
9
,
16
].

For convenience, we introduce

\displaystyle X_{T}(\mathcal{M})=\{\xi\ |\ \xi(\cdot,T)\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;H^{1}(\mathcal{M})),\ {\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}\xi)(\cdot,T)\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;L^{2}(\mathcal{M}))\}

endowed with the norm

\displaystyle\|\xi(T)\|_{X_{T}(\mathcal{M})}=\|\xi(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;H^{1}(\mathcal{M}))}+\|{\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}\xi)(\cdot,T)\|_{L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;L^{2}(\mathcal{M}))}.

The main result in this section is the following discrete Carleman estimate.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\left(f,g\right)\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;L^{2}(\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}))
,
y_{0}\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;H^{1}(\mathcal{M}))
,
y_{1}\in L^{2}_{\mathcal{F}}(\Omega;L^{2}(\mathcal{M}))
,
\beta\in(0,1)
and let
T
satisfy

\displaystyle T>\frac{\sup_{x\in I}|x-x^{*}|}{\beta}.

(3.5)

Then for all fixed
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
with a sufficient large constant
\lambda_{0}
depending on
x^{*},\beta,M,T
, there exist positive constant
C
depending on
x^{*},\beta,M,T
, and
C(\lambda)
,
s_{0}>1
,
0<\varepsilon<1
,
0<\widehat{\Delta x}<1
also depending on
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}r^{2}|y|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}{\bf t}^{+}\left(|{\bf D}_{t}y|^{2}\right)+\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\times\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}|{\bf D}_{x}y|^{2}+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}|g|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{\mathcal{M}}s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}r^{2}|y_{0}|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}|{\bf D}_{x}y_{0}|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{\mathcal{M}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}|y_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}r^{2}|f|^{2}+C\mathbb{E}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi{\bf tr}(|{\bf D}_{x}y|^{2})\bigg|_{x=0}+C(\Delta x)^{2}\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}s\lambda^{2}\varphi|{\bf D}_{t}{\bf D}_{x}y|^{2}+

\displaystyle C(\lambda)s^{3}e^{C(\lambda)s}\|y(T)\|^{2}_{X_{T}(\mathcal{M})}

(3.6)

for all
s\geq s_{0}
,
0<\Delta x\leq\widehat{\Delta x}
,
0<\Delta t\leq 1
satisfying the conditions

\displaystyle s\Delta x\leq\varepsilon\quad{\rm and}\quad\Delta t=\varepsilon\mathcal{O}\left((\Delta x)^{2}\right).

Remark 3.1.
For fully-discrete deterministic parabolic equations, the following conditions on
\Delta x
and
\Delta t
:

\displaystyle\frac{s^{4}\Delta t}{\delta^{4}T^{6}}\leq\varepsilon\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{s\Delta x}{\delta T^{2}}\leq\varepsilon

is imposed to prove discrete Carleman estimates [
9
,
16
]. These conditions imply that
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(s^{-4})
, which is more restrictive than our condition
\Delta t=\mathcal{O}(s^{-2})
. In other words, for discrete hyperbolic equations, the requirement of
\Delta t
in the proof of Carleman estimate can be taken less restrictive than the one for discrete parabolic equations. Additionally, the parameter
\varepsilon
in
\Delta t=\varepsilon\mathcal{O}\left((\Delta x)^{2}\right)
is introduced to address the terms involving random
{\rm d}z
. However, for the fully-discrete deterministic hyperbolic equation, this
\varepsilon
can be removed from
\Delta t=\varepsilon\mathcal{O}\left((\Delta x)^{2}\right)
when proving Carleman estimate.

Remark 3.2.
Similar to spatial semi-discrete deterministic hyperbolic equation [
3
], in comparison to Carleman estimate under continuous settings [
20
], the discrete Carleman estimate (
3.1
) has an additional term of
|{\bf D}_{t}{\bf D}_{x}y|
on the right-hand side, which arises from the spatial discretization. Though this term can not be removed in discrete Carleman estimate, it is still compatible with the Lax argument, details see [
3
]. In other words, as
\Delta x\rightarrow 0
, under suitable regularity we can eliminate this term and then obtain the corresponding Carleman estimate in continuous settings.

Remark 3.3.
In this Carleman estimate, the boundary observation at
x_{0}
can be replaced by the one at
x_{N+1}
if we take
x^{*}<0
to satisfy
\partial_{x}\phi>0
for all
x\in I
.

Remark 3.4.
Under the same assumptions in Theorem 3.1, for fully-discrete deterministic hyperbolic equation (i.e.
g=0
), we can obtain the following Carleman estimate:

\displaystyle\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}r^{2}|y|^{2}+\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}{\bf t}^{+}\left(|{\bf D}_{t}y|^{2}\right)+\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\times\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi r^{2}|{\bf D}_{x}y|^{2}+

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{M}}s^{3}\lambda^{3}\varphi^{3}r^{2}|y_{0}|^{2}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}s\lambda\varphi|{\bf D}_{x}y_{0}|^{2}+\int\limits_{\mathcal{M}}s\lambda\varphi|y_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle C\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}r^{2}|f|^{2}+C\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}}s\lambda\varphi{\bf tr}(|{\bf D}_{x}y|^{2})\bigg|_{x=0}+C(\Delta x)^{2}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}s\lambda^{2}\varphi|{\bf D}_{t}{\bf D}_{x}y|^{2},

where the terminal condition
(y,{\bf t}^{+}\left({\bf D}_{t}y\right))|_{t=T}=0
is imposed, and this condition is achievable by a standard cut-off argument, see [
5
]. This Carleman estimate has potential application to reconstruction of initial conditions of fully-discrete hyperbolic equations by additional boundary observation.

Proof of Theorem 3.1.
The whole proof of the Carleman estimate (
3.1
) is divided into the following four steps.

Step 1. The change of variable for fully-discrete stochastic hyperbolic operator

Firstly, we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(y):={\bf d}_{t}z-{\bf D}_{x}^{2}y\Delta t,\quad z={\bf D}_{t}y.

(3.7)

We introduce the change of variable
y=\rho Y=r^{-1}Y
with
r
given by
(\ref{2.15})
. A direct calculation gives

\displaystyle z=\

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{t}(\rho Y)={\bf t}^{+}(\rho)Z+{\bf D}_{t}\rho{\bf t}^{-}(Y),

(3.8)

\displaystyle{\bf d}_{t}z=\

\displaystyle\rho{\bf d}_{t}Z+{\bf t}^{+}({\bf d}_{t}\rho){\bf t}^{+}(Z)+{\bf t}^{-}({\bf d}_{t}\rho){\bf t}^{-}(Z)+{\bf d}_{t}({\bf D}_{t}\rho)Y,

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{x}^{2}y=\

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{x}^{2}(\rho Y)={\bf D}_{x}^{2}\rho{\bf A}_{x}^{2}Y+2{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y+{\bf A}_{x}^{2}\rho{\bf D}_{x}^{2}Y

(3.10)

with
Z={\bf D}_{t}Y
. Together with (
2.6
), substituting (
3.8
)-(
3.10
) into (
3.7
) yields

\displaystyle r\mathcal{L}(y)=\

\displaystyle{\bf d}_{t}Z+r{\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}\rho){\bf t}^{+}(Z)\Delta t+r{\bf t}^{-}({\bf D}_{t}\rho){\bf t}^{-}(Z)\Delta t+r{\bf D}^{2}_{t}\rho Y\Delta t-

\displaystyle r{\bf D}_{x}^{2}\rho Y\Delta t-2r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y\Delta t-r\left({\bf A}_{x}^{2}\rho+\frac{1}{4}(\Delta x)^{2}{\bf D}_{x}^{2}\rho\right){\bf D}_{x}^{2}Y\Delta t.

(3.11)

On the other hand, we easily see that

\displaystyle r{\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}\rho){\bf t}^{+}(Z)+r{\bf t}^{-}({\bf D}_{t}\rho){\bf t}^{-}(Z)=2r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho{\bf t}^{-}(Z)+r{\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}\rho){\bf d}_{t}Z.

Then, (
3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle r\mathcal{L}(y)={\bf d}_{t}Z+\mathcal{A}(Y)\Delta t+\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)\Delta t-\mathcal{R}(Y,Z)\Delta t,\quad Z={\bf D}_{t}Y,

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\mathcal{A}_{i}(Y),\quad\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)=\mathcal{B}_{1}(Z)+\sum_{i=2}^{3}\mathcal{B}_{i}(Y)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(Y)=-r\left({\bf A}_{x}^{2}\rho+\frac{1}{4}(\Delta x)^{2}{\bf D}_{x}^{2}\rho\right){\bf D}_{x}^{2}Y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y)=\left(r{\bf D}_{t}^{2}\rho-r{\bf D}_{x}^{2}\rho\right)Y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1}(Z)=2r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho{\bf t}^{-}(Z),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2}(Y)=-2r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3}(Y)=r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)Y,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(Y,Z)=\

\displaystyle r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)Y-r{\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}\rho){\bf d}_{t}Z.

Furthermore, multiplying both sides of (
3.12
) by
2\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)
, it follows that

\displaystyle 2r\mathcal{L}(y)\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{B}(Y,Z){\bf d}_{t}Z+2\mathcal{A}(Y)\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)\Delta t+2|\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)|^{2}\Delta t-2\mathcal{R}(Y,Z)\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)\Delta t.

(3.13)

Then, integrating (
3
) over
\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}
, applying the equation in (
3
) and taking mathematical expectation, we find that

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}rf\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)+2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{R}(Y,Z)\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{B}(Y,Z){\bf D}_{t}Z+2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{A}(Y)\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)+2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}|\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)|^{2},

(3.14)

where we have used that

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}rg\mathcal{B}(Y,Z){\bf t}^{+}({\bf D}_{t}B)=0.

(3.15)

By applying Young’s inequality to the two terms on the left-hand side of (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}r^{2}|f|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}|\mathcal{R}(Y,Z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{B}(Y,Z){\bf D}_{t}Z+\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{A}(Y)\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}|\mathcal{B}(Y,Z)|^{2}.

(3.16)

Step 2. Estimates for the terms involved in
\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{B}(Y,Z){\bf D}_{t}Z

We set

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{B}(Y,Z){\bf D}_{t}Z=K_{1}+K_{2}+K_{3},

(3.17)

where
K_{i}
is the expectation of the inner product of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
and
{\bf D}_{t}Z
.

For the first term
K_{1}
, we apply identity (
2.10
) for time difference operator and discrete integration by parts formula (
2.12
) to obtain

\displaystyle K_{1}=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho\left({\bf D}_{t}(Z^{2})-\Delta t({\bf D}_{t}Z)^{2}\right)=B_{1}+D_{1}+D_{2},

(3.18)

where

\displaystyle B_{1}=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho{\bf t}^{+}\left(|Z|^{2}\right)n_{t},

\displaystyle D_{1}=\

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf D}_{t}(r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho)|Z|^{2},

\displaystyle D_{2}=\

\displaystyle-\Delta t\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}r{\bf A}_{t}{\bf D}_{t}\rho|{\bf D}_{t}Z|^{2}.

By discrete integration by parts formula (
2.13
), discrete identity (
2.9
) and

\displaystyle K_{2}=\

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}}{\bf t}^{+}\left({\bf t}^{-}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y)\right){\bf D}_{t}Z

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y{\bf t}^{+}(Z)n_{t}+2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf t}^{-}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho){\bf D}_{t}({\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y)Z+

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf D}_{t}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho){\bf t}^{+}({\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y)Z.

(3.19)

Furthermore, applying discrete integration by part for the average operator
{\bf A}_{x}
to the second term on the right-hand side of (
3
) and noticing that
z=0
on
\mathcal{\partial}\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}
due to

on
\partial\mathcal{M}\times\overline{\mathcal{N}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle K_{2}=\

\displaystyle B_{2}+D_{3}+D_{4}+D_{5},

(3.20)

where

\displaystyle B_{2}=\

\displaystyle-2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y{\bf t}^{+}(Z)n_{t},

\displaystyle D_{3}=\

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf t}^{-}\left({\bf A}_{x}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho)\right){\bf D}_{x}Z{\bf A}_{x}Z,

\displaystyle D_{4}=\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\Delta x)^{2}\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}^{*}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf t}^{-}\left({\bf D}_{x}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho)\right)|{\bf D}_{x}Z|^{2},

\displaystyle D_{5}=\

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf D}_{t}(r{\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}\rho){\bf t}^{+}({\bf A}_{x}{\bf D}_{x}Y)Z.

As for the last term
K_{3}
, by using discrete integration by parts formula (
2.12
) with respect to
{\bf D}_{t}
we have

\displaystyle K_{3}=\

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf D}_{t}\left(r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)Y\right)Z+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)Y{\bf t}^{+}(Z)n_{t}=B_{3}+D_{6}+D_{7},

(3.21)

where

\displaystyle B_{3}=\

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\partial\mathcal{N}}r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)Y{\bf t}^{+}(Z)n_{t},

\displaystyle D_{6}=\

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf D}_{t}\left(r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)\right){\bf t}^{+}(Y)Z,

\displaystyle D_{7}=\

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\iint\limits_{\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{N}^{*}}{\bf t}^{-}\left(r\varphi^{-1}\left({\bf D}_{t}\varphi{\bf D}_{t}\rho-{\bf D}_{x}\varphi{\bf D}_{x}\rho\right)\right)|Z|^{2}.

Therefore, substituting (
3.18
), (
3.20
) and (
3
) into (
3.17
) and applying the estimates in Lemma A.1 and Lemma A.2, we obtain
