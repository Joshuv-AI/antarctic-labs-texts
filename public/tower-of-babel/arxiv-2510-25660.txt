mitransient : Transient light transport in Mitsuba 3
Diego Royo, Jorge Garcia-Pueyo, Miguel Crespo, Oscar Pueyo-Ciutad, Guillermo Enguita, Diego Bielsa

Abstract
. mitransient is a light transport simulation tool that extends Mitsuba 3 with support for time-resolved simulations. In essence, mitransient extends conventional rendering by adding a temporal dimension which accounts for the time of flight of light. This allows rapid prototyping of novel transient imaging systems without the need of costly or difficult-to-operate hardware. Our code is trivially easy to install through pip , and consists of Python modules that can run both in CPU and GPU by leveraging the JIT capabilities of Mitsuba 3. It provides physically-based simulations of complex phenomena, including a wide variety of realistic materials and participating media such as fog or smoke. In addition, we extend Mitsuba 3’s functionality to support time-resolved polarization tracking of light and transient differentiable rendering. Finally, we also include tools that simplify the use of our simulations for non-line-of-sight imaging, enabling realistic scene setups with capture noise to be simulated in just seconds of minutes. Altogether, we hope that mitransient will support the research community in developing novel algorithms for transient imaging.

1.
Introduction

Transient imaging aims to capture and analyze how light propagates and interacts through a scene with an ultra-high temporal resolution. Thanks to recent advances in hardware, current technology can reach resolutions in the order of picoseconds.

This increase of information about the temporal domain provides several benefits that have led to numerous applications, such as visualization of light in motion
(
Jarabo et al. 2014
)
(Fig.
1
), vision through highly-scattering media
(
Luesia et al. 2022
)
, and inferring material properties from a distance.
One of the most prominent applications of transient imaging is the reconstruction of hidden objects from captured indirect illumination (Fig.
3
). This problem, known as non-line-of-sight (NLOS) imaging, is especially relevant to our work.

Unfortunately, most methods that rely on transient imaging require expensive hardware, including ultra-fast cameras and pulsed illumination devices, which are difficult to calibrate and operate.

Transient light transport simulation
emerges as an alternative tool for developing, prototyping, and testing such systems, without hardware difficulties
(
Jarabo et al. 2014
)
.
However, there are no available state-of-the-art systems that fulfill the needs of the users, meaning that they are ad-hoc implementations for specific tasks (difficult to extend to other applications) or they lack novel optimized strategies (they are not as fast as they could be).

Our rendering tool,
mitransient
, fixes these issues by being able to simulate different sorts of time-resolved sensing devices (including time-gated and transient cameras), with easy-to-extend modules written in Python that run in both CPU and GPU by using vectorized JIT compiled code from Mitsuba 3
(
Jakob et al. 2022
)
.

This article showcases a few of the capabilities of
mitransient
:

•

Complex materials and participating media
(Sec.
2
).

•

Tracking the polarization
of light in time, including a suite of polarization visualization tools (Sec.
3
).

•

Inverse/differentiable rendering
in the time domain. This allows you to compute gradients of transient renders with respect to scene parameters, enabling applications such as inverse rendering in the transient domain (Sec.
4
).

•

Simulation of non-line-of-sight (NLOS) setups
, allowing to simulate the capture process of illuminating and capturing points in the relay wall (Sec.
5
).

2.
Transient rendering

Our additions to Mitsuba 3 include extensions to steady-state path-tracing algorithms following the theory of the work of Jarabo et al.
(
Jarabo et al. 2014
)
, including both surface and volumetric transport, referred in our code as the
transient_path
and
transient_volpath
integrators.
Notice that our system is capable of simulating conventional, time-gated, and transient cameras, including effects such as time unwarping
(
Velten et al. 2013
)
.
Furthermore, both RGB and spectral rendering are supported.

3.
Transient polarization tracking

mitransient
supports tracking polarization through time, capturing the polarization state of incoming light at each time bin, represented by a
4\times 1
Stokes vector (per color channel):

(1)

\mathbf{S}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}S_{0}(t)\\[3.0pt]
S_{1}(t)\\[3.0pt]
S_{2}(t)\\[3.0pt]
S_{3}(t)\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}=\text{total intensity of the light}\\[3.0pt]
=\text{linear polarization (horizontal\textendash vertical)}\\[3.0pt]
=\text{linear polarization (diagonal)}\\[3.0pt]
=\text{circular polarization}\end{array}

Rendering polarization in transient state is akin to rendering polarization in steady-state rendering
(
Wilkie and Weidlich 2012
)
.
The throughput value in polarized rendering is a Mueller matrix
\boldsymbol{\beta}
that tracks the polarization interaction each scattering event in each path vertex.
Following
Mitsuba3
convention, each interaction is computed in local coordinates and transformed into its global coordinates through the
si.to_world_mueller()
function.
Therefore, as we are tracking radiance in transient path tracing, the path’s throughput is updated as
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{\beta}\;\boldsymbol{M}_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{M}_{i}
is the Mueller matrix of the scattering event at the
i
-th path vertex.

Spaceship

(

.

\psi=\text{arctan}2(S_{2},S_{1})
and degree of polarization
D=\sqrt{S_{1}^{2}+S_{2}^{2}+S_{3}^{2}}/S_{0}
over time.

4.
Differentiable transient rendering

Differentiable rendering allows to track derivatives through the rendering computations, enabling a wide variety of inverse rendering and optimization applications.
mitransient
supports forward and backward mode differentiation. To understand the difference, consider
x
to be the set of scene parameters (e.g., material properties, albedos…) and let
f
be our transient path tracing algorithms, which transform them into a time-resolved image
y
as:

(2)

\textbf{y}(t)=f(\textbf{x},t).

Using this formulation, we can define two differentiation modes:

Forward
mode differentiation allows you to compute
\partial\textbf{y}(t)/\partial\textbf{x}_{i}
for one parameter
\textbf{x}_{i}
of the scene. We showcase this mode in Fig.
1

(a)
, where we compute the time-resolved derivatives of the rendered image with respect to the albedo of the wooden floor. As expected, earlier frames of the video show high derivatives near the floor, and later frames of the video show how light reflected at the floor would also contribute to other parts of the image.

Backward
mode differentiation, on the other hand, can be used to solve inverse problems. For example, consider that you want to adjust your scene parameters
x
such that the rendered video
y
matches a desired video
\textbf{y}^{\ast}
. You can define a loss function:

(3)

L=\text{loss}(\textbf{y}(t),\textbf{y}^{\ast}(t))

With this formulation, backward mode differentiation allows you to compute
\nabla_{\textbf{x}_{i}}L
for a subset
\textbf{x}_{i}\subseteq\textbf{x}
of the scene parameters, which can be used for optimization:

(4)

\textbf{x}_{i}^{j+1}\leftarrow\textbf{x}_{i}^{j}-\eta\nabla_{\textbf{x}_{i}}L

with a learning rate
\eta
, updating the scene parameters
\textbf{x}_{i}^{j}
at timestep
j
. Note that, contrary to conventional differentiable rendering, the loss function in Eq.
3
has much more information through the temporal dimension of the render
y
.

For more details on how to use our transient differentiable rendering features, you can check our tutorials for forward and backward differentiable rendering here

5.
Non-line-of-sight (NLOS) scene capture simulation

Non-line-of-sight imaging refers to the family of algorithms that reconstruct objects hidden from direct view by analyzing time-resolved indirect illumination on a visible wall.

Conventional path-tracing techniques are ill-suited for NLOS scenes, as most of the signal that is relevant for this problem comes from very specific multiple-bounce paths which the algorithm has a very low probability to find.
For this purpose, we have added custom sampling techniques tailored to NLOS scenes
(
Royo et al. 2022
)
, resulting in an algorithm denoted as
transient_nlos_path
.

To showcase the potential of our work in this area, we have recreated the setup from Liu et al.
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
, as seen in Fig.
3

(a)
.
The hidden scene consists of shelves, a wooden chair, and a cardboard box.
Similar to the original experiment, we compute the impulse response of the hidden scene on a
180\times 130
grid of points on the relay wall, and as can be seen in Fig.
3

(b)
, our system can be used for NLOS reconstructions.

Our sampling techniques greatly improve the convergence time: this experiment requires only four minutes of execution time on an Intel Xeon E5-2697 CPU using 500.000 samples per pixel, which would take hours otherwise.
Nevertheless, using 5.000 samples (with four seconds of execution time) gives almost the same result.

Realistic hardware noise.

Light transport simulations ignore many sources of noise that happen using real hardware (e.g. temporal jittering of the signal
(
Hernandez et al. 2017
)
).
To help bridge the gap between perfect simulations and the real world, our system can also use measures from real hardware devices to process the signal, adding realistic noise.
