Strike-slip Pull-apart process of the Jiyang depression during the Yanshanian tectonic cycle and its response to Paleo-Pacific plate movement
Authors: Zhongyu Wang, Shuping Chen, Rui Zhang, Zhihui Wang, Yemei Hu, Xueyao Huang, Yujie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resolving the Mesozoic tectonic nature of the Jiyang Depression is critical for understanding Yanshanian tectonism in the Bohai Bay Basin and its response to regional plate reorganization. However, its tectonic evolution during the Yanshanian tectonic cycle remains debated. Here we integrate 3D seismic interpretation, drill cores data, sedimentary-facies analysis, and quantitative fault-activity analysis to reconstruct the Yanshanian evolution of the Jiyang Depression. The results show that the depression records a staged strike-slip pull-apart evolution. In the Early Yanshanian stage (J₁–₂), NW-trending faults were few and weakly active, and the basin was characterized by limited fault-controlled subsidence and deposition of coal-bearing clastic and dolomitic siliciclastic rocks in shore-shallow lacustrine, fan-delta, and alluvial-fan settings. In the Mid-Yanshanian stage (J₃–K₁), NW-trending en echelon normal faults increased markedly in number and activity, and the basin evolved into a strongly fault-controlled system characterized by braided-channel, braided-river delta, and alluvial-fan deposition accompanied by intense volcanism. In the Late Yanshanian stage (K₂), regional compression terminated the pull-apart evolution and caused widespread uplift and erosion, removing most of the Upper Cretaceous succession. These results indicate that the Jiyang Depression was a Yanshanian strike-slip pull-apart basin that evolved through weak and strong developmental stages before late compressional inversion. The timing and style of this evolution were closely linked to changes in the direction and rate of Paleo-Pacific plate subduction, highlighting a clear tectonostratigraphic response of an intraplate strike-slip basin to far-field plate reorganization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54353-y.

Introduction

The Jiyang Depression is located in the southeastern Bohai Bay Basin of eastern China. The Bohai Bay Basin is a major intracontinental basin developed on the eastern North China Craton (Fig. 1a), and the Jiyang Depression is one of its most important structural depressions (Fig. 1b). The Yanshanian evolution of the Jiyang Depression controlled basin filling and volcanism and also recorded key information on major Mesozoic tectonic reorganization in eastern China1–3. However, despite decades of study, the basin nature and evolutionary history of the depression during the Yanshanian tectonic cycle remain controversial. Resolving this issue is important not only for understanding the tectonic development of the basin itself, but also for placing East Asian examples in a broader global framework of strike-slip basin evolution.

The Jiyang Depression lies between the Tan–Lu Fault Zone to the east and the Lan–Liao Fault Zone to the west (Fig. 1). Previous studies have proposed four main interpretations for the Yanshanian tectonic nature of the Jiyang Depression. These include: (1) a back-arc extensional basin related to NNW-directed subduction of the Paleo-Pacific Plate beneath Eurasia and the resulting regional extension in eastern North China4–10; (2) a mantle-upwelling or detachment-related extensional basin associated with lithospheric thinning, metamorphic core complex development, and regional rifting in eastern North China11–13; (3) a strike-slip pull-apart basin, in which sinistral motion along the Tan–Lu Fault Zone generated a sinistral shear regime, induced negative inversion of pre-existing NW-trending reverse faults, and formed en echelon pull-apart depocenters14–19(Fig. 2); and (4) a compressional-inversion basin, in which multiple phases of compression and inversion occurred during the Yanshanian, with Middle Jurassic deformation dominated by folding and Late Jurassic deformation characterized by stronger negative inversion20,21. Although these models differ in tectonic mechanism, they are all based on several widely recognized first-order observations, including the widespread development of NW-trending faults, strong spatial heterogeneity in Jurassic–Cretaceous thickness, onlap of younger strata onto regional unconformities, and marked stage-like variations in sedimentary and volcanic records.

These studies are the reflection of a broader problem: how strike-slip fault systems govern basin formation and evolution. Strike-slip basins record how regional stress is transferred, partitioned, and released along major fault zones. In releasing bends and stepovers of active strike-slip systems, transtensional deformation commonly produces pull-apart basins, fault-bounded depressions, and en echelon normal faults22–24. Classical examples from the San Andreas transform system, including the San Gregorio Basin formed in a transtensional stepover setting and basins related to the Rodgers Creek–Maacama fault system, where later fault-zone reorganization modified earlier depocenters, show that basin subsidence, depocenter segmentation, and later structural overprinting are all strongly controlled by the geometry and kinematics of strike-slip fault systems25,26. More generally, studies of Riedel shear systems and strike-slip basins have demonstrated that en echelon fault arrays, releasing zones, and segmented basin architecture are common geometric expressions of strike-slip-related deformation across a wide range of tectonic settings27.

In eastern China, the Mesozoic evolution of the Jiyang Depression was shaped by two major tectonic events. In this study, the Indosinian orogeny broadly refers to the Late Triassic compressional tectonic event associated with amalgamation between the North China Craton and the South China Block28,29(Fig. 1a), whereas the subsequent Yanshanian tectonic cycle broadly refers to the Jurassic–Cretaceous tectonic evolution of eastern China30,31. Structurally and stratigraphically, the preserved Yanshanian succession of the Jiyang Depression consists mainly of Jurassic–Lower Cretaceous strata separated by major regional unconformities, and records a transition from earlier compressional inheritance to subsequent Yanshanian fault-controlled basin development. In the Bohai Bay Basin and the Jiyang Depression, this tectonic evolution is further expressed by reactivation of inherited structures, development of fault-controlled depocenters, stratigraphic truncation, volcanic–sedimentary successions, and regionally developed unconformities14,32,33. More broadly, multiple strike-slip pull-apart basins have been recognized along the Tan–Lu Fault Zone34,35. These regional comparisons suggest that Mesozoic basin development in eastern China was controlled not only by local structural inheritance, but also by a broader strike-slip tectonic regime associated with the Tan–Lu fault system and its surrounding fault networks.

Against this background, the key scientific question is the tectonic nature of the Jiyang Depression during the Yanshanian tectonic cycle and how it responded to Paleo-Pacific plate motion. To address the question, this study integrates 3D seismic interpretation, drill cores data, sedimentary-facies analysis, and quantitative fault-activity analysis to reconstruct the Yanshanian evolution of the Jiyang Depression. We aim to clarify the geometry and kinematics of the major Mesozoic fault systems, constrain the tectonostratigraphic response of the basin, and evaluate the relationship between staged basin development and regional plate reorganization.

Geological setting

The Jiyang Depression, located in the southeastern Bohai Bay Basin, covers approximately 2.6 × 10⁴ km² and represents a major Mesozoic–Cenozoic structural depression in eastern China1–3. It is bounded by the Luxi Uplift to the south, the Chengning Uplift and Huanghua Depression to the north, the Tan–Lu Fault Zone to the east, and the Lan–Liao Fault Zone to the west (Fig. 1). Its sedimentary cover unconformably overlies the Archean and Paleozoic basement, and the basin preserves a relatively complete Mesozoic–Cenozoic tectono-sedimentary record.

The tectonic history of the Jiyang Depression comprises several major stages, including Paleozoic platform development, Triassic thrusting and erosion, Jurassic–Early Paleogene rifting, and Late Paleogene to present-day post-rift subsidence14,36,37. The rift stage can be further divided into Yanshanian and Himalayan substages4,5(Fig. 2). In this study, the Yanshanian tectonic cycle broadly refers to the Jurassic–Cretaceous tectonic evolution of the basin, during which NW-trending faults developed widely within the depression8,38–41. In contrast, the Himalayan substage was characterized mainly by the development of NEE-trending normal faults5,30.

Stratigraphically, the basement consists of Archean crystalline rocks overlain by a series of Phanerozoic sedimentary successions, including the Cambrian–Ordovician depositional cycle, the Carboniferous–Permian paralic depositional cycle, the Mesozoic continental–volcanic depositional cycle, and the Cenozoic continental depositional cycle42(Fig. 2). The preserved Mesozoic succession comprises, in ascending order, the Fangzi Formation (J1f), Santai Formation (J2s), Mengyin Formation (J3m), and Xiwa Formation (K1x), where J1, J2, J3, and K1 denote the Lower Jurassic, Middle Jurassic, Upper Jurassic, and Lower Cretaceous, respectively. These units are characterized respectively by coal-bearing clastic rocks, dolomitic siliciclastic and coarse clastic deposits, clastic rocks with local tuffaceous intervals, and volcanic rocks with associated coarse clastic deposits21,42–47. Three major unconformities are recognized: between the Permian and Lower Jurassic, between the Middle and Upper Jurassic, and between the Lower Cretaceous and Paleogene43,44. These unconformities record multiple episodes of tectonic activity, uplift, and erosion (Fig. 3).

Data and methods

Datasets

Seismic dataset

This study is primarily based on a high-quality 3D seismic volume covering the Jiyang Depression, Bohai Bay Basin. This seismic dataset, mosaicked from 14 individual 3D seismic surveys, has a total coverage of 4.7 × 10⁴ km², enabling full spatial coverage of the entire Jiyang Depression and its principal Mesozoic structural units. The seismic volume contains 72.303 million traces, each with 6,000 samples, a trace spacing of 25 m, and a maximum record length of 5 s two-way travel time (TWT). In this study, structural and stratigraphic interpretation was conducted in the time domain.

Drill Cores dataset

This study analyzed 14 drilling wells. The available well information includes formation tops, lithologic logs, published well reports and conventional wireline logs, such as SP, AC, DEN, etc. These drill cores data were used to calibrate seismic horizons, identify lithologic successions in the Jurassic–Cretaceous interval, and support the interpretation of sedimentary facies. Representative wells, including ZG37, were used to characterize the lithologic and stratigraphic features of the Fangzi, Santai, Mengyin, and Xiwa formations. The drill cores data were interpreted in combination with previous stratigraphic and sedimentological studies.

Seismic interpretation and well–seismic calibration

Seismic interpretation was carried out using Petrel. The basic stratigraphic framework of the Mesozoic succession throughout the 3D seismic survey was previously calibrated by Sinopec Shengli Oilfield. On this basis, the seismic profiles presented in this study were reinterpreted in detail, with emphasis on horizon continuity, horizon tie point, recognition of Mesozoic unconformities, and fault interpretation. The interpretation focused on the Mesozoic structural and stratigraphic framework of the Jiyang Depression, including major Jurassic–Cretaceous boundaries, key unconformities, and the principal NW-trending fault systems. Well–seismic calibration was established by first applying the time–depth relationship to the drill cores data, and then generating synthetic seismograms from the sonic and density logs (Fig. 2). Horizon picking was guided by well ties, reflection continuity, reflector terminations, and regional stratigraphic relationships. Fault interpretation was based on reflector offsets, thickness changes across fault blocks, fault-plane continuity, and the geometry of growth strata in fault hanging walls.

Quantitative fault analysis

To quantify fault displacement, a regional time-to-depth conversion was first performed to construct depth sections. Representative wells were tied to seismic profiles using synthetic seismograms, and interval velocity data from the calibrated wells were used to establish a regional velocity model for converting interpreted TWT horizons and fault geometries into depth. Quantitative fault analysis was used to assess the timing and intensity of Yanshanian fault activity. Fault activity rate was calculated as A = D/T, where A is fault activity rate (m·Ma− 1), D is fault displacement (m) within a given stratigraphic interval, and T is the deposition time (Ma)48,49. Displacement was measured on interpreted seismic profiles by correlating equivalent stratigraphic markers across faults. The analysis was applied to 12 representative faults or fault segments across the Jiyang Depression and used to distinguish the Early Yanshanian stage (J1–2) from the Mid-Yanshanian stage (J3–K1). Owing to uncertainties related to horizon picking, erosion, and fault-zone complexity, the calculated rates are regarded primarily as comparative measures of relative fault activity.

Sedimentary-facies interpretation

Sedimentary-facies interpretation was based on the integration of seismic reflection characteristics, stratal stacking patterns, lithologic successions, and well-log responses. The interpretation focused on the preserved Jurassic–Cretaceous successions, especially the Fangzi, Santai, Mengyin, and Xiwa formations, which record clear changes in depositional style through time.

On seismic profiles, fault-margin depositional systems were identified through the integration of seismic data and well information. The key criteria include their close spatial association with active boundary faults, abrupt stratigraphic thickening toward the hanging wall, and distinctive seismic geometries. Near fault zones, laterally continuous parallel reflections commonly disappear and are replaced by wedge-shaped or lens-shaped reflection bodies. Alluvial-fan deposits are commonly expressed as wedge-shaped bodies with chaotic to weak continuous internal reflections adjacent to steep fault margins, whereas nearshore subaqueous-fan and fan-delta deposits are characterized by wedge-shaped to progradational reflection packages in fault hanging walls. Lithologic information from representative wells was also used to support the facies interpretation50. These seismic facies interpretations were calibrated against lithologic and log data from representative wells.

Structural dynamics analysis

To constrain the geodynamic formation mechanism of the Jiyang Depression within the plate tectonic framework, we conducted structural-dynamic analysis based on the classic Riedel shear model35,51,52. Using fault attitude, spatial distribution, and geometric association data from seismic interpretation and structural mapping, we focused on the en echelon arrangement of NW-trending secondary normal faults, their angular relationship with the Tan–Lu Fault Zone (TLFZ, regional strike-slip principal displacement zone), and local extensional structures. We verified the compatibility of the observed fault array with the structural pattern of a TLFZ-related sinistral strike-slip stress regime, with the results providing robust kinematic constraints for interpreting the basin’s structural evolution and formation dynamics.

The Jiyang Depression developed NW-trending faults during the Mesozoic. Some NW-trending normal faults are negative inversions of Indosinian thrusts (Fig. 4a), whereas others are newly formed Yanshanian faults (Fig. 4b).

Indosinian negative-inversion faults

Before the Indosinian orogeny, no major compressional deformation is recognized on the North China Platform. Where Paleozoic strata show pinch-outs or erosion along fault planes and the overlying section is unaffected, the thrusting is attributed to the Indosinian29–31. The Jurassic–Paleozoic unconformity records this event53. This Indosinian compression is closely related to the west-to-east scissor-like collision between the North China and South China blocks in the Late Triassic (T₃). The collision produced far-field compressional stress in the eastern North China Craton, and drove deformation transmission through major tectonic belts including the Tan–Lu Fault Zone. In the Jiyang Depression, this event formed a suite of NW-trending thrust faults that were negatively inverted during the Yanshanian period.

A set of NW-trending faults—including Shaojia, Luoxi, Guxi, Chengbei, Yihezhuang, Chengnan, Shicun, Wanggu-1, and Wuhaozhuang (Figs. 5, 6, 7 and 8) are identified as Indosinian thrusts. Evidence includes eroded Paleozoic rocks in their hanging walls and angular unconformities. Newly formed Yanshanian normal faults

Seismic data show widespread NW-trending normal faults in the Yanshanian, which strongly controlled Jurassic–Cretaceous sedimentation.

Geometric characteristics

On NE–SW seismic profiles (Figs. 5, 6 and 7), Mesozoic strata in the hanging walls of faults such as Binxi (F1), Shaojia (F2), Luoxi (F3), Guxi (F4), Chengbei (F5), Yihezhuang (F6), Chengnan (F7), Shicun (F8), Wanggu-1 (F9), and Chennan (F10) are notably thicker. Jurassic–Cretaceous strata are truncated beneath the Cenozoic. Wedge-shaped chaotic reflections and marginal-facies sand bodies next to boundary faults point to fault-controlled deposition.

In the northeastern depression, Jurassic–Cretaceous thickness varies due to two processes: syndepositional faulting and later fault modification. The Wuhaozhuang fault (F13), with fault-margin facies in its hanging wall, reflects syndepositional normal faulting. In contrast, the Changdi (F14), Gudong (F15), and Kendong faults (F16) lack margin facies but show truncated unconformities in their footwalls, implying that thickness differences mainly reflect later normal-fault modification (Fig. 8). In the western depression, normal faulting was weaker. The Baiqiao fault (F11) was active mainly in the Mid-Yanshanian Stage (J₃–K₁), and its hanging wall contains relatively thin Mesozoic strata. The Chexi fault (F12) shows no signs of Early Yanshanian Stage (J₁–₂) activity—Middle–Lower Jurassic thickness is similar across both walls—and displays clear normal-faulting only by the Mid-Yanshanian Stage (J₃–K₁) (Fig. 9).

On a NW–SE profile across the Bamianhe (F18) and Wangjiagang (F17) structural belts and the Central Uplift Belt of the Dongying Sag (Fig. 10), large NE-trending faults (e.g., the central–western Chennan Fault) formed mainly in the Cenozoic under Himalayan tectonism. By contrast, strike-slip faults (e.g., Bamianhe, Wangjiagang) and stress-overlap zones (e.g., the Central Uplift Belt) were activated later, mostly during deposition of the Shahejie Formation. This documents a timing pattern of “NW-trending in the Mesozoic, NE-trending in the Cenozoic.”

In summary, Mesozoic structures in the Jiyang Depression are dominated by NW-trending faults. Among them, Shaojia, Shicun, Yihezhuang, Luoxi, Wanggu-1, Chengnan, Guxi, Chennan, Chengbei, and Wuhaozhuang are negative-inversion faults, whereas Baiqiao, Chexi, and Binxi were newly formed.

In the Early Yanshanian Stage (J₁–₂), only some NW-trending normal faults (e.g., Luoxi, Shicun) began to move. Activity rates were low overall, averaging 5.56 m/Ma; Shaojia was highest at 15.76 m/Ma (Table 1). Normal faulting was localized and weak, and the fault system was not yet linked. Accordingly, Middle–Lower Jurassic strata are relatively thin and show parallel reflections on seismic data (Fig. 5).

In the Mid-Yanshanian Stage (J₃–K₁), most major faults became active. Average rates rose to 33.71 m/Ma, much higher than in the early stage; Chennan reached 83.13 m/Ma (Table 1). Widespread, strong normal faulting integrated the fault system. The Upper Jurassic-Lower Cretaceous strata extensively onlap the Middle Jurassic in the Zhanhua Sag (Fig. 5), Chezhen Sag (Fig. 6), and Dongying Sag (Fig. 7). The sedimentary bodies thicken in a wedge shape, producing a seismic facies defined by wedge-shaped reflections whose terminations converge at the underlying unconformity.

Mesozoic sedimentary characteristics of the Jiyang depression

Affected by multiple tectonic events, the Triassic and Upper Cretaceous in the Jiyang Depression were widely eroded, leaving an incomplete Mesozoic record. The preserved succession, in ascending order, comprises the Fangzi Formation (J₁f), Santai Formation (J₂s), Mengyin Formation (J₃m), and Xiwa Formation (K₁x) (Fig. 11). Marked differences in lithologic assemblages and sedimentary facies between the Lower–Middle Jurassic (Fangzi and Santai formations) and the Upper Jurassic–Lower Cretaceous (Mengyin and Xiwa formations) record a staged response to Yanshanian strike-slip pull-apart activity.

Lithologic characteristics

The Fangzi Formation (J₁f) is dominated by dark, coal-bearing clastic rocks. Its lower part consists mainly of interbedded gray-black to gray-green sandstone and mudstone, the middle part contains coal seams, and the upper part grades upward into light-gray to purple sandstone and mudstone (Fig. 3). The coal-bearing intervals indicate humid lacustrine-swamp conditions, whereas the overall lithologic assemblage suggests relatively weak tectonic activity and very limited volcanic input during this stage45.

The Santai Formation (J₂s) is characterized by gray-white to purple-red dolomitic siliciclastic rocks. The lower part contains conglomerate and pebbly sandstone, whereas the middle and upper parts are composed mainly of interbedded siltstone and mudstone (Fig. 3). The abundance of coarse clastic material in the lower part indicates enhanced proximal sediment supply, reflecting stronger fault activity and increased relief along basin margins compared with the Fangzi Formation. Volcanic material is still scarce, indicating that volcanism remained weak during this stage21,46,47.

The Mengyin Formation (J₃m) consists mainly of interbedded variegated pebbly sandstone and gray sandstone in the lower part, and interbedded gray-purple mudstone and sandstone in the upper part. Tuffaceous sandstone and tuffaceous conglomerate in the WG2 well indicate the onset of volcanic activity and eruptive facies21,46,47(Fig. 3). These lithologic features suggest that faulting remained active and increased in scale, while basin fill became more strongly influenced by both coarse clastic input and volcanism.

The Xiwa Formation (K₁x) is characterized by a marked increase in volcanic rocks. Its lower part contains siliceous rocks, lamprophyre, tuff, and basalt, whereas the upper part is dominated mainly by andesite (Fig. 3). In addition, coarse clastic deposits are locally developed in association with volcanic rocks. These lithologic associations indicate intense volcanic eruptions, widespread eruptive facies, and close coupling between magmatism and fault activity during the final stage of basin filling21,46,47.

Sedimentary-facies characteristics

During the Early–Middle Jurassic, sedimentation in the study area was already influenced by NW-trending fault activity, although the basin as a whole remained in a relatively weak pull-apart stage. The Fangzi Formation is dominated by shore-shallow lacustrine and fan-delta facies (Fig. 3), indicating the presence of shallow lacustrine basins21,46,54,55. Faulting had begun to influence basin distribution and basin-margin geometry, but its intensity remained low (Fig. 11a). During deposition of the Santai Formation, alluvial-fan facies became widespread (Fig. 3), reflecting increased fault activity, greater basin-margin relief, and enhanced supply of proximal coarse clastics21,46,54,55. Even so, the lacustrine basins remained relatively small and isolated, and the fault network was not yet fully connected (Fig. 11b). The climate during this interval was relatively humid. Sediment supply was probably derived mainly from local transverse input from adjacent fault-block uplifts and basin-margin catchments rather than from a large regional axial sediment-routing system56. The predominance of shore-shallow lacustrine, fan-delta, and alluvial-fan deposits, together with the absence of deep-water facies, indicates that these early depocentres were generally balanced to slightly oversupplied under limited accommodation creation.

From the Late Jurassic to Early Cretaceous, the climate became progressively drier, and the depositional system changed markedly as the major NW-trending faults remained active and expanded. From deposition of the Mengyin Formation onward, braided-channel and braided-river delta facies became widespread, accompanied by tuffaceous sandstone and eruptive volcanic facies (Fig. 3). These features indicate continued fault activity, increased fault scale, enlarged accommodation, and expansion of the water body. Previously isolated lacustrine basins began to connect, and the basin area increased significantly (Fig. 11c). Individual braided-river delta systems extend up to 15.32 km along strike parallel to the boundary faults and prograde up to 15.16 km toward the basin center47. During deposition of the Xiwa Formation, alluvial-fan facies again became dominant and were accompanied by intense eruptive volcanic facies (Fig. 3), indicating another increase in fault activity (Fig. 11d). During this stage, basin fill was controlled mainly by proximal local sediment supply and intense volcanism. The near-fault basin-margin zones (steep-slope belts) were locally high-supply and strongly progradational, whereas the depositional center areas (gentle-slope belts), where subsidence was more rapid, were relatively accommodation-dominated and locally underfilled56. The peak in volcanic activity was temporally coupled with the peak in fault activity rates55,57,58. These facies changes record a pulsed tectonic evolution and correspond well to the weak and strong pull-apart stages recognized in the study area.

Discussion

Basin nature of the Jiyang depression during the Yanshanian tectonic cycle

The Yanshanian geodynamic origin of the Jiyang Depression remains controversial. Our results suggest that the Yanshanian evolution of the depression is better interpreted as a strike-slip-influenced pull-apart process rather than as a purely extensional or compressional basin system. This interpretation is supported by the predominance of NW-trending faults, their en echelon arrangement, their syndepositional control on Jurassic–Cretaceous thickness and facies distribution, and their geometric compatibility with a sinistral strike-slip regime related to the Tan–Lu Fault Zone. This compatibility is also reflected in the consistent angle of about 35°–55° between the NW-trending Mesozoic faults and the Tan–Lu Fault Zone, which matches the expected geometry of subsidiary faults in a strike-slip stress field.

Two-stage pull-apart evolution of the Yanshanian

The Yanshanian evolution of the Jiyang Depression records a single pull-apart process expressed in two stages of different intensity, followed by late compressional overprinting. This subdivision is constrained not only by fault activity rates, but also by integrated fault geometry, kinematics, seismic facies, stratigraphic architecture, lithologic assemblages, and sedimentary-facies changes. The Middle–Upper Jurassic unconformity is the critical boundary between the two stages. Below this unconformity, the Lower–Middle Jurassic succession is characterized mainly by parallel seismic reflections, whereas above it the Upper Jurassic–Lower Cretaceous succession shows wedge-shaped growth strata, indicating significantly enhanced growth faulting and differential accommodation.

In the Early Yanshanian stage (J₁–₂), the basin was already in a pull-apart stage, but faulting was still weak. Geometrically, NW-trending normal faults were few in number, arranged broadly in parallel, and constituted the primary fault system. Kinematically, fault activity rates were low, with an average of 5.56 m/Ma and a maximum of 15.76 m/Ma. Correspondingly, subsidence was modest, basin-wide linkage was limited, and the Lower–Middle Jurassic succession is characterized mainly by parallel seismic reflections. Sedimentation during this stage was dominated first by shore-shallow lacustrine and fan-delta facies in the Fangzi Formation, and then by widespread alluvial-fan deposition in the Santai Formation (Fig. 3), indicating a weak pull-apart system with increasing but still limited fault control and little to no volcanism.

In the Mid-Yanshanian stage (J₃–K₁), pre-existing thrusts underwent negative inversion, many new NW-trending normal faults formed, and the fault system increased markedly in both number and length. Fault activity rates rose sharply, with an average of 33.71 m/Ma and a maximum of 83.13 m/Ma. Seismic facies changed from dominantly parallel reflections to wedge-shaped reflections, with reflectors onlapping onto the Middle–Upper Jurassic unconformity. Depositional systems shifted to braided fluvial and braided-river delta facies, accompanied by mixed volcaniclastic and coarse-clastic deposits. (Fig. 3). The appearance of tuffaceous sandstones and eruptive volcanic facies indicates strong coupling between pull-apart deformation and volcanism. During deposition of the Xiwa Formation, renewed alluvial-fan development together with intense volcanic eruptive facies suggests that fault activity reached another peak within this strong pull-apart stage (Fig. 3).

These contrasts indicate that the Mesozoic evolution of the Jiyang Depression records a single pull-apart process expressed in two stages of different intensity. The Late Yanshanian stage (K2) marks the end of this process, when widespread uplift and erosion removed much of the Upper Cretaceous and the tectonic regime shifted from pull-apart extension to compression.

Response of staged basin evolution to Paleo-Pacific plate movement

Paleo-Pacific plate subduction changed during the Yanshanian tectonic cycle and exerted an important influence on the tectonic evolution of the North China Craton40,59–61. The subduction direction changed from NNW to NWW, and the convergence rate varied from ~ 4.7 cm/yr to ~ 20.7 cm/yr and then to ~ 13.1 cm/yr38,59,60,62. In this regional setting, the staged evolution of the Jiyang Depression can be interpreted as the response of an intraplate basin to changing far-field stress transmitted through major regional fault systems, particularly the Tan–Lu Fault Zone and the Lan–Liao Fault Zone.

During the Early Yanshanian stage (J₁–₂), NNW-directed Paleo-Pacific plate subduction likely produced a regional sinistral strike-slip stress field in eastern North China. Under this stress regime, sinistral strike-slip movement along the Tan–Lu Fault Zone promoted negative inversion of pre-existing NW-trending Indosinian thrusts and initiated pull-apart development in the Jiyang Depression. At this stage, however, the overall scale of strike-slip deformation was still limited. This is reflected by the relatively small number of active NW-trending faults, the low average fault activity rate (5.56 m/Ma), and the predominance of parallel reflections in the Lower–Middle Jurassic succession. The lithologic and sedimentary response was also relatively weak: the Fangzi Formation is dominated by shore-shallow lacustrine and fan-delta facies, whereas the Santai Formation is characterized mainly by alluvial-fan deposition. These features indicate that fault-controlled accommodation had already begun to develop, but that basin linkage, basin-margin relief, and volcanism were still limited (Fig. 12a).

During the Mid-Yanshanian stage (J₃–K₁), the increase in Paleo-Pacific plate convergence rate was accompanied by a marked strengthening of regional strike-slip deformation. Under this tectonic setting, the strike-slip influence of the Tan–Lu Fault Zone became stronger, and the Lan–Liao Fault Zone also became involved in the regional stress-transfer system. As a result, pre-existing NW-trending reverse faults were more extensively reactivated by negative inversion, while newly formed NW-trending normal faults, such as the Baiqiao, Chexi, and Binxi faults, developed progressively. Together with intensified activity on the inherited fault systems, these faults controlled the large-scale development of fault-bounded half-grabens within the depression. This tectonic strengthening is reflected by the sharp increase in average fault activity rate to 33.71 m/Ma, the widespread development of wedge-shaped growth strata, and the regional onlap of Upper Jurassic–Lower Cretaceous strata onto the Middle–Upper Jurassic unconformity (Fig. 12b).

The sedimentary and lithologic response to this stronger pull-apart stage was equally clear. From deposition of the Mengyin Formation onward, the depositional system shifted from the relatively restricted lacustrine, fan-delta, and alluvial-fan assemblages of the Early Yanshanian stage to braided fluvial and braided-river delta systems, accompanied by tuffaceous deposits and eruptive volcanic facies. These changes indicate stronger fault control, greater basin-margin relief, enlarged accommodation, and progressive basin expansion and linkage. During deposition of the Xiwa Formation, renewed alluvial-fan development together with widespread volcanic rocks indicates that tectonic activity and volcanism reached their strongest coupling. This transition is consistent with progressive strengthening of strike-slip pull-apart development under intensified Paleo-Pacific plate subduction.

By the Late Yanshanian stage (K₂), the Paleo-Pacific plate had shifted to NWW-directed subduction, and the regional stress regime changed accordingly. Under a near E–W compressional stress field, pull-apart development in the Jiyang Depression was terminated and replaced by regional uplift and erosion. This late compressional overprint accounts for the widespread truncation of the Mesozoic succession and the removal of most of the Upper Cretaceous strata in the study area. Although temporal correspondence alone does not prove a direct geodynamic linkage, the combined structural, stratigraphic, sedimentary, and volcanic evidence is broadly consistent with that the staged Yanshanian evolution of the Jiyang Depression was closely related to changes in Paleo-Pacific plate motion (Fig. 12c).
