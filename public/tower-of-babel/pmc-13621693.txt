Diagnostic performance of a centrifugation-free stool processing kit in conjunction with Xpert®MTB/RIF Ultra for paediatric tuberculosis: a multicentre prospective study in five low and middle-income countries

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiagnosing paediatric tuberculosis (TB) remains challenging. Currently available diagnostic tests have limited accuracy, and most sampling methods are invasive. In 2021, the World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed the use of molecular WHO-recommended rapid diagnostic tests (mWRDs) with stool as an initial diagnostic tool for paediatric TB. In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) of stool samples processed with the Stool Processing Kit (SPK), one of three centrifugation-free methods available at the time.MethodsWe conducted a multi-centre prospective diagnostic validation study in South Africa, Tanzania, Malawi, Mozambique, and India. Children (< 15 years) under investigation for TB provided at least two (induced) sputum samples at enrolment, one stool sample, and one nasopharyngeal aspirate (NPA) if aged < 5 years and a chest x-ray. TB testing included Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra (Ultra) and culture. Diagnostic classification followed NIH consensus statements. Stool samples were processed using the SPK and tested with Ultra. We calculated DTA in the overall cohort and predefined subgroups of interest.ResultsOut of 5313 children screened, 975 were enrolled, and data from 817 children recruited between January 2019 and June 2021 were analysed. The overall microbiological confirmation rate was 203/817 (24.9%), and an additional 358/817 (43.8%) were classified as unconfirmed TB and 256/817 (31.3%) were classified as unlikely TB. The median age was 5.3 years (IQR: 2.0–9.2), and 390/817 (47.7%) of participants were under the age of five. 134/817 (16.4%) of children were living with HIV, and 88/817 (10.8%) had severe acute malnutrition (SAM). The overall sensitivity and specificity of SPK/Ultra against a Strict Reference Standard (SRS) were 33.8% (95% CI: 27.6–40.6) and 86.8% (95% CI: 80.5–91.3), respectively. Among children under one year of age and those with SAM, sensitivity was 43.3% (95% CI: 27.4–60.8) and 41.4% (95% CI: 25.5–59.3), respectively, with specificity reaching 100% in young children and 85% (95% CI: 64.0-94.8) in children with SAM. Classic TB symptoms and chest X-ray abnormalities at baseline were associated with increased likelihood of SPK/Ultra positivity.ConclusionsThis is one of the largest studies to evaluate stool as an alternative specimen for diagnosing paediatric TB. Although the WHO’s target product profile was not fully met, stool testing with SPK/Ultra represents a viable alternative to invasive sampling methods, especially in subgroups of interest.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03734172, June 22, 2018.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13902-6.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Child*, Diagnostics, Stool, mWRDs

Introduction

Paediatric tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality globally. In 2023, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that children accounted for 11% of all TB cases, with this proportion reaching up to 15% in high-burden settings such as sub-Saharan Africa [1]. While treatment is often well-tolerated and successful, diagnosing TB in children remains difficult due to the paucibacillary nature of the disease and the limited accuracy of current diagnostic tools. In addition, most respiratory sampling methods, such as gastric aspirates and induced sputum, are invasive or semi-invasive, often distressing for both children and caregivers, and require infrastructure and expertise that are frequently unavailable in resource-limited settings [2].

Recent efforts have focused on identifying pain-free, easy-to-collect specimens, such as stool, for use with molecular WHO-recommended rapid diagnostic tests (mWRDs). In 2021, the WHO endorsed the use of stool samples tested with Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra (Ultra) as an initial diagnostic tool for TB and rifampicin resistance in children with presumptive pulmonary TB [3, 4].

In a recent meta-analysis of 14 studies involving children under 15 years of age, stool testing with Ultra has demonstrated diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of respiratory specimens: stool-pooled sensitivity 56.1% (95% CI 39.1–71.7), specificity 98.0% (95% CI 93.3 to 99.4) and sputum sensitivity 75.3% (95% CI 64.3–83.8), and specificity was 97.1% (95% CI 94.7–98.5) [5]. Several processing methods have been developed to enable the use of stool for TB diagnosis in low-resource settings, with an emphasis on centrifugation-free approaches: the Optimised Sucrose Flotation (OSF), described by the TB-Speed Consortium [6], which concentrates Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) DNA using sugar-based flotation, but requires moderate infrastructure, including a biosafety cabinet, due to its aerosol-generating potential; the Simple One-Step (SOS), which relies solely on the Ultra sample buffer, requires no additional equipment or reagents, and poses minimal aerosolization risk, making it attractive for decentralized implementation [7] and lastly, the Stool Processing Kit (SPK), developed by the Foundation for Innovative New Diagnostics (FIND), which provides a simplified, standardized method for stool preparation using a proprietary buffer designed to remove PCR inhibitors [8]. Like SOS, it avoids the need for centrifugation and generates minimal aerosols, making it suitable for low-resource settings. No significant difference in diagnostic accuracy has been observed across the three methods, with reported sensitivity ranging between 46 and 52% and 97% specificity, with overlapping confidence intervals [2]. However, the data from previous studies are based on relatively small, heterogeneous cohorts, and there is limited evidence available on the accuracy of the SPK in large-scale accuracy studies and subgroups of interest [9].

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of SPK combined with Ultra (SPK/Ultra) within a large multi-country cohort of children investigated for TB. We assessed diagnostic performance indicators in the overall cohort as well as key subgroups, against multiple reference standards, and evaluated the factors associated with a higher likelihood of test positivity.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective, multi-centre diagnostic accuracy study in India, Malawi, Mozambique, South Africa, and Tanzania (RaPaed-TB). The overall study methodology has been described in detail elsewhere [10]. Briefly, children under 15 years of age with presumptive TB were consecutively enrolled between January 2019 and June 2021 from peripheral health facilities and tertiary hospitals. Children were excluded if they had presented with one of the following: a critical condition, body weight less than 2 kg, or if they had received more than three doses of anti-TB treatment at the time of screening.

At enrolment, microbiological investigations included two sputum samples at least 6 h apart, and one stool sample collected at baseline. For children under five years of age, a NPA (Nasopharyngeal Aspirate) was also collected. Sputum samples were processed for MTB detection using Xpert® MTB/RIF Ultra (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA), culture using BACTEC™ MGIT™ 960 system (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and Löwenstein-Jensen (LJ) solid media, as previously described [10, 11]. Stool and NPA samples were tested with Ultra only [11]. In the event of an indeterminate result, the test was repeated with the remaining sample.

Clinical and demographic assessments included a structured medical history, anthropometric measurements, physical examination, HIV testing, and chest X-ray. All children diagnosed with TB were referred for treatment in accordance with national guidelines. After enrolment, follow-up visits were conducted at months 1 and 3. For those who were unwell or receiving TB treatment, an additional follow-up visit occurred at month 6.

Stool collection and processing

At baseline, caregivers were provided with a stool collection container and instructed to collect a fresh stool sample the following day. For infants and toddlers wearing diapers, the study team collected samples on-site. All specimens were transported to the laboratory on the same day of collection and stored at 2–8 °C for a maximum of 48 h before testing, following previously described protocols [12].

Stool samples were processed using the Stool Processing Kit (SPK; FIND) [8]. Briefly, 1 mL of stool was mixed with the provided processing buffer and Ultra Sample Reagent, incubated for 30 min, and then transferred to an Ultra cartridge for testing on the GeneXpert platform. Results were reported qualitatively (MTB detected or not detected) and semi-quantitatively (high, medium, low, very low, or trace). Rifampicin resistance was reported only when MTB was detected with a signal other than “trace.” At the time of the study, stool testing was not part of the standard of care; therefore, results were blinded to clinicians and not used for clinical decision-making.

Clinical case definitions and reference standards

Children were retrospectively classified as Confirmed TB (if culture and /or Ultra positive on sputum or NPA), Unconfirmed TB (if sputum or NPA Ultra or culture negative, but suggestive signs and symptoms or Chest X-ray abnormalities (as per expert review), or Unlikely TB (if the previous did not apply) based on the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) consensus clinical case definitions [13] (Supplement, Table 1).

Due to the low sensitivity of sputum culture in children, diagnostic test accuracy (DTA) was assessed using two reference standards. The Strict Reference Standard (SRS) compared children with Confirmed TB to those with Unlikely TB restricted to those with either an Xpert or culture result available. The Composite Reference Standard (CRS) compared children with either Confirmed or Unconfirmed TB to those with Unlikely TB. Unless otherwise stated, data presented from this point forward are based on the SRS.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort. The diagnostic performance of SPK/Ultra was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), each with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used to explore associations between SPK/Ultra positivity and clinical or demographic factors; variables included in both the univariable and multivariable model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unclassifiable children were removed from the analysis and missing data were treated on a complete case analysis basis. Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: Stata Corp LLC) and R Studio 4.4.2 (2024 The R Foundation for Statistical Computing), ggplot2 (ggplot function), ggVennDiagram, UpSetR and patchwork packages.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The study protocol and informed consent materials were reviewed and approved by the coordinating centre, institutional ethics committees, and relevant regulatory authorities at all participating sites (Supplementary Table 2). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from caregivers or legal guardians of all participants, and assent was obtained from children in accordance with local ethical guidelines.

Results

Of the 5,313 children screened for eligibility, 4,338 were excluded. A total of 975 children were enrolled, and SPK/Ultra testing was attempted in all those who provided a stool sample (817/975, 83.7%). Out of these, 18/817 (2.2%) were inconclusive, primarily due to invalid results. Of the remaining 799 (14.4%) tested SPK/Ultra-positive, and 684/799 (85.6%) tested SPK/Ultra negative. Final diagnostic classifications were as follows: among SPK/Ultra-positive children, 68/115 (59.1%) had confirmed TB, 23/115 (20.0%) had unconfirmed TB, and 24/115 (20.9%) were classified as unlikely TB; among SPK/Ultra-negative children, 133/684 (19.4%) had confirmed TB, 326/684 = 47.7% had unconfirmed TB, and 225/684 = 32.9% were classified as unlikely TB (Fig. 1).

The median age was 5.3 years (IQR: 2.0-9.2), and age distribution was similar between diagnostic groups, with 390/817 (47.7%) being under five years of age. The gender distribution was balanced, with 393/817 (48.1%) being female. Undernutrition was common across all TB diagnostic groups: Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM) was present in 88/817 (10.8%) of participants, with a higher prevalence among those with confirmed TB 29/203 (14.3%), compared to unconfirmed, 31/358 (8.7%), and unlikely TB, 28/256 (10.9%). 134/817 (16.4%) of children were living with HIV (CLHIV), with the highest proportion observed at the Malawian site (64/179; 35.8%) (Supplement, Table 3). ART coverage at baseline was lower than optimal, with 88/134 (65.7%) of children living with HIV being on ART (Table 1).

Overall sensitivity and specificity against the SRS were 33.8% (95% CI: 27.6–40.6) and 86.8% (95% CI: 80.5–91.3), respectively. Diagnostic accuracy varied across clinical and demographic subgroups (Fig. 2). Female participants demonstrated higher sensitivity (44.8%, 95% CI: 35.2–54.7) compared with males (23.8%, 95% CI: 16.7–32.8), while specificity was low in girls (83.8% vs. 89.7%). Among children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM), sensitivity was 41.4% (95% CI: 25.5–59.3) and specificity was 85.0% (95% CI: 64.0–94.8). Children living with HIV had lower sensitivity (23.8%, 95% CI: 10.6–45.1) and specificity (80.5%, 95% CI: 66.0–89.8) than HIV-negative children (35.0%, 95% CI: 28–42.8 and 92.3%, 95% CI: 85.0–96.2, respectively).

When stratified by MGIT time to culture positivity (TTP) and sputum Ultra semi-quantitative results, sensitivity was highest at 94.7% (95% CI: 74.0–99.9) among samples with TTP between 0 and 9 days, and 81.8% (95% CI: 48.2–97.7) among those with high semi-quantitative PCR results. There were no significant differences in diagnostic accuracy between samples processed on the same day and those processed more than 48 h after collection.

When stratified by country, sensitivity ranged from 27.1% to 43.8%, and specificity ranged from 64% to 100%. However, the confidence intervals overlapped, showing no clear differences between sites. (Supplement, Fig. 1a).

When comparing trends across the two reference standards (SRS and CRS), DTA estimates were similar, with CRS showing relatively lower estimates when compared to SRS. Overall sensitivity increased to 41.4% (95% CI:33.6–49.5) when children confirmed only by Ultra sputum trace call were recategorized as reference-standard negative (SRS), and specificity remained comparable to that of SRS.

Twenty-four children had a positive SPK/Ultra result despite being classified as unlikely TB and thus reference standard negative both in SRS and CRS. Among these, 13/24 (54.2%) had a trace result, 5/24 (20.8%) were very low, and 6/24 (25.0%) had a low semi-quantitative result. The median age was 5.1 years (IQR: 2.2-9.0), 11/24 (45.8%) children were under the age of five, and only one child had previous TB. A total of 8/24 (33.3%) were living with HIV. Four children had SAM, and 4/24 (16.7%) had a chest X-ray retrospectively classified as likely TB. One child initiated TB treatment but died during follow-up. At the end of follow-up, 4/24 (16.7%) children remained symptomatic. No child had a positive TST. We outlined the clinical and demographic characteristics of these participants in detail (Supplement, Fig. 2).

Per-patient analysis showed that 335/586 (57.2%) children were diagnosed solely on the basis of clinical/radiological findings. Only 55/586 (9.4%) were positive on SPK/Ultra, culture, and Xpert simultaneously. Notably, SPK/Ultra identified an additional 23/586 (3.9%) children who would not otherwise have achieved microbiological confirmation (Fig. 3). Among the 115 children with positive SPK/Ultra results, 45/115 (39.1%) were classified as trace, 24/115 (20.9%) as very low, 31/115 (27.0%) as low, 11/115 (9.6%) as medium, and 4/115 (3.6%) as high. Among children with results above the trace category, rifampicin resistance was detected in 3/70 (4.3%); of these, only 1/3 had culture confirmation.

Discussion

The results presented here on the prospective evaluation of SPK/Ultra’s diagnostic accuracy in children with presumed TB, show moderate sensitivity and specificity against a SRS. Notably, DTA estimates were highest in infants and children with SAM, likely due to disease presentation in this age group: similar to infants, children with SAM may present with disseminated disease and severe illness at the time of diagnosis.

Our sensitivity estimates fall at the lower end of the confidence interval reported by Kay et al. [5] but are similar to more recent findings on SPK/Ultra from comparable settings, with estimates ranging from 34% to 54% [14, 15]. A study done in Uganda and Zambia reported [16] a higher sensitivity of 69.7% (95% CI: 51.3–84.4); however, that cohort included very young children (median age 1.8 years) and a higher proportion with severe acute malnutrition (60%), further supporting the assay’s applicability in this age group.

Male children had lower odds of a positive SPK/Ultra result when compared to females, a finding that has been previously reported [14, 15, 17]. One hypothesis is that female children may also harbour a higher bacillary burden due to sex-based immune differences, though the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood [18]. Likewise, children presenting with chest X-rays suggestive of TB had higher odds of testing positive, and SPK/Ultra had a higher sensitivity when time to culture positivity was shortest, supporting the hypothesis that more severe or disseminated disease and high bacillary load are more likely to result in detectable MTB DNA in stool samples [19].

SPK/Ultra demonstrated suboptimal sensitivity against sputum Ultra and culture. Similarly, specificity was particularly high in infants but remained below 90% in most clinical subgroups, which is lower than previously reported [5, 15, 17, 20]. In our cohort, a high proportion of reference standard false-positive results were seen. Our SRS was robust, incorporating children with at least two respiratory samples and stringent clinical, microbiological, and radiological assessment as well as follow-up. A high prevalence of non-TB respiratory viral infections may explain X-ray features suggestive of TB and TB-like symptoms that prompted their visit to the health facility and consequent enrolment in our study. This suggests that some false positives are more likely to reflect non-replicating bacilli, rather than active TB disease. However, the possibility of incorrect case classification, which was done retrospectively and matched clinical decisions not to treat these children for TB, cannot be completely ruled out. Due to the study design and clinicians being blinded to SPK/Ultra results, long-term follow-up to assess whether there was development of active disease was not performed for these children. Several studies reported similar findings [17, 19, 21]. In one study of hospitalised children, stool-only positive results were retrospectively attributed to active disease; however, cross-contamination at the time of testing could not be confidently ruled out [17]. In a study done with adult people living with HIV, stool-positive results in the absence of sufficient clinical findings were not necessarily attributed to active disease [22]. Yet, this highlights two crucial topics: first, validating childhood diagnostics is challenging in the presence of an imperfect reference standard. While culture or microbiological confirmation is the gold standard, the suboptimal sensitivity of this method implies that a high proportion of children will be missed. Second, in a real-world setting, this potentially leads to overtreatment, which is preferable to undertreatment, but raises the potential to miss and correctly address other non-TB conditions, such as recurrent viral infections or post-TB lung disease, that are not uncommon in low-resource high-TB burden settings [23]. Nonetheless, further research is needed to understand TB progression dynamics in children with a positive stool Ultra, regardless of method, in the absence of other TB suggestive symptoms and radiological abnormalities, particularly the trace call.

While feasibility was not directly assessed, it is important to note that, in our cohort, more than 80% of children successfully provided stool samples, reflecting a relatively high level of feasibility under a well-controlled research environment. However, this may not be representative of real-world outpatient settings, where logistical barriers, such as the need for patients to return on the following day, could significantly reduce uptake. As such, the utility of stool-based molecular testing may be greater in inpatient settings, where feasibility has been reported to be greater than 80% [17, 24], similar to what we report here.

The SPK was originally developed as an optimized version of centrifuge-free methods described by Banada et al. and Walters et al. [12, 25] and we evaluated a prototype version. While indeterminate rates were reported to be higher than the other two methods (8.5%-11%) [16], our findings showed a lower indeterminate rate at less than 3%, which is comparable to the other two methods; similarly, DTA estimates were consistent with those reported for SOS and OSF [15, 20]. Yet, the SPK did not go into further production, and the SOS was WHO-endorsed. Importantly, the high proportion of children microbiologically confirmed based on SPK/Ultra alone, including its ability to detect rifampicin resistance, should not go unmentioned. This highlights the value of stool-based molecular testing, irrespective of processing method. While it does not replace a sputum sample, our findings support WHO’s recommendation to collect an additional stool sample alongside respiratory samples [2–4].

Our study is not without certain limitations. First, there are significant differences between research centers; for example, children in India were most likely to be above the age of 10, and almost all children in Mozambique were outpatients, as opposed to those in four other centers. In addition, different sites had varying levels of available infrastructure, capacity, epidemiologic profiles, and even sampling strategies, despite centralized protocol and procedure training. Second, subgroup-specific estimates were limited by sample size in some strata, resulting in wide confidence intervals. Third, recruitment was focused on children with presumed TB, disease confirmation, and higher levels of care, potentially overestimating DTA estimates and therefore limiting generalizability in real-world settings, where some children may present with minimal or lymph node TB, which seldom confers microbiological confirmation [26].

This study represents one of the largest evaluations of SPK/Ultra to date, providing valuable insights into its performance across diverse paediatric populations in high TB burden settings. Stool testing shows promise in infants, children with undernutrition and those with severe disease, where diagnostic challenges are most significant and timely treatment is lifesaving. Future work should explore novel methods for processing stool, the feasibility of sample collection under programmatic settings, and the role of stool samples in integrated diagnostic algorithms.
