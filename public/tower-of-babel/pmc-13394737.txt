Spatial Transcriptomics and Bulk RNA‐Seq Analysis Revealed Molecular Classification of Invasive Lobular Carcinoma
Authors: Momoko Tokura, Jun Nakayama, Hironori Suzuki, Mieko Ochi, Miyaka Umemori, Sho Shiino, Masayuki Yoshida, Shin Takayama, Yasushi Yatabe, Yusuke Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInvasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is a special type of breast cancer. The histological subtypes of ILC exhibit diverse morphological features, and the prognosis differs accordingly. Compared with patients with classic‐ILC (C‐ILC), patients with pleomorphic‐ILC (P‐ILC) have a worse prognosis, owing to high‐grade nuclear atypia and mitotic cells. However, the molecular differences between C‐ILC and P‐ILC remain unclear. To address this gap, we performed spatial transcriptomic profiling on four fresh‐frozen C‐ILC samples and four fresh‐frozen P‐ILC samples, followed by confirmation of reproducibility in bulk RNA‐seq datasets. We identified significant enrichment of cellular response to heat stress in P‐ILC. Furthermore, molecular clustering analysis using genes differentially expressed across ILC samples in spatial transcriptome revealed that ILC has three molecular subtypes: proliferative (PR), immunoreactive (IM), and stroma‐rich (ST), associated with distinct prognostic outcomes. Although these molecular subtypes did not completely correspond to C‐ILC or P‐ILC, PR tended to include P‐ILC, and ST tended to include C‐ILC. These molecular clusters exhibited features comparable to previously reported subtypes. Despite these phenotypic features, ILC is generally treated similarly to invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), with limited consideration of molecular subtype classification. Our findings suggest that molecular profiling may more accurately reflect prognosis than conventional histological classification and may provide potential diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets.

Introduction

Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is the most common special type of breast cancer, accounting for approximately 10%–15% of all breast cancer cases [1, 2, 3]. The clinical features of ILC are multifocal, multicentral, and bilateral [4, 5, 6]. Classically, discohesive cells spread individually or proliferate in single files; reduced cell–cell adhesion due to the lack of E‐cadherin (CDH1) is a hallmark of ILC, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) of CDH1 is used to diagnose ILC [7, 8, 9]. Compared with invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), ILC has luminal characteristics, low proliferation, and low immune cell infiltration, with a high rate of metastasis to the ovaries and gastrointestinal tract [4, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

However, ILC has various pathological subtypes with different characteristics [2, 4, 7]. Classic‐ILC (C‐ILC) features small tumor cells with a uniform chromatin concentration, and mitotic cells are rare in C‐ILC. In contrast, pleomorphic‐ILC (P‐ILC) shows high‐grade nuclear atypia and mitotic cells. Compared with C‐ILC, P‐ILC has more HER2‐positive type and triple‐negative type, higher immune cell infiltration, and worse outcome [2, 16, 17, 18]. Genomic analysis has revealed that loss of p53 and high rates of ERBB2 amplification or mutation are associated with poor prognosis, and these changes are observed more frequently in P‐ILC than in C‐ILC [2, 7, 19, 20, 21, 22]. According to gene expression analysis, Ciriello et al. classified ILC into three mRNA subtypes: reactive‐like, immune‐related, and proliferative [23]. The reactive‐like subtype presents epithelial and stromal‐associated signaling and has a good prognosis, whereas the proliferative subtype is associated with a poor prognosis. The immune‐related subtype is not associated with prognosis. Thus, it is suggested that ILC is a heterogeneous tumor, and tumor proliferation is associated with a worse prognosis in ILC. However, the molecular differences between C‐ILC and P‐ILC remain unclear.

The spatial transcriptome investigates intratumoral heterogeneity by analyzing gene expression profiles while preserving spatial information [24]. This approach can be applied to characterize cancers with different phenotypes [25]. Shah et al. revealed the molecular difference between the IDC region and the ILC region in mixed IDC and ILC samples using multi‐omics [26]. However, spatial transcriptomic data about C‐ILC and P‐ILC have not been revealed. In this study, we investigated the biological differences between C‐ILC and P‐ILC using spatial transcriptomics and confirmed reproducibility using bulk RNA‐seq datasets.

Material and Methods

Patients and Tumor Specimens

Eight ILC samples were obtained from patients who were diagnosed with ILC and underwent breast‐conserving surgery or total mastectomy without prior treatment. Four patients underwent lymph node dissection, owing to lymph node metastasis. Postoperative pathology revealed four cases of C‐ILC and four cases of P‐ILC. Spatial transcriptomics was performed on fresh frozen tissues from all eight samples. A trained breast pathologist made a histological diagnosis of the area sampled for analysis. We also collected 20 luminal ILC samples (10 C‐ILC and 10 P‐ILC) for IHC, independent of the eight spatial transcriptomics samples.

Spatial Transcriptomics

Tissue samples stored at −80°C were obtained from the biobank of the National Cancer Center Hospital. A fresh‐frozen tissue sample was embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and stored at −80°C. The tissue blocks were cut into 10‐μm sections, which were analyzed with a Visium Spatial Gene Expression Kit (10× Genomics) according to the manufacturer's instructions. First, the breast tissue permeabilization conditions were optimized using the Visium Spatial Tissue Optimization Kit (10× Genomics). The optimal permeabilization time was 24 min. The tissue sections were stained with H&E, imaged with a BZ‐X710 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan) at 10×, and processed for spatial transcriptomics. The resulting complementary DNA library was checked for quality control and then sequenced using a DNBSEQ system (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). The cycling conditions were set to 28 and 90 for Read 1 and Read 2, respectively. The dataset supporting the conclusions of this article is available in the NCBI GEO database under accession number GSE290670.

Normalization for Spatial Transcriptomics

Raw FASTQ files and histological images were processed using Space Ranger software v2.0.0. Analyses of spatially resolved RNA‐seq data, including normalization, dimensional reduction, clustering, detection of spatially variable features, and interactive visualization (scaling, clustering of cells, and identification of cluster marker genes), were performed using the Seurat package in R version 5.4.0. Sctransform [27] normalization was performed with the “SCTransform” function in Seurat (https://satijalab.org/seurat/articles/spatial_vignette.html).

Data Clustering and Dimensionality Reduction

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction by the Seurat package in R. Clustering of spots was performed with the “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions in Seurat. The dimensionality‐reduced cell clustering was visualized as a UMAP plot with the “runUMAP” function.

Spot Annotation Using STdeconvolve

STdeconvolve [28] package (version 1.9.0) was used for reference‐free deconvolution. After quality control, we estimated the number of topics and spot‐level cell type proportion using latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) in the STdeconvolve package. The “getCorrMtx” function was utilized to confirm the correlation between the deconvoluted cell types and the cell types annotated by previously reported marker genes. Finally, we adopted the reference with the highest correlation for the annotations in STdeconvolve.

Spot Annotation Using SpaCET

Visium data were analyzed using the SpaCET [29] package in R (version 1.2.0). After quality control, deconvolution of the spots into various cell types was performed with the “SpaCET.deconvolution” function, setting the cancer type parameter (cancerType = “BRCA”) to model malignant cells. Cell type proportion data were extracted from a SpaCET object, and each spot was annotated with the cell type showing the highest proportion.

Copy Number Variations (CNV) Analysis

CNV calculation was performed by the inferCNV package [30] (version 1.20.0), referencing spots identified as normal by SpaCET.

Analysis of the Integrated Eight Samples

Integration of the eight ILC samples (NCCLC1–8) was performed using the Seurat package in R. To correct batch effects between datasets, the Harmony algorithm was applied [31, 32].

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Enrichment analysis was performed with the signature gene list obtained from each cluster of the cancer‐dominant spots and stroma‐dominant spots using the “ClusterProfiler” package (version 4.16.0) in R [33]. For enrichment analysis, gene symbols were converted to ENTREZ IDs using the “org.Hs.eg.db” package (Carison M, R package version 3.21.0., 2019). Reactome pathway analysis using the “ReactomePA” function was performed by the BH method.

Feature Selection for Spatial Transcriptomics

Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA; https://www.digital‐biology.co.jp/allianced/products/ipa/) was used to analyze spatial transcriptomic data for predicting enriched canonical pathways and upstream regulators.

Pseudobulk Analysis

The Seurat package was used to calculate the average expression of the customized gene set for each sample. Heatmaps were generated with Morpheus software from the Broad Institute (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

Gene set enrichment scores were calculated using the “GSEAplot” function of the SeuratExtend package (version 1.2.3).

Calculation of the Tertiary Lymphoid Structures (TLS) Score

The TLS score for each spot was calculated via “SpaCET.GeneSetScore”, and the average TLS score for each sample was subsequently compared using a t test.

Interspot Interaction Analysis Integrating the Eight Samples

Interspot interactions analysis was performed using the CellChat [34] package (version 2.1.2). We generated a CellChat object for C‐ILC and P‐ILC, respectively. “Secreted Signaling” interactions database was used for interaction analysis.

Calculation of the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) Score in Bulk RNA‐Seq Datasets

GSVA scoring of spatially derived signatures to the bulk RNA‐seq data was performed using the GSVA package [35] (version 1.52.3).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining

IHC was performed using primary antibodies for Ki67, αSMA, CD31, and CD3 (Table S1).

Clustering and Survival Analysis Using Bulk RNA‐Seq Datasets

We collected clinical data and bulk RNA‐seq datasets of ILC from the three public datasets: TCGA [36], RATHER (GSE68057) [37], and METABRIC [38]. The sample sizes for each cohort were 201, 77, and 146 cases. The histological types, grade, and subtypes were defined based on the pathological information recorded in each cohort's clinical data. The hierarchical clustering of bulk RNA‐seq datasets in the TCGA, RATHER, and METABRIC cohorts was performed with the signature gene list obtained from comparison of C‐ILC and P‐ILC in spatial transcriptomics. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used for the evaluation of survival between clusters.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio and R software (version 4.4.0). Wilcoxon rank sum exact test was used for comparisons between C‐ILC and P‐ILC datasets. In the survival analysis in the TCGA, RATHER, and METABRIC data sets, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used, and the log‐rank test was performed to evaluate survival. The Bonferroni method was used to adjust the p‐values. Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn test were used to compare the three groups. Significance was defined as p < 0.05.

For further details, please refer to Data S1.

Results

Spatial Transcriptome Atlas of ILC

We performed spatial transcriptome profiling on four C‐ILC and four P‐ILC samples (Figure 1A). The histopathological information is shown in Table S2. The P‐ILC samples included one triple‐negative type sample and one luminal‐HER2 sample, while the other six samples were luminal. All the samples were primary tumors. A summary of the sequencing results is shown in Table S3. Although NCCLC5 and NCCLC7 had low median gene and UMI counts per spot, some genes were detectable in these samples as well, so they were included in the analysis. Of the 12,483 spots, those detected in overlapping tissue areas or distant from the tissue were excluded, leaving 12,099 for final analysis. Because single‐cell transcriptome data for ILC were unavailable, STdeconvolve and SpaCET, which are reference‐free approaches, were used to annotate cell types for each spatial spot. Based on gene expression data of spots for each sample, clustering was performed by the Seurat package in R (Figure S1A). According to the expression of cell marker genes, the cell types of each cluster were annotated (Figures S1B, S2A, 1B). To estimate the dominant cell type of each spot more precisely, STdeconvolve was performed by referencing the Seurat clustering annotation (Figure S2B–D). Additionally, we used SpaCET, a tool that estimated cell fractions by referencing breast cancer‐specific copy number variations (CNVs) and gene expression in TCGA data (Figure 1C). Although SpaCET cannot directly output CNV data, Figure S3A shows the NCCLC1 CNV heatmap generated using the inferCNV package, referencing spots identified as normal by SpaCET. The cell type with the highest fraction was assigned as the annotation for each spot. Finally, the annotation match between STdeconvolve and SpaCET was adopted. Figure 1D shows annotated images of NCCLC1 from pathology, STdeconvolve, and SpaCET, as well as a merged representative image. Spots with different results were classified as mixed spots. We annotated spots expressing basal cell marker genes, including KRT14 (Figure 1B), and possessing morphologically normal mammary ducts (Figure 1D, red circles labeled “duct”) as basal cell‐dominant spots. These spots also expressed stem cell‐like markers (SOX10, IRX1, PROM1, and KIT) and myoepithelial cell markers (TP63, ACTA2, and TAGLN) (Table S4, Figure S4). This suggests that these spots contain both stem cell‐like cells and myoepithelial cells. Figure S3B shows CNV heatmap of all spots classified by cell type in the final annotation of NCCLC1. CNV frequency was high in cancer‐dominant spots and low in stroma‐dominant and basal‐dominant spots. Mixed spots showed moderate frequency.

Differences in Cancer‐Dominant Spots Between C‐ILC and P‐ILC

To investigate molecular differences between C‐ILC and P‐ILC, gene expression profiles of C‐ILC and P‐ILC were compared in cancer‐dominant and stroma‐dominant spots. Based on pathological diagnosis, NCCLC1, 5, 6, and 8 were categorized as C‐ILC, and NCCLC2, 3, 4, and 7 were categorized as P‐ILC (Figure 2A, Table S2). Representative histological images of the P‐ILC and C‐ILC samples are shown in Figure 3A. The spot proportions between the C‐ILC and P‐ILC samples are shown based on the cell types (Figure 3B). First, the cancer‐dominant spots were extracted from all samples, followed by clustering and differential gene expression analysis. A total of 4799 spots from cancer‐dominant spots were classified into 7 clusters (Figure S7A). Figure 3C shows the UMAP plot with C‐ILC and P‐ILC states. The cluster proportions of each sample showed that Clusters 4 and 7 were NCCLC3 (triple‐negative P‐ILC)‐specific (Figure S7B). Each cluster expressed different marker genes (Figure S7C). Reactome pathway analysis revealed that each cluster was associated with distinct pathways: Cluster 1 with estrogen‐dependent gene expression; Cluster 3 with the extracellular matrix; and Cluster 5 with the immune response (Figure S7D). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to confirm Cluster 4's function, revealing that regulation of stem cell differentiation was enriched in Cluster 4 compared with the other cancer clusters (Figure S7E). This result suggested that the triple‐negative P‐ILC sample included a stem cell population. Moreover, the marker genes of Cluster 4 included NANOS1 (Table S5), which has been reported to be associated with prognosis in TCGA data [41]. While some clusters were sample‐specific, no clear differences were observed between C‐ILC and P‐ILC.

Next, the differentially expressed genes between C‐ILC and P‐ILC were analyzed (Tables S6 and S7). Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA) revealed that cellular response to heat stress, PI3K‐AKT signaling, and cell cycle‐associated pathways, including the mitotic G2‐G2/M phase, DNA replication preinitiation, and S phase, were upregulated in P‐ILC, whereas immune‐related pathways were upregulated in C‐ILC (Figure 3D). The GSEA plot revealed that all these pathways were significantly enriched in P‐ILC compared with C‐ILC (Figure 3E,F, Figure S8A,B). The heatmap shows the genes in each pathway and their expression levels calculated by pseudo‐bulk analysis. P‐ILC samples exhibited higher expression levels of cellular responses to heat stress compared to C‐ILC samples (Wilcoxon rank sum exact test; p = 0.029) (Figure 3G). Although the difference was not significant, the expression of PI3K‐AKT signaling, cell cycle, and DNA repair tended to be higher in P‐ILC than C‐ILC (Wilcoxon rank sum exact test; PI3K‐AKT signaling p = 0.1, cell cycle p = 0.2, DNA repair p = 0.89) (Figure 3H,I). Spot‐based analysis revealed that cancer‐dominant spots in P‐ILC exhibited greater upregulation of PI3K‐AKT signaling, cell cycle, DNA repair, and heat stress responses compared to cancer‐dominant spots in C‐ILC. However, pseudo‐bulk analysis indicated that only the heat stress response was upregulated in P‐ILC compared with C‐ILC.

Differences in Stroma‐Dominant Spots Between C‐ILC and P‐ILC

The gene expression in stroma‐dominant spots was compared between C‐ILC and P‐ILC. A total of 1512 spots from the stroma were clustered, and the UMAP plot of the stroma‐dominant spots revealed four distinct clusters (Figure S9A). Figure 4A shows the UMAP plot with C‐ILC and P‐ILC states. The cluster proportions of each sample revealed that Cluster 4 was included only in NCCLC1, NCCLC2, NCCLC3, and NCCLC4. (Figure S9B). Each cluster expressed different marker genes (Figure S9C). Reactome pathway analysis revealed that each cluster was associated with different pathways as follows: Cluster 1 was associated with platelet degranulation; Cluster 2 and Cluster 4 were associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) organization; and Cluster 3 was associated with adjustment of transcription (Figure S9D). While the cluster proportions differed between samples, no clear differences were observed between C‐ILC and P‐ILC. Therefore, IPA was performed using genes showing expression differences between C‐ILC and P‐ILC (Tables S8 and S9). Signal interaction network analysis revealed that the migration of endothelial cells, vasculogenesis, and vessel growth were activated in P‐ILC (Figure 4B). Upstream regulators, such as PDGF‐BB, VEGF, RELA, FGF2, and HGF, were upregulated in the P‐ILC stroma‐dominant spots (Figure 4C). In contrast, upstream regulators associated with immune regulation, such as triamcinolone acetonide and INPP5D, were upregulated in C‐ILCs (Figure 4C). The GSEA plot revealed significant enrichment of endothelial cell proliferation and ECM disassembly in P‐ILC compared with C‐ILC (Figure 4D,E). To compare the gene expression levels in each sample, pseudobulk analysis was performed. P‐ILC stroma‐dominant spots tended to express higher levels of angiogenesis and ECM remodeling marker genes than C‐ILC spots (Figure 4F,G), but this difference was not statistically significant (Wilcoxon rank sum exact test; angiogenesis p = 0.11, ECM remodeling p = 0.49). Spot‐based analysis revealed that stroma‐dominant spots in P‐ILC exhibited greater upregulation of angiogenesis and ECM remodeling compared to stroma‐dominant spots in C‐ILC. However, pseudo‐bulk analysis showed no statistically significant difference between P‐ILC and C‐ILC in stroma‐dominant spots.

Immunity Between C‐ILC and P‐ILC

Immune‐associated genes were enriched in C‐ILC in both cancer‐dominant and stroma‐dominant spots. To compare immunity levels, the average tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) score in all spots was calculated for each sample. There was no significant difference in TLS score between C‐ILC and P‐ILC, but NCCLC6 (C‐ILC sample) had a higher TLS score than P‐ILC samples (Figure S10A). When the expression of cytotoxic T cell marker and Treg marker was compared between samples by pseudobulk analysis, NCCLC6 had higher expression of cytotoxic T cell marker than the other samples (Figure S10B). However, NCCLC3 (triple‐negative P‐ILC) had higher expression of Treg marker than NCCLC6. Furthermore, the expression of M1 macrophage marker was higher in NCCLC6 than in the other samples (Figure S10C). Conversely, the expression of M2 macrophage marker was higher in NCCLC3 than in NCCLC6. In the spatial feature plots, NCCLC6 had expression of immune cell markers in cancer‐dominant spots, whereas NCCLC3 had less expression of immune cell markers in cancer‐dominant spots (Figure S10D). NCCLC6 is in a relatively anti‐tumor immune status, while NCCLC3 is in a suppressed anti‐tumor immune status.

Differences in Cell–Cell Interactions Between C‐ILC and P‐ILC

To further explore potential differences between C‐ILC and P‐ILC, we performed the cell–cell interactions analysis. To confirm the interaction at the annotated cell level, we calculated the number of interactions and the interaction strength. When examining the number of interactions for signals from mixed to cancer, there were 7 and 14 interactions in the C‐ILC and in P‐ILC groups, respectively (Figure S11A). In addition, the number of interactions for signals from cancer to mixed was 10 in C‐ILC but 20 in P‐ILC. Although some of the interactions, such as “cancer to mixed”, were high in P‐ILC, most of the interaction numbers were high in C‐ILC. Similarly, a greater interaction strength was calculated for all the C‐ILC spot types (Figure S11B). Compared with the information flow, which is the sum of the communication strength of each signaling pathway among all pairs of the cell types in the C‐ILC and P‐ILC samples, midkine (MK) signaling and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF) signaling were strongest in both C‐ILC and P‐ILC (Figure S11C). However, we could not find the signaling pathway enriched in P‐ILC compared with C‐ILC. In P‐ILC cancer‐dominant spots, the input signaling from the MK signaling pathway network was greater than the output signaling from this network (Figure S11D). However, the output signaling from the MIF signaling pathway network was greater than the input signaling from this network (Figure S11E). These findings suggested that MK signaling contributes to P‐ILC cancer cell progression and that MIF signaling contributes to the formation of the tumor microenvironment. Interestingly, a small population of basal cells was detected only in NCCL1 and NCCL3, but the number and strength of interactions were relatively high (Figure S11A,B). In addition, basal cells were nearly the only senders of pleiotrophin (PTN) signaling in both C‐ILC and P‐ILC (Figure S11F).

Orthogonal Confirmation of Spatial Transcriptome Between C‐ILC and P‐ILC

To assess the reproducibility of our findings in spatial transcriptome, we examined the cell cycle, ECM remodeling, and immune status of C‐ILC and P‐ILC in a larger sample size. For this purpose, we performed GSVA using bulk RNA‐seq datasets. In the TCGA cohort, GSVA scores of G2/M checkpoint regulation and cellular response to heat were significantly higher in P‐ILC than in C‐ILC (Figure 5A,B). Conversely, GSVA scores related to regulation of ECM disassembly and endothelial cell proliferation tended to be higher in C‐ILC than in P‐ILC (Figure S12D). However, no statistically significant differences were observed in gene sets related to PI3K signaling, DNA repair, regulation of ECM disassembly, endothelial cell proliferation, and interferon gamma (IFNγ) response (Figure S12A–E).

Furthermore, we performed immunostaining for Ki67, αSMA, CD31, and CD3 in 20 luminal ILC samples (10 C‐ILC and 10 P‐ILC) (Tables S1 and S10). Figure 5C shows representative IHC images. The proportion of Ki67‐positive cells in P‐ILC was higher than that in C‐ILC (Figure 5D, Table S10). Consistent with the GSVA results, CD31 positivity rates tended to be higher in C‐ILC, but no significant difference was observed between C‐ILC and P‐ILC for αSMA, CD31, or CD3. However, clustering of IHC results showed a tendency for groups of samples with high positivity rates to differ by antibody (Figure S13A). The correlation heatmap also indicated a negative relationship among αSMA, Ki67, and CD3 (Figure S13B). In analyses of larger samples, proliferation was associated with P‐ILC. Although protein or gene expression related to angiogenesis, ECM remodeling, or cytotoxic immunity did not differ between histological subtypes, these markers may serve as markers of molecular subtypes in ILC classification.

Molecular Classification of ILC Using Bulk RNA‐Seq Datasets

Finally, we examined whether molecular classification of ILC is possible using gene sets identified by spatial transcriptomics. We performed hierarchical clustering of ILC bulk RNA‐seq from TCGA, RATHER, and METABRIC using the gene sets of cell cycle, DNA repair, response to heat stress, PI3K‐AKT signal, ECM disassembly, endothelial cell proliferation, cytotoxic T cell, Treg, M1 macrophage, and M2 macrophage marker. In all datasets, ILC was divided into three clusters consistent with existing reports: proliferative (PR), immune‐reactive (IM), and stroma‐rich (ST) clusters (Figures 6A, S14A, S15A). Cell cycle, DNA repair, and response to heat stress were enriched in PR. PI3K signaling showed low specificity for PR. Moreover, ECM and angiogenesis‐related markers were enriched in ST rather than PR. Treg and M2 macrophage markers were enriched in a triple‐negative sample in our spatial transcriptomics, but the large‐scale dataset revealed that IM expressed both antitumor and protumor immune markers. GSVA in the TCGA cohort also showed that the pathways activated differ across the three clusters (Figure S16). PR tended to include P‐ILC or grade 3 tumors. However, except for the RATHER cohort, ST tended to include C‐ILC or grade 1 tumors (Figures 6B, S14B,C, S15B). By subtype, PR tended to include basal or triple‐negative and luminal B, while ST tended to include luminal A or luminal (Figures 6C, S14D, S15C). In survival analysis, PR were significantly associated with poorer overall survival (OS) in the METABRIC cohort. (Figure S15E). Although no significant differences were observed in disease free survival (DFS), disease specific survival (DSS), and OS in the TCGA cohort and in the RATHER cohort, PR showed a tendency toward poorer prognosis (PR vs. ST: DFS (HR = 0.26), DSS (HR = 0.68), and OS (HR = 0.37) in the TCGA cohort) (Figure 6D–F, S14E–G). DFS in the METABRIC cohort showed a significant difference by the log‐rank test but not by the Bonferroni method (Figure S15D). However, ST had a good prognosis. Interestingly, HER2‐positive samples were classified as IM rather than PR (Figures 6C, S14D, S15C). However, ERBB2 expression was higher in PR than IM (Figure 6G). Moreover, NANOS1 expression tended to be higher in PR than IM and ST (Figure 6H). High expression of NANOS1 tended to be worse OS than low expression, but there was no statistically significant difference (Figure 6I).

Discussion

The prognosis of patients with P‐ILC is poorer than that of patients with C‐ILC [2, 16]. However, the molecular biological differences between the two subtypes have not been revealed. In the present study, we compared the gene expression profiles of C‐ILC and P‐ILC using spatial transcriptomics and examined reproducibility via pseudo‐bulk analysis, IHC, and bulk RNA‐seq. We identified genes involved in cell cycle, DNA repair, response to heat stress, PI3K‐AKT signaling, ECM disassembly, endothelial cell proliferation, cytotoxic T cells, Treg cells, M1 macrophages, and M2 macrophages as differentially expressed across ILC samples from spatial transcriptomics. Except for the genes related to cell cycle and response to heat stress, these genes did not differ between C‐ILC and P‐ILC in the larger cohort. However, they enabled us to classify ILC into three clusters, consistent with the report by Ciriello et al. [23]. They classified ILC into three clusters using 60 genes derived from transcriptome data of 106 luminal A ILC samples. Although our gene sets were different from theirs, the characteristics of each cluster were similar to those observed in their data. Our findings suggest that distinct cell populations with different proliferative capacities and microenvironments exist within the ILC.

Genes related to cell cycle, DNA repair, and cellular response to heat stress were enriched in cancer‐dominant spots of P‐ILC compared to C‐ILC, proving useful for classifying proliferative clusters, which had a poor prognosis. This cluster tended to show elevated expression of the stemness marker NANOS1 and ERBB2. Our data and public datasets suggested that ERBB2 expression levels did not necessarily correlate with immunohistochemical staining results. Discrepancy between IHC and mRNA has been reported [39, 40], and one possible explanation might be due to post‐translational regulation, such as microRNAs and modification [42]. While interpretation may differ between protein and mRNA expression, the ERBB2 gene is involved in proliferation signaling and aligns with the characteristics of proliferative clusters. PI3K‐AKT pathway is known to enhance tumor progression and resistance to antiestrogen therapy in breast cancer [43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. Genomic studies have reported PIK3CA mutation in 30%–50% of ILC [48] but this pathway is not related to the clustering and prognosis in analyses of publicly available ILC data. Furthermore, ECM remodeling and angiogenesis‐related genes were useful for identifying stroma‐rich clusters in bulk RNA‐seq data. These processes play a crucial role in tumor proliferation and metastasis [49, 50, 51], however, this cluster tended to include C‐ILC, grade 1, and luminal A tumors, which had a favorable prognosis. ILC cancer cells infiltrate the stroma individually [7, 8, 9] and bulk RNA‐seq may reflect a stroma‐rich state. In spot‐based analysis of spatial transcriptomics, these signatures were more abundantly expressed in stroma‐dominant spots of P‐ILC than C‐ILC. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in either pseudo‐bulk analysis, IHC, or GSVA. In spot‐based analysis, the expression of ECM remodeling and angiogenesis‐related genes in C‐ILC samples may have been masked by a single immunologically activated sample and by outlier P‐ILC samples that exhibited high stromal marker expression. Regarding immunity, bulk RNA‐seq analysis revealed that immune‐reactive clusters expressed both anti‐tumor immune markers and tumor‐promoting immune markers. Triple‐negative sample expressed tumor‐promoting immune markers in spatial transcriptomics, but these markers were not enriched in proliferative clusters. Anti‐tumor immunity and tumor‐promoting immunity are in a complementary relationship [52], and it is thought that the expression levels of these genes in ILC are also interconnected.

The present study had several limitations. First, the resolution of spatial transcriptome was not single‐cell level. The amount of gene expression at each spot corresponds to the average of approximately 10–20 cells. Although single‐cell analysis is required for cell‐subtype classification, our method enabled analysis of molecular differences among ILC. Second, the present study utilized reference‐free cell annotation methods because scRNA‐seq data for ILC is not available. scRNA‐seq data from tissues adjacent to the sections used for spatial transcriptomics are useful for cell type annotation but require fresh samples. To address this problem, several reference‐free methods have been developed, and appropriate methods are being considered, but no standard method has been determined [53, 54, 55]. In this analysis, both gene expression and CNV data were used to ensure accurate annotation. SpaCET is a package that predicts cell types by referencing existing breast cancer‐specific CNV data and gene expression patterns. It does not detect CNV relative to normal breast tissue, and the potential for misclassification cannot be ruled out. However, given the limited available data, analyzing not only gene expression but also CNV data may improve accuracy. Third, sampling may have introduced bias because tumors are heterogeneous, and the proportions of cancer, stromal, and immune cells vary across sampling locations. Fourth, in spatial transcriptomics, sample sizes were small, and subtypes varied across specimens. Overcoming sampling bias requires single‐cell analysis or spatial analysis using larger samples, which is not easily achievable. To address these challenges, we analyzed bulk RNA‐seq datasets using genes identified by spatial transcriptomics and demonstrated that ILC can be classified into three clusters with distinct prognoses.
