A New Special Function and Its Application in Probability
Zeraoulia Rafik, Alvaro H Salas, David L. Ocampo

Abstract
In this note we present a new special function that behaves like the error function and we provide an approximated accurate closed form for its CDF in terms of both Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind and the error function. Also, we provide its series representation using Padé approximant. We show convincing numerical evidence of an accuracy of 10 − 6 10^{-6} for the approximants in the sense of the quadratic mean norm. A similar approach may be applied to other probability distributions, for example, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution and the normal distribution, and we show its application using both of those distributions.

The well-known formula which expresses the relationship between the error function and the cumulative density function, see (2), is defined as

\operatorname{Erf}(x)=2\bigl(\Phi(x\sqrt{2})-\Phi(0)\bigr)=2\Bigl(\Phi(x\sqrt{2})-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)=2\Phi(x\sqrt{2})-1.

(13)

And it is easy to check that it always holds for every real number by the following short proof.

Proof.

By definition, the error function

\operatorname{Erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt.

(14)

Writing
t^{2}=z^{2}/2
implies
t=z/\sqrt{2}
(because
t
is not negative), whence
dt=dz/\sqrt{2}
. The endpoints
t=0
and
t=x
become
z=0
and
z=x\sqrt{2}
. To convert the resulting integral into something that looks like a cumulative distribution function (CDF), it must be expressed in terms of integrals that have lower limits of
-\infty
; thus

\operatorname{Erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{x\sqrt{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz=2\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x\sqrt{2}}e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz\right).

(15)

Those integrals on the right hand side are both values of the CDF of the standard normal distribution:

\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{x}e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz.

(16)

Specifically,

\operatorname{Erf}(x)=2\bigl(\Phi(x\sqrt{2})-\Phi(0)\bigr)=2\Bigl(\Phi(x\sqrt{2})-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)=2\Phi(x\sqrt{2})-1.

(17)

Now since the LHS of (18) has a known value which is
0.97210699\cdots

0.97210699\cdots

\int_{0}^{a}\left(e^{-x^{2}}\right)\operatorname{Erf}(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{a}e^{x^{2}}\bigl(1-2\Phi(x\sqrt{2})\bigr)\,dx.

(18)

Now we shall call the function defined in (1)

T(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\left(e^{-t^{2}}\right)\operatorname{erf}(t)\,dt

T(x)

Lemma 1.1

T(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}\left(e^{-t^{2}}\right)\operatorname{erf}(t)\,dt
cannot be expressed in terms of elementary function.

Proof.

It is a theorem of Liouville
[
5
]
, reproven later with purely algebraic methods, that for rational functions
f
and
g
,
g
nonconstant, the antiderivative

\int\bigl[f(x)\exp(g(x))\bigr]\,dx

(19)

can be expressed in terms of elementary functions if and only if there exists some rational function
h
such that it is a solution to the differential equation:

f=h^{\prime}+hg.

(20)

Now if we apply Liouville theorem we can come up with the following ODE:

1=h^{\prime}(x)+h(x)\bigl(-x^{2}\operatorname{erf}(x)\bigr)

with
g(x)=-x^{2}\operatorname{erf}(x)
and
f(x)=1
. It is first ordinary differential equation. The computation we made with Wolfram Alpha gives the following solution:

\begin{split}&\exp\!\Biggl(\frac{e^{-x^{2}}\Bigl(e^{x^{2}}(\sqrt{\pi}x^{3}\operatorname{erf}(x)-1)+x^{2}+1\Bigr)}{3\sqrt{\pi}}\Biggr)\\
&\quad\times\Biggl(\exp\Biggl\{\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{\pi}\Biggl(\int_{1}^{x}\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{3}\Bigl(t^{3}(-\operatorname{erf}(t))-\frac{e^{-t^{2}}(t^{2}+1)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\,dt-\int_{0}^{1}\exp\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{3}\Bigl(t^{3}(-\operatorname{erf}(t))-\frac{e^{-t^{2}}(t^{2}+1)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Bigr)\Bigr)\,dt\Biggr)\Biggr\}+1\Biggr)\end{split}

(21)

with
h(0)=1
. Really the function
h
can be written as follows:

h(x)=\ell(x)\left[c_{1}+\int_{x}^{1}\ell(-\xi)\,d\xi\right].

(22)

Now it is clear that
\ell(x)
is a transcendental function and the defined integral in the right hand side of the
h(x)
expression is also transcendental function because we have derivatives of rational functions being rational functions. Therefore, if the antiderivative is rational, then the original function was rational. The function
h
is rational only at
x=0
, and since
h(x)\neq 0
, then the sum of two transcendental functions is always transcendental function. According to definition of the rational function,
h(x)
cannot be called a rational function; then we are done.

Lemma 4.1

Series expansion of
T(x)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\xi^{2}}\operatorname{erf}(\xi)\,d\xi
is
defined by this identity:

\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\xi^{2}}\operatorname{erf}(\xi)\,d\xi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots+nk_{n}=n\\
k_{1}\geq 0,k_{2}\geq 0,\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{A_{j,\varepsilon}^{\,k_{j}}}{k_{j}!}\right)\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1},

(29)

where

A_{j,\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2(-1)^{(j-1)/2}}{(j-2)\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(j-3)\bigr)!\sqrt{\pi}},&\text{if }j\geq 3\text{ and $j$ an odd integer},\\[4.30554pt]
\varepsilon,&\text{otherwise }(0<\varepsilon<1),\end{cases}

(30)

which is the key idea to get.

Proof.

Suppose that we have the Taylor expansions:

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{n!}x^{n}

(31)

and

g(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{n}}{n!}x^{n}.

(32)

Then we have the standard result:

g(f(x))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}B_{n,k}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n-k+1})\right)\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

(33)

where
B_{n,k}(\cdot)
are the partial Bell polynomials, which are defined by the following formula:

\widehat{B}_{m,j}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m-j+1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{0}+k_{1}+\cdots+k_{N}=j\\
k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots+Nk_{N}=m\end{subarray}}\binom{j}{k_{0},k_{1},\dots,k_{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{k_{i}}.

(34)

The key idea to get series expansion of the
n
-th derivative of
T(x)
which is defined in (29) is to use Taylor expansion of
g(x)=\exp(-x)
and
f(x)=-x^{2}\operatorname{erf}(x)
coming up for using one of the important formulas in mathematics called Bruno–Fadi formula such as that defined above in (33) using (34). It is well known that the Taylor expansion of
\exp(-x)
is given by the following:

g(x)=\exp(-x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}x^{n}.

(35)

Probably the interesting here for readers to know is Taylor expansion of
\operatorname{erf}(x)
; we give a simple proof about its expansion series using Hermite polynomial.

Lemma 4.2

Taylor expansion of
\operatorname{erf}(x)
at each point
a
is given by this identity:

\operatorname{erf}_{a}(x)=e^{-a^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}H_{n}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^{n},

(36)

with
H_{n}(a)
the Hermite polynomial of degree
n
.

Proof.

f^{(n)}(a)
can be written in terms of Hermite polynomials
H_{n}
:

H_{0}(x)=1,\quad H_{1}(x)=2x,\quad H_{2}(x)=4x^{2}-2,\quad H_{3}(x)=8x^{3}-12x,\quad H_{4}(x)=16x^{4}-48x^{2}+12,\quad H_{5}(x)=\cdots.

(37)

We may recognize that
H_{2n-1}(0)=0
, which gives the power series for
e^{-x^{2}}
at
a=0
:

e^{-x^{2}}=1-\frac{2}{2!}x^{2}+\frac{12}{4!}x^{4}-\frac{120}{6!}x^{6}+\cdots.

(38)

After multiplying by
2/\sqrt{\pi}
, this integrates to

\operatorname{erf}(z)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(z-\frac{z^{3}}{3}+\frac{z^{5}}{10}-\frac{z^{7}}{42}+\frac{z^{9}}{216}-\cdots\right).

(39)

Since
\dfrac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}e^{-x^{2}}=(-1)^{n}e^{-x^{2}}H_{n}(x)
, one can do a Taylor series for every

:

\operatorname{erf}_{a}(x)=e^{-a^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}H_{n}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^{n}.

(40)

Then we are done.
Now by composition of (40) with (35) after multiplying (40) by the term
-x^{2}
, we come up to Bruno–Fadi formula which is defined as

e^{-x^{2}}\operatorname{erf}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots+nk_{n}=n\\
k_{1}\geq 0,k_{2}\geq 0,\dots,k_{n}\geq 0\end{subarray}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{A_{j,\varepsilon}^{\,k_{j}}}{k_{j}!}\right)x^{n},

(41)

where

A_{j,\varepsilon}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2(-1)^{(j-1)/2}}{(j-2)\bigl(\frac{1}{2}(j-3)\bigr)!\sqrt{\pi}},&\text{if }j\geq 3\text{ and $j$ an odd integer},\\[4.30554pt]
\varepsilon,&\text{otherwise }(0<\varepsilon<1).\end{cases}

(42–43)

Integrating this equation term by term gives

\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\xi^{2}}\operatorname{erf}(\xi)\,d\xi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1}+2k_{2}+\cdots+nk_{n}=n\\

(44)

which gives the series expansion for the new special function.

\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2x}{\sqrt{\pi}}-\frac{x^{3}}{15\sqrt{\pi}}\left(1+\frac{3x^{2}}{10}\right)^{-1}

(69)

we should get

I_{2k}=\int_{-1}^{1}x^{4k}\bigl(\operatorname{erf}(x)\bigr)^{2k}\,dx=\frac{2}{6k+1}\left(\frac{4}{\pi}\right)^{k}\,F_{1}\!\left(6k+\tfrac{1}{2};-2k,2k;6k+\tfrac{3}{2};\tfrac{1}{30},-\tfrac{3}{10}\right),

(70)

where the Appell hypergeometric function of two variables appears. Finally we conclude the series representation as follows:

I(t)=\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\bigl(-x^{2}\operatorname{erf}(x)\bigr)\,dx\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}I_{2k}.

(71)

7
Approximation of
T(x)
Function by Means of a Polynomial

Lemma 7.1

The function
f
which is defined as

f(x)=T\!\left(\frac{b+a}{2}+\frac{b-a}{2}x\right),\qquad-1\leq x\leq 1

(72)

could be approximated by means of Chebytchev polynomial.

Proof.

We may approximate the function
f
on the interval
[-1,1]
by using Chébyshev polynomials
[
8
]
of the first kind. To this end, we choose some positive integer
n
and we define the coefficients
c_{n}
by the formula

c_{j}=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{T_{j}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}f(x)\,dx,\qquad j=0,1,\dots,n.

(73)

Then the polynomial

P_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2}c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}T_{j}(x)

(74)

approximates
f(x)
in the best possible way. Since

T(x)=f\!\left(\frac{a+b-2x}{a-b}\right)\quad\text{for }a\leq x\leq b

(75)

we see that the polynomial

Q_{n}(a,b,x)=P_{n}\!\left(\frac{a+b-2x}{a-b}\right)

is an approximant to
T(x)
function on
[a,b]
. Calculations give

\displaystyle Q_{11}\Bigl(0,\frac{3}{2},x\Bigr)

\displaystyle=0.0137936039435\,x^{11}-0.135129528505\,x^{10}+0.548169602543\,x^{9}-1.16161653976\,x^{8}+1.31691631085\,x^{7}

\displaystyle\quad-0.746480407376\,x^{6}+0.338453415662\,x^{5}-0.370071852413\,x^{4}+0.0133517048763\,x^{3}

\displaystyle\quad-0.00104123958376\,x^{2}+1.00003172454\,x,

(76)

and

\displaystyle Q_{11}\Bigl(\frac{3}{2},3,x\Bigr)

\displaystyle=-0.0000675632422240\,x^{11}+0.00188305739843\,x^{10}-0.0239397852528\,x^{9}+0.183255163671\,x^{8}

\displaystyle\quad-0.937675010268\,x^{7}+3.35913844398\,x^{6}-8.55140470408\,x^{5}+15.3046428836\,x^{4}

\displaystyle\quad-18.4622672665\,x^{3}+13.5920479951\,x^{2}-4.69093970289\,x+1.04191571066.

(77)

For both approximations the error is less than
10^{-6}
. Indeed, numerical integration gives

\bigl\|T(x)-Q_{11}(0,\tfrac{3}{2},x)\bigr\|=\sqrt{\int_{0}^{3/2}\bigl(T(x)-Q_{11}(0,\tfrac{3}{2},x)\bigr)^{2}\,dx}\approx 2.26\times 10^{-7}

(78)

and

\bigl\|T(x)-Q_{11}(\tfrac{3}{2},3,x)\bigr\|=\sqrt{\int_{3/2}^{3}\bigl(T(x)-Q_{11}(\tfrac{3}{2},3,x)\bigr)^{2}\,dx}\approx 3.66\times 10^{-10}.

(79)

Thus, we may evaluate the
T(x)
function with high accuracy on the interval
[0,3]
. For
x>3
we may use the following approximation formula in terms of the error function:

T(x)\approx\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{3}\exp\bigl(-t^{2}\operatorname{erf}(t)\bigr)\,dt+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\bigl(\operatorname{erf}(x)-\operatorname{erf}(3)\bigr),\qquad x\geq 3.

(80)

The quadratic mean error on
[3,100]
is

\|T(x)-\varphi(x)\|=\sqrt{\int_{3}^{100}\bigl(T(x)-\varphi(x)\bigr)^{2}\,dx}\approx 2.02\times 10^{-8}.

(81)

Now we are ready to present application of
T(x)
in probability and thermodynamics using one of the most important distributions which is called Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

8
Application of
T(x)
in Probability

Let

F_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\xi^{2}(\lambda+\mu\operatorname{erf}(\xi))}\,d\xi\qquad(\lambda>0).

(82)

Define

c=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi^{2}(\lambda+\mu\operatorname{erf}(\xi))}\,d\xi

(83)

and let

T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}c^{-1}F_{\lambda,\mu}(x),\qquad x\geq 0.

(84)

The function
T(x)
is the new special function we have studied in this paper. This function defines a cumulative probability distribution function (CDF) with probability distribution function (PDF)

f_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=e^{-x^{2}(\lambda+\mu\operatorname{erf}(x))},\qquad x\geq 0.

(85)

Indeed, we have

T^{\prime}_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=e^{-x^{2}(\lambda+\mu\operatorname{erf}(x))}>0,\qquad T_{\lambda,\mu}(+\infty)=1.

(86)

The ODE for this function not involving the error function
\operatorname{erf}
may be obtained by differentiating (84) twice and eliminating the expression containing that error function. This gives us the following ODE:

\sqrt{\pi}\,x\,y^{\prime\prime}(x)=2e^{-x^{2}}y^{\prime}(x)\bigl(\sqrt{\pi}e^{x^{2}}\log(y^{\prime}(x))-\mu x^{3}\bigr).

(87)

Letting
\mu=0
gives the ODE

x\,y^{\prime\prime}(x)=2y^{\prime}(x)\log\bigl(y^{\prime}(x)\bigr)

(88)

whose general solution is as follows:

y(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-c_{1}/2}\sqrt{\pi}\,\operatorname{erfi}\bigl(e^{c_{1}/2}x\bigr)+c_{2}.

(89)

If we compare (89) with

cF_{\lambda,0}(x)=c\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\lambda\xi^{2}}\,d\xi=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda\xi^{2}}\,d\xi\right)^{-1}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-\lambda\xi^{2}}\,d\xi=\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{\lambda}x)

(90)

we must have

\frac{1}{2}e^{-c_{1}/2}\sqrt{\pi}\,\operatorname{erfi}\bigl(e^{c_{1}/2}x\bigr)+c_{2}=\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{\lambda}x),

(91)

so that

c_{1}=i\pi+\log\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr),\qquad c_{2}=0.

(92)

We showed that in the case when
\mu=0
our function
T_{\lambda,0}(x)
coincides with the error function
\operatorname{erf}(\sqrt{\lambda}x)
with the value
\lambda=\pi/4
. When
\mu\neq 0
we cannot obtain the solution to the ODE (87) in closed form. We may try a numerical procedure or another method to solve it. Our aim is to show how we may apply the new special function
T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)
in probability and physics.

Example

We look for
\lambda
and
\mu
in order to adjust the error function by means of the function
y(x)=T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)
. To this end, we impose the following conditions:

\operatorname{erf}(1)=T_{\lambda,\mu}(1),\qquad\operatorname{erf}^{\prime}(1)=T^{\prime}_{\lambda,\mu}(1).

(93)

Solving this system gives

\lambda=0.1671645,\qquad\mu=0.8449657.

(94)

The function
T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)
converts into

T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=1.05021\int_{0}^{x}\exp\bigl(-\xi^{2}(0.167164+0.844966\,\operatorname{erf}(\xi))\bigr)\,d\xi.

9
Application of
T(x)
in Thermodynamics

In physics (in particular in statistical mechanics), the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is a particular probability distribution named after James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann. It was first defined and used for describing particle speeds in idealized gases, where the particles move freely inside a stationary container without interacting with one another, except for very brief collisions in which they exchange energy and momentum with each other or with their thermal environment. The term “particle” in this context refers to gaseous particles (atoms or molecules), and the system of particles is assumed to have reached thermodynamic equilibrium. The energies of such particles follow what is known as Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics, and the statistical distribution of speeds is derived by equating particle energies with kinetic energy. Mathematically, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is the chi distribution with three degrees of freedom (the components of the velocity vector in Euclidean space), with a scale parameter measuring speeds in units proportional to the square root of
T/m
(the ratio of temperature and particle mass); see Figure 11.

Figure 11:
Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

The CDF for the Boltzmann distribution may be approximated by means of the new special function
T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)
as follows:

\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}a}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{x}{a}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right)\approx T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{x}{a}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2a^{2}}\right),

(96)

where
T_{\lambda,\mu}(x)
is an approximation to
\operatorname{erf}(x/\sqrt{2}a)
for some parameters
\lambda
and
\mu
depending on
a
. This approximation may be obtained in a similar way to what we illustrated in Example 1, Figure 11. On the other hand, in the case when
0<a\leq 1
we may approximate the CDF for the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution as shown in Figure 12 for the value

10
Conclusion

We have studied a new probability distribution defined on
[0,+\infty)
and we gave series representations for
T(x)
function using Padé approximant. Really we approximated the CDF for that distribution by means of Chébyshev polynomials and the error function. The methods we applied are suitable for approximating other CDF for probability distributions, since their CDF are bounded and they take values from
0
to
1
. And it is well known that Chébyshev polynomials are the optimal ones for approximating continuous functions. On the other hand, it is also possible to approximate such functions by means of rational Chébyshev approximants. This technique may be used in future works.
