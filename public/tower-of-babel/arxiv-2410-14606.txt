Streaming Deep Reinforcement Learning Finally Works
Mohamed Elsayed, Elena Sorina Lupu sorina.lupu@openmindresearch.org, Gautham Vasan vasan@ualberta.ca, A. Rupam Mahmood armahmood@ualberta.ca Department of Computing Science, University of Alberta Canada CIFAR AI Chair Alberta Machine Intelligence Institute (Amii) Openmind Research Institute

Abstract
Learning from a stream of experience as it arrives, also known as streaming learning, is a core part of natural learning. However, reliable streaming learning has remained a persistent challenge in modern deep reinforcement learning (RL). Instead, most deep RL algorithms learn from old experience by storing past interactions in a buffer—an approach that can be restrictive in resource-constrained and privacy-sensitive applications. We show that both classical streaming RL, such as Q-learning and actor-critic, when used with deep neural networks, and batch deep RL, such as PPO, SAC, and DQN, when adapted to the streaming setting, often fail to learn. Across 58 58 Atari games and 50 50 continuous-control tasks, we find that these methods, in aggregate, perform close to random policies despite extensive task-specific hyperparameter searches. We call this pattern stream barrier . Here, we introduce Stream-X, a shared recipe for streaming deep RL algorithms that combines signal normalization, representation stabilization, and controlled parameter updates. By applying Stream-X to several base streaming RL algorithms, we provide the first family of deep RL algorithms to overcome the stream barrier. Using one prescribed hyperparameter configuration per algorithm across tasks, Stream-X substantially improves aggregate performance, often on par with batch RL algorithms. Beyond these benchmarks, we demonstrate learning with Stream-X algorithms under nonstationarity and resource constraints. Stream-AC, one of the Stream-X algorithms, repeatedly recovers performance across alternating floor-friction regimes in simulation, outperforming the evaluated PPO and SAC baselines. It also learns a heading tracking task on a robot using proprioceptive and visual features from the on-board camera in a naturally changing laboratory environment. Stream-Q learns a Pong game from pixels directly on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller, a device with limited compute and memory. Together, these results demonstrate that Stream-X achieves effective learning without storing past transitions and enables adaptation and on-device learning.

Introduction

A long-term vision of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is to build agents that continue to learn indefinitely as they interact with their environments
(
Kudithipudi et al. 2022
)
.
A household robot, a personal assistant, or a planetary rover cannot encounter all the useful experiences before deployment, nor can it assume that the world remains fixed once deployed.
Such agents must learn from live interactions as they arrive, under limits on memory, computation, and communication.
Reinforcement learning (RL), inspired by natural learning from live interactions
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
Schultz et al. 1997
)
, is a framework suited to this setting.
Modern deep reinforcement learning, which combines reinforcement learning with deep neural networks, has enabled many successes in artificial intelligence, including game-playing agents
(
Silver et al. 2017
;
Berner et al. 2019
)
, scientific control systems
(
Degrave et al. 2022
;
Bellemare et al. 2020
)
, and post-training of large language models
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
.
In all these applications, deep reinforcement learning follows a specific training procedure: past interaction transitions are stored in a buffer, and network weights are updated using batches of these transitions.
This batch-learning procedure fits the broader machinery of deep learning, where batch updates exploit dense matrix multiplications on large GPU clusters.
However, in all these settings, training completes before deployment.
The agent learns from pre-collected or synthetic interactions generated in simulation, rather than from the live stream of experience in the real-world setting where it ultimately operates.

Applying batch learning to learn from real-world live interactions exposes three limitations and points to an alternative, which we call
streaming learning
.
First, in batch learning, interaction samples are not used when they arrive; they are placed in a buffer, and their influence is delayed until they are sampled in a later batch.
Second, batch learning requires storing past samples, which can be large in practical deep RL, where observations often include high-dimensional sensory data such as images.
This can be difficult to facilitate when agents are operating under limited memory, computation, communication, or privacy constraints, such as on edge devices or embodied robots.
Third, batch training departs from models of biological memory, which store transformed representations of experience rather than literal copies
(
Bartlett 1932
;
Neisser 1967
;
Hinton et al. 1986
;
Nagy et al. 2025
)
.
In streaming learning, by contrast, the agent learns from each interaction as it arrives, without storing raw interactions (
a).
Early RL algorithms such as Temporal Difference (TD), Q-learning, and SARSA were designed to learn this way.
Similarly, adaptive control systems
(
Slotine and Li 1991
)
update their parameters online, using each new interaction to continuously refine the controller.
Streaming learning is natural and well-motivated, yet its realization with deep networks,
streaming deep RL
, was found to be unstable in early deep RL work
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
.
We call this phenomenon
stream barrier
; overcoming it remains an open problem, which we address in this work.

We present
Stream-X
, a recipe that overcomes the stream barrier in the evaluated value-based and actor-critic algorithms with a single shared hyperparameter configuration per algorithm.
En route, we show that the stream barrier is pervasive across the evaluated deep RL algorithms.
Our demonstration of stream barrier spans both deep RL algorithms and deep learning versions of classical streaming RL algorithms.
Across the RL benchmarks studied, when the evaluated classical streaming RL algorithms are run with deep networks or the evaluated deep RL algorithms are run without batch updates or buffers, they show substantial learning difficulties.
These results show that the evaluated deep RL algorithms rely heavily on batching and buffering, masking instabilities that emerge once these supports are removed.
To address them, we introduce Stream-X, a recipe that mitigates these instabilities by combining signal normalization, representation stabilization, and controlled parameter updates, which we apply to a varied class of RL algorithms for value-based control and actor-critic methods.
Each Stream-X algorithm uses a single prescribed hyperparameter configuration across its evaluated benchmark tasks.

Stream-X algorithms enable two long-sought capabilities that are missing in batch RL: fast adaptation to nonstationary environments (
) and on-device learning on resource-constrained devices (
).
Using a nonstationary Ant task adapted from prior continual learning work, we show that the evaluated PPO and SAC baselines, which learn from buffered experience that can become stale as conditions change, fail to match Stream-AC’s sustained recovery and improvement across repeated changes in floor friction.
On a real Ant robot, Stream-AC can learn to perform heading tracking using proprioceptive and visual features in a naturally changing laboratory environment over many hours. Stream-Q, another instance of Stream-X, learns pixel-based Pong directly on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller, achieving effective learning without batching or buffering.

Overall, Stream-X revitalizes streaming deep RL as a natural learning regime by resolving a long-standing obstacle and brings fast adaptation and on-device learning within reach.

2
Stream barrier in deep reinforcement learning

We define stream barrier as the phenomenon in which deep RL algorithms fail to learn stably, even after hyperparameter tuning, when learning directly from a stream of experience without storing raw interactions.
Prior work contains hints of this phenomenon.
The most prominent evidence is provided in the work that popularized modern deep RL and introduced the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
.
The authors observed that when DQN is used in a streaming manner, it “could get stuck in a poor local minimum, or even diverge catastrophically,” whereas batch learning led to “smoothing out learning and avoiding oscillations or divergence.”
Instead of overcoming the instability of streaming learning, modern deep RL largely bypasses it through batch learning.
Deep RL took off partly due to the massive parallelization capability of batch learning but left streaming learning largely unexplored.

The same algorithms learn successfully once their buffers and batch updates are restored (see

This breakdown occurs for both a value-based algorithm, DQN, and policy-based algorithms, SAC and PPO, showing that stream barrier is not confined to a single algorithm class.

We further test whether stream barrier is merely an artifact of modern deep RL algorithms reduced to streaming learning.
We evaluate two classical streaming RL algorithms: Q-learning
(
Watkins 1989
)
and incremental actor-critic
(
Barto et al. 1983
)
, originally developed for linear function approximation and here extended to deep neural networks.
These algorithms were designed to learn from each interaction as it arrives.
Yet, with deep networks, they too fail to learn effective policies across both domains. We add their aggregate performance to the same plot in

Stream barrier therefore arises across both evaluated deep RL and classical streaming algorithms, and across both value-based and policy-based methods, establishing it as a systematic phenomenon rather than a property of any single algorithm or benchmark.

To test whether stored experience is what allows modern deep RL to learn stably, we sweep the buffer size of standard deep RL algorithms, namely, PPO, SAC, and DQN (
c).
If stream barrier is caused by removing stored experience, then performance should be poor at small buffer sizes and recover as algorithms regain access to larger archives of past interactions.
Across all three algorithms, median normalized performance is low at small buffer sizes and improves at larger settings, although the dependence is not always monotonic.
This shows that stream barrier is not an isolated failure at a buffer size of one: poor median performance persists across small and moderate buffers, and stable learning emerges only once the buffer is large enough.

The ingredients of the Stream-X recipe are signal normalization, representation stabilization, and controlled parameter updates (
b). We present them here in detail.

1) Normalize the signals.
A batch update averages over samples of differing magnitudes, whereas under streaming learning each update is exposed to the raw scale of a single state and a single reward, so both the network inputs and the temporal-difference error can vary by orders of magnitude across tasks and over time within a task. Signal normalization removes task-specific scale from the learning signal.
We maintain running estimates of the mean and variance of each input coordinate, standardize the input entries
O_{i},\forall i
before it enters each network, and scale the reward
R
using the running variance of a discounted reward trace

(1)

where

2) Stabilize the representations.
When an agent learns from a temporally correlated stream of experience, its ability to learn can degrade as the network representation distribution shifts
(
Lyle et al. 2025

(
Ba et al. 2016

\displaystyle\phi({\bm{a}})

\displaystyle\doteq\frac{{\bm{a}}-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma+\varepsilon}},\quad\text{where }\mu\doteq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}a_{i}\text{ and }\sigma\doteq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}(a_{i}-\mu),

(2)

where

Furthermore, we initialize linear and convolutional layers sparsely, so that each unit begins with a limited number of nonzero incoming connections (see

3) Control the update.

Without batching, every transition acts directly on the parameters, so an unusually large increment is not averaged with other samples and can dominate the update. We introduce a new optimizer,

\mathrm{Opt}

For each parameter vector, StreamingOptimizer maintains an element-wise decaying running maximum of the raw increment, initialized to zero,

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}_{t}=\max\!\left(\beta{\bm{v}}_{t-1},\lvert{\bm{u}}_{t}\rvert\right),\qquad{\bm{w}}_{t+1}={\bm{w}}_{t}+\alpha\frac{{\bm{u}}_{t}}{{\bm{v}}_{t}+\varepsilon},

(3)

where
\beta\in[0,1)
is the retained fraction,
\alpha>0
is the step size,
\epsilon>0
a small constant for numerical stability, and all operations are element-wise.
Because
v_{t,i}\geq\lvert u_{t,i}\rvert
for every coordinate
i

\lvert\Delta_{i}\rvert<\alpha
, independent of the magnitude of
u_{t,i}

The maximum responds immediately to a large increment and relaxes through the decay, limiting the spike itself and reducing subsequent updates relative to this recent scale.

4
Fast adaptation with streaming learning

Streaming learning agents learn from the most recent observation without relying on a stored replay buffer of past experiences, which makes them well-suited in nonstationary environments where previously collected data can be stale, e.g., due to changes in the environment.
We examine this adaptation hypothesis in two complementary settings: simulated locomotion with abrupt, repeated changes in floor friction, and vision-based navigation on a physical robot in a naturally changing laboratory over many hours.

4.1
Locomotion with an alternating-friction floor in simulation

We used an alternating-friction version of the Ant environment
(
Todorov et al. 2012
)
, adapted from the nonstationary locomotion setting of
Dohare et al. (2024)
. The coefficient of friction between the robot’s feet and the floor alternated between
2.0
(high-traction) and
0.02
(slippery) every
2
million time steps, resulting in ten regimes over
20
million steps (
a). Each change occurred instantaneously with no explicit signal to the agent, requiring it to adapt to the changed contact dynamics from its ongoing stream of experience.
The alternating friction therefore tests whether the agent can recover performance after repeated changes in the environment.

Related work

Continual learning.

The goal of continual learning research is to develop algorithms that allow agents to keep learning indefinitely in a nonstationary environment.
The two obstacles in continual learning are loss of plasticity
(
Dohare et al. 2024
)
and catastrophic forgetting
(
McCloskey and Cohen 1989
;
Hetherington and Seidenberg 1989
)
.
Loss of plasticity reduces the agent’s ability to learn gradually over time, while catastrophic forgetting prevents the agent from retaining and utilizing past memories, ultimately hindering its performance improvement.
Some works focus on maintaining plasticity primarily in nonstationary supervised learning
(
Dohare et al. 2024
;
Kumar et al. 2023
;
Lewandowski et al. 2023
;
Elsayed and Mahmood 2024
;
Lewandowski et al. 2025
;
Lee et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, whereas others focus on reinforcement learning setting
(
Delfosse et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2024
;
Ma et al. 2024
;
Lyle et al. 2024b
;
Lyle et al. 2024a
;
Lyle et al. 2023
;
Elsayed et al. 2024a
, e.g.,)
. On the other hand, a few works
(
Elsayed and Mahmood 2024
, e.g.,)
address catastrophic forgetting and loss of plasticity at the same time. In particular,
Elsayed and Mahmood (2024)
addresses catastrophic forgetting by protecting useful weights from drastic change when using gradient-based updates. Another approach to address forgetting is to promote sparse representations. Sparse representations
(
Lan and Mahmood 2023
, e.g.,)
address forgetting since their gradient-based updates make significant changes to the weights of fewer connections than dense representations.

Our work evaluates Stream-AC in two nonstationary environments: varying friction in a simulated Ant (
) and a vision-based navigation task on-board the Open Ant robot in naturally changing laboratory conditions (
).
Apart from these two nonstationary Ant environments, the other environments we studied are stationary, yet there are commonalities between instability issues in single-task learning and continual learning issues such as loss of plasticity
(
Dohare et al. 2023
)
.
For example, LayerNorm, recently shown effective against loss of plasticity
(
Lyle et al. 2023
)
, is also found beneficial against instability in our work.
Moreover, StreamingOptimizer was found effective against learning instability.
Our update control may also be beneficial for addressing catastrophic forgetting to some extent, as it may alleviate interference by bounding the update.

Leaky maximum normalization.

AdaGrad
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
, RMSProp
(
Tieleman and Hinton 2012
)
, and Adam
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
)
normalize updates using accumulated or exponentially averaged squared gradients. In contrast, a
leaky maximum
,
v_{t}=\max(\beta v_{t-1},|u_{t}|)
, where
\beta\in[0,1)
and
u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
, tracks an upper envelope: it captures peaks immediately, forgets them gradually, and always dominates the current
u_{t}
. AdaMax
(
Kingma and Ba 2014
, Section 7.1)
derives this recurrence as the infinity-norm limit of exponentially weighted
p
-norm accumulators and uses it to normalize Adam’s bias-corrected first moment. An earlier precedent is AutoStep
(
Mahmood et al. 2012
)
, which uses maximum-based normalization in step-size adaptation to control the response to sudden changes in scale. Its normalizer captures peaks immediately but relaxes toward the current sample, rather than decaying toward zero at a fixed rate as is the case in our optimizer.

StreamingOptimizer applies the leaky maximum to the full TD increment
|\delta_{t}z_{t}|
without an additional first moment. Matching the normalized increment to the quantity tracked by the leaky maximum bounds each coordinate’s displacement by
\alpha
, as shown in Appendix
, independently of the decay rate. Instead, AdaMax normalizes a first moment by a leaky maximum of gradient magnitudes, so its update is not guaranteed to be in the interval
(-\alpha,\alpha)
regardless of the decay rates. In contrast, applying the leaky maximum to the bias-corrected first moment instead of the gradients gives the displacement bound,
\Delta\in(-\alpha,\alpha)
, in a general-purpose optimizer (see Algorithm
in Appendix
).

Streaming reinforcement learning.

Most algorithms introduced by
Sutton and Barto (2018)
are streaming. However, their usage remained limited to tabular and linear approximation. Later, several attempts were made to use neural networks with those streaming methods. There are a few works that target the problem setting of streaming reinforcement learning. For example,
Elfwing et al. (2018)
proposed a new activation function that gave more stability and showed promise with deep Q-learning but still with limited performance. Similarly,
Young and Tian (2019)
demonstrated the effectiveness of the approach by
Elfwing et al. (2018)
on their MinAtar benchmark, a simplified version of the Atari benchmark
(
Bellemare et al. 2013
)
.
De Asis et al. (2020)
introduced an incremental version of REINFORCE that works well in small problems
(
Kimura et al. 1995
, see also)
.
Javed et al. (2024)
introduced the SwiftTD algorithm for prediction problems by applying it to the last linear layer of a deep network and TD
(\lambda)
to all other layers.
Modayil and Abbas (2023)
introduced a streaming method for prediction that learns from unstructured observations.

Concurrently with the first version of this work,
Vasan et al. (2024)
introduced a policy-gradient method with deep neural networks that learns without batch updates, target networks, or replay buffers.
The main differences are twofold: 1) their method is specific to reparameterized policy gradient, whereas we provide a general recipe that spans value-based and policy-based control, and 2) unlike their work, our results use a single prescribed hyperparameter configuration per algorithm, held fixed across 58 Atari and 50 continuous control tasks, as well as on hardware.
Our work is a comprehensive step toward reviving streaming deep RL by providing a class of algorithms for both value-based and policy-based control, showing success on most commonly used complex benchmark tasks.

Adaptive control.

Adaptive control algorithms
(
Slotine and Li 1991
;
Ioannou and Sun 1996
;
Krstic et al. 1995
;
Narendra and Annaswamy 2012
)
, such as Model Reference Adaptive Control (MRAC) and Self-tuning Control (STC), can also be considered forms of streaming learning.
They update the controller parameters incrementally after each new observation, without storing or replaying past interactions in a buffer.
This allows the controller to continuously adapt to changes in the environment.
However, adaptive control uses a known parametric structure of the system, with parameters that appear linearly, whereas Stream-X considers deep reinforcement learning with neural network policies and value functions.
Examples of streaming learning using adaptive control on hardware include NASA’s X-15 crewed hypersonic rocket-powered aircraft
(
Dydek et al. 2008
)
,
remote-controlled airplanes
(
Shi et al. 2020
)
, quadcopters
(
O’Connell et al. 2022
)
, and ground vehicles
(
Lupu et al. 2025
)
.

Resource-constrained learning.
Learning under tight memory and computational constraints connects our work to TinyML, which brings machine learning to microcontrollers and other resource-constrained devices, including tiny robots
(
Neuman et al. 2022
)
and edge devices
(
Lin et al. 2022
;
Zhu et al. 2024
)
. Recent work has made such learning more practical by reducing the memory and computational costs of backpropagation
(
Cai et al. 2020
;
Profentzas et al. 2022
;
Patil et al. 2022
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
.
Deep RL introduces an additional challenge: replay buffers and rollout batches store past interactions, adding memory demands beyond those of the network and its updates. Stream-X addresses this algorithmic challenge by learning from each transition without storing past transitions for learning.
Our Stream-Q experiment demonstrates this connection concretely: the agent learns Pong from pixels directly on a microcontroller (
; Appendix
).
Prior work
(
Eschmann et al. 2024
)
demonstrated learning using SAC
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
directly on a microcontroller for a pendulum simulated environment using low-dimensional state observations. Our contribution on resource-constrained learning is not the first deep RL learning on a microcontroller, nor a claim that such devices cannot fit experience buffers.
Rather, vision-based learning or more complex observation vectors make stored experience expensive. Our small buffer results from
c show why fitting a small buffer is not sufficient for reliable learning in the evaluated methods.

Limitations and future work

Although we have explored a few representative streaming RL algorithms, our approach is compatible with many other algorithms, such as double Q-learning
(
Van Hasselt et al. 2016
)
, dueling Q-learning networks
(
Wang et al. 2016
)
, noisy networks Q-learning
(
Fortunato et al. 2018
)
, or even in the continuing setting
(
Naik et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
. Additionally, we have considered only simple MLP and CNN architectures, leaving the exploration of more advanced modern architectures like residual networks, also including those designed specifically for streaming learning (e.g.,
Modayil and Abbas 2023
), to future work.
Our paper focuses on model-free methods, which are less sample-efficient compared to model-based ones; thus, a promising direction is to discover how the agent can incrementally learn a model of the environment to improve sample efficiency following the success of batch model-based methods
(
Hafner et al. 2025
;
Samsami et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024

. Another promising direction is to combine our approach with real-time recurrent learning
(
Williams and Zipser 1989
)
to handle partial observability, especially with the recent scalable approaches
(
Irie et al. 2024
;
Zucchet et al. 2023
;
Elelimy et al. 2024
;
Javed et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. In addition, we focus mainly on on-policy methods (except for Stream-Q) and leave comprehensive experimentation with our approach to a broader range of off-policy methods
(
Sutton et al. 2016
;
He et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
for future work.

9
Conclusion

Deep reinforcement learning agents can learn effectively from a stream of experience without replay or batch updates as demonstrated by Stream-X. Across the benchmarks studied in this work, streaming variants of representative deep RL algorithms and classical streaming algorithms with deep networks show substantial learning difficulties despite the reported hyperparameter searches. Stream-X algorithms overcome this empirical pattern, which we call stream barrier, through a recipe shared across value-based and actor–critic learning.

Stream-X combines representation stabilization, signal normalization, and bounded parameter updates with streaming algorithms that use eligibility traces. The resulting value-based and actor–critic methods learn effectively across diverse tasks using a single prescribed hyperparameter configuration per algorithm without per-task tuning.

The experiments illustrate the practical capabilities of streaming learning. Stream-AC recovers performance across repeated changes in floor friction more effectively than the evaluated PPO and SAC baselines. Stream-Q learns pixel-based Pong on a microcontroller, and the Open Ant study evaluates learning with proprioceptive and visual features in a nonstationary laboratory environment. These demonstrations motivate streaming RL for adaptive, resource-constrained agents. By making deep RL effective across these benchmark domains without storing past transitions, Stream-X establishes a practical foundation for agents that learn directly from ongoing interaction.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{(1-\beta_{1})u_{t,i}}\sum_{k=1}\beta_{1}|g_{k,i}|

\displaystyle\left(\begin{subarray}{c}\text{using the triangle inequality }\left|\sum_{k}a_{k}\right|\leq\sum_{k}|a_{k}|\end{subarray}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{(1-\beta_{1})u_{t,i}}\sum_{k=1}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}u_{t,i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{1-\beta_{1}}\sum_{k=1}\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{2}}\right).

When
0\leq\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}
, this is a geometric sum with ratio
\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}<1

\sum_{k=1}\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{2}}\right)=\sum_{j=0}\left(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\beta_{2}}\right)=\frac{1-(\beta_{1}/\beta_{2})}{1-\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\beta_{1}/\beta_{2}}=\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}.

Consequently,

\frac{|\widehat{m}_{t,i}|}{u_{t,i}}\leq\frac{(1-\beta_{1})\,\beta_{2}}{(1-\beta_{1})\,(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})},\qquad 0\leq\beta_{1}<\beta_{2},

with strict inequality when
\beta_{1}>0
.
As
t\to\infty
, the bound approaches
(1-\beta_{1})\beta_{2}/(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})
. With
\beta_{2}=0.999
fixed, this is approximately
1.009
,
1.11
, and
1.25
for
\beta_{1}=0.9
,
0.99
, and
0.995
, respectively. In contrast, normalizing the corrected first moment by its own running maximum, as in BoundedOptimizer, bounds each coordinate’s displacement by the step size independently of both decay rates.

Shared subroutines

The shared subroutines are given below, together with TrackMeanVar, the estimator used by both signal-normalization subroutines. Each is itself streaming: it keeps a constant amount of state per parameter or per observation coordinate and never revisits a past transition.

SparseInit
(Algorithm
) allocates
(1-s)\sum_{l}n_{l}
nonzero weights across layers following
Yildirim et al. [2024]
, with
s=0.95
. Here,
n_{l}
counts layer
l

m_{l}
its incoming weights per unit. Allocations are proportional to
\phi_{l}=\texttt{fan\_in}+\texttt{fan\_out}
for linear layers or
C_{\text{in}}+C_{\text{out}}+k_{h}+k_{w}

n_{l}

m_{l}=C_{\text{in}}k_{h}k_{w}

TrackMeanVar
(Algorithm
) is the estimator underlying both signal-normalization subroutines. It maintains a running mean and variance for a scalar or vector stream in a single pass. We used Welford’s algorithm
[
Welford, 1962
]
, which accumulates the corrected sum of squares
p
and reports the unbiased sample variance. It weights every observation equally and converges to the true moments of a stationary stream. Although we track the mean and variance using sample estimates based on Welford’s algorithm in this work, we also tried relying on exponential moving averages that track the mean and variance with a decay of
0.99999
and observed very similar performance.

NormalizeObservation
(Algorithm
) applies TrackMeanVar to the observation vector, coordinate by coordinate, and returns the standardized observation that is fed to the network. The statistics are updated with the observation before it is used, so the network never sees an unstandardized input. For frame-stacked image observations the statistics are shared across the stacked frames, since the frames are samples of the same image distribution.

ScaleReward
(Algorithm
) divides the reward by the running standard deviation of the discounted reward trace
U_{t}=\gamma U_{t-1}+r_{t}
without subtracting a mean. The trace is reset at episode boundaries, but its accumulated statistics are retained.

Appendix C
Relationship to the earlier version

This manuscript builds on
Elsayed et al. [2024b]
, released as arXiv:2410.14606 on 18 October 2024. The comparison here refers to its revised version of 6 December 2024 (v2). That work introduced the term
stream barrier
and the Stream-X approach, combining eligibility traces, signal normalization, layer normalization, sparse initialization, and update control. It presented Stream-AC, Stream-Q, Stream-SARSA, and Stream-TD, with experiments in continuous control, MinAtar, Atari, and time-series prediction. Here, we discuss how the current manuscript differs from the earlier version.

Revised update control.

The earlier algorithms used Overshooting-bounded Gradient Descent (ObGD), which scales an update using the trace’s
L_{1}
norm and the TD-error magnitude, motivated by control of the effective step size. Its adjusted step size is

\alpha_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{\max\left(1,\alpha\kappa\max(1,|\delta_{t}|)\|{\bm{z}}_{t}\|_{1}\right)},

where
\kappa
is the overshooting-control parameter. This global scaling couples every parameter’s update to the summed trace magnitude across the network. As a network grows, an increase in
\|{\bm{z}}_{t}\|_{1}
makes the shared step size smaller. For example, with
d
nonzero trace coordinates of equal magnitude, each coordinate’s displacement in this regime is
|\delta_{t}|/[\kappa\max(1,|\delta_{t}|)d]
, decreasing as
1/d
. Thus, ObGD can become increasingly conservative per parameter in larger networks. Instead, StreamingOptimizer divides each coordinate of the full increment (e.g.,
{\bm{u}}_{t}=\delta_{t}{\bm{z}}_{t}
for TD-based updates) by its own decaying running maximum, avoiding this network-wide normalization. Appendix
establishes a per-coordinate displacement bound and characterizes the accumulator’s response to large increments; it also introduces the general-purpose BoundedOptimizer variant.

Layer-aware sparse initialization.

The earlier initialization applied the same sparsity fraction
s
to every layer. Its implementation zeros
\lceil sm\rceil
incoming weights per unit, where
m
is the layer’s fan-in. If
(1-s)m<1
, this rounds up to all
m
weights, making the entire weight matrix zero. In a fully connected first layer, high sparsity or a low-dimensional observation can therefore remove every input connection at initialization: for example,
s=0.95
and
m=10
leave no nonzero weights. With zero biases, the first-layer pre-activations are then zero for every observation, so observation information cannot propagate into the network at initialization. This is an initial loss of input dependence, not necessarily permanent disconnection, because the zeroed weights remain trainable. Instead, the current initialization based on
Yildirim et al. [2024]
allocates a global weight budget across layers, allowing low-fan-in layers to receive higher densities rather than imposing the same sparsity everywhere (Algorithm
). This addresses the uniform-allocation limitation, although budget allocation and rounding alone do not guarantee nonzero connections at arbitrarily high sparsity.

Broader algorithmic and empirical evaluation.

The current family adds Stream-RAC, applying the recipe to reparameterized actor–critic learning. The benchmark study now includes explicit
30
-configuration baseline searches, per-task configuration selection, and PPO/SAC/DQN buffer-size sweeps (Figure
). Figure
reports median all-best-normalized performance over
50
continuous-control tasks and
58
Atari games under the protocol in Appendix
. Atari streaming-method reporting uses
30
runs rather than the earlier
10
, and continuous-control reporting uses a
5
M-step budget rather than
20
M. We also extend the ingredient ablations to both Stream-AC and Stream-Q and report network diagnostics. Because the algorithms, budgets, and reporting procedures differ between versions, differences in their reported scores are not a controlled comparison of the two versions.

Adaptation and on-device learning.

The earlier work studied learning in stationary benchmark environments and motivated resource-constrained and continual learning applications. This manuscript presents a continual learning experiment through the alternating-friction Ant setup, including a SAC replay-capacity comparison (Figures
and
). It also adds a robot heading-tracking study with proprioceptive and visual features, and pixel-based Pong learning on a microcontroller (Figures
and
). These studies extend the experimental scope from benchmark learning to adaptation and resource-constrained learning.

Development and reporting.

Appendix
now makes the development protocol explicit: the recipe was developed on five MuJoCo Gym tasks, while discrete-control hyperparameters were selected on MinAtar before the first Atari evaluation without algorithm changes or retuning. This documents how configurations were obtained. The current reporting specifies all-best normalization with median aggregation across all tasks in Figure
. Divergence is treated per run, rather than assigning zero to an entire method–task result when any run diverges as in the earlier version.

Appendix D
Variance equivalence of returns and reward traces

A return-based normalization scheme is appealing because TD targets are governed by the scale of discounted returns rather than by the scale of one-step rewards. The return, however, is
non-causal
: computing it at time
t
requires rewards that have not yet been observed, making it unavailable in a strict streaming update. A natural causal substitute is the discounted reward trace, which depends only on present and past rewards. Here, we show that, under a second-order stationarity assumption, the return and reward trace have the same variance when measured jointly over fixed-horizon episodes and uniformly sampled timesteps.

Consider episodes of fixed horizon
T
. Let episode
m
produce rewards
r_{1},\ldots,r_{T}
. At each timestep
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, define

G_{t}=\sum_{k=0}\gamma r_{t+k},\qquad U_{t}=\sum_{k=0}\gamma r_{t-k}.

Under our assumption, there exist a constant
\mu
and a lag-covariance function
c
such that

\mathbb{E}_{m}\left[r_{t}\right]=\mu,\qquad\operatorname{Cov}_{m}\left(r_{i},r_{j}\right)=c(j-i)

for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
. Since covariance is symmetric,

c(\ell)=c(-\ell)
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\tau
be uniformly distributed over
\{1,\ldots,T\}
and independent of the episode index
m
. Under the assumption above,

\operatorname{Var}_{m,\tau}\left(G_{\tau}\right)=\operatorname{Var}_{m,\tau}\left(U_{\tau}\right).

Proof.

For
L\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
, define

a_{L}=\mu\sum_{k=0}\gamma,\qquad v_{L}=\sum_{i=0}\sum_{j=0}\gamma c(j-i).

By the stationarity assumption,

\mathbb{E}_{m}\left[G_{t}\right]=a_{T-t+1},\qquad\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(G_{t}\right)=v_{T-t+1},

whereas

\mathbb{E}_{m}\left[U_{t}\right]=a_{t},\qquad\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(U_{t}\right)=v_{t}.

For
U_{t}
, the covariance terms involve
c(i-j)
. Since covariance is symmetric,
c(i-j)=c(j-i)
, and therefore
\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(U_{t}\right)=v_{t}
.

Because
\tau
is uniform over
\{1,\ldots,T\}
, the random variables
\tau
and
T-\tau+1
have the same distribution. Therefore, by the law of total variance,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{m,\tau}\left(G_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(G_{\tau}\mid\tau\right)\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\tau}\left(\mathbb{E}_{m}\left[G_{\tau}\mid\tau\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[v_{T-\tau+1}\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\tau}\left(a_{T-\tau+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[v_{\tau}\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\tau}\left(a_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\left[\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(U_{\tau}\mid\tau\right)\right]+\operatorname{Var}_{\tau}\left(\mathbb{E}_{m}\left[U_{\tau}\mid\tau\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}_{m,\tau}\left(U_{\tau}\right).

∎

The equality holds jointly over episodes and uniformly sampled timesteps. It does not generally hold at each fixed timestep, since
\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(G_{t}\right)=v_{T-t+1}
and
\operatorname{Var}_{m}\left(U_{t}\right)=v_{t}
.

Appendix F
Diagnostics

To examine learning dynamics, we show in
three network statistics used by
Dohare et al. [2024]
, measured for PPO, PPO1, and Stream-AC with the same procedure. These statistics characterize representations and weights, but they are not direct tests of the ability to learn a new task and do not by themselves establish preserved plasticity.

We log three quantities, separately for the policy network and the value network, following the reference implementation of
Dohare et al. [2024]
.

•

Dormant units (%)
: the percentage of hidden units that are strictly inactive on every state in the measurement batch. This is a meaningful notion of deadness only for ReLU-like activations. For this reason all three diagnostic agents use ReLU activations rather than the default Tanh activation of PPO/PPO1 implementations or the default LeakyReLU activation used for Stream-AC.

•

Effective rank
: the smallest number of singular values of the last hidden layer’s activation matrix whose normalized sum reaches
0.99
. Because this is a count bounded by the layer width, and the agents use different widths, we report it divided by the width, i.e. as the fraction of directions retained.

•

Average weight magnitude
: the mean of
|w|
over the linear mapping weights of the network, excluding biases and the policy’s log-standard-deviation parameter. Its absolute scale is fixed by the fan-in dependent initialization, so we report it relative to each run’s own value at the first checkpoint, which makes the plotted quantity the drift away from initialization.

Each agent keeps a rolling measurement buffer of the
2048
most recent observations it has encountered, and the current networks are re-evaluated on that buffer every
10{,}000
environment steps to produce the three statistics above. At each checkpoint, we pass states recently visited by the agent through the checkpoint network to compute the diagnostics. This captures the network’s responses to the agent’s recent state distribution. In contrast,
Dohare et al. [2024]
accumulate activations during interaction, while the network is changing. We recompute activations at each checkpoint to apply the same diagnostic procedure to all three algorithms. Apart from the ReLU activation choice above, Stream-AC uses the
128\times 128
networks and hyperparameters of Appendix
; PPO uses the CleanRL defaults (
64\times 64
networks,
2048
-step rollouts, learning-rate annealing, gradient clipping); and PPO1 uses
64\times 64
networks with one environment, a one-transition rollout, one minibatch, and one update epoch, with advantage normalization disabled. Each curve is the mean over
30
runs with a
30
M-step budget. We show the plots smoothed with a centred
50
-checkpoint moving average, corresponding to approximately
0.5
M environment steps.

: every linear and convolutional layer uses the default dense initialization instead of the layer-dependent sparsities allocated with global target
s=0.95
. Dense weights follow
U[-1/\sqrt{\texttt{fan\_in}},\,1/\sqrt{\texttt{fan\_in}}]
; the sparse routine also zeros biases.

•

No StreamingOptimizer
: the update becomes
{\bm{w}}\leftarrow{\bm{w}}+\alpha\,\delta_{t}{\bm{z}}_{t}
, corresponding to plain stochastic gradient descent on the eligibility trace at the same step size, with no running maximum and hence no displacement bound.

•

No NormalizeObservation
: raw observations are provided to the network.

•

No RewardScaling
: the raw reward is used to compute the temporal-difference error.

•

No eligibility traces
:
\lambda
is set to
0
, so the trace reduces to the current gradient and credit no longer propagates backward in time.

Appendix H
The nonstationary robot vision task details

For the vision-based heading task, the agent receives histories of proprioceptive observations, actions, and visual features. Each proprioceptive observation is
24
-dimensional, comprising eight joint positions, eight joint velocities, a two-dimensional desired heading vector, and six IMU measurements. We stack the three most recent observations and actions, yielding a
72
-dimensional proprioceptive history and
24
-dimensional action history.

Figure 15
:
Actor and critic architecture: camera images are encoded by a frozen DINO ViT-S/16 backbone, projected to a lower-dimensional visual embedding, and concatenated with proprioceptive and action history before entering fully connected actor and critic heads.

At each time step, the on-board RGB image is resized and center-cropped to
224\times 224
pixels and processed by a frozen DINO ViT-S/16 visual encoder. We extract the
384
-dimensional CLS token and
\ell_{2}
-normalize it.
We use the CLS token rather than the spatial patch tokens since it already provides a compact, semantically-rich global summary of the scene.
In earlier experiments, we also evaluated using the full set of DINO patch tokens rather than the CLS token alone. This achieved comparable final performance, but required more hardware time to learn, likely due to the larger input dimensionality of the visual projection.
The three most recent CLS embeddings are stacked, producing a
1152
-dimensional visual input. The visual history is passed through a learned linear projection from
1152
to
128
dimensions, followed by a ReLU and layer normalization. The resulting
128
-dimensional visual embedding is concatenated with the
96
-dimensional proprioceptive and action history.

Both actor and critic use the same MLP after fusion. The resulting
224
-dimensional input is mapped to two
512
-dimensional hidden layers, each followed by layer normalization and LeakyReLU.
The policy outputs eight continuous actions in
[-1,1]
, corresponding to the eight actuated joints. These normalized actions are subsequently mapped to the physical joint ranges, with
\pm 30
for the hip joints and
\pm 20
for the knee joints. The DINO backbone is kept fixed, while the visual projection and actor–critic networks are learned online using Stream-AC.
