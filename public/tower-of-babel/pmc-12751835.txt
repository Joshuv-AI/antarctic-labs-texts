Dietary intake of total, animal, and plant proteins and risk of chronic kidney disease: systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies
Authors: Sepide Talebi, Sara Sadeghi Majd, Sheida Zeraattalab-Motlagh, Mehran Rahimlou, Hamed Mohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveKidney health and dietary protein consumption are incompatible. This study aimed to examine the association between different types of proteins (total, animal, and plant) and chronic kidney disease (CKD).MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus for articles published up to May 7, 2023. We included prospective cohort studies that reported the association between dietary intake of total, animal, or plant proteins and the risk of CKD, based on predefined eligibility criteria.ResultsTen prospective cohort studies involving 218,741 participants and 10,794 CKD events were included. Meta-analysis indicated that higher intake of animal protein (relative risk [RR] = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.77–0.97; GRADE: high certainty) and plant protein (RR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.67–0.98; GRADE: moderate certainty) was significantly associated with a reduced risk of CKD. However, no significant association was found between total protein intake and CKD risk (RR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.75–1.19; GRADE: low certainty). A significant linear dose-response relationship was observed between plant protein intake and lower CKD risk (P < 0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, while no significant association was observed between total protein intake and CKD risk, both animal and plant protein consumption were associated with a reduced risk. These findings highlight the potential protective role of protein sources in kidney health. Future research should explore the underlying mechanisms of these associations and examine the effects of specific protein sources in diverse populations over longer follow-up periods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01204-0.Keywords: Animal protein, Total protein, Plant protein, Chronic kidney disease, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a progressive systemic disease, which is defined by a decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of less than 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or by signs of kidney damage, or both, for at least 3 months [1, 2]. CKD is one of the top ten noncommunicable diseases and it is expected to be the 5th most common cause of years of life lost globally by 2040 [3, 4]. Due to the progressive and irreversible nature of the disease, interventional measures for primary prevention are highly warranted [5].

Current guidelines highlight modifiable risk factors, such as diet, obesity, physical inactivity, and substance use (e.g., alcohol and tobacco), as key contributors to CKD risk [6, 7]. In terms of preventing CKD and slowing its progression, nutritional factors especially protein intake levels have received more attention [8]. Although high-protein diets have become increasingly popular for purposes such as weight loss, muscle maintenance, and improved physical performance, they are not generally recommended for individuals with CKD due to their potential to worsen kidney function [8–10]. Additionally, higher consumption of dietary protein may increase glomerular pressure, leading to “intrarenal hypertension” and hyperfiltration. This process can result in glomerular sclerosis and renal function deterioration, even in individuals with no prior kidney issues. The underlying mechanism involves the increased filtration load on the glomeruli, creating sustained pressure and eventually causing kidney damage. However, further research is needed to fully understand the role of different dietary protein sources and their non-protein constituents in this process [5, 11–13].

Several studies have been conducted in this regard; for example, results from three cohort studies suggested that higher total protein intake is not associated with renal function decline in people with normal renal function [5, 14, 15]. However, there are studies that mention the positive correlation between these two factors [16, 17]. In addition, findings from prospective cohort studies indicated that higher consumption of animal protein (mainly processed and ultra-processed meat) is associated with an increased risk of CKD [11, 18, 19]. However, one of the previous studies reported that the substitution of red meat with plant protein sources decreased the risk of CKD [20]. Also, the results of a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, which examined the effects of soy protein intake in predialysis CKD patients; showed protective effects of this plant protein on serum creatinine and serum phosphorus concentrations [21]. As a result, the relationship between total, animal, and plant proteins and the risk of CKD is mixed and unclear.

With this regard, the present study aimed to provide a systematic review of the prospective cohort studies which had evaluated the longitudinal relationship between total, animal, and plant proteins and the risk of CKD.

Methods

We implemented the current dose-response meta-analysis using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [22]. The results were documented by implementing the PRISMA, which is the Preferred Reporting Item for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses framework [23]. The PROSPERO (CRD42023466538) database comprises current study protocol registration details.

Search strategy

We searched for pertinent studies in Scopus, Web of Science, and PubMed until May 7, 2023, applying proper keywords. No restrictions were implemented concerning language or time of publication. More information about the literature search is available in Table S1. A search strategy was performed and completed (S.T), and two researchers (S.SM and H.M) assessed the titles or abstracts. The same two investigators thoroughly assessed the relevant complete papers to determine their eligibility. Moreover, the references of eligible reviews of cohort studies were manually assessed to discover further studies.

Study selection

Prospective cohort studies were entered if (1) study individuals were healthy adults; (2) containing data on the relation between consumption of dietary protein (total, animal, and plant protein) and incidence rate of CKD; and 4) reporting hazard ratios, relative risks (RR), odds ratio (OR), and rate or risk ratios with related 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs). Studies were excluded if cohort study subjects had a history of kidney disease, diabetes, cancer, stroke, or myocardial infarction since they may have different dietary habits; and 2) intervention trials, conference or abstract summaries or reviews. If multiple publications comprised the same dataset, we selected the one with more subjects or a longer follow-up.

Data extraction and quality evaluation

From each entered cohort study, the following details were extracted by both researchers (S.SM and S.ZM): the surname of the first author, sex, research name, type and methods for the examination of exposure and outcome, publication year and location, average age of the subjects, follow-up, overall number of subjects and CKD events, controlled covariates, comparison group, as well as relevant effect estimates with associated 95%CIs. The third reviewer (S.T) checked the extracted information for any discrepancies.

The quality of every entered study was examined by the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies-of Interventions (ROBINS-I) [24]. The Cochrane approach investigated study quality and biases [25]. Two researchers (M.R and S.SM) independently did the ROBINS-I assessment and any discrepancies were solved by the third researcher (H.M) (Table S2).

Data synthesis and analysis

Current review reported the effect estimate as RR with related 95%CIs. We treated hazard ratios as equivalent to RRs [26]. For studies that presented their results in ORs, we considered them the same as RRs if the ORs fell between 0.5 and 2.5 or if the incidence of CKD was less than 10%. If this was not the case, we followed the method outlined by Zhang and Yu [27] and converted the ORs to RRs. To obtain the overall effect estimate for the highest versus lowest category of dietary protein (total, animal and plant) intake, we employed random effects pairwise analysis. We evaluated inconsistency by implementing the Cochrane Q and I2 statistics [28].

A series of stratified analyses were done according to the region (USA/Asia), sex (men/women/both), follow-up (< 10/≥10 years), number of subjects (< 4000/≥4000) and CKD events (< 500/≥500), dietary assessment (food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)/dietary history questionnaire/24-h dietary recall), as well as controlling for main covariates, comprising sex, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, alcohol and energy intake and smoking. In addition, we performed meta-regression analyses to identify the source of heterogeneity. We judged publication bias through Begg’s [29], as well as Egger’s [30] tests. Our analysis also involved assessing the influence of individual studies on the final result by eliminating every research article one at a time. Additionally, we e conducted a dose-response meta-analysis using a random effects model to estimate the risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) associated with increasing intake of total, animal, and plant proteins. For total protein intake, we assessed the risk per 100 g/day increase, and for animal and plant proteins, we assessed the risk per 20 g/day increase.

To estimate these dose-response relationships, we used a method developed by Greenland and Longnecker’s [31] approach and Orsini et al.‘s [32] and applied with STATA software. RRs with their respective 95%CIs were produced for increments of 100 g/d in total protein consumption and increments of 20 g/d in both animal and plant protein consumption [33]. Finally, we utilized a random effects model to combine effect estimates from research articles that reported their effect estimates for the considered increments in dietary protein intake. If a research article did not note its effect estimate, we calculated it by multiplying the log effect value by the study’s certain consumption of dietary protein and then exponentiating it. This allowed us to obtain the effect estimate for every additional gram per day of dietary protein intake [33, 34]. For cohort studies indicating the effect estimate in different categories of protein intake (not continuous effect estimate), the Greenland et al. [31, 32] framework was applied to generate the RR.

Furthermore, the median of every dietary protein group, the number of subjects, as well as CKD events, person-year, and controlled effect estimates were summarized from each cohort study. In studies where exposure ranges were specified, we computed the approximate median values by averaging the lower and upper bounds. We treated categories without specified limits as having the same range as the adjacent category. When studies provided RRs separately for either sex, we combined separate RRs for men and women using fixed effects model. According to Hamling et al. [35], when studies did not specify the lowest group as a reference, we deemed that group as a reference.

We used a restricted cubic line with three constant percentiles at the 10th, 50th, and 90th values to estimate linear and non-linear dose-dependent relationship among dietary protein and the incidence of CKD [36]. An analysis was conducted using mixed effects to combine the effect sizes related to each study and provide a summary of the results [37]. We used Stata, version 17.0, to conduct our analysis. P value < 0.05 was regarded as statistically considerable.

S.ZM and S.T separately implemented Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) to examine the evidence quality [38, 39].

Results

Studies identified

We recognized 1,932 papers in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science. Non-human research (n = 15), duplicate records (n = 554) and non-original research (n = 32) were removed. After reviewing the titles/abstracts, 1,303 additional articles were eliminated, resulting in 28 full texts for investigation. Among the 28 remaining articles, those with irrelevant outcomes (n = 3) and exposure (n = 2), those with irrelevant effect estimates (n = 5), those with different study populations (children) (n = 2) and those with similar exposure and outcome variables (n = 6) were removed (Table S3). Overall, ten prospective cohort articles with 218,741 subjects and 10,794 CKD events were entered in our review [5, 11, 15, 40–46] (Fig. 1).

Research articles characteristics

Table S4 displays the characteristics of the ten articles entered in our review. The number of individuals per article spanned from 1135 to 117,809 and included papers published from 2003 to 2023. By geographical area, four articles were carried out in the United States [5, 40, 45, 46], one in the UK [41], two in China [11, 42], one in Japan [43], one in Korea [44] and one in Iran [15]. By sex, two articles included only women [5, 45]; the remaining eight were done in men and women [11, 15, 40–44, 46]. The follow-up length of articles spanned from 6.1 to 23 years. To assess protein consumption, except for four studies that used 24-hour dietary recalls [42] and dietary questionnaires [5, 43] or did not specify the assessment method [41], others implemented FFQ. All cohort studies controlled for age. Most articles adjusted for potential covariates comprising smoking [5, 11, 15, 40, 41, 43–46], physical activity [11, 15, 40, 44, 45], energy intake [11, 15, 40, 41, 43, 46], sex [11, 15, 40, 41, 43, 44, 46] and BMI [5, 11, 15, 40, 41, 43, 45, 46].

According to ROBINS-I, except for one paper which indicated a serious risk of bias [46], nine papers [5, 11, 15, 40–45] displayed a moderate risk of bias. Either residual confounding or insufficient information about exposure assessment could result in biased outcomes (Table S2).

Total protein

Eight cohort studies (nine arms) (total subjects = 97,584, CKD events = 6,669) investigated the relationship among total protein and the incidence of CKD [5, 11, 15, 40, 42–44, 46]. We discovered no considerable relation between total protein and CKD incidence (RR high vs. low =0.95, 95%CI: 0.75–1.19; I2 = 69.7%, Pheterogeneity=0.001; GRADE = low certainty) (Table 1). We also observed similar results in subgroup analysis based on region, sex, follow-up, number of CKD events, dietary assessment, as well as controlling for main covariates (Table S5). However, higher total protein intake could significantly reduce the risk of CKD by 26% (RR high vs. low =0.74, 95%CI: 0.58–0.96; I2 = 0.0%, Pheterogeneity=0.922; n = 4) in research articles with < 4000 subjects.

Additionally, in a dose-dependent analysis, we observed a non-significant linear relation between greater consumption of total protein and reduced CKD incidence (RR = 0.75, 95%CI: 0.43–1.33; I2 = 49.8%, Pheterogeneity=0.093; n = 5; per 100 g/d; Table 1).

Animal protein

Five cohort studies (nine arms) (total subjects = 27,546, CKD events = 4,165) investigated the relationship among animal protein and the incidence of CKD [15, 40, 43–45]. Higher animal protein intake was related to a decrease in CKD risk by 0.87 (RR high vs. low =0.87, 95%CI: 0.77–0.97; I2 = 0.0%, Pheterogeneity=0.698; GRADE = high certainty) (Table 1).

On the other hand, we conducted a dose-dependent analysis and discovered that there was no considerable linear connection between greater consumption of animal protein and reduced CKD incidence (RR = 0.95, 95%CI: 0.91–1.00.91.00; I2 = 0.0%, Pheterogeneity=0.441; n = 3; per 20 g/d; Table 1).

Plant protein

The risk of CKD was examined in six cohort studies regarding the correlation with the intake of plant protein, comprising 145,355 individuals, of whom 4,165 developed CKD [15, 40, 41, 43–45]. Greater plant protein intake was linked to reduced incidence of CKD (RR high vs. low =0.81, 95%CI: 0.67–0.98; I2 = 69.3%, Pheterogeneity=0.006; GRADE = moderate certainty) (Table 1).

On the other hand, we conducted a dose-dependent analysis and discovered that there was a non-significant linear connection between greater consumption of plant protein and reduced incidence of CKD. (RR = 0.70, 95%CI: 0.38–1.28; I2 = 82.2%, Pheterogeneity=0.004; n = 3; per 20 g/d; Table 1).

Rating the evidence

We obtained the evidence quality by implementing the GRADE tool. The evidence was rated as “high” for animal protein, “moderate” for plant protein, and “low” for total protein in relation to CKD incidence (Table S6). Of the included studies, one was assessed as having a serious risk of bias due to inadequate adjustment for potential confounders and unclear outcome assessment. To evaluate the potential influence of this study on the overall results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding it. The pooled effect estimates remained consistent, suggesting that the primary findings were not substantially affected by this study.

Non-linear analysis

We identified no considerable record of non-linearity among total protein (Pnonlinearity=0.576; Fig. 2A), animal protein (Pnonlinearity=0.279; Fig. 2B), and plant protein (Pnonlinearity=0.662; Fig. 2C) intake and incidence of CKD.

Publication bias and influence analysis

In regards to CKD incidence, our analysis indicated no proof of publication bias for total (PEgger=0.843, PBegg=0.602), animal (PEgger=0.603, PBegg=0.624) and plant protein (PEgger=0.669, PBegg=0.573). The influence analysis determined that removing the paper by Kwon et al. [44] for animal protein and Kwon et al. [44], Haring al. [40], and Heo et al. [41] for plant protein altered the final effect estimates. In contrast, none of the papers influenced total protein results.

Discussion

Our systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis explored the relationship between dietary protein intake (total, animal, and plant proteins) and the risk of CKD based on data from prospective cohort studies. The synthesis of evidence revealed several noteworthy findings, which contribute to the understanding of the complex interplay between dietary protein consumption and CKD risk. We didn’t find any significant correlation between total protein intake and CKD risk. However, we found a considerable protective effects of animal and plant protein against CKD risk.

Previous studies on the relationship between protein intake and the decline in renal function yielded limited and inconsistent findings. The interest in the decline of eGFR in humans prompted the investigation into the impact of low-protein diets, given that a heightened consumption of animal protein has been linked to increased renal damage in experimental animal models [47]. In the Nurses’ Health Study, which monitored 1,624 American nurses aged 42–68 over an 11-year period, no connection was found between total protein intake and eGFR decline in women with normal renal function (eGFR ≥ 80 mL/min/1.73 m²). However, increased consumption of nondairy animal protein was correlated with a more pronounced decline in eGFR among women with mild renal insufficiency (eGFR 55–80 mL/min/1.73 m²) [5]. Lin et al. demonstrated an association between kidney dysfunction and increased consumption of animal fat, as well as the consumption of two or more servings of red meat per week [45]. However, some studies have observed a protective effect of animal protein consumption or have not found a significant relationship between animal protein intake and the risk of CKD [15, 43]. Our findings showed that there was an inverse association between animal protein intake and CKD risk. The correlations between the intake of protein from particular sources and the risk of CKD exhibited variability. Research conducted in both the United States and Iran indicated a negative correlation between vegetable protein intake and the risk, whereas there was no observed association between animal protein intake and the risk [15, 40]. But, among the Japanese people, higher animal protein intake led to a significant reduction in CKD risk [43]. The variation in results among these studies might be attributed, at least in part, to substantial differences in the distribution of animal and vegetable protein. Japanese individuals had a somewhat reduced consumption of animal protein compared to Americans, attributed to the considerably lower intake of meat and the higher consumption of fish in the Japanese diet [48]. The elevated risk of CKD associated with animal protein intake was noted in Americans, possibly attributed to the predominant sources of animal protein in their diet being red meat and processed meat. A 23-year follow-up involving 11,952 Americans aged 44–66 revealed that a higher intake of red and processed meat was linked to an elevated risk of CKD, while increased consumption of fish and seafood was associated with a lowered risk [40]. Therefore, it can be interpreted in this way that a high intake of animal protein from sources such as fish or seafood and not red meat and processed foods can reduce the risk of CKD.

The results of the present study demonstrated that greater plant protein intake was linked to reduced incidence of CKD. The connection between dietary plant protein and kidney function can be elucidated through various potential mechanisms. Consuming more plant protein results in higher proportions of glutamic acid, proline, phenylalanine, cysteine, and serine in the diet. The variation in amino acid composition may account for the distinct impact of plant and animal protein on kidney function [49]. There are reports indicating that elevated intake of plant protein derived from gluten has the potential to decrease serum triacylglycerol, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and uric acid levels [50]. In a comparable investigation, a noteworthy reduction in LDL cholesterol, total serum cholesterol, and triglycerides was documented with increased soy protein consumption [51]. The consumption of plant protein might be beneficial in mitigating glomerular damage induced by oxidized lipoproteins and in slowing down the progression of CKD through the reduction of these serum lipid levels [52]. Protein derived from plant-based sources is high in calcium, magnesium, potassium, and vitamin C. These components have been linked to a decreased dietary acid load and advancements in kidney function [53].

The present study has several strengths such as: (1) the study employed a thorough search strategy across multiple databases without language or time restrictions, enhancing the inclusiveness of relevant studies. (2) The implementation of the PRISMA framework for reporting, along with registration details in the PROSPERO database, reflects a commitment to transparency and methodological rigor. (3) The study assessed the quality of included studies using established tools such as ROBINS-I and the Cochrane approach, providing a comprehensive evaluation of potential biases, and finally, the inclusion of 10 prospective cohort studies with a substantial number of participants (218,741 subjects) adds strength to the overall findings. However, due to some limitations, the results of this study should be interpreted with caution. First, the analysis revealed significant heterogeneity, especially in the total protein and plant protein categories (I² >50%), which might affect the robustness of the overall findings. To address this, we used a random-effects model, conducted subgroup analyses (e.g., by region, follow-up duration, dietary assessment tool), and performed sensitivity analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Second, variability in study characteristics—including geographical location, sample size, dietary assessment methods, and follow-up duration—likely contributed to the observed heterogeneity and may have influenced the pooled estimates. Third, although we assessed the impact of total animal protein consumption, we were unable to analyze specific animal protein sources (such as red meat, poultry, fish, or dairy) due to data limitations, which may reduce the precision of the conclusions. Finally, while most included studies exhibited a moderate risk of bias, one study was assessed as having a serious risk of bias. However, sensitivity analysis excluding this study did not materially change the overall results, suggesting that our findings are generally robust. Nevertheless, the potential influence of methodological limitations in individual studies should still be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our comprehensive systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis, based on data from ten prospective cohort studies involving 218,741 subjects, aimed to elucidate the relationship between dietary protein intake (total, animal, and plant proteins) and the risk of CKD. The synthesis of evidence did not reveal any significant correlation between total protein intake and the incidence of CKD. However, it indicated a protective effect of both animal and plant proteins against CKD risk. Notably, higher total protein intake was associated with a significant 26% reduction in CKD risk in studies with fewer than 4,000 subjects. While our study contributes valuable insights into the complex interplay between dietary protein consumption and CKD risk, there remain avenues for further research to enhance our understanding and guide public health recommendations. Future studies could explore the following aspects: elucidating specific animal protein sources, longitudinal studies with diverse populations, assessment of protein quality, interaction with other dietary factors, and designing mechanism-based studies with the aim of identification the pathways through which different protein sources influence kidney function could provide a more in-depth understanding of the observed associations.
