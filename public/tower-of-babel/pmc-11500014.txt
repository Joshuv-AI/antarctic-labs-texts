Boosting uranium extraction from Seawater by micro-redox reactors anchored in a seaweed-like adsorbent
Authors: Yinshan Zhang, Yingcai Wang, Zhimin Dong, Youqun Wang, Yuhui Liu, Xiaohong Cao, Zhibin Zhang, Chao Xu, Ning Wang, Yunhai Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient extraction of uranium from seawater is expected to provide virtually infinite fuel sources to power nuclear reactors and thus enable sustainable development of nuclear energy. The extraction efficiency for uranium greatly depends on the availability of active adsorption sites on the adsorbents. Maximization of the utilization rate of the binding sites in the adsorbent is vital for improving adsorption capacity. Herein, micro-redox reactors functioned by Cu(I)/Cu(II) conversion are constructed internally in an adsorbent bearing both amidoxime and carboxyl groups to induce active regeneration of the inactivated binding sites to enhance uranium capture. This adsorbent has high adsorption capacity (962.40 mg-U/g-Ads), superior anti-fouling ability as well as excellent uranium uptake (14.62 mg-U/g-Ads) in natural seawater after 56 days, placing it at the top of high-performance sorbent materials for uranium harvest from seawater.Subject terms: Nuclear chemistry, Self-assembly

Introduction

Nuclear energy, providing constant, reliable and almost carbon-free power to people around the world, plays a vital role in energy supply to achieve carbon neutralization1,2. Currently, uranium is the fuel most widely used to power nuclear plants and produce nuclear energy. However, the scarcity of onshore uranium deposits is expected to severely constrain the sustainable development of nuclear power in the future3,4. Although uranium is a relatively common element on earth, the mineable terrestrial resources are quite limited, while in contrast a significant quantity of uranium (4.5 billion tons) resides in the ocean5–7. Such a gigantic amount of uranium in seawater, if successfully being harvested in large-scale, is practically limitless to meet world-wide demand in the foreseeable future. In the past decades, great efforts both at the fundamental and practical levels around the world have been devoted to develop efficient techniques for the enrichment of uranium from seawater8. However, the super low concentration of uranium (3.3 ppb) in seawater as well as the great complexity of oceanic environment presents a huge challenge to fulfill this recovery task2.

Owing to versatile availability and easy operationality, selective adsorption by functional materials has been regarded as the most feasible way to recover oceanic uranium resources9–13. A large number of adsorbents, including carbon materials, biomaterials, polymers, covalent organic frameworks (COFs), and metal-organic framework (MOFs), have been developed for this purpose14–16. However, most adsorbents exploited at present suffered from poor utilization rate of the binding sites and the recovery efficiency of uranium remains unsatisfactory. One underlying cause is the number of effective or accessible binding sites in many adsorbents is limited. Once these accessible sites are occupied and saturated by uranyl ions (U(VI)), further adsorption of U(VI)would be practically terminated due to Coulomb repulsion effect from the existing cation or steric hindrance effect from the intrinsic structure of the adsorbents3,17. Taking the amidoxime group that has an ultra-high affinity to U(VI) as an example, the de-facto utilization rate of these groups in most adsorbents does not even exceed 1% during uranium extraction from seawater14,18,19. Recently, to improve the adsorption capacity, nano-materials such as MOFs and COFs have been further modified by incorporating photocatalytic or electrocatalytic platforms into these materials5,20–22. Although higher adsorption capacity has been guaranteed by the modification, these types of adsorbents are either vulnerable to climate influence or requiring external energy input in function. Moreover, these materials are usually in powder form and thus not easy to be anchored under oceanic conditions, undermining their potential for practical application.

Another troublesome issue associated with uranium extraction from seawater is the dysfunction of the adsorbents by biofouling from marine microorganisms. Biofouling will lead to severe blockage of the binding sites or even degradation of the adsorbents in long-term exposure, thus significantly lowering the uranium extraction capability. In recent years, a variety of strategies have been used to enhance the anti-biofouling ability of the adsorbents. For example, anti-biofouling components such as black phosphorus nanosheet, zwitterions, and quaternary ammonium ions have been introduced into the adsorbents to prevent the growth of marine bacteria6,23,24. Nevertheless, some of these composite adsorbents still require external power to activate the anti-biofouling function, and the weakly-bind anti-biofouling components may not be able to withstand the force of strong ocean waves and their disintegration will cause secondary pollution to marine environment. Therefore, it is still an urgent task to design eco-friendly functional adsorbent for uranium extraction from seawater.

Herein, we present a novel strategy to address the above issues by designing and synthesizing a bio-mimic and seaweed-like material with enhanced utilization rate of binding sites for uranium and at the same time having satisfactory anti-biofouling ability. As shown in Fig. 1, micro-redox reactors driven by valence-variable Cu atoms were self-assembled into a matrix consisting of carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) and amidoximized acrylonitrile (AN)—acrylamide (AM) copolymers. Cu single atoms with excellent redox activity are easily doped in the adsorbents through coordination with N/O donor atoms in the matrix25–28. The Cu(I)/Cu(II) micro-redox reactors, without the input of external powers, are able to convert soluble hexavalent uranium (U(VI) in the form of uranyl ion) adsorbed by the amidoxime or carboxyl groups into insoluble tetravalent uranium (U(IV) in the form of UO2), thus reactivating the binding sites to enhance further uranium extraction. This strategy raises the utilization rate of uranium binding sites by more than 25% as compared to the reference material without incorporation of micro-redox reactors, and achieves an appreciable uranium adsorption capacity of 962.40 mg-U/g-Ads in 32 ppm U-spiked seawater. Moreover, the Cu(I)/Cu(II) micro-redox reactors in the material can produce a large number of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which significantly inhibit the passivating effect of marine bacteria growth on the adsorbent and endows the adsorbent with an extraordinary performance (14.62 mg-U/g-Ads) for uranium extraction from natural seawater.

Schematic representation of enhanced uranium adsorption in seawater by seaweed-like adsorbents bearing micro-redox reactors.

The Cu(I)/Cu(II) couple in the adsorbent helps convert soluble U(VI) ions adsorbed by the carboxyl and amidoxime groups to insoluble U(IV) species, enabling the reactivation of the binding sites and thus boosting uranium adsorption capacity. Meanwhile, the reactive oxygen species produced during the redox process effectively inhibit bacteria passivation of the adsorbent.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of the seaweed-like adsorbents

Synthesis of the adsorbents is generally started with copolymerization of AN and AM and amidoximization of the obtained copolymers, followed by self-assembly of the amidoximized copolymers with CMCS and copper ions. Details of the adsorbent synthesis and fabrication are provided in the supplementary materials (Supplementary Fig. 1 and associated content). The adsorbent (CMCS/P(AO-co-AM-Cu)) is made into the shape of seaweed-like balls or strings under continuous flow conditions and is of a uniform size (Fig. 2a and Video S1). Notably, the seaweed-like adsorbents form immediately when the co-polymer and CMCS precursors are dropped into a Cu(NO3)2 solution, and the whole cross-linking process is almost completed within 1 s (Supplementary Fig. 2). The fast fabrication can be attributed to rapid cross-linking of the precursors through the coordination of Cu ions with a large number of amidoxime groups in AO and carboxyl groups in CMCS. Moreover, this simple fabrication method obviously can be easily scaled up, showing great potential in industrial application. In the adsorbent, Cu not only acts as the linking metal ion to drive the self-assembly process, but also plays a key role in constructing micro-redox reactors29. To verify the universality of the fabrication method and allow for comparisons of uranium extraction performance by similar adsorbents, different metal ions with valence states from +1 to +4 were employed to synthesis the material in a similar way (Supplementary Fig. 4). Interestingly, except for +1 (Ag+ and Na+), other metal ions of +2, +3, +4 valence states (Ca2+, Zn2+, Pr3+, Er3+, Zr4+) can all quickly induce the self-assembly of the precursors and result in the formation of seaweed-like balls, suggesting the method is universal for rapid preparation of composite materials.

The microscopic features of the adsorbents were characterized by electron microscopy. The bright spots marked with yellow circles in high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image prove the isolated distribution of Cu atoms in CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu (Fig. 2b), suggesting that Cu exists as single atoms rather than clusters or aggregates. Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) was employed to reveal local coordination environment of Cu in the polymer network at atomic level. EXAFS spectra of the samples only exhibit an isolated peak around 1.46 Å (Fig. 2c) and this is in contrast to the Cu-O peak of CuO shown at 1.56 Å, suggesting that the Cu atoms at least partly coordinate to the smaller N donors from amidoxime groups in the adsorbents20. Moreover, there is no observation of Cu-Cu peak similar to that observed in Cu foil at 2.17 Å, further confirming that no clusters of Cu atoms formed in the network28. The optimal fitting results clearly indicated that the number of N/O atoms coordinated to Cu was about 3.1 at a distance of 1.9 Å. (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5, Supplementary Table 1). Wavelet transform (WT) analysis of EXAFS suggests that only one intensity maximum located at approximately 5.45 Å (Fig. 2d), which can be assigned to the coordination between Cu and O/N atoms. And Cu−Cu signals of the Cu foil or CuO at ~7.69 Å were not observed, again demonstrating the atomical disperse of Cu in the CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu network30. It should be noted that activation of the adsorbent by NaOH treatment is required to ensure efficient uranium capture. As depicted by SEM, compared with the smooth surface before activation with NaOH (Fig. 2e(i) and f(i)), the material after NaOH activation exhibits a wrinkled morphology containing macropore (Fig. 2e(ii, iii) and f(ii, iii)), which can be attributed to water swelling by the more hydrophilic deprotonated groups after activation. EDS analysis also proves that Cu atoms are uniformly scattered on the surface of the adsorbent (Fig. 2g).

As illustrated in Fig. 3a, FT-IR spectra was used to reveal the chemical bond information. The band at 1423 cm−1 corresponding to the C = C bond in AN and AM disappeared in P(AN-co-AM), indicating successful condensation between AM and AN. In addition, the band of C ≡ N bond at 2243 cm−1 was not observed in P(AO-co-AM), indicating that AN was completely amidoximized into AO31. The structure of the product was also verified by 13C NMR (Fig. 3b) and 1H NMR (Supplementary Fig. 6)32. While an obvious nitrile (C ≡ N) signal peak at 120.5 ppm was observed in P(AN-co-AM), a new signal peak of amidoxime group appeared at 156.4 ppm in P(AO-co-AM) (Fig. 3b)33. P(AO-co-AM) also exhibits new signal peaks at 5.27 ppm (–NH2) and 8.85 ppm (–OH) in 1H NMR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 6)10. All the above results confirm that P(AN-co-AM) was successfully converted into P(AO-co-AM). The specific surface area and pore size distribution of the adsorbents were determined by N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and the results are illustrated in Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 7. The adsorbent of ball shape (purple line) has a larger surface area (7.71 m2 g−1) as compared to that (4.11 m2 g−1) of the adsorbent of string shape (red line). TG analysis shows that CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu exhibits no significant weight loss before 200 °C, demonstrating excellent thermal stability of the adsorbent (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Mechanical strength is important to ensure the usability of adsorbents in oceanic conditions. In general, pure covalent bond crosslinking tends to form rigid polymers but it is usually lack of flexibility, which cannot withstand well the crashing waves34. To resolve the dilemma between rigidity and flexibility, hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds can be introduced as sacrificial bonds to construct energy-dissipating units35. In this study, a triple crosslinking strategy (covalent bonds, ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds) was used to construct adsorbents of high toughness to cope with the harsh application environment. As shown in Fig. 3d, CMCS, CMCS-PAO and CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu all exhibit good mechanical properties. Nevertheless, CMCS and CMCS-PAO, assembled only through hydrogen or ionic bonds, could not restore to their original shapes under external force. In contrast, CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu not only exhibits excellent toughness, but also presents remarkable fatigue resistance under different straining conditions (Fig. 3e). As depicted in Video S2 and Fig. 3f, the seaweed-like ball is intact and shows excellent resilience after falling free from an altitude of 10 cm, suggesting the energy dissipation mechanism functioned by both covalent and non-covalent bonds effectively cushions the impact of external forces. The inclusion of CMCS and AM also endows CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu with high hydrophilicity, which is beneficial to uranium adsorption in aquatic solution. The spread rate of water droplets on CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu is obviously quicker than on PAO (Fig. 3g). The foregoing results suggest the introduction of AM and CMCS into the adsorbent not only effectively improves the fatigue resistance but also enhances the hydrophilicity, laying a physical foundation for promoting uranium adsorption from seawater.

Uranium adsorption performance

The adsorption of uranium by CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu was first explored under different pH values since the pH not only controls uranium species in solution, but also affects the local charge distribution at the adsorbent interface. The zeta potential changed apparently with the change of pH. The highest adsorption was achieved at pH 6 (Fig. 4a). The decrease of uranium adsorption at lower pH values can be attributed to the competition effect of protonation of the functional groups such as carboxylate and amidoxime, while the lower adsorption at higher pH values might be caused by the strong competitive effect from carbonate complexation or uranium hydrolysis10. Temperature was also found to have a significant influence on the adsorption (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 2). Higher temperature leads to higher uranium uptake, which is largely consistent with previous reports using adsorbents bearing amidoxime groups36. Thermodynamic fitting results show that ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0 and ΔG < 0, indicating that the adsorption process is an endothermic spontaneous reaction with increased entropy. Therefore, we expect higher temperature will not only accelerate the mass transfer of uranyl ions and improve the kinetics of adsorption, but also result in higher equilibrium adsorption capacity in real applications24,37. This finding also suggests the adsorbent may show a better performance in warm-area seawater.

Moreover, the adsorption ability of CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu was found to be higher than CMCS, CMCS-AM and CMCS-PAO under the same conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10), proving the effectiveness of our strategy in material design. The CMCS-P(AO-co-AM)-Cu adsorbent was further tested to adsorb uranium from U-spiked (8, 16, and 32 ppm) seawater (Fig. 4c) and the equilibrium adsorption capacities reached 438.20, 649.59, and 962.40 mg g−1, respectively. Compared with U-spiked seawater, natural seawater has much lower uranium concentration and presents a huge challenge in efficient uranium extraction. Encouragingly, without the input of external energy, uranium adsorption capacity of 6.21 mg g−1 in natural seawater was achieved in the first week, and the value reached 14.62 mg g−1 after 8 weeks, which exceeded most adsorbents including porous polymers, fiber and nanoparticles (Fig. 4d, j and Supplementary Table 3). The results show that CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu is a promising high-performance adsorbent for uranium extraction from seawater.

Different adsorption kinetics models including pseudo-first order, pseudo-second order and internal diffusion were used to reveal the adsorption characteristic and elaborate the mass transfer process (Supplementary Fig. 11, Fig. 4e, f, Supplementary Tables 4-5)9,11. Obviously, the pseudo-second-order model results in the best fitting, demonstrated by consistency between theoretical equilibrium adsorption capacity (Q2,cal = 446.61, 665.53, and 980.39 mg g−1) and experimental values (Qe,exp = 438.20, 649.59 and 962.40 mg g−1). Therefore, the adsorption process proceeds through chemisorption by forming chemical bond between uranyl ion and functional groups in the adsorbent37. The internal diffusion model was further used to explore the rate-determination step of the adsorption. The adsorption data can be fitted into three separated straight lines, corresponding to the boundary diffusion of uranyl ions, the surface-to-interior mass transfer diffusion, and capillary diffusion of particles in pore space, respectively. The non-zero intercept indicates that internal diffusion is not a single rate-determining step and external mass transfer is also involved37–39. The interaction between the adsorbent and uranyl ion was also investigated by adsorption isotherm (Fig. 4g, h, Supplementary Fig. 12, Supplementary Table 6) and it conforms to Langmuir model (R2 = 0.999), indicating that the interaction mainly manifests a monolayer adsorption23,24.

To reveal the effect of cross-linking metal ions on the adsorption, CMCS-P(AO-co-AM) adsorbents prepared using metal ions of different valence states were tested (Supplementary Figs. 13-14). Although all the adsorbents exhibit appreciable adsorption to uranium, the capacity varies among different adsorbents. The adsorbent consisting of metal ions of higher valence states generally shows weaker uranium adsorption, which could be explained by the fact that high valent metal ions will preoccupy more binding sites in the material. Notably, among all the adsorbents, the one cross-linked by Cu ions exhibits the highest adsorption capacity. Such a much stronger adsorption is apparently linked to the redox properties of Cu ions. The redox potential of Cu(I)/Cu(II) couple fit well with the reduction gap of uranyl ion, while the other redox couples are not be able to induce a reducing effect on the uranyl ion due to the limitation of their intrinsic redox potential (Supplementary Fig. 13b and Supplementary Table 7). To prove this point, we also constructed a non-redox adsorbent by using the similar-size but non-valence-variable element Zn as the linking metal ion and examined its performance of uranium capture from U-spiked seawater. As shown in Fig. 4i, the adsorption capacity of CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu is 26.8% and 25.3% higher than that of CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Zn in 8 and 32 ppm U-spiked seawater, respectively, and the adsorption process is also slightly faster. The above results suggest more than 25% of the adsorbed uranyl ions might have been guaranteed by the employment of redoxable Cu ions that could reduce U(VI) to insoluble U(IV) species, which then disassociate from the adsorption site and deposit on the adsorbent interface. The reductive adsorption of U(VI) on CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu will be further discussed later in the following context.

The adsorbent undergoes apparent morphological change after adsorption. In contrast to the porous wrinkled structure before adsorption, thick sediments appears at the interface after adsorption (Fig. 5a i and ii), which can be ascribed to precipitate formation of the adsorbed uranium after reduction37. EDS also verified the dense distribution of uranium on the surface after adsorption (Fig. 5b). Uranium was estimated to account for about 70% of the elemental mass after adsorption by EDS analysis (Supplementary Figs. 15-16), indicating high utilization rate of the adsorption sites on the adsorbent.

Many metal ions co-exist in seawater and may compete with uranyl ion for adsorption. It has been reported that transition metals such as Pd(II), Ni(II) and alkaline earth metal such as Ca(II) and Mg(II) are not expected to cause strong competition due to their much weaker complexation with the commonly used amidoxime group as compared to that of uranyl ion40,41. However, a large number of previous studies suggest that the pentavalent vanadium ion is the main competitor in uranium extraction from seawater, which is caused by the preferential complexation of vanadyl ion by the amidoxime group42. In contrast, CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu in the present work shows a Uranium/Vanadium selectivity of 2.6 after adsorption in natural seawater for 56 days (Fig. 5c). One reason behind this encouraging performance might be that both carboxyl and amidoxime groups serve as the binding sites in this adsorbent and they function synergistically in adsorption. Moreover, as mentioned earlier, a significant portion of uranium is captured through the reduction of uranyl ion by the micro-redox reactors in CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu, thus enhancing the overall selectivity of uranium over vanadium.

The CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu adsorbent not only exhibits high adsorption capacity to uranium from seawater, but also shows very promising reusability. A mixture of H2O2 (0.2 M) and Na2CO3 (1.0 M) solution is able to strip 98% of uranium from the adsorbent within 1 min (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 17), probably through the oxidation of U(IV) back to U(VI) as well as the complexation of carbonate ions with U(VI). FT-IR also clearly demonstrated the highly efficient desorption performance (Supplementary Fig. 18). Moreover, even after 5 cycles of adsorption-desorption, the adsorbent still sustains 86.6% of the original adsorption capacity (Fig. 5e). In addition to static adsorption, a prototype of continuous flow column was constructed to test the dynamic adsorption and desorption of uranium (Video S3 and S4). The seaweed-like balls were packed within a glass column, through which U-spiked seawater (100 ppm) was continuously flowing (flow rate = 80 mL min−1). After 140 h of adsorption, the color of the adsorbents changed from green to yellow, demonstrating apparent adsorption of uranium. A mixture of H2O2/Na2CO3 solution was then pumped into the column to strip the adsorbed uranium. High stripping efficiency could be visually observed as yellow eluent quickly flowed out. Another advantage of the CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu adsorbent is it can be easily degraded if out of service, thus minimizing secondary pollution to the environment. The adsorbent could be completely degraded in 0.1 mol L−1 HCl in less than 1 h (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Figs. 19-20). The easy operation of adsorption/desorption, good reusability, and convenient degradation all demonstrate great potential of the adsorbent for industrial application.

Antibacterial activity

Numerous studies have shown that the redox process of Cu(I)/Cu(II) can produce a large number of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as ·O2- and 1O2 that can effectively destroy the proteins at the bacterial cell interface, leading to the loss of bacterial cell integrity and thus inactivation of bacteria43,44. For CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu, a large amount of ·O2- and 1O2 were also detected by Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) due to the present of micro-redox Cu(I)/Cu(II) reactors inside the adsorbent (Fig. 5g). With such a high ROS generation ability, the absorbent exhibits high inactivation rates for common Marine bacteria such as Gram-negative Escherichia coli (99.8%), Gram-positive Bacillus subtilis (89.5%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (83.2%) and Staphylococcus aureus (81.1%). In contrast, the resistance to biofouling of bacteria decreased sharply for the reference adsorbent using non-redoxable Zn ion as the linker (Fig. 5h, i and Supplementary Fig. 21), proving the effectiveness of Cu(I)/Cu(II) micro-redox reactors in enhancing antibiofouling ability5,6,45.

Discussion

Enhanced adsorption by micro-redox reactor

As aforementioned, the CMCS/P(AO-co-AM) adsorbent shows much higher extraction ability to uranium as well as better antibiofouling performance by incorporating redoxable Cu as the metal linker than that with non-redoxable Zn. And we attribute the higher extraction ability to the reduction of uranyl ion by the Cu(I)/Cu(II) micro-redox reactors inside the adsorbent. It should be noted that the starting species of Cu in the adsorbent synthesis is Cu(II), and no Cu(I) added in the whole synthesis process. Comparative tests of different reducing agents and amidoxime monomers on Cu(II) reduction shed light on how Cu(I) was generated in the materials. It is proved that reagents containing amidoxime groups are able to reduce Cu(II) to Cu(I) mildly (Supplementary Fig. 22). Therefore, there is a redox cycle in the CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu adsorbent to enhance uranium adsorption. On the other hand, the reduction of Cu(II) is expected to consume some of the amidoxime groups in the materials, but likely the gains in adsorption ability through U(VI) reduction well offset the losses caused by amidoxime oxidation.

To further confirm such an adsorption mechanism involving Cu(II)/Cu(I) micro reactors, a variety of characteristic techniques as well as DFT calculations were employed to reveal the reductive adsorption process. The reduction of U(VI) to U(IV) on the adsorbent was first clearly evidenced by the presence of a strong signal (g = 1.99) corresponding to U(IV) in electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra of the adsorbent after uranium adsorption (Fig. 6a)46,47. Moreover, as suggested by U XPS analysis of the adsorbent after uranium adsorption, a U 4f7/2 peak at 380.61 eV and a U 4f5/2 peak at 391.31 eV corresponding to U(IV) can be apparently observed in addition to the 382.31 eV and 393.07 eV peaks of U(VI), proving the formation of U(IV) species from U(VI) reduction on the adsorbent (Fig. 6b, c)48. In sharp contrast, there are no observable U(IV) peaks in the reference adsorbent using Zn ion as the linker. The redox process can be also illustrated from the change of Cu XPS (Fig. 6d). In prior to the adsorption of uranium, peaks corresponding to both Cu(I) (952.88, 933.26 eV) and Cu(II) (955.45, 935.67 eV) can be well observed. All the Cu peaks were blue shifted after adsorption of uranium, suggesting the involvement of Cu in the uranium adsorption process49,50. More importantly, the intensity of Cu(I) peaks decreased significantly after uranium adsorption, while the Cu(II) peaks enhanced accordingly. The valence changes of Cu before and after uranium adsorption can also be observed from XANES analysis (Fig. 6e). The normalized Cu K-edge XANES spectra of the adsorbent before and after uranium adsorption was located between the related Cu(0) and Cu(II) references (Cu foil and CuO), suggesting the coexistence of Cu(I) and Cu(II) inside the adsorbent. Moreover, the Cu K-edge XANES spectra of the adsorbent after uranium adsorption resemble more closely to that of CuO, indicating higher Cu(II) content in this sample than that before uranium adsorption.

To further probe the redox process, the initial oxidation potential of the adsorbent was determined by cyclic voltammetry (CV). As shown in Fig. 6f, the onset oxidation potential of the adsorbent is about −0.627 V vs. SHE at different scanning speeds, much lower than the reduction potential of UO22+/UO2 (0.411 V vs. SHE)46,51, proving the feasibility of U(VI) reduction on the adsorbent. In contrast, the reference adsorbent using Zn ion as the linker exhibits no redox peaks. All the above observations clearly indicate that a portion of U(VI) has been reduced to U(IV) by Cu(I) during the adsorption process. It should be emphasized that during the process of adsorption-assisted reduction for uranium, no ion exchange occurs between copper and uranium, because the coordination distance and coordination number of Cu with N/O are almost unchanged before and after adsorption Supplementary Fig. 23. TEM and HRTEM were used to reveal the interface status before and after uranium adsorption (Fig. 6g, h). Aggregates of different sizes can be clearly seen at the interface of the adsorbent after uranium adsorption in TEM images (Fig. 6h). These aggregates are assumed to be UO2 clusters formed after U(VI) reduction, since the insoluble UO2 is electroneutral and prone to form nanoclusters. The lattice spacings of 0.19 nm in the HRTEM image can be assigned to UO2 (220) planes46. The crystal phase of UO2 in the adsorbent after adsorption was also detected by XRD (Supplementary Fig. 24), with the peak displayed at 2θ values of ~12.16 being assigned to the (060) planes of UO2 (JCPDS card No. 46–0951). HAADF-STEM imaging further reveals the existence of the aggregates (Fig. 6i). It should be noted that EDS mapping shows that uranium ions are almost evenly distributed on the surface but not only concentrated on the aggregates, suggesting that a significant portion of U are still in the form of U(VI) adsorbed evenly by the binding sites of the adsorbent.

Density functional theory calculations (DFT) provide more insights into the redox-assisted adsorption mechanism. The donor atoms involved in the adsorption process are mainly O from the carboxyl groups in CMCS and N from the amidoxime groups in AO (Supplementary Fig. 25). Electrostatic potential analysis (ESP) illustrates the transfer of electron cloud during the coordination of U(VI) with carboxyl group and amidoxime group and suggests the coordination is mainly electrostatic interaction in nature (Fig. 7a)47,52. Furthermore, the state density shows that the orbital hybridization between the adsorbent and U(VI) leads to significant peak broadening, which results in a decrease of the binding energy (Fig. 7b)23. The calculated Gibbs free energies of CMCS and AO binding to U(VI) are −1.62 and −1.72 eV, respectively, indicating that the amidoxime group is more easily bound to U(VI) than the carboxyl group (Fig. 7c).

The reductive conversion of U(VI) to U(IV) was also analyzed. The HOMO and HOMO-1 orbitals of both CMCS-U(VI) and AO-U(VI) complexes are of O/C-2pπ characteristics, while the LUMO and LUMO + 1 orbitals consist of U-fφ/σ atomic orbitals acted as electron acceptors when U(VI) is reduced (Supplementary Fig. 26). As described in Fig. 7c, the reduction of U(VI) to U(V) is energetically favorable with a Gibbs free energy of −0.49 eV. However, the reduction of U(V) to U(IV) is highly endothermic and energetically unfavorable (ΔG = 2.78 eV). In summary, we make use of the coordination and redox ability of valence-variable copper ion to construct self-assembly materials with amidoximized polymers and carboxymethyl chitosan, resulting in a novel seaweed-like adsorbent (CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu) for efficient uranium extraction from seawater without the input of external energy. CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-Cu with appreciable mechanical strength is easy in fabrication and exhibits high uranium extraction capacity/selectivity, excellent anti-biofouling ability and reusability. The results indicate that the incorporated Cu(I)/Cu(II) micro-reactors is responsible for the enhanced extraction and anti-biofouling ability. The proposed design/fabrication strategy is expected to help achieve sustainable and cost-effective uranium extraction from seawater. On the basis of this work, future investigations can be performed on larger scale oceanic demonstrations.

Methods

Materials

Acrylonitrile (AN, 99%), Acrylamide (AM, 99%), Hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH·HCl, 99%), Azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN, 99%) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS, ≥90%) was purchased from Macklin. All the reagents used without further purification. Other reagents are described in detail in Supporting Information.

Preparation of materials

In general, the seaweed-like adsorbent was synthesized via three-steps: the acrylonitrile (AN) and acrylamide (AM) was first copolymerized to obtain the prepolymer P(AN-co-AM), which was then amidoximized to form P(AO-co-AM), and finally the homogeneous mixture of P(AO-co-AM) and carboxymethyl chitosan (CMCS) was dropped into metal solution to obtain CMCS/P(AO-co-AM)-M (M is the metal linker). The specific preparation procedure is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Synthesis of P(AN-co-AM)

The copolymer P(AN-co-AM)x (where x represents the molar percentage of AM to AN) was synthesized as follows: Take P(AN-co-AM)0.1 as an example (Supplementary Fig. 1 (1)), 20 mL of DMF was added into a 50 mL three-neck flask, into which 0.231 g AM (Mw = 71.078, 3.250 mmol), 1.908 g AN (Mw = 53.06, 35.9 mmol) was then added in turn under nitrogen atmosphere and the mixture was continuously stirred for 30 min, and finally 0.05 g AIBN (Mw = 164.208, 0.3 mmol) was added into the homogenous solution. The polymerization was allowed to proceed for 7 h under magnetic stirring at 65°C. Afterwards, the reaction products were naturally cooled, precipitated by methanol, and filtered. After being washed with ethanol and deionized water twice, the obtained sediment was freeze-dried and recovered. The preparation process of P(AN-co-AM)0.2 and P(AN-co-AM)0.3 followed the similar steps except for the change of AM content.

Synthesis of the P(AO-co-AM)

Typically (Supplementary Fig. 1 (2)), 2.15 g P(AN-co-AM) was dissolved in 20 mL DMF at room temperature, then 4.17 g NH2OH·HCl (60.0 mmol) and 20 mL triethylamine were added. The mixture was stirred magnetically for 16 h at 65°C. After thorough reaction, the mixed solution was dribbled into deionized water under magnetic stirring to wash the residual triethylamine. After being washed several times by deionized water and filtration with Brinell funnel, the precipitation was freeze-dried to obtain as-prepared P(AO-co-AM).

Fabrication of CMCS/P(AO-co-AM) balls and strings

CMCS/P(AO-co-AM) was obtained by cross-linking P(AO-co-AM) and CMCS with different metal ions (Supplementary Fig. 1 (3)). Take Cu2+ as the linker metal as an example to describe the synthesis procedure. Typically, 0.16 g CMCS was dissolved in 5 mL deionized water, and 0.08 g P(AO-co-AM) was added into 5 mL 0.1 M NaOH solution. The two solutions were mixed under continuous stirring for 5 h. The above mixture was then added into 50 mg mL−1 Cu(NO3)2 solutions in different ways such as dropwise or continuous injections, which would lead to the production of adsorbent materials in the shape of balls or strings, respectively.

Materials characterization

The types of functional groups in the polymers were characterized on FT-IR (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher). TG-DTA (STA 449 F3, Jupiter) was used to measure the thermal stability of the samples. Dynamic water contact angle was determined on DSA100 (Kruss). The morphologies of samples were characterized by SEM (Nova Nano SEM 450, FEI) and TEM (Jem-2100F, Jeol). The microstructure of the materials was measured by XRD (Bruker D8-A25). The elemental chemical states on the surface of the materials were measured by XPS (Escalab 250Xi, Thermo Fisher). The BET specific surface area was measured by nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm measurements at 77 K (ASAP 2020 HD88). The electronegativity values of materials were measured with a Particle Metrix flowing current potential analyzer (Stabino). UV−vis Spectra (PE lambda 750) was used to determine U content. The concentration of various ions was analyzed by ICP-MS (PerkinElmer NexION 300X). The chemical shifts of carbon before and after polymerization were recorded on Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR, Bruker Avance III 400 WB). A model CMT4104 universal testing machine fitted with a 200 N load cell was used for mechanical testing. The elasticity test was measured by a high-speed camera (FASTCAM Mini AX). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker EMXplus spectrometer. 5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) was used to trap (·O2-) and 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine (TEMP) was used to detect singlet oxygen (1O2). The local chemical structure, oxidation state and coordination pattern of the materials were determined by synchrotron-based soft X-ray absorption spectroscopy (easyXES150, KG230675). X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and Extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) measurement were performed to investigate the electronic valence state, local chemical environment, and coordination pattern of materials.
