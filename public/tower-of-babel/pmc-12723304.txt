Frontier sites in clinical trials: Opportunities, challenges, and models
Authors: Andrea Bastek, Amanda Zenere, Heather Manley, Camille Finkle, Chad Jaeger, Janet Matthews, Kristie Moffett, Megan Solomon, Paula Underhill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical trials industry faces a growing capacity gap driven by an increasing number of studies combined with site and staff shortages. The Site Enablement League (SEL) Task Force on Frontier Sites was established to explore the potential of non-traditional research sites—termed "frontier sites"—to address these issues by expanding access to underserved populations and embedding clinical research in community settings.MethodsThe task force defined frontier sites and conducted a survey to gather perspectives on their benefits, challenges, and support needs. Twenty-nine valid survey responses were collected. Respondents self-identified as having direct experience with frontier sites (“experienced respondents”) or answered based on their perception of working with frontier sites (“perception respondents”). Free-text responses were coded into categories and analyzed qualitatively and quantitatively.ResultsAccess to new participant populations was the most cited benefit, and experienced respondents noted additional gains in site engagement and education. Operational infrastructure deficiencies were the primary challenge identified by both experienced and perception respondents. Experienced respondents highlighted greater concerns about compliance and community resistance. The primary support need identified was enhanced operational support and quality oversight. Differences between experienced and perception respondent groups suggested the need for broader industry education.ConclusionFrontier sites offer significant promise for increasing research capacity and diversity, but require substantial operational, educational, and community engagement support. These survey findings provide a foundation for shared understanding across the industry. From that foundation the industry can work to develop a playbook to guide the successful integration of frontier sites into clinical trial operations.

Introduction

The task force

The Site Enablement League (SEL) is focused on fostering effective communication among industry stakeholders to generate ideas aimed at streamlining processes, implementing advanced technologies, and creating a more agile and innovative clinical research environment, ultimately leading to the faster development of life-saving treatments. The SEL Task Force on Frontier Sites formed to understand how new study operational models could help address the capacity gap in clinical trials [1]. The group was composed of representatives from sponsors (17.1%), Contract Research Organizations (CROs, 7.3%), sites (53.7% Sites, 9.8% Site Networks, 4.9% Integrated Research Organizations (IROs)), and Vendors (7.3%), as shown in Fig. 1. Background

There is a well known capacity gap in clinical trials today. The number of trial starts continues to increase [1], but there is a shortage of clinical trial sites, principal investigators (PIs), clinical site staff, and clinical research associates (CRAs) [2,3]. In addition, the industry has an aging population of PIs and there continues to be a low percentage of provider participation in clinical trials [4]. This is in part due to the fact that healthcare practices are centered around time/revenue metrics for economic survival, making it difficult for interested physicians to get started or to continue with clinical research, which often requires additional time and resource commitments [5,6].

Site and staff shortages make it difficult to meet enrollment targets, with many studies taking longer than planned or ending earlier due to failure to enroll [7,8]. Precision medicine is also a confounding factor, making clinical trials increasingly complex and harder to enroll due to narrow inclusion criteria. The success of clinical trials, and therefore the availability of new treatments in the market, is jeopardized as all of these issues compound [7,8]. The growing demand for additional sites, an expanded workforce, and more potential participants is becoming increasingly urgent.

These challenges are causing the industry to consider strategies for engaging new sites, which are often in previously untapped geographies [7,[9], [10], [11]]. This expanded outreach has the potential to increase site capacity in the industry and accelerate enrollment timelines. Further, it may support the inclusion of under-represented populations and increased access to clinical trials as a care option by embedding trials in existing community care settings. This task force was formed to explore the definition, benefits and challenges of working with such sites, known as “Frontier Sites”.

Definition of frontier sites

The first goal of the task force was to define the term “Frontier Sites.” The task force determined that frontier sites meet the following key criteria:●Serve patients that may not have access to traditional trial locations like academic medical centers or large health centers●Reach underrepresented populations (i.e. rural, african-american, LGBTQ+, etc.)●Offer convenience and familiarity factors of local and known providers●May use or integrate with traditional trial locations●Have limited resources, experience, and access to clinical trials

The group agreed on a definition that considered these criteria and acknowledged some of the perceived benefits, barriers and challenges to working with this type of site:

Frontier sites: Clinical research sites residing outside of the traditional clinical research model that (1) have limited resources, experience, infrastructure, and/or research workforce, (2) have access to underserved patient populations and/or expand the general catchment area for potential participants, and/or (3) can reduce patient burden by providing access to clinical trials closer to home and with known Health Care Providers (HCPs).

The survey

The second goal of the task force was to design and conduct a survey to understand industry experiences with frontier sites, as well as perceptions of frontier sites from those stakeholders without personal experience. The data from this survey would be used to raise awareness across the industry of how to use frontier sites as a new study operational model to help address the capacity gap.

Methods

Survey design

The desire to collect data from various industry stakeholders (without limiting the pool of recipients via survey fatigue) drove group members to devise a short and simple survey (Appendix A). The 5-question survey (estimated to take 5 min) asked respondents to choose their organization type (Site, Sponsor, CRO, Vendor/Service Provider or Other, with text box to specify) and identify if they had experience working with or for frontier sites. The survey then asked about the benefits of utilizing frontier sites, challenges to successful partnerships, and suggested methods of support for frontier sites. The questions were free text and asked respondents to answer based on what they had experienced or what they perceived to exist, so that respondents with and without experience would all feel welcome to respond.

Survey administration

Outreach for survey completion was primarily conducted in two ways: (1) through professional platforms such as LinkedIn, and (2) email survey invitations sent to personal contacts of the group members, including other industry associations or groups that may want to cross-distribute. The goal was to collect as many responses as possible from a variety of industry stakeholders in a 6 week timeframe. Because the primary distribution method was by posting on professional platforms, it was not possible to track the total number of “invitations” sent.

Qualitative analysis

The survey responses were analyzed by the task force. First, the free text responses were discussed for each section: Benefits/Advantages, Challenges/Risks and Support/Resources Needed. The responses were categorized, or coded, by the task force to identify trends. For example, the free text responses “Exposing sponsors to untapped patient populations” and “Additional catchment area” were both categorized as “Patient Population” in the first round of data coding. Each section had 10 or more categories after the first round of coding. The group created a definition for the initial categories to ensure alignment and allow for further review of the data. In subsequent meetings the categories, definitions and raw data were reviewed to consolidate the data. Categories were logically combined and their definitions were updated.

Quantitative analysis

For each section and category the responses were also coded by the respondent type (those with direct frontier site experience vs. those answering based on their perceptions). The response counts were then normalized by the total number of respondents answering based on “Experience” or “Perception,” such that all responses are shown as a percentage. Because the responses were free text, there was no restriction on the number of categories suggested by any one respondent, and also one respondent might give several free text responses/examples that all fell into the same category. Each respondent was only counted one time per category. The categorized and normalized data were graphed for each section and then discussed as a group.

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size the task force did not analyze the statistical significance of the response data but did calculate the effect size using Cramér's V for categorical data. The responses from the groups of “Respondents with experience” and “Respondents without experience” were compared across the categories in each section to see if there was a similarity in how the groups responded.

Statistical analysis

The responses from the groups of “Respondents with experience” and “Respondents without experience” were compared across the categories using Cramér's V for categorical data:

Benefits: Medium effect size (Cramér's V = 0.29)

Challenges: Small effect size (Cramér's V = 0.17)

Support Needs: Medium-to-large effect size (Cramér's V = 0.35)

The medium and medium/large effect sizes indicate there are differences between how the two groups (those with direct experience and those without) perceived the benefit and support needs of frontier sites. The small effect size for the challenges section indicates that the two groups were aligned in the challenges they expected to encounter with Frontier Sites.

Although the volume of data was low, these results suggest that challenges with frontier sites are clear but the benefits and support needs are not clear across the industry, and the task force could help inform and create alignment.

Discussion

Survey findings - benefits

The primary benefit of frontier sites identified by both groups was the access to new participant populations. 78% of respondents without frontier site experience had the perception that frontier sites would increase access to new and underrepresented participant populations and improve study enrollment, while 50% of respondents with experience also reported this to be true. One explanation for the 25% difference from perception to reality is that people working at/with frontier sites often do not perceive the population to be “novel," since this is their typical population. In their experience, the benefit of being included in clinical trials is increasing access to new therapies and clinical trial sites and providers (Site Engagement). This explanation is validated by the result that more respondents with experience found Site Engagement and Education to be benefits than those who had no experience (38% vs. 11% and 13% vs. 6%, respectively). Educating the community about clinical trial opportunities is also a secondary benefit of including new sites and investigators in clinical trials. Respondents with and without experience felt equally that using frontier sites reduces the burden for participating in clinical trials.

Survey findings - challenges

Site operational infrastructure was the most substantial and congruent challenge among both groups of respondents. Respondents without experience correctly expected challenges in operationalizing studies, with frontier sites requiring more guidance, training, technology, and support: 89% of respondents without frontier site experience and 100% of the respondents who had experience with frontier sites listed this as a challenge. Difficulties operationalizing trial requirements can lead to other downstream challenges. For example, having insufficient or inadequate training, oversight, and support may result in compliance and regulatory challenges, as reported by 63% of experienced respondents. Surprisingly, only 29% of respondents who had no experience with frontier sites perceived quality oversight to be a challenge. This indicates that sponsors, CROs, and sites underestimate the difficulty of executing regulatory and compliance requirements for the first time.

How to Close the Gap from Perception to Reality: As sponsors, CROs, and sites work together to operationalize studies, they must take into account the range of site operational infrastructure and each site's experience complying with quality standards. Providing content, references, toolkits, playbooks, SOP support, and additional monitoring support to help sites build their operational infrastructure could benefit all parties.

Another challenge that emerged in the survey results was community receptiveness or resistance to participation in clinical trials. The majority of respondents perceived or experienced new populations as a benefit of using frontier sites, but 25% of experienced respondents found community resistance was a challenge. This may not be surprising in the wake of mistrust exposed during the COVID-19 pandemic. Interestingly, only 11% of respondents who had no experience with frontier sites perceived community resistance to be a challenge. This illustrates the misconception that increasing access to potentially eligible participants automatically correlates with easier and more successful study recruitment.

How to Close the Gap from Perception to Reality: While frontier sites may have the benefit of having a unique, untapped population, it is imperative to account for and invest in the community's understanding, trust, and interest in participating in clinical research [11]. These types of initiatives are often most successful when they are ongoing in the community, and not just related to a single study opportunity. Sites often have to initiate and fund this work themselves. New sites may benefit from learning how successful sites accomplish this work, and sponsors and CROs could consider facilitating those mentorship connections and offering funding for such efforts.

Survey findings - support needs

When asked about the resources needed to encourage the use of frontier sites, the majority of respondents in both groups (88% with experience and 78% without) recommended operational support (people, processes and plans), aligning with the highest reported challenge. Respondents who had experience with frontier sites also recommended providing increased quality and compliance oversight as a resource, while those lacking experience with frontier sites did not (38% vs 6%). While other common support areas such as community engagement, CRO/Sponsor education, and mentorship were identified, the areas of operational support and quality and compliance oversight were of highest need. Overall, these data support great alignment between identified challenges and greatest needed areas of support.

How to Close the Gap from Perception to Reality: Frontier sites may be a new tool to help bridge the capacity gap, remove enrollment challenges and meet diversity goals in clinical trials. However, as with most innovations, there are new and unexpected challenges to their adoption. Our survey shows the perceived challenges tend to be different from the real challenges. Sponsors and CROs should be prepared to offer more support, engagement and oversight to be successful in working with frontier sites.

Frontier site models

Frontier sites is a broad term that can encompass a number of operational models. The definition and models continue to evolve with mixed results. Having a shared understanding of how the benefits, challenges and support needs relate to each model will be critical to success across the industry.

Retail pharmacies are one often talked about as an example of a frontier site network. The recent failures of CVS and Walmart in launching clinical trials programs across their networks highlight the difficulties of taking research into new site models on a large scale. This may be due to commercial misalignment with their core corporate strategy or may be related to the need for deeper investment in capabilities and specialized staff training to make frontier sites successful in a commercial environment [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Other companies, such as Walgreens and LabCorp, continue their efforts and presumably face challenges such as those outlined in these survey results. Shifting the resources and attention of corporate staff to a task outside the core business is difficult, and it is even harder when the new workflow is demanding.

Other models occur when Sponsors, CROs, and other sites choose to engage with frontier sites directly by providing resource support and funding for trial opportunities. In the past sponsors and CROs have offered investigator training programs in an effort to bring physicians into clinical research. Additionally, Sponsors and CROs occasionally leverage community engagement partners to aid in outreach to local providers in an effort to both seek patient referrals and identify new physician and site partners. Large sites or academic medical centers often use this same approach to engage community locations and primary care physicians at affiliated hospitals. Sponsors and CROs can also offer staff augmentation to support new sites and physicians with clinical research activities. All of these approaches allow new physicians and research sites to participate in some capacity without the up front overhead costs and staffing needed to start a full research program.

Frontier sites can also engage in research directly. Getting started in research requires investment of time and resources that few research naive sites can support on their own. For example, training on good clinical practice and research regulations, building processes and SOPs for contracting, budgeting, regulatory compliance, etc. all take time, effort and expertise. These types of site organizations are supporting organic growth in the industry because de novo site growth is inherent in all of these business models. When research is the primary focus and business model of an organization there is a strong incentive to add study sites and physicians who can support new studies and increase enrollment in current studies. Such organizations must conduct geographic assessments to locate underserved research areas and then invest time identifying physicians that might be available and interested in getting started with research. Building trust with these sites takes time. A larger organization has the ability to fund this investment to build its portfolio for future work. By including a new site in the parent organization's ongoing studies new physicians get experience as a sub-Investigator, which helps them build credibility toward being included as a PI in the future. These organizations help sites and physicians de-risk the entry into clinical research, and reduce the risk that sponsors/CROs face when working with completely research naive sites and physicians. These organizations can be instrumental in getting started and being able to continue in clinical research.

Conclusion

The results of this task force highlight both the potential and the challenges of trying to bring clinical research deeper into the community by using frontier sites. Although frontier sites may be research-naive, they bring many advantages to the clinical trial ecosystem, such as access to new physicians and populations. To keep up with the pace of drug development and the complexity of precision medicine and regulatory requirements, the industry can benefit from including this new model in future clinical trials. The task force presents these findings to help change the mindset about frontier sites and help educate the industry on the best approaches for success in this new working model. These insights can assist in navigating the complexities of expanding clinical research into underserved communities.

The task force acknowledges the low sample size of the survey responses, but believes that the responses are directionally correct and that the conclusions are valid and useful as a framework for thinking about new operational models. The group encourages further dialogue and collaboration to refine these approaches and ensure their successful implementation and plans to continue this work by creating a playbook for working with frontier sites.
