Diagnosis and Management of Soft-Tissue Sarcomas: Current Insights
Authors: Nelo Joao Zeca Chihal Lima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soft-tissue sarcomas (STSs) are a complex and remarkable heterogeneous group of uncommon malignancies originating from fat, muscle, and other connective tissues. They constitute approximately 1% of all cancers with 50 to 70 distinct histologic subtypes or tumor entities. This type of tumor, also known as “the loneliest cancer” because of its rarity, can manifest at any age, but they are most commonly diagnosed in individuals older than 40 years. They can rise from any anatomic location (somatic, visceral, or bone), but the extremity is the most common primary site. STS presents a notable clinical challenge due to its gradual onset and variable symptoms. Consequently, soft-tissue masses should be thoroughly evaluated to rule out malignancy. Some STSs can be aggressive and tend to have high recurrence and metastasis rates (40-50%), particularly in the lungs (80% of the cases) and less often in the liver, brain, bones, and peritoneum, where their occurrence might indicate a poor prognosis. The fact that they are rare and their histologic morphology varies and sometimes can overlap with different genetic, molecular, and clinical profiles contributes to their delayed and complex diagnoses. A high index of suspicion, coupled with appropriate clinical and radiologic assessment and biopsy, can help identify STS.A systematic, multidisciplinary team (e.g., orthopaedic surgeons, oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists) approach is essential for accurately diagnosing and treating soft-tissue sarcomas. Correlating clinical and radiologic information and involving a multidisciplinary team aid in accurate diagnosis. Biopsy, a crucial step, requires rigorous rules and appropriate technique selection. Histologic diagnosis can be challenging because of limited material and divergent interpretations. Advanced tools such as immunophenotypic panels and genetics can enhance accuracy. The fifth edition of the WHO manual can classify soft tissue, while the AJCC-TNM system can stage tumors.Surgical resection, often supplemented with radiation and chemotherapy, is the primary treatment for STS. However, some subtypes (e.g., liposarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma) have high local recurrence rates or metastasis, leading to inconsistent treatment outcomes. Innovative immune and radiation therapy treatment regimens and targeted therapy can offer potential effective alternatives for these challenging cases.

Soft-tissue sarcomas (STSs) are a group of malignancies comprising less than 1% of adult tumors and 6 to 8% of pediatric tumors.1,2 The rarity of this tumor has led some to refer to it as “the loneliest cancer.” More than 50 histologic subtypes or tumor entities are recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO), but 30% remain unclassified because of the limitations of current classification systems.3 They arise from various tissues, including bone, cartilage, fat, muscle, nerve sheath, blood vessels, and other connective tissue. The National Cancer Institute reported a 3.4 per 100,000 incidence in 2019.4 This form of cancer is more prevalent in men, with a 1.4:1 ratio compared with women.5 Typically, soft-tissue sarcomas are diagnosed in individuals in their 40s to 60s, averaging an onset age of 59.2 years, with notable incidence peaks during the fifth and 8th decades.5 Some sarcomas have been associated with genetic and environmental risks; however, most occur randomly and without a known cause.1 They can occur at any anatomic location (somatic, visceral, or bone). Sixty percent occur in the extremities (46.4% in the lower extremity and 13.1% in the upper extremity), 17.9% in the trunk, 12% in the retroperitoneum, and to a lesser extent in the head, neck, and mediastinum.5,6 STSs recur locally in 20 to 30% of cases and metastasize in 40 to 50%. STS metastasis occurs mainly in the lungs (approximately 80% of the cases) and less often in the liver, brain, bones, and peritoneum, where its occurrence might indicate a poor prognosis.7,8 Patients with metastasis to the lung have a median survival period of 12 months.9-11 STS's 5-year survival rate is approximately 50%, which varies depending on the histologic subtype.

Diagnosing and treating these tumors presents notable challenges because of their rarity, complexity, insidious onset, sometimes-aggressive nature, recurrence, and metastasis. Misdiagnoses or delayed diagnosis of these tumors can be devastating for patients during treatment and markedly affect their survival rates. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of knowledge in soft-tissue sarcoma, highlighting recent advancements in diagnosis and treatment.

Diagnostic Workup

A systematic approach involving a multidisciplinary team (e.g., orthopaedic surgeons, oncologists, radiologists, and pathologists) is essential for accurately diagnosing and treating patients with these tumors. Diagnosing sarcomas requires clinical examination, imaging assessment, and histopathologic evaluation. Usually, the patients may present with nonspecific concerns or soft-tissue masses that can be easily ignored. Moreover, most of these tumors have an insidious and slow development and are often deceptively painless, especially at the early stages. A mass is a common presentation in extremities, while the symptoms of STSs are vague in other body regions.12,13 Because these symptoms vary depending on the sarcoma type and its location, a high level of clinical suspicion is required to make an accurate diagnosis of these types of tumors. As a principle, any detected soft-tissue mass should be treated as malignant until additional investigation proves otherwise.

A concise and detailed history that includes patient age, symptoms, site, size, rate of growth, and history (cancer, radiation therapy, comorbidities, nonhealing wound, trauma, etc.) is mandatory because it can offer valuable clues for the type of sarcoma. Associated syndromes or genetic disorders (Li-Fraumeni, Ollier, NF-1, RB1) should be investigated. The physical examination should focus on the features of the mass, including its location, size, skin discoloration above it, its consistency (soft or hard), its stability or mobility, and pain status.

Tumor imaging is essential for diagnosing these tumors. Initially, radiographic images should be obtained although they offer little diagnostic value unless there is osseous involvement, phlebitis, or bone matrix production.

Ultrasonography can be used as a cost-effective screening/triage tool for small soft-tissue mass evaluation or, in some instances, for definitive diagnosis of soft-tissue mass.14 Ultrasonography is beneficial (1) for distinguishing between cystic and solid lesions, (2) in the presence of an orthopaedic implant, (3) in cases where tumor recurrence is clinically suspected, and (3) for increasing the accuracy of biopsy.15 Adding Doppler evaluation might help distinguish recurrent tumors from avascular fibrous tissue at the postoperative resection site, although hypovascular tumor recurrence could mimic benign appearances.14

Computer tomography (CT) is used mainly and critically for tumor staging, distant tumor evaluation, or recurrence surveillance. It has a limited role as a primary diagnostic tool for STSs.16 Alternatively, it can be effectively used (1) in patients in whom MRI is contraindicated or not tolerated; (2) to assess the degree of osseous involvement/destruction, infiltration, or metastasis to other organs; (3) to evaluate patterns of tumors that might suggest their aggressiveness; and (4) to guide biopsy.16

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the benchmark for soft-tissue sarcoma imaging.17-19 In addition to that, MRI can be used for preoperative and postoperative treatment, mass evaluation, and tumor recurrence.20 A recent systematic review concluded that a noncontrast MRI will suffice to determine the underlying identity of a mass.19 Thus, contrast MRI should be done only on an individualized basis. MRI can be used in specific organs or applied to the entire body (whole-body MRI, WBMRI).21 WBMRI has proven helpful in assessing the extent of suspected metastatic disease in soft tissue and bone. It can also locate the site of an unknown soft-tissue sarcoma presenting with lung metastases. In addition, WBMRI is beneficial in pediatric patient cohorts when there are concerns about CT-related radiation exposure.21 Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is another novel technology that has demonstrated the potential to quantify stiffness and perfusion parameters, which might be incorporated to improve STS diagnostic accuracy.22

Advanced nuclear imaging techniques such as positron emission tomography (PET), PET/CT, and PET/MR can also be used.23 PET has a role in the primary diagnosis of STS, and it is used mainly to help predict the stage of STS and distant tumor metastasis, evaluate treatment response, and detect recurrence. PET/CT or PET/MR should be judiciously used in specific cases to detect the malignant transformation of benign lesions into biologically aggressive lesions.16 Some potential pitfalls include false positives from infections or inflammatory processes and false negatives in certain low-grade tumors. All imaging should be done before the biopsy and must not replace the biopsy.16 Subsequently, a comprehensive differential diagnosis should be formulated.

A biopsy is critical to diagnosing musculoskeletal tumors and should be done following rigorous principles.24 Key principles include selecting the correct biopsy type (e.g., core, incisional, or excisional), choosing the appropriate site to avoid contaminating unaffected tissue, and planning incisions to not interfere with future surgical excisions or reconstructions. If done incorrectly, poor biopsy technique can lead to diagnostic errors, seeding or spreading of tumor cells, or unnecessary harm to surrounding structures. Complications, such as infection, nerve apraxia, hemorrhage, hematoma, or misinterpretation of results due to inadequate sampling, can adversely affect treatment outcomes. Thus, meticulous biopsy protocols are essential for accurate diagnosis and optimal patient management. When the orthopaedic and trauma team lacks sufficient resources for effective diagnosis and treatment, referring the patient to an expert center before a biopsy is done is essential. Biopsy specimens must be examined at a single center—a centralized specimen evaluation—because there might be variability in diagnostic criteria or interpretation between centers, which might undermine the proper patient treatment. If additional evaluation is necessary, samples can be reviewed by another center later. The pathologist must have access to the patient's complete clinical and imaging records, as these records inform the pathologist's interpretation and ensure alignment with the clinical context.25

The selection of the appropriate biopsy technique should also be carefully considered. Core, incisional, and excisional biopsies are commonly performed.24 The choice of biopsy technique depends on the lesion's size and location. Consulting a pathologist before a biopsy can be beneficial, for example, in optimizing the specimen quality and quantity for accurate diagnosis. Core biopsies are the preferred initial soft-tissue evaluation method.26 They can be performed with CT-guided or US-guided techniques to enhance accuracy.15 However, this technique may be limited by the available tissue for histologic analysis. The core biopsy's accuracy in determining the tumor's histology is 62 to 76%.27 An incisional biopsy can be performed if a core biopsy is inconclusive.28 It should be superficial to allow for excision of the mass once sarcoma is confirmed. It should be done to avoid disrupting tissue planes or creating flaps, and adequate hemostasis is crucial to prevent tumor cell seeding through hematoma. Ideally, the surgeon performing the incisional biopsy should also excise the tumor once sarcoma is confirmed. Excisional biopsy of the entire tumor has limited utility in suspected soft-tissue sarcoma and may only be considered for small tumors (<5 cm). Misuse of this type of biopsy could potentially affect future surgeries if sarcoma is diagnosed.24 Initially, biopsy material should be prepared for standard histology (H&E stain) to identify the lesion's mesenchymal nature, determine whether it is benign or malignant (sarcoma), and identify the type of sarcoma (histiotype and grading when possible). Cellular morphology and matrix should also be examined. Subsequently, it is essential to develop a comprehensive differential diagnosis.

The histologic grade of the tumors is the most important independent prognostic factor and guide for treatment. In addition, tumor grading enables clinicians to predict the likelihood of distant metastases and overall survival (OS), but it lacks insights into local recurrence.

There are various grading systems used to evaluate these tumors, including the French Federation of Cancer Centers Sarcoma Group (FNCLCC), the National Cancer Institute (NCI), and the Pediatric Oncology Group (POG), exclusively used for pediatric patients with sarcoma.29-31

The Pediatric Oncology Group (POG) was shown to have improved the correlation between histologic findings and clinical outcomes. For example, in children, grading soft-tissue sarcoma is complicated by several factors, including prognosis, patient age, the extent of surgical resection, and the likelihood of metastasis.29

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) grading system, described in 1984, evaluates tumor histologic type, mitotic index, and the extent of tumor necrosis. However, cellularity, nuclear pleomorphism, and the matrix of the primary lesion are also considered for specific tumor types. Necrosis best predicts both outcomes and is independent of age, sex, location, and tumor size. Clinicians find this grading system unhelpful because its prognostic value is widely variable.

The FNCLCC grading system is most commonly used for tumor grading and the best in predicting overall and metastasis-free survival. This highly predictable grading system has notable management implications for chemotherapy effectiveness. This system primarily evaluates three histologic parameters: tumor differentiation, mitoses per 10 high-power fields, and the percentage of tumor necrosis. The differentiation component of the FNCLCC grading system is its weakest aspect. It is relatively subjective and challenging to evaluate when there is no normal tissue reference for comparison. It is worth noting that, although both systems are highly reliable in over 90% of soft-tissue sarcomas, they do not apply to all types of sarcomas (e.g., undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, epithelioid, and clear cell sarcomas). It can be explained that the STS grading systems have been developed based on the entire heterogeneous STS population. Consequently, these systems may not be generalizable to every histologic subtype. Some histotypes define the dominant clinical behavior of the sarcoma, making histotyping more critical than grading in such cases. Tumors should be graded before neoadjuvant therapy because radiation therapy and chemotherapy may result in more notable tumor necrosis and diminished mitotic activity. Clearly defining the histologic type of sarcoma is essential before attempting grading or clinicopathologic tumor staging. Modern molecular techniques can potentially enhance STS grading, but additional research is necessary.31

Next, clinicopathologic staging should be performed using the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC)–TNM system. The TNM staging system comprises four crucial components: (1) the size or extent of the tumor (T), (2) the spread to nearby lymph nodes (N), (3) the spread (metastasis) to distant sites (M), and (4) the grade (G) of the cancer. In this clinical setting, histologic grade holds the most notable prognostic value for trunk, extremities, and retroperitoneum sarcomas. Localized grade 1 sarcomas are classified as stage IA or IB, whereas grade 2 to 3 sarcomas or metastatic sarcomas are staged as stage II-IV. Higher grade tumors often necessitate adjuvant therapy (chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or a combination) to mitigate their elevated recurrence risk.

The TNM staging system is central for prognostic disease progression and treatment. The backbone of the TNM staging system is the pathologic grading of the tumors, while at the same time, the tools used for tumor grading are not entirely reliable; thus, the TNM system carries notable prognostic limitations when it comes to sarcomas. Newer tools (e.g., nomograms) can be incorporated to improve the staging of sarcomas, which may lead to more appropriate treatment and better prognosis. Nomograms, first introduced in 2002, are predictive tools that incorporate multiple clinical and pathologic characteristics of the patient and the tumor and provide clinicians with a numerical probability of a clinical event: the commonly used prognostic nomogram, SARCULATOR, and STS response evaluation criteria (RECIST v1.1). Several nomograms for sarcomas have recently been developed and validated, providing experts with a comprehensive array of prognostic instruments.

Emerging Diagnostic Tools: Radiologic, Molecular, and Genetic

Over the past decade, advancements in radiology, molecular biology, and genomics have markedly helped in complex cases and profoundly enhanced our comprehension of soft-tissue sarcoma (STS) pathogenesis.32Radiomics involves extracting many quantitative features from medical images to enhance the accuracy of interpreting images regarding diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment response assessment, with implications for optimizing patient care and treatment strategies.33-36 This term emerged in the early 2010s, and radiomics has become a tool in surgical oncology, primarily for STS. The most common imaging tool for radiomics is magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, currently, there is no routine clinical use of radiomics because of (1) the lack of standardized protocols in radiomics and (2) the fact that the results of this tool are still unvalidated and obtained from retrospective studies.37-39 Radiomics can be combined with deep learning algorithms to enhance diagnostic accuracy, assessment of the response to treatment, and the patients' survival and probability for presenting distant metastases.34,40 Furthermore, radiomics could be used to predict the histologic grade of sarcoma (i.e., the most important prognostic marker of STS), essentially serving as a noninvasive virtual biopsy.17In these complex cases, genomic profiling techniques such as cytogenetics (karyotype), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and next-generation sequencing (NGS) can be integrated for diagnostic confirmation.41 These techniques can also differentiate sarcomas with unusual clinical and morphologic presentation, identify potential therapeutic targets, and assess treatment responses.42-44 For example, FISH or NGS can be used to differentiate undifferentiated round cell sarcomas (such as Ewing sarcoma, non-STD EWSR1 sarcomas, and rhabdomyosarcoma) and confirm a sarcoma with discrepancies between histologic and molecular features.45 Some hospitals have integrated next-generation sequencing (NGS) into routine diagnostic workflows for soft-tissue sarcoma.42Immunohistochemistry (IHC) facilitates differential diagnosis, establishes the line of differentiation (myofibroblastic, vascular, neural, smooth muscle, skeletal muscle), and detects molecular genetics alterations in sarcomas (translocations, amplifications, deletions, mutations, loss of expression, etc.).46 IHC and other molecular techniques can be integrated into the diagnostic process. However, its extensive use has evident limitations. These tests have inherent limitations because of genetic overlap, where the same genetic alterations occur in unrelated entities and tumors of different lineages. Second, they use a technically demanding and expensive technology that is equally hard to access. Despite these advancements in molecular techniques, which have markedly improved the accuracy and specificity of soft-tissue sarcoma diagnosis, morphologic diagnosis remains the benchmark.

Current Treatment Modalities

Wide surgical resection (en bloc) remains the standard of care and the cornerstone of STS treatment.47 Surgical treatment aims to achieve negative surgical margins, yet current guidelines do not give a standard definition of what constitutes a negative margin.48 The success rates of surgical resection can vary depending on the local recurrence or metastasis of specific subtypes. In some instances, sacrificing nerves, vessels, or tendons may be necessary to achieve negative margins; however, it might result in notable functional loss depending on the anatomic structure. After surgery, up to 50% of patients experience recurrence.49 Factors such as tumor subtype, size, grade, depth, location, and the achievement of negative surgical margins influence the recurrence rate and the likelihood of distant metastases. Intraoperative frozen section analysis is frequently used to verify the adequacy of these margins. Another crucial aspect is that resected tumor tissue, especially connective tissue with muscle fibers, can alter shape and shrink because of formalin fixation. The surgeon and the pathologist should document the resected tissue; ink may even be applied to the surgical resection surface to prevent errors during examination. There is a difference in opinion regarding the extent of surrounding healthy tissue that needs to be resected alongside the tumor. For most soft-tissue sarcomas, planning to excise the tumor with a margin of 1 to 2 centimeters of normal tissue is crucial. However, less extensive tissue resection may be acceptable if there is a durable barrier such as fascia or periosteum. For highly invasive soft-tissue sarcomas such as myxofibrosarcoma, a margin of potentially 3 centimeters or more is required.

Among the various classifications trying to define and assess the adequacy of surgical margins for soft-tissue sarcoma, the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) classification described by Enneking in 1980 stands out as the most prominent and widely recognized.50,51 This classification distinguishes between intralesional, marginal, wide, and radical margins. However, it lacks detailed instructions on assessing margins for intercompartmental resections (marginal and intralesional resections). Another classification is the R (Residual) classification, which was introduced by the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) in 1988. Surgical margins are categorized as R0, R1, and R2. In this system, R0 represents a microscopically negative margin, R1 signifies a microscopically positive margin, and R2 indicates a macroscopically positive margin. The Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) later revised this classification to the R + 1 mm classification, stipulating at least a distance of 1 mm between the tumor and the resection margin for a margin to be considered R0. These binary systems report the margins as positive or negative, which might fail to provide insight into the distinction between clear but close margins and broader margins. Thus, even if a surgical negative margin is achieved, evaluating it for its size (quality) and type of anatomic barrier (quality) is essential.52 The Toronto Margin Context Classification (TMCC) includes four categories: (1) negative margins (R0 according to the R classification), (2) unplanned positive margins, (3) a planned close margin with an ultimately positive microscopic margin along a critical structure, and (4) a positive margin after a tumor bed re-excision in patients initially treated with inadequate surgery elsewhere. Several studies have attempted to correlate surgical margins with tumor recurrence, and it is consensually accepted that negative surgical margins are related to lower tumor recurrence.

Depending on various clinical factors such as patient age, comorbidities, the tumor's location, and its grade, the decision to include radiation therapy (RT) or neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) in the treatment plan may be considered. Radiation therapy and chemotherapy can be administered either before or after surgery. When administered before surgery, their primary objective is to reduce the tumor's size, facilitating a less invasive and less extensive surgery and a more favorable resection margin. Conversely, when used after surgery, they aim to address any microscopic residual disease and lower the risk of recurrence, particularly in high-grade sarcomas. However, the optimal usage and treatment protocols for these therapies remain a subject of ongoing debate. Some studies have suggested that there may not be a notable difference in survival rates between patients who receive adjuvant radiation therapy and those who undergo surgery alone.

Historically, radiation therapy (RT) was used as an adjuvant therapy when necessary. However, preoperative (neoadjuvant) RT is now preferred because it increases the chances of achieving an R0 resection with a reduced risk of long-term toxicities and functional impairments compared with postoperative RT. It is particularly advantageous for large tumors (>10 cm).48 However, it may increase surgical complexity due to radiation-induced changes in tissue. The decision to administer neoadjuvant RT involves considering various factors, including the risk of local recurrence, the possibility of surgical margin positivity, the tumor's size, and its histologic subtype. A typical daily dose of RT ranges from 180 to 200 cGy. Postoperative radiation can address residual microscopic disease after surgical resection, but it can be more challenging if scarring and anatomic distortion occur after surgery. Postoperative radiation can hinder wound healing, so surgical clips can be placed during excisional biopsy or wide resection of the tumor to facilitate the delivery of RT. Research from the National Cancer Institute has shown that radiation therapy is effective in limb-saving surgical procedures for deep, high-grade soft-tissue sarcomas near critical structures or bones. It is crucial to note that the full effects of radiation therapy may take 3 to 6 weeks to manifest. To assess the tumor's response to treatment, a follow-up MRI should be conducted three weeks after completing radiation therapy.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) shows varying efficacy across different sarcoma subtypes, particularly with chemotherapy. Some sarcoma types, such as clear cell sarcoma, spindle cell sarcoma, and epithelioid sarcoma, are chemotherapy-resistant. NACT, administered alone or combined with radiation therapy, can enhance the chances of achieving an R0 resection. However, with conflicting data, the benefits of adjuvant chemotherapy for overall survival are uncertain. Notably, 5-year survival rates are reported to be 60 to 70% with chemotherapy and 11% without treatment. Surgical removal of both primary and metastatic lesions may be appropriate after systemic treatment in cases with limited metastatic involvement.53,54

Surgical intervention should be considered alongside systemic therapy in cases of limited metastasis. The primary chemotherapy drugs used in sarcoma cases are anthracyclines such as doxorubicin or adriamycin, often used in monotherapy or combined with dacarbazine, trabectedin, or ifosfamide. For specific sarcoma subtypes, FDA-approved first-line class medications such as imatinib, larotrectinib, entrectinib, avapritinib, tazemetostat, nab-sirolimus, pexidartinib, and crizotinib (only approved for pediatric patients) offer viable treatment options. In advanced-stage disease, second-line cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents such as ifosfamide, dacarbazine (DTIC), gemcitabine, taxanes, trabectedin, eribulin, and pazopanib can be used. The emergence of olaratumab as an option for patients with advanced, unresectable soft-tissue sarcoma and its effect on the treatment landscape are noteworthy. A randomized study demonstrated that providing at least three cycles of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) was equally effective as five cycles. The survival rate for soft-tissue sarcoma is 70% over five years. However, the survival rate is approximately 50% if the tumor is high grade.

Surveillance After STS Treatment

Post-treatment surveillance for soft-tissue sarcoma (STS) is challenging because of the disease's heterogeneity and the lack of a global consensus guideline. Follow-up strategies, including clinical evaluations and imaging studies, often vary by tumor type, grade, and stage at resection. Major organizations such as the American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria, National Comprehensive Care Network (NCCN), European Society of Skeletal Radiology (ESSR), and European Society of Medical Oncology (ESMO) recommend a 10-year follow-up, adjusting intervals from 3 to 6 months in the first two to three years to annual reviews thereafter.55-59 High-risk subtypes such as angiosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and fibromyxoid sarcoma may require extended monitoring. For instance, the NCCN guidelines outline surveillance for extremity STS, recommending history and physical examinations every 3 to 6 months for early stages (stage 1A/1B) and similarly structured frequency for higher stages (stage 2 or 3A) with additional imaging considerations.59 These guidelines differ from those of other prominent societies, such as the ESMO and the British Sarcoma Group, highlighting the need for evidence-based standardization of surveillance protocols to detect recurrent disease at a treatable stage. Follow-up surveillance visits should include physical examination, chest computer tomography (or enhanced tomography if required), MRI, or ultrasonography. A study revealed that a notable portion of local recurrences was identified through clinical signs and symptoms. By contrast, approximately one-third of these recurrences were detected using MRI, which could detect more than one-third of local recurrences even before any signs or symptoms appeared.49 When the MR imaging results are inconclusive or there are clinical suspicions of recurrence, an ultrasonography with or without biopsy may be necessary. However, the need for re-biopsy of recurrent lesions presents another clinical challenge. Regardless, the lesion must be biopsied whenever there is a clinical and radiologic suspicion of possible recurrence to prevent the catastrophic consequences of a missed recurrence.

Efforts to use prediction models and nomograms for personalized follow-up have faced obstacles, including a lack of external validation, outdated models because of evolving treatments, and limitations in predicting recurrence specifics.9,60 There is an urgent need for enhanced tools that can more precisely estimate recurrence risk, design personalized follow-up schedules, and optimize resource allocation. These efforts are being advanced through multinational collaborative projects, such as the SARveillance trial, which focuses on postoperative sarcoma surveillance, and the Surveillance After Extremity Tumor Surgery (SAFETY) trial.61

Future Directions and Challenges

Sarcoma continues to present numerous challenges, spanning from diagnosis and grading to treatment.48

Diagnostic

Molecular genetics and nomograms have shown many advantages. It helps confirm diagnoses in complicated cases, identify new gene fusions in certain tumors, predict how patients will respond to treatments, identify specific molecular targets, and discover new diagnostic markers. These discoveries can improve how we assess treatment responses and check surgical margins. In addition, research has been done to determine the role of biomarkers in soft-tissue sarcoma and the development of personalized treatment modalities such as immunotherapy.

Treatment

Modifying known treatment regimens (e.g., radiation therapy) and new treatment modalities (e.g., targeted therapies and immunotherapy) have shown potential for better results in certain types of sarcomas. The effectiveness of radiation therapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapies for sarcoma is still being discussed. Brachytherapy is a subset of radiation therapy that uses precise techniques in the treatment and local control of STSs.65 It involves placing catheters in the tumor's excision bed to administer radiation. This technique aims to reduce the complications related to radiation treatment.66 It can be used as a neoadjuvant, intraoperative, adjuvant, and separate treatment method for surgically unresectable tumors. When used as monotherapy, the local control rates have been reported to be 50 to 96%, depending on the patient's risk factors.67 However, the most notable advantage is combining additional resection with brachytherapy.65

3. Immunotherapy

3. Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy is emerging as a promising treatment option for certain sarcoma types that were previously considered less responsive to standard cytotoxic chemotherapy. However, the application of immunotherapy in soft-tissue sarcoma (STS) remains a complex challenge.72 Immunotherapy drugs include zalifrelimab (a CTLA-4 inhibitor), relatlimab (a LAG-3 blocking antibody), and balstilimab (a PD-1 inhibitor), which can be used in advanced STS.72 Two ongoing randomized studies investigate novel combinations: the CONGRATS and TRUST studies.724. 4. Antibody-Drug Conjugates

4. Antibody-Drug Conjugates

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) represent a groundbreaking advancement in targeted cancer therapy, combining the specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potent cytotoxicity of small-molecule drugs. These drugs include mipasetamab uzoptirine (Mipa), which targets AXL, a receptor tyrosine kinase, and GD2, a disialoganglioside. Mipa antibodies are widely distributed across various tumor types, particularly in sarcomas, which are expressed in over 90% of cases.735. Other Therapies

Despite progress in these techniques, several challenges persist for their routine implementation: (1) genetic overlap among unrelated entities and tumors of different lineages, (2) some gene fusions are passengers without any pathogenetic significance, and (3) the increasing frequency of unexpected fusions without any therapeutic options.

Conclusion

Soft-tissue sarcomas (STSs) are a diverse group of malignancies that pose a notable clinical challenge for orthopaedic surgeons. Multidisciplinary team involvement is essential for optimal results during diagnosis and treatment. Although advanced diagnostic tools such as immunophenotypic panels and genetics can be integrated, they should not replace the morphologic diagnosis (the diagnostic benchmark) when necessary. Currently, surgery remains the primary treatment modality for STS. Radiation or chemotherapy can be incorporated into the treatment protocol. Recent advancements in molecular research and therapeutic developments offer promising opportunities for enhancing diagnosis, prognosis, and overall quality of life for patients with STS.
