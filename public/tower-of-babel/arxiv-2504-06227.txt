LExT: Towards Evaluating Trustworthiness of Natural Language Explanations
Krithi Shailya, Shreya Rajpal, Gokul S Krishnan, Balaraman Ravindran

Abstract
. As Large Language Models (LLMs) become increasingly integrated into high-stakes domains, there have been several approaches proposed toward generating natural language explanations. These explanations are crucial for enhancing the interpretability of a model, especially in sensitive domains like healthcare, where transparency and reliability are key. In light of such explanations being generated by LLMs and its known concerns, there is a growing need for robust evaluation frameworks to assess model-generated explanations. Natural Language Generation metrics like BLEU and ROUGE capture syntactic and semantic accuracies but overlook other crucial aspects such as factual accuracy, consistency, and faithfulness. To address this gap, we propose a general framework for quantifying trustworthiness of natural language explanations, balancing Plausibility and Faithfulness, to derive a comprehensive Language Explanation Trustworthiness Score (LExT) 1 1 1 The code and set up to reproduce our experiments are publicly available at https://github.com/cerai-iitm/LExT . Applying our domain-agnostic framework to the healthcare domain using public medical datasets, we evaluate six models, including domain-specific and general-purpose models. Our findings demonstrate significant differences in their ability to generate trustworthy explanations. On comparing these explanations, we make interesting observations such as inconsistencies in Faithfulness demonstrated by general-purpose models and their tendency to outperform domain-specific fine-tuned models. This work further highlights the importance of using a tailored evaluation framework to assess natural language explanations in sensitive fields, providing a foundation for improving the trustworthiness and transparency of language models in healthcare and beyond.

. A key aspect of their utility is explainability, as LLMs can generate insights into their reasoning, enhancing user trust and human-AI collaboration
(
Ali et al., 2023
)
. Early AI systems were criticized as "black boxes," making decisions without clear explanations
(
Adadi and Berrada, 2018
)
. Recent advancements have evolved modern LLMs to provide detailed, natural language justifications, improving their transparency and interpretability
(
Zhao et al., 2023
)
,
(

)
. However, as LLMs are increasingly applied to trust-critical fields like healthcare, law, finance, and education, rigorous evaluation of their outputs becomes increasingly relevant
(
Chen et al., 2024b
)
. While these models demonstrate impressive linguistic capabilities, their explanations, i.e. the reasoning behind their outputs or predictions, must be verified to ensure reliability and trustworthiness
(
Ferdaus et al., 2024
)
.

The explanations generated by these models play a crucial role in ensuring that their predictions or decisions can be understood and trusted by both expert and non-expert stakeholders. In medical settings, for instance, it is not sufficient for an LLM to predict a correct treatment; it must also justify that prediction in a manner that aligns with established medical knowledge, patient-specific contexts, and the expectations of medical professionals
(
Wiegreffe and Marasović, 2021
)
. Without this alignment, the model’s outputs may lack the transparency and trust required for deployment in high-stakes medical environments.

From a regulatory perspective, frameworks like the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act
(
Union, 2025
)
and the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s (NIST) AI Risk Management Framework
(
of Standards and, NIST
)
emphasize transparency and accountability in AI systems. The EU AI Act mandates that high-risk AI systems be designed to be transparent, providing clear instructions about the system’s capabilities, limitations, and any potential risks. This makes the evaluation of explanations a cornerstone for developing AI systems that are not only accurate but also reliably integrated into high-stakes domains to meet standards for accountability, auditability, and ethical compliance.

While much attention has been placed on evaluating model accuracy, less focus has been given to the quality, reliability, and transparency of the explanations that accompany model outputs.
(
Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020
)
(
Lyu et al., 2024
)
. However, a convincing but unreliable explanation can be more dangerous than no explanation at all
(
Ajwani et al., 2024
)
. Thus, the need for a robust framework or new metrics to evaluate explanations becomes evident, as generic Natural Language Generation metrics fail to capture critical aspects like coherence, factual accuracy, and reasoning fidelity.

This paper focuses on developing a comprehensive evaluation framework that evaluates LLM-generated explanations in multiple aspects. Our framework evaluates model explanations across two key dimensions: Plausibility and Faithfulness. Plausibility assesses the perceived validity of a model’s explanation by evaluating its alignment with expert knowledge, semantic accuracy, and contextual relevance
(
Agarwal et al., 2024b
)
. Faithfulness, on the other hand, measures how accurately an explanation reflects the model’s actual reasoning process and its adherence to the specific task
(
Madsen et al., 2024
)
.
Existing research underscores the importance of these metrics.
Agarwal et al. (2024b)
highlight the relationship between plausibility and faithfulness, noting that plausible explanations may not always reflect the true reasoning processes of large language models. These studies emphasize the need for evaluation frameworks that comprehensively address both dimensions to ensure reliable and transparent AI systems.

2.
Related Work

As large language models (LLMs) continue to evolve and expand in their applications across various domains, concerns regarding the trustworthiness of their outputs have become increasingly important. While these models excel at generating coherent and plausible results, their internal reasoning processes remain largely opaque. Existing evaluation metrics, such as BLEU
(
Papineni et al., 2002
)
and ROUGE
(
Lin, 2004
)
, are commonly used to assess text generation quality, yet they fail to capture a comprehensive assessment of model explanations. Similarly, techniques emphasizing semantic similarity, such as similarity of BERT embeddings, neglect critical dimensions like consistency and robustness to counterfactual or paraphrased prompts, which are essential for a thorough evaluation of explanation reliability.

Recent research has introduced frameworks and metrics to improve explainability in LLMs. The XplainLLM framework by
Chen et al. (2024a)
uses Knowledge Graphs (KGs)
(
Hogan et al., 2021
)
and Graph Attention Networks (GATs)
(
Veličković et al., 2018
)
to generate context-grounded explanations, incorporating a dataset with annotations and ranked reasoning elements focused on faithfulness and completeness.
Chen et al. (2023b)
propose the Counterfactual Simulatability Framework to evaluate if natural language explanations allow humans to predict model outputs on modified inputs, introducing metrics like simulation precision to highlight the gap between plausible and faithful reasoning.
Agarwal et al. (2024a)
propose OpenXAI, which benchmarks post-hoc explanations across metrics such as stability, and fairness, revealing trade-offs between reliability and variability across datasets and tasks. Additionally, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) explanations by
Turpin et al. (2023)
are shown to often favor plausibility over faithfulness, misrepresenting the true reasoning processes of LLMs.

Recent works have also explored the dichotomy between faithfulness and plausibility in LLM-generated explanations. While plausible explanations facilitate user-friendly interfaces, they often fail to faithfully represent the reasoning processes of LLMs.
Agarwal et al. (2024b)
highlight this dichotomy, emphasizing that enhancing plausibility may inadvertently reduce faithfulness, particularly in high-stakes applications. Their work calls for systematic approaches to balance these dimensions and ensure that the explanations meet application-specific requirements. However, current studies largely conceptualize this challenge without providing quantitative frameworks for assessment. Similarly, prior research by
Madsen et al. (2024)
on self-explanations from LLMs has introduced metrics for faithfulness but has not integrated these into a unified paradigm for trustworthiness.

This paper seeks to address these limitations by proposing a comprehensive framework that quantifies both faithfulness and plausibility, working toward a robust metric for trustworthiness. By integrating these dimensions, the research aims to advance the development of transparent, reliable, and trustworthy AI systems capable of supporting diverse real-world applications, particularly in high-stakes domains where the cost of error is significant.

3.
Proposed Methodology

Figure 2.
Overview of key metrics proposed to evaluate trustworthiness of a model-generated explanation

The trustworthiness of LLM-generated explanations is evaluated using a framework focused on two key aspects: Plausibility and Faithfulness. Various metrics, illustrated in
Figure 2
, assess alignment with domain expectations and factual adherence. These metrics are calculated for each prompt and averaged across the dataset to yield an overall LExT score for each model. The framework aggregates these metrics, enabling a flexible and comprehensive evaluation of trustworthiness. The following sections detail each metric and its contribution to the overall evaluation.

3.1.
Plausibility

Figure 3.
Plausibility Evaluation Metrics with Medical Case Example

Plausibility refers to the degree to which a model’s explanation appears reasonable and robust from a human perspective.
(
Agarwal et al., 2024b
)
. It encompasses alignment with domain-specific expectations through multiple iterations. This analysis is vital for building trust in the model’s outputs and ensuring they are useful for real-world applications. An overview of the framework is shown in
Figure 3
. Under plausibility, we incorporate different metrics in terms of correctness and consistency, as discussed below.

3.1.1.
Correctness

Correctness assesses how closely a model’s generated explanation aligns with a ground truth or expert-provided explanation. This metric is critical in ensuring that the model’s outputs are factually accurate and aligned with established truths or expert knowledge, thereby minimizing the risk of misleading or erroneous information. In this work, we evaluate two aspects of correctness to compare and quantify explanations, providing a deeper understanding of how models adhere to factual alignment and ensuring their reliability in high-stakes applications.

•

: The accuracy of an explanation is measured by calculating the cosine similarity between the BERT embeddings
(
Devlin et al., 2019
)
of the model-generated and ground-truth explanation. This ensures that the model’s reasoning captures the core ideas of the expert-provided ground truth, verifying that it is semantically accurate.

3.1.2.
Consistency

Language Models, are often known to be inconsistent. This is a significant setback in deploying them to applications, especially in significant fields like healthcare
(
Kim and Vajravelu, 2025
)
. Thus we employ a consistency score, that quantifies how stable the model is by calculating the variance through different iterations or semantically modified prompts. Consistency measures the robustness of the model to different instances of the same or similar inputs. Ideally, the variance is expected to be minimized, and thus the final value is negated as
Consistency=(1-Variance)
to accurately reflect the model’s performance.

•

: Iterative Stability measures a model’s consistency in generating semantically coherent explanations across multiple iterations of the same or slightly varied input. The model is prompted five times, and the variance in cosine similarity between the explanations and the ground truth is calculated. Low variance indicates high consistency.

3.2.
Faithfulness

Figure 4.
Faithfulness Evaluation Metrics with Medical Case Example

Faithfulness evaluates the extent to which a model’s generated explanations remain reliable, ensuring they are firmly rooted to its context. Existing research highlights the importance of faithfulness and define it as how accurately an explanation reflects a model’s reasoning process
(
Jacovi and Goldberg, 2020
)
. Based on this, we include metrics like Question Answer Generation (QAG), Contextual Faithfulness, and Counterfactual Stability as shown in
Figure 4
, to assess different dimensions of faithfulness, each focusing on the model’s ability to accurately reflect it’s reasoning under varying circumstances.

3.2.1.
Question Answer Generation (QAG) Score:

The QAG score measures the model’s ability to generate explanations that answer key questions derived from the explanation. Using a larger LLM, several questions are generated based on the predicted explanation and passed back to the target model prompting the model to return a boolean label 1 if the question can be answered with the explanation and 0 otherwise. The QAG score is then defined as:

(7)

QAG\_Score=\frac{Number\ of\ Positive\ Labels}{Total\ Number\ of\ Questions}

This score quantifies the model’s faithfulness by assessing how well its explanation answers questions based on its own context, ensuring alignment with the predicted explanation.

3.2.2.
Counterfactual Stability

Counterfactual stability tests the model’s ability to adapt to contradictory explanations. Using a larger model, the explanation is rephrased to imply the opposite label (e.g., “Yes” becomes “No”)
(
Madsen et al., 2024
)
. The revised explanation and question are then re-prompted to the smaller model, and the responses are scored as follows:

•

+1
: The model flips its label to match the counterfactual explanation.

•

0
: The model cannot provide a meaningful response.

•

-1
: The model remains consistent with its original label.

The counterfactual stability score is then scaled using min-max normalization to [0 to 1], reflecting a more standardized evaluation. While the QAG Score assesses faithfulness to the predicted context, Counterfactual Stability complements it by evaluating the model’s logical adaptability to contradictory inputs. In critical domains, where contradictory evidence is common, this metric ensures that the model adapts its reasoning rather than rigidly repeating its initial response.

3.2.3.
Contextual Faithfulness

This metric evaluates how dependent a model’s explanations are on specific contextual elements by systematically removing key information and analyzing the impact on the model’s responses
(
Madsen et al., 2024
)
. In two experiments, the model is asked to provide 5 important keywords essential for evaluating its response, and these are identified and redacted in two phases:

•

Complete Redaction:
All critical keywords are removed simultaneously, and the model is re-prompted to identify if it can highlight the lack of enough information in its explanation.

•

Sequential Redaction:
Keywords are removed one at a time, followed by re-prompting, to assess how the absence of each keyword individually impacts the explanation.

If the model passes the first phase (complete redaction), the fraction of "Unknown" responses in the sequential phase quantifies the model’s reliance on context. If the model fails in the first phase, it is given a score of 0. The contextual faithfulness score is calculated as:

(8)

Contextual\_Faithfulness=\frac{{Unknown\ Responses}}{{Total\ Prompts}}

If the model can still generate plausible explanations after redacting key keywords, it may indicate that the explanations are not grounded in the predicted context. This metric helps detect discrepancies between the model’s reasoning and its output, ensuring transparency and mitigating overconfidence. A trustworthy model should recognize and communicate when it lacks adequate context to deliver a reliable explanation.

3.3.
Evaluation Framework

Figure 5.
Evaluation Framework for Calculating the LExT Score of a model

To evaluate model trustworthiness, we propose a framework that assesses two critical dimensions of explanation quality: Plausibility and Faithfulness. As illustrated in
Figure 5
, each dimension is computed by averaging its components, individually scaled to a uniform range [0,1] to ensure equal representation of all metrics. The final metric combines Plausibility (alignment with human expectations) and Faithfulness (reflection of the model’s actual reasoning) to balance these aspects, providing a comprehensive measure of explanation quality.

The Final LExT Score (
T
) of a model is given by
Equation 9

(9)

T=\text{Average}(P,F)-\text{g}\big((P-F)^{2}\big)

Where
P
represents Plausibility and
F
represents Faithfulness, and
g
is a function of the squared difference
(P-F)^{2}
. This metric incorporates two essential components:

•

Magnitude
: The magnitude
(P+F)
represents the overall performance of the model across plausibility and faithfulness. A high average is desirable because both dimensions are independently important.

•

Disagreement
: The difference
(P-F)
measures the Disagreement between plausibility and faithfulness. Ideally, these values should be minimized, meaning the model produces explanations that are both plausible and faithful.

The first term,
(P+F)/2
, represents the average of plausibility and faithfulness. This ensures that models are rewarded for performing well in both dimensions. The average ensures that both terms are given equal weight, promoting a balanced evaluation of trustworthiness. However, trustworthiness also requires that there be an agreement between plausibility and faithfulness. A model that generates implausible but faithful explanations risks misleading users, while one that is plausible but unfaithful may produce explanations that are truthful but unreliable. To address this, the second term
(P-F)^{2}
penalizes large disagreements between plausibility and faithfulness. By squaring the difference
(P-F)
, the penalty increases with increasing disagreement, highlighting significant mismatches between the two dimensions, while also ensuring that positive and negative disagreements are penalized equally. The function
g
scales this penalty appropriately to ensure that models with overall low performance in both dimensions are not disproportionately penalized for small disagreements. For instance, if both plausibility and faithfulness are near zero, any disagreement between them is unlikely to be meaningful. The scaling mechanism ensures fairness among the metrics and prevents the metric from being overly sensitive to noise brought in by low-performing models.

Through further analysis, we derived the function
g(x)
to be
\frac{x}{2(P+F)}
2
2

2

The function
g
was chosen because it derives the metric into the harmonic mean of
P
and
F
, providing a balanced evaluation of the two dimensions. The detailed derivation is provided in the Appendix.
, resulting in the final form of the metric:

(10)

T=\frac{P+F}{2}-\frac{(P-F)^{2}}{2(P+F)}

Thus, by incorporating magnitude and disagreement, the metric avoids overemphasizing one at the expense of the other and ensures that:

•

High overall performance is rewarded, but only if plausibility and faithfulness are in reasonable agreement.

•

Models with low magnitude are appropriately downweighted, as their explanations lack sufficient quality.

In practical terms, this metric addresses key challenges in explainable AI. It discourages models from overemphasizing plausibility in expense of faithfulness. At the same time, it ensures that faithful explanations are also plausible and comprehensible, enhancing their utility for decision-making. By integrating both magnitude and agreement of plausibility and faithfulness, the trustworthiness metric provides a robust evaluation framework, capturing the relationships between these two critical dimensions of explanation quality.

4.
Implementation

The proposed framework for evaluating the trustworthiness of large language models was implemented on a variety of models and datasets to ensure a comprehensive assessment of their explanation capabilities.

4.1.
Datasets

This work includes the implementation of the evaluation framework on two datasets that are widely used in the medical domain: QPain and PubmedQA. Both datasets offer complex, expert-level explanations and questions that are suitable for evaluating the quality of explanations generated by LLMs. These datasets were chosen to ensure the framework’s applicability in a wide range of healthcare applications from medication management to treatment recommendation.

QPain
(
Logé et al., 2021
)
is a dataset focused on pain management in clinical settings. It features prompts with medical scenarios and placeholders for attributes like gender and race, along with expert-generated ground truth explanations for treatment recommendations and pain assessment. This dataset is ideal for evaluating models that incorporate patient-specific information into clinical decisions.

PubmedQA
(
for Biotechnology Information, 2023
)
is a large-scale biomedical question-answering dataset that consists of biomedical research questions, extracted abstracts, and ground-truth answers or explanations generated by domain experts. It covers a broad range of biomedical topics, providing a rigorous set of long answers for models to learn from. In our study, we use these long answers as the ground explanation as it provides a structured reasoning using the context, making it suitable for evaluating ground-truth dependent metrics.

4.2.
Models

Six models were selected for the evaluation, including three domain-specific models fine-tuned for healthcare and three general-purpose models. These models were chosen based on their ability to handle medical tasks, while also offering a comparison between specialized and general LLMs.

5.4.
R4: How generalizable are these metrics across datasets and domains?

The metrics in this framework, including correctness, consistency, QAG score, counterfactual stability, and contextual faithfulness, are designed to be dataset-agnostic, allowing application across diverse domains. The overall LExT score aggregates these metrics, balancing their individual contributions to provide a unified assessment of model performance. This ensures that models are evaluated holistically, with equal emphasis on all aspects of their reasoning and output quality. These features make the framework adaptable for use in other high-stakes areas, such as legal or financial question-answering, where accuracy and reliability are essential.

6.
Conclusion and Future Scope

This paper examines faithfulness, plausibility, and trustworthiness as key metrics for evaluating language models, particularly in high-stakes domains. While these metrics were theorized previously, their practical use and generalization have been limited. We propose a structured framework (LExT) to evaluate and compare model explanations, providing insights into their strengths and weaknesses. By integrating these metrics, the framework allows comprehensive evaluation across dimensions and analyzes trade-offs between performance and interpretability.

The future scope involves tackling key issues such as biases in explanations, fairness, and ensuring equality across diverse contexts. It also includes developing comprehensive ground truth datasets for factual verification to enhance model reliability. Furthermore, there is potential to optimize smaller language models, making them more efficient while maintaining high-quality explanations, thus reducing dependence on large models for complex tasks.

A.2.
Derivation of the LExT Score

We derive the LExT metric as follows:

The proposed general form of the metric is:

(11)

T=\frac{P+F}{2}-g\big((P-F)^{2}\big).

To achieve a meaningful scaling of disagreement, we set
g
such that the penalty term relates naturally to the harmonic mean of
P
and
F
. Substituting
g(x)=\frac{x}{2(P+F)}
, we obtain:

(12)

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\frac{P+F}{2}-\frac{(P-F)^{2}}{2(P+F)}

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{P+F}{2}-\frac{P^{2}-2PF+F^{2}}{2(P+F)}

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{(P+F)^{2}-(P^{2}-2PF+F^{2})}{2(P+F)}

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ P\ F}{P+F}

which is the harmonic mean of
P
and
F
.

Rationale for Choosing
g

The choice of
g=\frac{1}{2(P+F)}
ensures that the disagreement penalty scales appropriately with the overall magnitude of
P
and
F
. This not only leads to a natural harmonic mean interpretation but also ensures fairness by preventing disproportionate penalties for low-performing models.

B.6.
Counter Factual Stability

Counterfactual stability measures the robustness of a model’s explanation by testing whether rephrased explanations lead to consistent label predictions. Specifically, it evaluates whether flipping an explanation results in the corresponding label being flipped. To compute counterfactual stability:

(1)

For a given question and model-provided explanation, rephrase the explanation to suggest the opposite label.

(2)

Feed the rephrased explanation back to the model alongside the original question.

(3)

Record the new label predicted by the model.

(4)

If the new label is flipped compared to the original label, counterfactual stability is satisfied. Otherwise, it is not.
