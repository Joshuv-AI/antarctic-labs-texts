Statin Initiation and Mortality After Colorectal Cancer Treatment
Authors: Hyeong-taek Woo, Aesun Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAmong adults with colorectal cancer (CRC), is statin initiation after initial treatment associated with improved survival when analyzed using target trial emulation?FindingsIn this cohort study of 88 516 adults treated for CRC, statin initiation was associated with a 1.1-percentage point difference in standardized 60-month CRC-specific survival, but the difference was not statistically significant.MeaningThese findings suggest that posttreatment statin initiation is unlikely to confer a large survival benefit in patients with CRC and that previously reported larger protective associations may reflect time-related biases and informative censoring.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is among the most common malignant diseases worldwide and remains a major contributor to cancer-related morbidity and mortality.1 In South Korea, CRC is among the leading cancers in terms of both incidence and mortality, reflecting a substantial disease burden.2 As screening and treatment have improved, more patients are living with CRC alongside chronic cardiometabolic conditions.2

Hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia are common among patients with CRC.3,4,5 These conditions share established risk factors with CRC, including obesity, physical inactivity, alcohol use, and smoking.6,7,8 As a result, medications prescribed for cardiovascular prevention are frequently used by this population.

Statins are among the most widely prescribed medications worldwide and are recommended for the primary and secondary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.9,10 Randomized clinical trials have consistently shown that statins reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.9 In addition to lowering lipid levels through the inhibition of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase, statins have been reported to have anti-inflammatory and antiproliferative properties.11,12,13,14 These biological observations have motivated interest in whether statins may also influence cancer progression or survival.

Several observational studies have reported improved survival among patients with CRC who use statins after diagnosis.15,16,17,18 However, pharmacoepidemiologic studies of postdiagnosis medication use are particularly vulnerable to time-related biases.19 Because statins are inexpensive and widely prescribed, they are an attractive candidate for repurposing as adjunctive therapy in CRC. It is therefore clinically important to determine whether the reported survival associations persist after addressing time-related biases and whether the absolute survival differences are meaningful.

When treatment initiation occurs after cohort entry, failure to align eligibility criteria, treatment assignment, and the start of follow-up can introduce immortal time bias. Patients classified as treated must survive long enough to receive treatment, which can artifactually inflate survival in the treated group.19,20 The inclusion of prevalent users may also introduce bias because patients who tolerate and continue long-term therapy differ systematically from nonusers.19,21,22 In addition, treatment changes during follow-up can induce informative censoring.19,22,23 Together, these design features may substantially exaggerate protective associations.

Target trial emulation provides a structured approach to reducing these biases in observational research.19,24,25,26 By explicitly specifying the protocol of the hypothetical randomized clinical trial that the analysis aims to approximate, including eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, time zero, follow-up, outcomes, and the causal estimand, this framework prioritizes study design before modeling. The clone-censor weight approach allows estimation of the observational analogue of the per-protocol effect while accounting for informative censoring caused by postbaseline deviations from assigned treatment strategies.19,24

South Korea offers a particularly suitable setting for target trial emulation using administrative health claims data. The National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database covers nearly the entire population under a universal health insurance system and includes comprehensive information on diagnoses, procedures, prescriptions, and mortality.27 These features allow near-complete follow-up and detailed ascertainment of medication initiation.

In this study, using nationwide claims data from South Korea, we emulated a target trial to estimate the observational analogue of the per-protocol effect of statin initiation within 6 months after initial CRC treatment on CRC-specific and all-cause mortality. By explicitly aligning eligibility, treatment assignment, and follow-up and accounting for informative censoring, we sought to reassess whether the large protective associations reported in conventional observational analyses persisted under a design that more closely approximated a randomized clinical trial, hypothesizing that these associations would be substantially attenuated.

Methods

Data Source

For this cohort study, we used the customized research database of the Korean NHIS, which contains nationwide claims data generated for reimbursement purposes and covers nearly the entire population of South Korea.28 Available variables included sex, year of birth, date of death, income-based insurance premium level (a proxy for socioeconomic status), residential region, diagnoses coded according to the Korean Standard Classification of Diseases (KCD), procedure and treatment claim codes, prescription records, health screening results, and hospital-level characteristics such as facility type and number of beds.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Keimyung University Dongsan Hospital, which waived the need for informed consent due to the use of deidentified claims data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Cohort Construction (Base Cohort)

We identified patients who were newly diagnosed with CRC between January 1, 2005, and December 31, 2015. CRC was defined as a primary diagnosis of malignant neoplasm of the colon (International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] code C18), rectosigmoid junction (ICD-10 code C19), or rectum (ICD-10 code C20), accompanied by at least 1 claim for CRC treatment. Treatment codes included surgical procedures, chemotherapy agents (eg, capecitabine, fluorouracil, irinotecan, oxaliplatin, bevacizumab, and cetuximab), and radiotherapy (a complete list is available in eTable 1 in Supplement 1). We excluded individuals diagnosed before 2005, those who died within 30 days of diagnosis, and those with missing eligibility information. Within the base cohort, eligibility was restricted to adults 30 years or older with newly diagnosed CRC who had no statin prescription during the 6 months before diagnosis.

Target Trial Specification

Target trial emulation proceeded in 2 steps: First, the protocol of the hypothetical target trial was explicitly specified to define the causal question; second, that protocol was emulated using observational data.19,24,25,26 The prespecified target trial protocol and its emulation using NHIS claims data are summarized in Table 1.

Treatment Strategies

Statin initiation was defined as starting any statin (simvastatin, lovastatin, pravastatin, fluvastatin, atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, or pitavastatin) within a prespecified 6-month grace period after the first CRC treatment. The grace period was specified a priori as part of the target trial protocol, consistent with a prior study evaluating postdiagnostic statin initiation in patients with cancer.29 The comparison strategy involved no statin initiation during the same period.

Treatment Assignment

Because random allocation is not possible in an observational emulation, treatment strategies were assigned according to observed data. To address baseline confounding, analyses adjusting for age, sex, socioeconomic status (insurance contribution level and insurance type), comorbidities, and proxies for cancer stage were performed. Comorbidity was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), which was updated every 3 months to account for time-varying confounding. Because cancer stage was not directly available in the claims data, treatment modality, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, was used as a proxy for disease severity, consistent with prior studies. Patients were classified according to treatment modality as detailed in eTable 1 in Supplement 1, based on the premise that earlier-stage disease was more likely to be treated with surgery alone, whereas advanced disease was more likely to be treated with chemotherapy or radiotherapy without surgery.30,31

Outcomes

The primary outcome was CRC-specific mortality, defined as an underlying cause of death with ICD-10 codes C18 through C20. The secondary outcome was all-cause mortality.

Follow-Up

A previous study using the same NHIS database reported a relatively short mean (SD) interval of 13.0 (21.0) days from CRC diagnosis to treatment.32 In this context, time zero was defined as the date of first CRC treatment to align the start of follow-up with posttreatment statin initiation strategies. Follow-up began at time zero and continued until death from CRC, death from any cause, loss of eligibility—defined as the date on which a participant was no longer recorded as eligible in the NHIS eligibility database—or 60 months after time zero, whichever occurred first.

Causal Estimand

The estimand was the observational analogue of the per-protocol effect of statin initiation within a 6-month grace period, regardless of subsequent discontinuation. This mirrors the intention-to-treat effect in a trial where all participants assigned to initiation began therapy during the grace period, even if some later discontinued.33 Per-protocol adherence was implemented using the clone-censor weight approach with a 6-month grace period: individuals were assigned to strategies based on statin initiation within 6 months of time zero, and follow-up was censored at the time of deviation from the assigned strategy.19,24 After the grace period, noninitiators were reevaluated every 3 months and were considered nonusers as long as no statin prescriptions were observed, whereas initiators were allowed to discontinue statin therapy at any time as clinically indicated.29

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed from August 22, 2023, to November 22, 2025. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the independent-samples t test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Because mortality within each 3-month interval during 60 months was uncommon, weighted pooled logistic regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs), which approximate Cox proportional hazards regression models under rare outcome conditions.34 Separate models were fitted for CRC-specific mortality and all-cause mortality, with adjustment for baseline confounders. For CRC-specific mortality, deaths from other causes were treated as censoring events. Robust SEs clustered at the individual level were used to account for within-person correlation because of repeated 3-month interval observations during follow-up.34,35 To address immortal time bias introduced by the 6-month grace period, we applied the clone-censor weight method.19,24 Each eligible individual was duplicated into 2 clones at baseline and assigned to each treatment strategy. Clones were artificially censored if they deviated from their assigned strategy: clones assigned to the initiation strategy were censored if no statin was initiated within 6 months, whereas clones assigned to the noninitiation strategy were censored at the time of statin initiation. Outcome events occurring while a clone remained uncensored were counted from time zero in that clone. Because this artificial censoring may induce selection bias, we applied a stabilized inverse probability of censoring weights. Stabilized weights were calculated using the probability of remaining uncensored conditional on baseline confounders in the numerator and on baseline plus time-varying confounders in the denominator. The time-varying confounder was the CCI, which was updated every 3 months. To reduce the influence of extreme weights, weights above the 99th percentile were truncated. Detailed results of weight estimation are provided in eTables 2 through 5 in Supplement 1. In addition to conditional HRs, we estimated marginal survival contrasts by fitting weighted pooled logistic regression models with time-by-treatment interaction terms and using the fitted models to estimate 60-month survival probabilities under 2 hypothetical scenarios: universal statin initiation and universal noninitiation. We then derived absolute risk differences and survival probabilities; the 60-month risk difference and its 95% CI were estimated using 500 bootstrap resamples.35

As sensitivity analyses, we performed additional analyses to evaluate the impact of design-related biases and to assess the robustness of the target trial emulation framework. To illustrate design-related bias, 3 conventional observational analyses were performed: (1) incorporating immortal time bias by defining exposure using postbaseline information and fitting a time-fixed Cox proportional hazards regression model; (2) incorporating prevalent user bias by including prediagnosis statin users and applying the clone-censor weight approach; and (3) incorporating both biases by including prevalent users while defining exposure post baseline and fitting a time-fixed Cox proportional hazards regression model. We also applied the same emulation design to a cohort of patients with ischemic heart disease (KCD codes I20-I25) as a positive control and evaluated all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, defined using KCD I codes and codes G45 to G46. All statistical tests were 2 sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant. SAS Enterprise Guide, version 7.1 (SAS Institute Inc), was used for the statistical analyses, and R, version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), was used to generate the figures.

Results

Study Population

The final cohort included 88 516 patients (mean [SD] age, 62.7 [11.7] years; 56 424 [63.7%] male and 32 092 [36.3%] female). Of these, 2071 patients (2.3%) initiated statins within 6 months after initial CRC treatment (mean [SD] age, 65.7 [10.5] years; 1298 [62.7%] male and 773 [37.3%] female) and 86 445 (97.7%) did not (mean [SD] age, 62.6 [11.7] years; 55 126 [63.8%] male and 31 319 [36.2%] female) (Figure 1 and Table 2). Insurance premium quintiles were broadly comparable, although statin initiators were more likely to be Medical Aid beneficiaries, had higher CCI scores, and more often underwent surgery alone as initial treatment (Table 2).

Observational Analogue of the Per-Protocol Effect

After adjustment for baseline confounders and a time-varying CCI using stabilized inverse probability weights, statin initiation within the 6-month grace period was associated with modestly lower CRC-specific mortality compared with noninitiation (HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92-0.99]). All-cause mortality was also lower, although the finding did not reach statistical significance (HR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93-1.00]).

Standardized Survival Estimates

When marginal survival probabilities were estimated under hypothetical scenarios of universal statin initiation vs universal noninitiation, the 60-month absolute differences were small: 1.1 percentage points (pp) (95% CI, −0.1 to 2.3 pp) for CRC-specific mortality and 2.4 pp (95% CI, 1.0-3.8 pp) for all-cause mortality (Figure 2). These marginal contrasts suggested that the absolute differences were modest despite the conditional hazard ratios.

Analyses Emulating Conventional Observational Approaches

When immortal time bias was introduced by defining exposure using postbaseline information and analyzing the data using a time-fixed Cox proportional hazards regression model without target trial emulation, statin use appeared strongly protective against CRC-specific mortality (adjusted HR, 0.17 [95% CI, 0.16-0.17]) and all-cause mortality (adjusted HR, 0.19 [95% CI, 0.18-0.19]). When prevalent users were included but the clone-censor weight method was applied, the magnitude of protection was attenuated but remained substantial (adjusted HR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.79-0.84] for CRC-specific mortality and 0.82 [95% CI, 0.80-0.84] for all-cause mortality). When both immortal time bias and prevalent user bias were simultaneously introduced, protective associations were again exaggerated (adjusted HR, 0.25 [95% CI, 0.24-0.26] for CRC-specific mortality and 0.27 [95% CI, 0.26-0.28] for all-cause mortality) (eTable 6 in Supplement 1).

Positive Control Analysis in the Cohort With Cardiovascular Disease

In the cohort with ischemic heart disease (n = 8212), application of the target trial emulation framework did not result in an association between statin initiation and cardiovascular disease–specific mortality (adjusted HR, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.65-2.32]). However, statin initiation was associated with lower all-cause mortality (adjusted HR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.65-0.92]) (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this nationwide cohort study emulating a target trial, statin initiation after CRC treatment was associated with modestly lower CRC-specific and all-cause mortality during the prespecified 60-month follow-up after explicit adjustment for time-related biases and informative censoring. The magnitude of these changes in mortality was substantially smaller than that reported in conventional observational analyses.15,16,17,18

The attenuation of the association estimates under the target trial emulation framework suggests that previously reported large protective associations may have been driven, at least in part, by design-related biases. When we deliberately introduced immortal time bias or prevalent user bias, statin use was associated with lower mortality, yielding implausibly large apparent survival differences. The most pronounced exaggeration occurred when immortal time bias was present. These findings underscore how the misalignment of eligibility criteria, exposure classification, and the start of follow-up can generate substantial noncausal associations.

Immortal time bias arises when exposure is defined using postbaseline information, thereby conditioning on survival during a period in which the outcome cannot occur. This creates a selection mechanism that favors survival in the exposed group.19,20 Prevalent user bias similarly reflects selection on factors related to treatment continuation and prognosis, potentially opening noncausal pathways between treatment and outcome when analyses are restricted to current users.19,21,22 Informative censoring resulting from treatment deviations during follow-up can further distort effect estimates if not appropriately addressed.19,22,23 By explicitly emulating the protocol of a hypothetical randomized clinical trial, the target trial framework provides a systematic approach to minimizing these biases.19,24,25,26

We also applied the same design to patients with ischemic heart disease, for whom randomized clinical trials have established cardiovascular benefits of statins. Statin initiation was associated with lower all-cause mortality, but the cardiovascular disease–specific estimate was imprecise and did not show the expected direction. Therefore, this positive-control analysis provided only partial reassurance regarding the design and should not be interpreted as eliminating residual confounding or outcome misclassification.

Our estimates are broadly consistent with prior target trial emulation studies and randomized evidence evaluating statins in populations with cancer.29,36 Although the underlying populations and outcomes differ, the observed magnitude is more compatible with randomized evidence than with the large protective associations reported in earlier conventional observational studies.9,15,16,17,18,37 This contrast highlights the importance of rigorous design in pharmacoepidemiologic research.

The increasing availability of clinical data, including nationwide claims databases, has created important opportunities for evaluating treatment effects. In South Korea, the single-payer NHIS enables near-complete follow-up and a comprehensive capture of prescription records. Because treatment is not randomly assigned in observational studies, residual confounding may remain even after adjustment for measured covariates. However, discrepancies between observational analyses and randomized clinical trials may also arise from design-related biases, including misalignment of eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, time zero, and follow-up. Therefore, the validity of causal inference from clinical data depends critically on study design. Explicit protocol specification, careful alignment of time zero, and appropriate adjustment for informative censoring are essential for reducing these design-related sources of bias.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, residual confounding remains possible despite adjustment for measured baseline and time-varying covariates. Cancer stage was not directly available in the claims data and was approximated using treatment patterns, which may incompletely capture disease severity. However, prior studies support the use of treatment and procedure information as a proxy for disease stage when registry-based staging is unavailable.30,31,38 Nonetheless, treatment modality may also be influenced by patient age, comorbidity, performance status, and physician or patient preference, and stage-related findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Second, the 6-month statin washout period may have misclassified remote former users as new users. Although any resulting prevalent user bias could have favored an apparent protective association, the observed absolute survival benefit remained small. Third, we considered statin initiation as a class effect and did not differentiate between specific statin types, which may have heterogeneous biological effects. Future studies incorporating richer clinical data and examining drug-specific effects are warranted. Fourth, the findings pertain to the prespecified strategy of statin initiation within a 6-month grace period after initial CRC treatment; alternative grace-period specifications, which would represent different target trial protocols, were not evaluated. Last, potential effect modification by disease severity could not be adequately evaluated because of the limited precision of interaction estimates resulting from the small number of statin initiators. Future studies with sufficient statistical power are needed to examine heterogeneity in the association.

Conclusions

In this nationwide cohort study, the apparent survival benefit associated with statin initiation after CRC treatment was substantially attenuated after accounting for immortal time bias, prevalent user bias, and informative censoring. The remaining benefits were modest in magnitude. These findings highlight the critical role of study design in observational pharmacoepidemiology. Target trial emulation offers a structured and transparent approach to improving causal inference when clinical data are used to evaluate associations between medication use and outcomes.
