The ‘Upstairs-Downstairs’ heart with complete main pulmonary artery agenesis and PDA-dependent pulmonary circulation: A rare structural intersection characterized by multi detector computed tomography
Authors: Aamir M Kanji, Tariq Aziz, Lulu Fundikira, Ummehani Akberali, Lilian Salingwa, Frederick Lyimo, Mboka Jacob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuperior-inferior ventricular arrangement is an exceedingly rare form of congenital cardiac malposition, representing fewer than 0.1% of all congenital heart diseases, and is almost invariably associated with complex intracardiac anomalies. We report a 3-year-old male who presented with persistent cyanosis, exertional dyspnea, and failure to thrive. Prospective ECG-gated CT angiography demonstrated situs solitus with levocardia. A superior-inferior ventricular arrangement was present, with the morphologic left ventricle positioned superior to the morphologic right ventricle. The great arteries were normally related, with a left-sided aortic arch. A large ventricular septal defect was identified. Pulmonary atresia was present, characterized by the absence of the right ventricular outflow tract, pulmonary valve, and main pulmonary artery. Native confluent branch pulmonary arteries were present and received pulmonary blood flow through a large patent ductus arteriosus supplying the left pulmonary artery, with additional small systemic-to-pulmonary collateral vessels. The morphologic right ventricle was dilated and hypertrophied, with associated enlargement of the right atrium. Pulmonary venous drainage was normal, and no abnormalities of the systemic venous drainage were identified. This case underscores the critical role of cardiac CT angiography in comprehensive anatomical delineation of rare and complex congenital cardiac malformations, with direct implications for surgical planning and prognostication.Keywords: Congenital heart disease, Cardiac CT angiography, Patent ductus arteriosus, Pulmonary atresia, Superior-inferior ventricles, Ventricular septal defect

Introduction

Superior-inferior ventricular arrangement, also termed upstairs-downstairs heart or superoinferior ventricles (SIV), is one of the rarest forms of congenital cardiac malposition, estimated to account for fewer than 0.1% of all congenital heart diseases [1]. In contrast to the normal side-by-side ventricular relationship, SIV is characterized by a horizontal interventricular septum with the two ventricles positioned in a superior-to-inferior (over-and-under) orientation rather than in the conventional left-right arrangement [1,2]. The morphogenesis of this anomaly is attributed to abnormal rotation or tilting of the ventricular mass along its long axis during embryological cardiac development [3].

SIV is rarely an isolated defect and is almost universally accompanied by complex intracardiac anomalies including ventricular septal defects (VSDs), transposition of the great arteries, double-outlet right or left ventricle, and pulmonary outflow tract obstruction [1,2].

Pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect (PA-VSD) represents the severe end of the tetralogy of Fallot spectrum and is characterized by the absence of continuity between the right ventricle and the pulmonary arterial system, requiring alternative sources of pulmonary blood flow [4]. The pulmonary circulation in PA-VSD varies depending on the underlying anatomy and may be supplied by a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) or major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) [4,5].

We describe the CT angiographic findings of this rare coexistence and emphasize the indispensable role of cardiac CT in comprehensive pre-operative anatomical mapping.

Case presentation

A 3-year-old boy presented to our hospital, with a history of bluish discoloration of the lips and extremities first noticed by his mother at approximately 2 months of age. By 3 months of age, he developed fatigue and excessive sweating during breastfeeding. Over the subsequent months, he exhibited poor weight gain, and by approximately 12 months of age, delayed developmental milestones became evident. Between 6 months and 3 years of age, he experienced recurrent episodes of pneumonia and received treatment at several local hospitals without sustained improvement. The pregnancy and delivery history were unremarkable. Owing to persistent symptoms and failure to thrive, he was referred to our National Referral Hospital for further evaluation.

On examination, the child was underweight and centrally cyanosed, with no jaundice, edema, or clubbing. The patient was cyanotic with low oxygen saturation (peripheral oxygen saturation 82% on room air), tachypnoeic (respiratory rate 44 breaths/min), and tachycardic (pulse rate 134 beats/min), while the body temperature was normal. Cardiac auscultation revealed a continuous machinery murmur.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated microcytic hypochromic anemia of 9 g/dL and mild leukopenia of 4.5 × 10⁹/L, while the platelet count and serum creatinine were within normal limits. Liver function tests and serum electrolytes were also normal.

CT findings

The CT scan confirmed a normal visceroatrial situs with normal atrioventricular connections. A superior-inferior ventricular arrangement was identified, characterized by a horizontal interventricular septum, with the morphological left ventricle situated superiorly and the morphological right ventricle positioned inferiorly (Fig. 1). The right atrium and right ventricle demonstrated marked dilation and diffuse wall hypertrophy (Fig. 2). Additionally, a ventricular septal defect (VSD) was present (Figs. 1 and 3).

Discussion

This case presents a highly unusual combination of rare congenital cardiac anomalies: superior-inferior ventricles with pulmonary atresia, ventricular septal defect (VSD), and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)-dependent pulmonary circulation.

Accurate anatomical characterization is essential in such complex cases because surgical strategy, staged palliation timelines, and prognosis depend heavily on the specific configuration of intracardiac and extracardiac findings.

Superior-inferior ventricular arrangement is among the rarest of all cardiac malposition, with an estimated prevalence of less than 0.1% of all congenital heart diseases [1]. The condition is also referred to as upstairs-downstairs heart, reflecting the over-and-under positional relationship of the ventricles. The interventricular septum assumes a horizontal rather than the normal oblique orientation, and the ventricular mass demonstrates abnormal tilting along its long axis [3]. Superior-inferior ventricular arrangement is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly characterized by a horizontal interventricular septum, resulting in a superior and inferior positioning of the morphological ventricles. The earliest descriptions of this unusual ventricular arrangement were reported by Sathe et al. in 1991 [6], who described cases demonstrating this complex cardiac anatomy. Subsequently, Deshpande et al [1] reported the first case of the less common variant featuring a superior morphological left ventricle with an inferior morphological right ventricle. More recently, Deshpande et al. [1] further highlighted the utility of a cardiac multidetector computed tomography in delineating the complex anatomy of the horizontal “sandwiched” interventricular septum and superior-inferior ventricular arrangement.

In our case, the right ventricle occupied the inferior (diaphragmatic) position and the left ventricle the superior position, consistent with this less common variant. The interventricular septum was horizontal. Cardiac multi-detector CT with multiplanar and three-dimensional reconstructions was critical in establishing this diagnosis, as the abnormal spatial orientation renders standard two-dimensional echocardiographic assessment challenging [2].

SIV is almost never an isolated finding. In published series and case reports, it is consistently associated with complex intracardiac anomalies including VSDs, great artery transposition, double-outlet right ventricle, and outflow tract obstruction [1,2]. Our case adds pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect and patent ductus arteriosus-dependent pulmonary circulation to the spectrum of associated anomalies, representing a rare and complex phenotype of superior-inferior ventricular arrangement.

Pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect (PA-VSD) represents the most severe form of the tetralogy of Fallot spectrum and is characterized by complete interruption of antegrade right ventricular outflow, with pulmonary blood flow maintained by extracardiac sources [4,5]. In our case, pulmonary circulation was ductus-dependent, with a large patent ductus arteriosus supplying confluent native branch pulmonary arteries and no major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs), consistent with Type A PA-VSD, the anatomical subtype generally associated with the most favorable surgical outcomes [4,5]. Although the pulmonary valve was absent, the findings do not represent classical absent pulmonary valve syndrome, which is typically associated with tetralogy of Fallot, severe pulmonary regurgitation, and aneurysmal dilatation of the pulmonary trunk and branch pulmonary arteries. Instead, the absent pulmonary valve occurred in the setting of pulmonary atresia, where the pulmonary outflow tract was completely interrupted and pulmonary blood flow was sustained via the patent ductus arteriosus [7,8].

The clinical significance of the large PDA in this case cannot be overstated. In patients with PA-VSD and PDA-dependent pulmonary circulation, maintenance of ductal patency is critical for survival, and ductal closure in the neonatal period can precipitate fatal hypoxia [9]. As surgical planning progresses, the management of this PDA must be carefully integrated into the overall strategy. Available palliative and corrective options include prostaglandin-maintained ductal patency, transcatheter PDA stenting, and surgical systemic-to-pulmonary shunt creation as a bridge to definitive repair [9,10]. Notably, PDA stenting has emerged as an effective alternative to the modified Blalock-Taussig shunt in selected patients with ductal-dependent pulmonary circulation, with comparable mortality and the advantage of preserving branch pulmonary artery growth [10].

The bilateral mosaic attenuation pattern observed on CT reflects heterogeneous pulmonary perfusion, a recognized finding in congenital heart disease with abnormal pulmonary vascular anatomy. Mosaic attenuation in this context is attributable to regional variation in pulmonary arterial perfusion, with areas of oligemia adjacent to areas of relatively preserved or increased flow [11]. This finding reflects heterogeneous pulmonary perfusion secondary to the abnormal pulmonary vascular anatomy associated with pulmonary atresia, with uneven distribution of pulmonary blood flow within the lungs.

From an imaging perspective, multi-detector CT angiography played an indispensable role in this case. While echocardiography remains the first-line imaging modality for congenital heart disease, it has recognized limitations in spatial resolution and acoustic window constraints, particularly in complex three-dimensional malformations such as SIV [12]. CT angiography provides superior delineation of intracardiac morphology, spatial ventricular relationships, great vessel anatomy, collateral vessels, and extracardiac structures within a single examination [12,13]. The sequential segmental approach described by Van Praagh is fundamental to systematic interpretation of complex CHD anatomy on CT, ensuring that each cardiac segment and connection is evaluated independently before synthesis [13]. Three-dimensional volume-rendered reconstructions are particularly valuable for communicating complex spatial relationships to surgical teams [14].

Definitive management of this anomaly requires surgical correction tailored to the underlying anatomy. In patients with Type A pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect (PA-VSD), definitive repair typically involves closure of the ventricular septal defect and establishment of right ventricle-to-pulmonary artery continuity using a valved conduit while preserving the native confluent branch pulmonary arteries [4,5]. In ductus-dependent pulmonary circulation, prostaglandin E1 is administered preoperatively to maintain ductal patency until definitive repair, while ductal stenting or a surgical systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunt may be considered as palliative interventions when appropriate [10,11]. Unfortunately, such specialized congenital cardiac surgical services are currently unavailable in our setting, limiting definitive management and necessitating referral to specialized international cardiac centers whenever feasible.

To our knowledge, this specific co-occurrence has not been previously described in the literature. The coexistence of SIV and PA-VSD complicates surgical planning, since the stacked ventricular arrangement disrupts standard RVOT reconstruction and RV-PA conduit routing [1,2,5]. Absent MAPCAs and confluent, PDA-supplied pulmonary arteries are favorable features that keep both biventricular and univentricular repair options open, pending the feasibility of VSD closure and conduit placement in this anatomy [2,4]. PDA stenting offers a reasonable temporizing strategy given the long, angulated duct, deferring ventriculotomy while preserving pulmonary flow [9,10]. Surgical shunting remains feasible but technically demanding, as the ventricular rotation distorts the normal subclavian-to-pulmonary-artery relationship [2]. Complete unifocalization with RV-PA conduit reconstruction remains the definitive goal, but routing a homograft from the displaced, diaphragmatic RV with no native RVOT will require custom geometric planning [1,2,5].These decisions require close multidisciplinary collaboration between pediatric cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, and radiologists with expertise in congenital cardiac imaging.

Conclusion

This case describes a previously unreported association of superior-inferior ventricular arrangement with pulmonary atresia, ventricular septal defect, and patent ductus arteriosus-dependent pulmonary circulation. Cardiac CT angiography accurately defined the complex anatomy and was indispensable for diagnosis and pre-operative assessment. A systematic segmental approach with multiplanar and three-dimensional reconstructions provides comprehensive anatomical characterization and guides surgical planning in complex congenital heart disease.
