Oral Acitretin Combined with Topical 0.1% Tretinoin Cream for Facial Basal Cell Carcinoma in an Elderly Patient: A Case Report
Authors: Zhirong Shu, Yu Jiang, Shilin Deng, Tingnan Chen, Jingyao Liang, Xibao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBasal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common skin malignancy, and surgical excision remains the first-line treatment for most lesions. However, therapeutic options for elderly patients who are unsuitable for or decline surgery are limited. Acitretin, a systemic retinoid, has shown potential antitumor effects, while topical tretinoin may enhance local therapeutic efficacy. We herein report an elderly patient with facial BCC presenting as an ulcerative lesion on the right forehead. Reflectance confocal microscopy revealed basaloid cells arranged in cord-like structures, and histopathological examination demonstrated nests of basaloid tumor cells with peripheral palisading and retraction clefts, confirming the diagnosis of nodular BCC. The patient was treated with oral acitretin in combination with topical 0.1% tretinoin cream. After 12 weeks of treatment, the ulcerative lesion showed near-complete healing with marked clinical improvement. During the treatment course, only mild adverse events, including skin dryness and cheilitis, were observed, and no significant laboratory abnormalities occurred. This case suggests that oral acitretin combined with topical 0.1% tretinoin cream may represent a feasible and well-tolerated nonsurgical option for selected elderly patients with facial BCC, and no recurrence was observed during the 4-month follow-up.

Introduction

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common cutaneous malignancy, accounting for approximately 75–80% of nonmelanoma skin cancers. It is characterized by indolent growth and low metastatic potential but may exhibit marked local invasiveness.1,2 According to current clinical guidelines, surgical excision remains the first-line treatment for most BCC lesions.3,4 However, in elderly patients, comorbidities, increased perioperative risk, and impaired postoperative wound healing often limit the feasibility of surgery. In addition, surgical procedures involving the head and neck may result in functional impairment and suboptimal cosmetic outcomes. Therefore, effective nonsurgical treatment options are of particular importance for this patient population.

For patients requiring nonsurgical management, several treatment modalities, including topical therapies, photodynamic therapy, radiotherapy, cryotherapy, laser ablation, and Hedgehog pathway inhibitors, may be considered. However, these treatment modalities have limitations in terms of efficacy, adverse effects, cosmetic outcomes, and cost, particularly in elderly patients with facial BCC.4,5

In this context, safer and better-tolerated alternatives are needed. Retinoids are well-established regulators of keratinocyte differentiation and exert antitumor effects by modulating cellular proliferation and differentiation, inducing apoptosis, and inhibiting abnormal keratinization. Recent studies have suggested their potential utility in cutaneous neoplasms and precancerous lesions; however, evidence supporting their use in BCC, particularly in elderly patients, remains limited. In the present case, an elderly patient with facial nodular BCC who declined surgical treatment was treated with oral acitretin combined with topical 0.1% tretinoin cream, resulting in marked clinical improvement with no evidence of recurrence after 4 months of follow-up.

Case Report

The patient was a 94-year-old woman who presented in December 2025 with a 5-year history of a lesion on the right forehead, which had progressively enlarged and ulcerated during the preceding 6 months. Five years earlier, a brown papule measuring approximately 6 mm in diameter developed on the right forehead without an identifiable trigger and was associated with occasional pruritus. No treatment was sought at that time. The lesion gradually enlarged and became progressively exophytic. One year before presentation, it had evolved into a nodule measuring approximately 1.5 cm in diameter, without significant discomfort. During the 6 months prior to presentation, the patient repeatedly applied a homemade herbal medicinal liquor of unknown composition for self-treatment. During this period, the lesion continued to enlarge and developed central ulceration, serous discharge, recurrent bleeding, marked pruritus, and mild tenderness, ultimately prompting her to seek medical attention. Since disease onset, the patient’s general condition remained stable, with no significant changes in appetite, sleep, or body weight. The patient had a history of hypertension for more than 30 years and was receiving oral amlodipine-atorvastatin calcium (5 mg/day), with well-controlled blood pressure. She denied a history of diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, or other significant systemic diseases. Physical examination revealed a 3.0×2.0 cm ulcerative lesion on the right forehead, covered by a dark brown hemorrhagic crust. The lesion was firm in consistency, with rolled borders and well-demarcated margins. Palpation revealed infiltration at the base of the lesion with mild tenderness, and slight bleeding was observed at the center. No regional lymphadenopathy was detected (Figure 1). Reflectance confocal microscopy (RCM) revealed elongated cords of basaloid cells in the superficial dermis with focal peripheral palisading. Dilated and tortuous vessels were observed, accompanied by scattered highly refractile inflammatory cells and pigment aggregates in the perivascular areas (Figure 2C and D). These findings were suggestive of basal cell carcinoma, and histopathological confirmation was recommended. Histopathological examination revealed partial epidermal loss with ulcer formation. In the dermis, multiple basaloid tumor nests were observed, some of which were connected to the overlying epidermis. The tumor cells showed mild-to-moderate atypia, with peripheral palisading and focal stromal retraction clefts. The tumor nests were surrounded by fibrous stroma with inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 2A and B). Based on these findings, a diagnosis of nodular basal cell carcinoma with ulceration was established. Considering the patient’s advanced age and the facial location of the lesion, surgical intervention might have adversely affected cosmetic and functional outcomes. The patient and her family declined surgical treatment; therefore, a nonsurgical therapeutic approach was chosen. After obtaining informed consent, the patient was treated with oral acitretin 20 mg twice daily (approximately 0.8 mg·kg−1·day−1) in combination with topical 0.1% tretinoin cream applied twice daily (total cumulative dose of approximately 120 g over 12 weeks). By week 2, the crust gradually detached. By week 12, the ulcer had largely healed, with marked regression of the lesion. During treatment, mild adverse events, including skin dryness, cheilitis, and desquamation, were observed but did not require treatment discontinuation. Routine laboratory tests, including complete blood count, liver and renal function, and lipid profile, showed no significant abnormalities. Discussion

Although several nonsurgical treatment modalities are available for basal cell carcinoma (BCC), each has limitations when used to treat facial nodular BCC in elderly patients. Topical 5-fluorouracil and imiquimod are approved primarily for the treatment of superficial BCC; however, their efficacy in nodular and more aggressive subtypes is limited, and they are frequently associated with marked local inflammatory reactions. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) is minimally invasive and can be repeated when necessary, but its limited tissue penetration and treatment-related pain may limit tolerability in elderly patients. In selected cases, PDT may also serve as an adjunctive treatment to reduce the risk of recurrence following primary therapy. Radiotherapy may be considered, particularly in older individuals; however, its use is limited by prolonged treatment courses and the potential for adverse effects, including radiation dermatitis, tissue fibrosis, and secondary malignancies. Cryosurgery achieves tumor destruction through repeated freeze–thaw cycles; however, treatment outcomes vary substantially depending on lesion size, tumor depth, and operator experience, and its use is generally limited in cosmetically sensitive areas such as the face. Laser ablation has shown favorable tumor control in small, low-risk BCCs, but evidence supporting its efficacy in larger lesions remains limited. In addition, Hedgehog pathway inhibitors, including vismodegib and sonidegib, are recommended for patients with locally advanced or unresectable BCC; nevertheless, their clinical use may be limited by treatment-related adverse events and high cost.

In the present case, the patient declined surgical intervention because of her advanced age, the facial location of the lesion, and concerns regarding postoperative scarring and cosmetic outcomes. Although vismodegib and sonidegib have demonstrated substantial efficacy in patients with locally advanced or unresectable BCC, our patient’s lesion was localized and did not meet the criteria for advanced disease. Furthermore, their use may be constrained by adverse events and financial burden. Therefore, oral acitretin combined with topical tretinoin was considered a reasonable nonsurgical therapeutic option because of its favorable tolerability, ease of administration, lower cost, and reported efficacy.

From a mechanistic perspective, the development and progression of basal cell carcinoma are primarily driven by aberrant activation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway. The most common molecular mechanisms include loss-of-function mutations in PTCH1 or activating mutations in SMO, which ultimately lead to constitutive activation of GLI transcription factors and uncontrolled proliferation of tumor cells.6,7 Acitretin, a synthetic vitamin A derivative, has been widely used in the prevention and treatment of various keratinization disorders and non-melanoma skin cancers.8,9 Previous studies have demonstrated that retinoids regulate gene transcription through activation of retinoic acid receptors (RARs) and retinoid X receptors (RXRs), thereby promoting cellular differentiation and inhibiting abnormal proliferation. In addition, retinoids have been reported to downregulate GLI1 expression, which may contribute to suppression of Hedgehog signaling. These findings suggest that retinoids may exert antitumor effects partly through modulation of the Hedgehog pathway, providing a theoretical rationale for the therapeutic strategy adopted in the present case.10–12 Topical retinoids primarily act on the epidermis, and their combination with systemic therapy may provide complementary therapeutic effects.

In addition, several studies from China have described elderly patients with basal cell carcinoma who were treated with oral acitretin (0.4–1 mg·kg−1·day−1), either alone or in combination with 0.1% topical tretinoin cream, with favorable clinical outcomes. In these cases, the lesions gradually decreased in size and eventually resolved completely, with no recurrence observed during follow-up.13,14 Consistent with these previous reports, the present case demonstrated marked clinical improvement without serious adverse events.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single case report, the level of evidence is inherently limited. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short. Given the slow-growing nature of basal cell carcinoma, recurrence may occur after a longer interval; therefore, extended follow-up is necessary to evaluate long-term disease control. Third, there is currently no standardized regimen regarding the optimal dosage, duration, or combination strategy of retinoids, and interindividual variability may influence both efficacy and safety. Therefore, further clinical studies are needed to optimize treatment protocols and better define the role of retinoid-based therapy in the management of basal cell carcinoma.

Conclusion

Oral acitretin combined with topical 0.1% tretinoin cream may offer a feasible nonsurgical option for selected patients with basal cell carcinoma. In this case, near-complete clinical resolution was achieved with acceptable tolerability. Further prospective studies with longer follow-up are warranted to confirm the efficacy, safety, and long-term outcomes of this regimen.
