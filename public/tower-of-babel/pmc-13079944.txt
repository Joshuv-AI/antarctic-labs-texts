Predicting and controlling laser-induced breakup and multidirectional propulsion of liquid droplets
Authors: Awanish Pratap Singh, D. Chaitanya Kumar Rao, Maik Rahlves, Alfred Vogel, Saptarshi Basu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceShort laser pulses can push and fragment microscopic liquid droplets, but the motion is difficult to control because the first laser-triggered plasma spark can form at varying locations in or near the droplet. We show that two measurable parameters, the droplet’s offset from the laser focus and the pulse energy, set the spark location and thereby determine whether the droplet moves forward, recoils, or expands mainly radially. Combining high-speed imaging with optical calculations anchored to measured breakdown thresholds and with simulations that track both the liquid and surrounding air, we map stable operating regions and narrow transition bands where outcomes are sensitive. This enables predictive control of laser–droplet processes relevant to manufacturing and biomedicine.

The interaction of laser pulses with liquid droplets is relevant to a broad spectrum of applications, ranging from inkjet printing and laser-induced forward transfer to combustion engines, microsurgery, and nanoscale surface cleaning, as well as related biophotonic/biomedical laser–matter interactions (1–12). Beyond droplet-specific settings, laser-induced plasma recoil and impulse coupling have also been studied extensively in laser-ablation propulsion and micropropulsion, providing broader context for how plasma expansion, plume recoil, and shock loading can generate net momentum transfer to a target (13–15). Advances in pulsed-laser impact experiments, time-resolved imaging, and laser–droplet studies have improved our understanding of droplet deformation, breakup, and propulsion under irradiation (16–24). By contrast with gradual thermal evolution under longer-timescale heating/evaporation conditions (25), nanosecond pulsed irradiation acts on much shorter timescales and can induce optical breakdown, form high-temperature plasma, and generate shock waves that strongly influence droplet deformation, breakup, and subsequent motion (6, 15–17, 22, 26–29).

A central practical challenge is that propulsion direction is often difficult to predict, control, and reproduce, because the first breakdown event may nucleate at different locations in or near the same droplet under nominally similar conditions. Optically transparent droplets act as refractive elements, and a common heuristic is that refraction concentrates intensity near the shadow side (16, 17, 30). However, this shadow-side picture does not always dominate: near-focus placements, refractive-index differences, system aberrations, and changes in incident energy can shift the dominant prebreakdown intensity maxima to the illumination side, into the droplet interior, or into the near-field air immediately behind the droplet on the shadow (downstream) side (hereafter, wake-side air), leading to unexpected propulsion directions and mixed initiation (16–24).

Related studies across laser-pulse impact and laser-driven droplet systems have documented forward, backward, and predominantly radial responses, together with strong placement-, focusing-, and fluid-dependent changes in deformation and breakup morphology across different laser–droplet interaction settings and application contexts (20–24, 30, 31). These observations suggest a unifying view: Directionality is fundamentally an initiation-site selection problem, because whichever location reaches breakdown first defines the geometry of the earliest overpressure source and, therefore, the sign of the axial impulse. Furthermore, the resulting droplet motion reflects how momentum is partitioned among shock loading in the gas and liquid, recoil associated with plasma/vapor expansion, and liquid ejecta driven by ablative recoil or bubble-mediated jetting and sheet formation (13–15, 21, 24, 32–35). Moreover, a purely geometric picture is not universally sufficient across all parameter ranges: Wave-optical structure and nonlinear energy-deposition physics can become important in strongly confined or microsphere-like configurations (36).

Despite the breadth of prior demonstrations, the field still lacks a predictive, experimentally anchored map that links directly controllable parameters to the first-breakdown site and the resulting propulsion polarity. Consequently, selecting a desired direction often relies on qualitative lensing intuition or empirical trial-and-error tuning, and transition regions where multiple initiation sites compete are difficult to anticipate. Here we develop and validate a threshold-referenced framework that separates i) where irradiance localizes before breakdown from ii) the strongly nonlinear energy deposition and hydrodynamic response after breakdown. Specifically, we study laser interaction with an acoustically levitated droplet while controlling the droplet’s axial placement relative to the external (no-droplet) optical focus and the incident pulse energy. Aberration-aware optical simulations determine the prebreakdown irradiance landscape in and around the droplet. By normalizing this landscape with experimentally calibrated breakdown thresholds in the liquid and in near-field air, we obtain a first-crossing criterion that identifies the first breakdown site (illumination surface, interior, shadow surface, or wake-side air) as a function of placement and energy.

We then evaluate these predictions with time-resolved shadowgraphy and interpret early-time deformation using multiphase simulations initialized at the predicted initiation sites. The resulting placement–energy maps capture forward-, backward-, and near-radial responses, and they identify narrow transition corridors where competing initiation sites are nearly degenerate under experimental jitter. The observed morphologies span jellyfish-like deformations and catastrophic sheet-like breakups to more stable, radially biased butterfly-type responses (18, 20, 22–24). These behaviors are analogous to classical impulsive breakup modes of liquid masses (vibrational, bag/umbrella, shear, and catastrophic), with secondary atomization of sheets and ligaments governing fragment yields and timescales (37, 38). Together, these results provide a practical route to selecting droplet propulsion polarity and breakup morphology in laser–droplet interactions, while explicitly delineating where optical modeling is sufficient (prebreakdown localization) and where the physics is inherently nonlinear (postthreshold plasma–shock–cavitation dynamics).

Results

We show that, in and around an acoustically levitated droplet, the initiation site of laser-induced breakdown (LIB), and hence the resulting propulsion direction and breakup morphology, can be selected by droplet placement relative to the external (no-droplet) optical focus as the primary control parameter, together with the incident pulse energy Ei. Our optical model determines the prebreakdown localization of irradiance and the associated first threshold crossing, whereas post-LIB energy deposition and hydrodynamics are inherently nonlinear. In the present regime, with πDd/λ≈O(103) (where Dd is the droplet diameter and λ is the laser wavelength), moderate numerical aperture (NA), and submillimeter propagation through the liquid, prebreakdown maxima; bounds on nonlinear propagation are given in the Discussion.

We first define the coordinate system and the normalized placement parameter xp/Dd (where xp is the droplet-center offset from the external focal position) and present representative outcomes spanning backward, near-radial, and forward responses (Fig. 1). We then link these outcomes to placement-dependent caustic/irradiance localization and early-time initiation signatures (Fig. 2A–D, G, and H). Using experimentally calibrated breakdown thresholds in the liquid and near-field air, we express computed irradiances in threshold-referenced form and formulate a first-crossing criterion that predicts the first LIB site (illumination surface, interior/center, shadow surface, or wake-side near-field air) across the operating plane (xp/Dd,Ei) (Fig. 2E, F, I, and J and SI Appendix, Table S2). We validate the predicted directionality using a polarity metric and a reliability analysis under experimentally relevant uncertainties (Fig. 3 A and B), corroborate the early-time response via experiment–simulation comparisons (Fig. 3 C and D), and interpret regime boundaries using energy-dependent superthreshold contour families (Fig. 4).

Experimental System and Placement Parameter xp/Dd.

We investigate laser–droplet interaction in acoustically levitated droplets positioned along the optical axis of a focusing system (setup in Fig. 5). The droplet center is translated reproducibly along the beam axis using a micrometer stage, enabling precise control of its position relative to the external (no-droplet) optical focus. We define a global axial coordinate x along the laser propagation direction, with +x pointing downstream (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The droplet-center position is denoted xc and the external focal position of the same optical train is xf. The placement is defined as xp≡xc−xf, and is reported in nondimensional form as xp/Dd, where Dd is the droplet diameter (here Dd=400μm). Thus, xp>0 indicates that the droplet center lies downstream of the external focus, xp<0 places it upstream, and xp=0 corresponds to xc=xf. To describe positions relative to the droplet, we introduce the local coordinate x′≡x−xc, so that x′/Dd=0 is the droplet center, while the illumination- and shadow-side interfaces on the optical axis are located at x′/Dd=−1/2 and x′/Dd=+1/2, respectively.

In the absence of the droplet, the focusing optics produce an external focal spot in air with characteristic diameter d0 at x=xf (Fig. 1). When a transparent droplet is placed near this focus, refraction at its curved interfaces reshapes the converging beam and generates a placement-dependent downstream caustic. We characterize this droplet-induced focusing by the droplet-to-caustic distance fbd and an effective wake-side air spot diameter dair associated with the strongest downstream irradiance maximum (Fig. 1, A and D). For later analysis, we track characteristic beam diameters at four droplet-tied sampling planes (Fig. 1C): illumination-side surface (din), droplet center (dc), shadow-side surface (dout), and wake-side near-field air (dair) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

At each placement, early-time imaging localizes the LIB initiation site(s), while later-time shadowgraphy quantifies deformation, breakup morphology, and center-of-liquid-mass (COLM) motion along the optical axis. Fig. 1B provides representative sequences for three placements at fixed incident energy, illustrating backward-biased, near-radial, and forward-biased responses (Movie S1). On the converging side (xp/Dd=−4.5), the response is strongly one-sided, producing a jellyfish-like morphology (cap/sheet with trailing filaments) together with net COLM propulsion in the −x direction. Near the focal region (xp/Dd=−0.5), competing, near-balanced loading produces a butterfly-like two-lobed sheet with weak net axial polarity. On the diverging side (xp/Dd=+0.5), the response again becomes one-sided and jellyfish-like, but with the propulsion direction reversed, consistent with a shift of the dominant impulse application to the opposite interface as placement crosses the focal region.

Placement-Controlled Initiation Topology and Resulting Propulsion/Breakup Outcomes.

Varying droplet placement xp/Dd changes where LIB initiates and thereby the propulsion direction and breakup morphology. Fig. 2A–D summarizes this dependence for a representative case (pentanol, n=1.41, Ei=4mJ) by combining i) optical-model context, ii) early-time multiphase simulations, and iii) experimental shadowgraphy at matched delays across discrete placements. Initiation signatures at Δt∼20 to 750ns are shown alongside the later-time outcome at Δt=25μs to make the initiation–outcome link explicit.

The earliest response is plasma/shock-dominated (tens to hundreds of nanoseconds), whereas cavity growth and sheet formation emerge later, when transmitted and reflected waves generate tensile stresses within the liquid. Bulk prebreakdown heating by linear absorption is negligible on these timescales (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Consistent with a plasma-driven thermomechanical (impulsive) mechanism, appreciable motion and shape change occur on microsecond timescales (Fig. 1B), while intermediate cavitation primarily modulates deformation and fragmentation dynamics (Movie S1). Once local irradiance exceeds the breakdown threshold, seed electrons trigger avalanche ionization (28, 29), leading to rapid plasma formation. Experimentally, LIB is evidenced by a bright flash and a concomitant acoustic emission near the initiation site (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4), supporting that propulsion is driven by the rapid mechanical impulse associated with plasma formation and shock launch.

In the absence of external forces, the total momentum of the coupled droplet–gas system is conserved, and we are interested in the resulting net axial COLM motion of the droplet. COLM is governed by how momentum is partitioned among: i) air blast-wave components propagating in opposite directions, ii) the rapidly expanding vapor/plasma plume, and iii) liquid ejecta in the splash/jet produced by ablative recoil or by plasma-driven bubble expansion inside the droplet (15, 21, 24, 33). The relative importance of these contributions depends on droplet placement relative to the focus and thus on where optical energy is deposited with respect to the interfaces and wake-side air.

For each placement, we assign an initiation topology based on early-time plasma/shock/breakup signatures. The classification is summarized by the pastel background bands in Fig. 2D. Principal archetypes are shadow-side surface breakdown (LIB near x′/Dd≈+1/2; class B), illumination-side surface breakdown (LIB near x′/Dd≈−1/2; class F), internal breakdown (bubble nucleation in the interior, including near-center x′/Dd≈0), and wake-side near-field air breakdown immediately behind the droplet (x′/Dd>+1/2; class A). Air breakdown appears most clearly together with shadow-side surface initiation (class B&A). Mixed or multisite outcomes (M-I through M-IV) occur when the breakdown threshold is exceeded at two or more locations within the laser pulse, producing competing recoil contributions. These cases tend to exhibit predominantly radial sheet expansion with an axial bias determined by the dominant side: Shadow-dominant mixed cases (M-I and M-IV) show a backward bias, the near-balanced mixed case (M-II) exhibits nearly symmetric radial expansion with weak COLM, and the illumination-dominant mixed case (M-III) shows a forward bias. A polarity metric quantifying COLM for the different cases is introduced below.

Overall, the sign of the net axial impulse associated with the motion of the main liquid mass is set by the geometry of the dominant initiation. Illumination-side initiation generally produces forward COLM propulsion (+x), whereas shadow-side initiation produces backward COLM propulsion (−x). When both sides initiate with comparable strength, axial impulses can partially cancel, yielding predominantly radial expansion with COLM motion close to zero. Internal breakdown often reduces axial bias, while still producing strong radial deformation via cavity growth and sheet ejection.

Placement-Dependent Caustics and Threshold-Referenced Irradiance Amplification.

The initiation regimes described above arise from how a transparent droplet reshapes the focused beam and redistributes optical energy relative to the droplet interfaces and the near-field air on the shadow side. Fig. 2A provides geometric context: aberration-aware ray tracing through the full optical train indicates that droplet-induced caustics and multiple axial maxima can arise as xp/Dd is varied. The rays are shown for geometric intuition only; initiation-site prediction is based on the computed irradiance field (SI Appendix, section ST3). In particular, a 2D projection of 3D ray paths can suggest apparent “ray-density” maxima that do not coincide with irradiance maxima. Continuous placement-sweep visualizations, including a synchronized optics overview and droplet-centered irradiance localization, are provided in Movie S2; complementary droplet-diameter sweeps at fixed normalized placement are provided in Movie S3.

To connect the optical field directly to breakdown propensity, we evaluate the peak irradiance at four droplet-tied sampling locations: the illumination surface (x′/Dd=−1/2), the droplet center (x′/Dd=0), the shadow surface (x′/Dd=+1/2), and the wake-side near-field air region immediately behind the droplet (x′/Dd>+1/2). For each placement, these peak values are plotted in threshold-referenced form as I/Ith,ℓ, where Ith,ℓ is the experimentally calibrated liquid breakdown threshold (Fig. 2 E and F). The calibrated 50% thresholds are Ith,ℓ≈2.97×109 W cm−2 for water and 1.47×109 W cm−2 for pentanol, while the near-field air threshold is Ith,air≈1.07×1011 W cm−2. Under the 6-ns Gaussian-pulse convention used throughout, the corresponding fluence is Fth=Ithτpπ4ln2, where τp=6ns is the pulse FWHM. Under this fixed pulse convention, I/Ith≡F/Fth, so the same threshold-normalized maps can be read equivalently as fluence margins. The resulting fluence thresholds are Fth,ℓ≈19Jcm−2 for water, ≈9.4 Jcm−2 for pentanol, and Fth,air≈680Jcm−2 (SI Appendix, Table S2).

These values are setup-specific operational (50%) LIB thresholds for the present wavelength, pulse duration, focusing/aberration state, and detection criterion, and are used here primarily as internally consistent calibration anchors for threshold-referenced initiation-site ordering; calibration details and a brief comparability discussion are provided in SI Appendix, section ST2. The calibrated values are consistent in order of magnitude with reported nanosecond-laser breakdown thresholds under comparable focusing and detection conditions, while quantitative differences across studies are expected because LIB thresholds depend strongly on pulse duration, wavelength, focal geometry/NA, medium condition, and the operational onset criterion (4, 7, 27–29).

For direct comparison between liquid- and air-side initiation within a single normalization, the air-breakdown threshold is shown on the same axis via the horizontal reference ratio Ith,air/Ith,ℓ. Values above unity (or above Ith,air/Ith,ℓ for the near-field air location) identify candidate sites capable of triggering LIB at that (xp/Dd,Ei). These threshold scales govern initiation onset and site selection; however, they do not determine postthreshold energy partitioning, because plasma formation rapidly alters absorption and the effective permittivity during and immediately after threshold crossing. For pentanol (Fig. 2E), different candidate locations exceed threshold over different placement intervals: Shadow-side surface exceedance dominates at large negative placements, interior/near-center exceedance emerges over intermediate placements, and illumination-side surface exceedance becomes dominant at positive placements. Water (Fig. 2F) exhibits the same qualitative structure but with reduced amplification and shifted crossing intervals due to its lower refractive index (n=1.33), consistent with weaker lensing and an altered caustic location.

To visualize how lensing relocates and concentrates irradiance relative to the droplet, Fig. 2 G and H shows axial distributions I(x′,xp)/Ith,ℓ as functions of local coordinate x′/Dd and placement xp/Dd for pentanol and water. These distributions show that (i) the dominant maximum can migrate between the illumination-side surface, the droplet interior, the shadow-side surface, and wake-side air as xp/Dd changes, and (ii) localized caustics can strongly amplify irradiance relative to the illumination-side value. We quantify this amplification using the lensing gain G(xp)≡Imax(xp)/Iillu(xp), where Imax(xp) is the maximum irradiance in the droplet/wake-side region and Iillu(xp) is the irradiance at the illumination-side surface at the same placement.

The threshold-referenced plane-wise intensities in Fig. 2 E and F provide the operational basis for predicting the first LIB site: For each (xp/Dd,Ei), we compare the four candidate locations using the appropriate liquid or air threshold and identify the location that first becomes superthreshold as Ei is increased. Below, we formalize this procedure as a first-crossing criterion and use it to construct first-LIB-site maps over the (xp/Dd,Ei) plane (Fig. 2 I and J).

First-Crossing Criterion and (xp/Dd,Ei) Maps of Breakdown-Site Selection.

The threshold-normalized intensities in Fig. 2E–H define a compact rule for predicting the first LIB site as a function of placement and incident energy. For the four candidate locations defined above (two interfaces, the droplet interior, and wake-side near-field air), we compute the peak irradiance and compare it to the appropriate breakdown threshold (liquid or air; SI Appendix, Table S2).

Let Ik(xp,Ei) denote the peak irradiance at candidate location k. We define a threshold-exceedance factor[1]Sk(xp,Ei)≡Ik(xp,Ei)/Ith,ℓ,k∈L,Ik(xp,Ei)/Ith,air,k∈A,

where L denotes liquid-associated locations (illumination-side surface, interior, shadow-side surface) and A denotes air-associated locations. In the present work, the air candidate evaluated in the optical postprocessing and shown in Fig. 2E–H corresponds to wake-side near-field air behind the droplet (A≡Ab). Implementation details (candidate-region sampling, near-degenerate cases, and uncertainty propagation) are provided in SI Appendix, section ST4. For a fixed placement, Ik(xp,Ei) scales approximately monotonically with Ei in the prebreakdown regime, so each candidate admits a crossing energy at which it reaches threshold. We define the first-crossing energy for each candidate as[2]Ek∗(xp)≡ min{Ei:Sk(xp,Ei)≥1},

and assign the predicted first LIB site as the candidate with the smallest crossing energy,[3]k∗(xp)≡ arg minkEk∗(xp).

Equivalently, at a fixed operating point (xp,Ei) one may evaluate all Sk and assign initiation to the location with the largest superthreshold margin; the two views coincide under monotonic scaling.

Applying Eq. 3 over the explored placement and energy ranges yields the predicted first-LIB-site maps in Fig. 2 I and J for pentanol and water. The maps summarize how placement-dependent lensing and caustic migration partition the (xp/Dd,Ei) plane into regions where initiation is expected on the illumination-side surface, in the droplet interior, on the shadow-side surface, or in wake-side near-field air (and regions where no threshold crossing occurs within the explored range). Differences between pentanol and water reflect refractive-index dependence of lensing strength and caustic location, consistent with Fig. 2 F and H.

The first-crossing criterion predicts a single first-breakdown site. Mixed or multisite outcomes arise when two (or more) candidates become superthreshold at nearly the same incident energy, such that a second site can be triggered shortly after the first. Such near-degenerate conditions cluster near boundaries between single-site regions in Fig. 2 I and J and motivate the continuous polarity and robustness analysis introduced below.

A Polarity Index for Propulsion Direction and Predictive Robustness.

The first-LIB-site analysis above yields a discrete predicted initiation site at each droplet placement xp/Dd and incident energy Ei. To connect initiation topology to experimentally observed COLM motion along the laser axis (+x), we introduce a polarity metric Π∈[−1,1] that quantifies whether initiation is biased toward a forward-driving or backward-driving pressure source.

Because air-side breakdown can, in principle, occur either upstream of the droplet (Af) or downstream in the wake (Ab), we distinguish these air locations explicitly in the polarity definition. The sign convention follows the location of the overpressure source relative to the droplet: Upstream air breakdown produces a forward-directed push (+x), whereas wake-side air breakdown produces a backward-directed push (−x).

For each operating point (xp/Dd,Ei) we classify the first observed initiation event into categories s∈{F,B,Af,Ab,Int,M}, corresponding to illumination-side surface breakdown (F), shadow-side surface breakdown (B), illumination-side air breakdown (Af, x′/Dd<−1/2), wake-side near-field air breakdown (Ab, x′/Dd>+1/2), internal/center breakdown (Int), and mixed/multisite sequences (M). From N repeat experiments at a given operating point, we estimate empirical probabilities[4]P^exp(s; xp,Ei)≡NsN,∑sP^exp(s; xp,Ei)=1,

where Ns is the number of trials whose first initiation event belongs to class s. We define the experimental polarity as a signed imbalance between forward-driving and backward-driving initiation,[5]Πexp(xp,Ei)≡P^exp(F; xp,Ei)+P^exp(Af; xp,Ei)−P^exp(B; xp,Ei)−P^exp(Ab; xp,Ei).

With this convention, Π=−1 corresponds to purely backward-driving initiation and Π=+1 to purely forward-driving initiation. Internal/center breakdown and mixed/multisite outcomes are treated as polarity-neutral; they reduce |Π| by occupying probability mass without imposing a consistent axial bias. In the present experiments, air-side LIB was observed only in wake-side near-field air (Ab); illumination-side air breakdown (Af) was not detected over the explored (xp/Dd,Ei) range. Accordingly, P^exp(Af)≈0, and Eq. 5 reduces to[6]Πexp(xp,Ei)≈P^exp(F; xp,Ei)−P^exp(B; xp,Ei)−P^exp(Ab; xp,Ei).

To obtain a predictive polarity from the optical–threshold model, we compute model probabilities Pmod(s; xp,Ei) by propagating experimentally relevant uncertainties in droplet placement and pulse energy. Specifically, we draw NMC Monte Carlo realizations[7]xp⋆=xp+δx,Ei⋆=Ei(1+δE),

with δx and δE sampled from the measured jitter/uncertainty distributions (implemented here as Gaussian perturbations with SDs matching the experiment). For each realization we apply the first-crossing rule and assign the corresponding initiation class s. This yields[8]Pmod(s; xp,Ei)≡1NMC∑k=1NMC1sk=s,Πmod(xp,Ei)≡Pmod(F)+Pmod(Af)−Pmod(B)−Pmod(Ab),

where 1[·] is the indicator function. Further implementation details are provided in SI Appendix, section ST4.

A complementary quantity for interpreting regime boundaries is the reliability of the outcome at each operating point. We define experimental and model reliabilities as the maximum class probability,[9]Rexp(xp,Ei)≡maxsP^exp(s; xp,Ei),Rmod(xp,Ei)≡maxsPmod(s; xp,Ei),

so that R≃1 indicates a robust (repeatable) outcome, while reduced R flags intrinsically sensitive operating points where modest perturbations in xp or Ei can change the first LIB site.

Fig. 3 summarizes polarity and reliability landscapes for pentanol and water. The maps Πexp(xp,Ei) and Πmod(xp,Ei) (Fig. 3 A and B) show that the model captures broad shadow-dominant regions and narrower placement windows where forward-driving initiation emerges as lensing and aberration-shaped caustics shift the superthreshold region toward the illumination-side surface. Parity plots quantify agreement between experiment and model. The reliability maps identify robust regions where a single site has a large threshold margin and narrow transition corridors where competing sites attain comparable margins; these corridors coincide with Π≈0 and predominantly radial responses with weak net COLM motion.

To corroborate that correct first-site prediction captures the relevant early-time dynamics, Fig. 3C compares experiment and multiphase simulations at matched delays for a representative case, showing agreement in plasma/shock morphology and the resulting deformation topology (Movie S5). Fig. 3D organizes the observations into a stage-resolved sequence linking initiation to recoil loading, cavitation, and breakup.

Regime Boundaries from Energy-Dependent Superthreshold Contours.

The analysis above shows that first-LIB-site selection, and thus the sign of the axial impulse, is governed by where the irradiance field first exceeds the appropriate breakdown threshold. To make the spatial origin of these transitions explicit, we map the regions of the optical field that are superthreshold in the liquid and in the surrounding air as functions of droplet placement xp/Dd and incident energy Ei. Fig. 4 provides this field-level backbone: Regime boundaries correspond to parameter values at which a superthreshold region first appears (or changes connectivity) in a specific subdomain (liquid versus air) and location (illumination-side surface, interior, shadow-side surface, or wake-side air).

For each placement, the optical model yields the peak irradiance distribution in a meridional plane, I(x′,r; xp,Ei), where x′ is the local axial coordinate and r is the transverse radial coordinate. To match the representation in Fig. 4, we define the plotted (transverse-maximum) field as[10]I(x′,xp; Ei)≡ maxrI(x′,r; xp,Ei),

and normalize it by the liquid breakdown threshold, I~(x′,xp; Ei)≡I(x′,xp; Ei)/Ith,ℓ. In Fig. 4, solid contours denote the liquid threshold I~=1 (inside the droplet) and dashed contours denote the air threshold I~=Ith,air/Ith,ℓ (in the surrounding gas). The background colormap shows log10(I~(x′,xp; Eref)) at a fixed reference energy Eref=4mJ, while the overlaid contour families show how superthreshold domains expand and migrate with Ei.

As Ei increases from subthreshold conditions, the first superthreshold region appears either on a droplet surface, in the interior, or in wake-side near-field air, consistent with the candidate sites used in the first-crossing maps (Fig. 2 I and J). Because the droplet acts as a refractive element, small changes in xp/Dd can shift the caustic and alter superthreshold connectivity, producing narrow placement windows where the dominant site switches; these switches correspond to polarity transitions and reduced reliability where competing lobes attain similar threshold margins.

Comparing pentanol and water in Fig. 4 highlights the role of refractive index and threshold in setting superthreshold topology. Water’s lower refractive index reduces lensing amplification relative to pentanol, shifting and reducing superthreshold regions and narrowing the placement window over which forward-driving initiation occurs, consistent with Figs. 2 and 3.

Discussion

The results show that multidirectional propulsion and distinct breakup morphologies can be interpreted—and, over broad operating windows, predicted—from where the prebreakdown irradiance localizes when a transparent droplet lenses an aberration-limited focused beam. The key modeling distinction is that optics is used to determine where irradiance first becomes superthreshold (first-LIB-site selection), whereas the breakdown event and subsequent response are intrinsically nonlinear (plasma formation, shock launching, and cavitation). We interpret the regime maps in terms of initiation topology and impulse partition, explain the transition corridors identified by the reliability analysis, clarify the optical origins of sharp placement-dependent switching, and discuss bounds, limitations, and transferability.

Initiation Topology, Impulse Partition, and Propulsion Polarity.

The polarity landscapes in Fig. 3 indicate that the sign of the COLM motion along the optical axis is governed primarily by the location of the dominant LIB event relative to the droplet. Breakdown produces a rapidly expanding plasma that drives an intense, short-lived overpressure source, launching a blast wave in the surrounding gas and a compressive wave transmitted into the liquid. The imparted impulse can be decomposed into an axial component (which sets the propulsion direction) and a radial component (which sets overall expansion, sheet formation, and subsequent breakup).

A useful conceptual link is the net axial impulse, Jx≡∫0T∫∂Ωℓp(x,t)nx(x)dAdt, where p is the pressure acting on the liquid boundary ∂Ωℓ, nx is the axial component of the outward unit normal, and T spans the strong-loading stage (nanoseconds to a few microseconds). For a better understanding of the propulsion mechanisms in the different initiation topologies, we first discuss the factors governing the size and shape of the plasma formed within the droplet, at its surface, and in air, and then deduce the resulting impulse. Plasma formation within the droplet starts at the laser beam waist and is followed by a breakdown wave moving upstream toward the incoming pulse until it reaches its maximum extent. Afterward, energy is deposited mainly into the already existing plasma (26, 29). Optical aberrations in the laser beam path, such as the spherical aberrations associated with focusing into a droplet, increase the breakdown threshold and enlarge the plasma region (39). Light reflection at the rear surface may lead to additional modifications of the plasma shape (40). These mechanisms explain the large size of the luminescent region in Fig. 2C. When the highest prebreakdown irradiance is located close to a droplet surface, plasma formation starts at the surface itself because the breakdown threshold in air is much higher than in water (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) and is usually lower at the surface of transparent dielectrics than in their bulk. However, once ignited in water, plasma formation can spread into the surrounding air because UV bremsstrahlung radiation emitted by hot electrons produces seed electrons that initiate gas breakdown (26, 29). Due to the lowering of the breakdown threshold in a radiation-ionization wave, plasma formation in air can extend to a fairly large distance from the droplet surface, as seen in Fig. 3C.

Plasma formation in the bulk of the liquid droplet leads to the rapid expansion of a cavitation bubble followed by droplet breakup (21, 24, 34), with little net axial center-of-liquid-mass motion. COLM is typically much larger when plasma is produced at the droplet surface and partially extends into the liquid. In that case, three components contribute to momentum transfer to the main liquid mass: the air shock (32), the directional movement of the rapidly expanding ablation plume from the plasma (13–15, 33), and the splash of ejected material produced by ablative recoil (15, 33, 35). The relative weights of these contributions depend on whether initiation is single-site or multisite within the pulse and on whether the plasma remains confined to the liquid or spreads into the adjacent gas. Consistently, COLM is typically largest for single breakdown sites on either the illumination-side or shadow-side surface, while predominantly gas-phase (wake-side) breakdown tends to produce weaker coupling to the main liquid mass. Within this view, the polarity metric Π compactly reflects whether the dominant pressure/recoil source is biased toward the illumination side or the shadow side, and therefore whether the net axial impulse drives propulsion along +x or −x.

Sensitivity Corridors and Repeatability: Interpreting Reliability from Competing Thresholds.

A prominent feature of the polarity landscapes is the presence of transition corridors where Π≈0 and reliability R is reduced (Fig. 3 A and B). These corridors follow directly from the optical–threshold interpretation: They occur where two or more candidate locations have comparable margins relative to their respective breakdown thresholds, so modest perturbations in droplet placement or incident energy can change the ordering of first crossings and therefore the observed initiation topology. In other words, reduced reliability is a quantitative signature of intrinsic sensitivity arising from near-degenerate competing initiation sites under experimentally relevant jitter.

This can be made explicit using the threshold-exceedance factors {Sk(xp,Ei)} (Eq. 1) evaluated at the droplet-tied candidate locations. When one location has a clearly dominant exceedance margin, the predicted outcome is insensitive to perturbations and R→1 (Eq. 9). When two locations are nearly tied, small changes in xp or Ei can swap the ordering that defines the first LIB site (Eq. 3), and the observed outcome varies across repeats. This mechanism explains why trial-to-trial variability concentrates within narrow placement windows rather than being uniformly distributed across the parameter map.

The same near-degeneracy explains why sensitivity corridors often coincide with near-radial responses. When two initiation sites compete, axial recoil contributions can fluctuate in sign and magnitude across repeats, while the radial component remains consistently large because the overall deposited energy (and thus pressure loading) is substantial. As a result, the droplet can undergo strong radial expansion and sheet formation even when net COLM motion along x is small and less repeatable. In this sense, R provides a threshold-based measure of repeatability without case-by-case relabeling: It highlights where outcome selection is intrinsically sensitive because the initiating field lies close to a boundary between competing threshold-dominant locations.

Practically, the reliability maps provide a direct operating guide. Deep inside single-site regions (high R), propulsion direction and morphology are reproducible under the measured σx and σE. Operating near boundaries yields richer phenomenology but reduced predictability unless placement/energy jitter is reduced. Thus, the reliability maps complement the regime maps by indicating not only which outcome is expected, but where that outcome is robust to realistic perturbations.

Role of Optical Aberrations and Droplet-Lensing Amplification in Regime Switching.

The observed regime switching with millimeter-scale shifts in placement xp follows from the droplet acting as a refractive element embedded in a strongly converging, aberration-limited beam (39). Two optical ingredients are central: Droplet-induced refraction that reshapes the converging wavefront and produces placement-dependent caustics, and system aberrations that distribute energy among multiple competing maxima whose relative strengths evolve with xp.

First, the droplet introduces curved refracting interfaces that generate a placement-dependent caustic structure (16, 17, 30). As shown by the axial distributions in Fig. 2 G and H, the location of the strongest irradiance maximum can migrate between the illumination-side surface, the droplet interior/near-center, the shadow-side surface, and wake-side near-field air as xp/Dd is varied. This caustic migration provides the geometric basis for initiation-site selection: For fixed xp, the first LIB site is the candidate that first becomes superthreshold as energy is increased (equivalently, has the largest superthreshold margin at fixed energy), consistent with Eq. 3.

Second, the focusing is aberration-limited: The optical train does not produce a single ideal focus but rather a structured field in which secondary maxima can compete with the dominant caustic. Accounting for measured aberrations is therefore essential for accurately predicting irradiance-maxima locations and reproducing the narrow placement windows over which polarity transitions occur (39). In particular, aberrations can elevate secondary maxima, widen sensitivity corridors, and enable rapid switching when two candidate locations have comparable threshold margins.

The lensing gain G(xp) provides a compact measure of how strongly the droplet concentrates optical energy relative to the illumination-side intensity. Large G corresponds to sharply concentrated caustics and therefore to lower energies required for threshold crossing at the dominant site. However, G should be interpreted as a predictor of threshold crossing and first-site selection, not of postbreakdown energy partition, because plasma formation rapidly alters absorption and local optical properties after threshold is reached (28, 29).

The comparison between liquids reinforces the optical origin of regime switching. Water’s lower refractive index reduces lensing strength and shifts caustic locations, narrowing the placement window over which forward-driving initiation occurs and shifting regime boundaries in the (xp/Dd,Ei) plane (Figs. 2F and H and 3B). This refractive-index dependence also points to transferability: For other transparent liquids, changes in n together with the relevant breakdown thresholds are expected to shift the same underlying caustic-driven structure, while the aberration state of the optical train determines how sharply switching occurs and how wide the sensitivity corridors become.

Can Nonlinear Propagation Reshape the Caustic Before Breakdown?

A natural question is whether nonlinear optical propagation inside the droplet (Kerr self-action and nonlinear absorption) could significantly reshape the prebreakdown caustic and thereby alter first-site selection. Here we bound these effects using standard criteria over the available interaction length in the liquid, which is limited to a submillimeter optical path (L∼Dd) before the beam exits the droplet (36).

A first check is Kerr self-focusing. For a Kerr medium, the critical power is Pcr≈0.15λ2n0n2 (for a Gaussian beam). Using conservative ranges for n2 and initiation-relevant intensities, the pulse peak power in our experiments is O(MW) and remains below, or at most comparable to, the nominal Kerr self-focusing critical power Pcr over the operating range emphasized here. Moreover, the short interaction length further limits nonlinear self-action. A more direct measure of whether Kerr effects can reorder the placement-dependent caustic maxima is the nonlinear phase accumulation (B-integral), B=k0n2IL, with k0=2π/λ. Evaluated at initiation-relevant intensities, including the largest modeled prebreakdown maxima, we obtain B≪1 (typically ≲10−1). Thus, Kerr self-action is perturbative on the droplet scale and is not expected to change the ordering of candidate-site threshold margins. Consistently, the Kerr-induced index change Δn=n2I remains negligible compared with the static droplet–air index contrast and is therefore too small to appreciably alter the droplet lensing geometry prior to LIB.

Nonlinear absorption can be bounded using the two-photon-absorption length LTPA=(βI)−1 and the fractional attenuation ΔI/I∼βIL. Even using conservative upper bounds for β, attenuation over L∼Dd remains modest at initiation-relevant intensities and therefore is not expected to establish a strongly modified propagation channel that would reorganize the caustic prior to the first threshold crossing.

Together, these bounds indicate that nonlinear propagation is unlikely to reorder the candidate-site threshold margins underlying Eq. 3. This operating regime also differs from microsphere/high-NA configurations where wave-optical interference and resonances can dominate field structure (36). Full parameter ranges and the resulting bounds are provided in SI Appendix, section ST6.

Scope, Limitations, and Transferability of the Framework.

The present framework is intentionally minimal: It predicts initiation-site selection by combining a prebreakdown optical model (aberration-aware ray tracing) with experimentally calibrated breakdown thresholds in the liquid and in the near-field air (SI Appendix, Table S2). This provides an interpretable map from the primary experimental control parameters xp/Dd and Ei to initiation topology and propulsion direction, while leaving several aspects of the coupled laser–plasma–fluid system outside scope.

First, the first-crossing criterion (Eq. 3) treats breakdown onset as a threshold-triggered transition and does not model plasma growth, plasma shielding, or time-dependent absorption during the laser pulse (26–29). These processes influence deposited energy and the pressure–time history, and they would be required to predict absolute impulse magnitude or detailed fragment statistics. However, the results here indicate that for predicting directionality and coarse initiation topology, the dominant requirement is correct localization of the first superthreshold site, because that location sets the geometry (and thus the sign) of the early-time axial impulse.

Second, the approach assumes that prebreakdown peak irradiance at each candidate location scales approximately monotonically with incident energy for a fixed optical configuration, consistent with the optical field calculations used to construct the regime maps. Near and above threshold, evolving plasma absorption and scattering can modify the effective field and may sharpen or smear transitions; this motivates the reliability analysis (Eq. 9), which explicitly identifies operating points where the outcome is intrinsically sensitive because competing threshold margins are near-degenerate under the measured σx and σE.

Third, the model evaluates threshold crossing at a small set of droplet-tied candidate sites (the four sites defined in Results). This set captures the dominant switching observed here; if future measurements identify systematic off-axis initiation, the candidate set can be extended by applying the same threshold-referenced criterion (Eq. 1) to off-axis locations in the computed field.

Transferability across transparent liquids follows naturally from the optical origin of the switching: For a given optical train, the caustic geometry depends primarily on refractive index, while the onset of LIB depends on breakdown thresholds for the relevant pulse duration and focusing conditions (SI Appendix, Table S2). The pentanol–water comparison demonstrates this refractive-index dependence directly, and the same workflow can be applied to other transparent liquids provided n and the relevant thresholds are calibrated. Transferability across optical configurations is also expected, but quantitative boundaries depend on aberrations, which can create multiple competing maxima and widen sensitivity corridors; aberration-aware modeling or experimental beam characterization is therefore important when precise regime maps are desired (39).

A practically important limiting case is an optically absorbing/opaque droplet, where the optical penetration depth is small compared with Dd and internal refraction-driven caustics are suppressed because energy is deposited predominantly near the illumination-side surface (10, 31). In this limit, initiation is expected to be pinned near x′/Dd≈−1/2 over a broad range of xp/Dd, and the multisite map reduces to a surface-trigger condition governed primarily by the local incident fluence/irradiance at the front interface (with, where relevant, an additional near-field air/plume criterion). Placement can still matter through defocus and aberrations that modulate the effective surface spot size and peak irradiance, but not through lensing-driven reordering of interior versus shadow-side maxima. This absorbing/opaque limit is therefore expected to exhibit less switch-like initiation behavior with respect to xp/Dd than the transparent-droplet cases studied here.

Finally, the analysis above clarifies when linear optics may cease to be sufficient for prebreakdown localization. Ultrashort pulses (fs–ps) can access strong Kerr dynamics and filamentation; substantially higher peak powers or tighter focusing can bring P closer to Pcr; and smaller droplets or microspheres with χ=2πR/λ≈O(10) may require wave-optical modeling where interference and resonances dominate (36). Within the present regime (nanosecond pulses, πDd/λ≈2.4×103, moderate NA, and submillimeter propagation length in the liquid), the ray-tracing-plus-threshold framework provides a practical and experimentally validated pathway to predict initiation-site selection, polarity, and robustness from droplet placement relative to the external focus. In summary, the central outcome of this work is a self-contained, threshold-referenced map that connects an experimentally controllable placement parameter and incident pulse energy to initiation-site selection, thereby enabling predictable multidirectional propulsion and breakup. The map also explicitly identifies robust operating windows and intrinsically sensitive transition corridors.

Materials and Methods

Experimental.

Experiments were performed on single acoustically levitated droplets using a single-axis acoustic levitator (tec5) driven at 100kHz (Fig. 5A). The levitator was mounted above a micrometer translation stage that translated the droplet center along the optical axis to control the placement parameter xp (stage not shown in the side-view schematic of Fig. 5A). The pressure node held nominally spherical droplets with diameter Dd≈400μm, measured in real time from the back-lit silhouette using the calibrated pixel scale. Laboratory conditions were maintained at T = 20 ± 1 °C and 40 to 50% relative humidity, with air currents minimized by draft shielding. Droplets were dispensed using calibrated micropipettes and inspected by back-lighting prior to levitation to exclude visible inclusions. Unless otherwise stated, the data focus on deionized water and 1-pentanol as transparent liquids with distinct refractive indices at 532nm (n=1.33 for water and n=1.41 for pentanol) (SI Appendix, Table S1).

A Q-switched Nd:YAG laser (λ=532nm) was operated in single-pulse mode with a pulse duration of τp=6ns (FWHM). The beam was expanded using a telescope and delivered to the droplet via a series of mirrors and final focusing optics (Fig. 5B). The external focal position, xf, and spot size in air were independently characterized using burn-paper trials for spatial localization and camera-based measurements for quantitative validation (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Table S1). Burn-paper measurements (n=25) yielded an external spot diameter of d0m=51.94±2.97μm, in close agreement with camera-based measurements (n=12), which gave 49.45±1.24μm. On this basis, a nominal spot diameter of d0≈50μm was adopted for all intensity estimates and optical-field calculations. The observed spot size exceeds the diffraction-limited prediction due to system aberrations and nonideal beam quality; accordingly, reported intensities are referenced to this measured spot size.

Pulse energy was monitored shot-by-shot using a beam splitter and two calibrated pyroelectric meters. Energy Meter-1 defined the incident energy Ei used throughout the regime maps, while Energy Meter-2 monitored transmitted energy downstream of the droplet. Meter-scale uncertainty (±2%) and shot-to-shot energy jitter (σE≃5% RMS) were propagated into robustness analyses (Fig. 3) (SI Appendix, Table S1 and section ST4). Droplet placement was scanned along the optical axis in 200μm increments over the range −3,200μm≤xp≤+2,400μm, where xp≡xc−xf. Stage repeatability was ±5 μm and placement jitter was σx≃10μm, defining the uncertainty envelope used in model–experiment comparisons (SI Appendix, Table S1). The droplet was maintained on-axis by centering its silhouette and verifying symmetry under weak alignment pulses. Initiation and early hydrodynamics were recorded using time-resolved shadowgraphy (Fig. 5 A and B). A pulsed diode laser at λ=644nm provided back-illumination, with scattered green light suppressed by a bandpass filter. Images were recorded with a high-speed camera using 50ns exposure and a scale of 9.98μm/pixel. Synchronization was controlled by a digital delay generator (SI Appendix, Table S1).

Initiation sites were identified from the earliest plasma or shock signatures (Δt=20 to 750ns) and classified as illumination-side surface, interior/near-center, shadow-side surface, or near-field air behind the droplet; simultaneous triggering yielded a mixed classification (SI Appendix, section ST1). The near-field air candidate was evaluated in the wake-side air region immediately behind the shadow surface as the location of the maximum predicted air irradiance (SI Appendix, section ST3). For each operating point (xp,Ei), repeated single-pulse trials (N≥10) were performed to estimate initiation probabilities, which were then used to compute polarity and reliability metrics (SI Appendix, section ST1). Liquid and near-field air breakdown thresholds, Ith,ℓ and Ith,air, were determined experimentally under identical pulse and focusing conditions. These thresholds provide the normalization used throughout the regime maps and site-selection criteria (SI Appendix, Table S2). Threshold-referenced normalization (I/Ith), equivalently F/Fth under our Gaussian-pulse convention, connects prebreakdown field calculations to the onset of strongly nonlinear LIB energy deposition. The threshold-calibration procedure and associated uncertainty are provided in SI Appendix, section ST2.

Optical Simulations.

Prebreakdown irradiance localization was computed using an aberration-aware geometrical optics (ray-tracing) model implemented in ZEMAX OpticStudio (Fig. 5D and SI Appendix, section ST3). Droplets were modeled as homogeneous spheres of diameter Dd and refractive index n at 532nm, placed at the measured xp relative to the external focus. The optical train followed the as-built layout and was validated against measured focal-spot sizes in air (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Table S1). For each placement, rays were traced until convergence of peak irradiance at droplet-tied sampling locations using a standard numerical convergence approach. The irradiance field was stored at a reference energy and then scaled linearly with Ei prior to breakdown. Peak irradiances at four candidate locations (illumination surface, interior, shadow surface, and near-field air) were compared to the corresponding thresholds to compute exceedance factors (SI Appendix, Table S2). Applying the first-crossing criterion across (xp/Dd,Ei) yields predicted first-breakdown maps (Fig. 2 I and J). Ray renderings are provided for intuition; predictions are based on computed irradiance fields rather than ray-density projections. Continuous placement sweeps and 3D renderings that support this interpretation are provided in Movies S2 and S3.

Multiphase Numerical Simulation and Modeling.

To complement the threshold-referenced initiation predictions, compressible multiphase simulations were performed to reproduce early-time shock-driven droplet deformation at nanosecond timescales (Figs. 2B and 3C). Simulations were initialized with a spherical droplet in quiescent gas and modeled LIB initiation as a localized impulsive energy or pressure deposition at the experimentally and optically inferred initiation site. The goal was to capture early shock and cavity morphology relevant to impulse transfer, rather than plasma microphysics. Representative simulation time evolutions are provided in Movie S5. Simulations were performed with the ECOGEN compressible multiphase solver (41); further numerical details are provided in SI Appendix, section ST7. Polarity and robustness metrics were computed from both repeated experiments and uncertainty-propagated model realizations. Experimental class probabilities yielded Πexp and Rexp, while model probabilities were obtained via Monte-Carlo perturbations of (xp,Ei) using measured σx and σE, producing Πmod and Rmod (SI Appendix, section ST4).
