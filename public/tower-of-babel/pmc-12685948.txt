Long-term biological surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 in critical points for municipal sewage catchment in light of wastewater-based epidemiology, public health and environmental hygiene
Authors: Karol Korzekwa, Tomasz Lepionka, Agnieszka Bisak, Oliwia Obuch-Woszczatyńska, Klaudia Bylińska, Agnieszka Kauc, Katarzyna Skuza, Bartosz Zaborski, Małgorzata Krzyżowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Routine monitoring and wastewater-based bio-surveillance represents a strong supporting tool for following and understanding SARS-CoV-2 endemicity or epidemic dynamics in society. The main objective of the research was a two-year qualitative and quantitative monitoring program of SARS-CoV-2 in sewage in a model urban agglomeration (the capital city), including the development of an algorithm for action in this area and a joint evaluation of procedures with a water and sewage company (public administration sector). To resolve problems, we continuously investigated inflows and outflows of four wastewater treatment plants and raw sewage of the whole catchment (including healthcare units, airport and military areas) within the Warsaw capital city for SARS-CoV-2, during two waves of COVID-19 in 2023 and 2024. The screening was performed by RT-qPCR (N1, N2, E probes) and NGS metagenomics analysis. The average cumulative load of the virus in sewage for the whole tested period was 3.5 × 105 genomic copies per litre. The highest load was found for wastewater inflows and the lowest one was found for housing estates and wastewater outflows. Higher viral concentrations in the wastewater than expected in medical records suggested a higher number of undiagnosed COVID-19 infections within the community. The obtained genotypes segregated within 11 clades (21I to 24 F), making ‘23 and ‘24 waves distinguishable. We consider our results and methodological approaches hold significant potential for public health, with particular emphasis on the needs of the Directive (EU) 2024/3019 of the European Parliament and of the Council.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-27171-x.

Introduction

Wastewater and its processing, treatment, distribution, and collection are the source of environmental anthropogenic pressure negatively affecting human health and natural environment1,2. By the early-stage monitoring of wastewater it is possible to predict the epidemiological status of the local human population3,4. From the wastewater-based epidemiological point of view (WWBE) agglomerations about significant number of population pose a potential primary sources of new variants of pathogenic viruses5,6. WWBE provides epidemiologists with an early warning system for viral outbreaks due to the comprehensive monitoring of new versions of bio factors in real-time7,8. The application of many WWBE tools during COVID-19 was the best example, especially the qualitative-quantitative assessment of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus type 2 (SARS-CoV-2)9,10.

Compared to the significant number of studies on the occurrence, epidemiology and diagnostics of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, there is little information taking into account the division of whole municipal load of wastewater into sub-catchments as healthcare units, housing estates, transportation hubs, military areas and their share in the total sewage load for cities with a population of close to or above 2 million inhabitants, during long-term investigation. Generally, in Poland, most studies concerning SARS-CoV-2 have been focused on (i) the epidemiological analysis of the 1389 cases in 202011 and mortality rate12, (ii) summary of the epidemiological situation of infectious diseases in 2021 from the routine national epidemiological surveillance system including SARS-CoV-213, (iii) comprehensive epidemiological analysis supported by genomic data over two years of the pandemic14 and (iv) wastewater quality during the COVID-19 pandemic15. However, the report on the three-year epidemiological monitoring of raw sewage influx to the main WWTPs in Warsaw has been presented by the Municipal Water and Sewerage Company of Warsaw (MWSCW)16. In Europe, studies of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater were undertaken by Bartel et al.17 in Berlin during 7 months of 2023. Additionally, a short-term study of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater was conducted by Veneri et al.18 in a period of the recent “2023 wave”, covering the period between September 2023 and January 2024 in 19 provinces of Italy. However, there is relatively no information on qualitative and quantitative survey for SARS in wastewater from health care facilities in large urban agglomerations. Moreover, little is known about the virus in wastewater from military complexes and transportation hubs19. Mostly, in the WWBE practice so far, the prevailing approach was to survey the microbiome of sewage inflowing to WWTPs from whole catchment. In a significant number of publications on the SARS-CoV-2 load in sewage inflowing to WWTPs, the contribution of individual housing estates, hospitals, military areas, and transportation hubs in large urban agglomerations has not been highlighted.

Based on many national programs and R&D (Research and Development) initiatives it was crucial to survey WWTPs outflows, especially in the light of water reusing in closed water circuit system20–22. Most of the main goals are to accelerate the research toward optimising faster, more accurate technologies for tracking COVID-19 and any new signs of the epidemic, in medical point of view (BE READY_PLUS, EU-WISH, EU4Health)23. The EU4Health consortium (62 participants) enforced public health surveillance activities for wastewater, knowledge exchange, and sharing evidence-based best practices. According to the adopted wastewater legislation in January 2025 (EU2024/3019), the EU obligated itself to stringent and more specific control of wastewater treatment standards to enhance the protection of public health and the environment24. European Health Preparedness and Response Authority (HERA) and the Joint Research Centre (JRC) prepared an innovative platform integrating data from across the EU, combining real-time wastewater monitoring with existing national and research-based dashboards and offering early warnings for emerging pathogens and health crises25. The existing standards and algorithms used for sanitary and epidemiological surveillance of the environment concerning SARS-CoV-2 do not determine specific sampling locations within urban watersheds. Ongoing discussions about sampling frequency, as well as the selection of days of the week and times of day for sampling, continue to spark significant debate. Therefore, it seems important to create algorithm of action and a system of cooperation with the water and sewage company (R&D). In alignment with the requirements of Article 17 of the “Urban wastewater surveillance,” the solutions proposed by the EU2024/3019 have been adopted in this study as a preliminary gold standard.

Based on the available literature, we proposed five hypotheses (H1-H5) to resolve during investigations: (H1) The SARS-CoV-2 load in sewage is a useful tool for epidemiological support during the pre-epidemic period. In the background, the question was whether the N1, N2, and E markers exhibit similar sensitivity and resolution in the study of sewage from the municipal town of Warsaw; (H2) The levels of the virus in sewage align with global figures (specifically Europe) but may vary locally (for voivodship and capital city); (H3) The loads of the virus in sewage differ significantly among sources such as housing estates, hospitals, military areas, and transportation hubs; (H4) The highest virus loads is suspected to be found in medical facilities and housing estates with about 100,000 residents; (H5) Two years of wastewater studies in the capital, in collaboration with a water and sewage company, enable and accelerate the development of a universal legislative algorithm for EU2024/3019 purposes.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Warsaw territory (517 km2) generates about 545,000 m3/d of domestic, hospital, and industrial wastewater, stormwater, urban run-off, and agricultural wastes16. The material for the analyses was obtained from the inflows to three sewage treatment plants (WTP1-WTP3) for the Warsaw capital city (population 2 M citizens) plus controlled one WTP4 (out of the Warsaw). Two sampling points were established at the northern wastewater treatment plant (WTP1 and WTP1a), one for each sewage inflow, along with one sampling point at each of the other wastewater treatment plants (WTP2-3). Samples from catchment were collected from inspection wells for twelve points, among others healthcare units, housing estates, military areas and transportation hub (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The tested wastewater from housing estates usually carried a load representative of 100,000 inhabitants. We also tested effluents (OF1-OF4) of four wastewater treatment plants. The level of twenty-seven bio-and chemical parameters were assessed including BOD, COD, N, NH4, P, SO4, Cl, B, metals (Fe, Zn, Cr, Cu, Ni, Pb), phenol, CN-, F-, Cd, Aldrin (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Sample collection and pre-treatment

The sampling period started in April 2023 and lasted until December 2024 (21 months). Samples were obtained once or twice a week. Finally, we analysed 2184 samples, made 86 partial reports and over 5,500 samples were stored for further purposes. The appropriate volumes of samples were taken automatically or manually, according to Polish and International standards26–28. Composite samples of 500 ml from the WTPs were collected from the gravity and pressure inlet (raw sewage) and outflow (treated sewage) collectors between Monday at 6.30 AM and Tuesday at 07.00 AM by flow- or time-proportional modes (Supplementary Table 1). The process of inflow sampling in WTPs was automated (WaterSam 312; no. 4121541, Germany). Outflow samples of treated water were automatically grabbed by autosampler Buhler 4011 (type BU4011.60.11111, HACH LANGE, UK) and stationary autosampler WaterSam312 (no. 4121352, Germany). Materials from HCU and catchment points (AH, HE, MCA) were grabbed directly from flow inspection wells, manually as qualified single samples between 7.00 AM and 4.00 PM the same day (5 samples per 2 h sampling time with a minimum of 2 min of intervals). Raw and treated sewage were collected into SCHOTT type transparent glass bottles with a screw-on cap. The average quality of sewage was assessed regarding the presence of SARS-CoV-2 at the time of collection and during the 24 h. Bottles with the material were placed in thermal packaging with cooling at 4 °C (± 2) in accordance with standard28. In the next step, sewage in the amount of 40 ml was divided into water fractions and pellets by centrifugation (10 min/3,000 x g/RT). The pellet was weighed and processed separately.

Nucleic acid extraction and amplification analysis

In all fractions, nucleic acids were concentrated and isolated using the column method by vacuum filtration and centrifugation according to the Wizard Enviro Total Nucleic Acid Kit procedure (Promega – A2991, Madison, WI, USA). The final volume of the isolated TNA was 50 µl. The quantity and quality of the obtained total nucleic acids (TNA) were estimated spectrophotometrically (Nabi UV/Vis Nano Spectrophotometer, NB1-A-180404; MicroDigital Co., Korea). Next, TNA was subjected to molecular analysis, including RT-qPCR. The reaction was performed as described in GoTaq Enviro Wastewater SARS-CoV-2 System procedure (Promega – AM2100, AM2110, AM2120 and AM2130). Amplification was performed in a 10 µl reaction mixture according to the standard thermal profile, i.e., reverse transcription (45 °C/15 min), transcription inactivation (95 °C/2 min), denaturation (95 °C/15 min) and probe/primer annealing with product extension (62 °C/1 min) during 40 cycles. The estimated presence and copy number of the RNA region of 68 bp in the Pepper Mild Mottle Virus (PMMoV) was simultaneously used as an internal control of amplification. The second internal control of amplification was a sequence of 285 bp tested in the presence of the VIC fluorophore. The standard for analyses was a series of dilutions for SARS-CoV-2 (N + E) and PMMoV virus RNA. Amplification was performed using the QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA USA). Comparative analyses of the entire material with the semi-automatic TNA isolation method were performed using a Maxwell RSC 48 robot, Maxwell RSC Enviro TNA kit (AS1831, Promega), and comparative amplification assisted by the CFX96 Touch Deep Well Real-Time PCR detection system (BioRad Polska, Warsaw, Poland).

Sequencing, data acquisition and deposition

Three positive samples were selected for nanopore sequencing using the Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT, Oxford, UK) MinION platform, according to Skuza et al.29. The sequencing procedure involved rapid barcoding (SQK-RBK110.96) and Midnight RT PCR Expansion (EXP-MRT001) protocols. The samples exhibiting 21 < Ct < 36 represented varying viral loads. For PCR amplification, 1200 base pairs of amplicon Midnight primers were used. Subsequently, sequencing libraries were generated using the rapid barcoding kit. Pooled barcoded libraries were purified by AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Diagnostics, Brea, CA, USA) in a 1:1 ratio and eluted. Libraries (800 ng) were loaded onto an R9.4.1 flow cell (FLO-MIN106D) and subjected to 72 h of sequencing on a MinION (Mk1B) device (ONT). The MinKNOW software (Oxford Nanopore Technology, version 21.11.8) was employed for data collection, basecalling, and demultiplexing, resulting in a total of 3.39 GB of raw sequencing data. The obtained genome sequences encompassed 29.9 kb nucleotides and a minimum coverage depth of 300. All ONT’s sequencing runs were conducted in February 2025 and supported by ARTIC EPI2ME v3.4.1 SARS-CoV-2 pipeline (FastQC plus Artic plus Nextclade v2022.03.08). All consensus sequences have been deposited in the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data30, with respective submission numbers (EPI_ISL_19832072 to EPI_ISL_19832087).

Statistical analysis

COVID-19 case data, in reference to the SARS-CoV-2 presence in wastewater, were presented as “raw” without any corrections or “normalised” and reported as cases per 10,000 people. Concentration factor (CF) and human faecal normalisation with PMMoV (HFN) were calculated according to the Promega technical manual (TM661) and presented as viral genomic copies per litre (gc/L). Three commonly use markers (E, N1, and N2) were applied during SARS-CoV-2 survey. Raw data underwent HFC normalisation because the number of people contributing to sewage was changing over the surveillance period in our study. Daily new cases of COVID-19 in Poland were monitored, compared, and analysed based on non-parametric Spearman r correlation coefficient (StatPlus LE 7.7.0, AnalystSoft Inc.Version v7, Statistica 5.0 StatSoft Inc.). For further statistical analysis, normality tests, descriptive statistics, Pearson, Spearman correlations, and ANOVA were implemented to describe the results (Statistica 5.0; StatPlus LE 7.7.0). Single catchment collection points (SCPs) were analysed individually and in pooled groups, named representatively (Table 1), for mean combining. All clinical data of COVID-19 for Warsaw, Mazovia voivodeship and Poland were obtained from the public dashboard31. The number of daily cases calculated as the average minimum concentration of SARS-CoV-2 excreted in feces per person, expressed as the number of cg/ml of tested sewage32–34. Daily new cases of COVID-19 positive and confirmed by governmental statistics for Poland, Mazovian voivodship, Warsaw and revealed in the present study was proposed as the assumption that one person can excrete with faeces about 1 × 10E + 4 copies of SARS-CoV-2 into 1 L of municipal sewer35.

Results and discussion

Markers E, N1, N2 in SARS-CoV-2 survey for the Warsaw sewage network and wastewater treatment plants

Envelope protein (E) and nucleocapsid (N1, N2) markers used in this study showed variable sensitivity in detecting SARS-CoV-2 isolated from Warsaw sewage. After 12 weeks of sampling, only the N2 marker was preselected for further analysis. Compared to N1 and N2, the E marker did not always identify the presence of the virus in sewage and, in some cases, indicated a two- to three-order higher number of viral genomic units. There were sporadic cases of detecting the presence of the virus in the reaction with marker E in the absence of a signal from markers N1 and N2, especially in the pellet after centrifugation. N1 and N2 demonstrated similar resolution and sensitivity trends, with N2 detecting a greater number of SARS-CoV-2 cases in samples from periods between COVID-19 waves when the virus was minimally present and undetectable by N1 and E probes.

Differences in the performance of markers in RT-qPCR reactions, apart from methodological ones, may be related to many polymorphic sites and percent of substitution rate for E and N (up to 0.31)30,36,37. From a population point of view, frequent random mutations and recombinations are two strong primary sources of genetic diversity determining the level of SARS-CoV-2 variability38–41. Genetic variations in the N and E regions of the virus, which can vary based on location, population size, and sewage type, all affect the effectiveness and sensitivity of SARS surveillance tools42,43. In this sense, the virus passively participates in the evolutionary mechanisms of adaptation to the environment in large urban agglomerations and manifest diverse symptom phenotypes in COVID-19 population44.

General presence of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater of warsaw’s catchment

There were two COVID-19 waves in Poland (wave 2023 and wave 2024) over the time this study was conducted. First, between October 25th, 2023 and January 24th, 2024 (named 2023) and the second from August 14th, 2024 until October 23rd, 2024 (named 2024) (Fig. 1). Official data of COVID-19 cases for Warsaw district, Mazovian voivodship and whole Poland originated from Polish governmental websites16,45 and the results of our study were concordant (Pearson’s R = 0.74; p < α). The first wave was characterized by two pre-epidemic and one post-epidemic peaks, in contrast to the second wave, which possessed two significant peaks with an intermediary minimum (Fig. 1).

There were no significant differences between the number of daily new cases in Mazovia and a hypothetical number of daily cases calculated as the average minimal concentration of SARS-CoV-2 excreted with faeces per one person observed as the number of cg/mL of tested wastewater32–34;46. However, these theoretical numbers were higher when comparing Warsaw’s daily official case reports and calculations in this work. The presented results were substantially higher compared with official case reports for Poland. Fewer cases were reported officially in Warsaw than those suggested by the number of viral RNA obtained as cg/L in sewage. A closer relationship existed between our calculated numbers and official numbers obtained for Mazovian Voivodship as a whole province (Fig. 2). It could be explained by the existence of many asymptomatic cases without SARS-CoV-2 tests performed or ignored symptoms of the disease. The usefulness of the cited calculations may be very restricted to recent research objects. First, the geographic localisation of the capital city on the route of the north-south and west-east direction of trade movement promoted migration and influenced an increasing number of SARS-CoV-2 in the sewers of the Warsaw capital city. Secondly, the localisation of great transportation hubs in Warsaw supported the migration of commuters, students, and travellers, within the Mazovian Voivodship, Poland, and abroad. They all substantially increased the general numbers of SARS-CoV-2 in the capital’s net sewer. Although the theoretical number of cases based on wastewater was higher compared to Warsaw and Mazovia official data, the conclusions should be cautious because of the problem of people with SARS-CoV-2 nasopharyngeal positive results and no evidence of the virus in stool samples35. Further epidemiological studies should be considered to test the number of case reports for neighbouring voivodships, main cities in voivodships, and directions of people’s movement. That is why further symptomatic/nonsymptomatic studies and conclusions for any cases were not undertaken.

Compared to our study, Gujski et al.11 conducted a cross-sectional study of confirmed COVID-19 cases in March 2020, emphasising general epidemiological numbers of deaths, hospitalisations, quarantines, and tests for the presence of the virus in human samples. Similarly, they also observed one of the highest incidence rates in the Mazovian administrative regions of interest presented in this study. According to the authors, their study had several limitations due to the beginning of the pandemic (the first case was recorded on 04 March 2020). Secondly, they brought up the problem of the WHO-recommended RT-qPCR method that generated reporting delays in case of multiple outbreaks, which was not a problem in our study. The method used in our study was optimised and prepared for fast analysis of 44 SCPs per week for two laboratory technicians. Barański et al.12 made computer simulations and re-computed retrospective data concerning a lower death rate in Poland, combining official data, but did not do any molecular analysis. Rosińska et al.13 analyzed the COVID-19 situation at the turn of 2020 and 2021 based on the content of Statistics Poland for 89 weeks, similarly to our work. No molecular analysis or wastewater surveillance was done. Concerning the similar period as in our study, Mirska et al.14 made a retrospective analysis of the two-year COVID-19 pandemic in Poland. However, they included molecular and evolutionary analysis and made a sequence analysis for depository based on GISAID, and 500 genomes originated from nasopharyngeal and post-diagnostic samples. Moreover, they finished their work almost at the very beginning of our study in March 2023. Simultaneously, Mirska et al. 14 pointed to Mazovian Voivodship as a Polish district with the most significant SARS-CoV-2 positive test results. Another study tested wastewater in Pomeranian Voivodship and focused on influents from the catchment of three cities and villages (367,000 p.e. in total), collected, and transported to the WWTP of capacity about 73,000 m3/d during five months15. In our study, the total load of raw sewage in the influx to WWTPs oscillated around 1M m3/d for a total catchment of about 2 M p.e., which makes our study representative for a capital city. Similarly, to eliminate the variable share of human-related material in sample normalisation approaches, we used tomato mosaic virus (N2/PMMoV) instead of faecal pollution bacteriophage (N2/AsPh) or thermotolerant faecal coliforms (N2/FC). Furthermore, the share of human faecal content represented by PMMoV was greater than SARS in all samples, especially when taken from housing estates.

The normalised ratio obtained in our study strongly correlated with raw results of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater (Pearson’s R = 0.66; p < α) as well as with the governmental statistics for SARS-CoV-2 (Pearson’s R = 0.91; p < α) (Figs. 1 and 3). Analysis made by MWSCW with which we cooperated, were conducted independently by their local lab personnel and were convergent with those presented in this study, but did not include sequencing data. Comparison of mean values for raw data and normalised data in this study is presented in Fig. 3.

Individual analysis for the presence of the virus in catchment points

Analysis of all catchment points revealed differences between means of SARS-CoV-2 loads in SCPs for raw data (p = 3.11E-7; n = 1150; 39 significances) as well as for normalised calculations (p = 0.01; n = 1150, 25 significances) (Table 2). A higher load of SARS-CoV-2 was detected in WTP (1–4), HCU (1–4), and MCA than in all outflows. Moreover, the load for communicable disease health care units HCU (1, 2, 4) was higher than that obtained for the transportation hub (AH) and half of the housing estates HE (1–4) (Table 2). Finally, a higher load of the virus was found for communicable disease units than for other healthcare units, including military medical centres. The general cumulative mean number of viral particles in raw sewage over the whole catchment during the whole period of investigation (COVALL) was estimated to be 3.54E + 5 cg/L, while for all WWTP outflows (COVOUT) it was two orders of magnitude lower (8.25E + 3 cg/L). The mean value of virus presence per SCP for a single collection date (COVDATE) was 3.91E + 5 cg/L (4.76E + 3 to 2.59E + 6). Finally, the mean value for the given SCP during the time assessment (COVSCP) was 3.91E + 5 cg/L in the range of 4.02E + 4 to 4.91E + 5. Mostly all SCPs were positively correlated with each other, especially the strongest correlation was observed for HE1 and HE2 with HCU2 (military medical centre) (0.98 ≤ R ≤ 1.0; 2.05E-12 > p > 2.25E-10).

Analysis of grouped catchment points

For further analysis, all catchments were divided and grouped (pooled) into: transportation hub (AH, N = 1; n = 76), health care units (HCU, N = 4; n = 303), mixed catchment area with housing estates (HE, N = 7; n = 91), mixed military and housing estates (MCA, N = 1; n = 76), wastewater treatment plants (WTP, N = 4; n = 409), outflows from plants (OF, N = 4; n = 196) (Table 1; Fig. 6). Analyse of variances revealed significant differences between the means for presence of SARS-CoV-2 in groups (Table 3a, b; Fig. 6). Considering raw and normalized data, the means for pooled healthcare units (HCU) and grouped mixed military catchment area (MCA) were significantly higher than those for all outflows (OF) and represented the strongest differences (Table 3; Fig. 6). Simultaneously, means for pooled health care unit (HCU) were higher than for grouped housing estate (HE) and all inflows to WTPs for normalised data. Moreover, pooled WTPs represented a moderately higher load of SARS-CoV-2 than pooled outflow from these plants (Fig. 6). Weak statistical differences were found for higher means of SARS-CoV-2 load in military mixed area (MCA) than for housing estates (HEs) and WTPs. Finally, pooled WTPs represented a higher load of the virus than in the case of the transportation hub and the pooled housing estates.

Further pooled analysis for HE and MCA as a general representation of housing estate, military area, and critical infrastructure was conducted and plotted in Fig. 6 as a semi-transparent grey “ghost-plot” (inserted between HE and MCA). For such a pool (HE-MCA), there were significantly higher numbers of SARS-CoV-2 genomic units (**) compared to outflows. Concomitantly, for normalized data, pooled, HE-MCA represented the lower value of viruses than in health care units (HCU) and higher than outflows (LSD p ≤ 0.05). The lower numbers of SAR-CoV-2 over pooled HE-MCA to HCU or transportation hub can be explained by the mobility rate of population, day commuting of workers and students, intra- and inter-continental movements via the capital city, periodic fluctuations during vacation period or governmental restrictions such as lockdown35. In the case of movement limitations during 2020–2021, the number of SARS-CoV-2 in wastewater from HE-MCA increased due to the immobilisation of people in their houses, apartments, and places of permanent location. When movement restrictions were abolished, statistically, more viruses in sewage could be sampled in transportation hubs, economic and industrial zones, educational areas, health care facilities, and military units. The capital city of Warsaw attracts a huge number of students, workers, and travellers. During the period of this study, nearly 3 million travellers were served at the two main airports and 1,5 million at the railway stations of the AH47,48. Careful analysis, together with epidemiological data of case reports, death rate, and the number of tests, may help to describe the proportion of infected people that can excrete viral RNA and, subsequently, the level of exposure to new infections49. It is crucial to conduct thorough surveillance of the house estate catchment area and integrate it with health care units, as a large proportion of SARS-CoV-2 infections could be asymptomatic, and typically visible symptoms occur 3–7 days after infection50. Another question is a proper selection of time for sampling, e.g. in the same manner as for WWTPs. However, pooled groups reacted statistically convergently with the changes of SARS-CoV-2 data obtained from official statistics.

Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed several strong positive relations (R ≥ 0.5; p ≤ 0.0) between transportation hub (AH), pooled healthcare units (HCUs) and mixed military area (MCA) (Table 4). In some cases, a very slight negative correlation was noticed during the investigation, caused by the high number of SARS-CoV-2 in outflows. While a slight increase of SARS-CoV-2 values in WWTP influx with a simultaneous decrease in output is not debatable51,52, the opposite situation might be an interesting subject for resolution. Such a phenomenon might be periodically amplified in secondary clarifiers by a floating excess of sediments with particles of viruses that escape to outflows.

Detailed analysis of SARS-CoV-2 loads within HCU, WTP, and OF pooled groups

From the statistical point of view, analysis of variance (one-way) did not show any significant differences within the HCU pool. However, the highest load of SARS-CoV-2 within HCUs was revealed for HCU1, next to the military medical centre, other medical institutes, and communicable diseases centres. The mean number of gc/L loads for the HCU4 unit was twice as low as the other three HCUs. The lowest number of gc/L in HCU4 is correlated with extraordinary emergency measures of protection against biological factors since COVID-19 started. Furthermore, HCU4 has its own system for raw wastewater disinfection. Kwok et al.53, Barios-Andres et al.54, and Kallem et al.55 described the most effective disinfection protocols and measures for hospitals or sewage. Interestingly, analysis of normalized data showed hospitals and centres of communicable diseases (HCU1 and HCU4) as an important source of SARS-CoV-2 in municipal sewers (p < 0.05). Moreover, all values of the virus load for HCU correlated positively. A higher number of HCU1 is highly correlated with a more frequent presence of SARS-CoV-2 in HCU2 (R = 0.59; p = 2.08E-8; N = 76) (Fig. 7).

Within the WTPs group, significant differences between the higher loads of the virus in the left inflow of WTP1 compared to the control wastewater treatment plant (WTP4; p = 0.02) were obtained. The rest of the SARS-CoV-2 load values were relatively similar for the three wastewater treatment plants. All virus loads in inflows to WWTPs were positively correlated (0.48 < R < 0.91), mostly for WTP3 and the right inflow of WTP1a (R = 0.9) (Fig. 7a, b). Further analysis of correlations between housing estates and wastewater treatment plants revealed strict dependencies between higher numbers of SARS-CoV-2 in HEs and wastewater facilities that operate (Table 5).

Surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 variants in Warsaw net sewer

The whole catchment area was pooled into five groups as previously, while HE and MCA were pooled as one (N = 8). Genomic epidemiology was conducted for 14 representative pooled samples of each local epidemic wave. The load of SARS-CoV-2 in the whole catchment of the capital city clustered differently from the phylogenetic point of view. The first wave (2023) in Warsaw was characterised with more phylogenetic heterogeneity - from clad 21 L to 24 A compared to the “2024” one, in which mostly clad 24 was present (Table 6; Supplementary Fig. 2). Omicron’s eight clads were revealed during the “2023 wave” and another four during the “2024 wave” of SARS-CoV-2 in Warsaw. Both waves were investigated at the end stage and just after the lockdown was withdrawn in Poland on 01 June 2023, under the regulation of the Ministry of Health57. Due to the high number of Ct values for outflows, only for the second wave, 21 K and 24 A clades were obtained in OF1 and OF2, respectively (Table 6; Supplementary Fig. 2). Similar variants and trends in SARS-CoV-2 RNA abundance in wastewater was revealed by Bartel et al.17 during investigation of Berlin sewages between June and December 2023.

The samples taken from the transportation hub and the complex of the northern hospital revealed the presence of variants 21 L and 21 K for Omicron’s BA.1 and BA.2 subvariants (Supplementary Fig. 2, 3a; Table 6). These lineages were discovered in South Africa and belonged to fast-mutating factors in the areas of genes coding spike proteins. The presence of a triple mutation at the furin cleavage site BA.1 was reported to be highly transmissible58. Moreover, Omicron BA.2 was characterised by the absence of the H69/V70 deletion associated with S gene target failure (SGTF). This significantly hindered transmission tracking. BA.2 subvariant was more transmissible than BA.1 and Delta, and less severe, but quickly increased hospitalisation of individuals touched by COVID-19 and co-morbidities. Variants isolated from HCU2, belonging to a clade 24 A, possessed 51 mutations in the S1 region and four unique ones. The observed JN.1 (BA.2.86.1.1.) strain was an offshoot of BA.2.86 and was revealed in late 2023. It was characterised predominantly with the Leu455Ser mutation that contributed to a higher transmissibility and immune escape ability59. The variant was abundant in France, Spain, and England in 2023. JN.1 showed resistance to monovalent XBB.1.5 vaccine sera, making it the most immune-evading variant compared to BA.2.86 and EG.5.1.60. At the beginning of November 2023, JN.1 and Eris (EG.5.1) were the variants most frequently discovered in Poland. However, JN.1, together with BA.2.86 and derived from XBB, stayed susceptible to Paxlovid, Molnupiravir, and Remdesivir during the “2023 waves” of COVID-19 in a recent study61. The following JN.1 variant revealed throughout housing estates and MCA emerged because of convergent evolution, especially recombinations in lineages BJ.1 and BM.1.1.1., harboured four main substitutions of the spike protein (R346T, K4444T, L452R, N460K) and predominated in India and Singapore60,62. Bartel et al.17 revealed in wastewater the same main clades of viruses (XBB, BA.2.86, JN.1, and EG.5.) as in the present study. Simultaneously, Veneri et al.18 confirmed our observation in Italian samples and revealed the same main clades and genotypes for SARS-CoV-2 as well as Rasmussen et al.63 in Denmark (Supplementary Fig. 2, 3a, Table 6).

The second wave of COVID-19 in Poland (“2024 Wave”) was characterized by the presence of predominating clad 24 (from A to G) with one exception in pooled outflows from WTP1 and WTP1a (BA.1)(Supplementary Fig. 4). In the time of our study, the number of some VOI variants decreased (KP.3), and some were in plateau (BA.2.86)65. The European Respiratory Virus Surveillance Summary65 detected that VOI BA.2.86 has decreased its trends in Europe constantly from 54% to 19%. Meanwhile, VOI KP.3 was present in samples at ± 20% (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The proportions for VUM XEC significantly increased from 18% to 60% between 40 weeks of 2024 and 1 week of 2025. VUM variants of XEC were continuously increasing during the second wave of COVID-19 in Poland (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The phylogenetic tree of revealed variants in this study for the “2024 wave” is plotted in Supplementary Fig. 4.

During “2024” wave clad 24E dominated over 90% of Warsaw, except for southern and western districts, which possessed their own SARS-CoV-2 patterns (Fig. 8). Clad 24 A in the central part of the city during the first wave gave way to 24E, except for the area operated by WTP2. Another situation was in WTP1, where two clades dominated (Fig. 8). In the case of the second wave of COVID-19, clades 21 K and 24 A could be considered as potentially dominant in the outflows to the Vistula River. Outflow from “2023” waves did not give satisfactory results, caused by high Ct (up to 36.2 for OF) during RT-qPCR and subsequently during NGS procedures. However, Cerrada-Romero et al.66 reported a wide range of values in positive samples taken from the stool and nasopharyngeal samples, as well when symptomatic patients were tested (17.0 < ct < 37.8; median = 30.17). Moreover, they indicated a higher Ct value in the case of positive stool samples in the first two weeks of symptoms and higher in the second week (Ct up to 39,6). Samples taken from sewages and analysed immediately in the lab could vary in their “age.” The mean wastewater retention time in Warsaw’s sewer net was on average equal 13 h (4 h 1 min – 25 h 23 min.). That is why the picture of Ct reflected one to two weeks of samples taken in a similar study in the case of Cerrada-Romero et al.66. However, stool samples did not contain such a massive amount of inhibitors as wastewater that could interfere with reagents during the isolation and amplification process.

The final aspect that needs to be pushed forward is the quality composition in samples taken from inflows to WWTPs. As plotted in Fig. 8, WTP1 in the north of Warsaw represented clades 23I and 24 C as dominant during the study. In the case of the “2023” wave, it was possible that WTP4 and OF4 (the control catchment for this research) had a more significant impact locally during sampling time. In the “2024” wave, a close genetic relationship of KP.3. clades might result in little frameshift during sequencing or inhibition effects. It was noteworthy to see that SARS-CoV-2 RNA from first WWTP outflows were more distant phylogenetically within the catchment than we expected. In the case of all other WWTPs, results from inflows met our expectations and hypothesis for SARS-CoV-2 presence, including the fourth wastewater treatment plant as a control.

Semi-algorithm for WWBE and course of action on the National level based on trained procedures

Directive EU2024/3019 obliges us, among other things, to tighten cooperation between national authorities for public health and competent authorities responsible for urban wastewater treatment. The document identified biological factors presented in the inlet to wastewater treatment plants, as mentioned by ECDC, HERA, and WHO. The presented work considered the SARS-CoV-2 virus and its variants as one of five factors included in Article 17. Proposed allocation of roles, responsibilities, determination of tasks, and financial allocation were trained locally between military authorities, institutions (DMDS of MND; MIHE), and civil branch operators (MWSC Inc.). In this area, the whole catchment was prepared, discussed, implemented into action, and trained. Moreover, locations and the frequency of urban wastewater sampling and analysis were determined, applied, and trained in the capital city of Warsaw, as a unique model (Fig. 9).

Plans and projects included other identified technological and biochemical parameters (standard, micro pollutions), information about the point of the catchment, and local availability of health data and needs addressed from healthcare points under conditions of the epidemiological situation. Finally, the results of SARS-CoV-2 obtained in this study were compared with official national statistics and an independent research team from MWSC. Some aspects of Article 8 of Directive (EU) 2020/2184 were applied by monitoring WWTP4 as a special control area. The whole catchment was divided by housing estates about 100,000 p.e. and above, then tested for simultaneous monitoring of antimicrobial resistance and micro pollution in urban wastewater. The minimum frequency of sampling was set up as once a week, between 8.00 AM in Monday and 8.00 AM in Tuesday for wastewater plants and between 6.00 AM and 19.00 PM daily for SCPs, including housing estates (HU ≥ 100k p.e.), healthcare units (HCU), mixed military and civil area (MCA ≥ 100k p.e.). Furthermore, people’s movements in transportation hubs, airports, railway stations, and metro stations should be recognised and investigated. In this work, airports and some aspects of railway stations were examined. We believe that more SCP points are needed to correct movements in all railway stations and the subway system in Warsaw. Therefore, symptomatic/asymptomatic calculations can be closer and more accurate. Moreover, conditions for WWTPs outflow sampling were proposed and trained, considering the special interest in environmental hygiene and epidemiology (Fig. 9). Simultaneously, proposed resolutions were intended to fulfil the provisions of information on monitoring of implementation (Article 22, point 1 h, EU2024/3019) and evaluation (Article 30, point 1a).

The question of frequency, location, and number of samples on the appropriate day of the week is highly dependent on the type of sample (bio- or chemical), type of collection points (WWTPs, transportation hubs, healthcare units, military facilities, housing estate heterogeneity), sewage retention time in collectors, maximal peaks of population in different period of the month and year. For WWTPs, the universal model of after-weekend sampling was applied. At the beginning of the week, samples from other catchment points were proposed to be collected. However, during this study, samples were taken on other days of the week too, but results were not statistically processed, yet due to the chain of custody organised between WWTPs management and courses of action in the laboratory. Moreover, special attention must be paid to the sampling on housing estates. In the case of this study, the precise calculation of the significance of industrial and office spaces in the whole part of a given HE was conducted regularly, and for a given HE, at last, one or two points were selected. More attention should be paid to such studies, considering that positive results of any tests between HE residents may require medical assistance, mandatory vaccination, or even termination of work in offices or factories68. Moreover, transportation hubs such as aviation or wheel transport generated interesting results, but as above, they should be divided by railway, underground, and airport units and statistically combined. In recent studies, some aspects of the airport and railway station network in Warsaw were only superficially touched in AH collection points and in some HE points. Finally, having in mind the hygiene of the environment and public health in two CPs in WWTPs, two additional collection points should be included in the route between activated sludge (or trickling filters, packed bed reactor - PBR, membrane bioreactors - MBR if any in another WWTPs) and second clarifier section, (if any tertiary filtration) and then outflows. It could be more precise for assessment of the impact of aerosolised infectious particles on staff, missed load of floated sludge throughout beds, effluent weirs of second clarifiers to outflow, and water receivers.

Conclusions

In this study, the N2 probe demonstrated the highest sensitivity and good resolution, proving to be just as useful as the WBE tool for providing epidemiological support during the pre-epidemic period in Warsaw (H1 = true). Two pre- and post-endemic situations and two maximal growths of SARS-CoV-2 cases were revealed. A slow increase in COVID-19 daily cases was observed in all samples about every 10 weeks before the maximum peak of the COVID-19 wave. Although the impact of WWTPs on the Vistula River environment was significant this did not indicate the presence of fully genetically armoured and infectious the virus particles. Tissue culture studies would be a very valuable addition to the procedure during the survey.

The virus level detected in the tested sewage networks varied locally between voivodships and the capital city (H2 = true). The results compared with official case reports for Mazovian Voivodship as a whole province were closed when recalculated for the theoretical excretion rates of SARS-CoV-2 by inhabitants. Although, further conclusions about the percentage of symptomatic or asymptomatic individuals in the Warsaw population should be undertaken carefully. First, geographic localisation and migration of commuters, students, and travellers, locally from Mazovian Voivodship, Poland, and abroad, can impact the fluctuation of SARS-CoV-2 in the Warsaw sewers as a capital city. Second, the study of migration’s impact on the load of biological or chemical factors in sewers might also be valuable, especially for transition cities. Further epidemiological studies should be considered to test the number of case reports for border voivodships, main cities in voivodships, and directions of people’s movement.

We confirmed a variable presence of the N2 marker in the sewage, which originated from the tested sewage collection points (SCPs), with healthcare units identified as the primary source of the viral load in the area (H3, H4 = true). Concomitantly, the highest number of cg/L was revealed for military healthcare centre. Individually, some housing estates manifested a close correlation in the load of the virus with samples grabbed from military medical health care units, which was expected territorially and by the work profile of inhabitants. However, the number of collected samples from housing estates was not equal to that of others, which could misbalance the statistics. Simultaneously, during the entire period of this study, the mean load of the virus in the sewer of the capital catchment was three orders of magnitude higher than that of outflows from wastewater.

The sampling methods and timing across the entire catchment differed from those used at WWTPs, leading to a partially positive confirmation of hypothesis 4, which will be further explored in upcoming studies. Moreover, the influence of some wastewater chemical compounds should be considered in light of RT-qPCR and sequencing analysis, mainly originating from health care units to test for tracing other emerging pathogens, neglected or seemingly eradicated etiological factors69. By focusing on the biological factors in the load of wastewater outflow, this approach is vital for environmental hygiene and early epidemic detection, helping to safeguard communities and promote a healthier future.

Wastewater studies conducted in the capital, in collaboration with a water and sewage company, have expedited the revision and development of a universal legislative algorithm for Article 17, “Urban wastewater surveillance,” point 1 in all subsections and 3 in the second paragraph of the EU2024/3019. This includes military water and sewage departments, water and sewage companies, local environmental protection inspectorates, analytical laboratories, sanitary and epidemiological stations, and the private industrial sector involved in wastewater testing (H5 = true). However, more SCPs are needed to correct people’s movement in all railway stations and the underground system in Warsaw. Then, symptomatic/asymptomatic calculations would be closer and more accurate.

At the end it should be pointed that not only the Warsaw sewage treatment plants pollute the Vistula River. Next near 63 WWTPs, polluting this river, are waiting for universal WBE tools and new procedures. It is also impossible to overestimate the importance of determining the impact of WWTPs outflows on environmental hygiene and epidemiology, especially for water reservoirs and ecological national assets.
