Pyroptosis-induced tissue damage in kidney disease
Authors: Zhiyuan Zhang, Xiaodi Li, Yang Han, Mianzhi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death that differs mechanistically from apoptosis and is characterized by a robust inflammatory response. It is primarily mediated by proteins of the gasdermin family, which form pores in the cell membrane, leading to osmotic cell lysis and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. As a process triggered by various pathological stimuli like infection, inflammation, and oxidative stress, pyroptosis contributes to a range of diseases, including autoimmune disorders and cancer. Evidence indicates that pyroptosis, a crucial innate immune component, promotes host defense by facilitating immune cell recruitment and tissue repair. However, dysregulated pyroptosis is a key driver of pathology, contributing to chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and tissue damage in a range of conditions such as kidney, cardiovascular, and metabolic disorders. More evidence suggests that pyroptosis may dominate the process of irreversible damage to renal tissue and may accelerate the time for patients to enter end-stage renal disease. At present, when diagnosing kidney diseases and performing kidney biopsy, more attention is paid to the extent of kidney tissue damage, which is strongly related to the prognosis of patients. Given that many kidney diseases, especially acute kidney injury, involve inflammatory responses, targeting dysregulated pyroptosis presents a potential strategy for alleviating irreversible renal damage. This review will provide a detailed description of the signaling pathways of pyroptosis, with a focus on the mechanisms of pyroptosis-induced renal tissue damage and new advances in treatment.Keywords: Pyroptosis, kidney tissue, podocyte, glomerular endothelial cells, renal tubule, acute kidney injury

Introduction

The kidney comprises over one million functional units called nephrons, each consisting of a glomerulus and a renal tubule [1]. Inflammation is a critical factor at every stage of kidney disease, whether acute or chronic [2]. Various injuries, such as ischemia, nephrotoxic medications, and toxins, can damage the renal tubules, triggering inflammatory responses that lead to renal fibrosis and a decrease in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) [3]. Glomerulonephritis is often accompanied by immune complex deposition and glomerular capillary damage. Renal perivascular cells can produce pro-inflammatory molecules that amplify inflammation and promote immune cell infiltration [4]. Consequently, targeting inflammatory pathways represents a major therapeutic strategy in nephrology. Pyroptosis is characterized by a sharp increase in inflammation. It most commonly happens after severe infections but also occurs in chronic diseases. Mild pyroptosis can elicit moderate inflammatory responses that help control bacterial infections. Dysregulated inflammation may progress to systemic pyroptosis, a pathological condition that can trigger sepsis [5]. The gasdermin (GSDM) proteins are recognized as the executioners of pyroptosis through their pore-forming activity. In humans, the GSDM family includes GSDMA, GSDMB, GSDMC, GSDME, and PJVK/DFNB59 [6]. The study has demonstrated that GSDMA, GSDMD, and GSDME are expressed in renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs) [7]. Furthermore, GSDMD expression has also been confirmed in podocytes and glomerular endothelial cells [8]. Another study on macrophage population analysis showed that GSDME and GSDMC were expressed in healthy murine renal tubular epithelial cells, while GSDMD was mainly found in macrophages. No studies currently support PJVK expression in murine or human kidneys [9]. The spatial distribution heterogeneity of GSDM protein within renal compartments dictates pyroptosis pathway activation and contributes to the pathogenesis of associated kidney diseases. GSDM proteins play a pivotal role in driving renal inflammation and tissue injury. Meanwhile, emerging evidence implicates GSDMB in the progression of renal cell carcinoma, where it promotes the proliferation and migration of cancer cells [10]. Previous studies show that pyroptosis primarily involves four signaling pathways: canonical inflammasome pathway, noncanonical inflammasome pathway, apoptotic caspases-mediated pathway, and granzymes-mediated pathway. These pathways highlight the complexity and versatility of pyroptosis in various physiological and pathological contexts, including infections, inflammatory diseases, and cancer. This review focuses on the signaling pathways involved in pyroptosis, highlighting the molecular mechanisms that lead to renal tissue damage. We also discuss potential therapeutic approaches for kidney diseases by targeting these pathways.

The signaling pathway of pyroptosis

The concept of pyroptosis has undergone significant evolution since its first description in the early 2000s. Initially misclassified as a form of apoptosis due to overlapping features such as caspase activation, it was distinguished by 2001 as a unique process marked by rapid plasma membrane rupture and release of pro-inflammatory contents [11]. A major breakthrough came in 2015 with the identification of GSDMD as the key executor of pyroptosis, sparking extensive research into the gasdermin family and their roles in cell death and inflammation. Subsequently, further investigation into GSDM family proteins was initiated, leading to the discovery of their additional pore-forming activity and pyroptosis-associated pathways [12,13]. Gasdermins typically share a common structure consisting of an N-terminal pore-forming domain, a C-terminal autoinhibitory domain, and a flexible linker [14]. Typically, the C-terminal domain inhibits the pore-forming activity of the N-terminal domain. The P30 fragment derived from GSDM-NT domain is primarily responsible for pore-forming activity. Their activation is tightly controlled by proteolytic cleavage, primarily by caspases such as caspase-1, caspase-4, caspase-5, and caspase-11. Reversible S-palmitoylation occurs at the Cys191 site of GSDMD. Other GSDM proteins are also palmitoylated at their N-termini as an essential prerequisite for acquiring pore-forming capability [15]. Upon cleavage, the N-terminal domain translocates to the plasma membrane, where it oligomerizes and creates pores with inner and outer diameters of around 18 nm and 28 nm, respectively. This pore formation disrupts the plasma membrane, leading to cell swelling, membrane rupture, and ultimately cell lysis [16,17]. Notably, PJVK (also known as GSDMF), deviates from this canonical structure and function, instead playing a specialized role in auditory hair cell maintenance [18] (Figure 1) [19].

Canonical inflammasome pathway

The first identified signaling pathway was the canonical inflammasome pathway. First, NLRP3 activation is required for inflammasome assembly. Pattern recognition receptors recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns or damage-associated molecular patterns, activating the MyD88/NF-κB pathway. This activation leads to the transcriptional upregulation of NLRP3 and other inflammasome components, as well as the production of pro-interleukin-1β (pro-IL-1β) and pro-interleukin-18 (pro-IL-18) [20]. During this priming phase, NLRP3 undergoes post-translational modifications, including ubiquitination, phosphorylation and sumoylation, while maintaining its autoinhibited state. NLRP3 activation is subsequently triggered, typically by potassium ion (K+) efflux or other ionic changes. In addition, the study has further demonstrated that NIMA-related kinase 7 can directly bind to NLRP3 and participate in the assembly and activation of inflammasome [21]. Upon activation, NLRP3 initiates inflammasome assembly by engaging the N-terminal pyrin domain (PYD) of ASC via homotypic PYD-PYD interactions. The C-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD) of ASC then binds to pro-caspase-1 through CARD-CARD interaction, leading to caspase-1 dimerization and activation [22]. Activated caspase-1 cleaves GSDMD at its central linker region, releasing an approximately 31 kDa GSDMD-NT fragment. This fragment targets the plasma membrane, inserts into it, and oligomerizes to form nonselective pores [12,23]. Caspase-1 also cleave pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature, active forms. These cytokines are then released through plasma membrane pores formed by GSDMD-NT, thereby triggering inflammation [24].

Non-canonical inflammasome pathway

This pathway is initiated by intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from Gram-negative bacteria. It activates atypical inflammasomes rather than caspase-1, engaging caspase-4 or caspase-5 in humans, and caspase-11 in mice. Caspase-4/-5/-11 can directly cleave GSDMD to produce N-terminal p30 fragments, which damages plasma membrane integrity and leads to pyroptosis [16]. Early studies established that caspase-11 activation is mediated by the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) –TRIF–IRF3 pathway. Subsequent research revealed that complement pathway (Cbp1-C3-C3aR) activation could amplify TLR/IFNAR signaling, participating in the priming and activation of caspase-11 [25]. However, more recent evidence demonstrates that LPS can activate caspase-11 independently of these pathways. First, caspase-4 and caspase-11 directly interact with LPS, leading to their oligomerization and activation. However, it is still unclear how LPS activates caspases. Emerging evidence indicates that guanylate-binding protein 1 (GBP1) recognizes Gram-negative bacteria and functions as an upstream LPS sensor, promoting caspase-4 recruitment and activation during Salmonella infection [26]. In interferon-γ-stimulated cells, GBP1 is recruited to cytosolic Gram-negative bacteria and binds to LPS through electrostatic interactions. Subsequently, GBP1 recruits GBP2-4 on the bacterial surface as an assembly platform to recruit caspase-4. GBPs are responsible for regulating the entire process of caspase-4 activation: GBP1 initiates platform assembly, GBP2 and GBP4 manage caspase-4 recruitment, and GBP3 regulates caspase-4 activation. The GBPs platform also induces caspase-4 to process IL-18 [27]. Another study showed that the CARD fragment of caspase-4/-11 can directly bind to the lipid A fragment of LPS, and this interaction leads to the self-cleavage of caspase-11 and activation of GSDMD [28]. Upon activation, caspase-4/caspase-5/caspase-11 cleaves human GSDMD at Asp276 (murine Asp275), to execute pyroptosis [12]. In the non-canonical pathway, caspase-11 is not involved in the activation and maturation of inflammatory cytokines. Caspase-4 can cleave IL-18, but cannot cleave IL-1β, which is similar to the efficiency of caspase-1 [29,30]. Therefore, the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 usually require activation of typical inflammasomes (e.g., NLRP3) and caspase-1. In macrophages, caspases-4 and caspase-11 cleave GSDMD, triggering K+ efflux and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby activating NLRP3 inflammasome and further promoting the maturation and release of inflammatory cytokines [31,32]. Consistently, LPS stimulation induces caspase-11-dependent pannexin-1 channel cleavage and ATP release. Extracellular ATP then activates the P2X7 receptor ion channel, driving K+ efflux that activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and caspase-1, culminating in IL-1β maturation [33].

Apoptotic caspases-mediated pathway

Pyroptosis can be triggered by certain apoptotic caspases. Since the discovery of caspase-3, it has been considered a key biomarker of apoptosis. Indeed, the key to determining the caspase-3-mediated cell death pattern is the expression level of GSDME. Caspase-3 cleaves GSDME and triggers pyroptosis when GSDME is expressed at high levels; otherwise, it triggers apoptosis. Caspase-3 activation occurs not through autoproteolysis, but via cleavage at Asp175 by upstream proteases such as granzymes or initiator caspases (e.g., caspase-8/caspase-9), which triggers its functional maturation [34]. Research found that the GSDMD-related protein DFNA5 was cleaved by caspase-3 at Asp270 to produce necrotic DFNA5-NT fragment. The fragments have similar intrinsic pore-forming activity to the GSDMD-NT and can disrupt the plasma membrane, inducing pyroptosis [35]. Subsequently, a study in 2017 found that under the induction of chemotherapy drugs, activated caspase-3 can cleave highly expressed GSDME at Asp267 site and induce pyroptosis [36]. Recently, it has been found that caspase-4 and caspase-11 may be involved in the activation of caspase-3. Elevated bile acids induce mitochondrial permeability transition, leading to the release of cytochrome c and ATP. Apaf-1 interacts directly with caspase-4/-11 to assemble the Apaf-1 pyroptosome. Subsequently, in the presence of cytochrome c and ATP, this pyroptosome promotes the cleavage of caspase-3. Simultaneously, bile acids inhibit the binding of caspase-11 to GSDMD. Ultimately, the cleavage of GSDME by caspase-3 induces pyroptosis [37,38]. Another is caspase-8, which can induce pyroptosis in several ways. First, caspase-8 can directly cleave GSDMD. During Yersinia infection, the effector protein YopJ blocks transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) activated kinase 1 and IκB kinase, enabling caspase-8 to directly cleave GSDMD and induce pyroptosis [39]. Caspase-8 can cause pyroptosis by participating in other pathways. Inactivated caspase-8 may act as an upstream component to form necrosomes (RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL). The scaffolding role of caspase-8 triggers caspase-1 activation, thereby inducing the caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated canonical pathway and leading to pyroptosis. However, this mechanism is restricted to specific cell types, such as bone marrow cells and endothelial cells [40,41]. Caspase-8 has the ability to directly cleave GSDMC and induce pyroptosis. TNFα typically induces apoptosis in cancer cells, but PD-L1 can convert this death pathway into pyroptosis under hypoxic conditions. Phosphorylated STAT3 interacts with PD-L1 to promote its nuclear transport and elevate GSDMC expression. Subsequently, TNFα activates caspase-8, which cleaves GSDMC at the 362LELD365 site to form pores on the plasma membrane, causing pyroptosis of tumor cells [42,43]. Further study demonstrated that caspase-8 can directly cleave GSDMC at Asp240 site (murine Asp233) under stimulation by the metabolite α-ketoglutarate. The glycolysis-induced acidic environment in the tumor microenvironment converts α-ketoglutarate to L-2HG and increases ROS levels. The membrane death receptor DR6 responds to ROS induction and begins to oxidize, then triggering its internalization to generate DR6 receptosomes. Caspase-8 and GSDMC are recruited to DR6 receptosomes via protein-protein interactions, enabling GSDMC cleavage and tumor pyroptosis [44]. These studies indicate that GSDMC possesses multiple cleavage sites susceptible to processing under different cellular contexts, thereby inducing pyroptosis. Caspase-8 can induce pyroptosis by activating caspase-3, which subsequently cleaves GSDME [45]. Under acidic conditions, caspase-8 can also directly cleave GSDME at Asp270 site to trigger pyroptosis. Upon dodecyl 6-hydroxy-2-naphthoate treatment, cyclic GMP-AMP synthase activation produces cyclic GMP-AMP, while intracellular acidification activates PKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase. PKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase phosphorylates stimulator of IFN genes (STING), promoting its polymerization into aggregates within the ER. These ER-STING aggregates serve as a platform to recruit and activate caspase-8 via Fas-associated via death domain. The activated caspase-8 then directly cleaves GSDME in the acidic environment, ultimately inducing pyroptosis [46].

Granzymes-mediated pathway

Granzymes are serine proteases stored in cytotoxic lymphocytes and natural killer cells. The granzyme family is comprised of 5 members in human, including granzyme A (GZMA), granzyme B (GZMB), granzyme H (GZMH), granzyme K (GZMK), and granzyme M (GZMM), and 11 members in mice [47]. Granzyme-mediated pyroptosis is a recently discovered cell death pathway. The cleavage of GSDMB by GZMA leads to pyroptosis of target cells by natural killer cells. Specifically, GZMA cleaves GSDMB at Lys224 site within its interdomain linker, liberating the autoinhibited GSDMB-NT domain. This activates its pore-forming function, executing pyroptosis [48]. GSDMB has structural differences from other gasdermin proteins, exhibiting stronger self-inhibition and lower pore-forming activity. Fifteen transcripts of the GSDMB gene are currently documented in the NCBI database (Gene ID: 55876), among which five encode functionally characterized protein isoforms (GSDMB iso1 to iso5). The pore-forming activity of the GSDMB-NT domain is determined by the presence of elements derived from exon 6, which only exist in GSDMB iso3 and GSDMB iso4. The UniProt database additionally annotates a GSDMB IsoU isoform that exhibits minimal pore-forming activity. This variant requires more than 27-fold higher GZMA concentrations for proteolytic cleavage to generate the GSDMB-NT fragment, thereby achieving pyroptosis levels equivalent to those induced by GSDMB iso3 [49]. GZMB can induce GSDME-dependent pyroptosis through direct cleavage of GSDME and indirect activation of caspase-3. Additionally, the site where GZMB directly cleaves GSDME is consistent with the site where caspase-3 cleaves GSDME [50,51].

The impact of pyroptosis on renal tissue

The pathophysiological impact of pyroptosis in renal tissue depends on its specific role in kidney disease progression. Damage to different parts of the kidney tissue can lead to different diseases. Early evidence established pyroptosis as a pathogenic mechanism in diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and AKI that mediates cytolytic destruction of TECs and podocytes through gasdermin pore formation, thereby driving disease progression. Pyroptotic products have been found to be released during other kidney diseases and renal tissue injuries, but whether pyroptosis is involved requires further investigation (Table 1).

Glomerulus

Within the glomerulus, we will discuss the roles of pyroptosis in two key cell types separately: podocytes and glomerular endothelial cells.

Podocyte

Podocytes are located within the Bowman’s capsule and are terminally differentiated epithelial cells arranged on the outer surface of glomerular capillaries, with limited self-renewal ability. The loss of podocytes causes irreversible damage to renal filtration barriers and promotes scarring [52]. During differentiation, podocytes develop foot processes at regular intervals, which interlock and wrap around the glomerular basement membrane to form a filtering slit covered by a slit diaphragm. The filtration slits and slit diaphragm act as negatively charged-selective barriers during glomerular filtration. They allow most small molecules (e.g., electrolytes) to pass through, but prevent negatively charged proteins from leaking into the urine [53,54]. More evidence suggests that abnormal glomerular filtration and podocyte damage are the central causes of proteinuria and glomerulopathy in kidney diseases. In DKD, podocyte loss and death are the main pathological features in the early stage, leading to disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier [55]. Enhanced glucose and sodium reabsorption mediated by sodium/glucose cotransporter in tubular filtrate activates tubulo-glomerular feedback at the macula densa. This increases single-nephron GFR, subjecting residual nephrons to hyperfiltration-induced podocyte stress. Subsequent podocyte detachment and loss drive severe albuminuria, accelerating disease progression [56]. In DKD, pyroptosis in podocytes is primarily mediated by the non-canonical pathway. Under hyperglycemia (HG) conditions, elevated caspase-11 expression in mice activated noncanonical pyroptosis pathway. Caspase-11-dependent cleavage of GSDMD promoted podocyte injury and loss, concomitantly triggering inflammatory responses [57]. Another study identified a novel mechanism of non-canonical pyroptosis in DKD podocytes. ABCA1 deletion triggers APE1 accumulation, which impairs transcription factor activity while upregulating IRF1 and its target inflammasome genes (e.g. NLRP3), thereby initiating pyroptosis [58]. In DKD podocytes, the glycolytic intermediate dihydroxyacetone phosphate activates the mTORC1/ROS/NLRP3 axis, thereby inducing canonical pyroptosis through caspase-1-mediated GSDMD cleavage [59]. Membranous nephropathy (MN) is a glomerular disease primarily characterized by podocyte damage. The main clinical manifestation is nephrotic syndrome, accompanied by typical pathological features of subepithelial immune deposits and membrane attack complex formation [60]. A study used qRT-PCR to evaluate the genes related to pyroptosis in the glomeruli of MN patients and demonstrated that caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated canonical pathway occurs in their podocytes. Subsequent experiments revealed that complement activation exacerbates pyroptosis on podocytes, directly contributing to glomerular injury. In membranous nephropathy, complement activation triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production in podocytes, resulting in inflammasome assembly and pyroptosis [61].

Glomerular endothelial cell

Pyroptosis of glomerular endothelial cells is particularly prevalent in DKD. As the primary barrier of renal filtration, glomerular endothelial cells (GECs) are in direct contact with blood circulation and are susceptible to hyperglycemia and inflammatory factors. Histopathological evidence indicates GEC injury contributes more significantly to glomerular damage than podocyte loss during early DKD progression [62]. Both patients with DKD and murine models exhibit high levels of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) in serum and glomeruli. These NETs induce GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis in GECs through histone-mediated cell membrane charge interactions. HG directly compromises GECs and disrupts the negatively charged barrier of the glomerular filtration membrane. The study also revealed that oleylamine enhanced the negative surface charge of GECs and significantly suppressed NETs-induced pyroptosis. These findings indicated that NETs may promote pyroptosis in GECs by modulating the cell surface charge [63]. Recent studies demonstrate that under HG conditions, pyroptosis of GECs in DKD occurs through caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated non-canonical pathway [64].

Renal tubules and interstitium

The renal tubules and interstitium are important components of the kidney, with tubular epithelial cells mediating renal reabsorption. Research has found that both GSDMD and GSDME are expressed in TECs, with GSDME expressed at higher levels than GSDMD [65]. TECs function as versatile immune responders, producing and releasing bioactive mediators that ultimately contribute to interstitial inflammation and renal fibrosis [66,67]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the risk of ESRD in patients with kidney disease is more strongly correlated with tubulointerstitial injury than with glomerular injury [68,69]. Interstitial inflammation is observed at all stages of kidney disease, and the inflammatory response triggered by TEC injury may be a potential determinant of interstitial inflammation development. Currently, TEC pyroptosis predominantly occurs in AKI and DKD. In DKD research, accumulating evidence indicates that the pivotal pathological alterations determining patient prognosis have shifted from glomerular to tubular compartments, which is closely linked to tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis [70,71]. Therefore, greater attention should be paid to TEC pyroptosis in DKD. A study demonstrates that caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated canonical pathway occurs in renal tubules under HG conditions in T2DM mice, and that GSDMD switches apoptosis to pyroptosis in TLR4-mediated tubular injury [72]. Another study also found that under HG conditions, the cytotoxic pattern recognition receptor Alpha-kinase 1 may contribute to TEC injury and renal fibrosis in DKD by regulating the canonical pyroptosis pathway. Alpha-kinase 1 can function as an upstream kinase to induce the NF-κB activation [73]. The most common histopathological features in various forms of AKI include TEC death, interstitial damage, and ultimately progressing to renal fibrosis [74,75]. The evidence demonstrates that in AKI induced by cisplatin or ischemia-reperfusion injury, caspase-11 expression is upregulated, triggering GSDMD cleavage in TECs and subsequent pyroptosis [76]. Further research demonstrates that endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress develops early in renal ischemia-reperfusion injury-induced AKI, accompanied by upregulation of the biomarker CHOP. Subsequently, caspase-1 and GSDMD respond to ER stress, activating canonical pyroptosis pathway in TECs [77]. The CHOP upregulation induced by ER stress can also participate in another pyroptotic pathway. CHOP can upregulate GSDME expression. When overexpressed, GSDME is cleaved by caspase-3, triggering noncanonical pyroptosis pathway in TECs. This process is accompanied by mitochondrial damage and cytochrome c release. Cytochrome c further activates caspase-3, forming a positive anterograde feedback damage response loop that exacerbates TEC pyroptosis. Concurrently, mitochondrial damage reduces ATP production, impairing the energy supply required for renal tubular reabsorption [78,79]. In sepsis-associated AKI, endotoxin LPS is released in large quantities, activating the caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated non-canonical pathway in TECs. This activation induces inflammation and exacerbates renal tissue injury [80]. In a separate murine model of sepsis-associated AKI mouse model, purified LPS stimulation upregulated key pyroptotic mediators, including caspase-1 and IL-1β. Conversely, intravenous sialic acid injection within the first 30 min post-induction inhibited pyroptosis [81]. The contrast agent iohexol induces TEC pyroptosis via the caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated non-canonical pathway. Iohexol disrupts the epithelial membrane, enters the cytoplasm of TECs, and then activates caspase-11, triggering GSDMD cleavage and subsequent pyroptosis [82]. The caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis pathway is activated by TNFα and contributes to TEC injury in obstructive nephropathy. In a unilateral ureteral obstruction mouse model, caspase-3 activation is mediated by caspase-9 or caspase-8 via two distinct ways, leading to cleavage of highly expressed GSDME. This is followed by pyroptosis and the HMGB1 release in TECs, ultimately driving the progression of AKI to CKD, which is characterized by hydronephrosis and renal fibrosis [83]. Another study in a unilateral ureteral obstruction model indicates that the caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis pathway promotes NET formation and severe inflammation, contributing to renal fibrosis progression in later disease stages [84]. Hyperuricemic nephropathy is mainly characterized by tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis. Chronic exposure of TECs to elevated uric acid levels dysregulates autophagy via the p53 pathway. This dysregulation upregulates NLRP3 inflammasome expression, which subsequently activates caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated non-canonical pathway in TECs. In addition, this process triggers lysosomal damage and the release of cathepsin B, which in turn drives the activation of NLRP3 and the assembly of the inflammasome, ultimately leading to the sustained upregulation of key pyroptotic mediators [85]. Another study found that TECs can undergo caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated canonical pathway in a hyperuricemic environment [86]. Emerging evidence demonstrates that the caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated non-canonical pathway accelerates renal fibrosis by sustaining persistent inflammation within renal tissue. Notably, during the process, a large amount of NETs was induced by GSDMD, exacerbating kidney inflammation [87].

Treatment

Research on pyroptosis is still in the early stages, with most therapeutic candidates currently in preclinical development or early-phase clinical trials. Current therapeutic strategies prioritize pyroptosis inhibitors and gene-targeted approaches. Notably, multiple pharmacological agents used for kidney disease treatment demonstrate pyroptosis-inhibitory effects, warranting further mechanistic investigation (Table 2).

Inhibitor

The inhibitors developed for each stage of pyroptosis are the most direct therapeutic strategy. Although numerous pyroptosis inhibitors have been developed, most remain in the preclinical stage. This review focuses on pyroptosis-targeted inhibitors for kidney diseases. However, few of these pyroptosis-targeted inhibitors have advanced to clinical trials. Additional clinical studies are required to assess the therapeutic potential of these inhibitors in kidney disease, including determining optimal dosing regimens and exploring potential benefits of combination therapy versus monotherapy.

Targeting NLRP3

NLPR3 is a cytoplasmic pattern recognition receptor of the NOD-like receptor family. NLRP3 participates in immune responses by assembling inflammasomes and mediating the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. However, its abnormal activation may cause excessive amplification of inflammatory responses, thereby triggering pathological cell death and disease pathogenesis [88]. The onset of pyroptosis is divided into two main phases, initiation and activation. The initial phase requires NF-κB activation and the NLRP3 priming, while inflammasome assembly executes proteolytic maturation of proinflammatory cytokines. Therefore, directly blocking NLRP3 activation and inflammasome assembly exerts a certain inhibitory effect on pyroptosis, and NLRP3-targeted inhibitors have become promising therapeutic candidates. A 2001 study discovered that glyburide and other sulfonylurea derivatives inhibit post-translational processing of IL-1β, effectively blocking mature IL-1β formation [89]. Subsequent studies have demonstrated that glyburide exhibits inhibitory activity during NLRP3 inflammasome activation [90,91]. However, its effective inhibitory dose induces clinically significant hypoglycemia, limiting therapeutic utility. This stimulated the development of NLRP3 inhibitors based on sulfonylurea drugs [90]. In 2015, an effective selective NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (a diaryl sulfonylurea compound) was developed [92]. Subsequently, it was found that MCC950 could reduce proteinuria and disappearance of podocyte processes in lupus nephritis (LN) mouse models and reduce renal tissue inflammation. However, the levels of NLRP3 inflammasomes in renal tubules and interstitium were not significantly reduced, which may indicate the presence of pyroptosis [93]. CY-09 is a selective NLRP3 inhibitor that can exert renoprotective effects against DKD by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasomes [94]. K+ efflux is a key trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation and promotes its assembly. As such, the pharmacological inhibition of K+ channels to attenuate NLRP3 activation represents a promising strategy for suppressing pyroptosis

Targeting caspase

There are many caspase-targeted inhibitors, but few have been tested in kidney disease models. VX765 is a selective caspase-1 inhibitor. Oral VX765 administration during the late stage of unilateral ureteral obstruction reduces the caspase-1 level and M1 macrophage infiltration, thereby inhibiting pyroptosis and renal inflammation [95]. AC-YVAD-CMK, another caspase-1 inhibitor, dose-dependently inhibits NF-κB signaling and inflammatory cytokine production [96]. AC-YVAD-CMK directly inhibits the caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated canonical pathway by reducing NLRP1 inflammasome expression. This can alleviate the sepsis-induced renal tubular injury in AKI, while increasing the activity of antioxidant enzymes and reducing oxidative products [97].

Targeting gasdermin

As the executioner of pyroptosis, targeted inhibition of GSDM proteins represents a highly specific therapeutic strategy. Several GSDMD targets have been identified, among which the most important and extensively studied active site is Cys191 (murine Cys192). The development of GSDMD inhibitors began more recently and is less advanced than that of NLRP3 inhibitors, with many studies still in the experimental stage. Most of the inhibitors suppress pyroptosis by covalently modifying GSDMD Cys191 (murine Cys192) [98]. Ac-FLTD-CMK is an indirect inhibitor of GSDMD. It binds to caspase-1 and forms a complex that prevents caspase-1 from recognizing GSDMD [99]. Evidence suggests that in LN murine models, Ac-FLTD-CMK reduces glomerulosclerosis and prevents ESRD by downregulating GSDMD expression, thereby reducing the production of inflammatory factors [100].

Gene treatment

CRISPR/Cas9 is a highly precise site-specific nuclease system for genomic editing [101]. Xu et al. demonstrated that macrophages can be targeted by CLAN, a PEG-b-PLGA-based nanoparticle encapsulating mCas9 and gNLRP3, to directly disrupt the NLRP3 gene at the genomic level. The CLANmCas9/gNLRP3 offers greater benefits over inhibitors, as it can better regulate NLRP3 levels and modulate the body’s immune system without inducing inflammatory diseases [102]. Recent studies indicate that upstream stimulatory factor 2 (USF2) is involved in regulating pyroptosis in patients with AKI and LN, and is highly expressed in these conditions. In DKD, HG induces USF overexpression, activating TGF-β and exacerbating renal tissue damage. USF2 does not directly regulate pyroptosis [103]. It primarily upregulates the expression of Thrombospondin1, activates TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway, and elevates pyroptosis-related factors to levels sufficient to induce pyroptosis, thereby activating the canonical pyroptosis pathway [104,105]. Therefore, modifying USF2 or its downstream effectors (e.g., Thrombospondin 1) can alleviate renal tissue damage and reduce pyroptosis-induced renal inflammation.

Other treatment

Gene therapy and targeted therapy are currently not widely used in clinical practice, and greater attention can be paid to the ability of existing drugs to inhibit pyroptosis. A study demonstrated that the combined treatment with mycophenolate mofetil, calcineurin inhibitor, and steroids can significantly inhibit the activation of NLRP3 and caspase-1, thereby delaying the progression of LN by inhibiting the canonical pyroptosis pathway. After combined treatment, the levels of inflammatory cytokines in the peripheral blood of LN patients were significantly reduced, which attenuated renal tissue damage [100]. Hyperlipidemic conditions upregulate NLRP3 expression and pyroptotic activity. Dapagliflozin protects against DKD by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This inhibition is mediated through dapagliflozin’s upregulation of heme oxygenase-1, which subsequently attenuates palmitic acid-induced pyroptosis in MPC5 podocytes [106]. Disulfiram, an anti-alcoholism drug, covalently modifies Cys191 in GSDMD (murine Cys192) at nanomolar concentrations. This modification blocks GSDMD pore formation in the plasma membrane without affecting the activation and processing of IL-1β and GSDMD itself, thereby preventing inflammation [107]. Further study revealed that disulfiram antagonizes TLR4 and inhibits caspase-11/GSDMD-mediated non-canonical pathway in TECs, thereby downregulating caspase-1 expression in AKI [108]. In cisplatin-induced AKI, dexmedetomidine mitigates renal inflammation and tubular injury by inhibiting the caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated canonical pathway [109]. Atorvastatin alleviates contrast-induced pyroptosis in TECs by inhibiting the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway [110]. Hydrogen sulfide inhibits pyroptosis by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome formation and caspase-1 activation, thereby alleviating renal tubular injury in ischemia-reperfusion [111].

Conclusion

The classification of pyroptotic signaling pathways can no longer be simplified into four categories. Instead, classification should be defined by the GSDM family, the terminal executors of pyroptosis. Recent studies provide compelling evidence that GSDM proteins can self-cleave independently of caspases or granzymes and form plasma membrane pores to execute pyroptosis.

Recent studies reveal that palmitoylation of GSDMD at Cys191 enables its full-length form (GSDMD-FL) to form plasma membrane pores with competency similar to the GSDMD-NT, thereby executing pyroptosis. Growing evidence indicates that this process may be triggered by diverse ROS-producing pathophysiological stressors. This finding offers novel mechanistic insights into pyroptosis and uncovers a previously unrecognized role for ROS in this cell death pathway. GSDMD oligomerization and pore formation are enhanced by elevated ROS levels, while palmitoylated GSDMD induces mitochondrial ROS production. This in turn enhances GSDMD palmitoylation, establishing a feedforward loop [15,98,112]. For this newly identified pyroptotic pathway, further investigations are warranted to determine whether GSDMD-FL-mediated pore formation occurs universally across conditions, thereby exploring additional potential mechanisms of pyroptosis.

Currently, research focusing on pyroptosis has primarily centered on oncology and cardiovascular diseases, given that pyroptosis in these contexts may directly correlate with patient mortality. Kidney diseases, which are intimately associated with inflammation, exhibit poor therapeutic responsiveness and dismal prognosis. Significant research gaps persist regarding pyroptosis pathogenesis in renal tissues, particularly concerning inflammation’s role in driving renal fibrosis. Our systematic summary above also indicates that pyroptosis is more prominently involved in the pathogenesis of DKD and AKI, particularly damaging podocytes and TECs. We anticipate that future studies will expand investigations into pyroptosis inhibitors or gene therapy in kidney disease models and patient populations, ultimately providing novel therapeutic options for patients.
