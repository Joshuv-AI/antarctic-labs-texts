Battle of the Brands: A Multicenter Comparative Analysis of Outcomes in Immediate Implant-based Breast Reconstruction With Acellular Dermal Matrices
Authors: Christian X. Lava, Karen R. Li, Nicole C. Episalla, Lauren E. Berger, Rachel N. Rohrich, Claire L. Holmvik, Nichole G. Andrade, Jie Jung Shih, Kana Behari, Laura K. Tom, Rajiv P. Parikh, Samer F. Jabbour, Kenneth L. Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:We conducted a head-to-head study comparing outcomes among commonly used implant brands, Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra, used in breast reconstruction.Methods:A multicenter retrospective review of patients undergoing acellular dermal matrix–assisted implant-based breast reconstruction (IBR) with Allergan, Mentor, or Sientra implants between January 2014 and July 2022 was conducted. Primary outcomes included rates of return to the operating room for implant removal. Secondary outcomes included complications occurring within 30 days, between 31 and 60 days, and after 60 days.Results:A total of 631 patients (1155 breasts) underwent IBR: Allergan (n = 280 breasts, 24.2%), Mentor (n = 714, 61.7%), Sientra (n = 161, 13.9%). Mean age was 49.2 ± 11.4 years. Mean body mass index was 25.6 ± 5.4 kg/m2. One hundred ninety-nine (17.2%) breasts underwent adjuvant radiation and 168 (14.5%) underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Implants were most often inserted in the prepectoral plane (n = 584, 50.6%). After 60 days, there were no significant differences in overall complication rates between brands. One hundred sixty-one (13.9%) breasts underwent eventual reoperation, including unplanned explantation (n = 95, 8.2%), elective revision (n = 94, 8.1%), oncological indications (n = 20, 1.7%), and infection (n = 15, 1.3%). Indications for unplanned explantation included infection (n = 26, 2.3%), capsular contracture (n = 15, 1.3%), and nonhealing wounds (n = 14, 1.2%). There were no differences in other complications or reoperations between cohorts. Body mass index, diabetes mellitus, smoking history, chemotherapy, radiation, skin-sparing mastectomy, type of incision, and tissue expander use were independent predictors of postoperative complications.Conclusions:Low rates of complications among Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra implants in IBR support their continued use. No single brand was superior to others regarding complication profile in our study. Patient characteristics and operative approach seem more predictive of postoperative outcomes than the implant brand alone.

Takeaways

Question: Do outcomes differ across implant brands used in immediate implant-based breast reconstruction (IBR)?

Findings: In a study of 631 patients (1155 breasts), complication and reoperation rates were similar across Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra implants. Patient and surgical factors, such as body mass index, diabetes, smoking, incision type, and prosthesis placement, were stronger predictors of complications than implant brand.

Meaning: Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra implants seem clinically equivalent in implant-based breast reconstruction. Implant choice can be based on surgeon preference and availability, whereas individualized risk assessment remains key to optimizing outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

According to the National Breast Cancer Foundation, around 277,000 cases of invasive breast cancer were reported in the United States in 2020.1 Consequently, many women choose to undergo mastectomy with implant-based reconstruction (IBR). IBR accounts for approximately 78% of reconstructions.2 It is favored for its short procedure time, limited scarring, lack of a donor site, and relatively fast recovery time.3–5

Although IBR is associated with serious complications, including infection, capsular contracture, and reconstructive failure, these risks have prompted investigation into their causes and mitigation strategies.5,6 Despite widespread use, there is a lack of high-quality evidence comparing long-term outcomes across implant brands used in reconstruction. Doren et al7 reported similar complication, reoperation, explantation, and capsular contracture rates across Food and Drug Administration–approved silicone implant brands: Allergan (AbbVie Inc., Irvine, CA), Mentor (Johnson & Johnson MedTech, Irvine, CA), and Sientra (Sientra, Inc., Irvine, CA). However, only 16% of the study population underwent breast reconstruction, underscoring the need for studies focused on oncological reconstruction cohorts.7

Within the MedStar Health System, we established an IBR registry. Although many complications stem from patient and surgical factors, implant selection remains clinically relevant due to differences in device characteristics, manufacturer claims, and surgeon preference. We conducted a large retrospective head-to-head study comparing rates of outcomes across Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra.

METHODS

Study Design

This study was approved by the Georgetown-MedStar institutional review board (STUDY00006171). We performed a retrospective cohort study of IBR patients from January 2014 to July 2022 across 4 hospitals within the MedStar Health System. Patients were identified via CPT code 15777. Patients were included if they (1) were 18 years or older; (2) underwent mastectomy for treatment or prophylaxis; (3) underwent immediate breast reconstruction, using either a direct-to-implant (DTI) approach or a staged tissue expander (TE); (4) underwent acellular dermal matrix (ADM)–assisted reconstruction during the first stage of reconstruction; and (5) had at least 3 months of follow-up. Both DTI and TE approaches were included to reflect real-world reconstructive patterns across manufacturers. Excluding TEs would have disproportionately reduced the sample size for certain manufacturers and limited the generalizability of interbrand comparisons. Both textured and smooth implants were included. Due to documentation limitations, surface-type comparisons were not performed. Patients were excluded if they (1) were younger than 18 years; (2) did not receive ADM; (3) underwent reconstruction following a prior failed reconstruction attempt; (4) underwent delayed reconstruction, defined as reconstruction initiated after recovery from mastectomy, regardless of whether a TE or DTI technique was used; and (5) had less than 3 months of follow-up. Patients were stratified by implant manufacturer at the index IBR (ie, Allergan, Mentor, Sientra) and analyzed by patient and by breast. Implant selection was based on surgeon preference and availability rather than randomization. Additionally, implant use shifted during the study period as new products were adopted, leading to temporal variation in brand distribution.

Data Collection

Electronic medical records were reviewed to collect demographic information, medical comorbidities, oncological history, surgical history, and postoperative outcomes. Charlson Comorbidity Index and 5-Factor Modified Frailty Index scores were calculated.8,9

Primary outcomes included rates of unplanned explantation or return to the operating room (RTOR) for implant removal. RTOR for implant removal was defined as the explantation of an implant for any reason. If the implant was immediately replaced during the same operation, this was considered a salvage procedure but still counted under RTOR for implant removal to ensure consistency in reporting.10 Secondary outcomes included postoperative complications occurring within 30 days, between 31 and 60 days, and after 60 days, including cellulitis, hematoma, seroma (defined as a noninfectious serous fluid accumulation in dead space), delayed wound healing (defined as incisions that failed to close within 30 d postoperatively), surgical site infection (SSI), red breast syndrome, capsular contracture, delayed reoperation (≥90 d), and breast-implant associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL). Complications were assessed from the time of implant placement (DTI or second-stage TE exchange).

Statistical Analysis

A complete case analysis was conducted, excluding missing EMR data. Shapiro–Wilk tests assessed normality. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± SD if normally distributed or median (interquartile range [IQR]) if not. Group comparisons used unpaired 2-tailed t tests or Mann-Whitney tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson χ2 or Fisher exact tests (n < 7). Multiple logistic regression included significant covariates and known predictors, excluding variables with perfect prediction or collinearity. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses were performed in StataBE (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas), with a P value of less than 0.05 considered significant.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

A total of 1132 patients were identified using CPT code 15777 for ADM placement. Exclusions included nonbreast ADM use (n = 125), delayed IBR (n = 269), and unknown implant manufacturer (n = 107). This final cohort included 631 patients (1155 breasts). Of these, Allergan was used in 280 (24.2%) breasts, Mentor in 714 (61.7%) breasts, and Sientra in 161 (13.9%) breasts.

Mean age was 49.2 ± 11.4 years. The Mentor cohort had the largest proportion of patients compared with the other 2 brands (Allergan: 49.4% versus Mentor: 58.8% versus Sientra: 52.2%; P < 0.001). Mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.6 ± 5.4 kg/m2. Patients in the Sientra cohort had a significantly lower BMI (Allergan: 26.4 ± 6.0 versus Mentor: 26.0 ± 5.9 versus Sientra: 24.2 ± 4.1 kg/m2; P = 0.003). Obesity was most prevalent in the Allergan cohort (Allergan: 24.7% versus Mentor: 19.9% versus Sientra: 8.9%; P = 0.008), consistent with the higher BMI in this group. Median Charlson Comorbidity Index and 5-Factor Modified Frailty Index scores were 2.0 (IQR 1.0) and 0.0 (IQR 0.0), respectively (Table 1).

Operative Details

There were no significant differences in the performing breast surgeon (P = 0.225) and plastic surgeon (P = 0.738) across implant groups. Mastectomy types differed significantly, with nipple-sparing mastectomy occurring more often in the Sientra group (n = 120, 74.5%) compared with Mentor (n = 452, 63.3%) and Allergan (n = 139, 49.6%; P < 0.001). Although inframammary fold incisions were most used (n = 533, 46.1%), incision type differed significantly by cohort (P < 0.001). The Allergan group had a significantly higher average resection weight (Allergan: 611.1 ± 401.5 g) compared with Mentor (489.7 ± 321.9 g) and Sientra (390.5 ± 273.4 g; P < 0.001).

All breasts had ADMs, using SurgiMend (Integra LifeSciences), AlloDerm (Allergan/AbbVie Inc.), or DermACELL (Stryker Corporation).11 TEs were most used in the Allergan group (Allergan: 75.7% versus Mentor: 36.7% versus Sientra: 34.2%; P < 0.0001), whereas DTIs were most common in the Mentor cohort (Allergan: 23.9% versus Mentor: 61.9% versus Sientra: 65.8%; P < 0.001). Prostheses were most commonly placed in the prepectoral plane (n = 584, 50.6%), followed by the subpectoral plane (n = 571, 49.4%). However, subpectoral placement was less frequent in the Mentor cohort (P < 0.001). Mean initial and final TE fill volumes were 150.0 ± 150.0 and 400.0 ± 200.0 mL, respectively. Breasts in the Allergan group had a significantly higher initial fill volume (P = 0.009), whereas those in the Mentor cohort had a higher final fill volume (P = 0.046). The mean time to TE exchange was 3.9 ± 3.7 months, with no significant differences across cohorts (P = 0.734). The median implant size was largest in the Allergan cohort (Allergan: 485.0 [IQR 225.0] versus Mentor: 450.0 [IQR 200.0] versus Sientra: 425.0 [IQR 180.0] mL; P = 0.005) (Table 3).

Complications

At 30 days, the incidence of overall complications (including a 2-stage procedure with time zero defined as the exchange for the permanent implant and the DTI approach) was significantly higher among Sientra breasts than among Allergan and Mentor breasts (21.1% versus 19.7% versus 12.9%, respectively; P = 0.025). The most common complication was delayed healing (n = 87, 7.5%), followed by seroma (n = 60, 5.2%), mastectomy flap necrosis (n = 51, 4.4%), hematoma (n = 28, 2.4%), cellulitis (n = 23, 2.0%), and SSI (n = 20, 1.7%). Mastectomy flap necrosis was significantly higher in the Mentor group compared with the Allergan and Sientra groups (5.3% versus 3.7% versus 2.5%, respectively; P = 0.033). There were no other differences in 30-day complication rates between cohorts.

At 31–60 days, there were no statistically significant differences between Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra in overall complication rates (8.6% versus 6.4% versus 3.1%, respectively; P = 0.521). The most common complication was seroma (n = 34, 2.9%), followed by cellulitis (n = 17, 1.5%), SSI (n = 16, 1.4%), and delayed healing (n = 14, 1.2%). At 60 days, overall complication rates did not differ significantly by implant manufacturer (P = 0.965). The most common complication at this time point was cellulitis (n = 26, 2.3%), followed by seroma (n = 23, 2.0%), SSI (n = 16, 1.4%), dehiscence (n = 13, 1.1%), and delayed healing (n = 11, 1.0%). There were no other differences in 31- to 60- or more than 60-day complication rates between cohorts.

Collectively, 161 (13.9%) breasts underwent delayed reoperation, including 95 (8.2%) breasts that necessitated unplanned explantation of the permanent implant. The incidence of unplanned explantation was highest in the Sientra group (n = 20, 12.4%), but it did not differ significantly between Mentor and Allergan groups (n = 59, 8.3% and n = 16, 5.7%, respectively; P = 0.573). Reoperation included elective cosmetic revision (n = 94, 8.1%), oncological indications (n = 20, 1.7%), and infection (ie, cases in which infection did not result in explantation; n = 15, 1.3%). Unplanned explantation was most commonly due to infection (n = 26, 2.3%), capsular contracture (n = 15, 1.3%), and nonhealing wounds (n = 14, 1.2%). Capsular contracture occurred in 132 (11.4%) breasts, with most classified as Baker class II (n = 32, 24.2%), followed by class III (n = 23, 17.4%) and class I (n = 21, 15.9%), when classification data were available. In 1 (0.1%) case, BIA-ALCL was suspected 52 months after DTI reconstruction with a textured Mentor implant. Polymerase chain reaction analysis revealed CD30(+)/ALK(−) atypical lymphoid cells. However, after switching to a smooth implant, final capsule pathology confirmed an oligoclonal T-cell response, not T-cell lymphoma (Tables 4, 5).

Univariate and Multivariate Linear Regression

In univariate logistic regression, postoperative complications were associated with implant manufacturer, age, BMI, adjuvant radiation, mastectomy type, and incision type. However, in multivariate analysis, implant manufacturer was not an independent predictor of complications. Instead, factors such as BMI, diabetes mellitus, smoking history, and chemotherapy were significant. Higher BMI was linked to increased odds of delayed wound healing (OR 1.05; 95% CI, 1.0–1.1; P = 0.026), cellulitis (OR 1.83; 95% CI, 1.75–1.92; P = 0.004), SSI (OR 1.08; 95% CI, 1.03–1.13; P = 0.002), and RTOR for implant removal (OR 1.05; 95% CI, 1.0–1.1; P = 0.020). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was associated with delayed wound healing (OR 2.82; 95% CI, 1.3–6.15; P = 0.009), and adjuvant chemotherapy with cellulitis (OR 3.24; 95% CI, 1.11–9.64; P = 0.030). Diabetes mellitus predicted mastectomy flap necrosis (OR 4.57; 95% CI, 1.91–10.92; P = 0.002) and unplanned explantation (OR 2.21; 95% CI, 1.11–4.5; P = 0.024). Smoking history predicted unplanned reoperation (OR 1.72; 95% CI, 1.05–2.82; P = 0.024).

Surgical factors also influenced outcomes. Wise-pattern mastectomy incisions increased the risk of unplanned explantation (OR 2.48; 95% CI, 1.23–5.02; P = 0.011). TE-based reconstructions demonstrated higher odds of seroma (OR 3.36; 95% CI, 1.91–5.91; P < 0.001), dehiscence (OR 3.59; 95% CI, 1.09–93; P = 0.034), and cellulitis (OR 5.12; 95% CI, 1.99–13.17; P = 0.002) compared with DTI reconstructions. Subpectoral/dual-plane placement was associated with hematoma (OR 2.47; 95% CI, 0.99–6.17; P = 0.049) and flap necrosis (OR 2.05; 95% CI, 1.03–4.11; P = 0.039). The receiver operating characteristic curve analysis yielded an area under the curve of 0.6 or greater, indicating at least modest discrimination across all logistic regression models. DISCUSSION

This study focuses on 3 silicone breast implant manufacturers approved by the US Food and Drug Administration: Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra. This retrospective study of 651 patients (1155 breasts) undergoing IBR using Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra found similar postoperative complication and reoperation rates among manufacturers. Furthermore, implant manufacturer was not found to be a predictor of postoperative complications.

Although Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra implants differ slightly in their processing and treatment, they all use similar types of shell materials and fillers, which are primarily silicone gels with varying degrees of cross-linking to achieve different levels of cohesivity, softness, and firmness. The unique cross-linking and processing methods developed by each manufacturer result in implants with distinct properties.12–14 However, despite minor differences in composition, all 3 implant types demonstrated comparable short- and long-term outcomes with low complication rates, reinforcing their clinical equivalence. Variability in patient comorbidities may still introduce residual confounding, and our findings should be interpreted with this in mind. Importantly, higher implant volumes and initial TE fills may predispose patients to flap necrosis. In our cohort, these decisions were guided by intraoperative assessment of perfusion rather than implant brand.

Although overall complication and reoperation rates did not significantly differ between implant brands, there was a statistically significant difference in capsular contracture rates (Table 4). However, this may be attributable to differences in patient comorbidities or surgical techniques rather than the implant manufacturer itself, as implant brand was not an independent predictor of capsular contracture in multivariate analysis. This aligns with a study conducted by Doren et al,7 who evaluated outcomes of Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra contoured cohesive gel implants for primary augmentation, secondary augmentation, and primary reconstruction. In reconstructive patients (16.0% of their total cohort), Doren et al7 reported a capsular contracture rate of 13.3%, leading to a 7.1% reoperation rate for this complication. For aesthetic breast augmentation, Martin et al15 have reported lower rates of capsular contracture when comparing Mentor and Allergan implants (2.0%). Our study extends these findings to a larger reconstructive population undergoing IBR, reporting similar rates of capsular contracture (11.4%) and related explantation (2.3%) across brands.7 Among all 3 manufacturers, Doren et al7 also observed similar rates of reoperation (57% in reconstructive patients) and explantation (9.7%). In our study, we observed lower rates of reoperation (13.9%) and explantation (8.9%); however, like the findings of Doren et al,7 these outcomes did not vary significantly based on implant brand. Additionally, the implant rupture rate did not differ significantly by implant brand: our observed rupture rate for Allergan (2.5%) aligned with the published range of 0.3%–6.4%,16–18 Mentor (2.7%) with the reported rate of 2.1%,19 and Sientra (1.2%) with the documented rate of 1.8% (P = 0.355).20 Thus, our results support long-term clinical equivalence across brands, indicating that it would be reasonable to select implant types according to surgeon preferences and financial considerations. Notably, the universal use of ADM in this cohort may have influenced complication rates, as ADM has been associated with an increased risk of complications such as seroma and infection in previous studies.11

The impact of adjunct treatments such as radiation and chemotherapy on breast implant integrity and longevity is a critical concern for patients and surgeons.21–23 In a large prospective cohort study (730 patients), Eriksson et al24 found that IBR failure occurred in 5.7% of nonirradiated cases, 25.0% after prior radiotherapy, and 14.8% after postoperative radiotherapy (P < 0.001). In terms of cancer treatment, Kronowitz and Robb25 reported that immediate IBR may limit the effective delivery of radiation to targeted tissues, potentially necessitating delayed reconstruction for optimal outcomes. Our findings, however, revealed a 6.4% cancer recurrence rate, which is comparatively lower than some recent studies.26,27 Neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy seems to have no effect on complication rates between prosthetic versus autologous reconstruction, as demonstrated in a study by Seth et al.28 In contrast, our multivariate logistic regression demonstrated that neoadjuvant chemotherapy was associated with delayed wound healing, whereas adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with cellulitis. This may be due to the negative impact chemotherapy may have on wound healing and soft tissue integrity, possibly contributing to higher rates of wound-related or infectious complications.29,30 This highlights the importance of careful patient selection and planning for patients considering IBR, especially those requiring radiation or chemotherapy.

In multivariate analysis, several comorbidity and clinical covariates were predictive of postoperative complications in IBR. First, elevated BMI was linked to delayed wound healing, cellulitis, SSI, and RTOR for implant removal, consistent with existing literature.31 Additionally, diabetes mellitus and smoking history were predictive of flap necrosis and unplanned explantation, highlighting the impact of these conditions on wound healing and the importance of close postoperative monitoring for these patients. Furthermore, operative factors such as incision type, prosthesis type, and insertion plane also influenced complications. DTI procedures had lower odds of seroma, dehiscence, mastectomy flap necrosis, and cellulitis compared with TE-based IBRs, supporting a shift toward DTI reconstruction in appropriate candidates.32–34 The higher complication rates in TE-based reconstructions likely reflect both the 2-stage nature of reconstruction and increased baseline risk in patients selected for TE. Our findings parallel those of Lam et al,35 who demonstrated that outcomes differ between fresh mastectomy wounds and healed wounds at the time of TE exchange. The higher use of TE in the Allergan cohort likely reflects surgeon caution in higher risk patients, which may explain the paradox of higher baseline risk yet lower explantation rates.35 Similarly, we observed that subpectoral/dual-plane placement was associated with higher odds of hematoma development and mastectomy flap necrosis, potentially due to the deeper dissection required and the increased total operative time, respectively.36,37 These findings highlight the benefits of prepectoral prosthesis placement. These results highlight the importance of thorough preoperative assessment and individualized surgical planning to reduce complications and optimize breast reconstruction outcomes, underscoring that implant selection should be based on patient factors rather than manufacturer.

Limitations

This study’s retrospective design precludes causality between implant brand and outcomes. Heterogeneity in cancer stage, adjuvant treatment, mastectomy surgeon, operative technique, and follow-up may confound results. Detailed data on implant texture, shape (round versus anatomical), and surface characteristics were unavailable, which may affect risks such as BIA-ALCL and capsular contracture. The ADM type was not consistently captured. However, prior multicenter data from our institution found no independent association between ADM brand (eg, SurgiMend PRS, AlloDerm, or DermACELL) and postoperative complications after adjustment, suggesting that patient and operative factors predominate.11 Including both TE and DTI reconstructions increases heterogeneity but reflects real-world practice and improves generalizability. Additionally, the 3-month minimum and variable follow-up intervals may be insufficient to capture delayed complications, such as late capsular contracture or implant rupture. Finally, the uneven distribution of implant brands may limit generalizability. Nonetheless, this study provides important insights into the comparative safety and efficacy of Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra implants in ADM-assisted immediate 2-stage and DTI reconstruction.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large retrospective study of 631 patients (1155 breasts) undergoing immediate ADM-assisted IBR, the use of Allergan, Mentor, and Sientra implants showed comparable complications and reoperation rates, with relatively low complications overall. Multivariate analysis confirmed clinical equivalence among implant brands, highlighting that patient factors and surgical technique, rather than manufacturer, more strongly influence postoperative outcomes. Thus, implant selection may reasonably be guided by patient and surgeon preference.
