Differences in cognitive control under negative emotion priming among college students with varying emotional regulation abilities
Authors: Jiangli Jiao, Shaolan Zhao, Xiaojuan Dong, Yi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUsing the AX-CPT paradigm, this study investigates the differences in cognitive control under negative emotion priming among college students with different emotional regulation abilities in reactive control mode and proactive control mode. The paradigm comprises four experimental conditions AY, BY, BX, and AX trials designed to distinguish between proactive control and reactive control modes.The experiment employed a 2 (high emotional regulation ability group vs. low emotional regulation ability group) × 4 (AY, BY, BX vs. AX) mixed experimental design, with reaction times and error rates recorded using E-prime software. The results revealed that: (1) the error rate was significantly lower in the high emotional regulation ability group compared to the low emotional regulation ability group (2) subjects displayed significantly longer reaction times on AY sequences than on BX sequences, with a significantly higher error rate on AY sequences; (3) in reactive control mode, the average reaction time for the high emotional regulation ability group was significantly shorter than that for the low emotional regulation ability group, with a higher d’-target value; in proactive control mode, the high emotional regulation ability group had a significantly higher d’-context value than the low emotional regulation ability group. These findings indicate that under negative emotion priming, college students with varying emotional regulation abilities exhibit enhanced proactive control and weakened reactive control. college students with high emotional regulation abilities exhibit superior cognitive control in both reactive and proactive control modes compared to those with low emotional regulation abilities.Keywords: Negative emotion, Emotional regulation ability, Reactive control, Proactive control

Preface

Cognitive control refers to an individual’s ability to inhibit task-unrelated dominant response tendencies and control interference from distracting stimuli [1, 2]. According to the dual mechanisms of control theory, cognitive control comprises two modes: proactive control and reactive control. Proactive control, which is goal-oriented, involves the active maintenance of goal-relevant information and the optimization of attention resource allocation at the early stages of a task, functioning as an “early selection mechanism.” Conversely, reactive control, which is demand-driven, mobilizes cognitive resources to resolve cognitive conflicts in situations of conflict interference, acting as a “late correction mechanism” [3].

Negative emotion refers to the transient states such as sadness, fear, and anger experienced by individuals in certain situations [4, 5]. The impact of negative emotions on cognitive control is a prominent area of interest within the field of emotion-cognition research. Several studies have focused on the detrimental effects of negative emotions on cognitive control [6, 7]. For example, Cohen et al. [8] utilized the Go/Nogo paradigm and found that subjects exhibited a lower accuracy rate on Nogo trials when in a state of negative emotion; Wang and Lv [9] employed ERP technology and also observed that negative emotions of varying motivational intensities interfere with cognitive control during both the conflict monitoring and inhibition stages. However, recent research has found that negative emotions can also have beneficial effects on cognition; moderate negative emotions can help individuals focus on current tasks and exhibit better cognitive behavior, even helping to avoid dangers and injuries, thus serving a protective role for the organism [10, 11]. For instance, an experimental study by Gupta and Singh [12] found that in the Go/Nogo paradigm, when angry faces were used as stop signals, subjects had shorter stop-signal reaction times and better cognitive control performance. Jia et al. [13] also discovered that high-arousal negative emotion priming could enhance subjects’ recognition and monitoring of conflicts, thus better completing cognitive tasks.Furthermore, studies have shown that a 12-week program of moderate-to-high-intensity physical exercise can improve cognitive control performance in college students experiencing high psychological stress by enhancing their emotion regulation ability. This finding underscores both the feasibility and importance of improving emotion regulation skills [14]. Fang et al. utilized modified Stroop and Flanker tasks and found that negative emotional cues similarly impaired performance under conditions of low cognitive control. However, when either control system (emotional or cognitive) was activated, performance showed comparable and significant improvement, supporting the view that cognitive and emotional control share underlying mechanisms [15].

The reason for the discrepancies in previous research may be due to several factors. First, cognitive control involves a series of complex information processing stages, and related studies often treat cognitive control as a signal psychological process. However, Braver et al. [3] suggest that cognitive control includes both proactive and reactive control modes, each involving different stages and functions of cognitive processing. The impact of negative emotions may vary between these two modes. This distinction is crucial for understanding how and why negative emotions influence cognitive control differently, potentially leading to mixed findings across studies. Secondly, related studies often do not differentiate between specific types of subjects, particularly differences in emotional regulation abilities related to personality traits that are relevant to emotions. This variability can influence subjects’ emotional responses: individuals with high emotional regulation abilities can effectively manage negative emotions, whereas those with low emotional regulation abilities may struggle to disengage from negative emotions and thus remain affected by them [16]. Recent studies further support this perspective, indicating that individuals with higher cognitive control capacity typically possess stronger emotion regulation abilities. These individuals can allocate cognitive resources more efficiently, thereby maintaining better cognitive functioning under emotionally challenging conditions [17]. This impact further increases their cognitive resources allocated to emotion regulation, thereby reducing the resources available for cognitive tasks, which in turn impairs cognitive function [18, 19].

Additionally, the AX-CPT paradigm effectively differentiates between the two modes of cognitive control [20, 21]. This paradigm is structured around “cue stimuli (A, B) + probe stimuli (X, Y)” to form AX, AY, BX, and BY sequences, where the AX sequence accounts for 70% and the other sequences each account for 10%. Responses to AX are considered target responses, while responses to the other sequences are non-target responses. The proportion of sequences leads to a strong predominant response tendency towards “A” and “X”. Consequently, in the AY sequence, following the appearance of “A”, the response depends on whether the probe stimulus is “X” or “Y”. Subjects tend to engage the reactive control, making an immediate response to overcome the predominant response tendency to “A”. In the BX sequence, after “B” appears, regardless of whether the probe stimulus is “X” or “Y”, the response is always a non-target response, and subjects tend to engage the proactive control, enhancing the maintenance of the “B” cue information in order to overcome the predominant response tendency to “X”. Performance in the AY and BX sequences serves as an indicator of cognitive control under reactive and proactive control, respectively [20, 21]. The discriminability index (d’-target) is unaffected by response tendencies, allowing for a separation of subjects’ sensitivity from their judgment criteria, thereby providing a more objective measure of cognitive control performance. In this paradigm, the discriminability index of the probe stimulus is used as an indicator for the reactive control mode, while the discriminability index of the cue stimulus (d’-context) serves as an indicator for the proactive control mode [21]. This study further analyzes the differences in cognitive control across these two modes through the discriminability index.

In summary, although existing studies have explored the relationship between negative emotions and cognitive control, the current literature exhibits two major shortcomings that lead to inconsistent conclusions and unclear mechanisms. First, most studies treat cognitive control as a unitary construct. However, according to the Dual Mechanisms of Cognitive Control theory [20], cognitive control comprises two distinct modes—proactive and reactive control. While the influence of negative emotion on these two modes may vary, prior research has largely failed to distinguish and examine them separately. Therefore, this study employs the AX-CPT paradigm to differentiate cognitive control into proactive and reactive modes, aiming to provide a more nuanced investigation into the differential effects of negative emotion on distinct subprocesses of cognitive control. Second, previous research has overlooked the critical individual difference variable of emotion regulation ability. This variable may determine whether individuals can effectively allocate cognitive resources when confronted with negative emotions [16]. Individuals with high emotion regulation ability can manage emotions more efficiently, thereby conserving cognitive resources for subsequent tasks, whereas those with low emotion regulation ability may become entangled in emotional experiences, depleting cognitive resources originally available for task performance [19]. Thus, this study further incorporates emotion regulation ability as an individual difference variable to examine its role in the process through which negative emotion influences dual cognitive control. This approach will help explain inconsistencies in previous findings and reveal underlying mechanisms from an individual differences perspective.

Based on the above analysis, under negative emotion induction, individuals with different levels of emotion regulation ability may exhibit differences in proactive and reactive control. We propose the following hypotheses: Under negative emotion induction, participants will generally demonstrate stronger proactive control and weaker reactive control. That is, performance in the reactive control condition (AY trials) will be worse than that in the proactive control condition (BX trials). The high emotion regulation ability group will outperform the low emotion regulation ability group in both control modes. Specifically, they will show shorter reaction times and higher d′-target values in AY trials, and higher d′-context values in BX trials.

Methods

Participants

Participants were selected using the Chinese version of the “Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale” (DERS), compiled by Gratz and Roemer [22] and translated by Wang and others [23] in China. This scale has a test-retest reliability of 0.86 and an internal consistency reliability of 0.89. The questionnaire employs a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 [almost never] to 5 [always]), with higher total scores indicating greater difficulties in emotion regulation.A total of 210 university students were recruited to fill out the scale. After discarding questionnaires from participants who did not answer seriously, 198 effective questionnaires were retrieved, yielding an effective rate of 94.29%. The high emotional regulation ability group consisted of the bottom 27% scoring participants, and the low emotional regulation ability group consisted of the top 27% scoring participants, with 29 participants in each group. There was a significant difference in the average scores between the two groups (t(58) = 22.16, P < 0.001), with the low emotional regulation ability group scoring significantly higher (111.16 ± 7.25) than the high emotional regulation ability group (58.87 ± 10.70). The age of participants ranged from 19 to 23 years (M = 21.00, SD = 1.41). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, were not color blind, had no history of psychiatric disorders, were right-handed, had not previously participated in similar experiments, and had signed informed consent forms.

Experimental materials

The stimuli used in the experiment were the four uppercase English letters A, B, X, and Y, with a vertical visual angle of 3.8 degrees and a horizontal visual angle of 2.9 degrees. These were organized into four sequences: AX, AY, BX, and BY, using the “cue stimulus (A, B) + probe stimulus (X, Y)” format. The AX sequence accounted for 70% of trials, while the other sequences each accounted for 10% [21, 24]. From the International Affective Picture System (IAPS), 200 negative emotional images were selected to serve as the negative emotion priming material. According to the self-assessment model’s 1 to 9 emotional rating scale, the selection criterion for the negative emotional images was a pleasantness rating below 5. After the selection of images, 30 university students (not participants in the formal experiment) were randomly chosen to evaluate the images. The evaluation procedure was programmed in E-prime: it started with a fixation point “+” displayed for 500 ms, followed by an emotional image displayed for 2000ms. Participants were required to rate the pleasantness and arousal of the image on a 1 to 9 scale on an answer sheet, and then press a key to proceed to the next trial. The evaluation results indicated that the average valence of the negative emotion images was 3.57 ± 0.34, and the average arousal was 6.33 ± 0.42.

Experimental design

The experiment employed a 2 (participant type: high emotional regulation ability group vs. low emotional regulation ability group) × 4 (sequence type: AY, BY, BX vs. AX) mixed experimental design. The first variable, participant type, was a between-subjects variable, distinguishing between those with high and low emotional regulation abilities. The second variable, sequence type, was a within-subjects variable, encompassing the different stimulus sequences presented to all participants. The dependent variables in the study were the participants’ reaction times, error rates, and discriminability indices. This design allows for the assessment of both intergroup differences in emotional regulation capabilities and intragroup responses to varying cognitive control demands induced by different sequences.

Experimental procedure

The experimental procedure was programmed using E-prime software, following the protocols established by Von et al. [25] and Masuyama et al. [26]. The sequence of events for a single trial is as follows: A fixation point is displayed for 500 ms with both vertical and horizontal visual angles at 0.5 degrees.This is followed by a blank screen for 400 ms.A negative emotion image is then displayed for 500 ms.Another blank screen appears for 400 ms.Subsequently, a cue stimulus, either “A” or “B”, is presented randomly for 1000 ms.After a further blank screen of 4000 ms, a probe stimulus, “X” or “Y”, is displayed randomly for 1000 ms.The sequence ends with a final blank screen of 300 ms (as shown in Fig. 1). During the experiment, half of the participants were assigned the task to press the “F” key in response to the AX sequence (when the cue stimulus is “A” and the probe stimulus is “X”), and to press the “J” key in all other sequences (AY, BX, BY). The other half of the participants received the opposite key assignment.Before the start of the main experiment, participants underwent a practice session consisting of 20 trials to familiarize themselves with the procedure. The main experiment consisted of 200 trials in total. This setup ensures that participants are well-prepared and understand the task demands, aiming to measure their reaction times, error rates, and discriminability indices accurately under the influence of negative emotional priming and different cue-probe configurations.

Analysis of average reaction times and error rates

Due to the focus of this study on data from the AY sequence and BX sequence, a 2 × 2 repeated measures ANOVA was conducted on the average reaction times. The results showed that the main effect of participant type was not significant, F(1, 56) = 3.51, p = 0.07, indicating no substantial differences between groups overall. However, the main effect of sequence type was significant, F(1, 56) = 35.12, p = 0.000, η2 p = 0.39, with participants exhibiting significantly longer average reaction times in the AY sequence (521 ± 10 ms) compared to the BX sequence (436 ± 10 ms). The interaction between participant type and sequence type was significant, F(1, 56) = 7.68, p = 0.000, η2 p = 0.12, suggesting that the impact of sequence type on reaction times varied across different participant groups.

Further analysis of simple effects revealed that within the AY sequence, the high emotional regulation ability group had a significantly faster average reaction time (488 ± 63 ms) than the low emotional regulation ability group (554 ± 70 ms), t(56) = −3.26, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.85. In contrast, there were no significant differences in average reaction times between the groups in the BX sequence, t(56) = 0.61, p = 0.54. These findings highlight differential effects of emotional regulation ability on reactive and proactive control.

For the analysis of error rates, a 2 × 2 repeated measures ANOVA was conducted, revealing several significant findings: The main effect of participant type was significant, F(1, 56) = 6.80, p = 0.03, η2 p = 0.06, indicating that the high emotional regulation ability group had significantly lower error rates compared to the low emotional regulation ability group.The main effect of sequence type was also significant, F(1, 56) = 13.67, p = 0.000, η2 p = 0.11, with the error rates being significantly higher in the AY sequence than in the BX sequence.The interaction between participant type and sequence type was not significant, F(1, 56) = 1.81, p = 0.47, suggesting that the differences in error rates between the sequences were consistent across different participant groups. Analysis of discriminability indices

In the AX-CPT paradigm, the discriminability index for probe stimuli (d’-target) serves as an indicator of cognitive control under reactive control. This index is calculated as d’-target = Z(AX hits) - Z(AY false alarms), where a higher value indicates greater sensitivity to the probe stimuli and stronger cognitive control in reactive mode. Conversely, the discriminability index for cue stimuli (d’-context) acts as an indicator of cognitive control under proactive control. This index is calculated as d’-context = Z(AX hits) - Z(BX false alarms), with a higher value representing greater sensitivity to the cue stimuli and enhanced cognitive control in proactive mode [21]. To further examine differences in cognitive control abilities between reactive and proactive control among participants, discriminability indices for probe and cue stimuli were calculated according to signal detection theory. The process for calculating the probe stimulus discrimination index is as follows: first, calculate the hit rate of the AX sequence, which corresponds to the Z value for the correct rate on the AX sequence, and then subtract the false alarm rate of the AY sequence (the Z value corresponding to the error rate on AY sequence). Next, in order to calculate the discrimination index of cue stimulus, follow this process: calculate the hit rate of AX sequence (Z value corresponding to correct rate on AX sequence), then subtract the false alarm rate of BX sequence (Z value corresponding to error rate on BX sequence). The Z values are obtained through reference to a PZO conversion table.

An independent samples t-test was then conducted on the discriminability indices of the two groups. The results (as illustrated in Fig. 2) revealed significant differences: There was a significant difference in the d’-target values between the groups, t(56) = 3.45, p = 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.61, indicating that the high emotional regulation ability group had a significantly higher d’-target value (2.88 ± 0.78) compared to the low emotional regulation ability group (2.13 ± 0.61). This suggests that participants with higher emotional regulation abilities exhibit greater sensitivity to probe stimuli and thus stronger cognitive control in the reactive mode.Similarly, the d’-context values also showed a significant difference between the groups, t(56) = 2.41, p = 0.019, Cohen’s d = 0.33, with the high emotional regulation ability group (3.41 ± 0.63) having a significantly higher d’-context value than the low emotional regulation ability group (2.86 ± 0.57). This indicates enhanced sensitivity to cue stimuli and stronger cognitive control in the proactive mode among those with higher emotional regulation capabilities.

Analysis and discussion

The results of this study indicate that under negative emotion priming, there were no differences in reaction times between participants with high and low emotional regulation abilities across the two modes of cognitive control. However, a significant difference was observed in error rates, where the high emotional regulation ability group exhibited significantly lower error rates than the low emotional regulation ability group. These findings suggest that cognitive control under negative emotional priming is influenced by an individual’s emotional regulation ability. Individuals with higher emotional regulation abilities, due to their superior capability to manage emotions, can effectively reduce cognitive impairments caused by negative emotions, thus performing better in tasks requiring cognitive control. These results are consistent with previous research. Düsing et al. [16], employing the Stroop paradigm, investigated the role of emotional regulation ability in modulating the effects of negative emotional regulation on interference inhibition in individuals with depression. They found that the trait of high emotional regulation ability compensated for the attentional biases towards negative emotional stimuli seen in highly depressed groups, reducing the adverse effects of negative emotions and subsequently improving performance on cognitive tasks. This underscores the protective role of effective emotional regulation in enhancing cognitive control despite the presence of emotionally challenging conditions.

The results showing that both participant groups had significantly longer reaction times and higher error rates in the AY sequence compared to the BX sequence suggest that negative emotions have a greater detrimental effect on reactive control than on proactive control. This result indicates that under the negative emotion priming, both the high and low emotion regulation ability groups of participants adopted stronger proactive control and weaker reactive control.This is similar with previous findings [26, 27]. Muscarella proposed that in proactive control, since both possible probe stimuli “X” and “Y” following the cue stimulus “B” require a non-target response, participants have a certain expectancy regarding these stimuli, resulting in less cognitive conflict [24]. This could explain why proactive control appears less affected by negative emotions, as the anticipated response strategy might buffer the impact of emotional disturbances on cognitive performance. From the perspective of the limited resource theory, in this study, under conditions of negative emotion, participants used fewer resources to resolve minor cognitive conflicts. After allocating certain cognitive resources to regulate negative emotions, there were sufficient residual resources left to address cognitive conflicts during the proactive control phase, thus diminishing the impact of negative emotions in proactive control. Conversely, in reactive control, where the cue stimulus “A” corresponds to the probe stimulus “X” as a target response and “Y” as a non-target response, the cognitive processing of these stimuli involves uncertainty and may trigger more extensive cognitive inhibition processes.Under the influence of negative emotion priming in this study, after the allocation of resources to regulate negative emotions, the remaining resources were insufficient to handle tasks requiring substantial cognitive inhibition. This resulted in longer reaction times and higher error rates, thereby causing greater impairment to reactive control under negative emotional states. This reflects the more significant impact of negative emotions on reactive control compared to proactive control, aligning with the hypothesis that cognitive resources are finite and certain emotional states can deplete these resources more extensively.Both groups of subjects adopted more proactive control mode in the negative emotional state.

Additionally, within the reactive control, the high emotional regulation ability group exhibited significantly faster average reaction times and higher d’-target values than the low emotional regulation ability group. This result suggests that, the high emotional regulation group had better cognitive control ability in weakened reactive control mode than the low emotional regulation group. Braver [3] noted that during the response process in the AY sequence, participants need to overcome the predominant response tendency towards the AX sequence, making flexible non-target responses between the probe stimuli “X” and “Y”. This process indicates that performance in reactive control mode is associated with an individual’s cognitive flexibility. Furthermore, related research has found that cognitive flexibility is linked to an individual’s emotional regulation ability. Individuals with low emotional regulation capabilities often display deficits in cognitive flexibility due to their inability to flexibly employ different emotional regulation strategies [28]. Studies in cognitive neuroscience have also discovered that the monitoring processes for cognitive conflicts share common neural mechanisms with emotional regulation, primarily involving activation of the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [29, 30]. This shared activation suggests a neurobiological basis for the interplay between cognitive control and emotional regulation. The differences in cognitive flexibility between individuals with different emotional regulation abilities may lead to varied behavioral performances during the monitoring stage of cognitive conflicts. In this study, individuals with high emotional regulation abilities demonstrated faster reaction times and higher d’-target values in the reactive control mode due to their better cognitive flexibility.

The findings of this study revealed that under the proactive control condition (BX trials), although there was no significant difference in reaction time between the high and low emotion regulation ability groups, the high-ability group demonstrated a higher context discrimination index (d′-context). This discrepancy was not observed in previous studies that relied primarily on reaction time as the key performance metric [24, 26].This seemingly “dissociative” pattern precisely reflects fundamental differences in cognitive resource allocation strategies between the two groups, illustrating the cognitive principle of the speed-accuracy trade-off. For the low emotion regulation ability group, the lower d′-context in BX trials may stem from insufficient overall cognitive resources available after negative emotion induction. According to the limited resource theory [31, 32], individuals with low emotion regulation ability need to expend more cognitive resources to regulate emotional responses elicited by negative images, thereby reducing the resources available for subsequent tasks requiring sustained maintenance of goal-relevant information. Consequently, these individuals invested insufficient resources in maintaining the meaning of the cue “B” (i.e., “neither X nor Y following B requires a target response”), leading to a weakened ability to inhibit the dominant response tendency to “X.” This resulted in higher error rates and lower d′-context values. In contrast, the high emotion regulation ability group exhibited a more optimized and strategic resource allocation pattern. Their superior emotion regulation ability reduced prolonged processing and resource depletion caused by negative emotional stimuli, preserving sufficient cognitive resources for task performance. Guided by the speed-accuracy trade-off principle, in BX trials, they allocated more resources to the deep encoding the cue “B” to ensure response accuracy. This prioritization of accuracy came at the cost of reaction speed, leading to statistically comparable reaction times with the low-ability group, but significantly higher accuracy when “X” appeared. These findings suggest that the high emotion regulation ability group adopted a “quality over speed” strategy in proactive control, with their behavioral advantage reflected in higher cognitive control quality (as captured by the d′-context index) rather than faster reaction times. This result further indicates that individual differences in emotion regulation ability are manifested not only in the total amount of cognitive resources but also in the strategic allocation of these resources. High-ability individuals not only possess more resources but also flexibly deploy them to the most critical cognitive operations based on task demands. The results of this study provide new evidence from the perspective of individual differences and emotional contexts for the dual mechanisms of cognitive control theory. They demonstrate that the advantage of proactive control may not always manifest as faster reaction speed; under specific conditions, it can also be reflected through a strategic, accuracy-oriented resource allocation.

Although this study has yielded meaningful findings, it is important to acknowledge several limitations in the experimental design. First, as the study only included a negative emotion induction condition without a control condition involving neutral or positive emotion induction, it remains difficult to fully attribute the observed differences in cognitive control patterns specifically to the effect of “negative emotion.” Future studies should adopt a multi-level experimental design incorporating neutral, positive, and negative emotion induction conditions. Direct comparisons of behavioral performance across different emotional conditions will allow for a clearer and more robust interpretation of the specific impact of negative emotion on proactive and reactive control. Second, although the images used in this study were selected based on the IAPS criteria for low valence and high arousal, participants were not asked to report their real-time subjective emotional experience while viewing the images after the task. Therefore, the results may also reflect individual differences in emotional susceptibility, which could interfere with the outcomes. Future research should include a manipulation check within the experimental procedure to more precisely elucidate the relationship between emotional experience and cognitive control performance. Furthermore, this study treated “negative emotions” as a homogeneous category and did not differentiate between their distinct subtypes (e.g., anger, sadness, fear). According to the motivational dimensional model, negative emotions associated with approach motivation (e.g., anger) and those linked to avoidance motivation (e.g., sadness, fear) may differentially influence cognitive control, potentially even in opposite directions. The specific patterns of these emotions might have been obscured in the current study. Future research should separate and compare different subtypes of negative emotions to uncover the deeper mechanisms of their interaction with cognitive control. Lastly, although we reported no significant differences in age or gender distribution between the two groups of participants, we did not measure or control for several key potential confounding variables, such as participants’ baseline mood states prior to the experiment and trait anxiety levels. These variables may themselves be associated with individuals’ emotion regulation abilities and cognitive control performance, and their effects could interact with the emotion regulation capabilities examined in this study, thereby complicating the interpretation of pure relationships between variables. Future studies should measure these variables before experiments and include them as covariates in analyses to more precisely reveal the role of emotion regulation ability itself. Despite these limitations, the findings of this study still carry significant implications. Within the framework of dual mechanisms of cognitive control, this research preliminarily reveals how individual differences in emotion regulation ability differentially influence dual cognitive control under negative emotional contexts. Although the conclusions should be generalized with caution, this study provides important theoretical hypotheses and empirical directions for future research employing more rigorous designs.

Conclusion

Under negative emotion induction, university students with varying emotion regulation abilities consistently employed stronger proactive control and weaker reactive control. However, those with high emotion regulation ability demonstrated superior cognitive control performance in both reactive and proactive control modes compared to the low-ability group. Their advantage in reactive control was reflected through faster reaction times and higher sensitivity, while in proactive control, it manifested as enhanced maintenance of target cues and greater response accuracy.
