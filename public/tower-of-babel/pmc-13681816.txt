Nursing students’ first clinical encounters with delirium: a qualitative study of experiential learning and professional development
Authors: Gülşen Kılıç, Sevcan Avcı Işık, Banu Çevi̇k
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelirium is a complex neurocognitive condition that is often difficult to recognise and manage in clinical practice. Although nursing students receive theoretical education about delirium during undergraduate training, they may feel inadequately prepared when encountering patients with delirium in real clinical settings, reflecting a persistent theory–practice gap in nursing education. This study aimed to explore nursing students’ experiences of their first clinical encounters with patients experiencing delirium.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study was conducted with 13 first-semester, fourth-year nursing students at a university in Türkiye who had recently completed delirium-related theoretical education and intensive care clinical placements. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling. Data were collected through face-to-face, semi-structured interviews and analysed using inductive reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFive main themes were identified: (1) making sense of delirium through diagnostic uncertainty; (2) emotional burden, including fear, inadequacy, and empathy; (3) clinical and ethical challenges related to behavioural disturbances and restraint practices; (4) professional learning through role modelling, communication, and increasing self-efficacy; and (5) critical reflections on education, highlighting the limitations of theory-based preparation. Students described their first encounters with delirium as emotionally intense and clinically demanding experiences. Although students initially struggled with uncertainty, fear, and communication difficulties, repeated clinical exposure and supportive nurse role models contributed to the development of professional confidence, communication strategies, and more empathic approaches to delirium care. Students also reported that nurses’ attitudes and approaches toward patients influenced their own professional perspectives on nursing care.ConclusionsNursing students’ first encounters with delirium were experienced as both emotionally challenging and professionally transformative. The findings suggest that theoretical education alone may be insufficient to prepare students for the complexity of delirium care. Integrating experiential and practice-oriented educational approaches, such as simulation, case-based learning, and communication-focused training, may help bridge the theory–practice gap and improve students’ preparedness, confidence, and competence in delirium care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09980-4.Keywords: Delirium, Nursing students, Experiential learning, Theory–practice gap, Clinical practice, Qualitative study

Background

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating brain disorder characterized by confusion, inattention, and behavioral changes [1]. It develops rapidly, significantly affecting cognition and perception. Linked to conditions such as infections, metabolic disturbances, surgery, medications, and ICU stressors, delirium presents a higher risk in older patients and those undergoing invasive procedures [1, 2]. Although the prevalence of delirium varies considerably depending on the patient population and clinical setting (15–88%) [3–6], early recognition and effective management remain significant challenges in daily clinical practice [1].

The clinical presentation of delirium is highly heterogeneous and may manifest as hyperactive, hypoactive, or mixed subtypes. Hyperactive delirium is typically characterized by prominent symptoms such as agitation, restlessness, aggression, and hallucinations, whereas hypoactive delirium may present with more subtle manifestations, including lethargy, withdrawal, reduced attention, and psychomotor slowing. These diverse clinical presentations can make it challenging, particularly for healthcare professionals with limited clinical experience, to accurately recognize delirium and distinguish it from other neurological or psychiatric conditions. The variable nature of the condition contributes to diagnostic uncertainty, complicating assessment and clinical decision-making processes. Consequently, the early identification of delirium requires continuous observation, comprehensive assessment, and well-developed clinical reasoning skills [2, 6, 7].

As frontline care providers, nurses must possess adequate knowledge and clinical competence to ensure patient safety and quality of care for this vulnerable population [2]. Although nursing students acquire theoretical knowledge about delirium during their undergraduate education, they frequently feel inadequately prepared when encountering complex and agitated patients with delirium due to their limited practical exposure [7]. This transition reflects a critical theory–practice gap, highlighting that students’ professional confidence and clinical reasoning are heavily shaped during their initial clinical placements [8–10]. Particularly during their initial clinical placements, nursing students may experience difficulties in assessing the fluctuating and unpredictable clinical manifestations of patients with delirium [11]. The need for continuous observation, rapid changes in patients’ conditions, and individualized care requirements can complicate students’ clinical decision-making processes. Furthermore, uncertainties related to establishing effective communication with patients, determining appropriate care priorities, and accurately interpreting behavioral symptoms may increase feelings of anxiety and inadequacy among students. These challenges may also compromise students’ psychological safety, which has been recognized as a key factor influencing learning, confidence development, and professional growth in clinical education settings [12, 13]. Consequently, caring for patients with delirium represents an important learning experience that requires students not only to apply their clinical knowledge and skills but also to further develop their clinical reasoning, communication, and professional adaptation competencies [8, 11, 12].Therefore, understanding how nursing students perceive and navigate complex clinical conditions such as delirium is crucial for informing curriculum design, strengthening clinical support mechanisms, and fostering professional competence in nursing education [11, 12]. Previous studies have shown that, particularly during their first clinical encounters, nursing students may experience difficulties in interpreting unpredictable patient behaviors, establishing effective communication, and prioritising care [13–15]. However, there remains a need to further explore how students perceive and experience processes such as emotional adaptation, ethical distress, observing nurses as role models, and the shaping of their professional perspectives, and how these experiences shape their approaches to clinical care.

Purpose

The aim of this study is to explore nursing students’ first clinical encounters with patients experiencing delirium. Specifically, the study seeks to:

Examine the gap between students’ theoretical understanding and their ability to recognise delirium in clinical settings,

Examine the gap between students’ theoretical understanding and their ability to recognise delirium in clinical settings,

Explore their emotional, cognitive, and professional responses during this transitional process, and.

Explore their emotional, cognitive, and professional responses during this transitional process, and.

Identify their perceived educational needs and expectations to provide targeted suggestions for improving nursing curricula and clinical practice integration.

Methods

Design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive design to explore nursing students’ experiences of encountering patients with delirium during clinical practice. This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [16].

Setting and context

The study was conducted at Başkent University’s Faculty of Health Sciences, Department of Nursing (Ankara, Türkiye) between November and December 2025. In this curriculum, students receive formal theoretical education on delirium during the second semester of their third year. During the same period, they begin clinical placements in various intensive care units, where exposure to patients with delirium is more likely. After completing their third year, students also undertake summer clinical placements, including intensive care settings, further increasing opportunities for delirium-related clinical encounters. Interviews were conducted in November–December 2025, approximately 4–5 months after the students’ summer internships in July 2025, during which they had encountered patients with delirium. In the first semester of the fourth year, students primarily continue with clinical practice, with only a brief orientation session and no formal theoretical coursework. Therefore, first-semester fourth-year students were considered the most appropriate group, as they had recently completed both theoretical and clinical training and were able to reflect on their initial encounters with delirium. In addition to theoretical instruction and clinical placements, the nursing curriculum includes simulation-based learning activities conducted in the skills laboratory. Standardized patient simulations are routinely used across a range of clinical topics to provide realistic and authentic learning experiences before students enter clinical practice. These activities allow students to develop communication, assessment, and clinical decision-making skills in a safe learning environment. However, despite the use of simulation-based learning in other areas of the curriculum, no simulation scenarios specifically focused on delirium management had been implemented at the time of this study. Delirium education was primarily supported through classroom-based instruction and video-based learning materials.

Recruitment and sampling strategy

Following ethical approval, students were informed about the study during scheduled class sessions and were asked whether they had experienced their first encounter with a patient with delirium during their third-year clinical placements or summer internships. Purposive sampling was then used to recruit first-semester, fourth-year nursing students who met the inclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being a first-semester, fourth-year nursing student, (2) self-reporting at least one clinical encounter with a patient experiencing delirium during third-year clinical placements or summer internships, and (3) voluntary willingness to participate. This group was selected because they had recently completed delirium-related theoretical education and intensive care clinical placements, enabling them to reflect on their initial clinical encounters with delirium.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Baskent University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee (No: 17162298.600-319; November 3, 2025). Before data collection, participants received written and verbal information about the study and provided written informed consent, including permission for audio-recording.

Participants were explicitly informed that participation or non-participation would not affect their academic grades, clinical evaluations, or progression within the nursing program. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Given that participants were invited to reflect on potentially challenging clinical experiences, they were also informed that they could decline to answer any question, pause, or terminate the interview at any point without providing a reason. Interviews were conducted in a supportive and non-judgmental environment to minimize potential emotional discomfort associated with recalling distressing experiences.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants’ confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained throughout the study.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The research team comprised three academics (one in surgical and two in internal medicine nursing) with extensive qualitative research backgrounds and intensive care experience. Notably, one researcher holds doctoral-level expertise specifically in delirium and remains actively engaged in clinical intensive care practice. To minimize bias from pre-existing clinical assumptions, reflexive notes were maintained.

Although some researchers had previously taught the participants, none had a direct teaching, supervisory, or evaluative role during the data collection period, thereby reducing potential power imbalances and supporting voluntary participation.

Data collection methods

Data were collected via face-to-face semi-structured interviews, guided by a researcher-developed framework focusing on students’ experiences of delirium, recognition of symptoms, emotional responses and approaches to care. The semi-structured interview guide was developed by the researchers, evaluated by nine faculty members with intensive care experience from different institutions in Türkiye, and is provided as Additional file 1. After completing a brief sociodemographic questionnaire, the interviews (lasting 20–40 min) took place in a private faculty setting to ensure confidentiality. With participants’ consent, sessions were audio-recorded and supplemented by field notes capturing non-verbal cues. Probing questions were used to clarify responses. Recordings were transcribed verbatim. Data collection continued until the desired depth and richness of data had been achieved to adequately address the research aim.

Data analysis

Interviews were transcribed verbatim, anonymised (e.g., P1), and analysed in Turkish to preserve contextual nuances. Selected quotes were translated into English and verified by a bilingual researcher. Following Braun and Clarke’s (2006, 2021) inductive reflexive thematic analysis, two researchers independently familiarised themselves with the data, assigned initial codes, and iteratively grouped them into subthemes and themes, reaching final consensus through team discussions [17, 18].

Techniques to enhance trustworthiness

Trustworthiness followed Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) criteria [19]. Credibility was established via prolonged engagement, member checking, independent double-coding, and verbatim quotations [18, 20]. Dependability and confirmability were maintained through a transparent audit trail, regular team discussions, and reflexive journaling to minimize bias [21]. Finally, detailed descriptions of the study setting, participants, and procedures ensured transferability.

Results

Demographics

Thirteen nursing students (mean age: 22.3 years; predominantly female) participated in the study. Most participants (n = 9) rated their knowledge of delirium as partially adequate, while three rated it as adequate and one as inadequate. Themes

Five overarching themes emerged, illustrating students’ experiential learning trajectories during initial delirium encounters: clinical sense-making, emotional responses, care-related challenges, professional development, and educational reflections. A detailed summary with representative quotations is provided in Fig. 1.

Overview of themes and subthemes related to nursing students’ first clinical encounters with delirium

Theme 1: the process of making sense of delirium

Subtheme: “something is not right”: recognizing clinical cues

Students primarily recognized early delirium through behavioral and motor cues. Actions such as removing medical devices, attempting to leave the bed, and resisting care served as primary warning signs of clinical deterioration.

For instance, regarding the removal of monitoring equipment:

“I would attach the devices when I went to take measurements, but when I came back the next time, I found them all on the floor. The patient had removed them and thrown them down.” (P7).

Similarly:

“We were monitoring him. He was pulling them out and throwing them.” (P2).

Self-termination of invasive procedures was particularly striking:

“At first, I did not realize that he had developed delirium. Then I noticed what was happening after he self-extubated. Later, he also tried to remove his IV lines.” (P13).

Attempts to leave the bed were also key indicators:

“He wanted to get out of bed; he was constantly sliding down from the bed.” (P7).

Furthermore, the inability to establish cooperation during care drew significant attention:

“He was not doing anything we told him to do.” (P6).

Subtheme: recognizing perceptual and cognitive changes

Beyond behavioral cues, students identified altered perceptual and cognitive functions—such as responding to nonexistent stimuli, disorientation, and incoherent communication—as key indicators of a changed mental state.

Observing patients responding to nonexistent stimuli was common:

“He was saying, ‘Look at the birds on the television,’ but there was nothing on the television. The TV was off.” (P11).

“For example, he told me, ‘I’m going to go out to the garden.’ But there was no garden in the room.” (P7).

Misidentifying people also occurred:

“The patient was very agitated and kept calling me by a man’s name.” (P12).

Disorientation to time and place was clearly evident:

“When we asked, ‘Where are you right now?’ he said, ‘I’m in the office right now.’ But of course that wasn’t the case.” (P5).

Furthermore, communication attempts were often fragmented and incomprehensible:

“He was trying to say something, but it couldn’t be understood.” (P6).

Subtheme: “Is it delirium or something else?”: difficulty with differential recognition

Initially, students struggled to associate observed changes directly with delirium. Behaviors were often misinterpreted as postoperative awakening, psychiatric conditions, or temporary altered consciousness. Recognition typically occurred later in the clinical course or following healthcare team feedback.

For instance, this initial lack of association was common:

“I knew something was unusual with the patient, but I never thought about delirium.” (P11).

Others attributed changes to normal recovery processes, relying on staff for identification:

“I didn’t think the patient was experiencing delirium. I just thought he had woken up. Later, when the nurses said the patient was in delirium, I realized what was going on.” (P3).

Diagnostic clarity often required direct team confirmation:

“I immediately turned to our nurse and asked, ‘Is this delirium?’ When she said yes…” (P2).

Theme 2: the emotional burden of the first clinical encounter

Subtheme: fear

Students experienced intense fear during initial encounters, primarily driven by unpredictable patient behaviors, the risk of self-harm, and potential sudden clinical deterioration. Uncontrolled movements exacerbated anxieties about being unable to intervene safely, especially when providing care alone.

Fears concerning self-harm and sudden deterioration were common:

“I was afraid that he might hurt himself.” (P11) “I was afraid that something might happen.” (P6).

The unpredictability of the situation was a major stressor:

“Not knowing what the patient might do is frightening.” (P9).

Anxiety intensified significantly when students felt isolated without sufficient clinical skills:

“Actually, not knowing what to do also creates fear. When I was alone, I became even more afraid. I thought, I’m alone, I’m inexperienced, I’m new and don’t have enough skills yet. For example, if the patient tries to stand up, what will I do?” (P3).

Subtheme: professional inadequacy and loss of control

Students experienced a pronounced sense of professional inadequacy and loss of control. This peaked when patients resisted care, ignored commands, or proved physically unmanageable, leaving students feeling unable to sustain interventions or manage the situation effectively.

This profound uncertainty and lack of clinical experience were starkly expressed:

“I didn’t know what to do.” (P11).

“I didn’t know how to approach the situation.” (P13).

Physical resistance directly hindered care delivery, amplifying feelings of helplessness:

“We try to administer the medication, but he bends his arm and we can’t do it.” (P4).

“We couldn’t hold the patient.” (P6).

Subtheme: empathy-based emotional burden

Beyond fear, encounters with delirium triggered profound empathic responses. Patients’ altered consciousness and inability to communicate evoked deep sadness, leading students to project themselves or their loved ones into the patient’s situation.

Witnessing the patient’s vulnerability caused significant emotional distress:

“I felt very sad seeing him like that.” (P11).

“It really hurts to see someone like that.” (P6).

Students often evaluated the situation through a familial lens:

“I thought about what it would be like if it were someone close to me.” (P13).

“I thought about it from the family’s perspective.” (P2).

Subtheme: emotional adaptation through experience

Initial fear and anxiety gradually decreased as clinical exposure increased. Subsequent encounters fostered greater confidence and a more controlled, comfortable approach to the clinical environment.

Students highlighted this shift from fear to comfort:

“At first I was very scared, but later I felt more comfortable.” (P11).

“I wasn’t that afraid the next time.” (P6).

Clinical experience directly enhanced professional confidence and approachability:

“Now I know how to approach it.” (P3).

“I can go in much more comfortably now.” (P13).

Theme 3: clinical and ethical challenges experienced while providing care

Subtheme: difficulties in implementing clinical interventions

Students faced significant barriers performing basic interventions—such as medication administration and vital sign monitoring—due to behavioral resistance. Persistent movement and attempts to remove equipment interrupted the care process, complicating continuity of care.

Difficulties with routine procedures were common:

“We try to give the medication, but he bends his arm and we can’t do it. We couldn’t measure the blood pressure at all because he wouldn’t extend his arm.” (P4).

Constant movement and resistance to monitoring devices significantly complicated care delivery:

“He was constantly sliding down in the bed. We put on the pulse oximeter, but he removed it. We tried placing it on his ear or big toe, but he removed it again. While providing care and administering medication, he kept pulling his hand away.” (P3).

Furthermore, physical strength during resistance was a notable challenge:

“We had restrained his arms, but he was still trying to pull things out. He was also trying to remove the mask. At that moment, it was really difficult because he was resisting a lot. Even though he was elderly, he was quite strong.” (P1).

Subtheme: behavioral barriers related to agitation

Patients’ resistant and unpredictable behaviors significantly hindered care management. Students struggled with verbal resistance, physical interference, and a profound lack of cooperation during procedures.

Resistance to instructions and the absence of cooperation were frequently highlighted:

“He wasn’t doing anything we told him to do.” (P2).

“We tried to talk to him, but he didn’t understand and wouldn’t listen to us.” (P6).

Physical resistance often required students to intervene manually, though often unsuccessfully:

“I was trying to hold him from his back while the dressing was being done, but he kept trying to lie down.” (P2).

“He wasn’t doing anything we told him to do; he didn’t understand. His caregiver was there and tried to hold him, but he didn’t respond at all. It was as if we weren’t even there.” (P11).

Subtheme: ethical and moral discomfort

Certain practices—such as physical restraint, holding patients forcibly, or failing to provide adequate explanations—triggered significant ethical and moral discomfort. These experiences led students to question the patient-centeredness of care and foster feelings of emotional distress.

The emotional impact of physical intervention and restraint was profound:

“I felt bad when we had to hold him just so the care could be done.” (P11).

“I saw that his hands and feet were restrained, and he was struggling in the bed. Seeing that made me feel very bad… I kept imagining him being like that all the time.” (P10).

Lack of communication and a perceived disregard for the patient’s condition caused further unease:

“No explanation was given there. No one said things like ‘You are in the hospital’ or ‘You are in this unit.’” (P12).

“It was as if nothing had happened.” (P13).

Furthermore, students felt distressed by their inability to fulfill the patient’s needs:

“You want to do something, but they don’t allow it or you just can’t do it. He keeps asking for things, but you can’t help. That made me feel a bit uneasy and sad.” (P8).

Theme 4: professional learning and its reflection in practice

Subtheme: theoretical knowledge gaining meaning in clinical practice

Theoretical education regarding delirium became concrete and meaningful upon encountering real-life clinical cases. A solid theoretical foundation enabled students to recognize symptoms rapidly, better understand complex clinical situations, and approach care with increased confidence and reduced prejudice.

The transition from classroom to bedside was perceived as a validating experience:

“We had studied delirium in class. Seeing something we had learned about directly in the clinical setting felt really good.” (P7).

Theoretical knowledge served as a critical trigger for clinical recognition:

“I immediately turned to our nurse and asked, ‘Is he experiencing delirium?’ We had learned in class that patients in the ICU can be like this. That’s why I recognized it right away; I was even surprised at myself.” (P2) “I may not have remembered the lecture notes one by one, but when I saw that situation, delirium came to my mind at least.” (P13).

Furthermore, education directly influenced students’ professional attitudes and willingness to engage:

“Honestly, if it hadn’t been explained in class, I would probably have approached the situation with a lot of prejudice and most likely wouldn’t have stayed near that patient.” (P3).

Subtheme: developing communication strategies

Over time, students developed adaptive communication strategies to manage agitation and enhance treatment adherence. Orienting patients to reality, maintaining a calm and patient tone, and utilizing reassuring explanations proved more effective than reacting to noncompliant behaviors.

Strategic orientation and validation were used to de-escalate patient distress:

“For example, the patient told me, ‘I’m going to go out to the garden.’ But there was no garden in the room… So I told him, ‘We’ll take you to the garden later. For now, we’re resting here and taking our medications.’ When I said that, he became a bit calmer.” (P7) “I would say things like, ‘You are in the intensive care unit right now, we are providing your care.’ As I kept talking like that, he became calmer.” (P3).

Maintaining emotional regulation and patience during resistant behaviors was key to gaining cooperation:

“When I was about to measure his blood pressure, I tried to calm him while talking. I said, ‘Look, we’re going to put this on your arm now, please don’t remove it.’ Each time he threw off the cuff, I put it back on again without getting angry or raising my voice.” (P2).

Some students used reassuring expressions, sometimes softening reality to avoid increasing patient distress or agitation:

“I would say that their relatives would come… even if it was a small white lie, saying things like that sometimes helped them calm down.” (P11).

Subtheme: learning through role models

Observing healthcare professionals significantly influenced students’ professional development. While calm, controlled, and communicative approaches by experienced nurses provided constructive templates for crisis management, negative behaviors—such as harshness or lack of empathy—prompted students to critically reflect on their professional values.

Positive role modeling directly guided students’ clinical behavior and communication:

“By watching them, I was able to behave in a similar way. When they first went in, I saw how they talked to the patients, so I acted the same way. For example, they called the patient’s relative; I wouldn’t have thought of that.” (P2).

“They were trying to talk to the patient. They were explaining things like, ‘Now we will administer this medication to you,’ or ‘We need to provide your care.’ Their professional approach really affected me.” (P3).

Observing the efficacy of experience in de-escalation was particularly impactful:

“At first the nurse had difficulty managing the patient, but then a more experienced nurse came. She behaved much more calmly and managed the patient better. I observed her approach and really thought she handled it well.” (P1).

Conversely, witnessing dismissive or abrasive attitudes caused significant moral disappointment:

“The nurses didn’t explain anything to the patient, it was really bad… They didn’t even try to talk to the patient… they were very harsh… like ‘let’s just finish and get out.’ Honestly, it made me sad.” (P6).

Subtheme: development of professional self-efficacy

Initial challenges eventually fostered a stronger sense of professional self-efficacy. As fear and hesitation subsided through repeated clinical exposure, students transitioned toward a more controlled and proactive approach, actively participating in the care process with increased comfort and competence.

Students reflected on their growing ability to cope and perform within their roles:

“I never thought of myself as inadequate. On the contrary, I think I was able to cope quite well… I had done most of the things I could do as an intern.” (P2).

Clinical experience directly transformed fear into empathetic confidence:

“I had never seen a patient like this before. At first I was scared, but if I saw one now, I wouldn’t be afraid. I would approach them more empathetically because I know it is a temporary condition.” (P4).

Furthermore, initial encounters served as a vital learning foundation for subsequent care:

“Because I gained experience from the first patient, I communicated much more with the second patient.” (P3).

Theme 5: awareness of and recommendations for the educational system

Subtheme: realizing that theory alone is not sufficient

Students highlighted that purely theoretical delirium education is insufficient for clinical readiness. Classroom knowledge often remains abstract until validated by real-life encounters, which provide the necessary context to make learning meaningful. Consequently, students emphasized a critical need for practice-based experiences when addressing complex conditions like delirium.

The gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice was a recurring concern:

“It mostly stays at the theoretical level… actually practicing with a patient is really very instructive.” (P2) “The theory remains somewhat abstract. Honestly, we don’t fully know what we should do. We saw it in theory, but unless you encounter it, you can’t fully grasp the condition.” (P3).

Direct exposure was perceived as the only way to achieve true clinical understanding:

“I think no one can fully understand delirium without encountering it.” (P10).

Furthermore, the impact of visual and experiential learning far outweighed verbal instruction:

“Everything was explained in class, we knew what would happen. But seeing it is really different; it’s much more effective than just hearing about it.” (P8).

Subtheme: need for practice-oriented education

Students advocated for a shift toward interactive and experiential learning. Specifically, they suggested that incorporating high-fidelity simulations, role-playing, and case-based video materials into pre-clinical curricula would bridge the theory-practice gap and enhance clinical preparedness.

Simulation was perceived as a powerful tool to foster deep learning and clinical confidence:

“I think simulation could be more effective. Because when we tried it with other topics and saw it in a live setting, we realized it had a very strong impact.” (P2) “Simulation makes a lot of sense. Because I used to be very afraid, since I didn’t know what I would encounter… If there were simulation, I think it would be more effective.” (P6).

Practical laboratory scenarios involving realistic patient behaviors were proposed to refine intervention skills:

“For example, in the laboratory a patient could be placed in bed… pulling out the cables and devices, and we could try to intervene there. We could learn how to talk to the patient. We see it theoretically, but it would be better if it were presented as a simulation or a case.” (P11).

Additionally, visual aids were highlighted as essential for concrete understanding:

“At least there could be a video-based demonstration of a patient with delirium. Seeing the types of behaviors they display would be helpful.” (P4).

Subtheme: integration of family-centered and empathic care into education

Students emphasized that delirium management requires a holistic approach incorporating the patient’s family. Observing the family’s vital role in de-escalation and reorientation, participants advocated for a stronger curriculum focus on family-centered care and empathic communication with relatives.

The calming and orienting influence of family presence was notably observed:

“When the patient’s relative came, even the granddaughter just sitting next to him calmed him down a lot. He didn’t shout or become agitated.” (P7) “His daughter came and tried to orient him. She kept saying, ‘Mom, I’m your daughter.’ After a while, the patient’s condition improved.” (P4).

Furthermore, students highlighted the need for education to address the psychological burden on families experiencing delirium for the first time:

“The patient may be experiencing this for the first time, and the family member is also going through it for the first time. Nurses also need to consider the patient’s relatives psychologically.” (P10).

Discussion

The findings of this study illustrate that nursing students’ initial encounters with delirious patients represent a transformative developmental process. This journey begins with acute diagnostic uncertainty and significant caregiving challenges, initially characterized by fear, anxiety, and a sense of professional inadequacy. However, the data suggest that through sustained clinical exposure, the observation of constructive role models, and the acquisition of adaptive communication strategies, this emotional burden gradually evolves into enhanced professional self-efficacy. Ultimately, these results underscore a critical pedagogical shift: delirium education must transition from purely theoretical models toward practice-oriented, experiential learning frameworks.

The process of making sense of delirium: from clinical cues to diagnostic uncertainty

The findings indicate that students initially recognized delirium not primarily as a neurocognitive syndrome but rather through behavioral disturbances that disrupted the flow of care and posed safety risks, such as pulling medical devices or resisting treatment. Consistent with the literature, hyperactive delirium is often more easily recognized by individuals with limited clinical experience, whereas placing these observations within a diagnostic framework requires more advanced clinical reasoning skills [22].

Although students noticed perceptual changes such as disorientation or hallucinations, they often struggled to label these observations directly as delirium and instead interpreted them as postoperative awakening, dementia, or psychiatric conditions. This difficulty suggests that differential diagnosis remains a fragile area in clinical education [13, 14]. The findings further indicate that nursing students may experience challenges in transferring theoretical knowledge learned in the classroom to clinical practice, highlighting the importance of real patient encounters in facilitating the meaningful interpretation of theoretical knowledge [9].

The fact that diagnostic understanding often became clearer after feedback from the healthcare team suggests that awareness of delirium is not constructed solely at the individual level but emerges as a socially mediated clinical learning process. Students’ ability to develop clinical understanding through questioning and intuition, followed by guidance from experienced professionals, underscores the critical role of structured clinical supervision in nursing education [14, 23]. This pattern of learning is also consistent with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, in which concrete experiences are transformed into conceptual understanding through reflection and guidance [9].

The emotional burden of the first clinical encounter: Fear, loss of control, empathic impact, and adaptation

The second theme demonstrates that the first encounter with delirium represents not only a cognitive challenge but also a powerful emotional experience for nursing students. Feelings of fear, being unsupported, and inadequacy reported by students suggest that this experience is not simply a “difficult patient” situation but also a clinical encounter in which students question their professional competence. Concerns that patients might harm themselves or become uncontrollable intensified students’ fear. This finding is consistent with literature indicating that the unpredictable nature of delirium can create significant stress and anxiety for caregivers [15]. However, this fear appears to stem not only from patients’ behaviors but also from students’ lack of sufficient clinical reference points regarding how to approach such situations. According to Kolb’s experiential learning theory, these gaps between theoretical knowledge and practical application, along with the need for guidance, may trigger emotional responses that influence the transfer of learning into practice [9].

In addition to fear, students also described experiencing empathy-driven emotional responses when confronted with the vulnerability of both the patient and their family members. This suggests that students perceived the patient not merely as a clinical problem but as a vulnerable individual, which aligns with educational literature emphasizing that patient-centered learning experiences strengthen empathy among healthcare students [24]. Furthermore, the gradual reduction of emotional burden as clinical experience increased indicates the development of emotional adaptation and professional self-efficacy among students. This progression corresponds with the active experimentation stage of Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, suggesting that an initially unsettling experience becomes more manageable through reflection and repeated clinical exposure [9].

Clinical and ethical challenges experienced while providing care: Implementation difficulties, agitation-related barriers, and ethical tensions

The third theme indicates that caring for patients experiencing delirium was challenging for students not only in terms of recognition but also in sustaining the care process. The disruption of basic nursing interventions such as medication administration, vital sign monitoring, and patient monitoring due to patients’ agitation and resistance suggests that delirium care can be a fragile process in practice. This finding is consistent with literature reporting that maintaining patient safety while continuing clinical assessment and treatment interventions represents a significant challenge for caregivers [15]. Students’ descriptions of the care process in terms of being “unable to perform” interventions indicate that these difficulties stem not only from gaps in knowledge but also from the behavioral complexity of patients experiencing delirium.

The presence of behavioral barriers related to agitation further highlights that delirium care is not solely a technical task but also a relational and situational aspect of nursing practice. When patients do not respond to verbal guidance or demonstrate physical resistance, students’ communication and behavioral management skills are tested alongside their procedural skills. This finding aligns with previous studies indicating that nursing students often struggle with communication, teamwork, and behavioral management when caring for patients with delirium [14].

An important dimension of this theme is the ethical component of the challenges experienced by students. Situations involving physically holding the patient, applying restraints, or failing to adequately explain procedures created feelings of ethical discomfort among participants. These experiences suggest that students were not only reflecting on how care was delivered but also questioning whether it was appropriate and patient-centered. In this sense, delirium care becomes an experience that fosters ethical awareness in addition to technical competence. Studies demonstrating that patient-centered educational approaches strengthen empathy and ethical sensitivity support this interpretation [24]. Furthermore, structured delirium education programs have been shown to improve patient–family communication and professional approaches to care [11, 12].

Within Kolb’s experiential learning framework, this theme suggests that agitation-related challenges and ethical discomfort function as powerful concrete experiences for students. By reflecting on these situations, students begin to recognize that clinical practice involves not only technical competence but also ethical and relational dimensions. Similarly, Kane and colleagues’ “making sense of praxis” model indicates that students transform the gaps and tensions they encounter in clinical settings into a more mature understanding of practice through reflective learning processes [9].

Professional learning and its reflection in practice: theoretical knowledge gaining meaning, communication strategies, observing role models, and self-efficacy

The fourth theme indicates that challenging clinical experiences gradually transform into lasting professional learning. The concretization of theoretical knowledge in the clinical setting supported students’ processes of recognizing the patient’s condition, establishing communication, and developing confidence, which is consistent with Kolb’s experiential learning theory. Although the theory–practice gap has frequently been emphasized in nursing education [9, 25–27], previous studies have reported that supportive educators facilitate the transfer of learning [28–31], and that integrating theory with clinical practice strengthens clinical decision-making processes.

The communication strategies developed by students during their encounters with patients experiencing delirium should be interpreted not merely as practical responses to agitation but as emerging therapeutic interaction competencies. Participants described using reality orientation, reassurance, calm verbal guidance, distraction, and emotionally supportive communication to reduce patient distress. These approaches reflect core components of therapeutic communication, including active listening, empathic responding, emotional validation, and relationship-building. However, students’ narratives suggest that such competencies were acquired largely through trial-and-error experiences and observation of experienced nurses rather than through structured preparation. Recent evidence from standardized patient-based education indicates that therapeutic communication skills can be systematically taught, practiced, and objectively evaluated using structured competency frameworks [32]. Such approaches enable educators to assess students’ readiness for complex patient interactions and provide targeted feedback before exposure to challenging clinical situations. Integrating structured therapeutic communication training into delirium education may therefore strengthen students’ confidence and preparedness when caring for cognitively impaired and behaviorally unpredictable patients.

Within this meaning-making process, observing role models emerged as an important mechanism for learning. Students reported that by observing the communication strategies and care approaches of experienced nurses, they were able to integrate these behaviors into their own practice. Notably, some students also described using reassuring expressions that involved gently softening reality to reduce patient distress or agitation. Within the clinical context of this study, such communication approaches may reflect a pragmatic strategy aimed at maintaining patient comfort and emotional stability during challenging interactions. At the same time, when they encountered negative attitudes such as indifference or harsh communication, they reflected on what kind of nurse they wanted to become and reconsidered their professional values. This finding is consistent with literature highlighting the role of observing role models in the transmission of professional values and behaviors [23–34]. Moreover, structured observational learning has been shown to strengthen clinical reasoning, performance, and the transfer of learning [35].

As a result of clinical experience and role model observation, students’ ability to recognize delirium symptoms and observe the effects of non-pharmacological interventions encouraged more active participation in the care process. Initial hesitation gradually evolved into a stronger perception of professional self-efficacy. Previous studies similarly emphasize that active learning and experiential reflection play a central role in the development of self-efficacy [36] and that this perception is an important determinant of clinical performance [37]. For this reason, increasing opportunities for role model observation and experiential learning in clinical education environments may represent an important educational strategy for strengthening nursing students’ ability to manage complex clinical conditions such as delirium.

Awareness of and recommendations for the educational system: The theory–practice gap, practice-oriented education, and the integration of a family-centered approach

The fifth theme indicates that, following their clinical experiences, students developed a critical awareness of nursing education and offered concrete suggestions for improvement. Students reported that addressing delirium only at a theoretical level may be insufficient for preparing them for clinical practice and that knowledge becomes more meaningful through real patient experiences. This finding is consistent with literature emphasizing the importance of experiential learning in reducing the gap between theory and practice in nursing education [12, 35, 38, 39]. For instance, Chen et al. (2024) demonstrated that structured simulation modules significantly improved nursing students’ knowledge, confidence, and practical competence in delirium care [12].

Students’ statements such as “we don’t fully know what to do” and “seeing it is more effective than just hearing about it” suggest that theoretical knowledge, when not supported by practical experience, may increase clinical anxiety. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that simulation-based learning improves nursing students’ knowledge, clinical skills, decision-making abilities, and confidence levels [40–43]. Similarly, although simulation experiences may initially create anxiety, they have been reported to increase clinical confidence and professional self-efficacy over time [39]. Furthermore, structured observer roles and active participation have been shown to strengthen students’ clinical reasoning and perceptions of psychological safety [35].

Recent evidence also suggests that the effectiveness of simulation-based education may be enhanced when multiple experiential learning modalities are combined. In addition to traditional simulation, approaches such as standardized patient encounters, peer-assisted learning, role-play simulation, and gamified educational activities provide opportunities for students to rehearse communication, clinical reasoning, and behavioral management skills in psychologically safe environments. A recent randomized controlled trial conducted in Türkiye demonstrated that peer education, role-play simulation, and virtual escape room interventions significantly improved nursing students’ therapeutic interaction skills, supporting the potential value of multimodal educational strategies for strengthening communication competence and reducing uncertainty in complex patient encounters [44]. Given the unpredictable nature of delirium and the communication challenges described by participants in the present study, integrating diverse simulation modalities into undergraduate nursing curricula may help students develop greater confidence, communication readiness, and clinical preparedness before encountering patients with delirium in real clinical settings. Future simulation-based delirium education should not focus solely on hyperactive or agitated patient scenarios, but should also include hypoactive delirium scenarios, enabling students to recognize patients who may appear quiet or compliant but are actually experiencing hypoactive delirium, with subtle signs such as withdrawal, reduced alertness, slowed responses, and decreased engagement.

Another notable finding of this study is that students emphasized the importance of integrating a family-centered approach into delirium education. Participants observed in the clinical setting that the presence of family members played a significant role in calming patients. The literature similarly suggests that delirium education should encompass not only clinical safety and treatment interventions but also effective communication strategies among patients, families, and healthcare teams [43].

For this reason, practice-oriented educational strategies—such as simulation, case-based learning, and the use of visual materials—may represent effective approaches for improving nursing students’ ability to recognize and manage unpredictable clinical conditions such as delirium. Increasing the use of these learning strategies within nursing education programs may help students enter clinical practice with greater preparedness, confidence, and a patient- and family-centered perspective.

Limitations

As with most qualitative studies, this research has several methodological limitations. First, the study was conducted with a limited number of nursing students from a single educational institution, which may restrict the transferability of the findings to different institutional, sociocultural, or curricular contexts. In addition, because most participants were female, the findings may not fully reflect how male nursing students experience, express, or report emotional burden, empathy, and communication challenges during first encounters with delirium. Second, because the data were obtained from participants’ self-reported past experiences, the findings may have been influenced by recall bias or individual differences in interpretation. Finally, the phenomenon was explored solely from the perspectives of nursing students. The absence of views from clinical nurses or educators involved in the care process limits data triangulation and partially restricts a more comprehensive, multidimensional understanding of the topic. In addition, students’ narratives predominantly reflected experiences with hyperactive delirium behaviours, such as agitation, resistance to care, and device removal. Consequently, experiences related to hypoactive delirium, which is often more difficult to recognize and differentiate in clinical practice, may have been underrepresented in the findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that nursing students’ first encounters with patients experiencing delirium are characterized by diagnostic uncertainty, emotional challenges, and difficulties in patient management. However, these demanding clinical experiences serve as a critical developmental learning opportunity that helps students transition from initial uncertainty toward a stronger sense of professional self-efficacy. The findings strongly suggest that integrating and strengthening experiential and simulation-based learning approaches in nursing education is essential to better prepare students for recognizing and managing complex clinical conditions such as delirium.
