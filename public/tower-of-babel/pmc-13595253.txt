From Model Lifecycle to Clinical Interaction Lifecycle: Extending the European Artificial Intelligence Act Governance for Foundation Models in Cardiovascular Research
Authors: Aykan Çelik
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article by Barucci et al., which provides a timely and thoughtful interpretation of the European Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) for ethics committees evaluating clinical research that involves artificial intelligence (AI).1

Their proposal to align ethical oversight with a three-phase lifecycle – training, testing under real-world conditions and post-marketing monitoring – offers an important operational framework for translating a complex regulatory document into practical research governance. In particular, the emphasis on proportional oversight according to the maturity and clinical impact of an AI system represents an important step towards harmonising innovation with patient safety under the AI Act.1,2

However, I believe that the framework implicitly relies on an assumption that deserves reconsideration. Across the proposed lifecycle, the AI system itself remains the primary object of governance. This assumption has served conventional medical AI well because most predictive algorithms were developed for narrowly defined clinical tasks, validated on fixed datasets and deployed as relatively stable software artefacts.3,4

Yet cardiovascularAI is undergoing a technological transition. Increasingly, clinical research is no longer limited to task-specific predictive models but is beginning to evaluate general-purpose AI models under the AI Act. I refer to these here as foundation models, including large language and multimodal models, which may support multiple clinical tasks without task-specific retraining.2,5

Although these systems remain within the broader scope ofAI governance, the mechanisms through which they influence clinical decision-making differ substantially from those of conventional supervised machine-learning systems. Consequently, the regulatory question is no longer simply how a model is developed but increasingly how it behaves during clinical use.5–7

This creates an important oversight gap for studies in which a foundation model is not formally marketed as a medical device but is nevertheless used as a clinical-reasoning aid, documentation assistant, triage support tool or comparator intervention.6,7

This argument does not imply that all such studies fall within the formal legal scope of the AI Act; where a study constitutes testing in real-world conditions, the Act is necessarily engaged, whereas the exemption applies most clearly to retrospective or silent evaluations that do not affect live clinical decisions. On the contrary, the Act’s research exemption makes ethical review more important for foundation-model studies that remain outside device-level regulation, but may still exert clinical influence through reasoning support, documentation or workflow integration.1,2

In such settings, the absence of formal device status should not be equated with the absence of clinical risk. A model that shapes the questions asked by a clinician, prioritises differential diagnoses, drafts management options or structures the content of a consultation may influence patient-facing decisions even when the final decision is formally made by a human professional.5,6

For this reason, ethics committees should consider a functional criterion based on demonstrable clinical influence: if the model output can plausibly alter diagnostic reasoning, treatment selection, escalation decisions, patient communication or documentation that affects care, the study should be reviewed with a level of scrutiny proportionate to that influence.5–7

This distinction is more than semantic because foundation models alter the relationship between development and deployment. For traditional machine-learning systems, behaviour is largely established during model development through dataset selection, algorithm design and external validation; once deployed, performance is expected to remain relatively stable until a formal software update occurs.

In contrast, the behaviour of a foundation model is continuously shaped at the point of use by model version, system prompts, user instructions, contextual information, inference settings, retrieval mechanisms, external tools and vendor-controlled updates. Many of these components are inaccessible to investigators and may evolve during an ongoing clinical study despite there being no change in the protocol. The clinically meaningful unit of analysis is therefore no longer the model in isolation but the interaction between the clinician, the foundation model and the surrounding digital environment.

I therefore suggest that ethical oversight should progressively evolve from focusing predominantly on the model lifecycle towards evaluating the clinical interaction lifecycle through which foundation models influence patient care. Rather than asking only whether an AI model has been appropriately developed and validated, ethics committees should additionally examine the conditions under which clinical interactions occur, because these interactions determine whether outputs are reliable, reproducible and clinically safe.

The clinical interaction lifecycle can be operationalised through four interrelated domains.

First, prompt governance should be recognised as a protocol-level variable because system prompts, user instructions and contextual framing can substantially influence model behaviour, even though the underlying model architecture remains unchanged.

Second, deployment governance should document the technical environment in which the model operates, including model version, inference configuration, connected retrieval systems, external tools and vendor-controlled updates that may alter performance during an ongoing study.

Third, competence governance should extend beyond conventional measures of discrimination or accuracy by evaluating whether a model appropriately recognises uncertainty, abstains when evidence is insufficient, and reliably escalates high-risk decisions to human clinicians.

Finally, locale governance should ensure that model performance is validated within the intended linguistic, regulatory and healthcare environment rather than inferred from benchmarks generated in different clinical systems.

Together, these domains determine how foundation models behave in routine cardiovascular practice and therefore represent critical targets for ethical oversight.

Locale governance is especially important for European cardiovascular research, where clinical practice is shaped by multilingual communication, national prescribing rules, reimbursement pathways and local interpretations of international guidelines. Competence demonstrated in one language or healthcare system may therefore mean clinical readiness is overestimated elsewhere. Protocols should explicitly justify the deployment language, the guideline references used for adjudication and the extent to which local workflows are represented in validation scenarios.5

Fortunately, much of the methodological infrastructure required to operationalise this broader perspective already exists. Rather than creating an entirely new regulatory framework, ethics committees could integrate established AI-specific reporting and evaluation standards into an interaction-centred extension of the lifecycle approach proposed by Barucci et al.1 Instruments such as TRIPOD+AI, PROBAST+AI, DECIDE-AI, CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI collectively address many domains that are becoming increasingly important for foundation models, including intended use, data provenance, validation strategy, human–AI interaction, user training, error analysis and post-deployment evaluation.3,4,8–10 Mapping these instruments onto the AI Act would transform high-level regulatory principles into auditable protocol requirements while avoiding unnecessary duplication of guidance.

For ethics committees, the practical implication is that protocol review should include not only the intended clinical task and validation dataset but also the interaction conditions under which the system will be used. A cardiovascular foundation-model study should therefore specify who interacts with the system, what information is provided to it, whether prompts are standardised or adaptive, whether external knowledge sources are used, how outputs are incorporated into clinical judgement and what safeguards prevent unsupported recommendations from directly influencing patient care. These are not merely technical details; they define the intervention being evaluated.

This distinction is relevant because conventional performance measures may not adequately capture clinically meaningful risk. A highly accurate model may still generate rare but consequential recommendations that remain difficult to detect during traditional validation. Similarly, fluent reasoning and persuasive language may create unwarranted confidence despite incomplete or uncertain evidence.5,6,8

Ethical oversight should therefore extend beyond aggregate performance metrics to include evidence of uncertainty calibration, appropriate abstention, transparent communication of limitations and mechanisms for timely human escalation when confidence is low or when recommendations carry substantial clinical consequences. This is especially important in cardiovascular medicine, where plausible, but unsupported, recommendations may alter antithrombotic therapy, reperfusion strategy, device selection or escalation of care.5–7

This broader view also clarifies responsibility. Interaction-centred governance should not rest solely on ethics committees. Sponsors should document the technical configuration and change-management plan; investigators should define the clinical workflow and boundaries of use; institutions should ensure appropriate training, access control and auditability; and clinicians should retain responsibility for patient-facing decisions. Such shared accountability is particularly important when the foundation model itself is developed and updated by a third-party vendor outside the study team.

These considerations should not be interpreted as criticism of the framework proposed by Barucci et al.1 Their proportional approach provides a strong foundation for interaction-centred governance. Extending oversight from model development towards clinical interaction preserves the spirit of the AI Act while keeping ethical review aligned with the evolving nature of cardiovascular AI.

The AI Act represents a landmark achievement in the regulation of clinical AI. As cardiovascular research increasingly adopts general-purpose foundation models; however, the principal challenge is no longer regulating algorithms alone, but also governing how clinicians interact with them in real-world practice. Recognising this shift from the model lifecycle to the clinical interaction lifecycle is the natural next step for ethics committees evaluating cardiovascular AI studies.

Such an evolution would preserve proportional regulation while strengthening reproducibility, transparency and patient safety for AI systems likely to shape cardiovascular research and practice in the coming years.
