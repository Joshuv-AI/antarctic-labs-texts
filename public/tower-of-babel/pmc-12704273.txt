Subject: Letters to the editor: Re: rates of lumbar vertebral body compression fracture after basivertebral nerve ablation: A retrospective study utilizing a large national database
Authors: Patricia Zheng, Conor O'Neill
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The results from randomized clinical trials (RCT's) of basivertebral nerve ablation (BVNA) have been encouraging: a 2022 systematic review reported that approximately 64 % of treated individuals (95 % confidence interval (CI), 43–82 %) achieved at least 50 % pain relief, with benefits sustained through 12 months of follow-up [1]. As with most clinical trials, the BVNA RCT's were powered to demonstrate efficacy, not to detect adverse events. Detecting even common adverse events requires large observational studies, such as the analysis by Chau and colleagues. Leveraging a national administrative database, the investigators examined rates of post-procedural lumbar vertebral compression fractures (VCFs) among patients with pre-existing osteoporosis or osteopenia. Using ICD-10 codes to define osteopenia and osteoporosis and subsequent VCFs, they observed a markedly higher incidence of post-BVNA fractures in this cohort compared with patients without underlying bone disease (4.64 % vs 0.37 %). This is perhaps not surprising, as animal studies have shown coagulation necrosis of bone tissue following radiofrequency ablation (RFA) [2,3]. The data from Chau et al. suggest that this is not clinically relevant in healthy bone tissue, but may lead to compression fractures in those with compromised bone quality.

There are three important implications of the study by Chau and colleagues. First, patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis should be advised that they have 4–5 % risk of a VCF following a BVNA. Second, more data is needed on the effect of thermal ablation on bone tissue. In its 510-k regulatory filings to the Food and Drug Administration, the BVNA industry sponsor cited predicate radiofrequency ablation devices that are used to ablate soft tissues, rather than bone [4]. While thermal ablation of bone metastases for pain control has been studied [5], the effect of BVNA on bone tissue has not. It is particularly important to understand the effects of the duration of heating during BVNA, which in clinical practice is variable, on bone quality. Finally, more data is needed on the efficacy of BVNA on patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis. Patients with osteoporosis (T-score < −2.5) or a history of fragility or traumatic vertebral fracture were excluded from BVNA RCT's [6,7]. In the real world, however, Chau et al. found that 28 % of patients undergoing BVNA had a diagnosis of osteopenia or osteoporosis prior to the intervention. These patients may have a variety of other co-morbidities that accompany an aging spine with poor bone health (e.g. facet arthritis, deformity, stenosis) which may make them less likely to respond to a BVNA. Without data on efficacy, it is not possible to define the risk-benefit profile of BVNA in this subgroup.

Given the realities of research funding, data on the effects of heating bone tissue is likely years away as are RCT's establishing the efficacy of BVNA in patients with compromised bone quality. In the meantime, clinicians should discuss with their patients with osteoporosis or osteopenia that efficacy may be uncertain, and there is likely increased risk of VCF.

The study by Chau et al. is an excellent example of the importance of large administrative database studies in defining the risks associated with spine interventions. While these studies cannot establish efficacy of interventions, they can help physicians fulfill a fundamental obligation to their patients - first, do no harm.
