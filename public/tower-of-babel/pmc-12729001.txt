Male fertility restoration of tetrad pollen sterility in BC1 and its derived populations from Solanum verrucosum
Authors: Rena Sanetomo, Sana Okamoto-Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTetrad sterility-type cytoplasmic male sterility (T-CMS) is characterized by sterile pollen grains that stick together to form a tetrad-like structure (tetrad pollen). T-CMS has been observed in interspecific hybrids with the cytoplasm of Solanum verrucosum (2n = 2x = 24) and in potato cultivars with the cytoplasm of S. stoloniferum (2n = 4x = 48).ResultsIn this study, we developed a BC1 population from a T-CMS-type F1 hybrid (S. verrucosum PI 498061 × 2x breeding clone) backcrossed with S. verrucosum PI 498061. We then generated two sib-mated and two self-mated populations from the BC1 plants. Among the 361 plants across these five populations, we identified plants with varying degrees of fertility restoration. Their pollen phenotypes included normal fertile pollen, tetrad pollen with acetocarmine-stainable grains, tetrad pollen containing only premature pollen, and solitary sterile pollen. Pollen tube germination was confirmed for some stained tetrad pollen grains. We measured the frequencies of normal pollen, stained pollen, and tetrad pollen in all plants. The frequencies of normal and stained pollen were strongly correlated, but both were weakly correlated with tetrad pollen frequency. A linkage map with 648 SNP loci was constructed, and QTL analysis was performed for these three frequencies. Five QTLs (QTL1, QTL3, QTL5, QTL11a, and QTL11b on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, and 11, respectively) were identified. Among these, QTL5 and QTL11a showed strong associations with the normal and stained pollen frequencies (R2 = 39%), while QTL1 and QTL3 were strongly linked to the tetrad pollen frequency (R2 = 84%). In QTL1 and QTL11a, S. verrucosum-type homozygotes improved pollen normality, whereas in QTL3 and QTL5, S. verrucosum-type homozygotes reduced pollen quality, resulting in tetrad pollen.ConclusionsAt least two genetic effects involving five QTLs are necessary to restore fertility in the T-CMS lines: one affecting maturation and stainability, and the other for separating tetrad pollen into individual pollen grains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07657-6.

Introduction

The nuclear and mitochondrial genomes coevolve within plant species, resulting in species-specific genotypes, expressions, and protein products [1–4]. Cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) is a well-known trait that prevents the production of viable pollen and has been associated with the mitochondrial genome [5, 6]. Expression of CMS is explained by a dysfunctional mitochondrial genome [7] or by interactions with the nuclear genome [5]. In the latter case, a plant with a CMS-causing mitochondrial genome is not always male sterile but often produces viable pollen due to nuclear-mitochondrial interactions [8]. CMS is frequently observed in interspecific or intergeneric hybrids and in somatic cell fusion of plants [5, 9–12], resulting from the disruption of nuclear-mitochondrial interactions caused by coevolution, which implies that this disruption most severely manifests as a failure in pollen development.

CMS is often associated with novel open reading frames (ORFs) in the mitochondrial genome [10, 12, 13]. These ORFs are genetically suppressed by the nuclear genome, which prevents the expression of CMS. The nuclear genes that suppress CMS are known as restorer-of-fertility (Rf) genes, which are found in various crops, including common bean [14], beet [15], maize [16], radish [17], rice [18, 19], and sorghum [20]. Fertility restoration by Rf genes involves the alteration in the abundance of mRNAs and proteins associated with cell division, membrane formation and degradation, and nutrient supply during pollen development [10, 21–26]. The major class of Rf genes encodes pentatricopeptide repeat (PPR) proteins, which generally bind to RNA molecules and participate in RNA processing of mitochondrial genes and ORFs [27–29].

Among potato varieties (Solanum tuberosum L., 2n = 4x = 48), several different cytoplasms have been reported [30], of which some are known to induce CMS. The most common, T-type cytoplasm, causes various types of CMS [31], and a dominant male fertility-restorer gene (Rt) has been found for this cytoplasm [32]. Tetrad sterility-type cytoplasmic male sterility (T-CMS) is phenotypically characterized by premature pollen grains that stick together, forming a tetrad-like clump. T-CMS has been observed in many varieties derived from the Mexican tetraploid species Solanum stoloniferum Schlechtd. et Bché. (2n = 4x = 48). This species is an allotetraploid with an AABB genome [33]. S. stoloniferum has been used as a genetic resource for introducing potato virus Y (PVY) resistance genes [34, 35]. However, S. stoloniferum can only be crossed as a female, because reciprocal crosses do not produce seeds for unknown reasons. Once used in breeding programs, all progeny exhibited T-CMS and were completely male sterile [36–39]. Currently, the increasing number of T-CMS varieties is gradually restricting the availability of pollen parents in potato breeding [40, 41]. T-CMS varieties have the W/γ-type cytoplasm derived from S. stoloniferum [30, 38, 41]. Nevertheless, not all accessions of S. stoloniferum have the W/γ-type cytoplasm [42]. Therefore, although potato cytoplasm has been classified based on chloroplast- and mitochondrial DNA polymorphisms, DNA markers alone are insufficient to identify the cytoplasm causing T-CMS. We analyzed mitochondrial DNA sequences of the T-CMS F1 hybrids of S. stoloniferum and identified a cytoplasmic variant, designated TSCsto, which contains a unique 859-bp mitochondrial DNA fragment associated with T-CMS [42]. Complete sequencing of the mitochondrial genome of TSCsto allowed us to identify a novel recombinant molecule of mitochondrial DNA (called RC-I), characterized by the rpl5-ψrps14 gene being joined to the nad6 gene [43]. RC-I was also found in the Mexican diploid wild species Solanum verrucosum Schltdl. (2n = 2x = 24), a maternal ancestor and A-genome donor of S. stoloniferum [33]. Consistent with this, T-CMS was first reported in interspecific hybrids between S. verrucosum as the cytoplasmic donor and other diploid species, such as Solanum chacoense Bitt. (2n = 2x = 24) [44–47]. Due to the lack of S-ribonuclease (S-RNase) expression in the style [48], S. verrucosum is highly self-compatible, self-fertile, and able to accept pollen from a wide range of wild Solanum species [49]. This ability allows S. verrucosum to serve as a bridging species between cultivated potatoes and more distantly related species [50, 51]. However, pollen of S. verrucosum is rejected in crosses with self-incompatible species, showing unilateral incompatibility [52]. We analyzed 92 interspecific hybrids using eight accessions of S. verrucosum and found that only progeny from one accession carrying RC-I presented T-CMS; the other hybrids produced fertile pollen. This suggests that RC-I, shared among S. verrucosum- and S. stoloniferum-derived male sterile hybrids, is responsible for T-CMS [43].

T-CMS varieties exclusively produce tetrad pollen, and their offspring also show T-CMS. Fertility restoration has never been reported for T-CMS. However, S. stoloniferum and S. verrucosum produce abundant pollen and set berries via naturally occurring selfing. To restore pollen fertility of a T-CMS F1 hybrid from a cross between S. verrucosum as the female and a diploid cultivated potato as the male, we backcrossed it with the RC-I-carrying accession of S. verrucosum and created BC1 plants. Among these BC1 plants, several pollen-fertile plants were found. In this study, we investigated pollen fertility restoration in T-CMS by analyzing pollen tube germination and developmental differences between normal and tetrad pollen. To identify genomic regions associated with pollen fertility restoration, we examined three traits related to pollen morphology and acetocarmine stainability in 361 plants from five populations using quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis with single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers covering the entire genome.

Pollen germination test

To examine the viability of normal and tetrad pollen, a pollen germination test was performed on five plants from the 21H2 population and six from the 21H3 population (Table 3). Pollen from three plants with n-pollen frequencies above 40% germinated at rates ranging from 17.9 to 58.6%. The average pollen tube lengths ranged from 30.0 to 53.0 μm. Tetrad pollen collected from five plants with t-pollen frequencies above 70% germinated at rates from 0.3 to 13.8% (Fig. 3). The germinated pollen tubes were observed only from stained grains in tetrad pollen, except for 21H3-16: in this plant, although 31.0% of the tetrad pollen grains were stained, the grains became destained after germination (Fig. 3c). Plants with high n-pollen frequency and low t-pollen frequency

21H2-15, 21H3-26, and 21H3-38 were the plants with high n-pollen frequencies (76.6, 48.4, and 43.1%, respectively; see Table 3) and very low t-pollen frequencies (0, 0.8, and 0%, respectively). Immediately after meiosis, these plants presented fewer visible nuclei and membranes appearing to form tetrads within the PMC (stage 3, Fig. 4g), followed by thicker cell walls of microspores and visible boundaries in tetrad pollen (stage 4, Fig. 4j). Then, the PMC callose membrane was degraded (stage 5, Fig. 4m), and the microspores were separated. Immature pollen grains with dimples (pollen pores) were observed (stage 6, Fig. 4p). Finally, the mature pollen gradually swelled and became well-stained (stage 7, Fig. 4s).

Plants with low n-pollen frequency and high t-pollen frequency.

21H2-55, 21H3-3, 21H3-5, and 21H3-46 were the plants with low n-pollen frequencies (0.3, 0.1, 0, and 0.1%, respectively) and high t-pollen frequencies (59.9, 99.9, 100, and 99.8%, respectively). After meiosis, the boundaries between the microspores were unclear in these plants. The four microspores were not evenly positioned at stage 3 (Fig. 4h), and the microspores either joined together or partially collapsed at stage 4 (Fig. 4k). The PMC membrane of the tetrad microspores was not fully degraded at stage 5, and microspore division did not occur (Fig. 4n, q). At stage 7, 21H3-3 differed from 21H2-55 (Fig. 4t) as the deformed sterile pollen grains in the former were firmly adhered within the tetrad (Fig. 4u).

Plants with low n-pollen frequency and high s-pollen and t-pollen frequencies.

21H2-28 and 21H3-16 were plants with low n-pollen frequencies (13.8 and 0%, respectively), high s-pollen frequencies (62.6 and 31.0%, respectively), and high t-pollen frequencies (73.2 and 100%, respectively). In these plants, although four uniform microspores were observed, the walls were thinner than those in 21H2-15 and 21H2-55 (Fig. 4l). The unseparated mature pollen was faintly stained at stages 5 and 6 (Fig. 4o, r), and then the tetrad pollen, containing stained and unstained pollen grains, was observed at stage 7 (Fig. 4v).

Linkage map construction

A commercially available microarray, the 22 K potato V3 SNP array, was used for genotyping genome-wide SNPs. An initial BC1 map was constructed from the 21H2 population with 8,119 SNPs using the mapping software SeSAM [53]. After removing cosegregating markers, 648 loci were mapped, covering a total length of 817.8 cM, with an average distance of 1.26 cM between loci (Table 4). Four F2 populations were derived from the BC1 population either through self-mating (21H3 and 21H4) or sib-mating (21H5 and 22H146). Consequently, the total map lengths of the 21H3 and 21H4 populations were 35.7 − 56.5% shorter, and chromosomes 1, 2, and 8 in the 21H3 population and chromosomes 2, 9, and 12 in the 21H4 population were almost entirely homozygous. This occurs because approximately half of the loci in the BC1 map were homozygous with the S. verrucosum alleles (ver/ver), resulting in increased homozygosity in the self-progenies. QTL detection

To identify genomic regions responsible for fertility restoration, QTL analysis was performed using 648 to 256 SNP markers across five mapping populations for n-pollen, s-pollen, and t-pollen frequencies. Single-point analysis with analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed multiple locations that significantly contributed to the n-pollen frequency (P < 0.001): chromosomes 5 and 11 in 21H2; chromosome 3 in 21H3; chromosomes 1, 6, and 11 in 21H5; and chromosome 11 in 22H146 (Fig. S3). Similar regions to those identified for n-pollen frequency were found for s-pollen frequency (Fig. S4). The t-pollen frequency was significantly associated with chromosome 11 in 21H2; chromosome 3 in 21H3; chromosome 1 in 21H4; chromosomes 1, 2, 6, 7, 11, and 12 in 21H5; and chromosome 1 in 22H146 (Fig. S5).

Genetic effects of QTLs on pollen traits

The effects of the detected QTLs on traits were investigated using the SNPs located at the LOD peaks within each QTL. At QTL1, compared to the genotype of the diploid breeding clone (-/-), the homozygous and heterozygous genotypes with S. verrucosum alleles (ver/ver and ver/-, respectively) significantly decreased the t-pollen frequency (Fig. 5), indicating that the S. verrucosum allele has a dominant effect on reducing the t-pollen frequency. At QTL3, the ver/ver genotype lowered n-pollen and s-pollen frequencies and increased the t-pollen frequency. In contrast, the ver/- genotype increased the n-pollen and s-pollen frequencies and decreased the t-pollen frequency (Fig. 5). However, the average frequencies of n-pollen and s-pollen in the -/- genotype were intermediate between those in the ver/ver and ver/- genotypes, suggesting an overdominant effect of the ver/- genotype on increasing normal pollen frequency. The mean t-pollen frequency in the -/- genotype was almost the same as in the ver/- genotype, indicating that the ver/- and -/- genotypes had a greater effect than the ver/ver genotype on decreasing the t-pollen frequency. At QTL5, the s-pollen frequency decreased in the ver/ver genotype and increased in the ver/- genotype (Fig. 5). At QTL11a, n-pollen and s-pollen frequencies were higher in the ver/ver genotype than in the ver/- genotype (Fig. 5). At QTL11b, the t-pollen frequency was reduced in the ver/ver genotype (Fig. 5). Therefore, the ver/ver genotype in QTL1, QTL11a, and QTL11b contributed to improving pollen normality, whereas that in QTL3 and QTL5 lowered pollen quality, resulting in tetrad pollen. Discussion

Multiple nuclear factors are necessary for restoring T-CMS

S. verrucosum (PI 498061) possesses TSCsto cytoplasm, which causes T-CMS in F1 hybrids [43]. S. verrucosum is an exceptional self-fertile species among other diploid potatoes that are self-incompatible due to an S-locus-based gametophytic self-incompatibility mechanism [55, 56]. Interspecific hybrids can be obtained from a wide variety of species when S. verrucosum is used as a female [48, 57], and their pollen fertility is often sufficient for further crossing. However, if the S. verrucosum parent carries the TSCsto cytoplasm, all derived F1 hybrids exhibit T-CMS [43]. Thus, T-CMS likely results from an interaction between the mitochondrial genome and nuclear gene(s). We then backcrossed the T-CMS F1 plant with S. verrucosum (PI 498061). Fertility-restored BC1 plants were obtained as expected. However, unlike the fertility restorations seen in some other crops, where sterile pollen becomes fertile [8, 58, 59], different levels of fertility restoration, with large variations in n-, s-, and t-pollen frequencies, were observed in the BC1 population and four populations derived from self- and sib-mating BC1 plants. Furthermore, a mixture of normal and tetrad pollen or tetrad pollen containing one to four stained pollen grains was observed. These observations suggest that the fertility restoration mechanism of T-CMS involves multiple factors during pollen development.

Developmental defects at the tetrad stage were partially restored

Pollen tubes elongated even from the tetrad pollen if the pollen grains were stained. These findings showed that the stained pollen grains within the tetrad pollen are viable. Therefore, by backcrossing the T-CMS F1 hybrid with S. verrucosum, pollen grains in the tetrad received the necessary nutrients for maturation and were stained, suggesting that fertility was partially restored in BC1 plants.

Pollen development in angiosperms, including the Solanaceae family, begins with the meiosis of the PMC, forming a tetrad reticulum with four cells separated by a callose wall. A membrane surrounding each microspore cell, known as the microspore membrane, then forms, and callose-degrading enzymes from the tapetum degrade the PMC membrane [60–62]. Subsequently, the degradation of the double-layered microspore membrane releases microspores as mature pollen [63, 64]. When we compared pollen development stages between the normal and T-CMS plants in this study, no differences were observed until meiosis was completed. In contrast, notable differences appeared between the tetrad stage and microspore development and separation. When the microspores were released, those in the T-CMS plants adhered to each other or to some destroyed microspores (Fig. 4). Shishova et al. [65] found that T-CMS pollen in potato cultivars differed significantly from normal pollen because of lower carbohydrate accumulation and greater amounts of amino acids, suggesting that callose synthesis and deposition are essential for altering cell wall morphology. As they indicated, we observed that T-CMS plants displayed varying thicknesses of callose walls at the tetrad stage. In tetrad pollen containing stained grains, the microspore walls were thinner than in normal pollen. In Arabidopsis, QUARTET 3 (QRT3), which encodes a polygalacturonase, degrades the PMC wall during microspore development [66]. An allelic gene to QRT3 is restorer of rvms-2 (res2), which causes delayed degradation of the tetrad pectin wall [67]. Similarly, genes involved in callose deposition to form temporary walls separating microsporocytes, callose wall degradation enzymes, and the biosynthesis of sporopollenin for outer membrane formation have been identified in Arabidopsis [68, 69]. A defect in one or more of these genes might be involved in T-CMS in potato.

Five QTLs restore the fertility of T-CMS pollen in a complementary manner

We identified five QTLs, with QTL5 and QTL11a mainly affecting n- and s-pollen frequencies, while QTL1, QTL3, and QTL11b mainly affect t-pollen frequency, although QTL3 and QTL11a affect all three traits. Since n- and s-pollen frequencies are highly correlated (r = 0.98) and associated with the same QTLs, these traits are likely controlled by the same genetic factors related to pollen maturation. The t-pollen frequency was weakly correlated with n- and s-pollen frequencies (r = − 0.37 and r = − 0.28, respectively) and was more affected by QTL3 and QTL11b than by n- and s-pollen frequencies. As a result, t-pollen frequency is likely controlled by different genetic factors, mainly associated with microspore separation or membrane degradation. Therefore, at least two genetic factors are needed to restore fertility in T-CMS plants. Similar genetic interactions are known to restore CMS in other crops, such as Rf5 and Rf6 in rice [70], and two additive-dominant epistatic Rf genes in pepper [71]. OsTMS18, which encodes a glucose-methanol-choline oxidoreductase in rice, restores male fertility through sporopollenin synthesis [72]. OsTMS18 is directly regulated by the transcription factor OsMS188 and indirectly controlled by other tapetum-associated transcription factors (UDT1, OsTDF1, TDR, bHLH142, and EAT1) that function upstream in pathways regulating tapetal cell development and pollen wall formation [73]. These factors work together to restore fertility. In our study, the ver/ver genotype at QTL1 and QTL11a increased n- and s-pollen frequencies, whereas the ver/ver genotype at QTL3 and QTL5 decreased them, and the ver/- and -/- genotypes increased pollen quality. Thus, both S. verrucosum and the diploid breeding clone may positively affect the restoration of sterile tetrad pollen. Notably, QTL3 had a significant effect on all three traits only in the 21H3 population, where the most effective QTL, QTL11a, was fixed to ver/ver homozygotes. Therefore, if all the detected QTLs were fixed to better genotypes to restore T-CMS, additional QTLs could be identified because complete restoration from T-CMS was not achieved in any of the five populations.

Recently, Behling et al. [49] identified two QTLs for interspecific compatibility on chromosomes 1 and 11, and three QTLs for male fertility on chromosomes 3, 8, and 9 in the S. verrucosum genome. Although the QTL3 identified in our study (43.5 cM) differs in its location from the male-fertility QTL on chromosome 3 (79.0 cM), other mechanisms besides tetrad pollen-type sterility could be involved in the current populations, as reported in other hybrids with S. verrucosum (47, 51 ).

Conclusions

In potatoes, various types of CMS, such as nonpollen, nonfunctional pollen, and deformed anther, are frequently observed [31, 74, 75]. Since tubers are commercial products, pollen fertility has not been a major concern in potatoes. However, the increasing use of CMS plants in potato breeding has significantly reduced the number of pollen parents used for cross-breeding [40, 41]. Fertility restoration has been reported in only a limited number of cases for T- and P-type cytoplasms [32, 76]. Recently, diploid inbred-based hybrid potato breeding methods have been developed, and male-sterile maternal inbred lines are needed for hybrid seed production [76]. The T-CMS plants presented complete male sterility. This study revealed that two genetic effects, mediated by at least five QTLs involved in pollen maturation and the separation of pollen grains from tetrads into single pollen grains, are necessary to restore fertility in T-CMS plants. Since the cytoplasmic factor for T-CMS is known to be the 1.5 kb intergenic region between rpl5 and nad6 of mitochondrial genes [43], identifying candidate gene(s) for the detected QTLs and exploring how the intergenic region of the mitochondrial genome interacts with nuclear genes are essential.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Seeds of S. verrucosum PI 498061 were obtained from the US Potato Genebank at Sturgeon Bay, Wisconsin, USA. S. verrucosum PI 498061, which carries RC-I, was crossed as the female with the S. phureja-based diploid breeding clone W872209-51 as the male. The interspecific hybrid 20H2-3, exhibiting T-CMS [43], was backcrossed with S. verrucosum PI 498061 as the male parent, resulting in two BC1 populations: 20H157 and 21H2. One hundred plants from the 21H2 population were used for pollen characterization and QTL analysis (Table 1). Of the male-fertile BC1 plants in the 20H157 population, 20H157-9 and 20H157-16, with n-pollen frequencies of 88.8 and 63.4%, respectively, were self-crossed, resulting in 48 plants from the 21H3 population and 50 plants from the 21H4 population. From a cross of a T-CMS BC1 plant (20H157-3) with a male-fertile BC1 plant, 20H157-16, 50 plants of the 21H5 population were generated. Another T-CMS BC1 plant, 21H2-55, was crossed with a male-fertile BC1 plant, 21H2-28 (n-pollen frequency of 13.8%), which derived 113 plants from the 22H146 population. A total of 361 plants from five populations (21H2, 21H3, 21H4, 21H5, and 22H146) were grown in 15-cm-diameter pots in a greenhouse under 16-h day-length conditions supplemented with artificial lights. The greenhouse temperature was maintained above 18 ℃ in the winter. In the summer, the windows were opened to allow for natural aeration, keeping the temperature under 25 ℃.

Observation of the morphology and viability of pollen grains

Fresh pollen was collected at least twice at weekly intervals from different flower clusters. The collected pollen was stained with 1% acetocarmine. More than 300 pollen grains were counted from at least three different locations on a single glass slide, and their stainability and morphology were observed under a microscope. Plants showing significant differences in stainability or morphology between the first and second observations were examined more than three times. Pollen that was stained uniformly with a round shape was considered normal, while unstained, partially stained, or deformed pollen was considered abnormal. A clump of four pollen grains was counted as tetrad pollen. If tetrad pollen contained one to four stained grains, the number of stained grains was recorded.

Observation of pollen morphology at different developmental stages

Anthers were collected from three plants with high n-pollen frequency and low t-pollen frequency, from four plants with low n-pollen frequency and high t-pollen frequency, and from two plants with low n-pollen frequency and high s-pollen and t-pollen frequencies. Immature reproductive cells were scraped from the anthers, placed on a glass slide, and stained with 1% acetocarmine.

Pollen germination test

A pollen germination test was performed following the method of Mortenson et al. [77]. Fresh pollen was collected from two flowers (two replicates) of each genotype. Pollen germination medium was prepared using a 10 × stock solution [(0.3 g Ca(NO3)2·4H2O and 0.05 g H3BO3 dissolved in 100 ml distilled water] with 1% Bacto agar (Difco Laboratories, Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD, USA) and 20% sucrose. The medium components were dissolved by heating, and the process was immediately stopped after boiling. Two drops of the dissolved medium were placed on a glass slide and kept in a moisture chamber for one hour at 100% humidity. After the medium solidified, the pollen was lightly spread over it by vibration using an electric toothbrush and incubated at room temperature for 21 h. The agar medium was then cut into a 1 cm square, stained with a drop of 1% acetocarmine, covered with a cover glass, and incubated for 1 min. The medium-placed glass slide was heated to melt the agar medium and then placed at room temperature to allow it to solidify naturally. Pollen germination was observed under a microscope. If even a slight sign of pollen tube elongation was observed, it was considered germinated pollen. More than 300 pollen grains were observed from at least three different locations on one glass slide, and the germination percentage was calculated as 100 × the number of germinated pollen grains divided by the total number of pollen grains. The germination percentage of tetrad pollen was also calculated as 100 × the number of germinated tetrad pollens/the total number of tetrad pollens. In this case, tetrad pollen was counted as one, and when one or more pollen grains of the tetrad germinated, it was considered germinated tetrad pollen. The lengths of the germinated grains, including those of tetrad pollen, were measured. Simultaneously, the n-pollen, s-pollen, and t-pollen frequencies were measured for the pollen collected from another anther in the same flower used for the pollen germination test.

SNP data

Total DNA was extracted from fresh leaves using the method described by Hosaka and Hanneman [78]. One microgram of DNA from each sample was dried and sent to GeneSeek (Neogen Corporation, NE, USA), from which 22 K potato V3 SNP array data were obtained. As Peterson et al. [79] reported, ambiguous SNP loci were discarded. SNPs with more than 10% missing data, those with lower than 5% minor allele frequency (MAF), and monomorphic SNPs were subsequently discarded. Plants with more than 10% missing data were also discarded. Based on the parent-progeny relationship, SNPs exhibiting unexpected segregation were excluded. Finally, SNPs from the chloroplast genome or unknown locations in the reference genome (DM1-3 v4.03, Potato Genome Sequencing Consortium 2011) were excluded.

Linkage map construction and QTL analysis

A linkage map was constructed for the 21H2 (BC1) population using filtered SNP data with SeSAM [48]. A map file was prepared using marker positions in the reference genome (DM1-3 v4.03), and a marker quality file was prepared using the Gentrain scores of the SNPs. Using graphical representations of the genetic and physical maps (Marey maps), markers showing deviations from the line were removed, and the map was reconstructed. This process was repeated until markers formed a straight line on the physical map. The same map positions of SNP loci as those used for the 21H2 population were applied to all other populations. For each population, SNP data were loaded into R/qtl [54], and cosegregating markers were identified with the “findDupMarkers” function and removed from the dataset. QTL analysis was initially performed using single-point analysis with ANOVA in JMP Pro 17.2.0 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, NC, USA). Next, simple interval mapping was performed using R/qtl [54], with the “scanone” function and a nonparametric model, Haley-Knott regression [80], and a step size of 2 cM. A genome-wide logarithm of odds (LOD) significance threshold was determined using a permutation test (n = 1000). A two-dimensional genome scan using a two-QTL model was performed with the “scantwo” function to identify interactions.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses, including the Kruskal–Wallis test, Dunn test, and ANOVA, were conducted using JMP Pro 17.2.0.
