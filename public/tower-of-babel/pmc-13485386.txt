Separation Anxiety in Emerging Adults: The Role of Attachment Styles and Personality Traits
Authors: Gülsüm Hançer, İbrahim Halil Deniz, M Murat Kırpınar, Neşe Kocabaşoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective To examine childhood and adult separation anxiety symptoms and their associations with attachment styles, personality traits, and sociodemographic factors among university students. Methods This cross-sectional study included 200 first-year university students. Attachment styles and personality traits were assessed using the Three-Dimensional Attachment Style Scale and the Five-Factor Personality Inventory, respectively. Based on validated cut-off scores from the Separation Anxiety Symptom Inventory and the Adult Separation Anxiety Questionnaire, participants were classified as screen-negative (n=48, 24%), childhood separation anxiety symptom-positive (CSAD) (n=30, 15%), adult separation anxiety symptom-positive (ASAD) (n=27, 13.5%), or childhood and adult separation anxiety symptom-positive (CSAD&ASAD) (n=95, 47.5%). Group differences were analyzed using non-parametric tests, and hierarchical regression examined factors associated with adult separation anxiety symptom scores. Results The CSAD&ASAD group showed significantly lower secure attachment and higher avoidant and anxious–ambivalent attachment scores compared with other groups. Emotional stability scores were significantly higher in the screen-negative group than in the ASAD and CSAD&ASAD groups. Parental separation/divorce and firstborn status were associated with higher symptom levels. In multivariable analyses, higher Beck Anxiety Inventory scores (β=0.275, p<0.001), lower emotional stability (β=-0.266, p<0.001), lower conscientiousness (β=-0.155, p=0.012), and firstborn status (β=-0.114, p=0.045) were independently associated with adult separation anxiety symptom scores, with the final model explaining 48.3% of the variance. Conclusion Separation anxiety symptoms were common among university students. Findings highlight the role of internalizing symptom severity, personality traits, and selected sociodemographic factors in adult separation anxiety symptom scores. These findings should be interpreted at the symptom level and require confirmation through longitudinal and clinically structured studies.Keywords: Separation anxiety, Attachment, Personality traits, Emerging adulthood

INTRODUCTION

Separation anxiety refers to distress related to actual or anticipated separation from major attachment figures. Although some degree of separation anxiety is developmentally normative in early childhood, such reactions become clinically significant when they are excessive, developmentally inappropriate, or associated with marked distress and functional impairment. Historically conceptualized as a childhood condition, separation anxiety is now recognized across the lifespan, including adulthood. Recent research has further emphasized that separation anxiety symptoms may persist beyond childhood and remain underrecognized in adult populations [1].

Epidemiological and clinical studies have shown that separation anxiety may persist or newly emerge in adulthood. In the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, the lifetime prevalence of separation anxiety disorder was reported as 6.6%, supporting its significance beyond early developmental stages [2]. A recent large-scale meta-analysis of age at onset across mental disorders identified phobias and separation anxiety among the earliest-onset conditions, further supporting the developmental relevance of separation-related symptoms before and during emerging adulthood. Clinical studies have also shown that adult separation anxiety is associated with greater anxiety and depressive symptom severity, higher neuroticism, and functional impairment in anxiety clinic samples, underscoring its relevance for psychiatric assessment [3,4]. Emerging adulthood represents a particularly sensitive developmental period in this regard, as it involves increasing autonomy, separation from family, and the formation of new interpersonal bonds. For university students leaving home, these developmental demands may increase the salience of separation-related distress and may reveal broader psychological vulnerability, particularly in individuals with insecure attachment patterns or elevated internalizing symptoms. Recent studies in emerging adults have consistently highlighted associations between separation anxiety symptoms, insecure attachment patterns, and broader internalizing psychopathology [2,5,6]. Empirical findings in university samples support this relevance: an exploratory study of first-semester college students reported clinically elevated separation anxiety symptoms in 21% of participants, while a Turkish study of medical students using a structured clinical interview reported adult separation anxiety disorder criteria in 20.1% of participants [7,8].

Attachment theory provides a useful framework for understanding individual differences in separation-related distress. Secure attachment is generally associated with a stable sense of safety and more adaptive emotion regulation, whereas insecure attachment, particularly anxious–ambivalent attachment, has been linked to heightened vulnerability to anxiety symptoms in close relational contexts. In emerging adults, recent studies have shown that both childhood and adult separation anxiety symptoms are more strongly associated with anxious–ambivalent attachment patterns than with secure attachment. In addition, parental separation or divorce has been associated with higher attachment-related anxiety and avoidance in adulthood, suggesting that relational experiences may be associated with attachment-related vulnerability during developmental transitions such as leaving home for university [5,9-12].

Personality traits may contribute to individual differences in separation anxiety. Contemporary research has consistently linked higher levels of neuroticism, or lower emotional stability, with increased vulnerability to anxiety-related symptoms, particularly in situations involving interpersonal stress and uncertainty. In emerging adults, emotional stability appears to function as a protective factor, whereas higher neuroticism is associated with greater emotional reactivity and sensitivity to separation-related distress. Recent studies have further suggested that personality traits may interact with attachment-related processes in shaping anxiety responses, supporting a multidimensional understanding of separation anxiety beyond categorical diagnostic frameworks [5,10].

Identifying elevated separation anxiety symptoms in nonclinical university populations may be clinically relevant because such symptoms may reflect broader internalizing vulnerability during a developmental period characterized by separation from family, increasing autonomy, and new interpersonal demands. Although previous studies have examined adult separation anxiety, attachment, or personality-related factors separately, relatively few studies have evaluated childhood and adult separation anxiety symptoms together with both attachment styles and personality traits in first-year university students.

Therefore, the present study aimed to examine childhood and adult separation anxiety symptoms and their associations with attachment styles, personality traits, and sociodemographic factors among first-year university students. We hypothesized that elevated separation anxiety symptom scores would be positively associated with anxious–ambivalent attachment and negatively associated with secure attachment and emotional stability. We also expected adult separation anxiety symptom scores to remain associated with attachment-and personality-related variables after accounting for selected sociodemographic factors.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a university faculty between April and May 2023. The study protocol was approved by Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul University–Cerrahpaşa, Cerrahpaşa Faculty of Medicine (approval no: 619848; approval date: 15 February 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants aged 18 years and older. For participants under 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from both the participant and their parents or legal guardians. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Population and sample

The study population consisted of 320 first-year university students aged 17–22 years who had relocated from other cities for their education. Students were informed about the study face to face at the university faculty and were invited to participate after providing written informed consent. Of the 320 students invited, 200 agreed to participate and completed the questionnaires, yielding a response rate of 62.5%. Students who were married, had a major medical illness, or reported current treatment with mood stabilizers or antipsychotic medications were not included. Students receiving treatment for depressive or anxiety symptoms were not excluded; these conditions were recorded through the sociodemographic and clinical data form and reported as clinical variables. Because no structured clinical interview was conducted, psychiatric exclusion criteria were based on self-reported treatment history rather than clinician-confirmed diagnoses.

Based on established cut-off scores, participants scoring 12 or higher on the Separation Anxiety Symptom Inventory (SASI) were classified into the childhood separation anxiety symptom-positive (CSAD) (n=30, 15%), those scoring 25 or higher on the Adult Separation Anxiety Questionnaire (ASA-27) were classified into the adult separation anxiety symptom-positive (ASAD) (n=27, 13.5%), and those scoring above the cut-off on both measures were classified into the childhood and adult separation anxiety symptom-positive (CSAD&ASAD) (n=95, 47.5%). The remaining participants were classified into the screen-negative group (n=48, 24%). These classifications were based on established self-report screening cut-off scores and should not be interpreted as clinically confirmed diagnostic categories.

Data collection tools

In addition to the SASI and ASA-27, which were used to assess childhood and adult separation anxiety symptoms, respectively, data were collected using a sociodemographic and clinical data form, the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), the Three-Dimensional Attachment Style Scale (TDAS), and the Five-Factor Personality Inventory (FFPI).

Sociodemographic and clinical data form

A sociodemographic and clinical data form was developed by the researchers based on the relevant literature. The form included questions on age, sex, family type (parents living together, divorced parents, deceased parents), number of siblings, tobacco, alcohol, and substance use, class attendance, family income level (low, medium, high), recent loss experiences, and the presence of any known medical or psychiatric conditions. It also included questions on current or previous psychiatric treatment and current psychotropic medication use.

Beck Depression Inventory

BDI is a 21-item self-report tool developed by Beck et al. [13] in 1961 to assess depressive symptoms. Each item is assigned a score between 0 and 3; hence, the total score that can be obtained from BDI ranges from 0 to 63. The validity and reliability studies of the Turkish version of BDI were conducted by Hisli [14]. In the Turkish validation study conducted among university students, the BDI demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.80.

Beck Anxiety Inventory

BAI is a 21-item, 4-point Likert-type self-assessment tool developed by Beck et al. [15] to assess anxiety symptoms. Each item is assigned a score between 0 and 3; hence, the total score that can be obtained from BAI ranges between 0 and 63. The validity and reliability studies of the Turkish version of BAI were conducted by Ulusoy et al. [16]. In the Turkish validation study, the BAI demonstrated high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.93.

Separation Anxiety Symptom Inventory

The 15-item, 4-point Likert-type SASI was developed by Silove et al. [17] to assess childhood separation anxiety symptoms in adults. Participants scoring 12 or higher on the SASI were considered positive for childhood separation anxiety symptoms [18]. These classifications were based on established screening cut-off scores derived from self-report instruments and do not represent clinically confirmed diagnostic categories. The validity and reliability studies of the Turkish version of SASI were conducted by Diriöz et al. [18] in 2012. In the Turkish validation study, the SASI demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.86.

Adult Separation Anxiety Questionnaire

The ASA-27 is a 27-item self-report measure developed by Manicavasagar et al. [19] to assess separation anxiety symptoms in adulthood. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, yielding a total score range of 0 to 81. Participants scoring 25 or higher were considered positive for adult separation anxiety symptoms. The Turkish validity and reliability of the ASA-27 were established by Diriöz et al. [18]. In the Turkish validation study, the ASA-27 demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.93.

Three-Dimensional Attachment Style Scale

The TDAS is an 18-item scale developed by Erzen [20] to assess secure, avoidant, and anxious–ambivalent attachment styles. In the original validation study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.69 for the secure attachment subscale, 0.80 for the avoidant attachment subscale, and 0.71 for the anxious–ambivalent attachment subscale [20].

Five-Factor Personality Inventory

The FFPI is a 50-item, 5-point Likert-type scale derived from the International Personality Item Pool [21,22]. It assesses five personality traits: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and openness to experience, each measured with 10 items. The Turkish validity and reliability of the FFPI were established by Tatar [23]. In the Turkish validation study, the FFPI demonstrated acceptable internal consistency across its five personality dimensions, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.68 to 0.85.

Five-Factor Personality Inventory

The FFPI is a 50-item, 5-point Likert-type scale derived from the International Personality Item Pool [21,22]. It assesses five personality traits: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and openness to experience, each measured with 10 items. The Turkish validity and reliability of the FFPI were established by Tatar [23]. In the Turkish validation study, the FFPI demonstrated acceptable internal consistency across its five personality dimensions, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.68 to 0.85.

Statistical analysis

A priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf) to determine the minimum required sample size. Assuming a medium effect size (f=0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80, the required sample size was calculated as 180 participants. The final sample consisted of 200 students.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and visual inspection of histogram plots. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean±standard deviation, median (minimum–maximum), or frequency (n) and percentage (%), as appropriate.

Comparisons of categorical variables between groups were performed using Pearson’s chi-square test. Differences in continuous variables between two independent groups were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and comparisons across more than two groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When the Kruskal–Wallis test was significant, all pairwise post hoc comparisons were performed using Dunn–Bonferroni correction.

Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Effect sizes were reported as Cramer’s V for chi-square tests, epsilon-squared (ε²) for Kruskal–Wallis tests, Spearman’s rho for correlation analyses, and standardized β coefficients with R² and ΔR² for regression models.

In addition, hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine factors associated with adult separation anxiety symptom scores. Adult separation anxiety score was entered as the dependent variable. Sociodemographic variables were entered in the first block, attachment styles in the second block, personality traits in the third block, and depressive and anxiety symptom severity, assessed using BDI and BAI scores, in the fourth block. Standardized regression coefficients (β), p-values, R², adjusted R², and changes in R² (ΔR²) were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor values, with acceptable thresholds applied.

A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the second step, the inclusion of attachment styles significantly increased the explained variance (ΔR²=0.145, p<0.001), with the model accounting for 22.7% of the variance.

In the third step, the addition of personality traits resulted in a further significant increase in explained variance (ΔR²=0.179, p<0.001), with the model explaining 40.6% of the variance in adult separation anxiety (R²=0.406, adjusted R²=0.363).

In the fourth step, the additional inclusion of BDI and BAI scores significantly increased the explained variance to 48.3% (R²=0.483, adjusted R²=0.438, ΔR²=0.076, p<0.001).

In the final model, lower emotional stability (β=-0.266, p<0.001), lower conscientiousness (β=-0.155, p=0.012), firstborn status (β=-0.114, p=0.045), and higher BAI scores (β=0.275, p<0.001) remained significantly associated with adult separation anxiety symptom scores.

DISCUSSION

The present study examined separation anxiety symptoms and their associations with attachment styles, personality traits, and sociodemographic factors in university students. The findings indicate that separation anxiety symptoms are relatively common in this population and are associated with a consistent pattern of psychological vulnerability characterized by anxious–ambivalent attachment and lower emotional stability. In contrast, individuals with lower symptom levels demonstrated more secure attachment characteristics and higher emotional stability. In addition, specific sociodemographic factors, including parental separation/divorce and firstborn status, were associated with higher symptom levels. In the hierarchical regression model including depressive and anxiety symptom severity, lower emotional stability, lower conscientiousness, higher BAI scores, and firstborn status remained significantly associated with adult separation anxiety symptom scores. These findings support a multidimensional framework in which separation anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood are associated with personality-related characteristics, internalizing symptom severity, and selected sociodemographic factors [5,6].

The relatively high proportion of participants with elevated symptom levels, particularly those with both childhood- and adult-onset symptom patterns, may reflect the developmental context of the study population. Emerging adulthood represents a period marked by increasing autonomy, separation from primary attachment figures, and restructuring of interpersonal relationships. In this context, separation-related distress may not necessarily indicate psychopathology but may reflect heightened sensitivity to developmental transitions [5-7]. However, the observed elevations in anxiety and depressive symptom scores, particularly in the dual symptom-positive group, suggest that, for some individuals, these symptoms may be part of a broader internalizing vulnerability profile. Recent evidence has further emphasized that separation anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood are associated with broader internalizing dimensions, including intolerance of uncertainty and negative affectivity [6].

It should also be noted that the present group classifications were based on self-report screening instruments and established cut-off scores rather than structured diagnostic interviews. Therefore, the relatively high proportion of participants in the dual symptom-positive group should not be interpreted as reflecting the prevalence of clinically diagnosed separation anxiety disorders. Screening-based classifications are intended to identify individuals with elevated symptom levels and may yield higher prevalence estimates in non-clinical populations. Cultural context may also be relevant to the interpretation of these findings. In collectivistic cultural settings, strong family connectedness and interdependence may make separation- related experiences particularly salient during the transition to university. Although cultural factors were not directly assessed in the present study, they should be considered when interpreting the elevated symptom levels observed in this sample.

The findings related to attachment styles are consistent with previous literature indicating that separation anxiety symptoms are more strongly associated with insecure attachment, particularly anxious–ambivalent patterns [5,6,24]. Individuals with anxious–ambivalent attachment are characterized by heightened fears of abandonment and hyperactivation of attachment-related processes, which may amplify emotional responses to separation and uncertainty. This pattern has been conceptualized as a transdiagnostic vulnerability factor underlying anxiety- related conditions in adulthood [25]. This may be particularly relevant during transitional periods such as entering university, where proximity to attachment figures is reduced. In contrast, secure attachment appears to function as a protective factor by facilitating more adaptive emotion regulation and a stable sense of interpersonal security. In the regression analyses, anxious–ambivalent attachment was significantly associated with adult separation anxiety symptom scores before depressive and anxiety symptom severity were added to the model; however, this association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the fully adjusted model. This pattern suggests that the association between anxious–ambivalent attachment and adult separation anxiety symptoms may partly overlap with broader internalizing symptom severity. In addition, parental separation or divorce has been associated with higher attachment-related anxiety and avoidance in adulthood, suggesting a potential relationship between early relational disruptions and attachment-related difficulties during developmental transitions [11,26].

Personality traits were also associated with this vulnerability profile. Lower emotional stability, reflecting higher neuroticism, was consistently associated with higher separation anxiety symptom levels. Individuals with lower emotional stability may exhibit greater emotional reactivity and sensitivity to stress, which may increase susceptibility to separation-related distress [6,10,27]. In the regression model, emotional stability showed the strongest independent association with adult separation anxiety symptoms, suggesting that personality-related characteristics may be more strongly associated with separation-related distress than attachment styles in this sample. Recent studies have also suggested that emotional stability may moderate the impact of attachment-related vulnerability on anxiety symptoms in emerging adults [10]. The observed pattern may reflect an interplay between personality-related characteristics and attachment processes in relation to separation-related emotional responses [10,27]. Additionally, lower conscientiousness was independently associated with higher symptom levels, which may reflect reduced self-regulatory capacity and increased susceptibility to emotional dysregulation. The positive association between extraversion and adult separation anxiety symptom scores observed in Model 3 should be interpreted cautiously, as it was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after depressive and anxiety symptom severity were included in the fully adjusted model. This suggests that the initial association may have reflected shared variance with broader internalizing symptom severity rather than a robust independent association. Therefore, this finding should be regarded as preliminary and interpreted cautiously until replicated in longitudinal or clinically assessed samples.

Importantly, the present findings also highlight the role of sociodemographic and early relational factors. Parental separation or divorce was associated with higher symptom levels, supporting the notion that early relational disruptions may have lasting effects on attachment-related processes and emotional security [26,28]. Similarly, the association with firstborn status may reflect differences in early caregiving experiences, parental expectations, or emotional responsibility. Notably, firstborn status remained significantly associated with adult separation anxiety symptoms in the final regression model, suggesting a possible association between early familial roles and separation-related emotional processes [29]. Although the mechanisms underlying these associations remain unclear, these findings suggest that easily identifiable background characteristics may serve as markers of increased vulnerability in non-clinical populations. Early caregiving experiences may further shape attachment-related processes in emerging adulthood [26].

Taken together, these findings support an integrative model in which separation anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood are associated with attachment-related characteristics, personality traits, and developmental context [6]. Notably, the regression model explained a substantial proportion of variance in separation anxiety (approximately 48.3% of the variance), highlighting the combined explanatory role of these factors. These findings may help characterize psychological profiles associated with elevated separation anxiety symptoms in nonclinical university samples. However, given the cross-sectional design, self-report assessment, and absence of clinical follow-up, they should not be interpreted as evidence for clinical screening or preventive intervention recommendations without further longitudinal and clinically structured validation.

This study has several limitations. The sample consisted of first-year university students from a single university, which may limit generalizability. The reliance on self-report measures, particularly retrospective assessment of childhood symptoms, may introduce recall and reporting bias. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and the use of cut-off-based classifications and the absence of structured clinical interviews limit diagnostic interpretation. Although regression analyses were conducted, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits conclusions regarding directional or causal relationships. Future longitudinal and multi-method studies are needed to clarify the developmental trajectory and clinical significance of separation anxiety symptoms in emerging adulthood.

Conclusions

Separation anxiety symptoms were common among first-year university students. In multivariable analyses, higher adult separation anxiety symptom scores were associated with higher BAI scores, lower emotional stability, lower conscientiousness, and firstborn status. The final model explained 48.3% of the variance in adult separation anxiety symptom scores, supporting a multidimensional perspective in which internalizing symptom severity, personality traits, and selected sociodemographic factors are associated with separation-related distress in non-clinical emerging adult populations.

These findings should be interpreted at the symptom level and within the context of a non-clinical, cross-sectional university sample. They may help characterize psychological profiles associated with elevated separation anxiety symptoms during developmental transitions; however, longitudinal, multicenter, and clinically structured studies are needed before implications for screening or preventive interventions can be drawn.
