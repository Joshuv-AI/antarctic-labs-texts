Vertebral metastatic disease: A paradigm shift
Authors: Annee Nguyen, Trupti Trivedi, Ellen O’Callaghan, Seeley Yoo, Tanner Zachem, Ramzy Ahmed, Rafael De La Garza Ramos, Raphaele Charest-Morin, Mark H Bilsky, Daniel Sciubba, Michelle Clarke, Claudio Tatsui, John H Shin, Ilya Laufer, Ori Barzilai, Ziya L Gokaslan, Arjun Sahgal, Michael Weber, Patricia Leigh Zadnik Sullivan, Nicolas Dea, Áron Lazáry, Trey Mullikin, C Rory Goodwin

ABSTRACT
AbstractVertebral metastatic disease results from many types of cancer and can have a devastating impact on patient mobility, psychological health, quality of life, and ultimately overall patient survival. However, the development of radiotherapy and surgical techniques has rapidly surged in conjunction with ongoing advances in basic science and translational studies. In this review, we discuss the paradigm shift in our understanding of the epidemiology and treatment algorithms for spinal oncology, ranging from preoperative optimization strategies, radiation and surgical techniques, the utilization of molecular markers and targeted therapeutics in medical oncology, and prognostication tools that underscore a new multidisciplinary approach to spinal oncology care.

Vertebral metastases are highly prevalent among cancer patients, occurring in about 30% to 40% of patients with disseminated disease.1,2 This number continues to rise as newer technologies and improved therapeutic options extend survival.3 Spinal metastases can have devastating effects on mobility, psychological health, and quality of life, in addition to poor overall survival (OS) rates.4–7 Additionally, spinal metastases may lead to highly disabling outcomes such as pathologic fractures and metastatic epidural spinal cord compression (MESCC), which often cause pain, gait instability, bladder and bowel dysfunction, and sensory disturbances.4

Currently, goals of therapy include pain management, preservation of neurological function, local tumor control, and spinal stabilization. The gold-standard treatment framework for spinal metastases is NOMS, developed by Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center.8 The NOMS framework uses factors like neurological function, oncological sensitivity to radiation, mechanical stability, and systemic burden of disease/ability to tolerate surgery to guide therapeutic decision-making. Depending on these factors, patients may receive a combination of treatments, including radiotherapy, surgical resection and stabilization, and/or systemic therapies (Figure 1).

Over the past few decades, there have been several paradigm shifts in the standard of care for treating vertebral metastases. The earliest spine oncology treatments were surgically oriented (ie, characterized by laminectomy and then subsequently laminectomy with radiotherapy). However early studies displayed no difference between combined laminectomy and radiotherapy and radiotherapy alone, which led to corticosteroids and conventional external beam radiation therapy (cEBRT) becoming the mainstay of treatment until the 1990s.9–11 Despite the shift toward a radiation-focused approach, new evidence in the early 2000s shifted the treatment paradigm back toward surgery for vertebral metastatic disease.12 Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) emerged as a durable treatment alternative due to its ability to overcome radio-resistance and enable a complete response to pain while minimizing radiation damage to nearby tissue and structures.13,14 Importantly, when combined with surgery, SBRT improved functional outcomes for patients with MESCC12,15,16 More specifically, separation surgery, first described in 2000, was demonstrated as an effective strategy to maximize the benefits of both surgery and concomitant SBRT10. The technique of separation surgery is a posterolateral transpedicular approach that stabilizes the spinal column, decompresses the spinal cord, and specifically allows for the tumor around the spinal cord to be removed or “separated”, creating optimal space for SBRT postoperatively.10,15 The increasing role of separation surgery in creating a secure target for SBRT and preserving or restoring neurological function has heavily influenced the treatment of MESCC in recent years.17

Other treatment advances in the last 20 years include minimally invasive surgical techniques,18–20 computer-assisted navigation,21 and laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT),22,23 which have further extended survival. Scoring systems, such as the Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (SINS),24 have aided patient selection for surgical treatments and more importantly, identified patients who would benefit most. Techniques and protocols for conventional radiotherapy25–28 and stereotactic radiosurgery29,30 continue to be assessed and improved, and utilization of personalized treatments31 has expanded the armamentarium of systemic options available to patients.

Our understanding of vertebral metastasis pathogenesis also continues to expand due to increasing basic science and translational research. In particular, recent studies have identified stem cell lines involved in tumor growth,32 genetic markers related to vertebral metastasis,33,34 and bone marrow genetic changes associated with metastatic progression35 characterized by Next Generation sequencing techniques.

The field of spinal oncology has made great strides in our understanding and treatment of metastatic spine disease. These changes have been accompanied by improved patient outcomes, while surgical trends have focused on less invasive procedures with the maintenance or restoration of functional control, stabilization of the spine, and durable local control.36 Here, we present the paradigm shifts within the treatment of metastatic spine disease, along with changes in management, radiation techniques, and evidence from basic science research.

Preoperative Planning

Patient Selection: Frailty Assessments and Algorithmic Survival Predictions

Operative management of vertebral metastasis can be associated with high complication rates, prompting increased investigation into factors that may predict postoperative morbidity and mortality.37–39 The most discussed survival predictor tools for spinal metastasis patients are the systems proposed by Tomita et al and the revised Tokuhashi score, which date back to the early 2000s.37 These tools consider factors such as the Karnofsky Performance Status score, Frankel score, as well as the impact and severity of the systemic burden of disease. Despite their validation in the metastatic spine population, both are limited because their development precedes the advent of newer therapeutic options, such as radiofrequency ablation, stereotactic radiosurgery, and improved systemic treatments. The Bauer score, modified later by Leithner et al, and the Katagiri system are other moderately predictive scales; both were initially designed for all bony metastasis and were not specific to spine.40 Similarly, these systems assess prognosis based on pathology, metastasis location, and performance status. Ahmed et al investigated and defined the most opportune tools to use based on results at various time points after surgery.37 The Oswestry Spinal Risk Index (OSRI) similarly attempted to maximize predictive value via assessment of the patient’s general condition and the histology of the primary tumor.41 Although frailty indices are widely utilized in the surgery literature overall, few indices demonstrate a robust correlation with survival after surgical interventions for spinal metastases.42 In the last decade, the metastatic spinal tumor frailty index (MSTFI) was created to combat the lack of applicability to this specific population.43 Created using a large inpatient hospitalization database, this tool incorporated the parameters of anemia, chronic lung disease, coagulopathy, electrolyte abnormalities, pulmonary circulation disorders, renal failure, malnutrition, emergent/urgent admission and anterior/combined surgical approach to predict the risk of major in-hospital complications and mortality.43 Most predictive scores, as mentioned, balance radiological evidence (ie, tumor location and extent of disease) with patient-specific functional status (ie, ability to complete activities of daily living or neurologic deficits), and the tumor histology similar to decision-making frameworks like NOMS.44

Recent studies have utilized machine learning and artificial intelligence to create nomograms or calculators that bypass subjective calculations by clinicians and often use laboratory data. For example, the revised Katagiri et al system incorporated serum albumin, platelets, calcium, total bilirubin, lactate dehydrogenase, or CRP into the prediction calculation. Several other systems were created including the New England Spinal Metastasis Score (NESMS), predictive algorithms created by the Skeletal Oncology Research Group (SORG), machine learning algorithms attributed to Karhade and colleagues, and the SpineMet scoring system developed by Choi and colleagues.45 In addition, machine learning algorithms have been utilized to optimize the MSTFI.46 Many of these scoring systems are available for use online, with persistent consideration of patient function, tumor pathology and systemic disease burden, and heterogeneous inclusion of laboratory data.40

With the increasing number of predictive models in spinal oncology, there have been several studies that have compared existing scores. Considering those before AI-based, machine learning algorithms (Tomita, modified Tokuhashi, modified Bauer, Rades, OSRI, Lei, NESMS, and SORG), it was found that only the SORG nomogram, OSRI, and modified Tokuhashi scores demonstrate moderate power for predicting long-term survival and only the OSRI demonstrates high predictive value for 1-year and 2-year survival as compared with the other tools.47 In other studies, the SORG machine learning algorithm outperformed the NESMS in predicting 90-day and 1-year survival after radiotherapy for patients with spinal metastasis using more modern cohorts.48 However, studies note the limitations of any one scoring system to be optimal at all timepoints and for all pathology types. Surgeons must exercise caution in anchoring to a predictive tool that may have variable power depending on the specific context.49 After the calculation of an algorithm’s score, there are variable recommendations per modality in deciding the next best step in treatment or the indication for surgery. Higher scores are often assigned to good prognostic factors, and recommendations for surgical intervention in the absence of other contradictory health data. Surgical patients are most likely to have intractable pain that is resistant to nonoperative measures, spinal instability, progressive deformity, and/or a neurological deficit.50 In contrast, moderate to lower scores may suggest palliative therapy, classified by limited decompression and stabilization.51 Despite the indications for treatment based on specific algorithm’s score, surgical intervention, especially for vertebral metastasis, remains a shared decision between patient and provider, encouraging the discussion of the burden of both disease and surgery.

Health-related quality of life can be utilized as a measurable postoperative outcome, which providers should aim to optimize. Although understudied in the metastatic spine population, social support for cancer patients is shown to improve quality of life and suggests likely improvement of outcomes for postoperative patients with spinal metastases.54 Similarly, current literature has understudied the rate of palliative care utilization in this population, despite the preponderance of evidence in other advanced cancer literature that palliative care consultation improves quality of life.55–58 Patients with metastatic spinal disease would benefit from increased palliative care utilization, as well as more nuanced counseling as to expected palliative care benefits, which is also understudied and poorly conceptualized.55,56,59

Operative Management

Implants and New Technologies

Titanium implants

Titanium implants are frequently used for spinal stabilization in surgery, as they have been shown to provide high fusion rates and good mechanical stability.60,61 However, their use can make imaging difficult by creating artifacts on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) because of their relatively high scattering density.62 This can make delineating tumor and neural structures more difficult postoperatively, decreasing visualization on surveillance imaging and therapeutic effectiveness with radiation planning.63 To combat these limitations, carbon fiber polyetheretherketone (CF/PEEK) implants and fenestrated screws that can be augmented with bone cement have emerged as 2 new technologies for spine metastasis treatment that can potentially reduce the limitations associated with titanium implants.

CF/PEEK implants

Carbon fiber polyetheretherketone results in reduced image artifact on MRI and CT scan, reduced radiation dose perturbation compared with titanium implants, and better approximates the elastic modulus of cortical bone.50 While no differences have been detected between CF/PEEK and titanium implants in terms of local tumor control directly, the radiolucency of CF/PEEK implants facilitates more precise radiation planning and may allow for earlier detection of local tumor recurrence.64,65 Additionally, Katsifis et al showed that CF/PEEK provides better osseointegration which could improve implant success for patients undergoing radiotherapy.62 Some studies postulate that the better elastic modulus approximation with human cortical bone may result in reduced biomechanical stress on adjacent structures while still demonstrating efficacy and durability without routine need for cement augmentation.66,67 Thus, CF/PEEK instrumentation appears to provide similar durability to titanium instrumentation with potential added benefit for patients receiving radiation (Figure 2).68

Although there are numerous benefits of CF/PEEK implants, potential disadvantages include difficulty with rod positioning intraoperatively (when tulip heads are not aligned adequately), inability to contour rods, and higher costs.69 A systematic review conducted by Khan et al concluded that the increased benefit of CF/PEEK implants to oncological outcomes is unclear.70 Overall, current data do not support any significant difference in complication rates between CF/PEEK implants and titanium implants.71,72 Further studies are needed to elucidate the impact of CF/PEEK implants on local control, survival, and other oncological outcomes.

Fenestrated screws

The use of fenestrated screws with cement augmentation has improved pedicle screw fixation and reduced surgical revision rates particularly in patients with poor bone quality.50 Given the majority of studies focus on osteoporotic patients, translation of the perceived benefits to vertebral metastatic disease patients is only postulated. In a retrospective observational study conducted by Kim et al, fenestrated screw augmentation was shown to be more effective than vertebroplasty augmentation as evidenced by decreased incidence of screw loosening in osteoporotic patients.73,74 Similarly, in a systematic review comparing the clinical effectiveness of fenestrated screws and conventional pedicle screws, there was significantly reduced screw loosening, reduced pain, and improved quality of life in osteoporotic patients with fenestrated screws. However, there were no significant differences in revision rates or all-cause reoperation rates when compared with the observed general population.74 Risk of cement leakage during insertion may be an area of concern for complication with fenestrated screw use.75–77 Most studies conclude that larger and more robust studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness and safety of fenestrated screw usage.75

Separation surgery

The adoption of separation surgery, creation of a safe space between the tumor and spinal cord, as a key adjunct for radiation planning has played a major role in the shift toward minimally invasive surgery.15 Effective decompression is critical with separation surgery, as residual high-grade epidural disease can compromise rates of local control rates.78 The effectiveness and safety of separation surgery are well established, with 1 retrospective study showing that only 2.8% of patients undergoing separation surgery had symptomatic hardware failure.79 Separation surgery along with radiotherapy provides an effective means for spinal decompression and stabilization, replacing the usage of more complex approaches and gross total resection of vertebral spine metastasis.15,80

Minimally invasive surgery

Minimally invasive surgical techniques have evolved since the late 1990s and have been shown to be as effective as open techniques for a wide variety of spinal conditions. Minimally invasive surgeries are shown to reduce hospital stay, reduce damage to surrounding tissue, decrease opioid dependence, and reduce intraoperative blood loss compared with conventional open surgical techniques.81–83 Emphasis on optimizing early postsurgical clinical outcomes has played a major role in the increased prevalence of minimally invasive surgery in metastatic spine disease management.83

Less invasive surgical stabilization methods for patients with vertebral metastatic disease include vertebral body cement augmentation through vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty. Kyphoplasty and vertebroplasty are performed through similar techniques, with the exception that kyphoplasty utilizes a balloon to improve/restore vertebral body height. No significant differences between the 2 techniques have been observed regarding pain relief, vertebral height restoration, and/or complication rate.84,85 Patients with significant posterior vertebral body wall erosion or retro-pulsed tumor/bone have a relative contraindication to these procedures, and thus the majority of patients who have improved with these techniques have tumor isolated to the anterior column of the spine.83 Additionally, kyphoplasty and vertebroplasty are usually not effective strategies for patients with unstable pathological features extending into the pedicle and facet joints.86 Incorrect level placement and cement leakage (similar to cement augmentation with fenestrated screws) are the most common complications and are often dependent on surgical experience and techniques.84 The consideration of these factors should be evaluated on an individual basis before surgery.

For patients with spinal instability or pathological fracture without spinal cord compression, percutaneous pedicle screw fixation with percutaneous vertebroplasty has shown benefit for metastatic spine disease.87 When decompression is warranted, mini-open procedures with expandable tubular retractors or alternatively, if the tumor is isolated to 1 nerve root, a single port transforaminal endoscopic approach can be utilized.88,89 The safety and efficacy profile of these procedures may provide greater benefit in patients with extensive comorbidities or systemic burden of disease. As minimally invasive approaches continue to expand in this space, technological advances such as computer-assisted navigation, robot-assisted procedures, and laser interstitial therapy are associated with reduced surgical site infections, hospital stay, and blood loss without compromising treatment.90

Radiation Techniques

Conventional EBRT is the dominant treatment for patients with spinal metastases globally due to its ease of delivery, benefits for the typical patient presenting with multiple metastases at the end stage of life, and relatively low risk of adverse events due to lower doses. However, complete response rates to pain are limited to ∼10% to 15% and the retreatment rate can approach 20% within 3 to 6 months of initial radiation.91-93 Initially, SBRT was applied to spinal metastases to improve outcomes, but with trepidation due to possible adverse effects on the spinal cord. As SBRT’s utilization has evolved, evidence-based research into spinal cord constraints has greatly resolved most fears.94 The SC24 randomized trial comparing 24 Gy in 2 SBRT fractions with 20 Gy in 5 conventional fractions showed greater complete response rates to pain, similar rates of VCF, and less re-irradiation and local failure.95,96 As a result, the 24 Gy in 2 fractions SBRT regimen has been adopted in international guidelines as a standard of care.97,98 However, the risk of VCF is shown to increase over time and the severity of the VCF can be greater following SBRT as opposed to conventional EBRT. Maximizing SBRT efficacy, specifically for radioresistant histologies and paraspinal and epidural disease, remains an area of active research, along with the determination of which patients will benefit from early cement augmentation or percutaneous instrumentation to mitigate the risk of VCF. Medical Oncology

As preoperative planning, operative management, and radiation therapy techniques for patients with metastatic spine disease have improved, medical oncology advancements have also occurred in parallel. In this section, we review current advances in systemic therapies and classic molecular markers that influence/synergize with current treatment paradigms in spinal oncology.

Molecular Markers and Current Systemic Therapy Advances

Molecular characterization of metastatic tumors is essential to (1) identifying patient cohorts most likely to be responsive to particular treatments, (2) providing nuanced prognostic information, and (3) developing targeted therapies to achieve optimal outcomes. Lung, breast, prostate, and melanoma are the most common cancer types known to metastasize to the spine.99 In this section, we review common molecular markers and known systemic treatments for patients diagnosed with metastatic spine disease (Table 2).

Lung Cancer

Nearly 70% of lung cancer patients present with locally advanced or metastatic disease at diagnosis, including bone and spine metastases.100 While both small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) can result in metastatic spine disease, NSCLC, which includes adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma, presents more commonly in metastatic spine disease cases and its treatment paradigm is heavily dictated by PDL-1 status or diagnostic molecular markers, such as HER2/HER4, EML4-ALK, CTLA-4, and, of particular note to radiation therapy and surgical intervention, EGFR.

EGFR (epidermal growth factor-like receptor) mutations present in patients with adenocarcinoma, primarily in female and nonsmoking patients, and are known to promote tumor cell proliferation. Systemic therapies targeting EGFR that are currently available and have shown efficacy in improving median survival include amivantamab, erlotinib, gefitinib, osimertinib, afatinib, and lazertinib.99,101,102 Patients with EGFR mutations and concurrent spinal metastases have been shown to have better survival (up to 18 months) compared with their EGFR-wild type counterparts and benefit from initiating systemic therapies postradiation therapy and surgical intervention.103,104 While the other noted mutations do have associated targeted therapies, there is still a distinct need for further investigation into their efficacy in combination with radiation therapy.

Breast Cancer

Breast cancer is the most common cancer type to metastasize to bone with a metastatic incidence rate of approximately 80%, with osseous spinal metastases accounting for approximately two-thirds of bone metastases.105,106 Key prognostication markers aside from patient age and histology grade include the following receptors: estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2). Subtypes of breast cancer based on hormone receptor status include luminal A (ER+/PR+) (70%-75% of cases), luminal B (ER+/PR+/HER2+) (10%-20%), HER2 enriched (HER2+) (15%-25% of cases), and triple-negative (ER−/PR−/HER2−) (approximately 20% of cases).107 Patients who undergo surgical intervention for spinal metastases from breast cancer with the best to worst prognosis postsurgical intervention are luminal B, luminal A, triple-negative, and, finally, HER2-enriched, according to a study of patients with spinal metastases observing 12-month postoperative survival rates.108

Treatment paradigms vary depending on breast cancer subtypes. In patients with ER+ metastatic breast cancers, current treatments involve endocrine therapy (selective estrogen receptor degraders such as fulvestrant or selective estrogen receptor modulators such as tamoxifen) or combination treatment of endocrine therapy with cell cycle or proliferation pathway inhibitors (such as CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib or mTOR inhibitor everolimus, respectively).109 In patients with HER2-enriched metastatic breast cancers, treatment includes targeted therapy with trastuzumab in combination with chemotherapy.110,111 Triple-negative breast cancer patients, due to their lack of hormone receptors, are generally treated with immunotherapies. At this time, however, there is less enthusiasm for aggressive local therapy in oligometastatic breast cancer due to NRG BR002, a randomized phase IIR/III trial showing no PFS or OS improvement in standard-of-care therapy with the addition of SBRT in patients with less than or equal to 4 extracranial lesions.112 Another prospective study of 90 patients with 155 unique breast cancer spinal metastases, primarily consisting of ER+ breast cancer spinal metastases, found that radiosurgery resulted in a local tumor control rate of 92%.113 Improvement in OS was significantly associated with age and preoperative neurological function scores (Karnofsky Performance Score, or KPS) rather than hormone receptor status. This positions SBRT as a potentially more efficacious therapeutic recommendation for breast cancer spinal metastasis use regardless of hormone receptor status, especially considering that long-term local control rates with SBRT appear to be independent of molecular status.114 To supplement these findings, further studies should be performed to identify prognostication markers that indicate increased radiosensitivity. For example, translational work has noted that ER+/PR+ tumors treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors in combination with endocrine therapy are radiosensitive by priming DNA damage pathways.115 Further studies are necessary to better identify key treatment time points and therapeutics that may be optimally paired with radiation therapy.

Prostate Cancer

Prostate cancer metastases most commonly occur in the spine and can result in painful bony lesions and/or spinal cord compression. Classical treatment paradigms for hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (HSPC) include androgen deprivation therapies (ADT) such as luteinizing hormone–releasing hormone antagonists and anti-androgens. Patients with metastatic disease who are newly diagnosed and may not require urgent surgical intervention can undergo more novel androgen axis-receptor targeted (ARAT) therapies that induce chemical castration and are good candidates for EBRT or SBRT. The SBRT has been increasingly studied for its efficacy in treating oligometastatic prostate cancer. The ORIOLE Trial demonstrated that SBRT can significantly improve PFS compared with observation, suggesting a potential for delaying systemic therapy.116 Similarly, the STOMP Trial indicated that metastasis-directed therapy (MDT), such as SBRT, can prolong ADT-free survival in patients with limited metastatic disease.117 Both trials highlight SBRT as a promising approach for managing oligometastatic prostate cancer by targeting specific metastatic sites. A combined genomic analysis of both trials found that patients with high-risk mutations, ATM, BRCA 1/2, RB1, or TP53, benefited more from MDT than observation. In patients with castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) and symptomatic spinal cord compression, surgical intervention for decompression is the preferred management strategy. The SBRT may have a more specific role in CRPC as compared with conventional EBRT as a result of the relative radioresistance of this histology.118

Common molecular markers for prostate cancer metastases include serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA), EGFR, c-MET, VEGFR2, HER2, CTLA-4, and PSMA (prostate-specific membrane antigen).99 While prostate cancer therapeutics for CRPC are still limited, targeted therapies for the above molecular markers include cabozantinib (c-MET and VEGFR2 inhibitor), gefitinib, erlotinib, and cetuximab (EGFR-inhibitors), lapatinib (EGFR and HER2 inhibitor), olaparib (PARP inhibitor), and ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4). Prostate-specific membrane antigen is a particular molecular target of interest in CRPC that has been investigated in the context of radiation therapy. In an international, open-label, phase III trial with 831 patients, a radioligand seeking PSMA-expressing cells that delivers beta-particle radiation, Lutetium-177 (177Lu)-PSMA-617, was found to extend imaging-based progression-free survival (median, 8.7 vs 3.4 months, P < .001) and OS (median, 15.3 vs 11.3 months, P < .001), although with some adverse events of grade 3 or higher.119 Further studies to investigate the compatibility of current treatment paradigms with radiation therapy in prostate cancer spinal metastases should continue to supplement standard of care.

Melanoma

Patients with metastatic melanoma to the spine have extremely poor prognoses, with an estimated median survival of up to 4 months.120 Treatment paradigms for metastatic melanoma exploit various secondary signaling messengers in the MAPK-PI3K-AKT pathway that are commonly dysregulated in melanoma, and thus molecular markers with associated targeted therapies include BRAF (a proto-oncogene), KIT, and NRAS. BRAF mutations (commonly identified as a V600E mutation) occur in two-thirds of melanoma cases and can be targeted by vemurafenib, imatinib, dabrafenib, and encorafenib, and, less commonly due to its lower efficacy, chemotherapy. Melanoma patients with BRAF mutations can generally show initial resistance (15% of cases) or develop resistance (over 50% of cases).121,122 A small subset of melanoma patients who have KIT mutations may respond to imatinib with sporadic efficacy.123 NRAF mutations are present in 15% to 20% of melanoma cases with aggressive tumorigenic phenotypes compared with NRAF-wild type melanoma and thus have fewer effective therapeutic options. In general, for patients with metastatic melanoma without molecular markers to target, standard of care includes immunotherapy regimens.99 Of all cancer types that metastasize to the spine discussed in this review, melanoma is considered the most highly radioresistant and thus may respond best to stereotactic body radiation rather than other radiotherapy techniques.107

Basic Science and Translational Advances

Translational advances, such as sequencing and multi-omic studies, in recent years have contributed to identifying novel molecular markers or cell subsets that may be potentially targeted therapeutically or may be involved in increased fracture and secondary complications post-systemic or radiation treatment. However, given the notoriously difficult-to-process nature of bone and spine due to the heterogeneity in tissue type, very few sequencing studies in bone, let alone spine tumors, currently exist.101,124 Only recently has a successful protocol for spine tumor–specific sequencing been published.34 In this section, we discuss how advancements in sequencing and adjacent novel biotechnologies contribute to the discovery of spine-specific cell subsets (such as vertebral stem cells) and to the identification of novel, spine tumor–specific molecular markers or genomic signatures that may be considered during clinical decision-making regarding radiation therapy or surgical intervention.

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing is a new generation sequencing technology that analyzes the transcriptomes of individual cells. Although this technique has become an integral part of modern research, sample preparation of spinal column tumors can be challenging due to bony architecture and tumor heterogeneity. Spine tumor heterogeneity is primarily due to the effects of standard of care on the specific tissues (for example, the generation of scar tissue post radiation therapy), the different cancer types that present in spinal metastases cases that can alter the landscape and tissue presentation, the location of the spine tumor tissue itself, and the presence of multiple tissue types such as tumor, muscle, nerve, and bone. Moreover, very few protocols attempting single-cell suspensions from bone rarely yield adequate quality or quantity to perform single-cell exploration studies with -omic platforms. A study published by Nguyen et al has optimized the protocol for preparing single-cell suspension from clinical specimen from the patient with prostate cancer spinal metastases.34 This protocol includes commonly used techniques performed in immune system profiling experiments to generate a single-cell suspension and various alternatives to performing tissue homogenization of spine tumors, making it accessible to researchers and groups with basic tools at their disposal while enriching the yield of their single-cell suspension. Having a validated protocol can be directly used to evaluate the immune microenvironment and tumor heterogeneity among various spine metastases with varying primary cancer types, or identify prognostic markers, therapeutic targets, and cell subsets that may drive metastatic spinal disease.125

Identification of Cell Subsets Contributing to Metastatic Spine Disease and Progression

In 2023, groundbreaking stem-cell research by Sun et al introduced a major paradigm shift that could have therapeutic implications in metastatic spine disease. By using cell surface markers, lineage tracing, and transcriptome analysis in preclinical mouse models and human tissues, this group reported that vertebral stem cells (vSSC) are distinct from long bone stem cells (lbSSC).32 Furthermore, they reported that vSSC-lineage cells are more effective compared with lbSSC in recruiting metastases by secreting MFGE8 factor. In line with these discoveries, Bok et al demonstrated that distinct stem cell populations reflect different functions in mature skeletal cells such as osteoblasts at different locations.126

Human skeletal stem cells (SSCs), a bone-specific cell type, were identified in the bone marrow stroma in 2018. Due to their self-renewal and differentiation potential, SSCs had been used in combination with various biomaterials for tissue regeneration and fracture healing in orthopedic procedures.127,128 In 2022, Zhu et al discussed stem cell populations specific to the bone marrow, growth plate, perichondrium, and periosteum. Authors of this publication reported that transplantation of region-specific stem cells can have translational utility in activation of endogenous SSC activity for the prevention and treatment of skeletal disorders.129

Identification of a vertebral-specific stem cell population provides a potential rationale for why and how the bone, specifically the spine compared with long bones, may be the preferential site for major solid tumor metastases. This may also explain the associated predisposition for cancer treatment–induced bone loss and greater incidence of fracture in the vertebral body.130,131 According to recent findings, vertebral stem cells release MFGE8 factor to modulate mature bone cell populations and potentially provide a supportive microenvironment for tumor growth in the vertebral bodies over long bones,32 which could explain the spine-specific morbidity associated with metastatic disease in comparison with other bone sites.130 Due to distinct SSCs in spine, clinical management of bone health in metastatic spine disease could be different than other bone sites.

In the future, prospective bone metastases studies should focus on the spine and the long bones separately, whereas retrospective studies should stratify comparator groups into vertebral compression fractures and nonvertebral fractures. Reevaluation of the effectiveness of bone-targeted therapies and SBRT based on vertebral and nonvertebral sites may provide information on targeting specific patients’ population as well as the mechanisms underlying cancer treatment–induced bone loss. This stratification may also identify specific molecular markers that could potentially aid the development of individualized therapeutic approaches for metastatic spine disease and/or treatment of vertebral fractures due to cancer treatment–induced bone loss.

Identification of Cancer Intrinsic Factors Contributing to Metastatic Spine Disease

Profiling tumor genomics for personalized cancer therapy has become routine in clinical practice. Reliability testing of genomic data is not frequently performed, as this testing would involve multiple biopsies in patients with spine metastases. A clinical study published by Barzilai et al examined the testing of spine tumor samples using next-generation sequencing assay. Primary and metastatic (visceral and spine) samples were matched to corresponding patients and concordance rate for genomic alterations was calculated from paired samples obtained from the same patient and aligned with tumor histology. Results of the study demonstrated a high concordance rate for the genetic alterations and particularly for driver mutations between primary tumor and metastatic spine tumor. Authors of the study had further concluded that spine tumor specimens are reliable for genomic-based decision-making in prostate, breast, and NSCLC tumors, thus minimizing the need for multiple biopsies and multisite genomic analysis.132

Identification of Cancer-Cell Extrinsic Factors Contributing to Metastatic Spine Disease

Single-cell sequencing and microarray analyses can also evaluate cancer extrinsic factors that may contribute to metastatic spinal disease and complicate treatment paradigms involving radiation therapy. Utilization of DNA microarrays has been a breakthrough in the field of experimental molecular biology, and has significantly contributed to advancing our understanding of the genetic make-up of deadly cancers. As previously noted, breast cancer has a higher propensity to metastasize to bone and develop metastases at this site. Spinal metastases are more common and can severely impact survival; thus, understanding the genomic profile of breast cancer spinal metastases is critical in developing therapeutic strategies. By using a GSE22358 dataset, He et al screened key deregulatory genes from tumors collected from patients with metastatic spinal disease and correlated those genes with OS. Results of this analysis revealed that key regulators were involved in cell proliferation, metabolic processes, and Toll-like receptor pathway that were directly correlated with OS in the patients.133 In a similar study, gene expression profiles of tumor received from ER + BC spine metastases, using the GSE14661 dataset identified Lysosomal protein transmembrane 5 (LAPTM5) expression was inversely correlated to progression of spine metastasis of ER + BC. Furthermore, downregulation of LAPTMS5 promoted proliferation, migration, and chemo-resistance of ER + BC cells in vitro, and demonstrated LAPTM5 as a tumor suppressor for ER + BC spine metastases, which could serve as a prospective therapeutic target.134

In the context of prostate cancer, a weighted gene co-expression analysis was performed to understand the cause of reduced survival in patients with prostate cancer bone metastases and to identify long noncoding RNAs (IncRNAs) from prostate cancer bone metastases specimens. GSE32269 and GSE26964 microarray datasets and omnibus database analysis demonstrated that the M2 macrophage infiltration regulators HCG18 and MCM3AP-AS1 could be potential targets for prostate cancer bone metastases and may be better prognosticators for the disease.135

Single-cell sequencing of vertebral metastases and their respective primary tumor of origin can provide vast insight into cell subsets and cancer-cell intrinsic/extrinsic factors that may aid in identifying prognostic and diagnostic markers of disease progression or development of more effective treatment paradigms. However, its implementation can be time-intensive and costly and thus more immediate-use technologies that can perform similar functions, such as spectroscopy, may be useful and complementary to single-cell-omic studies.

Spectral signatures

Many biotechnologies have been developed to provide clinicians with novel methods for prognostication, before and during surgical interventions. As current methods of intraoperative frozen section pathology can be time-consuming and ill-equipped to predict genetic markers, multiple groups have attempted to utilize the metabolic signatures to provide additional data through the means of spectroscopy. The 2 main forms investigated are endogenous Fluorescence and Raman spectroscopy, with the former leveraging differences in preexisting fluorophores in cellular respiration, and the latter targeting vibrational signatures.136,137 Sperber et al demonstrated a dye-free fluorescence spectroscopy system to differentiate ex vivo metastatic spine tumors from normal muscle and normal bone in a nondestructive manner amenable to being used prior to resection.136 Zachem et al demonstrated the systems classification ability on CNS tumors.138 Significant studies have been conducted on simulated Raman histology, whereby Raman spectroscopy is used to generate a predicted H&E image in a chemical-free fashion, although it is unlikely that it could be done prior to resection.139 Both methods are exciting new developments that could allow surgeons to understand in real time the likely pathology and prognosis of the disease, faster than traditional pathology can provide, which can better inform surgical decision-making. Additionally, the ability to biopsy tissue and potentiate a prediction of key genetic and molecular markers could inform radiation oncologists to make faster treatment decisions in a metastatic setting. These new and exciting advances could provide additional information for data-driven surgery while being easily integrated into current treatment paradigms.

Conclusion

Our understanding of the treatment paradigms for and epidemiology of spinal oncology has significantly advanced with more holistic approaches to patient care. Additionally, developments in radiation, surgery, basic and translational research as well as emerging biotechnologies have rapidly surged to identify novel diagnostic and treatment paradigms, molecular markers, and targeted therapies for more effective prognostication and clinical management of vertebral metastases. Ultimately, further optimization of preoperative planning, operative management, radiation techniques, and medical oncology through basic science and preclinical explorations can continue developing this new multidisciplinary approach to spinal oncology care.
