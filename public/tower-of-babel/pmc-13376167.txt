Cell death modalities modulate inflammatory and regenerative programs in zebrafish sensory organs
Authors: Daniela Münch, Shiyuan Chen, Elizabeth Ellis, Nicolas Denans, Mark E. Lush, Kaitlyn Petentler, Jose E. Javier, KyeongMin Bae, Tatjana Piotrowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Regeneration is triggered by cells dying due to various types of injuries. However, it remains unclear whether different types of cell death elicit distinct regeneration responses. Here, we systematically profile dynamic transcriptional responses of macrophages and lateral line cells to different cell death modalities during zebrafish sensory hair cell regeneration. We show that chemogenetically induced programmed hair cell death triggers a diminished inflammatory response compared to pharmacologically induced cell lysis, characterized by minimal neutrophil recruitment, distinct transcriptional profiles in phagocytosing macrophages and reduced expression of injury-responsive genes in lateral line cells. Nevertheless, regeneration ultimately converges on a shared set of regeneration-specific genes. Importantly, preventing immune cell recruitment enhances injury-induced support cell proliferation in response to programmed, but not unprogrammed cell death, highlighting cell death-dependent regenerative outcomes following immune cell inhibition. Our findings demonstrate that different forms of cell death trigger distinct molecular events, with implications for tailoring regenerative therapies to specific injury contexts.

Introduction

Tissue regeneration replaces cells lost to mechanical injury, chemical ablation or due to natural cellular turnover. A key variable distinguishing these different injury types is the nature of the cell death modality1. Understanding the molecular events underlying regeneration following distinct types of cell death is therefore essential for developing tailored regenerative strategies to specific injury contexts. Numerous injury paradigms have been used to study regeneration of specific cell types or entire organs and tissues, such as chemical and genetic cell ablations, stab wounds, cryo-injuries and tissue transection2. While some comparative studies between distinct injury paradigms have been conducted3–7, detailed side-by-side comparisons of the response to specific cell death types are lacking.

Cell death has profound effects on the surrounding tissue. For example, apoptotic cells induce compensatory proliferation of the surrounding cells through the release of mitogenic factors, as described in Drosophila wing disks8–11, zebrafish skin12 or lesioned Hydra13. In addition, dying cells affect their surroundings through the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), intracellular molecules that are exposed upon cell damage14–16. Distinct cell death modalities, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis and pyroptosis, differ in the extent and nature of DAMP release, thereby exerting distinct effects on the surrounding tissue environment and cells involved in their clearance, such as immune cells17.

Understanding how distinct cell death types impact immune cell function is particularly relevant in organ regeneration, where macrophages play a central role in determining regenerative outcomes18–24. A transient inflammatory response is a hallmark of successful regeneration in the zebrafish spinal cord, brain, retina or fin25–28. After fin amputation, macrophages often adopt a pro-inflammatory phenotype that facilitates debris clearance, promotes the recruitment of additional immune cells and triggers regenerative programs28,29. While this initial inflammatory phase is crucial for blastema formation, its timely resolution is equally important, as persistent inflammation is associated with impaired regeneration in the zebrafish fin and spinal cord25,28. Contrary to necrosis induced by mechanical injury, apoptotic cell clearance is typically described as the silent removal of dying cells without inducing inflammation30. For example, engulfment of apoptotic cells by macrophages induces the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators, such as TGF-β31,32. How macrophages respond in regenerative contexts defined by more extensive apoptotic cell death is less understood.

The regenerating zebrafish lateral line provides a unique system for the visualization and investigation of context-specific cellular behaviors, such as immune cell recruitment, dying cell phagocytosis and support cell proliferation. It consists of sensory organs called neuromasts (Fig. 1a, b), which contain mechanosensory hair cells that are structurally and functionally analogous to those in the mammalian inner ear33,34. Unlike in mammals, zebrafish hair cells rapidly regenerate following damage through the proliferation and differentiation of the underlying support cells35–37. Importantly, distinct types of hair cell death have been observed in both the mammalian inner ear and zebrafish lateral line following exposure to different aminoglycosides and other ototoxic compounds38–41, highlighting the need to study regeneration in different cell death contexts. While cell death dynamics are challenging to study in closed systems like the inner ear, the superficial location of the lateral line in the zebrafish skin makes it ideally suited for high-resolution live imaging techniques42. Lastly, lateral line hair cell death also triggers the recruitment of nearby macrophages to the injury site, allowing for the analysis of the interplay between dying cells and the innate immune system43–45. Previous studies showed that while macrophages may influence synapse formation during hair cell regeneration through IL-4 and IL-10 signaling43, they are not required for the reappearance of new hair cells following neomycin-mediated ablation44. Conversely, macrophages contribute to timely hair cell regeneration following copper sulfate (CuSO4)-induced hair cell death45. Here, we systematically compare the cellular and molecular responses to two distinct hair cell death modalities in the regenerating zebrafish lateral line. We employ pharmacologically- or chemogenetically induced paradigms of hair cell ablation using neomycin46 or the nitroreductase (NTR) / prodrug system47–49 to induce two distinct types of cell death. We show that the mode of hair cell death influences the number and type of recruited immune cells, as well as the transcriptional responses of macrophages and lateral line support cells. Specifically, metronidazole (MTZ)-induced programmed hair cell death elicits a prolonged macrophage response with increased efferocytosis, while only neomycin-induced cell death induces rapid neutrophil recruitment. These cellular differences are accompanied by differential expression of inflammatory mediators in macrophages, as evidenced by single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq). scRNA-seq of lateral line support cells revealed distinct, but also common gene expression changes during the injury response to programmed compared to unprogrammed cell death. Importantly, inhibiting immune cell migration to the injury site specifically increased regenerative proliferation after MTZ-induced, but not neomycin-induced, cell death. These findings highlight that the inflammatory and injury response programs in the lateral line are dependent on the nature of cell death and provide insights into the context-specificity of tissue repair.

Results

Triggering distinct modes of sensory hair cell death through chemogenetic or pharmacological approaches

To investigate the influence of the cell death modality on regeneration programs, we established a comparative approach using two distinct methods to ablate sensory hair cells. First, we used a chemogenetic ablation system based on a variant of the bacterial nitroreductase NfsB (NTR2.0, hereafter NTR), which converts non-toxic prodrugs, such as Metronidazole (MTZ), into genotoxic compounds49. To ablate sensory hair cells, we generated a transgenic line expressing NTR and mCherry under the control of a hair cell-specific promoter (Tg(myo6b:mCherry-P2A-NTR2.0)psi102Tg, hereafter myo6b:NTR). This transgenic line marks sensory hair cells in the lateral line and ear, as indicated by the overlap with the hair cell-specific Et(krt4:EGFP)sqet4 (hereafter sqET4:GFP) line (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 1a). To quantify the dynamics of MTZ-induced hair cell death, we incubated 5 days post fertilization (dpf) larvae with 5 mM MTZ for 2 h and counted the number of live hair cells using Tg(myo6b:hist2h2l-mScarletI) psi66 (hereafter myo6b:H2B-mScarlet) expression. Live hair cells were distinguished from dying hair cells based on their uncondensed, non-fragmented nuclear morphology (Supplementary Fig. 1b). MTZ incubation resulted in robust hair cell death over a 2h period (Fig. 1e, f, i). Importantly, no significant hair cell death was observed following incubation of myo6b:NTR + larvae with 0.5 % DMSO or myo6b:H2B-mScarlet + larvae (i.e., myo6b:NTR-negative controls) with 5 mM MTZ for the same duration (Supplementary Fig. 1d–f). To induce cell death using a second method, we used the aminoglycoside antibiotic neomycin (neo)46. Consistent with previous studies50, incubation with 300 μM neomycin for 30 min induces rapid hair cell death during treatment, as indicated by the decrease in the number of live myo6b + hair cells (Fig. 1g–i). As functional mechanotransduction channels are required for neomycin uptake51, and mature hair cells are more susceptible to neomycin-induced cell death52,53, on average, two immature hair cells remain after neomycin treatment (Fig. 1g). MTZ-induced cell death on the other hand leads to the death of most hair cells, with an average of 0.5 hair cells remaining (Fig. 1e). This shows that while both paradigms induce robust cell death, a slightly lower proportion of hair cells survives MTZ treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

As the two paradigms differ in their cell loss kinetics, with maximum hair cell loss occurring 4 h after the beginning of MTZ and 30 min after neomycin treatment, we designated time points corresponding to the completion of cell death ( + 0 h, Fig. 1i) to facilitate the comparison between the two paradigms. Time points during the cell death period are labeled with negative values (e.g., MTZ-1h, neo-15 min), whereas time points following maximum cell loss are designated with positive values (e.g., MTZ + 1 h, neo + 1 h). In conclusion, we established that both the MTZ/NTR system and neomycin induce robust cell death in lateral line hair cells, albeit with different cell death kinetics.

We reasoned that different cell ablation methods may induce distinct types of cell death. To test this hypothesis, we first investigated cell morphology. Confocal time-lapse imaging of a hair cell membrane marker (Tg(pou4f3:gap43-GFP)s356t) revealed signs of membrane blebbing and the shedding of membrane-enclosed cell fragments, resembling apoptotic bodies, following MTZ-induced cell death (Fig. 2a, blue arrowheads, Supplementary Movie 1). Consistent with programmed forms of cell death, such as apoptosis54, myo6b:H2B-Scarlet+ hair cell nuclei underwent condensation and fragmentation (Fig. 2a, orange and red arrowheads, respectively). Neomycin treatment on the other hand resulted in rapid hair cell lysis, as indicated by the loss of pou4f3:gap43-GFP membrane signal (Fig. 2b, blue arrowheads and asterisks, Supplementary Movie 2). Hair cell nuclei quickly adopted a condensed morphology (Fig. 2b, orange dots). To investigate if the two cell death modalities differed molecularly, we tested whether cells displayed Caspase-3 activation, a hallmark of apoptosis55, using immunostaining. MTZ-induced cell death resulted in robust Caspase-3 activation in hair cells starting at MTZ-2h (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1g). Conversely, few Caspase-3 + hair cells were observed during neomycin-induced cell death (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 1h), consistent with previous studies, which showed that neomycin induces caspase-independent cell death40. Taken together, our data demonstrate that MTZ and neomycin induce morphologically and molecularly distinct cell death modalities in lateral line hair cells. To test whether distinct forms of cell death affect the timing of hair cell regeneration, we interrogated the expression dynamics of genes implicated in hair cell formation. The transcription factor atoh1a is essential for hair cell development56,57 and is upregulated in support cells at neo + 3h50. Hybridization chain reaction (HCR) showed a strong increase in atoh1a expression between MTZ + 2 h and MTZ + 3 h, comparable to equivalent time points following neomycin-induced cell death (Fig. 2e). Similarly, the Notch ligand dld is strongly upregulated in hair cell progenitors at neo + 4 h and MTZ + 4 h compared to the respective +3 h time points (Supplementary Fig. 1i), suggesting that despite differences in cell loss kinetics, the timing of key molecular events during hair cell regeneration is comparable. To test whether hair cells regenerate in both paradigms, we counted hair cell numbers using myo6b:H2B-Scarlet. Despite initial differences in the number of surviving hair cells (Fig. 2f, g, 0.4 vs 1.9 hair cells remaining at MTZ + 2 h vs neo + 2 h), hair cell numbers at 24 h and 48 h do not differ significantly between treatments (Fig. 2f, g, 7.5 vs 8.1 hair cells at MTZ + 24 h vs neo + 24 h, and 9.5 vs 9.7 hair cells at MTZ + 48 h vs neo + 48 h). Together, these data show that hair cell regeneration occurs within a comparable time window in both paradigms. In sum, we have established a comparative approach in which hair cells die through distinct mechanisms yet regenerate similarly, therefore facilitating the side-by-side comparison of regeneration programs across cell death modalities.

The immune response to distinct types of hair cell death differs at the cellular level

A distinguishing feature of programmed cell death, such as apoptosis, is its immunologically silent nature30,32. Apoptotic cells are rapidly cleared by professional phagocytes, such as macrophages, through a process called efferocytosis, preventing the release of intracellular components and protecting the surrounding tissue from damage58. Macrophages are present in the skin surrounding the lateral line in homeostatic conditions and are quickly recruited to neuromasts upon neomycin-induced hair cell death43,44. To visualize phagocytosis by macrophages following hair cell death, we performed confocal time-lapse recordings of macrophages and hair cells, using Tg(mpeg1:EGFP)gl22 (hereafter mpeg1:GFP) and myo6b:NTR double transgenic larvae. Since the mCherry protein in the myo6b:NTR transgenic localizes to the hair cell cytoplasm, it can serve as a proxy for intact cells that have not undergone lysis. Time-lapse recordings following MTZ treatment showed that macrophages primarily engulf entire hair cells prior to cell lysis, as indicated by the uptake of mCherry + cells (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Movie 3). Individual macrophages were often seen internalizing multiple hair cells, resulting in the fusion of multiple mCherry+ phagosomes and accumulation of large mCherry+ aggregates within macrophages (Fig. 3a, arrowheads), reminiscent of microglia gastrosomes59. Neomycin on the other hand induced rapid hair cell death characterized by loss of cytoplasmic mCherry fluorescence, suggesting that most hair cells underwent lysis prior to engulfment (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Movie 4). Macrophages quickly migrated to the neuromast but mostly phagocytosed smaller mCherry+ particles or formed phagosomes without labeled debris (Fig. 3b, arrowheads). Different types of hair cell death may influence immune cell dynamics at the injury site. To compare the temporal dynamics of macrophages, we treated larvae with either MTZ or neomycin, fixed them at time points before and after cell death, and counted the number of mpeg:GFP + cells inside neuromasts (phagocytosing macrophages). Macrophages started to migrate into neuromasts during MTZ-induced hair cell death (MTZ-1h, Supplementary Fig. 2a) but their numbers did not peak until 1 h after completed cell death (MTZ + 1 h, Fig. 3c, g). Similarly, macrophage recruitment peaked at 1 h after neomycin-induced cell death (Fig. 3d, g), as previously described43,44. Intriguingly, higher numbers of phagocytosing macrophages were observed at MTZ + 0 h and MTZ + 1 h compared to the respective neomycin time points (Fig. 3g). Similar numbers of a second, non-phagocytosing population of macrophages are recruited to the basal side of neuromasts in both paradigms, although their dynamics appeared temporally shifted (Fig. 3h). These findings suggest that apoptotic hair cell death may warrant a larger recruitment of phagocytosing macrophages to aid in the efferocytosis process.

Besides macrophages, neutrophils are key players in acute inflammation. To test whether neutrophil dynamics differ between cell death paradigms, we fixed larvae at corresponding time points after cell death and compared neutrophil numbers surrounding neuromasts using Tg(lyz:gap43-EGFP)psi103 as a marker. While neomycin-induced hair cell death led to the rapid recruitment of neutrophils which peaked at 1 h after cell death (Fig. 3f, i), MTZ-induced cell death did not significantly increase neutrophil migration towards the site of cell death compared to untreated larvae (Fig. 3e, i and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Time lapse imaging showed that lyz:gap43-EGFP + neutrophils migrated near myo6b:H2B-mScarlet+ hair cell debris following neomycin-induced hair cell death but did not internalize it (Supplementary Fig. 2c), suggesting that neutrophils do not participate in the phagocytosis process. Collectively, these data underscore that the cell death modality influences the number and type of immune cells recruited to the injury site.

Identification of effector macrophage clusters

Differences in the observed immune cell dynamics may arise from distinct transcriptional changes within macrophages upon sensing, recognizing or ingesting dying hair cells. To profile the transcriptome of macrophages responding to hair cell death, we sorted mpeg1:GFP+ and mpeg1:GFP + ;myo6b:mCherry-NTR+ cells during their recruitment following MTZ- (MTZ-1h, MTZ+ 0 h, MTZ+ 1 h) and neomycin-induced cell death (neo+ 0 h, neo+ 1 h), as well as from untreated larvae (homeostasis) using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and performed single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) using the 10X Genomics Chromium platform (Fig. 4a, b). Following quality control and integration of the different time points of the macrophage datasets, we visualized the data in two-dimensional space using UMAP dimensionality reduction (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Consistent with previous studies, we identified macrophage clusters but also other immune cell types, such as metaphocytes43,60. To identify the population of macrophages responding to hair cell death, hereafter referred to as effector macrophages, we first subsetted all macrophage clusters based on mpeg1.1, mfap4 and EGFP expression (Supplementary Fig. 3b–d). Unsupervised clustering of this subset revealed 12 transcriptionally distinct cell clusters (Fig. 4c). To identify effector macrophages, we calculated the proportion of each cluster across conditions. We reasoned that effector macrophages would adopt a distinct transcriptional state and increase upon cell death. Two clusters, which we designated Effector 1 and 2 (Fig. 4c) strongly increased in proportion relative to homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 3f). To identify markers for the putative Effector clusters, we ran the Seurat function FindConservedMarkers. This analysis showed that Effector 2 was defined by the presence of hair cell transcripts, such as otofb, pcdh15a, s100t and myo6b (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i and Supplementary Data 2), indicating that it may correspond to a small subset of actively phagocytosing macrophages in which remnant hair cell mRNA can be detected prior to degradation in phagolysosomes. Cluster Effector 1 on the other hand shared most cluster markers with Effector 2 (Supplementary Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 3i) but did not contain detectable hair cell transcripts, suggesting that it contains phagocytosing effector macrophages in which hair cell mRNA was not detected, possibly due to it being rapidly degraded. To test whether these two clusters indeed correspond to phagocytosing macrophages, we performed HCRs for the cluster marker cd83 (Supplementary Fig. 3g, orange box, Supplementary Fig. 3j). We found that cd83 is expressed in macrophages responding to both MTZ- and neomycin-induced cell death (Fig. 4d, f, arrows). Quantification of HCR fluorescence intensity signal revealed significantly higher signal in phagocytosing macrophages during MTZ- and neomycin-induced cell death compared to most non-phagocytosing macrophages in controls, or presumptive microglia in the brain of the same fish (Fig. 4d, f, arrowheads; Figs. 4e and 4g and Supplementary Fig. 3k), showing that its expression is induced in effector macrophages after hair cell death. Interestingly, non-phagocytosing macrophages outside of neuromasts upregulated cd83 at neo + 0 h, but not MTZ + 0 h. This suggests that neomycin-induced hair cell death also induces cd83 expression in macrophages surrounding the lateral line which may either remain non-phagocytosing or later migrate into the neuromast. As hair cell death attracts macrophages that are near neuromasts43 and macrophages surrounding neuromasts in controls do not express cd83 (Fig. 4d–g), the Effector clusters appear to be a cell state, rather than a pre-existing population of macrophages. Nevertheless, we cannot fully rule out the possibility of the migration of a pre-existing population of cd83 + macrophages some distance away from the injury site. In sum, our analysis shows that effector macrophages adopt a transcriptionally distinct state in response to both cell death modalities. To avoid transcriptional changes potentially stemming from phagocytosed hair cells rather than macrophages themselves, we focused our downstream analysis on the Effector 1 cluster.

Effector macrophages undergo shared and specific transcriptional changes depending on the hair cell death modality

To identify cell death-specific transcriptional changes in effector macrophages, we performed differential gene expression analysis in the Effector 1 cluster between each neomycin time point and all MTZ time points, and vice versa. This analysis revealed 58 MTZ- and 170 neomycin-enriched genes (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Data 3). For shared genes across conditions, we considered conserved cluster markers of Effector 1 that were not significantly differentially expressed in the MTZ- or neo-enriched categories (181 genes, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Data 3).

Macrophages respond to environmental cues and adopt distinct functional states through the expression and secretion of specific cytokines and inflammatory mediators. It was previously shown that macrophages responding to neomycin-induced hair cell death undergo a brief pro-inflammatory phase followed by three independently activated anti-inflammatory activation states43. To test whether this activation sequence was shared with MTZ-induced hair cell death, we first analyzed the expression dynamics of the previously identified genes in each state. We found that most genes in the pro-inflammatory phase, glucocorticoid signaling and Interleukin-10 (IL-10) signaling were largely absent after programmed hair cell death (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Instead, only the third anti-inflammatory state, Interleukin-4 (IL-4)/polyamine signaling, was partially shared between neomycin- and MTZ-induced hair cell death. More specifically, our differential expression analysis indicated that pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., il1b and cxcl8a) were strongly expressed in neomycin conditions, while not expressed after MTZ-induced cell death (Fig. 4h). A wide range of cytokine receptors were also specifically upregulated in response to neomycin-induced hair cell death, such as cxcr4b, ccr9a and tnfrsfa. This was accompanied by the upregulation of many transcription factors, such as nfkb1, cebpa, cebpb, foxo1a and egr3 (Fig. 4i), crucial regulators of macrophage function61–64. In contrast, no cytokines and only few transcription factors were upregulated specifically in MTZ conditions (Fig. 4h, i). These results suggest that neomycin-induced hair cell death induces drastic inflammatory and transcriptional remodeling in macrophages, while hair cell efferocytosis is instead characterized by the absence of known pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Given the lack of a strong cytokine response, we next asked whether macrophages responding to MTZ-induced cell death displayed other unique transcriptional changes. MTZ-enriched genes included the cell surface proteins cd63 and cd9b, genes involved in cell-cell signaling, such as igf2r, sema4ab, manf and wnt2bb, and the heme oxygenase hmox1a (Fig. 4h), some of which have anti-inflammatory or pro-regenerative functions in mammals65–68. Using HCR, we validated that wnt2bb was specifically upregulated in phagocytosing macrophages at MTZ + 1 h, but not neo + 1 h (Fig. 4k–n). Another major group of MTZ-specific upregulated genes included those related to phagocytosis, such as lgmn and mrc1b, or phagolysosomal degradation, like the cathepsins ctsla, ctsc and ctsd (Fig. 4j), consistent with the increased efferocytic activity observed in time lapses. In addition, distinct sets of solute carrier (slc) genes, important metabolic regulators during efferocytosis59,69, were expressed after MTZ-induced cell death (Fig. 4j). Taken together, our results help identify genes important for signaling and phagolysosomal function during macrophage efferocytosis.

Our analysis also allows for the identification of shared genes, whose expression across conditions points to central roles in the macrophage response. These included genes implicated in cytokine signaling, like tnfb, ccl35.1, tnfrsf18 and tnfrsf1b (Fig. 4h). These were accompanied by the shared expression of transcriptional regulators, like rxrga and pparg (Fig. 4i), which are implicated in immune cell activation and phagocytosis70,71. Other shared genes are involved in protein degradation (Fig. 4j), like the cathepsins ctss2.1 and ctsk. We also identified amino acid transporters, such as slc66a1 and slc7a7, the latter being implicated in zebrafish macrophage efferocytosis72. These results point at shared metabolic mechanisms that potentially support hair cell phagocytosis across different cell death paradigms. Altogether, we have identified shared and context-specific transcriptional changes in macrophages exposed to distinct cell death environments. These findings suggest that macrophages tailor their transcriptional programs based on the nature of cell death, with potential implications for modulating tissue repair and regeneration.

Programmed cell death causes a diminished transcriptional injury response in neuromast support cells compared to lytic cell death, but regeneration-specific genes are shared

Cell death has profound effects on neighboring cells. Neuromast support cells are in close vicinity to dying hair cells and proliferate to give rise to new hair cells as early as 5 hours after cell death37,50,73. To investigate whether the transcriptional programs initiating hair cell regeneration differ between cell death modalities, we used the transgenic Tg(she:h2ax-mCherry)psi57 (hereafter she:H2A-mCherry), which labels all lateral line cells, isolated mCherry+ cells from the same fish as we sorted macrophages using FACS and performed scRNA-seq (Fig. 4a). In addition to an untreated control sample (homeo), we focused our analysis on the immediate time points following cell death (MTZ-1h, MTZ + 0 h, MTZ + 1 h, neo + 0 h, neo + 1 h), which have been implicated in triggering the hair cell regeneration response50. Following integration of all datasets and removal of non-neuromast cells, we obtained a dataset containing all expected neuromast cell types (Fig. 5a). Apart from mature hair cells, which were greatly reduced in MTZ and neomycin conditions, all populations were present across the examined time points (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 4). Support cell populations can be divided into three groups: AP cells located in the anterior and posterior poles of the neuromast, DV cells, found in the dorsal and ventral poles, and central cells, located in the center37,74,75. We focused our analysis on central support cells, as they make up the biggest support cell population, give rise to hair cells and are in the immediate vicinity to dying hair cells. To test whether central support cells undergo distinct transcriptional changes depending on the hair cell death modality, we performed differential expression analysis between each MTZ or neomycin time point and homeostasis, respectively. This analysis revealed a large group of genes that were differentially expressed in response to neomycin-induced cell death (Fig. 5c, d, 412 up- and 189 downregulated) or shared between both paradigms (Fig. 5c, d, 395 up- and 541 downregulated, Supplementary Data 5). Only a relatively small group of genes was MTZ-enriched (Fig. 5c, d, 243 up- and 136 downregulated genes). Following neomycin-induced hair cell death, support cells are known to undergo an injury response (neo + 0 h, module 1) that is likely involved in triggering a group of regeneration-specific genes50 (neo+1 h, module 2, Fig. 5e). Analysis of the differentially expressed genes in central support cells showed that 74% of genes expressed in module 1 were neomycin-enriched, while only 26% were shared across paradigms (Fig. 5f). Conversely, 54% of genes in module 2 and 73% of constitutively upregulated genes were shared between paradigms (Fig. 5f). These results suggest that, despite differences in the injury response, regeneration programs converge on regeneration-specific genes in module 2.

Given the striking differences in the injury response to distinct cell death modalities, we first focused our analysis on the immediate time points after hair cell death. Among the most strongly upregulated genes in central support cells during programmed hair cell death (MTZ-1h) were enzymes involved in the metabolism of glutathione, such as gclc, gclm, gss, slc7a11 and gpx1a (Fig. 5g). Similarly, ferritin-like genes fthl28, fthl29, fthl30 and fthl31 were upregulated during MTZ-induced hair cell death. In addition, genes related to protein processing in the ER, such as calr3b, pdia6, hspa5, hsp90b1 and hspa8b were enriched slightly later (MTZ + 0 h). To validate our scRNA-seq findings, we performed an HCR for prdx1, which revealed strong expression in support cells after MTZ-induced cell death, while its expression after neomycin was limited to hair cells, as indicated by the overlap with otofb (Fig. 5g, orange box, Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 5c). These data suggest that central support cells undergo oxidative stress after MTZ-induced cell death, possibly in response to extracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) released by dying hair cells.

In contrast to MTZ treatment, neomycin-induced cell death triggered the upregulation of a large group of transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. 5a). These included the AP-1 transcription factors fosb, fosab, jund, junbb, atf4a and jdp2b, which were not upregulated after MTZ treatment (Fig. 5h). This finding was validated by HCR, which revealed strong expression of the neomycin-enriched genes fosab and fosb in support cells following neomycin- but not MTZ-induced cell death (Fig. 5k, Supplementary Fig. 5b). Interestingly, a different subset of AP-1 family members was upregulated in both paradigms in the scRNA-seq dataset, such as atf3, fosl1a, fosl2, junba and jun (Fig. 5i). This was confirmed by HCR, as junba showed strong upregulation following neomycin-induced cell death and diminished, but detectable expression following MTZ-induced cell death (Fig. 5l). To avoid the possibility of trigger-specific transcriptional artifacts, we performed HCRs for fosab and junba following Copper Sulfate (CuSO4)-induced unprogrammed and Cisplatin-induced programmed cell death76–79. Expression levels of fosab and junba after CuSO4 and Cisplatin treatment recapitulated those of neomycin and MTZ, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c), suggesting that similar injury responses may be at play in comparable cell death contexts induced by other ototoxins. Together, these results show that distinct hair cell death modalities influence the profile of transcription factors expressed during the injury response. Specifically, members of the AP-1 complex family are differentially regulated, with only a subset of members being shared across cell death paradigms. Whether a core transcriptional program triggers hair cell regeneration in all environments or whether distinct transcription factors initiate the regenerative process in different contexts remains to be determined.

Our comparative analysis also allows for the identification of shared genes that are likely involved in core hair cell regeneration signaling across cell death conditions. Many of these genes belong to module 2 (Fig. 5f). For example, we identified members of the TGFβ, BMP and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways, the semaphorins sema4ba and sema4c and cytokines such as il11b and cxcl14 that were upregulated in central cells in both paradigms (Fig. 5m). Similarly, a large group of shared downregulated genes in central cells included Wnt and Notch signaling genes, as well as extracellular matrix (ECM) related genes. The fact that expression of these genes changes in two independent paradigms points to a conserved function in regeneration. Taken together, we identified shared candidate genes whose function in hair cell regeneration can be experimentally tested in future studies.

Immune cells attenuate the support cell injury response to hair cell death

We observed increased levels of hair cell efferocytosis by macrophages and decreased injury response gene expression in neuromasts following MTZ-induced hair cell death compared to neomycin-induced cell lysis. While fosab was expressed in dying hair cells at MTZ-1h in siblings, its expression often spread across support cells in the absence of immune cells (Fig. 6i). No significant differences were observed at later time points (Fig. 6i–l, MTZ + 0 h, neo + 1 h) when debris was still present (Fig. 6g, h), suggesting that lingering cell debris does not continue to increase fosab expression. In sum, these data indicate that immune cells attenuate the transcriptional injury response to hair cell death in both cell death paradigms. This suggests that the reduced transcriptional injury response following MTZ-induced cell death can at least partially be attributed to the removal of cell debris by macrophages.

Blocking immune cell recruitment increases injury-induced support cell proliferation following MTZ-induced cell death

Previous studies showed that macrophages are not essential for lateral line hair cell regeneration following neomycin-induced cell death43,44 but may delay regeneration in the context of CuSO4-mediated hair cell death45. This raises the possibility of injury context-dependent roles for macrophages in influencing regenerative outcomes. Given the involvement of macrophages in hair cell efferocytosis following MTZ-induced cell death, we therefore asked whether blocking immune cell recruitment would impact hair cell regeneration in this context. We first tested whether neuromasts of dnRac2 larvae displayed any phenotype in homeostatic neuromasts prior to cell death. Quantification of the number of hair cells and total cells revealed a slight decrease in neuromast cell number (Fig. 7a–c). To corroborate these results using a different approach to interfere with macrophage and neutrophil function, we analyzed irf8st96 mutants82 crossed to the Tg(mpx:mCherry,rac2_D57N)zf307 transgenic (herafter mpx:dnRac2)83. Disrupting irf8 function reduces macrophage numbers during early larval stages82,84, while mpx:dnRac2 blocks neutrophil migration83. Consistent with our first approach, irf8-/-;mpx:dnRac2(+) larvae showed enhanced amplifying support cell proliferation rates 24 h after MTZ-, but not neomycin-induced hair cell death, compared to irf8+/+;mpx:dnRac2(-) larvae (Supplementary Fig. 9a–c, e). Hair cell differentiation was also slightly, but not significantly, increased following MTZ, but not neomycin treatment (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b, d, e). Together, our data show that immune cells are not required for hair cell regeneration and even attenuate it in the context of MTZ-induced hair cell death.

To visualize hair cell formation in the presence and absence of immune cells, we performed time-lapse recordings of myo6b:H2B-mScarlet during the first 20 h following cell death. Quantification of the number of regenerating hair cells over time showed an increase in hair cell formation in dnRac2 larvae compared to siblings following MTZ-induced cell death (Fig. 7k and Supplementary Movie 5). Conversely, hair cell formation following neomycin-induced cell death was initially delayed in the absence of immune cells but caught up to control levels by 20 h post-cell death (Fig. 7l and Supplementary Movie 6), further corroborating our EdU experiment data. In sum, we show that support and progenitor cell proliferation increase in the absence of immune cells following MTZ- but not neomycin-induced cell death. Collectively, our results highlight that immune cell recruitment has context-specific effects on regenerative outcomes depending on the cell death modality.

Discussion

Tissue regeneration involves the context-dependent replacement of cells, but the influence of the cell death modality on regeneration programs is not fully understood. Our side-by-side comparison of different types of hair cell death shows comparable hair cell regeneration dynamics despite striking differences in the initial molecular responses of immune and sensory organ cells.

Inflammatory and injury responses differs depending on the cell death modality

Our study presents a systematic in vivo comparison of early inflammatory responses to distinct types of cell death within the same tissue context. We found that neomycin-induced hair cell death specifically triggers upregulation of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as il1b, in macrophages. This is comparable to responses in other regenerating zebrafish organs, such as the fin and spinal cord, where transient il1b-mediated inflammation is required for successful regeneration25,28. In contrast, hair cell regeneration following MTZ-induced cell death proceeds normally in the absence of il1b+ macrophages or neutrophils, another major source of il1b, indicating that pro-inflammatory signaling from immune cells is not required for hair cell regeneration in this context.

While pro-inflammatory cytokines and signaling pathways have been well characterized during epimorphic regeneration in zebrafish25,28,29 far less is known about how macrophages respond in less inflammatory settings, such as during homeostatic cell turnover or apoptosis. Our study addresses this gap by identifying putative mediators involved in the suppression or resolution of inflammation during MTZ- or neomycin-induced cell death, respectively. Although we did not find unique MTZ-enriched cytokines, our list of putative anti-inflammatory markers, such as cd9b and hmox1, offers new targets for future functional studies in zebrafish.

Downstream targets of Il1b or TNF-alpha signaling in the zebrafish fin and spinal cord include AP-1 complex members23,28. AP-1 has been suggested to initiate regeneration programs in many contexts, such as in zebrafish heart85, axolotl spinal cord86 and planaria87 whole body regeneration. In our study, while both cell death paradigms induce expression of junba, jun, fosl1a and atf4b in support cells, neomycin-induced hair cell death selectively induces an additional subset of AP-1 components, such as fosb, fosab, jund, atf4a, and junbb. Since AP-1 can function either as a heterodimer between Jun and Fos proteins, or a homodimer of two Jun proteins, this raises the possibility that distinct sets of AP-1 dimers play different roles in either cell death context. This scenario is consistent with previous studies, such as in the context of axolotl spinal cord injury, where overexpression of AP-1cFos/cJun in glial cells led to defects in axonal regeneration, while AP-1cFos/JunB promoted a pro-regenerative glial response86. The shared upregulated set of AP-1 complex genes in neuromast support cells may therefore play a role in initiating regeneration programs irrespective of the cell death modality. Conversely, AP-1 members transcribed selectively following neomycin-induced cell death could instead be involved in responding to elevated cellular stress rather than directly impacting regeneration. Together, our results raise the possibility of functional divergence between AP-1 family members during hair cell regeneration.

Growing evidence indicates that inflammatory and regenerative responses to injury are highly context dependent. For example, transcriptional responses of Müller glia in the zebrafish retina vary depending on which retinal cell types are selectively ablated using the NTR/MTZ system7,88. Likewise, both shared and distinct changes in gene expression and chromatin accessibility occur following light-induced damage compared with NMDA-induced excitotoxicity6. Importantly, knockout of genes within these regeneration programs can lead to different regenerative outcomes depending on the injury paradigm89. While these studies focused on responses to the loss of distinct cell types, our work complements them by demonstrating that ablation of the same cell type can elicit context-dependent responses depending on the mode of cell death. Notably, several injury response genes detected in our data, including cebpd, fosab, jun, junba and fosl2, are upregulated in both the lateral line and retina following injury88,89. Future studies will determine the extent to which gene expression programs triggered by comparable ablation paradigms are shared across regenerating sensory organs.

Mechanisms of macrophage and neutrophil recruitment

Consistent with previous studies, such as in the context of zebrafish liver injury90 or retinal photoreceptor ablation27, we show that reduced numbers of neutrophils are recruited to MTZ-induced cell death, suggesting that the type of cell death influences the extent of leukocyte recruitment. One possible reason is that dying hair cells directly influence immune cell migration, for example, through the differential release of find-me signals, such as ATP/UTP, lysophophatidylcholine or sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) in the case of apoptosis91–93, or DAMPs, such as DNA, RNA, histones and actin that may be exposed as hair cells rupture94. Macrophages may exhibit enhanced recruitment to find-me signals from programmed cell death compared to neomycin-induced unprogrammed cell death. Our scRNA-seq data also revealed selective upregulation of neutrophil-attracting chemokines, such as cxcl8a, in macrophages responding to neomycin-induced cell death. These data indicate that macrophages may play a role in regulating neutrophil recruitment in response to injury, consistent with previous work showing that macrophages influence neutrophil behavior at the injury site95. Alternatively, necrotic hair cell rupture may release hydrogen peroxide, a diffusible signal known to attract leukocytes to sites of injury96.

Roles of immune cells during hair cell regeneration

Our results demonstrate that hair cell regeneration can occur in the absence of immune cells, consistent with previous studies showing that depletion of macrophages has minimal impact on hair cell regeneration following neomycin treatment44, or only causes a slight delay in the appearance of new hair cells following ablation using CuSO445. This contrasts with other tissues, like the zebrafish fin, muscle, spinal cord, brain and retina, where the inflammatory response, and macrophages in particular, have been shown to play central roles in promoting regeneration18,22,25–27. Many of these studies involve more severe injuries, such as spinal cord transection or fin amputation. It is possible that macrophages are required after more severe injuries to the lateral line or repeated rounds of hair cell ablation.

In addition to macrophages, dead cell debris can be cleared by other cell types, such as skin cells and fibroblasts, so called non-professional phagocytes97–103, which can contribute to the inflammatory response104. Given the superficial location of neuromasts in the skin and the involvement of zebrafish epidermal cells in the clearance of axonal debris101, it is possible that skin cells surrounding neuromasts compensate for the absence of functional macrophages in our dnRac2 experiments by phagocytosing hair cell debris. Consistent with this possibility, we occasionally observed hair cell debris accumulating in skin cells outside of neuromasts. Whether these potentially phagocytic cells signal to support cells during hair cell regeneration remains to be investigated. In addition, support cells themselves are involved in debris clearance in the sensory epithelia of the mammalian and avian inner ear105–107 but whether lateral line support cells also possess phagocytic capacity in the zebrafish remains to be determined.

Blocking immune cell migration to neuromasts selectively enhanced support cell proliferation in the context of MTZ-, but not neomycin-induced cell death. One potential explanation for this divergence in the proliferative response is that dying hair cells release mitogenic factors that stimulate proliferation in neighboring support cells, as has been observed during compensatory proliferation8–10. Our results show that when macrophage recruitment is inhibited, dying hair cells and their debris persist longer in the neuromast following MTZ treatment, potentially prolonging exposure to these mitogenic cues and therefore amplifying the regenerative response. The fact that lingering cell debris does not impair hair cell regeneration is contrasting with previous studies, which showed impaired spinal cord regeneration in zebrafish mutants with decreased phagocytic capacity108 or increased secondary neuronal cell death after brain injury when phagocytic activity is diminished109.

Taken together, we uncover distinct molecular and cellular responses to different cell death modalities in a regenerating sensory organ. Our data offer insights into the context-dependent nature of the injury response during regeneration and highlight the importance of paradigm choice in regeneration biology. Our work will be a reference point for future studies exploring the functional consequences of the identified context-specific and shared genes.

Methods

Zebrafish husbandry

All experiments were performed following the guidelines of the Stowers Institute for Medical Research IACUC review board (IACUC protocol 2023-159), with the exception of experiments using potassium chloride, phenol red, copper sulfate, and cisplatin which were approved by the IACUC subsequently. Zebrafish embryos and larvae were kept in 0.5x E2 medium (7.5 mM NaCl, 0.25 mM KCl, 0.5 mM MgSO4, 75 mM KH2PO4, 25 mM Na2HPO4, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 0.5 mg/l NaHCO3 (pH 7.4)) and incubated at 28.5 °C.

The following published transgenic lines were used in this study: Et(krt4:EGFP)sqet4 110, Et(krt4:EGFP)sqet20111, Tg(pou4f3:gap43-GFPs356t 111, Tg(she:h2ax-mCherry)psi57 112, Tg(mpeg1:EGFP)gl22 113, Tg(mpeg1:mCherry)gl23[ 113, Tg(lyz:TagRFP)uwm11 114, Tg(myo6b:hist2h2l-mScarlet)psi66 112, Tg(coro1a:GFP-2A-rac2_D57N)psi92 80, Tg(she:Hsa.HIST1H2BJ-EGFP)psi59 112, Tg(mpx:mCherry,rac2_D57N)zf307 83.

The following mutant was used in this study: irf8st96 82.

Transgenic line generation

To generate the Tg(myo6b:mCherry-2A-NTR2.0)psi102 transgenic, a p5’-Entry (p5’E) vector containing the myo6b promoter115, a middle entry (pME) vector containing mCherry and NTR2.0 separated by a P2A peptide (modified from Addgene plasmid #15865349) and a p3’-Entry (p3’E) polyA116 were cloned into the pToneDest Tol1 destination vector (Addgene plasmid #67691,117) using a Gateway LR reaction (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11791020). Tol1 mRNA was synthesized from pToneTP vector (Addgene plasmid #67692,117) using the mMessage T7 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Zebrafish one-cell stage embryos were injected with Tol1 mRNA (20 ng/μl), expression vector (12.5 ng/μl), 0.3 M KCl and 0.05% phenol red, screened for mCherry fluorescence at 2 dpf and raised to adulthood. Founders were identified based on mCherry expression in hair cells and outcrossed to wildtype fish to generate stable F1 generations. Multiple founders were screened to identify the one with the most specific mCherry expression in hair cells.

To generate the Tg(lyz:gap43-EGFP)psi103 transgenic line, the lysozyme C (lyz) promoter118 was cloned into a vector containing GAP43-EGFP and Tol2 recombination sites using Gibson cloning. Zebrafish one-cell stage embryos were injected as described above, screened for GFP fluorescence at 1-2 dpf and raised to adulthood. Founders were screened for GFP expression in neutrophils and crossed with the established neutrophil transgenic line Tg(lyz:TagRFP)uwm11 to confirm co-labeling.

To generate the Tg(coro1a:EGFP)psi104 transgenic line, the coro1a promoter (7.03 kb upstream of the translation start site81) was cloned into a vector containing EGFP and Tol2 recombination sites using Gibson cloning. Zebrafish one-cell stage embryos were injected with an expression vector (15 ng/μl) and Tol2 transposase mRNA (40 ng/μl), screened for GFP fluorescence at 1-2 dpf and raised to adulthood. Founders were screened for GFP fluorescence in immune cells.

Sensory hair cell ablation

All hair cell ablation experiments were performed on Tg(myo6b:mCherry-P2A-NTR2.0) larvae. Larvae were placed in cell strainers in six-well plates to allow for the transfer of larvae between solutions.

To induce hair cell death, 5 dpf zebrafish larvae were incubated in 5 mM MTZ (Sigma-Aldrich) with 0.5% DMSO for 2 hours, 300 µM neomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min, 400 µM Cisplatin with 0.1% DMSO for 4 hours, or 10 µM CuSO4 for 30 minutes in 0.5x E2 medium at 28.5 °C.

Larvae were washed with 0.5x E2 medium three times and allowed to recover in 0.5x E2 medium at 28.5 °C until the desired time point.

EdU incorporation experiments

To label cells that underwent cell division, 5 dpf larvae were incubated in 3.3 mM EdU (Carbosynth) with 1% DMSO for 24 h, starting at the respective + 0 h time points following MTZ or neomycin treatment, and fixed with 4% PFA at 4 °C. EdU staining and GFP immunostaining was performed as previously described73. To visualize hair cells in irf8 mutant EdU experiments, immunostaining for Myosin-VI (Proteus Bioscience 25-6791, dilution 1:100) was performed.

Cell numbers and EdU indexes were quantified performed as described37,73. EdU indexes were calculated by dividing EdU+ cells by the total neuromast cell number.

Hybridization chain reaction (HCR)

In situ HCR v3.0 was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Molecular Instruments)119 with minor changes as described previously120. Samples were incubated in amplifiers for two days prior to washing and staining with DAPI (1 µg/ml).

HCR probes and amplifiers were purchased from Molecular Instruments. The following probes were used: atoh1a-B2, dld-B5, cd83-B1, wnt2bb-B1, fosab-B4, junba-B1, fosb-B2, prdx1-B1.

Immunostaining

To stain for cleaved Caspase-3 in dying hair cells, 5 dpf sqET4 + ;myo6b:NTR + larvae were fixed with 4 % PFA at 4 °C for 24 h. Fixed larvae were washed with PBS-Tween (0.1%) three times for 10 minutes and blocked in 5% normal goat serum (GEMINI BIO-PRODUCTS, #100-109) with 0.8% Triton X-100 and 1% DMSO. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies, rabbit anti-Caspase 3 Asp175 (Cell Signaling, #9661, 1:200 dilution) and chicken anti-GFP (abcam, # ab13970 1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. After thorough washing with PBS-Tween, larvae were incubated in goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor Plus 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A32733 1:500), goat anti-chicken IgY 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A11039, 1:500) and DAPI (1:1000) at 4 °C overnight. The samples were extensively washed with PBST prior to confocal imaging.

Confocal imaging

Confocal z-stacks were acquired on a Nikon Ti2 Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disk equipped with a Hamamatsu Orca Fusion sCMOS. Objectives used were CFI Apo LWD 40 × WI 1.15 NA Lambda S and CFI Plan Apochromat Lambda D 10 ×.

For all time-lapse imaging experiments, larvae were anesthetized with MS-222 up to 80 mg/l and mounted in 0.8% low melting point agarose dissolved in 0.5 x E2 medium and 140 mg/l MS-222 in glass-bottomed dishes (Cellvis)42. A Stage Top Incubator (OkoLab) was used to keep a consistent temperature of 28.5 °C and 85% humidity.

For time lapses during neomycin-induced cell death, 300 µM neomycin was prepared in 0.5 x E2 medium/MS-222 and added on top of the larvae mounted in agarose. To image neuromasts following neomycin-induced cell death, neomycin was then removed from the imaging dish, and the mounted larvae washed three times with 0.5x E2/MS-222 prior to continuing the imaging.

For time lapses after MTZ-induced hair cell death, larvae were first treated with 5 mM MTZ for 2 h in a 6-well plate as described above. After rinsing the larvae with 0.5x E2, the larvae were anaesthetized with MS-222 and mounted in 0.8% low melting point agarose for imaging.

Cell dissociation and FACS

Adult double-homozygous mpeg1:EGFP;myo6b:mCherry-NTR2.0 zebrafish were crossed to homozygous she:H2A-mCherry zebrafish to generate progeny for scRNA-seq experiments. 600 mpeg1:EGFP;myo6b:mCherry-NTR2.0;she:H2A-mCherry triple-positive larvae were anaesthetized using MS-222 and cells dissociated on ice using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as previously described73. While tissue dissociation was performed entirely on ice, no transcriptional or translational inhibitors were used during the dissociation, which may impact immune cell transcriptional signatures.

To stain nucleated cells, the suspension was incubated with DRAQ5 (1:200 dilution, Biostatus, DR50200) for 5 min on ice. Cells were sorted by the Stowers Institute Cytometry Core Facility using BD Influx Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences) and BD FACSymphony S6 (BD Biosciences) instruments. The samples were first gated on nucleated DRAQ5 + cells, then gated for single nucleated cells using forward scatter area vs height. Single nucleated cells were then presented in a bivariate GFP vs mCherry plot, and the WT control was used to create gates for GFP + /mCherry- (macrophages), GFP + /mCherry + (macrophages), and GFP-/mCherry+ cells (lateral line). DRAQ5 + /GFP+ and DRAQ5 + /GFP + /mCherry + macrophages were sorted into 1X DPBS/0.04% BSA while DRAQ5 + /GFP-/mCherry + lateral line cells were sorted into a separate tube. Cells were spun down at 350 rcf at 4 °C for 2 min in a swinging bucket centrifuge with slow deceleration. The cell pellet was gently resuspended in 100 µL of 1X DPBS/0.04% BSA.

10X Chromium scRNA-seq library generation

The concentration and viability of the sorted cells was assessed using a Luna-FL cell counter (Logos Biosystems). Cells with a viability of 80% or higher were loaded onto a Chromium X Series instrument (10x Genomics), and libraries were prepared using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Generated cDNA and short fragment libraries underwent quality and quantity assessment using a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies) and Qubit Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). With estimated cell capture at ~ 8300-12,900 cells per sample, libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument using RTA and instrument software versions current at the time of processing, targeting a minimum average of 30,000 reads per cell with paired read lengths of 28*10*10*90 bp.

Read alignment and quantification

Raw reads were processed using 10X Genomics software Cellranger v7.0. Reads were demultiplexed into Fastq file format using cellranger mkfastq with default parameters. The genome index was built under zebrafish danRer11, Ensembl version 102 and used cellranger count function for cell gene count matrix generation.

Pre-processing, quality control and dimensional reduction

Feature count matrices generated by cellranger were loaded into the R package Seurat (v4.3). We first processed each sample individually for quality control. Cells with more than 10% (neuromast datasets) or more than 15% of mitochondrial expression (macrophage datasets) were filtered out. The top 2000 variable genes were selected to run PCA analysis for nearest neighbor graph and clustering, as well as UMAP visualization. Then, all samples from different time points were merged based on Seurat’s CCA scRNA-seq integration method for downstream analysis.

Differential gene expression analysis

To identify cluster markers for the macrophage clusters that did not vary between cell death conditions, the Seurat function FindConservedMarkers was used with default parameters. To identify differentially expressed genes enriched after neomycin or MTZ treatment in the Effector 1 cluster, we ran FindMarkers between each individual neomycin time point (neo + 0 h or neo + 1 h) and all MTZ time points (neo-enriched) or each individual MTZ time point (MTZ-1h, MTZ + 0 h or MTZ + 1 h) and all neomycin time points, respectively. Genes with an adjusted p-value lower than 0.05 and a log2 fold change greater than 0.585 were considered differentially expressed between treatments. For shared genes expressed in the Effector 1 cluster across cell death conditions, we considered those conserved cluster markers of Effector 1 that were not significantly differentially expressed between time points.

For differential gene expression analysis of the central support cell cluster in the neuromast dataset, we compared each cell death time point with homeostasis using the Seurat FindMarkers function. Genes with an adjusted p-value lower than 0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change of 0.585 were considered significant. This threshold was purposefully kept low to detect even modestly differentially expressed genes during the relatively longer MTZ-induced cell death. Genes were categorized as shared if differentially expressed at more than one neomycin- and one MTZ timepoint. Consequently, genes were considered neomycin-enriched or MTZ-enriched if they were significantly up- or downregulated at one or more time point of one paradigm but none of the other.

Image analysis

All image analysis was performed using ImageJ121.

To quantify HCR fluorescence intensity in lateral line neuromasts, maximum projections of confocal z-stacks were generated using the Z Project function and background was subtracted using the Subtract Background function with a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels. A region of interest was drawn using the Polygon selection function, and the fluorescence intensity measured using the Measure function.

To quantify HCR fluorescence intensity in mpeg1:EGFP+ macrophages, background was subtracted on confocal z-stacks using the Subtract Background function and a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels. To avoid signal from autofluorescent cell debris in phagosomes, a region of interest was drawn around the nucleus of each mpeg1:EGFP + cell in a single z-plane based on DAPI signal. Fluorescence intensity was then measured using the Measure function. Cells were grouped as phagocytosing macrophages if their cell body or one of their projections reached into the neuromast. Other mpeg1:EGFP + cells within a 164 µm x 164 µm field of view were considered non-phagocytosing trunk macrophages. Individual mpeg1:EGFP+ in the tegmentum of the same fish were considered for the microglia analysis.

The number of myo6b:H2B-mScarlet + hair cell debris particles was calculated using maximum intensity projections of confocal z-stacks at selected time points of time lapse recordings following MTZ- or neomycin-induced cell death. The Threshold and Analyze Particles functions were used to count individual debris particles with an area larger than 0.5 µm2.

Quantification of hair cell numbers during cell death

To assess the temporal dynamics of neomycin- and MTZ-induced cell death, zebrafish larvae were fixed at the indicated time points with 4 % PFA at 4 °C overnight. The number of live hair cells was quantified using myo6b:H2B-mScarlet and DAPI fluorescence. Hair cells were considered live when their nuclei were of stereotypical spherical morphology without signs of nuclear condensation or fragmentation.

Macrophage and neutrophil recruitment assays

For macrophage and neutrophil recruitment assays, myo6b:NTR zebrafish were crossed to mpeg1:EGFP or lyz:gap43-EGFP, respectively. MTZ and neomycin treatments were performed at 5 dpf on the same clutch of larvae. Larvae were fixed at the indicated time points after MTZ or neomycin treatment with 4 % PFA at 4 °C over night. Fixed larvae were rinsed with PBST three times before staining with DAPI (1 µg/ml) and washing with PBST three times for 5 min prior to mounting and confocal imaging. Three neuromasts (L2, L3, L4) were imaged per larva.

mpeg1:EGFP+ cells were considered phagocytosing macrophages when their cell body was located inside a neuromast or one of their protrusions reached in between neuromast support cells based on DAPI signal. mpeg1:EGFP + cells were considered recruited basal non-phagocytosing macrophages following three criteria: (1) their location immediately below support cells of the neuromast, (2) the absence of visible phagosomes and internalized mCherry + debris and (3) their unique morphological features, such as bean-shaped nuclei and fuzzy cell shape. All lyz:gap43-EGFP + cells in a 164 µm x 164 µm field of view were counted for neutrophil recruitment assays.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.6.1).

Normal distribution of data was assessed using a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. When comparing data from two groups, a two-tailed unpaired t test or a two-tailed Mann-Whitney test were used. For data from more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA with Dunn’s multiple comparisons test were used. To test two independent variables, a two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test was used. A p-value lower than 0.05 was considered significant.
