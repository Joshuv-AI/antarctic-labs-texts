Searching for Optimal Prices in Two-Sided Markets
Yiding Feng, Mengfan Ma, Bo Peng, Zongqi Wan

Abstract
We study online pricing in two-sided markets where a platform repeatedly posts prices to multiple buyers and sellers, observes only binary accept/reject feedback, and aims to maximize either gains-from-trade (GFT) or profit . We analyze three natural classes of price-posting mechanisms—Single-Price, Two-Price, and Segmented-Price—and characterize the regret achievable under each. For profit maximization, we design an algorithm using Two-Price Mechanisms that achieves O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ log ​ T ) O(n^{2}\log\log T) regret, which is optimal in the time horizon T T , where n n is the number of traders. For GFT maximization, the optimal regret depends critically on both market size and mechanism expressiveness. Constant regret is achievable in bilateral trade, but this guarantee breaks down as the market grows: even in a one-seller, two-buyer market, any algorithm using Single-Price Mechanisms suffers regret at least Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ T log ⁡ log ​ log ⁡ log ​ T ) \Omega\!\big(\frac{\log\log T}{\log\log\log\log T}\big) , and we provide a nearly matching O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ T ) O(\log\log T) upper bound for general one-to-many markets. In full many-to-many markets, we prove that Two-Price Mechanisms inevitably incur linear regret Ω ⁡ ( T ) \Omega(T) due to a mismatch phenomenon , wherein inefficient pairings prevent near-optimal trade. To overcome this barrier, we introduce Segmented-Price Mechanisms , which partition traders into groups and assign distinct prices per group. Using this richer mechanism, we design an algorithm achieving O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ log ⁡ log ⁡ T + n 3 ) O(n^{2}\log\log T+n^{3}) regret for GFT maximization. Finally, we extend our results to the contextual setting, where traders’ costs and values depend linearly on observed d d -dimensional features that vary across rounds, obtaining regret bounds of O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ d ​ log ⁡ log ⁡ T + n 2 ​ d ​ log ⁡ d ) O(n^{2}d\log\log T+n^{2}d\log d) for profit and O ⁡ ( n 2 ​ d 2 ​ log ⁡ T ) O(n^{2}d^{2}\log T) for GFT. Our work delineates sharp boundaries between learnable and unlearnable regimes in two-sided dynamic pricing and demonstrates how modest increases in pricing expressiveness can circumvent fundamental hardness barriers.

Introduction

Two-sided online marketplaces have become a major force in the global economy, enabling real-time interactions between distinct user groups such as consumers and merchants (e.g., Amazon Marketplace and eBay), riders and drivers (e.g., Uber), guests and hosts (e.g., Airbnb), or clients and freelancers (e.g., Upwork).
Aggregate evidence underscores the scale of platform-mediated exchange across both goods and services.
In goods e-commerce (e.g., Amazon Marketplace and eBay), a recent report estimates that business e-commerce sales across 43 economies (representing around three quarters of global GDP) reached almost 27 trillion U.S. dollars in 2022
(
46
)
.
In labor and services platforms (e.g., Uber), the gig economy was estimated to have a market size of 557 billion U.S. dollars in 2024 and is expected to grow to 1,847 billion U.S. dollars by 2032
(
47
)
.
This rapidly expanding economic sector raises numerous complex algorithmic challenges.

A central operational decision for these platforms is pricing. In many online marketplaces, the platform posts take-it-or-leave-it prices to both sides. These prices may either coincide—resulting in a single transaction price faced by both buyers and sellers—or differ, allowing the platform to capture profit from the price gap. A well-designed pricing policy is crucial to a platform’s success, as it balances supply and demand: prices that are too high deter buyers, while prices that are too low discourage sellers. By steering participation on both sides, pricing directly shapes matching efficiency and, consequently, key performance objectives such as gains-from-trade (GFT) and profit. In practice, the platform typically does not know traders’ types—i.e., buyers’ values and sellers’ costs—a priori, especially for new users, new products, or under rapidly changing market conditions. As a result, it must infer the underlying willingness-to-pay and willingness-to-accept from observed accept/reject feedback and adapt its posted prices over time. Below, we provide three concrete
motivating applications.

Example 1.1

(Two-sided pricing in online retail marketplaces)
.

In some online retail marketplaces (e.g., Temu, The RealReal, and thredUP), the platform centrally controls pricing on both sides of the market.
Take Temu as an example.
Temu has been pivoting toward a fully managed model with greater control over product selection, pricing, and logistics
(
41
)
; under this model, producers deliver goods to a designated warehouse, while the platform handles downstream operations such as promotion, warehousing, and shipping
(
42
)
.
Crucially, Temu, rather than the producers, sets the consumer-facing posted price for each product and separately determines the producer-side settlement payment to the upstream supplier.
If weak consumer demand is observed at the current retail price (e.g., slow sell-through), Temu can lower the posted price or run promotions to stimulate purchases; if insufficient supply is observed at the current settlement terms, Temu can raise the settlement payment (e.g., by accepting higher supplier bids) to attract more supply and stabilize availability
(
44
)
.
An advantage of this mode is that centralized control allows the platform to coordinate pricing with fulfillment and promotion decisions, enabling faster adjustments to shocks and a more consistent consumer experience.
In this model, the gap between what consumers pay and what producers receive is Temu’s margin, and Temu adaptively adjusts both prices over time.

Example 1.2

(Two-sided pricing in ride-hailing platforms)
.

Ride-hailing platforms (e.g., Uber, Lyft, DiDi, and Grab) provide a prominent example of two-sided differentiated pricing in online service and labor markets. For example, Uber typically shows riders a fixed upfront price before confirmation; this price is computed from estimated time and distance, local traffic, and a dynamic pricing multiplier that reflects current demand conditions. The driver also sees an upfront fare offer, but it is computed under a different pricing rule rather than as a fixed percentage of the rider’s payment; it is based on base fare components, estimated time and distance, pickup distance, and the same dynamic pricing multiplier. The difference between the rider’s payment and the driver’s earnings constitutes Uber’s variable service fee, in addition to taxes, insurance, and other operating expenses. Uber adopts Surge Pricing as its dynamic pricing mechanism to respond to demand–supply imbalances in real time (e.g., during peak hours or bad weather)
(
43
)
.

Example 1.3

(Uniform pricing in wholesale electricity markets)
.

Wholesale electricity day-ahead markets in the U.S. and Europe provide a canonical goods-market example of uniform pricing. These markets mediate trade between two distinct groups: on the supply side, electricity generators submit offers to produce power for delivery in specified future intervals (e.g., hourly blocks on the next day), and on the demand side, load-serving entities (such as utilities and retailers) submit bids to purchase power to serve end customers in those intervals. In each day-ahead auction, the market operator aggregates offers and bids and computes a single market-clearing price for each delivery interval. Under a pay-as-cleared rule, every accepted generator is paid this market-clearing price and every accepted buyer pays the same price, so all executed trades share one common price rather than different prices on the two sides
(
18
;
39
)
.
Prices therefore adjust from interval to interval in response to changes in demand and supply conditions and transmission constraints, even though within any given interval the same clearing price applies to both sides.
These auctions are run by regulated market operators (often not-for-profit ISOs/RTOs in the U.S. and regulated power exchanges/NEMOs in Europe) and are cleared by selecting feasible trades to maximize total surplus subject to network and market constraints.

Motivated by this paradigm, we study an online two-sided market model driven by posted prices. Time is discrete and indexed by rounds. In each round, the platform interacts with a set of sellers and buyers with private types—costs for sellers and values for buyers—and the platform posts prices to both sides.
In the base model, we assume that each trader’s private type remains fixed across rounds (though types may differ across traders). We also consider a contextual extension in which traders’ types depend linearly on observed features that vary from round to round.
Trades occur only among traders who accept their posted prices, subject to feasibility constraints on matching between the two sides.
The platform’s per-round payoff depends on the chosen objective:

•

Under
profit maximization
, the payoff is the total amount collected from matched buyers minus the total payments made to matched sellers.

•

Under
GFT maximization
, the payoff is the total surplus generated by executed trades—i.e., the sum of buyers’ values minus sellers’ costs over all matched pairs.

The platform observes only the binary accept/reject decisions of each trader and uses this feedback to adapt its pricing strategy in future rounds.

To model the platform’s pricing capability, we consider three natural classes of price-posting mechanisms, ordered by increasing expressiveness:

•

Single-Price Mechanisms
, where one price is posted to all traders;

•

Two-Price Mechanisms
, where one price is offered to all sellers and another to all buyers;

•

Segmented-Price Mechanisms
, where sellers and buyers are each partitioned into
up to two groups
, with a distinct price per group.

These mechanisms capture a spectrum of practical pricing policies—from uniform clearing prices (e.g., in electricity markets) to differentiated take-it-or-leave-it offers (e.g., in retail platforms)—and allow us to precisely characterize the interplay between pricing expressiveness and learnability.

In an idealized scenario where the platform knows traders’ private types, the repeated pricing problem reduces to an offline optimization task, and one can compute an optimal fixed price-posting mechanism for the chosen objective. This paper focuses on the more realistic—and challenging—setting in which the platform has no prior knowledge of either side’s types, so learning must occur online. We evaluate performance via
regret minimization
: for GFT, regret is benchmarked against the first-best GFT, which is achievable under full information using
Single-Price Mechanisms
; for profit, it is benchmarked against the optimal profit attainable by any
Two-Price Mechanism
, the simplest mechanism class that yields non-trivial profit. The main goal is to design online pricing policies that adapt based on past accept/reject feedback and achieve vanishing regret.

1.1.1
Bilateral Trade Model

We first study the online bilateral trade problem, which is a special case of the online two-sided market problem where there is only one seller and one buyer. In this setting, traders’ private type (i.e., seller’s cost and buyer’s value) remains constant across rounds. This problem generalizes the classic dynamic pricing problem
(
28
)
when the seller’s cost is fixed at
0
and the platform pursues profit maximization. Recently,
21
investigated a contextual learning version of bilateral trade for GFT maximization, proposing an algorithm with
O(d^{2}\log T)
regret.

Gains-from-trade maximization.

The approach of
21
maintains two separate uncertainty sets for the seller’s cost
c
and the buyer’s value
v
, utilizing ellipsoid search
(
15
)
on these two sets independently to shrink these sets until they are both sufficiently small. In contrast, we propose a different methodology by
reinterpreting the learning target
. Since any price within the interval
[c,v]
ensures optimal GFT (and thus zero regret), it is sufficient to locate any feasible price rather than the exact cost
c
and value
v
.

By applying a single binary search process on the union of these two uncertainty sets rather than independently shrink two uncertainty sets, we develop an algorithm achieving an
O(1)
regret bound (
Theorem

3.1
).
Furthermore, by integrating this approach with intrinsic volume techniques
(
30
;
31
)
, we achieve an
O(d\log d)
regret bound in the contextual setting (
Theorem

5.3
), which notably remains independent of the time horizon
T
.
This improves upon the
O(d^{2}\log T)
result in
21
.

Profit maximization.

While profit maximization may appear similar to dynamic pricing, naively treating bilateral trade as two independent pricing problems often violates the
weak budget balance
constraint, which requires the seller’s price to be no greater than the buyer’s price to ensure non-negative per-round profit.
To address this, we first identify a threshold price in
[c,v]
that effectively separates the buyer’s and seller’s uncertainty sets. This separation enables independent pricing on two disjoint intervals while guaranteeing that the budget balance constraint is never violated.
Our approach can be viewed as a two-phase algorithm: an initial exploration phase inspired by our GFT maximization algorithm to locate a separating price, followed by independent conservative pricing on each side.
Overall, we design an algorithm that achieves an
O(\log\log T)
regret bound (
Theorem

3.2
), matching the optimal double-logarithmic regret established for dynamic pricing
(
28
)
. Building on this idea, we further generalize both the algorithm and its regret guarantee to contextual two-sided markets (see discussion below).

1.1.2
GFT Maximization in Two-Sided Market Model

We now turn to the problem of maximizing gains-from-trade (GFT) in two-sided market models.

Mismatch phenomenon.

GFT objective in general two-sided markets exhibits structural differences from the classical bilateral trade setting. In bilateral trade, where transactions are restricted to a single buyer and a single seller, the optimal mechanism is relatively well understood. By contrast, in many-to-many two-sided markets (i.e., multiple buyers and multiple sellers), the platform must solve a nontrivial matching problem: a high-value buyer may be matched with a high-cost seller instead of a low-cost one (or vice versa), even when mutually beneficial trades with better partners exist. We refer to such inefficient pairings as mismatches, which induce substantial regret.

Analysis of one-to-many markets.

Notably, this mismatch phenomenon persists even in
one-to-many markets
, such as those with one seller and multiple buyers, which serve as a special case of the two-sided framework. Intuitively, the potential for mismatches
precludes the achievement of constant regret in two-sided markets.

We commence our analysis with the one-to-many setting, where we identify a GFT regret structure analogous to dynamic pricing (for revenue maximizing): consider a single-seller, multiple-buyer scenario. Any price situated between the highest value and
\max\{\text{2nd-highest value},\allowbreak\text{seller cost}\}
constitutes a GFT-optimal price. The regret dynamics are twofold: under overpricing (no trade), regret is upper bounded by a constant; under underpricing (trade occurs), regret is upper bounded by the size of the uncertainty set. The critical distinction from standard dynamic pricing lies in the underpricing case: in a one-to-many market, regret corresponds to the value gap of all accepted buyers, whereas in dynamic pricing, it is determined solely by the difference between the buyer’s value and the posted price. This structure renders the problem more tractable, allowing us to establish an
O(\log\log T)
regret upper bound (
Theorem

4.4
). However, establishing the lower bound requires novel techniques to rigorously control the shrinkage of the uncertainty set. We prove a lower bound of
\Omega(\frac{\log\log T}{\log\log\log\log T})
in
Theorem

4.1
using an one-seller two-buyer instance, which nearly matches the
\Omega(\log\log T)
bound established for pricing by
28
.

Impossibility in general two-sided markets.

Interestingly, the landscape shifts dramatically when extending the analysis to general two-sided markets with multiple buyers and sellers. In particular, we show in
Theorem

4.6
that
sublinear regret is unattainable under
Single-Price Mechanisms
or even
Two-Price Mechanisms
.
Remarkably, this hardness stems not from computational or incentive constraints, but from the
intrinsic difficulty of learning
: when all traders’ types are known, the first-best GFT benchmark can be achieved by a
Single-Price Mechanism
.
The fundamental obstacle is again the
mismatch phenomenon
. By constructing the adversary in
using a three-seller three-buyer instance, we show that any online algorithm implementing
Two-Price Mechanisms
inevitably suffers inefficient pairings, incurring constant regret per round and thus
\Omega(T)
cumulative regret.

Segmented-price mechanism.

To circumvent this fundamental limitation, we study a more expressive posted-price mechanism: the
Segmented-Price Mechanism
. This mechanism partitions each side into up to two disjoint subgroups and assigns a distinct price to each group, resulting in at most
2\times 2=4
prices. This increased granularity enables precise control over mismatched trades.
By strategically constructing the price profile, we ensure that every mismatching round yields quantifiable informational progress: it either identifies a trader belonging to the optimal matching of the first-best GFT benchmark or substantially shrinks the corresponding uncertainty set. This property underpins our analysis via a
multi-dimensional potential function
. Specifically, by tracking both the number of identified optimal traders and the size of the uncertainty sets, we tightly bound the number of mismatching rounds.

Intuitively, this two-group segmented structure allows the algorithm to decouple the exploitation of “safe” trades involving known traders from the exploration of uncertain ones, thereby effectively limiting regret accumulation. Consequently, our algorithm achieves an
O(n^{2}\log\log T+n^{3})
regret bound for general two-sided markets (
Theorem

4.7
).

Finally, we extend our framework to the contextual setting. By integrating the
Segmented-Price Mechanisms
with ellipsoid-based search techniques, we establish a regret bound of
O(n^{2}d^{2}\log T)
(
Theorem

5.7
).

1.1.3
Profit Maximization in Two-Sided Market Model

Building on insights from bilateral trade, we extend profit maximization to general two-sided markets with multiple buyers and multiple sellers. This general setting introduces two new challenges. First, both the optimal trade size and the identities of the most efficient traders are unknown. Second, independent pricing generates many candidate prices, yet
Two-Price Mechanisms
are restricted to selecting only two—one for all sellers and another for all buyers. To overcome these challenges, we develop the following two algorithmic ideas.

Optimistic fictitious markets.

We resolve the above challenges by constructing an
optimistic fictitious market
. Our algorithm will maintain an uncertainty set for each trader’s cost or value.
In each round, the algorithm assigns the highest possible value to each buyer and the lowest possible cost to each seller based on their current uncertainty sets. This construction provides a monotone upper bound on the optimal profit, allowing the platform to identify a target trade size
k^{\star}
and a corresponding set of fictitious participating traders.
Technically, the optimal profit of this fictitious market will be a proxy to the profit of the true market. Our goal shifts to exploiting profit from these fictitious participating traders. At a high level, we will use the dynamic pricing algorithm to obtain some independent conservative prices for each fictitious participating trader. Among these prices, the most conservative price pair plays an important role.
A key finding in our design is, the regret per round can be connected with the uncertainty set of the trader pair which has the most conservative price. This finding enables us to select two from many different candidate prices and employ a potential argument to bound the cumulative regret.

Switching between
Single-Price Mechanism
and
Two-Price Mechanism
.

To maintain the weak budget balance constraint, recall that we use a two-phase separate-then-independently-pricing approach in the bilateral trade model. However, separating becomes vague in the multi-trader scenario, and the identity of those two representative traders changes over rounds. Our algorithm is no longer a two-phase algorithm, but rather an algorithm that constantly switches between these two phases.

Overall, we propose an algorithm that achieves a cumulative regret of
O(n^{2}\log\log T)
(
Theorem

4.15
), matching the optimal double-logarithmic regret bound for the bilateral trade model and dynamic pricing problem, in terms of
T
. We further combine our idea with the intrinsic volume techniques to obtain an
O(dn^{2}\log\log T+n^{2}d\log d)
regret algorithm for the contextual learning setting (
Theorem

5.14
).

Preliminary

We consider an online two-sided market with
m

sellers
and
n

buyers
that interact through a principal (or learner) over
T
rounds.

Environment.

The set of sellers is denoted by
\mathcal{S}
, with
\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert=m
, and the set of buyers is denoted by
\mathcal{B}
, with
\left\lvert\mathcal{B}\right\rvert=n
. Let
\mathcal{N}\triangleq\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{B}
be the set of all traders (or agents). Each seller
i\in\mathcal{S}
can produce at most one unit of a homogeneous product and has a fixed private
cost

c_{i}\in[0,1]
for producing that unit, while each buyer
j\in\mathcal{B}
demands at most one unit and has a fixed private
value

v_{j}\in[0,1]
for receiving it. These costs and values are traders’ private information, which are unknown to the learner, and remain constant throughout the
T
rounds.

Price-posting mechanisms.

In each round
t\in[T]
, the learner adopts a mechanism to post prices to all traders.
Given these prices, each trader returns a binary decision:
accept
or
reject
.
A seller (resp. buyer) accepts if and only if the price he faces is at least his cost (resp. at most his value), and rejects otherwise.
†
†

When a trader is indifferent between accepting and rejecting, we assume ties are broken in favor of the mechanism.

Among all possible price-posting mechanisms, we focus on the following three subclasses, ordered by increasing expressive power:

•

Single-Price Mechanism
: A single price
p
is posted uniformly to all traders. Each trader then decides whether to accept or reject based on this common price
p
.

•

Two-Price Mechanism
: A seller-side price
p
is offered to all sellers, and another (possibly different) buyer-side price
q
is offered to all buyers. Every trader then decides whether to accept or reject based on their corresponding prices
p
and
q
.

•

Segmented-Price Mechanism
: The set of all sellers
\mathcal{S}
(resp. buyers
\mathcal{B}
) are partitioned into at most two disjoint segments
(\mathcal{S}^{(1)},\mathcal{S}^{(2)})
(resp.
(\mathcal{B}^{(1)},\mathcal{B}^{(2)})
). Each seller and buyer segment is offered with a (possibly different) price, denoted by
p^{(1)}
,
p^{(2)}
,
q^{(1)}
,
q^{(2)}
, respectively. Traders then decide whether to accept or reject based on the price assigned to their respective segment.

Upon the accept/reject decisions of all traders, a maximum bipartite matching is formed between the buyers and sellers who accepted their posted prices. A unit of the product is transferred between each matched buyer-seller pair, with payments charged and paid according to their respective prices. All other traders receive zero allocation and zero payment. Note that, regardless of which unweighted maximum matching is selected,

all three price-posting mechanisms following this allocation and payment rule are

and
ex post individually rational

In addition, we impose a
weak budget balance
constraint, requiring that the price offered to every seller be weakly less than the price offered to every buyer.

As mentioned earlier, the principal (learner) does not know the traders’ costs and values and interacts with them over
T
rounds. We study different settings for the principal, depending on the class of price-posting mechanisms used (namely,
Single-Price Mechanism
,
Two-Price Mechanism
, or
Segmented-Price Mechanism
). In each setting, for every round
t\in[T]
, the principal selects prices within the corresponding mechanism class and offers them to all traders. She then observes the accept/reject decisions from all traders, as well as the resulting trading pairs (i.e., the allocation-payment outcome).

Principal’s objective: GFT and profit.

We consider the two most fundamental economic objectives in two-sided markets for the principal: the
gains-from-trade
(
GFT
) and the
profit
.

We begin by introducing the gains-from-trade (GFT), which depends only on the allocation outcome (i.e., the matching between buyers and sellers). Specifically, given any feasible matching
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{B}
, the induced GFT is defined as the difference between the total values of all matched buyers and the total costs of all matched sellers, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textsc{Gft}[\mathcal{M}]\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{M}}\left(v_{j}-c_{i}\right).

We define the
optimum GFT benchmark

\textsc{Gft}^{\star}
as the highest GFT that can be achieved among all feasible matchings between sellers and buyers. By definition, it can be computed as

\displaystyle\textsc{Gft}^{\star}=\sum\nolimits_{\ell\in[k]}\left(v_{(\ell)}-c_{(\ell)}\right),

where
v_{(\ell)}
(resp.
c_{(\ell)}
) denotes the
\ell
-th largest (resp.
\ell
-th smallest) order statistic among all buyers’ values (resp. sellers’ costs), and the
efficient trade size

k\in[\min\{n,m\}]
is defined as the largest index such that the buyer’s order statistic is no less than the seller’s order statistic, i.e.,
v_{(k)}\geq c_{(k)}
.
†
†

We remark that the optimum GFT benchmark
\textsc{Gft}^{\star}
can be achieved by a
Single-Price Mechanism
.

Profit depends on both the allocation outcome (i.e., the matching between buyers and sellers) and the payment outcome (i.e., the prices charged to and paid to all matched traders). For the profit objective, we only focus on the class of
Two-Price Mechanisms
. Specifically, given any
Two-Price Mechanism
with seller-side price
p
and buyer-side price
q
, the induced profit is defined as the difference between the total prices charged to all matched buyers and the total prices paid to all matched sellers,
†
†

Note that in any
Two-Price Mechanism
, every unweighted maximum matching induces the same profit.
i.e.,

\displaystyle\textsc{Profit}[p,q]=\underbrace{\min\left\{|\{i\in\mathcal{S}:c_{i}\leq p\}|,|\{j\in\mathcal{B}:v_{j}\geq q\}|\right\}}_{\text{number of trading pairs}}\underbrace{\cdot\left(q-p\right)}_{\text{profit from each trading pair}}

We define the
optimum profit benchmark

\textsc{Profit}^{\star}
(within the class of
Two-Price Mechanisms
) as the highest profit achievable by any
Two-Price Mechanisms
. By definition, it can be computed as

\displaystyle\textsc{Profit}^{\star}\triangleq\max\nolimits_{k\in[\min\{n,m\}]}k\cdot(v_{(k)}-c_{(k)}).

Namely, it is achieved by selecting the best index
k\in[\min\{n,m\}]
(which we refer to as the
profit-maximizing trade size
) and setting the seller-side price to
p=c_{(k)}
and the buyer-side price to
q=v_{(k)}
.

Regret minimization.

We evaluate an online learning algorithm by its
regret
relative to the aforementioned optimum GFT (resp. profit) benchmark
\textsc{Gft}^{\star}
(resp.
\textsc{Profit}^{\star}
).

For the GFT objective, given any online learning algorithm
ALG
, its immediate regret in each round
t\in[T]
and its cumulative regret over all
T
rounds are defined as
†
†

Throughout the paper, we focus on deterministic online learning algorithms.

\displaystyle\textsc{Regret}_{t}[\texttt{ALG}]\triangleq\textsc{Gft}^{\star}-\textsc{Gft}[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\texttt{ALG}}]\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\textsc{Regret}[\texttt{ALG}]\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t\in[T]}\textsc{Regret}_{t}[\texttt{ALG}],

where
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\texttt{ALG}}
is the unweighted maximum matching between all buyers and sellers who accepted their prices in round
t
under algorithm
ALG
, chosen to minimize the induced GFT.

Similarly, for the profit objective, given any online learning algorithm
ALG
(that implements
Two-Price Mechanisms
), its immediate regret in each round
t\in[T]
and its cumulative regret over all
T
rounds are defined as

\displaystyle\textsc{Regret}_{t}[\texttt{ALG}]\triangleq\textsc{Profit}^{\star}-\textsc{Profit}[p_{t}^{\texttt{ALG}},q_{t}^{\texttt{ALG}}]\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\textsc{Regret}[\texttt{ALG}]\triangleq\sum\nolimits_{t\in[T]}\textsc{Regret}_{t}[\texttt{ALG}],

where
p_{t}^{\texttt{ALG}}
and
q_{t}^{\texttt{ALG}}
are the seller-side and buyer-side prices in the
Two-Price Mechanism
implemented in round
t\in[T]
under algorithm
ALG
.

When the online algorithm
ALG
is clear from the context, we sometimes simplify notations and omit them, e.g., writing
Regret
instead of
\textsc{Regret}[\texttt{ALG}]
. This includes the notations
Regret
,
\textsc{Regret}_{t}
,
\mathcal{M}_{t}
,
q_{t}
and
p_{t}
, among others.

Variants.

Besides the base model, we also consider the following three closely related variants.

•

(Special case: Bilateral trade)
This is the classic setting with a single seller and a single buyer. As a sanity check, the optimum profit benchmark and optimum GFT benchmark in the regret definition coincide, and
Segmented-Price Mechanism
collapses to
Two-Price Mechanism
.

•

(Special case: One-to-many market)
This setting consists of either a single seller and multiple buyers or a single buyer and multiple sellers. As a sanity check, the optimum profit benchmark and optimum GFT benchmark in the regret definition coincide, and
Segmented-Price Mechanism
collapses to
Two-Price Mechanism
.

•

(Extension: Products with contextual information)
In this extension, each seller
i\in\mathcal{S}
(resp. buyer
j\in\mathcal{B}
) is associated with a private
d
-dimensional feature vector

\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
(resp.
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
) in the unit ball
\mathbb{B}_{d}
. The principal does not know the traders’ feature vectors. In each round
t\in[T]
, an adversarially chosen
product context

x_{t}\in\mathbb{B}_{d}
is revealed to the principal, and each trader’s cost (for sellers) or value (for buyers) is given by the inner product of their private feature vector and the product context, i.e.,
c_{i,t}\triangleq\langle\theta_{i},x_{t}\rangle
and
v_{j,t}\triangleq\langle\beta_{j},x_{t}\rangle
. Both the optimum profit and GFT benchmarks are defined based on round-dependent profiles
\{(c_{1,t},\dots,c_{m,t},v_{1,t},\dots,v_{n,t})\}_{t\in[T]}
.

4.1.1
Regret Hardness in One-to-Many Markets with
Single-Price Mechanisms

We begin by proving an
\Omega(\frac{\log\log T}{\log\log\log\log T})
lower bound on regret for algorithms implementing
Single-Price Mechanism
in general two-sided markets.

Our construction uses the minimal extension beyond bilateral trade: a one-seller, two-buyer market with zero seller cost and adversarially chosen buyer values
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
The zero-cost seller accepts every posted price, and the two buyers can generate three possible feedback patterns: both accept, exactly one accepts, or both reject.
Assume without loss of generality that
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
. The optimal GFT is
v_{2}
, achieved by posting any price in
(v_{1},v_{2}]
.
If exactly one buyer accepts, the algorithm has identified an optimal price in
(v_{1},v_{2}]
and incurs zero regret thereafter.
If both buyers accept, the trade occurs between the seller and the lower-value buyer under our adversarial matching rule, resulting in immediate regret
v_{2}-v_{1}
.
If both buyers reject, no trade occurs, and the immediate regret is
v_{2}
.

Given the single-seller, two-buyer market setup above, it remains to specify how the adversary chooses buyer values and feedback in response to any online algorithm.
Our adversary construction builds on the phase-based hard instance of
28
for the (zero-cost-seller single-buyer profit-maximizing) pricing problem, but is necessarily more involved to account for the differences in our setting.

Intuitively, GFT maximization appears easier than the pricing problem in two respects. First, any price in

4.1.4
No-Regret Algorithm in Two-Sided Markets with
Segmented-Price Mechanisms

As we have discussed in
Section

4.1.3
, any algorithm implementing
Two-Price Mechanism
fails to achieve sublinear regret. A key reason is that one can not avoid the mismatches of traders unless the algorithm posts the prices exactly in the interval between

and
v_{3}

This section introduces a more powerful

that bypasses the lower bound. Using the construction in
Section

as an example, if the gap between
c_{3}
and
v_{3}
is sufficiently small, specifically

O(\log T)
rounds of binary search to identify an uncertainty interval of length at most

containing a seller with cost
c_{2}=c_{3}
and a buyer with value
v_{3}
. Subsequently, in each round where the algorithm ceases exploration of new information, it can implement

by posting a seller price at the left endpoint of the uncertainty interval for two sellers: one with
c_{1}=0
and another chosen arbitrarily. This is feasible because only one seller remains outside the uncertainty interval, enabling its identification. Regarding the buyers, the algorithm similarly posts the buyer price at the right endpoint of the uncertainty interval for buyers with values
v_{1}=v_{2}=1

and the highest buyer price to

O(1)
. This approach suffices to bypass the lower bound in
Section

4.1.3
and achieve an

regret algorithm utilizing
Segmented-Price Mechanism
.

In fact,

exhibits even greater utility. Through a more sophisticated construction, we can ensure that each mismatching round either identifies at least one additional trader in the optimal matching or significantly reduces the uncertainty interval. This allows for a potential argument to bound the number of mismatching rounds, using either the number of identified optimal traders or the length of the uncertainty interval as the potential. Intuitively, this is because segmented pricing permits trading with “known” traders while simultaneously exploring new ones, which helps bound the number of mismatching rounds to a very low order.

Algorithm overview.

In each round,
Balance Two-Segmented Pricing
maintains an uncertainty interval
\Phi_{t}=[\underaccent{\bar}{\phi}_{t},\bar{\phi}_{t}]
, a seller set
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
recording the sellers who accept the price
\underaccent{\bar}{\phi}_{t}
, and a buyer set
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
recording the buyers who accept the price
\bar{\phi}_{t}
.
We also maintain a buyer subset
\mathcal{B}_{t}
and a seller subset
\mathcal{S}_{t}
. We aim to ensure
\mathcal{B}_{t}
is composed of all buyers whose values are larger than
\underaccent{\bar}{\phi}_{t}
and
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is composed of all sellers whose costs are smaller than
\bar{\phi}_{t}
.

†
†

In fact, we will see that our algorithm can only satisfy this condition for some special rounds (

As a sanity check, the following relation holds between these subsets:
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{t}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.

At a high level, we will ensure that
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
are traders belonging to the optimal matching, our goal is to identify all such traders. Whenever
\lvert\Phi_{t}\rvert\geq 1/T
, our algorithm tries to find new traders in the optimal matching, increasing either
\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert
or
\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert
. To facilitate this search, the algorithm invokes two symmetric subroutines

Buyer-Side Balancing Search
(
) and
Seller-Side Balancing Search
(
), depending on the comparative cardinality of
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
.

We now focus on the case
\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert\geq\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert
where our goal is to find new buyers, as the other case is symmetric. In this case, we post a conservative price (e.g.,
\underaccent{\bar}{\phi}_{t}
) to exactly
\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert
buyers to match with sellers in
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
. Simultaneously, we offer an exploration price (e.g.,
\bar{\phi}_{t}-2^{-2^{k_{t}}}
) to the remaining buyers to see if there are buyers of value in the interval
[\bar{\phi}_{t}-2^{-2^{k_{t}}},\bar{\phi}_{t}]
. Ideally, we should post the exploration price to all buyers who are in
\mathcal{B}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
to maximize the exploration effect.
However, this means we can only post the conservative price to buyers in
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
and many sellers in
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
may not join the trade, which would lead to a large immediate regret.
Therefore,
Buyer-Side Balancing Search
ensures that all sellers in
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
can be matched by posting the conservative price to additional
\left(\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert\right)
buyers in
\mathcal{B}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
so that we have a total number of
\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert
buyers who are offered with the conservative price, guaranteeing that all sellers in
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}
can join into trade. For the remaining buyers, we offer the exploration price to them. This way, we encounter another problem, that is, we can not cover each possible buyer during our search process since there are some buyers who are offered a conservative price.
To address this issue, we simply repeat the above process for
\left\lceil\frac{\lvert\mathcal{B}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{B}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert-(\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert)}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{\lvert\mathcal{B}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}\rvert}{\lvert\mathcal{B}_{t}\rvert-\lvert\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{s}}_{t}\rvert}\right\rceil

each round we select different buyers to offer the conservative price, ensuring that all buyers in
\mathcal{B}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{b}}_{t}
are explored at least once.

After all these consecutive rounds, the algorithm obtains a buyer subset

, which contains all the buyers whose values are in
[\bar{\phi}_{t}-2^{-2^{k_{t}}},\bar{\phi}_{t}]

This section studies the profit maximization in a two-sided market.
Our main result is an algorithm implementing the

which achieves

This result is the no-context special case (or fixed-context case) of the more general contextual two-sided market studied in

Next, we provide a high-level sketch of the algorithm, deferring the detailed algorithmic description and rigorous analysis to

5.3
.
Without loss of generality, we assume the number of buyers and sellers is equal, i.e.,
n=m
, since we can always add dummy buyers with value
0
or dummy sellers with cost
1
to balance the market.

Algorithm overview.

Our algorithm maintains an uncertainty interval for the cost or value of each trader. For buyer
j\in\mathcal{B}
(resp. seller
i\in\mathcal{S}
), the uncertainty interval at round
t
is denoted by
[\underaccent{\bar}{\buyerValue}_{j,t},\bar{v}_{j,t}]
(resp.
[\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{i,t},\bar{c}_{i,t}]
). All uncertainty intervals are initialized to
[0,1]
at the beginning of the algorithm.
The algorithm implements the
Two-Price Mechanism
as introduced in
Section

3.2
.

At each round
t
, our algorithm considers the following optimistic fictitious market: for each buyer
j\in\mathcal{B}
, its value is set to be
\bar{v}_{j,t}
; for each seller
i\in\mathcal{S}
, its cost is set to be
\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{i,t}
. We denote the optimal profit in this fictitious market as
\smash{\overline{\textsc{Profit}}}_{t}^{\star}\triangleq\max\nolimits_{k\in[n]}k\cdot(\bar{v}_{(k),t}-\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{(k),t})
,
where
\bar{v}_{(k),t}
is the
k
-th largest value in
\{\bar{v}_{j,t}\}_{j\in\mathcal{B}}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{(k),t}
is the
k
-th smallest cost in
\{\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{i,t}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}}
. The following lemma establishes that the optimal profit is monotone with respect to the traders’ types and costs.

Lemma 4.14
.

As a function of trades’ values and costs, the optimal profit
\textsc{Profit}^{\star}[\{c_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{S}},\{v_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{B}}]

is non-decreasing in each buyer’s value and non-increasing in each seller’s cost.

Proof.

The first part of the lemma follows directly from the definition of optimal profit.
Consider two markets that differ only in the value of a single buyer
w
.
Let the value of buyer
w
be
v_{w}
in the first market and

A straightforward approach to extend

15
and the intrinsic volume (Steiner polynomial) based technique by

We adopt the Steiner polynomial-based technique to address this challenge. The key idea is to select a fixed price

that bisects the volume of a padded version of the hypothesis set, rather than the original set. The padding scale is adapted to the width of the hypothesis set along the context direction. Specifically, we define a series of padding parameters

for all integers
\ell\geq 0
. At round

be the largest integer satisfying
0pt{\Phi_{t}}{x_{t}}\leq 2^{-\ell_{t}}

The algorithm then sets the fixed price

to be the median of the padded hypothesis set

5.2
Gains-from-Trade Maximization in Contextual Two-sided Market Model

The core strategy of our algorithm in this section is to first explore the uncertainty sets of all traders to a sufficient precision, and then exploit the learned knowledge to deploy a
Segmented-Price Mechanism
for GFT maximization.

While exploring uncertainty sets is straightforward in the non-contextual setting, where prices can directly bisect the uncertainty intervals, it becomes non-trivial in the contextual setting. The difficulty arises because posted prices are coupled with varying context vectors. Consequently, reducing the uncertainty width along one direction does not necessarily reduce it along others.
To address this challenge, we utilize the power of the
Ellipsoid Method

(
15
)
.

Ellipsoid search.

An ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}\left(A,c\right)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with shape matrix
A
is defined as:

\mathcal{E}\left(A,c\right)\triangleq\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\mid\,(\theta-c)^{\top}A^{-1}(\theta-c)\leq 1\right\}.

(3)

The eigenvalues of
A
correspond to the squares of the lengths of the semi-axes of the ellipsoid.

In the ellipsoid search algorithm, we initialize with
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathbb{B}_{d}
and aim to locate an unknown vector in
\theta\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
. At each iteration
t
, the algorithm receives a direction vector
x_{t}
and selects a value
y_{t}
to query whether
\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle\geq y
.
The ellipsoid method employs the following strategy: it first computes the width of the current ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{t}
along the direction
x_{t}
. If the width exceeds a pre-determined threshold
\delta
, the algorithm queries
y_{t}=\left(\min_{\theta\in\mathcal{E}_{t}}\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle+\max_{\theta\in\mathcal{E}_{t}}\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle\right)/2
and observes the feedback
\sigma_{t}={\mathbbm{1}\left\{\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle\geq y_{t}\right\}}
.
Then, instead of directly cutting the ellipsoid using the half-space defined by the query, the algorithm updates the ellipsoid to its
Löwner-John ellipsoid
defined as the minimum volume ellipsoid containing the intersection of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
with the valid half-space:
\left\{\theta\,\mid\,\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle\geq y_{t}\right\}
if
\sigma_{t}=1
, and
\left\{\theta\,\mid\,\langle\theta,x_{t}\rangle\leq y_{t}\right\}
if
\sigma_{t}=0
. The new ellipsoid is denoted as
\mathcal{E}_{t+1}
.
Conversely, if the width is smaller than
\delta
, the algorithm skips the query and maintains the current ellipsoid, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{t+1}=\mathcal{E}_{t}
.

We refer to the iterations where the algorithm performs a query and updates the ellipsoid as
exploration steps
.
The follow theorem provides an upper bound on the number of exploration steps regardless of the sequence of direction vectors
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
.

the context vectors revealed in each round as query directions.

Lemma 5.5

(Lipschitz Continuity of GFT)
.

For any seller cost vectors

(5)

Proof.

i

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\textsc{Gft}^{\star}(u,v)-\textsc{Gft}^{\star}(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\right\rvert

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lvert\max\left\{\textsc{Ord}_{j}(v)+\textsc{Ord}_{j}(-u),0\right\}-\max\left\{\textsc{Ord}_{j}(v^{\prime})+\textsc{Ord}_{j}(-u^{\prime}),0\right\}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{\leq}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{\infty}+\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\right)

Here, (a) follows from the triangle inequality; (b) uses the

p_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}\leq\bar{c}_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}
and
q_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}\geq\underaccent{\bar}{\buyerValue}_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}
.
Thus,
\textsc{Profit}_{t}\geq k^{\star}\cdot(\underaccent{\bar}{\buyerValue}_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}-\bar{c}_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}

Then, we have

\displaystyle\textsc{Regret}_{t}

\displaystyle=\textsc{Profit}^{\star}_{t}-\textsc{Profit}_{t}\leq\smash{\overline{\textsc{Profit}}}_{t}^{\star}-\textsc{Profit}_{t}

\displaystyle\leq k^{\star}\cdot\left(\bar{v}_{(k^{\star}),t}-\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{(k^{\star}),t}\right)-k^{\star}\cdot\left(\underaccent{\bar}{\buyerValue}_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}-\bar{c}_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}k^{\star}\cdot\left(\bar{v}_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}-\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerValue}_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}\right)-k^{\star}\cdot\left(\underaccent{\bar}{\buyerValue}_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}-\bar{c}_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}\right)

\displaystyle\leq k^{\star}\cdot\left(0pt{\Phi_{i^{\prime}_{t},t}}{x_{t}}+0pt{\Psi_{j^{\prime}_{t},t}}{x_{t}}\right).

Here, inequality (a) holds because
i^{\prime}_{t}\in\underaccent{\bar}{\sellerSet}_{t}
and
j^{\prime}_{t}\in\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{t}
.
∎
