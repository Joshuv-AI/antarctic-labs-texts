Severe hypercapnia and hemodynamic instability following a sudden elevation in end-tidal CO₂ during thoraco-laparoscopic esophagectomy: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yan Xu, Hong Deng, Jie Zhu, Chunling Jiang, Xiaoqiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEstablishing thoracoscopic pneumothorax in patients with extensive pleural adhesions often increases the risk of trocar-related lung injury, potentially causing abrupt extreme elevations in end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) and precipitating life-threatening hemodynamic instability. Given the rarity of such events and complication in recovery, clearer recommendations for early recognition are needed to inform subsequent perioperative management.Case presentationA 61-year-old male undergoing elective thoraco-laparoscopic esophagectomy for esophageal cancer who had an abrupt elevation in PETCO₂ of 213 mm Hg, accompanied by severe hemodynamic instability—including hypertensive crisis, tachycardia, and subsequent progressive bradycardia during the establishment of a thoracoscopic pneumothorax. Surgical exploration revealed extensive pleural adhesions and a trocar-related injury of the right lower lung, which allowed a large volume of CO₂ to enter the alveoli and airways, resulting in a dramatic rise in PETCO₂. The excessive CO₂ rapidly diffused into the pulmonary circulation, causing a significant increase in PaCO₂ and precipitating severe hypercapnia with acute hemodynamic instability. Following surgical intervention to the injured lung and after showing visible ventilation improvement, and hemodynamic stabilization, the procedure was successfully completed without any complications during recovery.ConclusionsPatients with extensive pleural adhesions are at increased risk of trocar-related lung injury during the establishment of CO₂ pneumothorax. Continuous intraoperative ETCO₂ monitoring can facilitate early detection with cases of this type. Prompt recognition, effective hemodynamic management, and appropriate use of ventilatory support are essential to prevent potentially life-threatening complications.

Introduction

Thoraco-laparoscopic esophagectomy is becoming increasingly popular for the treatment of esophageal cancer worldwide [1]. During thoracic or abdominal procedures, CO₂ insufflation is routinely used to improve surgical exposure [2]. Although generally safe, unintended systemic absorption or abnormal entry of CO₂ into the respiratory system may still lead to gas-related complications, sometimes progressing to severe hemodynamic instability [3, 4]. End-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO₂) serves as a critical real-time indicator of a patient’s ventilatory status and CO₂ elimination, allowing for early detection of abnormal CO₂ loads during surgery [5, 6]. Sudden elevations in PETCO₂ may reflect potentially life-threatening conditions and require prompt recognition and intervention. Here, we present a rare case in which trocar-related lung injury in the setting of extensive pleural adhesions caused a massive CO₂ influx, resulting in severe hypercapnia and hemodynamic instability. By analyzing this case in the context of existing literature, we aim to underscore key considerations for the recognition and management of such critical perioperative events.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old man, weighing 60 kg and 180 cm in height, was admitted for thoraco-laparoscopic esophagectomy following a diagnosis of esophageal cancer. His medical history was notable for allergic asthma, hypertension, and hypothyroidism, all well-controlled with standard treatment. Physical examination showed no significant abnormalities. Preoperative cervicothoracic and abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed irregular thickening of the mid-esophageal wall, along with bilateral pulmonary emphysema and scattered bullae. Blood tests, electrocardiogram, transthoracic echocardiography (EF 66%), pulmonary function testing, and airway evaluation showed no evident abnormalities. The patient was classified as ASA II, according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification system.

Upon admission to the operating room, standard monitoring was used. The patient subsequently underwent intravenous induction with midazolam, propofol, sufentanil, and vecuronium, followed by an uneventful endotracheal intubation with a 7.5-mm reinforced tube. A left radial arterial catheter was placed for continuous arterial blood pressure (ABP) monitoring and blood gas monitoring. The patient was then positioned in a left-lateral decubitus position. At the beginning of surgery (commencing at 09:10 am), all vital signs were stable with a sinus rhythm of 85 bpm, ABP of 141/65 mm Hg, respiratory rate of 14 breaths/min, PETCO₂ of 47 mm Hg, with a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg, respiratory rate 13 breaths/min, and peak airway pressure of 23 cm H₂O. A few minutes after the use of CO₂ for the creation of pneumoperitoneum, PETCO₂ gradually increased to 60 mm Hg. During this period, the heart rate remained between 85 and 90 bpm, ABP ranged from 120 to 140 and 60 to 70 mmHg. Airway pressure showed no significant change, and the breathing circuit remained patent. Soda lime was observed to be approximately 50% exhausted and was replaced. Within one to two minutes after replacing the soda lime, PETCO₂ suddenly rose sharply, peaking at 213 mm Hg. Immediate communication with the surgical team revealed that the patient had extensive pleural adhesions, requiring repeated trocar adjustments during pneumothorax creation. A newly identified right lower-lobe lung laceration—suspected to be trocar-related—had just been repaired. The surgeon explained that CO₂ could have been insufflated directly into the airway during exhalation through the defect before repair. The surgery was suspended, CO₂ insufflation was stopped, ventilator settings were adjusted, and intermittent manual ventilation was applied to facilitate CO₂ elimination. During this period, the patient developed marked hemodynamic instability: ABP surged from 166/67 mmHg to 260/189 mmHg, heart rate initially increased from 86 bpm to 106 bpm and subsequently declined progressively to a nadir of 27 bpm, accompanied by an idioventricular rhythm (absence of P waves) on electrocardiogram. Bispectral index (BIS) decreased from 32 to 3, and PETCO₂ gradually decreased from 213 mmHg to 166 mmHg (Fig. 1).

Targeted interventions were immediately administered, including intermittent intravenous nicardipine for hypertensive control, intravenous atropine for progressive bradycardia, optimization of anesthetic depth, and ventilatory adjustments to enhance CO₂ clearance. Within approximately 1 min, the ABP decreased to 160/92 mmHg, HR increased to 102 bpm, PETCO₂ decreased to 83 mmHg, SpO₂ remained at 100%, and BIS improved to 17. Repeat ABG values showed significant improvement (Table 1). The procedure was completed uneventfully with a total duration of 6 h. Discussion

Hypercapnia during thoracoscopic surgery is generally caused by pleural CO₂ absorption or inadequate ventilation [7]. Systemic absorption of carbon dioxide during this procedure has not been extensively studied but is generally not reported to cause severe hypercarbia [8]. Several prior case reports have described hypercapnia during thoracoscopic or laparoscopic procedures, with varying mechanisms including direct airway entry via lung injury, or CO₂ embolism (Table 2). These cases highlight differences in ETCO₂/PaCO₂ patterns, hemodynamic manifestations, and management strategies.

In this patient, PETCO₂ increased abruptly from 42 mmHg to 213 mmHg, a rise that cannot be explained by pleural CO₂ absorption or inadequate ventilation alone. Intraoperative findings revealed dense pleural adhesions and a trocar-related injury of the right lower lung, allowing direct CO₂ entry into the alveoli and airways (Fig. 2). The sudden spike in PETCO₂, accompanied by a PETCO₂ > PaCO₂ discrepancy, indicates that CO₂ originated directly from the airway rather than from systemic absorption or hypoventilation [17–19]. The abrupt alveolar CO₂ load rapidly diffused into the pulmonary circulation, producing severe hypercapnia, which subsequently led to marked hemodynamic instability. Initially, the patient developed a hypertensive crisis and tachycardia, followed by progressive bradycardia and BIS depression [20]. These abnormalities resolved promptly with targeted ventilatory adjustments and hemodynamic support. This sequential observation of intraoperative findings, ETCO₂/ABG changes, underlying pathophysiology, and hemodynamic response illustrates the causal chain linking trocar-related lung injury to life-threatening hypercapnia.

Hypercapnic acidosis exerts dose-dependent cardiovascular effects. Mild hypercapnia enhances cardiac output through activation of sympathoadrenal mechanisms, resulting in increased sympathetic tone, augmented preload, reduced afterload, and increases in myocardial contractility, heart rate, venous return, and systemic capacitance [21–23]. These adaptive responses collectively produce a transient cardiovascular stimulatory effect. With increasing severity, however, hypercapnia progressively depresses cardiovascular function [24]. Excessive CO₂ directly inhibits cardiac myocyte and vascular smooth muscle contractility—an effect that occurs independently of pH—leading to myocardial depression, reduced systemic vascular resistance, and hemodynamic instability. Severe hypercapnia markedly increases the risk of malignant arrhythmias, circulatory collapse, and even death [21]. The hemodynamic disturbances observed in the present case closely mirrored this characteristic progression. Most notably, carbon dioxide overtly alters vasomotor responses: every 1 mmHg increase in PaCO2 corresponds to a roughly 4% increase in cerebral blood flow [25], and PaCO₂ levels around 80 mmHg can double cerebral perfusion, leading to cerebral vasodilation, brain edema, and a significant rise in intracranial pressure [26, 27]. These alterations may precipitate Cushing’s response or even a state of “CO₂ narcosis.” In this patient, the BIS value transiently fell to 5, indicating marked central nervous system depression, likely reflecting a synergistic effect between anesthetic agents and extreme hypercapnia.

Management of acute hypercapnic crises during CO₂ insufflation requires a structured and prioritized approach. First, the CO₂ source should be discontinued immediately. Second, augmentation of alveolar ventilation (manual or mechanical) [28].During this process, attention must be paid to avoiding dynamic hyperinflation and excessive driving pressure, as aggressive RR escalation may precipitate intrinsic PEEP and right ventricular strain [28]. Third, hemodynamic stability should be optimized through the judicious use of agents such as atropine or vasodilators, while simultaneously adjusting anesthetic depth to avoid excessive central nervous system depression arising from the synergistic effects of anesthetic drugs and severe hypercapnia. Finally, frequent ABG analysis is crucial for guiding ventilatory adjustments and evaluating the progression of hypercapnic acidosis.

Conclusions

This case underscores the unique risk of abrupt, extreme hypercapnia from trocar-related lung injury in patients with extensive pleural adhesions. Continuous ETCO₂ monitoring allows early detection of abnormal CO₂ loads. Understanding the sequential relationship of intraoperative findings, ETCO₂/ ABG patterns, pathophysiology, and hemodynamic consequences clarifies the mechanism and guides management. Moreover, patients with underlying pleural adhesions or structural lung abnormalities—such as those identified on preoperative CT—may carry a higher risk of trocar-related lung injury; thus, trocar placement should be planned carefully and confirmation of trocar position under thoracoscopic visualization before initiating CO₂ insufflation.
