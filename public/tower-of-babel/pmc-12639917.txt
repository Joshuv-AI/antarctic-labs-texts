Safety and efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors in patients with triple-negative breast cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xin Yan, Xiaoling Zhang, Zengyan Wang, Leyi Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are under extensive investigation as a treatment for triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) in numerous trials. Given the need to evaluate their therapeutic profile, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively assess the safety and efficacy of ICI therapy for TNBC.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English from inception to March 10, 2024. Primary endpoints included treatment-related adverse events (trAEs) of any grade and Grade ≥ 3, as well as immune-related adverse events (irAEs).ResultsThirteen RCTs involving 5,890 TNBC patients were included in the meta-analysis. ICI treatment was associated with a significantly increased risk of serious trAEs (risk ratio [RR]: 1.41; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.12–1.79; p = 0.004) but did not significantly affect the risk of any grade trAEs (RR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.99–1.02; p = 0.584) or grade ≥ 3 trAEs (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.87–1.18; p = 0.872). In contrast, ICIs markedly elevated the risk of any grade irAEs (RR: 1.54; 95% CI: 1.22–1.95; p < 0.001) and grade ≥ 3 irAEs (RR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.56–5.11; p = 0.001). Specific irAEs, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, pneumonitis, adrenal insufficiency, and infusion-related reactions, occurred more frequently with ICI therapy. Network meta-analysis revealed that atezolizumab had the lowest risk of any grade irAEs (surface under the cumulative ranking curve [SUCRA]: 60%) and grade ≥ 3 irAEs (SUCRA: 66%). Importantly, ICIs significantly improved pathological complete response (pCR) rates (RR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.25–1.94; p < 0.001) and were associated with enhanced overall survival (hazard ratio [HR]: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.74–0.96; p = 0.011) and progression-free survival (HR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.61–0.79; p < 0.001).ConclusionWhile ICIs demonstrate clinically meaningful benefits in pCR rates and survival outcomes for TNBC patients, they also substantially increase the risk of serious trAEs and irAEs, including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, pneumonitis, adrenal insufficiency, and infusion-related reactions.RegistrationINPLASY202450094.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15127-3.Keywords: Triple-negative breast cancer, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Treatment-related adverse events, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Despite considerable progress in medical research, breast cancer continues to pose a significant threat to women’s health worldwide. In 2022, there were over 2.3 million new cases and more than 665,000 deaths attributed to the disease [1]. Among breast cancer subtypes, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—classified as “basal-like” due to its lack of estrogen receptors (ERs), progesterone receptors (PRs), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)—accounts for 15–20% of all cases [2, 3]. TNBC is the most aggressive subtype, exhibiting high metastatic potential, frequent recurrence, and limited targeted treatment options [3]. Consequently, its prognosis is poorer than that of hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, with nearly 50% of patients experiencing recurrence within 3–5 years of diagnosis [4].

Recent advances in genomic sequencing have enabled promising therapeutic strategies for TNBC, including immunotherapy and personalized treatments [5, 6]. Among these, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors—have demonstrated encouraging results in neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings by enhancing immune recognition and targeting of cancer cells, thereby suppressing tumor progression [7, 8]. However, patient responses to ICIs remain heterogeneous, with efficacy varying significantly across individuals [9]. A critical limitation of ICI therapy is its potential to induce immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which may affect multiple organ systems [10–12]. While severe adverse events (SAEs) are uncommon, their clinical impact can be life-threatening [13, 14]. Notably, some patients develop concurrent immune-related adverse events (irAEs), and paradoxical disease progression may occur due to immune dysregulation. To address these challenges, this study systematically evaluates the safety and efficacy of ICIs in TNBC, with the dual objectives of optimizing therapeutic benefits and mitigating SAE risks.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This study was conducted in accordance with the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for network meta-analysis [15]. Prior to commencement, the study protocol was registered on the INPLASY platform (No. INPLASY202450094, https://inplasy.com/inplasy-2024-5-0094/). Our inclusion criteria encompassed all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the safety and efficacy of ICIs in TNBC, without restrictions regarding publication language or status. To identify relevant studies, we performed a comprehensive systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases up to March 10, 2024. The search strategy employed key terms including “Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors,” “breast cancer,” and “randomized controlled trial” (detailed search strategy provided in File S1). To ensure completeness of our data collection, we supplemented our database search with: (1) a review of completed but unpublished trials listed on ClinicalTrials.gov (NIH), and (2) manual examination of reference lists from all potentially relevant studies to identify additional eligible publications.

Two investigators independently conducted the literature search and screening following standardized procedures, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus discussions. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients: all patients were adults diagnosed with TNBC; (2) intervention and control: patients received either ICI or placebo treatment, with other treatment regimens consistent across groups; (3) outcomes: any grade and grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (trAEs), irAEs, serious trAEs, specific grade ≥ 3 trAEs, specific any grade irAEs, pathological complete response (pCR), overall survival (OS), and progression-free survival (PFS). For adverse event classification, trAEs encompassed all treatment-associated complications regardless of mechanism, while irAEs specifically denoted those resulting from ICI-induced immune hyperactivation. SAEs included fatal outcomes, life-threatening conditions, hospitalization-requiring events, persistent disabilities, congenital anomalies, and other medically significant occurrences as determined by investigators. Event severity was graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, ranging from mild asymptomatic findings (grade 1) to life-threatening conditions requiring urgent intervention (Grade 4) and fatal outcomes (grade 5). For efficacy assessments, pCR was evaluated in patients with measurable baseline disease per RECIST v1.1 criteria. OS was measured from randomization to death from any cause (censored at last follow-up for surviving patients), while PFS represented the time from randomization to either disease progression (assessed via RECIST v1.1) or death, whichever occurred first. The analysis exclusively included peer-reviewed randomized controlled trials, excluding animal studies, trial protocols, conference abstracts, case reports, correspondence, and studies with incomplete or unreported outcome data.

Data collection and quality assessment

Two authors independently extracted data from the included studies using a standardized protocol. The extracted information comprised study characteristics (e.g., group name, publication year, register number, phase), demographic and clinical data (e.g., sample size, mean age, proportion of females, and metastatic patients, disease status), intervention details (e.g., treatment and control arms), and reported outcomes. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. For quality assessment, two reviewers independently evaluated the risk of bias in included studies with the Cochrane tool [16]. Full-text review was conducted to resolve any disagreements, ensuring consensus on final judgments.

Statistical analysis

The safety outcomes and pCR were analyzed as categorical variables, with relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated prior to data synthesis, while hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CI were used for time-to-event outcomes including OS and PFS. Given the anticipated heterogeneity across studies, we employed a random-effects model to pool effect estimates [17], with significant heterogeneity defined as I2 ≥ 50.0% or Cochran’s Q test p < 0.10 [18, 19]. For comparative effectiveness assessment, we implemented a network meta-analysis that incorporated both direct and indirect evidence [20]. This approach included rigorous evaluation of consistency between direct and indirect estimates [21] and utilized a design–treatment interaction model to validate network coherence [20]. Treatment rankings were objectively quantified using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), where higher percentages (0–100%) indicate more favorable outcomes [22]. Complementary pairwise analyses were performed to examine specific ICI effects, with result stability verified through sensitivity analyses [23]. Subgroup analyses stratified by trial phase, disease status, treatments, and interventions employed interaction tests under normality assumptions [24]. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [25] systematically evaluated evidence certainty for adverse events across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, with final ratings ranging from high to very low certainty. Publication bias was assessed through funnel plot visualization supplemented by Egger’s and Begg’s tests [26, 27]. All statistical tests were two-tailed with significance threshold at p < 0.05, implemented using STATA 12.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Literature search and study selection

Our systematic literature search initially identified 541 potentially relevant articles. After removing 222 duplicates and excluding 258 irrelevant studies through title/abstract screening, we further identified 2 additional eligible trials from reference lists. Of the 63 studies selected for full-text review, 50 were subsequently excluded (29 affiliated studies, 13 lacking appropriate controls, and 8 review articles), yielding 13 RCTs for final inclusion in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [28–40].

Treatment-related adverse events

The meta-analysis incorporated seven trials evaluating any grade trAEs, eight assessing Grade ≥ 3 trAEs, and five examining serious trAEs (Fig. 2). While ICIs showed no significant difference from placebo in any grade trAEs risk (RR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.99–1.02; p = 0.584), or grade ≥ 3 trAEs (RR: 1.01; 95% CI: 0.87–1.18; p = 0.872), substantial heterogeneity was noted for these outcomes (I2 = 58.2%, p = 0.026 and I2 = 85.5%, p < 0.001, respectively). Importantly, ICI treatment demonstrated a 41% increased risk of serious trAEs (RR: 1.41; 95% CI: 1.12–1.79; p = 0.004) with minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 28.8%; p = 0.229). Sensitivity analyses revealed nuanced findings: ICIs might potentially reduce any grade trAEs while increasing grade ≥ 3 trAEs (Figures S1-S2), though the elevated serious trAEs risk remained robust across all sensitivity tests (Figure S3). Subgroup analyses confirmed the overall findings for any grade trAEs while identifying important variations—PD-L1 inhibitors specifically increased grade ≥ 3 trAE risk, and the serious trAE association persisted across Phase III trials, metastatic TNBC populations, and adjuvant settings regardless of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor type (Table 2). Notably, no significant association emerged between ICIs and any particular grade ≥ 3 trAE subtype (Figs. 3 and 4).

Immune-related adverse events

The pooled analysis of eight trials demonstrated that ICIs significantly increased the risk of any grade irAEs by 54% (RR: 1.54; 95% CI: 1.22–1.95; p < 0.001) compared to placebo, with seven trials showing an even more pronounced 2.8-fold higher risk for grade ≥ 3 irAEs (RR: 2.82; 95% CI: 1.56–5.11; p = 0.001). Both outcomes exhibited substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 90.0%, p < 0.001 for any grade; I2 = 72.6%, p = 0.001 for grade ≥ 3), though sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings (Figures S4-S5). Subgroup analyses consistently revealed elevated irAE risks across Phase III trials, metastatic TNBC populations, and adjuvant therapy settings, with no significant differences between PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors (Table 2). Organ-specific analysis identified significantly increased risks for thyroid disorders (hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism), pneumonitis, adrenal insufficiency, and infusion reactions. In contrast, ICIs showed no significant association with dermatological (rashes, severe skin reactions), hepatic (hepatitis, hepatotoxicity), endocrine (hypophysitis, diabetes), or other organ-specific toxicities (colitis, ocular inflammation, pancreatitis, nephritis) (Fig. 5).

Network meta-analysis

The network meta-analysis of any grade trAEs revealed distinct safety profiles among different ICIs (Figure S6). Oleclumab emerged as the most favorable option for any grade trAEs (SUCRA: 58%; Figure S7), though pairwise comparisons showed no significant differences between ICIs in this category (Figure S8). For higher-grade toxicities, pembrolizumab demonstrated the lowest risk of grade ≥ 3 trAEs (SUCRA: 81%; Figures S9-S11), while durvalumab showed optimal performance in minimizing serious trAEs (SUCRA: 87%). Notably, atezolizumab and toripalimab were consistently associated with elevated risk of serious trAEs in direct comparisons (Figures S12-S14).

Analysis of any grade irAEs produced contrasting results (Figure S15). Atezolizumab ranked most favorably for both any grade (SUCRA: 60%; Figure S16) and grade ≥ 3 irAEs (SUCRA: 66%; Figure S18), suggesting a 60–66% probability of being the safest ICI regarding immune-related toxicities. However, paradoxically, pairwise comparisons revealed that atezolizumab, along with pembrolizumab and toripalimab, still significantly increased irAE risks compared to placebo (Figures S17, S19-S20). This apparent contradiction between SUCRA rankings and direct comparisons may reflect differences in network estimation methods versus head-to-head trial results.

Quality of evidence

The GRADE evaluation of evidence certainty for ICI-related outcomes yielded distinct confidence levels across different agents (Table 3). High-quality evidence supported the safety and efficacy profiles of atezolizumab, pembrolizumab, and toripalimab, reflecting robust clinical trial data with minimal methodological limitations. In contrast, the evidence base for oleclumab and durvalumab warranted moderate certainty ratings, indicating either fewer supporting studies or greater variability in observed outcomes. This gradation in evidence strength provides important context for interpreting the comparative effectiveness and safety results across the ICI class.

pCR and survival outcomes

The meta-analysis of ten clinical trials demonstrated a significant 55% increase in pCR rates with ICI treatment compared to control (RR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.25–1.94; p < 0.001), though substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 64.7%; p = 0.003) (Fig. 6). Subgroup analyses revealed this pCR benefit persisted across most clinical scenarios, with the exception of Phase II trial populations (Table 2). Regarding survival outcomes, pooled data from nine trials showed ICIs significantly improved OS (HR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.74–0.96; p = 0.011), while eight trials demonstrated even greater PFS benefits (HR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.61–0.79; p < 0.001). These survival advantages showed minimal heterogeneity (OS, I2 = 38.7%, p = 0.110; PFS, I2 = 23.8%, p = 0.232; Fig. 7). The OS benefit was particularly evident in Phase III trials, metastatic TNBC patients receiving adjuvant therapy, and PD-1 inhibitor-treated subgroups. Notably, PFS improvements remained consistent across all evaluated patient subsets (Table 2).

Publication bias

We systematically evaluated potential publication bias across all key safety outcomes (Figures S21-S25). Comprehensive analysis revealed no statistically significant publication bias for any of the examined endpoints, including any grade trAEs (Egger: p = 0.071; Begg: p = 0.764), grade ≥ 3 trAEs (Egger: p = 0.665; Begg: p = 0.711), serious trAEs (Egger: p = 0.857; Begg: p = 0.806), any grade irAEs (Egger: p = 0.085; Begg: p = 0.266), or grade ≥ 3 irAEs (Egger: p = 0.188; Begg: p = 0.133). These consistent null findings across multiple toxicity categories suggest robust evidence synthesis unaffected by selective publication.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis represents the most extensive quantitative evaluation to data of ICIs in TNBC, incorporating data from 13 RCTs involving 5,890 patients. While confirming previous findings regarding ICI efficacy [7, 8], our study provides novel insights through comprehensive safety analyses encompassing both trAEs, and irAEs. Key findings demonstrated a complex risk-benefit profile: ICIs significantly improved pCR and both OS and PFS, while exhibiting distinct toxicity patterns. Although showing no overall increase in general or high-grade trAEs, ICIs were associated with a 41% elevated risk of serious trAEs and substantially higher risks of any grade (54% increase) and severe grade (2.8-fold increase) irAEs. Notably, organ-specific toxicity analysis revealed significant increases in endocrine (hypothyroidism/hyperthyroidism), pulmonary (pneumonitis), adrenal, and infusion-related events, while other organ systems remained unaffected. Network meta-analysis identified atezolizumab as having the most favorable irAE profile among ICIs, though all agents showed increased immune toxicity compared to placebo. These results provide clinicians with crucial evidence for balancing the demonstrated survival benefits against the specific toxicity risks when considering ICI therapy for TNBC patients. The findings particularly highlight the need for vigilant monitoring of endocrine and pulmonary function during treatment.

Previous research has established foundational knowledge regarding ICI therapy in TNBC, though with notable limitations. Liang et al.‘s network meta-analysis of eight RCTs failed to detect significant differences in grade ≥ 3 trAEs or any-grade irAEs between ICI monotherapies, while demonstrating that chemotherapy combinations exacerbated Grade ≥ 3 irAEs—particularly endocrine disorders like hypo-/hyperthyroidism [8]. Their work notably identified pembrolizumab and atezolizumab as particularly effective in PD-L1-positive populations. Complementing these findings, Mittal et al.‘s analysis of five RCTs confirmed that neoadjuvant chemotherapy-ICI combinations enhanced pCR rates at the cost of increased serious trAEs and both any-grade/grade 3 irAEs [7]. However, these seminal studies primarily emphasized survival outcomes, leaving critical gaps in systematic toxicity profiling.

The safety profile of ICIs in our analysis presents a clinically important dichotomy. While showing no significant impact on overall rates of any-grade or grade ≥ 3 trAEs - likely due to the comparable chemotherapy backbones in both treatment arms - ICIs were consistently associated with a 41% increased risk of serious trAEs. This distinct pattern suggests two key mechanistic insights: (1) the comparable rates of conventional toxicities imply that ICIs do not exhibit broad synergistic toxicity with chemotherapy regimens, as evidenced by the standardized non-ICI components across trials. This pharmacological neutrality toward chemotherapy-induced side effects may reflect non-overlapping toxicity pathways between these drug classes; (2) the selective increase in serious trAEs points to ICI-specific mechanisms, potentially involving off-target immune activation exacerbating subclinical organ inflammation, unmasking of pre-existing autoimmune susceptibilities, and heightened toxicity in patients with advanced disease or comorbid conditions. The clinical implications are particularly relevant for risk stratification, as serious trAEs directly impact treatment continuity and quality of life.

Consistent with prior reports [41, 42], ICIs significantly increased the risk of irAEs of any grade and grade ≥ 3. IrAEs arise from excessive immune system activation, which mimics autoimmune responses, with our data highlighting specific vulnerabilities including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, pneumonitis, adrenal insufficiency, and infusion-related reactions. The observed patterns may be attributed to several interconnected mechanisms. By disrupting immune regulatory pathways, ICIs can induce systemic overactivation of the immune system, leading to attacks on normal tissues. This effect is compounded by the nonspecific activation of T cells, which promotes widespread inflammatory responses across multiple organ systems. The resulting irAEs exhibit considerable diversity, manifesting as skin inflammation, gastrointestinal disturbances, hepatic injury, and other tissue-specific pathologies. Furthermore, individual variations in immune responses and differences in treatment protocols—including specific ICI regimens and dosage levels—may collectively influence both the likelihood and severity of these adverse events [43, 44].

Subgroup analyses revealed that the risk of serious, any grade, and grade ≥ 3 irAEs was particularly elevated in Phase III trials, metastatic TNBC patients, those receiving adjuvant therapy, and those treated with PD-1 inhibitors. These findings may reflect the interplay of multiple contributing factors. Phase III trials, which typically enroll broader and more heterogeneous patient population with varying baseline characteristics, may inherently exhibit higher adverse reaction rates due to differences in age, comorbidities, and treatment exposure—often involving extended durations or intensified dosing regimens. Similarly, patients with metastatic TNBC or those undergoing adjuvant therapy may face heightened adverse event risks stemming from the necessity for more aggressive treatment strategies. The pronounced toxicity observed with PD-1 inhibitors could be mechanistically linked to their broader immune activation, as they disrupt PD-1/PD-L1/PD-L2 interactions systemically, thereby unleashing T-cell responses not only against tumors but also against healthy tissues. In contrast, PD-L1 inhibitors exhibit a more restricted mechanism, primarily blocking PD-L1-mediated immune evasion while preserving alternative inhibitory pathways, potentially resulting in a milder toxicity profile [45].

Immunotherapy demonstrated significant clinical benefits across different treatment settings, with ICIs consistently improving pCR rates, OS, and PFS. In early-stage TNBC, the enhancement in pCR is particularly meaningful given its established association with long-term disease-free survival, reinforcing the role of ICIs in neoadjuvant and adjuvant regimens. Meanwhile, the observed gains in OS and PFS in metastatic disease solidify ICIs as a fundamental component of systemic therapy, mirroring the paradigm-shifting results from pivotal Phase III trials. Subgroup analyses further validated these advantages across diverse patient populations, with PD-1 inhibitors and adjuvant applications standing out for their pronounced survival benefits—findings that may help refine individualized treatment strategies.

Our network meta-analysis provided clinically actionable insights into the comparative safety profiles of different ICIs through SUCRA scoring (0–100%), where higher scores reflect a greater likelihood of being the safest option. Notably, atezolizumab emerged as particularly favorable for high-risk patients, demonstrating a 66% probability of having the lowest risk of grade ≥ 3 irAEs - a critical consideration for those with preexisting pulmonary conditions or autoimmune histories who require careful mitigation of severe immune-mediated complications. Similarly, durvalumab showed promise for vulnerable populations such as elderly patients or those with multiple comorbidities, given its superior SUCRA ranking for serious irAEs where avoiding hospitalization is paramount. These findings must be interpreted with appropriate caveats. Durvalumab’s evidence comes from only one TNBC study [29], limiting the stability of its safety profile assessment. Furthermore, atezolizumab’s distinct mechanism as an anti-PD-L1 antibody-which specifically blocks PD-L1/PD-1 interactions to restore T-cell antitumor activity [28]-may contribute to its differentiated safety profile compared to other ICIs. The SUCRA-based rankings ultimately enable clinicians to make more informed, patient-specific decisions by weighing these safety considerations against therapeutic efficacy when selecting immunotherapies.

These safety profiles provide actionable guidance for immunotherapy selection in TNBC, enabling risk-stratified treatment decisions. For patients at elevated risk of immune-mediated complications—particularly those with preexisting autoimmune disorders or pulmonary comorbidities—atezolizumab represents the most judicious choice given its consistently favorable irAE profile, including the lowest incidence of both any-grade and severe (grade ≥ 3) toxicities. This consideration is especially relevant for pneumonitis risk, a potentially life-threatening complication of ICIs. Conversely, durvalumab’s association with the lowest risk of serious trAEs may position it as preferable for vulnerable populations where avoiding hospitalization is paramount, such as elderly patients or those with advanced metastatic burden. Regardless of agent selection, the class-wide increase in serious trAEs and characteristic irAEs—including thyroid dysfunction, pneumonitis, adrenal insufficiency, and infusion reactions—mandates rigorous monitoring protocols. Clinical practice should incorporate baseline thyroid function testing with serial assessments, periodic pulmonary imaging, and heightened awareness of infusion-related symptoms throughout treatment. This proactive approach balances the transformative potential of ICIs with their distinct safety challenges, allowing for optimized risk-benefit tailoring in TNBC management.

This study has several limitations. First, only a few of the included trials reported the risk of specific trAEs and irAEs associated with ICIs. Second, substantial heterogeneity in several outcomes may compromise the reliability of the pooled estimates. Third, the subgroup sample sizes for early-stage and metastatic TNBC remain imbalanced, potentially affecting the precision of the findings. Fourth, although the network meta-analysis suggested the optimal treatment option for TNBC patients, it did not fully account for the treatment effects of ICIs. Fifth, the inherent limitations of meta-analysis, including multiple comparisons based on patient characteristics and potential publication bias, should be acknowledged.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this study offers comprehensive evidence to guide the safety profiles of ICIs in TNBC patients. Clinical decisions regarding ICI use should account for individual patient characteristics to minimize the risk of trAEs and irAEs. Future large-scale RCTs should prioritize evaluating the risks of trAEs and irAEs.
