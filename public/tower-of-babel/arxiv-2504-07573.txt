Abstract
. We explore the concept of additive diameters in the context of group representations, unifying various noncommutative Waring-type problems. Given a finite-dimensional representation ρ : G → GL ⁡ ( V ) \rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(V) and a subspace U ≤ V U\leq V that generates V V as a G G -module, we define the G G -additive diameter of V V with respect to U U as the minimal number of translates of U U under the representation ρ \rho needed to cover V V . We demonstrate that every irreducible representation of SL 2 ⁡ ( 𝐂 ) \operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C}) exhibits optimal additive diameters and establish sharp bounds for the conjugation representation of SL n ⁡ ( 𝐂 ) \operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C}) on its Lie algebra 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 n ​ ( 𝐂 ) \mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}) . Additionally, we investigate analogous notions for additive diameters in Lie representations. We provide applications to additive diameters with respect to images of equivariant algebraic morphisms, linking them to the corresponding G G -additive diameters of images of their differentials.

Introduction

1.1.
Additive diameters

The study of additive decompositions in algebraic structures has a rich history. In an abelian semigroup
(S,+)
, one can ask if every element of
S
is expressible as a sum of a bounded number of elements from a subset. For
X\subseteq S
, the
additive diameter
of
S
with respect to
X
is the smallest
d
such that every element of
S
can be expressed as a sum of at most
d
elements from
X
:

\operatorname{diam}_{+}(S,X)=\min\{d\mid dX=S\},\quad\text{where }dX=\{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d}\mid x_{i}\in X\cup\{0\}\}.

This framework encompasses diverse problems across various structures. Here are some concrete examples.

1.2.
Diameters in finite groups

Beyond abelian semigroups, similar diameter questions arise in nonabelian group theory. For a finite group
G
with a generating set
X
containing the identity, the
diameter
is the smallest
d
such that every element of
G
can be expressed as a word of length at most
d
in the generators:

\operatorname{diam}(G,X)=\min\{d\mid X^{d}=G\},\quad\text{where }X^{d}=\{x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{d}\mid x_{i}\in X\cup\{1\}\}.

Babai’s conjecture
[
1
]
posits that every nonabelian finite simple group
G
has a very small diameter (polylogarithmic in
|G|
) with respect to
any
generating set. While still open, significant progress has been made, particularly for groups of Lie type of bounded rank (e.g.,
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{F}_{p})
with fixed
n
and
p\to\infty
). The driving force behind this are theorems on growth of sets under multiplication.

Theorem 1.2

(Product theorem,
[
17
,
29
,
9
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite simple group of Lie type of bounded rank. There exists
\epsilon>0
, depending only on the rank of
G
, such that for any generating set
X\subseteq G
, we have

|X^{3}|>|X|^{1+\epsilon}\qquad\text{or}\qquad|X|>|G|^{1-\epsilon}\ \text{(in which case $X^{3}=G$)}.

The first case of the product theorem asserts that
X
“uniformly expands” under multiplication. In the second case,
X
is already so large that it covers
G
in
3
steps. Behind this is Gowers’ argument
[
15
]
exploiting the high dimensions of the lowest-degree representations of
G
, leading to the strong result
X^{3}=G

[
26
]
. These results prove Babai’s conjecture for bounded Lie rank and even support the construction of expander families
[
4
,
8
]
.

Stronger results hold for generating subsets
X
of finite simple groups consisting of specific elements, such as commutators, conjugacy classes, or images of word maps. For
X=\operatorname{im}(w)
, where
w
is a word in the free group evaluated in group elements, Larsen, Shalev, and Tiep
[
24
]
show that for any nontrivial
w
, the diameters of all nonabelian finite simple groups of sufficiently large order are at most
2
. This result is a noncommutative analogue of the classical Waring problem.

1.3.
Diameters in infinite groups

In finitely generated infinite groups, the analogue of “uniform expansion” is uniform exponential growth, meaning
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert X^{d}\right\rvert}\geq\omega^{d}
for some
\omega>1
, for all
d
, and for
all
finite generating sets
X
containing the identity. Eskin, Mozes, and Oh
[
14
]
showed that any finitely generated subgroup of
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
either is virtually solvable or exhibits uniform exponential growth. For example,
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{Z})
has uniform exponential growth. The product theorem borrows ideas from these results.

Gowers’ argument extends to compact groups: subsets with sufficiently large Haar measure cover the group under multiplication in a bounded number of steps (see
[
13
, Theorem 1.7]
for a recent result).

Stronger results hold for generating subsets
X
consisting of specific elements. For complex linear groups, Borel’s theorem asserts that the image of a nontrivial word map is always dominant for connected semisimple groups. This dominance implies, via Borel’s trick, that the diameter of the image of a nontrivial word map is at most
2
. This result is mirrored in the Larsen-Shalev-Tiep theorem for finite simple groups.

1.4.
Additive diameters in algebras

In rings and algebras, additive decompositions have been widely studied, particularly in matrix algebras. A key focus is the noncommutative Waring problem, which examines the additive diameter of an algebra with respect to
X=\operatorname{im}(f)
, where
f
is a noncommutative polynomial.

For finite matrix algebras, the problem has been explored for power words. For instance,
[
23
]
shows that for
k\geq 1
, every matrix in
\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{F}_{q})
can be expressed as a sum of two
k
-th powers, provided
q
is sufficiently large.

Over
\mathbf{C}
, the problem is better understood. Brešar, Šemrl, and Volčič
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
study
\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
for arbitrary noncommutative polynomials
f
. A key result asserts that every matrix in
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
can be written as the difference of two elements from
\operatorname{im}(f)
if
n
is large relative to
\deg(f)
. On the multiplicative side, it is shown that every nonscalar matrix in
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
can be expressed as a product of two elements from
\operatorname{im}(f)
under similar conditions.

For Lie algebras, related results appear in
[
2
]
for Lie polynomials. If
P
is a nontrivial Lie polynomial that is not a polynomial identity in
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
, the induced polynomial map is dominant on any Chevalley Lie algebra (e.g.,
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
for
n\geq 2
). This is an infinitesimal analogue of Borel’s theorem.

1.5.
Contributions – Additive diameters in representations

In this paper, we extend and unify some of the previous themes by studying additive diameters in the context of group representations.

1.5.1.
Definition and examples

Let
G
be a group with a finite-dimensional representation
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
, and let
U
be a subspace of
V
that generates
V
as a
G
-module. The
G
-additive diameter
of
V
with respect to
U
is the smallest number of translates of
U
under
\rho
needed to cover
V
:

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{G}(V,U)=\min\left\{d\;\middle|\;\rho(g_{1})\cdot U+\dots+\rho(g_{d})\cdot U=V\text{ for some $g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\in G$}\right\}.

The diameter is
optimal
if
\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{G}(V,U)=\lceil\dim V/\dim U\rceil
. If this holds for
every
subspace
U\leq V
, we say
V
exhibits
optimal
G
-additive diameters
.

Below, we provide examples of representations with both optimal and nonoptimal diameters, illustrating the challenges of identifying the necessary translates of
U
. These examples show that not all irreducible representations exhibit optimal diameters, even for large subspaces.

Example 1.3
.

•

Let
G=\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
act on
V=\mathbf{C}^{n}
by matrix multiplication, and let
U\leq V
be any
d
-dimensional subspace with
d<n
. Since
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
acts transitively on such subspaces,
V
exhibits optimal
G
-additive diameters.

•

Let
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
act by conjugation on
\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
, and let
U=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\langle I,E_{12}\rangle
. Since every conjugate of

contains

, the diameter is at least
3
, which is not optimal. On the other hand, three conjugates of

can cover
\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
(

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})}(\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C}),U)=3
. Note that this representation is not irreducible.

1.5.2.
Dimension expanders

Bounding these diameters can be approached in two stages. The first step involves establishing “uniform expansion” of the subspace
\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\rho(g_{i})\cdot U
as
k
increases, measured by its dimension. This dimension expansion, analogous to the first case of the product theorem in groups, has been studied by Lubotzky and Zelmanov
[
25
]
and relies on expander families and their unitary representations.

Theorem 1.5

(Dimension expanders, Proposition 2.1 in
[
25
]
)
.

Let
G
be a group generated by a finite set
S
with Kazhdan constant
3
3

3

The Kazhdan constant is
\inf_{\rho}\inf_{0\neq v\in V}\max_{s\in S}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\rho(s)\cdot v-v\right\rVert}/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v\right\rVert}
, where the infimum runs over unitary representations
\rho
of
G
on a Hilbert space
V
without non-trivial
G
-fixed points.

\epsilon>0
. For any irreducible unitary representation
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
, we have

\textstyle\dim\left(U+\sum_{s\in S}\left(\rho(s)\cdot U\right)\right)\geq(1+\epsilon^{2}/12)\cdot\dim U

for all subspaces
U\leq V
of dimension at most
\dim V/2
.

Kassabov
[
22
]
showed that symmetric groups
S_{n}
are expanders with respect to explicitly constructed generating sets of size
k\leq 30
, implying uniformly bounded Kazhdan constants. Thus, the dimension expanders theorem applies to
G=S_{n}
with its conjugation action on the irreducible representation
\mathbf{C}^{n-1}
induced by the natural permutation representation on
\mathbf{C}^{n}

1.5.3.
Completing the covering

The results above demonstrate that starting with a subspace
U\leq V
, we can cover at least half of
V
using translates of
U
under
G
. However, beyond this point, the theorem no longer guarantees uniform dimension expansion. Indeed, for dimension reasons, uniform expansion cannot hold for all subspaces of sufficiently large dimension. To fully cover
V
in a bounded number of steps, we require a variant of the second part of the product theorem or Gowers’ argument. This is the primary goal of the present paper.

We focus on addressing the conjugation representation of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
on its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
. We show that sufficiently large subspaces exhibit optimal diameters.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
U\in\operatorname{Gr}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),d)
with
(n-1)^{2}<d<n^{2}-1
,
n\neq 4
.
Then

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),U)=2.

Surprisingly, the dimension bound is sharp. We provide examples of subspaces of dimension
(n-1)^{2}
with diameter
3
. We then generalize the above result to all large subspaces, exhibiting optimal group-additive diameters up to logarithm factor.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
0<\epsilon<1/3
and
n\geq\max\{9,2/\epsilon\}
. If

U\leq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
has
\dim U>\epsilon n^{2}
, then

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}\bigl(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),U\bigr)\leq\frac{3456}{\epsilon}\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\epsilon}\right\rceil.

1.5.4.
Lie-additive diameters

We also explore additive diameters in Lie algebras and their representations, drawing parallels and highlighting contrasts with the group-theoretic results. These problems are often more tractable. We provide several nonequivalent definitions of Lie-additive diameters. For the most natural definition, we show that irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
exhibit optimal diameters, similar to the group case, but with a significantly simpler argument. However, the precise relationship between group-additive and Lie-additive diameters remains unclear, raising further questions about their connection.

1.6.
Applications – Additive diameters in equivariant morphisms

Finally, we explore applications of the results above to additive diameters of vector spaces with respect to images of equivariant morphisms. This is a representation-theoretic analogue of the Waring problem and generalizes the results in the noncommutative setting discussed earlier.

Suppose
G
acts on vector spaces
W
and
V
via representations. A morphism between these representations is a
G
-equivariant linear map
f\colon W\to V
, meaning
f(g\cdot w)=g\cdot f(w)
for all
g\in G
and
w\in W
. Here, we consider an extension of these to polynomial maps (algebraic morphisms) and show that the additive diameter of
V
with respect to
\operatorname{im}(f)
can be bounded in terms of the
G
-additive diameter of
V
with respect to the image of the differential of
f
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group. Let
f\colon W\to V
be a
G
-equivariant algebraic morphism. Then, for any
w\in W
,

\operatorname{diam}_{+}(V,\operatorname{im}(f))\leq 2\cdot\operatorname{diam}^{G}_{+}(V,\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)).

We further show how the images
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
can be understood in terms of the derivative of the representation
\rho
.

This theorem unifies several known results. For example, applied to the conjugation representation of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
on
\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
, it recovers (up to constants) some results from
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
on the noncommutative Waring problem in matrix algebras, as noncommutative polynomials are specific examples of equivariant morphisms. Additionally, when
Z
is a homogeneous variety in
V
with a
G
-equivariant polynomial parametrization
f\colon W\to Z\subseteq V
, this theorem can be compared to Terracini’s lemma
[
12
, Proposition 10.10]
, linking tangent spaces of secant varieties to images of differentials of parametrizations.

Borel Stable Subspaces

2.1.
Strategy for bounding diameters

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group with a representation
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
. Suppose we wish to prove that the
G
-additive diameter of
V
with respect to
all
subspaces
U\leq V
of a given dimension
d
is at most
k
. For the sake of contradiction, suppose there is a counterexample. Collect all the potential counterexamples into a set

X_{d,k}=\{U\in\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)\mid\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{G}(V,U)>k\},

where
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
is the Grassmanian variety of
d
-dimensional subspaces of
V
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The set
X_{d,k}
is a closed subvariety of
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an ordered basis of
V
, and let
J
be a
d
-tuple of distinct indices from
\{1,\dots,\dim V\}
. Let
M_{J}
be the set of
d\times\dim V
matrices whose columns indexed by
J
form the identity matrix. The row spans of matrices in
M_{J}
form an open affine subset
\mathcal{O}_{J}\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
. These open subsets give a chart for
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
as
J
varies. Let

Y_{d,k}=\{(U,g_{1},\dots,g_{k})\in\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)\times G^{k}\mid\textstyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\rho(g_{i})\cdot U=V\}.

The set
Y_{d,k}\cap(\mathcal{O}_{J}\times G^{k})
corresponds to matrices
M_{J}
with rows
m_{1},\dots,m_{d}
(where
m_{i}\in V
) for which the matrix with rows
\rho(g_{i})\cdot m_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq k
and
1\leq j\leq d
has full rank. This is an open condition, so
Y_{d,k}
is an open subset of
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)\times G^{k}
. Consider the projection
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)\times G^{k}\to\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
onto the first component. This is an open map, so its image, consisting of subspaces
U\in\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
that cover
V
with at most
k

\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
. Its complement
X_{d,k}
is then closed.
∎

Note that the variety
X_{d,k}
is closed under the action of
G
on
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
. Supposing this set is not empty, it must then contain a very particular subspace.

4

for

Theorem 2.2

(Borel fixed point theorem, III.10.4 in

]
)
.

Let

be a nonempty closed subvariety that is invariant under the action of
G
on
\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)
. Then
X
contains a subspace

stable under the action of the Borel subgroup of
G
.

5

5

This means that for all
g

\rho(g)\cdot u\in U
for all

.

Therefore, to show that

Proposition 2.3

Let
G

\operatorname{Gr}(V,d)

3.
Irreducible Representations of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})

Here, we prove that irreducible representations of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
always exhibit optimal diameters with respect to any subspace.

Theorem 3.1
.

Every irreducible representation of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
exhibits optimal group-additive diameters.

Proof.

Let
V=\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k}
for some
k\geq 1
. For simplicity, let us further identify
\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k}
with polynomials of degree at most
k
, denoted as
\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}
, under
X=x,Y=1
. By
Proposition

2.3
, it suffices to prove the claim for every upper closed subspace
U=\langle e_{j},e_{j+1},\dots,e_{k}\rangle
, which is, by the above identification, equal to
\langle x^{j},x^{j+1},\dots,x^{k}\rangle
. For any
0\leq i<d
and
a\in\mathbf{C}
, we have

\rho_{k}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
a&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot x^{k-i}=(x+a)^{k-i}.

Let
d=\dim U=k-j+1
. We can assume
d<k+1
. Let
n=\lceil(k+1)/d\rceil
. We claim that for distinct
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbf{C}
, we have

\sum_{0\leq\ell<n}\left(\rho_{k}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
a_{\ell}&1\end{pmatrix}\cdot U\right)=V.

This is equivalent to saying that the polynomials
(x+a_{\ell})^{k-i}
for
0\leq\ell<n
and
0\leq i<d
span
\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}
, which is in turn the same as

\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\left\langle\partial_{x}^{i}((x+a_{\ell})^{k})\mid 0\leq\ell<n,\ 0\leq i<d\right\rangle=\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k},

where
\partial_{x}
is the derivative operator. The latter holds by the following lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
p\in\mathbf{C}[x]
be of degree
k
. Let
d<k+1
and
n=\lceil\frac{k+1}{d}\rceil
. Then

\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\left\langle\partial_{x}^{i}(p(x+a_{\ell}))\mid 0\leq\ell<n,\ 0\leq i<d\right\rangle=\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}

for any distinct
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbf{C}
.

Proof.

Using the Taylor series, we can expand the polynomial
p(x+a)
as

p(x+a)=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\frac{1}{j!}a^{j}\partial_{x}^{j}(p(x)).

Hence

\partial_{x}^{i}(p(x+a))=\partial_{a}^{i}(p(x+a))=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\partial_{a}^{i}(a^{j})\cdot\frac{1}{j!}\partial_{x}^{j}(p(x)).

We can therefore express
\partial_{x}^{i}(p(x+a))
in the basis
\partial_{x}^{j}(p(x))/j!
of
\mathbf{C}[x]_{\leq k}
with the matrix of coefficients

V_{a}=\left[\partial_{a}^{i}(a^{j})\right]_{0\leq j<k,0\leq i<d}\in\operatorname{M}_{(k+1)\times d}(\mathbf{C}).

Now, for
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in\mathbf{C}
, the block matrix

V=\left[V_{a_{1}},\dots,V_{a_{n}}\right]\in\operatorname{M}_{(k+1)\times nd}(\mathbf{C})

is exactly the confluent Vandermonde matrix corresponding to the Hermite interpolation problem (see
[
21
]
). Taking
a_{\ell}
to be distinct, the first
k+1
columns of
V
form an invertible matrix.
∎

6.
Lie-Additive Diameters

6.1.
The three Lie-additive diameters

Let
L
be a Lie algebra with a Lie representation
\rho\colon L\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
. For
X\subseteq V
and
a\in L
, define
\rho(a)\cdot X=\{\rho(a)\cdot x\mid x\in X\}
. Analogous to the group-additive diameter, we can define the Lie-additive diameter of
V
with respect to
X
. There are several natural but nonequivalent ways to define this concept. As we illustrate through examples below, some definitions are overly restrictive, others too broad, while one appears to strike the right balance.

In order to state the definitions, let
\operatorname{ass}(\rho(L))
be the associative subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
generated by
\rho(L)
. Its elements are sums of scalar multiples of monomials
\rho(x_{1})\cdots\rho(x_{k})
, where
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in L
and
k\geq 0
. Let
\operatorname{mon}(\rho(L))
be the set of all such monomials. The
L
-additive diameters
of
V
with respect to
X
are:

elementary:

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L}(V,X)=\min\left\{d+1\mid V=X+r_{1}\cdot X+\cdots+r_{d}\cdot X,\ r_{i}\in\rho(L)\right\}
,
6
6

6

We take
X
as a separate summand to ensure that
X
is contained in the sum.

monomial:

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L,\operatorname{mon}}(V,X)=\min\left\{d\mid V=m_{1}\cdot X+\cdots+m_{d}\cdot X,\ m_{i}\in\operatorname{mon}(\rho(L))\right\}
,

associative:

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L,\operatorname{ass}}(V,X)=\min\left\{d\mid V=a_{1}\cdot X+\cdots+a_{d}\cdot X,\ a_{i}\in\operatorname{ass}(\rho(L))\right\}
.

Clearly we always have

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L,\operatorname{ass}}(V,X)\leq\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L,\operatorname{mon}}(V,X)\leq\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L}(V,X).

Let us think through that when
V
is an irreducible representation of
L
, it exhibits optimal associative Lie-additive diameters, so these diameters are, in a sense, useless.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
V
be an irreducible representation of
L
. Then
\operatorname{ass}(\rho(L))=\mathfrak{gl}(V)
, and so
V
exhibits optimal associative Lie-additive diameters.

Proof.

The first part is immediate from Burnside’s irreducibility theorem, and the second part follows since
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
acts transitively on subspaces of
V
of the same dimension.
∎

We inspect the other two diameters for the case of irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
and the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
on itself in the following sections. We give several examples when the monomial and elementary diameters differ.

6.2.
Irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})

We inspect diameters of irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
. We prove that the monomial diameter is always optimal, as in the group case. On the other hand, the elementary diameter can easily be infinite.

Smooth irreducible representations of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
can be realized on the same vector space
V=\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k}
for
k\geq 1
as with
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
, where
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
acts by the derivative
D_{I}\rho_{k}
of the standard irreducible representation
\rho_{k}
of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
. Write
e,h,f
for the standard basis of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
, and let
E_{k},H_{k},F_{k}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k})
be their images under
D_{I}\rho_{k}
. Then we have

E_{k}\cdot e_{i}=(k-i)e_{i+1},\quad H_{k}\cdot e_{i}=(2i-k)e_{i},\quad F_{k}\cdot e_{i}=ie_{i-1}.

Theorem 6.2
.

Every irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
exhibits optimal monomial Lie-additive diameters.

Proof.

By
Proposition

2.3
, it suffices to prove the claim for upper closed subspaces
U=\langle e_{j},e_{j+1},\dots,e_{k}\rangle
with
0<j<k
. Let
d=\dim U=k-j+1
. The operator
F_{k}
maps
U
into
\langle e_{j-1},e_{j},\dots,e_{k-1}\rangle
. Hence

F_{k}^{d}\cdot U=\langle e_{j-d},e_{j-d+1},\dots,e_{k-d}\rangle.

Thus
F_{k}^{d}
moves the subspace
U
downward in the basis, so after enough iterations it fills the whole space:

\sum_{0\leq i<(k+1)/d}\left(F_{k}^{di}\cdot U\right)=V.\qed

On the other hand, the elementary diameter can easily be infinite.

Example 6.3
.

Let
V=\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k}
be the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
of dimension
k+1\geq 3
. Let
U=\langle e_{j},e_{j+1},\dots,e_{k}\rangle
be an upper closed subspace with
j>1
. Then
E_{k},H_{k}
preserve
U
, whereas
F_{k}\cdot U=\langle e_{j-1},e_{j},\dots,e_{k-1}\rangle
, and so
U+\sum_{i}D_{I}\rho_{k}(x_{i})\cdot U\leq\langle e_{j-1},e_{j},\dots,e_{k}\rangle\neq V
for all
x_{i}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
and any number of them. Therefore the elementary Lie-additive diameter of
V
with respect to
U
is infinite.

These results suggest that out of the three Lie-additive diameters,
\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L,\operatorname{mon}}
is the most meaningful notion (finiteness of
\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L}
can fail even in simple cases, while
\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{L,\operatorname{ass}}
is always optimal).

7.
Additive Diameters in Equivariant Morphisms

7.1.
Equivariant algebraic morphisms

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group. In this section, we show how to apply the diameter bounds developed in earlier sections to the images of
G
-equivariant algebraic morphisms
f\colon W\to V
, where
W
and
V
are representations of
G
. After choosing a basis for
V
, one can interpret
f
as a tuple of polynomial functions on
W
. Thus,
f
can be viewed as an element of
k[W]\otimes V
, and
G
-equivariance implies that
f
actually lies in the invariant subring
(k[W]\otimes V)^{G}
. Alternatively, we can regard
f
as a linear map in the space of
G
-equivariant maps

\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}\hom_{G}\left(\operatorname{Sym}^{k}(W),V\right),

where
\operatorname{Sym}^{k}(W)
denotes the
k
th symmetric power of
W
. In this correspondence, the summand
\hom_{G}(\operatorname{Sym}^{k}(W),V)
represents
G
-equivariant homogeneous polynomial maps of degree
k
from
W
to
V
.
9
9

9

For instance, if
W=\mathbf{C}^{2}
and
V=\mathbf{C}
, then the polynomial map
f(x,y)=x^{2}+2xy
corresponds to
(e_{1}\otimes e_{1})^{\ast}+2(e_{1}\otimes e_{2})^{\ast}
in
\hom(\operatorname{Sym}^{2}(W),V)
.

Therefore many such maps
f
can be obtained by identifying subrepresentations of
\operatorname{Sym}^{k}(W)
that are isomorphic to
V
.

Example 7.1
.

Let
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
and let
W=\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{1}=\mathbf{C}^{2}
, the standard representation of
G
. Then
\operatorname{Sym}^{k}(W)
is precisely the irreducible representation
\rho_{k}
on
\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k}
. Therefore we have, up to a scalar, a unique algebraic morphism
f
of degree
k
from
\mathbf{C}^{2}
to
\mathbf{C}[X,Y]_{k}
. For example, the case
k=3
gives the parameterization of the twisted cubic
f\colon(s,t)\mapsto(s^{3},s^{2}t,st^{2},t^{3})
.

Example 7.2
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
acts on

by conjugation, and take
W=V^{m}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})^{\oplus m}
. Let
f\colon W\to V
be an
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
-equivariant morphism. Then

is a trace polynomial by
[

. For example,
f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})=X_{1}^{2}X_{2}-\operatorname{tr}(X_{m})X_{3}+\operatorname{tr}(X_{m-1}^{3})
.

7.2.
Images of derivatives of equivariant maps

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group with a representation
\rho\colon G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
. For any

D_{g}\rho\colon T_{g}G\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
of

10

10

D_{I}L_{g}\colon\mathfrak{gl}(V)=T_{I}\operatorname{GL}(V)\to T_{g}\operatorname{GL}(V)

In particular, for

T_{I}G=\mathfrak{g}
of

. Say

is another representation of

be a differentiable map that is equivariant with respect to

\rho

f(\lambda(g)\cdot w)=\rho(g)\cdot f(w)
for all

\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)

Lemma 7.3
.

For any

, let
\rho_{v}\colon G\to V
,
\rho_{v}(g)=\rho(g)\cdot v
. Then for any
g\in G

D_{g}\rho_{v}\colon T_{g}G\to V,\quad x\mapsto(D_{g}\rho\cdot x)\cdot v.

Proof.

Writing
\operatorname{eval}_{v}\colon\operatorname{End}(V)\to V
for the evaluation map at

, we have

(D_{g}\rho_{v})\cdot x=D_{g}(\operatorname{eval}_{v}\circ\rho)\cdot x=\operatorname{eval}_{v}\cdot D_{g}\rho\cdot x=(D_{g}\rho\cdot x)\cdot v.\qed

Lemma 7.4
.

For any
w\in W
and
g\in G

\operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g)\cdot w}f)=\rho(g)\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f).

Proof.

Since

G

f(\lambda(g)\cdot w)=\rho(g)\cdot f(w)

D_{\lambda(g)\cdot w}f\cdot\lambda(g)=\rho(g)\cdot D_{w}f.

It follows that
\operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g)\cdot w}f)=\rho(g)\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
, as claimed.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

For any
w\in W
and
g\in G
, we have

\operatorname{im}(D_{g}\rho_{f(w)})\leq\rho(g)\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f).

Proof.

Letting
\gamma\colon(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to G
be any smooth curve with
\gamma(0)=g
and
D_{0}\gamma=x\in T_{g}G
, we have

(\rho_{f(w)}\circ\gamma)(t)=\rho(\gamma(t))\cdot f(w)=f(\lambda(\gamma(t))\cdot w)

by equivariance. Thus,

D_{g}\rho_{f(w)}\cdot x=D_{g}\rho_{f(w)}\cdot D_{0}\gamma=D_{0}(t\mapsto f(\lambda(\gamma(t))\cdot w))\in\operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g)\cdot w}f).

Since
x\in T_{g}G
was arbitrary, we conclude that

\operatorname{im}(D_{g}\rho_{f(w)})\leq\rho(g)\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
by using the previous lemma.
∎

Let us gather the results above in a single proposition.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group. Let
f\colon W\to V
be a
G
-equivariant algebraic morphism, and let
\rho
be the representation of
G
on
V
. For any
w\in W
and
g\in G
, we have:

\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)\geq\rho(g^{-1})\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{g}\rho)\cdot f(w)\quad\text{and}\quad\dim\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)\geq\dim(\rho(G)\cdot f(w)).

Proof.

It follows from the first lemma that
\operatorname{im}(D_{g}\rho_{f(w)})=\operatorname{im}D_{g}\rho\cdot f(w)
, and then the first containment is immediate from the last lemma. As for the second inequality, we have
\operatorname{im}\rho_{f(w)}=\rho(G)\cdot f(w)
, and hence
\dim(\overline{\operatorname{im}\rho_{f(w)}})=\dim(\rho(G)\cdot f(w))
. It now follows from
[
16
, Chapter III, Proposition 10.6]
that
\operatorname{rank}(D_{g}\rho_{f(w)})\geq\dim(\rho(G)\cdot f(w))
for all
g
belonging to a nonempty Zariski open subset of
G
. By the previous lemma, we also have
\operatorname{rank}(D_{g}\rho_{f(w)})\leq\dim\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Example 7.7
.

Let
G=\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
act on its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
by conjugation. The derivative of this representation at the identity is the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
on itself. Now let
f\colon W\to\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be any equivariant map. Then for any
w\in W
, the proposition gives
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)\geq[\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(C),f(w)]=\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ad}_{f(w)})
. If there is an element in
\operatorname{im}f
that is regular semisimple, then by
G
-equivariance there is also an element
d
that is diagonal with distinct eigenvalues, and so
\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ad}_{d})=\operatorname{M}_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{C})
. In this case, we thus obtain, for some
w\in W
, that
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)\geq\operatorname{M}_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{C})
.

7.3.
Additive diameters in equivariant morphisms

For any function
f\colon W\to V
and positive integer
k
, we define the
k
-fold sum

f^{[k]}\colon W^{k}\to V,\quad(w_{1},\dots,w_{k})\mapsto f(w_{1})+\cdots+f(w_{k}).

Our aim is to show that the smallest
k
for which
f^{[k]}
is surjective is controlled by group-additive diameters of images of derivatives.
11
11

11

The main idea behind this connection in the context of groups goes back to Andrei Jaikin’s paper
[
20
, Lemma 2.1]
. On the other hand, in the context of algebraic varieties, the connection between sums of points on a projective variety (secant variety) and tangent spaces is known as Terracini’s lemma
[
12
, Proposition 10.10]
(see also
Remark

7.12
).

Theorem 7.8
.

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group. Let
f\colon W\to V
be a
G
-equivariant algebraic morphism. Then, for any
w\in W
,

\operatorname{diam}_{+}(V,\operatorname{im}f)\leq 2\cdot\operatorname{diam}^{G}_{+}(V,\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)).

Proof.

Let
\lambda,\rho
be the representations of
G
on
W,V
. Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in G
, and set
p=(\lambda(g_{1})\cdot w,\dots,\lambda(g_{k})\cdot w)\in W^{k}
. We have

D_{p}f^{[k]}=D_{\lambda(g_{1})\cdot w}f\oplus\dots\oplus D_{\lambda(g_{k})\cdot w}f,

and so its image
\operatorname{im}(D_{p}f^{[k]})
is equal to

\operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g_{1})\cdot w}f)+\dots+\operatorname{im}(D_{\lambda(g_{k})\cdot w}f)=\rho(g_{1})\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)+\cdots+\rho(g_{k})\cdot\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)

by
Lemma

7.5
.
Taking
k=\operatorname{diam}^{G}_{+}(W,\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f))
, we can thus find
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in G
so that
\operatorname{im}(D_{p}f^{[k]})=W
.
The map
f^{[k]}
is then a submersion in some neighbourhood of the point
p
. By the implicit function theorem, it follows that
\operatorname{im}(f^{[k]})
contains an open subset of
f^{[k]}(p)
. By Chevalley’s theorem (see
[
16
, Chapter II, Exercise 3.19]
), the image of
f^{[k]}
is constructible, therefore it contains a nonempty Zariski open dense subset of
V
. It now follows from Borel’s trick (see
[
3
, Chapter I, 1.3]
) that
(f^{[k]})^{[2]}=f^{[2k]}
is surjective.
∎

The theorem is of course only useful if the group-additive diameter in question is finite. In other words, the image
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
should generate
V
as a
G
-module. This is clearly not always the case, and might depend on the point
w
. The ideal situation is when
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
is very large for some
w
.

Example 7.9
.

Let
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
act on
\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
by conjugation. Let
f\colon\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})\to\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be defined as
f(X)=X^{2}
. Then
D_{0}f=0
. On the other hand,
D_{I}f=2I
, so
\operatorname{im}(D_{I}f)=\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
. By the previous theorem,
f^{[2]}
is surjective. Every matrix is thus a sum of two squares (but not every matrix is itself a square).

Example 7.10
.

Let
f\colon\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})^{2}\to\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be defined as
f(X,Y)=I+[X,Y]
. Then
\operatorname{im}(f)
is contained in the set of matrices of trace
n
, so there is no
k
making
f^{[k]}
surjective. Correspondingly, the derivative
D_{(A,B)}f
maps into
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
, so the
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
-additive diameter of
\operatorname{im}(D_{(A,B)}f)
is infinite.

The last example demonstrates that the image of
f
may be contained in a translate of a subrepresentation. We will now argue that this is the only obstacle to the finiteness of the associated group-additive diameter. Therefore, if there is any hope of proving that
f^{[k]}
is surjective, then additive diameters always provide an approach.

Proposition 7.11
.

Let
G
be a complex linear algebraic group, and let
f\colon W\to V
be a
G
-equivariant algebraic morphism. Suppose that
V
has only finitely many subrepresentations. If
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\langle\operatorname{im}(f-f(0))\rangle=V
,
then for some
w\in W
,

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{G}(V,\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f))<\infty.

Proof.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\ell}
be all the proper subrepresentations of
V
. It suffices to prove that for any
1\leq i\leq\ell
, it cannot happen that
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)\leq Z_{i}
for all
w\in W
, since the subrepresentation generated by
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
must then be equal to
V
and so the diameter is finite. For the sake of contradiction, assume that this does occur for some
i
. Let
\lambda_{i}\colon V\to\mathbf{C}
be a linear functional with
\operatorname{ker}\lambda_{i}\geq Z_{i}
. Then we have, for all
w\in W
, that
\lambda_{i}\cdot D_{w}f=0
. But then by the chain rule
D_{w}(\lambda_{i}\circ f)=0
,
so the polynomial
\lambda_{i}\circ f
is constant. This implies
\operatorname{im}(f)\subseteq f(0)+\operatorname{ker}(\lambda_{i})
, contradicting our assumption that
\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\langle\operatorname{im}(f-f(0))\rangle=V
.
∎

Remark 7.12
.

This can be compared with Terracini’s lemma
[
12
, Proposition 10.10]
from classical algebraic geometry in the case when
\operatorname{im}(f)
is a homogeneous variety. If a projective variety
X\subset\mathbf{P}(V)
has the property that its
d
-th secant variety is the whole
\mathbf{P}(V)
, then the sum of the tangent spaces at
d
general points of
X
equals
\mathbf{P}(V)
. Our result can be seen as an equivariant analogue: if a homogeneous variety
X=\operatorname{im}(f)
has finite additive diameter in
V
, then the sum of
some
number of tangent spaces at suitable
G
-translates of a single point
also spans
V
.

We can use
Proposition

7.6
to ensure
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
contains a special subspace or at least that
\dim\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
is large. After that, we can apply our diameter bounds from the previous sections. Here are some examples of how this can be done.

Example 7.13
.

Let
G=\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
. Let
W=\mathbf{C}^{2}
be the standard representation and let

be the irreducible representation of
G
of degree

. Let

be the corresponding
G
-equivariant morphism. Note that for any nonzero
w\in W
, the derivative
D_{w}f
is of rank
2
, so
\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)
is
2

and then
\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{G}(V,\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f))=\lceil(k+1)/2\rceil
by Theorem

. Hence
\operatorname{diam}_{+}(V,\operatorname{im}f)\leq 2\lceil(k+1)/2\rceil
by the previous theorem. In particular, the case
k=3
gives that every element of
\mathbf{C}^{4}
is a sum of
4
elements from the twisted cubic.

Example 7.14
.

Suppose that
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
acts on

\dim C_{\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}(f(w))<(1-\epsilon)n^{2}-1

\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f)\geq\operatorname{im}(D_{I}\rho)\cdot f(w)=\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ad}_{f(w)})

\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),\operatorname{im}(D_{w}f))\leq\frac{3456}{\epsilon}\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\epsilon}\right\rceil,

\operatorname{diam}_{+}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),\operatorname{im}f)\leq\frac{6912}{\epsilon}\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{4}{\epsilon}\right\rceil.

In the extreme case, we could have

\operatorname{diam}_{+}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),\operatorname{im}f)\leq 2\cdot\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),\operatorname{M}_{n}^{0}(\mathbf{C}))\leq 4.

Example 7.15
.

Let
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
act on
\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})

.

12

12

This means that
\operatorname{im}(f)

Appendix A
Examples

Example A.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbf{C}^{2}\to\mathbf{C}^{4}
be the polynomial map given by
f(x,y)=(x^{3},x^{2}y,xy^{2},y^{3})
. Its image
\operatorname{im}(f)
is a subvariety of
\mathbf{C}^{4}
, whose projectivization in
\mathbf{P}^{3}
is the twisted cubic
\mathcal{C}
. Its secant variety (the Zariski closure of the union of all lines between two points in
\mathcal{C}
) is the whole
\mathbf{P}^{3}

[
12
, Proposition 10.11]
. Since
\operatorname{im}(f)
is a homogeneous variety, every point on the line between two points on it is a sum of two elements in
\operatorname{im}(f)
. It follows that the Zariski closure of the sumset
\operatorname{im}(f)+\operatorname{im}(f)
is
\mathbf{C}^{4}
, and so
\operatorname{diam}_{+}(\mathbf{C}^{4},\operatorname{im}(f))\leq 4
by Borel’s trick
[
3
, Chapter I, 1.3]
. Alternatively, the same conclusion follows from
Example

7.13
. The diameter is certainly not equal to
2
, since the point
(0,1,0,0)\in\mathbf{C}^{4}
is not a sum of two elements in
\operatorname{im}(f)
. In order to deduce that the diameter is in fact equal to
3
, a bit more work is needed (see
[
10
, Example 3.10]
).

Example A.2
.

Let
G=\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
act by conjugation on
V=\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
, and let
U=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\langle I,E_{12}\rangle
. Every conjugate of
U
contains
I
, so the diameter is at least
3
for dimension reasons. On the other hand, let
F
be the permutation matrix that swaps the two standard basis vectors. Then
FUF^{-1}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\langle I,E_{21}\rangle
, and let
E=I+E_{21}
. We have
EUE^{-1}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbf{C}}\langle I,-E_{11}+E_{12}-E_{21}+E_{22}\rangle
. This shows that
U+FUF^{-1}+EUE^{-1}=\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
. Therefore the
\operatorname{GL}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
-additive diameter of
\operatorname{M}_{2}(\mathbf{C})
with respect to
U
is
3
, which is not optimal.

Example A.3
.

Let
G=\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
act by conjugation on
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
. This is an irreducible representation. Let
\operatorname{M}^{0}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be the set of matrices with zero diagonal. Let us show that

\operatorname{diam}^{\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}_{+}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),\operatorname{M}^{0}_{n}(\mathbf{C}))=2.

Let
\operatorname{D}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be the set of diagonal matrices in
\operatorname{M}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
.
It suffices to find an element
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
such that the map

\operatorname{M}^{0}_{n}(\mathbf{C})\to\operatorname{D}_{n}(\mathbf{C})\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),\quad z\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(g^{-1}zg)

is surjective. We take
g=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(I+E_{i,i+1})
. Note that
g=[\delta_{i\leq j}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
, and we have
g^{-1}=I+[-\delta_{j=i+1}]_{i,j=1}^{n}
. The
(i,i)
-term of
g^{-1}zg
is equal to
e_{i}^{T}g^{-1}zge_{i}
, which computes to

(g^{-T}e_{i})^{T}z(ge_{i})=(e_{i}-e_{i+1})z(e_{1}+e_{2}+\cdots+e_{i})=\sum_{1\leq k\leq i}(z_{i,k}-z_{i+1,k}).

Let
v_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be the matrix whose
\ell
-th row is equal to
[\delta_{j<\ell}]_{j=1}^{n}
, and all other entries are zero. Then
v_{\ell}\in\operatorname{M}^{0}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
and the
(i,i)
-term of
g^{-1}v_{\ell}g
is
-(\ell-1)
if
i=\ell-1
,
\ell-1
if
i=\ell
, and
0
otherwise. Thus
\operatorname{diag}(g^{-1}v_{\ell}g)=(\ell-1)(-e_{\ell-1}+e_{\ell})
. Since the
-e_{\ell-1}+e_{\ell}
span
\operatorname{D}_{n}(\mathbf{C})\cap\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
as
\ell
varies, the claim follows.

Example A.4
.

Let
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
act by conjugation on
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
. Let
U\leq\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
be the subspace of matrices with zero last column and row. We then have

\dim U=(n-1)^{2}-1\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),U)=3.

The argument goes as follows. Let
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
. We first claim that
g\notin U+gUg^{-1}
. Indeed, if
g=u+gvg^{-1}
for
u,v\in U
, then
I=g^{-1}u+vg^{-1}
. Applying this to
e_{n}
, we obtain

\langle e_{n},e_{n}\rangle=\langle(g^{-1}u+vg^{-1})e_{n},e_{n}\rangle=\langle vg^{-1}e_{n},e_{n}\rangle=\langle g^{-1}e_{n},v^{\star}e_{n}\rangle=0

since
v\in U
. This is a contradiction, and so
\operatorname{diam}_{+}^{\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})}(\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C}),U)\geq 3
.

Now let
g=P_{1n}
be the permutation matrix that exchanges
e_{1}
and
e_{n}
, and let
h=P_{2n}
be the permutation matrix that exchanges
e_{2}
and
e_{n}
. Then
U+gUg^{-1}
consists precisely of traceless matrices whose
(1,n)
and
(n,1)
entries are zero, and similarly
U+hUh^{-1}
consists of traceless matrices whose
(2,n)
and
(n,2)
entries are zero. The sum of these two spaces is
\mathfrak{sl}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
. This completes the proof.
