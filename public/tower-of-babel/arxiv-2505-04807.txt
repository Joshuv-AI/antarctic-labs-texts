A Fast Newton Method Under Local Lipschitz Smoothness
S. Gratton, S. Jerad and Ph. L. Toint

Abstract
A new, fast second-order method is proposed that achieves the optimal 𝒪 ( | log ( ϵ ) | ϵ − 3 / 2 ) \mathcal{O}\left(|\log(\epsilon)|\epsilon^{-3/2}\right) complexity to obtain first-order ϵ \epsilon -stationary points. Crucially, this is deduced without assuming the standard global Lipschitz Hessian continuity condition, but only using an appropriate local smoothness requirement. The algorithm exploits Hessian information to compute a Newton step and a negative curvature step when needed, in an approach similar to that of [ 18 ] . Inexact versions of the Newton step and negative curvature are proposed in order to reduce the cost of evaluating second-order information. Details are given of such an iterative implementation using Krylov subspaces. An extended algorithm for finding second-order critical points is also developed and its complexity is again shown to be within a log factor of the optimal one. Initial numerical experiments are discussed for both factorised and Krylov variants, which demonstrate the competitiveness of the proposed algorithm.



Standard nonlinear optimization algorithms such as gradient
descent or Newton’s method are widely used in a vast array of
applications. However their theoretical analysis
typically uses the somewhat restrictive assumptions that
gradients or higher derivatives are globally Lipschitz continuous

[
6
]
. Unfortunately, this assumption does not hold in
simple cases such as polynomials or exponentials. While it can be
argued that the assumption is only necessary on the “tree of iterates”
(that is the union of all segments defined by algorithm’s steps
(see
[
6
, Notes p.47]
for instance), it remains
desirable to relax it more significantly. Some progress
in this direction has been made in
[
24
,
37
]
,
where only local Lipschitz smoothness is assumed, but it only applies to
the convex case.

In the nonconvex case, motivated by the geometry of the
loss-landscape in modern deep learning problems and the success of
normalized gradient descent in this context,
[
38
]
proposed
a new smoothness condition for the analysis of this latter method, called

(L_{0},L_{1})
smoothness, which assumes that there exist constants
L_{0}\geq 0
and
L_{1}>0
, such that,

\|\nabla_{x}^{2}f(x)\|\leq L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x)\|.

(0.1)

This new assumption was shown to be more relevant in practice as it
covers univariate polynomial functions of all degrees, for instance.
We refer the reader to
[
38
,
36
]
for
additional motivation for the study of first-order method under
(
0.1
) and for a complexity
analysis in both the deterministic and stochastic cases.
This proposal generated a new interest in the analysis of nonconvex
gradient descent. New classes of smoothness
and refinements of the initial analysis have been proposed in

[
21
,
36
,
22
,
7
]

both in the exact and stochastic case. Extensions to other classes of
problems or different update rules have been proposed, such as
adaptive-gradient methods
[
15
,
23
]
, variance
reduction
[
28
]
and variational problems
[
30
]

to name just a few. Note also that (
0.1
) can be rephrased
as

\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x)-\nabla_{x}^{1}f(y)\|\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x)\|),\;\;\mbox{ if }\;\;\|x-y\|\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}},

(0.2)

to avoid the use of second-order derivatives (see

[
28
,
36
]
for further discussions on
the equivalence between (
0.1
) and (
0.2
)).

Extending the approach to second-order methods for optimization is
thus of interest, in order to enlarge the applicability of such
results beyond what is used for standard approaches

[
6
]
. To the best of our knowledge, only

[
33
]
has so far proposed an optimal
trust-region method under a Lipschitz smoothness assumptions similar
to (
0.2
). However, the proposed algorithm
unrealistically requires the knowledge of the problem’s Lipschitz
constant, uses a trust region with a fixed radius, and computes an
exact solution of the trust-region subproblem, thus requiring intensive
numerical linear algebra to compute the step at each iteration.

On the other hand, a vast array of work
[
29
,
35
,
39
,
18
]
has been proposed in order to develop fast regularized Newton methods with a complexity guarantee close to
{\cal O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
evaluations to find an

\epsilon
-first order stationary point. Among this work, some required the knowledge of the criticality threshold
[
29
,
35
]
to achieve the bound
{\cal O}\left(\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
. This requirement was bypassed in
[
18
]
by alternating carefully between regularized Newton step and negative curvature and utilizing the current gradient norm to regularize the Newton system. However, this approach incurs an additional
|\log(\epsilon)|
in the complexity. Recently,
[
39
]
combined the regularization proposed in
[
18
]
with the capped-CG analysis developed in
[
29
]
and reduced the extra cost of not knowing the criticality threshold
\epsilon
to
|\log(\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon}))|
which is better than the
|\log(\epsilon)|
of
[
18
]
.

In the present paper, inspired by the new Hessian Lipschitz
smoothness condition proposed in
[
33
]
, we
develop an adaptive Newton’s method that requires at most

{\cal O}\left(|\log(\epsilon)|\epsilon^{-3/2}\right)
evaluations to find an

\epsilon
-first order stationary point. The met hod proposed is in
spirit of
[
18
]
as it
alternates between Newton directions and directions of negative
curvature and also uses negative-curvature information to regularize
the Newton step. Beyond requiring significantly weaker smoothness,
it however differs from the proposal in this
reference in crucial aspects, such as the power of regularization
and the mechanism to accept or reject trial steps. The new method
is fully adaptive and knowledge of the problem’s geometry is
not assumed. Two implementations
are proposed, the first requiring exact negative curvature computation
and exact linear-system solves, and the second allowing more
inexactness and exploiting the structure of nested Krylov subspaces.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
1
starts by
stating our new Lipschitz smoothness condition, describes the general

2

complexity for finding first-order critical
points. Section
3
details an algorithmic enhancement for
the search for a second-order critical point, with its associated
complexity proof discussed in Appendix
B
. Section
4
describes
proposed procedures to compute the step and the incorporation
of preconditionning. The numerical behaviour of the proposed algorithms is
considered in Section
5
and some conclusions and perspectives
are discussed in Section
6

The motivation for our algorithm is similar to that used in
[
18
]
and we first describe its mechanism in very broad lines.
At each iteration, the idea is to minimize a ”doubly regularized” quadratic model of the form

m_{k}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}g_{k}^{\intercal}s+{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}}s^{\intercal}(H_{k}{+}(\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|+\mu_{k})I_{n})s

(1.6)

where
g_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k})
,
H_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\nabla_{x}^{2}f(x_{k})
,
\sigma_{k}
is an adaptive, iteration dependent regularization parameter and
\mu_{k}
and additional (hopefully small) regularization term depending on the type of step taken. The algorithm attempts to select a ”small”
\mu_{k}
and an associated Newton-like trial step using the regularized Hessian
H_{k}+(\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|+\mu_{k})I_{n}
. If this
\mu_{k}
is too large or its associated trial step cannot be accepted (according to criteria detailed below), then a trial step along an approximate negative curvature direction is attempted.
The algorithm then proceeds, as is typical for adaptive regularization methods, by deciding if the trial step can be accepted, or has to be rejected. The parameter
\sigma_{k}
is then updated before starting a new iteration.

The computation an a-priori regularization and an associated trial step is clearly important, and
can be organized in more than one way. Because we have two different implementations in mind (one uses matrix factorizations and the other an iterative Krylov approach, see Section
4
), we take for now a more abstract point of view of this computation and only assume, at this stage, that there exist a
Stepcomp
procedure, which, given the current gradient, Hessian and a few algorithmic constants, produces both a regularization parameter
\mu_{k}
and a trial step
s_{k}^{trial}
.

This remains admittedly vague at this stage, but hopefully provides some intuition for the formal definition of the
AN2CLS

Further comments are in order at this point.

1.

The reader familiar with the
AN2C
algorithm proposed in
[
18
]
will notice the similarities, but also the differences between this algorithm and

, the most important being the power of the regularization. In

and inspired by the analysis of the convex case
[
25
,
12
]
, the regularization has the form
\sqrt{\sigma_{k}\|g_{k}\|}
whereas
the regularization in
AN2CLS
is of the order of
\|g_{k}\|
as
is clear from (

). Note that choosing a
regularization proportional to
\|g_{k}\|
has already been considered in

[
11
,
10
]
for the convex case, with good initial
numerical results and optimal complexity rates. In

[
11
]
a regularization proportional to
\|g_{k}\|
was
shown to be universal for a large class of convex functions ranging from those with Lipschitz continuous gradient to those with Lipschitz continuous
third-order derivative. In
[
10
]
, it was proved
to be optimal for a class of quasi-self concordant functions (see the
above references for more details).
AN2CLS

generalizes this proposal to the nonconvex case with local smoothness only,
while keeping the global rate of convergence close to optimal, as we show below.
Older proposals considering a Newton step regularized with a
\|g_{k}\|
term in a linesearch setting may be found in
[
31
,
26
]
but the derived complexity is suboptimal in both cases.

2.

The new method also differs from that of
[
18
]
in the mechanism used to accept or reject the trial step. Because the Lipschitz condition is now only local, one needs to explicitly check that the gradient at the trial point does not grow out of reasonable bounds (see (
1.12
)) or too fast compared to the length of the trial step to be accepted (see (

)). Large gradients at the trial point can only be accepted if the step is sufficiently large.

3.

When

)-(

). Considering both conditions (

) and (

) has already been proposed in
[

in order to develop scalable variants of cubic regularization.

4.

In Step 1 of

, the words ”If not already available” are justified by the observation that, if iteration

) and accepted it, the value of

Condition (

1.14
) implies that

\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\,\|g_{k}\|\,\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}\leq(s_{k}^{trial})^{\intercal}(H_{k}+(\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|+\mu_{k})I_{n})s_{k}^{trial},

and (
1.22
) thus follows.
The second equation results from (
1.18
) and (
1.19
).

\Box

It results from this lemma that the bound on local Lipschitz error stated in Lemma
1.2
holds for
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
with
\sigma_{k}\geq\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}
or for
k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}
with
\sigma_{k}\geq\frac{\theta\kappa_{C}}{\delta^{2}}
.

We now give a lower bound on the local second-order Taylor approximation at iteration
k
depending on the step’s type.

Lemma 1.5

Let
k
be an iteration of
AN2CLS
. Then,

-(g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}+\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k})\geq\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}\|^{2}\;\;\mbox{ for}\;\;k\in{\cal I}^{newt}

(1.24)

and

-(g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}+\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k})\geq\frac{1}{2}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{k}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}\|^{3}\;\;\mbox{ for }\;\;k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}.

(1.25)

Proof.

Consider first the case where
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
. By using the definition of
r_{k}^{trial}
in (
1.17
), (
1.16
) and (
1.14
), we obtain that

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}^{trial}+\frac{1}{2}(s_{k}^{trial})^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}

\displaystyle=(r_{k}^{trial})^{\intercal}s_{k}^{trial}-\frac{1}{2}(s_{k}^{trial})^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}-\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}-\mu_{k}\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(s_{k}^{trial})^{\intercal}(H_{k}+\mu_{k}I_{n})s_{k}^{trial}-\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{k}\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2},

yielding (
1.24
).

Suppose now that
k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}
. Since (
1.18
) and (
1.19
) hold and since Step 4 of
AN2CLS
is executed when
\mu_{k}>\kappa_{C}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|
, we deduce that,

\displaystyle g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}^{trial}+\frac{1}{2}(s_{k}^{trial})^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}^{trail}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}u_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}u_{k}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}}\mu_{k}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}

(1.26)

yielding the first inequality in (
1.25
).
The second inequality is obtained by substituting the bound

\frac{1}{2}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}\sigma_{k}\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\sigma_{k}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{k}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{3}

in (
1.26
), where we used (
1.23
) to deduce the last equality.

2
Complexity Analysis of
AN2CLS

We start our analysis by examining under what conditions an iteration of the
AN2CLS
must be successful. We have already seen in Lemma
1.4
that
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|
must be less than
\delta
for large enough
\sigma_{k}
, and now investigate the conditions of a successful iteration under this assumption. We first
consider the case of Newton iterations and examine the conditions under which the algorithm moves to Step 5, which is necessary if iteration
k
is to be successful.

Lemma 2.1

Suppose that AS.1 and AS.3 hold and that
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\leq\delta
. If
k\in{\cal I}^{g\nearrow}
, then we have that

\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\left((1+\kappa_{\theta}+\kappa_{C})+\sqrt{(1+\kappa_{\theta}+\kappa_{C})^{2}+\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{\sigma_{k}}}\right)}.

(2.1)

Moreover, the algorithm proceeds to Step 5 for all
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
provided

\sigma_{k}\geq\frac{1}{\vartheta}\left(\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}\right).

(2.2)

Proof.

Consider
k\in{\cal I}^{g\nearrow}
such that
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\leq\delta
.
Using (
1.5
), the fact that

\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{trial})\|{>}\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{2}
since
k\in{\cal I}^{g\nearrow}
, (
1.15
) and the inequality
\mu_{k}\leq\kappa_{C}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|
, we derive that

\displaystyle\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{2}

\displaystyle<\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{trial})\|\leq\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{trial})-g_{k}-H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}\|+\|g_{k}+H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)}{2}\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}+(\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|+\mu_{k})\|s_{k}^{{trial}}\|+\|r_{k}^{trial}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)}{2}\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}+(1+\kappa_{C})\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{{trial}}\|+\kappa_{\theta}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|.

This gives a quadratic inequality in
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|
whose solution is given by

\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\geq\frac{-(1+\kappa_{\theta}+\kappa_{C})\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|+\sqrt{(1+\kappa_{\theta}+\kappa_{C})^{2}\sigma_{k}\|g_{k}\|^{2}+\|g_{k}\|(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)}}{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)}.

Taking the conjugate in the last inequality and then factorizing
\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|
in the resulting denominator gives (
2.1
). A comparison of this bound with the second part of (
1.8
) and the definition of
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny slow}}
in Step 0 shows that the second part of (
1.8
) must fail if (
2.2
) holds. Thus the algorithm proceeds to Step 5 if
k\in{\cal I}^{g\nearrow}
and
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\leq\delta
.
Now, if
k\in{\cal I}_{k}^{g\searrow}={\cal I}^{newt}\setminus{\cal I}^{g\nearrow}
, then the first part of (
1.8
) fails and the algorithm also proceeds to Step 5. Thus it does so for all
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
.

\Box

Assuming now that Step 5 is reached, iteration
k
can only be successful if (
1.12
) fails. The condition under which this must happen is the object of the next lemma.

Lemma 2.2

Suppose that AS.1 and AS.3 hold and let
k
such that
\|s_{k}\|\leq\delta
. Then,

\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{{trial}})\|\leq\left(\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{2\sigma_{k}}+1+\kappa_{C}+\kappa_{\theta}\right)\|g_{k}\|\;\;\mbox{ for }\;\;k\in{\cal I}^{newt}

(2.3)

and

\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{{trial}})\|\leq\left(\kappa_{C}^{2}\theta^{2}\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{2\sigma_{k}}+1+\frac{\kappa_{C}\mu_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\right)\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\epsilon}\;\;\mbox{ for }\;\;k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}.

(2.4)

Moreover, condition (
1.12
) fails provided

\sigma_{k}\geq\frac{1}{3(1-\eta_{2})}\left(\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}\right).

(2.5)

Proof.

First note that Lemma
1.2
holds because
\|s_{k}\|\leq\delta
.
Let
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
. From the triangular inequality, (
1.5
), (
1.15
),
the fact that
\mu_{k}\leq\kappa_{C}\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|
when
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
and
(
1.22
), we deduce that,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{trial})\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\frac{\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}}{2}+\|H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}+g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\frac{\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}}{2}+\|(\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|+\mu_{k})s_{k}^{trial}\|+\|r_{k}^{trial}\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\frac{\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}}{2}+(1+\kappa_{C})\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|+\kappa_{\theta}\|g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|}{2\sigma_{k}}+(1+\kappa_{C}+\kappa_{\theta})\|g_{k}\|

and (
2.3
) follows.

Now, if
k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}
, we obtain from (
1.18
) and (
1.19
) that

\|H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}\|=\frac{\theta\kappa_{C}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\|H_{k}u_{k}\|=\frac{\theta\kappa_{C}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\sqrt{u_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}^{2}u_{k}}\\
\leq\frac{\kappa_{C}\mu_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}.

Using (
1.5
), (
1.23
), the last inequality and the fact that
\|g_{k}\|\geq\epsilon

before termination and the bound
\epsilon\leq 1
, we derive that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}^{1}f(x_{k}+s_{k}^{trial})\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\frac{\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}}{2}+\|H_{k}s_{k}^{trial}+g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\frac{\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}}{2}{+}\frac{\kappa_{C}\mu_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}+\|g_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{C}^{2}\theta^{2}\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|}{2\sigma_{k}}+\frac{\kappa_{C}\mu_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\epsilon}+\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{\epsilon},

which yields (
2.4
).
Finally, a comparison of (
2.3
) with the definition of
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny k}}
in (
1.9
) and
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upnewt}}
in Step 0 shows that (
1.12
) fails for
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
if (
2.5
) holds. Similarly, comparing (
2.4
) with the definition of
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny k}}
in (
1.10
) shows that (
1.12
) also fails for
k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}
if (
2.5
) holds. It therefore fails for all
k\geq 0
under this condition.

\Box

We are now in a position to establish the conditions under which iteration
k
is successful, from which we derive a crucial upper bound on the regularization parameter
\sigma_{k}
.

Lemma 2.3

Suppose that AS.1 and AS.3 hold. Then, for all
k\geq 0
,

\sigma_{k}\leq\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max}}}{\epsilon},

(2.6)

where

\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\gamma_{3}\max\left(\sigma_{0},\,\frac{\kappa_{C}^{2}\theta^{2}}{\delta^{2}},\,\frac{1}{\delta^{2}},\,\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1})}{3(1-\eta_{2})},\,\frac{L_{0}+L_{1}}{\vartheta}\right).

(2.7)

Proof.

First consider
k\in{\cal I}^{newt}
and suppose that

\sigma_{k}\geq\max\left[\sigma_{0},\frac{1}{\delta^{2}},\,\frac{(\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1})}{{3}(1-\eta_{2})},\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{\vartheta}\right].

(2.8)

so that (
1.22
) yields that
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\leq\delta
and Lemma
1.2
applies.
We may thus use Lemma
2.1
to deduce that the algorithm proceeds to Step 5.
Now, using the Hessian tensor Lipschitz approximation error stated in (
1.4
),
(
1.24
) and (
1.22
), we obtain that

\displaystyle 1-\rho_{k}=\frac{f(x_{k}+s_{k})-f(x_{k})-g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}-\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}}{-g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}-\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{3}}{6\,\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\|s_{k}^{trial}\|}{6\,\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\|g_{k}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1})}{6\,\sigma_{k}}.

(2.9)

Hence, if (
2.8
) holds, then
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{2}
. Moreover, (
2.8
) also ensures that
(
1.12
) fails, as shown in Lemma
2.2
. As a consequence, iteration
k
is successful.

Consider now the case where
k\in{\cal I}^{ncurv}
, in which case Step 5 of the algorithm is always executed. Suppose that

\sigma_{k}\geq\max\left[\sigma_{0},\frac{\kappa_{C}^{2}\theta^{2}}{\delta^{2}},\,\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{3(1-\eta_{2})}\right],

(2.10)

again ensuring, because of (
1.23
), that
\|s_{k}^{trial}\|\leq\delta
and that the Lipschitz error bound (
1.4
) holds. Using this bound and (
1.25
), we derive that

1-\rho_{k}=\frac{f(x_{k}+s_{k})-f(x_{k})-g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}-\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}}{-g_{k}^{\intercal}s_{k}-\frac{1}{2}s_{k}^{\intercal}H_{k}s_{k}}\leq\frac{(L_{0}+L_{1}\|g_{k}\|)\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{3}}{6\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{k}\|g_{k}\|\|s_{k}^{trial}\|^{3}}\leq\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{3\sigma_{k}}.

Thus, (
2.10
) ensures that
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{2}
. We then apply Lemma
2.2
and deduce from (
2.10
) that (
1.12
) fails and therefore that iteration
k
is successful.

As a consequence, we obtain by taking the maximum of both (
2.8
) and (
2.10
), that iteration
k
is successful and
\rho_{k}\geq\eta_{2}
provided

\sigma_{k}\geq\max\left[\sigma_{0},\frac{1}{\delta^{2}},\,\frac{\kappa_{C}^{2}\theta^{2}}{\delta^{2}},\,\frac{(\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1})}{3(1-\eta_{2})},\frac{\frac{L_{0}}{\|g_{k}\|}+L_{1}}{\vartheta}\right].

The update formula (
1.13
) and the facts that
\|g_{k}\|\geq\epsilon
before termination and that
\epsilon\leq 1
then imply (
2.6
)-(
2.7
).

We now provide also an upper-bound on
|{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}|

[

Lemma 2.4

Suppose that AS.1, AS.3 and AS.4 hold. Then,

|{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}|\leq\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upnewt}}}{{\epsilon}}\right)\frac{|{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}|}{\log(2)}+\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upncurv}}}{{\epsilon}}\right)\frac{|{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}|}{\log(2)}+\frac{|\log(\epsilon)|+\log(\|g_{0}\|)}{\log(2)}+1,

(2.11)

where
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upnewt}}
is defined in Step 0 and
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upncurv}}
is given by

\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upncurv}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\frac{3}{2}\theta^{2}\kappa_{C}^{2}(1-\eta_{2})+1+\frac{\kappa_{C}\kappa_{B}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}}.

(2.12)

Proof.

First observe that if
k\in{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}

\|g_{k+1}\|\leq\frac{\|g_{k}\|}{2}

k\in{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}

1.12

\kappa_{k}
defined in (

\sigma_{k}\geq\sigma_{\min}

{\mu_{k}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{B}}{\theta}

\frac{\|g_{k+1}\|}{\|g_{k}\|}\leq\frac{\frac{3}{2}\theta^{2}\kappa_{C}^{2}(1-\eta_{2})+1+\frac{\kappa_{C}\mu_{k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}}{\epsilon}\leq\frac{\frac{3}{2}\theta^{2}\kappa_{C}^{2}(1-\eta_{2})+1+\frac{\kappa_{C}\kappa_{B}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\min}}}}{\epsilon}=\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upncurv}}}{\epsilon}.

(2.13)

Successively using the fact that
{\cal S}_{k}={\cal S}_{k}^{decr}\cup\,{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}\cup\,{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}

\|g_{k+1}\|

\|g_{k}\|

i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{\|g_{0}\|}

Combining the previous lemmas, we are now able to state the complexity of the

Theorem 2.5

6
Conclusions and Perspectives

We have proposed a second-order method that can be proved to handle functions whose Hessians only satisfy a local Lipschitz condition. Our algorithm is inspired by
[
18
]
and alternatively uses a regularized Newton step or a negative curvature step when appropriate.
The proposed algorithm automatically adapts to the problem’s geometry and its use does not require prior knowledge of the Lipschitz constant.
We show that at most
\mathcal{O}(|\log(\epsilon)|\epsilon^{-3/2})
iterations are required to find an
\epsilon
first-order approximate point. We have also proposed an algorithmic variant that requires at most
\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{-3}\right)
iterations to find an approximate second-order critical point.

Two implementations of the framework have been discussed, the first using exact solutions of the involved linear systems and eigenvalue subproblems, the second employing approximations based on nested Krylov subspaces. Numerical experiments suggest that these methods improve slightly on
AN2CK
and
AN2CE

[
18
]
, which were already show to be competitive with more standard algorithms using trust-regions or cubic regularization.

Promising lines of work include inexact or stochastic variants
[
35
,
34
]
, variants where the function value is never evaluated to improve reliability on noisy problems
[
16
,
17
]
, subspace variants and application to further classes of problems, such as minmax optimization
[
32
]
.

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}}\,\frac{\eta_{1}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}}{2\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}}\,\|g_{i}\|\geq\frac{\eta_{1}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}\epsilon_{1}|{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}|}{2\max\left(\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max1}}}{\epsilon_{1}}},\,\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max2}}}}{\epsilon_{2}}\right)},

and hence after rearranging and using Young’s inequality with
p=\frac{3}{2}
and
q=3
, we derive that

|{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}|\leq\frac{2(f(x_{0})-f_{\rm low})\max\left[\sqrt{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max1}}}\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2},\sqrt{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max2}}}(\frac{2}{3}\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\frac{1}{3}\epsilon_{2}^{-3})\right]}{\eta_{1}\theta^{3}\kappa_{C}^{3}}\leq\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny ncurvhess}}(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})

(2.13)

with
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny ncurvhess}}
defined in (
3.7
).

We now provide a bound on
|{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}|
.
Using AS.2, (
1.24
), that
\|s_{i}^{decr}\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny slow}}}
, the fact that
\|g_{i}\|\geq\epsilon_{1}
before termination, and (
2.5
) yields that

\displaystyle f(x_{0})-f_{\rm low}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}}f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1})\geq\sum_{i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}}\eta_{1}\,\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}\|g_{i}\|\|s_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}}\eta_{1}\frac{\|g_{i}\|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{i}}\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny slow}}^{2}}\geq\frac{\epsilon_{1}|{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}|\eta_{1}}{\max\left(\sqrt{\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max1}}}{\epsilon_{1}}},\,\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max2}}}}{\epsilon_{2}}\right)\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny slow}}^{2}}.

Again, by the same arguments used to prove (
2.13
), we derive that

|{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}|\leq\frac{(f(x_{0})-f_{\rm low})\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny slow}}^{2}\max\left(\sqrt{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max1}}}\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2},\sqrt{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max2}}}(\frac{2}{3}\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\frac{1}{3}\epsilon_{2}^{-3})\right)}{\eta_{1}}\leq\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny decrhess}}(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})

(2.14)

with
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny decrhess}}
defined in (
3.7
).

We finally prove a bound on
|{\cal S}_{k}^{so}|
.
Using AS.2 and the lower bound on the decrease of the function values
(
2.3
), (
2.5
) and the fact that
|\lambda_{\min}(H_{i})|\geq\epsilon_{2}
, we conclude that

f(x_{0})-f_{\rm low}\geq\sum_{i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{so}}f(x_{i})-f(x_{i+1})\geq\sum_{i\in{\cal S}_{k}^{so}}\,\frac{\eta_{1}|\lambda_{\min}(H_{i})|}{2\sigma_{i}}\,\geq|{\cal S}_{k}^{so}|\,\frac{\eta_{1}\epsilon_{2}}{2\max\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max1}}}{\epsilon_{1}},\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max2}}}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}}\right)}.

Rearranging the last inequality and using Young’s inequality, we obtain that

|{\cal S}_{k}^{so}|\leq\frac{2(f(x_{0})-f_{\rm low})\max\left({\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max2}}}\epsilon_{2}^{-3},\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny max1}}(\frac{2}{3}\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\frac{1}{3}\epsilon_{2}^{-3})\right)}{\eta_{1}}\leq\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny so}}(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})

(2.15)

where
\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny so}}
is defined at (
3.6
).

Substituting now (
2.15
), (
2.14
) and
(
2.13
) in the bound (
2.7
) on
{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}
yields

\displaystyle|{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}|

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upnewt}}}{{\epsilon_{1}}}\right)\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny decrhess}}(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})}{\log(2)}+\log\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny upncurv}}}{{\epsilon_{1}}}\right)\frac{\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny curvhess}}(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})}{\log(2)}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{|\log(\epsilon_{1})|+\log(\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny gpi}})}{\log(2)}+1\right)\Big(\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny so}}(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})+1\Big).

Combining this last inequality with (
2.15
), (
2.14
)
and (
2.13
) in

|{\cal S}_{k}|=|{\cal S}_{k}^{g\searrow}|+|{\cal S}_{k}^{ncurv}|+|{\cal S}_{k}^{so}|+|{\cal S}_{k}^{decr}|

and using the definitions of
\kappa_{\star,hess}
(
3.5
) and
\kappa_{\star,log}
(
3.6
) gives that

|{\cal S}_{k}|\leq\kappa_{\star,hess}\left(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3}\right)+\kappa_{\star,log}|\log(\epsilon_{1})|(\epsilon_{1}^{-3/2}+\epsilon_{2}^{-3})+\frac{|\log(\epsilon_{1})|+\log(\kappa_{\mbox{\tiny gpi}})}{\log(2)}+1,

which proves the first part of the theorem.
The second part follows from the last inequality, Lemma
1.3
and the bound (
2.5
).

\Box
