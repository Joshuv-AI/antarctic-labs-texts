Introduction

Rubiaceae also known as the madder or coffee family, is one of the largest family of angiosperms with 620 genera and 13,500 species. They are well known for their rich diversity of alkaloids. Mitragyna, a genus under this family is extensively studied for its medicinal properties and have a long history of traditional usage. They are known for their antinociceptive, antidepressant, anxiolytic, sedative, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, stimulant, gastrointestinal, and antidiabetic activities, making it a potential source of development of new drugs.1

Botanical aspects of Mitragyna consist of shrubs or trees with dense inflorescence. The flowers with heart shaped stamens arranged in compact round heads, fused with ovaries and abundant fruit.2 The Mitragyna genus is well known for its alkaloid with extensive studies done on M. speciosa. A total of 79 secondary metabolites including alkaloids, polyphenols, flavonoids, terpenoids, triterpenoids, saponins and secoiroids has been documented 1 till date. There is a total of 9 species under the genus which includes M. speciosa, M. diversifolia, M. rubrostipulata, M. inermis, M. stipulosa, M. hirsuta, M. parvifolia, M. rotundifolia and M. tubulosa.

This review is based on a comprehensive search of databases such as Google Scholar, Scopus, PubMed, and ScienceDirect. Most reviews till date focus heavily on M. speciosa, emphasizing its pharmacological activities, traditional uses, toxicity, clinical implications and biosynthesis. However, there remains a notable scarcity of review on their plant biotechnological approaches. Search terms included “Mitragyna species” “Mitragyna biotechnology,” “Mitragyna in vitro culture,” “shoot culture,” “Mitragyna applications,” “Mitragynine,” “Indole alkaloids,” “Mitragyna ethnobotany” “Mitragyna chemistry,” “Kratom”, “indole alkaloid biosynthesis” “mitragynine biosynthesis”.

In addition to the biotechnology of M. speciosa, this review aims to compile the research papers of other eight Mitragyna species published until December 2024 and is updated following the two key reviews by Ahmad et al., 2022 and Annuar et al 2024.1, 2, 3 This review focuses on their ethnobotany, pharmacological applications, phytochemical constituents, indole alkaloid biosynthesis, and majorly on the biotechnological approaches and synthetic biology of kratom alkaloids.

Ethnobotany of Mitragyna spp

Mitragyna species have a long history of traditional uses in different parts of the world (Table 1). M. speciosa is one of the most studied and documented species of Mitragyna. It is also commonly known as ‘kratom’. Phytochemicals reports include alkaloids (Fig. 1), terpenoid saponins, flavonoids, glycosides and polyphenols. The two most active alkaloid found in M. speciosa are mitragynine (23) and 7-hydroxymitragynine (27), with higher affinity toward opioid receptors.4 Mitragynine is major constituent in the plant.4 The two active compounds exhibit morphine-like effect. Though 7-hydroxymitragynine is present in low amount,4 it is more potent than mitragynine and morphine.5 The plant has shown numerous pharmacological effects such as analgesic, antinociceptive, sedative, depressant, muscle relaxant, anticancer, neuromuscular activity, anti-inflammatory, opiate, antimicrobial and antioxidant activity.3, 6

Mitragyna diversifolia is a small deciduous tree found in Bangladesh, Philippines and other Southeast Asian countries. It is one of the least explored species of Mitragyna. It consists of alkaloids (Fig. 1), triterpenoids, flavonoids phenolic acid and coumarin. The main alkaloids found were mitradiversifoline (58), specionoxeine-N (4)-oxide (59), 7-hydroxyisopaynantheine (60) and 3-dehydropaynantheine (61).7 Some studies have revealed it to exhibit properties such as antioxidant, antidiabetic, antidiarrheal, cytotoxic, cholinesterase inhibitory and anticancer.7, 8, 9, 10, 11 Recently, presence of rhynchophylline and mitraphylline was reported in the Thai species of M. diversifolia.12

Mitragyna rubrostipulata grows primarily in wet tropical environment. It is indigenous to East Africa. Some of the alkaloids reported in the plant are shown in Fig. 1. The leaves, bark and root of the plants were reported to have medicinal properties.13 Water decoction of leaves of the plant is taken orally to treat fatigue, weakness, as a stimulant and to treat anemia.14 The powdered bark is taken orally or inhaled to treat gastrointestinal pain, headache and urogenital infections.14 The root is reported to have antimalarial, antipyretic and stimulant activities.15

Mitragyna inermis is a shrub found in swampy areas of tropical Africa. They have white fragrant flowers. It is an important febrifuge drug.16 Reports have shown the presence of alkaloids (Fig. 1), tannins, flavonoids, triterpenes, steroids, coumarins, saponosides, carotenoids and quinone compounds.1 Numerous pharmacological studies have been done which revealed the presence of antimicrobial, antibacterial, antiviral, antiamnesic, neuroprotective, myorelaxant and antispasmodic properties.17 From hydromethanol extract of M. inermis, it was found that there was significant decrease in the proliferation of Plasmodium falciparium.18 Leaf, stem bark and root extract (acetone and methanol) of M. inermis was tested against Bacillus subtilis, Pseudomonas syringae and Cladosporium herbarum. The results indicated strong inhibition against these bacteria.19 A significantly reduced number of fecal egg counts of Haemonchus contortus was observed using powdered leaves of M. inermis in animal feed for anthelmintic activity.20 The root acetone extracts of M. inermis were found to have active antioxidant compounds with strong radical scavenging activity against DPPH.21 The leaf decoction of M. inermis was tested on mice with cognitive impairments. After the treatment, the activity levels of superoxide dismutase and catalase were found to increase, indicating the plant to be neuroprotective.22

Mitragyna stipulosa is native to tropical Africa. The leaves, bark and root of the plants were reported to have medicinal properties.13 Some of the alkaloids reported in the are shown in Fig. 1. Leaf and bark decoction is used to treat urinary disease.14 Bark is also reported to treat fatigue, anemia and sickle cell.23 The bark is combined with leaf of Mangifera indica and taken orally to treat madmen disease or eye infections.24

Mitragyna hirsuta is commonly known an Kra thum khok. It is found in the tropical and subtropical regions of Asia and Africa. One of the important oxindole alkaloid, mitraphylline is found in M. hirsuta13 (Fig. 1). Root water decoction is taken orally to treat pregnancy or reproductive problem.25

Mitragyna parvifolia also known as Kaim or Kadam is a deciduous tree native to India and Sri Lanka. It is highly endangered and economically beneficial tree. The leaves and stem barks of M. parvifolia are reported to have a range of indole and oxindole alkaloids (Fig. 1). They are known for their properties such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticonvulsant, antimicrobial, antipyretic, anthelmintic and antinociceptive.26 Acetone extract of stem and leaves of M. parvifolia was found to exhibit cytotoxic effect on HeLa cell lines.27 Ethanolic extract of M. parvifolia leaves were found to have analgesic potential and were nontoxic even at high doses of 1500 mg/kg.28 The tree is also used as a source of timber to manufacture furniture, paper and construction materials.29

Mitragyna rotundifolia is a tree that grows primarily in the seasonally dry tropical biome and is native to Darjiling to China (South Yunnan) and Indo-China. Some of the alkaloids reported in the plant are shown in Fig. 1. Leaves of M. rotundifolia are used to treat diarrhea and stomach pain and is boiled and used in bath for facture and lumbago.30 Its bark is used to treat malaria.15 Root decoction of the plant is used to treat rheumatoid arthritis.31

Mitragyna tubulosa is a tree native to Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Sri Lanka. Some of the alkaloids reported in the plant are shown in Fig. 1. It grows in the wet tropical biome. There is no record of pharmaceutical uses till date.32

Mitragynine pharmacology

Mitragyna speciosa (Korth.) Havil. (Kratom) is the species most reported to contain psychoactive alkaloids especially mitragynine. This alkaloid content depends on the season,70 geographical feature 71, 72 and age or part of the plant.73 The optimal cultivation for higher yield of mitragynine was reported to be in soils with rich calcium and magnesium content and during late summer especially for Kratom from East Coast region in Southern Thailand.72 Mitragynine is metabolized into 7-hydroxymitragynine which is another psychoactive alkaloid but found in lesser abundance in the plant.4 They are found to act on the μ-, δ-, and κ-opioid receptors in the brain resulting in opium-like effects.74 They act as partial agonists at μ −opioid receptor and competitive antagonists at δ- and κ-opioid receptors.75 Some activities of mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine extensively studied over the past years are antinociceptive, antidepressant, anxiolytic, sedative, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, stimulant, gastrointestinal, and antidiabetic activities (Fig. 2).

Antinociceptive activity

Antinociceptive effects are produced by opioids in spinal, supraspinal and peripheral areas through receptors such as μ-, δ-, and κ-opioid receptors (Fig. 2).76 In pre-clinical animal studies, mitragynine was found to act as partial agonist at μ −opioid receptor (MOR).77 When an opioid bind to MOR, it activates G-protein-coupled-receptor (GPCR) initiating β-arrestin pathway activation resulting in sedation, constipation and respiratory problems.78, 79 However, when mitragynine binds to MOR, the activated GPCR does not initiate β-arrestin pathway instead initiates several protein–protein interactions and protein-kinases interactions, including c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1 and 2 (ERK1/2) and p38 kinase (p38K), regulating the antinociception and opioid withdrawal properties of mitragynine.80 Mitragynine acts as competitive antagonist at δ-opioid receptor (DOR) and κ-opioid receptor (KOR). This also results antinoceptive and antidepressant effects, reducing addiction by alleviating opioid withdrawal symptoms.79, 81 Although mitragynine is often reported to exhibit less addictive potential compared to classical opioids, accumulating evidence indicates that kratom use may lead to dependence and withdrawal symptoms. Studies in regular users have demonstrated significant levels of dependence and craving, while regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration have issued warnings regarding its abuse potential and associated health risks.81

Sedative activity

Interaction of mitragynine with MOR was found to induce sedative activity (Fig. 2). An in vivo study using rota-rod test was done to evaluate the sedative activity of mitragynine. The amount of time spent on the rod was calculated using sedated rat. It was found that sedative effects of mitragynine resulted in increase in dopamine accumulation in the brain, leading to relaxation and lower locomotor activity.82 Similar results were found in a study conducted by Henningfield and team (2022), where they observed signs such as lethargy, impaired motor functions, abnormal behavior and respiratory effects in rats administered with oral mitragynine.83

Stimulant activity

Mitragynine interacts with MOR, DOR and adrenergic receptors in brain for stimulant activity 84 (Fig. 2). Adrenergic receptors (α and β) releases neurotransmitters such as epinephrine and norepinephrine and regulates the sympathetic nervous system.85 The stimulant activity of mitragynine has been demonstrated by a previous study, through the administration of mitragynine at different doses (10, 30, and 100 mg/kg bw), followed by a locomotor test in rodents. The lower doses of mitragynine were found to exert stimulant effects with increased number of line crossings, an indicator of locomotor activity and suppression of dopamine. Another study found that mitragynine binds with α- adrenoreceptors, facilitating locomotion, memory and mood in rodents.86

Anti-inflammatory and analgesic activity

Inflammation is a biological response caused due to harmful stimulus and analgesia is to relieve pain. The cyclooxygenase isoform; cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) catalyzes the synthesis of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) which mediates inflammatory responses (Fig. 2). Studies found mitragynine to inhibit COX-2 and reduce the production of PGE2 showing potential anti-inflammatory activity.87, 88 The inhibition of COX-2 and protein expression results in non-opioid receptor pain relieving effects.38, 89 Mitragynine blocks the neuronal Ca2+ channels which inhibits the release of neurotransmitters and reduces the transmission of pain.90 Mitragynine is converted to 7-hydroxymitragynine in mouse and human liver, which is found to be much more MOR agonist.91 Mitragynine was also found to alleviate pain via non-opioid receptor pathways. Rats showed strong writhing behavior when injected with acetic acid. However, when treated with high dose of mitragynine, this behavior was reduced. It was found that mitragynine suppressed the expression of TRPV1, a pain receptor which plays crucial role in thermoregulation and nociception. While TRPV1 involvement has been suggested, current evidence is largely limited to preclinical and mechanistic studies, and its role in human analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects remains to be further explored.92

Antipsychotic activity

Psychosis is condition of mind where people face difficulties in determining reality. Psychotic symptoms are related to abnormal dopaminergic D2 receptors (Fig. 3). Antipsychotic drugs are made to inhibit these receptors. The animal models used in psychosis are developed based on the Schizophrenia neurochemical theory including the dopamine and glutamate neurotransmitters.93 Mitragynine was found to block the dopaminergic D2 receptors and serotonin, alleviating psychotic symptoms.94 In 2011, a study investigated the effect of alkaloid extract of M. speciosa leaves (mitragynine) on exploratory behaviour, short-term memory and motor coordination in Male Swiss albino mice using Y-maze and rota-rod tests, respectively. The mice were orally administered with 20, 40 and 80 mg/kg alkaloid extract using an intragastric gavage needle. They observed enhanced exploratory behaviour of mice which were correlated to decrease in stress.95

Neurobehavioral modulation of mitragynine: antipsychotic, anxiolytic and antidepressant activities. HPA, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis; CRH, corticotrophin-releasing hormone; ACTH, adrenocorticotropic hormone; GABAergic, ɣ-amino-butyric acid.

Anxiolytic activity

Anxiety disorder is a common type of mental illness related to anxiolytic activity. Mitragynine interacts with dopaminergic, γ-amino-butyric acid (GABA)ergic and opioidergic systems in brain, producing anxiolytic activity (Fig. 3). A study was conducted with different concentrations of mitragynine administrated to rats in an open field. There was decrease in locomotor activity indicating the decrease in anxiety level. The results indicate that the anxiolytic activity of mitragynine induces calmness and reduced the feeling of anxiety in the rats.104 Other studies also found that activation of KOR and DOR, and the interaction of GABAergic and dopaminergic pathways resulted in lower level of fear and anxiety.96, 97

Anti-depressant activity

Mitragynine shows antidepressant activity by regulating the central and autonomic nervous system (Fig. 3). Dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis is commonly associated with stress, where increased corticosterone levels reflect the physiological response to stress.107 may involve modulation of the HPA axis along with recovery of monoamine neurotransmitter levels.98 Mice subjected to stress, when administered with mitragynine were found to have lower level of corticosterone. Thus, the anti-depressant activity of mitragynine may involve modulation of the HPA axis along with recovery of monoamine neurotransmitter levels.99 Mice treated with different extracts of Kratom revealed that Kratom syrup showed similar effects on brain to the standard antidepressant drug, fluoxetine based on their local field potential. This result also indicates the possibility of using Kratom for antidepressant drug development.100

Antidiabetic activity

Mitragynine was found to exhibit insulin like action on stimulation of glucose transport. It stimulates the glucose uptake in muscle cells especially L8 cells. This is achieved by activation of glucose transporter (GLUT) 4. GLUT4 is redistributed in the plasma membrane via phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)- dependent pathway, which is a common signaling route activated by insulin (Fig. 4). A study examined water, methanolic and crude alkaloidal extracts from M. speciosa leaves (mitragynine) for the enhancement of glucose transport in L8 myotubes of rats. The results showed significant increase in the rate of glucose uptake with the tested extracts. This activity of M. speciosa was linked to enhanced activity of key enzymes involved in insulin-stimulated glucose transport, contributing to its acute effects, as well as increased GLUT1 content for its long-term effects.101

Peripheral physiological effects of mitragynine; antidiabetic and gastrointestinal effects. GLUT, glucose transporter; PI3K, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase, 2-DG, 2-deoxy-D-glucose.

Gastrointestinal effects

Mitragynine is suggested to reduce gastric acid secretion by interacting with MOR in the CNS (Fig. 4).102 It was found to decrease the ulcers induced by reflux esophagitis and ulcer index like sucralfate and omeprazole respectively.103 Administration of 3–30 µg mitragynine to the fourth ventricle of brain of rat caused an inhibition of 2-deoxy-D-glucose, a compound which mimics glucose to stimulate gastric acid production. These findings suggested that mitragynine has a morphine-like action on gastric acid secretion in the CNS.102

Besides M. speciosa several studies report similar pharmacological properties on various extracts of other Mitragyna species. Mitragyna species exhibit a wide range of pharmacological properties across various plant parts. Leaves across species are particularly known for their analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects. M. rubrostipulata and M. parvifolia leaves offer antipyretic and anti-inflammatory benefits, while M. inermis and M. stipulosa include neuroprotective and antimalarial actions in traditional medicine.22, 104 Stem bark among several species, such as M. inermis, and M. stipulosa, shows significant potential in analgesia, muscle relaxation, and antiseptic treatments. The bark of M. inermis is used for muscle relaxant properties, while M. stipulosa stem bark is used for malaria and gastrointestinal relief.105 Bark from M. rotundifolia also shows anti-inflammatory and antimalarial effects.30 Stem bark of M. diversifolia is used to treat diarrhea and cancer.7, 8 The roots of M. rubrostipulata and M. rotundifolia is used to relief muscle pain and has antipyretic effects, extending their use to fever management and pain alleviation.15, 31

Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline pharmacology

Rhynchophylline is a tetracyclic oxindole alkaloid mainly found in Uncaria species and has been also reported in Mitragyna species. Rhynchophylline is interconvertible with its stereoisomer isorhynchophylline. Recently the presence of isorhynchophylline was detected in Thai species of M. diversifolia.12 They are known for their effects mainly on cardiovascular and central nervous system. They exhibit antihypertensive effects and vasodilation, antiarrhythmic activity, neuroprotection, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects (Fig. 5, Fig. 6). They act as calcium channel blocker, opens potassium channels and modulates neurotransmitters.106

Antihypertensive effects and vasodilation

Hypertension is a condition where the blood pressure in arteries remains persistently elevated. Antihypertension is characterized by lowering the blood pressure to its normal range (Fig. 5). Vasodilation that is widening the blood vessels helps decrease the blood pressure. Rhynchophylline and isohynchophylline act as vasodilator by blocking the calcium channels like L-type calcium channels are crucial for contraction of smooth muscle cells. By blocking the influx of Ca2+ ions from outside the cells, preventing the smooth muscle cells from contracting and leads to vasodilatation. This reduces the peripheral resistance and lowers the blood pressure. Rhynchophylline and isohynchophylline has the ability to open potassium channels which causes hyperpolarization and promotes vasodilation. 107, 108

Antiarrhythmic activity

Arrhythmic is a condition where there are abnormal heart rhythms. The electrical impulse in heart is dependent on the influx of Ca2+. This influx is crucial for pacemaker cells to initiate and conduct electrical signals. rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline inhibit L-type calcium channels, reducing the rate of depolarization in pacemaker cells and slows down the heart rate. It also reduces the myocardial excitability and prevents or suppresses irregular rhythms (Fig. 5). Some studies have reported that rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline may modulate potassium channels, which will repolarize the cardiac action potential and may also have effect of sodium channels leading to rapid depolarization of the action potential.109, 110, 111.

Neuroprotection

The neuroprotective effects of rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline are multifaceted and primarily involves the regulation of key neurotransmitter systems and cellular pathways (Fig. 6). Both alkaloids act as non-competitive antagonists of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor. NMDA is a type of glutamate receptor which leads to excitotoxicity when over activated. This results in harmful influx of calcium ion into the neurons, leading to cell damage and apoptosis. Excitotoxicity is a key process in conditions such as stroke, cerebral ischemia and other neurodegenerative diseases.112 Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline also activate P13K/Akt pathway which is a master regulator of cell survival and metabolism. Activation of PI3K (phosphoinositide 3-kinase) leads to phosphorylation and activate Akt (protein kinase B). Akt inhibit its downstream, GSK3β (glycogen synthase kinase-3 β) which is an apoptotic enzyme hyperactive in many neurodegenerative diseases. This inhibition leads to downstream activation of MEF2D (Myocyte enhancer factor 2D), a transcription factor which protects dopaminergic neurons from damage particularly in Parkinson’s disease. The P13K/Akt pathway also regulates the expression of anti-apoptotic protein, Bcl-2. The increase in expression Bcl-2 decreases the level of Bax and inhibits another apoptotic protein, caspase-3.113

Anti-inflammatory effects

Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline suppression the activation of troll like receptor (TLR) and nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB), a major cause of chronic inflammation (Fig. 6). NF-κB activates the transcription of genes in cell nucleus responsible for pro-inflammatory cytokines and enzymes. By inhibiting the pathway, it prevents the translocation of NF-κB and reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF- α), interleukin- 1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) and inflammatory enzymes like nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) responsible for producing nitric oxide and prostaglandin respectively.111 Isorhynchophylline is found to have more specific inhibitory effect on NLRP3 inflammasome which is a protein complex responsible for inflammatory response and activation of IL-1β and IL-6.114

Antioxidant effects

Oxidative stress is a state where there is imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body’s ability to neutralize them. Chronic oxidative stress leads to inflammation and cellular damage in cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases. Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline has the ability to directly scavenge free radicals like superoxide ions. This prevents cellular damage by neutralizing ROS (Fig. 6). Activation of P13K/Akt pathway also increases the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) which neutralizes ROS. When mitochondria are damaged, they release majority of ROS. Rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline are found to protect mitochondrial integrity and reduce oxidative stress.115

Mitragyna alkaloids biosynthesis

The chemical constituents of Mitragyna spp. includes wide range of compounds such as alkaloid, flavone, flavonol, flavonoid, lignan, phenylpropanoid and triterpenoid. Specifically, M. speciosa produces various kinds of monoterpene indole alkaloids (MIAs), but the unique and valuable MIAs includes mitragynine and 7-hydroxymitragynine. Mitragynine is corynanthe-type indole alkaloid, one of the major alkaloids in the pant. In contrast, 7-hydroxymitragynine was first isolated as a minor alkaloids and occurs naturally only in trace amounts.116 It is thought to arise partly from oxidative transformation of mitragynine in planta or during processing.5, 117 The biosynthesis of mitragynine and the other MIAs are thought to involve multi-enzymatic steps, which is distributed among several subcellular compartments (Fig. 7). Biosynthetically, mitragynine was supplied from strictosidine as suggested by feeding experiment of strictosidine in M. speciosa.118 Therefore, mitragynine is composed of moieties of indole and iridoid as same as vincristine in Catharanthus roseus and other higher plants such as Strychnos nux-vomica and Campthotheca acuminate. The condensation of one molecule of tryptamine and one molecule of secologanin forms the indole nucleus. Tryptamine is supplied from the shikimate pathway by decarboxylation of tryptophan, whereas secologanin from terpenoids pathway. Nagakura and his coworkers in 1979 investigated that, strictosidine serve as a precursor of MIAs biosynthesis.119

Biosynthetic pathway of Mitragyna alkaloids (modified from Kim et al., 2023; Garza-Garcia and Qu 2024 and McDonald et al., 2024). 120, 121, 122

Formation of strictosidine a common precursor

The biosynthesis of Mitragyna alkaloids proceeds in the typical indole alkaloid pathway where tryptamine and the monoterpenoid glucoside secologanin are their building blocks. The conversion of tryptophan into tryptamine is catalyzed by tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC). It is therefore regarded as a putative site for regulatory control of alkaloid biosynthesis. TDC has been significantly studied and cloned from variety of plants such as Catharanthus roseus,123 Camptotheca acuminata124 and Ophiorrhiza pumila.125 Strictosidine synthase (STR) gene, 1-deoxy-D-xylulose 5-phosphate synthase (DXS), 2C-methyl-D-erythritol 4-phosphate synthase (MEPS) and TDC were identified from M. speciosa by cloning and expressing in Escherichia coli. Strictosidine β-glucoside (SGD) is involved in TIA biosynthesis. SGD removes the glucose from strictosidine resulting an unstable aglycone structure forming a highly reactive dialdehyde which undergoes further diversification and arrangements.126 The intermediate is found to yield ajmalicine in Catharanthus system.126

STR and SGD genes from C. roseus were co-expressed in yeast by feeding tryptamine and secologanin which led to high yields of strictosidine further converting to cathenamine.127 Strictosidine produces various MIAs through reduction by NADPH-dependent cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase-like reductase in higher plants.120, 128, 129

Key intermediate formation

Corynantheidine

The aglycone strictosidine undergoes reduction forming various isomers. Dihydrocorynantheine, one of its isomers is found to have the same scaffold as mitragynine and speciogynine indicating the likeliness to be their biosynthetic intermediate. During the biosynthesis of quinine, a medium-chain alcohol dehydrogenase, demethyldihydrocorynantheidine synthase (CpDCS) was reported from Cinchona pubescens that converts strictosidine alglycone to 20R-dehydrocorynantheine129 which in contrast was found to form 20 S-dehydrocorynantheine in M. speciosa during mitragynine biosynthesis128 (Fig. 8). Some MIAs, like corynanthe alkaloids, have a reactive C17 enol group that contributes to their stability and reactivity. In the Apocynaceae family, enzymes like geissoschizine oxidase (GO) and sarpagan bridge enzyme (SBE) stabilize through oxidation, leading to important MIA types, such as vinblastine.128 In contrast, M. speciosa and other plants in the Rubiaceae family, lack these specific enzymes (GO and SBE).120 Instead, evolved a different enzyme, Enol-methyltransferase (MsEnolMT) which showed methyl transferase activity converting the strictosidine aglycone to either 20-S or 20-R corynantheidine precursors.130 C17 O-methyl transferase (C17OMT) stabilizes the C17-enol group by adding a methyl group. The enzyme belongs to the SABATH (salicylic acid/benzoic acid/theobromine) methyl transferase family, which usually methylate acids but is methylated to a unique enol oxygen in the MIA structure. Mitragynine biosynthesis includes 9-hydroxylation and 9-O-methylation. This methylation step is crucial for forming mitragynine and related compounds in the Rubiaceae plants, showing an alternate route for MIA stabilization in the absence of GO or SBE.120

Enzymatic mechanism of demethyldihydrocorynantheidine synthase.

Ajmalicine and tetrahydroalstonine

The unstable strictosidine aglycone structure is converted to more stable isomers such as 4,21- dehydrogeissoschizine, cathenamine, and epi-cathenamine. Reduction of strictosidine by THAS (tetrahydroalstonin) and HYS (heteroyohimbine synthase) forms the heteroyohimbine skeleton. Cathenamine reduction reaction with HYS and THAS produces ajmalicine and tetrahydroalstonin respectively.131 In Hamelia patens the biosynthesis of MOA is derived from heteroyohimbine-type MIA, tetrahydroalstonine and aricine. HpaTHAS1 expression was found responsible for activation of HpaSGD leading to formation of tetrahydroalstonine from strictosidine aglycone and further continuation of MOA biosynthesis.132 Dehydrogeissoschizine is proposed to be an intermediate that produce mitraphylline and isomitraphylline, pentacyclic monoterpenoid oxindole alkaloid (MOA) through oxidation of ajmalicine. It is also involved in the formation of tetracyclic MOA rhyncophylline and isorhyncophylline.133

Biosynthesis of Mitragyna 3S and 3R indole alkaloids

Oxidase-reductase enzyme pair epimerizes variety of 3S-MIAs to 3R-MIAs. A recent study by McDonald and coauthors (2024), discovered the presence of iminium species 20S-3-dehydrocorynantheidine. Metabolic profiling of Kratom tissues found that young leaves contain both 3S-MIAs (corynantheidine and mitragynine) and 3R-MIA, mature leaves contained mostly 3S-MIAs while roots and stems contained 3R-MIAs and spirooxindole alkaloids. Two abundant peaks were found in young leaves which were shown to be the iminium species 20S-3-dehydrocorynantheidine (DHC) and 3-dehydromitragynine (DHM) by NMR analysis. Only DHM has been previously reported to be present in Kratom leaves. These compounds led to hypothesize that reduction of one or both iminium species could form 3R-MIAs. They fed Kratom tissues with isotopically labeled MIAs. Young leaves and stems were found to produce significant incorporation of labeled MIAs especially speciociliatine and mitragynine from d5-tryptamine (d5-1). Hirsutine was detected after isotopologue feeding. Matured contained high levels of corynantheidine oxindoles and mitragynine while roots contained speciociliatine but both showed minimal isotopologue incorporation. Feeding of d4-strictosidine showed similar incorporation like d5-tryptamine with lower efficiency, d4-vincoside (d4-2b) was not incorporated in any tissue, d2-9-OMe-strictosidine (d2-9OS) resulted in labelled mitragynine and speciogynine. These suggest that epimerization occurs in young leaves prior to methoxylation at C9 after formation of strictosidine. Agroinfilteration of oxidase gene candidates (cytochrome P450, polyphenol oxidases and berberine bridge-like (BBE) enzymes) into N, benthamiana along with 20S-corynantheidine resulted in identification of two BBE-like enzymes termed corynantheidine oxidases 1–2 (CO1 and CO2) that catalyzed the oxidation of 20S-corynantheidine to DHC and no activity was shown with mitragynine. Comparison of reductase genes among the known 3R-MIA producing and non-producing species led to discovery of dehydrocorynantheidine reductase (DCR), an isoflavone reductase which only specifically reduced DHC to 20S isocorynantheidine.122

Variety of substrates with MsCO1, MsDCR, and MsSAS were assayed in N. benthamiana. MsSAS, MsCO1 and MsDCR with 20S-corynantheidine were found to be converted to a mixture of corynoxine isomers, corynoxine A, corynoxine B and 3-epicorynoxine. 20S-9-hydroxycorynantheidine was readily oxidized by MsCO1. Neither mitragynine nor speciogynine were oxidized but DHM was reduced to speciociliatine by MsCO1 and MsDCR. THA with MsCO1, MsDCR, and MsSAS formed isopteropodine, pteropodine and primarily 3-epipteropodine with additional compounds including dehydro-THA and iso-THA. No activity was observed in ajmalicine with MsCO1. However, with MsCO1 and MsDCR formed 3-epi-ajmalicine. Corynanthine was also converted to 3-dehydro-corynanthine.122

Biosynthesis of spirooxindole alkaloids

Oxidative rearrangement in tetrahydro-β-carboline moiety of hetero- and seco-yohimbine type alkaloids such as ajmalicine and corynantheine methyl ether resulted in spirooxindole alkaloids.140 3-epicorynoxeine/ isocorynoxeine (3eCIS) synthase also known as spirooxindole synthase (MsSAS) catalyzes the oxidation of β-carboline from hirsuteine leading to rearrangements of oxindole ring (Fig. 9). The enzyme also rearranges 3R-seco-yohimbine into mixtures of 3R- and 3S-spirroxindoles. 3eCIS shows high selectivity towards 3R- tetracyclic corynanthe-type alkaloids.134 Speciociliatine and mitraciliatine were reduced by MsSAS, forming mitragynine oxindoles and speciogynine oxindoles. Reconstitution of the full pathway from tryptamine to spirooxindole alkaloids by coexpressing Kratom pathway enzymes in N. benthamiana led to a conclusion that 20R-corynantheidine also acts as a substrate for MsCO1, MsDCR, and MsSAS.122

Enzymatic mechanism of 3-epicorynoxeine/ isocorynoxeine synthase.

Recently chromosome-scale analysis of M. parvifolia integrated with tissue metabolomics uncovered the later stages involved in mitraphylline biosynthesis. Using strictosidine synthase (MpSTR), several oxidoreductase enzymes were identified including FAD-dependent oxidoreductase (FAD1), isoflavone reductase (IR1) and five CYPS (CYP1-5). FAD1 was found to have similar sequence to C. roseus gene O-acetylstemmadenine oxidase (CrASO). These three oxidoreductases were coexpressed in N. benthamiana with 3S-ajmalicine substrate feeding which led to formation of mitraphylline and isomitraphylline. FAD1 and IR1 were found to be involved conversion of 3S-ajmaciline to 3R-epi-ajmalicine. Therefore, the two enzymes were named as ajmalicine oxidase (AO) and dehydroajmalicine reductase (DAR) respectively. MpAO was hypothesized to catalyze the oxidation of 3S-corynanthe substrate at C3-N bond, yielding the iminium intermediate. Then MpDAR reduces the iminium intermediate at C3 yielding a 3R-corynanthe epimer. Further expression of the two-enzyme combination using 3S-tetrahydroalstonine, 3S-yohimbine, 3S-mayumbine, 3S-rauwolscine and 3S-corynantheidine resulted in conversion into their respective 3R-epimer. However, using MIA substrates like strictosidine, vincosamide and strictosamide didn't result in any alteration indicating the specificty of the two enzymes to seco/heteroyohimbane alkaloids. The coexpression of MpDAR, MpAO and MpCYP1 with substrate 3S-ajmalicine yielded mitraphylline and isomitraphylline. This suggested the role of MpCYP1 in the formation of the two major spirooxindole alkloids in M. parvifolia from 3R-epi-ajmalicine. MpCYP1 combined with the two enzymes yielded pteropodine alkaloids and mayumbine oxindoles from 3S-tetrahydroalstonine and 3S-mayumbine respectively. Like spirooxindole synthase reported in M. speciosa,134 MpCYP1 alone was found to convert 3R-hirsutine to rhynchophylline oxindoles. Therefore, MpCYP1 was designated as M. parvifolia spirooxindole synthase for its ability to convert several seco/heteroyohimbane to their corresponding tetracyclic and pentacyclic spirooxindole scaffolds. Phylogenomic analysis showed that Rubiaceae derived spirooxindole are unique to the family. While MpDAR was found to be species specific to M. parvifolia.135

Plant biotechnology of Mitragyna spp

With increase in popularity of Mitragyna spp. for their medicinal and therapeutic properties, there is a major pressure on the natural resources. Plant biotechnology is an alternative for sustainable harvesting and conservation of the plants, preventing over exploitation. The major limitation in plant tissue culture is the low yield in secondary metabolites. The production of these metabolites can be enhanced through various factors and approaches such as media manipulation, precursor feeding, plant growth regulator and elicitor treatments (Table 2).

Media manipulation

A plant culture medium consists of essential nutrients such as salts, minerals, vitamins, carbon source, ideal pH and plant growth regulators. Sugar is the source of energy for cells. It plays a major role in cell proliferation and maintaining osmotic potential. Sucrose is the most common source of carbon used in tissue culture.136 Gelling agents give support for the cultured tissue. It can affect the water availability, humidity, volatile compositions and availability of other media composition.137 Agar is one of the most used gelling agents due to its stability, high clarity and resistance to metabolism. Ideal pH should be between 5.5–6. Fluctuation in pH may lead to reduced uptake of nutrient by tissue leading to damage or death.138

Cell suspension culture of M. speciosa was found to show the best result with 3% sucrose among the various concentration tested.139 Study on M. parvifolia with different media and various concentration of gelling agent found that full-strength MS medium demonstrated the highest efficacy for inducing bud-breaking and MS medium supplemented with 0.8% agar yielded best results in both shoot multiplication and quality.140

Precursor feeding

Precursor refers to the primary building block that initiates the biosynthetic pathway leading to production of specific secondary metabolites in the plant. The increased availability of precursors upregulates key biosynthetic enzymes boosting the metabolic flux toward desired compounds and increase bottleneck pathways.141 They are derived from primary metabolites such as amino acids, fatty acids, sugars and other organic acids.142, 143

The optimal mitragynine level (13.226 mg L−1) in M. speciosa culture was observed with a six-day treatment of 3 μM tryptophan, which was 5-fold higher than the control.139 The shoot cultures of M. speciosa treated with tryptamine and loganin found that feeding of tryptamine (21 days) caused increase in mitragynine production whereas loganin had no effect. However, combination of the two resulted in stimulation of mitragynine production on day 14.144

Plant growth regulators

Plant growth regulators (PGRs) plays a major role in organogenesis. Major classes of PGRs are auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, abscisic acid and ethylene. The ratio of auxin to cytokinin results in different organogenesis responses. Higher ratio of auxin to cytokinin induces root whereas the vice versa induces shoot. Intermediate ratio of auxin to cytokinin promotes callus induction.145 Auxin plays various roles in cell division, elongation, somatic embryogenesis, shoot and root initiation, and apical dominance. Cytokinins are involved in cell division and shoot initiation.146

A study investigated the effects of different hormone combinations on callus induction and proliferation in M. speciosa culture where combination of 2,4-D and kinetin resulted in higher organogenic potential, 2,4-D yielding the best callus proliferation and NAA with BAP showed optimal callus induction.139 Another study on the same species with addition of 0.5 mg L−1 NAA to WPM, MS and B5 media showed improvement in the growth of hairy roots while the media without NAA showed slower growth.147 M. parvifolia cultured on MS medium supplemented with either BAP or Kin found that the axillary shoot buds were induced within 1 to 2 weeks. The treatment with 3 mg L−1 BAP in MS media had the best result.140

Elicitation

Elicitation is one of the most effective biotechnological tools used to enhance production of secondary metabolites. Elicitors are compounds which triggers the plant’s defense mechanism which results in production of secondary metabolites. Depending on the origin, elicitors can be biotic and abiotic. Biotic elicitors are compounds of biological origin with pathogenic origin (exogenous) or produced by plants in response to pathogens (endogenous).148 Abiotic elicitors are compound of non-biological origin usually inorganic compounds such as salts, metal ions or physical factors.149, 150 When elicitors bind to the receptors on cell plasma, signal transduction involves second messengers which amplifies the response and leads to sequential events such as protein phosphorylation, ion fluxes, MAPK activation, ROS/RNS production and pH change in cytoplasm. Which further triggers gene expression, jasmonate production and secondary metabolite accumulation.151

Shoot cultures of M. speciosa treated with elicitor yeast extract and methyl jasmonate was observed. Maximal elicitation of mitragynine was seen in cultures with 10 μM methyl jasmonate and 0.1 mg L−1 yeast extract.152 However, in another study the optimum concentration of mitragynine was observed with 250 mg L−1 yeast extract. The same study conducted salicylic acid elicitation which resulted in lower mitragynine concentrations across all treatments compared to the control.139

Synthetic biology of Kratom alkaloids

Synthetic biology is redesigning and reconstruction of organisms by engineering to develop new abilities for useful purposes. It provides a more reliable, sustainable, scalable and consistent approach for production of alkaloids153 compared to traditional cultivation which has environmental and variability in alkaloid contents. Yeast cell factories can be used to generate monoterpene indole alkaloid derivatives that can be challenging to produce by feeding precursors and enzymes.154 Synthetic biology of Kratom alkaloids were explored in yeast, E. coli and Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Fig. 10).

Kim and colleagues, (2023) investigated the production and biosynthesis of mitragynine in Yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and E. coli (Fig. 10A). The bacteria were fed with 4-hydroxytryptamine, tryptamine and secologanin, precursor molecules essential for the pathway as substrate. Five genes: CrSTR, CrSGD, MsDCS1, MsC17OMT, and HpOMT9 were co-expressed in E. coli. These genes were proposed to be involved in different steps of the mitragynine biosynthesis pathway based on the enzymatic and structural properties of the genes and their involvement in plant alkaloid biosynthesis. The engineered E. coli successfully converted the substrates into mitragynine (18.7 ng/ml) and speciogynine (24.7 ng/ml) with minimal by-products. They found that replacing MsDCS1 with MsDCS4 led to the accumulation of only speciogynine, replacing MsDCS1 with CrGS resulted in the biosynthesis 9-methoxy-19Z-geissoschizine methyl ether and removal of HpOMT9 resulted in the production of non-methylated versions of the alkaloids.120 While, in yeast six genes: CrSTR, CrSGD, MsDCS1, MsC17OMT, HpOMT9 and PcuT4H were co-expressed. PcuT4H from the psychedelic mushroom Psilocybe cubensis was used to supply 4-hydroxytryptamine. The engineered yeast successfully produced mitragynine (145 ng/ml) with corynantheidine being the major product (1190 ng/ml. Without PcuT4H, yeast primarily produced corynantheidine, since no hydroxylation of tryptamine occurred.120

Additionally, Schotte and colleagues, (2023) expressed CrSTR, CrSGD, MsDCS1 and MsEnolMT in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves and infiltrated with 4-methoxytryptamine and secologanin (Fig. 10C). They found trace amounts of 20S mitragynine. Replacing MsDCS1 with CpDCS resulted in 20R epimer.130

Lastly, Holtz and colleagues, (2024) studied the de novo Kratom alkaloid biosynthesis in engineered yeast (MIA-CZ-1; strictosidine platform strain) (Fig. 10B). The strain was optimized by deleting ROX1 and overexpressing IN02 and ZWF1 from the parental strain MIA- CM-5. ROX1 deletion was found to improve cytochrome P450 activity154, overexpression of ZWF1 boosts NADPH (a cofactor for P450s), and overexpression INO2 was found to increase endoplasmic reticulum for better P450 function.120 The team co-expressed a fungal enzyme, PcPsiH from Psilocybe cubensis and its reductase PcCPR in MIA-SZ-1, generating MIA-KM-1. Next three enzymes, SGD, DCS and MsEnolMt were integrated in the MIA-KM-1 genome. Three versions of MIA-KM-1 were assembled: MIA-KM-2 (MsDCS1), MIA-KM-3 (MsDCS2) and MIA-KM-4(CpDCS). MsDCS1 produced mainly corynanthe MIAs with 20S stereochemistry with minor 20R product, MsDCS2 produced 20R stereochemistry and an additional unidentified peak while CpDCS only produced 20R corynanthe MIAs. Finally, Hp90MT from Hamelia patens was introduced in MIA-KM-2, MIA-KM-3 and MIA-KM-4 producing MIA-KM-5, MIA-KM-6 and MIA-KM-7 respectively. All the versions produced secologanin with MIA-KM-5 producing both mitragynine (1.4 ± 0.1 µg/L) and speciogynine (1.7 ± 1.0 µg/L), whereas the highest speciogynine production was achieved by MIA-KM-7 with 6.3 ± 1.4 µg/L.155

Other approaches of plant biotechnology under explored in Mitragyna spp

Although application of methyl jasmonate, yeast extract and precursor feeding (tryptamine) in other medicinal plants have demonstrated significant potential in enhancing their secondary metabolite production, these studies remain limited in Mitragyna species. Alternative strategies such as coronatine treatment and light elicitation offers new opportunity to enhance the metabolite production and optimize biosynthesis of pharmacologically important alkaloids in Mitragyna species.

Methyl jasmonate

Methyl jasmonate (MeJA) is a volatile methyl ester derivative of jasmonic acid (JA). MeJA are plant specific signaling molecules with the ability to activate important physiological and developmental process.156 It is reported to stimulate secondary biosynthetic pathways, leading to production of diverse secondary metabolites such as terpenoids, phenylpropanoids, flavonoids and alkaloid.157, 158, 159 Activation of phospholipase (PLAs) is essential for MeJA biosynthesis and signaling as they produce linoleic acid from the plasma membrane which acts as precursor for MeJA,160 JA and MeJA enters the plasma membrane through ATP- binding cassette transporter, JAT1 (jasmonic acid transporter1) (Fig. 11). The bioactive molecule of JA and MeJA is jasmonyl isoleucine (JA-lle) formed by conjugation of JA with isoleucine. JAR1 gene is responsible for the conjugation of JA with amino acids.161 JA response is controlled by a group of proteins called Jasmonate–ZIM-domain (JAZ) repressors. JAZ proteins block MYC2 and other transcription factors. JA-Ile binds with JAZ and a F-box protein COI1 (coronatine insensitive1), which results in degradation of JAZ repressors through proteasome pathway and activations of MYC2 and other transcription factors including MYB, NAC, ERF and WRKY. This leads to JA signaling and expression of genes involved in growth, development and defense response of the plants.162, 163 MeJA is found to increase taxane production in cell cultures of Taxus.164 MeJA showed increased chlorogenic acid and its derivatives contents as well as higher antioxidant capacity in Gardenia jasminoides cell suspension cultures.165 It was also found to enhance the yield of tropane alkaloids scopolamine and hyoscyamine in Hyoscyamus niger transgenic hairy roots.166

Coronatine

Coronatine (COR) is a bacterial blight toxin produced by Pseudomonas syringae pathogen strains. It is formed by coronafacic acid and coronamic acid conjugated by amide linkage.167 COR mimics the bioactive molecule, JA-lle which plays a major role in JA signaling (Fig. 11). It triggers the plant defense mechanisms resulting in production of phytoalexins and secondary metabolites.168 COR is more stable and has higher affinity towards COI1 resulting in higher induction of secondary metabolites compared to JA-lle. It enters plant cell by promoting opening of stomata. COR is reported to induce the accumulation of phytoalexins sakuranetin and momilactone A in Oryza sativa leaves.169 The production of taxane in cell suspension of Taxus was significantly increased by 3.3 folds with COR compared to MeJA.164

Yeast extract

Yeast extract (YE) is a natural product obtained from fungi, Saccharomyces cerevisiae through separation and purification process. It consists of polypeptide, amino acids, nutrients, vitamins and growth factors essential for plant cells. It is used as biotic elicitor with its ability to stimulate metabolic responses and transduction signals, enhancing the synthesis of proteins, nucleic acid and bioactive molecules.170 It also enhances plant growth and secondary metabolite production (Fig. 12). Yeast extract function as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) localized on cell surface recognizes PAMPs leading to PAMP-induced defense responses.171 The early defense responses include ion flux and Ca2+ signaling across plasma membrane and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), reactive nitrogen species and ethylene (ET). Later followed by alterations is plant cell wall, expression of pathogenesis-related (PR) genes and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites through nitric oxide (NO), salicylic acid (SA) and JA signaling pathways.172, 173 YE extract is reported to enhance the accumulation of stilbene phytoalexins in Vitis vinifera callus, 174 flavonoid in Fagopyrum tataricum hairy roots175 and rosmarinic acid individually and in combination with methyl jasmonate in cell suspension culture of Lavender.176

Tryptamine

Supplementation of precursors into the in vitro culture medium can enhance yield of targeted end products. Precursors serve as building blocks in biosynthesis as well as increase biomass growth by expression of growth genes and hormones.177 The entry of precursor in plant cell is facilitated by various transporters such as amino acid transporters for introducing amino acid precursor, ATP- binding cassette transporter for lipid-derived compounds and organic intermediate compounds in the biosynthesis pathway.178 Some precursor molecules can also enter through passive diffusion. Combination of precursor and elicitor can have positive effect on growth and enhance production of secondary metabolites in plant cell. Tryptamine (TRY) is a indolamine made of an indole backbone and an ethylamine side chain. It is formed by decarboxylation of L-tryptophan catalyzed by tryptophan decarboxylase (TDC). TDC is one of the major enzymes that links both carbon and nitrogen from primary metabolites into secondary metabolites. TRY enters the cell through passive diffusion and via specific transporters although the exact details are unknown. TRY is converted into variety of metabolites which is divided into four major classes: monoterpenoid indole alkaloids (MIAs), indole alkylamines, β-carbolines and tryptamine amides (Fig. 13). In Mitragyna, MIAs are abundant. The strictosidine synthase facilitates the condensation of indole structure of tryptamine and geraniol-derived iridoid-monoterpene nucleus of secologanin, forming the common precursor strictosidine which undergoes rearrangement and diversification into different MIAs.179

Light

Light is an abiotic elicitor which influences physiological responses, biosynthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites in plants.180 Light intensity, light quality and photoperiod affect the secondary metabolite production. Plant utilizes photoreceptors for light sensing and light signal transduction. Signal transduction leads to modified gene expression and triggers metabolic and physiological responses. Different photoreceptor is responsible for perception of various light; phytochromes (Phy) detect red/far-red lights within 600–700 nm, cryptochromes(CRYs), phototropins (PHOTs), zeitlupes (ZTL), kelch repeat F-box 1(FKF1) and low oxygen or voltage kelch protein 2 (LKP2) perceive blue and UV-A light within 315–400 nm and UV resistance locus 8 (UVR8) perceive UV-B light within 280-.315 nm.181 The transcription factor, elongated hypocoyl 5 (HY5) is a central regulator in light-mediated responses. It promotes gene expression involved in biosynthesis of flavonoid, alkaloid and terpenoid.182 Another protein transcription factor, phytochrome interacting factor (PIFs), suppresses light-mediated signaling. The activity of these transcription factors is controlled by E3 ubiquitin ligase constitutively photomorphogenic 1(COP1) which induces protease-mediated degradation of HY5 and other positive regulatory transcription factors in dark. When plants are exposed to light, COP1 activity is suppressed, leading to stabilization of HY5 and activation of secondary metabolite pathways.183

UV-B signaling in plant is mediated by UVR8 which remains inactive in the cytoplasm. Under UV-B, UVR8 is activated into its monomeric form (Fig. 14). UVR8 interacts with COP1 leading to stabilization of HY5 and initiating a signaling cascade.184 HY5 induces repressor of UV-B photomorphogenesis 1 and 2 (RUP1 and RUP 2) which converts UVR8 back to its ground state and deactivates UV-B signaling.185 In several medicinal plants UV-B has found to be an effective elicitor for secondary metabolite production. Caratharanthus roseus suspension culture treated with UV-B for 5 mins was found to increase the production of catharanthine by 3-fold and vindolin by 12-fold.186 It also increased the content of indole alkaloid in Clematis terniflora followed by dark incubation.187

Blue light is a vital component of the visible spectrum which regulates plant growth and development. Blue light at 400–500 nm activates cryptochormes and phototropins, regulate inhibition of hypocotyl elongation, promote leaf expansion and stomatal opening.188 At metabolic levels, blue light upregulates expression of transcription factors which activates expression of enzymes in phenylpropanoid pathway 189 and MEP pathway.190 During light signaling, blue light activates CRY1, CRY2, PHOT1 and PHOT2 (Fig. 14). CRY1/CRY2 undergoes phosphorylation and inhibits the complex between COP1 and suppressor of phyctochrome A (SPA1). COP1-SPA1 complex promotes degradation of HY5 in dark. Inhibition of this complex stabilizes HY5, resulting in expression of genes involved in secondary metabolism. Low and high intensity blue light are perceived by PHOT1 and PHOT2 which activates calcium channel, responsible for downstream response.181 Study on effects of various monochromatic wavelengths such as blue, green, yellow and red in Prunella vulgaris suspension cultures found that highest total flavonoids and phenolic compound accumulation under blue light.191 Study with Stevia rebaudiana callus culture exposed under different light showed the highest total phenolic and flavonoid content as well as total antioxidant potential under blue light.192

Red light activates phytochrome which triggers a series of complex signal transduction pathways (Fig. 14). Phytochrome exist in two interconvertible forms, Pr which absorbs red light (650–670 nm) and Pfr which absorbs far red light (705–740 nm).193 Pr is inactive whereas Pfr is active form. Pr remains in cytosol and is transported to nucleus when red light activates and converts it to Pfr.194 Pfr activates its protein kinase activity resulting in autophosphorylation. Pfr interacts with PIFs and COP1-SPA1 complex, leading to stabilization of HY5 and induction of secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene expression.195 Red light is found to induce vindoline production in C. roseus by increasing the expression of transcription factor genes.196 Combination of blue and red light has also been found to elicit the production of secondary metabolites and plant growth. Ocimum basilicum seedlings cultured under combined red and blue light showed more biomass, rosmarinic acid and caffeic acid content compared to white light.197

Future prospective

Mitragyna species with a long history of traditional medicine practices across Asia, offer a promising source for new metabolite and drug discovery, owing to their diverse phytochemical composition. Scientific research has reported numerous pharmacological potentials, particularly in alleviating pain, inflammation and wound treatments. The presence of numerous alkaloids and other secondary metabolites contributes to their therapeutic significance. Conducting more studies on application of methyl jasmonate, yeast extract and precursor feeding (tryptamine) and alternative strategies such as coronatine treatment and light elicitation can offer new opportunities to enhance the metabolite production and optimize biosynthesis of pharmacologically important alkaloids in Mitragyna species. Synthetic biology of Kratom alkaloids can provide a more sustainable, consistent and scalable alternative which holds significant potential in scientific research and pharmaceutical developments. Further research in this field will not only increase our understanding of Mitragyna species but also open opportunities for the development of novel therapeutics with enhanced efficacy and safety profiles.
