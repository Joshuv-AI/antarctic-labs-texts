Pediatric to adult transition in chronic kidney disease: a scoping review
Authors: Zachariah Alkhayri, Rama Safadi, Andrea Varghese, Mustafa Husein, Shay Tanna, Sahithi R. Mallyala, Nikhil Rayarakula, Carol L. Vincent, Rupesh Raina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) presents unique medical and psychosocial challenges in children and adolescents that differ from adult patients with CKD. Transitioning from pediatric to adult care is a critical period. Insufficient planning and inadequate preparation increase the risk of medication nonadherence, loss to follow-up, graft failure in transplant patients, and increased morbidity. Transition models address barriers to successful transition, yet vary in their approach.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review of the transition of care in pediatric CKD in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. We included PubMed/MEDLINE full-text articles, KDIGO guidelines, and grey literature on transition models between 1st January 2000 and 31st August 2025. Eligible studies included original research articles in English, human clinical trials, and guidelines specifically addressing pediatric-to-adult CKD transition of care.ResultsWe identified barriers to effective transition at the patient, provider, and system levels. Patients often lacked health literacy, emotional readiness, or independence. Providers faced challenges with communication across pediatric and adult care teams. System-level obstacles included gaps in insurance coverage and limited resources. To assess readiness, models such as ON TRAC, TRAQ, RTQ, and the UNC TRxANSITION offer valuable questionnaires. Early preparation between the ages of 11–14, a gradual increase in patient autonomy, multidisciplinary teams’ involvement, and structured transfer clinics are recommended for proper CKD transition.DiscussionAn effective transition is vital for improving both pediatric and adult health outcomes. Future research should focus on standardized protocols such as the Six Core Elements of Health Care Transition and the integration of technology-based tools to better engage adolescent patients. Structured and evidence-based transition models supported by healthcare systems can improve transition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05019-4.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is progressive and irreversible kidney damage that can progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), a condition linked to a high mortality rate and cardiovascular complications [1]. The global prevalence of CKD is estimated at 9.1% [2]. While the CKD diagnostic criteria were created for adult populations, they have been adapted for children. For adults, children (< 10 years old), and adolescents (10–19 years old), CKD is defined as the presence of structural or functional abnormalities sustained for at least three months. CKD is classified using the CGA system, with Cause (C), GFR category (G1-G5), and Albuminuria category (A1-A3). Diagnostic criteria include the presence of decreased GFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (G3a-G5) or markers of kidney damage, such as the Albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR > 30 mg/g2). While there is clinical overlap with adult CKD, children face unique challenges, including growth delays and developmental complications not seen in adults [3].

As adolescent patients with CKD approach adulthood, transition from pediatric to adult care teams becomes necessary to ensure continuity of care. Inadequately structured transitions result in medication nonadherence, returning to dialysis, or even losing the graft in transplant recipients. Transition refers to a structured, long-term process designed to prepare adolescent patients for the addition of care responsibilities associated with adult healthcare. This is distinct from the “transfer”, which is a single event involving handoff of the patient from pediatric to adult providers without considering the patient’s preparedness. The transition process involves a shift from the highly supervised, family-centered model of pediatric care to a more autonomous, self-managed focus of adult care. Further complicating this transition are adolescent behaviors, such as increased risk-taking and reduced adherence to treatment. Notably, kidney transplant recipients face the highest risk of graft failure during this age range of 17–24 years [4]. This age range is derived from the transplant literature reviewed, though other transition and developmental models describe wider or overlapping age ranges depending on the outcomes of interest.

Current guidelines suggest that transition programs should be structured, involve multidisciplinary teams, and initiate early at 11–14 years of age. They recommend the use of such tools as Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ), Readiness for Transition Questionnaire (RTQ), and the Got Transition checklist. Autonomy should be gradually increased in children, and they should be provided with a written summary. Transfers are best scheduled when the adolescent is medically and socially stable. Young adults under 25 years of age should be considered as high-risk groups due to ongoing neurodevelopment requiring close monitoring [2].

Given the medical and psychosocial vulnerabilities during this critical window, patients must be adequately prepared for self-management. Structured transition programs have evolved to adopt a multidisciplinary team approach tailored to the adolescent’s developmental stage, involving parents or guardians to build patient readiness [4].

Despite the implementation of structured transition programs and processes at individual health centers, there is a scarcity of comprehensive studies examining the existing models and providing directions for the future. This scoping review, therefore, aims to examine the barriers to effective transition, evaluate components of existing transition protocols, summarize recommendations from recent guidelines, and propose actionable strategies to optimize outcomes during this critical period.

Methods & materials

This scoping review was performed using the PRISMA-ScR checklist (Fig. 1). We collected information from three primary sources: PubMed/MEDLINE, grey literature on transition models, and guideline repositories, notably the 2024 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO). These sources were selected to capture peer-reviewed clinical evidence, established transition frameworks, and authoritative guideline-based recommendations relevant to pediatric-to-adult CKD transition of care.

PRISMA flow chart

We included eligible studies such as original research articles, clinical trials, study protocols, case reports, or qualitative studies with pediatric or adolescent patients with CKD. We also incorporated reports discussing pediatric to adult transition of care, barriers to transition, and multidisciplinary transition care models. Only publications available in the English language and published between January 1st, 2000, and August 31st, 2025, were considered. Eligible studies included original research articles, clinical trials, study protocols, case reports, and qualitative studies involving pediatric or adolescent patients with CKD, encompassing non–dialysis-dependent CKD, dialysis, and kidney transplant populations. We also incorporated reports addressing pediatric-to-adult transition of care, barriers to transition, and multidisciplinary transition care models. Included references represented international populations, with data originating from North America, Europe, Australia, and Asia. This provides a global perspective on CKD transition practices.

To maintain clinical relevance, we excluded publications without full-text availability, studies unrelated to the CKD transition of care, those published before January 1st, 2000, and not involving human subjects.

Two independent reviewers (Z.A. and N.R.) screened titles and abstracts retrieved from the database searches for topic relevance. The full texts of articles were obtained and assessed against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements regarding categorization of articles were resolved by an independent third reviewer (R.R.). No ethical considerations were applied, as this review used publicly available data.

Data synthesis

All eligible manuscripts were reviewed completely and systematically integrated into the narrative to address the major components of CKD transition. Sources were grouped according to transition protocols, barriers to transition, multidisciplinary coordination, psychosocial challenges, and long-term patient outcomes. Real-world evidence from expert guidelines was further included to provide context and support a comparative analysis across existing transition models.

Results

Consequences of poor transition

Poorly structured transition of care in adolescents with CKD can lead to non-adherence to medications, accelerated disease progression, and even graft loss in transplant recipients [5–7].

Non-adherence accounted for up to half of adverse transplant outcomes in a systematic review [8]. Beyond graft survival, non-adherence was also associated with higher healthcare costs, loss to follow up and increased hospitalizations [9–11].

Psychosocial consequences are also significant. Pediatric patients are often unprepared for the long-term management of CKD and struggle with autonomy, gaps in self-management, and family stress. Evidence suggests that young people without structured transition miss critical opportunities to build skills for adulthood [5, 9]. Adolescence is consistently identified as a period of heightened vulnerability, as reduced parental supervision, developmental changes, and psychosocial stressors contribute to lapses in adherence [12]. Adolescents and young adults also find it difficult to maintain complex treatment regimens. Recent studies highlight the potential of tailored self-management strategies, transition readiness checklists, social support, education manuals, and technology-based care [2, 13].

Barriers to effective transition

Although transition programs have been shown to significantly improve clinical outcomes, several barriers prevent their successful implementation [14]. These barriers can be classified as patient-level, provider-level, and system-level factors (Fig. 2).

Patient-level barriers are often the most impactful and include cognitive and emotional readiness, health literacy, and dependence on family members. Transitions are frequently determined by age, typically 18, rather than readiness. According to a systematic review by Crawford et al., the percentage of adolescents who did not feel ready for their care to be transferred varied from 20 to 80% [4]. Many of these adolescents reported feeling unprepared due to a lack of engagement in decision-making, as well as emotional and practical readiness. Adolescents and their families are frequently hesitant to transition because of the different levels of care given by each unit. Pediatric units typically have fewer patients, allowing them to give comprehensive care and assistance while also communicating effectively with the family. Patients in adult units are expected to be self-sufficient and adherent [4]. This stark difference can make the transition overwhelming for adolescents. Transition programs aim to improve patient health literacy while decreasing reliance on family and staff.

System-level and provider-level barriers include limited funding, poor communication between care teams, and insufficient access to health insurance [4]. Difficulty accessing or finding providers in adult care settings, especially those who accept public insurance, can be a barrier to transitioning out of pediatric care [15]. Young adults may age out of their parents’ insurance plans, limiting coverage options and increasing out-of-pocket costs [15]. These factors can lead to delays in care, reduced access to appropriate providers, and poor continuity of care, all of which negatively impact health outcomes.

Important clinical differences between pediatric- and adult-onset CKD may further complicate transition. Rarer etiologies underlying congenital CKD, including congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract and inherited disorders such as Bartter syndrome and primary hyperoxaluria, are less frequently encountered in adult practice. Limited exposure to these etiologies results in gaps in provider training and experience in managing select congenital causes of kidney disease. These limitations in exposure may contribute to challenges in maintaining continuity of care following transfer [2, 16].

Additionally, in order to conduct research initiatives and expand technology’s role in the transition of the care process, funding is required. Lack of funding can hinder advancements in care transitions. The proposition of joint healthcare facilities that are staffed by renal specialists with pediatric and/or adult expertise would allow for a more structured transition of care for patients. Given that these patients will likely be of the same age, these facilities could potentially provide a space for support. However, this idea of joint pediatric and adult renal clinics is not necessarily feasible, primarily due to the associated finances. These extra costs may be viewed as justified if there was more evidence to support a reduction in hospitalizations or graft loss. A number of studies have emphasized the necessity for research to support such initiatives [4].

Although much of the literature on CKD transition is derived from high-income healthcare settings, caregiver experiences and transition challenges are influenced by broader cultural, systemic, and policy contexts. In resource-limited settings, constrained access to pediatric subspecialty care, fragmented referral pathways, workforce shortages, and financial barriers may intensify caregiver burden and delay transition readiness. Cultural expectations regarding family involvement and patient autonomy may further shape the transition process, underscoring the need for transition models that are adaptable to differences in healthcare infrastructure and resource availability.

Transition questionnaires and models

For adolescents with CKD, the KDIGO provides the most up-to-date clinical guidelines for the Evaluation and Management of CKD. These guidelines recommend initiating transition planning between ages 11 and 14, allowing adequate time for patients to acquire the knowledge, self-management skills, and confidence needed for a successful transition [2].

Before the more recent KDIGO guidelines for managing CKD transition, multiple other holistic models for the transition process had been suggested and built upon [2]. These models include the ON TRAC Transition Clinical Pathway (ON TRAC), the original then later refined TRAQ, RTQ, and the UNC TRxANSITION Interview, with transition readiness domains specified in Table 1 [17–20]. While not specific to the transition of care for CKD patients, each of these models has been used in the treatment of children with a range of kidney-related illnesses. Additionally, they each expanded the transition process to be more patient need-focused, rather than outlining methods of conducting a simple transfer of medical records.

Each of these models has a different approach for how to achieve a successful transition via their differing formats and areas of questioning (domains). Some models, such as the TRAQ, RTQ, and partially the ON TRAC models, rely on the adolescent patient to complete self-administered questionnaires, journals, and planners to record their own progress in transitioning their care (Table 2) [17–19]. Other models, such as the UNC TRxANSITION, are formatted in the style of a clinician interview of the patient, grading their responses to questions based on the patient’s demonstrated level of knowledge [21]. In addition to the format of each model varying, the domains of the questions asked in each transition model differ slightly. The models are similar in that the bulk of questions from each questionnaire are designed to gauge the adolescent patient’s level of knowledge and self-management around their appointments, chronic illnesses, and medications. However, models such as the ON TRAC and UNC TRxANSITION model expanded this scope to ask more encompassing and holistic questions outside of the patient’s chronic diseases. These additional questions included assessing the patient’s knowledge of sexual health as well as inquiring about their future educational and vocational plans [17, 21].

Due to the lack of randomized controlled trials comparing the results of each of these different models, it is impossible to prove which approach to transition would yield the best results. However, each of these models works towards a successful transition by evaluating a patient’s understanding of their condition, medication management abilities, and capacity to schedule and attend appointments independently, enabling providers to identify gaps in readiness and tailor patient education accordingly. The work done to create and implement these models in their respective hospital systems paved the way to the recent KDIGO clinical guidelines for transitioning CKD.

Current strategies for transition of care in accordance with KDIGO 2024 guidelines

Healthcare professionals play a central role in ensuring a successful transition. Pediatric providers are advised to gradually increase patient independence by seeing adolescents alone for part of each visit, encouraging their participation in decision-making, and introducing self-management tasks over time. This approach aligns recommendations to shift from family-based consultations toward more adolescent-focused care, which emphasizes autonomy and fosters ownership of health. Providing a detailed written and verbal summary of the patient’s medical history, cognitive ability, and social support network to the adult care team further supports a smooth handoff (Fig. 3) [2, 10].

Once the young person reaches the age of transfer of care, it is critical to assess the patient’s medical and social situation routinely to find an opportune time to transition care. An ideal transition occurs when the patient is medically and socially stable. At the age of 18 to 25, adult providers can ease the transition by arranging introductory visits to their clinics before transfer, offering more frequent appointments early on, and involving caregivers or significant others in their visits when appropriate. Once the transfer is complete, pediatric providers are advised to check in with patients to ensure the process was successful [2].

Communication between pediatric and adult teams is critical to minimize adverse outcomes, nonadherence, and loss to follow-up.2 Structured transfer clinics, when available, facilitate this process by bringing together a multidisciplinary team such as social workers, psychologists, and youth workers to address both medical and psychosocial needs during transition. Although these clinics may be limited by funding, their collaborative approach exemplifies best practice in improving transition outcomes [24, 25].

Future directions

While various recommendations have been made to combat challenges faced during the transition from pediatric to adult care, there is a lack of randomized trials assessing the effectiveness [2]. However, one such randomized clinical trial evaluating pediatric and adolescent patient navigation during the transition process to adult-oriented care with chronic conditions, including CKD, found that navigator support was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in emergency department utilization overall, though trends in some subgroups and study limitations underscore the need for larger, kidney-specific transition intervention trials [26]. The Society of Adolescent Health and Medicine (SAHM) reiterates this and emphasizes that this limited research should be addressed, particularly in terms of quality improvement approaches on both a local and population level [27]. The Six Core Elements of Health Care Transition is highlighted by the SAHM, as it outlines a structured, population health-based method that can be applied to both primary care and subspecialty practices [27]. This approach can be used as a framework to conduct future research projects, with the hope of formulating universally standardized protocols [27]. When building on this foundation to create guidelines, specific subgroups within the patient pool should also be considered. There is limited research regarding the transition of care process in patients with CKD and co-existing developmental disabilities, particularly given the cognitive and executive function demands of kidney-specific self-management, including medication adherence, dialysis or transplant care, and appointment coordination. In their transition process, a unique approach must be considered, likely involving neuropsychiatric testing [27]. Future research exploring kidney-specific neuropsychiatric screening and transition pathways to provide more accessible neuropsychiatric testing for securing support and resources for CKD patients with developmental disabilities is called for [27].

Technology-based interventions in CKD transition

Given that the use of technology and electronic health records is continually advancing, incorporating methods such as planning, assessment, and support tools can improve the care transition process for both patients and providers. Applying these resources in the form of both mobile and telehealth formats can ensure a smooth transition, where patients feel comfortable and providers can communicate more effectively with each other. These components have already been implemented in the platform Good2Go, a medical passport program established in Toronto, Canada, that allows for data-sharing [27]. This program is evidence that introducing technology to the transition of care can optimize process outcomes for both patients and the healthcare team involved [27].

Discussion

Transition from pediatric to adult care in CKD is a vulnerable phase. Poorly structured transition is associated with loss of follow-up, non-adherence, increased morbidity, and mortality. Adolescents face the highest risk of graft loss in the case of transplantation. Despite transition being a critical period, wide variability exists among transition models in their format and goals. Some models use questionnaires, others with structured pediatric-adult clinics, and self-management manuals. However, these models differ in clinician training requirements and feasibility across centers [2, 5, 7]. Beyond medical risks, adolescents face unique challenges such as developmental, psychosocial, and health literacy challenges, which complicate the transition process [4, 12].

Validated instruments such as ON TRAC, TRAQ, and UNC TRxANSITION scale assess transition readiness. These tools help adolescents, families, and clinicians identify gaps in self-management, medication adherence, allowing for early intervention. While widely used in nephrology, comparative studies are lacking. Structured models like pediatric-adult clinics and RISE to transition protocols have shown benefits for adherence and continuity of care. However, widespread adoption of pediatric-adult clinics is limited by funding and by the availability of pediatric and adult care provider staffing at all locations [2]. The KDIGO guidelines emphasize early initiation of transition as early as 11 years, relying on using transition tools and gradual autonomy.

Emerging technology provides new avenues for improving transition. Patient portals, mobile apps, and medical passport programs such as Good2Go in Canada have shown enhanced self-management in transition.

This scoping review has several limitations; only PubMed/ MEDLINE articles were systematically searched, and other databases such as Embase, Google Scholar, and CINAHL were not included, limiting the retrieval of additional relevant studies. We excluded studies without full-text availability, potentially missing out on insights into the real-world transition process. Additional limitations include the potential for publication and language bias, as only English-language studies with full-text availability were included, and relevant publications may have been missed despite a comprehensive search strategy. Furthermore, heterogeneity in study designs, populations, outcome measures, and definitions of transition and readiness across included sources limited direct comparison and precluded quantitative synthesis. Despite these limitations, this review provides an overview of barriers to transition, models, questionnaires, and strategies for improving transition.

Conclusion

The transition from pediatric care to adult care for adolescents with CKD is critical, as it profoundly impacts long-term health outcomes. Poorly managed transition processes are linked to higher rates of loss to follow-up, medication nonadherence, and graft failure. Successful transition models must prioritize these key metrics to improve their transition efficacy. Despite well-documented risks, transition processes lack standardization across institutions. A systematic and gradual transition plan beginning early in adolescence, customized to the patient’s developmental needs, can help them take ownership of their own care.

Successful transition models also emphasize multidisciplinary team collaboration, regular patient follow-up, communication, and education. Moving forward, advocating at the health system and policy level for investment in dedicated pediatric-adult transition clinics, conducting longitudinal studies to assess these models, and measuring patient outcomes such as medication adherence and follow-up, ultimately to improve pediatric and adult health outcomes.
