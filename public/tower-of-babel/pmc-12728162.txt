Revisiting convolutional design for efficient CNN architectures in edge-aware applications
Authors: Onur Erdem Korkmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile Vision Transformers (ViTs) have recently demonstrated impressive performance in computer vision tasks, their high computational demands and memory usage limit their applicability in real-time and edge AI scenarios. In contrast, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) remain the preferred choice for such environments due to their lower latency, inductive bias, and efficiency. This study examines the impact of five widely used convolutional operations (standard 2D spatial, grouped, shuffle, depthwise separable and shift) when integrated into the ResNet-50 architecture. All model variants are trained on the Tiny-ImageNet and CIFAR-10/100 dataset under standardized GPU-based settings and evaluated across three edge AI platforms: Raspberry Pi 5, Coral Dev Board and Jetson Nano. The analysis includes parameter count, FLOPs, accuracy, inference time, power consumption and detailed runtime decomposition. Results show that while depthwise separable convolutions offer theoretical efficiency, they suffer from increased memory access on memory-bound platforms. In contrast, shuffle and shift convolutions yield better trade-offs between accuracy, computational load, and inference speed. These findings provide actionable insights for designing hardware-aware, deployment-optimized CNN architectures suitable for resource-constrained applications.Keywords: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Convolution types, Edge AI, Embedded systems, Real-time inference, Hardware-aware designSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The evolution of deep learning in computer vision has witnessed a remarkable transition from convolution-based architectures to attention-driven models. Originally introduced for natural language processing, transformers were successfully adapted to vision tasks via the Vision Transformer (ViT) framework1,2, which partitions images into fixed size patches and employs self-attention to capture global context. ViTs have demonstrated impressive performance3,4 on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet5 and COCO6, often outperforming conventional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in classification and detection tasks. However, their high computational and memory demands, prolonged training schedules and reliance on large-scale annotated data pose significant challenges for real-time and resource constrained applications7. This limitation becomes even more pronounced when deploying models on embedded AI hardware, where latency, memory usage and energy efficiency are critical constraints.

In contrast, CNNs remain the preferred backbone for such environments due to their lower latency, inductive biases, and efficiency1,2. Despite the rise of transformer based designs, most state of the art vision models still incorporate CNN modules or hybrid structures. For instance, Swin Transformer4 introduces hierarchical stages inspired by ResNet8 to extract local features prior to applying global attention. Moreover, many widely deployed models such as YOLOv59, YOLOv810, Faster R-CNN11, and Mask R-CNN12 continue to rely entirely on convolutional architectures. These models dominate practical applications thanks to their fast inference, robustness under limited data and compatibility with embedded and mobile deployment frameworks13,14. Although convolution based models are considerably lighter than transformers, they still incur non negligible computational costs on embedded hardware. Identifying and optimizing convolution types is therefore essential to enable real-time performance in resource constrained environments. To this end, five convolution variants (standard 2D spatial, grouped, shuffle, depthwise separable, and shift) are evaluated within a unified ResNet-508 backbone. The primary evaluation uses Tiny ImageNet15. Consistency is assessed on CIFAR-10/10016 under the same standardized conditions (details in the Appendix). All models are implemented in PyTorch, trained on a high performance GPU, and exported with appropriate deployment optimizations for three edge AI platforms: Raspberry Pi 517, Google Coral Dev Board18 and NVIDIA Jetson Nano19. This investigation is motivated by real world applications such as autonomous driving20, where multi modal sensory data (e.g., radar, lidar, GPS, and high resolution video) must be processed at rates up to 1 GB/s, making convolutional efficiency a critical design consideration. Our benchmarking includes parameter count, inference time, power consumption and classification accuracy, with special attention to runtime decomposition across edge platforms.

Thus, this study presents the first comprehensive analysis that systematically evaluates five distinct convolutional operations within a unified CNN backbone, combining theoretical formulation with empirical runtime profiling across edge AI platforms. The results deliver actionable insights and establish a component level benchmarking framework critical for the design of efficient, deployment aware CNN architectures.

Related work

To contextualize the analysis, relevant literature on convolutional operations is first reviewed, followed by recent benchmarking efforts that evaluate CNN performance on resource constrained hardware platforms.

Recent studies have systematically examined different convolutional variants and layer-level operations with the goal of improving model accuracy, reducing computational complexity and accelerating inference in CNN architectures. Among these, depthwise separable convolutions (depthwise + pointwise)21 have emerged as a particularly influential design, as they decouple spatial and channel-wise operations, thereby achieving substantial reductions in parameters and FLOPs while preserving competitive accuracy. Grouped convolutions22,23, which later became integral to lightweight models such as ShuffleNet24, enable efficient parallel group-wise processing; however, the inclusion of channel shuffling is essential to mitigate information bottlenecks and maintain strong representational capacity. More recently, shift operations25, which substitute costly multiplications with simple spatial shifts, have been highlighted as effective “zero-FLOP” alternatives to standard convolutions, particularly when integrated with pointwise layers to retain expressive power.

Beyond layer level analysis, several recent studies have benchmarked CNNs on edge AI platforms to evaluate their performance under real world resource constraints:

(i) DeepEdgeBench26 provides a comprehensive benchmarking of popular CNN architectures namely MobileNets21 and ResNets8 on three edge AI platforms: NVIDIA Jetson Nano19, Raspberry Pi 517, and Google Coral Dev Board18. The study evaluates each model in terms of latency, power consumption, and overall responsiveness, offering a standardized framework for assessing deep learning inference efficiency on low power devices. The findings highlight significant performance differences across platforms and models, emphasizing the importance of hardware model compatibility when deploying CNNs in real-time edge applications. Coral Dev Board emerged as the most power efficient platform, particularly when running MobileNets, due to its Edge TPU acceleration;

(ii) Suzen et al.27 investigates the performance of a custom lightweight CNN across three embedded platforms: Jetson TX228, Jetson Nano19, and Raspberry Pi 429. Key performance indicators include inference latency, accuracy and hardware resource utilization, with specific attention to GPU, CPU, RAM, and power consumption metrics. The researchers demonstrate that while Jetson TX2 achieves the fastest inference times, the Jetson Nano offers a strong balance between efficiency and cost effectiveness. The Raspberry Pi 4, though limited in GPU capabilities, still delivers acceptable performance for less demanding tasks. This study highlights the trade offs between hardware sophistication and energy consumption, especially in constrained deployment settings;

(iii) Alqahtani et al.30 benchmarks state of the art object detection models including YOLOv810, EfficientDet Lite14, and SSD MobileNet31 on Raspberry Pi 3/4/5 and Jetson Orin Nano, both with and without Edge TPU32 acceleration. The evaluation focuses on inference latency, energy efficiency, and detection accuracy across diverse deployment scenarios. Notably, the Raspberry Pi 5 with Edge TPU offers a competitive edge in inference speed for quantized models, while Jetson Orin Nano demonstrates superior performance for FP32 (32-bit Floating Point) models. The findings suggest that model selection should align with hardware acceleration capabilities and application specific latency constraints, particularly in mobile or battery powered environments.

These comparative studies and other related work33,34 highlight the growing importance of edge deployment in computer vision, offering valuable insights into end-to-end model performance on embedded devices. However, most focus primarily on model-level benchmarking and overlook detailed layer-level evaluations, particularly regarding how specific convolution operations affect computational efficiency and accuracy. To address this gap, the present study provides a systematic layer-level analysis of five core convolution types (standard 2D spatial, grouped, shuffle, depthwise separable and shift) within a standardised ResNet-50 backbone, thereby offering novel insights that complement existing model-level benchmarks and guide the design of lightweight, hardware-aware CNNs for real-time, resource-constrained applications.

Grouped convolutions offer three key advantages over standard 2D spatial convolutions. First, they facilitate model parallelization by allowing the convolutional operations to be distributed across multiple GPUs, enabling more efficient computation. Second, grouped convolutions reduce memory consumption per GPU, making it possible to process more input samples per batch. Additionally, each group learns distinct feature representations, potentially leading to more structured and diverse learning. Third, grouped convolutions improve parameter efficiency. Depthwise separable convolutions

Xception35, developed by Google, builds upon the ideas introduced in Inception-v336 by replacing traditional convolution blocks with depthwise separable (depthwise + pointwise) convolutions, as illustrated in Fig. 4. These consist of a depthwise convolution where the number of input and output channels must be equal, as shown in Fig. 4 (a) followed by a pointwise convolution Fig. 4 (b). In Xception, depthwise separable convolutions were used in place of standard 2D spatial convolutions to improve computational efficiency. While conventional convolutions employ 11 kernels to capture inter channel correlations and 33 or 55 kernels to capture spatial features, depthwise separable convolutions decouple these functions, modeling spatial and cross channel relationships separately and more efficiently, without requiring intermediate activation layers. This architectural refinement enables Xception to outperform Inception-v3 in both accuracy and efficiency.

Channel shuffle operation

ShuffleNet24 is a computation efficient CNN architecture specifically designed for mobile and resource constrained devices. It draws upon the principles of grouped and depthwise convolutions, integrating them with a novel channel shuffling mechanism to enhance information flow. In essence, shuffled grouped convolutions combine standard group convolutions with a permutation of feature channels to mitigate the representational limitations inherent in grouped structures.

While grouped convolutions significantly reduce computational cost, they confine each group of filters to operate on a fixed subset of input channels, which restricts inter group communication and weakens feature representation (see Fig. 3). Channel shuffling addresses this issue by rearranging the output feature maps from grouped convolutions before they are passed to the next layer. For instance, in Fig. 5, the feature map produced by three filter groups is first partitioned into smaller channel subgroups and then spatially mixed. This operation ensures that subsequent grouped convolutions operate on a more diverse set of features, promoting richer representations across the network.

Channel shuffle operation.

Furthermore, ShuffleNet replaces conventional 33 convolutions with grouped 11 pointwise convolutions to improve computational efficiency. This modification retains the benefits of grouped operations while leveraging the lower overhead of 11 kernels. By strategically combining grouped, depthwise, and pointwise convolutions with channel shuffling, ShuffleNet achieves a highly efficient and lightweight architecture without compromising accuracy making it a preferred choice for real-time inference on mobile platforms.

Shift operation

The widespread adoption of depthwise separable convolutions in CNNs has spurred numerous subsequent studies, including a noteworthy contribution by Wu et al.25, who introduced a novel perspective on convolutional efficiency. Although depthwise separable convolutions are theoretically appealing due to their reduced parameter count and computational load, Wu et al. emphasized the importance of analyzing the trade off between computational cost and memory access a factor often overlooked in typical FLOP based comparisons. In response to this challenge, Wu et al. proposed a novel operation known as the shift convolution, which minimizes memory access by replacing multiplications with spatial shift operations offering a promising alternative for efficient inference in edge-oriented systems.

The shift operation functions as a set of memory manipulations that spatially displace the input tensor’s channels in predefined directions (see Fig. 6), effectively mimicking convolution through shift kernels. Unlike standard 2D spatial or depthwise separable convolutions, the shift operation introduces no additional parameters or FLOPs overhead. This efficiency is achieved by coupling the shift with a subsequent pointwise convolution, which directly accesses the shifted features from the cache without requiring intermediate computation. As demonstrated in the study, this mechanism enables the design of extremely lightweight CNNs that maintain competitive accuracy while significantly reducing model complexity.

Theoretical overview of common convolution types and operations

Given the widespread use of CNNs in image classification, many studies have aimed to reduce their high memory and computational demands, leading to compact yet high-performing architectures. A key finding is that strategically chosen convolutional operations, as shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, strongly influence both efficiency and accuracy. While some emphasize representational power, others optimize computational resources. In this study, we systematically evaluate the training and inference performance of these convolution types within the ResNet-50 framework8.

The standard 2D spatial convolution depicted in Fig. 2 is a common choice in computationally intensive models22,36,37. However, alternative convolution types have been proposed to address the limitations of such operations. For instance, grouped convolutions22, shown in Fig. 3 offer three primary advantages: enabling model parallelism, promoting structured feature learning through sub group specialization, and reducing the number of multiply add operations for improved computational efficiency. The shuffle mechanism24 in Fig. 5 further enhances the effectiveness of grouped convolutions by promoting inter group information flow. Meanwhile, depthwise separable convolutions, illustrated in Fig. 4, are widely employed in lightweight CNN designs21,35, due to their substantial reductions in parameter count and FLOPs. However, despite their theoretical efficiency, they often suffer from fragmented memory access patterns, which impair practical deployment, as discussed in Eq. (6) and (7). To mitigate this drawback, the shift operation25, shown in Fig. 6, provides a low cost alternative by leveraging pointwise convolutions for spatial feature aggregation, without incurring additional computational overhead.

Thus, the theoretical distinctions among convolution types and operations have been systematically outlined, highlighting their structural characteristics and expected computational behaviors. These conceptual insights serve as preliminary expectations prior to experimentation. In the following sections, we examine to what extent the empirical results align with these theoretical predictions.

Integration of convolutional variants into the ResNet-50 framework

First, it is important to clarify why ResNet-508, originally composed of standard 2D spatial convolutions, was chosen as the experimental backbone. As illustrated in Fig. 7, ResNet-50 features a modular and hierarchically structured bottleneck architecture that enables the systematic substitution of convolutional operators without disrupting the overall network topology. Its residual connections ensure stable gradient flow even when architectural variations are introduced, making it particularly suitable for controlled comparative analyses. Moreover, ResNet-50’s widespread adoption and well-characterized computational profile establish it as an ideal benchmark for evaluating efficiency-oriented convolutional designs across diverse hardware platforms.

ResNet-508 architecture.

To ensure a controlled and fair comparison, all five convolutional variants illustrated in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 were individually embedded into a standardized ResNet-508 backbone. As depicted in Fig. 7, ResNet-50 architecture begins with a 77 convolutional layer (64 filters) followed by a 33 max-pooling layer and concludes with a 11 global average-pooling layer before the fully connected classifier. These three stages (the initial convolution, initial pooling layer, and final average-pooling layer) were kept identical across all variants to preserve consistent preprocessing and feature aggregation. Since the original ResNet-50 was designed for the ImageNet5 dataset (224224 input resolution), directly applying it to Tiny-ImageNet15 (6464 resolution) would result in aggressive spatial downsampling and early loss of fine-grained detail. To mitigate this issue, the stride values in the initial 77 convolutional layer and the subsequent 33 max-pooling layer were reduced from 2 to 1. This simple adjustment preserved the spatial resolution of feature maps in the early stages, preventing premature reduction while maintaining a hierarchical structure similar to the original ImageNet configuration. As illustrated in Fig. 7, this modification allowed the model to retain sufficient spatial granularity within the bottleneck modules while remaining fully compatible with the ResNet-50 topology. Apart from the stride reduction from 2 to 1 in the initial layers, the architectural modifications were restricted exclusively to the bottleneck modules, which form the core computational blocks of ResNet-50.

Each bottleneck block in the architecture consists of three convolutional layers (11 33 11) with input–output channel progressions of 64256, 128512, 2561024, and 5122048, respectively. These channel configurations, along with the residual shortcuts used for identity mapping, were preserved for all model variants to ensure architectural consistency and gradient stability. The baseline configuration (Model 1) corresponds to the original ResNet-50 architecture, which employs standard 2D spatial convolutions in all bottlenecks. Model 2 replaces each of the three convolutional layers within every bottleneck by grouped convolutions, where the number of groups was set to 4 to balance channel diversity and computational efficiency. Model 3 extends this by introducing channel shuffle operations after grouped convolutions to enable cross-group information flow. Model 4 implements depthwise-separable convolutions, in which the first and third layers perform 11 pointwise convolutions, while the middle layer applies a 33 depthwise operation. Finally, Model 5 substitutes the 33 depthwise layer with a shift operation, achieving convolution-like behavior through spatial channel displacements combined with pointwise filters.

All five model variants thus share identical architectural depth, residual topology, and channel progressions, differing only in their internal convolutional operators. The final fully connected layer was adjusted to produce 200 output neurons, corresponding to the 200-class structure of Tiny-ImageNet.

Dataset, trainig and evaluation metrics

All experiments were conducted on the Tiny-ImageNet15 dataset, a reduced version of ImageNet5 containing 200 classes with 500 training, 50 validation, and 50 test images per class (120k images, all resized to pixels). It retains ImageNet’s diversity but on a smaller scale, making it suitable for efficiency benchmarking.

All five ResNet-508 model variants (Models 1–5) were trained under identical conditions for 50 epochs using the official training and validation splits, with the test set reserved for final evaluation. Training was performed on an NVIDIA A40 GPU (48 GB) at the ATAHEM HPC Facility38. Each model used SGD (momentum 0.9, weight decay , batch size 128) and an initial learning rate of 0.1, reduced by a factor of 10 at epochs 15 and 30. The standard ImageNet augmentation protocol8 (random crop, horizontal flip, and per-channel normalization) was applied, adapted to resolution.

For evaluation, training and validation loss curves were recorded to assess convergence, and Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy metrics were computed on the test set. Additionally, training time per model was measured to quantify computational cost. Architectural metrics included parameter count and floating-point operations (FLOPs). Deployment efficiency was assessed through inference time and power consumption across a CPU (2.2 GHz Quad Core Intel i7), A40 GPU, and edge AI devices (Raspberry Pi 5, Coral Dev Board, and Jetson Nano). Power consumption was preferred over total energy measurements to better reflect real-time efficiency and instantaneous hardware utilization during inference.

As demonstrated in Table 2, the choice of convolution type significantly impacts both model complexity and classification performance. Notably, grouped and shuffle convolutions reduce parameter count and computational load compared to standard 2D spatial (original ResNet-508 architecture) convolutions while also achieving higher accuracy, corroborating the architectural advantages previously discussed for these operations. For example, Model 3 (Shuffle) achieves a higher Top-1 accuracy (63.13%) than Model 1 (Spatial, 61.32%) while using almost three times fewer parameters and roughly half the number of FLOPs. Moreover, depthwise separable and shift operations yield the lowest parameter and FLOP values among all, offering extremely lightweight alternatives. Despite their simplicity, these two types maintain competitive accuracy levels, demonstrating their effectiveness in resource-constrained scenarios. Model 5 (Shift) attains 58.13% Top-1 accuracy with around five times fewer parameters and about one-tenth the FLOPs compared to Model 1.

Compared to parameters or FLOPs, training time do not decrease proportionally from Model 1 to Model 5. For example, relative to Model 1, Model 5 (Shift) uses approximately five times fewer parameters and one-tenth the FLOPs, yet the training time drops only from 7.8 hours to 6.5 hours. Another crucial observation from Table 2 is that Model 4 (Depthwise Separable) breaks the monotonic trend: despite lower parameter and FLOP counts than Model 3 (Shuffle), its training time is longer. These results indicate that parameter and FLOP counts alone are not sufficient to predict runtime. Memory-bound behavior depends on access patterns and bandwidth, which are not directly captured by parameter or FLOP counts, so runtime inefficiencies may persist even as parameters and FLOPs decrease.

As seen in Table 3, convolution types that reduce computational complexity (grouped, shuffle and shift) consistently lead to faster inference times than standard 2D spatial or depthwise separable convolutions. Across all platforms, the shift operation yields the lowest latency (4.3 ms on Jetson Nano), while depthwise separable convolutions incur the highest (22.1 ms on Raspberry Pi 5). This reaffirms that although depthwise separable convolutions are theoretically efficient in terms of FLOPs and parameters, this efficiency does not directly translate into runtime gains. In contrast, grouped and shuffle variants maintain a more balanced trade-off between computational and memory demands, achieving lower latency without substantial memory overhead. (i) A key observation from Fig. 9 is that convolution layers dominate the total runtime across all variants and hardware types, whereas the relative share of element-wise and depthwise operations increases on memory-bound devices such as the GPU and Jetson Nano. For example, in Fig. 9 (CPU, Model 1 (Spatial: original ResNet-50 architecture)), it accounts for only 4% of the total inference time, while rising to 16% on the GPU within an averaged inference cycle. This behavior suggests that concatenation and other element-wise operations are more execution-efficient on compute-oriented architectures, making them better suited to CPU platforms.

(ii) Figure 9 (GPU, Model 4 (Depthwise)) reinforces the theoretical analysis in expressions (6) and (7), emphasizing the memory-bound nature of depthwise separable convolutions. Although these filters are lightweight in terms of parameters and FLOPs, they impose a substantial burden on memory access. This leads to a significant runtime share on the GPU, with depthwise convolutions occupying up to 53% of total inference time, an indicator of inefficiency on memory-bound platforms and increased energy cost per operation. Even with reduced parameter counts and lower FLOPs, their inference time remains comparable to that of standard 2D spatial convolutions (see Table 3), revealing a clear drawback in practical efficiency for depthwise designs.

(iii) Figure 9 (CPU, Model 5 (Shift) and GPU, Model 5 (Shift)) demonstrates that the shift operation yields a more favorable runtime profile. Although it still incurs minor memory-related delays, it occupies a smaller GPU runtime share (22%) compared to depthwise convolutions. This indicates a more balanced relationship between memory access and computational demand, offering a promising alternative for energy-constrained edge scenarios.

(iv) On the Raspberry Pi 5, the shift operation shows weaker inference performance compared to its CPU counterpart, falling behind both grouped and shuffle variants. Specifically, while the CPU achieves a 5.2 ms inference time for the shift variant, the Raspberry Pi 5 records 18.1 ms, which is over three times slower, indicating that limited compute resources and cache bandwidth create hardware-related inefficiencies for this operation. In contrast, the Coral Dev Board equipped with an Edge TPU exhibits a more balanced runtime profile, maintaining 5.2 ms for the shift variant and distributing computation more efficiently across its tensor cores.

(v) Examining Fig. 9 across Raspberry Pi 5, Coral Dev Board, and Jetson Nano reveals a clear trend: the proportional runtime share of convolution operations decreases progressively, while that of depthwise convolutions increases (18% 28% 33%). This pattern once again confirms that depthwise separable convolutions impose heavier memory traffic and synchronization overhead on memory-bound accelerators, contradicting their theoretical efficiency advantages.

(vi) The relationship between convolutional variants and hardware types can be interpreted through the compute-bound and memory-bound framework. Theoretical explanations in the Convolutional Variants section also define these variants using the same distinction, which is clearly reflected in the experimental findings. As an illustrative example, depthwise separable convolutions, being memory-bound, impose heavier memory-access overhead and therefore perform less efficiently on platforms such as the GPU and Jetson Nano, where they occupied approximately 53% and 33% of total runtime, respectively. Compared to the Raspberry Pi 5 and Coral Dev Board, the Jetson Nano can be classified as the most memory-bound device among the three, featuring a GPU-similar architecture. This structure directly mirrors the theoretical expectations and results in higher runtime shares for depthwise separable layers on both the GPU and Jetson platforms. These values are notably higher than their counterparts (GPU: 53% vs. CPU: 27%; Jetson Nano: 33% vs. Raspberry Pi 5: 18% and Coral: 28%). If a newly selected edge device is compute- or memory-bound, or tends more toward one of these characteristics, the convolution operator will likely perform according to the same distinction that was theoretically and empirically observed in this study. Overall, these consistent theoretical and empirical findings establish a solid baseline for understanding how hardware characteristics affect convolutional efficiency. However, given the diversity of existing edge processors, slight variations in real-time performance are expected across different architectures.

(vii) Compared to Raspberry Pi 5 and Coral Dev Board, Jetson Nano consistently achieves the lowest overall inference times across all convolution types (for instance, 6.1 ms for standard 2D spatial and 4.3 ms for shift) demonstrating its capacity to leverage both algorithmic and architectural optimizations through its parallel CUDA-based execution model.

Taken together, the insights from Table 3 and Fig. 9 reinforce and expand upon the inference time results, showing that the efficiency of convolutional operations cannot be evaluated in isolation from hardware characteristics. While depthwise convolutions appear efficient in terms of parameter count and FLOPs, they suffer disproportionately from fragmented memory access patterns, particularly on GPU and TPU-based systems. In contrast, grouped and shuffle convolutions provide more balanced trade-offs between latency and internal runtime distribution. Notably, the shuffle operation achieves competitive inference performance compared to the shift operation, despite ResNet-50 models using shuffle-based convolutions having approximately twice as many parameters (0.37M vs. 0.17M) and nearly five times more FLOPs (1660M vs. 345M). Although the shift operation is both parameter-free and FLOP-free by design, it introduces non-negligible element-wise overhead, especially on GPU platforms, which can diminish its theoretical efficiency advantage in practice. Overall, Table 3 and Fig. 9 jointly validate the practical outcomes of different convolution types under uniform experimental conditions, offering detailed insight into the runtime behavior of each convolutional variant in real-world inference scenarios.

Insights on Power Consumption:

As reflected in Table 3, standard 2D spatial convolutions exhibit the highest power draw due to their dense multiply–accumulate workload and full-channel memory access. Depthwise separable convolutions, although computationally lighter, display inefficient power characteristics caused by fragmented memory access and limited parallel utilization. Grouped and shuffle variants maintain moderate energy usage, balancing computational and memory efficiency, while the shift operation consistently delivers the lowest steady-state power consumption thanks to its multiplication-free and memory-light nature. Nevertheless, certain non-standard trends were observed in Table 3. For instance, the depthwise separable configuration shows unexpectedly lower power consumption on the Coral Dev Board (1.8 W) compared to the grouped and shuffle variants. This anomaly likely arises from platform-specific runtime scheduling, the Edge TPU’s kernel-fusion optimization or transient variations in memory-access management during quantized inference, which can temporarily mask the typical memory-bound inefficiency of depthwise convolutions.

Limitations and future directions

Although the overall results presented in Table 3 and Fig. 9 align well with theoretical expectations, several non-standard observations were also noted. For instance, the shift operation did not consistently achieve the lowest inference time on all edge platforms, showing slightly higher values on the Raspberry Pi 5 and Coral Dev Board. Similarly, in terms of power consumption, the shuffle variant recorded 6.1 W on the Raspberry Pi 5, surpassing the shift model’s efficiency. These deviations highlight the complex interaction between hardware design, software runtime scheduling, and memory access characteristics. They also underscore that while the obtained results are robust and insightful, they should not be overgeneralized across architectures or platforms. Future studies should therefore aim to extend this analysis across newer hardware generations and heterogeneous computing environments to validate the observed trends.

(i) An important consideration involves the distinction between offline (batch-based) and online (query-per-second, QPS-based) deployment modes, which can significantly affect timing behavior. While the present work focuses on offline inference benchmarking, incorporating online deployment scenarios in future studies would provide a more comprehensive understanding of real-time system performance and scalability across varying load conditions.

(ii) Another promising avenue involves the integration of advanced inference optimizations such as CUDA Graphs or Torch Compile, which were intentionally omitted in this study to maintain a uniform experimental setup. These techniques can substantially enhance runtime efficiency, particularly for element-wise operations, by reducing kernel launch overhead and fusing multiple operations into a single execution graph. Incorporating such methods in future experiments could further refine latency measurements and uncover additional optimization opportunities at the framework level.

(iii) Regarding the analysis of memory behavior, it is acknowledged that the current interpretation of memory-bound performance was primarily derived from parameter counts and FLOPs, which only indirectly approximate actual memory access patterns. Future work should include low-level profiling of memory bandwidth utilization and cache access latency to more precisely quantify memory-driven inefficiencies and understand their contribution to overall runtime behavior.

(iv) Exploring deployment-oriented frameworks such as TVM39, MNN40, or KleidiAI41 represents another promising direction. These compilers perform platform-specific kernel optimization and code generation, which could alter absolute runtime values and even change the relative rankings observed in this study. Evaluating such frameworks would provide valuable insights into the portability and adaptability of convolutional variants across different deployment toolchains.

(v) Incorporating vendor-specific tuning parameters and hardware libraries (for example, ArmNN42 or OpenMP43) could further enhance inference efficiency on certain devices. Although such optimizations were beyond the scope of this standardized evaluation, their inclusion in future work may yield improved performance fidelity and better reflect platform-specific strengths.

(vi) Evaluating training performance directly on edge devices also represents a valuable future direction. While this study primarily focused on inference-side evaluation, extending the analysis to include on-device training and fine-tuning would provide deeper insights into computational feasibility, energy efficiency, and optimization strategies for resource-constrained environments.

(vii) The present analysis identifies ResNet-50 as a strong and balanced experimental framework because it can incorporate all five convolutional variants under comparable architectural conditions without altering its structural integrity. Its three-layer bottleneck configuration (11 33 11) provides a stable and fair foundation for evaluating algorithmic efficiency and runtime behavior. This level of integration is not feasible in ResNet-188, as its two-layer block design (33 33) prevents depthwise and shift operations from being applied without extensive architectural modification. While the results demonstrate broad generalization potential, future studies could extend this evaluation to lightweight and hardware-aware architectures such as MobileNet21 to further test the transferability of these findings. Such work would help validate the consistency of the observed trends across models of varying complexity and enhance the practical relevance of the proposed framework.

Taken together, these directions highlight the potential for deeper investigation into runtime optimization, memory-access characterization, and deployment-level inference strategies. Addressing these aspects in future studies will further strengthen the practical applicability and generalization of convolutional design insights presented in this work.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated five convolutional variants (standard 2D spatial, grouped, shuffle, depthwise separable, and shift) within a unified ResNet-50 framework trained on Tiny ImageNet and deployed across CPU, GPU, and edge AI devices. The results reveal that theoretical efficiency based on parameter count and FLOPs does not always translate to practical gains, as runtime behavior strongly depends on hardware characteristics. Depthwise convolutions, while computationally lightweight, exhibit pronounced memory-bound inefficiencies, whereas grouped and shuffle convolutions achieve a more balanced trade-off between computation and memory access. The shift operation consistently delivers the fastest inference times across most platforms, validating its suitability for latency-critical edge deployments. Overall, the findings highlight the necessity of hardware-aware design in CNN development and provide actionable insights for optimizing convolutional architectures in real-time, resource-constrained environments.

Appendix: CIFAR-10/100 experiments

To further examine the robustness of the findings obtained on the Tiny ImageNet dataset, additional analyses were conducted using the CIFAR-10/100 datasets. These follow-up experiments aimed to examine whether the comparative tendencies observed across convolutional variants remain consistent when applied to datasets with different spatial resolutions and class granularities. All architectural, optimization, and evaluation settings were preserved as closely as possible to ensure comparability. The following subsections summarize the integration procedure, training configurations, and key outcomes of these CIFAR-based experiments.

Adaptation of tiny ImageNet–trained ResNet-50 variants to CIFAR

Each ResNet-50 model variant (Models 1–5), originally introduced in the subsection Integration of Convolutional Variants into the ResNet-50 Framework, was retained to maintain full consistency with the main experimental design. Only minor structural adaptations were introduced for the CIFAR experiments. Specifically, since CIFAR images have a resolution, the initial convolution layer was replaced with a convolution using a stride of 1 to prevent excessive spatial reduction during the early stages. The subsequent max-pooling layer with stride 1 was kept unchanged. Apart from this input-level modification, the entire bottleneck hierarchy (comprising three convolutional layers (11, 33, 11) in each block) remained identical across all five variants. The final fully connected layer was adjusted to output 10 neurons for CIFAR-10 and 100 neurons for CIFAR-100, while preserving all other architectural and optimization parameters from the Tiny ImageNet configuration.

Training and evaluation protocol for CIFAR experiments

The CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets each contain 50,000 training and 10,000 test images across 10 and 100 categories, respectively. All five ResNet-50 variants (Models 1–5) were trained for 50 epochs using exactly the same optimization, augmentation, and evaluation procedures as in the Tiny ImageNet experiments to ensure a fair and controlled comparison. Stochastic Gradient Descent (momentum 0.9, weight decay ) was employed with a batch size of 128 and an initial learning rate of 0.1, reduced by a factor of 10 at epochs 15 and 30. The same data-augmentation protocol (random crop, horizontal flip, per-channel normalization) was applied at the input resolution. All training and inference evaluations were conducted on the same hardware platforms (CPU, GPU, Raspberry Pi 5, Coral Dev Board, and Jetson Nano) so that any performance differences arise solely from dataset characteristics rather than configuration changes. Similar to the Tiny ImageNet experiment results presented in Tables 2, 3, Figs. 8 and 9, all outcomes of the CIFAR experiments are summarized in Tables 4, 5, Figs. 10, and 11, respectively, each presented with its corresponding index for direct comparison. Results and discussion for CIFAR experiments

As presented in Table 4 and Table 5, and illustrated in Fig. 10 and Fig. 11, the CIFAR experiments produced results that remain largely consistent with the Tiny ImageNet findings reported in Table 2, Table 3, Fig. 8edcba, and Fig. 9. The shuffle variant again achieved the highest classification accuracy (96.04% on CIFAR-10 and 77.33% on CIFAR-100) while maintaining a considerably lower parameter count and FLOPs, reinforcing its efficiency advantage across datasets. Grouped and shift variants also preserved their favorable trade-offs between accuracy and complexity, displaying the same architectural balance observed in the Tiny ImageNet experiments.

The convergence curves in Fig. 10 demonstrate stable learning behavior across all convolutional types, with smooth loss reduction and no indication of early overfitting. Similarly, inference-time and power-consumption trends in Table 5 and Fig. 11 follow the same tendencies observed in Fig. 9. The shift operation continues to deliver the lowest latency and power draw on most devices, while the depthwise separable variant maintains a memory-bound profile comparable to earlier results. Two minor deviations were noted: (i) on the Jetson Nano, the depthwise separable convolution exhibited a slightly longer inference time (5.6ms) than the standard 2D spatial model (original ResNet-50 architecture) (5.4ms), differing slightly from the Tiny ImageNet results; and (ii) on the Raspberry Pi 5, power consumption for the shuffle and shift variants was identical at 5.9W, whereas in the Tiny ImageNet experiment, it measured 6.1W (shuffle) and 6.3W (shift).

Overall, the CIFAR experiments produce results that are consistent with and supportive of the Tiny ImageNet analyses. The relative ranking of convolutional variants, convergence dynamics, and runtime characteristics remain stable across datasets, indicating that the architectural insights derived from the main experiments generalize reliably to smaller-scale benchmarks.
