Milder winters would alter patterns of freezing damage for epiphytic lichens from the trans-Himalayas
Authors: Fiona Ruth Worthy, Stefanie D. Goldberg, Vinodhini Thiyagaraja, Li Song Wang, Xin Yu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Trans-Himalayan winters are projected to become milder, with shifting precipitation patterns and freeze-thaw cycles; changing stressors for their lichen communities. Lichens from Antarctica and high latitudes are cryoresistant when dry, but susceptible to cell damage if frozen when wet, or subjected to repeated freeze-thaw events. Little is known regarding cryoresistance in high-elevation, mid-latitude lichens. We collected thalli of nine species of epiphytic lichenized fungi, from three regions of the trans-Himalayas; at ≈ 4000 m, 3400 m and 2400 m elevation. We subjected thalli to differing freezing (continuous − 18 °C and − 36 °C or freeze-thaw cycles in natural daylight) and moisture conditions. Even dry thalli suffered some damage. Frozen wet thalli had greater chlorophyll degradation and reduced chlorophyll content. There were no clear elevational trends in freeze-thaw susceptibility: it caused more damage than continuous freezing. The most freeze-thaw resilient lichens were Dolichousnea longissima (from 4000 m) and Usnea florida (from 2400 m). However, species from coldest sites were most resilient to extreme freezing. Under predicted climate change conditions these sites would experience fewer annual freeze-thaw cycles, annual sub-zero days and frost days. Reduced freezing constraints might allow range expansion of mid-elevation lichens, but increase competitive pressures and temperature stressors impacting high-elevation lichens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-79321-2.

Introduction

With the impending threat of climate change1, high latitude and high elevation regions are anticipated to experience accelerated rates of warming2–4. Striking changes to winter temperatures are already causing permafrost thaw5 and receding snow cover6. Winter warming conditions are likely to allow previously temperature-limited species to shift range upwards in elevation7, with some already marching towards mountain summits7. Competitively dominant species that successfully track climate might be able to expand their range8 at the cost of stress-tolerant species. Thus, the highest elevation cold-tolerators may suffer range contraction9,10.

Lichens are the ultimate freezing-tolerant organisms; achieving remarkable resilience to sub-zero temperatures. There is high inter-specific variation in freezing tolerance, with photobionts of some lichens surviving deep-cooling to −78.5 °C, whereas others suffer serious damage11. Trebouxia algae are especially cryoresistant12 and are the predominant photobiont in Antarctica13, whereas freezing-susceptible Trentepohlia photobionts appear to impose latitudinal limits on lichen distribution14. Surprisingly, some tropical lichens maintain freeze-tolerance capacities, but freezing-survival is not necessarily matched by photosynthetic capacity at sub-zero temperatures: the tropical Cora pavonia (Weber & D. Mohr) Fr. survives − 78.5 °C cooling, but ceases photosynthesis below 0 °C11. In southern Norway, Lasallia pustulata L. (Mérat) and Umbilicaria spodochroa Hoffm. maintain photosynthesis while temperatures fluctuate between − 2 °C to – 5 °C, despite ice formation on the thallus surface15. Some photosynthetic activity can be maintained by Usnea antarctica Du Rietz, from maritime Antarctica, until − 20 °C and by Umbilicaria cylindrica (L.) Delise, from Svalbard, until − 30 °C16. Whilst facilitating survival, sub-zero photosynthetic activity may be insufficient to support growth. At one of the most extreme habitats occupied by lichens, in the Dry Valleys of Antarctica, Buellia frigida Darb. grows a mere 0.01 mm y− 117. Because very few studies have addressed the freezing tolerance of high-elevation lichens, current understanding of underlying freezing-tolerance mechanisms is primarily based on studies in Antarctica and high latitudes.

To survive low temperatures, lichens must either enter dormancy or maintain metabolic activity and avoid tissue damage caused by ice formation18. They control their own microclimate by a low stature growth form and melanic pigmentation which increases absorption of solar radiation18. They achieve freezing tolerance by physiological mechanisms of accumulation of antifreeze substances19, and ice nucleation substances produced by the mycobiont which serve as a means of water acquisition and minimise the size of ice crystals which might otherwise cause cell rupture20,21. Alternatively, lichens can avoid freezing damage by complete desiccation and dormancy18. Dehydration resistance allows survival of the mycobiont and photobiont and the ability to rapidly regain metabolic activity upon hydration18.

Rehydration is enabled not only from liquid water and water vapour beneath snow22, but even from solid snow and frozen water, due to their maintenance of a steep water potential gradient and thallus temperature equal to or below that of the ice23. For Umbilicaria aprina Nyl., cellular structural changes during freezing24 are similar to those during desiccation25. Cell walls experience negative turgor until cavitation26, reducing protoplast water potential, supressing or mitigating intracellular freezing24. All these processes are reversible when the thallus is warmed24.

Nonetheless, repeated freeze-thaw cycles can cause visible damage to otherwise cryoresistant lichens27, aggravate short-term reductions in viability, and might reduce long-term fitness28. Paradoxically, warmer climatic conditions may cause more freeze-thaw cycles, causing potential freezing damage to some freezing-tolerant lichen species29.

For lichens in above-zero temperature conditions, diurnal temperature fluctuations can be a favourable condition, generating morning dew and allowing optimal hydration levels during the morning hours as temperatures rise and the water content of thalli gradually declines30. Because both suprasaturation and desiccation tend to supress photosynthesis, the highest photosynthetic rates are likely to be obtained during these periods of intermediate thallus hydration30. However, such photosynthetic patterns seem less likely to occur during winter15. When diurnal fluctuations involve temperatures rising above and dropping below zero, although some lichens may still thrive15, for most species temperature fluctuations appear likely to switch from being advantageous to highly disadvantageous climatic conditions27–29.

Crucially, global precipitation patterns are expected to change and become more variable31. With warming at high latitudes, winter precipitation may shift from snow to rain. The resulting snowmelt can cause ice encapsulation that actually protects the lichen32, yet milder, fluctuating freezing temperatures can cause death of the algal layer33. In other cases, photobionts may still survive being frozen while hydrated, even in the case of some European, tropical and sub-tropical species11. However, warmer, moister snow, when lichens are metabolically active, can be more damaging than colder, drier snow34. Thus, the outcomes of changing snow patterns will be unpredictable, as tolerance of snow cover can differ even between lichen species of the same genus, growing in adjacent microhabitats35. It is important to understand future species-specific climate change impact on lichens because they make major contributions to ecosystem properties36, comprise the food source of animals37,38 and are used by local people for food, beverages and traditional medicine39,40.

Our study regions in Yunnan Province, China, are at the south-eastern limit of the trans-Himalayas (Fig. 1). The geological history of Yunnan has led to geographically isolated plant populations, with consequent divergent evolution41 and speciation42. Lichen species diversification has also clearly occurred across the Himalayas and trans-Himalayan regions43. Thus, lichen chemical compounds and degree of freezing adaptations may differ considerably to those in Antarctica and high latitudes. In Yunnan province, although spring and summer precipitation are expected to decrease, autumn and winter precipitation should increase44. This could potentially alter whether lichens will be wet or dry at the time of freezing, changing the likelihood and extent of cell rupture overwinter. The only previous lichen freezing study in China, indicated that this is likely to threaten local lichen populations. Two species from Mount Wulingshan in northern China; Flavopunctelia soredica (Nyl.) Hale and Peltigera elisabethae Gyeln. had progressively reduced net photosynthesis after repeated freeze-thaw cycles, with the worst outcomes for wet thalli29.

Theoretically, we would expect lichens to be best adapted to freezing in places where they previously underwent the most severe freezing. Thus, the highest elevation lichens could be anticipated to be best able to tolerate the lowest freezing temperature conditions. However, these highest elevation lichens would previously have had more days in which temperatures never rose above freezing – thus might be less well-adapted to freeze-thaw cycles.

Therefore, we conducted a laboratory freezing experiment using lichen thalli collected from the trans-Himalayas. We selected nine species of epiphytic lichenized fungi (hereafter ‘lichen species’), which are at especial risk of range contraction, because epiphytes cannot expand upwards above the tree-line.

We aimed to test the following series of non-mutually exclusive hypotheses:

Methods

Study sites and species

We selected three study regions of the trans-Himalayas (Diqing, Lijiang and Baoshan) in the northwest of Yunnan Province, China (Fig. 1). From May to June 2018, we surveyed study sites and species. In each region we selected three main study sites and three epiphytic lichen species. These sites were along an elevational gradient from 4045 m to 2357 m above sea level (Fig. 1; Table 1), and were located at either the top of mountain ridges or at the treeline. Our sites in Diqing and Lijiang were in mature, old-growth forest. Our sites in Baoshan were in an approximately 50-year-old plantation, in which epiphytes had successfully re-established.

Because temperature-driven physiological responses may differ between fruticose and foliose lichens, we selected at least one representative of each at each region (Supplementary Table S1), ensuring that impacts of growth form were not confounded with elevation. All species are of importance locally or regionally as food or medicine40,45. No locally over-harvested species were included. Our species selected from Diqing were: Dolichousnea longissima (Ach.) Articus (fruticose, previously named Usnea longissima Ach.), Hypogymnia flavida McCune & Obermayer and Lobaria retigera (Bory) Trevis. group (both foliose). D. longissima is a major food source for the endangered, endemic, lichenivorous black-and-white snub-nosed monkey Rhinopithecus bieti (also called ‘Yunnan snub-nosed monkey’)38,46. In Lijiang we selected Sulcaria sulcata (Lév.) Bystrek (fruticose), Lobaria pindarensis Räsänen, and Dendriscosticta hookeri (Trevis.) Moncada & Lücking (both foliose). In Baoshan we selected Usnea florida (L.) F. H. Wigg. (fruticose), Hypotrachyna cirrhata (Fr.) Divakar, A. Crespo, Sipman, Elix & Lumbsch (foliose), and Ramalina conduplicans Vain. (fruticose). All these were chlorolichens (i.e., with green algae as the primary photobiont), with the exception of L. retigera. For the purposes of this experiment, none of these species should be considered cephalolichens, as in the study locations, none of these species usually possess cephalodia. No cephalodia were observed on any of the experimental specimens. Elsewhere, lichens of the L. retigera group are cyanolichens (i.e., with cyanobacteria as the primary photobiont), but regionally they have acquired green algae and become chloro-cyanolichens, with green algae and cyanobacteria as co-primary photobionts47. Samples collected in the Diqing sites were obtained from three of the locations included in the study of Worthy et al.47, thus the green algae within the L. retigera specimens would be anticipated to be Parachloroidium sp.

Microclimate

In June 2018, we attached data loggers to tree-trunks at each of the nine specimen collection sites, plus an additional nine comparison sites: each lower down the same mountain (Fig. 1; Table 1). The tree canopy acted as a solar shield. Attachment to trunks ensured that data reflected the actual microclimate conditions experienced by epiphytes. Insulation by the tree means that these conditions may differ substantially from data generated from DEM-based climate models. Temperature and relative humidity were logged at 30-minute intervals, using UniT loggers (UT330) in 2018, HT-160 loggers in 2019 and Hygrochron iButton loggers (DS1923) from 2019 to 2020. Loggers were cross-calibrated. Because freezing conditions sometimes caused logger failure, we analysed mean daily data from this period.

Sample collection

We collected healthy lichen samples from the study sites in September 2018; a period at which we did not expect recent frosts, thus minimising the risk of prior freezing damage. Thalli were selected which showed no visible tissue damage (i.e., excluding those with tissue discoloration, decay or torn thalli (see Supplementary Fig. S1). Where available, ten thalli of each species were collected from each of their three sites (N = 30). In Baoshan, uneven species spatial distribution meant that more thalli were collected from some sites but fewer from others. Species identification was made on the basis of external morphology. The appearance of D. longissima, H. flavida, S. sulcata, Hy. cirrhata and R. conduplicans are distinctive, with no common very similar species in the locality (although the taxonomy of H. flavida is disputed48). Phylogenetic methods would be necessary to confirm species identity within the other genera, within which new species continue to be described in this region, but such molecular approaches are beyond the scope of our ecological study.

Samples were air-dried and taken to the Kunming Institute of Botany. Thalli (per species N = 30; total for all nine species N = 270) were assigned as matched pairs, assigned to wet or dry conditions (per species: wet N = 15, dry N = 15). Each thallus was cut into three parts of approximately equal area comprising complete sections of thallus body and tips to create sets of six matching specimens (per species: sets N = 15; each matching set = two thalli, each cut into 3 parts; samples N = 90, for all nine species samples N = 810). For species with smaller thalli, samples were matched by size and appearance. One thallus from each set was assigned to each of six different experimental conditions (two moisture conditions × three freezing conditions). Our matching design means that even if specimens from more than one species were inadvertently included as L. retigera, L. pindarensis, De. hookeri or U. florida, this would not have confounded the results of the study. Furthermore, inter-specimen differences in thallus thickness, specific thallus mass and consequent water holding capacity, would not have biased the experimental outcomes.

After the experiment described below, voucher specimens were deposited in the KUN-L herbarium. Specimen collection details and herbarium numbers are provided in Mendeley Data repository at DOI:10.17632/sn5py5wbfn.1.

Freezing experiment

In October 2018, the dried specimens described above were placed into transparent plastic containers. These were either kept dry or received 1 mL addition of deionised water. This was sufficient to visibly fully hydrate thalli (see Supplementary Fig. S1 for appearance of dry versus wet thalli). The standardisation of water addition means that thallus water content (water input as % of thallus air-dried mass) would have differed between specimens if all water had been absorbed (624 ± 24.5%, mean ± s.e.m., N = 393). Specimens were not raised above the surface of the container, thus in the water addition condition specimens were partly in contact with water. All specimens were frozen in the dark at –5 °C, then at –18 °C: conditions recommended to preserve air-dried specimens without damage to cell membranes or degradation of photosynthetic pigments50. In late February 2019, specimens were transferred to three different freezing conditions. These were: (a) being frozen in the dark at –18 °C, (b) frozen in the dark at –36 °C, or (c) alternating freeze-thaw conditions in natural daylight. Light exposure conditions at the start of this period were 11:30 h light – 12:30 h dark. By the end of this phase of the experiment, light exposure conditions were 13:20 h light – 10:40 h dark. The freeze-thaw condition was obtained by placing specimens in a cooling unit with a glass top, situated indoors, but in a sun-exposed, south-facing position. Thin, semi-opaque blinds were placed over the windows to minimise the risk of potential tissue damage due to excessive sun exposure. Containers were pierced to allow gas exchange. The glass top of the cooling unit was opened daily to ensure ventilation. Overnight temperatures were set for −5 °C. Sun exposure was used to simulate field conditions by gradually warming specimens, such that daytime temperatures were brought above zero (see Supplementary Fig. S2). Loggers (HT-160) were placed beside specimens in each cooling unit to record actual experimental conditions. If necessary, settings were adjusted to ensure the intended stable freezing or freeze-thaw conditions were achieved.

In mid-May 2019, the freezing phase was terminated. Analysis was conducted for all specimens from the freeze-thaw or –36 °C treatment. Specimens from the −18 °C freezing condition were examined to determine if there were healthy sections of the thallus from which recovery could occur. ‘Healthy’ specimens appeared to have live areas of thalli and lacked obvious signs of decay. ‘Unhealthy’ specimens had areas that were discoloured and appeared decayed. Unhealthy specimens were removed and immediately analysed. Healthy specimens were divided into two parts: (a) immediate analysis, (b) recovery condition. For the recovery treatment, specimens (N = 142) were placed into the glass-topped cooling unit, with settings adjusted to obtain 5 °C overnight temperatures. The recovery treatment was terminated after two weeks, then specimens were analysed.

Electroconductivity

Because the responses of mycobiont and photobiont to adverse climatic conditions may be decoupled51, we measured not only factors related to the photobiont (photosynthetic pigments), but also a parameter that relates to both mycobiont and photobiont cells. Electroconductivity (EC) is a measure of cell integrity, because high values indicate damage to cell membranes52,53 resulting in leaching of ions through the cell membranes into an aqueous solution54. Specimens were examined and a note made as to apparent health or decay. They were each rinsed with deionised water, then placed into 20 mL deionised water, for 3 h at ≈ 15 °C. Electroconductivity, pH and water temperature were measured using a TRIMETER portable sensor, calibrated with pH 4.01 and pH 7.0 standards. The sensor was rinsed with deionised water between each measurement.

Specimens were then air-dried. Small sections were removed from three tips of each thallus, to obtain 0.02 g samples, used for chlorophyll testing. Completely decayed specimens were excluded (freezing experiment, remaining: N = 786, recovery treatment, N = 142). The remainder of each specimen was weighed, oven-dried, then weighed again. The mean mass difference was used to correct values for thallus mass in the calculations below. EC values were standardised as per gram oven-dried thallus mass.

Chlorophyll analysis

Chlorophyll and carotenoid content were tested, adapting standard methods in lichenology. Acidic lichen substances, such as usnic acid, can cause chlorophyll to degrade to phaeophytin55, reducing the accuracy of chlorophyll testing. It is critical to avoid this issue if phaeophytinisation is to be used as a measure of lichen tissue degradation, as in the present study. To overcome this problem, thalli can be pre-washed for one minute, six times with CaCO3 saturated acetone55; a method which has the further advantage of likely eliminating any epiphytic algae that might otherwise influence results. This approach has been widely followed, with some authors using even longer rinse times or more rinses56,57. For microplate spectrophotometers, such as the Infinite M200 Pro KZ10924, Tecan 2013072-333 used in this study, up to 96 samples can be tested in a single plate, making it feasible to test hundreds of samples. However, to repeatedly pre-wash for such short time intervals becomes logistically impossible. For a set of freshly collected, control thalli (split between three conditions, N = 108) we tested how the results of extraction in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) without pre-washing would compare to extraction in DMSO after a single, 30-minute wash in CaCO3 saturated acetone or five 5-minute washes. Samples were kept in the dark between all subsequent steps.

There was a marginally significant difference in OD ratio between species (N = 108, p = 0.07; Supplementary Table S3, Supplementary Fig. S4). The lower bounds were comparable between species, but the highest OD ratios were for L. pindarensis and De. hookeri (Supplementary Fig. S4). For three species (Hy. cirrhata, L. retigera, and U. florida) there was no difference at all between pre-washing methods, perhaps indicating a very low content of acidic lichen substances. For the other six species, the high variance in OD ratio could indicate inter-specimen variation in concentration of acidic lichen substances or alternatively could simply reflect the variable conditions to which thalli are exposed in their natural habitat. However, there was no significant difference (N = 108, p = 0.11) in OD ratio between the three pre-washing methods, indicating no statistical basis for preferring five pre-washes versus one pre-wash with CaCO3 saturated acetone. Nonetheless, we did detect a slight, apparent trend for lower OD ratio in the DMSO only pre-wash condition (Supplementary Fig. S4). Therefore, as a precaution, we chose a single 30-minute pre-wash in CaCO3 saturated acetone for all subsequent tests, rather than extraction in DMSO alone. The pre-wash treatment was then followed by extraction in DMSO for the chlorophyll analysis.

Cool, well-ventilated storage of chemicals and use of personal protective equipment are recommended for these procedures. DMSO and acetone are both flammable. CaCO3 is not considered hazardous, but skin contact and inhalation should be avoided. DMSO readily penetrates skin and may carry dissolved chemicals into the body. Acetone is considered hazardous with exposure risking eye or organ damage. There is a clear safety advantage in decreasing the number of pre-washes with acetone.

Chlorophyll was extracted in 2 mL DMSO58,59. Samples were incubated at 20 °C for 24 h. Then 400 µL was extracted and added to 800 µL fresh DMSO and incubated for 30 min. The upper 250 µL was extracted and injected into microplates, then refrigerated at 5 °C. This extraction volume, with subsequent dilution was generally sufficient to obtain the low sample turbidity and volumes suitable for microplate-based spectrophotometry. Prepared microplates were warmed to 20 °C, then inserted into the spectrophotometer. Absorbance was measured at 1 nm resolution. Absorption was recorded for each sample at 415, 435, 480, 649, 665 and 750 nm. Raw values at 415, 435, 480, 649 and 665 nm were corrected by subtracting the reading at 750 nm for the same sample, to control for any influence of sample turbidity or position on the spectrophotometer plate. Negative values were excluded.

Chlorophyll and carotenoid content (g mL− 1) were determined following the equations developed by Wellburn60 for 1–4 nm resolution spectrophotometers and DMSO extraction, with an adjustment factor J for volume and sample mass. Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R version 3.6.162. Graphs were produced using either R or GraphPad Prism 9.2.0. ANOVA (function ‘aov’) was applied to determine the importance of pre-rinsing on the OD ratio of different species. The residuals of these data followed a normal distribution. Mixed models (function ‘lmer’ within ‘lme4’63 were used to test the impact of moisture and freezing treatments (fixed effects) on EC, all chlorophyll and carotenoid measures. Thallus water content was not included as an additional independent variable, because it was partially nested within ‘moisture’ – its inclusion would have caused model singularities. Data analysis was conducted separately for each species. Identity of each matching set of samples (‘sample’) and the site from which specimens were collected (‘site’) were included as random effects. Non-significant interactions were deleted unless doing so caused an increase in AIC values. Although linear mixed effects models are generally robust to violations of distributional assumptions64, we examined residual plots and rejected models where residuals departed from a normal distribution. In such cases, log or square transformations were applied; this is indicated as relevant in each table.

We compared our microclimate data to past records from the nearest meteorological stations to our study sites, for which the temperature anomalies are likely to be similar. These meteorological stations are located at elevations of 3404 m (Diqing), 2394 m (Lijiang) and 1652 m (Baoshan). Data was obtained from Berkeley Earth (http://www.berkeleyearth.org). Since 1960, temperatures have been rising by 0.53 °C / century in Diqing, 1.57 °C / century in Lijiang, and 1.08 °C / century in Baoshan. These temperature anomalies were applied to our half-hourly temperature records, to compute likely past temperatures, then annual freezing days were calculated.

Results

Microclimate

All regions had lower relative humidity (RH) and lower minimum, maximum and mean temperatures during winter (Fig. 2). Throughout most of the year, temperatures were highest in Diqing and lowest in Baoshan. All regions could reach high RH during the summer, but Lijiang tended to be drier in winter. During hours at > 90% RH, while lichen thalli would be moist, freezing conditions would be common in Diqing, frequent in Lijiang, but rare in Baoshan (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2Average daily microclimate conditions at our study sites from 2018 to 2020, displayed with loess fitted lines.

Average daily microclimate conditions at our study sites from 2018 to 2020, displayed with loess fitted lines.

Our sites experienced far fewer continuously sub-zero days than expected. In Baoshan, temperatures never remained below 0 °C for an entire 24-hour period, and in Lijiang there was a maximum of four annual sub-zero days (Table 1). Even the coldest sites at Diqing had less than a month of sub-zero days. Thus, although future warming might eliminate sub-zero days in Lijiang, this would not substantially increase the number of freeze-thaw days. In Diqing, DQ1 had only three annual sub-zero days, probably due to its south-facing slope allowing midday sun exposure. The north-facing sites DQ2 and DQ3 had comparable annual sub-zero days (28 and 27 days respectively).

Combining our microclimate data with temperature anomaly records, we calculated likely past microclimate conditions at our study sites in 1960, then compared this with current conditions. Overall, since 1960, warming probably caused fewer annual sub-zero days, frost days and freeze-thaw days, but more annual frost-free days (Supplementary Table S2).

The temperature differences between the collection and microclimate sites, indicate potential future climate scenarios. The lower sites had fewer annual sub-zero days, frost days and freeze-thaw days, but more annual frost-free days (Table 1). Thus, future climate conditions could be associated with reduced freezing damage.

Within our experimental cooling units, natural light exposure in the intended freeze-thaw condition did reliably achieve daily freeze-thaw cycles. Overnight temperatures typically reached ≈ –6 °C, with a short midday peak temperature of ≈ 11 °C (Supplementary Fig. S2); daily temperature fluctuations which most closely represent current Lijiang winter or Diqing spring microclimate conditions (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S3). However, temperatures during our ‘recovery’ condition were higher than even summer conditions in Baoshan (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. S2); potentially constituting an additional stressful condition. Therefore, although we have displayed the results of the ‘recovery’ condition in all relevant graphs (Figs. 3, 4 and 5), we excluded it from the statistical analyses (Tables 2 and 3, Supplementary Tables S4 to S9).

Dry versus wet thalli

Summary statistics demonstrated substantial variation for every parameter measured, both between and within species (Supplementary Table S4). Notably, D. longissima and U. florida showed the least damage across all freezing conditions, with median EC values of 0 µs cm− 1, and no significant impact of any moisture or freezing condition (Table 2). For the other seven species, thallus moisture content during freezing only marginally affected EC values, with interactions with differing freezing conditions (Table 2; Fig. 3). For L. pindarensis, De. hookeri and R. conduplicans, at –36 °C, dry thalli had higher EC values, but for L. pindarensis and De. hookeri their OD ratio did not differ between these conditions (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 3). OD ratio was a more consistent measure of tissue damage than was EC, with lower variance attributed to inter-sample differences (based on comparison of random effects between Tables 2 and 3). The maximum OD ratios were comparable across species, ranging from 1.00 for H. flavida to 1.36 for L. retigera (Supplementary Table S4). Wet thalli had lower OD ratios for H. flavida, S. sulcata, U. florida, Hy. cirrhata and R. conduplicans. This difference was most apparent for thalli frozen at –36 °C (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Chlorophyll content was generally lower for thalli that had been frozen while wet; a pattern that was most visibly apparent for chlorophyll a (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables S5 – S7). Wet thalli had distinctly lower chlorophyll a for L. retigera, U. florida (especially in the freeze-thaw condition) and Hy. cirrhata (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S5): a pattern that was matched by their total chlorophyll content (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S7). Wet U. florida also had lower chlorophyll b, with an especially clear difference in the freeze-thaw condition (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S6).

These changes jointly impacted chlorophyll a/b, but the extent and direction of change differed between freezing conditions for D. longissima, L. retigera, U. florida and R. conduplicans. Only L. pindarensis had reduced chlorophyll a/b ratio for wet thalli in every freezing condition (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table S8). For U. florida and Hy. cirrhata, carotenoid content was lower for the wet condition. For D. longissima, this effect was only found in the − 36 °C freezing condition, whereas for R. conduplicans, being wet led to lower carotenoid content for both the freeze-thaw and − 36 °C freezing condition.

Freeze-thaw versus continuous freezing

Freeze-thaw cycles generally raised EC values more strongly than continuous freezing at –18 °C, but not necessarily than at –36 °C (Fig. 3). For all species, EC values were higher in the freeze-thaw than − 18 °C condition, although this was not statistically significant for D. longissima or U. florida (Table 2; Fig. 3). OD values only showed a matching trend for H. flavida, L. pindarensis and De. hookeri (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Differences between freezing temperatures in chlorophyll and carotenoid content primarily arose from altered chlorophyll a (with consequent shifts in total chlorophyll and a/b ratio), with comparatively minor changes in chlorophyll b (Fig. 4). Chlorophyll a was mostly lower in the freeze-thaw than the − 18 °C condition (Fig. 4), although this was not significant for three species (Supplementary Table S5).

Total carotenoid content was very low for D. longissima, S. sulcata and U. florida, with no significant difference arising from freezing temperatures for D. longissima and U. florida (Fig. 5, Supplementary Tables S4 & S9). The highest carotenoid content was in the strongly pigmented chloro-cyanolichen, L. retigera (Fig. 5). Its carotenoids were lowest in the freeze-thaw treatment (Fig. 5). For H. flavida, carotenoids were clearly lower in the freeze-thaw than either continuous freezing condition (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table S9).

Elevation and freeze-thaw damage

There was no general pattern for freeze-thaw cycles to cause most damage to lichen from higher elevations. To the contrary, the lowest EC values were for the high-elevation D. longissima, followed by the low-elevation U. florida and R. conduplicans (Fig. 3). The highest EC values caused by freeze-thaw damage were rather for two mid-elevation species: L. pindarensis and De. hookeri (Fig. 3). OD ratios fell within a comparable range for all species (Fig. 3). Chlorophyll contents fell within a similar range for eight species, with only D. longissima having markedly lower chlorophyll (Fig. 4). Neither was the impact of freeze-thaw on chlorophyll apparently linked to elevation. The greatest reductions in chlorophyll content with freeze-thaw cycles were for lichen at all three elevations. For wet thalli these were: L. retigera (only for chlorophyll a), De. hookeri, U. florida and Hy. cirrhata. For dry thalli these were: H. flavida, De. hookeri, U. florida and Hy. cirrhata (Fig. 4). There were also notable changes in chlorophyll a/b at all elevations (Supplementary Table S8).

Elevation and severity of freezing

For thalli subjected to continuous freezing, –18 °C was never more damaging than − 36 °C. The severer freezing resulted in greater EC or lower OD values for some lichen from all elevations: H. flavida, L. pindarensis, U. florida, De. hookeri, Hy. cirrhata and R. conduplicans (Tables 2 and 3; Fig. 3).

However, there was an association of elevation and impact of freezing severity on chlorophyll and carotenoid content (Fig. 4, Supplementary Tables S5 – S7, S9). In Diqing, all three species had higher chlorophyll a for dry thalli at –36 °C than at –18 °C, with H. flavida also having higher carotenoid content. In Lijiang, more severe freezing caused slightly reduced chlorophyll a content for L. pindarensis, and approximately halved both chlorophyll a and b for De. hookeri (Fig. 4). In Baoshan, all three species had reduced chlorophyll with more severe freezing.

Field temperatures versus post-freezing recovery

The intended ‘recovery’ condition did not promote recovery for most species. Reduced EC values were probably an artifact of selecting comparatively healthy thalli for the recovery condition. Nonetheless, OD ratio and chlorophyll were linked to the field conditions. The recovery conditions were only representative of summer in Baoshan. The three species from Baoshan all had slightly increased OD ratio (Fig. 3). Only Hy. cirrhata truly recovered. The highest chlorophyll contents recorded for any species were for wet thalli in its recovery condition (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Microclimate

Our microclimate data showed that most of our experimental conditions simulated climate regimes which were representative of field conditions in at least some of the study sites. Only the experimental − 36 °C freezing temperature fell far below any temperature that we actually recorded, even in our ≈ 4000 m elevation sites in Diqing. This was unexpected, given that temperatures can regularly reach − 25 °C at exposed sites at 3200 m in the same region (F. R. Worthy, personal observations from 2010 to 2021) and historically dropped to –30 °C at 3300 m65. The discrepancy likely arises from the thermal heat capacity of the trees to which microclimate loggers had been attached, combined with an overall tendency for forests to act as thermal insulators which buffer extreme cold temperatures, increasing understory temperatures relative to external climatic conditions66. Thus, epiphytic lichens are less exposed to severe freezing than are saxicolous or terricolous lichens at exposed sites at comparable elevations. This probably has reduced the extent of selective pressure for complete freeze-tolerance. Presumably there is an energetic cost for producing antifreeze chemicals and ice nucleation substances18, making it unfavourable to produce a higher concentration than required to survive local winter conditions. Elsewhere, environmental conditions do shape the production of secondary metabolites67.

Conversely, at all sites, there should have been past selective pressure to survive being frozen while hydrated and to minimise tissue damage during both continuous sub-zero temperatures and daily freeze-thaw cycles. There were probably more freeze-thaw days in the recent past. Future, warmer climates are likely to further reduce annual sub-zero days, frost days and freeze-thaw days, but increase the number of frost-free days. Thus, contrary to our initial conjectures, under future regional climate conditions, epiphytic lichen will be less exposed to every potential cause of freezing damage.

Nonetheless, related species occur at lower elevations in northern latitudes where daily freeze-thaw cycles are indeed likely to increase in frequency. Thus, our results may have useful predictive power for those species. Future research into freezing tolerance of lichen of the same genera will allow interesting comparisons between adaptations at high latitudes and high elevations.

Dry versus wet thalli

Surprisingly, thallus hydration made little difference to cell rupture as measured by EC values. However, wet thalli did indeed suffer greater chlorophyll degradation and reduced chlorophyll content. Although we did not directly measure precipitation, our RH data indicates the likelihood of thalli being frozen while hydrated. Many lichen species with green algal photobionts can rehydrate and resume photosynthesis after water vapour uptake from humid air68. Humid air can be sufficient to achieve nearly 100% of their net photosynthetic rate if hydrated with liquid water69. As overwinter RH was high enough to hydrate thalli, freezing seems likely to impact overwinter photosynthesis.

Previous studies have found that lichens frozen at –60°C for 3.5 years, then thawed, could regain similar photosynthetic responses to those of fresh specimens70. Recovery after 10 years at –20 °C varied between species and treatments, with the most damage caused by freezing hydrated thalli71. Whereas lichen died after 3 years of storage at room temperature, freezing desiccated thalli at –20 °C could maintain viability for up to 13 years72. Freezing air-dried thalli at –18 °C has been proposed as a successful means to preserve them for subsequent physiological experiments50.

We found that, even for dry thalli, some damage occurred at all our freezing temperatures. The extent of damage differed between species. Future lichen researchers need to check any ‘no damage’ assumption specifically for the lichen species in their study, to avoid unintentionally compromising lichen health by freezing thalli prior to their experiments. Freezing lichen tissue will remain a suitable method to preserve DNA for later extraction. But if material is to be preserved for short periods for ecological or physiological experiments, it could be preferable to store dry thalli in low humidity conditions at room temperature.

One caveat for the interpretation of our study is that the experimental moisture conditions could be considered sub-optimal73. For thalli to be kept in continually moist conditions can lead to photosynthetic depression74, thallus decay72 and death74 – with such impacts differing between genera, species and growth-forms75,76. Saturated thalli of Fulgensia fulgens (Sw.) Elenkin show severely reduced net photosynthesis (NP) at high thallus water contents, Diploschistes scruposus (Schreber) Norman has moderately reduced NP, whereas no NP depression occurs for Diploschistes muscorum (Scop.) R. Sant76. For some Lobaria spp., thalli remain healthy and maintain higher growth rates with continuous hydration77. In our study, the majority of wet thalli survived, even in the freeze-thaw condition. We speculate that this might be related both to species-specific traits and to the fact that suprasaturation-induced reduction in CO2 diffusion resistances, and therefore CO2 uptake, are temperature-dependent74. Lange et al.74 showed that for Ramalina maciformis (Delile) Bory, the maximum CO2 uptake rates were for thallus water contents < 40% at 25 °C, but at 0 °C maximum CO2 uptake rates were at 80% thallus water content.

Freeze-thaw versus continuous freezing

As expected, freeze-thaw cycles were generally worse for lichen than continuous freezing. Interestingly, the least affected was D. longissima, followed by U. florida. These belong to closely related genera within Parmeliaceae (Dolichousnea and Usnea), previously treated as sub-genera of Usnea s. lat78. They both have a solid central axis78. The cells in this inner cartilaginous central cord, with its highly thickened cell walls79, are probably least likely to rupture or suffer extensive cell membrane damage. Elsewhere, Usnea’s photobionts must also be cryoresistant, as the Trebouxia photobionts of U. antarctica and Usnea aurantiacoatra (Jacq.) Bory from Antarctica maintained some PSII photosynthetic activity down to –20 °C11. Further research is required to establish the identity of Usnea and Dolichousnea photobionts across the trans-Himalayas, and whether photobiont identity determines freezing tolerance. However, other D. longsissma specimens collected from near to our Diqing sites, had Trebouxia as the primary photobiont, with Poterioochromonas algae also present in some samples80.

One potential shortcoming of the present study is that freezing temperatures would not be the only variables that differ between the freeze-thaw and continuous freezing conditions. Whereas comparisons between the − 18 °C and − 36 °C conditions are robust, and comparisons between moisture conditions within freezing treatments would be reliable, some caution is needed in the interpretation of freeze-thaw versus continuous freezing comparisons. The most serious confounding variable is that lichens in –18 °C and − 36 °C conditions were kept in the dark, whereas in the freeze-thaw condition lichens were exposed to natural daylight, with diurnal fluctuations in light exposure. Light exposure can cause photoinhibition and photo-damage to lichens81,82. Efficiency of photoprotective mechanisms can differ with thallus hydration, with inter-specific differences in whether high light is more disadvantageous for hydrated or desiccated thalli81. Nonetheless, whereas photo-damage might account for some patterns in EC and OD ratios, it could not produce the observed patterns of chlorophyll and carotenoid content in this study. At high elevations, such as across the Tibetan Plateau, UV exposure is especially strong83, thus lichens must evolve especially effective photo-protective mechanisms to survive in such regions84. Even within-species, synthesis of UV-filtering secondary compounds can be greater at higher elevations84. Our laboratory location in Kunming is at 1900 m elevation, thus under experimental conditions the lichens in this study would have been exposed to lower levels of UV radiation than they would have adapted to at their source locations83.

Elevation and freeze-thaw damage

Contrary to our hypothesis, freeze-thaw cycles did not cause the most damage to lichen from higher elevations. The impact on chlorophyll was comparable across elevations. The only elevational trend was that the worst freeze-thaw damage to cell membranes was for specimens from Lijiang; L. pindarensis and De. hookeri. Lijiang had fewer freeze-thaw days than did Diqing, so Lijiang’s lichen have likely been under lower selective pressure to resist freeze-thaw damage. However, this does not account for their higher freeze-thaw sensitivity than lichens from Baoshan, where past freezing events would have been infrequent. Possibly, larger foliose thalli are more susceptible to frost damage27,85.

Furthermore, phylogenetically-linked physiological, functional-trait and consequent ecological differences86 might have mediated lichen adaptations to freeze-thaw cycles. In this case, one would expect that the most closely related species would have the greatest mutual similarity in freeze-thaw resilience. This hypothesis is supported by visual comparisons of trends between D. longissima and U. florida (see above) versus L. retigera and L. pindarensis.

Elevation and severity of freezing

As expected, more severe freezing did cause greater cell membrane damage for six species. There was also a clear elevational trend in their resilience to severe freezing. The Diqing species, exposed to the coldest field temperatures, were best at retaining chlorophyll a and carotenoids at − 36 °C. The Baoshan species, which would rarely freeze under field conditions, consistently suffered worse loss of chlorophyll after severe freezing. The ability to maintain chlorophyll content, and thus photosynthetic capacity, during winter, appears better developed in the species for which selective pressures are likely to have been greatest. Future research could explore whether freezing adaptation mechanisms differ between these species. It is generally difficult to keep lichens alive in lab conditions over extended periods73. Therefore, field experiments such as Pannewitz et al.22 are required to determine inter-specific differences in photosynthesis during sub-zero conditions.

Conclusion

In our study regions, climate change will probably lead to fewer annual sub-zero, frost and freeze-thaw days, with a greater frequency of frost-free days. This should reduce the selective pressures currently favouring investment in freezing adaptations. Lichen may maintain their current range, while reducing investment in ‘anti-freeze’ chemicals. This could potentially allow increased growth rates. Alternatively, lichen currently at lower elevations may be released from current low-temperature constraints and be able to expand their ranges upwards to higher elevations. Thus, winter climate change might favour mid-elevation lichens while increasing competitive pressures on high-elevation lichens, which already experience summer temperature stress. Field-experiments are required to investigate these possible outcomes.
