‘You grow with the allergy’: a grounded theory study of families’ experiences with food allergy risk or diagnosis in early childhood
Authors: Madlen Hörold, Katharina Gerhardinger, Mara König, Magdalena Rohr, Julia Weigt, Christian Apfelbacher, Susanne Brandstetter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChildhood food allergy is increasing in western societies. Preventing and managing food allergies is therefore essential for both parents and children. Given the limited previous research on parents’ experiences of food allergy prevention in early childhood, this study aimed to develop a grounded theory to understand the experiences and interactions of families whose children are at risk of, or have been diagnosed with, food allergy.DesignUsing a constructivist grounded theory approach, we used initial, focused and theoretical coding, as well as constant comparative analysis and memoing to interpret our data.SettingThematic interviews were conducted in Germany between March and September 2022.ParticipantWe included 28 carers (25 mothers and 3 fathers) of children aged 0–3 years diagnosed with food allergy or at risk of food allergy. We recruited participants using snowball and theoretical sampling.ResultsWe developed a theory that we named ‘negotiating uncertainty in childhood food allergy’. This theory describes the experiences and interactions of families with a child at risk or diagnosed with food allergy with the central phenomena of negotiation uncertainty. Negotiation is used to transform perceived challenges of uncertainty into feelings of competence in relation to (the prevention of) food allergy. Core themes included ‘parental roles’, ‘healthcare’, ‘childcare’, ‘informal support’ and ‘family routines’. To cope with the challenges, parents relied on two main resources: self-efficacy and health literacy.ConclusionThe study highlights the dynamic process of negotiation within families at risk of or coping with paediatric food allergy. Furthermore, our findings highlight the need to promote parental health literacy.

Introduction

Paediatric food allergy (FA) affects approximately 8% of children worldwide and represents a major public health issue, particularly in the western world.1 2 The prevalence of FA has increased over the past 30 years, a trend that has been referred to in the literature as the ‘second wave of the allergy epidemic,’ following the earlier increase in asthma and respiratory allergies in previous decades.2 3 However, data from two German birth cohorts (KUNO Kids and SPATZ Health Study) suggest that the prevalence of FA in Germany seems to have plateaued over the last 10 years: A recent study, for example, shows that the prevalence of a physician-diagnosed FA at 12 months of age was 2.4% in KUNO Kids and 2.3% in SPATZ.4

FAs are hypersensitive immunological reactions to proteins found in foods (eg, peanuts, nuts, fish and shellfish, sesame seeds, soy, eggs, wheat and milk) and often require lifelong allergen control and avoidance.5

Previous research has explored how healthcare professionals (HCPs) and parents experience the journey from allergy diagnosis to management.6 In addition, in recent years, research on the parental burden of FA in children (FAIC) has been summarised in several reviews.710 Childhood allergy is constructed as a ‘family condition’.11 The psychosocial burden for parents of children with FAs results in part from the unpredictable risk of exposure and the practical (grocery shopping and cooking) and social burden of managing FA.710 There is growing evidence that families raising a child with FA also bear higher financial costs. Thus, there are social inequalities in the prevention and management of FA in early childhood that need to be better understood.12

We understand the family as an interactional process in which the family is constantly recreated as a collective whole. This includes the various activities and organisational efforts of family members to create and maintain family ties, to define the boundaries of the family and to determine appropriate behaviour for different family members in order to make family life liveable every day.1316 The phenomenon of parenting is part of this active process and includes tasks and roles that parents are expected to perform.17 Parenting has the power to promote children’s health, well-being, and emotional and physical development.17 It is particularly important in the ongoing process of doing family, especially when faced with illness, chronic conditions or psychosocial stress.13 16 Furthermore, HCPs, such as paediatricians and midwives, who work with families, should be aware of the dimensions and processes of family life, as they also have an impact on health outcomes.17 Due to the paradigm shift in allergy prevention from allergen avoidance to early exposure,18 parental requirements for allergy prevention in early childhood have changed, for example, early introduction of ‘allergenic’ foods. As a result, parents and HCPs are challenged to deal with new information and evidence. Sometimes this information contradicts previous assumptions.19

To the best of our knowledge, no research has explored families’ experiences of FA risk in early childhood in the context of risk prediction and prevention. We wanted to close this research gap by developing a grounded theory to understand the experiences and interactions of families whose children are at risk of or have been diagnosed with FA.

Methods

Methodology

We followed the constructivist grounded theory (CGT) approach, guided by the work of Charmaz.20 21 Grounded theory methodology seeks to develop theories that are grounded in the data themselves, thus conceptually explaining processes or actions of a phenomenon of interest.20 In doing so, the concepts generated and the specified relationships between them foster the understanding of the phenomenon under study.20 22 A comprehensive description of the methodology can be found in the published study protocol.23

Study setting

This study is embedded in the NAMIBIO (Food Allergy Biomarker) app consortium, which aims to identify early predictors for FAIC and to develop apps to assist FA prediction and prevention. The ‘Parents’ study identified, conceptualised and systematically described parental information needs, as well as their health information-seeking behaviours related to the risk prediction and prevention of FAIC, including the digital information environment using qualitative content analysis.24

Participants and recruitment

Our target population included carers (mothers or fathers) of children aged 0–3 years diagnosed with FA or at risk of FA. We considered children to be at risk of FA if they had pre-existing allergies or allergic diseases, for example, allergic asthma and hay fever, pre-existing atopic eczema, or if there were genetic or environmental risk factors for FA in the family history (first generation), for example, FA, atopic eczema, allergic asthma or hay fever. This information was requested during the recruitment process.23 We recruited participants using a snowball sampling, followed by theoretical sampling. In other words, starting with data collection, constructing preliminary categories and then testing these categories on further empirical data to develop our emerging theory.21 First, we used personal contacts to gain local access. Then we used digital contacts: the project website, social media posts and the newsletter of the German Allergy and Asthma Association (a patient organisation), letters to municipal institutions and associations and letters to nurseries and paediatricians. We continued the sampling process within the time constraints of the study until no new categories and characteristics of our categories emerged.20 As an incentive for participation, parents were paid €30 (US$32.7) to compensate them for their time and effort.

Data collection

A thematic interview guide23 was developed with a narrative prompt at the beginning (‘How are you currently affected by the topic of food allergy in children?’). In doing so, our participants had the opportunity to set their own priorities to articulate their subjective perspective and, if necessary, to reflect on what they had already experienced. We also collected data on parents’ information needs and their information-seeking behaviour regarding the risk prediction and prevention of childhood FA as well as sociodemographic information and field notes.

The four interviewers (MH: nurse scientist, MR: public health researcher, KG: psychologist and CD: health scientist, all women) had varying levels of experience with qualitative methods. We interviewed our participants individually between March and September 2022 face to face, by telephone or via a privacy-compliant videoconferencing system. In some cases, children were present during the interviews. The interviews lasted between 23 and 71 min. Transcriptions were made verbatim.

Data analysis

We started our CGT research with sensitising concepts and background information on FA in children.18 The sensitising concepts focused our attention on important aspects of social interaction in the families and provided the basis for the analysis of the research data,25 such as ‘doing family’13 16 (an approach to capture the processual understanding of the family), health literacy,26 Corbin and Strauss’s theory of illness trajectories27 and the burden of treatment theory.28

With the sensitising concepts in mind, we used initial, focused and theoretical coding as well as memoing for interpretation.20 Once the initial interviews were conducted and transcribed, the data were coded. In the initial coding phase, the transcripts were coded line by line or section by section. In the focused coding, these initial codes were thematically separated, sorted and synthesised to decide which initial codes made the most analytical sense to determine which ones best captured the core of the data. Theoretical codes were then used to identify potential relationships between the categories developed in focused coding, helping to construct a coherent theory. To minimise bias and increase accuracy, constant comparison and memoing were used throughout our data analysis process. This included comparing statements and experiences within the same interview or across different interviews and discussing different interpretations. We did theoretical sampling by recruiting, for example, families with children at-risk and fathers, to develop the properties of our categories until no new properties emerged.20 By analysing the data, we developed a theoretical model.

Participant characteristics.

Weekly online interpretations with a small group of four researchers (MH, KG, MR and JW) and three additional rotating researchers (MK, TB and MB) ensured an iterative process of data collection and analysis and established intersubjectivity and comprehensibility.29 30 Prior to conducting our study, we reflected on and discussed prejudices and assumptions about recruitment, data collection and results as part of the reflective process. We initially coded the data in German and translated the codes/categories and quotations into English for publication (TB). We tried to find English equivalents for idioms and colloquial expressions, for example through ‘deepl’ (www.deepl.com). We did not include respondent validation. MAXQDA Analytic Pro 2022 was used to manage and code the data.

Patient and public involvement

This study involved carers (mothers or fathers) of children aged 0–3 years. Open-ended questions allowed participants to prioritise their personal experiences and preferences when interviewed. Participants were not involved in the design, conduct, analysis, reporting or dissemination of the research. Results will be disseminated in plain language through social media.

Results

Study sample

The sample consisted of 28 participants: 25 mothers and 3 fathers (aged 30–48 years). Participants had a total of 40 children: 15 participants had a child aged 0–3 years diagnosed with FA, 10 participants had a child aged 0–3 years at risk of FA and 3 participants had children aged 0–3 years diagnosed with FA and at risk of FA. Five women with children diagnosed with FA were pregnant at the time of the interview. These unborn children were considered to be at risk of FA based on family history. Further details on the characteristics of the participants are shown in figure 1.

Theoretical model

The analysis of the data resulted in a comprehensive theory that we have named ‘negotiating uncertainty in childhood food allergy’. Our theory was inspired by ‘doing family’, an approach in family sociology that refers to a dimension of family life and describes the joint activities and organisational achievements of families under the working conditions of employed parents.14 31

Our theory describes the experiences and interactions of families and applies to both families with a child at risk of FA and families with a child diagnosed with FA. Participants described their experience as a journey (‘It was simply a long journey’, P15, she, mid 30s): We understood that the journey began by facing challenges and uncertainties that disrupted the family dynamic (‘completely overwhelmed’, P15, she, mid 30s). In order to regain a sense of competence as a family with FA (risk), the family members engaged in negotiation processes. ‘Yes, there’s no one right way. That is the difficult thing.’ (P19, she, mid 30s).

The core of the participants’ experience focused on negotiating uncertainty in five areas: ‘parental roles’, ‘healthcare’, ‘childcare’, ‘informal support’ and ‘family routines’. ‘Perceiving challenges due to uncertainty’ and ‘feeling competence related to (the prevention of) FA’ defined the contrasting experiences of our participants. Families were striving for growth and a sense of coping and competence in dealing with FA (prevention) (figure 2).

Family in the field of tension between perceiving challenges due to uncertainty and feeling competence related to (the prevention of) FA

The notion ‘perceiving challenges due to uncertainty’ referred to the starting point of the family journey (‘quite an odyssey’, P27, she, early 30s), which, according to parents, often occurred in the first few months of the child’s life. When parents received their child’s FA diagnosis or became aware of their child’s risk of FA, for example, due to initial symptoms, they sometimes lost self-confidence. Meanwhile, some of their current parenting roles and tasks changed, leading to anxiety and uncertainty.

The participants described that challenges and uncertainty grew, for example, when they noticed symptoms in their babies. They questioned their behaviour. ‘Did you do the right thing? Otherwise, could you have done more?’ (P19, she, mid 30s). Once the diagnosis was made, ‘you have to see for yourself as a parent how to prepare yourself and also see for what you have the time and obviously the nerves to do’ (P17, she, mid 30s). For each family, the perceived challenges were different, shaped by personal factors (health literacy) and the understanding of parental roles and tasks. Regardless of the nature of the challenges related to FA (risk), participants described feelings of helplessness (‘feel a little bit at the mercy’, P06, she, early 40s), powerlessness as well as coping with guilt (‘[…] if I miss something now, I will blame myself for the rest of my life’, P03, she, early 40s).

Data suggested that families wished to overcome uncertainties and strived to feel safe and competent. Through the results of negotiation, they felt more competent over time—‘And I guess I have worked it out by now. […] And so you have grown into it with and with it a little bit.’ (P27, she, early 30s). Perceived allergy competence was demonstrated by a self-determined management of (the risk of) FA in early childhood. ‘We have also learnt in training that you don’t go to the ice-cream parlour with children who have such severe nut allergies. We do it anyway’ (P23, she, mid 30s).

Negotiating uncertainty

Negotiating uncertainty helped the parents to regain their agency, to accept the change and to shape their family life. In the above, we have outlined the tensions faced by families with children at risk with a diagnosis of FA. Parents are primarily concerned with ‘somehow making it the best possible for [the little one]’ (P16, he, mid 30s). Negotiation enabled development. ‘I think that’s also the interesting thing about allergy, that the perspective is different now than it was two years ago. And, if we are to talk now in two years time, it will be a different perspective again. You just grow with your allergies’ (P11, she, mid 30s).

In the following, we describe five fields, in which uncertainties were negotiated.

Parental roles

Based on these early experiences of uncertainty, participants in our study described how they unexpectedly renegotiated parental roles and tasks. This was demonstrated, for example, by the issue of allergy-specific knowledge. Parents negotiated who was responsible for seeking information and whether and which information sources (eg, social media) and external support services (eg, patient organisation) they should use. ‘Well, my husband did all the education, indeed. And, in everyday life, yes, we share it. For example, he is responsible for the drug bag. So, he checks the expiration date and the stock. Yes, I am mainly responsible for the food planning and grocery shopping. So, we both kind of have our jobs with that, I think’ (P23, she, mid 30s).

However, mothers often took on much of the responsibility. ‘As a mother, I always felt that we (mothers) were more responsible for taking care of things like that than the men. Because we are always the ones who are basically with them, just because we (the mothers) are also on parental leave’ (P28, she, early 30s). Parents did not always succeed in negotiating consensually from the beginning. They described that different approaches to gender roles and responsibilities within parenthood were ‘sometimes very difficult […]. These are the conflicts that exist.’ (P15, she, mid 30s). In addition, the need for control as well as the experience of mutual trust seemed to be important in this process. Trust led to less need for control.

Healthcare

Paediatricians were described as the main point of contact for parents regarding their children’s health. Some participants perceived the path to an FA diagnosis as ‘quite an ordeal from one paediatrician to the next’ (P27, she, early 30s). ‘At first you trust them 100 percent and do it that way’ (P28, she, early 30s). ‘However, […] as a mother, I actually felt left alone in those first six months until the diagnosis was made. Because […] our paediatrician was not an expert in this field’ (P27, she, early 30s). Others reported ‘that fortunately they (had) or have a paediatrician’s practice that is fairly fit in the whole area’ (P23, she, mid 30s). ‘I think we were very lucky in our way of getting the right contact person quickly’ (P04, she, late 30s). ‘We communicate a lot with midwives and paediatricians’ (P26, she, early 30s).

Parents described how they negotiated their relationship with HCPs and their use of healthcare services, especially in situations where they doubted the professional expertise and missed the understanding (of HCPs) for their needs—‘So I was completely on my own for three or four months’ (P06, she, early 40s). They questioned whether their paediatrician was the right person to talk to. In some cases, this led to consultation with new or additional providers, such as paediatricians, allergists and nutritionists. For the parents, it was important to have a relationship of trust and confidence. This ensured adherence to paediatric care recommendations or prescriptions, and it positively influenced parents’ perceptions of competence in the prevention and management of FA. Midwives were evaluated less critically—‘[…] Unfortunately, my midwife isn't up to date (laughs). […] She looked after me and accompanied me very nicely. […] But midwives are all completely overworked’ (P15, she, mid 30s). Consequently, these negotiations about families’ care needs and opportunities for shared decision-making sometimes led to challenging care constellations. ‘Yes, for us it is a bit of a problem that we have two different paediatricians. One paediatrician is responsible for the normal things, as I said. And we go to the other one […]. He is also an allergist. And that’s why we're sitting a bit between two stools’ (P27, she, early 30s).

Childcare

Parents reported that (the risk of) FA changed their perspective on childcare. Families negotiated the relationship with childcare providers by questioning the childcare approach of the nurseries and the competence of their staff to care for their child with (at risk of) FA. ‘[…] we have to take our child out of the childcare next year and look for another nursery, because they have such a stupid concept that the children are allowed to take their own food from the age of three’ (P08, she, early 40s).

Parental leave provided an opportunity for mothers and fathers to proactively focus on (health) needs of the child. ‘You don't want to be perceived as a helicopter mom at all. However, it’s totally difficult not to do that, because I also want to’ (P04, she, late 30s).

Sending the child to the nursery was described as a challenging loss of control. ‘The nursery time is about to start […] I think that’s when the interesting part starts for us, […] we really have to take a closer look and pay attention when-. You let the child out of your hands and you no longer have such a complete overview. […] such a challenge for all of us, you pass the responsibility for him […]’ (P28, she, early 30s). Accordingly, the families negotiated with the professionals working in the nurseries about cooperation and the possibilities to meet their child’s needs. ‘Because at the end the children spend three quarters of their day in the childcare facilities and um yes. It would be nice if we could work even more closely together. And that something like that is also taken into account.’ (P25, she, early 30s).

In order to regain control and to feel safe, in some cases, long distances to childcare were necessary and accepted. ‘Now we are with a day care mum, where I have to drive more than two hours every day. And we actually had a day care spot, but we lost it because the nursery didn't have the confidence to do it’ (P06, she, early 40s).

Informal support

Parents described how they negotiated support from the social network and the extended family. Lack of networks or unhelpful support was perceived as a burden. Nevertheless, trust, mutual understanding and the consideration of the family’s needs played a decisive role in negotiating support. ‘However, I cannot talk about it. I do not know anyone else who has the same problem.’ (P06, she, early 40s). ‘Even the grandparents. […] they just give it [the food] and pay no attention to it. That’s just the way it is, […] where you always take a bit of fear with you’ (P19, she, mid 30s).

The social circle and the extended family also served as a reflective platform and support for health-related decisions. ‘We have built up quite a good network. I mean, […] I am already in contact with quite a lot of people. All around the disease, these whole topics. Those are usually the first people I ask when I have something new’ (P05, she, early 40s).

Some participants also mentioned virtual networks. Again, trust in the content and credibility of information, and the perceived usefulness of information and support, were important criteria in the negotiation process. ‘Trust is always the problem. Usually I make sure that when I get information from somewhere on the internet, then of course I look for more information.’ (P05, she, early 40s).

Family routines

Parents reported how structures and routines in family life were negotiated and (re)established after becoming aware of the risk or receiving a FA diagnosis. They want to be prepared for ‘in case something happens’ (P16, he, mid 30s). Some parents in our study strictly followed the recommendations of the paediatricians (‘The paediatrician said […]’, P28, she, early 30s); others spoke of a perceived gap between theory and practice. ‘Simply trying out’ food was not implemented in all families. The reasons for this varied, but were mostly driven by uncertainty and fear. ‘But we wouldn't do it with eggs, because the risk is simply too great that he will get another allergic shock’ (P01, he, late 40s). ‘So I stayed with the paediatrician, OK, I'll buy a nut puree like this, I'll smear it on their skin for an hour, and if nothing happens then I'll take a small amount and test it. That is the theory. In practice, I have not yet dared to do that’ (P12, she, late 30s).

Especially when families ‘are moving in (their own) cosmos, it (food allergy) really hardly matters’ (P23, she, early 30s). Families negotiated how to create their own individual safe places by building new routines (‘we can live with it well’—P08, she early 40s). These cover everything in their homes from the fridge (‘whatever is in the fridge is free to eat’—P23, she, early 30s) to the snack box.

However, parents did not always make these decisions amicably. Thus, negotiation required many skills: adapting, collaborating, compromising and making decisions. ‘[…] in the future we will always eat the same food together. He (the husband) definitely has to make some sacrifices. […]. I think it’s already hard for him that he also has to show renunciation’ (P28, she, early 30s).

Participants wished to create a safe place outside home for family activities, too. Here, challenges emerged (‘when you ask that you just get looked at stupidly.’) that were discussed and negotiated within the family. ‘[…] my husband learned that you have to be careful with chips, because sometimes they are pre-fried in peanut oil. And I think to myself, no snack bar in the world sells chips for €1.50 if they are fried in peanut oil. So I always don't ask about that.’ (P23, she, mid 30s).

Resources

The data suggest that resources in families (self-efficacy and health literacy) can positively influence ‘negotiating uncertainty in childhood food allergy’ in regaining a sense of competence (in managing FA as family).

Families considered having the internal belief that one can cope well with difficult or challenging situations (self-efficacy) as an important resource for feeling competent in FA (prevention). In retrospect, many participants associated not knowing about the risk of FA together with the first symptoms of FA as a starting point for uncertainty and loss of control. Moreover, they expressed a wish for HCPs to provide predictive information during pregnancy or postpartum. Participants who were expecting their second child (at risk of FA) at the time of the interview felt better prepared than for their first pregnancy because their experience with their first child had given them confidence that they could cope well with difficult or challenging situations. They were convinced that they could successfully meet the challenges of FA (prevention) on their own (from within the family). ‘The first time, I may have been clueless about it’ (P23, she, mid 30s). Therefore, they took proactive measures: ‘[…], my second child will be super high risk for allergy. I'm actually going to get care from [paediatricians] while I'm pregnant’ (P11, she mid 30s).

Furthermore, the parents in our study described it as helpful if they were able to access, appraise and apply health information on early childhood FA. It supported them ‘to inform myself and to make decisions’ (P23, she, mid 30s) and to perceive themselves as competent in caring for their child.

‘Yes, and I think that fortunately we can assess sources quite well, whether they are reputable or not’ (P23, she, mid 30s). However, the participants were often overwhelmed by the amount of information and had difficulties in finding ‘good’ health information. ‘I find it is even more difficult to find good information [about FA] regarding children than it is for adults’ (P25, she, early 30s).

Discussion

We were able to show that families negotiate the uncertainties of FA in early childhood. Parents shared their journey of overcoming uncertainties related to FA, in order to experience themselves as a competent family, a social process that leads to partial redefinition of roles and relationships. The shared experiences are gender neutral. They are also independent of whether the families have children with diagnosed FAs or children at risk of FAs. We managed to recruit 28 parents from different regions of Germany. The sample consisted mainly of white, German-speaking women. Recruitment aimed to cover our environment (young parents, FA in childhood, Germany) while taking into account the existing heterogeneity. This was only partially achieved. Although our sampling continued until no new categories or characteristics of the categories emerged, the inclusion of more individuals with a history of migration, fathers or same-sex parents might have provided a different perspective on parental roles, nutrition and experiences of health risks and illness in the context of cultural diversity.

We organised our data collection and analysis in a continuous loop, promoting openness and transparency in the collaborative virtual interpretation work. This approach addressed the challenges of physical distance between researchers and allowed for adjustments in data collection and analysis (theoretical sampling).20 Reflection on prejudices and assumptions about recruitment, data collection and findings were part of the reflective interpretation and analysis process. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the subjectivity of theorising and thus the role of the discursive process in the interpretation.20

We translated our findings (codes/categories and quotes) from German into English with the risk of meaning lost. Translation between languages also means interpretation.32 33 Additionally, it is important to acknowledge that the quotes did not directly reveal the negotiation process, but primarily documented what families had negotiated in the past.

Gender dynamics cannot be excluded, as only woman, two of whom are themselves mothers of young children, collected the data.34 Assigning characteristics and roles based on gender may have raised expectations of responses; at the same time, it may have contributed to the openness of the mothers who participated.35

‘Negotiating uncertainty in childhood food allergy’ as described above promoted family bonding (functioning and sense of togetherness), while individual and parental roles may have varied within the families. Previous research has defined negotiation as an interaction involving, for example, individuals, groups, organisations and communities with the aim of agreeing on certain joint or mutual actions.36 37 Negotiations are always dynamic decision-making processes that are interlinked through communication, offers and concessions.37 38 Accordingly, Lian et al explained that negotiating uncertainty involves seeking, receiving and sharing information, interpreting it and deciding how to act.39 This confirmed the participants’ perceptions of the supportive role of health literacy.

Back in 2007, Schier and Jurczyk described ‘doing family’ a dimension of family life. In this concept, family is understood as an achievement under the working conditions of employed parents, for example, creating times of physical copresence, initiating joint leisure activities, making ‘meaningful’ use of scarce and often spontaneous time together and developing practices to maintain family life despite separation.14 Although our focus was different, the families in our study were also confronted with different approaches to life and illness, as well as different structures, needs and interests that had to be negotiated with each other.14 31 Gunnarsson and Hydén argued that there is a greater moral responsibility to ‘do the right thing’ when FA is suspected than for parents dealing with other chronic conditions in their children, as the social and physical conditions and lifestyle choices become particularly apparent.40

Some of our findings support the work of other studies.10 11 41 42 Our study showed that even the risk of FA in early childhood, as well as the diagnosis of FA in a child was perceived as a family condition11 and that parents acted as risk managers42 on a journey towards health/ illness competence. Among other things, family members were challenged to actively create opportunities for ‘negotiating uncertainty in childhood food allergy’ and to redevelop practices and routines, because the conditions under which a family has to be produced and under which it performs have changed with the FA risk/ FA diagnosis. Thus, ‘negotiating uncertainty in childhood food allergy’ described a continuous process that could be repeated again as changes occurred in families, for example, when children were born or further FAs were diagnosed. We found that the negotiation process is not systematically planned or conducted; instead, it occurs on an ad hoc basis, depending on the relationships within the family and the relationships between the family and the HCPs, childcare providers or even the extended family and the social network.43 Trust has been identified as a key factor in this process, particularly in relation to interactions with HCPs and childcare providers. In this sense, Thiede44 argued in 2005 that trust promotes communicative interaction and thus creates the conditions for information to be effective. Information can increase options and motivate people to use healthcare services.44

Veltkamp et al42 pointed out that families’ health practices and health competence are influenced by gender and class. Accordingly, further research is needed to explore how same-sex couples and families with a history of migration, as well as families with low levels of education, low income or occupational status, experience family life with children at risk of or already suffering from FA. Considering these perspectives may contribute to a broader and more diverse understanding of uncertainty in the context of FA (prevention).

Conclusion

Families experienced and negotiated uncertainty in the areas of ‘parental roles’, ‘healthcare’, ‘childcare’, ‘informal support’ and ‘family routines’. The process of negotiating uncertainty involved a transition from ‘perceiving challenges due to uncertainty’ to ‘feeling competence in relation to (preventing) FAs’. Our findings are consistent with previous research and emphasise the role of health literacy and competence in the context of FAIC. This calls for HCPs to prioritise promoting FA competence in parents, including improving parental health literacy and perceived self-efficacy. However, this requires a careful balance between the provision of expertise and practical considerations, such as family settings, FA knowledge and social networks. Such efforts may help to reduce uncertainty about FA or the risk of FA and may open up new opportunities for early support for families.
