Valvular Heart Diseases
Authors: Maurice Enriquez-Sarano
Published: 2024

Why do we do what we do? Often, we do not know. I certainly did not when I was informed that I had been selected as one of the top 10% of medical students nominated to the prestigious “Internat des Hôpitaux de Paris,” the most esteemed medical training program in France. At the time, I had no clear idea of what I wanted to specialize in. I knew I wanted to be a cardiologist, but beyond that—what? I secretly feared that I was falling behind, because my peers seemed to know so much more than I did. This uncertainty sparked a deep desire to learn and understand more. The first thing I did was buy The Heart, the cardiology textbook by J.W. Hurst.

Then, I had to fulfill my military service, which seems so outdated now that the military is a fully professional force. I was assigned to Tunisia as a “cooperating doctor”—a title that made me think, “Doctor? Who, me?”—and I managed to secure a position in the cardiology unit, the only one with a catheterization laboratory. I was placed in charge of the young female patients’ unit. The “unit” was cramped—1 room with 10 beds and 14 patients. My self-esteem took a hit as I struggled to distinguish systole from diastole while auscultating them. Most of these patients had rheumatic heart disease, and I could not explain why, but I developed an immediate fascination with valvular heart diseases. Perhaps it was the physiological background, or maybe it was the availability of commissurotomy and valve prostheses to treat them.

I also became passionate about clinical research and collaboration. I was tasked with gathering data on the outcomes of post–mitral valve replacement patients. We set out to publish our findings with 2 other young “interns,” and I bought a statistical textbook in Paris. I meticulously collected data the old-fashioned way—paper and pencil—and did the calculations by hand, learning the painstaking art of manual statistics. That experience also taught me a crucial lesson: health care cannot exist in isolation. I will never forget the 14-year-old girl who came in febrile, with a systolic murmur and joint pains. We did not have the advanced diagnostic tools we take for granted today—no echocardiography, transesophageal echocardiography, computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography, or even blood cultures. I was told that the difference between endocarditis and acute rheumatic fever could be discerned from the sedimentation rate—rheumatic fever if it was >100 in the first hour. I was instructed to treat her with steroids, and for one brief, hopeful day, the fever subsided. Then, the horrors of metastatic abscesses appeared. We did our best, but I learned the stark difference between assuming a diagnosis vs seeing and truly diagnosing the patients’ actual conditions. That moment also reinforced the harsh reality of our profession: as sad and defeated as we may feel, we can never abandon our patients. We must gather our strength and continue to examine, learn, and care for the next patient whose life also depends on us.

When I emerged from that grueling experience, I had a new resolve—to be the best at what I do—and my love for valve diseases and clinical research remained intact. I was fortunate in my subsequent training and assistant professorship to work in the best units, under masters who, despite their flaws, each had something valuable to teach. I also witnessed groundbreaking advances in cardiology, particularly in echocardiography. I was there as M-mode evolved into 2-dimensional echocardiography, then into transesophageal echocardiography and Doppler echocardiography, which allowed us to measure gradients noninvasively for the first time, and color-flow imaging, which allowed to see regurgitations noninvasively for the first time. I saw the advent of bioprostheses and bileaflet mechanical valves, but it was mitral repair that truly captivated me. I will never forget the young woman with severe mitral regurgitation caused by prolapse. We referred her to Professor Carpentier, and when she returned, there was no murmur, no regurgitation. That experience convinced me that repair must be superior to replacement, although it would take time to prove it. We also witnessed the early days of valvular interventional cardiology, with the introduction of mitral balloon valvuloplasty.

Unfortunately, because of the lack of a full professorship position, I found that clinical research had to take a back seat to clinical practice for a while. But, I was fortunate again, this time through my wife, who had trained at Mayo Clinic. She introduced me to Dr Robert Frye, who kindly took a chance on me and invited me to join Mayo. It was a tough transition for a Parisian to leave Paris, but it turned out to be a stroke of luck. My clinical practice kept me excited every day, wondering which incredible cases and patients I would encounter. I worked with extraordinary colleagues, trainees, and partners, and I had the privilege of engaging in international collaborations that helped shape my career and provided lifelong friendships around the world. My old and new friends and colleagues, now at Minneapolis Heart Institute, emphasize this immense joy of collaboration for the advancement of the care we give to patients with valve diseases.

This experience also gave our team a strong voice in the global medical community, helping to awaken a field that had grown complacent. We often see a single patient, treat a disease, and feel satisfied, but it is all too easy to forget those who will never be served or healed. Since 2002, when the first transcatheter aortic valve replacement was performed in Rouen, France, by Alain Cribier, we have seen remarkable advances in minimally invasive procedures, endoscopic techniques, and transcatheter therapies that make a valve treatment acceptable to the patients and more feasible to us. Many therapies remain imperfect, and we need to continue to innovate toward more physiologic interventions. We are learning more than ever about the biological and genetic nature of valve diseases, the underlying mechanisms, clinical outcomes, and multimodal imaging. We are also becoming more aware of the undertreatment of valve disease—an issue so profound and widespread that we must unite to find new ways to identify and treat the patients who need us most, even if they do not know it (Figure 1).

Despite the challenges and the immense tasks ahead, I would be remiss if I did not take a moment to reflect on the sheer joy of being a doctor. It is the joy of offering patients empathy, the steady support of a caring physician who is also at the top of his/her professional game, the elation of the investigator with new data and new understanding, and the privilege of the educator who sparks the flame of curiosity in trainees and colleagues. I love it. I loved it then, and I love it now. It is an infinite journey. I worked tirelessly throughout this ongoing journey, but in many ways, I never felt like I worked a day in my life.
