Congenital hyperinsulinism as part of a multisystem disease caused by a compound heterozygous variant in the YARS1 gene
Authors: Anna Bolmasova, Anna Zaretskaia, Sofia Mikhalina, Anna Postrigan, Anna Kolodkina, Valentina Peterkova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTyrosyl-transfer RNA (tRNA) synthetase, encoded by YARS1, is essential for protein synthesis, catalyzing the attachment of tyrosine to its cognate tRNA during translation. Pathogenic biallelic YARS1 variants cause infantile-onset multisystem neurological, endocrine, and pancreatic disease type 2 (IMNEPD2; OMIM #619418), an orphan disorder. Hypoglycemia in IMNEPD2 remains poorly characterized, with few reported cases. We describe an infant with a compound heterozygous YARS1 genotype (c.176T>C and c.1261G>A) who presented with severe hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, notable for a discordance between insulin and C-peptide concentrations and a lack of response to diazoxide and octreotide.Keywords: congenital hyperinsulinism, hypoglycemia, YARS1 gene, infantile-onset multisystem neurological, endocrine, and pancreatic disease type 2 (IMNEPD2)

Introduction

Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases are essential for protein translation. Tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, a cytoplasmic homodimer encoded by YARS1, catalyzes the aminoacylation of tyrosine to its cognate tRNA. In addition to its tRNA anticodon-binding domain, the enzyme contains N-terminal and C-terminal endothelial monocyte-activating polypeptide II (EMAP-II)–like domains with noncanonical functions.

The N-terminal domain exhibits interleukin-8–like activity and contributes to angiogenesis and thrombopoiesis. The C-terminal EMAP-II–like domain demonstrates cytokine-like activity, including regulation of leukocyte migration and tumor necrosis factor-α expression [1-4]. These noncanonical functions are thought to be central to the multisystem phenotype described below.

Heterozygous pathogenic YARS1 variants were first associated with Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type C (OMIM #608323) in 2003. Biallelic variants, initially reported in 2017, are linked to infantile-onset multisystem neurological, endocrine, and pancreatic disease type 2 (IMNEPD2; OMIM #619418) [5, 6]. To date, approximately 25 patients with IMNEPD2 caused by biallelic YARS1 variants have been reported worldwide [7].

IMNEPD2 manifests with facial dysmorphism, growth retardation, developmental delay, central nervous system involvement, hepatic and pulmonary dysfunction, anemia, hearing loss, hypothyroidism, hypoalbuminemia, proteinuria, and recurrent infections. Hypoglycemia is a severe manifestation that requires further investigation. We report an infant with IMNEPD2 in whom the predominant manifestation was severe hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, unresponsive to diazoxide and characterized by a marked dissociation among insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin concentrations.

Case presentation

A 3.5-month-old male infant was referred to our clinic with failure to thrive, recurrent asymptomatic hypoglycemia, anemia, hypoproteinemia, generalized hypotonia, and feeding difficulties. He was born at term to non-consanguineous parents of European descent as the second child of an uncomplicated pregnancy, and the family history was unremarkable. Apgar scores were 8 and 9, and birth parameters were within the normal range (length 54 cm, SD score +2.54; weight 3.29 kg, SD score +0.01).

On day 2 of life, he developed respiratory distress due to congenital pneumonia and required antibiotic therapy until day 9. Breastfeeding was initially attempted but was replaced with an extensively hydrolyzed formula because of dyspeptic symptoms and poor intake; neither tube feeding nor gastrostomy was required. The infant was discharged on day 12 of life.

Diagnostic assessment

At 1 month of age, routine evaluation revealed anemia (hemoglobin 7.6 g/dL [76 g/L]; reference range, 11.0-13.5 g/dL [110-135 g/L]). Oral iron supplementation proved ineffective, prompting hospitalization in the hematology department at 1.5 months of age. During this admission, recurrent hypoglycemia was documented, with blood glucose as low as 34 mg/dL (1.9 mmol/L; reference range, 56-110 mg/dL [3.1-6.1 mmol/L]). Additional investigation demonstrated hypoalbuminemia and moderately elevated transaminase concentrations. Dysmorphic features included deep-set eyes, prominent cheeks, micrognathia, and hypertrichosis of the shins and lumbosacral region (Fig. 1).

Patient phenotype at 3.5 months of age, showing deep-set eyes, prominent cheeks, and micrognathia.

At 3.5 months of age, spontaneous hypoglycemia to 50 mg/dL (2.8 mmol/L) was accompanied by an insulin concentration of 0.6 μIU/mL (4.17 pmol/L; congenital hyperinsulinism [CHI] diagnostic criterion, >1.25 μIU/mL [>8.7 pmol/L]) and a C-peptide concentration of 1.43 ng/mL (0.47 nmol/L; CHI diagnostic criterion, >0.5 ng/mL [>0.17 nmol/L]), demonstrating a dissociation between these markers. A glucagon stimulation test was positive, with glucose rising from 40 mg/dL (2.2 mmol/L) to 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L).

During a subsequent hypoglycemic episode (glucose 52 mg/dL [2.9 mmol/L]), the insulin concentration was 1.5 μIU/mL (10.42 pmol/L), C-peptide was 2.52 ng/mL (0.83 nmol/L), and proinsulin was 1.0 pg/mL (0.106 pmol/L; reference range, 0.5-3.5 pg/mL [0.053-0.371 pmol/L]). We excluded other endocrine and metabolic causes of hypoglycemia, including insulin counterregulatory hormone deficiencies, aminoacidopathies, organic acidemias, and fatty-acid oxidation disorders, and the diagnosis of CHI was confirmed (Table 1). Additional findings were hypochromic microcytic anemia, hematogenous thrombophilia, hypoalbuminemia, and cryptogenic hepatitis.

Whole-exome sequencing identified 2 compound heterozygous variants in YARS1 (NM_003680.4): a maternally inherited exon 2 variant, previously reported and likely pathogenic (GRCh38, chr1:32810939 A>G; c.176T>C; p.Ile59Thr), and a novel paternally inherited exon 11 variant (GRCh38, chr1:32780158 C>T; c.1261G>A; p.Val421Met). The exon 11 variant is absent from the Genome Aggregation Database v4.1.0, affects a highly conserved residue, and is predicted to be pathogenic by multiple in silico tools (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion, Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant, Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner, and MetaSVM). It is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance in ClinVar (Variation ID 2959125).

Follow-up evaluations at 3.5, 6, 11, and 18 months demonstrated persistent hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia, anemia, hypoproteinemia, elevated transaminases, and growth retardation, with persistent dissociation among insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin concentrations (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Treatment

Beginning at 2 months of age, the patient required a continuous intravenous dextrose infusion to maintain euglycemia, with a glucose utilization rate of 3.5 mg/kg/min at admission. At 3.5 months, we initiated diazoxide (7.3 mg/kg/day) and hydrochlorothiazide (1.8 mg/kg/day), which allowed discontinuation of the dextrose infusion. A fasting test confirmed compensation of CHI, although the insulin-C-peptide dissociation persisted (Fig. 2).

At 4.5 months, hypoglycemia recurred, and increasing the diazoxide dose to 9.4 mg/kg/day was ineffective. At 6 months, a further increase to 11.5 mg/kg/day induced vomiting without improvement in glycemic control. We then initiated a continuous subcutaneous octreotide infusion via an insulin pump at 5 mcg/kg/day, titrated to 16.6 mcg/kg/day, but observed no clinical benefit. Management was subsequently modified to frequent nasogastric feedings (every 2 hours) with complex-carbohydrate supplementation (0.5 g/kg per feeding), resulting in partial stabilization of glycemia.

Anemia was initially treated with oral iron (5 mg/kg/day) and erythropoietin (900 IU 3 times weekly for 5 weeks, starting at 2 months of age), without sustained response. At 4 months, a red-blood-cell transfusion stabilized hemoglobin at 9.0 to 9.6 g/dL (90-96 g/L). Albumin infusions led to only transient improvement, and serum albumin remained borderline (Table 2). At 13 months, hemoglobin decreased to 7.3 g/dL (73 g/L), prompting a second transfusion and resumption of erythropoietin (1500 IU twice weekly).

By 18 months, we managed hypoglycemia with nasogastric formula feedings every 30 to 90 minutes without pharmacologic therapy. However, glycemic control progressively deteriorated, with reduced fasting tolerance and lower glucose concentrations, and a gastrostomy tube was placed at 19 months.

Outcome and follow-up

Linear growth remained markedly below age-related norms (Fig. 3). Abdominal ultrasonography at 11 months demonstrated moderate hepatomegaly with biochemical evidence of hepatic involvement, including an elevated aspartate aminotransferase concentration and hypoalbuminemia. By 18 months, laboratory evaluation indicated progressive cholestasis, cytolysis, and dyslipidemia, with persistently impaired hepatic synthetic function (Table 2).

At 11 months, frequent nasogastric feedings combined with cornstarch supplementation maintained relative normoglycemia without pharmacologic therapy. By 18 months, refusal of complementary foods, maternal discontinuation of cornstarch because of dyspeptic symptoms, and absence of medication led to reduced fasting tolerance and more frequent, severe hypoglycemic episodes. Congenital hyperinsulinism remained active, with biochemical evidence of dysregulated insulin secretion characterized by persistent dissociation among insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin concentrations (Fig. 2).

At 11 months, the patient demonstrated generalized hypotonia and moderate motor delay; achieved milestones included head control, supported sitting, and rolling over, and audiological assessment was normal. By 18 months, the child had begun crawling and speaking a few words but had not yet achieved independent standing or walking. Hypochromic microcytic anemia and leukocytosis persisted (Table 2), and progressive cheek prominence and ptosis were observed, likely secondary to reduced muscle tone associated with impaired sucking.

Discussion

Heterozygous YARS1 variants are associated with Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, whereas biallelic variants cause multisystem organ dysfunction [6]. The pathogenic mechanism is thought to involve disruption of the noncanonical functions of tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase rather than impaired protein synthesis itself. These functions are mediated by the N- and C-terminal EMAP-II–like domains, which regulate angiogenesis, cytokine signaling, and immune responses [4]. Both of the patient's parents were heterozygous carriers of a YARS1 variant and showed no clinical features of peripheral neuropathy.

One of the YARS1 variants identified in our patient (c.176T>C; p.Ile59Thr) has previously been reported in a premature infant with compound heterozygosity (c.237C>G; p.Tyr79*/c.176T>C; p.Ile59Thr) [9]. That patient developed severe pulmonary fibrosis, liver failure, anemia, hypoglycemia, hypoalbuminemia, and dysmorphic features; tyrosine supplementation was ineffective, and the child died at 2 years of age. Our patient exhibited similar manifestations—liver dysfunction, anemia, hypoproteinemia, and hypoglycemia—but notably had no evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. The second variant (c.1261G>A; p.Val421Met), inherited paternally, has not previously been reported.

Current evidence suggests that IMNEPD2-associated YARS1 variants are predominantly missense, as observed in our patient, although clear genotype–phenotype correlations have not yet been established. The largest cohort analysis to date, reported by Kuan et al [7], aggregated data from multiple publications and included 25 patients with IMNEPD2. The most frequent clinical features were craniofacial dysmorphism (deep-set eyes, 80%; prominent cheeks, 16%), short stature (84%), developmental delay (92%), cholestasis (40%), liver disease (46%), anemia (76%), hypothyroidism (23%), and hypoglycemia (36%).

In our patient, the core manifestations (growth retardation, developmental delay, hepatomegaly, anemia, and facial dysmorphism) were accompanied by a less common but clinically significant severe hypoglycemia, the pathogenesis of which appears to relate to CHI. Williams et al [10] described a family with a homozygous p.Pro167Thr variant in which 5 of 7 affected individuals had hypoglycemia; one patient developed hypoglycemia (glucose <47 mg/dL [<2.6 mmol/L]) after only 1 hour of fasting, with “normal” C-peptide and undetectable insulin, consistent with hypoketotic insulin hypersecretion.

A detailed description of hypoglycemia was provided by Zeiad et al [11], who reported a premature infant (34 weeks of gestation) with a homozygous p.Tyr204Cys variant. At 3 months of age, the patient developed hypoglycemia (43 mg/dL [2.38 mmol/L]) with an insulin concentration of 1 μIU/mL (6.945 pmol/L), hypoketosis (β-hydroxybutyrate 1.04 mg/dL [0.1 mmol/L]; CHI diagnostic criterion, <18.7 mg/dL [<1.8 mmol/L]), and a positive glucagon stimulation test. Congenital hyperinsulinism was diagnosed but proved refractory to maximal-dose diazoxide, and [18F]fluorodihydroxyphenylalanine positron emission tomography ([18F]DOPA-PET) confirmed a diffuse form of CHI. A further case of IMNEPD2 with hypoglycemia of unspecified etiology was reported by Esteve et al [9] in a child carrying the same c.176T>C variant present in our patient.

International diagnostic criteria for CHI include hypoglycemia (<50 mg/dL [<2.8 mmol/L]) with detectable insulin (>1.25 μIU/mL [>8.7 pmol/L]), detectable C-peptide (>0.5 ng/mL [>0.17 nmol/L]), and hypoketosis [8]. Our patient fulfilled these criteria, demonstrating persistent hypoketotic hypoglycemia with detectable but relatively low insulin concentrations (0.6-1.5 μIU/mL [4.2-10.4 pmol/L]) and disproportionately elevated C-peptide concentrations (1.43-2.52 ng/mL [0.47-0.83 nmol/L]). Proinsulin, although not required for diagnosis, was measured to further characterize the underlying mechanism and was elevated in all critical samples (Fig. 2), supporting ongoing insulin hypersecretion. The pronounced dissociation among insulin, C-peptide, and proinsulin concentrations remains unexplained and warrants further investigation.

Diazoxide produced only transient clinical benefit, and dose escalation failed to restore glycemic control; octreotide was likewise ineffective. [18F]DOPA-PET imaging was not performed, as the absence of pathogenic variants in ABCC8 and KCNJ11 effectively excluded a focal form of CHI. This report highlights the phenotypic heterogeneity of IMNEPD2 and underscores CHI as a clinically significant manifestation. Further studies using appropriate biological models are required to clarify the mechanisms of dysregulated insulin secretion, define evidence-based management strategies, and evaluate potential therapeutic approaches, including tyrosine supplementation.
