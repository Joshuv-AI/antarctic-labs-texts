Ferroelastic hysteresis, shear modulus softening, and the tetragonal↔cubic transition in davemaoite
Authors: Tianqi Wan, Chenxing Luo, Zhen Zhang, Yang Sun, Renata M. Wentzcovitch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cubic CaSiO3-perovskite (davemaoite), the third most abundant mineral in Earth’s lower mantle, remains a key uncertainty in deep-Earth models. Using machine learning interatomic potentials and molecular dynamics simulations, we present a direct evidence of pronounced ferroelastic hysteresis near its tetragonal↔cubic transition. By isolating the intrinsic elastic response from anelastic relaxation and nonhydrostatic stress, we establish a robust upper-bound benchmark for sound velocities and elasticity models while revealing pronounced shear modulus softening across the transition. The confirmed ferroelasticity suggests substantial strain memory, seismic wave scattering, and strong attenuation if the transition is activated in regions of deeply subducted mid-ocean ridge basalt (MORB) accumulation. While pure CaPv may not undergo this transition at relevant conditions, it could occur in Ca(Si,Ti)O3, contributing to the low shear velocity regions in the deep mantle and above the core-mantle boundary.

INTRODUCTION

The lower mantle, extending from the seismic discontinuity at ∼660-km depth to the core-mantle boundary at ∼2890 km, has been shown by seismic tomography models to contain large-scale velocity heterogeneities whose origins are still debated. Davemaoite, i.e., cubic CaSiO3-perovskite (CaPv), comprises 7 to 10 vol % in pyrolite (1, 2) and up to 30 vol% in subducted mid-ocean ridge basalt (MORB) at depths below 560 km (3–5). As the third most abundant phase in the lower mantle, its presence significantly affects this region’s properties (1) and those of subducted basaltic crust (3). Experimental (6) and theoretical (7–9) studies suggest that the solubility of CaPv in bridgmanite (MgSiO3-perovskite), the most abundant mantle phase, increases with temperature and is completely dissolved at ∼1800-km depth along the geotherm (10). Furthermore, CaPv is an important Ti-bearing phase in subducted slabs, forming a Ca(Si,Ti)O3 solid solution (11, 12) with altered properties. It has been suggested that the key tetragonal↔cubic transition in Ti-bearing CaPv occurs at mid-mantle depths and may account for the seismic properties of large low shear velocity provinces (LLSVPs) (13). Despite the potential influence of the Ca(Si,Ti)O3 and (Mg,Ca)SiO3-perovskite solid solutions on deep mantle properties, the elastic behavior of pure CaPv under the mantle conditions remains highly relevant but debated and has yet to be fully addressed. Therefore, it is crucial to investigate its elastic properties first, particularly across the tetragonal↔cubic transition.

CaPv undergoes a tetragonal (I4/mcm) to cubic (Pm3¯m) phase transition with increasing temperature above ∼12 GPa. Experimental (14, 15) and theoretical studies (16–18) of this transition have previously offered dissimilar phase boundaries. However, a recent large-scale computational study examining this phase boundary from a thermodynamic viewpoint (19) confirms detailed measurements, pinning this transition at submantle-geotherm conditions. However, it remains unclear how this phase change may affect the elastic properties and acoustic velocities in CaPv at mantle conditions. The tetragonal↔cubic symmetry change in a perovskite system has long been predicted to exhibit ferroelastic behavior in the low-symmetry phase (20), characterized by non-Hookean deformation with a hysteresis loop, similar to other ferroic properties such as ferroelectricity and ferromagnetism. As CaPv belongs to the same perovskite family, one may anticipate that tetragonal CaPv also exhibits ferroelastic behavior. However, no complete ferroelastic hysteresis loop in tetragonal CaPv has been observed, unlike the one first identified in 1976 in the prototypical ferroelastic material Pb3(PO4)2 (21). Furthermore, shear-wave velocity anomalies have been reported near ferroelastic transitions in several geophysically important materials, such as SiO2 (22, 23). Because ferroelastic transitions vary in mechanism among systems, their associated elastic anomalies can differ in both form and the pressure-temperature range over which they occur. Unfortunately, the challenges posed by CaPv’s spontaneous amorphization during decompression (24) have limited similar studies in this material. The struggle to capture such hysteresis loops and elastic anomalies in ultrasound measurements emphasizes the importance of accurate computational studies to understand the seismic properties of CaPv and similar ferroelastic systems. Here, we apply deep learning techniques to generate interatomic potentials that accurately model CaPv’s compression curve and elastic properties at several temperatures. Exploring the hysteresis curve provides direct evidence of ferroelastic behavior in tetragonal CaPv. This method allows us to fully address the predicted yet previously unobserved shear modulus anomalies around the phase transition.

RESULTS

Ferroelastic behavior and hysteresis loop

We first assessed the accuracy of our trained deep learning potential (DP) model by comparing energies, forces, and virial terms using a validation dataset (fig. S1). The root mean square errors of the energy, force, and virial term are 3.6 meV/atom, 71 meV/Å, and 20 meV/atom, respectively. These uncertainties are comparable to those reported for other machine learning potentials (MLPs) under extreme conditions (19, 25–27). To further validate the model, we computed phonon dispersions using the phonon quasiparticle method (28, 29) to take the anharmonicity into consideration. The DP model accurately reproduces both harmonic and anharmonic phonon dispersions of CaPv (fig. S2), capturing the soft phonon modes at the R point in cubic CaPv at 0 K and their renormalization to positive frequencies at 3000 K due to the strong anharmonicity. The excellent agreement with such sensitive density functional theory (DFT) predictions highlights the DP model’s reliability in describing lattice dynamics and its suitability for calculating elastic properties with comparable accuracy.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations using a deep learning potential trained with the strongly constrained and appropriately normed meta–generalized-gradient approximation functional (DFT-SCAN) (30) can accurately reproduce the high-pressure elastic behavior of minerals, even across complex phase transitions (31). Comparison of our predicted 300 K DP-SCAN compression curve with experimental measurements (13, 32–36) is shown in Fig. 1A. We reproduce most of the measurements with unprecedented accuracy, with discrepancies smaller than 1%, except for data from Ricolleau et al. (37). This potential also reliably reproduces axial compressibility (Fig. 1B) and Si─O bond lengths (Fig. 1C). Notably, the Si─O bond length along the [001] direction remains slightly longer than along [100]. Compared to previous local density approximation (LDA) and generalized gradient approximation (GGA)–based predictions, SCAN reproduces these structural properties more accurately. Except for Ricolleau et al. (37) data, the compressive behavior of cubic CaPv measured by x-ray diffraction experiments at 2000 K was also well reproduced in our simulations up to 120 GPa (fig. S3). In addition, the thermal equation of state (EoS) parameters for cubic and tetragonal CaPv (table S1) and the isothermal EoS parameters for tetragonal CaPv (table S2) are consistent with both previous calculations and experimental measurements. Such agreement indicates that DP-SCAN describes the behavior of both tetragonal and cubic CaPv well at relevant conditions, validating the DP-SCAN method to explore the CaPv system throughout a broad range of conditions.

One of the key findings in this study is that tetragonal CaPv exhibits elastic hysteresis characteristic of ferroelastic systems under a stretch-compression cycle with uniaxial stress applied along the c axis (Fig. 2A). Such a feature was not seen in prior simulations or experiments whereas is consistent with the ferroelastic behavior observed in other perovskites (20). At 150 K and 12 GPa, compressive stress along the c axis causes the symmetry-breaking strain et=[(c−a)/a] to approach 0, indicating a transition toward a cubic-like structure. However, the cubic structure is unstable under these conditions, leading to a switch from a standing form (SF) to a lying-down form (LF) (Fig. 2A), corresponding to different orientations of the longer side of the tetragonal phase (text S2). Further analysis of the trajectories of oxygen ions reveals that SiO6 octahedra rotate around the c axis in the SF state and around the [110] direction in the LF state (Fig. 2, D and E). In the SF state, the tilting angle decreases with increasing compressive stress. In contrast, in the LF state, the tilting angle increases with compressive stress along the original c axis (Fig. 2F) since the compressive stress axis and the longer length axis are now perpendicular. The mechanical switching between these two states under external compressive stress results from a shear strain within the ab plane of the tetragonal cell, as illustrated in Fig. 2C. Microscopically, the system’s stress versus strain relation still follows Hooke’s law before and after the switching (Fig. 2A). Also, the ferroelastic hysteresis loop’s amplitude depends significantly on temperature. At 150 K and 12 GPa, the induced stress required to change the longer axis orientation is ∼1.7 GPa. As the temperature approaches Ttr, the hysteresis loop shrinks and eventually disappears (fig. S5). This is the archetypical behavior of a ferroelastic material undergoing a transition from a low-symmetry ferroelastic phase to a high-symmetry paraelastic one (38), I4/mcm to Pm3¯m in this case. As shown in Fig. 2B, the ferroelastic hysteresis loop disappears entirely in cubic CaPv, the paraelastic counterpart. Ferroelastic↔paraelastic transitions occur naturally in oxides and silicates within Earth’s crust and mantle and have been reported to cause seismic velocity anomalies (39, 40). The effects of ferroelastic transitions on seismic parameters (22) are expected to differ significantly from those caused by other nonferroelastic structural transitions and temperature-compositional variations more commonly observed in the mantle (41). To better understand the nature of these transitions, we further investigate the complete set of elastic moduli across the transition and a broad range of pressures and temperatures around it.

Softening region near the phase transition

CaPv undergoes an improper ferroelastic↔paraelastic transition at T = Ttr similar to octahedral tilting perovskites systems such as SrTiO3 (20). Individual components of the elastic tensors (Fig. 3) display strong anomalies near Ttr. The three sets of elastic coefficients for cubic CaPv, i.e., longitudinal (c11 = c22 = c33), shear (c44 = c55 = c66), and off-diagonal (c12 = c13 = c23), gradually diverge at Ttr, indicating that the cubic lattice experiences increasingly anisotropic contraction and shear strains as temperature decreases. In tetragonal CaPv, the shear elastic coefficient c66 associated with shear strain εxy has a significantly larger value than the c44 or c55 coefficients. The smaller c44 and c55 values can be attributed to the rotation of SiO6 octahedra along the longer axis. This rotation would result from the lattice distortion transition where the cubic a axis of CaPv splits into the tetragonal a and c axes. The three principal longitudinal coefficients follow the trend c11 > c33, which indicates anisotropic lattice distortions. The gradual symmetry reduction across the transition with a symmetry-breaking strain is usually accompanied by a marked anomaly of the shear modulus near Ttr.

To further evaluate CaPv’s elastic properties across the ferroelastic↔paraelastic transition, we analyzed the behavior of the adiabatic bulk modulus (BS) and shear modulus (G) computed using Voigt-Reuss-Hill averages (Fig. 4, A and B). BS aligns closely with previous measurements (13, 36) at 12 GPa and retains a nearly linear dependence in tetragonal and cubic CaPv away from the phase boundary. In addition, the modest reduction in BS near Ttr at relatively low pressures is consistent with prior observations (fig. S6). However, our calculations reveal a strong, systematic dependence of this bulk modulus reduction (ΔBS) when pressure increases beyond 60 GPa (fig. S7). Extreme compression of the crystal lattice inherently amplifies the coupling between the volume strain and the octahedral tilting. Consequently, ΔBS grows nonlinearly, reaching a much more pronounced reduction of ∼2.1 GPa at 120 GPa. The discrepancies between our calculations and experimental measurements stem from different mechanisms depending on the structural phase. In the tetragonal phase, the less accentuated reduction of moduli observed in our calculations is primarily due to the absence of macroscopic domain structures and domain wall relaxations in our perfect single-crystal simulations. In the stable cubic phase, where ferroelastic domains are strictly absent, the measured and calculated elastic moduli are expected to converge. The remaining discrepancies in the cubic stability field likely arise from nonideal factors in the experimental samples, such as pure or defect-induced local strain fields that locally break cubic symmetry, unrelaxed deviatoric stresses inherent to high-pressure experimental assemblies or frequency-dependent anelastic relaxation observed in experiments, which are naturally excluded in our MD simulations. A recent similar simulation (42) does not show this small anomaly in BS near Ttr, likely because it examined the elastic anomaly only in the high-pressure cubic phase. Notably, the transition does not cause significant changes in density (fig. S8) (43–45). In contrast to BS, calculations reveal a significantly stronger reduction in G compared to measurements at 12 GPa. The characteristic dip in G occurs in the ferroelastic phase when the symmetry-breaking strain et approaches zero around the ferroelastic↔paraelastic transition. An even stronger G softening occurs in the parealastic phase near Ttr. Our predicted phase boundary occurs at a lower temperature compared to the latest computational estimate by Zhang et al. (42). We used different but equivalent approaches to outline the phase boundary—tracking elastic anomaly versus tracking lattice parameters in theirs [see Fig. 4 (B and C)]. Besides, our elastic properties agree well with theirs (fig. S9). Therefore, the difference in our phase boundaries should be primarily due to using different DFT functionals. Zhang et al. (42) used LDA with an approximate 4.3-GPa pressure shift, whereas we used SCAN, which produces the most accurate EoS to date (see figs. S1 and S2).

Determining the phase boundary of the tetragonal↔cubic transition in ferroelastic CaPv has long been challenging due to the system’s sensitivity to stress, pressure, and temperature, resulting in large uncertainties and discrepancies in reported boundaries (see Fig. 5A). Early computational studies in the mid-2000s used GGA-Perdew-Wang-1991 (GGA-PW91) (17, 46) and LDA (43) functionals, using either MD (17, 46) on relatively small supercells (∼80 atoms) or free energy modeling based on Landau theory (43). These phase boundaries with large uncertainties suggested that the phase boundary could occur at subducting slabs or lower mantle conditions. Contemporaneous measurements performed on an externally laser-heated diamond anvil cell (LHDAC) (14, 15) offered much lower transition temperatures at mantle pressures. The tetragonal and cubic stability fields derived from these experiments were supported by DAC experiments in 2016 (47). In 2019, an elasticity-based determination of this boundary (13) indicated agreement with the previous measurements (14, 15) at ∼12 GPa, but the extension/prediction of this phase boundary to mantle pressures based on ab initio MD on relatively small supercells (∼80 atoms) using the GGA-Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional again suggested a phase boundary comparable to that determined using Landau theory (43). It was not until recently that MLPs trained on LDA and PBE/GGA using thermodynamic integration with MD (TIMD) simulations on large supercells 2004–2007 LHDAC measurements (14, 15) and constrained DFT uncertainties within ∼300 K throughout most of the mantle. Such a constrained boundary was also in good agreement with ab initio PBE calculations based on statistically converged quasi-harmonic approximation (QHA) calculations (16), a remarkable result given that CaPv is a highly anharmonic phase. A recent PBEsol calculation (48) using the stochastic self-consistent harmonic approximation yielded a phase boundary very similar to those determined using the LDA with TIMD (19) or PBE with QHA (16). These LDA/PBEsol/PBE-GGA statistically converged predictions, i.e., phonon-based with thorough Brillouin zone wave number sampling or MD-based large-scale (several thousand atoms) calculations, all point to lower temperature phase boundaries consistent with LHDAC experimental determinations (14, 15, 47). Alternative approaches, such as inferring the boundary from elastic softening (13, 42) or deviatoric stresses (48), have also been used. A recent MD-based LDA-trained MLP calculation of the elastic softening in cubic CaPv (42) suggested a phase boundary at intermediate temperatures, between the experimental ones (14, 15, 47), and the earlier ab initio MD (13) or Landau theory–based ones (43), which is still slightly high. Thus, their conclusion that the elastic softening in cubic CaPv induced by the transition could affect the seismic properties of subducting slabs, the D″ region, or LLSVPs, owing to the accumulation of CaPv-rich MORB in these regions, may not be entirely reliable for pure CaPv. However, the present large-scale SCAN-trained MLP MD simulations provide new constraints. First, it is statistically converged with respect to the number of atoms (10,240 atoms) and simulation run times (8 ns). Second, the SCAN density functional gives the most accurate thermal equations of state for tetragonal (300 K) and cubic CaPv (2000 K) phases, shown in Fig. 1 and fig. S3. Besides, the maximum elastic softening temperature (the phase boundary) at 12 GPa essentially coincides with that experimentally determined by Thomson et al. (13). The current phase boundary is also very close to those determined by statistically converged calculations using multiple DFT functionals and methods (16, 19, 48) and the experimental phase boundaries determined by LHDAC (14, 15, 47).

Notably, the shear modulus, G, exhibits a nearly linear temperature dependence well above and below Ttr but displays nonlinear behavior over a broad temperature range around Ttr (blue shaded area in Fig. 5B). The physical origin of this pronounced softening lies in intense local structural fluctuations near the phase transition point. As the temperature approaches Ttr, the lattice parameters a and c become nearly identical, and small thermal perturbations allow the system to dynamically fluctuate between the tetragonal and cubic structures. This dynamic structural instability strongly couples with macroscopic shear strain, leading to the pronounced dip in the shear modulus. Strictly speaking, the macroscopic ferroelastic behavior, characterized by structural hysteresis loops, is confined exclusively to the tetragonal phase and disappears entirely in the paraelastic cubic phase (Fig. 2B). However, the intrinsic structural effects of this ferroelastic transition persist well into the higher-temperature cubic stability field. Thus, we define the “softening region” (blue shaded area in Fig. 5B) as the temperature interval around the tetragonal-cubic transition where G deviates markedly from its nearly linear high- and low-temperature trends. This softening region spans 300 to 700 K, with the magnitude of softening strongly dependent on pressure. This broad softening interval contrasts with the narrow precursor softening (typically tens of kelvin) observed in analogous perovskite systems such as (Ca,Sr)TiO3 at ambient pressure (49). Such extreme broadening is primarily driven by high pressure. At 12 GPa, the softening occurs over a narrower interval of ∼300 K. With increasing pressure, the softening temperature range extensively broadens, reaching ∼700 K at lower mantle conditions. Therefore, ferroelastic transitions can anomalously affect seismic parameters over a broad temperature range far beyond that of a sharp thermodynamic phase boundary. Furthermore, it is important to note that its exact width remains quantitatively sensitive to the chosen DFT functional and MLP architecture. For instance, a recent study (42) using a kernel-based Machine Learning Force Field (MLFF) trained on LDA reported a much wider precursor softening of ∼400 K at 12 GPa. Although the G softening temperature range broadens with increasing pressure, it remains below the cold slab geotherm (Fig. 5B) where CaPv is expected to be abundant. This suggests that the significant softening associated with the tetragonal↔cubic transition in pure CaPv alone is unlikely to cause seismic wave speed anomalies in the lower mantle. This result contrasts with previous elasticity results (42) that extend the softening region to the slab geotherm conditions (fig. S10). This discrepancy is due to their apparent overestimation of the tetragonal↔cubic transition temperature (see Fig. 5B). Nonetheless, we do not rule out the possibility that certain young, fast, and ultracold slabs, like Tonga (50), may meet the criteria for this transition, potentially giving rise to local seismic velocity anomalies.

DISCUSSION

Our work confronts a primary uncertainty in lower-mantle mineralogy: the elastic behavior of CaPv. Unlike MgSiO3, the most abundant mantle phase, which has been well characterized over the past decades (52–57), CaPv remains one of the largest unresolved uncertainties in lower-mantle mineral physics. Properly constraining this uncertainty requires recognition of the ferroelastic nature of CaPv’s tetragonal↔cubic transition and the hysteresis it introduces, which has been largely overlooked in previous studies (13, 19, 36, 58). By isolating the purely elastic response through MD simulations free from frequency-dependent relaxation and nonhydrostatic artifacts, we establish a conservative upper bound on seismic velocities in CaPv.

Ferroelastic systems are inherently complex and highly sensitive to external conditions, where even minor variations in stress, including chemical stress caused by alloying, can significantly alter the phase boundary, as well as the shape of elastic anomalies. Before this work, the ferroelastic behavior of CaPv had only been inferred indirectly, either by analogies with other ferroelastic minerals or through the observation of related elastic anomalies (20, 42). Such a ferroelastic system may appear cubic on average, but it can host coexisting domains of lower symmetry due to spontaneous symmetry-breaking strain fluctuations. Even a small stress can shift the system toward one orientation or another, giving rise to shear modulus softening. This sensitivity to stress and strain is illustrated by our calculation of the ferroelastic hysteresis loop, providing a predictive demonstration of strain memory associated with domain switching in a ferroelastic system.

Beyond the hysteresis itself, our simulations reveal a pronounced shear modulus anomaly that arises as an intrinsic precursor softening of CaPv, directly linked to the tetragonal↔cubic transition. This dip exists even in the absence of domain wall relaxation or memory effects, making it a fundamental feature of the transition. This intrinsic softening corresponds to the same phenomenon highlighted on the cubic (T > Ttr) side in recent computational work (42), but here we capture the full transition and demonstrate its ferroelastic origin. Regarding inelasticity and seismic attenuation, our finite-size and finite-time MD calculations are designed to isolate the purely uniform elastic, unrelaxed high-frequency response, free from nonhydrostatic artifacts. Although slower macroscopic relaxation processes at seismic frequencies are not resolved directly, attenuation and velocity dispersion are strictly coupled, thereby reducing wave speeds at lower frequencies. Consequently, true seismic-frequency velocities can be significantly lower than the unrelaxed high-frequency values reported here, firmly establishing our purely elastic MD results as a conservative upper bound on seismic velocities. Within this intrinsic framework, the hysteresis loop reflects the system’s strain memory, while the softening dip represents the fundamental elastic anomaly of the tetragonal↔cubic transition. These two features together capture the complete elastic signature of ferroelasticity in CaPv, providing a baseline upon which further anelastic mechanisms, such as domain wall relaxation, act to drive the additional velocity reductions observed in experiments and in the real mantle.

Furthermore, nonhydrostatic stresses are expected to be present and significant in subducted MORB crust within descending plates or in D″ accumulations (59, 60). This issue is also highly relevant to experimental measurements at extreme pressures and temperatures, where the stress cannot be perfectly controlled. For instance, in experimental settings such as diamond anvil cell (DAC) measurements, differential stresses can be exceptionally large, reaching 5.3 to 7.2 GPa at bulk pressures around 145 GPa (61, 62). In the natural deep-Earth context, while macroscopic deviatoric stresses are generally expected to relax in hot mantle flow, the critical stress required to trigger the ferroelastic mechanical switching shrinks drastically as the temperature approaches the phase boundary (fig. S5). Consequently, even the remarkably small residual nonhydrostatic stresses sustained in deeply subducted slabs can profoundly influence ferroelastic domain populations and the apparent extent of elastic anomalies, provided that the slab geotherm intersects the phase boundary. Any additional stress-induced or anelastic effects associated with background deviatoric stress would further enhance seismic attenuation and reduce effective wave velocities relative to our intrinsic upper bound.

A further consequence of ferroelasticity is the strong frequency dependence (dispersion) of sound velocities near the ferroelastic↔paraelastic transition (63, 64), which arises from strain-induced ferroelastic domain wall motion and the strain direction switching (49). At ultrasonic frequencies, twinned perovskites are considerably softer than predicted from calculations, as a portion of the applied stress can be accommodated by local spontaneous strain, as twin walls displace, rather than solely by a uniform and global crystal deformation (65). This relaxation mechanism reduces effective velocities and enhances attenuation of elastic waves. Domain wall motion amplifies elastic softening well beyond that produced by the phase transition itself (45). Because shear-wave velocities in CaPv are typically measured by ultrasound at 30 to 60 MHz, such domain wall dynamics provide a natural explanation for the consistent underestimation of the experimental shear modulus in the ferroelastic phase, and even in the paraelastic phase near Ttr, relative to the intrinsic upper-bound values reported here (13, 36). Therefore, as previously noted (36), CaPv near Ttr may exhibit even greater velocity reductions at seismic frequencies than those reported here. Similar behavior has been observed in LaCoO3, where twin wall motion results in pronounced attenuation—particularly at seismic-frequency and high-temperature conditions relevant to Earth’s interior (66, 67).

However, domain wall attenuation alone cannot fully explain the sustained underestimation of shear wave speed and the 30% discrepancies between the measurements themselves away from the transition, up to ∼2 Ttr. Additional contributors, such as deviatoric or unrelaxed stresses, local microstructural distortions, or other anelastic processes, are likely required to account for these discrepancies, specifically in the cubic phase where domain walls are absent. Furthermore, anelastic mechanisms beyond ferroelasticity—such as grain boundary sliding and creep—are known to affect seismic velocity but are exceedingly difficult to compute. Since dispersion in acoustic wave measurements always indicates anelasticity, targeted experimental investigations of anelastic mechanisms under these conditions are essential to clarify the origin of the differences between experimental and computational shear moduli, as well as discrepancies among measurements themselves. In addition to classical phonon softening, some perovskites exhibit central peak (relaxational) dynamics near the transition (e.g., LaAlO3), which arise from disorder between local displacement states and can couple to acoustic modes, modifying the effective small-q elastic response (68). Our MD-based elasticity calculations and phonon-quasiparticle analysis capture anharmonic phonon renormalization and local fluctuations (fig. S2), but the finite supercell size and trajectory length limit the resolution at very small wave vectors and ultralow frequencies needed to quantify a central peak and the renormalized acoustic slope in the q → 0 limit. Characterizing such contributions will require dedicated future calculations with enhanced small-q/low-ω resolution.

In this context, despite these inherent computational constraints on ultralow-frequency phenomena, the ab initio quality calculations presented here provide a necessary counterpoint: a robust and controlled means to isolate and quantify the elastic softening associated solely with ferroelasticity. Unlike experiments, they are free from frequency-dependent anelastic or nonhydrostatic artifacts and thus establish a conservative upper bound on seismic velocities. Combined with the dense sampling performed near the transition point, our results provide a critical benchmark for future investigations of ferroelastic behavior in CaPv. Together, our results indicate that the ferroelastic↔paraelastic transition in CaPv, particularly near Ttr, can act as a source of additional anelasticity. Such behavior would manifest as reduced velocities, enhanced attenuation, and seismic anomalies that might otherwise be interpreted as arising solely from temperature variations, compositional heterogeneities, or phase changes.

While pure CaPv does not introduce shear modulus anomalies under typical lower-mantle conditions via a pure elastic effect, the mineral likely incorporates substantial amounts of other elements. For example, since incompatible titanium (Ti) naturally enriches in MORB during partial melting of the upper mantle, CaPv acts as its primary host in the deep Earth (11). Previous studies indicate that CaPv in MORB can contain up to 28 wt % TiO2 (3, 37, 69) or 4.8 wt % Al2O3 (3, 5, 70, 71) under lower-mantle conditions. This incorporation of other elements profoundly alters properties of CaPv through different mechanisms. First, as shown in Fig. 5B, the incorporation of Ti or Al stabilizes the low-symmetry phase and markedly shifts Ttr to higher temperatures than reported here, extending the influence of the tetragonal-cubic transition into the ambient mantle (12, 13, 15). Compositional complexity also broadens the softening interval. Analogous perovskite systems, such as (Ca,Sr)TiO3 solid solutions, exhibit massive acoustic attenuation and broadened shear softening over ∼400 K, in contrast to the much narrow intervals of pure endmembers (49). Such compositional broadening in Al2O3-bearing CaPv within cold descending slabs may provide a plausible origin for certain seismic discontinuities observed in the lower mantle (72). In addition, the incorporation of Ti intrinsically reduces the acoustic wave velocities of CaPv significantly (69). While Wu’s static calculations yield velocities that happen to align closely with the Preliminary Reference Earth Model (PREM; fig. S10) (73), these velocities will be substantially reduced under realistic high-temperature mantle conditions, causing the velocity profiles of Ti-bearing CaPv to drop significantly below PREM values. A recent study has also demonstrated that Fe,Al,Mg,Ti-bearing CaPv can exhibit a shear wave velocity 10 to 20% slower than pure CaPv (74). Consequently, the superposition of these effects firmly establishes that the moderate enrichment of MORB, characterized by high CaPv content and its capacity to host incompatible elements, provides a robust physical mechanism for the extreme low wave velocities characterizing LLSVPs (13, 74–76).

The influence of CaPv on the bulk elastic properties of the mantle must also be understood in the context of the CaSiO3-MgSiO3 solid solution system. A recent prediction (9) and observation of the full (10) or partial (77) dissolution of CaPv in bridgmanite raise questions about how the seismic properties of the most abundant mantle phase, bridgmanite, might be affected by ferroelasticity in the CaPv endmember. Without a well-constrained understanding of the pure CaPv endmember, including its phase behavior and elastic response, any inference on the behavior of more complex solid solutions such as Ca(Si,Ti)O3 or (Ca,Mg)SiO3 risks being misleading or even fundamentally incorrect. While experimental measurements and computations have yet to advance to the point of accurately capturing or predicting fully dispersive acoustic wave speeds under geophysically relevant conditions, their interplay provides a deeper understanding of ferroelastic material properties. This interaction underscores the importance of integrating methodologies to address the nuanced complexities of ferroelasticity and its potential contributions to the overall anelastic behavior of minerals under the extreme conditions of the Earth’s interior. In this regard, our results provide the essential benchmark and upper bound against which all experimental and geophysical inferences of CaPv elasticity must be evaluated.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations

To create benchmark datasets for CaPv, Born-Oppenheimer MD simulations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (78). The effectiveness of SCAN meta-GGA functional (30) was carefully investigated. The simulations used a 2 × 2 × 2 supercell containing 40 atoms, alongside a 2 × 2 × 2 k-point grid and a kinetic energy cutoff of 550 eV, which was shown to be sufficient to achieve convergence in calculating anharmonic phonon dispersions (79). One independent DP model, trained on SCAN datasets, was developed and is referred to as DP-SCAN. The model training used the smooth version descriptor se_e2_a proposed by Zhang et al. (80, 81) (text S1).

Deep potential MD simulations

We conducted deep potential MD simulations using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) code (82). A simulation was first performed in the Isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) to equilibrate the cell shape under specific temperature and pressure conditions. This simulation involved 10,240 atoms, ran for 0.1 ns, and used a time step of 0.5 fs. The Nosé-Hoover thermo-baro-stat that incorporates modular invariance (83, 84) was applied for temperature and pressure control. After the system reached equilibrium, subsequent simulations started from the equilibrated unit cell shape. To determine the elastic tensor through the stress-fluctuation approach, simulations were first conducted in the Canonical ensemble (NVT) to gather atomic coordinates and velocities at target temperatures. These were followed by the 8-ns-long simulations in the Microcanonical ensemble (NVE) with a time step of 2 fs to compute the adiabatic cijs tensor components under defined conditions.

The stress-fluctuation formalism for thermoelasticity

The thermodynamic elastic tensor Aijkl(T,V) (where i,j,k,l=1,2,3) represents the second derivative of the free energy F density with respect to strain under equilibrium conditions, while the volume V is fixed in MD simulations. It is expressed as (85–87)Aijkl(T,V)=1V∂2F∂εij∂εkl=⟨AijklB⟩−kBTV[⟨σijσkl⟩−⟨σij⟩⟨σkl⟩]where ⟨⋯⟩ denotes the ensemble average, εij is the strain tensor, N is the number of atoms, T is the temperature, and δij is the Kronecker delta function. The stress tensor σij and Born matrix tensor AijklB are derived from the first and second derivatives of strain energy density U/V with respect to εij, respectivelyσij=1V∂U∂εij,AijklB=1V∂2U∂εij∂εkl

During the equilibrated MD runs, both AijklB and σij are periodically calculated. Since the convergence of AijklB is faster than that of σij, different sampling frequencies are used to optimize storage while maintaining computational accuracy (calculating AijklB every 200 MD steps and σij every 20 MD steps). The Born matrix AijklB is computed as follows (87)AijklB=∂σij∂εkl=σijδkl+σikδjl

For an average stress σij calculated during the simulation, the effective elastic tensor Cijkl is given by the following expression (88, 89)Cijkl=1V∂2F∂εij∂εkl−σijδkl+12(σikδjl+σkjδil+σilδjk+σljδik)

Under hydrostatic stress condition [i.e., σij=−Pδij, where −13Tr(σij)], this equation can be further simplified to (88)Cijkl=1V∂2F∂εij∂εkl+P(δijδkl−δilδkj−δikδjl)

Here, Aijkl and Cijkl are equal only when the system is free from external pressure or stress. The adiabatic elastic tensors AijklS and CijklS can be obtained from NVE simulations.
