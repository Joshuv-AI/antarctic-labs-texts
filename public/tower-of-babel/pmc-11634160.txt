The Living Lab at Home: Feasibility and Acceptability of Multimodal In-Home Data Collection Among Youth Across the Developmental Spectrum
Authors: Katelynn E. Boerner, Veronica Dudarev, Leora Pearl-Dowler, Marie-Noelle Wharton, Harold Siden, Liisa Holsti, Tim F. Oberlander
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This article has supplementary material on the web site: www.jdbp.org.

Research on child development, behavior, and health has been historically conducted in laboratory or clinical contexts. While these settings may be optimal for scientific rigor, they do not approximate real-world experiences. Many children and families describe laboratory-based experiences as not representative of their behavior at home because of increased anxiety or the novelty of the environment.1 Measuring phenomena at a single time point misses daily fluctuations of experiences and symptoms.2 In addition, factors such as geographic location, socioeconomic status, parental employment, mental health, and medical/developmental complexity can affect families' ability to attend laboratory-based research at all, creating an equity and accessibility barrier.3

In the past, the emphasis on laboratory studies was needed because of limited ability to collect rigorous data in people's familiar environments. However, with wide availability of smartphones, high fluency of technology use by even young children, and availability of wearable sensors of movement and physiology, data collection in the home is now possible.2 Yet, adoption in research with young people with developmental, psychiatric, and medical complexity has been minimal. Here, we start this process by codeveloping at-home data collection procedures in collaboration with patients and their families. While there are examples of specific in-home methods being developed/evaluated for a specific population,4,5 our intention was to develop multimodal data collection—including active and passive methods—in a diverse group of youth across the developmental spectrum.

In the first research phase,1 we interviewed patients and families about their perceptions of in-home data collection and research methods in the context of our Living Lab at Home (LLAH) research program. We solicited feedback regarding barriers, facilitators, priorities, and adaptations required to increase accessibility. This feedback was incorporated into the design of the present pilot project. The aim of this study was to determine the feasibility of these family-informed methods.

METHODS

Design

This study was a pilot feasibility trial of a multimodal microlongitudinal 14-day study protocol.

Recruitment and Eligibility

Families were recruited from local hospital clinics, social media, and past participants who had consented to future research contact (including families from initial engagement interviews1). Families were eligible if their youth was 12 to 18 years old, had a diagnosis of cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, and/or chronic pain, lived in British Columbia, Canada, and the family was fluent in English (allowing a parent to interpret for a nonverbal youth). We selected these specific diagnoses to capture a range of developmental and medical considerations that may affect data collection, including motor, physical, social, and emotional needs.

Procedure

Following consent, families completed a demographic questionnaire and were mailed the LLAH kit containing instructions and materials for data collection. The family was oriented to the procedures through phone calls and emails with the research team. The youth completed 14 days of data collection (Fig. 1), followed by a semistructured interview regarding their experience. Data collection took place between April 2022 and November 2022.

Measures

Demographic Questionnaire

The investigator-designed demographic questionnaire was completed on Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap).6 Youth self-reported their age, sex assigned at birth, gender identity using validated transgender-inclusive questions,7 location of birth, language spoken at home, ethnicity using options from the Statistics Canada ethnic categorizations,8 perceived socioeconomic status (adapted from the Health Behavior in School-age Children Study),9 and any physical and mental health diagnoses.

Ecological Momentary Assessment

Youth completed Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) for 14 days, with 3 prompts a day (morning, afternoon, and evening, exact times selected by the family, with option of opting out of the afternoon EMA). Prompts with a link to a REDCap6 survey were sent through Short Message Service (Twilio). Families could complete the questionnaires on their phone or other web-enabled device. EMA questions included youth self-report of affect, physical well-being, and context (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JDBP/A469). Questions were adapted from a previous EMA study of children with anxiety disorders,10 modified based on family feedback,1 and trialed in a study of young people with chronic pain who also completed assessments 3 times a day for 2 weeks.11 Youth also had the opportunity to identify up to 3 variables they wished to track for themselves and receive a visualized EMA data summary at the end of the study. The surveys did not expire. See “Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JDBP/A469” for the full protocol.

Physical Activity: Accelerometer

Youth wore an accelerometer (ActiGraph GT9X Link, ActiGraph, Pensacola FL) for all waking hours except when bathing, showering, or swimming. Youth had the choice of wearing the accelerometer on their waist or nondominant wrist. The choice to focus on waking hours was to facilitate calculation of physical activity (i.e., distinguish between sleep and sedentary/no wear time), to reduce participant burden, and in recognition of the finding that accelerometery has limited reliability for some measures of sleep.12

Biomarker of Stress (Salivary Cortisol)

Saliva was collected 3 times a day for the first 8 consecutive days of the study. Samples were collected at awakening, 20 minutes postawakening, and evening; the times necessary to establish a diurnal pattern across the day.13 Text messages were sent at those times to remind the participant to complete the sample and an electronic diary of sample conditions using the same system as the EMA. Families stored samples in their refrigerator until the end of the study, when samples were sent by courier, along with the accelerometer, back to the research team. Salivary cortisol was chosen as an accessible stress biomarker that would be presumably sensitive to daily variations in mood, stress, and behavior, to allow for measurement of intra- and interday variations in concordance with the EMA and accelerometer data.

Poststudy Interview

Youth and the parent/caregiver that supported them during data collection (which family members took part in the interview was decided by the family) were invited to take part in a semistructured interview over videoconferencing inquiring about their perception of barriers, facilitators, and suggested changes, as well as quantitative ratings of satisfaction, perceived burden, interference, motivation, and acceptability (protocol in “Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JDBP/A469”). Interviews took place from May 2022 to January 2023. Fourteen interviews were analyzed; 1 family was lost to follow-up, and the other had a corrupted audio recording file. Interviews were an average of 23 minutes in duration (range = 9–43 min). Six parent-youth dyads (including 1 interview where additional caregivers contributed as well) contributed to the interview, 6 youth were interviewed alone, and 2 parents were interviewed alone. Interviews were conducted by a research assistant (M.-N.W.) who had previous contact with the families in study administration, or a medical student (L.P.-D.) or a postdoctoral fellow clinical psychologist (K.E.B.), who had no prior relationship with participating families.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the University of British Columbia Children's & Women's Research Ethics Board (H21-02270), preregistered at https://osf.io/uktva. All participants provided consent (parents) and assent (youth) before participation. Families received up to $60 Canadian dollar in gift cards to incentivize participation in all study aspects: $20 for signing up, $20 for completing 2/3rds of data collection points over the 14 days, and $20 for taking part in the interview. The research team engaged in twice-weekly data monitoring in case participants reported an actionable event (e.g., abuse, suicidality) in the free-text fields.

Qualitative Data Analysis

Interviews were recorded and transcribed professionally. Quantifiable responses were summarized descriptively, and the remainder of the interview content was analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis, with the aim of capturing key sentiments present among family experiences, not specific to individual experiences.20 In keeping with a constructivist epistemological position, and the ethos of thematic analysis, saturation was not sought.21 Qualitative analysis was led by K.E.B., with themes reviewed with the study's senior investigator (T.F.O., a developmental pediatrician and scientist) and the co-investigator that led the quantitative data analysis (V.D., a cognitive psychologist with experience conducting microlongitudinal research in other settings), to triangulate findings from the perspective of members of the research team with diverse disciplines and backgrounds.

RESULTS

Of the 57 families that were contacted with an invitation to participate in the study, all were eligible but 18 (31.6%) did not respond, 23 (40.4%) declined to participate, and 16 (28.1%) enrolled in the study. The primary reasons for declining were that the family was too stressed, busy, or did not have time (either due to the time of year or that they could not commit to 14 days). Of those enrolled, 1 family was lost to follow-up (i.e., they completed parts of the study but not their follow-up interview). Table 1 presents demographic information for participating youth. The average number of days with all 3 modalities of data present was 3.3, with half of the participants collecting 4 or more days, and a quarter of participants collecting 7 to 8 days with all 3 modalities present.

Ecological Momentary Assessment

Ecological momentary assessment adherence computed as proportion of days with at least 1 response (out of 14) was 73.2% (M = 10.25 days, SD = 3.45, range = 3–14 days). Two families (12.5%) participated for only 3 to 5 days, 14 (87.5%) participated for at least a week, and 5 (31.3%) completed 13 to 14 days. Time to complete each EMA, based on logged start/stop times, was an average of 3.5 minutes across all times of day (averages computed excluding cases where participants were interrupted during a survey [6 instances total across all participants and days]). Average completion time differed slightly between the survey time points, as each time point had a different number of questions: morning = 3.9 min, afternoon = 2.8 min, evening = 3.5 min.

Adherence computed as the percent of responses out of the total number of administered prompts per participant was 61.2% (SD = 26%), ranging from 16.7% to 96.4%. However, some prompts were backfilled after receiving a subsequent prompt. Taking time of answering into account, 54.3% of the total number of administered prompts were answered before the next prompt arrived. There were no differences in probability that a prompt would be answered on time (i.e., before the next prompt arrives) in the morning (54.5%), noon (3 families opted out from the mid-day prompt, and adherence computation was adjusted to the actual number of prompts possible) (54.4%), or afternoon (54.3%), all p value >0.4. More details are available in “Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JDBP/A469”.

An EMA feature added based on family feedback1 was the inclusion of a free-text box to provide context on their responses or more information. On average participants commented on 8.6% of the prompts, 5 participants never used this feature. This feature was used by families to document potential reasons for gaps in their data, reflect on their participation experience, provide explanation for an EMA response, continue a response where there was not sufficient space, comment on experiences not captured by the EMA (e.g., other symptoms, stressors, interventions), or make general comments about the day.

Accelerometer

As mentioned above, accelerometer data were only available for 12 out of 16 participants (one participant [6.3%] opted out of wearing the accelerometer, and 3 accelerometers [18.75%] were lost/thrown out resulting in complete loss of the data). Of those, all 12 (100%) had the accelerometer on for over 10 hours for at least one 1 day, and the average number of days with more than 10 hours of accelerometer wearing was 10.2. Ten participants (83.3%) wore the accelerometer on for at least 10 hours for at least 7 days.

Accelerometer adherence was also considered by the proportion of time (hours) the accelerometer was worn out of the number of hours the participant reported being awake (although participants were only asked to wear the accelerometer during the day, 2 of them kept the accelerometer on 24 h a day [i.e., during sleep and wakeful time] for several days) that day. Six participants (50%) wore the accelerometer 80% to 100% of their wakeful time, and 11 (91.7%) wore the accelerometer >50% of their wakeful time. Average proportion of wakeful time with accelerometer on was 68.8% (SD = 25.7%).

Salivary Cortisol

Adherence with saliva sampling computed as the number of samples provided out of the total number of samples expected (3 families opted out of saliva collection, leaving 13 participants total) was 67.3%, yet excluding samples collected with violations of the protocol (after eating or at a wrong time) reduced this number to 34.9%.

Of the samples returned to the research team, 27.7% were dry (i.e., there was no saliva to be extracted from the swab), but they were not equally distributed among participants: 6 participants had 0% of their samples dry, while 2 participants had >80% of their samples dry. Of the nondry samples, 77.7% were at least 0.1 mL.

Out of 8 possible days of data collection, less than half of the participants (n = 7, 53.8%) provided 2 valid morning samples for at least 1 day, with the second morning sample being the most problematic. Collecting saliva in the evening appeared to be less challenging, with 13 participants (100%) collecting this sample at least once. For more details about saliva sample collection, see “Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JDBP/A469”.

Adverse Events

No significant adverse events were noted. Four participants reported emotional distress in response to study procedures, though none required further debriefing or follow-up. Three indicated that the EMA served to draw their attention to upsetting cognitions/sensations, and 1 indicated that tracking their experience with EMA highlighted distressing trends in their experience (e.g., more extreme responses).

Technical Issues

Logs kept by the research assistant team were summarized for technical challenges and issues. For each participant that successfully completed the study, there was ∼4 hours of research assistant work required in study administration, including set-up, mailing, checking in with families, and uploading and processing data. This does not account for missed contact attempts, time spent making the necessary adjustments if the family needed to postpone the study, recruitment efforts where the family opted not to participate, or management of other unexpected logistical issues. See “Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JDBP/A469” for details.

Family Perspectives

Quantitative ratings (all ratings were provided verbally as part of the interview and rated on a 0–10 Likert scale; higher scores indicated greater satisfaction, burden, interference, willingness to take part again, and acceptability) provided by families of their experience indicated generally high satisfaction (M = 7.4, SD = 2.2, range = 3–10), willingness to participate again (M = 7.4, SD = 2.8, range = 0–10), and acceptability (M = 8.3, SD = 1.9, range = 5–10). Family burden (M = 4.9, SD = 2.5, range = 1–8.5) and interference (M = 4.0, SD = 2.4, range = 0–8) were rated as moderate. Families were roughly equally divided on whether the protocol could be optimized by introducing breaks in data collection, changing study duration, and focusing the EMA prompts and questions. DISCUSSION

This feasibility study aimed to evaluate the use of multimodal micro-longitudinal data collection method in families with developmental, medical, and psychiatric complexity across 2 weeks. The 3 modalities chosen were done so to represent both passive (accelerometry) and active (EMA, saliva) methods of data collection, as well as to incorporate both subjective (EMA) and objective measurements (accelerometry, saliva).

Adherence was highest for accelerometry, lowest for saliva samples, and lower than benchmarked for the EMA. While some families opted out of the accelerometer, most wore it for waking hours for at least half of the study duration, and the benchmarks were met. Saliva was inherently the least flexible part of the protocol due to the relatively strict timing of the sample collection and constraints (e.g., on eating) to capture the diurnal pattern and collect enough saliva.13 This was acknowledged by many families as being significantly challenging to incorporate in their daily routine. Although adherence with the EMA was lower than benchmarked, and backfilling appeared frequently (on about 11% of the answered prompts), still three-quarters of the study days had at least 1 EMA response. It is challenging to compare to published rates of adherence as many studies exclude participants with missing data.14,15 Adherence was highest in the first week and decreased in the second week. It is not clear whether having saliva collection stop after day 8 affected adherence with the other study components.

No significant adverse events were reported, though some participants mentioned mild emotional distress, consistent with findings in adult populations with multiple chronic conditions.22 Some negative aspects of the study may have been magnified with repeated exposure. Despite this, families reported generally high satisfaction, willingness to participate again, and acceptability, with moderate burden and interference, similarly to previous research of adolescents with chronic health conditions.23

An unexpected finding from the qualitative interviews was that families referenced lower motivation to participate because it was a feasibility study, i.e., not “real” research. This highlighted the importance of framing the feasibility study so families could understand the potential value to their community. Considering incentives is also key, in addition to remuneration, we offered families a visualized copy of their data at the end of the study, in line with previous requests from families to have a more reciprocal relationship with the research team.1 Having data visualization embedded within the project so families can track progress or increase their understanding of their experiences day-to-day may have been more appealing.11 This finding highlights the importance of engaging with communities to determine their priorities for research.24

Our sample included youth with a spectrum of health conditions, and qualitative interviews yielded some information about specific symptoms or levels of functioning that affected participation. For example, some families described both challenges and benefits to having a more rigid cognitive style to study procedure adherence, and while some youth had higher care needs, they also may have benefitted from more parental support in adhering to the protocol. We were able to collect rich data from families on their experience to inform future adaptation; however, the small sample size should be considered in the context of the diverse range of health conditions, each with their own barriers to participation and study-related accommodation needs. Future research may consider including measures of potentially relevant variables (e.g., communication level, motor function, executive function, perfectionism, trauma history) to further understand which families experience emotional distress in response to EMA completion, barriers/facilitators to participation, and whether there are systematic differences between families with more versus less complete data. Further research may also examine potential benefits of integrating just-in-time interventions into EMA data collection and balancing of positively and negatively worded questions to further mitigate iatrogenic distress.

Our findings have practical implications (Table 2) for future research, understanding and supporting participation in in-home child developmental health research. Feedback from families regarding the EMA wording raised questions regarding how young people with different levels of cognition and developmental function use self-report questionnaires. While EMA offers advances over traditional questionnaires, many challenges with self-report are still embedded within questions that could prompt extreme or neutral response bias or social desirability bias that could potentially be altered with repeated exposure. Even among neurotypical adults, methodological questions have been raised regarding how respondents interpret EMA questions, whether it indeed captures momentary experiences, and the importance of EMA-specific training.25

Recommendations for Researchers

Single time-point questionnaires are subject to memory biases, including the recency bias (i.e., favoring memories of recent events) and negativity bias (i.e., experiencing negative events as more salient). Some data suggest that the use of an EMA mitigates this bias;27 however, concerns have been raised regarding the coverage model of EMA, where the same biases may be present, just on a smaller scale.25

EMA, ecological momentary assessment.

Ultimately, the implementation of a real-world approach to in-home data collection will be project-specific. EMA design will vary depending on whether the research question prioritizes specific times or sequence of events. Saliva collection, the most time constrained aspect of this study, was determined by the need to account for diurnal cortisol patterns.13 The use of accelerometry will vary depending on whether researchers are studying physical activity or sleep. Data collection time, staffing needs, and budget allocation will differ from typical lab-based studies. Notably, the time investment of the research assistant in this study challenged the notion that data collection is merely “set it and forget it,” and that frequent communication with the family to support participant, problem-solve technical issues, and coordination with courier services to transport materials is needed.

In conclusion, the LLAH offers a promising opportunity to increase the accessibility and ecological validity of child developmental health research. While these methods are well-validated and widely used in healthy, neurotypical adult populations, the developmental needs and priorities/concerns of families living with chronic conditions necessitate thoughtful and separate study.28 This study illustrates the importance of conducting initial engagement and codesign with patients and families, which has been mirrored in reviews finding benefits to research relevance, feasibility, accessibility, and empowering families.29 It also illustrates the benefit of implementing a pilot feasibility stage to assess how family-driven modifications function and are experienced in the “real world,” followed by further iteration, and researchers may benefit from reviewing the literature on implementation science to inform their approach.30 This approach offers great potential to meet the needs of traditionally underrepresented families, while balancing researcher and family resources.
