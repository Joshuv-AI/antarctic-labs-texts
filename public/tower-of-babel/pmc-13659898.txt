Cerebellar Ataxia, Neuropathy, and Vestibular Areflexia Syndrome (CANVAS): A case report of RFC1-associated disease
Authors: Dhanush Amin, Raman Deep, Vidhi Dhaduk, Riyaz Ahmed FK, Jay Kakadiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebellar ataxia, neuropathy, and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) is a late-onset neurodegenerative disorder associated with biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions. We report a 55-year-old woman with progressive imbalance, sensory neuronopathy, bilateral vestibular hypofunction, chronic cough, and orthostatic symptoms. MRI demonstrated cerebellar atrophy involving the vermis, particularly the posterior vermis, with additional involvement of the posterosuperior cerebellar hemispheres, including Crus I, and upper cervical spinal cord atrophy. Integration of the characteristic imaging pattern with the clinical, electrophysiologic, and vestibular findings raised suspicion for RFC1-associated disease and prompted targeted genetic testing, which demonstrated biallelic pathogenic AAGGG repeat expansions in RFC1. This case emphasizes the educational value of recognizing the characteristic neuroradiologic pattern of RFC1-associated CANVAS in patients with adult-onset progressive ataxia and its role in guiding appropriate targeted genetic evaluation.Keywords: CANVAS, RFC1, Sensory neuronopathy, Cerebellar atrophy, Case report

Introduction

Cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy, and vestibular areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) is a late-onset neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the classic triad of progressive cerebellar ataxia, sensory neuronopathy, and bilateral vestibular hypofunction [1]. CANVAS is an increasingly recognized genetic disorder associated with biallelic repeat expansions in the RFC1 gene [2]. The prevalence of biallelic pathogenic RFC1 expansions is estimated to range from 1 in 20,000 to 1 in 625 at birth, depending on population ancestry and assumptions regarding allele frequency [3].

CANVAS is characterized by an insidious onset and sequential evolution of symptoms over several years [4]. The initial presentation may include isolated ataxia, sensory disturbances, or vestibular symptoms, with the complete triad developing over time. Routine laboratory investigations may be unrevealing, and motor function is typically preserved, which can further complicate early recognition [2]. In addition to the classic triad, chronic nonproductive cough [5] and autonomic symptoms are increasingly recognized as part of the phenotypic spectrum [6]. The characteristic neuroimaging pattern includes cerebellar atrophy with preferential involvement of the posterior vermis, particularly the declive, folium, and tuber, and the posterosuperior cerebellar hemispheres, particularly Crus I [2,7,8].

Despite growing awareness, CANVAS remains underrecognized, particularly in settings with limited access to specialized genetic testing. Recognition of the characteristic imaging pattern in the appropriate clinical setting may prompt targeted RFC1 testing, reduce unnecessary diagnostic investigations, and facilitate genetic counselling and multidisciplinary symptomatic management. We present a genetically confirmed case of RFC1-associated CANVAS to highlight the characteristic neuroradiologic pattern and its integration with clinical, electrophysiologic, and vestibular findings in the diagnostic evaluation of adult-onset ataxia.

Case presentation

A 55-year-old right-handed woman with no significant prior neurological history presented with a 3-year history of progressive imbalance and unsteady gait. Her symptoms were initially intermittent but gradually worsened, resulting in frequent near-falls and difficulty ambulating in low-light conditions. She described her gait as resembling “walking on a boat.” During the preceding year, she developed bilateral distal lower-extremity numbness, worsening hand clumsiness, and oscillopsia characterized by visual instability during head movement. She denied headache, diplopia, bowel or bladder dysfunction, focal weakness, or acute neurological events. Additional symptoms included intermittent dysphagia, chronic nonproductive cough, and episodic light-headedness on standing, which she had previously attributed to dehydration. Her symptoms had been evaluated independently by multiple specialists without a unifying diagnosis. There was no family history of neurodegenerative disease, and her medication history was non-contributory.

Neurological examination demonstrated a wide-based ataxic gait, impaired tandem walking, and postural instability. Mild bilateral upper-extremity dysmetria was noted. Sensory examination demonstrated reduced vibration and proprioception in the lower extremities. Cranial nerve examination was otherwise unremarkable except for impaired vestibulo-ocular reflexes on bilateral rapid head impulse testing. Motor strength was preserved.

Laboratory evaluation for acquired causes of adult-onset ataxia and sensory neuropathy was unrevealing. Vitamin B12, folate, vitamin E, HbA1c, and thyroid-stimulating hormone levels were within reference ranges. Serum protein electrophoresis and serum free light-chain analysis demonstrated no evidence of a monoclonal gammopathy. Autoimmune and paraneoplastic evaluation, including antinuclear antibodies, anti-SSA/SSB antibodies, and anti-Hu, anti-Yo, and anti-Ri antibodies, was negative. Serologic testing for HIV, syphilis, and Lyme disease was also negative.

Electrodiagnostic studies demonstrated a severe non-length-dependent sensory neuronopathy, with markedly reduced or absent sensory nerve action potentials involving the sural, superficial peroneal, median, and ulnar nerves. Motor responses in the peroneal, tibial, median, and ulnar nerves were preserved, with normal or minimally reduced conduction velocities and no conduction block or temporal dispersion. Needle electromyography showed no evidence of active denervation. These findings supported a sensory neuronopathy (ganglionopathy) with motor sparing.

Vestibular testing confirmed bilateral peripheral vestibular hypofunction. Video head impulse testing demonstrated reduced vestibulo-ocular reflex gains involving the horizontal and vertical semicircular canals, with bilateral overt and covert corrective saccades. Bithermal caloric testing demonstrated markedly reduced bilateral vestibular responses, and rotational chair testing demonstrated reduced vestibulo-ocular reflex gain across tested frequencies with abnormal phase responses. These findings were concordant with the patient's oscillopsia and abnormal bilateral bedside head impulse test.

MRI of the brain (Fig. 1) demonstrated cerebellar atrophy with predominant involvement of the vermis, including the culmen, declive, and pyramis, with additional involvement of the posterosuperior cerebellar hemispheres, particularly Crus I. Upper cervical spinal cord atrophy was also present. There was no significant pontine or other brainstem atrophy, putaminal atrophy, or hot-cross-bun sign. No intracranial mass, acute infarction, or abnormal enhancement was identified. Mild nonspecific chronic white matter changes were stable compared with prior imaging. The characteristic distribution of cerebellar atrophy, together with sensory neuronopathy and bilateral vestibular hypofunction, raised suspicion for RFC1-associated disease and prompted targeted genetic testing. Genetic analysis using flanking PCR and repeat-primed PCR (RP-PCR) demonstrated biallelic pathogenic AAGGG repeat expansions in RFC1, confirming the diagnosis of CANVAS.

Axial T2-weighted (A) and postcontrast T1-weighted (B) images demonstrate bilateral cerebellar atrophy predominantly involving the posterosuperior lobes, including Crus I (red arrows). Sagittal T1-weighted image (C) demonstrates vermian atrophy involving the culmen (thick long white arrow), declive (green arrow), and pyramis (thin short white arrow), with mild associated upper cervical spinal cord atrophy (yellow arrow).

The patient subsequently underwent vestibular and gait rehabilitation emphasizing balance training and gaze-stabilization exercises. Neuropathic symptoms were managed with gabapentin. Formal swallowing evaluation resulted in dietary modifications and compensatory strategies, while orthostatic symptoms were managed with optimization of hydration and compression garments. At 12-month follow-up, the patient reported improvement in balance with continued rehabilitation, while distal sensory deficits and oscillopsia persisted without new neurological deficits. No standardized quantitative ataxia or functional scale was obtained during follow-up. The patient remained adherent to the rehabilitation program and tolerated the prescribed symptomatic therapies without reported adverse effects. Follow-up brain MRI demonstrated stable cerebellar atrophy without appreciable interval progression.

Discussion

This case illustrates a characteristic presentation of CANVAS, a neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic RFC1 repeat expansions. The identification of the causative genetic alteration has established CANVAS as one of the most common identifiable etiologies of previously idiopathic adult-onset ataxia. Despite this, diagnosis is often delayed because of its insidious progression, sequential evolution of symptoms, and overlap with other neurologic disorders. RFC1 repeat expansions account for approximately 15–25% of late-onset idiopathic ataxia cases, with detection rates reaching up to 90% among patients with the full CANVAS phenotype [2,9]. The typical onset in the fifth to sixth decades of life and gradual evolution of symptoms, which frequently delays recognition of the syndrome, are consistent with the clinical course in our patient [10].

CANVAS is characterized by combined degeneration of cerebellar Purkinje cells, dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons, and vestibular ganglia, resulting in the characteristic convergence of cerebellar, sensory, and vestibular dysfunction [11,12]. Unlike many peripheral neuropathies, CANVAS is characterized by sensory neuronopathy with relative preservation of motor function, as observed in this patient. Chronic cough may precede neurologic symptoms by years or even decades and is increasingly recognized as an important early clinical clue [5,6,13]. Autonomic features, including orthostatic hypotension and gastrointestinal dysmotility, further expand the phenotypic spectrum of RFC1-associated disease [10]. The presence of chronic cough and orthostatic symptoms in this case further supported the multisystem nature of the disorder.

Cerebellar atrophy is the most consistent neuroimaging finding in CANVAS, involving the cerebellar vermis in approximately 87% and the cerebellar hemispheres in approximately 70% of genetically confirmed cases [7]. A characteristic distribution is preferential involvement of the posterior vermis, particularly the declive, folium, and tuber, together with the posterosuperior cerebellar hemispheres, particularly Crus I [2,8]. Brainstem atrophy, primarily affecting the pons and midbrain, is seen in approximately 13% of cases and is associated with longer disease duration and worsening eye-movement abnormalities [2,7]. The cerebellar atrophy pattern may become more diffuse at later disease stages while maintaining vermian predominance [14]. In our patient, cerebellar atrophy involved the culmen (anterior vermis), declive and pyramis (posterior vermis), with additional involvement of Crus I. This pattern is compatible with advanced RFC1-associated disease. The involvement of the posterior vermis and Crus I was particularly relevant because, in the appropriate clinical context, this distribution provides an imaging clue to RFC1-associated disease. The principal radiologic differential diagnoses include MSA-C, alcohol-related cerebellar degeneration, and age-related cerebellar atrophy.

In MSA-C, cerebellar atrophy is typically accompanied by more prominent pontine atrophy and middle cerebellar peduncle involvement, while the hot-cross-bun sign and putaminal abnormalities may provide additional diagnostic clues. In contrast, these imaging features are not characteristic of CANVAS.² In our patient, the absence of significant pontine atrophy, middle cerebellar peduncle involvement, putaminal atrophy, or a hot-cross-bun sign, together with the characteristic cerebellar distribution, sensory neuronopathy, and bilateral vestibular hypofunction, favored CANVAS over MSA-C.

Alcohol-related cerebellar degeneration typically demonstrates preferential anterior vermian atrophy, including involvement of the culmen [15]. Although the culmen was involved in our patient, concurrent posterior vermian and Crus I involvement, together with the sensory neuronopathy and bilateral vestibular hypofunction, favored CANVAS. Additionally, the patient had no history of chronic alcohol use.

Age-related cerebellar volume loss may also involve the vermis but is generally mild to moderate and may be clinically silent. In contrast, CANVAS produces symptomatic ataxia [16]. Age-related cerebellar volume remains relatively stable until approximately age 50 and subsequently declines, whereas CANVAS typically presents in the fifth to sixth decades with progressive neurologic symptoms [17]. Therefore, disproportionate posterior vermian and Crus I atrophy in a symptomatic patient, particularly when accompanied by sensory or vestibular abnormalities, should not be attributed to aging alone.

Spinal cord abnormalities may provide an additional imaging clue. Upper cervical cord atrophy and posterior column T2-weighted signal abnormalities have been described in RFC1-associated disease, reflecting the underlying dorsal root ganglionopathy and sensory neuronopathy. The upper cervical spinal cord demonstrates progressive reduction in cross-sectional area that can be quantified on follow-up imaging [2,18]. Upper cervical cord atrophy was present in our patient and provided an additional supportive imaging feature. Supratentorial abnormalities are less characteristic; nonspecific abnormalities or cerebral atrophy have been reported in 37–42% of RFC1-positive patients [7]. Diffuse cortical atrophy has also been described but is not considered a characteristic imaging feature and may reflect age-related or concurrent pathology [9]. Our patient demonstrated only mild nonspecific chronic white matter changes with mild cerebral atrophy.

From a neuroradiologic perspective, recognition of disproportionate posterior vermian and posterosuperior hemispheric atrophy, particularly involving Crus I, should prompt consideration of RFC1-associated disease in patients with otherwise unexplained adult-onset progressive ataxia [8]. Suspicion should be further increased when imaging findings are accompanied by sensory neuronopathy, bilateral vestibular hypofunction or oscillopsia, chronic unexplained cough, or autonomic symptoms. Because the components of CANVAS may develop sequentially, the complete clinical triad need not necessarily be present when the characteristic imaging pattern is first recognized [8].

Definitive diagnosis relies on the identification of biallelic pathogenic RFC1 repeat expansions. The role of imaging is therefore not to establish the diagnosis in isolation, but to recognize a characteristic pattern that, when integrated with clinical, electrophysiologic, and vestibular findings, can appropriately prompt targeted genetic testing. Establishing the diagnosis can reduce unnecessary investigations, support genetic counselling, and guide multidisciplinary symptomatic management [19]. No disease-modifying therapy is currently available; therefore, early recognition should not be interpreted as altering disease progression. Rather, its principal value lies in diagnostic clarification and appropriate supportive management. Accordingly, the educational value of this case lies in demonstrating how recognition of the characteristic neuroradiologic pattern can contribute to the diagnostic pathway in RFC1-associated CANVAS.

Conclusion

This case highlights the importance of recognizing the characteristic neuroradiologic pattern of RFC1-associated CANVAS in patients with progressive adult-onset ataxia. Posterior vermian and posterosuperior cerebellar atrophy, particularly involving Crus I, should raise suspicion for CANVAS when accompanied by sensory neuronopathy and bilateral vestibular hypofunction. Recognition of this clinicoradiologic pattern can prompt targeted RFC1 testing, reduce unnecessary investigations, support genetic counselling, and guide multidisciplinary symptomatic management.
