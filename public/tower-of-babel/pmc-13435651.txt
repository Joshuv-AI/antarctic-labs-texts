Response to comments on AI cephalometric landmarking study
Authors: Huayu Ye, Yongchao Gou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This letter responds to the insightful concerns raised regarding our study on AI versus expert identification of cephalometric landmarks (Is automatic cephalometric software using artificial intelligence better than orthodontist experts in landmark identification? BMC Oral Health. 2023 Jul 8;23(1):467. doi: 10.1186/s12903-023-03188-4). We appreciate their constructive feedback and address the central criticisms below, specifically regarding image resolution, reproducibility, the manual-AI comparison, and data transparency.

Image standardization and resolution

We agree that differences in image export capabilities across platforms, particularly MyOrthoX’s reliance on screenshots versus direct exports from Angelalign and Digident, which may represent a methodological limitation. In our study, all images imported into ImageJ were calibrated using embedded scale markers to ensure consistent pixel-to-mm conversion. This process was explicitly documented in Methods, where we noted that MyOrthoX images were obtained through screen capture at 100% zoom to minimize interpolation. While we attempted to control subtle resolution inconsistencies for the calibration, we agree with the critique that such differences could influence landmarking accuracy, a point we should have discussed more explicitly in the original manuscript.

Reproducibility and error rate discrepancies

We do not dismiss the replication attempt, but we disagree that its findings invalidate our original results. Several critical factors explain the discrepancies:Sample heterogeneity: Our original 43 images were drawn from a single clinical center (2018–2022), acquired under standardized protocols. The critique’s use of 49 images from “multiple centers and public datasets” introduces greater anatomical and technical variability.Exclusion criteria: The removal of 6 images due to “failure in scale calibration” may have selectively excluded more challenging cases, potentially skewing error distributions.Software versioning: Commercial AI platforms are frequently updated. Performance may have changed between our data collection and the replication.

While the statistical method used was appropriate, a direct comparison is inherently limited without an identical image set.

Manual vs. automated landmark comparison

Although manual placements served as the reference standard for calculating AI errors, we did not visually or statistically juxtapose manual and AI landmark positions in the main manuscript. We regret this omission and will incorporate direct visual/statistical comparisons in all future publications.

However, the landmarks you mentioned—Sn, Ls, Li, Si, and Pog’—are all soft tissue points.

The primary reason for their significant errors lies not in flaws of the software itself but in their inherent ambiguity. Unlike bony landmarks with clear boundaries, these soft tissue points lack well-defined anatomical borders and are highly susceptible to the patient’s lip posture and head positioning during imaging. As a result, AI struggles to learn stable and precise localization rules for these landmarks.

Data source and transparency

We thank this critical comment and agree that detailed methodological reporting is essential for reproducibility and scientific rigor. The 43 lateral cephalograms were obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University (2022-077), with patient consent for research use. All images were collected and saved in uncompressed JPG format. No contrast enhancement, sharpening, or other filters were applied to the raw images to ensure the anatomical points were identified without algorithmic bias. To ensure consistency and efficiency, we adhered to a pre-defined, systematic landmarking sequence (Fig. 1).

Additional functionalities not included in the comparison

Our study specifically focused on the accuracy of cephalometric landmark identification and did not evaluate other software features, such as user interface, workflow integration, or auxiliary diagnostic tools. We acknowledge that a more comprehensive evaluation could be valuable in future work.

Accuracy of commercial software versus reported results

As a commercially available AI solution, these software’s accuracy has been validated for clinical adoption. The reported results for MyOrthoX, which starkly contrast with this established market reality, suggest a potential misalignment in the study’s evaluation methodology or dataset. We agree that discrepancies in image resolution handling, particularly MyOrthoX’s inability to process images at original resolution, may have unduly penalized its performance. Therefore, the reported accuracy for MyOrthoX may not reflect its true capability under standardized conditions, and the conclusion that its accuracy is unacceptably low warrants re-evaluation.

Conclusion

We thank the authors for their rigorous critique and replication effort. We welcome collaboration on a multi-center, pre-registered benchmarking study to establish robust, generalizable performance metrics for clinical AI tools. Science progresses through scrutiny. We are grateful to be part of this essential process.
