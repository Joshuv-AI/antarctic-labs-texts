Associations of TyG index with coronary heart disease risk and coronary artery sclerosis severity in OSA
Authors: Xuan Qiu, Gulimire Aimaiti, Yulan Chen, Yu Li, Xiaojing Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is involved in the pathogenesis and progression of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and coronary heart disease (CHD). The triglyceride glucose(TyG) index, an alternative indicator of IR with high reliability, is linked to CHD development and severity. However, the association of TyG index with CHD in OSA cases remains undefined. This study explores the association of TyG index with CHD risk and coronary artery sclerosis severity in the OSA population.MethodsOSA cases with suspected CHD, enrolled at the Department of Hypertension, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between February 2020 and June 2024, were assigned to the OSA and OSA + CHD groups, followed by TyG index assessment. The t-test, Mann-Whitney U-test and one-way analysis of variance were utilized to compare continuous data, while comparisons of categorical data utilized the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine factors independently predicting OSA with CHD and Gensini scores. Restrictive cubic spline (RCS) was used to assess potential non-linear associations of TyG index with CHD risk and Gensini score in OSA patients.ResultsTotally 1059 OSA patients were included, with 514 diagnosed with CHD (48.54%). Multivariable logistic regression analysis upon adjustment for age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking history revealed TyG index as a risk factor for CHD in OSA cases. CHD risk in the high-TyG index group was 1.977 fold higher versus the low-TyG index group (OR = 1.977, 95% CI 1.424-2.800, P < 0.001). In addition, TyG index had a linear relationship with CHD (P for nonlinearity = 0.0709). In moderate to severe OSA cases, the high-TyG index group had a significantly higher CHD risk (OR = 2.430, 95% CI 1.601–3.690, P < 0.001). Gensini score and TyG index (P for non-linearity = 0.0033) had a non-linear relationship, while high TyG index was a risk factor for high Gensini score.ConclusionTyG index is a risk factor for CHD in OSA cases and reflects the severity of coronary atherosclerosis. TyG, a surrogate indicator for IR evaluation, may help predict CHD in OSA cases, especially in moderate to severe OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) features the repeated collapse of the upper airway during sleep, causing respiratory pauses and leading to hypoxemia and fragmented sleep [1]. OSA represents a commonly encountered sleep disorder with an increasing incidence, especially in obese populations. Research has shown individuals with sleep apnea are at markedly higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD) [2], which has become a major cause of death in OSA cases. Although the underpinning mechanism remains obscure, increasing evidence suggests insulin resistance (IR) plays an essential role in this process.

IR refers to the weakened physiological function of insulin in humans, reducing insulin’s capability of utilizing glucose, as a significant feature of metabolic syndrome [3]. Intermittent hypoxia (IH) caused by OSA leads to insulin resistance through pathways such as overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system, enhanced liver glucose synthesis, and promotion of adipose tissue inflammation. At the same time, IR also induces atherosclerosis, heart failure, and other diseases by increasing vascular stiffness, affecting myocardial energy and fat metabolism [4, 5]. IR and cardiovascular disease mutually reinforce each other, forming a vicious cycle.

The hyperinsulinemic eugenic clamp technique (HIET) is considered the gold standard for IR evaluation but has complex and time-consuming operation, which limits its widespread use in clinic [6]. At present, the triglyceride glucose (TyG) index is considered an alternative indicator of IR [7] and is associated with cardiometabolic disorders such as heart failure(HF), atrial fibrillation(AF), and diabetes [8–10]. Previously reported findings reveal the TyG index as an effective indicator for OSA diagnosis and prognosis [11]. Indeed, a high TyG index independently predicts OSA [12]. In addition, the TyG index has positive correlations with disease severity and poor prognosis in coronary heart disease (CHD) [13, 14]. However, the association of the TyG index with CHD risk in OSA cases remains undefined. Therefore, the current work aimed to explore the associations of the TyG index with CHD risk in OSA cases. We look forward to developing more effective screening and intervention approaches for OSA cases in the future, reducing cardiovascular risk and improving their quality of life.

Methods

Participants and study design

Totally 1059 OSA cases with suspected CHD were enrolled at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University from February 2020 to June 2024 (Fig. 1). All patients underwent coronary angiography or coronary CT imaging and were assigned to the OSA (n = 545) and OSA + CHD (n = 514) groups. They included 895 men and 164 women.

Fig. 1Flowchart of subject selection in the study

Flowchart of subject selection in the study

Inclusion criteria were: ① 18 to 75 years old; ② CHD diagnosis per the “Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Stable Coronary Heart Disease“ [15]; ③ OSA diagnosis is based on the “American Academy of Sleep Medicine Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis of Obstructive Sleep Apnea in Adults“ [16].

Exclusion criteria were: ① Central or mixed sleep apnea; ② unstable lung pathologies, e.g., bronchial asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, pulmonary heart disease, and pulmonary arterial hypertension; ③ congenital heart disease and severe heart failure (heart function level III or IV); ④ concurrent endocrine system diseases, e.g., hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, and acromegaly; ⑤ severe liver or kidney dysfunction, chronic wasting diseases, and malignant tumors; ⑥ concurrent mental illnesses and current use of sedatives and hypnotic drugs. Each patient provided signed informed consent.

Clinical data

①Clinical data mainly included patient gender, age, medical history, snoring condition, height, and weight. Body mass index (BMI) was derived as weight/height² (kg/m²). ② Fasting blood specimens were obtained in the morning following an 8-hour fast. The Roche C8000 biochemical analyzer was utilized to analyze blood samples, measuring alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), serum creatinine (Scr), uric acid (UA), fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels. ③ A Welch Allyn 6100 dynamic blood pressure monitor was used to perform 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure measurements. The 24-h mean systolic (24hSBP) and diastolic (24hDBP) blood pressure levels were recorded.

TyG index calculation

TyG = Ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2]; TG:1 mmol/L = 88.5 mg/dL, FPG:1 mmol/L = 18 mg/dL.

Coronary angiography (CAG)

Coronary angiography was performed by cardiovascular specialists. Preoperative procedures included routine electrocardiography, blood pressure measurement, and oxygen monitoring. A GE Innova 2100 digital subtraction angiography machine was employed. The Seldinger puncture technique was applied, with a specific cardiac catheter introduced via the radial or femoral artery into the openings of the left and right coronary arteries. A contrast medium was injected, and selective left or right CAG was carried out using the standard Judkins technique with many positions and angles. The results of coronary angiography were analyzed by two or more cardiovascular specialists. The Gensini score was utilized to determine the severity of coronary artery lesions [17]: The score for each vessel stenosis degree was multiplied by the score of the lesion location to obtain a lesion score for each vessel. The total score for all lesions constitutes the patient’s overall coronary score.

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA)

Image acquisition utilized a Siemens dual-source CT (Somatom Definition Flash, Siemens) from Germany. Patients positioned supine were scanned from 1 cm below the tracheal carina to the level of the cardiac diaphragm. An enhanced scan employed a two-phase injection technique: during the first phase, 60–80 mL of non-ionic contrast agent was administered by injection into the median cubital vein at 3.5–5.0 mL/s; during the second phase, 30 mL of saline was administered by injection at the same flow rate. The voltage and current were 120 kV and 380–410 mA, respectively. The data were imported into the workstation, and corresponding volumetric data were calculated to obtain three-dimensional images of coronary arteries and observe stenotic lesions. The diagnosis was independently completed by two experienced radiologists. The Gensini scoring system was used to assess CCTA results [18].

Polysomnography (PSG)

A Compumedics polysomnography device from Australia was used to conduct a 7-h overnight PSG. Simultaneous monitoring included blood oxygen saturation, pulse, respiratory rate, snoring, and nasal airflow. Pre-monitoring education on precautions was provided to all patients. Post-monitoring, data analysis was performed with the Remlogic software, with the sleep report interpreted by a professionally trained physician and reviewed by a senior physician. Collected monitoring data included key indicators such as the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), mean arterial oxygen saturation (MSaO2), and lowest arterial oxygen saturation (LSaO2).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS 25.0 and the R 4.3.2 software. Data normality assessment for continuous variables employed the Shapiro-Wilk test, with M (P25, P75) representing non-normally distributed data. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for inter-group comparisons. Count data were expressed in frequency (%) and compared between groups by the chi-square test. Unconditional multivariable logistic regression analysis was carried out for variables showing statistical significance in univariable logistic regression analysis, to screen for risk factors for OSA combined with coronary heart disease. Results were presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (95%CI). Restricted cubic spline was employed to assess the association of the TyG index with CHD risk in OSA patients and the potential non-linear relationship linking the TyG index and Gensini score. P < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

OSA cases with CHD had more men, older people, more diabetics, and more smokers compared with the non-CHD group. In addition, patients with concomitant coronary heart disease had higher AHI, FBG, TG, and TyG index, and reduced MSaO2, LSaO2, and HDL-C versus the non-CHD group (all P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Logistic analyses of CHD in OSA patients by TyG index

TyG index used as a continuous variable was a risk factor for CHD in OSA patients (OR = 2.011, 95%CI: 1.597–2.532, P < 0.001). Association of TyG index with CHD risk based on OSA severity

In OSA patients with AHI < 15, the TyG index had no correlation with CHD risk (OR = 1.266, 95%CI: 0.581–2.758; P = 0.553). In OSA cases with AHI ≥ 15, a high TyG index still independently predicted CHD (OR = 2.430, 95%CI: 1.601–3.690; P < 0.001) even upon adjustment for gender, age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and smoking history. Discussion

Our research revealed that the TyG index is a risk factor for CHD in OSA patients, especially in moderate-to-severe OSA, and the TyG index is positively related to the severity of coronary artery sclerosis. These results suggest that the TyG index may be a promising indicator for screening of CHD in the OSA population.

OSA represents a commonly diagnosed sleep-breathing disease with intermittent hypoxia and close associations with various cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases such as coronary heart disease [1]. CHD incidence in OSA patients is as high as 20–30% [19]. Compared with individuals with only CHD, CHD cases accompanied by OSA have higher odds of myocardial infarction at night [20]. Individuals with ST-segment elevation acute myocardial infarction accompanied by OSA have a low 18-month event-free survival rate [21]. Therefore, early diagnosis and prevention of concurrent OSA and CHD are of great significance. OSA and CHD have many common pathogenic mechanisms, among which IR is considered one of the potential common mechanisms.

IR is a systemic disease that affects multiple organs and plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of OSA and CHD. Acute or chronic intermittent hypoxia exposure induces insulin resistance by causing oxidative stress, pancreatic beta cell dysfunction, glucose and lipid metabolism disorders, and systemic chronic inflammatory reactions [22]. IR is also involved in CHD pathogenesis and progression. It can not only directly cause vascular endothelial cell injury but also induce vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and enhance atherosclerosis, ultimately leading to coronary artery stenosis by triggering hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, etc [23]. As an alternative indicator of IR with high reliability, the TyG index has received increasing attention for its relationship with cardiovascular disease. Numerous findings indicate that the TyG index independently predicts CHD. An observational study of 11,937 participants reported high TyG index is positively correlated with CHD risk in the subjects [24]. Liang et al. demonstrated TyG index is significantly correlated with CHD risk and prognosis, as well as the severity of coronary artery lesions [25]. A prospective cohort study examining 1618 participants demonstrated TyG index is an effective predictive factor of high mortality in critically ill coronary heart disease cases [26]. The above reports revealed the relationship between TyG index and coronary heart disease but focused on the general population and did not pay attention to OSA cases, a special population with a high incidence of IR resistance. Previous evidence suggests TyG index has good diagnostic value in OSA [27]. A high TyG index is closely correlated with OSA occurrence and severity [12]. Given that OSA elevates the odds of CHD and the TyG index could predict both OSA and CHD, this study explores the association of the TyG index with CHD risk in OSA patients.

Baseline data analysis in the present study demonstrated that versus the non-CHD group, male proportion, age, diabetes history, smoking ratio, TG, FBG, AHI, TyG index and Gensini score in the CHD group were higher, while HDL-C, MSaO2, and LSaO2 were lower, indicating an association of OSA accompanied by CHD with IR. Multivariable analysis revealed TyG index, male, age, and AHI as risk factors for OSA combined with CHD. Framingham and colleagues confirmed male, age, hypertension, and diabetes as traditional risk factors for coronary heart disease [28], and these factors also increase the risk of OSA patients [29]. The current study corroborates these reports. The TyG index represents a non-traditional risk factor for OSA combined with CHD. For each 1 unit increase in the TyG index, CHD risk in OSA patients increased by 66.5%, indicating TyG index might be effective for screening CHD in OSA patients.

Based on the TyG index as a risk factor for CHD in OSA cases, we grouped patients according to the third quartile of the TyG index to further explore the association of the TyG index with CHD risk in OSA patients. Baseline data showed that high TyG levels were associated with higher ALT, AST, UA, TC, TG, LDL-C, FBG, and AHI levels and reduced HDL-C, MaSO2, and LaSO2 levels, reflecting an association of TyG index with metabolic disorders. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that upon adjustment for age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, smoking history, and other confounders, the TyG index was still associated with CHD in OSA cases, indicating that the TyG index is a risk factor for CHD in OSA patients. Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a linear correlation between the TyG index and CHD risk, i.e., the higher the TyG index, the higher the risk of concurrent CHD. As shown above, CHD risk in the high-TyG index group (T3 group) was 1.977 fold higher versus the low-TyG index group (T1 group), consistent with previous studies [30].

Although the TyG index is a known risk factor for CHD in OSA cases, its association with CHD risk in OSA cases with different severity levels remains undefined. Subgroup analysis in the current study showed that elevated TyG index was significantly correlated with higher CHD risk in moderate to severe OSA cases. Upon adjustment for potential confounders, CHD risk was 2.430 times higher in moderate to severe OSA cases with high TyG index than counterparts with low TyG index, indicating TyG index independently predicts CHD in moderate to severe OSA cases.

Moreover, there was an interaction between the TyG index and AHI (P for interaction = 0.002), suggesting that the relationship between the TyG index and coronary heart disease varied according to the severity of OSA. Previous reports have also demonstrated that moderate to severe OSA is positively correlated with the risk and poor prognosis of multiple cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Adverse cardiovascular events have significantly elevated incidence in elderly moderate to severe OSA cases [31]. Meanwhile, moderate to severe OSA is an independent predictor of ischemic stroke in men [32] as well as a risk factor for CHD [33]. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) cases with moderate to severe OSA have a markedly elevated risk of ACS recurrence and ischemia-induced revascularization [34]. Increased frequency and duration of apnea and hypoventilation in moderate and severe OSA cases lead to severe hypoxia, which may over-activate the sympathetic nervous system, trigger oxidative stress, induce IR, and initiate inflammatory cascade reactions, leading to metabolic disorders and vascular endothelial dysfunction and promoting platelet aggregation, which triggers a series of pathophysiological changes and induces atherosclerosis [35].

The Gensini score is an indicator for evaluating coronary artery stenosis, first described by Gensini in 1975 [17]. Research has shown that the Gensini score is closely related to the OSA and TyG index. Additionally, OSA severity is positively correlated with the Gensini score [36, 37]. A cross-sectional study involving 943 patients by Liu and colleagues showed that a high TyG index increases the risk of severe coronary stenosis in coronary heart disease cases [30]. Yuan and co-workers showed TyG index and Gensini score are positively correlated [38]. However, none of these studies elucidated the association of the TyG index with the Gensini score in OSA cases. The present work showed that OSA cases with elevated TyG index have higher Gensini scores, but the TyG index and Gensini score had a non-linear relationship. Although IR is a risk factor for CHD, mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammatory response in OSA may play a dominant role in the progression of CHD [39]as the severity of insulin resistance increases, resulting in a non-linear relationship between the Gensini score and the TyG index. Logistic regression analysis revealed a high TyG index as a risk factor for high Gensini scores in OSA cases. Upon adjustment for potential confounders, this relationship persisted, suggesting the TyG index may be used as an evaluation indicator for CHD severity in OSA cases, consistent with Zhao et al.‘s study [40].

The current study first examined the associations of the TyG index with CHD risk and CHD severity in the OSA population, but there were also some limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, and data collection may have been biased. This may affect the general applicability of the results and make it difficult to infer a causal relationship between the TyG index and CHD risk in OSA cases. Multi-centre further studies are warranted for verification. In addition, in this study, the time and dosage of antiplatelets, lipid-lowering agents, antihypertensives, and hypoglycemic drugs were not obtained from participants, which may improve the severity of coronary lesions to some extent and may have weakened the relationship between the TyG index and the severity of coronary lesions.

Conclusion

In summary, these research findings indicate TyG index is correlated with CHD risk and CHD severity in OSA cases, with a high TyG index independently predicting CHD and coronary artery stenosis degree in OSA cases. The TyG index, an estimated assessment indicator in IR, may predict CHD risk in OSA cases, especially those with moderate to severe disease. Future large-scale clinical trials are needed for more in-depth research, providing a solid basis for early diagnosis and treatment of CHD in OSA patients.
