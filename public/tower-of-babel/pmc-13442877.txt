Expected-depth selection for interpretable decision trees
Authors: Zhigao Huang, Yuzhuo Pan, Miao Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterpretable decision trees are usually selected by worst-case structural measures such as maximum depth or node count, although many deployments incur cost along the particular root-to-leaf path followed by each case. We study expected depth, the test-sample average number of internal decision tests, as a direct measure of average inference burden. The proposed soft-depth (Soft-D) selector chooses, from a cost-complexity pruning candidate path, the tree with minimum validation expected depth among candidates whose validation accuracy remains within a user-specified tolerance of the best candidate. Across eleven benchmark datasets and 25 outer test folds per dataset, Soft-D reduces expected depth by 47.4% relative to unpruned CART and by 12.1% relative to accuracy-first CCP at ε =0.02; a new depth-limited CART baseline confirms that the gain is strongest when a practitioner-selected maximum depth still leaves many samples on long paths. The analysis also shows that ε is a real accuracy–complexity hyperparameter, that refitting after selection can change expected depth in a measurable minority of folds, and that expected-depth selection coincides with node minimization in equal-cost CART paths but separates naturally under heterogeneous query costs. These results position expected-depth selection as a lightweight deployment-time model-selection layer rather than a replacement for globally optimal tree induction.Keywords: Decision trees, Interpretability, Model selection, Pruning, Average-case complexity, Cost-sensitive inference, Explainable data miningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Interpretability is often a critical deployment requirement in data mining and decision support systems where outcomes must be justified to experts, regulators, or affected individuals1–3. Among interpretable model families, decision trees remain exceptionally popular because they implement explicit, conditional rules with a clear, step-by-step mapping from features to outcomes4,5. However, traditional tree induction and model selection pipelines typically control complexity through structural proxies such as total node count or maximum depth, which summarize the graph rather than the routes actually used by deployed cases. These metrics bound the worst-case complexity, but they do not necessarily reflect the typical cognitive load, interaction time, or feature acquisition cost encountered during practical deployments.

In many real-world scenarios, different samples are routed along different paths of varying lengths. Consequently, the average-case decision burden—the expected number of internal tree tests encountered before reaching a prediction, or the corresponding expected feature-acquisition cost—often aligns more closely with typical user experience and operational cost than worst-case proxies. The tests are not survey questions: in a standard automated classifier they are feature-threshold evaluations, while in interactive or cost-sensitive settings they correspond to acquiring or confirming the feature values needed along the path. Despite its practical significance, selecting decision trees to explicitly optimize average-case complexity while maintaining predictive accuracy has received relatively less systematic focus in standard post-hoc pruning paradigms.

Existing decision tree pruning or selection heuristics often rely solely on validating accuracy or directly minimizing overall structure. Thus, a unified selection principle that minimizes average decision cost within a specified accuracy tolerance over a structured candidate family is lacking. Deployment decisions are frequently made under strict accuracy constraints rather than favoring unconstrained model simplification; therefore, selecting models based on their expected depth naturally frames this as a constrained model selection problem rather than merely another pruning heuristic.

In this paper, we address this gap by treating expected depth not simply as an alternative objective, but as an average-case operational complexity measure. We do not aim to propose a completely new tree induction algorithm, but instead present a systematic framework to improve interpretable tree selection at deployment time.

The paper makes four contributions. First, it formalizes expected depth as an average-case operational criterion for decision-tree model selection, complementing conventional worst-case proxies such as maximum depth and node count. Second, it introduces Soft-D, an accuracy-constrained selector over cost-complexity pruning (CCP) candidate trees, and reports where the selector lowers average decision burden with limited predictive degradation. Third, it empirically characterizes when expected-depth-aware selection agrees with size-based simplification and when this agreement should be interpreted as a limitation rather than as independent evidence. Fourth, it extends the same selection principle to heterogeneous query-cost settings, illustrating how expected-depth selection generalizes when features have unequal inference costs.

Related work

The literature on interpretability emphasizes the importance of using transparent models in high-stakes decisions1,3. Classical decision tree learning (e.g., CART, C4.5) builds trees greedily and controls complexity via pruning to preserve transparency and prevent overfitting4,5. A growing line of optimal-tree research learns the entire tree under explicit structural constraints, including mixed-integer optimal classification trees6, sparse globally optimized trees such as GOSDT7, dynamic-programming and branch-and-bound solvers such as DL8.58, and recent multivariate or globally optimized variants9,10. These methods are important baselines for tree induction, but they optimize a different object: they search for a new tree under size/depth/sparsity constraints, often with solver-specific scalability and feature-handling assumptions. Our work is complementary: rather than attempting to solve the NP-hard global optimization problem of full tree induction, we formulate a post-hoc, average-case selection criterion that operates over an efficient, already-extracted candidate space like the CCP path. This distinction is reflected in the experiments: we include the common practitioner baseline of cross-validated depth-limited CART, while treating exact optimal-tree solvers as related induction methods rather than as drop-in replacements for CCP-path deployment selection.

Inherent interpretability requires matching the conceptual structure of the model to human cognitive constraints. Most existing literature uses structural measures such as total node count, maximum tree depth, rule length, feature sparsity, or minimal interpretable model size as proxies for interpretability11–15. While these bounded metrics are useful, they assume worst-case bounds. However, in practice, cognitive load and operational cost largely depend on the specific inference path triggered by an input. Our focus is directly modeling the average-case path complexity (i.e., expected depth). Consequently, an interpretable model may be globally large but heavily skewed such that the vast majority of samples observe only a very short decision logic path.

A deeply related stream of research involves inference-time cost-sensitive learning or budgeted feature acquisition, where acquiring a feature for a decision has an explicit numerical cost. These algorithms typically modify the tree-splitting objective to explicitly penalize expensive features during induction or adaptively route samples to different feature-acquisition policies. To distinguish our approach: those methods usually construct new complex dynamic routing models or alter the core learning mechanism. Conversely, our framework addresses a more fundamental, lightweight problem: how to enforce an average-case complexity-aware selection principle within a standard, existing candidate tree family. This allows our approach to be easily paired with existing tree induction pipelines as a robust deployment-time model selector.

Soft-depth (Soft-D) via CCP path selection

To separate the effect of pruning from the effect of the expected-depth objective, we also consider an accuracy-first CCP baseline that selects α to maximize validation accuracy (then refits on full training data), denoted CART-CCP, as well as two constrained-selection baselines that minimize node count (MinNodes) or max depth (MinMaxDepth) under the same tolerance. The tolerance ε should be treated as a genuine accuracy–complexity hyperparameter, not as a universal default. A small value such as ε =0.02 is a conservative starting point because it allows only a 2% relative validation-accuracy loss from the best CCP candidate, but the sensitivity analysis below shows that larger values can substantially change both expected depth and accuracy. Using a validation fraction is a reasonable default; smaller datasets may require larger ρ or nested cross-validation for stability.

Experiments

Setup

We evaluate on eleven diverse datasets from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, scikit-learn’s bundled benchmark loaders, and OpenML16,17: Breast Cancer, Wine, Iris, Digits, Spambase, Ionosphere, Sonar, Glass, Vehicle, Diabetes, and German Credit. The model comparison includes: (i) unpruned CART4, (ii) CART-CCP (CCP parameter selected by validation accuracy), (iii) MinNodes and MinMaxDepth (CCP selection minimizing node count or maximum depth under the same tolerance), and (iv) Soft-D (CCP selection minimizing expected depth under the same tolerance). To include the baseline most practitioners would tune before deployment, we also evaluate Depth-CV CART, a standard CART classifier whose maximum depth is selected by inner cross-validation over depths 1–15 on the outer-training fold. For a limited comparison with modern optimal-tree induction, we further evaluate DL8.5 and an OCT-style CP-SAT formulation on Iris, Wine, and Breast Cancer. These solvers are run as depth-3 optimal-tree baselines after quantile binarizing each continuous feature into three one-hot bins using the outer-training fold only.

We use 5× 5 repeated stratified cross-validation (25 outer test folds per dataset). For each outer fold, model selection is performed strictly inside the outer-training data using an inner validation split with fraction ρ =0.2. The selected is then refit on the full outer-training fold and evaluated on the held-out outer test fold; outer-test data are never used for model selection. We report mean ± std for accuracy and expected depth over the 25 outer folds. For each dataset and model pair, we run paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the 25-fold differences, apply Holm correction within each metric family, and report paired directional Cliff’s delta. All models are implemented with scikit-learn16 and use default CART settings unless specified. Soft-D uses ε =0.02 and validation fraction ρ =0.2 unless otherwise stated.

Empirical findings

The experimental results support four main conclusions about average-case complexity selection. First, as shown in Table 2, Soft-D consistently yields a strictly shallower average-case path than unpruned CART. By accommodating a strict 2% relative validation accuracy drop (ε =0.02), our expected-depth selection framework significantly compresses the average path length. For instance, on Breast Cancer, expected depth decreases by 58.8% accompanied by an absolute accuracy change of only -0.84 percentage points. Averaged across all eleven datasets, expected-depth-aware selection compresses the average inference route by 47.4% relative to CART, and 12.1% relative to CART-CCP. This highlights that standard accuracy-first selection leaves substantial average-case optimization on the table.

The overhead comes from evaluating paths already constructed during regularization, and the resulting deployment-time selection achieves the described Pareto gains within seconds on the studied datasets. Against Depth-CV CART, Soft-D has lower expected depth on 10 of 11 datasets, although it sacrifices accuracy on Digits, Spambase, and Vehicle; this comparison clarifies that expected-depth selection is a deployment-cost selector rather than a universal accuracy improvement method. Discussion and limitations

Our study reframes interpretable tree selection around average-case complexity, but the scope is intentionally narrower than full tree induction.

Our approach executes selection over a structured candidate family. Consequently, the conclusions primarily concern candidate-family model selection rather than replacement of globally optimal tree induction algorithms8,6,7. Exact optimal-tree methods can be preferable when the deployment goal is to search a new tree under explicit depth, sparsity, or fairness constraints; Soft-D is preferable when a standard CART/CCP pipeline is already used and the deployment question is which candidate subtree best controls average inference burden. Exploring structure-preserving or cross-fit stabilization could further enhance test-time selection consistency.

In standard, equal-cost CART settings, expected depth heavily correlates with structural node count. This should be read as a boundary condition: on these CCP paths, expected-depth minimization does not outperform MinNodes as a separate selector. Its independent role becomes clearer when deployment costs differ by feature, as in the cost-sensitive extension, or when candidate families contain trees with similar size but different routing probabilities.

By definition, expected depth relies fundamentally on the underlying feature distribution. This is appropriate when the validation distribution approximates the deployment population, but it can be fragile under distribution shift. Future work should characterize group-robust or deployment-prior-weighted expected-depth formulations.

Because the framework acts as a lightweight post-hoc selector to ensure compatibility with scalable induction systems, it does not enforce expected-depth reductions natively during greedy node splitting. Integrating average-case objectives directly into solver-based or greedy tree construction remains a promising algorithmic direction.

Conclusion

This paper develops an accuracy-constrained expected-depth selection principle for interpretable decision trees. The central point is that model selection can control average inference burden directly, rather than only through maximum depth or node count. When applied to CCP candidate paths, Soft-D substantially reduces average path length relative to unpruned CART and often improves over accuracy-first CCP and depth-limited CART, while preserving an explicit accuracy-tolerance constraint. The empirical results also identify important limits: ε must be tuned as an accuracy–complexity hyperparameter, refitting can modestly change the selected tree’s expected depth, and equal-cost CCP paths can make expected-depth minimization indistinguishable from node minimization. These limits sharpen the intended use of the method: Soft-D is a lightweight deployment-time selector, not a universal replacement for optimal tree induction. Its extension to heterogeneous query costs suggests a broader direction for tree deployment, where interpretable models are selected according to the actual cost structure faced by the prediction setting.
