Distinguishing variability in hoarding symptom course across time
Authors: Sara K. Nutley, Catherine W. Striley, Linda B. Cottler, Joseph Eichenbaum, Rachel L. Nosheny, Michael Weiner, R. Scott Mackin, Carol A. Mathews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough hoarding disorder (HD) is commonly characterized as a chronic and progressive condition, relatively little is known about its longitudinal course and the factors that contribute to the progression, maintenance, or remission of hoarding symptoms. The current study, the first prospective investigation of hoarding symptom course, aims to identify and characterize patterns of hoarding symptom chronicity in a large sample of adults with clinically significant hoarding.MethodsParticipants who were enrolled in the internet-based Brain Health Registry completed online self-report measures of hoarding symptom severity at 6-month intervals over a period of 5 years. In a sample of 1,275 participants with clinically significant hoarding symptoms (79% with follow-up ≥ 4 years, n = 1,004), latent class mixed modeling (LCMM) was used to identify subgroups of individuals with similar symptom trajectories. Data-driven trajectories were then compared to four previously described theory-driven trajectories, and the demographic and clinical characteristics associated with trajectory class membership were assessed.ResultsLCMM yielded three distinct hoarding symptom trajectories: (1) persistent clinically severe symptoms (“severe”: n = 203, 15.9%), (2) persistent mild or subclinical symptoms (“mild”: n = 955, 74.9%), and (3) rapidly improving symptoms (“improving”: n = 117, 9.2%). Although this data-driven model characterized most participants as exhibiting a predominantly chronic course (90.8%), individuals in the “improving” class experienced sustained remission of hoarding symptoms within 18 months and were distinguished by later age of onset (median age = 47.5 years) and elevated prevalence of self-reported PTSD (20.2%). However, when integrated with the theory-driven framework, substantial heterogeneity in symptom progression emerged among those in the “mild” and “severe” classes, with 29% improving and 20% worsening over time. Individuals in the “severe” class with worsening symptoms (n = 41) were uniquely characterized by childhood-onset hoarding symptoms (median age = 13 years) and elevated prevalence of self-reported ADHD (35.0%).ConclusionIndividuals with clinically relevant hoarding exhibit marked variability in longitudinal symptom course, including a distinct subgroup characterized by rapid improvement; these individuals had unique clinical and demographic characteristics compared to the other groups. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring intraindividual change and may inform early intervention and prevention strategies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08192-w.

Introduction

Hoarding disorder (HD) is a highly debilitating neuropsychiatric condition defined by the persistent inability to part with possessions, often resulting in an extensive and overwhelming accumulation of clutter in living spaces. HD affects between 2 and 4% of the general population [1], with an additional 5–8% reported to have subclinical hoarding symptoms [2–4]. In addition to the health and safety risks associated with household clutter [5, 6], individuals with HD or subclinical HD experience impairment in social and occupational functioning, as well as difficulties with self-care and mobility [2, 7–11]. Despite the considerable burden of hoarding, relatively little is known about the progression and stability of HD symptoms over time – information that is critical for improving early identification and intervention, as well as the development of targeted prevention initiatives.

Retrospective studies suggest that hoarding symptoms frequently emerge in adolescence (6–17 years) or young adulthood (18–28 years), with the average age of symptom onset estimated at 16.7 years [12]. Although data describing the long-term course of HD are limited, retrospective reports indicate that hoarding symptoms are often mild in early life and rise to clinically meaningful levels by middle adulthood, at which point, symptoms often remain chronic and unremitting in the absence of intervention [13, 14]. However, the data are not consistent, as several reports document the continued progression of hoarding symptoms through older adulthood [15–17]. Consistent with this result, previous investigation has found that the age-specific prevalence of clinically relevant hoarding (as determined using self-report measures) increases linearly by approximately 20% for every 5 years of age such that the prevalence of (self-reported) hoarding in those over age 70 is greater than 6% [18]. Nevertheless, a small minority of affected individuals in these studies have endorsed symptoms that improved or relapsed and remitted with time [13, 14]. Importantly, however, previous studies are cross-sectional or retrospective in nature, and as such, the true longitudinal course of hoarding symptoms remains largely unknown.

There is also conflicting evidence for possible predictive factors of longitudinal hoarding symptom trajectory. For example, Dozier and colleagues (2015) found that men report more severe clutter than women in early life, but women report experiencing a more rapid progression of clutter, saving, and discarding behavior over time [17]. In contrast, Tolin et al. (2010) observed no variability in self-reported retrospective symptom course in relation to most demographic and clinical characteristics, including gender, age, race, hoarding symptom severity, and general psychological distress [13]. Similarly, several studies have identified a relationship between stressful or traumatic life events and the onset or progression of hoarding symptoms, but the types of life events associated with symptom course varied across samples. For example, in a retrospective investigation of 751 adults with self-reported hoarding symptoms, Tolin and colleagues (2010) found that periods of hoarding symptom onset or exacerbation were temporally associated with changes in social relationships and exposure to interpersonal violence, but unrelated to employment or financial problems [13]. However, qualitative studies of individuals with hoarding have characterized changes in home setting and financial well-being as factors that contribute to symptom onset or progression [16, 17].

Despite the lack of consensus on symptom course and associated risk factors, studies aiming to identify and characterize possible heterogeneity in the longitudinal patterns of hoarding symptoms, particularly prospective studies, are limited. Additionally, in the small body of existing research, hoarding symptom trajectories have typically been defined using theory-driven classification approaches. In one notable example, Tolin and colleagues (2010) asked individuals with self-identified hoarding to retrospectively rate symptom severity across their lifespan. Four hoarding symptom trajectories were specified a priori, and participants were classified as (1) chronic, (2) increasing (i.e., worsening), (3) decreasing (i.e. improving), or (4) relapsing and remitting based on their individual slope of symptom change. In contrast to theory or hypothesis-driven classification, data-driven statistical modeling techniques have been widely used in psychiatric research to identify subgroups of affected individuals with varying symptom trajectories, revealing heterogeneity in disorder progression without imposing predetermined patterns of symptom change (i.e., a hypothesis-free approach). In the present study, we use a data-driven classification approach to identify and characterize short-term (i.e., 3–5 year) trajectories of hoarding symptom chronicity and change. Guided by prior research, we expected heterogeneity in longitudinal symptom trajectories across individuals; however, as trajectories were defined empirically, no specific hypotheses were formulated regarding their number or structure. We then compare symptom trajectories derived from this hypothesis-free, data-driven approach with the four trajectories outlined by Tolin and colleagues [13], effectively replicating their theory-driven, a priori classification approach in our sample. Finally, we assessed the predictive ability of demographic and clinical characteristics to distinguish hoarding symptom trajectories—information that may allow for the identification of individuals with hoarding symptoms who are likely to worsen (or improve) over time. We hypothesized that the symptom trajectories derived from both data-driven and theory-driven methods would differ with respect to clinical characteristics, including age of hoarding symptom onset and psychiatric comorbidity, but not in demographic features.

Methods

This analysis utilizes data collected via the Brain Health Registry (BHR), an online research platform developed to facilitate the recruitment and longitudinal assessment of participants for age-related health research [19]. The registry comprises more than 105,000 adult participants (aged 18+) recruited through news stories, digital media, word of mouth, and other research and advocacy groups. Upon study registration and completion of electronic informed consent, newly enrolled participants are encouraged to complete computerized self-report questionnaires assessing a wide range of medical, psychiatric, and functional outcomes, including current hoarding symptoms. At six-month intervals, email reminders invite participants to return to the BHR for follow-up assessment. As of June 2023, a total of 40,964 participants completed an initial (i.e., baseline) assessment of hoarding symptom severity included in the Hoarding and Cluttering module, which was first introduced to the BHR in February 2017. More than half of these participants (n = 23,541, 57.5%) subsequently returned to the BHR to complete one or more follow-up assessments of hoarding symptoms, with the number of completed assessments ranging from 1 to a maximum of 13 (i.e., 7 years follow-up). Individuals included in the analyses described here completed the BHR Hoarding and Cluttering module at 7 or more timepoints (i.e., at least 3 years of follow-up) and endorsed hoarding symptoms consistent with probable HD or probable subclinical HD (described below) at one or more of these timepoints.

Demographic characteristics

Participants self-reported their age, gender (male; female; non-binary or other gender identification/preferred not to say), race (reclassified as Asian; Black or African American; Other [Native American or Alaskan Native, Pacific Islander, other] or more than one race; White or Caucasian), and educational attainment (recoded as less than college [grammar school, high school/GED, or some college], two or four-year degree, and graduate or professional degree [master’s degree; doctoral degree; professional degree]).

Hoarding symptoms

Hoarding symptoms were evaluated using an online version of the Hoarding Rating Scale, Self-Report (HRS), a five-item assessment for the core features of HD previously validated for use in the BHR [20, 21]. Individual items ask participants to rate the severity of current clutter, discarding, and acquiring behaviors, as well as hoarding-related emotional distress and impairment, using a nine-point scale ranging from 0 (no/none) to 8 (extreme). Total scores were calculated by summing individual items for a maximum score of 40, with cutoff values of 10 and 14 used to identify individuals with probable subclinical HD (psHD; 10 ≤ HRS Total ≤ 13) and probable HD (pHD; HRS Total ≥ 14), respectively, based on previously published validation studies [20, 21].

Age of hoarding symptom onset

At the first completed assessment of current hoarding symptom severity, participants were asked about age of hoarding symptom onset using the following question: “If you rated any of the questions in this survey as problems for you, at what age did your symptoms begin?” Participants were instructed to enter a value of “0” if the question was not relevant to them.

Psychiatric comorbidity

At the baseline hoarding assessment, past two-week depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [22], a widely used assessment that quantifies depressive symptom severity on a scale ranging from 0 (unaffected) to 27 (severe). Total scores were dichotomized using a cutoff value of 10 to distinguish individuals with no or mild symptoms (PHQ-9 Total Score < 10) from those with moderate to severe symptoms (PHQ-9 Total Score ≥ 10). Additionally, participants were asked to self-report (yes/no) lifetime diagnoses of HD and its commonly comorbid psychiatric disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

Statistical analysis

Heterogeneity in hoarding symptom course was examined using latent class mixture models (LCMM), a longitudinal approach that combines features of mixed modeling and latent class analysis for identifying subgroups of affected individuals who follow similar patterns of change over time [23, 24]. Consistent with a hypothesis-free approach to trajectory formation, we estimated a series of LCMMs varying in both the number of latent classes and the functional form of the trajectories, allowing the data to determine the optimal number and structure of symptom change patterns. In models with one to five latent classes, HRS total scores were regressed on time, which was person-centered as the number of six-month follow-up periods since baseline hoarding assessment. Trajectories were separately modeled as linear and quadratic in (person-centered) time, allowing for examination of various growth patterns. Given that the distribution of HRS total scores was bounded and right-skewed, a rescaled beta cumulative distribution function was used to transform the outcome from its natural scale while simultaneously estimating the LCMM model predicting hoarding symptom severity as a function of time. Following recommendations that LCMMs be estimated without constraints on variance parameters when the data permit [25], models were first estimated under the assumption of within- and between-class heterogeneity in class-specific variance components. As all unrestricted models converged properly, the addition of model constraints was not considered.

To ensure convergence to the global maximum, LCMMs were run for a maximum of 50 iterations from 100 random vectors of initial values. Final models were compared using relevant goodness of fit statistics (i.e., Akaike Information Criterion [AIC], Bayesian Information Criterion [BIC], sample size-adjusted BIC [SABIC]). Solutions were considered suboptimal if characterized by one or more latent classes representing less than 5% of the study sample or by multiple latent classes following similar developmental trajectories. Classification quality was assessed by model entropy, with higher values (ranging between 0 and 1) indicative of more orderly classification (i.e., clearly classifies individuals to a specific class and adequately separates between classes).

Comparison to theory-driven classification structure

Data-driven trajectories identified in the best-fitting LCMM were next compared to the theory-driven trajectories described in previous literature. In the theory-driven approach, membership in one of four trajectory classes (i.e., increasing, decreasing, chronic, or relapsing and remitting) was determined using methodology outlined by Tolin and colleagues (2010) [13]. For each participant, z-transformed HRS total scores were regressed on (person-centered) time for calculation of individual-level slope and corresponding standard error. Sample mean standard error of the slope (0.016) provided cut-off criteria for classifying participants to trajectory classes, wherein individuals with slope < − 0.016 (i.e., 0 − 1 SE) were classified to the “improving” class, those with slope > 0.016 (i.e., 0 + 1 SE) were classified to the “worsening” class, and those with a slope between − 0.016 and 0.016 (i.e., slope within 1 SE of 0) were classified to the “chronic” class. Irrespective of slope, individuals were classified to the “relapsing and remitting” class if hoarding symptoms fluctuated between clinical and non-clinical levels across three consecutive time points (i.e., hoarding symptoms fluctuated from (1) ‘subclinical’ (10 ≤ HRS ≤ 13) to ‘none’ (HRS < 5) to ‘subclinical’ (10 ≤ HRS ≤ 13) or (2) ‘clinical’ (14 ≤ HRS) to ‘none/mild’ (HRS < 10) to ‘clinical’ (14 ≤ HRS)). Cutoff criteria for inclusion in the “relapsing and remitting” class were selected to reflect the classification scheme employed by Tolin et al. (2010), which classified participants to the “relapsing and remitting” course when hoarding severity fluctuated from ‘at least some hoarding’ to ‘no hoarding’ to ‘at least some hoarding’ across consecutive time points.

Predictors of trajectory course

While predictors of trajectory course can be added to LCMM estimation, covariates may alter class structure and membership, and guidelines for selecting between models with and without covariates are not clear [26]. Accordingly, after individuals were assigned to their most probable latent class based on posterior probabilities from the best fitting model, Pearson’s Chi-squared test, Fisher’s exact test, and Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test were used to compare the demographic and neuropsychiatric characteristics of the data-driven trajectory courses, as appropriate. Analyses were then replicated using trajectory courses identified via the theory-driven approach. Post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction were used to identify pairwise differences among trajectory groups.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.3).

Results

Of the 40,965 participants who completed the Hoarding and Cluttering module as of June 2023, nearly one in five (n = 7,901, 19.3%) had hoarding symptoms consistent with probable HD (HRS Total ≥ 14) or probable subclinical HD (10 ≤ HRS Total ≤ 13) at one or more timepoints. In order to retain a sufficiently large sample (i.e., N ≥ 1000) while maximizing the number of measurement points [27], growth analyses were limited to a subsample of 1,275 participants with probable or probable subclinical HD (i.e., HRS Total ≥ 10 at any timepoint) who completed the Hoarding and Cluttering module at 7 or more timepoints (i.e., more than 3 years of follow-up). Of these individuals, 78.8% (n = 1,004) completed 8 or more assessments (4 years of follow-up) and 43.5% (n = 555) completed 10 or more assessments (≥ 5 years of follow-up). The maximum number of follow-up assessments was 13 (n = 69). As highlighted in Table 1, the LCMM sample was 73.3% female, 90.4% White, and had a median age of 64.0 years (IQR = [57.3, 69.6]; measured at baseline). Overall, only 2% of participants reported a lifetime diagnosis of HD.

Data-driven trajectory courses

Model fit criteria for linear and quadratic LCMMs with one to five latent classes are summarized in Supplemental Table 1. Across all class solutions, there was a slight decrease in information criteria (i.e., AIC, BIC, SABIC) following the addition of quadratic time, indicating improved model fit relative to the linear model with the same number of classes. Considering quadratic LCMMs with one to five latent classes, reductions in model AIC, BIC, and SABIC were observed through the addition of the fourth class. While AIC and SABIC provided support for further class enumeration, the five-class solution was characterized by worse fit according to model BIC. In terms of classification quality, both the four and five class solutions were characterized by multiple average latent class posterior probabilities less than 0.80, indicating less discriminatory classification (Supplemental Table 2). Correspondingly, model entropy decreased following the addition of a fourth class, though it is noted that the classification quality of all models was suboptimal (< 0.80). In accordance with these findings, the three-class quadratic LCMM was selected as the final class solution. Observed class-specific trajectories for all candidate models are provided in Supplemental Figs. 1 and 2.

The first trajectory class, labeled “severe”, represented 15.9% of the study sample (n = 203) who endorsed clinically relevant hoarding symptoms that were chronic and unremitting over the course of the follow-up period (Fig. 1; Supplemental Table 3). Most “severe” participants endorsed hoarding symptoms consistent with probable HD at baseline (i.e., baseline HRS ≥ 14: n = 160, 78.8%; Supplemental Table 4), and nearly all endorsed hoarding symptoms consistent with probable HD at most (i.e., ≥ 50%) timepoints (n = 189, 93.1%). However, despite the sustained severity of their hoarding symptoms, only 9.6% of individuals in the “severe” class reported a lifetime diagnosis of HD (n = 18). Presenting with similar longitudinal trajectories were those in the “mild” trajectory class, who endorsed persistent hoarding symptoms of mild or subclinical severity, and represented the largest proportion of participants (n = 955, 74.9%). In contrast, individuals characterized to the final trajectory class, labeled “improving” (n = 117, 9.2%), experienced initial clinical or subclinical hoarding symptoms that rapidly improved following initial hoarding assessment. While nearly two-thirds of those in the “improving” class endorsed hoarding symptoms consistent with probable or probable subclinical HD at baseline (i.e., baseline HRS ≥ 10; n = 76, 65.0%), only 4.9% endorsed hoarding symptoms consistent with probable or probable subclinical HD at 24-month follow-up (n = 5).

Notably, all three trajectory classes were characterized by substantial inter-individual variation in baseline symptom severity and longitudinal trajectories. Additionally, in terms of intra-individual variation, participants frequently experienced large fluctuations in symptom severity between subsequent timepoints, with 55.1% of the sample (n = 703) experiencing a 6-month change in HRS total score greater than or equal to 7-points (i.e., the index of reliable change for the HRS as identified in a previous investigation [28]). As expected, these large fluctuations in symptom severity were most common among individuals in the “severe” trajectory class (6-month change in HRS total score ≥ 7: n = 142, 70.0%), and resulted in a transition between clinical and non-clinical status (i.e., crossing the threshold for probable HD or probable subclinical HD to no HD) in 65.5% of cases (n = 93). In contrast, only one-half of those in the “mild” class (n = 521, 54.6%) and one-third of those in the “improving” class (n = 40, 34.2%) experienced similar timepoint to timepoint variation in overall hoarding symptom severity, although these changes more frequently led to transitions between clinical and non-clinical classifications (> 99% of cases). Accordingly, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to examine the potential benefit of smoothing individual time series data using a two-point simple moving average (see Supplemental Materials). However, the best-fitting smoothed model produced a classification structure nearly identical to that of the best-fitting LCMM identified in the planned analysis (i.e., the unsmoothed 3-class model), and it was determined that the smoothed model provided little improvement in conceptualization relative to the unsmoothed 3-class LCMM.

Attributes of data-driven trajectory courses

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the three data-driven trajectory classes are presented in Table 2. While trajectory classes were invariant with respect to most demographic features, the “severe” class comprised a greater proportion of female participants than the “mild” class (82.2% vs. 71.1%, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.002). Additionally, the three trajectory classes differed in psychiatric health history and age of hoarding symptom onset. As depicted in Fig. 2, individuals in the “severe” class were three times more likely than those in the “mild” class to report lifetime OCD (9.9% vs. 3.3%, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0012) and ADHD (19.9% vs. 7.0%, Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.0001), and also more likely to report anxiety and depressed mood (Lifetime GAD: 30.9% vs. 19.2%, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0077; Lifetime MDD: 40.8% vs. 25.1%, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0002; Current Depressive Symptoms: 36.2% vs. 15.9%, Bonferroni-adjusted p < 0.0001). While individuals following a “improving” trajectory were 2.2 times more likely than those in the “mild” class to report lifetime PTSD (20.2% vs. 9.1%, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.0098), no additional sources of variation emerged between individuals in the “improving” class and those in the “mild” and “severe” classes with respect to clinical characteristics.

Theory-driven trajectory courses

Classification of hoarding symptom trajectories via the theory-driven approach indicated that, as was seen in the data-driven approach, hoarding symptom severity in this sample most often remained stable (chronic n = 455, 35.7%) or improved (“improving” n = 433, 34.0%) over the follow-up period (Fig. 3). Although there was substantial inter-individual variation in the magnitude of improvement, individuals classified as having “improving” symptoms showed, on average, a reduction in mean hoarding symptom severity from 12.8 points on the HRS (SD = 7.4) at baseline to 9.4 points (SD = 7.4) at 24 months, and further to 6.6 points (SD = 6.0) at 48 months (Supplemental Table 5). However, nearly one in five individuals experienced symptoms that increased in severity with time (“worsening” n = 232, 18.2%), with mean hoarding symptom severity rising from 6.8 points on the HRS (SD = 6.0) at baseline to 11.2 (SD = 6.9) at 48 months, and a small minority of participants endorsed symptoms consistent with a relapsing and remitting course (i.e., hoarding symptoms fluctuated between clinical and non-clinical levels across three consecutive time points; n = 155, 12.2%). Comparison of data- and theory-driven trajectories

A graphical comparison of data- and theory-driven trajectory classes is provided in Fig. 4. Although both classification structures included subgroups of participants whose hoarding symptoms improved with time (i.e., “improving”), we observed notable differences between the data- and theory-driven approaches regarding the rate of symptom change. More specifically, the theory-driven “improving” class (N = 425) consisted of all participants whose symptoms improved over the follow-up period, including a portion of those in the “mild” and “severe” data-driven trajectory classes who, despite some reductions in hoarding severity, continued to report clinically relevant hoarding symptoms throughout the follow-up period. In contrast, the data-driven “improving” class was limited to the small number of individuals (N = 117) who experienced a rapid and lasting improvement in hoarding symptom severity. Nearly all members of the data-driven “improving” class (88.0%) were classified as “improving” in the theory-driven framework, compared to approximately 26.9% and 36.0% of those in the data-driven “mild” and “severe” classes, respectively (Supplemental Table 7).

The data-driven classification framework did not identify a subgroup of individuals whose symptoms worsened over the follow-up period, however, approximately one-fifth of those in the data-driven “mild” (n = 191, 20.0%) and “severe” (n = 41, 20.2%) classes were assigned to the theory-driven “worsening” class. Among those with “mild–worsening” symptoms (i.e., individuals classified to both the data-driven “mild” class and the theory-driven “worsening” class), the proportion of participants who met criteria for probable HD or probable subclinical HD (HRS ≥ 10) increased from 13.1% at baseline (n = 25) to 69.6% by Year 5 (i.e., 60-month follow-up; Supplemental Table 8). Similarly, while only 63.4% (n = 26) of those with “severe–worsening” symptoms (i.e., individuals in both the data-driven “severe” class and the theory-driven “worsening” class) met criteria for probable HD or probable subthreshold HD (HRS ≥ 10) at baseline, all participants met criteria by the 30-month follow-up assessment (100%, n = 36).

Finally, among those with “mild” and “severe” hoarding symptoms (as determined by the data-driven framework), comparable proportions of participants experienced “chronic”, and “relapsing and remitting” (theory-driven) symptoms (Supplemental Table 7). In both data-driven trajectory classes, mean hoarding symptom severity did not differ substantially between the “chronic” and “relapsing and remitting” (theory-driven) subgroups (Fig. 4; Supplemental Table 9). However, “mild” and “severe” individuals who were categorized in the theory-driven “relapsing and remitting” class were more likely than those in the theory-driven “chronic” class to experience large fluctuations in overall hoarding symptom severity between subsequent timepoints (i.e., proportion with a 6-month change in HRS total score ≥ 7: “relapsing and remitting” n = 139, 93.3% vs. “chronic” n = 284, 63.5%, p < 0.0001).

Attributes of integrated trajectory classes

Integration of the data- and theory-driven trajectory classes offered a more comprehensive view of hoarding symptom stability and progression, prompting an exploratory examination of the demographic and clinical features of the nine resulting classes depicted in Fig. 4. In this portion of our investigation, pairwise differences between classes were evaluated without correction for multiple testing, consistent with the exploratory intent of the analyses. As observed in the primary analysis, individuals with “severe” hoarding symptoms (i.e., “severe-improving”, “severe-chronic”, “severe-worsening”) consistently reported greater psychiatric burden than those with “mild” symptoms (i.e., “mild-improving”, “mild-chronic”, “mild-worsening”; Supplemental Table 10). However, in contrast to participants with “mild” hoarding—whose clinical features were consistent across theory-driven subgroups—age of hoarding symptom onset and psychiatric health history varied among individuals with “severe” symptoms based on theory-driven classification. In particular, “severe-worsening” participants reported markedly earlier hoarding symptom onset than individuals in the “severe-improving” (median [IQR] = 12.5 [8.0, 37.5] vs. 30.0 [12.0, 42.0], p = 0.0356) and “severe-chronic” (median [IQR] = 12.5 [8.0, 37.5] vs. 30.0 [18.0, 40.0], p = 0.0325) classes, whose age of onset resembled that of the mild classes (e.g., “mild-improving”: median [IQR] = 36.0 [18.0, 50.0]; “mild-chronic”: median [IQR] = 30.0 [17.5, 50.0]). Additionally, the “severe-worsening” class exhibited higher lifetime prevalence of ADHD compared to the “severe-improving” (35.0% vs. 13.2%, p = 0.0076) subgroup, as well as a slightly elevated—albeit nonsignificant—lifetime prevalence of GAD (“Worsening”: 42.5% vs. “Improving”: 32.4% vs. Chronic: 27.1%).

Discussion

Although previous reports have characterized HD as a chronic condition that increases in severity with age [13, 15, 17, 18], much of the literature assessing hoarding symptom course over time has been limited to retrospective investigation of individuals who self-identify as having HD. To our knowledge, this analysis provides the first prospective longitudinal investigation of hoarding symptom course among adults with clinically significant hoarding, termed here probable or probable subclinical HD. In addition to conducting a data-driven analysis of the longitudinal trajectory of hoarding symptoms, we also compared our results to a previously published theory-driven model that used retrospective data to classify individuals into four predefined hoarding symptom trajectories: (1) worsening, (2) improving, (3) chronic, and (4) relapsing and remitting. Contrary to the previously published investigation, our data-driven analysis revealed three distinct patterns of longitudinal change in hoarding symptom severity: (1) persistent symptoms of clinical severity (“severe”), (2) persistent symptoms of mild or subclinical severity (“mild”), and (3) rapidly improving symptoms (“improving”). Although the two analytic approaches yielded divergent classifications, each highlighted distinct facets of hoarding symptom progression, and we found a combination of the two approaches to be the most informative, as each provided unique insights into hoarding symptom trajectories. While the theory-driven framework emphasized the direction of symptom change over time (i.e., worsen vs. improve), the data-driven framework captured heterogeneity in overall hoarding severity and revealed a group of individuals with a novel trajectory distinguished by an accelerated rate of symptom decline.

The data-driven model classified approximately 90% of participants to the “mild” or “severe” trajectory classes—subgroups that largely captured individuals with persistent hoarding symptoms of subclinical (“mild”) or clinically significant (“severe”) intensity over time. At first glance, these severity-based classes appeared to represent individuals with stable hoarding symptoms, lending support to prior studies that frame HD as chronic and unremitting [13, 14]. However, when integrated with insights from the theory-driven framework, we observed considerable variation in hoarding symptom progression among individuals with “mild” or “severe” hoarding, challenging our initial interpretation of chronicity. Indeed, only 39% of participants in the “mild” or “severe” classes were also classified as “chronic” by the theory-driven model, while 29% reported symptoms that improved with time (“improving”) and 20% reported symptoms that worsened over time (“worsening”)—a trajectory entirely overlooked by the data-driven approach. On average, individuals with non-chronic symptom trajectories (i.e., “worsening” or “improving”) experienced substantial changes in hoarding severity over time (e.g., mean HRS total scores progressed from 14.2 [baseline] to 22.6 [month 60] in the “severe-worsening” group and declined from 23.5 to 13.2 in the “severe-improving” group). However, the extent to which these changes reflect clinically meaningful improvement or deterioration remains uncertain, particularly as most participants did not experience a change in symptom status as a result of this variation (e.g., from clinical to nonclinical or from nonclinical to clinical). Taken together, the longitudinal trajectories of these classes suggest that, independent of theory-driven classification, individuals in the data-driven “severe” class likely represent true HD cases marked by persistent and functionally impairing symptoms. In contrast, the “mild” subgroup may encompass a more heterogeneous population of individuals with moderate hoarding symptoms that do not consistently meet clinical criteria, including individuals at risk for HD, those undergoing treatment, and those exhibiting hoarding behaviors in the context of other psychiatric conditions or life circumstances. Nevertheless, all participants in the “mild” class endorsed clinically relevant hoarding symptoms (i.e., 10 ≤ HRS total score) at some point during the follow-up period, indicating that continued evaluation and potential intervention may be warranted.

As expected, participants with “severe” hoarding reported higher lifetime prevalence of psychiatric comorbidities and slightly earlier age of hoarding symptom onset than those with “mild” hoarding—findings that are consistent with reports demonstrating a positive association between hoarding severity and psychiatric burden [4, 29]. This pattern likely reflects the complex clinical presentation of HD, which rarely occurs in isolation from other psychiatric symptoms. For individuals with chronic and severe hoarding, comprehensive assessment of co-occurring psychiatric disorders is essential to treatment planning and clinical management, as interventions that simultaneously address hoarding and associated psychiatric symptoms may be warranted. As a competing hypothesis, the elevated psychiatric burden observed in the “severe” class could suggest that these individuals experience greater overall psychiatric distress rather than clinically relevant hoarding per se; however, several observations argue against this interpretation. Most participants in the “severe” class consistently scored above the well-validated HRS cutoff for probable HD (HRS ≥ 14), and it is unlikely that individuals would exceed this threshold without endorsing core, disorder-specific symptoms such as difficulty discarding. Additionally, a general distress interpretation would imply strong correspondence between hoarding severity and other psychiatric symptoms, such as depression. Prior studies, however, show only modest covariation between hoarding and depressive symptoms over time including in the BHR, reinforcing the conceptual distinction of hoarding from broader psychiatric distress.

Although characterized by pronounced psychiatric comorbidity, “severe” participants did not represent a clinically uniform subgroup; instead, integration of the data- and theory-driven approaches revealed variation in the clinical features of “severe” participants based on theory-driven classification. The subgroup of “severe” individuals with “worsening” symptoms (i.e., “severe-worsening”) emerged as particularly noteworthy; among participants in this class, hoarding symptoms frequently emerged in childhood, with a median onset of 13 years—more than a decade earlier than that of “severe” participants with “chronic” or “improving” symptoms, whose median age of hoarding symptom onset was 30 years. Additionally, “severe-worsening” individuals were substantially more likely to report a lifetime diagnosis of ADHD, with an estimated prevalence of 35%, relative to 22% in the “severe-chronic” class and 13% in the “severe-improving” class. Given the hypothesized role of executive functioning deficits in HD–ADHD comorbidity [30], the observed variation in ADHD prevalence points to executive dysfunction as one potential mechanism underlying hoarding symptom progression and severity. Consistent with this perspective, executive functioning deficits implicated in HD—including difficulties with inhibition, planning, and working memory [30]—are also commonly observed in children with ADHD [31–34] and may contribute to the significantly earlier onset of hoarding symptoms in this trajectory class.

The predominance of females in the “severe” class also merits attention, especially within the “severe-chronic” and “severe-improving” subgroups, where females comprised 84–86% of the sample. This pattern is broadly consistent with research indicating that women with HD report more pronounced clutter, difficulty discarding, and acquiring symptoms than men, despite epidemiological evidence showing equal prevalence of HD across genders. Although gender differences in symptom course may exist, it is also possible that women are more likely than men to acknowledge or report hoarding symptoms, potentially inflating gender differences in hoarding severity measured via self-report. While additional research is needed to clarify gender-specific patterns of symptom progression, these findings emphasize the importance of considering gender-related differences in symptom severity and course when evaluating and treating individuals with HD.

Although the data-driven framework assigned most participants to the “mild” and “severe” trajectory classes, the model also uncovered a novel subgroup of individuals who experienced rapid improvement of hoarding symptoms, with 95.1% of participants in this class achieving sustained remission within 24 months (“improving”). As expected, nearly all participants in this data-driven class were also classified as “improving” in the theory-driven model; however, this pattern of rapid and lasting improvement represented only a small subset of the broader theory-driven “improving” class, which encompassed all forms of symptom decline, including the 29% of participants with “mild” or “severe” symptoms who experienced gradual improvement over time. At initial assessment, participants in the data-driven “improving” class were distinguished by substantially later-onset hoarding symptoms (10–15 years later than “mild” and “severe” participants, on average) and high rates of self-reported PTSD, as well as intermediate rates of self-reported anxiety and mood disorders. Interestingly, small groups of affected individuals with later age of onset and increased frequency of traumatic experiences have also been identified in previous studies [13, 14, 35, 36], though the trajectory of hoarding symptoms in these individuals has not been previously examined. Although exposure to traumatic or stressful life events was not assessed in the BHR, the high prevalence of self-reported PTSD among participants in the data-driven “improving” class is consistent with these previously reported findings. Unfortunately, participants in the current investigation were not asked about the onset and severity of comorbid psychiatric conditions, and we were unable to determine whether PTSD (or trauma exposure) preceded the onset of hoarding symptoms. Additionally, participants did not provide information about past hoarding symptoms and thus the trajectory of hoarding symptom severity from onset to initial BHR assessment is not known. Accordingly, the interpretation of these findings requires some speculation. It is possible that the “improving” class characterizes a unique subgroup of individuals who meet diagnostic criteria for HD and have a trajectory that includes a later age of onset and improvement over time. In this scenario, assessment of hoarding symptom onset and co-occurring stress and anxiety disorders may assist in the identification of affected individuals with HD whose hoarding symptoms are likely to improve with time. However, it is also possible that the “improving” class includes individuals who do not have HD, but rather experience temporary development of or increases in subsyndromal hoarding behavior as a response to a stressful or traumatic life event—although we cannot confirm this hypothesis using the current dataset, it is a likely possibility, at least for some of our participants.

The data-driven model did not generate a distinct subgroup of participants characterized by worsening symptoms, which is particularly notable given that nearly one-fifth of participants (18.2%) were characterized as “worsening” by the theory-driven model. It is possible that the data-driven approach failed to identify an “worsening” class because patterns of symptom escalation were subtle or highly variable, resulting in their absorption into larger, more stable classes. This result may, in part, reflect the age distribution of our study sample. As indicated in previously published retrospective reports, hoarding symptoms often rise to clinically meaningful levels by middle adulthood, at which point symptoms often remain chronic in the absence of intervention [13, 14]. Accordingly, our sample of predominately older adult participants may under-represent those who are most likely to experience pronounced increases in hoarding severity. Nevertheless, even modest escalation of hoarding behavior may be clinically significant, highlighting the importance of ongoing monitoring in this subgroup.

Challenging the notion that hoarding symptoms are persistently stable, our findings further indicate that a large proportion of affected individuals (across all data-driven groups) experience substantial fluctuations in hoarding severity across time. Indeed, approximately 44% of participants demonstrated a six-month change in HRS total score greater than 7 points, exceeding the threshold for reliable change established in previous research (4 to 7 points) [21, 28, 37]. Unfortunately, it remains unclear whether the observed fluctuations represent clinically meaningful changes in symptom severity or, alternatively, an artifact of measurement. Rather than asking participants to reflect on symptom severity over a pre-defined recall period, participants were asked to self-report symptoms as they are currently–a time interval that is largely subject to participant interpretation. However, the optimal recall period for assessing hoarding symptom severity is not known, and additional research is needed to determine whether alternative recall periods (e.g., past one week or past one month) provide a more accurate evaluation of symptom severity.

Although not identified by the data-driven classification, one in ten participants demonstrated “relapsing and remitting” symptoms when classified via the theory-driven model, which, following Tolin et al. (2010), operationalized the “relapsing and remitting” subgroup as individuals whose symptoms fluctuated between clinical and non-clinical levels across three consecutive time points [13]. Although this approach was effective for identifying participants whose symptoms fluctuated around the diagnostic threshold, this definition failed to capture other potentially relevant patterns of temporal variation in hoarding severity, including individuals with pronounced symptom fluctuations occurring entirely above the diagnostic threshold, as well as those who experienced extended periods of remission before reverting to the clinical range. Nevertheless, the “relapsing and remitting” subgroup remains of interest given its unique association with lower educational attainment. Although this relationship is not easily explained by existing literature, it may be that lower educational attainment serves as a proxy for economic well-being, wherein financial distress and hoarding severity fluctuate in tandem across time. However, while previous studies have demonstrated a link between socioeconomic disadvantage and HD [8, 38–40], the nature and directionality of this association remain unclear; additional research is needed to clarify this relationship and to examine the potential impact of other individual, familial, and societal factors on the temporal stability of hoarding symptoms.

Limitations

While this report details the first prospective investigation of hoarding symptom course, it is not without limitations. Although hoarding symptom severity was evaluated using a well-validated assessment of current hoarding symptoms, assessment relied on participant self-report, and it is not known how many individuals included in this analysis meet DSM-5 criteria for HD. Moreover, this investigation focused exclusively on longitudinal change in overall symptom severity and did not include examination of individual hoarding symptoms (e.g., difficulty discarding, clutter, etc.). However, previous research has identified some variability in the progression of discarding, saving, and clutter symptoms across the lifespan [17], and prospective evaluation of individual symptoms may be needed to better understand variability in hoarding symptom course.

As the BHR does not ask participants about healthcare utilization, we were unable to identify participants receiving treatment for HD or co-occurring psychiatric disorders. However, self-reported lifetime diagnosis of HD was exceedingly rare in this sample (2.0%), which may indicate that relatively few participants have received clinical evaluation or treatment for hoarding symptoms. Nevertheless, it is possible that psychiatric care contributed to longitudinal changes in hoarding severity, alongside other unmeasured clinical factors, including both stable (e.g., personality traits) and time-varying influences (e.g., co-occurring psychiatric symptoms). Importantly, while these broader clinical factors warrant consideration, it is unlikely that they fully explain the observed patterns of symptom change. While the data are limited, prior research has shown that fluctuations in hoarding severity are only weakly associated with changes in depression severity, challenging the interpretation that co-occurring psychiatric symptoms largely determine fluctuations in hoarding severity. Clarifying the extent to which these clinical factors influence hoarding symptom progression represents an important priority for future research. Finally, our sample was predominated by white individuals of older age and high educational attainment, and the generalizability of findings to more diverse groups of individuals is unknown.

Conclusions

While past research has largely portrayed HD as a chronic disorder, our findings indicate that longitudinal hoarding symptom trajectories may be more heterogeneous than previously documented. Drawing on the complementary strengths of the data- and theory-driven classification frameworks, we offer a richer understanding of hoarding symptom course, capturing variability in both the direction of symptom progression and overall severity. Additionally, this study provides new evidence for the existence of substantial intraindividual variation in hoarding severity across time and identifies a novel subgroup of affected individuals whose hoarding symptoms rapidly improve following initial assessment. While additional research is needed to better understand the mechanisms that underlie the identified growth patterns, our findings indicate that assessment of hoarding symptom onset and co-occurring psychiatric disorders—most prominently ADHD, as well as stressor-related and anxiety disorders—may assist in the identification of individuals experiencing specific, clinically relevant patterns of hoarding symptom chronicity and change.
