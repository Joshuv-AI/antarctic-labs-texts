Photothermal catalytic synthesis of cyclohexanones from lignin-derived phenolic bio-oils enabling transparent and elastic polyamides
Authors: Shufang Zhao, Maolin Wang, Xiangxiang Chen, Huiwen He, Zhuohao Xia, Meng Wang, Xiaojie Wu, Xian Zhou, Wenqing Xu, Yakun Liu, Qidi Ran, Yao Xu, Binjun Xu, Xiao-nian Li, Siyu Yao, Lili Lin, Ding Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCyclohexanones, as critical precursors for polyamide 6, are inherently challenging to synthesize from lignin-derived phenolic bio-oils due to the high dissociation energy of the aryl-OCH3 bond and the competitive risk of C=O bond over-hydrogenation. To address this intrinsic selectivity trade-off, we report the selective synthesis of cyclohexanones from lignin-derived phenolic monomers via a H2-free photothermal catalytic process using a RuPd/TiO2 photocatalyst at 150 °C, with a selectivity of 94% and an activity of 12 mol-one∙molRu+Pd−1∙h−1. Through the proton-coupled electron transfer process, photogenerated electrons drive precise aryl-OCH3 cleavage, and the resulting methoxy groups and water are utilized as internal hydrogen source. The low pressure of H* generated in situ is key to maintaining the high selectivity. The alkyl-substituted ketone is subsequently converted into the corresponding caprolactam monomer and copolymerized with conventional caprolactam. The propyl-functionalized polyamide 6 exhibits enhanced properties, including 88.9% transparency and 523% elongation at break, whereas conventional polyamide 6 (no alkyl) has only 71.5% transparency and 120% elongation, respectively. This work not only establishes a robust and chemically precise photothermal platform for selectively converting lignin to cyclohexanones, but also demonstrates that alkyl functionalization unlocks unprecedented properties in polyamide 6, thereby greatly increasing its value.Subject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Catalytic mechanisms, Biopolymers

Introduction

Biomass is the most significant large-scale renewable carbon resource, which is crucial for the realization of a carbon-neutral society1–3. Lignin, a major component of biomass, offers both challenges and opportunities because of its intricate structure4–6. At a closer inspection of lignin and its derived guaiacol-based phenolic compounds, the dominant features are aromatic rings accompanied by hydroxyl groups, methoxy groups, and assorted short alkane substituents7. Although we can rearrange chemical bonds among carbon and hydrogen atoms by first reforming lignin into CO and H2 (bio-gas)8, followed by reconnecting carbon with other carbon and/or hydrogen atoms to form desired products like biomethane, olefins, aromatics, or liquid fuels, these processes are highly energy-intensive and, more critically, result in the loss of lignin’s valuable natural carbon backbone. A promising alternative is to preserve, wholly or in part, these natural building blocks and integrate them into high-value products like monomers and intermediates of commodity polymers9,10. This approach not only provides a sustainable alternative to the petrochemical-dependent polymer industry but also potentially reduces the carbon footprint of polymer products without compromising their utility and efficacy.

The past century has witnessed the rise of petroleum-based plastics. Caprolactam, the monomer for the key synthetic plastic material polyamide 6 (PA6), has an annual production of 6 million tons via a multi-step petroleum-based process11. Efforts have been made to develop synthetic routes to produce PA6 precursors from lignin-derived feedstocks. For cellulose, another primary biomass component with a more uniform structure based on glucose, pathways already exist to derive polymer monomers like diols, 2,5-furandicarboxylic acid, and lactic acid12. However, establishing similar routes for lignin conversion, given its complex nature, remains challenging13,14. Specifically, for lignin depolymerization, the resulting bio-oil is often a diverse mixture of various phenolic compounds15,16. Existing transformation strategies for such mixtures are typically substrate-specific, necessitating complex separation processes that hinder economic implementation. Moreover, conventional approaches often rely on high-pressure hydrogen gas to cleave the methoxy groups, which further increases safety risks and energy costs. Consequently, the lack of a general method to convert these mixed phenolics into high-value polymer precursors under mild conditions remains a critical bottleneck.

Notably, a common strategy for bio-based PA6 synthesis, depicted as Route a in Fig. 1, relies on converting phenol, obtained via the acid-catalyzed de-functionalization of lignin, into cyclohexanone and subsequently caprolactam17–21. Within this pathway, recent breakthroughs such as the elegant electrocatalytic strategy by Han et al. have enabled the direct upgrading of phenol into cyclohexanone oxime17. These phenol-based pathways fundamentally lead to standard PA6, as the native alkyl side-chains are stripped away during the de-functionalization step. In addition, Route b targets the direct valorization of lignin-derived phenols (e.g., substituted guaiacols), which are obtained via reductive catalytic fractionation as the primary products of lignin depolymerization22,23. Yet, transforming these actual lignin derivatives presents a formidable challenge due to the intrinsic selectivity trade-off between demethoxylation and ketone preservation24,25. As illustrated in Route b-I (Fig. 1), conventional thermal catalysis typically suffers from over-hydrogenation, yielding cyclohexanols that require an energy-intensive dehydrogenation step to restore the ketone functionality. While recent advances by Li et al. (Route b-II) successfully achieved direct cyclohexanone production, they necessitate harsh conditions (e.g., 200–240 °C, 2 MPa H2) to drive the reaction26. Furthermore, although these alkyl-substituted cyclohexanones were proposed as potential nylon precursors, the actual synthesis and property evaluation of the corresponding alkyl-functionalized polyamides remain largely underexplored (Fig. 1b, gray dashed area).

Herein, we introduce a hydrogen-free photothermal catalytic strategy for the selective synthesis of bio‑cyclohexanone derivatives from lignin‑derived phenolic bio‑oils, using a RuPd/TiO2 bimetallic catalyst (Fig. 1b‑III). Operating at a mild temperature of 150 °C, the system achieves an activity of 12 mol-one molRu+Pd−1 h−1 with 94% chemoselectivity. Mechanistically, the reaction proceeds via a hole‑assisted proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) pathway, where photogenerated electrons drive efficient Ar-OCH3 bond cleavage and selective ring hydrogenation. Critically, the methoxy groups and water serve as hole scavengers, triggering the in‑situ generation of reactive hydrogen species, which effectively suppresses over‑hydrogenation, thereby overcoming the inherent selectivity trade‑off between demethoxylation and ketone preservation. The selective conversion of authentic lignin bio-oil (obtained via reductive catalytic fractionation) primarily yielded 4-propylcyclohexanone. Capitalizing on this selective transformation, we demonstrated its downstream value by employing the commercial analog to synthesize high-purity (99.9%) 4-propylcaprolactam and copolymerized with caprolactam to yield a propyl‑functionalized PA6 copolymer (Co‑PA6; Fig. 1b‑III). Compared with conventional PA6 prepared from unsubstituted caprolactam, the alkyl‑functionalized Co‑PA‑6 shows a dramatic improvement in key properties, with optical transparency increasing from 71.5 to 88.9% and elongation at break rising from 120 to 523%. This study delivers a dual achievement: a precise H2-free platform for selective lignin valorization, and functionalized PA6 with superior properties, thereby providing a compelling framework for high-value bio-plastics.

Results

Photothermal selective synthesis of cyclohexanones from phenolic compounds

As illustrated in Fig. 1, in order to synthesize cyclohexanone or substituted cyclohexanones from lignin-derived phenolic compounds, the primary task is to activate the Ar-OCH3 bond and remove the methoxy group. However, due to the p-π conjugation, the dissociation energy of the Ar-OCH3 bond in these compounds (409–421 kJ mol−1)27 is much higher than that of C–O bonds in alkyl ethers (around 350 kJ mol−1)28. High temperature and pressurized hydrogen are typically required to cleave the Ar-OCH3 bond in the thermal catalytic hydrodeoxygenation (HDO) process29,30. However, a significant challenge arises under these harsh conditions because the hydrogenation of the C=O bond in cyclohexanone is thermodynamically favorable and kinetically facile. This inevitably leads to the over-hydrogenation of the desired product into cyclohexanol31,32 or deoxygenation to cyclohexane33. Therefore, activating the Ar-OCH3 bond in phenolic compounds and preventing over-hydrogenation of desired cyclohexanone products are the main challenges to realize the high yield of cyclohexanone in the HDO catalytic process.

To achieve this objective, we propose a H2-free photothermal catalytic deoxygenative reduction process in an aqueous solution. Guaiacol (2-methoxyphenol), a representative lignin-derived phenolic compound, serves as the model substrate for assessing the efficiency of this process (Fig. 2a). Among the catalysts investigated, the TiO2 (P25)-supported bimetallic RuPd catalyst (5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2, see Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1 for its structural characterization) shows the highest activity (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The unique catalytic superiority of the Ru–Pd bimetallic pair was further validated by benchmarking against other transition metals and bimetallic combinations, which exhibited significantly lower performance (Supplementary Fig. 4). Optimization of reaction temperature identifies 150 °C as the ideal balance between conversion and selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 5). At this temperature, 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 achieves 94% selectivity for cyclohexanone at 45.5% conversion under ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) light irradiation (Supplementary Figs. 6–9). Control experiments at 150 °C using the same catalyst and reaction conditions but without light irradiation (thermal catalysis) yielded no substrate conversion (Fig. 2c, column 3), confirming the process is light-driven. Support screening (Supplementary Table 2) shows that RuPd on TiO2 (P25) delivers the highest activity, whereas negligible conversion is observed on SiO2. The apparent activation energy (Ea) for this photothermal process is 42.20 kJ/mol (Supplementary Fig. 10). Time-dependent profiles (Supplementary Fig. 11) reveal that phenol acts as a reaction intermediate, while over-hydrogenation to cyclohexanol remains minimal throughout the reaction. The decrease in reaction rate at higher conversions could be attributed to a product inhibition effect, supported by the negative reaction order with respect to cyclohexanone (Supplementary Fig. 12). To circumvent this inhibition, a cumulative yield exceeding 90% was achieved by simple separation and recycle experiments (Supplementary Table 3). Moreover, we observed that the addition of hydrogen gas to the reaction mixture, either in the dark or under light irradiation, resulted in the conversion of guaiacol. However, over-hydrogenation occurred, leading to the formation of a substantial amount of cyclohexanol, which increased with increasing hydrogen partial pressure (Fig. 2c). This result underscores the superiority of the current photothermal reaction process and, in particular, highlights the critical role of the internal hydrogen source generated via the photocatalytic reforming of the methoxy group with water. Additionally, the catalyst demonstrated good stability in successive photothermal reactions, maintaining relatively stable activity over 5 reaction runs (Fig. 2d).

Catalyst structure and the photothermal catalytic mechanism

The coexistence of Ru and Pd over TiO2 modifies the light-harvesting ability of photocatalysts in the visible region (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Crucially, transient photocurrent experiments demonstrated that the bimetallic 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 exhibits a consistently high and stable photocurrent throughout light on-off cycling (Supplementary Fig. 13b). This superior charge separation efficiency is attributed to the formation of a unique bimetallic RuPd-TiO2 Schottky junction. To gain mechanistic insights, we conducted scavenger-based studies on the 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 photocatalyst under UV-Vis light irradiation (Fig. 2e). When methanol was introduced as a hole scavenger34, the conversion of guaiacol remained largely unchanged. Instead, the activity was mostly quenched when a much stronger hole scavenger, Na2SO335, was employed. This suggests that excited holes are indispensable participants in the photocatalytic reaction, and it is likely that the phenolic hydroxyl group in guaiacol is more reactive than the C–H and O–H bonds of methanol. We introduced a conventional •OH radical scavenger, isopropyl alcohol (C3H8O)36, into the reaction mixture, which only marginally reduced guaiacol conversion. In contrast, the addition of a non-selective radical scavenger, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy (TEMPO), effectively halted the reaction, indicating that cyclohexanone is formed through a radical pathway. Furthermore, in the presence of an electron scavenger (Ag+)37, a pronounced inhibitory effect was observed, confirming that photogenerated electrons serve as reducing agents in the H2-free photothermal catalytic reaction.

To probe the major radical intermediates generated during the photothermal conversion of guaiacol, we employed TEMPO as a trapping reagent and utilized liquid chromatography (LC) in conjunction with high-resolution electrospray ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (ESI-TOF MS) to capture and identify the reactive radicals38 (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). Analysis of the chromatography profiles revealed the presence of two major radicals, namely CH3O-C6H4O• (dehydro-guaiacol radical) and C6H9O• (dehydro-cyclohexanone radical), suggesting that the photocatalytic reaction may be initiated through a hole-assisted PCET process involving oxidation of the phenol moiety. The structural resonance of the CH3O-C6H4O• radicals indicated the formation of quinone-like intermediates, which significantly facilitates the dissociation of the robust Caryl–O bond in Ar-OCH3. This demethoxylation step is followed by partial hydrogenation, giving rise to the C6H9O• radical. We postulate that this resonance stabilization directs the selective hydrogenation of the aromatic ring, thereby effectively suppressing side reactions39.

Based on the radical trapping results and catalyst characterization, we propose a plausible mechanism for the H2-free photothermal conversion of guaiacol derivatives over RuPd-TiO2 catalyst, as illustrated in Fig. 2g. Specifically, the reaction is initiated through the hole-assisted PCET process. Under light irradiation, the catalyst generates electron-hole pairs, which are efficiently separated by the formation of a Schottky junction. The photogenerated holes preferentially oxidize the methoxy group of the adsorbed phenolic substrate, which functions as an internal hole scavenger. This oxidation, assisted by water molecules, facilitates the cleavage of the Ar-OCH3 bond (demethoxylation) and triggers the in situ generation of reactive hydrogen species from water and the methoxy group. Subsequently, these surface-active hydrogen species serve as reducing agents for the hydrogenation of the aromatic ring. Crucially, this mechanism differs fundamentally from traditional thermal hydrogenation. By utilizing the methoxy group as an internal sacrificial donor, the system establishes a kinetic balance between demethoxylation and hydrogenation. This intrinsic control prevents the over-hydrogenation of the ketone group (which typically occurs under high-pressure external H2), thereby securing high selectivity toward cyclohexanone derivatives.

Substrate scope and valorization of authentic lignin bio-oil

To assess the generality of this H2-free photothermal catalytic process, we investigated the conversion of a series of representative lignin-derived phenolic compounds (1–8)3,40 (Fig. 3). Notably, all eight phenolic compounds (Fig. 3a, b, left) demonstrated consistently high selectivity (>80%) toward cyclohexanone and its alkylated variants, with conversions ranging from 40.6 to 66.2% under identical reaction conditions. Mechanistically, the alkoxy groups were cleaved to serve as in situ hydrogen sources alongside water for the hydrogenation of the aromatic ring. Crucially, the alkyl side chains were retained, selectively yielding the corresponding cyclohexanone derivatives. To further evaluate the robustness of the catalytic system toward diverse functional groups in a competitive environment, we investigated the one-pot light-driven photothermal conversion of a multi-component mixture containing compounds 1–8. The reaction exhibited a high selectivity of 86.7% toward cyclohexanone and its alkylated variants at a conversion of 42.0% (Fig. 3b, right and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17), underscoring the efficacy and robustness of our H2-free photothermal strategy in handling complex multi-component substrates.

Moving beyond model substrates, authentic lignin bio-oil obtained from birch lignocellulose via reductive catalytic fractionation was employed to validate the practical applicability of this strategy. As analyzed in Fig. 3c, this bio-oil comprises typical 4-propyl guaiacyl (G) and syringyl (S) monomers alongside complex lignocellulose-derived impurities. Remarkably, despite this complex matrix, the catalyst exhibited high efficiency (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 4), selectively yielding the corresponding 4-propylcyclohexanone as the primary product.

Such performance contrasts with conventional thermal HDO summarized in Supplementary Table 5, which typically requires 180–280 °C and 0.04–4.0 MPa H2 (Entries 1–10). The photothermal route operates under much milder, H2-free conditions while maintaining high selectivity, offering a distinct approach to managing the selectivity-energy trade-off in lignin valorization.

Synthesis of 4-propylcaprolactam and its copolyamides

Motivated by the abundance of propyl-substituted phenolics in authentic lignin bio-oil (from reductive catalytic fractionation of birch) and their selective conversion into the corresponding 4-propylcyclohexanone, the synthesis and properties of alkyl-functionalized polyamide materials were explored. First, the synthesis of 4-propylcaprolactam from commercial 4-propylcyclohexanone was successfully scaled up to the kilogram level via sequential oximation14 and Beckmann rearrangement41 (Supplementary Fig. 18). Following rigorous purification (including extraction, crystallization, and distillation), the monomer was isolated with polymer-grade purity (>99.9%, Supplementary Fig. 19). Employing this high-purity monomer, a series of propyl-functionalized copolymers (denoted as Co-PA6) were synthesized by co-polymerization with conventional caprolactam at molar ratios ranging from 5% to 35% (Fig. 4a). The polymerization was conducted at 230–260 °C under an N2 atmosphere for 5 h42 (see methods).

Comprehensive properties and practical applications of Co-PA6

Structural characterization via 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, and 1H-13C HSQC spectroscopy (Supplementary Figs. 20–22) confirmed that the propyl-functionalized units were successfully integrated into the polymer chain. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis revealed that Co-PA6-15% achieved a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 55 kDa (dispersity Ɖ = 1.69), which is comparable to that of conventional PA6 (46 kDa, Ɖ = 1.75). This similarity ensures that the observed property enhancements stem intrinsically from structural modifications rather than variations in chain length (Supplementary Fig. 23). Crucially, the incorporation of these propyl side chains significantly altered the thermal properties and crystallinity of the polymer. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) profiles (Fig. 4b) revealed that the melting point of Co-PA6 gradually decreased by 10–40 °C, and the crystallinity of Co-PA6 declined significantly as the insertion ratio of 4-propylcaprolactam increased (Supplementary Table 6). For instance, the degree of crystallinity for Co-PA6-15% decreased to 19.6% compared to 36.7% for conventional PA6. When the proportion of the propyl-functionalized monomer exceeded 30%, the copolymer became amorphous. This trend was corroborated by two-dimensional wide-angle X-ray diffraction (2D-WAXD), which showed broadening diffraction rings with increasing propyl content (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 24). These results provide definitive evidence that the presence of propyl branches effectively disrupts the hydrogen bonding network, reducing inter-chain interactions and crystallinity. Furthermore, the hygroscopicity of the polymer was suppressed; per ISO 62:2008 (Supplementary Table 7), the 48-h water absorption decreased from 2.24% for PA6 to 1.43% for Co-PA6-20% due to the hydrophobic nature and steric shielding of the propyl groups. The mechanical implications of these structural modifications were further evidenced by dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA), which revealed a significant shift in the glass transition temperature (Tg) from 79 °C for PA6 to 34 °C for Co-PA6-15% (Supplementary Fig. 25). This reduction in Tg and cohesive energy density underscores the transition from a rigid matrix to a more ductile and elastic framework, explaining the enhanced toughness of the copolymers.

The incorporation of propyl-functionalized monomers into the main chain significantly enhanced the transparency, flexibility, and elasticity of the copolymers. Most notably, the elongation at break increased severalfold while critically maintaining the high tensile strength of conventional PA6. As shown in Fig. 4d, the tensile strengths of Co-PA6-15% (58 MPa) and Co-PA6-30% (55 MPa) remained comparable to that of conventional PA6 (58 MPa). In contrast, the elongation at break of Co-PA6-15% reached 346%, which is 2.88 times that of conventional PA6 (120%), and further extended to 523% for Co-PA6-30% (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Movie 1). The enhanced flexibility allows the Co-PA6-30% sample to bend over 90 degrees without fracture, a deformation unattainable for the rigid conventional PA6 (Fig. 4d, inset and Supplementary Movies 2 and 3). Consistent with the reduction in crystallinity, the Co-PA6 copolymers exhibited significantly improved transparency and lower density. The transmittance increased from 71.5% for PA6 to 88.9% for Co-PA6-30%, while the density decreased from 1.152 to 0.908 g cm−3 (Fig. 4f). Notably, this density is lower than that of water, offering a lightweight advantage over commercial transparent materials.

Conventional PA6 is highly valued commercially for its superior mechanical strength, making it a dominant engineering plastic. However, its intrinsic high crystallinity, stemming from strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding, naturally results in opacity and limited ductility, restricting its utility in applications requiring both transparency and flexibility. To demonstrate how the synthesized copolymer expands the performance envelope of polyamides, we benchmarked the representative Co-PA6-15% against both conventional PA6 and, significantly, against CXT8113—a high-performance commercial transparent PA12—to serve as an optical and mechanical reference (Fig. 4g). The comparative analysis reveals that Co-PA6-15% successfully integrates the best attributes of both materials. It matches the excellent transparency and low density of CXT8113 while retaining the superior tensile strength of conventional PA6. Moreover, it uniquely distinguishes itself with superior mechanical robustness. Specifically, the elongation at break and Young’s modulus of our material are 1.87 and 1.45 times those of the commercial PA12, respectively (Fig. 4g).

By enabling the creation of such transparent and elastic polyamides, the lignin-derived propyl-functionalized monomer significantly expands the potential applications of modified PA6. To validate this practical applicability, we demonstrated the exceptional processability of the propyl-functionalized copolymer by fabricating diverse value-added products using the high-strength Co-PA6-15%. Specific examples include high-viscosity slices (relative viscosity ~2.77, Fig. 4h-I), impact-resistant goggles (Fig. 4h-II), low-density glasses frames (Fig. 4h-III), polymer dental blister braces (Fig. 4h-IV), highly elastic filaments (Fig. 4h-V, Supplementary Table 8 and Supplementary Fig. 26), and transparent tubes (Fig. 4h-VI). Furthermore, by leveraging the distinct elastomeric characteristics accessed at 30% monomer content, the Co-PA6-30% copolymer enabled supercritical foaming to produce highly resilient foam beads suitable for high-performance sports equipment (Fig. 4h-VII). These results confirm that the lignin-derived propyl-functionalized caprolactam serves as a valuable building block for tailoring the properties of polyamides. The unique combination of suppressed crystallinity, lower glass transition temperature, and excellent flexibility opens avenues for high-value applications beyond traditional structural materials. Looking ahead, future directions include exploring these advanced functional applications and investigating the chemical recycling behavior of the copolymers to establish a sustainable lifecycle for these high-performance bio-based materials.

Discussion

In summary, this work establishes a distinct H2-free photothermal catalytic strategy leveraging a RuPd/TiO2 bimetallic catalyst for the selective valorization of lignin-derived phenolic bio-oils into valuable bio-cyclohexanone derivatives. The utility of this strategy was further validated by processing authentic lignin bio-oil obtained via reductive catalytic fractionation, where the dominant propyl-substituted phenolics were selectively transformed into the specific 4-propylcyclohexanone. Due to the abundance of propylcyclohexanone monomer from bio-oil, 4-propylcaprolactam was synthesized and successfully copolymerized with conventional caprolactam to obtain propyl-functionalized Co-PA6 to explore its practical applicability. The preservation of native alkyl side-chains in monomers endows alkyl-polyamide with exceptional transparency, high elasticity, superior elongation (523%), and low density. Notably, these properties enable the fabrication of value-added products such as impact-resistant goggles, transparent frames, elastic filaments, and resilient foams. Ultimately, the success of this H2-free photothermal platform provides a precise chemical roadmap for lignin valorization, and, critically, lays the foundation for designing high-value PA6 variants that significantly expand the functional scope of their conventional counterparts.

Methods

Materials

P25 titanium oxide was purchased from Degussa AG. Methoxyphenols (2-methoxyphenol, 3-methoxyphenol, and 4-methoxyphenol), dimethoxyphenols (2,3-dimethoxyphenol, 2,4-dimethoxyphenol, and 2,5-dimethoxyphenol), 2-ethoxyphenol, and 2-methoxy-4-propylphenol were purchased from Macklin. RuCl3∙3H2O, PdCl2 were purchased from Aladdin.

Catalyst Synthesis

Synthesis of Ru/TiO2, Pd/TiO2, and RuPd/TiO2 photocatalyst

The P25 titanium oxide-based catalysts of Ru/TiO2, Pd/TiO2, and RuPd/TiO2 were synthesized by liquid phase reduction43,44. The loading of x wt% Ru and y wt% Pd on TiO2 was annotated as RuxPdy/TiO2. For a typical synthesis of 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2, 500 mg of TiO2 has been dispersed by 50 mL of deionized water in advance. A suspension of TiO2 was added dropwise to the metal solution under constant stirring for 2 h. Next, sodium citrate was introduced into the mixed suspension with a stochiometric ratio of 3:1 (the moles of sodium citrate/the moles of Ru + Pd = 3:1) and stirred for another 0.5 h. Subsequently, the freshly prepared solution of NaBH4 (the moles of NaBH4/the moles of Ru + Pd = 5:1, 10 g/L) was added slowly and stirred for 12 h. Then, the sample was collected by centrifugation at 10,845 × g for 20 min and washed with deionized water three times and with absolute ethanol two times. And the sample was collected after 12 h of drying at 60 °C. Finally, the 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 catalyst was obtained after reduction by H2 gas in a sealed quartz glass tube at 200 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. For comparison, monometallic 5%Ru/TiO2 (5.0 wt% Ru) and 5%Pd/TiO2 (5.0 wt% Pd) were synthesized by a similar procedure.

Structure characterizations

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of 5%Ru/TiO2, 5%Pd/TiO2, and 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 were obtained with a JEOL JEM-1230 operating at 300 kV. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry elemental mapping were performed using an FEI Tecnai G2 F30 Super-Twin HRTEM at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. The size distributions of Ru, Pd, and RuPd nanoparticles were analyzed based on the statistics of over 80 deposits. Atomic-resolution scanning transmission electron microscopy of the reduced 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 was performed using a state-of-the-art transmission electron microscope (Thermo Scientific Spectra 300) equipped with double (image and probe) spherical aberration (Cs) correctors and operating at 300 kV.

Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) diffuse reflectance spectra were obtained on a Shimadzu UV-3700 spectrophotometer equipped with an integrated sphere attachment. The transient photocurrent spectra of 5%Ru/TiO2, 5%Pd/TiO2, and 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 catalysts were collected by the electrochemical workstation, which was manufactured by Shanghai Chenhua Instrument Co., LTD.

The i–t curves were established with a sensitivity of 1 × 10−6. Photothermal catalytic activity evaluation

The selective photothermal catalytic conversion of guaiacol was tested in a stainless-steel autoclave (100 mL) with a sapphire window at the bottom. Before the reaction, the catalysts were reduced by H2 gas in a sealed quartz glass tube at 200 °C for 2 h with a heating rate of 5 °C/min. Typically, 4.0 mmol of guaiacol and 100 mg of the reduced catalyst were mixed with 25 mL of H2O in the photothermal reactor. It was sealed and purged with N2 three times to remove air. And then, the reaction was heated to 150 °C while stirring at a speed of 1000 rpm in the dark conditions. Subsequently, the reaction was carried out at 150 °C under UV-Vis light (Xe light, λ = 320–780 nm) for 3 h. After the reaction, the autoclave was cooled to room temperature using an ice water bath. Dodecane was added as an internal standard, and the reaction mixture was extracted with ethyl acetate (25 mL). The organic phase products were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC, Agilent 8860) equipped with a HP-5 column of HP-5 (0.32 mm in diameter, 30 m in length). The conversion of guaiacol, the selectivity of each product, and the carbon balance were determined by Eqs. 6, 7, and 8, respectively. Furthermore, the photothermal reaction activity was defined as moles of cyclohexanone produced cyclohexanone (μmol)/reaction time (h), as shown in Eq. 9.

For comparison, the pure thermal conversion of guaiacol over the 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 catalyst was carried out in an H2-free atmosphere (H2O) and in the reactor purged with 0.37 MPa of H2 at 150 °C in the dark, respectively. Note that no H2 was introduced into the feed gas in any of the other catalytic tests in this work.

The photothermal performance of the 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 catalyst was further tested at different catalyst masses of 25–150 mg, reaction temperatures from 75 to 180 °C, and reaction times from 10 min to 9 h under UV-Vis light (Xe light, λ = 320–780 nm).

A similar procedure was used for the photothermal catalytic selective convection of other phenolic compounds. In this work, phenolic compounds including methoxyphenols (2-methoxyphenol, 3-methoxyphenol, and 4-methoxyphenol), dimethoxyphenols (2,3-dimethoxyphenol, 2,4-dimethoxyphenol, and 2,5-dimethoxyphenol), 2-ethoxyphenol, and 2-methoxy-4-propylphenol were selected as representatives to evaluate the photothermal catalytic activity of the 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 catalyst at 150 °C under UV-Vis light for 3 h (λ = 320–780 nm). Furthermore, the mixed phenolic substrates included the eight above compounds with the mole ratio of 1:1:1:1:1:1:1:1.

The authentic lignin bio-oil was extracted in our laboratory via reductive catalytic fractionation (RCF). Specifically, birch lignocellulose (1.0 g) and 5% Ru/C (0.1 g) were mixed in methanol (20 mL) and reacted at 250 °C for 3 h under an initial H2 pressure of 3 MPa. The phenolic components in the obtained lignin bio-oil (derived from 1.0 g of lignocellulose) were used as substrates to evaluate the photothermal catalytic activity of the 5%Ru₁Pd₁/TiO2 catalyst at 150 °C under UV-Vis light (λ = 320–780 nm) for 3 h.

The apparent activation energy was calculated using Arrhenius plots for the photothermal catalytic conversion of guaiacol on the 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 photocatalyst. The reaction conditions were as follows: 20 mg 5%Ru1Pd1/TiO2 photocatalyst, 4 mmol guaiacol, 5 mmol dodecane as the internal standard, and 25 mL of H2O. The reactions were carried out in an autoclave at a temperature of 130–150 °C and a reaction time of 0.5–1.5 h under UV-Vis light (Lamp house MC-PF300B, λ = 320–780 nm). The conversion of the photothermal reaction was controlled in the kinetic range.

Oximation and Beckmann rearrangement of cyclohexanone and 4-propylcyclohexanone

Oximation

The procedure was conducted according to Opatz et al.14. A solution containing cyclohexanone derivatives (0.10 mol), hydroxylamine hydrochloride (2.4 mol/L), sodium carbonate (1.6 mol/L), methanol (120 mL), and water (60 mL) was stirred at room temperature for 4 h. Then the reaction mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure at 65 °C and extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic layer was separated and concentrated at 60 °C to afford the product as translucent, oily crystals.

Beckmann rearrangement41

A solution of the oxime (5 g), obtained from oximation in H2SO4, was added dropwise to 3 mL of 90% H2SO4, under stirring for 15 min at 100 °C. After cooling the reaction mixture to 0–5 °C, aqueous ammonia (25–28%) was slowly added until the pH reached 8. The resulting solution was extracted with dichloromethane (80 mL × 3). The combined organic phases were dried over anhydrous MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure at 60 °C. To achieve the high purity necessary for transparent and elastic polyamides, the crude product was subjected to further recrystallization to yield high-purity 4-propylcaprolactam as the final monomer.

Co-polymerization of the mixture of caprolactam and 4-propylcaprolactam

Co-polymerization of the mixture of caprolactam and 4-propylcaprolactam could be accomplished with various caprolactam/4-propylcaprolactam ratios. For example, for a caprolactam/4-propylcaprolactam ratio of 80:20, 9.04 g (0.08 mol) of ε-caprolactam, 3.10 g (0.02 mol) 4-propylaprolactam and 12 mL of deionized water were mixed and heated to 230–260 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere in a steel reactor equipped with a nitrogen inlet and mechanical stirring. Simultaneously, the pressure was increased to 0.5–1.0 MPa. Afterwards, it was maintained for 5 h to achieve polymerization of monomers in the presence of water. Then, the pressure began to level off, and the reaction was further carried out under a nitrogen atmosphere and atmospheric pressure for 1 h, respectively. Finally, the system pressure was reduced to about 50 Pa and kept for 1 h. After reaction, the system was cooled naturally to room temperature45. The solidified polymer mass was mechanically removed and crushed into small pieces. The resulting polymer was then subjected to intensive hot water extraction (typically 90 °C, repeated three times) to fully remove residual unreacted caprolactam and water-soluble oligomers. The purified Co-PA6 copolymer was subsequently filtered, collected, and dried under vacuum at 80 °C for 24 h until a constant weight was achieved, ensuring the high purity required for mechanical and thermal characterization. The obtained Co-PA6 copolymer exhibited a relative viscosity of 2.05. At the same time, we could synthesize Co-PA6 copolymer with a caprolactam/4-propylcaprolactam ratio of 8:2.

For the purpose of comparison, PA6 was synthesized with pure caprolactam following the same method, and the relative viscosity is 1.99.
