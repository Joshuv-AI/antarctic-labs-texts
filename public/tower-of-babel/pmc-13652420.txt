Introduction

Situs inversus totalis (SIT) is a rare congenital condition, with an estimated incidence of approximately 1 in 10 000–20 000 individuals, characterized by complete mirror-image transposition of the thoracic and abdominal organs[1–3]. Although typically asymptomatic, the condition may complicate clinical assessment and perioperative management due to reliance on standard anatomical assumptions in routine practice.

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is one of the most frequently performed orthopedic procedures worldwide. With its increasing utilization in aging populations, surgeons and anesthesiologists are more likely to encounter patients with uncommon anatomical variants during otherwise routine procedures. Despite this, the perioperative implications of SIT in orthopedic surgery remain sparsely reported.

This case report highlights that, while SIT does not alter knee anatomy or necessitate modification of the surgical technique for TKA, it represents a clinically relevant perioperative consideration. Recognizing SIT as a perioperative risk modifier, rather than a surgical contraindication, is essential for ensuring patient safety and optimal outcomes.

Case presentation

A woman in her late 60s presented with progressive right knee pain for more than 1 year, associated with reduced mobility and difficulty performing daily activities. The pain was predominantly mechanical and partially relieved by rest. Previous conservative management, including oral analgesic medications and repeated intra-articular injections of hyaluronic acid, provided only temporary relief.

Her medical history included well-controlled hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and chronic gastritis. She denied any history of recurrent respiratory infections, chronic cough, sinusitis, or bronchiectasis. On examination, the right knee showed tenderness, crepitus, and a limited range of motion. As the patient reported intermittent, non-specific upper abdominal discomfort during the pre-anesthetic evaluation, a non-contrast abdominal CT was obtained to rule out occult pathology. On the scout view, the cardiac silhouette appeared right-sided, prompting inclusion of the chest; the full thoracoabdominal CT unexpectedly revealed complete mirror-image transposition of the thoracic and abdominal organs, consistent with SIT (Fig. 1). This finding, previously undiagnosed, prompted a careful review of cardiopulmonary status and perioperative risk. Upon confirmation of SIT, the following perioperative adjustments were implemented: a multidisciplinary team meeting involving orthopedic surgery, anesthesiology, cardiology, and radiology; reversal of ECG and telemetry lead placement with clear documentation at the patient’s bedside and in the electronic health record. Right TKA was performed under general anesthesia using standard surgical techniques without modification. The intraoperative course was uneventful. Postoperatively, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, experienced no anaesthesia-related or surgical complications, and began ambulation with assistance on the first postoperative day (Fig. 2). Methods

This work is also reported in line with the SCARE criteria, which helped improve the transparency and quality of this case report[4].

The surgery was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. A conventional medial parapatellar approach was utilized. Femoral and tibial bone resections, soft-tissue balancing, and component positioning were performed according to established techniques for primary TKA. No anatomical abnormalities of the knee joint were encountered intraoperatively. A cemented posterior-stabilized knee prosthesis was implanted. Intraoperative assessment confirmed satisfactory limb alignment, joint stability, and range of motion prior to wound closure. Operative duration and blood loss were comparable to those of routine primary procedures.

Postoperative care followed institutional standards. Physiotherapy-guided mobilization was initiated on the first postoperative day. The rehabilitation program included progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, knee range-of-motion exercises, and quadriceps strengthening. The patient was discharged with advice to continue outpatient physiotherapy. No off-label drug use, experimental interventions, or clinical trial–related treatments were involved at any stage of management.

Results

The postoperative course was uneventful. The surgical wound healed satisfactorily, and sutures were removed 14 days after surgery. The patient was followed up regularly in the outpatient clinic according to standard postoperative surveillance protocols. Follow-up assessments included clinical evaluation of knee function, wound condition, and general health status, as well as radiographic assessment of prosthesis position, when clinically indicated, in accordance with current guidelines for monitoring outcomes after TKA.

At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained in good general health. The uneventful postoperative course, similar to that of patients without SIT, confirms that SIT itself does not adversely affect recovery after TKA when appropriate perioperative adjustments are made.

Discussion

SIT is a rare congenital condition characterized by complete mirror-image transposition of the thoracic and abdominal organs. Although SIT has been extensively described in the context of cardiothoracic, spinal, and abdominal surgery[5–7], its relevance to orthopedic procedures, particularly TKA, remains rarely reported.

The clinical value of this case lies primarily in perioperative risk recognition rather than in surgical novelty. In patients with SIT, reversed cardiac and visceral anatomy may complicate preoperative cardiopulmonary assessment if it is not explicitly acknowledged.

An important emergency consideration in patients with SIT and dextrocardia is the reversal of defibrillator pad placement. Preoperative identification of the cardiac apex by physical examination or bedside echocardiography is recommended, and the entire resuscitation team should be alerted to the patient’s anatomy.

As the volume of elective joint arthroplasty continues to increase globally, surgeons and perioperative teams will inevitably encounter patients with rare anatomical variations during otherwise routine procedures. This case, therefore, serves as a practical reminder that uncommon systemic anatomical variants may still carry perioperative implications even when the operative field itself is unaffected.

Recognizing SIT as a perioperative risk modifier, rather than a surgical contraindication, allows standard orthopedic procedures like TKA to be performed safely while minimizing avoidable perioperative errors through heightened awareness and communication. All preoperative imaging, including knee radiographs, should be flagged with “situs inversus” to alert radiologists and referring clinicians.

Conclusion

SIT should be recognized as a perioperative risk modifier rather than a contraindication to TKA. This case highlights the importance of preoperative identification, accurate documentation, and structured multidisciplinary communication to avoid preventable perioperative errors. Heightened awareness of uncommon systemic anatomical variations is essential to ensure patient safety during routine orthopedic procedures.
