Exploring lack of trust and its impact on access and utilization of primary healthcare services among African immigrant women in the United States: a qualitative study of Ethiopian immigrant women
Authors: Gashaye Melaku Tefera
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLack of trust is a critical issue in healthcare that contributes to the growing disparities in access and utilization of health among minoritized and disadvantaged populations in the U.S. This study explored how lack of trust affects Ethiopian immigrant women’s (EIW) primary healthcare (PHC) experience and the personal and structural factors exacerbating the problem.MethodA qualitative cross-sectional design was used to collect data through in-depth interviews with EIW (N = 21) living in six states (MO, MD, VA, KS, MI and IN) and Washington DC using a pilot-tested semi-structured interview guide. A mix of purposive and snowball sampling was used to recruit participants who: (1) identify as an Ethiopian immigrant; (2) identify as a female; (3) be 18 years and older; (4) arrived in the U.S. within the last five years; and (5) speak Amharic or English languages. The interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, translated and cleaned for errors. An inductive thematic analysis was followed to analyze data using Nvivo12 software.ResultsThe findings showed that lack of trust is a significant concern among EIW negatively affecting their PHC experience and decisions in seeking care. The thematic analysis revealed five major factors contributing to the erosion of trust in PHC among EIW: (1) reliance on medication in U.S. healthcare system (2) preference for traditional homemade remedies over PHC recommendations and modern medicine (3) faith and its influence on trust and PHC decisions (4) lack of transparency in PHC, and (5) multilevel discrimination in PHC.ConclusionThe analysis suggested that PHC professionals provide understandable and contextualized health information, practice transparency, familiarize themselves with the healthcare system in immigrants’ countries of origin by asking their patients, and prioritize culturally competent care to gain trust. At structural and policy levels, reform is needed to dismantle systemic problems that enable and perpetuate discrimination, diversify the healthcare workforce, revise healthcare training curriculums and procedures to reflect immigrant needs, establish multi-sectoral collaboration between health, education, and immigration sectors to create conducive care environment for immigrants. Further research and conversation around alternative medicine is vital to expand evidence-based and inclusive PHC services for immigrants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11798-z.Keywords: Lack of trust, Access to healthcare, Primary healthcare, African immigrant women, Ethiopian immigrant women, Immigrants

Background

Trust in healthcare refers to the patient’s voluntary acceptance of vulnerability to the consequences of the assessment, decisions, and actions of the provider [1]. Trust is considered the fundamental basis of a healthcare relationship between the patient and the healthcare provider. It is based on the patient’s expectations that the provider’s decisions and actions are to the best of the patient’s interest and well-being [2]. Although trust is often portrayed as the interaction and relationship between the patient and the provider, it has a systemic and institutional dimension where people analyze whether a given healthcare system can be trusted or not [3]. Hence, it is important to understand people’s trust both in their relationship with professionals as well as the healthcare system they interact with. It is also important to note that with the increasing technology and digitization of healthcare services, patient-provider relationships and interactions are evolving. Corporatization of medical care on the one hand and discourses of healthcare as a human right on the other have varied roles in shaping what trust looks like in healthcare [2, 4]. Having a closer look into the dynamic and evolving nature of trust in healthcare is vital in efforts to ensure health equity and address disparities in access to healthcare.

Lack of trust is a growing concern but a less discussed issue that impacts access and utilization of health services. For example, a study showed lack of trust is significantly associated with the utilization of routine check-ups and prostate-specific antigen tests and mammograms [5]. It is reported that trust in U.S. healthcare declined swiftly moving down from 73% of residents having confidence in the healthcare system and medical leaders in 1966 to only 34% of residents in 2012, and 18% in 2017 [3]. Some of the reasons for the continued erosion of trust included cultural insensitivity and discounting of symptoms by providers, providing incorrect information, inattention to service users’ needs, and not engaging and listening to patients’ concerns [6]. In addition to these individual and interpersonal level causes, lack of trust is fuelled by historical and structural problems in the healthcare system and contributes to the growing disparities in access and utilization of healthcare services [5].

Although lack of trust in healthcare is prevalent across populations in the U, S., research shows that the problem is deep rooted, and remains complex among minoritized and underserved communities [7]. For example, it was reported that compared to non-Hispanic Whites, non-Hispanic Black individuals are less likely to trust their physicians, and are more likely to be worried about personal privacy, and the potential for harmful experimentation in hospitals [8]. A number of studies documented that lack of trust is highly widespread and is one of the main barriers among African Americans hindering their ability to access and utilize healthcare services [9–11]. The legacy of past racially discriminating events such as the Tuskegee Syphilis Study, as well as sustained systemic racism and abuses substantially eroded African American’s trust in the healthcare system [12]. Lack of trust not only constrains access and utilization, but also impedes African Americans’ participation in clinical trials thereby affecting the development of effective medical and health interventions [13].

While the issues of trust in healthcare is considered critical in ensuring health equity, research remains inadequate and is mainly focused on the African American population, and only in relation to historic events like the Tuskegee Syphilis Study [14]. This created the following two major gaps: (1) it leaves out the growing and diverse minority groups, including immigrants and refugee populations from the discussion, and (2) by focusing on past discriminatory events, it ignores the current systemic discriminations reinforced by unjust policies and regulations that perpetuate distrust on healthcare among these diverse populations. Hence, this study intends to address the above gaps by exploring how lack of trust affects African immigrant women’s healthcare experiences in the U.S. with particular focus on Ethiopian immigrant women (EIW). The study mainly focuses on both personal and structural level factors associated with trust and how they shape Ethiopian immigrant women’s access and utilization of Primary healthcare services (PHC). The study intends to answer the following questions:

How does lack of trust affect Ethiopian immigrant women’s healthcare experience?

How does lack of trust affect Ethiopian immigrant women’s healthcare experience?

What are the factors contributing to lack of trust in healthcare utilization of Ethiopian immigrant women?

What are the factors contributing to lack of trust in healthcare utilization of Ethiopian immigrant women?

Methods

Research design

The study employed a qualitative cross-sectional design [15–17]. The qualitative design helped to investigate the lived experiences and challenges of trust among EIW in accessing PHC in the U.S. It also helped to generate exploratory information on the main factors behind the erosion of trust in healthcare and develop in-depth understanding of EIW’s healthcare experiences [18].

Sampling and recruitment

A mix of purposive and snowball sampling was utilized to recruit participants. Given the hard-to-reach nature of the immigrant population, the use of a mixed approach helped to achieve the recruitment goals. The selection criteria were: (1) identify as an Ethiopian immigrant; (2) identify as a female; (3) be 18 years and older; (4) arrived in the U.S. within the last five years; and (5) speak Amharic or English languages. Participants were recruited using flyers dispatched through two organizations working with immigrant communities, and word of mouth [19]. Participants used email and phone to contact the researcher and enroll in the study. First, two participants were selected to pilot test the interview guide and input from the pilot interviews were used to improve the clarity and organization of the interview questions. After finalizing the interview guide, 21 participants were recruited to conduct the main data collection.

Data collection

The study proposal was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of [ANONYMIZED]. A pilot-tested semi-structured interview guide (See Appendix A) was used to collect through one-on-one, in-depth interviews that lasted between 45 and 90 min. Interviews took place both in-person (n = 5) and remotely (phone and zoom, n = 16). Data saturation was reached after the 21 interviews, and it was decided to end recruitment. Interviews were audio-recorded and stored in the researcher’s password-protected computer. To ensure confidentiality, data were stored using pseudo names, and all identifiable information was removed from the interview transcripts. Participants were oriented about the purpose of the study, the research process, confidentiality, and the terms of their participation before interviews took place. Verbal consent was gained from each participant.

Research team

The author is male, was a Ph.D. candidate at the time of data collection and is trained in qualitative data collection and analysis. The author conducted all the interviews. Demographic information was collected using a structured questionnaire before each interview session (See Appendix B). The author established relationship with Refugee Immigration Services and Ethiopian Community Center to recruit participants. Participants were informed about the researcher’s goals in carrying out the study.

Analysis

The study followed an inductive thematic analysis process to identify emergent themes and categories from the raw data. The thematic analysis started by organizing data into accessible folders, transcribing verbatim and cleaning for errors and inconsistencies. The transcripts were then de-identified for any sensitive information and read repeatedly to develop familiarity and to keep important memos. Then a comprehensive codebook was developed based on the themes and patterns identified in the data. The author coded the transcripts using the Nvivo12 software and code reports were generated. Reports under each code were classified and interpreted based on themes and subthemes. Finally, structural, and textural description of EIW’s experiences and views on trust in PHC were presented.

Various techniques were applied to ensure rigor. Peer debriefing was conducted to assess de-identified transcripts, the codebook, methodological appropriateness, and the presentation of findings. Member checking was also performed with two participants who were willing to review and comment on the emergent themes and preliminary findings. Efforts were made to provide thick descriptions and direct quotes to increase trustworthiness. To ensure accurate representation of participants views, the researcher engaged with the data from prolonged period of time and used interview notes and memos to support the analysis process. The researcher applied reflexivity to reduce the influence of his positions including nationality, gender, linguistic and subjective biases in data collection and analysis. The 32-item Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was used to ensure rigor and adequate reporting of the methods and findings of the study [20, 21].

The thematic analysis uncovered five factors that contributed to the lack of trust among EIW and were perceived to have affected their PHC access and utilization: (1) reliance on medication (2) preference of traditional homemade remedies (3) faith and its influence on trust and PHC decisions (4) lack of transparency in PHC, and (5) discrimination in PHC. Pseudonyms were used after each quote to maintain confidentiality. The following section presents each of the major themes.

Reliance on Medication in the U.S. Healthcare erodes Trust

Participants believed that PHC in the U.S. is highly reliant on medication/drugs and that erodes their trust. First, coming from a less-medication-intensive cultural and healthcare environment, most participants stated that they are hesitant to fully accept the healing power of modern medicine/drugs, and some avoided taking all prescribed medications. The strong dependence on medications in the U.S. PHC is opposite to their experience in Ethiopia where providers tend to suggest fewer prescription drugs. For example, Ametsash said “My blood test showed diabetes and I don’t take all the prescribed medicine. I wanted to control it through a healthy diet” Hager added, “I don’t take prescribed drugs immediately. I will come home and prefer my body to heal by its own natural process and mostly I hesitate to take medicine”. Participants agreed that the over-emphasis on medication in the U.S. PHC is a red flag. Meba said, “Whatever the issue is, they tend to rush into prescribing medicine. I believe there are a lot of issues that can be addressed through diet, exercise, or something natural”.

Second, for some participants, past experiences of ineffective prescribed drugs increased distrust on modern medicine and the U.S. PHC. Seble said:

When you take medicine, you expect that it will cure you. That is not the case here. Medicine here (in the U.S.) is a way to hook you up with the never-ending system that keeps you jumping from one drug to another. I usually have a headache. But I prefer to get enough sleep than to take medicine. I am afraid of going to healthcare places. (Seble)

Similar stance was also held by participants who work in the healthcare system. Haben, a nurse, added,

Here (in the U.S.) there is this culture that portrays everything can be treated by taking medicine/drugs. Society here seems to accept this drug culture. I can’t understand it. I am a nurse and I don’t often see recommendations to address issues through natural and dietary mechanisms. (Haben)

Third, most of the participants believed that reliance on modern medicine in the U.S. is associated with profit making and greed in the pharmaceutical industry. The influence of such a belief in EIW’s health-related decision-making was clear in the interviews. Gelila stated, “The healthcare system here is a business and I don’t trust it”. Some participants shared, when they have a chance, they will try to consult with Ethiopian professionals before taking medications. Hager said, “During my pregnancy, I had a lot of pain, and the physician prescribed me medicine. I didn’t trust the judgment and consulted friends with a medical background, they said ‘no’ and I didn’t take it”.

Preference of traditional homemade remedies

Contrary to their views on modern medicine in the U.S. PHC, most participants stated they have trust in the healing effects of traditional remedies. Participants shared that they would prefer to try traditional homemade remedies first and delay healthcare services if it is not serious. As Maya said, “Homemade remedies like black oil, lemon, ginger, and others are my protections. To be honest, I prefer them over (modern) drugs”. Ruth also added “My children don’t take the flu shot, no. I treat them with some Ethiopian honey and ginger, that is it”. Some participants believe that using traditional remedies reduces the risk of side effects and the danger of drug dependence. Ametsash shared, “I use fenugreek (), garden cress (), Ruta chalepensis () and black seed then I mix them all together and take it daily”.

Faith and its influence on Trust and PHC decisions

When asked directly, most participants were hesitant to admit that their faith impacted their trust and decision making in utilizing PHC but most of them opened up about it during the in-depth conversations. Participants avoided seeking PHC or undertaking some procedures that contradict their religious beliefs or expectations. A typical case shared by many participants was a fetal deformity test which most of them declined to undertake. Except for one participant, Marda, all other participant mothers shared that they were told that there might be a risk of deformity on the fetus and a test is needed to decide whether to terminate the pregnancy or not. Participants, including those with a nursing background, expressed that they were not familiar with the recommended tests until they moved to the U.S. Samiya, who was a mid-wife nurse in Ethiopia, said this:

They told me I might give birth to a deformed baby because my protein level is high, and they need to get a sample from me and the fetus’ spine. They threatened me and I was very anxious. Imagine how devastating this could be. I didn’t agree and I said I will accept what Allah gives me. If I weren’t a midwife myself, the stress would have killed me. (Samiya)

According to participants, such experiences left them very uncomfortable and forced them to be suspicious of the healthcare system. Most claimed that it was against their faith, and they cannot question God’s/Allah’s decision but accept what they will be given. Participants also stated that they exchanged this information and consulted with other EIW who gave birth in the U.S. before making decisions. Some also asked for further explanations. Maya said:

When the nurse told me that, I said ‘I need to ask the doctor’. I asked the doctor ‘is this mandatory or is this for research?’ She said it is not mandatory and I said ‘well I don’t need it. I am a Christian and I take what God gives me. It still feels weird. (Maya)

For participants like Gelila, the PHC in the U.S. cannot be trusted in general. She said, “My belief is in God, not in the healthcare system. I don’t want to go to the hospital for every health issue unless it is serious”. Some participants discussed COVID-19 as an example to demonstrate the central role of their faith in making healthcare decisions. For example, Meba strongly believed that the COVID-19 pandemic is not real and had spiritual and political motives behind it, so she avoided getting vaccinated. She said, “I don’t trust the vaccine and what it is made from. It has a connection with human fetus and abortion, which is forbidden in my religion”. Halima, 33, although she has been vaccinated for COVID-19, said “I don’t totally believe in it, and I took the vaccine endorsed by the Catholic church. I rather trust God, and my future decisions to use service like reproductive health will depend on what my church says”.

Although most participants expressed allegiance to their religious values and reliance on their faith in making healthcare decisions, there was one exceptional participant, Marda, who said she was able to revisit her religious values that are in contradiction with healthcare since moving to the U.S. She said,

I was one of those naïve Ethiopians who thinks abortion is bad, republicans are good, children are good, the unborn matter. I came here and saw how politicized health care itself is. I had an eye-opening experience as I went through two miscarriages, the care I received with my miscarriage was abortion so if your body couldn’t naturally completely expel the miscarriage. I know that abortion is a wonderful thing for those women instead of putting them through that pain and then the eventual death of the baby and putting their body through that trauma of caring for this non-viable pregnancy….My views, my religious perspective on abortion got significantly challenged since I came here due to my own personal experience as well as the health literacy I have right now compared to when I was back home. (Marda)

Lack of transparency in PHC

The interviews uncovered that most participants believe that the PHC system lacks transparency hence cannot be trusted. For example, participants believe that there is a lack of transparency on vaccine preparation and dissemination. For example, Samiya said “In Ethiopia, vaccines are open and prepared in front of parents. Here you don’t see the nurses when they prepare the vaccine. You will wait outside and when they are ready, they will call you in. It makes me suspicious”. Part of the distrust is related to participants’ belief that the healthcare system treats immigrants differently or discriminates them. Some participants clearly stated that they do not feel comfortable taking drugs, as they suspect that immigrants and people of color could be subjected to drug trials or experiments without their knowledge. Etalem said, “I am afraid they would test new drugs on immigrants/foreigners because we don’t have the privilege to ask why because of our legal status and the fear of avoiding deportation”. Hager also said, “Pregnancy and childbirth-related death is higher among Blacks, and it makes me question the integrity of the system. I feel anxious whenever I go to healthcare”.

For some participants, getting treated and having a diagnostic history makes them anxious. They believe that having a medical history that can be accessed by authorities or any other unknown party can make them vulnerable, and they feel afraid of that. For example, Meba said, “The medical history is very centralized and accessible even after years. I sometimes feel afraid the information might be used at some point to harm me medically, or employment or even immigration-related eligibilities”.

Discrimination in PHC

The interviews also revealed that experiencing discrimination eroded EIW’s trust in the PHC system. Participants reported that being Black, African and an immigrant multiplies the layers of discrimination and increases their vulnerabilities for discrimination. Hager shared “We are not only Black, but we are also foreigners with no experience to deal with the racial tension in the U.S., the accent, and linguistic limitations. So, we suffer a lot because some consider us undeserving of services.” Mistreatments and discriminatory acts by receptionists and providers deepen insecurities and uncertainties among EIW about the care they receive. As Black people, participants stated that they were subjected to racial stereotypes in the healthcare. For example, on her first pregnancy, Marda was scared about the pain of labor and asked her provider who replied, “Black women have better pain resistance than White women, so you don’t have to worry much”. Similar encounters were shared by other participants who could not get proper pain medication and management because of the ‘black is pain resistant’ or ‘Blacks abuse pain killers’ stereotypes. Yetim who works in healthcare confirms, “Providers avoid giving pain killers for Black patients, even after labor and it clearly shows their biases”.

In addition, participants shared that they struggled because some providers would not take their concerns as valid or credible. Participants believed that providers discredit their words, pain and any concerns they bring up. Such experiences led to misdiagnosis and pressuring participants to undertake unwanted procedures. For example, Meba shared that “the physician made me take a pregnancy test because he didn’t believe what I said”. According to her “they prefer to listen to their stereotypes than a Black woman’s words”. Participants also indicated that some providers would automatically assume that immigrants, particularly those from Africa and other developing world, as burdens on the healthcare system and treat them differently. Marda shared “We were repeatedly asked the question ‘what brought you to the U.S.’ while we they have all my documentation that shows I am a professor working at the university.” Besides their own experiences, participants also referred to reports on disparities in maternal and infant mortality in the U.S. where Black are disproportionately affected compared to other races, mainly White. The combination of their lived experience and awareness about the injustice in the healthcare significantly wrinkled their trust in PHC in the U.S.

Discussion

This study explored how lack of trust affects African immigrant women’s PHC access and experience with particular focus on Ethiopian immigrant women (EIW). Since the issue of trust in healthcare is not well studied among diverse minority groups, except for studies on Africa Americans, the study plays a pivotal role in initiating and expanding the discourse around immigrants’ trust on PHC in the U.S. and how it shapes their healthcare experiences. The overall findings from the study demonstrated that lack of trust played a central role in immigrant women’s healthcare decisions, mainly resulting in delays or avoidance of care. The main themes emerged from the interviews can be categorized and discussed under personal and structural factors that cause and perpetuate lack of trust among EIW in accessing PHC.

At a personal level, EIW believe that the PHC in the U.S. is highly reliant on medication which is different from what they are used to in Ethiopia. According to EIW, unlike providers in the U.S., providers in Ethiopia do not tend to prescribe medicine for every case. Instead, they tend to advise non-medicine options, such as getting enough sleep, giving it time, or taking herbs. EIW’s critical view of medication reliance in the U.S. PHC is associated with their belief that the healthcare system in the U.S. is profit oriented and is manipulated by the pharmaceutical industry that prioritizes money over people’s lives. This belief is further strengthened by instances that increase suspicion such as occasional ineffective medicine, the higher tendency of providers to prescribe drugs, or receiving notices to stop taking a drug because it is no longer produced. The beliefs held by EWI could also signal toward the possibility of misinformation having a strong influence on their views and utilization of PHC. Although research documented the impact of health misinformation and its impact on vaccination and global pandemics such as COVID-19, Ebola and Zika Virus; there is gap in investigating and documenting the problem among immigrants and their decision making in PHC [22]. The findings a previous study that argue health misinformation and beliefs could contribute to widening access and health disparities, and needs further in-depth investigation [23].

Opposite to their views on modern drugs/medications, EIW demonstrated a favorable view toward traditional homemade remedies. Similar findings were reported by a previous survey on Ethiopian immigrants in the U.S [24]. Distrust in medications and preference of alternative medicine was also reported previously among Somali immigrant women that was specific to mental health (depression) medications because of stigma [25]. The study also supports previous reports that culture and faith play a central role in shaping immigrants’ views of modern medicine and medication adherence [26–28]. Particularly, contradictions with faith and religious beliefs were stated as reasons for EIW’s hesitance to undertake fetal deformity test (e.g., amniocentesis) during pregnancy. The findings strengthen previous research that reported the prevalence of resistance to prenatal screening among immigrant women, particularly for religious reasons [29, 30].

In addition to the personal-level perceptions and beliefs, EIW also shared structural issues that affected their trust in PHC system and services. The first aspect is lack of transparency in PHC services that made it difficult for EIW to develop trust on various tests, vaccines, and medications. Examples of such uncertainties included not knowing what the amniocentesis (fetal deformity test) could cause to their unborn baby, or not getting clear information about potential side effects of prescribed medicines. The lack of transparency discussed by EIW is strongly associated with another structural factor which is inherent biases and systemic discrimination toward immigrants. Both perceived and lived discriminations were transpired in the study findings as reasons for eroding trust and avoidance of PHC among EIW. EIW are afraid of being subject to secretive medical/research trials without their knowledge. These concerns resonate with problems of trust among African Americans and Native Americans in engaging in clinical trials due to unethical and stigmatizing research practice [31, 32]. EIW’s awareness about the disproportionate infant and maternal death, and unethical past and present medical practices against minorities was evident in the findings. It demonstrates, despite the knowledge gaps in navigating PHC, immigrants are aware and alert to potential harms in the healthcare system.

Beyond their awareness and perceptions, the lived discriminatory experiences further jeopardized EWI’s trust in PHC services. Ethiopian immigrant women experienced a muti-layered discrimination as females, immigrants, Blacks and Africans. The findings demonstrated that racial stereotypes experiences by African Americans and other persons of color in the U.S. healthcare also apply to Black immigrants but with more complex and added lines of discrimination [33]. Ethiopian immigrant women were looked at as burdens, pain resistant, untrustworthy, and incompetent to understand healthcare information. Despite their socioeconomic status, type of insurance or nature of legal status, EIW experienced discrimination and were often seen as someone who takes advantage of the healthcare system. In some instances, limitations in language and accent were weaponized against them. The findings support previous reports that provider biases and stereotypes could lead to mistrust of healthcare among users [34]. The lack of trust in healthcare also extended to the fear of losing legal status and employment opportunities because of potential violation of privacy, sharing of health information to authorities and other parties. Hence, immigrants’ trust issues in healthcare could be more complex and need extensive and deeper investigations.

Implications for practice

The findings of the study suggest that healthcare providers need to actively seek information about the healthcare system in immigrants’ countries of origins. Doing so will help them develop a better understanding of the needs of immigrants and provide them with customized information that address patients’ knowledge gaps. To build trust around medications, PHC providers should present immigrants with adequate information on the rationale of every prescription drug and explain potential side effects in clear terms. Effective use of quality translation services could help efforts aimed at increasing immigrants’ awareness around the nature of PHC in the U.S. and building trust among immigrants. This also will help tackle possible health misinformation that could shape immigrants’ perceptions and adherence to healthcare recommendations. However, it is also important to note that there is a need to reevaluate medical procedures and the medication culture in U.S. healthcare system and explore options in alternative medicine when serving immigrant communities. More conversations around alternative medicine (traditional and homemade remedies) should take place in PHC and care involving immigrants.

Building trust requires effective and consistent communication that validates immigrants’ views and lived experiences. Creating a helping environment that puts immigrants at the center and establishing a collaborative helping process is vital to build trust in the helping process. In order to achieve this, providers need to develop cultural awareness and competence in care relationships. This includes making sure that medical procedures and prescriptions are not in conflict with the beliefs/faith of immigrant service users. In addition, providers should adopt a strengths-based approach that identifies and capitalizes on the potentials of immigrant EIW in the care process [35]. It is also critical to find the right balance between the procedures and cultural aspects that could lead to unwanted consequences on the wellbeing of immigrants. Since EIW tend to rely on other immigrants for information and guidance, it is important to work with immigrant networks, faith-based, and community organizations in raising awareness and building trust.

Another important implication to restore trust is the need to mitigate discrimination against immigrants in PHC. Although measures such as promoting inclusive, culturally, and linguistically relevant healthcare practice are important, it is not adequate to leave these responsibilities to individual providers and professionals. The problem needs more structural reform and the dismantling of systemic problems that perpetuate discrimination. Measures such as diversifying the healthcare workforce, instilling accountability, revisiting healthcare training curriculums and procedures, and producing professionals with the capacity to address health issues among minoritized and immigrant populations are vital. Taken together, building trust in healthcare among EIW and other immigrants requires a coordinated effort from individual professional-level to structural and policy levels. It also needs multi-sectoral collaboration between health, education and immigration sectors to create conducive care environment for immigrants.

Limitations and implications for Research

This study is limited by the nature of the sample. Due to the hard-to-reach nature of the population, selecting participants within a single immigration category as well as a single state or geographic region in the U.S. was not possible. Hence, some of the experiences and accounts of participants presented in the findings vary and may not apply to all participants. Future studies may focus on each individual immigration category and specific geographies or individual states to understand the unique circumstances of immigrants and suggest more contextualized solutions. The study focused on lack of trust in healthcare and its contributing factors. Hence, the findings highlighted the negative experiences of EIW and the problems in the healthcare system. Future researchers are encouraged to explore the experiences of those who exhibit trust in the healthcare and the current strengths in the healthcare system that promote trust. Despite the stated limitation, this is study has important implications for healthcare research among immigrants. As the first known study on EIW’s healthcare experience and the study of healthcare trust among African immigrants, it paves the way for future research. Since there is great deal of research on other access-related issues including acculturation, linguistic and communication barriers, this study will help to bring attention to the issues of trust/distrust and help understand its impact in healthcare access among immigrants. The study also demonstrated that, because of their unique circumstances, the factors behind immigrants’ trust in healthcare could be different from native-born minorities such as African Americans and other minoritized groups. It also showed the need to expand research efforts to other unresearched and minoritized groups such as immigrants, religious and sexual minority groups.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study significantly contributes to understanding the trust dynamics in primary healthcare (PHC) access among Ethiopian immigrant women (EIW) in the U.S., highlighting the critical role trust plays in their healthcare decisions. It reveals that personal beliefs about the U.S. healthcare system, influenced by cultural practices and misinformation, lead to a preference for traditional remedies and a skepticism towards medication. Structurally, a lack of transparency and experiences of discrimination within the healthcare system exacerbate distrust. The findings underscore the necessity for healthcare providers to gain a deeper understanding of the healthcare backgrounds and cultural beliefs of immigrant communities. By integrating this understanding with efforts to address systemic biases and provide clear, culturally sensitive communication, there can be a significant improvement in trust. Furthermore, addressing misinformation and incorporating alternative medicine into discussions could bridge gaps in healthcare access. This research not only illuminates the specific experiences of EIW but also sets a foundation for broader inquiries into healthcare trust among diverse immigrant populations, underscoring the need for multi-faceted and culturally competent approaches in healthcare delivery.
