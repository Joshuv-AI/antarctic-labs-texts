Associations between heart failure-related quality of life and clinical parameters in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: a cross-sectional analysis of data from a multicentre Japanese registry
Authors: Masao Saotome, Keitaro Akita, Shigetaka Kageyama, Sayumi Suzuki, Kazuto Ohno, Masamitsu Kamakura, Ryuzo Nawada, Chiei Takanaka, Yasushi Wakabayashi, Takahiro Kanda, Kei Tawarahara, Masahiro Muto, Masaki Matsunaga, Satoru Suwa, Yasuyo Takeuchi, Hiroki Sakamoto, Hideki Saito, Kazusa Hayashi, Nobuyuki Wakahara, Kyoko Unno, Takenori Ikoma, Ryota Sato, Keisuke Iguchi, Terumori Satoh, Makoto Sano, Atsushi Sakamoto, Kenichiro Suwa, Yoshihisa Naruse, Hayato Ohtani, Yuichiro Maekawa, Masao Saotome
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between a quality of life (QOL) score and clinical parameters in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).DesignA multicentre cross-sectional study.SettingWe analysed data from the Searching for Atrial Fibrillation and Early Recruitment of Heart Failure in HCM registry, collected between 2018 and 2023.ParticipantsPatients with HCM (n=499) aged ≥18 years from 12 institutions (Shizuoka Prefecture, Japan) were consecutively enrolled.Outcome measuresClinical parameters, along with data from a short form of the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ-12), were collected. The association between each clinical parameter and the KCCQ-12 score was analysed. Clinical parameters with a significant univariable association (p<0.05) were used in a multiple regression model to investigate independent clinical parameters associated with the KCCQ-12 score. The coefficients and relative importance of each parameter included in the multiple regression model were also evaluated.ResultsIn the univariable analysis, KCCQ-12 scores exhibited significant associations with 21 clinical parameters, including sex, left ventricular morphology and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The multiple regression model with 12 parameters that had a significant univariable association exhibited an adjusted R2 of 0.48. In this model, the PSQI (standardised coefficient −0.39; p<0.001), history of sustained ventricular tachycardia or fibrillation, history of atrial fibrillation (−0.23; p<0.001), female sex (−0.19; p=0.035), systolic blood pressure (0.17; p=0.040), lateral E/e prime (−0.17; p=0.049) and A wave (−0.14, p=0.049) were independently associated with the KCCQ-12 score with high relative importance.ConclusionsIn patients with HCM, we investigated the association between the KCCQ-12 score and various clinical parameters. PSQI, as well as known heart failure-related clinical parameters, was significantly associated with the KCCQ-12 score. Visualising the associations of various clinical parameters with the KCCQ-12 score will help physicians to consider factors linked to the decline in QOL in patients with HCM.

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a common genetic heart disease, affecting approximately 0.2%–0.5% of the general population,1 2 and most patients with HCM remain asymptomatic throughout their lives. However, some patients develop symptoms many years after the initial diagnosis of HCM and often manifest heart failure (HF) symptoms. In patients diagnosed with HCM before the age of 40 years, comprising approximately one-third of the HCM population, 65% will experience HF symptoms with lifestyle restrictions during their lifetime.3 Moreover, severe HF symptoms in HCM are associated with a worse prognosis,4 often necessitating therapeutic intervention.1 2 However, in clinical practice, patients with HCM often manifest HF symptoms due to complex factors, which make it difficult to identify the primary contributors to their symptoms and reduced quality of life (QOL). For example, HF symptoms in HCM arise from both reduced cardiac output (forward HF) and elevated filling pressures (backward HF) and are further exacerbated by various comorbidities.4 Therefore, clinicians often face challenges in determining which factors or comorbidities should be prioritised to improve their QOL.

The Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) is a self-administered 23-item questionnaire that quantifies physical limitations, symptoms, QOL and social interference. It is a validated instrument for assessing HF-related QOL and provides meaningful information regarding the health status of patients with cardiomyopathies,5 6 including HCM.7 The KCCQ-12 score is a shortened version of the original 23-item KCCQ score developed to improve the feasibility. A good correlation between KCCQ and KCCQ-12 scores has already been reported.6 Since previous studies have rarely explored which clinical status contributed most to the decline in QOL in HCM populations, this study investigated the relationships between KCCQ-12 scores and various clinical parameters through multiple regression analysis, using a multicentre registry of Japanese patients with HCM.

Methods

Study design and sample

We have conducted a multicentre cross-sectional study, using the Searching for Atrial Fibrillation and Early Recruitment of Heart Failure in HCM (SAFER-HCM) registry, among patients with a diagnosis of HCM aged ≥18 years.8 Patients with HCM in 12 institutions (Shizuoka Prefecture, Japan) were consecutively enrolled between 2018 and 2023. Patients who were diagnosed with HCM and agreed to participate in the study were included. Patients who did not agree to participate or declined after enrolment were excluded from the study.

Diagnosis of HCM was established as reported previously,8 specifically by echocardiographic evidence of left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy (maximum LV wall thickness ≥15 mm) disproportionate to systemic loading conditions. HCM phenocopies, including Fabry disease, Noonan syndrome, Danon disease and cardiac amyloidosis, were ruled out through detailed medical history assessments, comprehensive physical examinations and additional tests.8 For patients with a family history of HCM, we regarded an LV wall thickness of ≥13 mm as HCM. Patient demographics, medical history and self-administered QOL questionnaires (ie, KCCQ-126 and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)9 10) were collected at the time of enrolment. The KCCQ-12 consists of 12 items grouped into four subdomains (physical limitation, symptom frequency, QOL and social limitation) with scores ranging from 0 (indicating the worst health status) to 100 (indicating the best).6 The overall KCCQ-12 score is calculated as the mean of four subdomains. PSQI is a self-reported questionnaire that assesses sleep quality and disturbances over a 1-month period, yielding a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality.9 10 Both the KCCQ-12 and PSQI scores were treated as continuous variables. Laboratory data, parameters of echocardiography, cardiac MRI and nocturnal oximetry were reported based on the results acquired closest to the time of enrolment.

Initially, the association between each clinical parameter and the KCCQ-12 score was examined. Specifically, for the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class and the PSQI, the association with each domain of the KCCQ-12 (ie, physical limitation score, symptom frequency score, QOL score and social limitation score) was also evaluated.

Statistical analysis for multiple regression model

Normally distributed data are expressed as mean±SD, non-parametric data as median (25th–75th percentile) and categorical data as percentages. For baseline characteristics, the Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon test or Pearson’s χ2 test was used as appropriate. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Among baseline characteristics, medications and intervention history were excluded from the subsequent multiple regression analysis since these variables are highly dependent on physicians’ and patients’ preferences. Among the remaining parameters, those with significant associations with KCCQ-12 scores were used to develop a multiple regression model. In this selection, univariable p<0.05 was used as the threshold of significance for minimising the model overfitting. Regarding the missing values, continuous variables were imputed using the median imputation, and categorical variables were imputed using the mode imputation, for the development of the multiple regression model. The performance of the multiple regression model was assessed using both unstandardised and standardised coefficients for each selected parameter. Finally, the significance of each parameter in the multiple regression model was assessed using the VarImp function from the R caret package. As a sensitivity analysis, another multiple regression model was developed using parameters that had <20% of missingness. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (V.2024.04.2).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

In the present study, 499 patients with HCM were enrolled as presented in baseline characteristics (table 1 and online supplemental table S1). KCCQ-12 scores were significantly correlated with NYHA functional class (NYHA I, median 95 (25th–75th percentile 85–98); NYHA II, 79 (60–90); NYHA ≥III, 64 (48–83); Spearman’s ρ=-0.54, p<0.001, figure 1), and each domain of the KCCQ-12 score also showed significant correlation (online supplemental table S2 and figure S1). The distribution of KCCQ-12 scores varied according to LV morphology (non-obstructive HCM, 91 (75–97); obstructive HCM, 81 (60–91); Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon p<0.001, figure 2) and sex (female, 79 (60–91); male, 91 (80–97); Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon p<0.001, figure 3). In contrast, KCCQ-12 scores did not significantly vary with age (table 1). KCCQ-12 scores were significantly correlated with PSQI (Spearman’s p<0.001, figure 4) and each domain of the KCCQ-12 score also showed significant correlations (online supplemental table S3 and figure S2).

Besides the NYHA functional class, which had already known associations with the KCCQ-12 score, 21 parameters were significantly associated with the KCCQ-12 score in the univariable regression analysis (table 1). A strong correlation was not seen within the 21 parameters (figure 5). The multiple regression analysis revealed that female sex, systolic blood pressure, history of sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF), history of atrial fibrillation (AF), A wave, lateral E/e prime and PSQI were independently associated with the KCCQ-12 score (p<0.05, table 2). The adjusted R2 of the model was 0.48 and the F-statistic was 8.66 (p<0.001). The diagnostic plots indicated that the assumptions of the regression model, including linearity, normality of residuals and homoscedasticity of residuals, were reasonably satisfied, and no influential outliers were identified (online supplemental figure S3). The variable importance plot identified PSQI as the parameter most strongly associated with the KCCQ-12 score, followed by a history of VT/VF and then a history of AF (figure 6). In the sensitivity analysis using parameters with <20% of missingness, another multiple regression model had an adjusted R2 of 0.38 and showed the PSQI was still the most important variable associated with the KCCQ-12 score (online supplemental table S4 and figure S4).

Discussion

Summary of findings

In this study, we first demonstrated a multiple regression analysis within a multicentre cross-sectional study in patients with HCM to examine the relationships between KCCQ-12 scores and various clinical parameters. The univariable regression analysis identified 21 parameters significantly associated with the KCCQ-12 score, other than the NYHA functional class. In the subsequent multiple regression analysis, the PSQI, history of sustained VT/VF, history of AF, female and systolic blood pressure were independently correlated to the KCCQ-12 score. This is the first study to investigate the association of various clinical parameters with HF-related QOL in patients with HCM.

Results in context

Although the symptoms of HCM vary depending on its phenotypes and/or stages, the primary manifestation is associated with HF, with dyspnoea on exertion occurring in over 90% of patients.4 The KCCQ-12 score is well known to be associated with both clinical status and the outcome of HF.11 12 It is more sensitive than the NYHA functional class in predicting subsequent clinical outcomes and is independently associated with reduced mortality and HF hospitalisation when an improvement of 5 or more points is achieved.13 Additionally, HF, accounting for 21.3%, is known to be the second leading cause of death in patients with HCM, following VT/VF of 31.9%.14 Although the association between the KCCQ-12 score and HF symptoms has been previously validated in patients with HCM,7 its relationship with various comorbidities has not yet been investigated.

In our study, we found that the KCCQ-12 scores in patients with HCM were associated with previously established factors related to HF symptoms, such as a history of VT/VF,15 AF,16 female sex17 and diastolic dysfunction.18 Age, a well-known deteriorating factor in HF, was not associated with KCCQ-12 scores in our cohort, although a previous single-centre study found a significant association in patients with HCM.18 This may be attributed to the enrolment of older patients with HCM (median age, 70 (25th–75th percentile 58–76) years), compared with other HCM registries. Along with the previous report, there was a significant association between peak pressure gradient of the LV outflow tract and the KCCQ-12 score.18 That implies the obstructive HCM is one of the main drivers of declined QOL, further buttressed by the concordant relationship between systolic blood pressure and the KCCQ-12 score, that is, lower systolic blood pressure was associated with a lower KCCQ-12 score. The association between female sex and the severity of HCM has been previously suggested.17 Although precise mechanisms remain unclear, the study indicated that it might be linked to delayed clinical recognition, possibly reflecting societal biases influencing sex-specific evaluation and diagnosis of HCM.17

We identified a significant association between the PSQI, a QOL score related to sleep-disordered breathing (SDB)9 10 and the KCCQ-12 score in patients with HCM. Similar trends were seen between PSQI and each component of KCCQ-12 (ie, physical limitation score, symptom frequency score, QOL score and social limitation score). Although univariate analysis revealed that T90, time spent below 90% oxygen saturation,19 20 also correlated with the KCCQ-12 score, the relationship was insignificant in the multiple regression analysis. PSQI incorporates questions about sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances and daytime dysfunction.9 10 Therefore, it directly reflects subjective sleep quality compared with objective scores for diagnosing SDB, such as apnoea-hypopnoea index, oxygen desaturation index and T90.9 10 This likely explains its stronger correlation with the KCCQ-12. However, the PSQI is also an established screening tool for SDB, especially in patients with cardiovascular diseases.21 22 While the correlation between sleep quality and the KCCQ-12 score has been reported in patients with general HF,23 it has not been reported in patients with HCM. SDB is a common comorbidity in HCM, with a prevalence of 55%–70%.24 25 The presence of SDB in patients with HCM is associated with worsening HF symptoms,25 reduced exercise capacity26 and increased incidence of arrhythmic events.27 28 Therefore, careful attention should be given to SDB in the HCM population with reduced QOL. Furthermore, although longitudinal follow-up should be needed, enhancing sleep quality may serve as a potential target for improving the QOL in patients with HCM.

Although our model demonstrated a reasonable explanatory power (adjusted R2=0.48), more than half of the variance in the outcome remains unexplained. This may be attributable to unmeasured factors, residual confounding or inherent variability in patient characteristics, including psychosocial factors. Future research is warranted to identify additional determinants that may enhance predictive accuracy.

Clinical implications

We conducted the multiple regression analysis to determine how much each comorbidity contributes to the decline in QOL in patients with HCM. Since HF symptoms in patients with HCM arise from multifactorial causes,4 patients often feel anxious and confused about their disease status and symptoms.29 Therefore, identifying the primary contributors to their HF symptoms is crucial for effective communication between physicians and patients. Especially when modifiable factors such as SDB,30 AF,31 pressure gradient of LV outflow tract32 and body weight33 are estimated as major contributors to HF symptoms, sharing this information facilitates the next treatment steps with shared decision-making.34 Furthermore, although further extended analysis and validations are needed, a prediction model that quantifies the contribution of each comorbidity to the decline in QOL may enhance the understanding of HF symptoms in patients with HCM and improve their management.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the present study are as follows: (1) patients with HCM were recruited from 12 diverse facilities, including primary care clinics, secondary distinct hospitals or tertiary care facilities, to enhance generalisability; (2) the patient profiles in our study were analysed using a wide range of modalities, in addition to those available in a contemporary HCM registry and (3) the importance of the clinical parameters was visualised with a relative importance plot.

The limitations of this study are as follows: (1) as our study focused on a single race/ethnicity (ie, Asian), careful validation is required when applying our findings to other ethnic groups; (2) variations in the diagnostic test accessibility across facilities resulted in missing data, such as laboratory data and cardiac MRI parameters, potentially influencing the accuracy of the regression model. Additionally, median and mode imputations were applied to handle missing data, which had a limited ability to incorporate different distributions within the data; (3) due to the lack of genetic data on HCM in our study, further research should investigate the impact of genetic variation on QOL scores; (4) since this study employed the KCCQ-12 score, the self-efficacy domain, which is included only in the original KCCQ score, was not reflected in the QOL assessment and (5) finally, longitudinal studies are required to further explore the relationship between cardiac outcomes and KCCQ-12 scores in patients with HCM.

Conclusions

In the multicentre registry of patients with HCM, we identified a significant association of the KCCQ-12 score with PSQI, alongside several HF-associated clinical parameters. This exploratory study may facilitate physicians in considering various clinical factors linked to the decline in QOL in patients with HCM.
