Mechanical inhibition of dissipation in a thermodynamically consistent active solid
Luca Cocconi, Michalis Chatzittofi, Ramin Golestanian

Abstract
The study of active solids offers a window into the mechanics and thermodynamics of dense living matter. A key aspect of the non-equilibrium dynamics of such active systems is a mechanistic description of how the underlying mechano-chemical couplings arise, which cannot be resolved in models that are phenomenologically constructed. Here, we follow a bottom-up theoretical approach to develop a thermodynamically consistent active solid (TCAS) model, and uncover a non-trivial cross-talk that naturally ensues between mechanical response and dissipation. In particular, we show that dissipation reaches a maximum at finite stresses, while it is inhibited under large stresses, effectively reverting the system to a passive state. Our findings establish a mechanism potentially responsible for the non-monotonic behaviour observed in recent experimental measurements of entropy production rate in an actomyosin material.

The frameworks that have so far been developed to study such dense arrays of active particles suffer from a number of key limitations. Firstly, they typically overlook the force-free nature of self-propulsion mechanisms that are designed to work in low Reynolds number conditions

;
Najafi and Golestanian 2004
;

;

;

Secondly, and most importantly for the purpose of this work, they are not thermodynamically consistent, in the sense that they lack a systematic access to the relevant equilibrium limit, which can be formally linked to the vanishing of a microscopic affinity and the re-establishment of global detailed-balance. Thirdly, they typically lack a systematic accounting of all relevant contributions to the entropy production, which can for example be dominated by the dissipation in the fluid medium
Daddi-Moussa-Ider et al. 2023

A number of works have begun to address these shortcomings by expanding the state space to include an explicit chemical cycle

This non-trivial cross-talk between chemical and mechanical degrees of freedom becomes apparent under the effect of external forces

Chatzittofi et al. 2024a

Baerts et al. 2013

Sarracino et al. 2016

These phenomena point to a key feature of active matter thermodynamics, namely, that the rate of free energy dissipation must depend dynamically on the local mechanical environment. In the context of MIPS, for instance, one may wonder to what extent the dissipation of a particle embedded in the bulk of the condensed phase is inhibited due to compressive forces generated by the surrounding particles. Indeed, this contribution is absent in field theoretic descriptions of MIPS, in which the informatic entropy production is localised at phase boundaries

Seara et al. 2018
;
Chen et al. 2024

We do so by introducing a new class of active solids, the elementary unit of which is an active elastic element whose dynamics is accounted for in a thermodynamically consistent way. The active elastic element—

Let us consider a dimer of passive Brownian particles connected by an active elastic element in one dimension (Fig.
1
). The positions of the two particles are denoted
x_{1,2}(t)
, while
\ell(t)
indicates the instantaneous rest length of the elastic element.
For simplicity, we henceforth assume the associated internal state
\gamma
to be discrete, with
\gamma\in\{A,B,C\}
and
\ell_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{L}=\{\ell_{A},\ell_{B},\ell_{C}\}
, noting that three is the minimum number of states required to construct a non-equilibrium chemical cycle
Cocconi et al. 2020
. Without loss of generality, we introduce a unit of length,
\bar{\ell}
, which represents the typical scale of the rest length of the springs.
This analysis is generalised to the case of a continuous internal variable in
sup
. Upon including the effect of an external (contractile or extensile) force of magnitude
F
, we can write the total internal energy as

\displaystyle U(x_{1},x_{2},\ell)=U_{\rm spring}+U_{\rm rep}+U_{\ell}+F(x_{2}-x_{1}),

(1)

where
U_{\rm spring}(x_{1},x_{2},\ell)=\frac{1}{2}k[(x_{2}-x_{1})-\ell_{\gamma}]^{2}
is the elastic contribution,
U_{\rm rep}(x_{1},x_{2})
arises from short-range hard-core repulsion between the beads, and
U_{\ell}(\gamma)
is the energy associated with the internal state of the spring
\gamma
. Denoting
\mu_{\rm p}
as the single-particle mobility, the Langevin equation for the inter-particle displacement
L\equiv x_{2}-x_{1}
thus reads

\dot{L}(t)=2\mu_{\rm p}[-F-k(L-\ell_{\gamma})-\partial_{L}U_{\rm rep}]+\sqrt{4\mu_{\rm p}k_{\rm B}T}\,\eta,

(2)

where
k_{\rm B}T\equiv\beta^{-1}
is the thermal energy scale and
\eta
is a Gaussian white noise of unit covariance,
\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. The rest-length evolves according to a Markov jump process with master equation
\dot{P}(L,\gamma)=M_{\gamma\delta}(L)P(L,\delta)
, where
M_{\gamma\delta}
is the transition rate from
\delta
to
\gamma
(with summation assumed over repeated indices). Thermodynamic consistency of the discrete dynamics is ensured by enforcing local detailed balance at the level of the transition rate matrix
Maes 2021
, namely

M_{\gamma\delta}(L)=\frac{k_{m}e^{\beta\Delta\mu\varepsilon_{\gamma\delta}/6}}{1+e^{\beta{\Delta U_{\gamma\delta}(L)}}},

(3)

where
k_{m}
is a microscopic rate, whose inverse sets a natural unit of time.
Here,
\Delta\mu
is the affinity of the chemical cycle defined to be driven in the clockwise direction
A\to B\to C\to A
, and
\varepsilon_{\gamma\delta}=\pm 1
depending on the direction of the transition. Moreover,
\Delta U_{\gamma\delta}(L)
denotes the change in total internal energy associated with a transition from state
\delta
to state
\gamma
, i.e.
\Delta U_{\gamma\delta}(L)=\frac{k}{2}\left[(L-\ell_{\gamma})^{2}-(L-\ell_{\delta})^{2}\right]+U_{\ell}(\gamma)-U_{\ell}(\delta)
. We thus have
\ln(M_{\gamma\delta}/M_{\delta\gamma})=\beta[\Delta\mu/3-\Delta U_{\gamma\delta}(L)]
and
\ln(M_{\gamma\delta}/M_{\delta\gamma})=-\beta[\Delta\mu/3+\Delta U_{\gamma\delta}(L)]
for clockwise and anti-clockwise transitions, respectively.

The dynamics given in Eq. (
2
) satisfies the fluctuation-dissipation relation and, upon setting
\Delta\mu=0
, the joint distribution of
L
and
\ell_{\gamma}
relaxes to an equilibrium steady state with Boltzmann measure
P_{\rm eq}(L,\gamma)\propto\exp[-\beta U(L,\gamma)]
characterised by zero entropy production. The marginal steady-state probability distribution of the internal state,
\pi_{\gamma}
, is obtained by integrating
P_{\rm eq}
with respect to the inter-particle displacement

\displaystyle\pi_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(F)}\;{\rm Erfc}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2k}}(F-k\ell_{\gamma})\right)

\displaystyle\times\exp\left(\frac{\beta F}{2k}(F-2k\ell_{\gamma})-\beta U_{\ell}(\gamma)\right),

(4)

where
Z(F)
is a force-dependent normalisation factor. Here, we have eliminated the hard-core repulsion
U_{\rm rep}
by restricting the support of
P_{\rm eq}
to
L\in(0,\infty)
. Equation (
4
) illustrates the importance of thermodynamic consistency, as it leads to a cross-talk between chemical and mechanical degrees of freedom, thereby enabling mechanical forces to effectively renormalize the energy landscape in chemical space. In particular, in the limit
|F|\to\infty
we observe an accumulation of probability in the chemical state
\ell_{\gamma}\in\mathbb{L}
that maximises
F-2k\ell_{\gamma}
. For
F<0
, this corresponds to the largest accessible rest length (see Fig.
2
). This force-dependent distortion of the chemical energy landscape is further discussed in
sup
for the case of continuous
\ell
(see also
Reimann et al. 2001
).

To determine the steady-state joint probability
P(L,\gamma)
for non-vanishing driving,
\Delta\mu\neq 0
, we perform an adiabatic approximation
Pavliotis and Stuart 2008
. We consider a separation of time scales between fast
L
and slow
\ell_{\gamma}
dynamics, corresponding to the limit
\mu_{\rm p}\to\infty
in Eq. (
2
). The joint probability thus factorises as
P(L,\gamma)=\pi_{\gamma}P(L|\gamma)
, with
P(L|\gamma)
being the steady-state conditional probability density of the process (
2
) for constant
\ell(t)=\ell_{\gamma}
. Here,
P(L|\gamma)\propto{\rm exp}[-\beta(k(L-\ell_{\gamma})^{2}/2+FL)]
with support
L\in(0,\infty)
.
After integrating the associated Fokker-Planck equation for
P(L,\gamma)
with respect to
L
, we obtain an effective Markov jump process governed by the adiabatically-averaged jump matrix
\overline{M}_{\gamma\delta}(F)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}dL\ M_{\gamma\delta}(L)P(L|\delta)
, which leads to the steady-state solution of the master equation shown in Fig.
2
(a) for different values of
F
.
With this solution at hand, we can then explore how the dissipation, quantified through the rate of entropy production
Seifert 2012
;
Cocconi et al. 2020
, as well as the average elastic energy stored in the active spring depend on both
F
and the driving affinity
\Delta\mu
. In order to calculate the entropy production
\dot{\sigma}(F,\Delta\mu)
(that we make dimensionless by reporting it in units of
k_{\rm B}
), we draw on Schnakenberg’s cycle-based decomposition
Schnakenberg 1976
, noting that only the chemical cycle possesses a non-vanishing affinity
\Delta\mu
. Thus
\dot{\sigma}=\beta\Delta\mu J_{\rm chem}
with
J_{\rm chem}(F)=\overline{M}_{BA}\pi_{A}-\overline{M}_{AB}\pi_{B}
the uniform current in the cycle. The results are shown in Fig.
2
(b). Remarkably, entropy production displays a non-monotonic dependence on
F
, with a maximum at a small positive force and inhibition for large (positive and negative) forces. Therefore, we find that under sufficiently large forces the active elastic element behaves effectively like a passive spring.
Additionally, we observe that the average elastic energy
\langle U_{\rm spring}\rangle
increases in the presence of compressive forces when
\Delta\mu\neq 0
(see Fig.
2
(c)).

Active solid—

We now proceed to treat our active elastic element as a fundamental unit from which we assemble a TCAS. We work in one dimension and impose periodic Born-Von Karman boundary conditions, effectively working on a ring of size
R
. A given bead with label
i
, where
i\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
, is located at position
x_{i}(t)\in[0,R)
and connected to its left and right nearest neighbours by active springs with internal states
\ell_{i-1}(t)
and
\ell_{i}(t)
, respectively, as shown schematically in Fig.

\mathcal{U}=\sum_{i}U^{(i)}(x_{i},x_{i+1},\ell_{\gamma_{i}})

\eta_{i}(t)

\langle\eta_{i}(t)\eta_{j}(t^{\prime})\rangle=K_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})

K_{ij}\equiv\delta_{ij}-\delta_{\langle ij\rangle}/2

The contribution to the total energy from each spring,
U^{(i)}

1

All chemical cycles are driven by an identical affinity
\Delta\mu

i

with
\Delta U_{\gamma\delta}
replaced by
\Delta U^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}

Finally, hard core repulsion between nearest neighbour beads is described via a Weeks-Chandler-Anderson potential

Conclusion—

We have introduced a class of thermodynamically consistent active solids assembled from elementary active elastic elements, whose rest-length dynamics are controlled by a dissipative chemical cycle. This setup allows us to illustrate a fundamental yet often overlooked property of strongly interactive active matter, namely, the non-trivial interplay between their mechanics and thermodynamics. In particular, we demonstrate that dissipation can be inhibited in the presence of large stresses, effectively reverting the active material to a passive state. The entropy production rate exhibits a non-monotonic behaviour as a function of the stresses, and in particular, reaches a maximum at some non-vanishing intermediate force strength. Our framework is in good agreement with experimental measurements of the energy dissipation in crowded systems of myosins that generate active stresses, and it can serve as a minimal model for similar systems
Seara et al. 2018
. Our predictions can be tested in a number of biological systems, including DNA tethers, bio-polymer networks, and biological membranes (see Fig.
1
), as well as crowded enzymatic condensates
Dindo et al. 2025
. While hydrodynamic effects were ignored in the discussion above, we have verified that our findings are not affected qualitatively for sufficiently small systems when they are included, as detailed in
sup
.

Due to the importance of out-of-equilibrium phase separation in many biological functions
Brangwynne et al. 2009
;
Sokolova et al. 2013
;
Hyman et al. 2014
;
Zwicker et al. 2017
, characterising the role of mechanical inhibition of activity in the bulk of active matter condensates remains a fundamentally important open question. From an engineering perspective, a more rigorous understanding of mechanochemical coupling in active solids could also be leveraged to design “smart” mechanosensitive materials capable of generating chemical signals in response to local mechanical perturbations
Haswell et al. 2011
;
Garcia-Manyes and Beedle 2017
.
In addition to providing a template for further studies of the thermodynamics of active solids in higher dimensions, the 1D passive-active bead-spring model studied here can be considered as a prototype for a thermodynamically consistent active polymer once embedded in 3D space (cf.
Winkler and Gompper 2020
;
Bianco et al. 2018
;
Locatelli et al. 2021
;
Anand and Singh 2018
).

\mathcal{P}_{N}(L_{1},...L_{N},\gamma_{1},...\gamma_{N},t)
,
which evolves according to the Fokker-Planck equation

\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{N}=\sum_{i}^{N}\left[\sum_{\delta_{i}}M^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{N}(...,\delta_{i},...)-\partial_{L_{i}}J_{i}\right],

where

\displaystyle\mu_{\rm p}^{-1}J_{i}

\displaystyle=-\beta^{-1}\partial_{L_{i}}\mathcal{P}_{N}-k[\Delta L_{i+1}+\Delta L_{i-1}-2\Delta L_{i}]\mathcal{P}_{N}

\displaystyle-[\partial_{L_{i+1}}U_{\rm rep}^{(i+1)}+\partial_{L_{i-1}}U_{\rm rep}^{(i-1)}-2\partial_{L_{i}}U_{\rm rep}^{(i)}]\mathcal{P}_{N},

(7)

The total state space is thus
\mathbb{S}=[0,R)^{\otimes N}\otimes\mathbb{L}^{\otimes N}

2(N-1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}(L_{i},\gamma_{i})=\left(\prod_{j\neq i}^{N}\sum_{\gamma_{j}}\int dL_{j}\right)\mathcal{P}_{N},

(8)

and truncate the resulting BBGKY hierarchy by using the closure approximation
\mathcal{P}_{2}(L_{1},L_{2},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\simeq\mathcal{P}_{1}(L_{1},\gamma_{1})\mathcal{P}_{1}(L_{2},\gamma_{2})
. We obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{P}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta=1}^{3}M_{\gamma\delta}\mathcal{P}_{1}+\beta^{-1}\mu_{\rm p}\partial_{L}^{2}\mathcal{P}_{1}

\displaystyle+2\mu_{\rm p}\partial_{L}\big([F_{1}-k(L-\ell_{\gamma})-\partial_{L}U_{\rm rep}]\mathcal{P}_{1}\big),

(9)

where

\displaystyle F_{1}=\sum_{\delta}\int dL^{\prime}[-k(L^{\prime}-\ell_{\delta})-\partial_{L^{\prime}}U_{\rm rep}]\mathcal{P}_{1}(L^{\prime},\delta).

(10)

We thus observe that Eq. (
Appendix A: Coarse-graining of the chain—
) is takes on the form of an effective single-dimer Fokker-Planck equation (presented in Eq. (
2
)) with an external force
F_{1}
. Equations (

) and (

The active elastic element: continuous internal state

To generalise the active dimer model introduced in the main text, we now assume that the spring rest length
\ell(t)
is controlled by an internal phase-like continuous variable
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, i.e. in the form of
\ell(\phi(t))

Agudo-Canalejo et al. 2021
;
Chatzittofi et al. 2025b
;
Chatzittofi et al. 2025a

\phi

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(L,\phi)=\frac{k}{2}\left[L-\ell(\phi)\right]^{2}-\frac{\Delta\mu}{2\pi}\phi+U_{\phi}(\phi)+FL+U_{\mathrm{rep}}(L),

(S1)

where the first term is the elastic contribution, the second term the non-equilibrium driving and
U_{\phi}
is the washboard part of the chemical dynamics. The remaining two terms are the same as in main text, accounting for external forces and hard core repulsion between beads, respectively.
Consequently, we have that the Langevin dynamics for
L
and
\phi

\displaystyle\dot{L}

\displaystyle=-2\mu_{\rm p}\partial_{L}\mathcal{V}+\sqrt{4\mu_{\rm p}k_{\rm B}T}=2\mu_{\rm p}[-F-k(L-\ell(\phi))-\partial_{L}U_{\rm rep}]+\sqrt{4\mu_{\rm p}k_{\rm B}T}\eta_{L},

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{\phi}\partial_{\phi}\mathcal{V}+\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T\mu_{\phi}}=\mu_{\phi}\left[\frac{\Delta\mu}{2\pi}+k(L-\ell(\phi))\ell^{\prime}(\phi)-\partial_{\phi}U_{\phi}\right]+\sqrt{2\mu_{\phi}k_{\rm B}T}\eta_{\phi}.

(S2)

with
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}
and
\mu_{\phi}
the length and phase mobilities, while
\eta_{L}
and
\eta_{\phi}
are uncorrelated Gaussian white noise terms.
At equilibrium,
\Delta\mu=0
, the joint probability is simply given by the Boltzmann measure with respect to the total energy function

P(L,\phi;F)\propto\exp\left[-\beta\mathcal{V}(L,\phi)\right].

(S3)

Far from equilibrium, we assume that the dynamics of the bead is relatively fast (as in the main text), by implementing the limit of
\mu_{\mathrm{p}}\to\infty
. This leads to the adiabatic approximation
P(L,\phi)=P(L|\phi)P_{\phi}
, where the conditional probability distribution
P(L|\phi)
is given by

P(L|\phi)=\mathcal{N}_{\phi}\exp\left[-\beta\left(\frac{k}{2}(L-\ell(\phi))^{2}+FL\right)\right],

(S4)

where the support is restricted to the range
L\in[0,\infty)
to account for the hard-core repulsion between the beads. Upon normalisation, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\phi}^{-1}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2k\beta}}\exp\left(\frac{\beta}{2k}F[F-2k\ell(\phi)]\right)\mathrm{Erfc}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2k}}[F-k\ell(\phi)]\right).

(S5)

Under this approximation, the full Fokker-Planck equation of the driven process can be integrated with respect to
L
to obtain

\partial_{t}P_{\phi}=\mu_{\phi}\partial_{\phi}\left[k_{\rm B}T\partial_{\phi}P_{\phi}-\left(\frac{\Delta\mu}{2\pi}-\partial_{\phi}U_{\phi}+\ell^{\prime}(\phi)\int_{0}^{\infty}dL\ k(L-\ell(\phi))P(L|\phi)\right)P_{\phi}\right].

(S6)

The phase dynamics is thus governed by an effective potential
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\phi)
of the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\phi)=-\frac{\Delta\mu}{2\pi}\phi+U_{\phi}-\int^{\phi}d\phi^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}(\phi^{\prime})\int_{0}^{\infty}dL\ k(L-\ell(\phi^{\prime}))P(L|\phi^{\prime}).

(S7)

where the second and third terms comprise the conservative part of the non-equilibrium dynamics.
In words, the phase dynamics experiences an
F

P(L|\phi)

k=0

As an example, we consider that during a cycle there are three minima that correspond to the three discrete chemical states studied in the main text. In particular, we take
U_{\phi}(\phi)=-v\cos 3\phi

\ell(\phi)

\ell(\phi)=L_{0}+\bar{\ell}\cos(\phi+\delta)

\delta
is an arbitrary phase and
L_{0}
is the average length of the spring. The upper panel of Fig.

\exp\left(-\beta\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)

) at the steady-state, we use the definition

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{lim}_{t\to\infty}\frac{\beta}{2\pi t}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\dot{\phi}(t^{\prime})\circ\partial_{\phi}\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\phi(t^{\prime})),

(S8)

\displaystyle=\beta\Delta\mu\ \mathrm{lim}_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi t}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi=\beta\Delta\mu J_{\mathrm{chem}}

(S9)

where
\circ

II
Details of numerical simulation

Here we summarize the integration method used to simulate the dynamics of the active solid. In this simulation, the main difficulty corresponds to correctly accounting for the concurrent Langevin dynamics of the beads and the time-inhomogeneous Markov jump process governing the internal state of each active elastic element.

For the dynamics of the bead positions,
x_{i}(t)\in[0,R)
with
i=1,...,N
, we implement a simple stochastic Euler forward method in the Itô discretisation with timestep
\Delta t=10^{-6}k_{m}^{-1}
,

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+\Delta t)=x_{i}(t)+\Delta t\mu_{\mathrm{p}}F_{i}(t)+\sqrt{2\mu_{\mathrm{p}}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\Delta t}\,\xi_{i}(t),

(S10)

where
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
F_{i}=-\partial_{x_{i}}U(\{x_{j}\},\{\ell_{j}\})
is the conservative force originating from the combination of nearest-neighbour elastic and repulsive interactions

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\{x_{i}\},\{\ell_{i}\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}U^{(i)}(x_{i},x_{i+1},\ell_{i}),

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{k}{2}[(x_{i+1}-x_{i})-\ell_{i}]^{2}+U_{\rm rep}^{(i)}(x_{i+1}-x_{i}).

(S11)

Since we assume periodic (Born-von Karman) boundary conditions, we effectively enforce
x_{i+1}\equiv x_{i}+R
for
i=N
and
x_{i-1}=x_{N}-R
for
i=1
.
We take the repulsive potential to be of the Weeks-Chandler-Anderson type, corresponding to a truncated Lennard-Jones potential with no attractive component

U_{\rm rep}^{(i)}(L_{i})=\begin{cases}4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{a}{L_{i}}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{a}{L_{i}}\right)^{6}\right]+\epsilon,\quad&\text{for }L_{i}\leq 2^{1/6}a,\\
0,\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(S12)

For the discrete-state Markov jump process governing the internal state of the active elastic elements, we implement a Gillespie algorithm with thinning
Moller and Waagepetersen 2003
, which we will describe here briefly. For simplicity, we assume that the internal state space
\mathbb{L}=\{\ell_{A},\ell_{B},\ell_{C}\}
is a 3-cycle.
We recall that the transition rate matrix associated with the
i
-th active elastic element reads

M^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}(L_{i})=\frac{k_{m}e^{\beta\Delta\mu\varepsilon_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}/6}}{1+e^{\beta{\Delta U^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}(L_{i})}}},

(S13)

with
\varepsilon_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}=\pm 1
depending on the direction of the transition. Here, we set
\varepsilon_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}=1
for clockwise transition (defined as
A\to B\to C\to A
), while
\varepsilon_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}=-1
for anti-clockwise transitions (defined as
C\to B\to A\to C
). The factor
\Delta U^{(i)}
in the denominator is written explicitly as

\Delta U^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}(L_{i})=\frac{k}{2}\left[(L_{i}-\ell_{\gamma_{i}})^{2}-(L_{i}-\ell_{\delta_{i}})^{2}\right]+U_{\ell}(\gamma_{i})-U_{\ell}(\delta_{i}),

(S14)

and corresponds to the change in total energy (
S11
) upon a transition of the
i
-th active elastic element from state
\delta_{i}
to state
\gamma_{i}
. Since
L_{i}
is itself a stochastic process governed by Eq. (
S10
), the concurrent Poisson processes determining the internal state transitions are time-inhomogeneous and therefore care needs to be taken to correctly sample the corresponding waiting time distribution. To do this, we first notice that the total rate of any one active element transitioning away from its current state is at all times bounded as follows

\bar{\lambda}=2N\mathrm{cosh}(\beta\Delta\mu/6)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\gamma_{i}\neq\delta_{i}}M^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}}(L_{i}).

(S15)

The thinning algorithm then consists in sampling exponentially-distributed waiting times
w
between transitions according to a homogeneous Poisson process with characteristic rate
\bar{\lambda}
(e.g. by defining
w=-\ln{u_{1}/\bar{\lambda}}
where
u_{1}
is generated from a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
). After the waiting time
w
has elapsed, the current total transition rate
\lambda(t)
is computed according to

\displaystyle\lambda(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\gamma_{i}\neq\delta_{i}}M^{(i)}_{\gamma_{i}\delta_{i}(t)}(L_{i}(t)),

(S16)

where
L_{i}(t)
and
\ell_{i}(t)
again denote the current values of the interparticle displacement and instantaneous rest-length for the
i
-th active spring. Finally, a second uniformly distributed random number
u_{2}\in[0,1]
is sampled and a single transition occurs if
u_{2}<\lambda(t)/\bar{\lambda}
. As per the usual Gillespie algorithm, a specific transition is selected amongst all the accessible ones based on the corresponding rates, which requires sampling a third uniform random number
u_{3}
.

All simulations are initialised with the internal states
\ell_{i}
sampled from
\mathbb{L}
with a uniform distribution and
x_{i}=i/(NR)
equally spaced. Data is only recorded after an initial relaxation time
T_{\rm relax}=10^{2}k_{m}^{-1}
to avoid transient effects due to relaxation of the initial configuration.

IV
Effective pressure and compressibility

We define the dimensionless effective (virial) pressure in the bulk of the active solid in terms of the derivative of the average total elastic energy with respect to the volume, here given by the chain length
R
, namely

P(R)\equiv-\bar{\ell}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\langle\beta U_{\rm spring}\rangle=-\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial\langle\beta U_{\rm spring}\rangle}{\partial\rho^{-1}}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{N}\frac{\partial\langle\beta U_{\rm spring}\rangle}{\partial\rho},\quad\quad\quad U_{\rm spring}=\sum_{i}U^{(i)}_{\rm spring}.

(S17)

The pressure vanishes at the natural rest length of the active solid and is negative (positive) under stretching (compression), as seen in Fig.
S6
. It also exhibits a weak dependence on the driving affinity
\beta\Delta\mu
. The effective (inverse) compressibility is thus given by

\kappa^{-1}\equiv-R\frac{\partial P(R)}{\partial R}.

(S18)

As shown in Fig.
S6
, the compressibility is peaked around
P(R)=0
, corresponding to the natural rest length of the active solid. While this trend does not change qualitatively in the presence of activity, we observe a decrease in the magnitude of the compressibility around
P(R)=0
, which indicates increased resistance to compression when dissipation is large (cf. Fig. 4(b)).

V
Hydrodynamics

Living and active matter systems are found predominantly in suspensions. Assuming that our one-dimensional bead-spring model is embedded in a three-dimensional Stokesian fluid, we now introduce hydrodynamic interactions between the beads in the thermodynamically consistent way along the lines of Refs.
Lau and Lubensky 2007
;
Kobayashi et al. 2023
. In particular, we first introduce the symmetric Oseen mobility tensor for particles constrained to one dimension as follows

K_{ij}(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{1}{4\pi\eta|x_{i}-x_{j}|},

(S19)

for
i\neq j
, and
K_{ii}=1/(6\pi\eta a)
, where
a
is the sphere radius and
\eta
is the fluid viscosity.
The mobility tensor reduces to the diagonal mobility tensor used in the main text in the limit
a\ll|x_{i}-x_{j}|
, where
6\pi\eta a=\mu_{\mathrm{p}}^{-1}
.
The discretised, Itô multiplicative Langevin equation governing the position of the
i
-th bead then reads

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+\Delta t)

\displaystyle=x_{i}(t)+\Delta t\left[K_{ij}F_{i}(t)+k_{\rm B}T\frac{\partial K_{ij}(t)}{\partial x_{j}}\right]+\sqrt{2k_{\rm B}T\Lambda_{nm}(t)\Delta t}\ Q_{in}(t)Q^{-1}_{mj}(t)\,\xi_{i}(t),

(S20)

which generalises Eq. (
S10
). Here,
\Lambda_{ij}(t)\equiv Q^{-1}_{in}(t)K_{nm}(t)Q_{mj}(t)
is the time-dependent (via its dependence on
\{x_{k}(t)\}
) diagonalised matrix. The second term in the square brackets is required to ensure global detailed balance in the equilibrium limit
Lau and Lubensky 2007
.
In simulations, the last term is computed from the Oseen tensor
K
and by calculating its principal square root via Cholesky decomposition
Benoit 1924
. The existence of such decomposition is contingent on
K
being positive definite, a condition which fails to apply when the distance between beads becomes comparable to their size and the far-field approximate hydrodynamic description breaks down
Kobayashi et al. 2023
. This occurs with finite probability, albeit transiently, due to thermal fluctuations, particularly in the presence of strong compressive forces. In simulations, we reduce the likelihood of this occurrence by choosing the accessible rest length of the active elastic elements to be sufficiently large. Comparison of Figs.
S4
and
S7
confirms that the introduction of hydrodynamic interactions does not qualitatively affect the results presented in the main text for sufficiently small systems.

VI
Local measure for the suppression of dissipation

The non-monotonic dependence of the average entropy production rate per bead on the bead density (as shown in Figs. 4 and
S4
) is a clear indication of the overall inhibiting effect of mechanical stress on dissipation. This is however a global measure, and as such it does not explicitly resolve how the dissipative current associated with a particular active elastic element is affected by changes in the local mechanical environment. To demonstrate that our physical picture holds at the local scale, we first introduce the empirical chemical current associated with the
i
-th elastic element as follows

\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\rm C,E}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{3}\sum_{r}\chi^{(i)}_{r}\delta(t-t_{r}^{(i)}),

(S21)

where
\{t_{r}^{(i)}\}
is the point process of transitions in the internal state of the elastic element and
\chi^{(i)}_{r}\in\{+1,-1\}
indicates the “handedness” of the transition, which we defined to be
+1
for clockwise transitions and
-1
for anticlockwise transitions. By isotropy of the chain, the average of the empirical current is proportional to the average entropy production rate per bead through the cycle affinity,
\dot{\sigma}_{\rm tot}/N=\beta\Delta\mu\langle\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\rm C,E}\rangle
. Analogously, we can define a
conditional
empirical current for a particular value of the spring’s length
L_{i}
, as follows

\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\rm C,E}(t|L_{i}=L_{c})\equiv\frac{1}{3}\sum_{r}\chi^{(i)}_{r}\delta(t-t_{r}^{(i)})\frac{\delta(L_{c}-L_{i}(t_{r}^{(i)}))}{P_{L_{i}}(L_{c})},

(S22)

where
P_{L_{i}}
denotes the steady-state probability density of the
i
-th spring length. Clearly,
\int d{L_{c}}P_{L_{i}}(L_{c})\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\rm C,E}(t|L_{c})=\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\rm C,E}(t)
since
\int d{L_{c}}\delta(L_{i}-L_{c})=1
.
Its average, denoted
\langle\mathcal{J}^{(i)}_{\rm C,E}(L_{c})\rangle
, gives us a sense of how strongly dissipative currents are suppressed due to local compression/stretching.
In particular, a sharply peaked dependence on
L_{c}
indicates strong mechanical inhibition of dissipation occurring as soon as the elastic element is forced away from its preferred length, while a flat profile indicates a weak mechano-chemical cross-talk.
Conveniently, this quantity can be obtained analytical by solving the master equation with transition rate (
S13
) for a single active elastic element at steady state after setting the interparticle distance
L_{i}
to a constant.

The average conditional current is plotted in Fig. 4 of the main text as a function of
L_{c}
for the particular case of
N=64
,
R=64
and
\mathbb{L}=\{0.9\bar{\ell},\bar{\ell},1.1\bar{\ell}\}
, for different values of
k
. For large
k
, it shows a clear non-monotonic behaviour with
L_{c}
. In other words, not only is dissipation inhibited globally under conditions where the average spring length is either much larger or much smaller than its accessible rest lengths, it is also the case that individual active springs tend to dissipate less when they are locally compressed/stretched.
