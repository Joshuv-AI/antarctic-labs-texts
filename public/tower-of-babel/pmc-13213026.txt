Free energy perturbation-derived identification of natural compounds targeting Mycobacterium tuberculosis thymidylate kinase
Authors: Rupesh V Chikhale, Md Ataul Islam, Vikramsinh Sardarsinh Suryawanshi, Ibrahim A Alsarra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb), the cause of tuberculosis, remains a major global health threat. TB spreads via airborne transmission through sneezing and coughing, thereby compromising respiratory health. The emergence of drug-resistant strains necessitates the development of new anti-tubercular agents. Natural products from plants, microbes, and marine sources are promising for drug discovery because of their structural diversity and biological activities. Current research seeks natural thymidylate kinase inhibitors to prevent TB, using methods such as binding-site identification, similarity searches, interaction analyses, and 100-ns MD simulations. Binding free energy is evaluated with MM-GBSA, and FEP studies identify molecules based on binding properties. In silico methods to explore natural compounds as TMPK putative inhibitors include fingerprint searches, AutoDock Vina docking, ADMET predictions, GROMACS MD simulations, and energy calculations. Molecular docking revealed binding energies of -12.10, -11.90, and − 13.40 kcal/mol for CNP0015705, CNP0363196, and CNP0217487, respectively, which are better than those of the co-crystal ligand and Lifirafenib. Moreover, the MM-GBSA-based binding energy ranged from − 14.50 to -22.86 kcal/mol, indicating a strong association between the proposed molecules and the TMPK protein. A virtual library was screened against Mtb TMPK, with promising candidates showing strong binding. Selected compounds CNP0015705, CNP0363196, and CNP0217487 preferentially bind to TMPK, as shown by various in silico analyses. This approach demonstrates the potential of natural products in drug discovery, highlighting their role in developing new therapies targeting TMPK.Keywords: Thymidylate kinase, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Molecular docking, Molecular dynamics simulation, Free Energy Perturbation, Virtual screeningSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) has a substantial global impact, affecting millions of people and posing significant challenges to public health worldwide. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), about 10.6 million cases of TB occur in 202 countries globally1. The emergence of drug-resistant strains, including multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB), has added more complications to the threat2. Recent reports of totally drug-resistant (TDR) tuberculosis have further complicated eradication efforts, as these strains show resistance to currently available anti-TB agents3. Thus, treating drug-resistant TB has now become a critical factor and requires more extended and more expensive courses of treatment. Currently, an expedition to new drug targets keeps researchers busy. In addition to exploring novel drug targets, the quest for effective compounds often relies on cutting-edge computational approaches.

In the modern Computer-Aided Drug Discovery (CADD) era, several proteins have been identified as potential drug targets, proving essential for bacterial survival and metabolism. A few noteworthy examples are the multidrug efflux pump (MmpL3), cell-wall biosynthesis enzyme (DprE1), respiratory enzyme (QcrB), etc4–6. One such drug target that proves crucial is Thymidylate Kinase (TMPK). The protein represents an essential consideration as it plays a vital role in a specific biological pathway or disease process7. TMPK is responsible for phosphorylation, wherein it transforms deoxythymidine 5’-monophosphate (dTMP) into deoxythymidine 5’-diphosphate (dTDP)8. In nucleotide metabolism, it is involved in the synthesis of thymidine monophosphate (TMP)2, a precursor of thymidine nucleotides crucial for cell DNA replication and repair processes. The biological significance of TMPK lies in its involvement in the deoxyribonucleotide9,10 synthesis pathway. The three-dimensional structure of TMPK reveals a conserved kinase fold with characteristic ATP-binding and substrate-binding sites11. Structural studies and computational approaches contribute to our understanding of TMPK’s catalytic mechanism and guide the design of inhibitors. Various small molecules have been reported and explored as potential inhibitors of TMPK, aiming to target its active site and impede its catalytic activity selectively12. Panda et al. presented a series of 3-Cyanopyridone and 1,6-Naphthyridin-2-one compounds based on high-throughput biochemical screening and a fragment-based approach using NMR (Fig. 1a-d). Molecules from these series showed hits and inhibitory activity ranging from 500 micromoles to 200 nanomoles, making them promising candidates for drug development. These molecules, also shown in Fig. 2, are non-thymidine-like inhibitors, which provided the crystal structure of ligand-bound Mtb TMK (Fig. 2)13. Mtb TMPK distinctly differs from human Thymidylate Kinase (hTMPK) in its sequential and structural features14. This makes Mtb TMPK a suitable target for drug therapy.

There are so many computational virtual screening studies of different targets of tuberculosis published in the literature journals, such as a structure-based virtual screening of diverse marine natural products to identify potential selective inhibitors of Mycobacterium tuberculosis enoyl-acyl carrier protein reductase (InhA), a key enzyme in mycolic acid biosynthesis with applied docking, ADMET prediction, molecular dynamics simulations, and binding energy analyses to prioritize lead compounds that showed strong interactions within the InhA active site and favourable pharmacokinetic profiles15. Another study reported a virtual screening-assisted discovery of novel natural product chemotypes from multiple databases (e.g., Specs, Enamine, NPACT-SANDB) with potential inhibitory activity against a specific enzyme target, using structure-based drug design approaches on the same InhA protein16,17. along with the above studies, as our research work regarding TMPK protein target lead identification, similar studies on TMPK also reported in scientific journals, such as the discovery of anti-methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus inhibitors by targeting thymidylate kinase (TMPK) via multi-step virtual screening, molecular simulations, and free energy calculations and Investigating Marine Natural Products as Potential Antivirals of Mpox Virus Replication by Targeting Thymidylate Kinase18,19.

The current study is significant because it uses high-throughput in silico screening combined with machine learning techniques to identify natural compounds that could inhibit TMPK protein [20]. The framework of this computationally efficient and comprehensive search algorithm encompasses essential and meticulous screening of a library of natural compounds, some of which share structural similarity with previously documented kinase inhibitors. These inhibitors are validated in advance using decoy set validation and positional assessment of the retrieved experimental complex. Pharmacophore-based atomic alignment further substantiates similarity searches and facilitates the optimisation of chemical space toward promising directions. In parallel, virtual screening performs multi-platform molecular docking analyses and estimates the binding free energy of the complexes, thereby enhancing the selection of lead molecules. Moreover, the drug likeness and toxicity screening predicted an assured drug safety feature. This was followed by comparative molecular dynamics simulations, binding energy calculations, and free-energy perturbation analyses of the complexes involving the most promising lead and standard compounds. The study’s comprehensive methodology validated the potential of three novel natural compounds (CNP0015705, CNP0363196, and CNP0217487) to inhibit or modulate activity by binding to the Mtb TMPK. This research could significantly impact the development of anti-tuberculosis therapies.

Materials and methods

Selection of the structural coordinates of the target protein

The primary prerequisite for drug discovery research is the availability of a high-resolution three-dimensional crystallographic structure of the TMPK protein with comprehensive atomic detail. The co-crystal structure of TMPK (PDB: 6YT1) was found to have a good resolution and to be structurally informative for the study in the RCSB-Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [21]. The selected PDB corresponds to a homodimer protein structure bound to a co-crystallised ligand, PK5 and citric acid [2]. One of the two chains, 214 amino acids long, was curated by removing heteroatoms and crystallised water. The curation was performed using PyMOL version 2.3.4. (https://www.pymol.org/) [22].

Formation of a library of standard compounds for data curation

The compound library utilised in this study was derived from the ChEMBL database repository [23], specifically a collection of compounds relevant to TMPK. The ChEMBL database, a comprehensive resource for bioactive molecules, was accessed to extract the TMPK-specific compound library. ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/) provides a wealth of curated and annotated chemical information, including bioactivity data, making it an ideal source for assembling a diverse and relevant compound collection. The resulting compound library comprised 445 unique compounds, each selected for its documented interaction with TMPK. This curated library forms the basis for subsequent computational analyses, including pharmacophore modelling, docking studies, and other structure-based drug design approaches. TMPK inhibitors with experimental validation were obtained from the ChEMBL database. Docking binding energies and reported IC₅₀ values were used to rank the compounds.

Lifirafenib (CHEMBL4209157, CAS No: 1446090-79-4) is an anticancer inhibitor that targets the Raf kinase and acts as an EGFR inhibitor, with an IC50 of 29 nM. It is not an anti-TB drug, and we found no articles reporting its study against TB targets. Most TMPK inhibitors belong to pyridopyrimidines, benzothiazinones, diarylureas and thiazolyl derivatives [23,24]. Lifirafenib does not structurally overlap with most of these molecules due to its bulky heteroaromatic core, multiple hydrophobic regions, and higher molecular weight [25]. However, it fits well within the Mtb TMPK binding site, forming strong, consistent interactions with surrounding residues, making it a suitable reference for our studies. Thus, it was selected as the reference molecule to set screening thresholds because it is biologically active, has been found safe for therapeutic use, has desirable ADMET properties, and shows promising docking interactions compared to other molecules in the dataset for targeting TMPK.

Standardisation of the derived inhibitors via decoy-validation

For a schematic and robust standardisation of the chemical space retrieved from ChEMBL, interaction validation is the utmost requirement. This validation is carried out by targeting the same receptor with a set of decoys and comparing the interaction affinities with those of the compounds from the initial library. The validation protocol begins with Molecular Docking of the ChEMBL-derived compounds. The docking program was executed considering the receptor as a rigid body in AutoDock Vina version1.2 (https://vina.scripps.edu/) [26]. The docking was confined to a grid covering all active-site residues of the protein. The grid area allocation is explained in a later section of this article. Ten compounds with the lowest IC50 values were selected to proceed to the subsequent steps. DUD-E database (https://dude.docking.org/), a decoy generator [27] was used to generate pseudo-positive compounds. For each standard molecule, 50 decoys were generated, and up to 500 decoys were generated against the 10 actives. The decoys were docked to the same target, and a list of binding affinities was prepared. The values for the decoy and active molecules were combined to generate ROC and AUC curves, along with the specificity and accuracy values for the observed dataset.

Screening of a large natural compound library following the ‘Similarity search algorithm’ against the COCONUT database

The COCONUT database (COlleCtion of Open Natural prodUcTs) (https://coconut.naturalproducts.net/) is a repository of over 100,000 organic chemical structures of medications, agrochemicals, and naturally occurring compounds, maintained by the Steinbeck group from the University Friedrich-Schiller in Jena, Germany. The database provides an option to retrieve its possession based on a similarity search. The similarity searches are ranked on Tanimoto scores ranging from 0 to 1 for each database compound in comparison to the query, fingerprints compared as a bit vector matching technique [28]. The 10 superior molecules considered for this new modality have a Tanimoto similarity range of 70%.

Scrutiny of the screened molecules based on ‘Ligand Pharmacophore Mapping’

Ligand pharmacophore mapping aims to identify key chemical arrangements within active molecules. Comparing these arrangements to other molecules helps us pinpoint the specific features crucial for the desired biological effect. The mapping was done on Discovery Studio Client 2022 (https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download) [29]. The ligands were prepared by converting their 2D structures into 3D representations, optimising the geometry, and assigning the required charges. Pharmacophore models, encompassing key features such as hydrogen bond acceptors, donors, hydrophobic regions, and aromatic rings, were generated for reference ligands and subsequently mapped onto the 3D structures of the selected compounds. A correlation between the mapped score and the corresponding binding affinities for the targeted protein was observed. The screened properties that modulated the correlation for these standard compounds were further assigned as the screening parameters for the newly driven bulk of natural compounds.

Molecular docking by AutoDock Vina

Molecular docking seeks to anticipate the preferred binding orientation of a ligand to its macromolecular target. This computational method predicts how potential drug candidates might interact with the protein’s active site, allowing us to prioritise and select molecules with the highest likelihood of therapeutic efficacy. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina version 1.2.0 (https://vina.scripps.edu/) employing the empirical scoring function. As briefed in the steps above, the current study utilises this docking protocol iteratively across the different segments of requirements. The docking experiment performed here is a rigid model in which the protein behaves as a rigid lock-like body, with the ligands targeted to the keyhole representing the active site. A rigid receptor was used to reduce computational complexity and maintain the experimentally determined active-site. computational complexity and maintain the experimentally determined active-site confirmation, as the active-site residues were well-defined. Protein flexibility was subsequently addressed through molecular dynamics simulations. A grid space configures the area of the active site. Throughout the study, the grid space is allocated at 96.95, 29.94, and 129.71 Å, corresponding to the x, y, and z axes. The box dimension is extended to 68 × 56 × 66 Å3 to encompass all interacting residues defined in the crystallised complex structure derived from the protein data bank.

The active site was validated by the revised docking (re-dock) of the co-crystallised ligand PK5. Following this, the decoy validation elaborated above was completed. The newly derived set of natural compounds was then set for the docking experiment with the same configurations. The screen list of candidate compounds is longer. So, for the current docking experiment, the program is executed utilising AutoDock Vina version 1.2.0 (https://vina.scripps.edu/) [24] via a Message Passing Interface (MPI), which runs the same program in parallel [30]. Multithreading speeds up docking operations. As a requirement, the protein and the subject ligands are formatted in the ‘pdbqt’ form for the docking program. During protein preparation, all the heteroatoms and water molecules were removed. Hydrogen atoms were added, and Kollman charges [31] were assigned to the protein crystal structure. All ligands were prepared through the command-line OpenBabel tool version 3.1.1 (https://openbabel.org/index.html) [32] by adding required partial charges and hydrogen atoms.

Re-evaluation of the molecular docking by PLANTS

Molecular docking through PLANTS version 1.2 (https://github.com/purnawanpp/plants) involves a sophisticated procedure for predicting the binding interactions between proteins and ligands. The methodology employs the PLANTS version 1.2 (https://github.com/purnawanpp/plants) software, utilising an algorithmic approach to explore the conformational space of ligands within the protein binding site [33]. The docking process integrates scoring functions that evaluate parameters such as van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interactions to assess the affinity and feasibility of binding between the protein and ligand. In more detail, PLANTS version 1.2 (https://github.com/purnawanpp/plants) employs a ligand-centric approach, in which the ligand’s conformational space is systematically explored to identify energetically favourable poses within the protein’s binding site. The algorithm uses a genetic algorithm for global optimisation, ensuring a comprehensive search for potential binding configurations. The scoring functions integrated into PLANTS version 1.2 (https://github.com/purnawanpp/plants) provide a quantitative assessment of predicted binding affinities. The ligand and protein structures are prepared appropriately and provided as ‘mol2’ files for optimal utilisation. The output from PLANTS version 1.2 (https://github.com/purnawanpp/plants) includes ranked poses based on binding affinity, aiding in identifying and prioritising potential protein-ligand interactions. This molecular docking approach, facilitated by PLANTS version 1.2 (https://github.com/purnawanpp/plants), proves instrumental in rationalising potential drug candidates and exploring molecular interactions at the atomic level.

Prediction of absolute binding energy through KDEEP

Prediction of protein-ligand absolute binding affinity through the KDEEP web server (https://playmolecule.com/KDeep/) relies on Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs) [34]. The method involves analysing eight pharmacophoric-like properties within the protein-ligand complex: hydrophobic, aromatic, hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor, positive and negative ionizable, metallic, and total excluded volume. This data is fed into a pre-trained DCNN model accessible at https://playmolecule.com/KDeep/, which was trained on the PDBbind v. 2016 database. Using this model, the KDEEP web server (https://playmolecule.com/KDeep/) computes the absolute binding energies between proteins and ligands. For KDEEP analysis, all ligands are converted to SDF files and stored in a single directory, while the protein remains in PDB format. An index Excel file with their paths is also included in the same folder. This setup ensures accurate and efficient predictions of protein-ligand binding affinities with KDEEP (https://playmolecule.com/KDeep/). The protein file, also in PDB format within that directory, is uploaded to KDEEP (https://playmolecule.com/KDeep/), which reports a mean absolute binding energy of 122 protein-ligand molecules in ΔG [kcal/mol]. Subsequently, KDEEP (https://playmolecule.com/KDeep/) analysis was performed on 122 conventional thymidylate kinase inhibitors to determine their absolute binding affinities.

Assessment of the pharmacological properties using pkCSM

The evaluation of ADMET properties is pivotal in the drug discovery process to ensure the safety of newly subjected molecules. The ADMET study was conducted on the above-screened 6324 compounds using the pkCSM web browser (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/) [35] 122 compounds were examined for compliance with ADMET standards, specifically their capacity to inhibit the TMPK protein. The study also examined other important ADMET features necessary for excellent therapeutic molecules to be filtered with acceptable ADMET cutoffs, such as possible inhibitors for further research. These compounds met the criteria and showed favourable ADMET profiles, making them attractive options for further drug development research. Out of the original 6324 molecules, 122 compounds were chosen for additional examination based on these criteria.

Comparative Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation of the selected complexes

Molecular Dynamics simulations were performed on selected docked complexes, using the standard protocol to assess the stability of the protein conformation and interactions with the identified compounds. MD is a vital method for understanding how the protein-ligand complex dynamically behaves. A 100-nanosecond MD simulation was run for each complex to observe atomic motion. The MD simulation was performed using GROMACS 2021.1 (https://manual.gromacs.org/2021.1/release-notes/2021/2021.1.html) on a Linux system equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2070 and a 10th-generation Intel Core i9-10885 H processor. The initial structure for the MD simulations was the receptor-ligand complex obtained from molecular docking. The ligand topology was generated from the SwissParam tool [36], whereas the protein topology was produced by the CHARMM36 force field [37]. The MD simulation was conducted with a time step of 2 femtoseconds, while maintaining a constant pressure of 1 atm and a temperature of 300 K. Each system was solvated utilising the TIP3P [38] water model and encapsulated within a cubic simulation box, ensuring a minimum distance of 10 angstroms between the centre and the edge of the box. To achieve system relaxation and mitigate steric clashes, energy minimisation was conducted via the Steepest Descent optimisation algorithm. Prior to the production phase, all systems underwent a 2.0 nanosecond equilibration in the NVT ensemble, employing a Berendsen thermostat set to 300 K for the protein-ligand complex, followed by an additional 2.0 nanoseconds in the NPT ensemble, utilising the Parrinello-Rahman barostat at 1 bar. Subsequently, we performed a 100-nanosecond run of the MD trajectory, analysing parameters such as the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the protein and ligand, the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), and the radius of gyration (RoG). Furthermore, we employed the MM-GBSA approach [39,40] for binding free energy. MM-GBSA is a computational method for estimating the binding free energy of a ligand to a protein from MD simulation trajectories. This approach selects representative snapshots from the MD trajectory for analysis, where molecular mechanical energies are computed alongside solvation energies derived from the generalised Born approximation and surface area estimates. To calculate the binding free energy using the MMGBSA approach for the final molecules bound to the TMPK protein, along with the standard compounds. The last 10 ns were used for the calculation of MM-GBSA-based binding energy of the selected molecules. For the calculation, the gmx_MMPBSA (v1.6.3) [41] package was used. Lastly, from MD Simulation trajectories, FEL and PCA plots were generated and analysed to assess the compounds’ stability for all TMPK inhibitors and Standard Lifirafenib and the PK5 co-crystal.

Free energy perturbation

Free Energy Perturbation (FEP) is a computer technique used to calculate the difference in free energy between two similar systems or states, such as a protein and a bound ligand. It is frequently employed to predict how modifications to a chemical would affect its binding affinity to a target protein, thereby helping to prioritise therapeutic options for clinical testing [42]. The FEP of chosen compounds CNP0363196, CNP0217487, CNP0015705 (TMPK inhibitors) was performed using GROMACS (https://manual.gromacs.org/2021.1/release-notes/2021/2021.1.html) [43]. To generate the input files for the FEP method, the CHARMM36 forcefield [44] and SwissParam server [36] were used to prepare the protein and ligand topology files, respectively. The topol.top and lig.itp files were then extracted and utilized for FEP. GROMACS [45] was used to create the protein and ligand complex solvation and ionization. FEP was conducted using the final topol.top, lig.itp, and solv_ions.gro files. Using a Perl script, the necessary MDP files for the NVT, NPT, and MD stages were produced for each of the 20 lambda windows. For a maximum of 5,000 steps at the start of FEP, energy was minimised using the steepest descent algorithm. With a step size of 0.01 nm, neighbour searching was updated at each step using the Verlet cutoff technique. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) technique was used to address long-range electrostatic interactions with a real-space cutoff of 1.2 nm, while van der Waals interactions were gradually switched off between 1.0 and 1.2 nm. Following minimization, the systems were equilibrated under NVT conditions. NVT equilibration was carried out for 100 ps (0.1 ns) using Langevin dynamics with a time step of 2 fs. The system’s temperature was maintained at 298 K. The pressure coupling was now switched off. NPT conditions were then used to equilibrate the systems. The Langevin dynamics with a 2-fs time step was used to perform NPT equilibration for 100 ps (0.1 ns). The temperature was maintained at 298 K and the pressure at 1 bar using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Lastly, MD simulations were performed for 1 ns using a 2-fs time step. After completing the FEP analysis, the free energies of all 20 windows were retrieved, with the final ΔG in kJ/mol. Graphs were produced for ΔG energy differences vs. lambda windows. The cumulative ΔG values were also calculated using GROMACS [46].

Results and discussions

Protein structure selection and binding site analysis

Mtb TMPK, an enzyme specific to Mycobacterium tuberculosis, is a distinctive target due to its essential role in bacterial replication and survival. The targeted receptor, whose x-ray diffraction structure (PDB: 6YT1) is a homodimer, is bound to the pyridine carboxamide derivative PK5. The structure was resolved at 1.90 Å, with 214 amino acid residues per chain. The protein comprises nine α-helices surrounding a five-stranded β-sheet core. The active site residues, as reported in the prior studies, were Pro37, Ala49, Phe70, Arg74, Arg95, Asn100, Tyr103, and Asp163 [47].

Exhaustive Virtual Screening (VS) and generation of a library of TMPK inhibitory hits

An advanced virtual screening strategy employing a Coconut database similarity search and multi-step molecular docking was performed against the TMPK protein. The workflow of the conceived study is given in Fig. 1. Following the retrieval of the protein complex, 445 potent inhibitors were retrieved from the ChEMBL database. These compounds were docked in a manner similar to the co-crystallised ligand. Later, after docking, 10 promising molecules with comparably lower IC50 values were selected. The highest binding affinity was − 11.7 kcal/mol, corresponding to a molecule named Lifirafenib. The decoy-based docking comparison validated the docking for the molecules derived from ChEMBL. Following this validation, a similarity search was conducted against the COCONUT database. Maintaining a 70% similarity search against its query search, 9148 compounds were derived. These hits were further mapped to the common pharmacophores found in the initial 10 compounds. The pharmacophore-based mapping procedure streamlined the initial library to 6324 compounds. These compounds were subjected to multi-platform docking analyses followed by ADMET screening. Three compounds satisfied all criteria for the virtual screening and are thus considered potential lead compounds. These leads undergo comparative MD simulations to assess the stability of the complexes they form relative to the standard inhibitor. Binding affinity estimation of the standard compound by molecular docking

ChEMBL is a manually curated database of compounds that possess drug-like properties. Analysing the active dataset of TMPK protein inhibitors in the database yielded 445 compounds. These compounds were sorted by their IC50 values. Using a threshold of 10 nM, 32 compounds were selected for further docking analysis with AutoDock Vina. Prior to the multi-ligand docking experiment, the co-crystal ligand PK5 was redocked to the apo form of the protein. The re-docking of the co-crystal ligand supports the grid allocation as the superimposition of the initial complex, and the re-docked complex had an RMSD of 0.40 Å (Fig. 3). The RMSD of the Co-crystal ligand and re-docked pose was calculated using the Dock RMSD tool [32]. The Binding affinity of the re-docked complex was found to be -8.9 kcal/mol. The amino acid interactions of PK5 involved Pro37, Ala49, Phe70, Arg74, Arg95, Asn100, Tyr103 and Asp163.

Based on their binding affinity to the co-crystal ligand, 10 out of 32 active compounds were chosen. These compounds showed binding affinities exceeding − 9.0 kcal/mol, with Lifirafenib having the strongest affinity for the TMPK protein at -11.7 kcal/mol. Therefore, Lifirafenib was selected as the reference molecule for further detailed studies.

Decoy validation

For decoy validation, the 10 active molecules from the ChEMBL-derived dataset were used to generate decoys through the DUDE database decoy generator [48]. 500 decoys against these active molecules were generated. The combined active and decoy compounds were docked using the best docking parameters identified in the self-docking approach. After docking, the analysis was done using several statistical parameters. The identified superior statistical indices include an accuracy of 0.49, an ROC AUC of 0.85, and a specificity of 0.99. These parameters collectively underscore the efficacy of the chosen docking protocol. The enrichment factor (EF) from the amalgamated active and decoy set yielded EF1% and EF2% values of 51.10 and 45.55, respectively. The above EF data indicate strong early enrichment of true actives in the top 1–2% of the ranked database, demonstrating that the docking protocol effectively discriminates active compounds from decoys and has reasonable predictive power for TMPK. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve (Fig. 4) further validates the docking protocol, providing a visual representation of its performance characteristics.

The relationship between the true and false-positive rates is derived from the docking results of the combined active and decoy molecules.

Similarity Search using the Coconut database

The similarity search using the Coconut database involved systematically comparing the molecular structures of the 10 selected active compounds with a diverse library of chemical compounds. The ‘Tanimoto search [49] method, a widely recognised approach in cheminformatics, was used to evaluate the structural similarity between the query compounds and those in the Coconut database. For each of the 10 compounds, a strict similarity search at 70% was performed, yielding a total of 9458 compounds. The 9148 unique compounds retrieved, after removing duplicates, represented active inhibitors with a significant structural similarity to the initially prioritised molecules. This thorough exploration helped us understand the chemical landscape, allowing the identification of compounds that may share key structural features necessary for binding to the TMPK protein. This detailed similarity search improves our understanding of potential ligands and offers a solid basis for discovering new compounds with promising interactions against the TMPK protein.

Ligand-based pharmacophore mapping

A charge-based orientation method was used to improve the precision of the newly discovered natural compounds. The Discovery Studio Client 2022 software was used to conduct this comparative study, including ligand-based pharmacophore mapping. Ligand-based pharmacophore mapping is a technique that searches for similar chemical features crucial for drug efficacy within a group of compounds. Of the 9148 compounds tested, 6324 matched the key features of the 10 active compounds. This approach identifies key molecular features that facilitate receptor interactions, such as hydrogen-bonding networks, hydrophobic regions, and aromatic residues. The selective pharmacophore map comprises five chemical features: an aromatic ring, a negatively charged ionizable group, two hydrophobic aromatic features, a hydrogen-bond donor, and a hydrogen-bond acceptor. Molecular docking – AutoDock Vina-based analysis

Molecular docking studies were conducted using AutoDock Vina to assess interactions between the mapped compounds (6324) and the TMPK protein. The receptor structure file and the 6324 compounds were prepared in the ‘pdbqt’ file format through the OpenBabel script. Retrieval of ADME and toxicity parameters of mapped compounds using pkCSM

We examined the interactions between 6324 experimental compounds and 10 active compounds to assess their effects on thymidylate kinase inhibition. Afterwards, we assessed how these compounds are absorbed, distributed, metabolised, and excreted in the body, alongside evaluating their toxicity. Combining pkCSM analysis with drug feature mapping provided a thorough understanding of their pharmacological behaviour and safety profile. This two-step approach helped identify the best lead compounds, streamlining the selection of candidates with high efficacy and low risk of adverse effects. Such a strategic method accelerates the discovery of new drugs targeting thymidylate kinase. After critically analysing the results from the pkCSM tool and conducting a comparative evaluation of ADMET properties across all compounds and filtered compounds using permissible cut-off values of pharmacokinetics features and toxicity such as Molecular weight ≤ 500 D, No.of rotatable bonds ≤ 10, Intestinal absorption (human) ≥ 90% Absorbed, BBB permeability ≤ -1 logBB, Skin permeability ≤ -2.5 logKp, CNS permeability ≤ -3.0 logPS, Minnows Toxicity ≤ -0.3 log mM, max. tolerated dose ≤ 0.477 mg and with the absence of AMES and hepatotoxicity.Then 122 compounds were carefully selected from the initial pool of 6324 compounds from above ADMET cut-offs and used for further investigation using PLANTS docking and KDeep scoring.

Selection of potential inhibitor-modulator compounds as TMPK inhibitors

The precise coordination of the hits was re-evaluated through PLANTS and KDEEP. While in PLANTS the docking procedure follows an algorithm that selects ligands that traverse the shortest path to the grid origin, KDeep relies on 3D-Convolutional Neural Networks (3D-CNN) for accurate binding predictions, as outlined by Jiménez et al. in 2018 [50]. This system categorises input molecules into eight distinct pharmacophore properties: hydrophobic, aromatic, hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor, positive and negative ionisable, metallic, and total excluded volume. Each time the docking was carried out for the hits, their results were tallied with those of the 10 active compounds. In the current scenario, 75 of 122 input compounds had scores over − 100. The standard active molecule had a PLANT score of -86.99. Thus, to streamline the chemical space, compounds were chosen that, in addition to having a PLANT score over − 100, also had a superior value in the KDeep result table. KDeep scores the molecules as per the ΔG(mean) value. The value for the standard actives was − 5.996 kcal/mol. Thus, from the ΔG(mean) value table, compounds with a value more than the active ones fulfilled the PLANTS result. The criteria identified 16 compounds, of which three were non-toxic and satisfied all ADMET criteria. The Coconut database IDs of these compounds are CNP0015705, CNP0363196, and CNP0217487, as presented in Fig. 6. Hence, the study progressed, considering these compounds as potential leads.

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of protein backbone

The protein-ligand complex was assessed using protein backbone RMSD calculated from MD simulation trajectories to evaluate conformational changes and binding stability. The comparative change of constitutive atomic coordinates from the initial frame of reference determines the strength of the complexes. Consistent low fluctuations or systematic variations in RMSD characterize the system’s equilibration, indicating a stabilized state in the protein’s structural dynamics. The RMSD of the TMPK backbone bound with all three selected molecules and Lifirafenib is shown in Fig. 9 (A). The average RMSD values are 0.272, 0.355, 0.359, 0.304, and 0.244 nm for CNP0015705, CNP0217487, CNP0363196, Lifirafenib, and PK5, respectively. Although there are two instantaneous peaks in the CNP0217487-bound protein at around 30 to 40ns, the trajectory tends to converge further. A similar scenario is also seen for CNP0363196. The trajectory of the protein bound to compound CNP0015705 is lower than the standard and the co-crystal compound-bound protein. Low average TMPK backbone RMSD and consistent backbone variation undoubtedly indicate the stability of the protein-ligand complex across dynamic states.

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of ligands

It is imperative to monitor the ligand’s deviation from its native conformation throughout the molecular dynamics simulation. The ligand-RMSD was calculated and plotted in Fig. 9(B). The average, maximum, and minimum ligand RMSD values for each of the studied ligands are given in Table 2. The difference between maximum and minimum ligand RMSD is found to be 0.321, 0.199, 0.275, 0.359, and 0.237 nm for CNP0015705, CNP0217487, CNP0363196, Lifirafenib, and PK5, respectively. Not a single molecule can be observed deviating more than 0.4 nm. It can be observed that almost all molecules remain consistent toward the end of the simulation, except for a few fluctuations. This observation can be explained by the fact that small molecules remain within the TMPK binding pocket or remain stable at the initial binding site throughout the MD simulation.

Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF)

The stability of the protein complex is closely affected by individual amino acid residues acting independently. Analysing the Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) parameter helps explore the dynamic movements of each amino acid residue. This metric is useful for understanding the subtle behaviour of individual residues and how they collectively contribute to the complex’s overall structural stability. It measures how much each amino acid backbone fluctuates relative to its initial position in the native state during the simulation. The RMSF values for each amino acid in TMPK bound with CNP0015705, CNP0217487, CNP0363196, Lifirafenib, and PK5 are shown in Fig. 9(C). Most fluctuations remain within 0.513 nm, except for residues between 130 and 170, which exhibit higher fluctuations. This region lies close to the active site, and the increased flexibility may correspond to a loop segment involved in ligand accommodation. Such mobility is often necessary for conformational adaptations and ligand accommodation during binding. The average RMSF for TMPK when bound to CNP0015705, CNP0217487, CNP0363196, Lifirafenib, and PK5 is 0.159, 0.160, 0.144, 0.155, and 0.137 nm, respectively. These low values suggest minimal conformational changes during the simulation. Overall, the low average RMSF and fluctuations within 0.513 nm indicate the complexes are stable in their dynamic states.

Radius of gyration

Another important parameter in MD simulation analysis is the RoG, which evaluates the system’s compactness and rigidity during dynamic states. The RoG values for each TMPK-ligand complex are shown in Fig. 9(D). To facilitate understanding, the average, maximum, and minimum RoG values for each complex are provided in Table 2. The difference between the maximum and minimum RoG offers insight into the system’s rigidity throughout the simulation. These differences are 0.109, 0.072, 0.113, 0.089, and 0.102 nm for complexes with CNP0015705, CNP0217487, CNP0363196, Lifirafenib, and PK5, respectively. With the exception of the TMPK-CNP0015705 complex, which experienced a slight fluctuation between 55 and 85 ns, all other complexes maintained a steady RoG around 1.72 nm. The RoG of TMPK bound to CNP0015705 is marginally higher, but no significant deviations are noted. Visual inspection indicates the rigidity and compactness of each system.

In MD simulations, H-bonds between the protein and the ligand play a crucial role in stabilising the molecule within the active site cavity. The co-crystal forms the most contacts in parallel with CNP0217487. Lifirafenib exhibits a maximum of four hydrogen-bond interactions at a specific point in the simulation. Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis is a widely used method for studying protein dynamics. Researchers can better understand how each atom moves during the simulation using this technology. The PCA technique helps reduce the dimensionality of complex data because it is very challenging to detect the direction and movement of many atoms. It establishes a few key directions as primary components and establishes the direction of motion of an atom. Instead of focusing on the smallest movements of individual atoms, PCA looks at the main ways the entire molecule is moving or changing form. The PCA analysis results reveal a cluster that forms as the protein’s atoms move. This cluster demonstrates the molecular flexibility. A spread-out plot indicates that the protein is more stable and flexible when a ligand is present, while a tightly packed graph indicates that the protein is less flexible.

The five ligand-protein complexes exhibit distinct conformational sampling patterns, as shown in the principal component analysis (PCA) plots. PCA plots of selected TMPK inhibitors with PK5 and Lifirafenib is displayed in Fig. 13. The cluster of CNP0363196 is comparatively compact and centred, reflecting a limited conformational space and consistent dynamic activity throughout the simulation. In comparison to CNP0363196, CNP0217487 and CNP0015705 exhibit somewhat elongated trajectories and substantially extended dispersion along PC1, indicating increased flexibility and focused motions. Lifirafenib, on the other hand, exhibits a more diverse and dispersed conformational landscape with several inhabited regions, reflecting more dynamic transitions and structural variability. Higher conformational flexibility and large-scale motions are indicated by the PK5 complex’s most noticeable distribution, especially along PC1 and PC2. Overall, the PCA results indicate that CNP0363196 produces the most dynamically stable complex, while PK5 and Lifirafenib show greater flexibility, which could affect functional dynamics and binding stability.

Free Energy Perturbation (FEP)

FEP is a simulation technique used to calculate the bound and unbound energies of protein- ligand complexes. Following the MDS and MM- GBSA studies, we narrowed down to a specific set of molecules. To further investigate these molecules as TMPK candidate inhibitors, we conducted FEP studies. These three molecules were prepared according to literature protocols for FEP execution to determine the free energy differences (ΔG) over 20 lambda windows [46]. Table 3 presents the free energy changes associated with the alchemical transformations and ΔG across λ windows for the selected compounds. CNP 0363196, CNP 0217487, and CNP 0015705 had total ΔG values of -23.31, -48.72, and − 0.69 kJ/mol, respectively. CNP0217487 exhibited the highest binding to the target protein, with a total ΔG of -48.72 kJ/mol, making it comparatively favourable molecule, followed by CNP0363196, which binds effectively (− 23.61 kJ/mol), while CNP0015705 showed weak to unstable binding. The ΔG bar chart (Fig. 15) indicates a gradual increase in ΔG from λ = 3–4 across all three compounds. However, consistent negative contributions appeared from λ = 5 onwards for CNP 0217487 and CNP 0363196, whereas CNP 0015705 showed a slight positive contribution from λ = 7 onwards. These findings suggest that the λ = 5–14 region is critical for binding stabilisation, as all molecules demonstrate consistent negative ΔG values in this region. According to Table 4, the calculated ΔG_A (binding free energies) for CNP0363196, CNP0217487, and CNP0015705 are − 23.61 ± 1.92, -48.72 ± 1.96, and − 0.69 ± 1.76, respectively. This indicates CNP0217487 is the strongest binder, CNP0363196 is a favourable binder, and CNP0015705 is a weak or non-binder. When a cumulative ΔG trend line (Fig. 16) was plotted, the simulation was considered well converged if the cumulative ΔG curve was smooth, as observed for CNP0217487. This blue line illustrates how Gibbs free energy accumulates across λ windows. It is smooth and deep for CNP0217487, but zigzagged for CNP0015705. Free-energy perturbation studies suggest that CNP0217487 is the best binder among these three molecules.

Conclusion

The investigation showed that the catalytic domain was intentionally selected for study in TMPK, as it is the primary and most favourable ligand-binding site for potential inhibition or regulation of the TMPK protein. Using various computational approaches, three natural compounds demonstrated notably higher binding affinities than the reference standard inhibitor for TMPK. All compounds exhibited binding interactions with key active-site residues in TMPK, including Pro37, Ala49, Phe70, Arg74, Arg95, Asn100, Tyr103, and Asp163. Most importantly, residues Pro37, Tyr39, and Arg95 are critical amino acids at the active binding site and were the most common residues forming binding interactions in docking analysis. The pharmacophore analysis focused on the characteristic features present among the inhibitors. This feature-finding methodology enhanced the precision of identifying hits among compounds in the coconut database. The docking-based interaction profiles revealed that the selected compounds display π-cation stacking interactions with certain residues; π-π stacking and hydrogen-bond interactions play significant roles in TMPK inhibition. It can be concluded that π-cation, π-π interactions, and hydrogen bonds are vital in the formation of complexes with the TMPK protein. The MD simulation study and the MM-GBSA binding free energy analysis also indicate that the proposed compounds exhibit high stability and substantial conformational integrity upon interaction with the TMPK protein. The FEP studies for the final three molecules showed that compound CNP0217487 is a superior binder to the binding site compared to the other two molecules. Comprehensive analyses of all identified compounds strongly suggest that the catalytic domain of the TMPK protein can be effectively targeted with enhanced specificity.

Nevertheless, the current study has some limitations to consider. All results are based on in silico computational methods, with no experimental (in vitro or in vivo) validation yet conducted to verify biological activity. The use of docking and MM-GBSA scoring may introduce approximations that affect binding free energy estimates, potentially affecting how compounds are ranked. MD simulations were carried out under specific conditions and over limited timescales, possibly missing long-term conformational changes or rare binding events. Additionally, pharmacokinetic and toxicity predictions are computationally estimated and need experimental validation, which is essential for progressing toward therapeutic applications.

In summary, the current study provides valuable insights into catalytic-site targeting of the TMPK protein and offers promising lead candidates for further experimental validation and optimisation. Future work involving biochemical assays, cellular studies, and selectivity profiling will be essential to translate these computational findings into viable therapeutic agents.
