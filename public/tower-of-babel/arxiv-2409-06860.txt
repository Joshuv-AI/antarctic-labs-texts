Designing reaction-cross-diffusion systems with Turing and wave instabilities
Edgardo Villar-Sepúlveda, Alan R. Champneys, Andrew L. Krause

Abstract
General conditions are established under which reaction-cross-diffusion systems can undergo spatiotemporal pattern-forming instabilities. Recent work has focused on designing systems theoretically and experimentally to exhibit patterns with specific features, but the case of non-diagonal diffusion matrices has yet to be analysed. Here, a framework is presented for the design of general n n -component reaction-cross-diffusion systems that exhibit Turing and wave instabilities of a given wavelength. For a fixed set of reaction kinetics, it is shown how to choose diffusion matrices that produce each instability; conversely, for a given diffusion tensor, how to choose linearised kinetics. The theory is applied to several examples including a hyperbolic reaction-diffusion system, two different 3-component models, and a spatio-temporal version of the Ross-Macdonald model for the spread of malaria.

Within that mathematical modelling setting, we ask how one could design diffusion matrices or linearized reaction kinetics to admit such instabilities. Our objective is to give conditions under which designing unstable systems is possible, alongside particular approaches to doing so.

Specifically, we shall consider either fixing the linearized kinetics of the system and varying the diffusion tensor or fixing the diffusion tensor and varying the linearized kinetics. Assuming stability to homogeneous perturbations (i.e. that the system’s linearized kinetics are stable in the absence of transport), we then ask how to design the remaining free matrix (the diffusion tensor or the linearized kinetics) so that the resulting reaction-diffusion system becomes unstable around its steady state after the addition of transport; that is so that the system undergoes a Turing or a wave instability. In the end, this can be seen as a matter of designing a reaction-diffusion system, subject to constrained transport or constrained kinetics, so that it generates diffusion-driven instabilities under the presence of cross-diffusion. Through examples, we will also explore situations where one does not have complete freedom over either of these matrices and must design the system more carefully. 1.1
Statement of the main results

For simplicity of presentation, we will focus on reaction-cross-diffusion systems on the real line, hence not being concerned with boundary conditions or geometry, as long as the eigenvalues of the Laplacian on the domain are sufficiently well-behaved. We note that this is essentially without loss of generality in terms of problems posed on Riemannian manifolds with Neumann or periodic boundary conditions; see
[
14
,
8
]
for discussion of this point in general, and
(
5
, Remark 1)
in particular. We do note that the use of Neumann or periodic boundary conditions can have important consequences for wave instabilities as these can give rise to different kinds of patterns,
travelling waves
or
standing waves

[
5
,
44
,
45
]
, and that other boundary conditions can induce a variety of behaviours
[
46
,
47
]
. Given a fixed geometry and boundary conditions, one can simply scale the free matrix discussed below to fit a domain of a particular geometry, in any spatial dimension, assuming the Laplace (or more generally, the Laplace-Beltrami operator) spectrum is known and well-behaved (i.e. consisting solely of point eigenvalues) for the given boundary conditions.

We pose a general reaction-cross-diffusion system as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})+\partial_{x}\left(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u})\partial_{x}\mathbf{u}\right),

(1)

where
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbf{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathbf{D}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the diffusion tensor of the system, which is not necessarily diagonal. We will assume throughout that
n\geq 2
, though note that
n=2
does not allow for wave instabilities even with cross-diffusion
[
31
]
. We assume that sufficiently smooth and unique solutions exist, noting that the existence and regularity theory for these systems is much more intricate than for simpler reaction-diffusion models, with blowup and singularities having a significant literature
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
; see
[
33
]
for an introductory review to these complexities and their analysis. We will henceforth neglect details regarding well-posedness and tacitly assume that everything at the nonlinear level is well-behaved.

We assume that
Eq.

1
admits a spatially homogeneous steady state
\mathbf{P}
such that
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P})=\mathbf{0}
, and that
\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P})
has strictly positive real eigenvalues, and hence is invertible for all the values of its argument. After linearizing about this steady state by writing
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{P}+\varepsilon\mathbf{u}_{1}
and keeping terms up to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, we
obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u_{1}}=\mathbf{J}\mathbf{u_{1}}+\mathbf{\hat{D}}\partial_{xx}\mathbf{u_{1}},

(2)

where the constant matrices

\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{P}),\qquad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{J}=\partial_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{P}),

correspond to diffusion and Jacobian matrices, respectively. Henceforth, we will work with these two constant matrices, dropping the explicit dependence on the steady-state
\mathbf{P}
.

We proceed to solve
Eq.

2
in the usual way using separable solutions composed of Laplacian eigenfunctions and solutions in time of the form
\exp(\lambda t)
. This leads to solvability conditions determining growth rates
\lambda
as solutions to

\displaystyle\det\left(\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}-\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)=0,

(3)

where
\mu=k^{2}
is an eigenvalue of the negative Laplacian, and
k
is a wavenumber. Instabilities of the full system,
Eq.

1
, correspond to solutions of
Eq.

3
with
\real(\lambda)>0
. Henceforth, we will assume that
\mathbf{J}
is a stable matrix; that is, all of its eigenvalues have strictly negative real parts.

In the classical two-species case, we need the Jacobian to have the right sign structure, with two positive and two negative entries
[
14
]
. Here, we only assume that it is a non-scalar matrix (i.e. not a scalar multiple of the identity matrix).
We then say that the system given by
Eq.

2
exhibits a
Turing instability
if there is at least one purely real value of
\lambda>0
satisfying
Eq.

3
. Also, we say that the system exhibits a
wave instability
if there is at least one complex conjugate pair of
\lambda
with positive real part and nontrivial imaginary part satisfying
Eq.

3
. In both cases, we can then say that the full nonlinear system
Eq.

1
exhibits these instabilities around the steady state
\mathbf{P}
.

We now state our main results. We consider
\ell
to be a non-negative integer in what comes below.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{J}
be a non-scalar stable matrix. Then, there exists a diagonalizable diffusion matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
such that
Eq.

2
exhibits a Turing instability. Furthermore, if
n\geq 3
and
\mathbf{J}
has at least

distinct eigenvalues with at least

\mathbf{\hat{D}}
such that

2
exhibits a wave instability.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
be a non-scalar diagonalizable matrix with strictly positive real eigenvalues. Then, there exists a stable matrix
\mathbf{J}
such that
Eq.

2
exhibits a Turing instability. Furthermore, if
n\geq 3
, then there exists a stable matrix
\mathbf{J}
such that
Eq.

2
exhibits a wave instability.

In all of these cases, we give a constructive proof for finding such matrices to induce specific instabilities and provide insights into how to tune parameters to design them to exhibit specific unstable wavelengths. We remark that having matrices inducing these instabilities then allows one to find bifurcation curves where marginal stability is crossed (i.e. where
\max_{\mu}(\real(\lambda))=0
and a transversality condition is satisfied). One can then make use of amplitude equation formalisms to study properties of the solutions near these bifurcation curves
[
52
,
44
]
, and we will explore this in the first example at the end of the paper.

The key idea of the proof for
Theorem

1
follows the methods introduced in
[
5
]
. That is, by constructing a diagonalizable diffusion tensor, the eigenvalue problem in
Eq.

3
is reduced to the analysis of a Jacobian matrix that looks like the ones treated in
[
5
]
, with the eigenvectors of the diffusion matrix allowing us to effectively control the linearization of the system. We then construct an unstable principal submatrix and set some eigenvalues of the diffusion matrix to zero to ‘pick out’ this submatrix of the (non-scalar) Jacobian matrix, which is guaranteed to destabilize the full system in the limit as
\mu\to\infty
. This effectively allows us to get unstable growth rates
\lambda
with
\real(\lambda)>0
as
\mu\to\infty
, while all other growth rates will tend to
-\infty
in this limit. In the end, this gives a way of constructing a suitable matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
so that the linear system exhibits the desired instability.

Next, one introduces a small positive parameter
\delta
that ‘relaxes’ these zero diagonal elements to be small positive numbers and, by continuity, arrives at a system with the correct dispersion relation; that is, there is a finite interval of
\mu
for which
\real(\lambda)>0
, ensuring the desired instability. Turing or wave instabilities can be selected based on the imaginary part of these eigenvalues (chosen via the construction of the unstable principal submatrix above), and the range of
\mu
exhibiting an instability can be tuned arbitrarily (see
Lemma

1
and
Appendix

A
). The proof of
Theorem

2
is qualitatively similar but proceeds more directly. In particular, we design the Jacobian matrix to ensure the existence of a
2
or
3
-component subsystem corresponding to at least two distinct diffusion rates to generate Turing or wave instabilities.

Fig.

1
illustrates the key idea of the constructions for the case of a Turing instability. We construct a principal unstable submatrix that has the largest positive eigenvalue indicated by the green line. When
\delta=0
, a dispersion curve of the full system (shown in orange) tends asymptotically to this value as
\mu\to\infty
. We then increase
\delta
to make the diffusion matrix nonsingular. This leads to the blue curves, which have the appropriate form of dispersion relation to ensure a Turing instability with a finite maximally unstable mode. The red curve illustrates the critical value of
\delta>0
, which gives this marginal stability criterion such that the dispersion curve is tangent to the horizontal axis. This represents the
\delta
-value at which a Turing bifurcation occurs. The blue dispersion curves thus represent a homotopy between the red and orange curves, parametrized by
\delta
. The proofs below will construct this homotopy explicitly, which will be illustrated in the examples.

Figure 1:
An example set of dispersion curves (i.e. plots of
\real\left(\lambda\left(k^{2}\right)\right)=\real(\lambda(\mu))
) with different curves parameterized by a small constant diffusion parameter
\delta
. When
\delta=0
there exists a branch of the dispersion relation that follows the orange curve which has a prescribed growth rate in the limit as
k\to\infty
that is given by the green line. For small discrete positive values of
\delta

\delta

Wave instabilities can also be designed similarly. Essentially, we have the same form of dispersion relations as illustrated in
Fig.

1
, but with each curve now corresponding to the real part of a complex conjugate pair of
\lambda
for each
k
. Transitions between Turing and wave instabilities as

Fig.

).

Note that the constructions we use for the proofs of
Theorem

1
lead to matrices
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
that have strictly positive real-part eigenvalues. However, it should be noted that such matrices may not be symmetric, nor satisfy other functional thermodynamic constraints that are required for a particular application. Similarly, the Jacobian matrix constructed in the proof of
Theorem

2
will be of a particular form, which may not satisfy particular physical constraints on the dependent variables. Nevertheless, the constructive proofs we develop are minimal in the sense that they provide exactly the core features of the matrices needed for the instabilities. Thus, in practice, one is left with many free parameters that can be chosen to satisfy additional modelling constraints.

Proof of

To prove
Theorem

1
, we will build some key lemmas extending those in
[
5
]

\mathbf{\hat{D}}
being in general non-diagonal). A key step will be to construct a diagonalizable diffusion matrix

in order to use previous results applicable only for diagonal diffusion matrices, which we illustrate in the proof of
Lemma

1
below. Throughout, we will use subscripts

to denote sequences of positive integer indices. We also define a matrix to be

if all of its principal submatrices are stable. Throughout the paper, the eigenvalues of

will be called

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
be diagonalizable matrix; specifically, there exists a non-singular matrix
\mathbf{Q}
such that
\mathbb{D}:=\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{D}}\mathbf{Q}
is a diagonal matrix. Suppose further that
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
has
\ell
eigenvalues
\sigma_{i_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{i_{\ell}}
equal to zero, while all others are positive. Then, there exist
\ell
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}
that tend to the eigenvalues of the principal submatrix of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
that comprises its
i_{1}^{\rm th},\ldots,i_{\ell}^{\rm th}
rows and columns as
\mu\to\infty
. Meanwhile, the real parts of the eigenvalues associated with the principal submatrix of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
that excludes these rows and columns all tend to
-\infty
as
\mu\to\infty
.

Proof.

First, if
\ell=n
, then all the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
are equal to zero. Therefore, as
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
is diagonalizable, this implies it must be equal to the zero matrix, entailing that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}
are equal to the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}
for every
\mu>0
, which proves the result in this case.

On the other hand, if
1\leq\ell<n
, we have that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{m}
of
\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}
are determined by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}_{m}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}\mathbf{v}_{m}-\lambda_{m}\mathbf{v}_{m}=0,

where
\mathbf{v}_{m}
is a corresponding eigenvector for each
m=1,\ldots,n
. Now, using that
\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}
, we know that this equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}_{m}-\mu\mathbf{Q}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{m}=\lambda_{m}\mathbf{v}_{m},

which implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{v}_{m}-\mu\mathbb{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{m}=\lambda_{m}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{m}.

(4)

Let
\mathbf{w}_{m}=\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{v}_{m}
. Therefore,
Eq.

4
becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{w}_{m}-\mu\mathbb{D}\mathbf{w}_{m}=\lambda_{m}\mathbf{w}_{m},

(5)

which is equivalent to the linearization of the general system analyzed in
[
5
]
, for
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
instead of
\mathbf{J}
. This implies that
(
5
, Lemma 5)
can be used to conclude the proof.
∎

To use the lemma above, we are going to focus on proving the existence of stable matrices
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
with unstable principal submatrices to conclude that the system can exhibit different diffusion-driven instabilities.
Lemma

1
tells us that, to construct the diffusion matrices that allow the appearance of these instabilities, we need to follow two steps: first, we find the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
of eigenvectors of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
such that
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
has an unstable principal submatrix; second, we change the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
in order to control the convergence of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}
as
\mu\to\infty
(i.e. set the corresponding eigenvalues to asymptotically small values to induce the instability). While the case of zero diffusion coefficients corresponding to this principal unstable submatrix will lead to an ill-posed linear system, we can fix this by simply using small positive values instead of zeroes; see
Fig.

1
for an illustration. An important point here is to understand what kind of structure we want to have for the unstable principle submatrix, and then use this structure to design the eigenvectors
\mathbf{Q}
of the diffusion matrix to ensure that this submatrix appears within
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
.

To continue, it is useful to recall a standard result from linear algebra: if two matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
have the same Jordan normal form, then
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
are similar. That is, there exists a non-singular matrix
\mathbf{R}
such that
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{R}^{-1}

[
53
]
. Furthermore, we recall that if
\mathbf{A}
is a diagonalizable matrix of size
n\times n
, then
\mathbf{A}
has only one eigenvalue (with algebraic multiplicity
n
) if and only if
\mathbf{A}
is a scalar matrix (i.e. a scalar multiple of the identity). We make use of the following procedure to reduce similarity in general to the question of similarity of principal submatrices arising from Jordan normal forms. We will also use the notation
\mathbf{I_{\ell}}
to stand for the
\ell
by
\ell
identity matrix, and
\mathbf{0_{i,j}}
for an
i\times j
block of zeros. In both cases, these matrices are omitted when any of their sizes is lower than one (i.e. if
\mathbf{I_{\ell}}
appears in an expression, and
\ell\leq 0
, then the matrix is omitted).

Remark 1
.

Let
\mathbf{J}
be similar to a matrix with
\ell\geq 1
square blocks given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\mbox{diag}\left(\mathbf{C_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{C_{\ell}}\right).

Let us assume that there exists
1\leq j\leq\ell
such that
\mathbf{C_{j}}
is similar to
\mathbf{K}
, with
\mathbf{K}
being a square matrix of the same size as
\mathbf{C_{j}}
. This implies that there exists an invertible matrix
\mathbf{R}
such that
\mathbf{C_{j}}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}
. Therefore, if we define
\mathbf{Q}=\mbox{diag}\left(\mathbf{I_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{I_{j-1}},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{I_{j+1}},\ldots,\mathbf{I_{\ell}}\right)
, then we note that

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{M},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\mbox{diag}\left(\mathbf{C_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{C_{j-1}},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{C_{j+1}},\ldots,\mathbf{C_{\ell}}\right),

implying that we can construct similar matrices only by establishing similarities of square blocks in the diagonal of the Jordan matrix of
\mathbf{J}
.

We now give a constructive proof for
Theorem

1
. The idea of the proof is to find a matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
that ensures that the system exhibits either Turing or wave instabilities. In
Lemma

1
, we show that the existence of an unstable principal submatrix is a sufficient criterion to ensure the appearance of any kind of linear instability. The proof will proceed then, for each kind of instability, by constructing a matrix
\mathbf{K}
similar to
\mathbf{J}
with an unstable principal submatrix. In each case, we proceed by directly writing down a matrix
\mathbf{K}
with the right unstable submatrix for a particular kind of instability and then showing that this can be made similar to
\mathbf{J}
through the correct choice of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
. Note throughout that there will be several free parameters at the end of the construction, leaving freedom one could exploit to match any further constraints arising from the modelling context.

2.1
Turing instabilities

We start by providing the following result for 2-component systems.

Lemma 2
.

Let
n=2
, and

\mathbf{J}
be a non-scalar stable matrix. Then, there exists a diffusion matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
so that
Eq.

2
exhibits a Turing instability.

Proof.

Let us denote the elements of
\mathbf{J}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}.

Consider the following matrix:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}a+d-1&&a\,d-b\,c-a-d+1\\
-1&&1\end{pmatrix},

which clearly fulfills
\tr(\m{K})=\tr\left(\mathbf{J}\right)
and
\det(\m{K})=\det\left(\mathbf{J}\right)
, implying that both matrices have the same eigenvalues.

We now prove that these matrices are similar. In fact, consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&&(d-1)\,\alpha-b\,\gamma\\
\gamma&&(a-1)\,\gamma-c\,\alpha\end{pmatrix},

for
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K},

and

\displaystyle\det(\m{Q})=b\,\gamma^{2}+(a-d)\,\alpha\,\gamma-c\,\alpha^{2},

which is either a quadratic or a linear function on
\alpha
or
\gamma
, as
a\neq d
,
b\neq 0
or
c\neq 0
. This is true since, as
\mathbf{J}
is not a scalar matrix, then the elements in the diagonal must not be equal or the off-diagonal terms must not be zero at the same time. We can then take any pair
(\alpha,\gamma)
so that
\det(\m{Q})\neq 0
.

This proves that
\mathbf{J}
is similar to
\mathbf{K}
, which is a stable matrix with an unstable principal submatrix. Therefore, by choosing a suitable diagonal matrix
\mathbb{D}
, we can construct the matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}
so that the result holds by using
Lemma

1
(i.e. by choosing a sufficiently small bottom-right diagonal element to ensure a positive
\lambda
for some
\mu>0
, thanks to the positive bottom-right element of
\mathbf{K}
).
∎

Using
Remark

1
, and the fact that any non-scalar matrix
\mathbf{J}
will have a
2\times 2
non-scalar block in its Jordan Normal Form, we can immediately use
Lemma

2
to construct a suitable matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
to induce Turing instabilities. The similarity matrix
\mathbf{Q}
constructed in the proof of the Lemma is the key to proving the general result. Note that
\mathbf{K}
was chosen in order to fulfill the conditions provided in
Lemma

1
, but there are infinitely many other matrices that are similar to
\mathbf{J}
and have an unstable principal submatrix.

2.2
Wave instabilities

We will now use similar constructions to demonstrate how to achieve wave instabilities with suitable choices of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\mathbf{J}
be a non-scalar stable matrix and
n=3
or

. If
\mathbf{J}
has
1\leq\ell\leq 3
distinct eigenvalues, then there exists a diffusion matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
so that
Eq.

2
exhibits a wave instability provided
\mathbf{J}
has at least
3-\ell
generalized eigenvectors.

Proof.

We first consider the case
n=3
. Let us denote the elements of
\mathbf{J}
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\\
g&h&i\end{pmatrix}.

Consider the following matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1,1}&\ell_{1,2}&\ell_{1,3}\\
\alpha&1&-1\\
\gamma&1&1\end{pmatrix},

where
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, and

\displaystyle\ell_{1,1}

\displaystyle=a+e+i-2,

\displaystyle\ell_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha(a+e+i-1)-2\gamma+\left(M_{12}+M_{13}+M_{23}\right)(\gamma-\alpha)-\gamma\det(\jac)}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

\displaystyle\ell_{1,3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\gamma(a+e+i-1)+\alpha)-\left(M_{12}+M_{13}+M_{23}\right)(\alpha+\gamma)+\alpha\det(\jac)}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

with

\displaystyle M_{12}=ae-bd,\qquad M_{13}=ai-cg,\qquad M_{23}=ei-fh.

Note that
\ell_{1,2}
and
\ell_{1,3}
are well-defined provided that
\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}\neq 0
.

Now, we consider the case when
\mathbf{J}
has three distinct eigenvalues. By construction, we have that the characteristic polynomials of
\mathbf{J}
and
\mathbf{K}
are the same, which implies that these matrices are similar (enforcing this similarity is precisely how the
\ell_{1,i}
were constructed). We highlight that the
2\times 2
submatrix at the bottom right corner of
\mathbf{K}
has eigenvalues
1\pm i
. Therefore, by arguments similar to those used at the end of the proof of
Lemma

2
, we can construct a matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
to conclude the first part of the proof using the result in
Lemma

1
.

On the other hand, if
\mathbf{J}
has repeated eigenvalues, then all of them must be real (as any complex eigenvalue of a real matrix comes in pairs). This implies that
\mathbf{J}
is similar to one of the matrices below:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}-y&0&0\\
0&-z&\ell\\
0&0&-z\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{C_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}-z&\ell_{2}&0\\
0&-z&\ell_{1}\\
0&0&-z\end{pmatrix}

where
y,z>0
are real numbers so that
y\neq z
, and
\ell,\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\{0,1\}
. In this case, we can use the same form of the matrix
\mathbf{K}
given above but need to find a similarity matrix
\mathbf{R}
to show that
\mathbf{J}
is similar to
\mathbf{C_{1}}
or
\mathbf{C_{2}}
.

Firstly, we set

\displaystyle\ell_{1,1}

\displaystyle=-y-2z-2,

\displaystyle\ell_{1,2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\alpha+2\gamma+2\alpha y-\gamma yz^{2}+2\alpha yz-2\gamma yz+\alpha z^{2}-\gamma z^{2}+4\alpha z}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

\displaystyle\ell_{1,3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{-2\alpha+2\gamma+2\gamma y+\alpha yz^{2}+2\alpha yz+2\gamma yz+\alpha z^{2}+\gamma z^{2}+4\gamma z}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

which implies that
\mathbf{J}
and
\mathbf{C_{1}}
have the same eigenvalues. With this, we note that these matrices are similar if there exists an invertible matrix
\mathbf{R}=\left(r_{i,j}\right)_{i,j}
so that
\mathbf{C_{1}}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}
. We omit the solution of this system here, but we highlight that, if we leave
r_{1,1},r_{2,1}
and
r_{3,1}
as parameters of the solution (solving for the other 6 elements of
\mathbf{R}
), then the determinant of
\mathbf{R}
will be given by

\displaystyle-\frac{\ell r_{1,1}r_{3,1}^{2}(y-z)^{2}}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

See
Appendix

B
, where we give the full form of
\mathbf{R}
, ensuring that it can always be found. With this, we note that
\mathbf{R}
will be invertible if and only if
\ell\neq 0
and
r_{1,1},r_{3,1}\neq 0
. This implies that
\ell=1
and, as the similarity of matrices is transitive, we have again constructed a matrix
\mathbf{K}
similar to
\mathbf{J}
with an unstable principal submatrix. Hence, we can conclude that a wave instability can be generated when
\mathbf{J}
has only two distinct eigenvalues with one generalized eigenvector.

In the case of similarity to
\mathbf{C_{2}}
, we need to set

\displaystyle\ell_{1,1}

\displaystyle=-3z-2,

\displaystyle\ell_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma\left(z^{3}+3z^{2}-2\right)-\alpha\left(3z^{2}+6z+2\right)}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

\displaystyle\ell_{1,3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma\left(3z^{2}+6z+2\right)+\alpha\left(z^{3}+3z^{2}-2\right)}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}}.

As before, we note that
\mathbf{K}
will be similar to
\mathbf{C_{2}}
if there exists a matrix
\mathbf{R}=\left(r_{i,j}\right)_{i,j}
such that
\mathbf{C_{2}}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}
. In this case, if we again leave
r_{1,1},r_{2,1}
and
r_{3,1}
as parameters of the solution (solving for the other 6 elements of
\mathbf{R}
), then the determinant of
\mathbf{R}
will be given by

\displaystyle-\frac{\ell_{1}^{2}\ell_{2}r_{3,1}^{3}}{\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}},

which is different from zero as long as
\ell_{1}\ell_{2}r_{3,1}\neq 0
and
\alpha^{2}+\gamma^{2}\neq 0
, which implies that
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}=1
. Again, see
Appendix

B
where we show that such an
\mathbf{R}
can always be found. Hence, by the same argument given above, we can conclude that the instability can be created when
\mathbf{J}
has only one eigenvalue (with multiplicity three) with two generalized eigenvectors.

In the case
n=4
, we can use
Remark

1
to reduce this to a
3\times 3
block of the Jordan normal form of
\mathbf{J}
, unless
\mathbf{J}
has repeated complex conjugate eigenvalues (note that a repeated real root and a complex-conjugate pair can be subsumed in the case of 3 distinct eigenvalues). The only case not covered by the arguments above then, is if there is a repeated complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues. In this case, we consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\ell_{1,1}&\ell_{1,2}&\ell_{1,3}&\ell_{1,4}\\
\gamma&\alpha&0&0\\
\delta&0&-\alpha&\alpha\\
\varepsilon&0&-\alpha&-\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad\text{and}\quad\qquad\mathbf{C_{3}}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\beta&\ell&0\\
\beta&\alpha&0&\ell\\
0&0&\alpha&-\beta\\
0&0&\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix},

where
\alpha<0
, and
\beta>0
are real numbers and
\ell\in\{0,1\}
. In this case, we note (via direct calculation) that the characteristic polynomials of
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{C_{3}}
are equal if

\displaystyle\ell_{1,1}

\displaystyle=5\alpha,

\displaystyle\ell_{1,2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta^{4}}{5\alpha^{2}\gamma},

\displaystyle\ell_{1,3}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{4}(10\varepsilon-55\delta)-10\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}(\delta-2\varepsilon)+\beta^{4}(\delta+2\varepsilon)}{5\alpha^{2}\left(\delta^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)},

\displaystyle\ell_{1,4}

\displaystyle=\frac{-5\alpha^{4}(2\delta+11\varepsilon)-10\alpha^{2}\beta^{2}(2\delta+\varepsilon)+\beta^{4}(\varepsilon-2\delta)}{5\alpha^{2}\left(\delta^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)}.

This implies that we have the constraints
\gamma\neq 0
and
\delta^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\neq 0
in order to be able to make these matrices similar. So, as before, we note that
\mathbf{K}
will be similar to
\mathbf{C_{3}}
if there exists a matrix
\mathbf{R}=\left(r_{i,j}\right)_{i,j}
such that
\mathbf{C_{3}}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}
. In this case, if we leave
r_{1,1},r_{2,1}
,
r_{3,1}
and
r_{4,1}
as parameters of the solution (and solve for the other
12
elements of
\mathbf{R}
), then the determinant of
\mathbf{R}
will be given by

\displaystyle-\frac{4\beta^{4}\ell^{2}\left(r_{3,1}^{2}+r_{4,1}^{2}\right)^{2}}{5\alpha^{3}\gamma\left(\delta^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)},

which is different from zero provided that
\ell=1
, and
r_{3,1}^{2}+r_{4,1}^{2}\neq 0
. Note that the
2\times 2
bottom-right submatrix of
\mathbf{K}
has eigenvalues
-\alpha\pm\alpha i
, which have a positive real part, as
\alpha<0
. As before, we can therefore construct a matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
to conclude the proof using the result in
Lemma

1
.

Once again, we can conclude that a wave instability can be generated when
n=4
and
\mathbf{J}
has only two distinct complex-conjugate eigenvalues as long as it has two generalized eigenvectors.

∎

We remark that the case
n=3
is minimal except that it does not account for repeated complex-conjugate eigenvalues, hence why we included the case
n=4
in the statement of the previous lemma. We also note that other matrices
\mathbf{K}
can be chosen to arrive at similar results via different constructions. For instance, in the
n=4
case above, the entries or the location of the principal submatrix at the bottom right corner of
\mathbf{K}
does not change the result, and the elements of the second row and column of the
4\times 4
matrix
\mathbf{K}
can also be non-zero and the result will be the same (with a suitable modification of
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
).

With this, we are ready to prove the first main result of this article.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

First, we consider how to induce Turing instabilities. Given that
\mathbf{J}
is non-scalar, we know there must exist a non-scalar
2\times 2
sub-block of its Jordan normal form which is also non-scalar. We can apply
Lemma

2
to find a matrix
\mathbf{K}
similar to this sub-block, and by construction, this
\mathbf{K}
will have a principle unstable submatrix that induces a Turing instability. We then use the construction given in
Remark

1
to embed this similarity inside of the matrix
\mathbf{M}
in that remark, and then apply
Lemma

1
on the matrix
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
, which now has a principal unstable submatrix by construction. This Lemma allows us to set one of the eigenvalues of the diffusion matrix (i.e. an element of
\mathbb{D}
) to zero in order to excite the corresponding unstable principal submatrix and induce a Turing instability in the full system. Note that we can take all other elements of
\mathbb{D}
as any arbitrary positive numbers in order to use
Lemma

1
.

The proof for inducing wave instabilities proceeds in an analogous way as above, except that we will make use of
Lemma

3
to construct a matrix
\mathbf{K}
similar to
\mathbf{J}
, instead. By assumption,
\mathbf{J}
has
1\leq\ell\leq 3
distinct eigenvalues with at least
3-\ell
corresponding generalised eigenvectors. Hence, either
\mathbf{J}
has a
3\times 3
sub-block of its Jordan normal form that exactly fulfills this criterion as well, or it has a
4\times 4
sub-block with two repeated complex conjugate eigenvalues. In either of these cases, we can again embed the appropriate matrix
\mathbf{K}
from the proof of
Lemma

3
into the matrix
\mathbf{M}
from above in order to construct a suitable similarity matrix
\mathbf{Q}
such that
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
has a principal unstable submatrix with complex-conjugate unstable eigenvalues. Again, through an analogous use of
Lemma

1
, we can set the appropriate two elements of
\mathbb{D}
to zero to induce a wave instability in the full system. This concludes the proof.

∎

We remark that, in practice, one needs the zero elements of
\mathbb{D}
to be asymptotically small rather than strictly zero to avoid ill-posedness of the linear problem, but the existence of the instability will persist for some range of ‘small’ diffusion constants. We note that in both the diagonal matrices
\mathbb{D}
and in the similarity matrices
\mathbf{Q}
constructed above, one has, in principle, freedom over elements not affecting the principal unstable submatrix to use more general constructions to gain additional flexibility over the elements we have not exploited here. We will illustrate some aspects of this in
Appendix

A
through alternative lemmas one can use to construct these matrices, and in the examples in
Section

4
.

3
Proof of
Theorem

2

As in the previous section, we prove
Theorem

2
by dividing it into cases, though as the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
are all real, the number of sub-cases we need to consider is fewer than in the proof of
Theorem

1
.

We assume, without loss of generality, that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
(i.e. the elements of
\mathbb{D}
) are sorted in ascending order so that
0<\sigma_{i}\leq\sigma_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.

3.1
Turing instabilities

Lemma 4
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
be a non-scalar diagonalizable matrix with real and strictly positive eigenvalues. Then, there exists a stable matrix
\mathbf{J}
such that
Eq.

2
exhibits a Turing instability.

Proof.

As
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
is a diagonalizable non-scalar matrix with positive real eigenvalues and the elements of its diagonalization
\mathbb{D}
are sorted, then there must exist at least one integer
1\leq\ell\leq n-1
such that
\sigma_{\ell}<\sigma_{\ell+1}
.

Therefore, as the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}
are the same as the ones of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}-\mu\mathbb{D}
, we consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}^{-1},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{I_{\ell-1}}&\mathbf{0_{\ell-1,2}}&\mathbf{0_{\ell-1,n-\ell-1}}\\
\mathbf{0_{2,\ell-1}}&\mathbf{S^{*}}&\mathbf{0_{2,n-\ell-1}}\\
\mathbf{0_{n-\ell-1,\ell-1}}&\mathbf{0_{n-\ell-1,2}}&-\mathbf{I_{n-\ell-1}}\end{pmatrix},

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{S^{*}}=\begin{pmatrix}b&b(b+c)+q\\
-1&-(b+c)\end{pmatrix},

and
b,c,q>0
are real constants to be determined. By checking its trace and determinant, one can see that
\mathbf{S^{*}}
is stable. By construction, we have that
n-2
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}-\mu\mathbb{D}
have the form
-1-\sigma_{i}
, for a corresponding integer
1\leq i\leq n
such that
i\neq\ell,\ell+1
. On the other hand, by considering the two eigenvalues we have singled out via
\mathbf{S^{*}}
, we see that the equations that determine them are uncoupled from all others and, hence, they form an
n=2
linear system which is precisely the classical Turing system, with
\mathbf{S^{*}}
playing the role of the
2\times 2
Jacobian matrix. Thus, the largest of the other two eigenvalues will be positive for some value of
\mu>0
if the system satisfies the classical 2-component Turing conditions (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
):

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell+1}b-\sigma_{\ell}(b+c)

\displaystyle>0,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\left(\sigma_{\ell+1}b-\sigma_{\ell}(b+c)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle>4\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell+1}q,

which hold provided that

\displaystyle b>\frac{\sigma_{\ell}c}{\sigma_{\ell+1}-\sigma_{\ell}}>0

and

\displaystyle q<\frac{\left(\sigma_{\ell+1}b-\sigma_{\ell}(b+c)\right)^{2}}{4\sigma_{\ell}\sigma_{\ell+1}}.

Hence, we can always pick values of
b,c,
and
q
to satisfy these, given any pair of distinct eigenvalues
\sigma_{\ell}<\sigma_{\ell+1}
. Therefore, if we set
\mathbf{J}
using this construction, we will induce a Turing instability in the original system given by
Eq.

2
.
∎

3.2
Wave instabilities

We next prove an analogous result to the previous lemma for the case of wave instabilities. Due to these requiring a 3-component subsystem, the constructions are a bit more involved.

Lemma 5
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
be a non-scalar diagonalizable matrix with real and strictly positive eigenvalues. Then, there exists a stable matrix
\mathbf{J}
such that the full system exhibits a wave instability.

Proof.

As
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
is a non-scalar diagonalizable matrix with positive real eigenvalues and the elements in
\mathbb{D}
are sorted, then there must exist at least one integer
1\leq\ell\leq n-2
such that
\sigma_{\ell}<\sigma_{\ell+2}
.

Let us consider
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}
, where

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf{I_{\ell-1}}&\mathbf{0_{\ell-1,3}}&\mathbf{0_{\ell-1,n-\ell-2}}\\
\mathbf{0_{3,\ell-1}}&\mathbf{S^{*}}&\mathbf{0_{3,n-\ell-2}}\\
\mathbf{0_{n-\ell-2,\ell-1}}&\mathbf{0_{n-\ell-2,3}}&-\mathbf{I_{n-\ell-2}}\end{pmatrix},

with

\displaystyle\mathbf{S^{*}}=\begin{pmatrix}b&-b&cy-b(2b+c)\\
b&b&bc+y(2b+c)+z\\
1&-1&-(2b+c)\end{pmatrix},

and
b,c,y,z>0
are real parameters to be determined. Note that
\tr{\m{S^*}}=-c<0
. Furthermore, the eigenvalues of the
2\times 2
submatrix at the top left corner of
\mathbf{S^{*}}
are given by
b\pm bi
. We also note that
\mathbf{S^{*}}
is a stable matrix, as its characteristic polynomial for an eigenvalue
\eta
is given by

\eta^{3}+c\eta^{2}+\left(2by+z\right)\eta+2bcy=0,

which satisfies the Routh-Hurwitz stability conditions.

Now, when we consider the full system, we note that (by the construction of
\mathbf{C}
)
n-3
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}-\mu\mathbb{D}
have the form
-1-\sigma_{i}
, for a corresponding integer
1\leq i\leq n
such that
i\neq\ell,\ell+1,\ell+2
. Moreover, the other three eigenvalues are solutions to the following equation:

\displaystyle-\det\left(\mathbf{S^{*}}-\mu\mbox{diag}\left(\sigma_{\ell},\sigma_{\ell+1},\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)-\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)=0,

which is given by

\lambda^{3}+p_{2}\lambda^{2}+p_{1}\lambda+p_{0}=0,

(6)

where

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=c+\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+1}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right),

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(b+c+\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)+\mu\sigma_{\ell}\left(b+c+\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell+1}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)\right)-2b\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}+2by+z,

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle=\mu\sigma_{\ell}\left(-2b^{2}+\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(2b+c+\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)-b\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}+2by+cy+z\right)

\displaystyle+\left(2b-\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\right)\left(b\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}+cy\right).

We will now show that we can ensure the existence of an imaginary root with a positive real part
\tilde{\lambda}=\beta+\omega i
for some values of
\beta,\mu,\omega>0
through particular choices of the parameters in
\mathbf{S^{*}}
. In particular, substituting
\lambda
into
Eq.

6
, we find that
\omega
needs to satisfy

\displaystyle r_{0}+r_{2}\omega^{2}+i\omega\left(r_{i}-\omega^{2}\right)=0,

(7)

where

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle=2\mu b^{2}\left(\sigma_{\ell+2}-\sigma_{\ell}\right)+b\left(2y(c+\beta)+\mu\sigma_{\ell}\left(2\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}-\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}+2y+\beta\right)-2\mu\beta\sigma_{\ell+2}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(\beta-\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)\right)+\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(-cy+c\beta+\mu\beta\sigma_{\ell+2}+\beta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\sigma_{\ell}\left(\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(c+\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}+\beta\right)+c(y+\beta)+\mu\beta\sigma_{\ell+2}+z+\beta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\left(\beta(c+\beta)+\mu\beta\sigma_{\ell+2}+z\right),

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=-c-\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+1}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)-3\beta,

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=b\left(2y+\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+1}-2\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)\right)+\mu\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(c+\mu\sigma_{\ell+2}+2\beta\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mu\sigma_{\ell}\left(c+\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell+1}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)+2\beta\right)+2c\beta+2\mu\beta\sigma_{\ell+2}+z+3\beta^{2}.

We will proceed to analyze solutions for large
b
(i.e. asymptotically in the limit of
b\gg 1
) in order to simplify the analysis. Throughout, we assume that all the other parameters are fixed and do not depend on
b
in the asymptotics. To ensure that
Eq.

7
has a solution for
\omega^{2}
, we must have
r_{i}>0
. We note that
r_{i}
has a leading-order coefficient in
b
given by

\displaystyle 2y+\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+1}-2\sigma_{\ell+2}\right),

which is positive if and only if

\displaystyle y>\frac{\mu}{2}\left(2\sigma_{\ell+2}-\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell+1}\right)>0.

(8)

Furthermore, to ensure the real part of
Eq.

7
vanishes for
\omega^{2}>0
, we need that
r_{0}r_{2}<0
. This is automatically true when
b
is sufficiently large as the leading-order coefficient of
r_{0}r_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle-2\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell+2}-\sigma_{\ell}\right)\left(c+\mu\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+1}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)+3\beta\right)<0,

where we have used the ordering of the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{D}
, i.e. that
\sigma_{\ell}<\sigma_{\ell+2}
.

Let
q>0
be a real number such that

\displaystyle y=\frac{\mu}{2}\left(2\sigma_{\ell+2}-\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell+1}\right)+q.

(9)

Setting the real and imaginary parts of
Eq.

7
equal to zero, we see that we need
\omega^{2}=r_{i}=-r_{0}/r_{2}
. Writing this out, we arrive at the following equation for
\mu
:

\displaystyle a_{3}\mu^{3}+a_{2}\mu^{2}+a_{1}\mu+a_{0}=0,

(10)

where

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+1}\right)\left(\sigma_{\ell}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)\left(\sigma_{\ell+1}+\sigma_{\ell+2}\right),

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=b\left(\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell+1}\right)\left(\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)+\left(\frac{3c}{2}+2\beta\right)\sigma_{\ell}^{2}+2\beta\sigma_{\ell+2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma_{\ell}\left(2(c+3\beta)\sigma_{\ell+1}+(c+6\beta)\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}(c+4\beta)\sigma_{\ell+1}^{2}+3(c+2\beta)\sigma_{\ell+1}\sigma_{\ell+2},

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=2b^{2}\left(\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell+2}\right)+b\left((c+2q)\sigma_{\ell+1}+2(q-c)\sigma_{\ell+2}+c\sigma_{\ell}\right)+\sigma_{\ell+2}(4\beta(c+2\beta)+z)

\displaystyle\quad+\sigma_{\ell}\left(c^{2}-cq+6c\beta+8\beta^{2}\right)+\sigma_{\ell+1}\left(c(c+q)+6c\beta+z+8\beta^{2}\right)

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=4q\beta b+(c+2\beta)(2\beta(c+2\beta)+z).

Here, we note that the coefficient of
b^{2}
in
a_{1}
is given by
\sigma_{\ell}-\sigma_{\ell+2}
, which is negative again due to the ordering of these eigenvalues. Hence, for sufficiently large
b
, the coefficient
a_{1}
will become negative, implying (by the Routh-Hurwitz criterion) that there exists a root of
Eq.

10
with a positive real part. We need to ensure that, in the limit of large
b
, there is a positive real root, which is a necessary property for
\mu=k^{2}
as an eigenvalue of the negative Laplacian.

The discriminant of
Eq.

10
is given by

\displaystyle 18a_{3}a_{2}a_{1}a_{0}-4a_{2}^{3}a_{0}+a_{2}^{2}a_{1}^{2}-4a_{3}a_{1}^{3}-27a_{3}^{2}a_{0}^{2},

and if it is positive, then we know that all of the roots of
Eq.

10
are real
(
54
, Section 10.3)
. Hence, for sufficiently large
b
, we would have a positive real root. The full expression of the discriminant is unpleasant, so we again simplify things by considering
b\gg 1
as an asymptotically large parameter. This leads to a lengthy but not overly tedious calculation as, for large
b
, it is relatively straightforward to see that one can work with the leading-order terms in
b
for each of the coefficients and no terms involving
a_{0}
appear in the leading-order expression. We then find a leading-order coefficient of the discriminant in
b
(specifically, collecting terms of order

4.2
Hyperbolic reaction-diffusion system

We next provide an example based on a hyperbolic system. In
[
31
]
, the authors study a general class of second order in time cross-diffusion systems. Such systems have particular motivations coming from understanding the microscopic inertia responsible for preventing the infinite speed of propagation that occurs in usual diffusion equations. See
[
34
]
for an overview of hyperbolic and other formulations of reaction-transport processes aiming to overcome this difficulty, and some of their connections with thermodynamic principles. They also arise as truncations or linearizations of some nonlocal-in-time systems, e.g. those due to fixed or distributed time delays arising in gene expression
[
59
]
. As an example of applying our constructions to these systems, we consider the following system from
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=d_{11}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+d_{12}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}+f(u,v),

\displaystyle\tau\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial t^{2}}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}=d_{21}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+d_{22}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}+g(u,v),

(15)

with a particular choice of kinetics of FitzHugh-Nagumo type

\displaystyle f(u,v)

\displaystyle=u-au^{3}+v-b,

\displaystyle g(u,v)

\displaystyle=b-u-3av.

This system turns out to be equivalent to a degenerate reaction-diffusion system given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=w,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=z,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(u-au^{3}+v-b-w+d_{11}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+d_{12}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(b-u-3av-z+d_{21}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+d_{22}\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}\right).

While the analysis in
[
31
]
can be used to determine if a given set of parameters admits Turing or wave instabilities, the approach we use below allows us to constructively build a particular set of diffusion parameters (for a slightly modified system). Importantly, the method here generalizes to systems of
n>2
hyperbolic equations, though it is easier to see in this simplified setting.

The diffusion matrix is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
\frac{d_{11}}{\tau}&\frac{d_{12}}{\tau}&0&0\\
\frac{d_{21}}{\tau}&\frac{d_{22}}{\tau}&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

As this system involves non-diffusible elements (see e.g.
[
60
]
), we cannot immediately apply the constructions developed throughout this article, as all the eigenvalues of
\hat{D}
are equal to zero for any diffusion rates. However, if we introduce a small change to this matrix, corresponding to a kind of parabolic perturbation, then we will be able to analyze it as a reaction-cross-diffusion system to produce Turing or wave instabilities using the theory we have developed. In particular, if we consider

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&\sigma_{1}&0&0\\
\frac{d_{11}}{\tau}&\frac{d_{12}}{\tau}&\sigma_{2}&0\\
\frac{d_{21}}{\tau}&\frac{d_{22}}{\tau}&0&\sigma_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(16)

then

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1},

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\frac{d_{12}\sigma_{1}\tau-d_{12}\sigma_{2}\tau}{d_{12}d_{21}-d_{11}d_{22}}&\frac{d_{22}\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)\tau}{d_{11}d_{22}-d_{12}d_{21}}&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{d_{11}\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)\tau}{d_{11}d_{22}-d_{12}d_{21}}&\frac{d_{21}\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)\tau}{d_{12}d_{21}-d_{11}d_{22}}&0&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&1&0&1\\
1&0&1&0\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&\sigma_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&\sigma_{2}&0\\
0&0&0&\sigma_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Now, if we consider the steady state given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\left(u^{*},v^{*},0,0\right),

where

\displaystyle u^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt[3]{2}M^{2}+6a^{3}-2a^{2}}{3\cdot 2^{2/3}a^{2}M},

\displaystyle v^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{-2^{2/3}M^{2}-6\sqrt[3]{2}a^{3}+2a^{2}\left(3bM+\sqrt[3]{2}\right)}{18a^{3}M},

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=\left(9(1-3a)a^{4}b+\sqrt{N}\right)^{1/3},

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=(1-3a)^{2}a^{6}\left(3a\left(27ab^{2}-4\right)+4\right),

and take, for example, the parameter values
(a,b,\tau)=(0.257,0.98,0.1)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1\\
6.30191&10&-10&0\\
-10.&-7.71&0&-10\end{array}\right),

which turns out to be stable. Furthermore, the submatrices
\mathbf{S_{1,2}}
and
\mathbf{S_{3,4}}
of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
turn out to be

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha_{1,2}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{d_{22}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}&\frac{d_{21}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}\\[4.30554pt]
\frac{d_{12}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}&\frac{d_{11}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha_{3,4}}:=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{d_{22}+\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}&-\frac{d_{21}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}\\
-\frac{d_{12}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}&-\frac{d_{11}+\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}}{\tau\left(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\right)}\end{pmatrix},

respectively. This implies that we can make any of these matrices unstable, and that will let us generate diffusion-driven instabilities through
\sigma_{1}
or
\sigma_{2}

\sigma_{2}=1

If we make

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha_{1,2}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{d_{22}}{\tau\sigma_{2}}&-\frac{d_{21}}{\tau\sigma_{2}}\\[4.30554pt]

The only thing we need to do is to find parameters so that

is unstable. In particular, if we set

\displaystyle\left(d_{11},d_{12},d_{21},d_{22}\right)=\left(-1,-1,0,-2\right),

4.3
3-component malaria model

In
[
5
]
, the authors provided an example of a 3-component malaria model that was shown to be unable to exhibit Turing or wave instabilities for any diagonal diffusion matrix. We now show that we can generate diffusion-driven instabilities in this model if we consider chemotactic fluxes among the populations. In particular, we consider said system as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})+\partial_{x}\left(\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u})\partial_{x}\mathbf{u}\right),

(18)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=(H,I,P)^{\intercal},\quad\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{pmatrix}(b_{H}-d_{H})H-cPH+rI\\
-d_{H}I+cPH-rI\\
-d_{M}P+b(Q-P)I\end{pmatrix},

(19)

and the nonlinear diffusion tensor is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1,1}&\chi\left(\frac{H}{H^{*}}\right)D_{1,2}&\chi\left(\frac{H}{H^{*}}\right)D_{1,3}\\
\chi\left(\frac{I}{I^{*}}\right)D_{2,1}&D_{2,2}&0\\
0&0&D_{3,3}\end{pmatrix},\quad\chi(V)=\frac{2V}{1+V^{2}},

(20)

The variables
H
and
I
represent the populations of healthy and infected humans, respectively, whilst
P
is the population of infected mosquitoes. Moreover,
b_{H}
represents the birth rate of healthy humans,
d_{H}
the death rate of humans,
c
the infection rate between humans,
r
the human recovery rate,
d_{M}
the death rate of infected mosquitoes, and
b
is the infection rate between mosquitoes (see
[
61
]
for discussion of substantially more complicated compartment-based models of malaria transmission dynamics, from which the present model is a simplified albeit spatially-extended variant). Note that healthy mosquitoes do not appear explicitly in this model, as
Q
represents the total (fixed) mosquito population, so
Q-P
is the population of healthy mosquitoes.

We interpret the cross-diffusion terms as chemotactic fluxes
[
62
]
with saturating sensitivities corresponding to volume-filling effects
[
63
]
. This represents populations moving along signalling gradients of the other populations. We have assumed several zeros in the diffusion matrix as we do not expect mosquitoes’ spatial movements to be largely influenced directly by human populations. To fix ideas, we will focus on generating Turing instabilities by varying the diffusion matrix, with a constrained diffusion tensor having the form given by
Eq.

21
. The variables
H^{*}
and
I^{*}
represent homogeneous steady-state values of these parameters. Due to the choice of the sensitivity function
\chi
, we have that
\chi(1)=1
. Hence, after linearizing around such a homogeneous steady state, we have the linear diffusion tensor:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\begin{pmatrix}D_{1,1}&D_{1,2}&D_{1,3}\\
D_{2,1}&D_{2,2}&0\\
0&0&D_{3,3}\end{pmatrix}.

(21)

To preserve the physical interpretations of this model, we explicitly state that all the variables and parameters are non-negative,
b_{H}>d_{H}>0
, and
P\leq Q
. We also assume the diffusion matrix to have positive real eigenvalues, so we need
D_{3,3}>0
, together with

\displaystyle D_{1,1}+D_{2,2}

\displaystyle>0,

5
Discussion

We have developed an approach to designing reaction-cross-diffusion systems exhibiting Turing or wave instabilities for systems involving several components. We showed how to constructively build unstable principal submatrices which essentially encode these instabilities, and place these within the design constraints of a given system. The assumptions in
Theorems

1
and
2
are rather mild, suggesting that this process can be employed for large classes of systems, leaving flexibility in the constructions to allow for additional design constraints if they arise. This demonstrates some of the freedom gained in allowing non-diagonal diffusion tensors, or in having complete control over linearized kinetics. Finally, we showed these ideas in four distinct models with a range of linear and nonlinear transport, highlighting further issues of model construction that may arise, such as in preventing blowups or ensuring the positivity of solutions when this is required. We remark that all but the first example demonstrated employing these ideas in cases where we did not allow complete freedom over either matrix in the linearization, but instead had to work in a more constrained setting. An important thermodynamic consideration is to restrict to diffusion matrices
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
that are symmetric, as these can more easily be justified from the thermodynamic perspective
[
66
]
. In such cases, Theorem
2
can be applied directly, although the constructions used in proving Theorem
1
would require significant changes that we leave for future work.

There are numerous future directions from the work carried out here. One could try to generalize
Theorem

2
by assuming that the diffusion matrix
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
admits complex eigenvalues. While such matrices are easier to work with in some sense (the set of diagonalizable matrices is dense in the set of invertible matrices), complex eigenvalues would require more care in carrying out the procedure given in
Lemma

1
, and hence may require entirely different constructions to arrive at something like
Theorem

2
. In a different direction, one could consider evolution equations with nonlocal spatial operators. Such integro-differential equations show stark differences with their classical local counterparts, allowing for spatiotemporal pattern formation in scalar models
[
67
]
, and showing dependence on the geometry not exhibited by local models even in the linear stability theory
[
68
]
. Such cases are more intricate as the symbol of the operator (i.e. the function of the wavenumber one obtains from the linearization in spectral space) is no longer a polynomial in general. Thus, developing an analogue of the procedure presented here would be substantially more involved. In contrast, the results we have presented essentially apply even to reaction-cross-diffusion systems posed on general Riemannian manifolds or even networks, modulo finite-size effects
(
8
, Section 3)
.

Although the linear theory developed is useful, Turing and wave instabilities do not ensure on their own that one will always be able to find specific kinds of solutions emerging from the instability of a spatially homogeneous equilibrium. We focused on the instability of a single homogeneous steady state, but realistic systems may admit more than one such state, leading to cases where the linear theory fails to capture the emergence of patterning due to other attracting states
[
69
]
. As we showed in the first example, calculations of the criticality of the bifurcations can be invaluable to ensure that a nearby stable pattern exists in the case of a supercritical bifurcation. In contrast, a subcritical bifurcation is a situation where the linear theory is not always a good indicator of subsequent emergent behaviour
[
4
,
57
,
70
,
44
]
. We note, in particular, that the two-species Keller-Segel model with logistic growth can exhibit spatiotemporal chaos after a subcritical Turing instability, despite such two-species systems not exhibiting wave instabilities or even Hopf bifurcations of homogeneous states
[
65
]
(though see
[
71
,
72
]
for cases where patterned states undergo Hopf bifurcations leading to these spatiotemporal behaviours). Hence, there is substantial work to be done in building design principles that extend to the weakly nonlinear setting, and even developing a language of constraints for nonlinearities. Related to this limitation, the assumption of homogeneous Neumann or periodic conditions precludes the interplay of more complex boundary conditions which can modify patterns and even initiate patterning outside of the Turing space determined here via linear theory
[
46
,
47
]
.

In a different direction, we note that, depending on the purpose of the mathematical model, having too much freedom in the design can lead to unsatisfying theories. Given full control over the nonlinearities, one can design even two-component reaction-diffusion systems that exhibit a wide variety of pre-defined behaviours
[
42
,
73
]
, with essentially the freedom to match any kind of patterned state. Given such freedom, one must impose constraints that involve both linear and nonlinear aspects of the system under study to ensure that the choices made make sense in the modelling context. Similarly, while the results we have presented are general and powerful, one can argue that they provide too much freedom. In particular, there are few systems where one has complete control over either transport or reactions. Nevertheless, experimental protocols are increasingly providing more ways to create and tune a number of systems to exhibit patterning
[
74
,
37
,
75
]
, and the fundamental routes to instabilities provided here can provide some guidance on pursuing these in increasingly sophisticated systems even under constraints. Importantly, we note that the ideas used apply even with more constraints in the given system, as shown in the examples.

Appendix A
Useful results for designing unstable systems

Here, we collect a few additional lemmas that provide alternative ways of viewing the constructions illustrated above. In practice, particularly given additional constraints (i.e. when one does not have complete freedom over
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
or
\mathbf{J}
), these results can help one find parameters to exhibit Turing or wave instabilities. We begin by showing that
Lemma

1
has a converse, implying that, for a diagonalizable diffusion tensor, the existence of a principle unstable submatrix of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
is both necessary and sufficient for diffusion-driven instability.

The authors in
(
21
, Theorem 1)
state that, when the diffusion matrix of a reaction-diffusion system is diagonal and the Jacobian matrix of the system in the absence of diffusion is S-stable, then the system does not admit Turing instabilities. In the proof of
(
5
, Theorem 2)
, the authors noted that the original proof also applies to wave instabilities. We can directly use this result to obtain an equivalent statement in the case of reaction-cross-diffusion systems, as in the case of a non-diagonal
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
.

The growth rates (eigenvalues) of the original system given in
Eq.

2
are equivalent to those of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
. Hence by directly applying
(
5
, Theorem 2)
to this system, we have the following Lemma:

Lemma 6
.

Assuming that
\mathbf{\hat{D}}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbb{D}\mathbf{Q}^{-1}
is diagonalizable with non-negative real eigenvalues, and
\mathbf{J}
is a stable matrix, then if
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
is an S-stable matrix, the system
Eq.

2
does not admit Turing or wave instabilities.

The proofs of
Section

2.1
and
Section

2.2
both relied on the use of
Lemma

1
, where some diffusion coefficients were taken to be zero in an asymptotic limit. We can instead use an alternative approach where some diffusion coefficients tend to infinity instead. We can obtain this as a corollary of
(
5
, Lemma 6)
applied to the diagonalized system
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
be a diagonalizable matrix with non-negative real eigenvalues. Assume that
\mathbf{\hat{D}}
has
\ell
eigenvalues
\sigma_{i_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{i_{\ell}}
that tend to
\infty
, while all the others remain positive but finite. Then, there exist
\ell
eigenvalues of
\mathbf{J}-\mu\mathbf{\hat{D}}
having real parts that tend to
-\infty
for every
\mu>0
, while
n-\ell
eigenvalues of said matrix tend to the eigenvalues of the principal submatrix of
\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}
formed out of the rows and columns that are complementary to
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}
, as
\mu\to 0^{+}
.

Next, we develop two lemmas useful in dealing with systems that have constraints (i.e. ones where we cannot control every element of
\mathbf{D}
or
\mathbf{J}
). By an elementary formula (see, e.g.
(

Let

\displaystyle\det\left(\mathbf{J}-\lambda\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=b_{n}\lambda^{n}+b_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1}+b_{n-2}\lambda^{n-2}+\ldots b_{1}\lambda+b_{0},

(29)

where

\displaystyle b_{m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{n-m}\leq n}M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{n-m}}\right),\quad\text{for }1\leq m<n,

Lemma

enables us to prove a general result that can be used to generate Turing and wave instabilities in systems with more constraints. To motivate the coming result, we note that although

where

\displaystyle b_{m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\left(c_{1,1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell-m-1}\leq\ell}M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell-m-1}}\right.

(34)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell-m-1}\leq\ell\\
r\in\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-m-1}\right\}\end{subarray}}c_{1,r}\left(\sum_{s\in\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-m-1}\right\}}c_{s,1}M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell-m-1}}^{s,r}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell-m}\leq\ell}M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell-m}}\right),\quad\text{for }1\leq m\leq\ell,

(35)

b_{0}=\det\left(\mathbf{B}\right)
, and
b_{\ell+1}=(-1)^{\ell+1}
. Observe that the first two sums in
Eq.

35
are linear combinations of the coefficients
\left\{c_{1,s}\right\}_{s=1}^{\ell+1}
whilst the third one is a constant term depending only on
\mathbf{K}
.

On the other hand, the characteristic polynomial of
\mathbf{A}
has the same form with different coefficients:

\displaystyle a_{\ell+1}\lambda^{\ell+1}+a_{\ell}\lambda^{\ell}+\ldots+a_{1}\lambda+a_{0},

where
a_{p}\in\mathbb{R}
for
p=0,1,\ldots,\ell+1
.

We need to solve the system of equations given by
a_{m}=b_{m}
, for each
m=0,1,\ldots,\ell
(clearly,
a_{\ell+1}=b_{\ell+1}
).

Thus, we need to solve a system of equations with the following form:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}p_{0,1}&p_{0,2}&\cdots&p_{0,\ell+1}\\
p_{1,1}&p_{1,2}&\cdots&p_{1,\ell+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
p_{\ell,1}&p_{\ell,2}&\cdots&p_{\ell,\ell+1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c_{1,1}\\
c_{1,2}\\
\vdots\\
c_{1,\ell+1}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{b},

where, for
0\leq m\leq\ell
,

\displaystyle p_{m,1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell-m-1}\leq\ell}M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell-m-1}},

\displaystyle p_{m,r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell-m-1}\leq\ell\\
s\in\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-m-1}\right\}\end{subarray}}c_{s,1}M_{i_{1}\ldots i_{\ell-m-1}}^{s,i_{r}},\quad\text{for }2\leq r\leq\ell+1,i_{r}\in\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-m-1}\right\}.

Note that, by assumption, there exist coefficients
\left\{c_{s,1}\right\}_{s=2}^{\ell+1}
so that the matrix of coefficients of this system of equations is invertible, which implies the existence of a solution
\left\{c_{1,s}\right\}_{s=1}^{\ell+1}
so that the conclusion holds.
∎
