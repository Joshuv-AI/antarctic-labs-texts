Alpha-Asarone Ameliorates Dry Age-Related Macular Degeneration by Modulating the P2X7/NLRP3 Inflammasome Axis
Authors: Xin Wang, Yuxiu Bi, Yuxiang Liu, Yao Nie, Junzhou Ding, Lei Yu, Shengjun Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo examine whether α-asarone (ASA) protects dry age-related macular degeneration (dAMD) from sodium iodate (SI) injury by modulating the P2X7-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome axis.MethodsMale Sprague-Dawley rats were treated with SI to establish a dAMD model. Retinal structure and function were evaluated using electroretinography (ERG), optical coherence tomography (OCT), and hematoxylin-eosin staining, whereas the expression of functional and inflammatory proteins was analyzed via immunofluorescence and Western blotting. Molecular docking, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) were performed to explore the potential interaction between ASA and P2X7. In vitro, SI-stimulated ARPE-19 cells were used to evaluate the protective effects of ASA and the involvement of P2X7 through Cell Counting Kit-8 assays, Annexin V-FITC/PI staining, Fluo-4 staining, YO-PRO-1 uptake detection, ELISA, and Western blotting.ResultsASA preserved retinal structure and function, maintained the expression of key functional proteins, suppressed microglial activation, and attenuated inflammatory responses. Computational modeling suggested a stable potential interaction between ASA and P2X7, and CETSA further supported ASA-induced modulation of P2X7 thermal stability in cells. In vitro, ASA enhanced cell viability, reduced apoptosis, attenuated Ca²⁺ influx and P2X7 macropore formation, decreased the release of inflammatory cytokines, and downregulated P2X7/NLRP3 pathway, all of which were effectively abolished by activating P2X7.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate that ASA mitigates SI-induced dAMD injury by suppressing the P2X7/NLRP3 inflammatory pathway. This study identifies a promising new therapeutic strategy for dAMD.

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a progressive chorioretinal disease and the leading cause of irreversible vision loss in older adults worldwide.1 Among its clinical subtypes, dry AMD (dAMD) is the most prevalent. In dAMD, impaired clearance of photoreceptor metabolic waste drives retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) degeneration and secondary photoreceptor loss, culminating in geographic atrophy (GA).2 Its pathogenesis is multifactorial—involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and dysregulated lipid metabolism—all of which converge on the RPE as the primary site of disease progression.3 Current pharmacological interventions for dAMD are restricted to intravitreal complement inhibitors (e.g., pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol), which prevent C3 convertase formation and C5 cleavage.4 However, repeated intravitreal injections impose a significant treatment burden, carry risks such as endophthalmitis, and yield suboptimal patient compliance.5 Consequently, identifying novel, RPE-targeted cytoprotective agents remains an urgent clinical need.

Chronic inflammation plays a pivotal role in dAMD.6 Age-related inflammatory cascades provoke RPE dysfunction and microglial activation, leading to secondary photoreceptor degeneration.7 Critically, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome serves as a major driver of RPE injury.8 NLRP3 inflammasome assembly triggers caspase-1 cleavage, promoting Gasdermin D–mediated pore formation and pyroptotic cell death.9 Concurrently, sustained inflammasome activation exacerbates lysosomal dysfunction and mitochondrial impairment, thereby disrupting retinal metabolic homeostasis.10 This complex pathological network underscores the need for therapies capable of addressing multiple mechanisms of RPE injury.6

The P2X purinoceptor 7 (P2X7), a ligand-gated cation channel activated by extracellular ATP, mediates various cellular signaling cascades.11 Within the retina, P2X7 is broadly expressed across multiple cell populations, including the RPE, photoreceptors, and microglia.12,13 Pathological P2X7 overactivation during retinal stress triggers the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and accelerating dAMD progression.14,15 Thus, dysregulation of the P2X7/NLRP3 axis is implicated in various retinal pathologies,16,17 making it a promising therapeutic target for RPE cytoprotection.

The α-asarone (ASA), a lipophilic secondary metabolite derived from the rhizome of Acorus calamus (L.), exhibits neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties.18 Mechanistically, ASA exerts these effects by scavenging free radicals, mitigating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress,19 and suppressing microglial activation and cytokine production.20 Notably, although ASA modulates the NLRP3 inflammasome in various central nervous system disorders,21 its therapeutic potential and underlying molecular mechanisms within the RPE during dAMD remain uncharacterized.

Here, we hypothesized that ASA protects against dAMD-associated retinal damage by targeting the P2X7/NLRP3 inflammatory axis. Using established in vitro and in vivo models of dAMD, we investigated the cytoprotective efficacy of ASA and elucidated its molecular mechanisms. Ultimately, our findings suggest that ASA preserves RPE and photoreceptor integrity by suppressing P2X7/NLRP3 inflammasome activation, potentially through interaction with P2X7.

Methods

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (6–8 weeks old, 180–220g) were obtained from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China). Rats were acclimatized for 1 week under controlled conditions (24°C ± 1°C, 12-hour light/dark cycle) before experimentation. All animal procedures strictly adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Ethics Committee for the Use of Laboratory Animals at Sichuan University (License No. SYXK [Chuan] 2018-113).

Animal Models and Drug Administration

Sodium iodate (SI) (S817792, Macklin, China) was used to induce the dAMD model. The ASA submicron emulsion was provided by Chengdu Xinrui Taikang Technology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Rats were randomly allocated into three groups: Sham, SI, and ASA. To evaluate prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy, rats in the ASA group received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of ASA (30 mg/kg/day) for 1 week before SI induction. Subsequently, the dAMD model was induced in both the SI and ASA groups via a single tail vein injection of SI (30 mg/kg).22,23 After induction, the ASA group continued to receive daily i.p. injections of ASA (30 mg/kg/day) for 3 weeks. The SI group received concurrent i.p. injections of an equal volume of the vehicle (an identical submicron emulsion lacking ASA). The Sham group received isovolumetric injections of saline both intravenously and intraperitoneally.

Electroretinogram (ERG)

Retinal function was evaluated using an ERG system (RetiMINER; AiErXi Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China) at days 7, 14, and 21 after induction. Rats were dark-adapted for 16 hours overnight. After general anesthesia, mydriasis was induced using topical tropicamide, and topical anesthesia was applied to the corneas. Correct electrode placement was ensured before initiating the measurement. The ERG protocols included scotopic 0.01 and scotopic 3.0 cd·s/m² testing, as well as scotopic oscillatory potentials (OPs) recordings. Data analysis primarily focused on the amplitudes of the a-wave, b-wave, and OPs.

Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)

In vivo retinal structural integrity was assessed using a SPECTRALIS HRA+OCT imaging platform (Heidelberg Engineering, Germany) at 3 weeks after induction. With the animals under general anesthesia and mydriasis (as described for ERG), cross-sectional retinal images were acquired. Retinal thickness was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

At the study endpoint, eyes were immersed in a standard ocular fixative overnight and embedded in paraffin. Sagittal sections (3 µm thick) containing the optic nerve head were collected. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were stained with H&E using standard histological protocols. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, mounted with neutral balsam, and imaged using an upright light microscope (CX-31; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

TUNEL Assay

Retinal apoptosis was assessed using the TUNEL BrightRed Apoptosis Detection Kit (A113; Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) on paraffin sections of eyeballs. The sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer at 98°C for 3 minutes. After cooling to room temperature, they were washed with PBS. Next, 50 µL of TdT incubation buffer was added to the sections and then placed at 4°C in the dark. The following day, the sections were stained with DAPI for 5 minutes in the dark and washed with PBS. Images were acquired using a fluorescence scanning microscope (P250 FLASH; 3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary). The number and distribution of TUNEL-positive cells in all retinas were quantified and analyzed.

Immunofluorescence

Retinal function and microglial activation were evaluated using immunofluorescence staining for RPE65, rhodopsin, ZO-1, and Iba-1/CD68. Paraffin sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer, followed by blocking with goat serum and incubation with primary antibodies (anti-RPE65, 1:500, ET1703-82 [Huabio, Hangzhou, China]; anti-rhodopsin, 1:100, ET1704-12 [Huabio]; anti-ZO-1, 1:100, ER41204 [Huabio]; anti-CD68, 1:100, ab303565 [Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA]; anti-Iba-1, 1:200, OB-PGP049-01 [Oasis Biofarm, Hangzhou, China]) overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, the sections were treated with a secondary antibody at 37°C for 30 minutes. The sections were counterstained with DAPI for 20 minutes and sealed using an antifade mounting medium. Images were acquired using a fluorescence scanning microscope (P250 FLASH). For quantification, fluorescence intensity was measured in three randomly selected fields per section using Image J, and the average value was calculated to represent each sample.

Western Blotting

The protein content of rats’ retinal samples and ARPE-19 cells was quantified using the BCA Protein Colorimetric Assay Kit (E-BC-K318-M; Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Subsequently, electrophoresis was performed on 10% SDS-PAGE gels loaded with 30 µg of protein per well, then transfer onto PVDF membranes and overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies (anti-P2X7, 1:500, ER1901-99 [Huabio]; anti-NLRP3, 1:500, ER1706-72 [Huabio]; anti-Pro-caspase-1, 1:500, ET1608-69 [Huabio]; anti-Cleaved-caspase-1, 1:500, YM9210 [ImmunoWay Biotechnology Company, Plano, TX, USA]; anti-IL-1β, 1:2000, HA601002 [Huabio]). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour with a secondary antibody and visualized using Gel Imaging Instrument (Doc XR+; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Ann Arbor, MI, USA).

Molecular Docking

The structures of human P2X7 (PDB ID: 9E3N) and ASA (PubChem CID: 636822) were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank and PubChem, respectively. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 4.2. Protein preparation included removing water molecules, adding polar hydrogens, and assigning Kollman charges. The ligand was energy-minimized and converted to PDBQT format via Open Babel 2.3.2. Using the Lamarckian genetic algorithm, docking was centered on the putative binding pocket, with the optimal pose selected based on the lowest binding free energy. Intermolecular interactions were subsequently analyzed using PyMOL 3.0.3. Meanwhile, the protein-ligand complexes were analyzed with LigPlot+ v2.2.8, which generates 2D interaction maps highlighting the participating amino acids and specifying the types and quantities of noncovalent interactions between the protein and the ligand.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation

All-atom MD simulations were performed using GROMACS v2022.03. The protein was parameterized using the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field, while the ligand topology was generated with the General AMBER Force Field (GAFF) and AM1-BCC charges via ACPYPE. The system was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a minimum clearance of 1.2 nm and neutralized with Na⁺ ions. After energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm, the system underwent 100 ps of NVT and 100 ps of NPT equilibration. Temperature (300 K) and pressure (1 bar) were regulated using the V-rescale and Berendsen methods, respectively. A 1.0 nm cutoff was applied for short-range non-bonded interactions, and the particle mesh Ewald method was used for long-range electrostatics. All hydrogen bonds were constrained by the LINCS algorithm. Finally, a 100 ns production run was conducted in the NPT ensemble with a 2 fs timestep, and trajectories were recorded every 10 ps for subsequent analysis.

Cell Culture

The adult retinal pigment epithelial-19 (ARPE-19) cell line, derived from human RPE, was provided by Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). These cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (KGL1201-500; KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Z7187FBS-500, Zeta Life, California, USA), and maintained in a cell incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

ARPE-19 cell lysates were incubated with ASA (25 and 50 µM) for 2 hours at room temperature. DMSO was used as a vehicle control. The mixtures were then subjected to a temperature gradient from 48°C to 64°C in 2°C increments, with each temperature maintained for 4 minutes. After a 3-minute cooling period, the samples were spun in a centrifuge at 20,000×g for 20 minutes at 4°C. The resulting supernatants were collected, mixed with protein loading buffer, and denatured at 70°C for 15 minutes. P2X7 protein levels were quantified via Western blot analysis to evaluate ASA-induced changes in protein thermal stability.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (BS350C; Biosharp, Beijing, China). ARPE-19 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well. To assess individual cytotoxicity, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of ASA (0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 25, 50 µM) or SI (0, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15 mM) for 24 hours. To evaluate the impact of ASA on SI-induced cell viability, the cells were pretreated with ASA (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4 µM) for 2, 4, or 6 hours before exposure to 12.5 mM SI for 24 hours. In a subsequent experiment, cells were pre-incubated with 2 µM ASA and 50 µM BzATP for 2 hours, followed by treatment with 12.5 mM SI for 24 hours. After the respective treatments, CCK-8 reagent was added, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Annexin V‑FITC/PI Assay

ARPE-19 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 hours before being subjected to various drug treatments. After the incubation period, cells were harvested, washed, and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI (E-CK-A211; Elabscience) for 20 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Cell apoptosis was subsequently analyzed using flow cytometry (NovoCyte 3005; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Fluo-4 Assay

ARPE-19 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 hours before exposure to the indicated drug treatments. After incubation, the cells were harvested, loaded with 0.7× Fluo-4 (S1061S; Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes in the dark. Subsequently, fluorescence intensity was analyzed using flow cytometry (NovoCyte 3005).

YO-PRO-1 Uptake Detection

ARPE-19 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 hours prior to the respective drug treatments. Following incubation, the cells were then incubated with 1 µM YO-PRO-1(C2022; Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology) at 37°C for 15 minutes, and the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Intracellular YO-PRO-1 fluorescence was captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM700; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

ELISA

ARPE-19 cells were seeded onto glass coverslips in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 hours before receiving various drug treatments. After incubation, the culture media were collected and spun in a centrifuge at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C to remove cellular debris. The cleared cell-free supernatants were then collected to quantify the concentrations of TNF-α (RX104793H), IL-1β (RX106152H), and IL-18 (RX106154H) using specific ELISA kits (all from Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Quanzhou, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical Analysis

The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). One-way ANOVA was used to assess differences between groups, followed by LSD post hoc analysis to determine the significance of the data, assuming normal distribution and equal variance. For data with non-equal variance but conforming to a normal distribution, Dunnett's T3 test was used to assess significance. Results associated with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

ASA Ameliorates Retinal Functional Impairment and Structural Damage in SI-Induced dAMD Rats

To evaluate the protective efficacy of ASA, we assessed retinal function and morphology using ERG, OCT, and H&E staining. ERG recordings demonstrated that SI induction significantly reduced the amplitudes of scotopic 0.01 and 3.0 cd·s/m² responses, as well as OPs; however, ASA treatment significantly attenuated these functional deficits (Figs. 1B, 1C) (Supplementary Fig. S1). Furthermore, OCT imaging revealed that ASA preserved total retinal thickness in SI-challenged rats (Figs. 1D, 1E). Histological analysis via H&E staining further confirmed that ASA prevented the SI-induced disorganization and thinning of the outer nuclear layer (ONL), maintaining a well-organized retinal architecture compared to the SI group (Figs. 1F, 1G). Collectively, these findings indicate that ASA effectively preserves retinal structural and functional integrity in a rat model of dAMD.

ASA Preserves RPE and Photoreceptor Integrity and Inhibits Retinal Cell Apoptosis

Immunofluorescence was performed to evaluate the expression of key functional and structural markers, including RPE65, rhodopsin, and ZO-1. As shown in Figure 2, SI induction led to a marked loss of these proteins, which was significantly prevented by ASA treatment. These results suggest that ASA preserves RPE metabolic function and structural scaffolding while protecting rod photoreceptors. Additionally, TUNEL staining was used to evaluate cell death in the retina. ASA significantly reduced the number of TUNEL-positive cells in the ONL, indicating that ASA effectively mitigates photoreceptor apoptosis induced by SI.

ASA Suppresses Microglial-Mediated Neuroinflammation and Regulates the P2X7/NLRP3 Signaling Pathway

We next investigated the effect of ASA on microglial-mediated neuroinflammation and the P2X7/NLRP3 axis. In dAMD rats, the number of activated microglia (Iba-1⁺/CD68⁺) was significantly increased. ASA markedly reduced the population of these activated cells, suggesting the suppression of microglial reactivity (Figs. 3A, 3C, 3D).

Given the role of P2X7 in retinal degeneration, we examined its expression and found that ASA significantly attenuated the SI-induced upregulation of P2X7. Furthermore, since P2X7 activation is linked to the NLRP3 inflammasome, we evaluated downstream inflammatory mediators. Western blot analysis revealed that the expression levels of NLRP3, Pro-caspase-1, Cleaved-caspase-1 and IL-1β were significantly elevated in the SI group but were markedly suppressed after ASA treatment (Figs. 3B, 3E). These data suggest that the protective effects of ASA are associated with the inhibition of the P2X7/NLRP3 inflammatory cascade.

P2X7 Is a Critical Target for ASA Therapy in dAMD

Having demonstrated the inhibitory effect of ASA on the P2X7/NLRP3 axis, we next investigated whether P2X7 serves as a direct molecular target. Molecular docking was initially performed to explore the interaction mode between ASA and the P2X7 protein. The docking results revealed an optimal conformation with a binding free energy of −4.733 kcal/mol. Notably, ASA stably inserted into the interface of the P2X7 trimeric complex, forming strong hydrogen bond interactions (Figs. 4A, 4B).

To comprehensively evaluate the dynamic stability of this complex, a 100-ns MD simulation was conducted. During the simulation, the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the complex rapidly increased to 0.7 nm within the first 20 ns and subsequently plateaued, indicating that the system quickly achieved conformational convergence (Fig. 4C). Root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis showed that fluctuations for most residues were less than 0.5 nm, suggesting that the binding interface maintained high structural rigidity (Fig. 4D). Both the radius of gyration (Rg, ∼4.78 nm) and the solvent-accessible surface area (SASA, ∼800 nm²) remained stable, reflecting the overall compactness of the complex (Figs. 4E, 4F). Throughout the trajectory, the complex maintained an average of 1 or 2 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 4G). The Gibbs free energy landscape (FEL) displayed a single distinct energy basin, confirming that the system was thermodynamically stable within this conformational cluster (Fig. 4H). Furthermore, MM/PBSA calculations of the final 20 ns yielded a total binding free energy of −19.10 ± 2.77 kcal/mol (Fig. 4I), with per-residue energy decomposition identifying SER-342, LEU-336, and TYR-343 of chain A as the primary interacting residues (Fig. 4J). Structural snapshots captured across the 100-ns trajectory illustrated a dynamic yet stable binding process, resulting in a stabilized contact mode (Fig. 4K).

To empirically validate this physical interaction in living cells, a cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) was performed (Fig. 4L). The melting temperature (Tm) of P2X7 in the vehicle control (DMSO) group was 56.24°C. Upon co-incubation with ASA, the Tm of P2X7 exhibited a significant, dose-dependent thermal destabilization, decreasing to 54.69°C at 25 µM and further dropping to 42.08°C at 50 µM ASA (Figs. 4M, 4N). Collectively, these results suggest that ASA may interact with P2X7 and support P2X7 as a potential molecular target of ASA.

Activating P2X7 Inhibits the Protective Effect of ASA in SI-Induced ARPE-19 Cell Injury

CCK-8 assays confirmed that ASA (0–50 µM) exhibited no cytotoxicity in ARPE-19 cells after 24 hours (Fig. 5A), and 12.5 mM SI was selected as the optimal concentration to induce cellular injury (Fig. 5B). To evaluate the protective role of ASA, cells were pretreated prior to SI exposure. Pretreatment with ASA (0.25–4 µM) for 2 hours significantly attenuated the SI-induced decline in cell viability, with 2 µM ASA demonstrating the optimal protective effect (Fig. 5C) (Supplementary Fig. S2) and significantly reducing SI-induced apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. S3). Importantly, this survival benefit was abrogated upon co-administration of the specific P2X7 agonist BzATP (Fig. 5D).

Consistent with these viability data, flow cytometry and imaging analyses revealed that ASA markedly reduced the proportion of SI-induced apoptotic cells (Figs. 5E, 5G), mitigated intracellular Ca²⁺ accumulation (Figs. 5F, 5H), inhibited P2X7-mediated macropore formation (Figs. 6A, 6C), and suppressed the expression of P2X7/NLRP3 pathway proteins (Figs. 6B, 6G–K), thereby decreasing the release of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18 (Figs. 6D–F). Crucially, co-treatment with BzATP effectively reversed all of these molecular and cellular benefits. Taken together, these data suggest that the anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects of ASA are associated with P2X7 antagonism.

Discussion

At present, pharmacological interventions for dAMD remain severely limited. Although intravitreal complement inhibitors slow lesion expansion in GA, they fail to confer clinically meaningful improvements in visual acuity and are associated with severe adverse events, including incident exudative AMD and retinal vasculitis, leading to high patient attrition.24 Beyond complement inhibition, direct RPE cytoprotection remains a critical unmet need in dAMD. Addressing the primary RPE injury and chronic inflammation central to dAMD pathogenesis, we identify ASA as a novel and potent protective agent. Mechanistically, our findings suggest that ASA exerts its protective effects by engaging the P2X7 in RPE cells. This interaction preserves transmembrane ion homeostasis and impedes P2X7 macropore formation, thereby blunting RPE apoptosis and downstream inflammatory signaling. Ultimately, this cascade preserves both retinal architecture and visual function, highlighting the modulation of the P2X7 pathway by ASA as a promising therapeutic strategy for dAMD (Fig. 7).

Proposed working model illustrating the protective mechanism of ASA in dAMD. In the RPE (highlighted in the left panel), ASA suppresses P2X7 receptor activation. This inhibition attenuates extracellular ATP-driven pathological ion fluxes (Ca²⁺ influx and K⁺ efflux) and limits macropore formation. Consequently, downstream NLRP3 inflammasome assembly is suppressed, reducing caspase-1 cleavage and the maturation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β and IL-18). Ultimately, by modulating the P2X7/NLRP3 inflammatory cascade, ASA mitigates RPE cell death and preserves the structural and functional integrity of the retina. Created with BioRender.com.

Plant-derived compounds characterized by multi-target profiles represent a promising reservoir for ocular therapeutics.25–31 Although ASA, a primary active constituent of Acorus calamus (L.), exhibits well-documented anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective properties in central nervous system pathologies,18,32,33 its therapeutic potential in ocular disease remains unexplored. In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that ASA exerts significant protective effects against SI-induced dAMD, and we elucidated its molecular target.

To determine the in vivo protective effects of ASA, we initially evaluated retinal functional and structural integrity in dAMD rats. ERG demonstrated that ASA effectively preserved retinal electrophysiological responses, indicating the maintenance of photoreceptor function. Morphologically, in vivo OCT and histological analyses confirmed that ASA maintained retinal structure and ONL thickness. This structural preservation was further supported by a significant reduction in ONL apoptosis, as evidenced by TUNEL staining. Furthermore, because normal rod activity depends on phototransduction,34 we assessed rhodopsin expression via immunofluorescence. ASA treatment markedly preserved rhodopsin levels, indicating the functional maintenance of rod outer segments and light-sensing capacity. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that ASA effectively mitigated secondary photoreceptor degeneration.

Given that photoreceptor survival depends on RPE homeostasis and metabolic support,35 we evaluated whether ASA preserved key functional and structural markers of the RPE. We found that ASA maintained the expression of RPE65, indicating the preservation of RPE metabolic capacity and visual cycle function.36,37 Furthermore, ASA prevented the loss of the tight junction protein ZO-1, thereby sustaining the integrity of the outer blood-retinal barrier.38,39 Collectively, these in vivo data demonstrated that ASA effectively protected both the functional and structural integrity of the RPE.

Aging is accompanied by the dysregulation of immune and inflammatory pathways in the eye. This low-grade, sterile, chronic inflammation, termed “inflammaging”, driven primarily by NLRP3 inflammasome hyperactivation, is central to dAMD progression.40–42 In this context, impaired RPE metabolic clearance triggers NLRP3 assembly, which subsequently promotes microglial activation and exacerbates retinal degeneration.43–47 Although ASA and its analogs have been shown to suppress NLRP3-mediated neuroinflammation in non-ocular models,21,48–50 their immunomodulatory role in dAMD remains unexplored. Our results demonstrated that dAMD pathology induced significant retinal microglial activation and upregulated NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β. ASA administration effectively attenuated these inflammatory markers both in vivo and in vitro. Together, these data suggest that the cytoprotective efficacy of ASA involves the concurrent suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome cascade and microglial reactivity.

To elucidate the upstream target mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of ASA, we focused on P2X7. During retinal degeneration, excessive extracellular ATP activates P2X7, leading to non-selective macropore formation on the RPE plasma membrane. The ensuing intracellular K+ efflux and Ca2+ overload trigger NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and subsequent RPE cell death.11,51,52 Here, our findings suggest that ASA modulates this pathway, potentially by engaging the P2X7. Molecular docking and MD simulations predicted a stable interaction interface; furthermore, CETSA in ARPE-19 cells provided complementary evidence supporting intracellular target engagement. Consistent with this putative interaction, ASA treatment functionally suppressed P2X7 activity. Specifically, ASA inhibited P2X7-mediated macropore formation and mitigated intracellular calcium overload, subsequently downregulating NLRP3-associated protein expression and attenuating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

Although our data identified the P2X7/NLRP3 axis as a primary mechanism underlying ASA-mediated RPE protection, certain limitations warrant consideration. Mechanistically, as a naturally derived compound, ASA likely possesses pleiotropic pharmacological properties. Although our findings highlight the central role of the P2X7/NLRP3 axis, the potential involvement of parallel molecular pathways requires further investigation to fully delineate its therapeutic profile. Furthermore, although in silico simulations and CETSA provide evidence of target engagement, definitive confirmation of the physical binding between ASA and P2X7 necessitates future advanced biophysical and genetic interrogations, such as surface plasmon resonance or site-directed mutagenesis of key binding residues. At the in vivo level, the systemic SI model induces acute oxidative toxicity, which cannot entirely recapitulate the chronic, progressive complexity of clinical dAMD. Nevertheless, this model reliably reproduces critical disease hallmarks—namely, selective RPE degeneration and secondary photoreceptor loss—making it a robust and widely accepted surrogate for studying RPE death mechanisms relevant to GA.53,54 Ultimately, translating these preclinical findings into clinical application requires optimizing the drug delivery strategy. Given the unique physiological barriers of the eye, developing targeted, topical ophthalmic formulations of ASA will be the focal point of our subsequent translational efforts.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that ASA protects against dAMD-like retinal degeneration by modulating the P2X7/NLRP3 inflammatory axis. Through the suppression of P2X7-mediated signaling and subsequent NLRP3 inflammasome activation, ASA preserves RPE homeostasis and mitigates secondary photoreceptor loss. These findings underscore the P2X7/NLRP3 axis as a critical therapeutic target and provide a mechanistic rationale for developing ASA as a complement-independent treatment strategy for dAMD.
