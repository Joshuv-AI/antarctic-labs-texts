Broiler performance, intestinal adaptation, and environmental responses to graded dietary inclusion of bacterial single-cell protein
Authors: Mahfuza Ferdous, M Elizabeth E Ball, James C Wangui, Ramesh Selvaraj, Ilias Kyriazakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We evaluated the effects of replacing soybean meal (SBM) with Methylococcus capsulatus bacterial single-cell protein (BSCP) at varying inclusion levels and early dietary exposure on broiler performance, gut physiology, nutrient utilization, and environmental emissions. A total of 480-day-old Ross 308 broilers were allocated to six dietary treatments across starter (d0–10), grower (d11–21), and finisher (d22–35) phases. During the starter phase, birds received either an SBM control diet (N) or a BSCP diet containing 3.125% BSCP (E). In the grower and finisher phases, a 2 × 2 factorial arrangements evaluated low (6.25%; L) or high (12.5%; H) BSCP inclusion within each starter treatment, alongside an SBM control (C). An adaptation treatment (E-l-H) received incremental BSCP inclusion (3.125→6.25→12.5%) across phases. Starter ADG and ADFI were unaffected by diet, although FCR tended to be better in N birds (1.19 vs. 1.23; P = 0.07). Overall growth performance was maintained across treatments (P > 0.05). Orthogonal contrasts showed higher ADG (67.5 vs. 64.1 g/bird/d; P = 0.002) and a tendency for improved FCR (1.31 vs. 1.37; P = 0.08) in C birds during the grower phase, whereas BSCP-fed birds tended to consume more feed during the finisher phase (160.4 vs. 152.3 g/bird/d; P = 0.08). Apparent ileal dry matter digestibility increased with high BSCP inclusion during the grower and finisher phases (P < 0.01). Lean tissue accretion was maintained, although fat mass increased during the finisher phase (P = 0.03) with BSCP inclusion. Early BSCP exposure interacted with dietary inclusion level to increase intestinal aminopeptidase-N gene expression during the grower phase (P = 0.03), suggesting enhanced digestive adaptation. Expression of ileal tight junction genes claudin-1, claudin-5, and zonula occludens-1 reduced in BSCP-fed birds, while claudin-2 expression increased in grower phase (P < 0.05). Starter excreta uric acid concentration was elevated in BSCP-fed birds (29.8 vs. 21.9 mg/g; P = 0.03), whereas litter gas emissions were unaffected (P > 0.05). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that early BSCP exposure facilitates adaptation to dietary inclusion levels up to 12.5% without compromising growth performance or environmental emissions, although a transient metabolic response during the grower phase supports a phased inclusion strategy.

Introduction

Although soybean meal (SBM) remains one of the most widely used and nutritionally effective protein sources in commercial broiler production (Dao and Moss, 2025), growing concerns have emerged regarding its long-term sustainability, particularly in regions heavily dependent on its import (Karlsson et al., 2021). These concerns stem from multiple interconnected factors, including vulnerability to geopolitical disruptions affecting global supply chains, the substantial environmental footprint associated with long-distance transportation, and, critically, the land-use change impacts linked to soybean expansion in exporting countries (Song et al., 2021). Consequently, there is increasing impetus within the poultry sector to identify and develop alternative protein sources to reduce reliance on environmentally costly, externally sourced SBM (Acar et al., 2024). This trend is clearly reflected in a growing body of recent research exploring sustainable feed innovations (Wangui et al., 2025). One particularly direction focuses on integrating principles of the circular bioeconomy, which emphasizes regenerative resource use and the valorization of biological materials to support more sustainable agri-food systems (Khanna et al., 2024; Muscat et al., 2021).

One promising alternative protein source emerging from circular bioeconomy strategies is single-cell protein (SCP). The use of SCP is potentially suitable for sustainable poultry nutrition because it can be generated through the bioconversion of waste and low-value co-products from the dairy, food, and sugar industries, as well as carbohydrate-rich agricultural residues (Gundupalli et al., 2024). These substrates support the growth of a wide range of microbial taxa including bacteria, yeasts, filamentous fungi, and microalgae, which can be cultivated to produce high-quality protein biomass (Nyyssola et al., 2022; Ritala et al., 2017). Offering a scalable and resource-efficient protein source with the potential to reduce dependence on imported SBM (Keohavong, 2026).

The focus of this paper is the potential use of bacterial SCP (BSCP) produced via methane-fed fermentation using Methylococcus capsulatus as a protein source for broilers, which utilizes methane as its primary carbon and energy source (Gundupalli et al., 2024; Overland et al., 2010b). This methane can originate from biogas recovered from anaerobic digestion of organic wastes, agro-industrial residues, or other renewable or waste-derived sources, thereby closing nutrient loops and reducing greenhouse gas emissions (Overland et al., 2010b).

Despite this, potential research in its use for broiler systems has been limited. As BSCP is rich in protein, its high nucleic acid content and bacterial cell walls can be hard for broilers to digest, partly due to the absence of the uricase enzymes in broilers (Wang et al., 2024). It thus remains unclear how growing birds manage this extra N load or how their uric acid excretion changes over time. Additionally, the physiological changes that help birds adapt, such as how their amino acids (AA) transporters react to this new protein, have not been studied. The overarching objective of this study was to evaluate the potential of BSCP as a replacement for SBM in broiler diets and determine the extent to which this substitution can be achieved without compromising growth performance, gastrointestinal function, or overall health. Based on this rationale, we tested two hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that the response of broilers to BSCP inclusion would vary with growth stage, with birds in the early post-hatch period being more susceptible to dietary manipulation because of the immaturity of their gastrointestinal tract (Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021). Second, we hypothesized that early dietary exposure to BSCP would prime the digestive and metabolic systems by inducing adaptive changes in intestinal AA transporter expression, mucosal barrier integrity, and N utilization, thereby enhancing the capacity of broilers to tolerate higher levels of BSCP inclusion during later production phases, when dietary protein requirements and reliance on SBM are greatest.

Materials and methods

The study was conducted under Project License 2892 at the Agri‑Food and Biosciences Institute (AFBI), Stormont, with approval from its Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body in accordance with the UK Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986.

Experimental design

A total of 480 day-old straight-run (unsexed) Ross 308 chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery (Ballymena Hatchery, UK) and randomly allocated on arrival to 30 floor pens. Sex was not determined at hatch or during the trial, and randomization was used to distribute birds among pens and treatments. Each dietary treatment was assigned five replicate pens of 16 birds each, a replication level consistent with previous broiler performance trials conducted under the same conditions at this facility (McMurray et al., 2022). The trial was conducted over 35 days over three growth phases: starter (0–10 days), grower (11–21 days) and finisher (22–35 days). Birds were reared under standard management conditions with ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experiment. The study employed a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement examining BSCP inclusion during the starter phase (Null, N) or (Exposure, E) and during the grower/finisher phase (Low, L) or (High, H). This arrangement resulted in four BSCP treatment combinations for the grower and finisher phases: N–L, N–H, E–L and E–H. In addition, a SBM–based control treatment (C) and a phased adaptation treatment (E–L–H), in which BSCP inclusion increased progressively across production phases, were included for comparative assessment of dietary responses. In total, six dietary treatments were applied (C, N–L, N–H, E–L, E–H, and E–L–H).

Ingredient composition and analysed and calculated nutritional composition of the formulated diets used during the starter, grower and finisher phases. Dietary treatments consisted of a soybean-based control diet1 and diets supplemented with bacterial single-cell protein (BSCP) (diets E, L and H)2.

Diets containing different levels of inclusion of BSCP were formulated for the grower and finisher phases. A high inclusion level (H) diet was obtained by complete replacement of SBM in the finisher control (C) diet with BSCP, resulting in a final inclusion level of 12.5% BSCP. This same BSCP inclusion level (12.5%) was applied to the grower-phase H diet, where it represented a partial replacement of SBM. A low inclusion level (L) diet was formulated to contain 50% of the BSCP inclusion level of the H diet, resulting in a BSCP inclusion of 6.25%. A single experimental diet was formulated for the starter phase containing 50% of the BSCP inclusion level of the L diet, resulting in a final BSCP inclusion level of 3.125%. This diet was used as an exposure (E) diet to introduce starter birds to BSCP. All experimental diets used were formulated to be isonitrogenous and isocaloric to the control diets and to meet the nutrient requirements for broilers at each stage of growth according to the Ross 308 performance objectives (Aviagen, 2022). Synthetic AA were added to standardise digestible AA profiles across treatments. A commercial mycotoxin binder (TOXO-XL®; Selko, Trouw Nutrition), composed primarily of smectite-based bentonite, was uniformly incorporated into all diets at 3 g/kg. Its acid-insoluble fraction, combined with endogenous feed-derived acid-insoluble ash, served as the indigestible marker for digestibility determination. The diets were manufactured and pelleted under standardised processing conditions at AFBI using a dry (cold) pelleting system (Wonder Farm Feed Pellet Mill, 3 mm die), with a processing time of approximately 10 min per 25 kg batch. Representative feed samples were analysed to confirm dry matter, crude protein, gross energy, crude fiber and other key nutrients according to AOAC International methods (AOAC International, 2023) and AA content was determined by ion-exchange chromatography according to European Commission methods (European Commission, 2009). All analyses were carried out by a UKAS-accredited commercial laboratory to the internationally recognised standard for competence (Sciantec Analytical Services, Cawood, UK). The detailed chemical composition of formulated and analysed diets is presented in Table 1, and the nutritional composition of BSCP is given in the appendix (Table A1).

Housing and management

The birds were reared in a floor-bedding system with wood shavings as litter and at a stock density of 33kg/m2. All chicks were individually weighed on arrival (day 0) and wing-tagged for identification, enabling individual body weight tracking and mortality recording. They were then randomly allocated to treatment pens. Brooding temperature was initially maintained at 32°C and was gradually decreased by 0.5°C per day until a final temperature of 22°C was reached. The lighting regimen provided one hour of darkness on day 0, which was gradually increased to six hours of darkness per day from day 6 onwards. Relative humidity was maintained between 40 and 60% to ensure optimal conditions for the birds.

Assessment of growth performance

Growth performance variables were assessed for each growth phase (starter, grower, and finisher). Birds were weighed on day 0 and at the end of each phase (d 10, 21, and 35) to determine body weight gain (BWG) and ADG. Feed intake was recorded daily on a per-pen basis. Mortality was monitored daily throughout the experiment and used to adjust ADFI and to calculate the FCR for each phase.

Apparent ileal digestibility determination

Four birds per pen at the end of the starter phase (d 10) and two birds per pen at the end of the grower (d 21) and finisher (d 35) phases were randomly selected for ileal digesta collection, ileal tissue sampling, and body composition analysis where appropriate. The birds were anesthetized using isoflurane and humanely euthanised by cervical dislocation at the end of each phase. The ileum segment was defined as the portion of the small intestine extending from Meckel’s diverticulum to a point approximately 1 cm proximal to the ileo-caecal junction. Digesta contents were collected from the distal two-thirds of the ileum by manual expression, avoiding contamination with blood or mucosal tissue. Samples were pooled by pen and immediately frozen at −20°C for transport and storage. In the preparation for analysis, samples were thawed, oven-dried for 48 h and ground to pass a 0.8‑mm sieve for the determination of acid-insoluble ash (AIA) and AA content. Approximately 2.5 g of the dried sample was oven-dried overnight at 85°C and ashed at 600°C for four hours in a muffle furnace according to a modified AOAC Official Method 942.05 (Thiex et al., 2019). After that, the AIA analysis was conducted following the protocol described by Liu (2022). This method was preferred over the AOAC method because it reduces sample loss and improves accuracy and repeatability. The ash residue was transferred to 50-mL tubes and mixed with 20 mL of 2 N HCl. The mixture was vortexed and incubated in a water bath at 85°C for 35 min, with two additional vortexing steps during incubation. After digestion, the samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min. The residue was washed twice with distilled water using the same vortex-centrifugation procedure, then oven-dried and weighed. Analytical blanks were included in each run. Ileal digestibility of dry matter was calculated using the acid-insoluble ash indicator method:AIA(%)=WeightofAIAWeightofdriedSample×100DMDigestibility(%)=100−(100×%AIAinfeed%AIAinilealdigesta)

AA digestibility was assessed during the grower and finisher phases, when birds were receiving L and H BSCP inclusion, to evaluate digestive adaptation to BSCP at standardized inclusion levels following the starter-phase exposure. The remaining samples were sent to the UKAS-accredited commercial laboratory (Sciantec Analytical Services, Cawood, UK) for the determination of DM and AA content. AA were analysed using ion-exchange chromatography according to European Commission guidelines (European Commission, 2009).

Reverse transcription and real-time PCR analysis

Ileal tissue (approximately 1 cm) was collected from two birds per pen at the end of the grower (d 21) and finisher (d 35) phases. Immediately after dissection, each sample was transferred to sterile tubes and stored directly in a − 80°C freezer to preserve RNA integrity. For international transport to the collaborating laboratory in the USA (USDA National Poultry Research Center), the frozen ileal tissues were later transferred to RNAlater to stabilise RNA during shipment. The gene expression analyses were conducted in collaboration with Dr Revathi Shanmugasundaram's laboratory at the U.S. National Poultry Research Centre, USDA-ARS, Athens, GA, USA. Total RNA was extracted from the ileum using TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cincinnati, OH, USA), and RNA concentration and purity were analysed using a Synergy HTX multimode microplate reader (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) by measuring the 260/280 and 260/230 ratios. A total of 2 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). mRNA expression was analyzed by real-time PCR (CFX96 Touch Real-Time System, BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA) using SYBR Green (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Primer sequences are provided in the appendix (Table A2). Samples were analysed in duplicates using 96-well plates, with each well containing 5 μL of SYBR Green PCR master mix, 2 µL of RNase-free water, 2 µL of cDNA (∼600 ng/μL), 0.5 µL of forward primer (10 µM), and 0.5 µL of reverse primer (10 µM). Real-time PCR was performed with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min (1 cycle), followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 45 s. After each PCR run, the melting profile of each sample was assessed to confirm PCR product specificity. The housekeeping genes β-actin (BAC), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and ribosomal protein S13 (RPS13) were selected, and their stability was analysed using Normfinder software, version 21 (Department of Molecular Medicine, Aarhus University Hospital, Denmark) as described previously (Shanmugasundaram et al., 2018). Relative gene expression was normalised using BAC and RPS-13 as reference genes during the grower phase, whereas BAC alone was used for normalisation in finisher-phase samples. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method (where Ct is the threshold cycle) (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Carcass composition analysis

Carcass composition, including bone mineral content (BMC), bone mineral density (BMD), lean mass and fat percentage, was determined using Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA Model Lunar Prodigy, GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA) for grower and finisher phases on the birds selected for digesta and tissue sampling which were excised. The remaining carcasses were immediately frozen at −20°C to arrest tissue degradation. Prior to scanning, carcasses were thawed for 48 h at room temperature. The DXA scanner was calibrated before each scan using a standard quality control phantom to ensure measurement accuracy, but the calibration was not specifically developed for poultry. Birds were positioned in a standardized orientation on the scanning bed to ensure consistency of the scan area. Images were acquired and analysed using the DXA scanner manufacturer’s software, and the resulting data were exported for subsequent statistical analysis.

Excreta uric acid concentration analysis

Excreta samples were collected during the final 24 h of each growing stage by manually collecting fresh, distinct excreta deposits from the litter, avoiding contamination with bedding material. Samples were then pooled, homogenised, and stored at −20°C. Uric acid was extracted following the method by Selle et al. (2021), with minor modifications. Briefly, frozen samples were thawed and homogenised in duplicate. Approximately 1 g of excreta was mixed with 0.1 M glycine buffer (pH 9.2) and incubated at 37°C to facilitate solubilization. To ensure complete mixing, samples were vortexed intermittently for 10 min and then centrifuged at 2,500 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, diluted, and centrifuged again at 2,500 rpm for 10 min. The final supernatant was passed through a 0.45 µm milipore syringe filter before analysis. Reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck Life Science UK Ltd., Gillingham, UK). Chromatographic separation was conducted using a ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 Rapid Resolution HD column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm; Agilent Technologies) maintained at 30°C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.15% formic acid in Milli-Q water (Solvent A) and 0.15% formic acid in acetonitrile (Solvent B). Separation was achieved using a gradient elution program with an injection volume of 1 µL, while samples were maintained at 5°C in the autosampler. Detection and quantification were performed using a triple quadrupole LC–MS/MS system equipped with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) source operating in positive ion mode. Uric acid was monitored using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with two transitions: m/z 167 → 96 and m/z 167 → 124 (collision energy: 25 eV), with a retention time of 0.42 min. Quantification was calculated using calibration curves prepared from uric acid standards.

Litter sampling and gas emission measurement

All litter from each pen was collected and weighed on the final day of the experimental period (d 35). The litter from each pen was thoroughly homogenised and ten subsamples of (approximately 1 kg each) were collected. These subsamples were pooled per pen to form a composite sample, from which five aliquots (approximately 500 g each) were prepared and preserved on dry ice for transport to the AFBI, Hillsborough.

Gas emissions were quantified over 21 days using a small-chamber incubation flux method coupled with a Gasera One Photoacoustic Analyser (Gasera Ltd., Turku, Finland). Prior to incubation, 500 g of the homogenised litter sample was placed into each chamber and the combined mass of the container and sample recorded. To standardise incubation duration, chambers were sealed sequentially at 4‑min intervals. Headspace gas concentration were measured one hour after sealing each container, once daily for 21 days. Headspace air samples were extracted through the septa for analysis. Concentration of ammonia (NH3), carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O) concentrations were quantified in parts per million (ppm). To correct for background concentrations, measurements alternated between chamber headspace and ambient air. After each measurement, chamber lids were removed to terminate the incubation until the subsequent sampling event.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1) using RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). Data were screened for outliers using box plots, and normality of residuals was assessed using QQ plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test. The pen was the experimental unit, with individual birds as nested observational units. Pen was included as a random effect in all models to account for this nested structure. Variables that deviated from normality were log-transformed prior to analysis and the results are presented as back-transformed means with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Performance variables (ADFI, ADG and FCR), carcass composition, digestibility, uric acid concentration, and gene expression data during the starter phase were analysed by a one-way ANOVA across treatments. For variables measured during the grower and finisher phases, two statistical approaches were performed:i.A two-way ANOVA with the level of inclusion of BSCP during the starter and during the grower/finisher phases as factors. This approach excluded the control and adaptation treatments.ii.A one-way ANOVA across treatments. When the latter was significant, orthogonal contrasts were performed to compare the control versus all other treatments and adaptation treatment versus pooled result of E-H & E-L treatments.

The ADG was analysed using a linear mixed-effects model (LME), with treatment as a fixed effect and pen as a random effect. Bird initial body weight was used as a covariate to adjust for baseline performance differences. ADFI and FCR were analysed on the pen level using the LM model. Gas emissions were analysed using repeated-measures ANOVA using a linear mixed model with treatment, day and their interaction as fixed effects and pen as a random effect. An autoregressive covariance structure of order 1 (AR1) was specified to account for temporal autocorrelation among repeated measurements within pens. For gene expression data, Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, expressed as fold change relative to the control, whose mean was fixed at 1.00 for each gene by construction; values above and below 1.00 indicate up- and downregulation, respectively. As the control served as the calibrator, it was excluded from the overall treatment-effect model, which compared the BSCP-supplemented treatments only, with a separate orthogonal contrast used to compare the control against the pooled BSCP treatments. When significant main or interaction effects were detected (P < 0.05), Tukey’s post hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons. Statistical trends were considered at 0.050 ≤ P ≤ 0.100.

Growth performance

Inclusion of BSCP (N vs. E) did not affect (P > 0.05) starter ADG (30.5 vs. 30.3 g/d; SEM=0.20) or ADFI (36.0 vs. 36.8 g/d; SEM=0.43), but FCR to be more efficient (1.19 vs. 1.23; SEM=0.02) tended (P = 0.071) in birds fed on N than E respectively. Apparent ileal digestibility

Table 4 shows the apparent ileal digestibility of DM (AIDD) across the different broiler phases. Exposure to BSCP had no (P > 0.05) effect on AIDD in birds during the starter phase. During the grower phase, a highly significant main effect of the inclusion level treatment was observed (P < 0.01). Birds fed high BSCP inclusion level (N—H and E-H) had significantly higher AIDD values (79.5 and 81.0%; SEM=1.01) than those fed low inclusion level (N-L and E-L) (71.1 and 71.5%; SEM=1.01). However, the main effect of the starter diet was not significant (P > 0.05), and no interaction between (exposure, N vs E) and (inclusion level, L vs H) was observed (P > 0.05). Orthogonal contrasts indicated a tendency (P = 0.055) for the Control group to have lower AIDD than the treatment groups. During the finisher phase, both the starter and finisher BSCP inclusion levels significantly influenced AIDD (P < 0.05), with no significant interaction observed between the two factors. Regarding the main effect of the starter diet, birds previously exposed to the BSCP-based diet during the starter phase exhibited higher AIDD in the finisher phase compared to those on the N diets, regardless of their subsequent finisher treatment. Similarly, finisher birds fed on the H diet had higher AIDD than those fed on the L diet, regardless of exposure to BSCP in the starter phase. Furthermore, orthogonal contrasts revealed that the AIDD of the Control group was significantly lower (71.7% vs 80.7%; SEM=0.98) than the treatment groups (P < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the E-l-H group and finisher birds fed E-L and E-H diets (P > 0.05). To further evaluate nutrient utilisation during the critical growth depression period, the apparent ileal digestibility of essential AA was analysed (Table 5). Consistent with the DM results, there was no interaction (P > 0.05) between the starter and grower dietary phases for any of the measured AA. However, a significant main effect (P < 0.01) was observed across almost all essential AA, including lysine, methionine, threonine, and valine. Contrary to the expectation that higher BSCP inclusion might reduce digestibility, birds on H diets demonstrated significantly (P < 0.001) superior AID of AA coefficients compared to those on L and control diets. Notably, the birds on E-H diets consistently exhibited the highest AID of AA values (e.g., Lysine: 93.3%; Methionine: 94.7%), significantly outperforming the N-L group (Lysine: 88.4%; Methionine: 84.3%). Discussion

We used BSCP strategically to investigate whether early exposure could enhance broiler gut adaptability, with the aim of reducing dependence on SBM. The novelty of the study lies in evaluating broiler adaptation to BSCP across multiple growth phases, addressing a gap in prior work that has focused almost exclusively on single‑phase responses, and in evaluating these responses mechanistically. The diet formulation strategy involved partial replacement of SBM with BSCP, rather than supplementation as an additive ingredient. Therefore, replacing SBM with BSCP simultaneously introduced an alternative protein source and reduced the contribution of SBM-derived nutrients and bioactive components. Consequently, the observed responses in growth performance, nutrient utilisation, physiological parameters, and environmental outputs represent the combined effects of BSCP inclusion and reduced SBM contribution. It has been suggested that the use of BSCP should be limited to low inclusion levels in poultry, largely due to its cell wall structure and high nucleic acid content (Ledbetter, 2025). Understanding whether BSCP differs across developmental stages is therefore critical for constructing biologically appropriate feeding strategies. The rationale was that gradual early exposure to low BSCP levels during the starter phase would allow birds to physiologically adapt to this novel protein source, thereby enhancing their capacity to utilise higher inclusion rates later in life. To characterise this adaptive response, we evaluated growth performance alongside key physiological and metabolic indicators.

Impact of dietary BSCP on broiler growth performance

Inclusion of BSCP in the starter broiler diet did not impair ADFI or constrain ADG but was associated with a numerical increase in FCR. The lack of differences in ADFI indicates that inclusion of BSCP at the evaluated level did not alter voluntary feed intake under the conditions of the present study, suggesting that diet acceptability and physical structure were not adversely affected by BSCP inclusion (Kyriazakis, 2025; Overland et al., 2010b). This can be associated with the relatively low BSCP inclusion level used in the present study, which was intended to partially replace SBM rather than to substantially modify the diet's nutrient content (Hombegowda et al., 2021). Under such conditions, major alterations in sensory characteristics or nutrient balance would not be expected. Lack of adverse effects on ADG further indicates that nutrient availability was not compromised and that short-term physiological adaptation to the diet was adequate at the BSCP inclusion level used. Although the increase in FCR was not statistically significant, the numerical reduction in feed efficiency is consistent with responses observed in broilers fed BSCP-containing starter diets (Hombegowda et al., 2021). Differences in chemical composition of BSCP compared to SBM, as well as effects on feed physical characteristics, have been suggested as factors influencing performance responses to alternative protein ingredients (Ledbetter, 2025; Øverland et al., 2006). BSCP contains a peptidoglycan-rich cell wall in which D-amino acids may confer resistance to proteolytic enzymes, along with high nucleic acid concentrations, both of which can limit complete enzymatic access to intracellular protein fractions affecting nutrient utilisation characteristics (Skrede et al., 2009). These characteristics have been associated with increased nitrogen losses and reduced nutrient utilisation efficiency (Greife et al., 1980). These effects are more pronounced in the starter phase, when gastrointestinal and enzymatic systems are still developing, and ingredients with comparatively lower digestibility can exert disproportionate effects on nutrient utilisation (Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021; Wangui et al., 2025). In the grower phase, inclusion of BSCP at both levels reduced ADG, whereas ADFI remained comparable to the control group. This response indicates that growth depression was primarily driven by reduced nutrient utilisation efficiency rather than changes in voluntary feed intake. The effect was more pronounced at higher inclusion levels. Although AID of the BSCP diets was higher than that of the SBM control, the lower growth response indicates that improved apparent ileal digestibility did not translate into improved growth performance. This suggests that factors beyond ileal digestion may have influenced nutrient utilisation efficiency in birds fed BSCP-containing diets. Potential contributors include differences in post-absorptive nutrient utilisation and the metabolic processing of bacterial biomass components, such as complex cell wall structures and relatively high nucleic acid concentrations (Skrede et al., 2009). These characteristics may influence the efficiency with which absorbed nutrients are utilised for growth (Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021). Consequently, greater substitution of SBM with BSCP was associated with reduced growth performance in the present study, consistent with previous reports showing lower ADG when BSCP inclusion exceeded 10%, including during the grower stage (Hombegowda et al., 2021; Najib, 2014; Pourelmi et al., 2018; Waldroup and Payne, 1974). In contrast, the apparent reduced efficiency observed during the starter and grower phases in birds fed the BSCP diet was not evident during the finisher phase. Birds previously exposed to higher BSCP inclusion levels during the grower period achieved ADG comparable to the those fed SBM despite similar ADFI, indicating improved feed utilisation efficiency during the finisher phase. This response reflects the ability of broilers to adjust to changes in dietary composition as they mature, although the physiological mechanisms underlying this response were not evaluated (Ravindran and Abdollahi, 2021). The recovery of growth performance during the finisher phase is consistent with previous reports describing compensatory growth responses following earlier reductions in growth rate (Kyriazakis and Emmans, 1992; Zubair and Leeson, 2019). Overall, the results across the growth phases indicate that high BSCP inclusion can cause a transient reduction in growth performance during the adaptation period, with birds recovering through compensatory growth in later stages.

Nutrient digestibility and utilization efficiency

Digestibility results across the rearing phases revealed a clear pattern of adaptation to the BSCP. Similar DM digestibility between SBM- and BSCP-fed birds during the starter phase indicates that low-level BSCP inclusion did not impair DM digestibility, consistent with previous reports demonstrating that moderate inclusion of single-cell protein can be effectively utilised in starter broiler diets (Hombegowda et al., 2021). However, grower birds fed higher BSCP diets showed higher DM and AA digestibility, suggesting that the presence of bacterial biomass components did not limit apparent nutrient digestion (Overland et al., 2010a). Lower BSCP inclusion levels, by contrast, may not have presented a sufficient compositional challenge to elicit this adaptive response. Similar improvements in DM and AA digestibility following replacement of SBM with BSCP have been reported previously in broilers, supporting the high digestibility of this bacterial protein (Schøyen et al., 2005; Skrede et al., 1998). High digestibility of BSCP has also been demonstrated when used as a replacement for SBM in Jian carp, indicating that its favourable digestibility is not limited to poultry (Yu et al., 2022). The enhanced DM digestibility observed during the grower phase persisted into the finisher phase, and the significant starter effect suggests that early BSCP exposure promoted a lasting digestive adaptation. Birds introduced to BSCP from the starter phase maintained improved nutrient digestibility regardless of subsequent dietary treatment, indicating that adaptation to the structural and compositional characteristics of BSCP may support sustained digestive efficiency throughout maturation.

The DXA measurements provide additional insight into the effect of BSCP on tissue accretion. Although BSCP inclusion reduced body weight during earlier rearing phases, the relative proportions of lean and fat tissue were maintained, indicating that BSCP did not substantially alter carcass tissue composition (Hellwing et al., 2006). Fat percentages were comparatively higher than those reported in some previous studies, which can be attributed to differences in DXA calibration approaches (de Aguiar et al., 2024; Mitchell et al., 1997). However, the relative differences among dietary treatments remain reliable because all treatments were evaluated using the same methodology. The reduction in BMD during the starter phase suggests that bone development may be more sensitive to BSCP inclusion than soft tissue accretion. Because mineral utilisation and bone mineralisation were not directly measured, the mechanisms responsible for this response remain unclear. The starter phase is characterised by high mineral requirements and ongoing development of intestinal absorptive capacity, which may increase the sensitivity of bone mineralization to dietary changes (Sanchez-Rodriguez et al., 2019). Further investigation of mineral digestibility and bone characteristics is required to determine the basis of the observed reduction in BMD. During the finisher phase, increased fat deposition was observed in BSCP-fed birds, contrasting with previous reports of reduced adiposity following bacterial protein inclusion (Schoyen et al., 2007; Skrede et al., 2003). This response coincided with recovery of ADG and may reflect changes in nutrient partitioning following earlier reductions in growth rate. The findings are consistent with a compensatory growth response (Kyriazakis and Emmans, 1992), although the underlying metabolic mechanisms were not determined. A possible explanation for this response is that, as growth performance recovered, energy utilisation may have shifted towards fat deposition, consistent with reports of increased abdominal fat during compensatory growth in broilers (Zhan et al., 2007).

Intestinal adaptation to BSCP-based diets

The expression of ileal AA transporter genes provided additional explanation for the transient reduction in growth performance during the grower phase, despite the higher apparent ileal digestibility of BSCP diets. Birds fed BSCP exhibited lower expression of the cationic and anionic AA transporters CAT2, y+LAT1, and EAAT3, coinciding with reduced ADG. As these transporters are involved in AA absorption (Sadr et al., 2025), their downregulation may have contributed to reduced nutrient utilisation despite enhanced digestibility. Similar regulation of intestinal AA transporter expression in response to dietary protein source has been reported previously in broilers (Gilbert et al., 2008). In the absence of sufficient transporter abundance, the synchronised uptake of luminal AA might become disrupted, limiting systemic AA availability for muscle protein synthesis during this rapid growth window (Bröer, 2023). In contrast, APN was upregulated, indicating differential regulation of peptide hydrolysis and AA transport in response to BSCP inclusion (Miska and Fetterer, 2019). Although this response counteracts the downregulation of other transporter genes, it imposes an additional metabolic cost that ultimately restricts growth. During the finisher phase, birds exposed to BSCP from the starter phase and subsequently fed the low-BSCP diet exhibited higher expression of CAT2, LAT1, bo+AT, and BOAT1, whereas broader suppression of cationic AA transporters was observed in birds maintained on the high-BSCP diet. These findings indicate that the intestinal transcriptional response to BSCP was influenced by both previous dietary exposure and inclusion level. The greater expression of transporter genes at the lower inclusion level supports the use of moderate, rather than maximal, replacement of SBM with BSCP to optimise nutrient utilisation. This pattern likely represents a homeostatic response. When AA availability is sufficient due to improved digestibility in this group, additional upregulation of transporters may not be biologically necessary. It supports optimization of BSCP inclusion in broiler diet rather than maximal replacement. Moreover, the heightened capacity of BOAT1 and LAT1 likely facilitated an influx of neutral AA (such as leucine) beyond immediate anabolic requirements, driving the excess amount of lipid deposition observed in the DXA results. The observed changes in tight junction proteins at the marketing age suggest a carry-over effect of early-life nutrition that functions as a strategic adaptation rather than gut damage. Lower expression of sealing proteins (Claudin‑1, Claudin‑5, and ZO‑1) alongside increased Claudin‑2 indicates a regulated shift toward greater paracellular permeability. Because Claudin‑1 tightens the epithelial barrier whereas Claudin‑2 forms small pores that permit water and small solutes to pass (Markov et al., 2017). This profile suggests a controlled increase in flux to support nutrient and water movement during periods of rapid growth (Greene et al., 2025). Rather than reflecting compromised integrity, the response represents a strategic, transient loosening of the barrier to facilitate recovery and maintain growth.

Effect of BSCP inclusion on uric acid excretion in broilers

The primary limitation associated with single‑cell proteins such as BSCP is their high nucleic acid content (Nyyssola et al., 2022; Ritala et al., 2017; Sharif et al., 2021). Purines derived from these nucleic acids are catabolised and converted into uric acid in birds (Wang et al., 2024). Excessive nucleic acid intake can therefore elevate circulating uric acid and increase the risk of hyperuricemia, which may progress to articular or visceral gout if renal clearance capacity is exceeded (Hong et al., 2020). An increase in uric acid excretion during the starter phase indicated that the BSCP diet imposed an immediate metabolic load. The elevated excretion suggests that birds were actively catabolising the additional purines and clearing them efficiently preventing systemic accumulation. This response also implies that renal capacity was sufficient to manage the surge, but likely at a higher metabolic cost, which is associated with the reduced feed efficiency observed during this period. Birds fed H inclusion of BSCP tended to excrete higher uric acid during the grower phase, in line with the higher dietary nucleic acid load. This dose-dependent rise indicates that the extra purines were being actively catabolised and voided rather than retained. This represented an adaptive response still in progress, whereby clearance was achieved at a metabolic cost consistent with the reduced weight gain observed during this phase. In contrast, the gradual reduction in uric acid excretion in finisher phases suggests a reduction in overall endogenous uric acid production. While maturing birds exhibit enhanced hepatic enzyme activity (including xanthine dehydrogenase) and renal filtration efficiency to handle purine loads (Nagahara et al., 1987; Sribhen et al., 2003), the reduced excretion indicates that less N was being wasted. More dietary N was retained for growth, reducing the substrate available for uric acid synthesis. Together, these adaptations suggest that the initial metabolic burden imposed by BSCP was transient, with maturing physiological pathways progressively restoring systemic uric acid homeostasis.

The mechanistic basis for this adaptation is supported by evidence that meat‑type chickens can modulate gene expression related to purine metabolism and N excretion, enabling more efficient use of nucleic acids through the purine salvage pathway (Aggrey et al., 2014). Under this mechanism, excess nucleic acids are increasingly channelled into anabolic processes rather than being catabolised to uric acid, which helps explain why the initial spike in uric acid excretion did not persist into the finisher phase. Early nutritional exposure to BSCP may therefore prime metabolic pathways (Leterrier et al., 2025), promoting longer‑term adjustments that support safe utilisation of high‑nucleic‑acid ingredients later in growth phases.

Impacts of BSCP-based diets on greenhouse gas and ammonia emissions from broiler litter

Litter gas emissions indicated that replacing SBM with BSCP did not alter ammonia or methane emissions, suggesting that BSCP inclusion did not substantially influence nitrogenous or fermentative processes in the litter. Ammonia volatilisation is largely driven by microbial degradation of undigested uric acid and other labile nitrogen fractions (Nahm, 2019). Given this relationship, the absence of treatment differences is consistent with similar quantities of readily degradable N entering the litter across dietary treatments. This observation supports earlier findings showing that the birds effectively utilised the bacterial protein rather than diverting surplus N into the litter (Nahm, 2019). Likewise, the similar CH₄ emissions across treatments suggest that BSCP inclusion did not markedly alter the fermentation characteristics of the litter. These observations agree with previous studies reporting that bacterial protein sources can contribute to environmentally sustainable poultry production by maintaining or reducing emissions associated with SBM-based diets (Pexas et al., 2023; Tallentire et al., 2018; Wangui et al., 2025). In contrast, the treatment and time interaction observed for CO₂ emissions suggests that BSCP influenced the temporal pattern of microbial decomposition without affecting cumulative CO₂ losses. The basis of this response was not investigated in this study. However, differences in the composition of excreta associated with BSCP inclusion may have influenced the rate of microbial metabolism during the early stages of litter decomposition. This likely reflects the presence of residual bacterial cell-wall components, such as peptidoglycans, which provide a distinct carbon substrate for litter microflora (Bai et al., 2024).

The strong temporal effect observed across all gases reflected the natural progression of manure decomposition rather than a treatment‑driven response. As the storage period advanced, the microflora actively mineralised the organic matter, producing the characteristic shifts in gas output that follow the typical composting curve described for poultry litter systems (Coufal et al., 2006). This reinforces that the observed emission dynamics were governed primarily by time‑dependent microbial activity and not particularly by the inclusion of BSCP in the diet.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that BSCP can be incorporated into broiler diets at levels up to 12.5% without compromising final body weight, feed efficiency, or litter gas emissions. BSCP-fed birds showed a transient reduction in growth performance during the grower phase compared to SBM-fed controls, alongside phase-specific shifts in AA transporter and tight junction gene expression, indicating a temporary physiological adjustment to BSCP inclusion, which is resolved by the finisher phase. Overall, these findings indicate that early-life exposure enables broilers to tolerate higher BSCP inclusion levels later in production without adverse effects. However, as higher inclusion did not confer additional performance benefits over moderate (6.25%) levels, a phased strategy incorporating early exposure followed by moderate rather than maximal BSCP inclusion is recommended to optimise resource use while maintaining growth performance. These findings support BSCP as a viable, sustainable alternative to SBM in broiler diets, offering a route to reduce reliance on arable land-based protein sources without compromising broiler performance, nutrient utilisation, or environmental emissions.
