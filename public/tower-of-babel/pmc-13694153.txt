Understanding AI Algorithms in Medical Imaging: A Radiologist's Guide to Models and Methods
Authors: Sharad Maheshwari, Sachin Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence is now embedded in radiology workflows across detection, triage, quantification, and reporting. Yet, most clinicians deploy these tools without a working understanding of how their outputs are generated or where they reliably fail. Unlike conventional rule-based clinical workflows, modern imaging AI systems generate probabilistic outputs whose reliability depends on training data, task definition, and deployment context. Radiologists must understand what these systems actually produce, know their predictable failure points, and never allow a confidence score to replace the clinical reasoning that only a trained human can apply. This review provides a clinician-oriented framework for understanding imaging AI by covering core model architectures, task-based applications, workflow integration, and the practical interpretation of algorithmic outputs. This is important because as imaging AI scales across institutions and populations, the radiologist's capacity to interrogate, contextualize, and, where necessary, override algorithmic outputs becomes not just a clinical skill but a professional responsibility.

Introduction: The Clinical–AI Gap

Artificial intelligence (AI) has become an important component of medical imaging rather than just theoretical research as it was in the past. 1 2 It involves automated lesion detection, anatomical segmentation, triage of examinations, and quantitative analysis encompassing all imaging modalities. 1 3 Many clinicians are using AI tools without fully understanding their training, biases, or context-dependent performance, even as these systems increasingly shape clinical decision making. 4 5 Thus, we have a clinical–AI gap that needs to be addressed. The data and guidance for clinicians on how the AI models behave in the real world based on heterogeneous imaging protocols and variable data quality are limited. AI systems in medical imaging should be understood as probabilistic pattern-recognition tools, and they should not be treated as systems with intrinsic clinical or pathophysiological understanding.

AI Fundamentals

AI broadly refers to computer systems that can perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as pattern recognition, decision-making, and problem-solving.

Within AI, machine learning (ML) refers to algorithms that learn statistical relationship from data rather than following explicit rules. Classical ML operates on features that humans define and extract in advance, whereas deep learning (DL), a subset of ML, uses multiple layers of neural networks to learn feature representations directly from raw data such as images or text ( Fig. 1A ). AI also includes non-learning rule based (symbolic systems), which remain important for implementing guideline-based clinical logic. Most of the AI systems that we encounter in clinical practice utilize one of these or a combination of these approaches. 6

Hierarchical and overlapping relationships among AI model categories. (A) Machine learning (ML) is a subset of artificial intelligence (AI), and deep learning (DL) is a subset of ML. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), transformers, large language models (LLMs), and generative models are common DL model architectures or classes. (B) Generative AI, foundation models, and multimodal AI are overlapping categories, rather than successive levels of hierarchy. Generative AI creates new content, such as images or text and is broader than large language models (LLM). Foundation models are trained on large and varied datasets and can be adapted for different tasks. Not all of them are generative AI. Multimodal AI works with more than one type of data, such as images, clinical text, or EHR data. LLMs occupy the overlap between generative AI and foundation models, while models in the central overlap combine all three properties: they generate content, work with more than one data type, and can be adapted to different tasks.

Neural networks and DL systems are built from simple computational units loosely inspired by the biological neuron ( Fig. 2A ). Understanding this architecture helps explain both how these systems learn and why their internal reasoning can be difficult to audit. A perceptron is the simplest artificial neuron ( Fig. 2B ), which acts like a human neuron. It receives numerical inputs, multiplies each by a weight (how important that input is), adds a bias (its baseline tendency toward “yes” or “no”), and then applies an activation function that decides whether the unit “fires” (outputs 1) or stays silent (outputs 0). Stacking many such units into layers gives us a neural network, and stacking many layers gives us DL ( Fig. 2C ). 7

In traditional ML for imaging, radiologists and engineers manually define features such as intensity histograms, tumor shape descriptors, and texture matrices and convert each image to a defined feature vector set. Classical algorithms like logistic regression, support vector machines, and random forests then learn how combinations of these features associate with diagnoses or outcomes. Thus, the quality of these human-assigned features limits the model's competency. 8

In DL, manual feature engineering by a human is eliminated. Networks observe raw pixel or voxel values directly and automatically learn hierarchical representations through layers of computational units. 9 Early layers detect edges and textures, intermediate layers identify shapes and anatomical structures, and deeper layers encode complex patterns. Because these representations are learned rather than defined, neither the radiologist nor the developer can fully specify what the model has learned to detect.

Clinically, this creates a key contrast. Classical ML is feature-centric and easily interpretable, as each input consists of predefined descriptors set by humans, and thus the reasoning of the model can be understood. On the other hand, DL is representation-centric and generally more opaque. DL learns complex internal representations directly from data rather than relying on explicitly defined features. Explainable AI (XAI) visualization tools such as saliency maps can show which part of the image influences the model's prediction, but they cannot determine whether the model relied on true pathology or a spurious correlation. 10

AI tasks in medical imaging

From a clinical viewpoint, these tasks differ not only in their technical objectives but also in their reliability, interpretability, and the extent to which their outputs can be trusted in practice. Perceptual tasks such as detection and segmentation are based on visual pattern recognition. They can often be compared directly against objective anatomical ground truth, making them reliable and clinically actionable. However, this reliability is heavily dataset-dependent; a U-Net model trained on portal-phase CT from European cohorts, for example, may perform measurably worse on late arterial phase imaging or diffuse fatty liver disease patterns more commonly encountered in the Indian subcontinent. This reflects the broader documented problem of AI underperformance in underrepresented populations and low- and middle-income country clinical environments. In contrast, prediction and prognostication are much more complicated because their outputs depend on complex statistical associations influenced by hidden confounders, treatment heterogeneity, and evolving disease biology. Thus, these outputs are inherently probabilistic and should generally inform, and not replace, clinical judgment.

Treating a liver segmentation model and a 5-year survival predictor model as equivalent AI systems reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of their respective clinical roles, risk profiles, and governance requirements. Radiologists must distinguish between AI outputs that can be directly verified against imaging anatomy and those that function primarily as probabilistic decision-support tools requiring human oversight.

Classical machine learning models

Unlike DL, classical ML does not analyze raw medical images directly. Instead, it relies on predefined features extracted from the image. This approach performs well with small to intermediate-sized datasets, making it particularly useful in specialized clinical cohorts where large annotated imaging datasets are unavailable. In radiology, classical ML is most commonly applied to prognostic modeling, risk stratification, and multimodal data integration, where structured inputs and model interpretability are important. Its principal limitation is the need for prior feature extraction and engineering, as it cannot automatically learn complex spatial patterns directly from raw medical images. 9

Radiomics

In medical imaging, far more quantitative information exists within an image than can be appreciated by the human eye alone. Radiomics is a feature extraction methodology that converts a carefully segmented region of interest into hundreds to thousands of predefined quantitative features, which then serve as inputs to classical ML or statistical models. These features describe image characteristics such as intensity, shape, texture, and higher-order spatial patterns. The underlying hypothesis is that these quantitative descriptors capture subtle tumor heterogeneity and tissue microarchitecture that correlate with genomic profiles, biological phenotypes, and clinical outcomes. Its principal weaknesses are that many features are unstable across scanners, acquisition and reconstruction settings, and across observers. Hence, standardized feature definitions and independent external validation are essential. 12 The radiomics workflow and quantitative feature extraction pipeline are further explained in Fig. 3.

Deep Learning in Radiology: Computer Vision/Convolutional Neural Networks

When people refer to “AI that analyzes images,” it often refers to computer vision systems that use convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which play an important role in analyzing medical images such as X-rays and CT/MRIs.

CNNs are a type of deep neural network specifically designed to recognize visual patterns. They identify spatial patterns within images by applying learnable filters across local regions and progressively extract increasingly complex visual features ( Fig. 4 ). The network progressively builds increasingly complex image representations across its layers like a trainee radiologist. Early layers detect simple features such as edges and lines. Intermediate layers recognize shapes, contours, and anatomical structures, and deeper layers combine these features to identify clinically meaningful patterns, such as lung nodules, intracranial hemorrhage, or liver lesions. 13

Medical images are processed through sequential convolutional layers containing learnable filters (2) that detect local spatial patterns and generate feature maps (3). As information propagates through progressively deeper network layers (4), increasingly abstract hierarchical representations are learned. (B) Hierarchical feature learning: early layers identify low-level features such as edges, lines, and orientations; intermediate layers recognize shapes, textures, and anatomical structures; and deeper layers detect complex imaging patterns associated with clinically relevant abnormalities.

From a Radiologist's Perspective, CNNs Serve Two Main Roles

Reading X-Rays and Imaging Like CT or MRI as a “Second Reader”

CNNs are used to analyze radiographs and CT scans for classification and detection tasks. These include identifying abnormalities such as pneumonia on chest radiographs, detection of lung nodules or fractures, and screening CT scans for acute findings such as intracranial hemorrhage or pulmonary embolism. 14 15 CNNs help by pointing out suspicious areas and generating probability scores, which helps prioritize studies and lowers the chance of missing something. But their results are still probabilistic and require confirmation by a radiologist. 16

Segmentation

In segmentation tasks, CNNs commonly use encoder–decoder architectures, such as U-Net. The model first analyzes the image to identify the structures present, and then it refines the information to determine exactly where those structures are located. This is achieved by combining global feature extraction with reconstruction of fine spatial detail using information from earlier processing stages. 17 The final output is a segmentation mask, where each pixel or voxel is labeled as belonging to a specific structure (e.g., liver, vessel, or tumor) or to the background.

This enables automated delineation of anatomical structures and lesions for measurement and treatment planning. For example, in living liver donor evaluation, DL-based segmentation accurately delineates the liver and vascular anatomy, enabling precise volumetric assessment for graft planning. 18 For example, in our practice, 3D reconstruction of the vascular anatomy and graft liver volumes is used routinely for donor assessment ( Fig. 5 ). Similar U-Net-based architectures are widely used for brain tumor segmentation on MRI, lung, lobe, and airway segmentation on CT, and delineation of organs-at-risk and target volumes for radiotherapy planning. 19

Because these outputs are directly visible on the image, radiologists can readily verify their accuracy, making segmentation a reliable clinical application of CNNs. Whenever AI highlights, outlines, or measures structures directly on an image, a CNN-based computer vision model is usually performing the underlying visual task.

Transformers, Generative AI, LLMs, and Multimodal AI

While CNNs excel at recognizing local image patterns, they are less effective at understanding long-range relationships within an image or integrating information across multiple data types. Transformer architectures, introduced in the landmark 2017 paper Attention Is All You Need ,' 20 were designed to overcome this limitation. Their self-attention mechanism allows the model to analyze relationships across an entire image or sequence simultaneously, enabling it to capture broader contextual information than CNNs, which primarily focus on local spatial regions.

One major application of transformer architectures is generative AI, particularly large language models (LLMs) such as initial versions of ChatGPT. These models are trained on massive text corpora to generate human-like language, summarize information, answer questions, and produce coherent text. Importantly, not all transformers are LLMs. Transformers are the underlying neural architecture, whereas LLMs are specialized language models built using transformer-based frameworks. Equally, not all generative AI consists of LLM. Generative adversarial networks, variational autoencoders, and diffusion models can generate or transform medical images and are used for application such as image synthesis, denoising, reconstruction, and cross-modality translation ( Fig. 1B ).

In radiology, LLMs are increasingly being integrated into reporting workflows rather than image interpretation itself. They can assist with speech-to-text transcription, report drafting, structured reporting, impression generation, language refinement, and communication of findings. When combined with computer vision models that analyze medical images, these systems enable AI-assisted reporting workflows in which image interpretation and report generation are integrated while preserving radiologist oversight.

Transformer architectures have also been successfully adapted for medical imaging through vision transformers (ViTs) and multimodal AI systems that integrate images, text, and other clinical data. 21 22 These systems support image classification, report generation, image-text reasoning, and multimodal clinical decision support, making transformers a key computational framework in contemporary medical AI. The current versions of general purpose systems like ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini are native multimodal models that can process and reason across multiple modalities like text, images, documents, and audio. Their principal limitation is that they generally require substantially larger training datasets than CNNs. Consequently, on the relatively small, carefully annotated datasets typical of many radiology studies, CNN-based models often continue to outperform ViTs/multimodal AI. 21 Summary has been provided in Table 4 .

Hybrid Clinical AI Systems and AI-Assisted Workflows

Why Hybrid Clinical AI Systems Are Necessary

Real clinical decision-making demands much more than pattern recognition alone. Radiologists do not simply detect imaging abnormalities; they integrate imaging findings with anatomy, physiology, temporal evolution, prior examinations, clinical history, laboratory data, treatment status, and guideline-based reasoning before arriving at a final interpretation. Since imaging interpretation is fundamentally contextual, multimodal, and workflow-dependent, no single AI architecture currently possesses reliable perception, contextual understanding, longitudinal reasoning, uncertainty management, and safe language generation simultaneously.

Hence, the most capable radiology AI systems in contemporary practice are not monolithic end-to-end models but coordinated, multicomponent pipelines, commonly termed as “hybrid clinical AI systems.” The term is descriptive rather than an established taxonomic category, and related concepts appear in the literature as hybrid or neurosymbolic AI and as compound AI systems. Rather than relying on a single model, hybrid architectures distribute different cognitive tasks across specialized components according to their respective strengths, improving interpretability, clinical alignment, and human oversight throughout the radiology workflow.

A Worked Clinical Example: Liver Lesion Characterization

Consider a patient with a focal liver lesion undergoing multiphasic CT. A CNN or ViT first detects and segments the lesion, characterizing its size, location, morphology, and enhancement pattern. A guideline-based engine checks the eligibility (e.g., cirrhosis) and then applies LI-RADS criteria, integrating the imaging findings with relevant clinical information such as cirrhosis, hepatitis serology, and prior locoregional treatment to assign a category and recommend an appropriate management pathway. Finally, an LLM converts the structured output into a fluent radiology report and, where appropriate, generates a lay-language explanation for the patient or referring clinician.

This functional division of labor reflects an important principle in clinical AI system design. Perceptual models such as CNNs and ViTs specialize in detecting, localizing, and quantifying imaging abnormalities. Deterministic logic engines provide governance by enforcing guideline-based reasoning, standardized reporting frameworks, and clinically acceptable decision boundaries. LLMs function primarily as communication layers, generating structured reports, summaries, and patient-facing explanations. Together, these components produce outputs that are not only diagnostically useful but also interpretable, traceable, auditable, and aligned with established clinical practice. Such hybrid architectures also preserve the radiologist's central role as the final integrator of imaging findings and patient-specific clinical judgment. This together forms the AI cognitive stack in radiology, as shown in Table 5.

These hybrid architectures also provide the foundations for emerging agentic AI systems in which a model autonomously plans a sequence of steps, invokes specialized tools and data sources, evaluates intermediate results and revises its plan, in contrast to the fixed control floor of pipelines described earlier. Such systems remain investigational in radiology.

Failure Modes in Hybrid Systems

Hybrid architectures also introduce unique failure modes. Errors may propagate across components, for example, when an inaccurate segmentation is accepted by a downstream guideline engine and carried into the final report. As D'Antonoli et al noted, the complexity of multicomponent AI pipelines requires prospective monitoring that current radiology AI governance frameworks have not yet fully addressed. 23

Classical ML and radiomics are best suited to structured data, biomarker discovery, prognostic modeling, and situations where feature-level interpretability is important, particularly when only small or intermediate-sized datasets are available. 24

CNNs remain the preferred architecture for image-based perceptual tasks, including lesion detection, organ segmentation, and image classification, where outputs can be directly verified against imaging anatomy. 25

Vision-language systems represent a specialized form of hybrid AI that combines an image encoder with an LLM to support image-aware report generation, visual question answering, and multimodal clinical reasoning. Hybrid Systems

Hybrid clinical AI systems extend beyond vision-language models by integrating multiple specialized components, including image analysis, guideline-based reasoning, structured reporting, and clinical decision support, within a coordinated workflow. They are particularly valuable for comprehensive oncology reporting and complex multidisciplinary pathways. 28 29

Evaluating AI Performance in Radiology

Understanding how AI performance is measured is essential for interpreting radiology AI studies and determining whether a model is clinically reliable. Different AI tasks require different evaluation metrics. For image-level classification models , commonly reported measures include sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUROC). However, the ESR essentials guidance on AI performance metrics emphasizes that no single metric is sufficient for clinical validation. The choice of metric must match the clinical task, and multiple complementary measures should be considered together to provide a complete assessment of model performance. 30

For segmentation tasks , overlap-based metrics such as the Dice similarity coefficient are commonly used to measure agreement between AI-generated contours and expert-defined ground truth. High Dice scores indicate accurate anatomical delineation but do not necessarily guarantee clinical usefulness, particularly when small boundary errors may influence diagnosis, surgery, or radiotherapy planning. 30 31

For prognostic and risk stratification models , standard classification metrics alone are insufficient. In addition to discrimination, calibration should be assessed to determine how closely predicted risks correspond to observed clinical outcomes. To determine whether a model adds value over an existing clinical risk score, decision analytic measures such as net benefit using decision curve analysis are preferred; the net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement may also be used to determine whether an AI model provides meaningful improvement over existing clinical risk scores. However, both measures have well documented statistical shortcomings and should therefore be interpreted with caution. 32

Clinicians must also distinguish between internal testing and true clinical validation. AI models that perform well on retrospective datasets may not maintain the same performance in routine clinical practice because of dataset bias, domain shift, and imaging variability. Model behavior: practical framework

For radiologists practicing in India, AI performance should be interpreted within the national regulatory context. AI-based diagnostic software is regulated as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) under the Medical Devices Rules, 2017 (as amended). Risk class is not fixed by specialty but derived from the manufacturer's stated intended use and the applicable risk-classification criteria, which consider the seriousness of the healthcare situation and the role of the software in clinical management, or provides diagnosis or treatment. Radiology AI applications may span class A to class D depending on their intended use and risk profile. For example, a triage aid that informs clinical management may fall into a lower risk class, whereas software intended to diagnose a critical condition such as intracranial hemorrhage may fall into class D. The ICMR Ethical Guidelines for Artificial Intelligence in Biomedical Research and Healthcare (2023) further emphasizes transparency, bias mitigation, accountability, and informed consent. When evaluating or procuring AI tools, clinicians should verify that the tool holds a valid CDSCO license appropriate to its risk class and intended use. They should also determine whether the reported performance has been validated on Indian or South Asian populations, given the well-documented reduction in performance when models trained predominantly on Western datasets are deployed in different clinical settings. 33

The AI-Assisted Radiology Lifecycle: An End-to-End Workflow

AI is distributed across the entire radiology workflow rather than confined to a single step. During onboarding and scheduling, ML models predict no-show risk, while LLM chatbots handle patient-preparation queries. During protocol selection and scanning, reinforcement learning optimizes protocols and CNNs monitor positioning in real time. Worklist triage is governed by CNNs that flag critical findings and ML tools that balance workload complexity. At the PACS workstation, CNNs are used to segment lesions, while radiomics-based malignancy risk estimation remains largely investigational. During reporting, LLMs draft structured reports and generate lay-language patient summaries. Finally, downstream decision support employs classical ML and radiomics to feed prognostic models estimating disease progression risk. This end-to-end view ( Fig. 6 ) illustrates why hybrid pipeline architectures, combining perceptual models, deterministic guideline logic, and language models, are required for comprehensive clinical AI deployment.

Model Behavior: What Radiologists Must Understand

The shift of an AI model from a regulated, laboratory setting to routine clinical practice often exposes important limitations that are not apparent during model development. Excellent statistical performance on a test dataset does not necessarily translate into safe or reliable clinical performance. 34 To use AI safely, radiologists should understand the characteristic behavioral traits and common failure modes of modern AI systems. 35 These concepts are summarized in Table 7 .

Indian radiologists should be aware of a limitation of AI, that is, demographic and population-level bias. Most commercially deployed radiology AI systems have been trained predominantly on datasets from North American and European institutions, where disease prevalence, patient demographics, imaging protocols, and equipment differ substantially from Indian clinical practice. A notable example is lung nodule detection AI, which developed and thresholded in populations with a low prevalence of pulmonary tuberculosis. In TB-endemic settings, the background prevalence of post-tuberculous scarring, upper lobe fibrosis, and calcified granulomas is far higher, so at any fixed operating threshold the absolute number of false-positive flags rises and the positive predictive value falls, even when sensitivity and specificity are preserved. This is not merely a technical limitation but a patient safety issue, as false-positive results may trigger unnecessary investigations or invasive procedures. Before relying on AI-assisted clinical decisions, radiologists should therefore ask for prevalence-dependent measures such as precision and F1 derived from a local or South Asian population, rather than AUROC alone, and determine whether the model has been validated in that setting. Bias can also run in opposite direction, as chest radiograph models trained on large North American dataset have been shown to systematically underdiagnose disease in underserved patient groups, including on the basis of sex, ethnicity, and insurance status. 36

Another important source of AI error is label noise arising from interobserver variability during training data annotation. When radiologists annotate images to create training datasets, they may legitimately disagree on lesion boundaries, disease extent, or diagnostic classification. Joskowicz et al demonstrated that manual contour delineation on CT shows substantial interobserver variability across different structures and pathologies, indicating that the “ground truth” used to train AI models may itself reflect expert interpretation rather than an absolute biological standard. Consequently, models trained on annotations from a single observer or a nonrepresentative group may inadvertently learn those interpretative biases, reducing their reliability when deployed in different clinical settings or patient populations. 37

The AI Override Decision: When and How to Disagree with an Algorithm

If your clinical interpretation differs from the AI output, document the reason for the override. Responsibility for the final report remains with the radiologist, regardless of AI involvement. A simple statement such as “ AI flagged [X]; independent review favored [Y] ” provides an auditable record of clinical reasoning. Departments should also maintain local override logs to monitor AI performance and identify recurrent failure patterns. 40 41

Key Questions before Adopting AI in Indian Practice

Before deploying any AI tool, verify that it has the appropriate Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) licence or registration as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD), for its risk classification, where applicable. Ensure that the tool's licensed intended use aligns with the proposed clinical deployment. Also to be checked are validation on Indian or South Asian datasets, clear disclosure of known failure modes, availability of calibration and external validation data beyond internal AUROC, and a mechanism for override logging and post-deployment performance monitoring. A tool that cannot provide these should not enter the clinical workflow.

Future Directions

The next generation of radiology AI will extend beyond pixel-level image interpretation toward clinically integrated decision support. 38 Future systems will increasingly combine imaging with electronic health records, laboratory data, pathology, genomics, and prior examinations, enabling multimodal AI models to generate more personalized and context-aware assessments. 23 42 43

Longitudinal AI capable of tracking imaging findings over time is also expected to improve disease monitoring, treatment response assessment, and prediction of clinical outcomes.

As these systems become more capable, robust governance will become equally important. Future AI platforms will require continuous validation, monitoring, and regulatory oversight to ensure safety, fairness, and clinical reliability across diverse patient populations. 44 45 46 47 The radiologist of the future will therefore serve not only as an expert image interpreter but also as an informed supervisor of AI systems, integrating algorithmic outputs with clinical judgment to deliver safe and patient-centered care.
