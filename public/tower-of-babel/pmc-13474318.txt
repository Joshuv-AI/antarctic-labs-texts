Segmental Distal Duodenectomy With Duodenojejunostomy and Gastrojejunostomy Reconstruction for Duodenal Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumor
Authors: Yusuke Hosoyamada, Shuhei Niki, Yoshiyuki Chiba, Yuzuru Ito, Yu Gyoda, Hiroyuki Sugo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 83‐year‐old man was admitted to the emergency department of our hospital with a chief complaint of melena. A computed tomography revealed a hypervascular tumor approximately 3 cm in size near the duodenojejunal flexure. On diagnosis of bleeding from the gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GST), emergency surgery was performed. At laparotomy, a whitish tumor measuring approximately 3 cm in diameter was identified on the serosal surface along the mesenteric border of the duodenojejunal flexure. Considering the need for secure hemostasis, a segmental duodenectomy including resection of the feeding vessels to the affected bowel segment was performed. For reconstruction, considering that anastomosis posterior to the superior mesenteric artery (SMA) was avoided and instead performed primary closure of the duodenal stump. To provide duodenal drainage, a Roux‐en‐Y jejunal limb was constructed and anastomosed to the 2nd part of the duodenum. Additionally, a gastrojejunostomy in the Billroth II configuration was performed. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 15, with no complications.

1. Introduction

The pancreatic head and periampullary region represent an anatomically complex area that includes the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), portal vein, and extrahepatic biliary system. When a malignant lesion is identified in the pancreatic head, pancreatoduodenectomy, including lymphadenectomy, is often indicated. In contrast, for benign lesions or low‐grade malignancies of the distal duodenum, particularly the 3rd and 4th segments, function‐preserving surgery, such as partial duodenectomy, is recommended whenever it is possible to preserve the ampullary region. Various surgical techniques for pancreas‐preserving partial duodenectomy are available, including wedge resection and segmental duodenectomy, with further variations depending on the location of the duodenal lesion [1–3]. However, there is no consensus regarding the optimal reconstruction method, especially after segmental duodenectomy.

Herein, we report a patient with a duodenal gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) involving the duodenojejunal flexure, for which segmental duodenectomy was performed, followed by reconstruction using duodenojejunostomy combined with gastrojejunostomy [4].

2. Case Presentation

An 83‐year‐old man was admitted to the emergency department of our hospital with a chief complaint of melena. He had a history of heavy alcohol consumption and had been diagnosed with alcoholic liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma approximately 4 years previously, for which radiofrequency ablation had been performed. On arrival, the patient exhibited massive melena, and laboratory tests revealed anemia with a hemoglobin level of 9.4 g/dL (compared to 13.0 g/dL 1 month prior). At that time, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography revealed a hypervascular tumor approximately 3 cm in size near the duodenojejunal flexure (Figure 1). Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a tumor in the horizontal portion of the duodenum, suggesting a duodenal GIST (Figure 2). On diagnosis of bleeding from the GIST, emergency transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) was performed using gelatin sponge particles to achieve hemostasis. Five days later, however, recurrent bleeding occurred, with a further decrease in the hemoglobin level to 6.7 g/dL. Given the difficulty of achieving sustained hemostasis through conservative measures, emergency surgery was indicated and subsequently performed.

Abdominal computed tomography, revealing a hypervascular tumor measuring 3 cm in distal duodenum (arrowheads). (a) Axial view. (b) Coronal view.

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, revealing a tumor in the horizontal portion of the duodenum.

During laparotomy, a whitish tumor approximately 3 cm in diameter was evident on the serosal surface of the mesenteric border at the junction between the duodenum and jejunum, specifically at the duodenojejunal flexure (Figure 3). Considering the need for secure hemostasis, a segmental duodenectomy, including resection of the feeding vessels to the affected bowel segment, was performed while preserving the pancreas. Accordingly, the proximal duodenal stump was transected at a position posterior to the SMA (Figure 4a). For reconstruction, considering that the duodenal stump was located dorsal to the SMA and that preoperative TAE may have compromised local blood flow, anastomosis posterior to the SMA was avoided, and the duodenal stump was closed with sutures, resulting in a blind end. First, to provide duodenal drainage, a Roux‐en‐Y jejunal limb was constructed, and a side‐to‐side hand‐sewing duodenojejunostomy was performed to the 2nd part of the duodenum. Next, a Billroth II gastrojejunostomy with Braun anastomosis was performed via the antecolic route (Figure 4b).

Intraoperative view of the duodenal tumor at the duodenojejunal flexure (arrowheads).

Schematic representation of segmental duodenectomy for tumor (a) and the reconstruction methods (b). The tumor was removed, preserving the pancreas. Roux‐en‐Y duodenojejunostomy and gastrojejunostomy were performed.

The resected specimen contained a 35 mm × 20 mm submucosal mass located in the distal duodenum (Figure 5a). Histopathological examination confirmed a low‐risk spindle‐cell GIST with negative surgical margins (Figure 5b,c). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated diffuse positivity for DOG1 (Figure 5d), whereas the tumor was negative for smooth muscle actin (SMA) and S100. The mitotic count was 1 per 10 high‐power fields, and the Ki‐67 labeling index was 6% (Figure 5e). Based on these histopathological findings, no adjuvant chemotherapy was administered. Postoperative gastrointestinal contrast studies confirmed satisfactory passage through both the gastrojejunostomy and the duodenojejunostomy (Figure 6). Oral intake was resumed on postoperative day 8 without any difficulty. The postoperative course was uneventful, with no evidence of vomiting or delayed gastric emptying (DGE), and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 15. At the 15‐month follow‐up, the patient remains under outpatient care for liver cirrhosis. Despite the underlying liver disease, oral intake has remained satisfactory, and the latest laboratory findings demonstrated a total protein level of 7.5 g/dL and a serum albumin level of 3.5 g/dL.

3. Discussion

GISTs are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract, with the stomach being the predominant site, accounting for approximately 50%–60% of cases [5, 6]. In contrast, GISTs arising from the duodenum are rare, comprising only about 5% of all cases [7, 8]. With respect to gastrointestinal bleeding from GISTs, Pih et al. [9] demonstrated in a review of 170 patients that the incidence of bleeding was significantly higher in duodenal GISTs (28.2%) compared with gastric GISTs (6.6%). Endoscopic hemostasis is generally considered the first‐line treatment for gastrointestinal bleeding. However, previous studies have reported that, in patients with bleeding duodenal GISTs, rebleeding occurred within 3 days in 41.2% of cases despite successful endoscopic hemostasis, highlighting the challenges and limitations of this approach. In the present case, endoscopic hemostasis was considered technically difficult, and TAE was therefore selected as the initial hemostatic intervention. However, because rebleeding subsequently occurred, emergency distal segmental duodenectomy was performed to achieve definitive hemostasis.

Various reconstruction methods following segmental duodenectomy have been reported, including end‐to‐end anastomosis at the transection site and patch repair of the resected segment [3, 10, 11]. Reconstruction following segmental duodenectomy is broadly categorized into two main approaches: one involves utilizing the duodenal stump directly for anastomosis, and the other entails closure of the duodenal stump with a separate anastomosis between the proximal duodenum and the Roux‐en‐Y jejunal limb [11]. However, the former approach carries potential risks, including compromised blood supply to the duodenal stump and technical difficulties associated with performing the anastomosis posterior to the SMA. In the present case, preoperative TAE raised concerns about impaired blood flow at the duodenal transection site. Furthermore, because the duodenal stump was located posterior to the SMA, reconstruction in this area was considered technically challenging. Therefore, we avoided anastomosis posterior to the SMA and instead performed primary closure of the duodenal stump. On the other hand, the latter technique, proximal duodenum and a Roux‐en‐Y jejunal limb anastomosis, carries risks such as increased intraluminal pressure in the blind‐loop of duodenal stump, which may lead to anastomotic fistula, as well as DGE due to stenosis at the anastomosis site. Previously, Liu et al. [11] reviewed 34 cases involving reconstruction after segmental duodenectomy (including the former approach in 21 patients and the latter in 13) and reported a high incidence of major postoperative complications (35%) and duodenojejunal anastomotic fistula (18%). Notably, DGE was observed in 42%–52% of patients in the former approach [1, 11].

In the present case, we modified the conventional reconstruction technique (closure of the duodenal stump and duodenojejunostomy using a Roux‐en‐Y jejunal limb) by adding a gastrojejunostomy using an identical Roux‐en‐Y limb in the Billroth‐II configuration. This approach offers several potential advantages: (1) it eliminates the need to use the duodenal stump for anastomosis and thus avoids anastomotic fistula, (2) it reduces food passage into the duodenum, thereby lowering intraluminal pressure in the blind stump, thus helping to prevent DGE, and (3) the dual pathway for food passage may promote increased oral intake while also ensuring an alternative route for food passage in the event of anastomotic fistula or stenosis at the duodenum.

Although the optimal reconstruction method following segmental duodenectomy remains controversial, our technique involving duodenojejunostomy using a Roux‐en‐Y jejunal limb with additional gastrojejunostomy might help prevent DGE and promote oral intake. Furthermore, this procedure is minimally invasive because it preserves the pancreas and is expected to facilitate the maintenance of postoperative oral intake, making it a suitable surgical option even for elderly patients. However, it is not appropriate for tumors requiring lymph node dissection. Therefore, from an oncological standpoint, this procedure should be reserved for benign or low‐grade neoplasms.
