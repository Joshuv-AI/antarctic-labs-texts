Analysis of risk factors for Clostridioides difficile infection in patients with ulcerative colitis and development of a predictive nomogram
Authors: Kanghua Qiu, Peng Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify the risk factors associated with Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) and to develop a predictive nomogram.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 331 UC patients from April 2022 to October 2024. We randomly assigned them to a training set (n = 232) and a validation set (n = 99) in a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, there were 51 patients had CDI (CDI group) and 181 patients did not (non-CDI group). Clinical data were recorded. Logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to screen risk factors for CDI in UC patients. R software was used to construct a nomogram model for predicting CDI. The calibration curve evaluated the model’s calibration, while Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve assessed its discriminative power. Clinical decision curve analysis was conducted to evaluate clinical utility.ResultsAge, obesity prevalence, and use of infliximab and glucocorticoids were higher in the CDI group compared to the non-CDI group, and albumin levels were lower (P < 0.05). Increased age, obesity, use of infliximab and glucocorticoids, and decreased albumin were independent risk factors for UC with CDI (P < 0.05). The training and validation sets showed that the calibration curve fitted well with the ideal curve, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.930 and 0.877, respectively, indicating good calibration and discrimination. Clinical decision curve analysis showed high clinical effectiveness.ConclusionThe proposed nomogram demonstrated excellent performance in predicting CDI risk among patients with UC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04503-9.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a subtype of inflammatory bowel disease that primarily affects the colon and rectum, with typical symptoms including diarrhea, abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, anemia, and fatigue [1]. Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) is an anaerobic, Gram-positive, spore-forming bacterium and the most common cause of infectious diarrhea [2, 3]. Patients with inflammatory bowel disease are particularly likely to develop C. difficile infection (CDI), however, the nature of this association remains uncertain, particularly whether CDI is a cause or consequence of inflammation [4]. CDI has become a significant public health concern in patients with inflammatory bowel disease. It not only increases the risk of UC-related hospitalizations and colectomy but also prolongs hospital stays, raises in-hospital mortality, and increases the likelihood of discharge to care facilities [5]. Therefore, clarifying the prevalence of CDI in UC patients and identifying the associated risk factors is crucial for early prediction and treatment. Previous studies have suggested that additional factors, including treatment medications (antibiotics, biologics), genetic predisposition, nutritional status, and hospitalization, may influence the development of CDI in UC patients [6]. The aim of this study was to identify potential risk factors for CDI in UC patients and to establish a nomogram model for visualized prediction.

Subjects and methods

Subjects

This study included 331 UC patients treated between April 2022 and October 2024. We divided them into a training set (n = 232) and a validation set (n = 99) in a 7:3 ratio. Among the training set, 51 patients had CDI (CDI group), while 181 did not (non-CDI group). Sample size calculation: The sample size was estimated using the events per variable (EPV) method, with EPV set at 10. In the training cohort, a total of 232 patients were included, of whom 51 (21.98%) had concomitant CDI. Based on an EPV of 10 and the plan to include five variables according to previous experience, the required number of CDI cases was 50. Accordingly, the minimum sample size needed was 50 ÷ 21.98% = 228, which was satisfied in the present study.

Inclusion criteria: ①UC diagnosis met the diagnostic criteria for inflammatory bowel disease [7]; ②Complete clinical data, including screening for C. difficile. Exclusion criteria: ①Use of vancomycin or metronidazole within 1 month prior to admission; ②Fecal microbiota transplantation prior to admission; ③Co-infection with other pathogens, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis or cytomegalovirus; ④Presence of other gastrointestinal diseases; ⑤History of infectious diseases, malignancies, or chronic systemic diseases.The case enrollment flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Methods

Data collection

The following data were collected for UC patients prior to CDI diagnosis: age, sex, disease duration, smoking history (smoking history (defined as continuous or cumulative smoking for ≥ 6 months during a lifetime), alcohol consumption history (defined as an average daily intake of pure alcohol > 60 g for men and > 40 g for women), fever (axillary temperature > 37.5 °C), abdominal pain, diarrhea, body mass index (BMI) (normal: 18.5–24.0 kg/m²;overweight: 24.0–27.9 kg/m²;obesity:≥28 kg/m²), disease activity (mild: Mayo score 3–5; moderate: Mayo score 6–10; severe: Mayo score 11–12), clinical type; Medication use: use of 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA), infliximab, corticosteroids, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), antibiotics, and immunosuppressants (drug use was recorded as “yes” if any administration was documented), as well as laboratory parameters including neutrophil count, white blood cell count, albumin, hemoglobin, creatinine, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and C-reactive protein.

CDI assessment

We diagnosed CDI following the guidelines of the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) and the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA) [8]. CDI diagnosis required that the isolated strain meet the following criteria: (1)Isolated from a patient with unexplained and “new onset” diarrhea, defined as more than three unformed stools within 24 h, with a positive clinical stool glutamate dehydrogenase test; ༈2༉Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) detection of tcdA or tcdB genes positive after culture on C. difficile-selective agar.

Statistical analysis

We performed statistical analysis using SPSS 25.0. Categorical variables were expressed as [n (%)], and the chi-square (χ2) test was used. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and the t-test was used for comparison. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included as independent variables in a logistic regression model to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) and to identify risk factors for CDI in UC patients. Variables with P < 0.05 in the logistic regression analysis were used to construct a predictive model, which was visualized as a nomogram using the “RMS” package in R 4.3.3 software. Internal validation of the nomogram was performed using the bootstrap method with 100 resamples. Calibration curves were used to assess the model’s calibration, ROC curves to evaluate its discrimination, and clinical decision curves to assess the model’s clinical utility. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Development of the predictive nomogram model

We developed a predictive nomogram for CDI risk using weighted scores for each predictor derived from the multivariable model. For an individual patient, draw a vertical line from each predictor value to the points axis, sum the points, and map the total to the predicted probability on the bottom axis. See Fig. 2. Usage instructions: For a given UC patient, the score corresponding to each of the five covariates can be obtained by drawing a vertical line from the variable value to the score axis. The scores are then summed to yield a total score, which corresponds to the predicted probability of CDI by drawing a vertical line from the total score to the probability axis.

Validation of the predictive nomogram model

Using bootstrap resampling (100 repetitions), internal validation showed good calibration. In both the training and validation cohorts, the calibration curves closely matched the ideal curve, showing a strong agreement between predicted probabilities and observed values. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded χ2 = 5.611,𝑃=0.691 for internal validation and χ2 = 3.792,𝑃=0.875 for external validation.The C-indexes were 0.924 (95% CI: 0.882–0.971) and 0.896 (95% CI: 0.859–0.948), respectively. ROC analysis showed high discrimination, with an AUC of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.891–0.968) for internal validation and 0.877 (95% CI: 0.824–0.931) for external validation, indicating only minor differences. See Figs. 3 and 4.

Discussion

Although the reasons for the increasing incidence of CDI in UC patients are not entirely clear, multiple factors are likely involved [9]. First, the growing awareness of the importance of CDI in acute exacerbations of UC has led clinicians to test more frequently for CDI, resulting in an increased detection rate [10]. Additionally, the widespread use of more sensitive diagnostic tests, such as nucleic acid amplification tests, may have further increased the detection rate of CDI in UC patients [11]. However, further clinical studies are still needed to clarify the exact contributing factors and to develop early prediction methods to reduce its incidence.

Song et al. [12] identified age, female sex, and the use of inflammatory bowel disease-related medications for more than 90 days as risk factors for CDI. Similarly, this study found that increased age was an independent risk factor for CDI in UC patients. This may be attributed to the decreased immunity and weaker resistance to CDI in older patient populations, increasing their infection risk. The results also showed that UC patients with obesity had a significantly higher risk of CDI compared to those with normal BMI, consistent with findings from Voth et al. [13]. Previous research has confirmed that obesity is associated with poor outcomes in inflammatory bowel disease, including an increased risk of colectomy [14]. In the UC patient population, the harmful effects of obesity may involve multiple factors, such as increased inflammation and further disruption of the gut microbiota. Infliximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting tumor necrosis factor-α, is widely used in UC treatment to suppress inflammatory responses in the colon [15]. Markovic et al. [16] demonstrated that infliximab could rescue acute severe UC complicated by CDI. Additionally, previous studies found that corticosteroid therapy for UC can improve clinical symptoms and accelerate intestinal mucosal recovery and healing [17]. However, this study revealed that the use of infliximab and corticosteroids were independent risk factors for CDI in UC patients, although the specific mechanisms remain unclear. These findings underscore the need for heightened vigilance in clinical settings for UC patients receiving infliximab and corticosteroids. Gros et al. [18] reported that patients with CDI had higher admission rates, with multifactorial results indicating that treatment with 5-aminosalicylic acid and albumin levels below 3.5 g/dL were associated with poorer outcomes. Sbeit et al. [19] found that age, serum albumin levels, and white blood cell counts were key predictors of in-hospital mortality among CDI patients. Consistent with these findings, this study identified reduced albumin levels as an independent risk factor for CDI in UC patients. Albumin is the most abundant protein in human plasma, with a small molecular size and high solubility, and serves multiple physiological functions, such as supporting immunity and nutritional balance [20]. Lower serum albumin levels may reduce nutritional status and immunity in UC patients, thereby increasing CDI risk.

It has been reported that a nomogram incorporating variables such as corticosteroid use, red blood cell count, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein can effectively predict opportunistic infections like C. difficile and Epstein-Barr virus in patients with inflammatory bowel disease patients following SARS-CoV-2 infection [21]. Xiang et al. [22] developed a nomogram model based on the UC endoscopic severity index, corticosteroid usage history, and C-reactive protein levels, achieving AUCs of 0.784, 0.825, and 0.837 for predicting hospital readmissions at 13, 26, and 52 weeks, respectively. However, few nomogram models have been specifically developed to predict CDI in UC patients. Accurate, individualized predictive nomograms are essential for clinicians to effectively identify high-risk patients with CDI, enabling targeted preventive measures to reduce readmission rates, healthcare costs, and improve the quality of life for UC patients. The ROC and calibration curve validation results of this study showed that the developed nomogram prediction model performs well in predicting CDI in UC patients. Within a risk threshold range of 0.02–0.98, the model consistently exhibited high net benefits. Additionally, both the internal and external validation cohorts demonstrated good discriminatory and calibration capabilities, with AUC values of 0.930 and 0.877, respectively.The predictive AUC in the validation cohort did not decrease significantly, and the P values in the Hosmer-Lemeshow test were all >0.05, indicating that the predictive model was unlikely to be overfitted. However, future validation using data from other institutions is still needed to exclude the risk of overfitting. In the nomogram, a recommended cutoff value of 0.5 was used to define the high-risk category for patients with CDI.When a UC patient’s total score corresponds to a predicted probability ≥ 0.5, based on this nomogram, the patient should undergo regular follow-ups and appropriate medication therapy. For UC patients exceeding the risk threshold, reassessment should be conducted 1–2 weeks after intensified medication treatment to determine their CDI risk and evaluate the treatment’s effectiveness.

However, this study has some limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, which is a common limitation among UC-related predictive models lacking multi-center validation. Therefore, further validation with multi-center data is necessary. Second, the study primarily focused on the clinical and demographic characteristics of UC patients, with relatively limited inclusion of other influencing factors, and did not include comorbidities, disease duration, or previous hospitalization history. Future research should aim to address this gap by incorporating a broader range of variables. Third, differences in CDI detection procedures across studies may lead to underestimation or overestimation of CDI incidence, thereby affecting the final statistical results and potentially introducing bias. Fourth, in this study, antibiotic use was recorded only as whether antibiotics were used or not, without specifying the types of antibiotics administered. Different classes of antibiotics pose varying risks for CDI; for example, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, and clindamycin may increase the likelihood of CDI, potentially affecting the results. Future studies should compare and analyze the impact of different antibiotic types on outcomes. In addition, this study was cross-sectional, and the causal relationship between variables and CDI occurrence needs further confirmation.

In summary, this study developed a cost-effective and clinically practical nomogram based on age, BMI, infliximab use, corticosteroid use, and albumin levels to predict the risk of CDI in UC patients. The model facilitates early identification of high-risk individuals and informs clinical practice.In this nomogram, only albumin requires clinical measurement using a biochemical analyzer, which is already available in our hospital, resulting in relatively low cost. Future work will include multicenter validation, integration with electronic medical record systems, and prospective intervention studies based on the nomogram.
