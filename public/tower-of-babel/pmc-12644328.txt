Development and validation of a nomogram for predicting fasciotomy requirement in lower extremity arterial injuries: a retrospective case–control study
Authors: Heng Zhang, Huiyang Jia, Haofei Wang, Qi Dong, Yingze Zhang, Zhiyong Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute compartment syndrome (ACS) following lower extremity arterial injuries necessitates urgent fasciotomy to prevent limb loss, yet current diagnostic tools lack specificity for ischemia–reperfusion pathophysiology. Our study aimed to develop a nomogram combining biomarkers and clinical indicators to predict fasciotomy risk, enhancing early risk stratification and optimizing surgical decision-making.Materials and methodsIn this retrospective case–control study (2010–2024), data were sourced from a tertiary hospital in China. A total of 146 patients with traumatic femoral or popliteal artery injuries were stratified into fasciotomy (n = 45) and non-fasciotomy (n = 101) groups. Adhering to the events-per-variable (EPV) principle (10:1), predictors were selected via least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and bootstrap validation. A multivariable logistic regression model was internally validated using tenfold cross-validation and 1000 bootstrap replicates.ResultsFour independent predictors were retained: limb ischemia severity (odds ratio [OR] = 4.25, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.97–10.02), K+ (OR = 6.99, 95% CI: 2.60–21.73), creatine kinase (CK; OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.08–1.30), and neutrophils (NEU) with a nonlinear threshold effect (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.10–1.33). The nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.877, 95% CI: 0.819–0.934), precise calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow P = 0.417), and broad clinical utility (net benefit threshold: 3–87%).ConclusionsThis study integrated accessible clinical and laboratory data and identified limb ischemia severity, K+, CK, and NEU as factors associated with fasciotomy risk. A nomogram based on these variables demonstrated reliable predictive performance and strong clinical applicability, enabling timely risk assessment and early intervention in patients with lower extremity arterial injuries.Level of evidenceLevel 4.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10195-025-00894-3.

Introduction

Vascular trauma accounts for approximately 3% of all trauma cases, with up to 87% affecting the extremities [1, 2]. Among these, femoral and popliteal artery traumas account for 30–40% [3]. Acute compartment syndrome (ACS), a devastating complication of vascular injuries, occurs in 27–51% of cases, particularly when both arteries and veins are compromised [4]. ACS results from a pathological rise in intracompartmental pressure (ICP) within a closed osteofascial space, leading to impaired capillary perfusion, tissue ischemia, and irreversible neuromuscular damage [5–7]. Ischemia–reperfusion injury exacerbates this process, forming a vicious cycle that necessitates immediate fasciotomy to prevent limb loss or mortality [8, 9]. Delayed intervention beyond 6 h significantly increases risk of amputation, renal failure, and death, highlighting the critical importance of early diagnosis and timely intervention [10–13].

ACS diagnosis primarily relies on clinical signs, such as disproportionate pain, paresthesia, and tense compartments, supplemented by ICP measurements [5, 6, 14]. Although absolute ICP thresholds (> 30 mmHg) or perfusion pressure (diastolic blood pressure − ICP < 30 mmHg) are widely adopted, they lack consensus [15–17]. However, clinical assessment alone is unreliable in sedated, polytraumatized, or hypotensive patients, leading to underdiagnosis and overtreatment [8, 18]. Moreover, traditional pressure monitoring devices are invasive and unreliable in hypotensive states, impeding timely decisions [1, 19, 20]. These limitations emphasize the need for objective, individualized risk stratification tools to guide fasciotomy.

Identifying ACS risk factors is critical for proactive management. Studies on tibial or femoral fractures have linked ACS to younger age, male sex, high-energy trauma, and concomitant injuries (e.g., fibular fractures or nerve damage) [2, 8, 20]. Crush injury, a major cause of extremity arterial trauma, may progress to crush syndrome in severe cases—characterized by myalgia, myoglobinuria, and elevated serum muscle enzyme levels—and is a key risk factor for ACS and its complications [21–23]. Blood biomarkers, such as creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB), Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), and monocyte (MON) levels, also show predictive potential [24]. However, most research focuses on fracture-related ACS, with scant attention to cases following acute vascular injuries [13, 25]. Unlike fracture-induced edema, arterial disruption triggers abrupt ischemia–reperfusion cascades, rapidly escalating ICP—a mechanism underexplored in current models [2, 26]. In addition, most models lack specificity, omit emergency blood parameters, and fail to provide personalized predictions [25]. For instance, while Kluckner et al.[1] identified ischemia duration and popliteal injury as predictors, their models excluded laboratory indicators. This gap necessitates comprehensive models that integrate clinical, radiographic, and biochemical variables specific to vascular trauma.

Nomograms, graphical tools synthesizing multivariable data into individualized risk probabilities, have demonstrated utility in oncology and critical care [9, 25]. However, none exist to predict fasciotomy requirements in lower extremity arterial injuries. This study aims to address this gap by developing and validating a nomogram incorporating demographic, injury-related, and laboratory parameters. The model aims to improve early risk stratification, minimize diagnostic delays, and optimize surgical outcomes in this high-risk population, ultimately empowering surgeons and emergency physicians to make timely, informed fasciotomy decisions.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective case–control study analyzed patients with traumatic femoral or popliteal artery injuries treated at a university-affiliated tertiary hospital between January 2010 and December 2024. Prior to data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (approval no. Ke 2023–107-1), and the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All Patient identifiers were replaced with unique codes, and data were stored in a password-protected database accessible only to the research team.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18–80 years and (2) hospitalization for traumatic femoral or popliteal artery injury confirmed by imaging or intraoperative findings. All consecutive patients meeting inclusion criteria during the study period were enrolled. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of peripheral arterial disease; (2) death or primary limb amputation before definitive surgical repair; (3) patient refusal of surgical intervention; (4) delayed fasciotomy performed after the initial surgery; and (5) missing key predictor variables exceeding 10% of the dataset.

Fasciotomy was performed immediately upon diagnosis of ACS by senior doctors in vascular surgery and trauma surgery (mean practice duration 24.7 ± 9.0 years) during preoperative rounding, either on the basis of classic clinical signs (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretching, palpable compartment tension) or objective ICP thresholds (absolute pressure > 30 mmHg or perfusion pressure < 30 mmHg). This protocol complies with the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) guidelines and is followed by all participating surgeons in the study. The standard surgical technique involved longitudinal incisions over the affected compartments, followed by meticulous dissection of the fascial layers surrounding the muscles to achieve complete decompression. Eligible patients were stratified into two groups on the basis of intraoperative decisions: the fasciotomy group (FT group, patients undergoing fasciotomy during initial vascular repair) and the non-fasciotomy group (NFT group).

Data collection

Patient data were collected from the electronic medical record system, including demographic information, comorbidities, injury characteristics, and emergency laboratory biomarkers. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, body mass index, and drinking and smoking status. Comorbidities comprised hypertension, diabetes, and stroke. Injury characteristics recorded injury cause, injury side, injured artery (femoral or popliteal artery), prehospital time, limb ischemia severity, collateral circulation, injury energy level, associated fracture or dislocation, multiple fractures, blister, associated soft tissue crush injury, degloving or open injury, associated nerve injury, associated venous injury, preoperative lower extremity deep vein thrombosis (LEDVT), shock, Mangled Extremity Severity Score (MESS), and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification. On the basis of previous studies, the injury causes were categorized as motor vehicle collision, fall from height, crush injury, blunt trauma, low-energy fall, or penetrating injury [18, 26]. The MESS, a scoring system for bone/soft tissue injury, shock, ischemia, and age, evaluates limb trauma severity and amputation risk [27, 28]. We extracted its bone/soft tissue injury and ischemia components from the MESS, categorizing them as limb ischemia severity (mild, moderate, or severe) and injury energy level (low, moderate, high, or extreme). Subjective assessments, such as MESS, were conducted by trained researchers using medical record data, with dual collection to enhance reliability.

Laboratory indicators were collected upon emergency room arrival, including white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NEU), lymphocytes (LYM), MON, eosinophils, basophils, red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit (HCT), platelet count (PLT), international normalized ratio, prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, thrombin time, D-dimer, antithrombin III, total protein, albumin, globulin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, cholinesterase (CHE), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatine kinase (CK), CK-MB, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), myoglobin (Mb), triglycerides, Na+, K+, Cl−, Ca2+, total carbon dioxide (TCO2), glucose (GLU), Urea, creatinine, osmolality, and uric acid. In addition, we further calculated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR = NEU/LYM), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR = MON/LYM), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR = PLT0 / LYM), SII (= PLT × NEU / LYM), and Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI = MON × NEU / LYM)[24].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3 for Windows). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared via independent t-tests; non-normal variables were reported as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies (%) and compared via chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Variables with > 10% missing data were excluded to minimize bias [29, 30]. For those with ≤ 10% missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was implemented [31]. After imputation, Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) tests evaluated distributional consistency between imputed and observed values for continuous variables, with reimputation for those showing P < 0.05.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression identified key predictors, with optimal lambda selected via minimum criterion and one-standard-error (1-SE) rule. Variables with non-zero coefficients were ranked by coefficient magnitude and validated through bootstrap resampling (100 iterations). Following the events-per-variable (EPV = 10:1) principle, top-ranked predictors were included in multivariable logistic regression.

Interaction terms (e.g., K+ × CK; limb ischemia severity × NEU) were tested but were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) and excluded. Regression coefficients and odds ratios (OR) assessed selected predictors, leading to nomogram development. Continuous predictors (K+, CK, NEU) were evaluated for nonlinearity using restricted cubic splines (RCS; three knots). NEU showed a significant nonlinear effect (P < 0.05) and was modeled with RCS.

Discrimination was measured via area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and concordance index (C-index), interpreted as low (< 0.5), moderate (0.5~0.7), high (0.7~0.9), and extreme accuracy (> 0.9)[9]. Calibration was evaluated using bootstrap-resampling calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Clinical utility was quantified with decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC). Internal validation included tenfold cross-validation and bootstrap validation (1000 resamples), with optimism-corrected performance metrics reported.

There were 136 (93.2%) males and 10 (6.8%) females, with a mean age of 43.2 ± 16.3 years. LASSO and multifactorial analysis

Variables with missing proportions exceeding 10% (CRP, 19.2%; Mb, 23.3%) were excluded prior to MICE because the missingness was not completely at random and could introduce bias if imputed. KS tests confirmed no significant distributional discrepancies between imputed and observed values for retained variables. Supplementary Table 1 presents the proportions of missing variables.

LASSO regression identified 17 non-zero coefficient variables, including smoking status, limb ischemia severity, degloving or open injury, associated nerve injury, LEDVT, ASA classification, NEU, SIRI, AST, CHE, CK, LDH, Na+, K+, Cl−, TCO2, and GLU. Regularization path plots and cross-validated mean squared error curves are shown in Fig. 2. Bootstrap resampling (100 iterations) demonstrated stable selection of these variables, with retention rates ≥ 80% (Supplementary Table 3). The top four variables by absolute coefficient magnitude—limb ischemia severity (β = 1.4475), K+ (β = 1.9441), CK (β = 0.1651), and NEU (β = 0.1824)—were retained in the final multivariable logistic regression model (Table 2). Nomogram to predict the probability of fasciotomy

A nomogram integrating these predictors was constructed (Fig. 4). The model demonstrated excellent discrimination, with an AUC of 0.877 (95% CI: 0.819–0.934) and a C-index of 0.877 (Fig. 5). Calibration plots (Fig. 6) and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (P = 0.417) indicated strong agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, suggesting no need for recalibration and model updating. Internal validation via tenfold cross-validation yielded an AUC of 0.842, while bootstrap validation (1000 replicates) produced an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.845 (95% CI: 0.748–0.929). Discussion

ACS following lower extremity arterial injuries is a life-threatening condition with an incidence rate of 27–51%[4], significantly higher than that observed in fracture-related cases [6, 8, 32]. Current diagnoses based on clinical signs and invasive ICP measurements are often subjective and unreliable [1, 15, 20]. The unique pathophysiology of arterial injury-induced ACS—driven by abrupt ischemia–reperfusion injury rather than gradual mechanical compression—necessitates specialized risk stratification tools [33]. This study addresses this critical gap by developing the first nomogram that integrates laboratory biomarkers with clinical parameters to predict fasciotomy requirements, offering a transformative approach to vascular trauma management.

Fractures are the main cause of ACS, with 36% of ACS cases attributed to these tibial shaft fractures, drawing primary clinical focus [14]. However, unlike fracture-related ACS, where ICP escalates gradually owing to mechanical compression, arterial injuries trigger rapid metabolic and inflammatory cascades [34]. Ischemia–reperfusion injury generates reactive oxygen species, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and fluid sequestration within hours [35]. Our findings reflect this via key biomarkers such as CK (muscle necrosis) and NEU (inflammation) [36, 37]. Thus, traditional fracture-based models, such as MESS, fail in vascular trauma by ignoring ischemia pathways [38].

The nomogram identified four independent predictors. Limb ischemia severity (OR = 4.25) directly correlates with tissue hypoxia and reperfusion injury magnitude, necessitating urgent intervention. K+ (OR = 6.99) likely reflects cellular lysis and acidosis, exacerbating edema through impaired ion transport [22]. Elevated CK (OR = 1.00 per unit) and NEU (OR = 1.20) signify rhabdomyolysis and neutrophilic inflammation, respectively, both amplifying compartment pressure through fluid shifts and cytokine release [39, 40]. Notably, NEU exhibited a nonlinear relationship with fasciotomy risk, with a sharp increase beyond 8 × 109/L (Fig. 3), likely driven by neutrophil-mediated microvascular occlusion via neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [41]. Although interaction terms were tested, none reached significance, suggesting independent contributions of these predictors in arterial injury-related ACS. These biomarkers provide real-time insights into the ischemia–inflammation axis, enabling earlier and more accurate risk stratification than clinical signs alone.

Compared with prior studies on ACS following vascular injuries, which primarily focused on anatomical factors and clinical indicators such as ischemia duration and popliteal artery involvement [1], our nomogram introduces biomarkers such as CK and NEU, capturing the ischemia–reperfusion injury cascade. For instance, elevated CK levels directly reflect rhabdomyolysis-induced fluid sequestration, while neutrophilic inflammation exacerbates microcirculatory obstruction through NET formation—mechanisms absent in earlier models. In addition, K+ emerged as a novel predictor, highlighting metabolic derangements unique to vascular trauma. Furthermore, existing nomograms in trauma, such as Liu et al.’s fracture-based model, lack comprehensive integration of clinical, inflammatory, and metabolic dimensions [25]. Our model’s multidimensional framework—incorporating limb ischemia severity (clinical), CK (tissue injury), NEU (inflammation), and K+ (metabolic)—outperforms the clinical features and inflammation index combined model of Wang et al. [24] (AUC = 0.869) and Wei et al. [9] (AUC = 0.849), providing robust risk assessment for trauma scenarios of lower limb arterial injury.

The LASSO–bootstrap–EPV workflow ensured methodological robustness. LASSO reduced 70 variables to 17 stable predictors, while bootstrap validation (≥ 80% selection frequency) confirmed reproducibility. Adherence to the EPV principle (10:1) prevented model overfitting, and MICE with Kolmogorov–Smirnov validation addressed missing data limitations. This approach aligns with Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD+AI) guidelines (Supplementary Table 4), enhancing transparency and reproducibility in prediction model development [42].

Designed for clinicians managing lower extremity arterial injuries, the nomogram requires active data handling. In practice, poor-quality or unavailable input data should be assessed and handled with care to ensure reliable predictions. Data quality can be evaluated by checking for missing values exceeding 10% per predictor, identifying outliers (e.g., K+ or CK values outside physiological ranges) and verifying measurement consistency with historical norms from the study cohort. For minor missingness (< 10%), MICE is recommended, using mean or median values from similar patient profiles. If a critical predictor (e.g., ischemia severity) is unavailable or of poor quality, a conservative approach—assuming the highest risk category—should be considered, followed by sensitivity analyses to assess the impact on predicted probabilities. These strategies build on the study’s exclusion of data with > 10% missingness and aim to maintain model accuracy in real-world settings with variable data quality.

Despite these strengths, this study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design, including potential selection bias and restricted generalizability to delayed presentations or diverse populations. Missing data excluded CRP and myoglobin, potentially limiting accuracy, while class imbalance (45 versus 101 cases) and small sample size hindered subgroup analyses or resampling. The high proportion of young male patients reflects the epidemiological characteristics of severe traumatic vascular injuries but may also limit the applicability of our nomogram to female and elderly populations. We emphasize that, in addition to its primary clinical use in the most affected demographic, external validation in more diverse cohorts will be necessary to confirm generalizability. Therefore, we plan to conduct a prospective multicenter study to externally validate the nomogram across diverse demographic groups and different types of clinical settings. In addition, integrating more diagnostic biomarkers with continuous ICP monitoring will be a key step toward improving the model’s practicality and predictive accuracy.

Conclusions

This study integrated readily available clinical and laboratory parameters to develop the first nomogram specifically designed to predict the need for fasciotomy in patients with lower extremity arterial injuries. Our findings identified limb ischemia severity, K+, CK, and NEU as independent risk factors for fasciotomy. The resulting nomogram demonstrated reliable performance in individualized risk prediction and holds promise for improving early surgical decision-making in this high-risk population. External validation and integration into clinical decision support systems are essential next steps to translate this tool into routine practice.
