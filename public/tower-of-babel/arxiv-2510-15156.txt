Magnetohydrodynamic-guiding-center-particle-in-cell Method for Multiscale Plasma Kinetic Simulations
Zitao Hu, Xue-Ning Bai, Xiaochen Sun

Abstract
We present the formulation, algorithm and numerical tests of the magnetohydrodynamic-particle-in-cell (MHD-PIC) method with particles treated under the guiding center approximation, which we term the MHD-gPIC method, and it is implemented in the Athena++ MHD code. The new MHD-gPIC model consists of thermal (cold) fluid and high-energy particles whose dynamics are integrated through guiding center equations including drift motion, with carefully evaluated source terms as particle backreaction. The code is validated with a series of tests, and it is expected to be primarily applicable to study particle acceleration and transport in systems where gyro-resonance is considered insignificant. We also present preliminary studies of particle acceleration during non-relativistic magnetic reconnection.

I
Introduction

The universe is filled with high-energy particles that exhibit an exponential power-law energy spectrum, yet their origins and acceleration mechanisms remain a subject of intense research.
Among the potential mechanisms, magnetic reconnection
(
F. Guo et al. 2024
)
stands out as a likely candidate for efficient particle acceleration, supported by a wealth of observational evidence such as solar flares and other astrophysical phenomena.
The physics of particle acceleration necessitates a deep understanding of the microscopic processes that occur during reconnection.
However, the initial scales at which particles are accelerated reside in the kinetic scales of the background plasma, which is vastly different from the macroscopic scales of the corresponding astrophysical system. More specifically, plasmoid instability in current sheets has been well recognized as a key mechanism that can lead to fast reconnection
(
N. F. Loureiro et al. 2007
;
D. A. Uzdensky et al. 2010
)
.
It leads to the formation of small-scale plasmoids, followed by a hierarchical merging processes towards macroscopic scales, which play
a crucial role in the acceleration of particles.
In the context of solar flares, the typical background plasma scale (electron skin depth) is on the order of
\sim
cm, as opposed to system scale of
\sim 10^{10}
cm
(
B. Chen et al. 2020
)
.
This huge scale separation poses significant challenges for numerical simulations, as it requires resolving the kinetic physics of particles while also capturing the fluid-scale dynamics.

Traditional magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations are capable of capturing the large-scale dynamics of astrophysical phenomena but do not account for the detailed kinetic effects of non-thermal particles.
On the other hand, particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations, which treat particles as individual entities, can resolve the microscopic scales but come with the significant computational cost associated with resolving the kinetic physics of particles (e.g.,
C. K. Birdsall & A. B. Langdon 1991
;
L. Fedeli et al. 2022
).
A hybrid-PIC approach (e.g.,
A. S. Lipatov 2002
;
M. W. Kunz et al. 2014
), attempts to bridge this gap by treating electrons as a fluid while allowing for kinetic ions evolution.
This method is computationally less expensive than full PIC but still requires a significant amount of computation to resolve the ion scale.
The MHD-PIC method is designed to overcome the limitations of these traditional approaches
(
X.-N. Bai et al. 2015
;
X. Sun & X.-N. Bai 2023
)
.
By treating the thermal plasma as a fluid described by MHD and only the non-thermal particles as kinetic particles, the MHD-PIC method allows for the resolution of large-scale dynamics while capturing the full kinetic nature of the non-thermal particles.
This method only requires the resolution of particle gyration, and
is particularly well-suited for simulating cosmic ray transport.
However, in the context of magnetic reconnection, the gyro-radius of even the energetic particles are in general significantly smaller than the scales over which the magnetic field varies, and it has been found that particle acceleration is primarily through drift acceleration mechanisms, instead of resonant interactions with waves at gyro-scale
(
J. F. Drake et al. 2006
;
J. F. Drake et al. 2013
;
X. Li et al. 2019
)
.

One potential approach to enhancing the MHD-PIC model for magnetic reconnection is to employ the guiding center approximation (GCA).
By treating particles as guiding centers (GCs), we can
bypass the gyro-scale, thus further alleviating the issue of scale separation
while still capturing the essential physics of particle acceleration.
We note that similar ideas have been explored in other works.
In
J. F. Drake et al. 2019
,
H. Arnold et al. 2019
, and
Z. Yin et al. 2024
, the authors developed their
kglobal
model and applied it to magnetic reconnection
(
H. Arnold et al. 2021
)
.
Nevertheless, their base MHD code
relies on artificial diffusion for stability, and uses different equation of states for background (thermal) electrons and ions.
In contrast,
A. Mignone et al. 2023
added a guiding center approximation for relativistic particles to the
PLUTO
code while particle feedback was not considered in their model.

In this paper, we introduce the MHD-gPIC model, which extends the MHD-PIC framework
(
X.-N. Bai et al. 2015
)
by incorporating the guiding center approximation.
We re-derived the guiding center equations and carefully treated the feedback of particles on the plasma rigorously.
We have implemented the new MHD-gPIC framework based on the MHD-PIC module of
(
X. Sun & X.-N. Bai 2023
)
built upon the grid-based Godunov MHD code
Athena++

(
J. M. Stone et al. 2020
)
. We have made substantial effort in mitigating numerical issues especially particle noise reduction, allowing smooth user-defined particle injection, and have validated our implementation through a series of benchmark tests.

This paper is organized as follows.
We derive the formulation of the MHD-gPIC model in Section
II
.
Implementation of this formulation to the
Athena++
MHD code is described in Section
III
.
Benchmark tests are provided in Section
IV
.
We summarize and conclude in Section
V

II
Methods

In our MHD-gPIC model, we simulate the motion of charged particles (both ions and electrons) in magnetic fields and their dynamic interaction with the surrounding thermal plasma at a macroscopic scale that greatly exceeds the scale of their gyro-radii.
The model is structured into two primary components: the equations of motion for the non-thermal components, which are treated as discrete particles, and the MHD equations that govern the behavior of the ambient thermal gas.
The gas is considered to be non-relativistic. Similar to the MHD-PIC model
(
X.-N. Bai et al. 2015
;
X. Sun & X.-N. Bai 2023
)
, the particles can be non-relativistic or relativistic, and in the latter case, particle velocity is limited by an artificial speed of light
\mathbb{C}
, and the formalism is valid as long as
\mathbb{C}
well exceeds any MHD velocities.
Similar to the standard PIC and MHD-PIC
models, non-thermal particles are considered as super particles that effectively stand for a large number of physical particles, carrying certain effective shape matching the simulation grids to facilitate interpolations and deposits.

Our formalism and its derivation inevitably involve a large set of symbols, which we summarize in Table
1
.
We will introduce the relevant notations as we delve into the derivation. In brief, for particles, we distinguish real and guiding-center averaged quantities in lower-case and upper-case letters, respectively.
In the fluid equations including the fluid-particle interface, we use subscript ‘
f
’ and ‘
p
’ to denote fluid and particle quantities (where fluid quantities are by definition assumed to be thermal),
within which we further distinguish between contributions from ions and electrons by subscripts ‘
i
’ and ‘
e
’.
We use
\perp
and
\|
to denote vector components perpendicular and parallel to magnetic field, respectively. We also note that we use
\bm{u}
to denote fluid velocities, while use
\bm{v}
to denote particle velocities.

to

.

Our definition of MHD pressure differs from the standard definition by incorporating the perpendicular pressure
{\sf P}_{p\perp}
from the gPIC part. The reason behind this definition is as follows.
In the presence of non-thermal particles, charge neutrality of the composite system demands
n_{\rm fe}+n_{\rm pe}=n_{\rm fi}+n_{\rm pi}
. The number densities of electrons and ions in the background thermal fluid are generally non-equal (
n_{\rm fe}\neq n_{\rm fi}
) , while they are considered to share the same temperature
T
. With electrons being essentially massless, they only contribute to pressure but not mass. As a result, the total fluid pressure is

{\sf P}_{f}={\sf P}_{\rm fe}+{\sf P}_{\rm fi}\propto n_{\rm f}T,

(6)

where
n_{\rm f}=n_{\rm fe}+n_{\rm fi}
is the total particle number density in the MHD part.
Generally, gas temperature
T
and total plasma particle number density
n_{f}+n_{p}
should be smooth in the simulation.
However, particle distribution
n_{p}
in the PIC part is generally not smooth due to bulk motion or local particle injection.
This makes it particularly inconvenient for computational purposes since the related gradient computation in equation (
3
) and (
4
) will amplify the noise at grid scale.
As a partial remedy, we add the perpendicular-
\bm{B}
pressure of the gPIC part
{\sf P}_{p\perp}
to the gas thermal pressure.
In this way, we have
{\sf P}\equiv{\sf P}_{f}+{\sf P}_{p\perp}
for total fluid pressure, i.e., the total pressure of the plasma by assuming parallel-
\bm{B}
pressure of the PIC part is the same as its perpendicular pressure.
The advantage of this trick is that it can effectively mitigate numerical noise generated by particle feedback (as we shall see in section
IV.4
), thereby enhancing the stability of our numerical scheme.

The terms on the right hand side of Equations (
3
) and (
4

\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}
and
\ W_{\text{gPIC}}

. We note that in the standard MHD-PIC formalism, the backreaction is simply treated by ensuring momentum and energy conservation following detailed derivations
(
X.-N. Bai et al. 2015
)

Our formalism for the backreaction is compatible with that in standard MHD-PIC, but further relaxes the assumption of small particle number density. This leads to a revision to the gas pressure and hence the entire derivation, and we will delve into the details in subsequent subsections.
Furthermore, the derivation in this section can be generalized to fully general-relativistic MHD in curved spacetimes, and we leave this part in Appendix
B
.
Additionally, we have included some intermediate steps of the derivations in Appendix
C
.

II.1
Particle Equarion of Motion

For a single particle labeled
k
, either an electron or an ion, we adopt the guiding center approximation (GCA), which replaces the particle’s actual trajectory with its center of motion.
This approach is justified by the fact that the particle’s gyroradius and gyro-timescale is significantly smaller than the corresponding characteristic scales of the MHD system.
The guiding center approximation of particle motion has been widely studied since
T. G. Northrop 1963
, with relativistic representations subsequently derived
(
A. Mignone et al. 2023
;
T. Trent et al. 2024
)
.
We list the main derivation in appendix
A
, where we provide the most general derivation in covariant form. In the main text below, we rewrite the results in the more familiar
3+1
form.

We use two sets of variables to distinguish primitive and gyro-averaged quantities for particles, where the former is denoted by lower-case letters, while the latter is represented by the corresponding upper-case letters.
For instance, the location of particle
k
’s guiding center is

\bm{X}_{k}\equiv\bm{x}_{k}+\frac{1}{\omega_{k}B}(\bm{v}_{k}\times\bm{B}+\bm{E}),

(7)

where
\omega_{k}=q_{k}B/\gamma_{k}m_{k}
is the gyro-frequency of the particle.
The guiding center velocity for the particle electron, denoted as
\bm{V}_{k}
, is given by

\bm{V}_{k}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{X}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=v_{k\|}\bm{b}+\bm{u}_{\perp}+\bm{v}_{\text{drift}},

(8)

where
\bm{u}_{\perp}\equiv\bm{E}\times\bm{B}c/B^{2}
is the perpendicular MHD gas velocity (a.k.a.
\bm{E}\times\bm{B}
drift velocity),

v_{\|k}
is the parallel the particle velocity,
\bm{v}_{\text{drift}}
is the
perpendicular velocity of the guiding center relative to the fluid (see Equation (
A12
)).
We keep the leading order
\mathscr{O}(\omega_{k}^{-1})
terms which corresponds to Speiser motion, curvature drift, inertial drift and gradient-
B
drift

\bm{v}_{\text{drfit}}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}\omega_{k}}\left[\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{k}J_{\|}}{m}\bm{b}}_{\rm Speiser}-\left(\underbrace{v_{k\|}^{2}\bm{\kappa}}_{\rm curvature}+\underbrace{\gamma_{k}v_{k\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}}_{\rm inertial}+\underbrace{\frac{\mu_{k}}{m}\nabla B}_{\text{grad-}B}\right)\times\bm{b}\right],

(9)

where
J_{\|}=(\nabla\times{\bm{B}})_{\|}
is the parallel current density of plasma.
As the GCA applies when the gyro-timescale
\omega_{k}^{-1}
is significantly smaller than the time scale corresponding to the variations in the magnetic field experienced by the particle, we only use the other two
\mathscr{O}(1)
terms in most cases, while provide the option to retain the above
\bm{v}_{\text{drift}}
terms in the code.

The momentum and energy equations (i.e., equation (
A16
) and (
A19
) in observer’s coordinate with
\text{d}t=\gamma_{k}\text{d}\tau
) in GCA are

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=q_{k}\left(\bm{E}+\bm{V}_{k}\times\bm{B}\right)-\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\nabla B,

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=q_{k}E_{\|}V_{k\|}+\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}B}{\mathrm{D}t}-u_{\|}\nabla_{\|}B\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\left(V_{k\|}\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\kappa}+V_{k\|}\bm{u}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}+u_{\perp}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{\perp}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right),

(11)

where
\bm{P}_{k}\equiv\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{V}_{k}
denotes the gyro-averaged momentum,
\gamma_{k}=\sqrt{1+P_{k}^{2}/m_{k}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2}+2\mu_{k}B/m_{k}\mathbb{C}^{2}}
is the Lorentz factor and
\mu_{k}\equiv{p_{k,\perp}^{2}}/{2m_{k}B}
is the magnetic moment which is an adiabatic invariant and
\Delta\bm{V}_{k}\equiv\bm{V}_{k}-\bm{u}
is the particle velocity in gas co-moving frame.
We also defined
\text{d}/\text{d}t\equiv\partial/\partial t+\bm{V}_{k}\cdot\nabla
and
\mathrm{D}/\mathrm{D}t\equiv\partial/\partial t+\bm{u}\cdot\nabla
when they act on MHD variables.
The energy equation will be used to derive the energy feedback of gPIC part in section
II.4
.

We can decompose the momentum equation into two distinct components: one aligned with the magnetic field and the other orthogonal to it.
These components are expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k\|}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\bm{b}+\bm{P}_{k}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\bm{P}_{k}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{d}t}+qE_{\|}-\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\nabla_{\|}B,

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\ 0.

(13)

In the parallel momentum equation (
12
), the first term on the right-hand side originates from the variation of the magnetic field direction over time. This term is typically treated with the approximation
\text{d}\bm{b}/\text{d}t\approx\bm{V}_{k}\cdot\nabla

in previous studies (e.g.
T. G. Northrop 1963
,
J. F. Drake et al. 2019
), which is valid when the particle speed
V_{k}
is much greater than the MHD speed
u
. However, we stress that even this condition is satisfied, the approach violates Galilean invariance, leading to different guiding-center orbits in different reference frames.

Instead, we transform the rate of change of the magnetic field direction from the particle’s frame to the MHD frame using:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{\kappa}+\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t},

(14)

where we have only kept the zeroth order velocities.
By combining (
12
) with (
14
), we obtain the parallel momentum equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k\|}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{P}_{k}\cdot\bm{\kappa}+\bm{P}_{k}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}-\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\nabla_{\|}B+qE_{\|}.

(15)

In ideal MHD, the second term on the right-hand side of (
15
) is:

\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}=(1-\bm{bb})\cdot\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}\ .

(16)

By substituting (
8
) and (
16
) in (
15
),
we derive the full particle momentum equation (
1
).
Our treatment by the usage of (
14
) preserves Galilean invariance, as we demonstrate in a test problem in Section
IV.1
.

For the remaining two terms in equation (
15
), the third term corresponds to betatron acceleration, as it arises from the conservation of magnetic moment in the perpendicular equation.
The final term is attributed to the non-ideal parallel electric field, to be specified in Section
II.3
.

II.2
Particle-Fluid Interface

In this subsection, we lay out the framework for the particle-fluid interface, so that we can estimate the momentum and energy exchange between the particle components with the thermal gas.
Inherited from the PIC approach, each particle carries an effective shape designed to facilitate interpolation from the grid, i.e., the macroscopic variable
G
at location
\bm{x}
is linked with microscopic variable
g_{k}
of particles by

G(\bm{x})\equiv\sum_{k}S(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{k})g_{k},

(17)

where
S(\bm{x},\bm{x}_{k})
is the shape function which follows the standard triangular-shaped cloud (TSC) scheme in our code.
For convenience, we use the Dirac-
\delta
function
\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k})
as the shape function which is intuitive in the following derivations.

With equation (
17
), we can get the macroscopic parameters of the gPIC part.
The number density
n_{p}
, mass density
\rho_{p}
and charge density
q_{p}
of particles are

\displaystyle n_{p}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}),

(18)

\displaystyle\rho_{p}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}m_{k}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}),

(19)

\displaystyle q_{p}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k}q_{k}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}).

(20)

Similarly, the bulk velocity and current density are

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{p}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k})/\rho_{p},

(21)

\displaystyle\bm{J}_{p}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k}q_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}).

(22)

We also use the particle stress tensor
\mathbf{T}_{p}
in the following sections, which is defined by

\mathbf{T}_{p}(\bm{x})\equiv\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}),

(23)

and the particle pressure tensor
\mathbf{P}_{p}
, which is the stress tensor in particles’ co-moving frame,

\mathbf{P}_{p}\equiv\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}m_{k}(\bm{v}_{k}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{p})(\bm{v}_{k}-\overline{\bm{u}}_{p})\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k})=\mathbf{T}_{p}-\rho_{p}\bm{u}_{p}\bm{u}_{p}.

(24)

The above definitions apply to all particles (without additional subscripts) or to each type of particle (with additional subscript ‘
e
’ or ‘
i
’).

II.3
Non-ideal Electric Field

In this subsection, we complete the derivation of parallel electric field needed for particle motion (
1
). Follow
H. Arnold et al. 2019
,
this can be determined using the generalized Ohm’s law:

\bm{E}=-\frac{\bm{u}_{i}}{c}\times\bm{B}+\frac{\bm{R}_{e}}{en_{e}}+\frac{1}{en_{e}}\frac{\bm{J}\times\bm{B}}{c}-\frac{1}{en_{e}}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}_{e}-\frac{m_{e}}{e}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{e}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(25)

where
\bm{u}_{i}
is the ion velocity,
\bm{R}_{e}
represents the
frictional force between ions and electrons in the MHD gas,
n_{e}
is the total electron number density,
\mathbf{P}_{e}
is the total electron pressure tensor, and
\bm{u}_{e}
is the averaged electron velocity.
The last term in the equation can be disregarded
in MHD scales given electron’s small inertia.
For the parallel component
E_{\|}
, the second term is usually significantly smaller than the fourth (pressure gradient) term under typical collisionless conditions,
leading to

E_{\|}\approx-\frac{1}{en_{e}}\bm{b}\cdot\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}_{e}.

(26)

We see that
E_{\parallel}
is primarily governed by the electron pressure gradient, and involves both fluid (thermal) and particle (non-thermal) electrons.

To compute
\mathbf{P}_{\text{e}}
, we write

\mathbf{P}_{e}=\mathbf{T}_{\text{fe}}+\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}}-\rho_{e}\bm{u}_{e}\bm{u}_{e}=\mathbf{P}_{\text{fe}}+\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}}+\rho_{\text{fe}}\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}-\rho_{e}\bm{u}_{e}\bm{u}_{e},

(27)

where
\mathbf{T}_{\text{fe}}
and
\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}}
are the stress tensors of gPIC electrons and MHD electrons respectively,
\rho_{e}
is the mass density of all electrons.
Using
\rho_{e}\bm{u}_{e}=\rho_{\text{fe}}\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}+\rho_{\text{pe}}\bm{u}_{\text{pe}}
in above equation, with
\bm{u}_{\text{pe}}
from Equation (
21

where
{\sf P}_{f}

Together with the pressure tensor of particle electrons

(31)

where we have assumed that

The second term on the left-hand side signifies the relocation of fluid electrons to maintain charge neutrality. We can derive

\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}=\frac{n_{\text{fi}}-n_{\text{fe}}}{n_{\text{fe}}}u_{\|}\bm{b}+\frac{J_{p\|}\bm{b}}{n_{\text{fe}}e}-\xcancel{\frac{\mathbb{C}\nabla\times\bm{B}}{4\pi n_{\text{fe}}e}},

(32)

where we have used
q_{f}=(n_{\text{fi}}-n_{\text{fe}})e

\bm{J}_{p}\approx J_{p\|}\bm{b}-q_{f}\bm{u}_{\perp}

II.4
Particle Feedback

Once the particle equation of motion is established, the feedback from the gPIC component can be consistently derived.
A key distinction between the present model and the conventional MHD-PIC framework lies in the fact that the guiding center position does not coincide exactly with the particle’s physical position.
This discrepancy necessitates careful treatment in the coupling between particle dynamics and the background gas.

For each particle, base on equation (
7
), the connection between its actual position
\bm{x}_{k}
and its guiding center position
\bm{X}_{k}
is encapsulated by the relationship

\bm{x}_{k}=\bm{X}_{k}+\delta\bm{x}_{k},

(33)

where the relative position
\delta\bm{x}_{k}

evolves as

\text{d}\delta\bm{x}_{k}/\text{d}t=\omega_{k}\bm{b}\times\delta\bm{x}_{k}
and
its autocorrelation satisfies

\left\langle\delta\bm{x}_{k}\delta\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle=r_{k}^{2}(1-\bm{bb})/2
, where
r_{k}
is the Larmor radius.
The force acting on the fluid
from the particles is

\bm{F}(\bm{x},t)\equiv\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{p}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}),

(34)

where
\text{d}\bm{p}_{k}/\text{d}t
corresponds to the Lorentz force experienced by individual particles.
Similarly, the work done on the fluid from the particles is

W(\bm{x},t)\equiv\sum_{k}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k}),

(35)

where
\varepsilon_{k}\equiv\gamma_{k}m_{k}\mathbb{C}^{2}
represents the energy of particle
k
.

Within the MHD-gPIC model, we only record the guiding center position
\bm{X}_{k}
rather than the full particle position
\bm{x}_{k}
. Consequently, we do not possess the momentum associated with the gyro-motion of the particles.
To address this limitation, we can derive from equation (
33
) that

\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k})\approx\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})-\delta\bm{x}_{k}\cdot\nabla\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k}),

(36)

and by applying gyro-averaging to equation (
34
), we obtain

\bm{F}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})-\sum_{k}\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{p}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle\cdot\nabla\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k}).

(37)

Note that the term within the bracket
\left\langle\right\rangle
is only a function of time, and hence can be placed behind the
\nabla
operator.
By using
\text{d}\left\langle\bm{p}_{k}\delta\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle/\text{d}t\sim\mathscr{O}(\omega_{k}^{-1})
in the above equation, we can derive

\bm{F}(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})+\nabla\cdot({\sf P}_{p\perp}(1-\bm{bb})),

(38)

where perpendicular pressure
{\sf P}_{p\perp}
corresponds to gyro-energy
\gamma_{k}m_{k}\omega_{k}^{2}r_{k}^{2}/2
.
The first term on the right-hand side represents the momentum acquired by the particles,
which is nearly equivalent to the
Lorentz force acting on the particles.
The difference lies in the fact that the charge density and current density are based on the guiding center position of the particles and include an additional gyro-averaged term.
By employing equation (
10
) and (
15
), we can derive after some algebra (see Appendix
C.2
for details)

\displaystyle\bm{F}=\rho_{p}\left.\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right|_{\perp}+\nabla_{\perp}{\sf P}_{p\perp}+q_{p}E_{\|}\bm{b}+\Delta{\sf T}_{p}\bm{\kappa}+2\rho_{p}\Delta u_{p\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t},

(39)

where
\Delta{\sf T}_{p}={\sf T}_{p\|}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}
is the particles’ stress tensor anisotropy in gas co-moving coordinates, and
\Delta u_{p\|}
corresponds to particle bulk parallel velocity
u_{p\|}-u_{\|}
in gas co-moving coordinates.

In a similar manner, by utilizing equation (
36
) and applying the gyro-averaging technique, we arrive at

\displaystyle W(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})-\sum_{k}\left\langle\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{p}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta\bm{x}_{k}\right\rangle\cdot\nabla\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}{\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})+\nabla\cdot\left({\sf P}_{p\perp}\bm{u}_{\perp}\right)\ .

The second term on the right-hand side accounts for the energy flow resulting from the anisotropy of particle acceleration and de-acceleration during a single gyro-cycle.
Given that particles acquire energy through acceleration by the electric field, by summing up all particles and applying gyro-averaging, we obtain

W=J_{p\|}E_{\|}+\left(2\rho_{p}\Delta u_{p\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\Delta{\sf T}_{p}\bm{\kappa}+\rho_{p}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\nabla{\sf P}_{p\perp}\right)\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp},

(41)

where
J_{p\|}
is the parallel current of the particle part, and the detailed derivation can be found in appendix
C.3
.
We can verify

W-\bm{u}\cdot\bm{F}=(J_{p\|}-q_{p}u_{\|})E_{\|}\equiv Q,

(42)

i.e., the energy gain in the gPIC part equals to work done on the MHD part plus the Joule heating.

II.5
Equations of Gas Dynamics

Built upon the particle dynamics and feedback equations described earlier, the MHD equations can be supplemented with source terms from the gPIC component.
In this subsection, we focus on the momentum and energy equations.
Naively, these gPIC source terms can simply be obtained by enforcing momentum and energy conservation, given by Equations (
39
) and (
41
). However, as we shall see in section
IV.4
, this approach is sensitive to inhomogeneous particle distribution especially Poisson noise, leading to large truncation errors.
By adding gPIC perpendicular pressure to MHD part,
we aim to retain the left-hand sides of equations (
3
) and (
4
). As the MHD momentum equation is coupled with the equation of energy feedback (
41
), further derivation is needed to obtain
\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}
and
W_{\text{gPIC}}
.

II.5.1
Source Term of Momentum Equation

We begin our analysis with the momentum equation.
In our gPIC model, both electrons and ions are taken into consideration, and they
follow the same equation of motion yet have largely
different masses.
This requires us to handle particle electrons and ions differently, with particle electrons only supply pressure without bulk momentum.
By summing the momentum equations for each component, we arrive at a comprehensive expression that captures the total momentum of the system

\rho_{f}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\sum_{k\in\text{ion}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{p}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{k})+\nabla\cdot({\sf P}_{f}+\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}})=\bm{J}\times\bm{B},

(43)

where the second term is a sum over the particle ions.
To further refine this equation, we employ the momentum closure equation (
39
) specifically for ions.

This allows us to rewrite the momentum equation as

\displaystyle\rho_{f}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\left.\rho_{p}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right|_{\perp}+\nabla\cdot({\sf P}_{f}+\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}})-\bm{J\times B}=

(44)

\displaystyle-\left(\nabla_{\perp}{\sf P}_{\text{pi}\perp}+q_{\text{pi}}E_{\|}\bm{b}+\Delta{\sf T}_{\text{pi}}\bm{\kappa}+2\rho_{\text{pi}}\Delta u_{\text{pi}\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right).

In this formulation, we move the term
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}}
to the right-hand side and utilize the decomposition
\mathbf{T}_{\text{pe}}={\sf T}_{\text{pe}\|}\bm{bb}+{\sf P}_{\text{pe}\perp}(\mathbf{I}-\bm{bb})
.

This transformation leads to a right-hand side that can be expressed as

-\nabla{\sf P}_{p\perp}-\bm{F}_{p\perp}-\bm{F}_{p\|},

(45)

where
\bm{F}_{p\perp}
and
\bm{F}_{p\|}
represent the perpendicular and parallel non-thermal forces, respectively, defined as

\bm{F}_{{p}\perp}\equiv\Delta{\sf T}_{p}\bm{\kappa}+2\rho_{\text{pi}}\Delta u_{\text{pi}\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t},

(46)

and

\bm{F}_{p\|}\equiv\left[q_{\text{pi}}E_{\|}-\Delta{\sf T}_{\text{pe}}\nabla_{\|}\ln B+\nabla_{\|}\left({\sf T}_{\text{pe}\|}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}\right)\right]\bm{b}.

(47)

On the left-hand side, we are left with

\rho_{f}\left(1+\frac{\rho_{p}}{\rho_{f}}(\mathbf{I}-\bm{bb})\right)\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\nabla{\sf P}_{f}-\bm{J}\times\bm{B}.

(48)

To simplify this expression, we introduce the ratio of the particle density to the total density,
R\equiv\rho_{p}/(\rho_{g}+\rho_{p})
, and express the inverse matrix of
1+\frac{\rho_{p}}{\rho_{f}}(\mathbf{I}-\bm{bb})
as
1-R(\mathbf{I}-\bm{bb})
.
By moving
\nabla{\sf P}_{p\perp}
to the left-hand side and multiplying
1-R(\mathbf{I}-\bm{bb})
to both sides, we derive the final form of the momentum equation

\rho_{f}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+(1-R(1-\bm{bb}))\cdot\left(\nabla{\sf P}-\bm{J}\times\bm{B}\right)=-(1-R)\bm{F}_{{p}\perp}-\bm{F}_{{p}\|}.

(49)

This equation encapsulates the intricate interplay between the MHD fluid and the gPIC particles, highlighting the anisotropic properties of the gPIC component through
\Delta{\sf T}
related terms in
\bm{F}_{p\perp}
and
\bm{F}_{p\|}
.
If the gPIC component were in thermal and dynamical equilibrium with the MHD component, sharing the same temperature and bulk velocity,
F_{p\perp}
would degenerate to zero as particles would drift with the MHD part together.

To maintain consistency with pure MHD format on the left-hand side of the momentum equation, we define the generalized gPIC force,
\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}
as

\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}\equiv-R\left(\nabla{\sf P}-\bm{J}\times\bm{B}\right)_{\perp}+(1-R)\bm{F}_{p\perp}+\bm{F}_{p\|},

(50)

which allows us to derive equation (
3
), completing the formulation of the momentum equation within the gPIC framework.

II.5.2
Source Term of Energy Equation

To derive the energy equation, we first consider the energy exchange from the gPIC part. Applying
\text{D}\bm{u}/\text{D}t
from equation (
49
)

\left.\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right|_{\perp}=-\frac{1-R}{\rho_{f}}\left(\bm{F}_{p\perp}+\nabla_{\perp}{\sf P}-\bm{J}\times\bm{B}\right)

to equation (
41
), leading to the following expression

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=J_{p\|}E_{\|}+\left(\bm{F}_{p\perp}+\rho_{p}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\nabla{\sf P}_{p\perp}\right)\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}

(51)

\displaystyle=J_{p\|}E_{\|}+\left[R(\bm{J}\times\bm{B}-\nabla{\sf P})+(1-R)\bm{F}_{p\perp}+\nabla{{\sf P}}_{p\perp}\right]\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=J_{p\|}E_{\|}+(\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}+\nabla{\sf P}_{p\perp})\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}.

The other part of the energy equation involves the evolution of internal energy from fluid electrons and ions.
The ion part is more straightforward, where energy conservation is expressed as

\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_{\text{fi}}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left[(\mathcal{E}_{\text{fi}}+{\sf P}_{\text{fi}})\bm{u}\right]=\bm{E}\cdot\bm{J}_{\text{fi}},

(52)

For fluid electrons, the equation in principle should be similar by replacing
\bm{u}
by
\bm{u}+\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}
. However, as massless fluid to neutralize the system, fluid electrons do not necessarily follow adiabatic evolution, and we also note that our single-fluid treatment implicitly assumes fluid electrons and ions share the same local temperature. Here we tentative adopt the following form

\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left[(\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}+{\sf P}_{\text{fe}})\bm{u}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}\right]=\bm{E}\cdot\bm{J}_{\text{fe}}.

(53)

The rationale behind this is that the baseline parallel motion of gPIC particles does not directly act on the bulk fluid.
Consequently, the redistribution of fluid electrons to balance the parallel current is treated as a combination of bulk motion (represented by the term above) and diffusion. The diffusion component can be incorporated, if necessary, together with the thermal conduction.

Adding up the above, we obtain the MHD energy equation as

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{E}_{f}+\nabla\cdot\left[\left(\mathcal{E}_{f}+{\sf P}\right)\bm{u}\right]-\bm{E}\cdot\bm{J}=\nabla\cdot\left({\sf P}_{p\perp}\bm{u}-\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}\right)-W,

(54)

where we used
W=\bm{J}_{p}\cdot\bm{E}
and
\mathcal{E}_{f}=\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}+\mathcal{E}_{\text{fi}}
.
Using equation (
51
), the right-hand side can be rearranged into

W_{\text{gPIC}}=\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}+J_{p\|}E_{\|}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}+\nabla\cdot\left(\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}u_{\|}\bm{b}\right),

(55)

where
\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}
can be calculated based on
\mathcal{E}_{\text{fe}}/n_{\text{fe}}=\mathcal{E}_{f}/n_{f}
, i.e., MHD electrons share the same temperature as fluid ions.

III.1
Interpolation Variables

Distinct from the MHD-PIC approach, the variables characterizing each particle in our guiding center method are the magnetic moment
\mu_{k}
and the parallel momentum
P_{k\|}
along the magnetic field direction, rather than the full three-dimensional momentum.
To determine the particle velocity
\bm{V}_{k}=V_{k\|}\bm{b}+\bm{u}_{\perp}
, which is essential for updating particle positions, we must interpolate the magnetic field direction
\bm{b}
and the perpendicular gas velocity
\bm{u}_{\perp}
based on the MHD framework for each particle..
In cases where higher-order perpendicular drift velocity
\bm{v}_{\text{drift}}
is incorporated, additional computations are necessary for each particle, including
J_{\|}
,
\nabla B
,
\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}
and
\bm{\kappa}
(refer to equation (
9
)).
For the update of a particle’s parallel momentum, as dictated by equation (
1
), the required variables are
\bm{u}
,
\nabla_{\|}B
,
\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}
and
\bm{\kappa}
.
To streamline computational demands, we have identified a key relationship

\nabla\cdot(\bm{bb})=\bm{\kappa}-\nabla_{\|}\ln B.

(60)

This insight allows us to interpolate
\nabla\cdot\left(\bm{bb}\right)
and directly compute the parallel and perpendicular components of
\bm{b}
, thereby eliminating the need for interpolating
\bm{\kappa}
and
\nabla_{\|}B
.
3
3

3

The preference for
\nabla\cdot(\bm{bb})
over
\nabla\cdot(\bm{BB})
is to circumvent numerical inaccuracies that arise when interpolated
B
value is close to zero at the particle’s location.

In essence, if the first-order perpendicular drift is neglected, we need to interpolate
13
variables (
\bm{u}
,
\bm{B}
,
\nabla\cdot(\bm{bb})
,
E_{\|}
,
\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}
) for each particle at each step.
However, if higher-order terms are considered, the number of variables increases to
17
, necessitating the additional interpolation of
\nabla B
and
J_{\|}
.
These cell-centered variables are essential for calculations that are performed at the cell centers. For clarity and consistency in our notation, we will use
[i,j,k]
to denote the indices of the cells throughout the remainder of this document.

Numerically, the magnetic field
\bm{B}
can be accessed directly from the cell centers.
To mitigate numerical errors, we employ the following approach for interpolating the gas velocity component

u_{x}[i,j,k]=\frac{(\rho_{g}u_{x})[i-1/2,j,k]+(\rho_{g}u_{x})[i+1/2,j,k]}{2\rho_{g}[i,j,k]},

where
(\rho_{g}u_{x})
represents the face-centered gas flux obtained from Riemann solver.
We prefer this approach over using cell-centered
u_{x}
(similar for other components of
\bm{u}
) as we find it is more robust in handling of sharp velocity gradients.

4
4

4

For instance, in magnetic reconnection simulations, the gas velocity towards the center plane exhibits a sharp directional change near the center plane. Interpolating particle velocities from cell-centered
u
can lead to particles settling towards the center plane, eventually decoupling from the gas mass distribution. This approach prevents such inaccuracies by providing a more accurate representation of the velocity field.

For the calculation of spatial gradients, other variables necessitate a careful approach.
Specifically, for
\nabla\cdot(\bm{bb})
,
\nabla B
and
J_{\|}\equiv\bm{b}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{B}
, we rely on the face-centered magnetic field components.
The face-centered
\bm{B}
values are crucial for these calculations, as they provide a more accurate representation of the magnetic field variations within the computational cells.
For instance, in the
x
-direction, the face-centered
B_{x}
can be directly accessed from the MHD component.
However, for
B_{y}
and
B_{z}
, we need to compute the averaged values from the nearby faces to ensure a proper interpolation.
Specifically, the face-centered
B_{y}[i-1/2,j,k]
at the cell interface in the
x
-direction is calculated as

\left(B_{y}[i-1,j,k]+B_{y}[i,j,k]\right)/{2}.

This averaging process is similarly applied to
B_{z}
and in other directions, ensuring that both components are accurately represented at the cell interfaces.
For the remaining components of
E\|
as derived from equation (

III.2
Particle Integrator

Based on these cell-centered variables, we can obtain all the necessary variables for updating particle positions and velocities.
Within the primary computational loop, we employ the two-stage van Leer time integrator to push particles, achieving second-order temporal accuracy
(
J. M. Stone et al. 2020
)
. In each time step, we use superscripts
{}^{\text{ini}}
,
{}^{\text{mid}}
,
{}^{\text{fin}}
to denote variables at the beginning, midpoint, and end of the step, respectively.
We optimize the particle integrator by performing the interpolation process only once per time step.
This interpolation is based on the predicted midpoint positions of the particles, which effectively reduces the number of interpolation operations required.

In the
n
-th iteration loop, during the first stage, we determine the midpoint state by extrapolating from the results obtained in the preceding step.
This process involves predicting the particles’ midpoint positions without updating their momenta.
For particle
k
, the midpoint position
X_{k,n}^{\text{mid}}
is computed as follows

\bm{X}_{k,n}^{\text{mid}}=\bm{X}_{k,n}^{\text{ini}}+\bm{V}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n}\Delta t_{n}/2,

(61)

where
\bm{X}_{k,n}^{\text{ini}}\equiv\bm{X}_{k,n-1}^{\text{fin}}
denotes the initial position of the particle at the beginning of the time step,
\bm{V}_{k,n}^{\text{ini}}
is the initial velocity, and
\Delta t_{n}
represents the time step.
The initial velocity
\bm{V}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n}
at
t=0
is provided by the problem generator, and for subsequent steps, it is derived from the interpolated variables and the final velocity of the last step.
The magnetic field direction at the particle’s initial location can be approximated by

\bm{b}_{n}^{\text{ini}}\equiv\bm{b}^{\text{fin}}_{n-1}\approx\bm{b}_{n-1}^{\text{mid}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{b}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\Delta t_{n-1}}{2},

(62)

and the gas velocity is determined in a similar manner

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\perp,n}^{\text{ini}}\equiv\bm{u}^{\text{fin}}_{\perp,n-1}\approx\bm{u}_{\perp,n-1}^{\text{mid}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}^{\text{mid}}_{\perp,n-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\Delta t_{n-1}}{2}

(63)

\displaystyle=\bm{u}_{\perp,n-1}^{\text{mid}}+\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}\cdot\left[1-(\bm{bb})^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}\right]-\bm{u}_{n-1}^{\text{mid}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}(\bm{bb})^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\}\frac{\Delta t_{n-1}}{2}.

We further assume
\text{d}{\bm{b}}/\text{d}t\approx\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{\kappa}
and
\text{d}{\bm{u}}/\text{d}t\approx\Delta V_{k\|}\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}
in the above equations, as
\text{D}\bm{b}/\text{D}t
and
\text{D}\bm{u}/\text{D}t
are unknown at particle’s location.
Combining these with

\bm{V}_{k,n}^{\text{ini}}=v_{k\|,n}^{\text{ini}}\bm{b}_{n}^{\text{ini}}+\bm{u}_{\perp,n}^{\text{ini}},

(64)

and neglecting terms of
\mathscr{O}(\Delta t_{n-1}^{2})
, we derive

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n}=

\displaystyle\bm{V}^{*\text{fin}}_{k,n-1}+\Delta{V^{\text{mid}}_{k\|,n-1}}^{2}\bm{\kappa}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}\frac{\Delta t_{n-1}}{2}

(65)

\displaystyle+{\Delta V^{\text{mid}}_{k\|,n-1}\left(\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}|_{\perp}-\bm{u}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}\cdot\bm{\kappa}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}\bm{b}\right)}\frac{\Delta t_{n-1}}{2},

where
\bm{V}^{*\text{fin}}_{k,n-1}\equiv V^{\text{fin}}_{k,n-1}\bm{b}^{\text{mid}}_{n-1}+\bm{u}^{\text{mid}}_{\perp,n-1}
represents the particle velocity based on the final parallel momentum and midpoint interpolated variables from the previous step.

5
5

5

Here,
V^{\text{fin}}_{k,n-1}
is based on the Lorentz factor calculated from
P_{k,n-1}^{\text{fin}}
and
\bm{u}_{\perp,n-1}^{\text{mid}}
.
This approximation is valid when
|u|\ll\mathbb{C}
.

By incorporating the magnetic field line curvature, we enhance the precision of the prediction for
\bm{V}_{k,n}^{\text{ini}}
, under the assumption that the relative velocity between the particle and the gas does not undergo significant changes within a single time step.
Right afterwards, feedback from the gPIC component is calculated from equations (
3
) and (
4
),
where we must deposit gPIC quantities number density, parallel momentum, parallel and perpendicular pressure for each kind of particle.
We note that these quantities are deposited based on particle locations at
t^{\rm mid}
rather than
t^{\rm ini}
. This does not affect the order of accuracy for this stage (first order), while it allows us to recycle the particle weights in the next stage to reduce computational cost.

The second stage of the time integration proceeds over a full time step, leveraging the midpoint state from the first stage as the foundation for the update.
Utilizing the deposited particle number density and pressure from the initial stage, we determine the parallel electric field via equation (
30
).
This provides the last necessary variables to evolve the particles’ parallel momentum using equation (
15
).
We interpolate these required variables based on the cell-centered data obtained from the first part of this subsection at
t^{\text{mid}}
, using particle
k
’s midpoint location
\bm{X}_{k,n}^{\text{mid}}
.
Subsequently, we calculate
\Delta P_{k\|}
using equation (
15
).
With the parallel momentum
P^{\text{mid}}_{\|k,n}=P^{\text{ini}}_{\|k,n}+\Delta P_{k\|}/2
, equation (
8
) yields the midpoint velocity
\bm{V}^{\text{mid}}_{k,n}
.
The particle position is then updated according to:

\bm{X}^{\text{fin}}_{k,n}=\bm{X}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n}+\bm{V}^{\text{mid}}_{k,n}\Delta t_{n}.

(66)

Following this,
\bm{V}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n+1}
and
P^{\text{ini}}_{k,n+1}=P^{\text{ini}}_{\|k,n}+\Delta P_{k\|}
are updated in preparation for the next iteration loop.
Finally, we deposit
{\sf T}_{p\|}
,
{\sf P}_{p\perp}
, parallel momentum
p_{p\|}
for each particle species, and employ equations (
50
) and (
55
) to compute the feedback effects.

This two-stage integration process ensures a balance between computational efficiency and numerical accuracy, allowing for the precise simulation of particle dynamics within the evolving electromagnetic fields.
The careful interpolation and update of variables at each stage are crucial for maintaining the consistency and stability of the simulation, particularly when dealing with complex interactions between particles and the surrounding plasma.

III.3
MHD-gPIC Module in Athena++

In this section, we provide a detailed outline of the numerical procedures that are specific to the gPIC component within the
n
-th iteration loop.
Additionally, we introduce the numerical methods employed for calculating the feedback effects of the gPIC part on the overall system.
It should also be noted that the MHD-gPIC module, inherited from the MHD-PIC module, supports both static and adaptive mesh refinement, and we simply refer to
X. Sun & X.-N. Bai 2023

III.3.1
Stage 1. Prediction

In the first stage of van Leer time integrator, we aim to predict the midpoint state of the system.

•

Stage 1.1: Particle Advancement
After computing hydro fluxes and integrating magnetic field, advance all particles by half a time step,
\Delta t_{n}/2

\bm{V}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n}

\bm{X}^{\text{ini}}_{k,n}
to their midpoint positions
\bm{X}^{\text{mid}}_{k,n}

•

Stage 1.2: Weight Calculation
Calculate the particles’ weights over neighboring cells based on their midpoint positions
\bm{X}^{\text{mid}}_{k,n}
. This step is crucial for accurately distributing the particle properties onto the grid.

•

Stage 1.3: Deposition of Particle Properties
Deposit the parallel stress tensor
{\sf T}_{p,\|}

{\sf P}_{p,\perp}
, parallel momentum
p_{p,\|}

n_{p}
for each type of particle.
From these deposited terms, compute the mass ratio
R=n_{\text{pi}}/(n_{\text{pi}}+n_{\text{fi}})

E_{\|}
using equation (
30

Add the gPIC perpendicular pressure
{\sf P}_{p\perp}
to the MHD pressure before integrating the MHD part.

•

After integrating the MHD part, compute the particle feedback using equations (
50
) and (
55

Specially, for the momentum feedback
\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}

-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{g}\bm{uu}+{\sf P})+\bm{J}\times\bm{B}=\frac{\partial\rho_{g}\bm{u}}{\partial t}

has already been calculated in the Riemann solver of the MHD part.
Therefore, the momentum feedback is calculated by

\bm{F}_{\text{gPIC}}=R\left(\frac{\partial\rho_{g}\bm{u}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{g}\bm{uu})\right)_{\perp}+(1-R)\bm{F}_{p\perp}+\bm{F}_{p\|},

(67)

where
\nabla\cdot(\bm{bb})
,
\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}
and
E_{\|}
are calculated here to compute
\bm{F}_{p\perp}
and
\bm{F}_{p\|}
using equations (
46
) and (
47

6

If high-order perpendicular drift of particle is included in simulation,
\nabla B
and
J_{\|}

Add the momentum and energy source terms from the particles based on equation (

To avoid numerical problems, we apply the following limiter for
\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}
when computing energy feedback:

\Delta\bm{u}_{\text{fe}}=\left((n_{\text{pe}}-n_{\rm pi})u_{\|}\bm{b}+\frac{J_{p\|}\bm{b}}{e}\right)\Delta V/\max\left(n_{\text{fe}}\Delta V,\sqrt{n_{p}\Delta V}\right),

(68)

where
\Delta V
represents the cell volume in the simulation.
Since the calculation of

is subject to an error on the order of
\sqrt{{n}_{p}\Delta V}
due to particle number fluctuations in the gPIC module, the computed

can become zero in regions where particle electrons dominate, leading to numerical instability. To prevent this, the limiter imposes a lower bound on

equal to the magnitude of this fluctuation.
Although the problem setup should be designed to prevent such situations by employing a sufficiently large number of particles to accurately represent a specific portion of the plasma, the inclusion of this limiter serves as a safeguard against extreme scenarios that could arise due to numerical fluctuations or insufficient particle sampling.

•

Stage 1.5: Application of Boundary Conditions
Apply boundary conditions to both the MHD gas and particles.

In this stage, the deposition occurs after particles have moved half a step, allowing for the calculation of the particle number density and stress tensor at the midpoint.
This midpoint evaluation is also crucial for the accurate calculation of non-ideal electric fields, as these fields are subsequently used to update particle momentum during the second stage of the simulation.
Furthermore, since the feedback mechanism in the second stage also relies on the particle distribution at the midpoint, we can optimize the process by avoiding the redundant deposition of the particle number density.

In the second stage, the entire system is advanced from the initial state to the final state, leveraging the predicted midpoint state as a basis for this progression.

In this stage, the feedback calculation is based on the particle state at midpoint time, considering both position and momentum.

Nevertheless,

III.3.3
Riemann Solver

Our MHD equations use modified pressure:

\mathsf{P}
is used which includes contribution from particles
\mathsf{P}_{p\perp}
instead of
\mathsf{P}_{f}
.
On the other hand, the definition of energy density uses
\mathsf{P}_{f}
instead of
\mathsf{P}
, thus the system is not equivalent to the MHD equations in standard form.

The wave speeds in the Riemann solver are based on
\mathsf{P}
rather than
\mathsf{P}_{f}
because this provides more accurate results—particularly for isotropic particles and the electron gPIC case.
The advantage of this treatment is demonstrated in Section
IV.4
, where severe noise arises without it.

III.4
Performance Test

To assess the performance our MHD-gPIC module, we employ the circularly polarized Alfvén wave test (Section
IV.2
) with varying particle number per cell in one to three dimensions.
The code is compiled using the GNU compiler and executed on a single compute node equipped with two Intel Xeon E5-2690 v4 CPUs, providing a total of 28 cores. As the handling of MPI communication is identical as in
X. Sun & X.-N. Bai 2023
, we do not repeat the scaling test there.
Code performance is quantified as the time (in seconds) required for each cell update per time-step.
The findings from our MHD-gPIC, MHD-PIC, and pure MHD simulations are depicted in Figure
1
, with distinct line styles and colors used to differentiate between the methods.
Note that in the MHD-gPIC simulations, high-order perpendicular drift of particles is not incorporated.
By establishing the pure MHD simulation performance as a baseline, we are able to dissect the computational demands imposed by particle dynamics.

In scenarios with a low particle count, the gPIC module introduces a more significant computational overhead compared to the MHD-PIC module, approximately 70% of the MHD cost.
This is attributed to the necessity of calculating additional interpolated variables and the computation of momentum-energy feedback from the particle component based on deposited variables.
In contrast, the MHD-PIC module only requires interpolation of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{B}
, with direct use of deposited variables for feedback.
As the particle number increases,
individual particle updates take over to dominate the computational cost.

Our simulation data indicates that the additional computational cost per particle relative to MHD-PIC is approximately 10% for 1D simulations, 13% for 2D simulations, and 18% for 3D simulations.
Despite interpolating 13 variables per particle in the MHD-gPIC module (or 17 variables when including high-order perpendicular drift) during the second stage—compared to the 6 variables in the MHD-PIC module—the performance remains well within acceptable limits. This is particularly encouraging when considering the MHD-gPIC’s advantage of permitting significantly larger numerical time steps. We further rerun the tests including high-order perpendicular drift, which we find to increases the cost per particle by approximately 6% for each dimension compared to simulations without high-order perpendicular drift. The marginal increase in cost, combined with the enhanced accuracy and stability provided by the high-order terms, underscores the cost-effectiveness of our MHD-gPIC approach.

IV.2
Circularly Polarized Alfvén Wave

To verify the precision of the feedback from energetic particles on plasma dynamics, we carry out a series of numerical tests employing circularly polarized Alfvén waves (CPAW) that are modified by the presence of energetic particles, including the onset of the firehose instability
(
J. F. Drake et al. 2019
)
.
The MHD component is configured with a standard CPAW setup, featuring a constant background magnetic field with a minor perpendicular perturbation.
The presence of particles cab induce a pressure anisotropy within the plasma, which influences the Alfvén wave dispersion relation.
In a uniform magnetic field of strength
\bm{B}_{0}
, when the total parallel pressure
P_{\|}
and the total perpendicular pressure
P_{\perp}
of the plasma are unequal, the momentum equation becomes

\rho\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla\left({\sf P}_{\perp}+\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{8\pi}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left[\bm{bb}\left({\sf P}_{\perp}-{\sf P}_{\|}+\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{4\pi}\right)\right].

(70)

Considering perturbations that are perpendicular to the background field, the dispersion relation is given by

\omega^{2}\rho=k^{2}\left({\sf P}_{\perp}-{\sf P}_{\|}+\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{4\pi}\right),

(71)

where
\rho
represents the background plasma density.
If
{\sf P}_{\perp}-{\sf P}_{\|}+B_{0}^{2}/{4\pi}>0
, one obtain a modified CPAW wave,
while the wave will grow exponentially if
{\sf P}_{\perp}-{\sf P}_{\|}+B_{0}^{2}/{4\pi}<0
, a condition known as the firehose instability.
The resulting eigen-states under a uniform background consist of perpendicular magnetic field in circularly polarized configuration, with perpendicular velocity given by

\delta\bm{u}_{\perp}=-\frac{\omega}{k}\delta\bm{B}.

(72)

Note that the system is degenerate between left and right polarizations.

In our tests, the direction of the background magnetic field and the wave’s propagation direction are aligned along the
x
-axis in 1D simulations, and are oriented diagonally with respect to the grid in 2D and 3D scenarios.
The gPIC component is initialized with pressure anisotropy, characterized by different temperatures for the parallel momentum and magnetic moment. If the parallel pressure of electrons in the gPIC part exceeds their perpendicular pressure, the perpendicular temperature of the particle electrons is set to match that of the background gas, and the converse is true if the perpendicular pressure is greater.
The temperature of ions in the gPIC component is consistently initialized to be equivalent to that of the background gas.
Particles’ velocities are set up based on a Maxwell distribution with
v_{th,i}=c_{s}/\sqrt{2}
for ions and
v_{th,e\perp}=\sqrt{m_{i}/m_{e}}v_{th,i}
,
v_{th,e\perp}=\sqrt{m_{i}{\sf P}_{\text{pe}\|}/m_{e}{\sf P}_{\text{pe}\perp}}v_{th,i}
for electrons, where
c_{s}
is the sound speed.
The mass ratio
m_{i}/m_{e}
between ions and electrons is set at
25
in this test.
The computational units are defined as
\rho=B_{0}=2\pi/k=1
.
The simulation box size and resolution, specified as
(L_{x}/N_{x},L_{y}/N_{y},L_{z}/N_{z})
, are (1/256) for 1D, (1/256, 0.25/64) for 2D, and (1/256, 0.25/64, 0.25/64) for 3D.
Eigen-state perturbations are initialized with an amplitude of
\delta B=10^{-2}B_{0}
.
We employ
100
electrons per cell to represent
20\%
of the total electrons in each test.
Additionally, we introduce 100 ions per cell in a separate set of tests, which corresponds to
50\%
of the total ion population for tests with particle ions.

IV.5.2
Field Evolution and Particle Acceleration without Feedback

Based on the simulation results from setup (i), we investigate particle acceleration during reconnection by tracking representative particle trajectories.

Initially, resistive damping of the magnetic field in the current sheet leads to an imbalance of total pressure, establishing a gas flow towards the current sheet. As we do not apply Ohmic electric field to particles, there is minimum particle energy growth in this phase. Together with random velocity perturbations, rapid magnetic reconnection is triggered, leading to the formation of a series of small plasmoids (Figure
7
).
The left four panels of this figure show the averaged particle energy at different time
snapshots, while the rightmost panel displays the gas temperature distribution a
t=15
. As reconnection progresses, plasmoids continue to form and merge, converting magnetic energy into thermal energy — a process characteristic of fast plasmoid-mediated reconnection. In the meantime, particles gain energy significantly.

Particles are accelerated primarily through Fermi acceleration, a mechanism particularly effective during the contraction and merger of plasmoids.
In our current model, the magnetic moment of each particle is assumed to be constant, and the resulting acceleration is primarily directed along the magnetic field lines, as the field is weakened during reconnection. This can be seen in Figure
8
(b) and (c), showing that for the overall particle population at late stages,
the perpendicular energy distribution remains relatively unchanged, (primarily governed by the local magnetic field strength), while particle parallel energy experience significant gain.

The trajectories of two representative particles (① and ②) are plotted in Figure

(a)-(d), and the time

x

(a) and (d).
Before particles significantly depart from the reconnection layer, they drift freely along the magnetic field lines towards the current sheet (Figure
7
(a)).
When particles approach the formed plasmoids, those with insufficient initial parallel momentum may become trapped between two adjacent plasmoids due to the mirror force.
This trapping occurs because the magnetic field is compressed within the plasmoids and weakened in the regions between them (e.g., particle ① in Figure

If these plasmoids subsequently move closer together, the trapped particles can experience significant acceleration during this process (e.g., particle ① between

This results in a lower particle temperature at the plasmoid’s center compared to the surrounding regions.

IV.5.4
Comparison with
kglobal
Results

Our test results can be compared with those from the
kglobal
reconnection simulations by
H. Arnold et al. 2021
on electron acceleration and
Z. Yin et al. 2024
who further included ions. The results are consistent at qualitative level, including plasmoid formation, Fermi acceleration, and more significant ion acceleration compared to electrons, but quantitative differences emerge in spectral properties.

Relative to
kglobal
, our simulations yield lower maximum particle energies and a different spectral index.
We attribute these differences, at least in part, to numerical origins, as discussed in section
II.6
.
We speculate that particles in
kglobal
are not tightly confined within plasmoids and can therefore undergo further acceleration.
This arises likely because
kglobal
model does not well preserve Galilean invariance and includes particle spatial diffusion, which allow particles to drift away from confinement regions.
These numerical effects inadvertently promote particle acceleration by reducing particle trapping.

Such technical differences, besides the difference in the base MHD framework between the two models, preclude direct and fair comparison between our results with theirs. We therefore stress that the outcome of particle acceleration in magnetic reconnection simulations can be subject to numerical subtleties and should be treated with caution.

V
Summary and Discussion

This paper presents the MHD-gPIC module implemented in the
Athena++
code, combining magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) and particle-in-cell (PIC) methods for multi-scale plasma kinetic simulations.
The module treats a fraction of the plasma (hot electrons in this study) as particles using the guiding center approximation, while the rest of the plasma is modeled with MHD equations, with carefully derived source terms from particle backreaction. Compared to the original MHD-PIC formulation
(
X.-N. Bai et al. 2015
)
, the guiding center approximation substantially alleviates the issue of scale separation in applications where gyro-resonance is known to be sub-dominant. It is designed to enable multi-scale simulations by capturing particle effects at small scales while maintaining the large-scale features of MHD.

The main innovations in our formulation and algorithm include:
(1). The particle momentum equation incorporates a missing term in conventional guiding-center equations to ensure the integrator is Galilean invariant. This is critical for confining particles to moving plasmoids during magnetic reconnection.
(2). Our particle backreaction source terms are derived so that they permit a non-negligible fraction non-thermal particles, significantly broadening the model’s applicability.
(3). We augment fluid pressure to further include the perpendicular pressure of gPIC particles
{\sf P}_{p\perp}
. This approach leverages the Riemann solver’s dissipation which significantly reduces Poisson noise from particle pressure gradients.

The module, built upon the
Athena++
MHD-PIC infrastructure
(
X. Sun & X.-N. Bai 2023
)
, is compatible with static and adaptive mesh refinement (SMR/AMR) and fully supports load balancing in parallel computations. The overall time evolution is second-order accurate. Despite interpolating more variables per particle for interpolation and deposits, the computational overhead remains modest (approximately 10–18% per particle relative to MHD-PIC module).

We validate the accuracy and performance of the MHD-gPIC module with a series of benchmark tests.
These tests demonstrate that this module can accurately integrate particle trajectory and capture its momentum, energy and electromagnetic feedback with reduced Poisson noise. As a particular example, by initializing particles with a distribution function that mimics background thermal plasma, the coupled particle-gas system behaves exactly as pure gas with added particle density.

As a primary target application, we conducted three magnetic reconnection tests with increasing physical ingredients to study particle acceleration.
These simulations successfully demonstrated that particles are primarily accelerated via the Fermi mechanism during plasmoid formation and mergers. Significant energy gain was observed, particularly in the parallel direction, leading to a pronounced pressure anisotropy (Figure
8
). The simulation without particle feedback form an extended power-law energy spectrum. Incorporating particle feedback, particles can be accelerated to even higher energies, but the energy spectra no longer exhibit a coherent power-law
(Figure
9
). We also find that the inclusion of gPIC ions, despite their small initial number fraction, significantly influences the MHD environment through feedback, leading to enhanced turbulence and more elaborate plasmoid structures. The ions are also more significantly accelerated than electrons.

Our test simulations of magnetic reconnection confirm the robustness of our implementation of the MHD-gPIC altorighm, while the idealized setup point to areas that call for future improvements, primarily in the following two aspects.
First, our test simulations treat particles as pre-existing with a uniform Maxwellian distribution. In reality, non-thermal particles are expected to emerge only at
specific locations and events (e.g.,
O. French et al. 2025
for relativistic reconnection) through kinetic processes, and our formalism are designed to capture subsequent processes of acceleration. More realistic study should properly identify potential injection locations
(e.g.,
Y. Wang et al. 2024
), with a prescription for initially injected particle spectrum.
Second, the guiding-center approximation, which assumes perfect magnetic moment conservation, neglects gyro-scale wave-particle interactions which leads to pitch angle diffusion. Moreover, the coarse resolution also neglects potential particle spatial diffusion due to field line wandering at sub-grid scale. Both effects depend on sub-grid turbulence not known
a priori
, but may be modeled by prescriptions
(
J. R. Jokipii 1971
;
X. Kong et al. 2019
;
B. Chen et al. 2024
)
which potentially lead to more efficient particle acceleration.
Incorporating such improvements in a physics-driven manner would be crucial
bridge the connection between kinetic and macroscopic scales toward a true multiscale characterization of particle acceleration process during non-relativistic magnetic reconnection.

\pm i\omega

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}u^{\mu}=\delta_{\mu}b^{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu}u^{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu}b^{\mu}=0,

(A6)

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}\sigma_{\nu}+\delta_{\mu}\sigma_{\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+u_{\mu}u_{\nu}-\hat{b}_{\mu}\hat{b}_{\nu},

(A7)

the last equation can be found directly in MHD gas co-moving coordinate.
Therefore,
(u^{\mu},\hat{b}^{\mu},\delta^{\mu},\sigma^{\mu})
make up a set of basis of the Minkowski spacetime.

In this basis, the particle velocity can be decomposed as

v^{\mu}=\gamma_{0}u^{\mu}+v_{\|}\hat{b}^{\mu}+v_{\perp}e^{i\omega\tau+i\phi}\delta^{\mu}+v_{\perp}e^{-i\omega\tau-i\phi}\sigma^{\mu},

(A8)

where
\gamma_{0}
is the Lorentz factor in MHD gas co-moving coordinate,
v_{\|}
and
v_{\perp}
are corresponding parallel and perpendicular momentum,
\phi
is some constant phase.
Obviously,
\gamma_{0}^{2}=1+v_{\|}^{2}+2v_{\perp}^{2}
.
We define the guiding center position
X^{\mu}
from real position
x^{\mu}
by

X^{\mu}\equiv x^{\mu}+\frac{mF^{\mu\nu}v_{\nu}}{qb^{2}},

(A9)

which corresponds to equation (
7
).
The guiding center velocity is

\displaystyle V^{\mu}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}X^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=v^{\mu}+\frac{F^{\mu\nu}F_{\nu\lambda}}{b^{2}}v^{\lambda}+\frac{m}{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}F^{\mu\nu}/b^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{\nu}=\underbrace{\gamma_{0}u^{\mu}+v_{\|}\hat{b}^{\mu}}_{V_{(0)}^{\mu}}+\underbrace{\frac{m}{q}v_{\nu}v_{\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}\frac{F^{\mu\nu}}{b^{2}}}_{V_{(1)}^{\mu}},

(A10)

where we used equation (
A2
) and
\text{d}/\text{d}\tau\equiv v_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}
in above derivation and omitted the
\mathscr{O}(E_{\|}^{2}/b^{2})
terms.
By dropping non-ideal electric field in
F^{\mu\nu}
, averaging the high frequency term in
V^{\mu}_{(1)}
with

\displaystyle\left\langle v_{\nu}v_{\lambda}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{0}^{2}+v_{\perp}^{2}\right)u_{\nu}u_{\lambda}+\gamma_{0}v_{\|}(u_{\nu}\hat{b}_{\lambda}+\hat{b}_{\nu}u_{\lambda})+\left(v_{\|}^{2}-v_{\perp}^{2}\right)\hat{b}_{\nu}\hat{b}_{\lambda}+v_{\perp}^{2}g_{\nu\lambda},

(A11)

and using

\displaystyle u_{\nu}\nabla^{\lambda}\frac{F^{\mu\nu}}{b^{2}}=-\frac{F^{\mu\nu}}{b^{2}}\nabla^{\lambda}u^{\nu},

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\nu}\nabla^{\lambda}\frac{F^{\mu\nu}}{b^{2}}=-\frac{F^{\mu\nu}}{b^{2}}\nabla^{\lambda}\hat{b}^{\nu},

\displaystyle g_{\nu\lambda}\nabla^{\lambda}\frac{F^{\mu\nu}}{b^{2}}=-2F^{\mu\nu}\frac{\nabla_{\nu}b}{b^{3}}+\frac{J^{\mu}}{b^{2}}=-F^{\mu\nu}\frac{\nabla_{\nu}b}{b^{3}}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}\left(J_{\|}\hat{b}^{\mu}-F^{\mu\beta}\kappa_{\beta}+F^{\mu\nu}\frac{\mathrm{D}u_{\nu}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right),

where we used Maxwell’s equation
\nabla_{\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=J^{\mu}
, and
\kappa_{\beta}\equiv\hat{b}^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\hat{b}^{\beta}
is the magnetic field curvature,
the 1st-order GC velocity includes

V_{\text{(1)}}^{\mu}=\underbrace{V^{\mu}_{\text{Speiser}}}_{\frac{\mu_{0}J_{\|}\hat{b}^{\mu}}{qb}}+\underbrace{V^{\mu}_{\kappa}}_{\frac{mv_{\|}}{qb^{2}}F^{\nu\mu}\left(v_{\|}\kappa_{\nu}+\gamma_{0}\frac{\mathrm{D}\hat{b}_{\nu}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right)}+\underbrace{V^{\mu}_{\nabla b}}_{\frac{\mu_{0}}{qb^{2}}F^{\nu\mu}\nabla_{\nu}b}+\underbrace{V^{\mu}_{\text{else}}}_{\frac{F^{\nu\mu}}{\omega b}\gamma_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}t}},

(A12)

where we defined the magnetic moment
\mu_{0}\equiv mv_{\perp}^{2}/b
,
\text{D}/\text{D}t\equiv u^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}
and
\nabla_{\|}=\hat{b}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}
.
The first term is the Speiser motion velocity
(
T. W. Speiser 1965
)
, the second term is the curvature drift velocity and the third term is the gradient-
B
drift velocity.

The GC momentum equation can be found from equation (
A10
)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}V^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\left(v^{\mu}+\frac{F^{\mu\nu}F_{\nu\lambda}}{b^{2}}v^{\lambda}+\frac{m}{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}F^{\mu\nu}/b^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}v_{\nu}\right)=\frac{q}{m}F^{\mu\nu}V_{\nu}+\frac{\mathrm{d}F^{\mu\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{F_{\nu\lambda}v^{\lambda}}{b^{2}}+\xcancel{\frac{\mathrm{d}V^{\mu}_{(1)}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}},

(A13)

here we omit the last term of equation to get the
\omega^{0}
order terms, and gyro-averaged result of the second term is

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}F^{\mu\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{F_{\nu\lambda}v^{\lambda}}{b^{2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\langle v^{\sigma}v^{\lambda}\right\rangle}{b^{2}}F_{\nu\lambda}\nabla_{\sigma}F^{\mu\nu}=\frac{v_{\perp}^{2}}{b^{2}}F_{\nu\sigma}\nabla^{\sigma}F^{\mu\nu},

(A14)

where we used equation (
A11
).
Using Maxwell’s equation

\nabla^{\sigma}F^{\mu\nu}+\nabla^{\mu}F^{\nu\sigma}+\nabla^{\nu}F^{\sigma\mu}=0,

and

F_{\nu\sigma}\nabla^{\sigma}F^{\mu\nu}=F_{\nu\sigma}\nabla^{\nu}F^{\sigma\mu},

we can find

\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}F^{\mu\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\frac{F_{\nu\lambda}v^{\lambda}}{b^{2}}\right\rangle=-\frac{v_{\perp}^{2}}{2b^{2}}F_{\nu\sigma}\nabla^{\mu}F^{\nu\sigma}=-\frac{v_{\perp}^{2}}{b}\nabla^{\mu}b.

(A15)

The final momentum equation is

\frac{\mathrm{d}V^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{q}{m}F^{\mu\nu}V_{\nu}-\frac{\mu_{0}}{m}\nabla^{\mu}b.

(A16)

The mass energy equation for guiding center motion is

V^{\mu}V_{\mu}+\frac{2\mu_{0}b}{m}=-1,

(A17)

we can find
\mu_{0}
is a constant by combining the above two equations.

Based on equation (
A12
) and (
A16
), we can find the parallel momentem equation of guiding center as

\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{\|}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{\mathrm{d}V^{\mu}\hat{b}_{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=V^{\mu}\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{b}_{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}+\frac{q}{m}E_{\|}-\frac{\mu_{0}}{m}\nabla_{\|}b.

(A18)

Similarly, the energy equation is

\frac{\mathrm{d}V^{0}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{q}{m}E_{\|}v_{\|}+\frac{\mu_{0}}{m}\frac{\mathrm{D}b}{\mathrm{D}t}+v_{\|}u_{\mu}\left(v_{\|}\kappa^{\mu}+\gamma_{0}\frac{\mathrm{D}\hat{b}^{\mu}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right)+\gamma_{0}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}u^{0}}{\mathrm{d}t},

(A19)

where we assume
u^{0}\approx 1
in above equation.
The momentum energy equations are used in Section
II.1
.

Appendix B
GRMHD-gPIC model

Similar to Section
II.2
, fluid variables can also link to individual particles in GRMHD.
For each particle
k
, its worldline
x_{k}^{\mu}
is a function of its proper time
\tau_{k}
.
The fluid density
\rho(x^{\nu})
can be expressed by

\rho(x^{\nu})\equiv\sum_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x^{\nu}-x^{\nu}_{k}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\text{d}\tau_{k},

(B1)

where
|g|
is the determinant of the local metric
g^{\mu\nu}
.
Similarly, the fluid 4-velocity is

\rho u^{\mu}=p^{\mu}(x^{\nu})\equiv\sum_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x^{\nu}-x_{k}^{\nu}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{k}^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{k}}\text{d}\tau_{k},

(B2)

and the stress tensor is

{\sf T}^{\mu\nu}(x^{\lambda})\equiv\sum_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x^{\lambda}-x_{k}^{\lambda}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{k}^{\mu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{k}}\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{k}^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{k}}\text{d}\tau_{k}.

(B3)

It is easy to proof
p^{\mu}_{\ \ ;\mu}=0
and

{\sf T}^{\mu\nu}_{\hskip 8.19447pt;\mu}=\frac{q}{m}F^{\nu}_{\mu}p^{\mu}=\sum_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x-x_{k}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x_{k}^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{k}^{2}}\text{d}\tau_{k}.

(B4)

If we apply guiding center approximation to part of particles, the momentum-energy closure equation of the gPIC part is

{{\sf T}}^{\mu\nu}_{p\ ;\mu}=\sum_{k\in\text{gPIC}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x-x_{k}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x_{k}^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{i}^{2}}\text{d}\tau_{k},

(B5)

from equations (
A8
) and (
A9
)

\displaystyle x_{k}(\tau_{k})

\displaystyle=X_{k}(\tau_{k})+\delta x_{k}(\tau_{k})\approx X_{k}(\tau_{k})+i\rho_{i}e^{i\omega_{k}\tau_{k}+i\phi_{k}}\sigma-i\rho_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}\tau_{k}-i\phi_{k}}\delta,

(B6)

where the Larmor radius is
\rho_{k}\equiv q_{k}v_{i\perp}/m\omega_{k}
.
Using

\delta^{4}(x-x_{k}(\tau_{k}))=\delta^{4}(x-X_{k}(\tau_{k}))-\delta x_{k}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\delta^{4}(x-X_{k}(\tau_{k})),

(B7)

the result of energy-momentum equation of the gPIC part is

\displaystyle{\sf T}^{\mu\nu}_{p\ ;\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x-X_{k}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}X_{k}^{\nu}}{\mathrm{d}\tau_{k}^{2}}\text{d}\tau_{k}+\nabla_{\mu}\sum_{k}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\delta^{4}(x-X_{k}(\tau_{k}))}{\sqrt{|g|}}\rho_{k}^{2}\omega_{k}^{2}(\sigma^{\mu}\delta^{\nu}+\delta^{\mu}\sigma^{\nu})\text{d}\tau_{k}.

(B8)

In near-ideal MHD, with equation (
A7
), equation (
B8
) degenerates to equation (
38
) and (
40
).

Following the deduction in Section
II.5
, the total energy-momentum equation is

{\sf T}^{\mu\nu}_{\text{MHD};\mu}=\nabla^{\nu}{\sf T}_{p\perp}-{\sf T}_{p\ ;\mu}^{\mu\nu},

(B9)

where
{\sf T}_{\text{MHD}}={\sf T}_{f}+{{\sf T}}_{p\perp}+{\sf T}_{\text{EM}}
and
{\sf T}_{p\perp}\equiv{\sf T}^{\mu\nu}_{p}(\delta_{\mu}\sigma_{\nu}+\sigma_{\mu}\delta_{\nu})/2
is the perpendicular pressure of gPIC part.
The above equation together with Maxwell’s equations
(
C. F. Gammie et al. 2003
;
C. J. White et al. 2016
)
and GC equation (
A16
) forms the GRMHD-gPIC model.

Appendix C
Derivation of Selected Equations

C.1
Equation (
16
)

To derive the expression for the time derivative of
\bm{B}
, we begin with the fundamental equation governing the magnetic field’s evolution in ideal MHD

\partial_{t}\bm{B}=\nabla\times\left(\bm{u}\times\bm{B}\right)=-\bm{u}\cdot\nabla\bm{B}+B\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}-\bm{B}\nabla\cdot\bm{u},

(C1)

where the electric field
\bm{E}
is approximated by
\bm{E}\approx-\bm{u}\times\bm{B}
.
To isolate the effect of the fluid velocity
\bm{u}
on the magnetic field
\bm{B}
, we rearrange the terms by moving the first term on the right-hand side to the left-hand side and recall the material derivative
\text{D}/\text{D}t
, which accounts for the change as advected by the fluid.
This leads to

\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{B}}{\mathrm{D}t}=B\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}-\bm{B}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}.

(C2)

Further insight is gained by decomposing
\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{B}}{\mathrm{D}t}
into components parallel and perpendicular to the magnetic field direction
\bm{b}
.
This decomposition is achieved by expressing
\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{B}}{\mathrm{D}t}
as the sum of
\frac{\mathrm{D}B}{\mathrm{D}t}\bm{b}
and
B\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}
, resulting in

\frac{\mathrm{D}B}{\mathrm{D}t}=B\bm{b}\cdot\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}-B\nabla\cdot\bm{u},

(C3)

which describes the change in the magnitude of the magnetic field, and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}=(1-\bm{bb})\cdot\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}.

(C4)

Equation (
C4
) is particularly significant as it quantifies the change in the direction of the magnetic field unit vector
\bm{b}
.
This equation is instrumental in updating the particles’ parallel momentum, as it captures the effect of the fluid velocity gradient on the orientation of the magnetic field lines, which in turn influences the motion of charged particles along these lines.

C.2
Equation (
39
)

To calculate the bulk format of momentum feedback of the gPIC part, we start from decomposing gyro-averaged momentum
\bm{P}_{k}
in to parallel and perpendicular components with respect to the magnetic field, expressed as
\bm{P}_{k}=P_{k\|}\bm{b}+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}
. The time derivative of this momentum is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k\|}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{b}+P_{k\|}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\gamma}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{\perp}}{\mathrm{d}t}

(C5)

To account for the variations along the magnetic field line, we use the operator relation
\text{d}/\text{d}t=\text{D}/\text{D}t+\Delta V_{k\|}\nabla_{\|}
, where
\Delta V_{k\|}\equiv V_{k\|}-u_{\|}
represents the difference between the particle’s parallel velocity and the fluid velocity.
This leads to a more detailed expression

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k\|}}{\mathrm{d}t}\bm{b}+P_{k\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}+P_{k\|}\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{\kappa}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}_{\perp}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\Delta V_{k\|}\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}_{\perp}.

(C6)

To further simplify, we consider the perpendicular component of the fluid velocity derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}_{\perp}}{\mathrm{D}t}=\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}\cdot(1-\bm{bb})}{\mathrm{D}t}=\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg|_{\perp}-\bm{u}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bm{b}-u_{\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}

(C7)

and the gradient of the perpendicular velocity along the magnetic field line

\displaystyle\nabla_{\|}\bm{u}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\|}\left(\bm{u}\cdot(1-\bm{bb})\right)=\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}-\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\kappa}\bm{b}-u_{\|}\bm{\kappa},

(C8)

where equations (
C4
) and (
15
) are applied in deriving above equation. We obtain a comprehensive expression for the time derivative of the particle’s momentum

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\left(q_{k}E_{\|}-\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\nabla_{\|}B\right)\bm{b}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg|_{\perp}+\Delta V_{k\|}^{2}\bm{\kappa}+2\Delta V_{k\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}\right),

(C9)

where we have neglected the perpendicular drift and utilized the approximation

\displaystyle(\bm{V}_{k}-\bm{u})\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}\approx\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{b}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}=0.

(C10)

Summing over all particles and considering the distribution function, we derive the total momentum change within a given volume element

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{P}_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})

\displaystyle=\left(q_{p}E_{\|}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}\nabla_{\|}\ln B\right)\bm{b}+\xcancel{\frac{\bm{u}_{\perp}}{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{E}_{p}}{\mathrm{d}t}}+\rho_{p}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}\bigg|_{\perp}+{\sf T}_{p\|}\bm{\kappa}+2\rho_{p}\Delta u_{p\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t},

here, we have employed the definitions from section
II.2
, particularly noting that the parallel component of the stress tensor should be calculated in the gas co-moving coordinate system.
In our target application, the third term on the right-hand side is negligible due to the small magnitude of the perpendicular velocity relative to the speed of light,
|\bm{u}_{\perp}|\ll\mathbb{C}
.
By incorporating the above equation and the expression for the divergence of the perpendicular pressure tensor

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot({\sf P}_{p\perp}(1-\bm{bb}))=\nabla_{\perp}{\sf P}_{p\perp}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}\bm{\kappa}+{\sf P}_{p\perp}\bm{b}\nabla_{\|}\ln B,

(C11)

into equation (
38
), we derive the closed-form equation for momentum conservation, as presented in equation (
39
).

C.3
Equation (
41
)

To derive the energy closure for gPIC part, we start with the mass-energy equation of particle
k

\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{k}m_{k}\mathbb{C}\right)^{2}=m_{k}^{2}\mathbb{C}^{2}+P_{k\|}^{2}+\left(\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}\right)^{2}+2m_{k}\mu_{k}B,

(C12)

the time derivative of the particle’s energy
\varepsilon_{k}
provides insight into the energy exchange processes

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=V_{k\|}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{k\|}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}_{\perp}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\xcancel{m_{k}u_{\perp}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}}+\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t},

(C13)

where we still drop the
\text{d}\gamma_{k}/\text{d}t
term on the right-hand side since

m_{k}u_{\perp}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\ll m_{k}\mathbb{C}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}.

(C14)

Using the momentum equation for electrons (
15
), the first term on the right-hand side can be expanded as

\displaystyle V_{k\|}\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{P}_{k}\cdot\bm{\kappa}+V_{k\|}\underbrace{\bm{P}_{k}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}}_{\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}}-\frac{\mu_{k}}{\gamma_{k}}V_{k\|}\nabla_{\|}B+qE_{\|}V_{k\|}.

(C15)

The second term, involving the perpendicular velocity

C7

\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}_{\perp}+(V_{k\|}-2u_{\|})\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}-u_{\|}\Delta V_{k\|}\bm{\kappa}\right).

(C16)

Combining these terms, we arrive at the following expression for the energy change

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}=

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}m_{k}\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+2\Delta V_{k\|}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{b}}{\mathrm{D}t}+\Delta V_{k\|}^{2}\bm{\kappa}\right)+q_{k}E_{\|}V_{k\|}-\frac{\mu_{k}B}{\gamma_{k}}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}+\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{u}\right),

(C17)

where we have utilized the relationship

\displaystyle\nabla B\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}+\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}=-B\nabla\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}-B\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{u}\ .

(C18)

This leads to the collective energy change for all particles, expressed as

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon_{k}}{\mathrm{d}t}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{X}_{k})=

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\left(\rho_{p}\frac{\mathrm{D}\bm{u}}{\mathrm{D}t}+2\rho_{p}\Delta u_{p\|}+{\sf T}_{p\|}\bm{\kappa}\right)+E_{\|}J_{p\|}-{\sf P}_{p\perp}\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{u}_{\perp}+\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{u}\right).

(C19)

By combining this result with the energy closure equation (
40

41
).
