ABSTRACT

Renslo et al. (2024), Shaw et al. Introduction

Radiation-induced fibrosis (RIF) is a major determinant of long-term morbidity in head and neck cancer survivors, substantially affecting physical function, psychosocial well-being, and overall quality of life. [3], [15] The pathophysiology of fibrosis involves dysregulated cytokine and growth factor signaling (particularly transforming growth factor-beta [TGF-β], tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], and interleukins), fibroblast activation and transdifferentiation to myofibroblasts, progressive extracellular matrix accumulation, and chronic vascular injury. [1], [2], [16], [17] Clinically, cervical and soft-tissue fibrosis can severely limit range of motion of the neck and shoulder, impair speech and swallowing, complicate salvage surgery, cause chronic pain, and markedly reduce QOL, yet no widely accepted method exists to quantify RIF after head and neck radiotherapy. [4], [5], [6], [18] Clinically significant late neck and soft-tissue fibrosis is reported in approximately 30–50% of head and neck cancer survivors followed long-term, with rates varying by treatment modality, anatomic site, and assessment method. [1] At the molecular level, ionizing radiation triggers sustained activation of TGF-β signaling. The downstream functional impact extends beyond cosmesis: patients commonly experience cervical hypomobility, persistent shoulder dysfunction (compounded by spinal accessory nerve manipulation in surgical patients), trismus, dysphagia, secondary lymphedema, and chronic neuropathic pain—collectively driving disability, and increased downstream healthcare utilization. [2], [19]

By contrast, NTCP models for xerostomia and dysphagia are well developed and integrated into routine intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) planning to guide organ-sparing strategies. [12], [14], [20], [21] Recent systematic reviews emphasize that while fibrosis represents a significant late toxicity in head and neck cancer survivors, measurement and modeling of fibrosis remain inconsistent, with approaches ranging from clinical grading scales (e.g., RTOG/EORTC) to imaging-based metrics (ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging, shear-wave elastography) and biomechanical assessments, but none have achieved the clinical acceptance of NTCP models for other endpoints. [4], [6], [13], [22]

Standard EORTC instruments (QLQ-C30, QLQ-H&N43) capture global and site-specific QOL but devote limited items to neck stiffness and skin changes, resulting in underestimation of fibrosis-related burden. [9], [23], [24] The scleroderma-derived SSPRO was specifically designed to assess physical, emotional, and social consequences of chronic fibrotic skin disease, with validated domains measuring physical effects (e.g., skin thickening, stiffness), physical limitations (e.g., restricted movement), and emotional and social impacts. [7] Recent literature demonstrates that scleroderma skin scores (e.g., physician-assessed modified Rodnan Skin Score) often discordantly correlate with patient-reported fibrosis burden, highlighting the complementary value of PROs in capturing patient-relevant endpoints. [7], [25]

Despite this clinical burden, fibrosis remains the most under-modeled major late toxicity in head and neck radiotherapy, and existing dose constraints for neck soft tissues are largely heuristic rather than evidence-based. With more than 65,000 new head and neck cancer diagnoses annually in the United States and improving long-term survival driven in large part by HPV-related oropharyngeal disease, the prevalent survivor population at risk for chronic radiation-induced neck fibrosis is large and growing, underscoring the need for PRO-anchored dose–response models that can inform planning constraints, replanning thresholds, and future de-escalation trial design. [19]

This study evaluates SSPRO as a neck-fibrosis–specific PRO in head and neck cancer survivors, examines its relationship with standard EORTC QOL scales and physical examination findings, and develops LKB-based NTCP models for neck skin, subcutaneous tissue, and SCM using SSPRO-defined fibrosis as the endpoint. [8], [22] By anchoring NTCP models to patient-reported symptom severity and translating model-derived constraints into practical replanning strategies, the analysis aims to identify dose thresholds predictive of clinically meaningful fibrosis that can be incorporated into routine treatment planning for patients receiving either adjuvant radiation after surgery or definitive chemoradiation.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This IRB-approved, single-institution, prospective cross-sectional study enrolled 95 adults previously treated with definitive or adjuvant radiotherapy, with or without chemotherapy and/or surgery, for head and neck cancer and in remission at the time of assessment. Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years and able to complete PROs in English, Spanish; exclusions included active malignancy, prior traumatic cervical or shoulder pathology, pregnancy, and incarceration. Treatment was delivered per NCCN guideline recommendations: surgically treated patients received 60 Gy adjuvant radiotherapy for clean margins or 66 Gy with concurrent cisplatin for positive margins or extranodal extension, while patients treated definitively received 70 Gy with concurrent cisplatin. In the present cohort, 44 patients (46.3%) had surgery prior to radiotherapy and 67 (70.5%) received concurrent chemotherapy; among surgical patients, 22 of 44 (50.0%) received concurrent chemotherapy, consistent with NCCN-defined adverse-feature criteria. All surgical patients underwent modified radical neck dissection, in which the SCM is preserved, rather than classical radical neck dissection. All patients were treated with intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT or volumetric-modulated arc therapy) delivered in conventional fractionation (2 Gy per fraction, 5 fractions per week) with daily kilovoltage or cone-beam CT image guidance. Complete neck dosimetry was available for 90 patients, who formed the dosimetric/NTCP cohort; 5 additional patients contributed only to PRO correlations.

Patient-reported outcomes

All participants completed the EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N43, providing global and head-and-neck–specific QOL measures. [9], [24] [7].

The 22-item SSPRO questionnaire, which assesses physical effects, physical limitations, emotional effects, and social limitations, was administered with permission from the developers (Supplementary Fig. S1). Each item is scored from 0 to 6, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 132.

SSPRO total scores were categorized as mild (0−20), moderate (21–40), and severe (≥41) fibrosis-related symptom burden. These cutpoints were selected a priori based on three complementary considerations. First, they were informed by the original SSPRO validation study, in which progressively higher SSPRO scores corresponded with patient-reported mild, moderate, and severe skin disease, with our mild/moderate threshold aligning with the mean score for patients reporting mild disease. Second, the cutpoints reflected the distribution of scores in our cohort (median, 11; IQR, 3.5–30), corresponding approximately to the 62nd and 85th percentiles. This approach identified a clinically meaningful subgroup of patients with greater symptom burden while preserving adequate sample size for NTCP modeling. Because radiation-induced neck fibrosis is generally more localized than systemic sclerosis, the population in which SSPRO was originally developed, we selected a lower threshold for severe symptom burden than would be predicted from the original validation cohort. Finally, these categories demonstrated good agreement with an independent composite fibrosis physical examination score (described below), providing an independent clinician-assessed anchor supporting the selected thresholds.

Convergent validity analyses correlated SSPRO totals and domains with QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N43 totals and neck/skin-specific QLQ-H&N43 items.

Physical examination substudy

A subset of 46 patients underwent standardized physical medicine and rehabilitation (PM&R) assessment, capturing cervical range of motion, presence and grade of neck and shoulder girdle fibrosis, and related musculoskeletal findings. (Supplementary Fig. S2, which summarizes the PM&R fibrosis examination components and severity grading) A composite fibrosis physical exam (PE) score was derived by summing graded findings for neck and shoulder soft-tissue induration, atrophy, and contracture, and patients were grouped into ordered PE severity categories. Associations between SSPRO categories (mild, moderate, severe), individual SSPRO items, and PE scores/categories were assessed to evaluate the clinical validity of SSPRO as a neck fibrosis measure.

Dosimetric and imaging analysis

Planning CTs were used to contour novel neck OARs: skin (3-mm inner rind from the external body contour), subcutaneous tissue (1-cm layer beneath the skin, excluding skin and SCM), and SCM muscle from skull base to sternum/clavicle; the platysma was consistently included in the subcutaneous contour. The geometric definitions and representative contours are illustrated in Fig. 1. Cervical lymph node levels were delineated according to the updated international consensus guidelines of Grégoire et al., and the SCM was contoured as an organ at risk following the DAHANCA/EORTC/HKNPCSG/NCIC CTG/NCRI/NRG/TROG consensus OAR atlas. DVHs were generated in MIM, and extracted parameters included mean and maximum dose and selected V_x metrics across clinically relevant thresholds for each structure, with percentage volumes used for SCM. A comprehensive set of point and volumetric dose–volume parameters (hotspot doses, maximum and mean dose, and V50–V75 for SCM, skin, and subcutaneous tissue, including side-specific metrics) was extracted for NTCP modeling (Supplementary Table S1, which lists every DVH metric extracted for each OAR).

Statistical analysis and NTCP modeling

Pearson correlations quantified relationships among SSPRO, QLQ-C30, and QLQ-H&N43 scores. Spearman correlations and chi-square tests were used to assess associations between SSPRO totals/categories and composite fibrosis PE scores/categories. Dose–response associations between OAR metrics and PRO outcomes were evaluated using uni- and multivariable regression with false discovery rate control.

For NTCP modeling, SSPRO-based dichotomous endpoints (moderate/severe vs mild, i.e., total score ≥ 21 vs ≤20) were used to fit LKB models for each OAR, stratified by prior neck surgery. TD₅₀, m, and n were estimated using gEUD as the dose descriptor, and model discrimination was assessed with AUC and odds ratios at selected gEUD thresholds.

Results

Patient characteristics and PRO distribution

Ninety-five patients completed SSPRO and EORTC QOL instruments; 90 had complete dosimetry and formed the analysis cohort (Table 1). In this cohort, mean age was 60.6 years, 80.0% were male, 62.2% had oropharyngeal primaries, 88.9% received bilateral neck irradiation, 47.8% had prior surgery, and 68.9% received concurrent chemotherapy; median time from radiotherapy to SSPRO was 24.5 months (range 11–101). Using SSPRO categories defined as mild (0–20), moderate (21–40), and severe (≥41), 58 patients (64.4%) were mild, 18 (20.0%) moderate, and 14 (15.6%) severe in the dosimetric cohort, with a similar distribution in the full PRO cohort (N = 95). Multivariable linear regression modeling found younger age as the only factor correlating higher SSPRO scores (Coef = −0.63, p = 0.020). Ordinal regression modeling demonstrated surgical patients reported higher SSPRO scores (OR > 1, p = 0.035); thus, surgical status was utilized for patient stratification in further analysis (Fig. 2). Fig. 2 displays the distribution of SSPRO total scores by prior surgical status, illustrating the higher symptom burden in surgically treated patients that motivated stratified NTCP modeling.

Convergent validity

SSPRO total correlated moderately with QLQ-C30 total (r = 0.591; p < 0.001) and more strongly with QLQ-H&N43 total (r = 0.708; p < 0.001); QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N43 totals were themselves highly correlated (r = 0.778; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S2). SSPRO showed its highest item-level correlations with QLQ-H&N43 questions on neck stiffness, shoulder stiffness, skin changes, mouth opening, and skin sensitivity, indicating fibrosis-specific content not fully captured by generic QOL scales. QLQ-H&N43 subset questions were used to create categorical grouping of mild: 4, moderate: 5–6, and severe: 7 and above, based on score distributions and clinical intuition to distinguish patient-reported severity in relation to physical exam findings. In the PE substudy (n = 46), higher SSPRO totals correlated with higher composite fibrosis PE scores (Spearman r = 0.376; p = 0.01) while no correlation was observed between the H&N43_Skin and composite fibrosis PE scores (r = 0.091, p = 0.5493). SSPRO severity categories (mild, moderate, severe) showed a significant association with ordered PE severity categories based on the PE summary score (χ2 = 11.24, p = 0.024), supporting clinical validity of the SSPRO cutpoints. Similarly, no categorical association was observed between H&N43_Skin and PE severity scoring (χ2 = 1.129, p = 0.8897).

Dosimetric correlates of fibrosis PROs

In the 90-patient dosimetric cohort, higher mean dose and selected V_x values to skin, SCM, and subcutaneous tissue were positively associated with worsening SSPRO scores among patients without prior surgery, whereas QLQ-H&N43 showed weaker dose–response patterns. In surgically treated patients, no consistent relationships between these OARs and SSPRO outcomes were identified, suggesting surgery-related factors dominate fibrosis risk. Across the cohort, mean SCM dose was 57.5 Gy (IQR 55.3–60.3) and maximum SCM dose was 73.6 Gy (IQR 66.5–75.7); SCM V60 was 38.6% (IQR 28.2–49.7) and V70 was 2.3% (IQR 0.0–9.1). ROC analyses showed several DVH parameters for skin and subcutaneous tissue had acceptable AUCs, especially in non-surgical patients, with peak AUCs generally in the low-to-mid dose range for skin and at higher doses for SCM. The variation in AUC across dose levels for skin, SCM, and subcutaneous tissue (total, left, right), stratified by surgery status, is displayed in Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4, and the mean dose and V_x with highest AUC for each OAR, by surgery status and SSPRO-defined toxicity, are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

Based on the highest-performing dose–volume metrics from the ROC analyses, dose–volume constraints with corresponding odds ratios and p-values were derived to define clinically meaningful toxicity thresholds (Table 2a, which reports the candidate threshold, odds ratio, and p-value for each structure–dose pair stratified by surgical status). In surgically treated patients, optimal constraints were skin V₁₅ < 20% and subcutaneous tissue V₁₅ < 25%; in non-surgically treated patients, constraints were SCM V₇₀ < 25%, and subcutaneous tissue V₆₅ < 2%. These candidate dose–volume thresholds showed statistically significant associations (p ≤ 0.05) with reduced fibrosis risk in the non-surgical cohort and are presented as preliminary, hypothesis-generating constraints that informed the exploratory replanning analyses described below.

LKB NTCP modeling

LKB fits for skin, SCM, and subcutaneous tissue produced plausible TD₅₀, m and n values with clearly different NTCP curves for patients with and without prior surgery. In non-surgical patients, NTCP curves showed steep sigmoidal shapes with low predicted risk at low gEUD and rapid rise over mid-dose ranges, whereas curves in the surgery cohort were flatter and shifted toward lower apparent thresholds, consistent with higher baseline susceptibility. The best-fit parameters and 95% confidence intervals are presented in Table 2b and show marked separation in TD₅₀ for skin and subcutaneous tissue between surgery and non-surgery cohorts, while SCM exhibits higher TD₅₀ values overall.

Clinical feasibility of NTCP-based constraints

To assess the practical feasibility of applying NTCP-derived OAR constraints, representative patients with SSPRO-defined toxicity were selected from the surgical and non-surgical cohorts for replanning and constraint-driven optimization. For surgical patients, constraints derived from Table 3 and LKB modeling—skin V15 < 20% and subcutaneous tissue V15 < 25%—were applied while maintaining target coverage and preserving other critical OAR constraints (Table 4a). Compared with the original clinical plan, reoptimization in the surgical patient reduced NTCP for fibrosis-related SSPRO toxicity from 27.0% to 21.7% for skin and 19.4% to 17.0% for subcutaneous tissue, demonstrating modest but measurable risk reductions while meeting the new constraints.

For a definitive (non-surgical) patient, constraints of skin V5 < 35%, SCM V70 < 25%, and subcutaneous tissue V65 < 2%, together with gEUD thresholds of 30 Gy for skin and subcutaneous tissue and 60 Gy for SCM, were explored at different prescription doses (70 Gy clinical plan vs 56, 59.5, and 63 Gy reoptimized plans). In this scenario, predicted NTCP for SSPRO-defined fibrosis decreased from 27.9% in the 70 Gy clinical plan to 11.6–17.8% across the reoptimized plans for skin, from 70.2% to 8.0–15.1% for SCM, and from 41.6% to 10.6–19.4% for subcutaneous tissue, illustrating substantial relative NTCP reductions when the constraints were achieved or closely approached (Table 4a). Discussion

SSPRO, a scleroderma-derived instrument, demonstrated convergent validity with EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N43 while identifying a non-trivial subset of patients with moderate–severe fibrosis-related morbidity not fully captured by standard head and neck QOL scales. [7], [8], [9], [23] The updated SSPRO categorization into mild (0–20), moderate (21–40), and severe (≥41) symptom burden showed coherent relationships with both QOL scores and fibrosis-focused physical examination findings, supporting its use as a clinically meaningful neck fibrosis endpoint. Dose–response relationships in non-surgical patients between SSPRO outcomes and dose to skin, SCM, and subcutaneous tissue extend NTCP modeling beyond salivary glands and swallowing structures to neck soft tissues, advancing the field toward comprehensive PRO-anchored toxicity models. [10], [11], [12], [14], [22] It's notable that ∼25% of patients had at least moderate emotional distress from skin fibrosis after radiation when assessed with the SSPRO QOL instrument (Supplementary Fig. 5).

The separation of the LKB curves between the surgery and non-surgery cohorts suggests that prior neck surgery increases baseline fibrosis risk and modifies the relationship between radiation dose and late toxicity. The markedly lower TD₅₀ estimates for skin (8.4 Gy) and subcutaneous tissue (13.5 Gy) in surgically treated patients, compared with 39.0–40.2 Gy in definitively treated patients, are far below any biologically plausible threshold for radiation-induced fibrosis and should not be interpreted as true tissue tolerance doses. Rather, they likely reflect limitations of fitting the LKB model to a relatively dose-homogeneous cohort in which surgery contributes substantially to fibrosis independent of radiation dose. Neck dissection produces baseline fibrosis, tissue devascularization, lymphatic disruption, and chronic wound-healing responses that increase susceptibility to subsequent radiation injury. Because the LKB model cannot distinguish surgery-related fibrosis from radiation-induced fibrosis, these effects are incorporated into the dose-response relationship, resulting in an artificially reduced apparent TD₅₀. The narrow prescribed dose range and limited number of severe fibrosis events in the surgical cohort likely further contributed to model instability. The higher SCM TD₅₀ observed in surgical patients (81.8 vs. 70.9 Gy) is similarly most consistent with model variability rather than a true protective effect of surgery.

These findings have important implications for NTCP-guided treatment planning. Although NTCP-based optimization can reduce predicted toxicity, dose constraints derived from definitively treated patients should not be applied directly to postoperative patients, whose fibrosis risk reflects both surgical and radiation-related injury. Consistent with prior studies, postoperative patients may experience substantial late soft-tissue morbidity despite receiving lower prescribed doses (60–66 Gy), whereas definitive patients typically receive higher doses (70 Gy) and more concurrent cisplatin but develop a different spectrum of late toxicity. Future NTCP models should therefore be developed and validated separately for definitive and postoperative populations, with independent calibration of patient-reported outcome–based dose constraints for each treatment paradigm. [3], [13], [16], [17], [20], [22], [28], [29]

Recent systematic reviews of NTCP modeling in head and neck cancer document maturation of dose-response models for xerostomia and dysphagia but highlight the absence of equivalent models for soft-tissue fibrosis. [11], [13], [20], [21], [22] The present study represents a critical step toward filling this gap by using PRO endpoints anchored to clinically meaningful symptom severity and by translating model-derived thresholds into practical planning constraints for skin, SCM, and subcutaneous tissue. The exploratory replanning analyses suggest that applying the candidate constraints may reduce predicted SSPRO-defined fibrosis risk in both surgically treated and definitive patients while preserving target coverage, providing proof-of-principle that these thresholds are technically achievable within contemporary IMRT planning. We emphasize, however, that the dose–volume thresholds proposed here are candidate constraints derived from a single-institution cohort and have not been independently validated. Prospective external validation in an independent, multi-institutional cohort, ideally with standardized contouring, SSPRO administration, and pre-specified outcome definitions, is required before these constraints can be recommended as planning targets, and any prospective study should be appropriately powered to refine the constraint values and to test their generalizability across institutions, treatment platforms, and patient subgroups (in particular, surgically treated vs definitively treated patients).

Placed alongside the established HN NTCP literature, the present findings both echo and extend prior work. The mean-dose paradigm anchoring xerostomia NTCP in which incremental whole-organ mean dose to the parotid and submandibular glands monotonically predicts function loss [10], [12], [13] is mirrored here for neck skin, subcutaneous tissue, and SCM, where mean dose and clinically relevant V_x metrics correlate with worsening PRO scores in non-surgical patients, consistent with parallel-architecture late-reacting tissues. The dysphagia NTCP literature, which identified pharyngeal constrictor and supraglottic mean dose as principal determinants of swallowing dysfunction [11], [28], [30], similarly informs our framework: just as constrictor sparing translated into measurable PRO benefit, our candidate skin/SCM/subcutis constraints suggest analogous PRO-defined gains may be achievable for neck fibrosis. By contrast, fibrosis-specific quantitative work has been heterogeneous and largely descriptive ranging from shear-wave elastography [5] and ultrasound-based approaches to clinical grading scales [6], [18] and lacks the unified dose–response architecture available for xerostomia and dysphagia; the present LKB models begin to fill this gap with PRO-anchored endpoints. Finally, the recent emergence of PRO-anchored toxicity modeling more broadly [31] supports the principle that patient-reported endpoints can serve as primary NTCP outcomes rather than secondary descriptors, and SSPRO extends this paradigm to neck fibrosis by providing a fibrosis-specific instrument that captures patient-relevant symptoms not addressed by generic HN QOL scales. [32]

Potential applications of PRO-anchored NTCP models include: (1) incorporation of structure-specific dose constraints for neck skin, SCM, and subcutaneous tissue into routine IMRT planning, analogous to existing constraints for xerostomia and dysphagia; [10], [11], [12], [13], [14] (2) use of PRO-based models to compare proton therapy and photon radiotherapy and to rationalize proton selection for high-risk patients; [33] (3) personalized risk prediction at the time of treatment planning, enabling shared decision-making with patients regarding modality choice and dose-fractionation; [33], [34], [35] and (4) development of biomarker-driven risk stratification to identify patients requiring enhanced supportive care, preventive interventions (e.g., prophylactic physical therapy), or modified dose constraints. [3], [11], [13], [22] At our institution, the structure-specific constraints derived from these NTCP models (Table 4b) are being prospectively evaluated within ongoing IMRT planning quality-assurance review for surgically treated and definitive head and neck cancer patients, providing a candidate framework for fibrosis-sparing planning that requires further validation before broad clinical implementation.

Limitations include the single-institution design, the modest cohort size, the relatively small number of patients with severe fibrosis, and lack of external validation. Additionally, SSPRO was originally validated in systemic sclerosis populations; further validation studies are warranted to confirm its reliability and responsiveness as a neck-fibrosis endpoint in head and neck cancer survivors. [7] Future studies aim to prospectively validate the SSPRO survey. Validation in external cohorts and refinement of multivariable NTCP models incorporating clinical and biological covariates (age, chemotherapy exposure, genetic polymorphisms) and objective fibrosis measures (elastography, ultrasound, clinical grading) are needed before firm planning constraints can be recommended. [5], [35], [36] Dose and fractionation do not account for intrinsic sensitivity with individualized RIF and future work aims to further define intrinsic radiation sensitivity for creation of a more comprehensive model. [16], [17] Future prospective studies should also assess longer-term fibrosis progression and compare photon versus proton delivery within a PRO-anchored NTCP framework to fully define modality-specific benefits for neck soft-tissue sparing. [33], [34]

Conclusions

In this prospective single-institution cohort of head and neck cancer survivors, the scleroderma-derived SSPRO demonstrated convergent validity with EORTC QLQ-C30 and QLQ-H&N43 while capturing fibrosis-specific patient-reported burden not fully represented by generic QOL instruments, supporting its use as a clinically meaningful neck fibrosis endpoint. PRO-anchored LKB modeling identified dose–response relationships between mean dose and selected V_x metrics to neck skin, subcutaneous tissue, and SCM and worsening SSPRO scores in non-surgically treated patients, providing the first quantitative dose–response architecture for radiation-induced neck soft-tissue fibrosis comparable in form to established xerostomia and dysphagia NTCP models. In surgically treated patients—all of whom underwent modified radical neck dissection with SCM preservation—dose–response signal was attenuated, and the apparent surgical TD₅₀ estimates for skin and subcutaneous tissue are best interpreted as model artifacts of a surgically conditioned cohort rather than intrinsic radiobiological tolerance, identifying prior neck surgery as a key modifier in future PRO-anchored NTCP work. The candidate dose–volume thresholds proposed here should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require prospective external validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts with standardized contouring and harmonized PRO administration before they can be recommended as planning constraints; nonetheless, the present analyses establish a PRO-based framework for quantifying and ultimately reducing radiation-induced neck fibrosis in head and neck cancer survivors.
