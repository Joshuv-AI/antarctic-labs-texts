Implementation of a novel retrograde implantation technique for distal limb perfusion during extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation
Authors: Yalin Yildirim, Stefan Kluge, Hermann Reichenspurner, Alexander M Bernhardt, Simon Pecha, the ECMODPC study group
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

In recent decades, the use of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) has increased [1, 2]. In acute cardiogenic shock, VA-ECMO implantation can serve as a bridging solution to facilitate recovery, provide durable mechanical support, or enable heart transplantation [3, 4]. Additionally, VA-ECMO can be used during resuscitation and extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has been shown to be a viable therapy option in several studies (5). Lower limb ischemia is a well-recognized complication in patients treated with VA-ECMO. Placement of a distal perfusion catheter (DPC) may prevent this severe complication. However, implantation of percutaneous DPC can be highly challenging in emergency situations or even under CPR. Here we present a novel retrograde DPC implantation technique for distal limb perfusion in patients with VA-ECMO.

Between 2019 and June 2024, 14 patients received percutaneous femoral VA-ECMO implantation with retrograde distal perfusion catheter during CPR (Fig. 1A–C). All patients received VA-ECMO implantation without the possibility of fluoroscopic guided implantation. The implantation was carried out in two different scenarios. In scenario one, the femoral artery was punctured a guidewire was placed. Subsequently, a second retrograde puncture of the common- or superficial femoral artery was performed 2 cm to 5 cm distally and also secured with a guide wire. In most cases, the second puncture could be performed easily, since angle and depth of the artery are known from the first puncture. In Scenario 2, the DPC implantation was performed after VA-ECMO installation. In this scenario a second retrograde arterial puncture, was performed 2–5 cm distally from the initial arterial puncture. However, this can also be challenging due to the obstruction of the femoral artery by the ECMO cannula. In those cases, alternatively, the second arterial puncture can be performed 10–15 cm distally from the initial puncture site in retrograde direction using ultrasound guidance.

In all patients a 7-F retrograde distal perfusion cannula was implanted. The installation of the arterial ECMO cannula was conducted as previously described with cannula sizes ranging between 15F and 19F. Accurate lower extremity perfusion was documented by ultrasound and clinical examination. Mean duration of ECMO support was 5.1 ± 3.7 days. ECMO weaning and explantation was feasible in 8 patients. Overall, in-hospital survival rate was 42.8%. 4 patients underwent surgical ECMO explantation while interventional fluoroscopy guided explantation was performed in 4 patients (Fig. 1D–F). During ECMO support and after decannulation no patient had signs of limb ischemia or needed further vascular interventions. When using the interventional explantation approach, the retrograde DPC cannula also carries certain advantages. When closing the arterial cannulation site, the DPC cannula can be used for angiography, confirming sufficient arterial closure without any leakage, stenosis or dissection (Fig. 1G, H). Furthermore, in cases of closure device failure, the retrograde distal arterial access allows for fast and easy over the wire access to treat any kind of bleeding by temporal blocking with a peripheral percutaneous transluminal angioplasty balloon to reduce blood loss until further open surgical- or interventional therapy.

In conclusion, this new technique facilitates a faster and easier DPC implantation in emergency situations. Thereby, preventing ischemic complications of the lower limb and also allowing for angiography and interventional treatment during ECMO explantation.
