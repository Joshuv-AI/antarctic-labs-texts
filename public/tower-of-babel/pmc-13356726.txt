A first-in-human study evaluating safety, PK, and PD of a novel universal anticoagulant reversal agent, OKL-1111
Authors: Edwin Spaans, Denisa Wilkes, Maxime Schreurs, Julien Beyrath, Jules A A C Heuberger, Alex Zwiers, Hash Patel, Kamran Bakhtiari, Joost C M Meijers, Stephan L M Peters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•OKL-1111 is a novel universal reversal agent for anticoagulants and platelet inhibitors with favorable PK and safety profile.•PD analyses of several procoagulant and fibrinolytic parameters indicate OKL-1111 as potentially efficacious drug for anticoagulant reversal.

Introduction

The use of anticoagulant (AC) and platelet inhibitor (PI) therapies is a cornerstone of the prevention and treatment of thromboembolism.1,2 However, in the clinical setting, ACs and PIs are associated with an increased risk of bleeding, which may progress to intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).1,3,4

ICH is the second most common form of stroke and affects ∼3 million people worldwide each year; the number increases to over 4 million when cases of subarachnoid hemorrhage are taken into account.5 With a reported mortality rate of 20% to 60% and ∼50% of disability related to all forms of stroke, it poses a serious unmet medical need.6,7 ICH may result from ≥1 factors, including high blood pressure, vascular abnormalities, mild to severe trauma, and the use of ACs and PIs.8 Regardless of the cause of ICH, AC/PI use has been shown clinically to increase both hematoma volume and mortality risk in patients with ICH.3,4,9 Accordingly, early reversal of AC or PI action may reduce mortality and disability in patients with ICH.

Currently, several reversal agents are available for different ACs used in clinical practice.10, 11, 12 However, these agents are selective for a specific AC or group of ACs, and no universal reversal agent is currently available. No reversal agents are currently approved for PIs. Accordingly, in the event of major bleeding, the culprit needs to be identified before action can be taken. In addition, current reversal agents require reconstitution, and these actions take valuable time; as such, door-to-needle time may be as long as 1.5 to 2.5 hours.13,14 Although these selective reversal agents are powerful enough to reverse AC action, in clinical practice, they are often given too late to be effective. In addition, some agents, such as prothrombin complex concentrate and andexanet, carry a thrombotic risk,10,12 which led to withdrawal of the latter product in the United States in 2025.

OKL-1111 is a small-molecule, derivatized γ-cyclodextrin that augments and restores coagulation potential in the presence of an AC or PI, without initiating coagulation. Although studies to fully elucidate its mode of action are ongoing, we have demonstrated that OKL-1111 does not physically interact with ACs or PIs but accelerates coagulation when initiated by an appropriate trigger (tissue factor), thereby restoring bleeding time in vivo.15 In vitro studies indicate that OKL-1111 binds to prothrombin and thrombin, increases Xa-mediated thrombin generation, prevents thrombomodulin binding and activation, and increases factor XI (FXI) activation by thrombin. In vivo, reversal of AC action was shown for dabigatran; the FXa inhibitors apixaban, rivaroxaban, and edoxaban; the vitamin K antagonist warfarin; the low-molecular-weight heparin enoxaparin; the pentasaccharide fondaparinux; and the PIs clopidogrel and ticagrelor.15 In these preclinical studies, OKL-1111 at a dose of 20 mg/kg resulted in an >80% lower bleeding time in a rat tail-cut bleeding model, and no signs of prothrombotic activity were observed.15 Combined with a very fast onset of action (<5 minutes) and a ready-to-use solution, OKL-1111 may substantially reduce door-to-needle time in ICH and thereby reduce mortality and disability.

This randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, single-ascending-dose, first-in-human study evaluated the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics (PK), and pharmacodynamics (PD) of OKL-1111 after IV doses administered alone or after the AC dabigatran etexilate (a direct oral AC [DOAC]) in healthy male volunteers.

Development of OKL-1111 was discontinued after completion of this first-in-human study because of safety findings in subsequent repeat-dose animal toxicology studies.

Methods

Study design and treatments

This was a 2-part, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, alternating-group, partial crossover study to assess the safety, tolerability, PK, and PD of OKL-1111 in healthy men.

Part A

Two groups of 6 volunteers each (groups A1 and A2) were enrolled. Each volunteer had 3 study sessions in which each one received a single ascending IV bolus dose of OKL-1111 or placebo in the fasted state. Each volunteer received 1 dose of placebo and 2 doses of OKL-1111 during the trial. Volunteers were randomized to 1 of 3 treatment sequences, such that in each session, 4 volunteers received OKL-1111 and 2 received placebo (Table 1). The groups were dosed alternately (Figure 1).

Part B

Two groups of 6 volunteers each (groups B1 and B2) were enrolled (Figure 1). Each volunteer had 2 study sessions in which each one received a single oral 220 mg dose of dabigatran etexilate in the fed state, followed 1 hour later by a single IV bolus dose of OKL-1111 or placebo. In each study session, 4 volunteers received OKL-1111 and 2 volunteers received placebo.

OKL-1111 was administered as an IV bolus through a cannula inserted into a vein in the arm not used for blood sampling. Placebo was a matching volume of sodium chloride 0.9% solution, and syringes were covered with amber tape to maintain blinding.

In part B, 220-mg dabigatran etexilate tablets (Pradaxa), administered orally after a standard breakfast with 240 mL of water, were used as the challenge agent. Dabigatran was chosen on the basis of its relatively large effect on lag time, which is the main parameter reversed by OKL-1111 in preclinical studies.15

Study volunteers

Male volunteers aged 18 to 50 years, with a body mass index of 18.0 to 32.0 kg/m2, who were deemed healthy on the basis of clinical history, physical examination, electrocardiogram, vital signs, and laboratory tests of blood, urine, and feces were eligible for inclusion; further details are provided in the supplemental Methods.

After providing informed consent, volunteers were screened within 28 days before their first dose of trial medication. In each treatment session, volunteers resided on the ward from the day before dosing (day −1) until completion of procedures 24 hours after dosing (day 2). A follow-up visit was scheduled ∼9 days (±2 days) after the final dose (days 8-12).

Safety and tolerability

Safety and tolerability were assessed 24 hours after dose administration and again 9 days (±2 days) after the last dose using standard methods, as described in the supplemental Materials.

Coagulation assessments included plasma levels of thrombin-antithrombin complexes (TAT) and the fibrin split product D-dimer, as well as activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and prothrombin time (PT), measured in seconds. Urinalysis included the urinary markers of renal tissue injury vanin-1,16 neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL),17,18 and N-acetyl-β-d-glucosaminidase17,18 in part A only, and kidney injury molecule 117, 18, 19 in both part A and part B. In all cases, renal marker results were corrected for creatinine levels. These markers were included because preclinical findings indicated the potential for mild, reversible renal tissue damage.

PK

Plasma

Blood samples for quantification of OKL-1111 were collected before and at frequent time points up to 24 hours after each dose of study treatment. Blood samples for quantification of dabigatran etexilate were collected before and up to 25 hours after each dose of dabigatran etexilate (part B only).

Urine

Urine was collected at different time points up to 24 hours after dosing for quantification of OKL-1111 (part A only). Total urine output for PK assay of OKL-1111 was collected in preweighed polypropylene containers.

PK analysis

Plasma and urinary PK concentration-time profiles were assessed, and the following PK parameters were derived using noncompartmental analysis: maximum observed concentration (Cmax), time to maximum observed concentration, area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) from time 0 to the last measurable concentration (AUClast), AUC extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), AUC from 0 to 12 hours, AUC from 0 to 24 hours, percentage of AUC extrapolated from the last measurable concentration to infinity, Terminal elimination rate constant, terminal elimination half-life (t½), clearance (CL), apparent volume of distribution during the terminal phase, and mean residence time extrapolated to infinity in plasma, and amount excreted in urine over 24 hours and renal clearance in urine.

PD

For PD assessment, coagulation markers were measured in citrated plasma using the calibrated automated thrombogram (CAT) assay. In part A samples, dabigatran (150 ng/mL) or apixaban (150 ng/mL) was supplemented ex vivo to plasma and introduced to the CAT assay.

Additional assays included the ecarin chromogenic assay (STA-ECA-II),20 ecarin clotting time (ECT),21 and direct thrombin inhibitor (DTI)22 assay. aPTT and PT were also measured. In addition, coagulant and fibrinolytic status was measured using a Thromboquest GTT3 device, including occlusion time, thrombus stability, and lysis time.

Statistical analyses

Because of the exploratory nature of the study, no calculation of sample size was performed, and the selected sample size was in line with common practice for first-in-human studies. Statistical analysis of CAT assay parameters was performed using a mixed-model analysis of covariance, and estimated differences (ED) between placebo and treatment were reported; statistical significance was defined at the 5% level.

Results

Volunteer disposition

Sixteen male volunteers entered part A, and 12 completed the study. Four volunteers were withdrawn from the study for different reasons: (suspected) infection, a dosing error, or veins unsuitable for venipuncture. Because the dosing error was considered a major protocol deviation, that volunteer was excluded from all analysis populations.

Fourteen male volunteers entered part B, and 12 completed the study. Two volunteers were withdrawn from the study because of an adverse event (AE) and nonattendance for a scheduled visit.

Fifteen volunteers in part A and 14 in part B were included in the safety and PD populations for all study sessions. Fourteen volunteers in part A and 13 in part B were included in the PK concentration and parameter populations for the study sessions in which they received OKL-1111.

Dose levels tested

Because of elevated D-dimer results recorded in group A1, session 1, the safety review group decided to repeat the 10 mg OKL-1111 dose (dose 1) in group A2. Subsequent dose levels followed the planned dose-escalation schedule.

In part B, 4 dose levels were initially planned to be administered. However, on the basis of data from part A, the safety review group decided to administer the same 2 doses in both groups B1 and B2.

Safety and tolerability

Overall, OKL-1111 was well tolerated across all dose levels in both parts A and B, with no serious or severe treatment-emergent AEs. Two volunteers were withdrawn because of AEs, both of which were considered unlikely to be treatment related. These 2 volunteers had AEs that were considered “otherwise significant” and were withdrawn from the study; 1 volunteer in part A was diagnosed with a mild COVID-19 infection, and 1 in part B had moderately increased fibrin D-dimer 27 days after 220 mg dabigatran coadministered with placebo (session 1), lasting for almost 32 days. Both AEs were considered not to be related to treatment by the investigator. Table 4 shows the distribution of treatment-emergent AE incidence depending on OKL-1111 dose for both parts A and B.

For coagulation parameters PT, aPTT, international normalized ratio, and TAT, there were no clear OKL-1111 dose-related changes or differences from placebo, although these parameters showed high between-volunteer variability. However, 5 volunteers in part A had aPTT values that decreased by more than the predefined 8 seconds compared to baseline, and 11 volunteers in part B had values that increased by more than the predefined 8 seconds compared to baseline after dabigatran coadministered with OKL-1111 or placebo. This led to further investigation and, finally, to the conclusion that this was not a safety concern.

There were no conclusive OKL-1111–related D-dimer changes from baseline in either part A or part B. In some cases, increased D-dimer values were recorded; however, the investigator concluded that several instances of high D-dimer values recorded early in the trial were likely the result of the blood sampling technique used.

Renal biomarkers

In part A, NGAL and ratio of NGAL to creatinine showed an increase from baseline after treatment with OKL-1111 or placebo; the effect of OKL-1111 at 10 and 30 mg was very similar to placebo (up to an increase of 0.13), whereas it was more pronounced at doses of 100 to 1000 mg (up to an increase of 0.84). The maximum mean change in the ratio of NGAL to creatinine was recorded at 1 hour after administration of placebo and 30 to 1000 mg OKL-1111 and at 4 hours after administration of 10 mg OKL-1111.

PK

In both study parts, parameters of OKL-1111 plasma concentration increased with dose, and mean AUC from 0 to 12 hours and AUC from 0 to 24 hours were similar to the corresponding AUCinf and AUClast, as expected for a drug largely eliminated within 24 hours of dosing. Between-volunteer variability in Cmax and AUC was low in both study parts.

Plasma concentration–time profiles (Figure 2) suggested biphasic elimination of OKL-1111. Across dose levels, except in 1 volunteer in part A and 3 volunteers in part B, OKL-1111 was eliminated by 24 hours after treatment with 1000 mg OKL-1111. Accordingly, the arithmetic mean t½ was 3.44 to 4.13 hours after doses of 100 to 1000 mg in both parts A and B (although somewhat lower [1.85 hours] after 30 mg in part A), and the individual volunteer t½ ranged from 1.17 to 5.16 hours overall. PD

In part A, CAT assay results measured in samples taken before OKL-1111 administration showed that ex vivo challenge with the DOACs dabigatran or apixaban seemed to decrease endogenous thrombin potential (ETP) and peak thrombin and to increase lag time and time to peak. Velocity index seemed to increase with dabigatran and decrease with apixaban.

Without an ex vivo challenge, after a single IV dose of OKL-1111, a decrease was observed for 1000 mg OKL-1111 on ETP (ED, −92.54 nM·min [95% confidence interval (CI), −151.6 to −33.5]; P = .002) and time to peak (ED, −0.52 minutes [95% CI, − 0.96 to −0.07]; P = .03) parameters in the CAT assay compared to placebo. The opposite effect was seen for ETP at the lowest dose, 10 mg OKL-1111 (ED, 73.62 nM·min [95% CI, 128.4-2.7]; P = .009). An increase in velocity index was observed at 100 mg (ED, 11.0 nM/min [95% CI, 2.7-19.4]; P = .01).

After ex vivo challenge with the DOAC dabigatran, the 10 mg OKL-1111 dose showed a similar increase in ETP compared to placebo, as seen in those without challenge (ED, 50.6 nM·min [95% CI, 89.5-2.6]; P = .01), but no effects of higher doses of OKL-1111 were observed in any of the parameters. After ex vivo challenge with the DOAC apixaban, an increase in peak thrombin for 100 mg OKL-1111 (ED, 17.6 nM [95% CI, 31.4-2.7]; P = .02) and a decrease in time to peak for 1000 mg OKL-1111 (ED, −1.9 minutes [95% CI, −3.9 to 0.01]; P = .049) were observed. Moreover, a dose-related decrease in lag time was observed for 300 mg (ED, −0.3 minutes [95% CI, ˗0.6 to ˗0.05]; P = .02) and 1000 mg (ED, −0.6 minutes [95% CI, − 0.9 to −0.4]; P ≥ .0001) OKL-1111 (Figure 3). In part B, apparent effects of dabigatran were observed on CAT (Table 7), DTI, ECA-II, and ECT, with a maximum response recorded 3 to 4 hours after dose administration. There was an apparent partial reversal of the dabigatran effect by 1000 mg OKL-1111 on several CAT parameters, although statistical analysis did not show significant effects of OKL-1111 on any of the markers at either 500 or 1000 mg. Thromboquest analysis showed that OKL-1111 could partially reverse the dabigatran-induced increase in occlusion time, although an effect on the other parameters could not be established. No effect of OKL-1111 on DTI, ECA-II, or ECT was observed. Discussion

In this study, we administered OKL-1111, a novel universal AC reversal agent, for the first time to healthy male volunteers. Single IV doses of OKL-1111 were well tolerated up to the highest dose tested, 1000 mg. Moreover, OKL-1111 also showed favorable PK, with a short time to maximum observed concentration, a relatively short half-life of ∼3 to 4 hours, and low intervolunteer variability in PK parameters. Finally, there was indication of reversal activity, as changes in the CAT assay were observed at the 1000 mg dose, including dose-dependent blunting of the effect of ex vivo apixaban on lag time, supporting further development of OKL-1111 for the treatment of ICH.

Overall, OKL-1111 was safe and well tolerated in this study. There was no clear relationship between drug-related AE incidence and OKL-1111 dose in either study part, and no conclusive differences from placebo were observed. In addition, dabigatran coadministration in part B did not have an effect on drug-related AE incidence, indicating no safety interaction between the 2 compounds. The most common drug-related AEs were injection site pain (26.7% of volunteers in part A) and contusion (14.3% of volunteers in part B). These events were likely related to the route of administration rather than to the investigational compound, as supported by the fact that these also occurred in volunteers receiving placebo. Related to this, mild local tolerability findings, including tenderness and erythema, were recorded in 4 volunteers in part A and 1 volunteer in part B. Each was recorded as a drug-related AE and may be considered expected owing to the method of dose administration.

Given OKL-1111’s intended function as a universal AC reversal agent, coagulation parameters were closely monitored during the study in the presence and absence of dabigatran. In part A, in which OKL-1111 was administered alone, CAT assay findings, with and without an ex vivo DOAC challenge, as well as Thromboquest findings, suggest that OKL-1111 alone does not have an effect on coagulation. No indications of abnormal procoagulant markers (eg, TAT and D-dimer) were identified, which is consistent with previous preclinical findings.15 The effects recorded in part A, in which aPTT decreased by >8 seconds from baseline in several volunteers, were not considered clinically significant. Importantly, these changes did not show a relationship with dose and were unlikely to be treatment related. The findings in part B, in which aPTT increased by more than 8 seconds from baseline, were expected as an effect of dabigatran. However, these elevated aPTT values were observed in volunteers treated with either OKL-1111 or placebo, suggesting that OKL-1111 could not entirely overcome the AC activity of dabigatran as measured by aPTT, which is in line with our preclinical data.15 The increased D-dimer levels that led to the decision to repeat the 10 mg dose in group A2, session 1, were attributed to the blood sampling technique used. Overall, no coagulation-related safety concerns were observed with OKL-1111.

Adverse kidney findings were observed in rats in a non–Good Laboratory Practice study at doses of 500 mg/kg. As such, this study assessed potential renal tissue injury by urinalysis. A transient, dose-related increase in NGAL corrected for creatinine was observed after single OKL-1111 doses of 100 to 1000 mg. Decreases in the ratio of kidney injury molecule 1 to creatinine were also observed, but the maximum change observed after OKL-1111 was similar to that after placebo, so this finding should be interpreted with caution. Other parameters, including the ratio of N-acetyl-β-d-glucosaminidase to creatinine and vanin-1, were very low and could not be evaluated. Overall, the effects observed after OKL-1111 dosing were not consistent across all markers and were variable and modest; therefore, they cannot be considered indicative of renal injury, although the potential effect of doses from 100 mg onward observed on NGAL may indicate early signs of tubular involvement. However, because these effects seemed reversible, the single doses of OKL-1111 used in this study were not considered harmful to renal function.

PK analysis showed that OKL-1111 plasma levels peaked within 0.25 hours after dosing, as expected for a drug administered by IV bolus. Cmax and AUC increased with dose in a generally dose-proportional manner. However, increases were supraproportional at lower doses (10-100 mg), likely owing to the limited number of samples above the limit of quantification at the lowest dose tested. Similarly, elimination half-life was similar across the higher dose levels (100-1000 mg) and was probably underpredicted in the 30 mg group because concentrations fell below the limit of quantification in that group. Overall, the quick attainment of Cmax, relatively short half-life, and low intervolunteer variability indicate a PK profile fit for purpose for this treatment intended as an acute AC reversal agent. Similar PK data in parts A and B indicate that dabigatran coadministration had little effect on OKL-1111 PK. Urine PK analysis in part A indicated that OKL-1111 is excreted primarily by the renal tubules, as reflected by the high mean renal CL compared with total systemic CL, consistent with prior nonclinical findings.

Several effects of OKL-1111 on CAT parameters were observed in part A. A decrease in time to peak and ETP was observed with the 1000 mg dose, in line with the expected mechanism of OKL-1111. Although the ED from placebo was not statistically significant for the 100 and 300 mg doses, there is some indication of a dose-related effect on these parameters, supporting the possibility that this may well be a true effect of OKL-1111. The observed increase in ETP at 10 mg and in velocity index at 100 mg was not consistent across dose levels and potentially are false-positive findings.

Ex vivo challenge of samples with dabigatran seemed to substantially affect CAT parameter values, but remarkably, no significant effects of OKL-1111 compared with placebo were observed, except for an increase in ETP at the lowest dose tested, which may possibly be a false-positive finding. Given the effects observed in untreated samples and the preclinical in vivo data, this was a surprising result, and it seems that OKL-1111 at the tested dose levels, at least in this study, was unable to reverse any of the effects of dabigatran measured by the CAT assay.

Apixaban had effects on CAT parameters similar to those of dabigatran, but in contrast to the dabigatran challenge, a clear dose-dependent effect of OKL-1111 on lag time was observed after apixaban challenge, which was apparent at 300 and 1000 mg. In line with the effect observed on time to peak in untreated samples, an effect was also observed with 1000 mg OKL-1111 after apixaban challenge. However, effect sizes for OKL-1111 tended to be larger after apixaban challenge than in untreated samples. Because effects on peak thrombin were observed only at 100 mg and were not consistent with the other data, this may be a false-positive finding.

Overall, these results indicate clear pharmacologic activity of OKL-1111 on CAT assay parameters, specifically time to peak, lag time, and ETP. This activity was observed in untreated samples, and a more prominent effect was observed after ex vivo apixaban challenge, but not after dabigatran challenge. Although both challenge agents are DOACs, dabigatran is a thrombin inhibitor and apixaban is an FXa inhibitor. Differences in their mechanisms of action, or in the effect size resulting from the selected dose in this ex vivo challenge, may explain the difference in the observed (or absence of) reversal effects of OKL-1111.

In part B, AC effects of dabigatran were observed on several parameters, including an increase in lag time and time to peak and a decrease in ETP on the CAT assay, validating the model. When OKL-1111 was coadministered with dabigatran, an apparent partial reversal with 1000 mg OKL-1111 was observed on the effect of dabigatran on several parameters (see change from baseline data in Table 6). However, statistical analysis did not confirm significant effects at either 500 or 1000 mg. This apparent discrepancy for 1000 mg may be explained by existing predose differences in baseline values between treatment groups (Table 6), which may have driven the change from baseline difference, although it should also be noted that the study was not statistically powered to identify small effect sizes. In any case, the lack of statistically significant effects in part B is in line with the observation from the ex vivo dabigatran challenge in part A. Although OKL-1111 showed more promising results with ex vivo apixaban challenge than with dabigatran challenge in part A, the study was not designed to select the challenge agent after an interim analysis of part A. Dabigatran had already been prespecified as the challenge agent in part B because of its prominent effect on lag time and the observed effect of OKL-1111 on lag time in preclinical studies. Therefore, it remains speculative what effects might have been observed had apixaban been used as the challenge agent in part B. Regardless, the findings from part A do indicate target engagement of OKL-1111 and warrant further investigation of its efficacy in reversal of anticoagulation. Similarly, Thromboquest assessment suggested that 1000 mg OKL-1111, when coadministered with a DOAC, can reverse the anticoagulation effect and restore coagulation potential. Comparable effects have been observed with idarucizumab, a specific antidote to dabigatran.23 Moreover, in a study of andexanet alfa, which is specific to direct FXa inhibitors, anti-FXa activity returned to pre-AC values, indicating efficacious dosing toward apixaban.24 These findings indicate reversal activity of OKL-1111 and provide valuable information on the exposure-response relationship that can inform dose selection in future trials.

This study also has limitations. The small sample size is an inherent limitation of early-phase studies such as this first-in-human study of OKL-1111, and the findings should be confirmed in future trials. This includes investigation of rare AEs such as anaphylaxis, as reported for another clinically used cyclodextrin, sugammadex.25 Because this was an exploratory study, we did not correct for multiple testing when analyzing the CAT data, and the statistical results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, this study included only male participants; future studies should confirm these findings in female participants. Regarding the PD results in part B, pre–OKL-1111 baseline values in the 1000-mg OKL-1111 group differed substantially from those in the placebo and 500 mg groups for many PD parameters. This apparently occurred despite randomization, likely because of the small sample size. These pre–OKL-1111 differences hamper interpretation of the PD data for this dose group in part B. Finally, future studies of OKL-1111 should confirm the relevance of the biomarkers used in this study and their relationship to the clinical end point. It is not yet fully clear how parameters such as those in the CAT assay relate to bleeding risk in the target patient population or how mitigation of these biomarker effects relates to clinical outcomes in ICH. However, inclusion of these biomarkers in this first-in-human trial added value by clarifying the dose-response relationship of OKL-1111, enabling proof of pharmacology, and aiding decision-making for future trials.

Conclusions

This first-in-human trial showed that OKL-1111 was safe and well tolerated at dose levels associated with reversal activity. Together with the favorable PK observed in this study, these findings supported further evaluation of OKL-1111 as a novel universal AC reversal agent. However, development of OKL-1111 was discontinued after completion of this first-in-human study because of kidney toxicity findings in subsequent repeat-dose animal toxicology studies.
