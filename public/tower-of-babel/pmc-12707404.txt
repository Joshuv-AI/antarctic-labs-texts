Safety of hydrodissection in low-risk posterior polar cataract assessed with preoperative swept-source optical coherence tomography
Authors: Dandapani Ramamurthy, S Tamilarasi, Shreesha K Kodavoor, Chitra Ramamurthy, Shreyas Ramamurthy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the safety of routine hydrodissection in a subset of posterior polar cataracts (PPC) in which the posterior capsule (PC) can be visualized beneath the polar opacity using preoperative swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography (SS-AS-OCT).Design:Prospective interventional study.Methods:The study included eyes with PPC undergoing cataract surgery by standard phacoemulsification or femtosecond laser platform. Preoperative SS-AS-OCT imaging was performed to assess the PC, which was then classified as continuous (Group 1), abrupt (Group 2), or dehiscent PC (Group 3). PPC with dehiscent PC was excluded. In Group 1, phacoemulsification was performed with routine hydrodissection and nuclear rotation. In Group 2, hydrodelineation was performed, and nucleus rotation was avoided. Chamber stability was maintained, and PC polishing was avoided in both groups. The outcome measure was to assess the incidence of posterior capsular rupture (PCR) in Groups 1 and 2.Results:The cohort included 70 eyes (57 patients), categorized as Group 1 PPC [54 eyes (77.1%)] and Group 2 PPC [16 eyes (22.9%)] based on preoperative SS-AS-OCT imaging. One eye in Group 1 developed PCR (1.9%) with routine hydrodissection, and four eyes in Group 2 developed PCR (25%). The negative predictive value of SS-AS- OCT with gentle hydrodissection was 98.1%.Conclusion:Preoperative SS-AS -OCT helps identify low-risk PPC cases, in which gentle hydrodissection and nucleus rotation can be performed for efficient nucleus and epinucleus management.

Posterior polar cataracts (PPCs) are characterized by a well-demarcated, disk-like opacity at the posterior pole. Phacoemulsification in PPC is always challenging, as these eyes are at risk for posterior capsule rupture (PCR) due to abnormal adhesion of the posterior capsule (PC) to the polar opacity or a pre-existing weakness of the PC. The incidence of PCR in PPC is variable, ranging from as high as 26% in older studies[1] to 4% in more recent studies.[2]

The use of Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) for preoperative assessment of the PC in PPC is well known. AS-OCT has a high negative predictive value (NPV) and is used effectively to predict the risk of PCR during phacoemulsification.[345] If the PC is noted to be intact in preoperative AS-OCT, it is unlikely to rupture during surgery.

Hydrodissection is routinely avoided in PPC to minimize undue stress on the thin PC and to reduce the risk of PCR. Avoiding hydrodissection, however, causes difficulty in removing the epinuclear cushion, thereby increasing the surgical manipulation and time,[6] which may, in turn, increase the risk of PCR.

Titiyal et al.[7] demonstrated the safety of performing hydrodissection in cases with Type I PPC, which was characterized by distinct PC using intraoperative OCT (iOCT), showing that no case with type I opacity developed PC dehiscence during hydrodissection.

We share our surgical experience using the ANTERION (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH), a SS-AS-OCT device, for the preoperative assessment of the PC in PPC. This enabled us to identify a low-risk morphological variant of PPC, in which gentle hydrodissection was performed to facilitate efficient nucleus and epinucleus management.

Methods

This is a prospective interventional study conducted at a tertiary eye care hospital in South India. Ethics committee approval was obtained (TEF/IEC/APPROVAL NO/2024/031-A), and the study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study period was from May 2024 to December 2024. Seventy-one eyes with PPC were enrolled, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Eyes with typical PPC on slit-lamp examination (with or without associated nuclear sclerosis (NS) or posterior subcapsular cataract) were included. Typical PPC is defined as a dense, circular, plaque-like opacity with a ringed appearance.

All patients underwent a comprehensive examination, including refraction, slit-lamp examination, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, dilated fundus evaluation, and macular OCT, when indicated. Biometry was performed using IOL Master 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG), and intraocular lens (IOL) power was calculated using the Barrett universal II TK formula for monofocal and multifocal IOLs and the Barrett TK toric formula for toric IOLs. Using the IOL Master 700, a swept-source-based biometer with a high success rate for axial length acquisition, measurements were successfully obtained in all PPC cases. The type of IOL implant was determined by the patient’s preoperative corneal astigmatism and their willingness to wear glasses after cataract surgery. If PC dehiscence was noted intraoperatively, the choice of IOL implant depended on the available capsular support. Patients were informed about the nature of the cataract, the potential need to change the type of implant, and any additional procedures (Anterior vitrectomy [AV]/Pars plana vitrectomy [PPV]) that might be required. Slit-lamp photo of the PPC and SS-AS-OCT images were taken and assessed by an independent reviewer. PPC is visualized as a hyper-reflective area in the posterior pole, and the PC is seen as a continuous hyperechoic concave line limiting the posterior aspect of the nucleus in AS-OCT. Eyes were classified based on the AS-OCT classification of PPC proposed by Dhami et al.[8] as follows: Group 1 – PPC with continuous PC – The PC could be traced below the PPC without any defect or discontinuity; Group 2 – PPC with abrupt PC – The PC could not be traced just under the PPC but was seen beyond it; and Group 3 – PPC with dehiscent PC – The continuity of PC could not be traced under the PPC with a defect and protrusion of contents in patellar fossa [Fig. 1]. One eye in Group 3 (Dehiscent PC) was excluded from the study, leaving 70 eyes for analysis.

SS-AS-OCT: Swept-source anterior segment optical coherence tomography

Considering the high NPV of AS-OCT, in Group 1 (PPC with continuous PC), we performed gentle routine hydrodissection with nuclear rotation and did not increase the posterior offset in nuclear fragmentation while performing femtosecond laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS). In Group 2 (PPC with abrupt PC), hydrodissection and nuclear rotation were avoided, and the posterior offset was increased to 800 microns in FLACS.

Surgeries were performed by five experienced surgeons, each with a minimum of 15 years of surgical experience. Either standard phacoemulsification or FLACS was performed depending on the patient’s choice of procedure. Surgery was performed under peribulbar anesthesia with 2.0% lidocaine hydrochloride (Xylocaine) or topical anesthesia (0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops), depending on the surgeon’s decision.

In Group 1, PPC cases, a temporal 2.4 mm clear corneal incision was made along with two side port incisions. A well-centered continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis was performed using a 26-gauge needle cystotome, and the size was aimed at 5.0 mm approximately to provide a uniform 360-degree coverage of the IOL optic by the capsulorhexis edge. Routine hydrodissection was performed, which is defined as a gentle but continuous infusion of balanced salt solution (BSS) using a 27-gauge blunt cannula placed under the rhexis margin, which separated the capsule from the cortex, and the fluid wave was visualized gently passing across the posterior pole to the other side. This was followed by a gentle tap on the edge of the nucleus to release any fluid trapped in the posterior capsular bag. Care was taken not to inject excess fluid, and vigorous decompression of the bag was avoided. Once the cortex was completely separated from the capsule, gentle but complete nuclear rotation was carried out to free any cortico-capsular adhesions. The nucleotomy technique was either direct chop or stop-and-chop in the presence of associated NS. Phacoemulsification was done using routine parameters of the surgeon’s preference, either with Centurion Vision System (Alcon, Fort Worth, USA) or Veritas Vision System (Johnson and Johnson Vision, Santa Ana, CA). Bimanual irrigation and aspiration (I and A) was performed. Since the PC in PPC could be thin and fragile, direct polishing of the capsule with the aspiration cannula was avoided. A gentle fluid wave was used for hydro-polishing to flush the PC. IOL was implanted in the bag. Anterior chamber (AC) stability was maintained throughout the procedure [Video Clip 1].

When FLACS (CatalysTM Precision Laser System, Johnson and Johnson Vision) was used, a capsulorhexis size of 4.9 mm (scanned capsule) was planned. We used either sextants without softening in case of PPC without NS and quadrants with softening in case of PPC with NS component. The posterior offset was set at 500 microns. Gentle hydrodissection was performed with consideration for pneumodissection caused by the release of air bubbles during nuclear fragmentation. The remaining steps followed those described for standard phacoemulsification.

In Group 2, PPC cases, hydrodissection was avoided, but hydrodelineation was performed. Nucleus rotation was avoided, and slow-motion phacoemulsification was done. Depending on the nucleus density, either layer-by-layer phacoemulsification or direct chop was performed. Sudden decompression of the AC was avoided, and dispersive viscoelastic was injected through the side port before withdrawing the phaco probe. The epinucleus was viscodissected, and cortex aspiration was done, starting from the periphery toward the center, keeping the central posterior polar component to be removed in the end. PC polishing/hydro-polishing was avoided [Video Clip 2]. In addition to the above safety precautions, in FLACS, the posterior offset was increased to 800 microns.

In both groups, when the PC remained intact at the end of I and A, the planned IOL of choice was implanted in the bag. If PC dehiscence was noted, dispersive viscoelastic was injected to plug the area of PCR, and triamcinolone-assisted AV was performed, if needed. AV was carried out using the maximum cut rate available on the phacoemulsification machine, along with a moderate aspiration flow rate and vacuum. In cases where the epinucleus, nucleus, or cortex dropped into the vitreous cavity, the case was taken over by the Vitreoretina (VR) team for PPV and pars plana lensectomy (PPL). Either a three-piece IOL in the sulcus or a single-piece IOL in the bag was implanted, depending on the residual capsular support.

The outcome measure was the occurrence of PCR in Groups 1 and 2 [Fig. 2].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows software (version 23.0, SPSS, Inc.). Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD. Nominal data were expressed as percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant

Results

The study included 70 eyes of 57 patients. The patient demographics and surgical data are provided in Table 1. There were 54 eyes in Group 1 PPC and 16 eyes in Group 2 PPC. This suggests that PPC with a continuous PC is more commonly encountered in practice. Intraoperative PCR occurred in one eye in Group 1 and four eyes (one of which had a nucleus drop) in Group 2.

One PCR in Group 1 occurred due to capsular blowout during hydrodissection. The case was converted to manual small-incision cataract surgery (MSICS) followed by nucleus delivery, AV, and implantation of a three-piece IOL in the sulcus with optic capture. This was the first case in the study done by one of the surgeons where hydrodissection was attempted. Retrospective analysis of the SS-AS-OCT image also confirmed that the PC was continuous preoperatively. A review of the surgical video revealed that hydrodissection was done forcefully, leading to PCR.

In Group 2, four eyes experienced PCR. In the first case, PCR occurred during lateral separation of the nucleus. Phacoemulsification was converted to MSICS, the nuclear quadrants were removed, AV was performed, and a three-piece IOL was implanted in the sulcus with optic capture. The second case of PCR occurred during IOL unfolding; the single-piece foldable IOL was explanted, and a 3-piece IOL was subsequently implanted in the sulcus with optic capture. The remaining two PCRs occurred in both eyes of the same patient who had been scheduled for multifocal IOL implantation. In the first eye, a small, punched-out PCR was noted at the end of I and A, without vitreous disturbance; the multifocal IOL was implanted in the capsular bag. In the second eye, operated a few weeks later, nucleus drop occurred during phacoemulsification. PPV and PPL were performed, and a multifocal IOL was implanted with reverse optic capture on the same day.

Seven eyes in Group 1 and two eyes in Group 2 had NS, graded as Nuclear Color (NC) 4/Nuclear Opalescence (NO) 4 according to the Lens Opacities Classification System III. In Group 1, the single case of PCR was not related to the NS grade or the nucleotomy technique. Of the four PCRs in Group 2, only one eye had an NS grade of NC4/NO4. Overall, the grade of NS did not appear to influence the PCR rate in either group.

No cases of PCR were observed in the FLACS subset. However, the small number of eyes in the FLACS group (eight), compared to the phacoemulsification group (62), precluded statistical comparison.

In three eyes (one in Group 1 and two in Group 2), the planned IOL had to be changed due to large PCRs. The originally intended monofocal hydrophobic single-piece IOL was replaced with a three-piece IOL implanted in the sulcus with optic capture.

Premium IOLs were implanted in 30 eyes (42.8%)—23 eyes in Group 1 and seven eyes in Group 2. No change in IOL type was required in any of the premium IOL cases, and all eyes had a good visual outcome postoperatively.

The NPV of SS-AS-OCT combined with gentle hydrodissection in PPC was 98.1%. The Positive predictive value, sensitivity, and specificity were 25%, 80%, and 81.5%, respectively.

Discussion

PPCs carry an increased risk of intraoperative PCR, highlighting the need for effective preoperative risk stratification tools to distinguish low-risk from high-risk cases. Various diagnostic imaging modalities such as AS-OCT, IOL Master 700, integrated OCT in FLACS, and intraoperative OCT (iOCT) have been utilized to visualize the PC status in PPC.[3457] AS-OCT enables detailed visualization of anterior segment ocular structures, including the tear film, cornea, conjunctiva, sclera, rectus muscles, AC angle, and lens.[910] The use of a 1300 nm light source combined with SS-OCT technology in the ANTERION device provides high-contrast imaging of the entire anterior segment and posterior lens.[11] These images help in planning of anterior segment surgeries. Conventionally, cortical cleaving hydrodissection is avoided in eyes with PPC as the resulting increase in intralenticular pressure raises the risk of rupture of the thin and fragile PC.[1516] However, avoiding hydrodissection can make epinucleus management significantly more challenging, thereby potentially increasing the risk of PCR. The feasibility of performing gentle hydrodissection in PPC has been explored in previous studies. Fine et al.[17] performed minimal hydrodissection in multiple quadrants without allowing the wave to transmit across the PC and also carried out hydrodelineation. In our study, the majority of PPC cases (56 eyes; 77.1%) were in Group 1. This is consistent with the higher prevalence of PPC with continuous PC, as reported in previous studies.[714] Gentle hydrodissection and nuclear rotation were performed in these eyes. In cases with associated NS (seven eyes; 13% in Group 1), the nucleotomy technique used was either direct chop or stop-and-chop. The standard nucleotomy approach involves rotating the nucleus and chopping it into quadrants for subsequent emulsification. Without nuclear rotation, executing the chop can be particularly challenging, especially in hard cataracts. Gentle hydrodissection and nuclear rotation facilitated optimal positioning of the nucleus opposite the clear corneal incision, enabling effective chopping. The epinucleus was removed along with the nucleus, contributing to smoother and more efficient phacoemulsification. This approach eliminated the need for separate epinucleus management and minimized stress on the PC. One case of PCR (1.9%) occurred in Group 1, attributed to vigorous hydrodissection, underscoring the importance of avoiding forceful hydrodissection in these eyes.

In Group 2 (16 eyes; 22.9%), hydrodissection was avoided. Associated NS was observed in two eyes (12.5%), for which direct chop was used for nucleotomy. In the remaining eyes, layer-by-layer phacoemulsification was performed. PCR occurred in four eyes (25%), of which only one had associated NS.

The PCR rate in Group 1 was 1.9%, while in Group 2, it was 25%, a difference that was statistically significant (P = 0.00158). Titiyal et al.[7] reported no cases of PCR in 19 eyes with Type I PPC (characterized by distinct PC) where gentle hydrodissection was performed. In our study, one case of PCR (1.9%) occurred in Group 1, which shares a similar PC morphology to their Type I PPC. In contrast, Type II and Type III PPC in the Titiyal et al.[7] study—where the PC was not traceable—are equivalent to the Group 2 cases in our study. Their reported PCR rate in Type II and III combined was 14.3%, compared to 25% in our Group 2. In the same study, the authors compared PCR rates between iOCT-guided and non-iOCT-guided phacoemulsification in PPC. They reported PC dehiscence in three of 40 eyes (7.5%) in the iOCT-guided group versus eight of 72 eyes (11.1%) in the non-iOCT-guided group—a difference that was not statistically significant (P = 0.74). Additionally, they noted that preoperative AS-OCT images correlated well with iOCT findings.

In our study, NPV of SS-AS-OCT combined with gentle hydrodissection was 98.1%, while the positive predictive value was 25%. This relatively low positive predictive value may be attributed to the fact that when the PC is not visualized beneath the polar opacity, it may be due to factors such as dense and tightly adherent opacity–PC complex, imaging artifacts/after shadowing of the opacity, or an actual deficiency in the PC. Importantly, not all cases with an abrupt or non-visualized PC result in PCR.

FLACS was performed in five eyes (9.2%) in Group 1 and three eyes (18.8%) in Group 2. The safety of FLACS in PPC has been previously described, with reported PCR rates ranging from 0% to 4.4%.[221] The Catalys laser system utilizes a segmentation and softening pattern for nuclear fragmentation; however, the femtodelineation method of using cylinders or concentric circles is not applicable. It is generally recommended to increase the posterior offset to preserve a thick epinuclear cushion, which helps buffer fluid turbulence and mechanical stress on the PC. In our study, we did not increase the posterior offset in Group 1 PPC eyes, but we did adjust it in Group 2.

The strength of our study lies in its prospective design and large overall sample size. However, limitations include the small number of cases in the FLACS subgroup, involvement of multiple surgeons, and variability in nuclear fracture techniques, all of which may serve as potential confounding factors. The PCR rate was compared between surgeons across both groups, with no statistically significant difference observed. The results obtained from five surgeons performing gentle hydrodissection in PPC with a continuous PC can be reasonably extrapolated to real-world clinical settings, where surgeons with varying levels of experience are involved. Further studies comparing phacoemulsification and FLACS with gentle hydrodissection in PPC with continuous PC may help validate and complement our findings.

Conclusion

Preoperative AS-OCT is a valuable tool for risk-stratifying PPC based on PC integrity. In low-risk cases, where the PC is traceable beneath the polar opacity, gentle hydrodissection and nuclear rotation can be safely performed. This preoperative insight allows for a tailored surgical approach, facilitating smoother and more efficient nucleus and epinucleus management.
