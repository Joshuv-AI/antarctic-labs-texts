Leveraging community engagement and human-centered design to develop multilevel implementation strategies to enhance adoption of a health equity intervention
Authors: Maggi A Price, Patrick J Mulkern, Madelaine Condon, Marina Rakhilin, Kara Johansen, Aaron R Lyon, Lisa Saldana, John Pachankis, Sue A Woodward, Kathryn M Roeder, Lyndsey R Moran, Beth A Jerskey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth equity intervention implementation (which promotes positive health outcomes for populations experiencing disproportionately worse health) is often impeded by health-equity-specific barriers like provider bias; few studies demonstrate how to overcome these barriers through implementation strategies. An urgent health equity problem in the U.S. is the mental health of transgender youth. To address this, we developed Gender-Affirming Psychotherapy (GAP), a health equity intervention comprising best-practice mental health care for transgender youth. This paper details the identification of implementation determinants and the development of targeted strategies to promote provider adoption of GAP.MethodsThis study represents part of a larger study of mental health provider adoption of GAP. Here we describe the first 2 stages of the 3-stage community-engaged and human-centered design process – Discover, Design/Build, and Test – to identify implementation determinants of adoption and develop implementation strategies with transgender youth, their parents, and mental health providers. This process involved collecting data via focus groups, design meetings, usability testing, and champion meetings. Data were analyzed using rapid and conventional content analysis. Qualitative coding of implementation determinants was guided by the Health Equity Implementation Framework, and implementation strategy coding was facilitated by the ERIC Implementation Strategy Compilation.ResultsWe identified 15 determinants of GAP adoption, and all were specific to the transgender population (e.g., inclusive record system, anti-transgender attitudes). Seventeen implementation strategies were recommended and 12 were developed, collectively addressing all identified determinants. Most strategies were packaged into an online self-paced mental health provider training (implementation intervention) with 6 training tools. Additional inner-setting strategies were designed to support training uptake (e.g., mandate training) and GAP adoption (e.g., change record system).ConclusionsCommunity-engaged and human-centered design methods can identify health equity intervention implementation determinants and develop targeted strategies. We highlight five generalizable takeaways for health equity implementation scientists: (1) implementer bias may be a key barrier, (2) experience with the health equity population may be an important facilitator, (3) stakeholder stories may be an effective training tool, (4) inner-setting-level implementation strategies may be necessary, and (5) teaching implementers how to build implementation strategies can overcome resource-constraints.Trial registrationNovember 11, 2022; NCT05626231.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-025-00809-7.Keywords: Health equity, Implementation strategies, Implementation determinants, Implementation intervention, Multilevel, Transgender, Community-engaged research, Human-centered design

Introduction

Health equity promotion is an increasingly important goal for implementation scientists [1–5]. Scholars recommend community-engaged research methods for identifying and overcoming barriers to health equity intervention implementation [1, 6–9]. Specifically, best practices involve members of health inequity populations and implementers throughout the implementation process, to maximize reach and uptake. This community-engaged process involves identifying barriers, then developing, evaluating, and implementing strategies targeting those barriers. Despite growing calls to action, very few implementation studies to date have employed these methods [8, 10–12].

Health equity problem: transgender youth mental health

An urgent health equity problem in the U.S. concerns the disproportionate burden of adverse mental health borne by transgender youth (whose gender differs from their birth-assigned sex; [7]). Compared to cisgender youth (whose gender aligns with their birth-assigned sex), transgender youth are 2–3 times more likely to be diagnosed with depression or anxiety [13, 14] and 6 times more likely to attempt suicide [15, 16]. This inequity is exacerbated by the anti-transgender sociopolitical climate [7, 17–21] and exemplified by the uptick of state-level bans on evidence-based gender-affirming medical care for transgender youth (e.g., hormone treatment; [22–26]). Gender-affirming medical care is associated with improved mental health [27–38]. Likewise, anti-transgender policies are linked to worse mental health and victimization among transgender youth [17, 39, 40]. Access to effective mental health care is thus critical for transgender youth.

Affirming mental healthcare: brief overview of effectiveness and implementation research

Affirming mental healthcare is designed to support a patient's gender (and often sexual) identity(ies) and experiences, and includes practices like using a patient’s affirmed (chosen) name and helping patients combat internalized bias through cognitive strategies [41–43]. It was developed because traditional and untailored mental health treatment was found to be less effective and acceptable for LGBTQ + people compared to cisgender heterosexual people [7, 44–46]. Several RCTs support the effectiveness of affirming mental health care, demonstrating that patients who receive affirming care experience significantly more treatment engagement and mental health improvements (e.g., steeper depression improvement) compared to those who do not [46–53]. Importantly, however, relatively few trials of affirming care employ randomization due to the considerable ethical drawbacks of assigning vulnerable populations to care known to be less helpful [7, 54–57] and acceptable for transgender patients [58–60]. Among the numerous non-randomized trials on affirming care, all demonstrate that it outperforms non-affirming care and waitlist conditions [59, 61–63] on measures of treatment satisfaction, engagement, and mental health [51, 63, 64]. For an exhaustive review of effectiveness research on affirming mental healthcare, we recommend a few excellent review papers: Burger and Pachankis [65], Tudor-Sfetea and Topcio [66], and Expósito-Campos [67].

The robust evidence on the effectiveness and acceptability of affirming mental health care has led to calls for relevant implementation studies [7, 68, 69], which scholars have recently begun responding to by evaluating fidelity [53, 70], feasibility [51, 59, 62, 71, 72], and novel implementation strategies [61, 73–79]. These practices have also been codified into clinical treatment guidelines by national professional bodies, like the American Psychological Association [37, 38, 80]. Despite this empirical and clinical progress, transgender patients are often unable to access affirming mental healthcare because of provider bias, communicated through treatment refusal or microaggressions [44, 81–84], and a dearth of adequately trained providers [85–87].

To address the widespread need for transgender-competent mental health providers, we developed Gender-Affirming Psychotherapy (GAP): an evidence-informed treatment tailoring approach (not a standalone intervention, which enhances scalability; [88]) designed to increase both treatment engagement (e.g., via improving therapeutic climate) and treatment outcomes (e.g., via GAP cognitive restructuring techniques targeting anxiety and depression). GAP was developed through a rigorous 4-year NIH-funded human-centered design (HCD) intervention development project [89] involving: a scoping review of research (see Additional file 1 for scoping review references; [43]) and best practice guidelines, and HCD-driven intervention refinement via focus groups and interviews with community stakeholders (transgender youth, their parents, and providers; [43]). GAP consists of evidence-informed and community-endorsed practice modifications encompassing 27 principles (knowledge that guides practice) and 38 skills (techniques or behaviors to use or avoid). The complete list of GAP principles and skills is available in Box 1 of [43].

Contributions to gender-affirming practice research and health equity implementation science

To facilitate the implementation of GAP, this study systematically identified implementation determinants of GAP adoption and addressed them through the development of targeted implementation strategies. This represents a contribution to health equity implementation science broadly, as very few studies targeting health equity problems identify determinants and design targeted implementation strategies (see exceptions led by Arnold [90], Cabassa [91–93], Oetzel [94, 95], and Rogers [96]). This study also contributes to gender-affirming practice implementation science, as no known research has identified determinants or strategies of gender-affirming practice specific to mental health care – which is distinct from other healthcare settings (e.g., primary care, endocrinology) and central to accessing gender-affirming medical services. For example, mental health services are characterized by frequent appointments (weekly vs. annual), psychological treatment foci (e.g., identities, psychosocial stressors), and the centrality of the provider-patient relationship (i.e., "therapeutic relationship”). Additionally, mental health providers often serve as gatekeepers to gender-affirming medical care because many insurance plans require a letter from a mental health professional for coverage.

A few studies have identified determinants of gender-affirming practice implementation in non-mental health settings (e.g., medical care, schools); examples include implementer knowledge and attitudes (e.g., about gender-affirming practices), implementer workload (e.g., protected training time), and institutional climate (e.g., agency support; [7, 97–100]). Research on implementation strategies for gender-affirming practice adoption is scarce, though some have recommended strategies (e.g., medical training, appeal insurance denials) based on their clinical experience and literature synthesis [101, 102]. Evaluations of in-person training suggest that it can enhance implementer knowledge about gender-affirming practices and improve attitudes toward transgender people [79, 103]. While promising, these trainings have not been systematically developed and tested, or they focused on affirming practices broadly (including sexual minority affirming practices). This study contributes to the growing literature on gender-affirming practice adoption specifically, and more broadly, to the use of best practices in health equity implementation science [1–5, 10, 104, 105] to identify and develop implementation strategies targeting a major health equity problem.

Current study

We employed community-engaged human-centered design (HCD) methods to identify implementation determinants (barriers and facilitators) and implementation strategies (methods to promote an implementation outcome) to support both provider-level (e.g., individual therapist use of GAP) and agency-level (e.g., clinic-wide use of GAP) adoption. Implementation strategies were then designed and refined to address identified determinants. This paper details a replicable process for systematically identifying and addressing health equity intervention determinants in collaboration with affected communities and describes the resulting implementation strategies.

To ensure a comprehensive assessment of health equity factors, we utilized the Health Equity Implementation Framework (HEIF; [4])—a determinants framework tailored to identify multilevel barriers and facilitators of health equity intervention implementation—throughout the research process. We chose HEIF over other determinant frameworks because it identifies whether an implementation determinant is specific to a health inequity and highlights structural level determinants, which are often central to health equity, but may be overlooked with standard frameworks. For instance, “data systems” (e.g., modifying the electronic health record system to include a patient’s affirmed name) was identified as both an inner-setting and health equity determinant. It is an inner-setting determinant to GAP adoption because it facilitates the provider’s use of the correct name, and it is a health equity determinant because its absence may result in harm to the patient because of their identity (e.g., distress due to a provider deadnaming the patient). In this study, we used the HEIF to identify determinants then linked them to implementation strategies from the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) Compilation of Implementation Strategies [106–109].

Methods

Overview and procedural framework

This study represents part of a larger project evaluating implementation strategies to promote mental health provider adoption of GAP [110]. To identify implementation determinants of GAP adoption and develop implementation strategies targeting those determinants, we completed the first 2 of 3 stages of Discover, Design/Build, and Test (DDBT), a community-engaged HCD framework for developing and refining interventions and implementation strategies (see framework and procedures in Fig. 1; [111, 112]). Across stages, we worked closely with community stakeholders central to the implementation goal (GAP adoption): transgender youth, their parents, and mental health providers (with and without expertise working with transgender youth). Several participants engaged in multiple DDBT stages to ensure that data were being interpreted accurately and that implementation strategies met participants’ needs (i.e., member checking; [113, 114]). In the Discover stage, we identified implementation determinants and strategies, and in the Design/Build stage, we developed the previously discovered implementation strategies. Focus group and interview protocols were tailored for each stage and participant type (see Additional files 2–4 for stage- and participant-specific protocols). The Test stage will be described in a forthcoming paper.

Process for identifying implementation determinants and developing bundled implementation strategies*

*DDBT was only applied to the 8 strategies bundled within the online training (implementation intervention). The 4 non-bundled (inner-setting) strategies were developed through our research-practice partnership; details about this process are in Additional File 8.

The larger study involved a research-practice partnership with a multi-site mental health agency headquartered in the Northeast serving youth on the East and West Coasts. We chose this agency because it is a setting that does not specialize in care for LGBTQ + youth but has a growing transgender patient population. To enhance the long-term reach of GAP [115], it was important to evaluate a setting representative of mental health care services for the target population, but not one where providers are expected to already have transgender-specific competency [116]. Reporting for this study follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (Additional file 5; [117]).

Recruitment and enrollment

Participant recruitment involved purposive sampling [118] and social media [119, 120]. We recruited a sample that was racially representative of Boston (participants’ primary location; all resided in the Northeast US). The Boston College IRB approved the study. Procedures, sample, and analyses

Discover stage

Discover focused on identifying implementation determinants and strategies through 10 separate virtual focus groups (February-April 2022) with transgender youth (n = 6, ages 13–23; 3 meetings), parents of transgender youth (n = 3; 1 meeting), mental health providers with expertise working with transgender youth (n = 11 providing 2 + years gender-affirming care; 3 meetings), and without expertise (n = 7; 3 meetings). Semi-structured interview protocols were used (Additional file 2).

Design/Build stage

Design/Build focused on developing implementation strategies, first by drafting previously identified implementation strategies (Design) and then refining those drafts (Build). Design involved six 2-h virtual meetings (June-July 2022) with separate groups of community stakeholders (4 youth; 2 parents; 12 providers). Meetings involved participants, professional designers, and researchers collaborating on MURAL [121], an online tool for real-time visual collaboration on a digital canvas through idea sharing (e.g., sticky notes, images) and information organizing. Protocols and canvases are in Additional file 3. Throughout this stage, meeting notes and video recordings were reviewed and rapidly analyzed [122] by the PI (first author) and project coordinator/co-builder (fourth author) to (a) tailor subsequent meetings to fit stakeholders’ expressed needs (e.g., adjust agenda topics based on their feedback) and (b) generate and iteratively refine of a list of suggested implementation strategies using ERIC. Those that could be feasibly built within study constraints (time, budget, scope) were drafted. For example, the strategy “develop educational materials” involved researchers drafting curriculum on Google docs. Once edited, the curriculum was transferred to an online Learning Management System to facilitate Build.

Build involved refining implementation strategy drafts through synchronous and asynchronous “usability testing” (protocols in Additional file 4). First, N = 3 participants (representing target users) complete the full 8-h training–comprising multiple implementation strategies (e.g., shadow, simulate change)—while providing real-time feedback on usability and acceptability to researchers observing them (November 2022-January 2023). Participants were directed to “think aloud,” meaning vocalizing thoughts and feelings while engaging with material; researchers used open-ended questions to encourage elaboration as needed (e.g., “Why did you answer the way you did?”; [123]). Each session lasted 2 h and covered 1–1.5 h of strategy content. Participants could participate in as many sessions as they liked; 1 completed four sessions (8 h), 1 eight sessions (16 h), and 1 nine sessions (18 h), totaling 21 sessions. Next, real-world usability was evaluated asynchronously by 1 provider participant and 1 researcher who was also a mental health provider, but unfamiliar with the study (excluded from the sample because they were not a study participant). Each person spent ~ 8 h interacting with the strategies and documenting their feedback on usability and content. Researchers addressed usability issues throughout this stage, ensuring that implementation strategies were ready for subsequent testing.

Participants across stages earned $25–50/hour of session participation. More compensation was given during stages with more engagement (e.g., participants who synchronously worked 1:1 with researchers were paid relatively more).

Champion meetings

Researchers met virtually with partner-agency provider “Champions” (who promote and facilitate the implementation of an innovation) throughout the study (July 2022—March 2025). Champions were 4 partner-agency directors with varying levels of expertise in gender-affirming mental health care and who supported GAP implementation (details in Additional file 6). Meetings focused on the prioritization, design, and roll-out of inner-setting implementation strategies specific to the partner agency. All Champions consented to participate in the subsequent Test phase of the larger project; demographic information is not provided herein to protect their confidentiality. Ample detail about Champions and other inner-setting implementation strategies are in Additional file 6.

Conventional content analysis

Building on rapid qualitative analyses conducted throughout data collection, we re-analyzed the data using conventional content analysis (February-August 2024; [124]) to validate results and potentially identify more determinants and strategies. However, consistent with other studies, no new determinants or strategies were identified through conventional content analysis [122, 125]. Data included transcripts from the Discover and Design/Build stages, and comprehensive meeting notes from 36 weekly Champion meetings held from the initiation of the partnership through end of Build (July 2022-May 2023).

Data were coded by the second and third authors, who met with the first author weekly to build consensus by reviewing codes and resolving discrepancies [126]. During meetings, researchers identified and reflected on how their identities, experiences, and biases may have influenced their interpretation of the data [127, 128]. Of note, we have diverse gender identities (e.g., transgender, nonbinary, cisgender), we are all proponents of GAP, and have varying levels of clinical experience with transgender youth.

Implementation determinants were coded deductively (guided by a codebook developed during rapid qualitative analysis) and inductively (allowing new codes to emerge). Implementation determinants were categorized using the HEIF, by both determinant level (e.g., outer-setting) and whether the determinants were health equity related. Health equity determinants are those specific to the health equity population (transgender youth) and uniquely influence implementation (provider adoption of GAP). Implementation determinants unrelated to health equity are common across populations and interventions (e.g., funding). Determinant code frequencies were calculated to inform the synthesis of results. Implementation strategies were deductively coded, such that each code represented a discrete ERIC implementation strategy [107–109]. Implementation strategies were categorized using both Waltz’s implementation strategy categories (e.g., train/educate, involve consumers; [107]) and HEIF levels (e.g., recipient, inner-setting; 4, [106]) to illustrate the linkage between ERIC strategies and HEIF determinants.

Results

Procedural overview

The development of GAP and the implementation strategies via DDBT required 11 months and 109 total participant hours. Discover involved 27 participants; each contributed 1–4 h in focus groups, totaling 13 group hours. Design involved 18 participants; each contributed 1–2 h in focus groups, totaling 12 group hours. Build involved 4 participants and 1 researcher; each contributed 8–18 individual hours, totaling 58 h. Champion meetings involved 4 Champions; meetings totaled 26 h. In sum, N = 37 participants were involved in this study; n = 12 engaged in more than one phase.

Determinants

Discover data analysis revealed 15 determinants of GAP adoption across all HEIF levels; 13 were categorized at one level and 2 at two levels. Among single-category determinants, 6 were at the provider-level, 5 were at the inner-setting-level, and 2 were at the outer-setting-level. The two double-classified determinants were: 1) “family support” for the youth’s gender, categorized at both patient- and clinical encounter-levels because it reflected a patient factor (e.g., youth not disclosing their gender identity to their parents) and affected the clinical encounter (e.g., in parent sessions the provider would not “out” the patient’s gender by using their affirmed pronouns); and 2) “time,” categorized as both provider and inner-setting-levels because it referred to time under the control of the provider (e.g., limited time for training due to a large caseload) or organization (e.g., no organizationally protected time for training). Determinants and their levels are in Fig. 2. All but one determinant was identified by participants as both a barrier and facilitator; “policy” (political, organizational, and professional rules and regulations affecting GAP adoption) was the exception, solely discussed as a barrier. Twelve of 15 determinants were primarily endorsed as barriers (details in Table 2). The 4 most commonly endorsed determinants were: provider knowledge (about gender-affirming practices and/or transgender youth; coded 83 times), provider attitudes (positive or negative towards gender-affirming practices and/or transgender youth; coded 53 times), family support (for the youth’s gender; coded 44 times), and provider exposure (to transgender youth; coded 23 times).

Health equity focus

HEIF-guided content analysis revealed that all 15 implementation determinants were specific to the health equity population (transgender youth) with 4 also reflecting implementation determinants unrelated to the population (see a in Table 2). Implementation strategies

Standalone implementation intervention: training incorporating eight discrete strategies and six training tools

Eight discrete strategies (detailed parenthetically upon first mention) were packaged within an 8-h training tailored to address 12 of the 15 determinants. The training—primarily designed to impart information and build skills—can be classified as an implementation intervention because it bundled several strategies [129], but we use the term “training” herein for clarity. The training was designed with community stakeholders (strategy 1: develop materials). It was tailored to meet the work-related needs of provider participants (strategy 2: tailor strategies) such that it was online, self-paced, comprehensive (for beginners and experts), delivered through text and read-aloud AI-driven-technology, and offered over 2 months during a slow work period determined in advance by the partner agency (strategy 3: distribute materials). The training consisted of 10 modules (self-contained training segments on specific topics); each aligned with a GAP domain [110] and involved stakeholder-endorsed training tools (strategy 4: dynamic training) across 6 categories: stakeholder stories, practice, instruction, evaluation and feedback, action plans, and commitments.

Training tool 1: stakeholder stories

The training incorporated 43 anonymized stories from youth, parents, and providers, co-written by researchers and stakeholders (strategy 5: involve consumers) based on data collected in the Discover and Design stages. To enhance population exposure, all stories had a read-aloud option with a voice matching the affirmed gender of the stakeholder and a stock photo representing them. Several stories also demonstrated how mental health providers implemented GAP in real-world clinical settings (strategy 6: shadow). Figure 4 provides an example of how several discrete implementation strategies were combined in a story.

Example of implementation strategies built in online training

Training tool 2: practice

Eighteen practice activities required rehearsal of a key skill. For example, after learning about gender dysphoria (distress some transgender youth experience when their birth-assigned sex differs from their gender), providers read and/or listened to an example session of a provider and patient discussing the drawbacks and benefits of a gender dysphoria diagnosis (strategy 6: shadow). Next, providers practiced; they were given the prompt “explain what gender dysphoria is and why you might (or might not) diagnose a client with gender dysphoria” and practiced using their own words by writing or audio-recording a response (strategy 7: simulate change). Another practice activity involved providers identifying 2–3 local gender-affirming medical providers; responses were added to a shared Google spreadsheet serving as a referral list that could be accessed during and after the training (strategy 8: network weaving).

Training tool 3: instruction

Thirty-five instances of instruction presented foundational concepts and actionable, step-by-step skill guidance in accessible language. Complex topics, like anti-transgender legislation, were taught in multiple ways. For example, providers first received information about the recent rise in anti-transgender legislation [20]. Next, providers were given information about the relevance of these policies to their work, such as the potential mental health effects this legislation may have on transgender patients (e.g., suicidal ideation; [19]). Finally, they were taught how to find state policies affecting transgender youth [130], which was reinforced with practice discussing the legislation with a hypothetical patient.

Training tool 4: evaluation and feedback

Twenty evaluations, consisting of 5-item quizzes before and after each module, assessed module-specific knowledge acquisition. Each pre-module quiz was scored immediately; if any item was incorrect, providers were shown “You are still learning and that's ok! Let's continue through the course.” After each post-module quiz, correct answers and explanations were provided to reinforce knowledge acquisition [131].

Training tool 5: action plans

Providers created 6 action plans documenting their goals and intentions to adopt GAP skills. Each action plan could be downloaded and trainees were encouraged to save them for future accountability. Action plans were presented after stakeholder stories or instruction for providers to apply what they learned. For example, after four stakeholder stories about providers using GAP, providers were given the action plan prompt: “Please share three practices you will use with caregivers of transgender youth that you learned from the stories.” The final action plan provided recommended activities to support GAP adoption beyond the training, many of which were implementation strategies suggested in the present study but not built due to feasibility constraints (e.g., start a learning collaborative, advocate for policy reform).

Training tool 6: commitment

Commitments are a behavioral change technique believed to facilitate behavior change [131, 132] by eliciting an active commitment to a specified behavior [133]. Providers were asked to commit to learning GAP at the beginning of each of the 10 modules. For example, providers were asked, “Do you commit to completing the next 25-min module?” and clicked either “yes” or “no.”

Training content to address inner and outer-setting determinants

Within the training we addressed 4 inner-setting determinants (data systems, work climate, procedures, physical space). For example, we targeted “data systems” through 4 types of content (each representing a different “training tool,” described above): 1) instruction on discussing the limitations of the record systems with patients (e.g., limited gender options), 2) a story detailing the benefits of affirming record systems, 3) an action plan wherein providers commit to advocating for an affirming record system, and 4) evaluation and feedback on knowledge about affirming record systems. Similar training content targeted 2 outer-setting determinants (insurance, policies/laws). For instance, policies/law (regulations that affect transgender youth and GAP adoption) involved 1) provider and youth stories chronicling their experiences with policies (e.g., insurance requirements, agency telehealth policies), 2) instruction on specific state and federal policies, 3) practice identifying local policies with recommended resources, and 4) an action plan wherein providers commit to advocating for policy reform.

Four built inner-setting implementation strategies

Our strong collaboration with the partner agency allowed us to build four implementation strategies targeting three inner-setting implementation determinants (data systems, work climate, and funding) typically constrained or prevented by real-world feasibility factors like money and organizational buy-in. These four strategies included establishing a group of Champions (strategy 9: champions), modifying the electronic health record system to enhance gender-inclusivity (strategy 10: change record system), the partner-agency CEO requiring providers to complete the training (but not the study; strategy 11: mandate change), and providing continuing education credits (CEs) to training completers (strategy 12: alter incentives). More detail about these strategies and their development are in Additional file 6 and Table 3, respectively.

Discussion

Using community-engaged human-centered design methods, we collaborated with transgender youth, their parents, and mental health providers to identify implementation determinants and develop implementation strategies to promote mental health provider adoption of a health equity intervention (Gender-Affirming Psychotherapy; GAP). Results revealed 15 determinants of GAP adoption across all HEIF levels. Of the 17 suggested implementation strategies, 12 were deemed feasible and developed, collectively addressing all determinants. Notably, 8 of the strategies were bundled within an implementation intervention; specifically, an innovative online training with 6 training tools. Herein, we synthesize our findings across 5 takeaways to guide health equity intervention researchers and implementers.

Takeaway 1: implementer bias may impede health equity intervention implementation

Provider attitudes, like anti-transgender bias, were the second most endorsed determinant of GAP adoption, after knowledge. Implementation research on attitudes focuses almost exclusively on attitudes about using a particular practice [133], not attitudes toward the patient population. Several health equity implementation science scholars have encouraged researchers to evaluate provider biases [10, 106, 134], yet few have [4, 96, 135]. Nonetheless, it is well-established that providers’ biases about health equity populations (e.g., racism) negatively affect patient engagement and healthcare outcomes [136–138]. It is thus unsurprising that such biases were identified by stakeholders as barriers to using practices that support this health equity population. Echoing other health equity implementation researchers, we argue that measuring implementer attitudes about the patient population (vs. only the practices) is critical. The present study provides data to support this argument and offers concrete implementation strategies (e.g., simulate change, shadow; adapt, develop, and distribute training materials) that address implementer bias via training tools, like practice (takeaway 2) and stakeholders stories (takeaway 3; see also Fig. 3 for all strategies and tools linked to bias).

Takeaway 2: experience with the health equity population can facilitate health equity intervention implementation

Provider bias in health care is attributable to factors like poor skills in culturally-responsive care, lack of knowledge about the patient population, and lack of experience with the patient population [139, 140]. Likewise, our participants shared that intervention adoption requires more than just intervention skills; it requires knowledge about the health equity population and experience with the population. Given the online and self-paced nature of our training, we were unable to utilize exposure-based strategies in provider education, like practice with standardized patients [141] and patient-teacher-led presentations [142]. Instead, we used multimodal and exposure-based training tools in an online training, like practice (e.g., responding to a patient after reviewing a hypothetical encounter) and stakeholder stories (expanded on below). Importantly, online training and rehearsal have both been shown to effectively change provider behaviors [142–147].

Takeaway 3: stakeholder stories may address barriers like implementer bias and emotion, and leverage facilitators like exposure to the health equity population

Our training included many stakeholder stories—narratives from patients (transgender youth), implementers (mental health providers), and other recipients (parents of transgender youth). Our study underscores extant research demonstrating that stakeholder stories are effective for learning and changing attitudes towards health equity populations [148–150]. We suspect that patient stories in particular may help reduce bias through increased relational experience with health equity populations; this notion is consistent with ample evidence supporting contact theory, the social science theory positing that intergroup contact can reduce prejudice [151–155]. To improve attitudes towards the population and/or GAP practices (e.g., acceptability, appropriateness), patient stories were designed to elicit empathy (e.g., about patients’ lived experiences) and demonstrated how GAP practices benefited patients. In addition to targeting several determinants simultaneously, stories leveraged multiple discrete implementation strategies (e.g., involve consumers, simulate change), and often did so in a single story (example in Fig. 4). We encourage researchers to co-create, utilize, and evaluate stakeholder stories specific to their health equity population, as they may be an especially efficient and effective training tool and may represent a yet-overlooked implementation strategy.

Takeaway 4: teaching implementers how to build implementation strategies can overcome resource-constraints

Though inner-setting implementation strategies may be important, they are often costly. The identified implementation strategies that we did not build due to financial and personnel constraints targeted inner-setting determinants (e.g., change physical space; see Fig. 3). Three of the four built inner-level strategies were also limited in scope, such that they could only benefit providers working in the partner agency (the exception was CEs). To maximize the potential scalability of built inner-setting implementation strategies, our training offer guidance on building these implementation strategies as an implementer. Teaching implementers how to build and/or advocate for inner-setting-level implementation strategies is likely a cost-effective and scalable alternative to building implementation strategies.

Takeaway 5: inner-setting implementation strategies are critical for health equity implementation

Implementation researchers consistently highlight the necessity of inner-setting-level (e.g., clinic, hospital) implementation strategies [156]. In this study, at least one organizational strategy was necessary for comprehensive GAP adoption: changing record systems, which involved modifying the partner agency’s electronic health record system to enhance the inclusivity of patients’ name and gender options (details in Table 3 and Additional file 6). This strategy was necessary because GAP practices include asking and recording a patient’s affirmed name, pronouns, and gender (i.e., aligning with one’s true identity; [43]). If providers could not record these data, they could not fully adopt GAP. While the other implementation strategies (mandate change, champions, alter incentives) may not be critical for GAP adoption, each targeted inner-setting determinants (work climate and funding). Echoing others, health equity intervention implementation may be especially dependent on implementation strategies targeting inner-setting determinants like workplace climate (e.g., organizational support for equity and justice) and funding to support new programs (e.g., bias training [10, 106]).

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several important strengths. First, it illustrates how to identify implementation determinants and build targeted implementation strategies to address a major health equity problem. Many commentaries make recommendations for health equity implementation research [2, 8, 10] and some studies have either identified health equity determinants [4, 96, 135] or adapted implementation strategies to address health equity [157–160], yet we are aware of few that achieve both (see exceptions [90–93]). Second, we employed many of the recommended health equity implementation research best practices [10, 115]. For example, we used community-engaged research methods and HCD methods centering the needs of the target population, chose a patient-endorsed intervention, and addressed contextual determinants. Third, we contribute what we believe is the first study to develop implementation strategies based on determinants identified using the HEIF (representing the “facilitation” portion of the HEIF; [4, 106]). Finally, we believe our findings (e.g., determinants, takeaways) and the extensively detailed procedural methods and results (see Additional files) have the potential to be a generalizable resource for other health equity implementation researchers.

Alongside these strengths are a few key limitations. First, we did not collect prioritization and/or feasibility data on implementation determinants and strategies [161, 162] or conduct implementation mapping [163]. Instead, we used qualitative analysis to prioritize determinants and strategies, and HCD methods to prioritize, build, and refine implementation strategies. While our approach has its merits (e.g., stakeholder collaboration), prioritization and feasibility data may have led to different built implementation strategies. Second, we built all implementation strategies that were feasible. While doing so may be appropriate for the development phase, this resulted in using many implementation strategies simultaneously. We will thus be unable to assess which are most potent in our initial evaluation. Future studies should consider using tools like the CFIR-ERIC Implementation Strategy Matching Tool [109, 164] to reduce the number of built implementation strategies [165]. Third, though our training was a blended implementation strategy (implementation intervention), it is possible that additional implementation strategies (e.g., learning collaborative) beyond those identified may be necessary for optimal adoption. Indeed, our stakeholder feedback mirrors prior research finding that training alone is often insufficient for successful implementation [166, 167].

Fourth, the Design/Build phase focused almost exclusively on the implementation intervention (online training). Though the development process for the 4 inner-setting strategies was rigorous, it was highly agency-specific and did not replicate DDBT methods—limiting the generalizability of these strategies. Fifth, the Design/Build process may not be scalable; it required a significant amount of participant time (109 h) with 12 participants engaging across multiple phases. While multi-phase participation helped ensure that stakeholder feedback was accurately implemented across stages, it limited sample diversity. Sixth, our sample was primarily located in the Northeast, a region with relatively low anti-transgender bias [17]. Accordingly, identified determinants and strategies may not be generalizable in less-inclusive regions. Finally, GAP training sustainability is limited by several factors, such as ongoing funding (e.g., online learning management software fees, hosting fees, accreditation fees) and person power from the intervention developer (e.g., staff who respond to trainee inquiries). Theoretically, these resources could be funded by providers and/or clinics paying for continuing education, but the large time-commitment (8 h) hinders training feasibility and uptake. As such, our future research will compare the effectiveness of several shorter trainings consisting of different implementation strategy combinations.

Conclusion

This paper details the rigorous use of best practices in health equity implementation science (e.g., community-engaged methods; [1–5, 10, 104, 105]) to develop targeted multilevel implementation strategies to address a major health equity problem. We also used these methods to develop the intervention [43]- engaging the community across all early stages of intervention development and implementation. To support other health equity implementation researchers, we provide ample detail about the study process and results (see also Additional files). Finally we provide five generalizable takeaways for researchers and implementers invested in promoting the adoption of health equity interventions: (1) implementer bias may be a key barrier, (2) experience with the health equity population may be an important facilitator, (3) stakeholder stories may be an effective training tool, (4) teaching implementers how to build implementation strategies can overcome resource-constraints, and (5) inner-setting implementation strategies are critical. This study provides a template for using community-engaged and HCD methods to successfully identify HEIF determinants and develop targeted multilevel implementation strategies to facilitate the implementation of a health equity intervention.
