Circulating potential biomarkers in polycystic ovary syndrome and gynecologic cancers: Diagnostic insights from a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sandeep Kumar, Sandeep Sisodiya, Jyoti Rani, Priyanka Shah, Chander Prakash Yadav, Komal Shah, Bindiya Gupta, Showket Hussain, Shalini Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological evidence indicates a higher risk of endometrial cancer (EC) and ovarian cancer (OC) in females suffering from Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS), highlighting that these disease groups might be sharing common molecular pathogenesis mechanisms. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the unique and common liquid biopsy-based markers for their diagnostic potential as well as their role in the pathogenesis of PCOS, EC, and OC.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed and Embase databases to identify studies investigating circulating markers, including microRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, circular RNAs, and other non-coding RNAs for their diagnostic potential in PCOS, EC, and OC. A total of 46 studies for PCOS, 27 studies for EC, and 91 studies for OC met the eligibility criteria. Data on diagnostic performance, including area under the curve (AUC with 95% CI), sample size, sensitivity, and specificity, were synthesized from eligible studies, and meta-analysis was performed where feasible.ResultsA wide array of markers was listed along with their diagnostic potential across these disorders. miR-222-3p, miR-4488, and miR-151-5p performed well in PCOS, miR-27a, miR-145, miR-150-5p in EC, and miR-21, miR-1246, miR-193a-5p, miR-200a, circBNC2 and circFOXP1 in OC. Pooled meta-analysis showed promising AUC values for miR-21 (0.81), miR-200a (0.86), miR-200b (0.84), and miR-200c (0.79) in OC, underscoring their strong diagnostic potential. For PCOS and EC, only two studies each were available for miR-223-3p and miR-27a, respectively. In subgroup analysis, miR-21 (0.81), miR-200c (0.78), miR-145 (0.89), and miR-27a (0.75) showed the highest diagnostic performance, but with high heterogeneity and a non-significant subgroup effect.ConclusionCirculating RNAs offer significant diagnostic, prognostic, and risk-stratification potential for PCOS and gynecologic cancers; however, standardized methodologies and large, independent, diverse cohort studies are needed to validate these findings and optimize clinical translation.Trial RegistrationPROSPERO (CRD42024573106)Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01836-7.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading global causes of mortality, responsible for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020, and approximately 19.3 million new incidences annually [1]. Early-stage detection, particularly of aggressive tumors, is associated with significantly improved survival and better quality of life [2]. In contrast, delayed diagnosis leads to poor outcomes, limited treatment efficacy, and higher toxicity from chemotherapy and radiotherapy, further increasing healthcare costs [3, 4]. In this context, disease screening serves as a vital secondary prevention strategy, enabling early detection and timely intervention, which significantly improves patient prognosis and quality of life. Liquid biopsy; the analysis of non-solid biological samples such as blood, urine, saliva, vaginal secretions, and endometrial fluid has emerged as a promising, minimally invasive tool for identifying diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Initially explored in cancers such as breast, prostate, colorectal, and lung, liquid biopsies are now gaining traction in gynecological disorders due to their ability to support repeated sampling, reduce the need for invasive procedures, and facilitate real-time monitoring of disease progression and therapeutic response.

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is among the most common endocrine disturbances that affect women of reproductive age [5]. Pathological manifestations include androgen excess, chronic anovulation, and polycystic ovarian morphology [6]. PCOS not only impacts fertility and quality of life but also poses long-term health risks, including an increased likelihood of developing other metabolic conditions such as diabetes [7] and cardiovascular disease [8]. Importantly, epidemiological evidence had suggested a higher risk of gynecological cancers, particularly endometrial cancer (EC) and ovarian cancer (OC) [9, 10], potentially driven by persistent hormonal imbalances such as unopposed estrogen [11, 12], in women with PCOS, though this relationship remains under investigation [13].

Recent studies implicate common genetic and epigenetic mechanisms, including non-coding RNA (ncRNA) dysregulation, in both PCOS and gynecologic cancers [14, 15]. Among these, miRNAs (microRNAs), lncRNAs (long non-coding RNAs), and circRNAs (circular RNAs) have shown particular promise as circulating biomarkers. This systematic review and meta-analysis examined the diagnostic potential of dysregulated cell-free markers, including non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), as liquid biopsy-based tools for early detection and risk stratification in gynecological cancers. Furthermore, the aim was to identify potential overlapping markers in PCOS and ovarian/endometrial cancer to gain insights into patterns of RNA dysregulation that warrant further investigation.

Materials and methods

A comprehensive literature search and data analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16], and adhering to the predefined protocol registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO - CRD42024573106).

Search strategy

Two different databases, PubMed and Embase, were explored, and a comprehensive search was conducted for related studies as per the protocol registered on PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024573106). Only peer-reviewed and original research articles published in English were selected for review and data synthesis. Date filters: 01st January, 2014 to 30th June, 2024. We limited our search to the studies published between 2014 and 2024, as the majority of research on circulatory non-coding RNA biomarkers in PCOS and gynecological cancers has emerged during this period, reflecting the significant advances in liquid biopsy and next-generation sequencing techniques. Studies prior to 2014 were mostly tissue-based and did not align with our study protocol. Besides, we performed a scoping assessment of studies before 2014 and observed that the studies were limited and did not meet our predefined inclusion criteria. The search strategy employed for PCOS, Ovarian cancer, and Endometrial cancer was as follows:

#1 Polycystic Ovary Syndrome

(“Polycystic Ovary Syndrome” OR “Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome” OR PCOS OR “Polycystic Ovary Disease” OR PCOD OR “Sclerocystic ovar*” OR “Stein-Leventhal syndrome” OR “Stein Leventhal syndrome”) AND (“liquid biopsy” OR circulating OR blood OR plasma OR serum OR urine) AND (microRNA OR “micro RNA” OR ncRNA OR “non-coding RNA” OR “non coding RNA” OR circRNA OR siRNA OR lncRNA OR “long non-coding RNA” OR “long noncoding RNA” OR “differentially expressed gene*” OR “massively parallel sequencing”).

#2 Endometrial cancer

(“Endometrial cancer” OR “Endometrium cancer” OR “Endometrial neoplasm*” OR “Uterine cancer” OR “Uterine neoplasm*” OR “Endometrial Carcinoma” OR “Endometrioid adenocarcinoma” OR “Endometrial adenocarcinoma”) AND (“liquid biopsy” OR circulating OR blood OR plasma OR serum OR urine) AND (microRNA OR “micro RNA” OR ncRNA OR “non-coding RNA” OR “non coding RNA” OR circRNA OR siRNA OR lncRNA OR “long non-coding RNA” OR “long noncoding RNA” OR “differentially expressed gene*” OR “massively parallel sequencing”).

#3 Ovarian cancer

(“Ovarian cancer” OR “Ovarian neoplasm*” OR “Ovarian carcinoma” OR “Epithelial ovarian cancer”) AND (“liquid biopsy” OR circulating OR blood OR plasma OR serum OR urine) AND (microRNA OR “micro RNA” OR ncRNA OR “non-coding RNA” OR “non- coding RNA” OR circRNA OR siRNA OR lncRNA OR “long noncoding RNA” OR “long noncoding RNA” OR “differentially expressed gene” OR “massively parallel sequencing”).

All three search strategies (#1, #2, #3) were individually searched.

Data synthesis strategy

Studies meeting inclusion criteria were grouped by disease condition (PCOS, EC, or OC) and assessed for diagnostic performance metrics such as area under the curve (AUC), fold change (gene expression), and sensitivity/specificity. Pooled meta-analysis was performed where at least three studies were available for the same biomarker using AUC with 95% CI (Confidence Interval) as the primary outcome. In cases where AUC was reported without CI, these were estimated using Hanley and McNeil’s method to enable inclusion. Studies lacking sufficient data for pooling were summarized narratively. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore inter-disease variability and biomarker consistency. A detailed data synthesis strategy covering inclusion/exclusion of eligible studies, data extraction, handling of missing CI values, quality assessment, pooled meta-analysis and subgroup analysis is described below.

Eligible studies – Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Two independent investigators screened the eligible studies based on the following inclusion criteria: (a) Studies that aim to assess the diagnostic potential of genes, miRNAs and lncRNAs under investigation in women with PCOS, EC, and OC, (b) No treatment or intervention has been attempted before, (c) healthy people with no disease symptoms have been included as control, (d) studies which have estimated mean difference or relative fold change in gene expression between cancer patients and healthy controls, or have estimated AUC characteristics with data on sensitivity, and specificity. Exclusion criteria: Reviews, comments, systematic reviews and meta-analysis, abstracts, non-English articles, case studies, case reports, opinion piece, editorials, conference proceedings, non-human studies, studies on cell lines, animal models, interventional studies, and studies on drugs targeting PCOS, EC, and OC, duplicate reports, reports with incomplete information (lack of gene expression data or diagnostic metrices) were excluded.

Data extraction

Studies including their supplementary files that provided information on the list of liquid biopsy-based markers (genes), their mean expression difference or fold change values, and AUC values in the context of PCOS, EC, and OC were selected. Data extraction was done by 2 independent investigators based on inclusion/exclusion criteria which comprised of author’s name, study location, year of publication, study sample size with disease and healthy controls, dysregulated genes, mean expression difference or relative fold change in gene expression, AUC characteristics with data on sensitivity and specificity, and other relevant statistical data. A third reviewer was consulted in case of disagreement between the two, and consensus was reached.

Handling of missing confidence intervals

For some studies, only AUC values were available without accompanying CIs. In such cases, to avoid missing out such studies, we estimated the 95% CIs based on the reported AUC value and sample sizes under diseased and healthy control groups, using the method described by Hanley and McNeil [17]. It allows for calculating the standard error (SE) of the AUC, and subsequently the CI limits (CI = AUC ± 1.96×SECI). This method ensures the inclusion of a greater number of studies in the meta-analysis while maintaining methodological accuracy.

Quality assessment

Included studies were subjected to methodological quality and risk of bias assessment. Two independent investigators performed this assessment, and any disagreements between the investigators were resolved by a third investigator. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for evaluating case-control and cohort studies, focusing on three areas: participant selection, comparability of the study groups, and exposure or outcome assessment. It assesses the studies for 8 different pre-defined sub-parameters as detailed in the (Supplementary material – Tables 1 and 2, and 3). All sub-parameters can be awarded maximum of 1 star except one which can be awarded max of 2 stars. Hence, a maximum scale of 9 can be achieved per study. Each study was scored out of a maximum of nine stars, with higher scores indicating lower risk of bias. In case of cross-sectional studies, the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist was employed, which includes eight items examining aspects such as inclusion criteria, validity, and reliability of exposure and outcome measurements, control of confounders, and the suitability of the statistical methods; hence maximum score of 8 can be achieved per study. A detailed breakdown of the scores under each sub-parameter assessed has been provided as part of the Supplementary material.

Statistical analysis

For the meta-analysis, studies that reported AUC values and included comparisons with healthy controls were considered eligible. The primary outcome was the AUC along with its corresponding 95% CI. A random-effects model was applied irrespective of the degree of heterogeneity. Study variability was assessed using the I² statistic, where value thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% signified low, moderate, and high heterogeneity across studies.

Subgroup analysis

To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity in the diagnostic performance of biomarkers across study groups (PCOS vs. EC, PCOS vs. OC, and EC vs. OC), we conducted a subgroup analysis for biomarkers that were studied between any two or all the disease groups using a random-effects model. Pooled meta-analysis was performed for markers evaluated in at least three studies under respective disease groups, and subgroup analysis was performed for markers that have been assessed across 2 or more disease groups. Pooled AUC values with corresponding 95% CI were calculated. Subgroup effects were tested using subgroup-specific p-values, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Sensitivity and specificity values were collected where available; however, they were not included in the meta-analysis to avoid excluding studies that did not report these metrics. In cases where meta-analysis was not feasible, results were summarized narratively. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (RStudio 2024.12.1 + 563 “Kousa Dogwood” Release, 2025-02-02) for Windows. The following R packages were used: meta, mada, forestplot, and ggplot2. A significance level of 0.05 was applied for all statistical tests.

Dysregulated circulating RNAs in PCOS

In PCOS, we observed a total of 30 unique circulating biomarkers, including miRNAs and lncRNAs, which have been assessed for their diagnostic performance (Supplementary material – Figs. 1 and 2). The AUC, along with 95% CI, sensitivity, and specificity, were extracted to evaluate each biomarker’s discriminative potential. The best performing biomarker was miR-222-3p with an AUC of 0.947 (CI: 0.907–0.998) along with sensitivity and specificity of 0.89 and 0.98, respectively. Similarly, miR-4488 and miR-151a-5p also demonstrated excellent diagnostic potential with both markers showing sensitivities ≥ 0.81.

Among the lncRNAs, SRLR (Sorafenib resistance-associated lncRNA in renal cell carcinoma) and ENST00000550337.1 showed promising diagnostic utility. XIST (X-inactive specific transcript), miR-222, miR-30d-5p, miR-130b, and miR-93-5p showed moderate diagnostic utility with AUC values in the range of 0.70 to 0.84. Although their sensitivity and specificity values did vary, they could be of significant value when used in combination as diagnostic panels. Biomarkers with AUC values below 0.70 were considered to have limited diagnostic potential when used individually.

Interestingly, biomarkers also exhibited sensitivity and specificity variations. MALAT1 (Metastasis Associated Lung Adenocarcinoma Transcript 1) had a specificity of 1.00 but a sensitivity of only 0.37. In contrast, IRS-2 and miR-223-3p exhibited high sensitivities (≥ 0.87) but lower specificities (≤ 0.52).

Dysregulated circulating RNAs in endometrial cancer

In EC, we observed a total of 31 circulating biomarkers that were dysregulated between disease groups and healthy controls (Supplementary material – Figs. 1 and 2). Several miRNAs demonstrated near-perfect diagnostic potential, such as miR-27a and miR-205 (AUC of 1.000; 95% CI: 1.000–1.000), with corresponding sensitivity and specificity values of 1.00. However, these findings were based on studies with small sample sizes, which may overestimate true diagnostic accuracy due to potential overfitting or lack of biological variation. Therefore, it remains to be seen how these markers will perform in larger independent cohorts. miR-29b (AUC: 0.976; 95% CI: 0.951–1.000) and miR-150-5p (AUC: 0.982; 95% CI: 0.955–1.000) also exhibited excellent diagnostic potential, with sensitivity/specificity of 0.96/0.98 and 0.89/1.00, respectively. Other high-performing markers included miR-135b and miR-145. Among other ncRNA, circEPSTI1 (circular RNA derived from Epithelial stromal interaction 1 gene) (AUC: 0.835; 95% CI: 0.794–0.876) exhibited robust discriminatory potential between EC and healthy controls with sensitivity of 0.84, and specificity of 0.86. LncRNAs such as DLEU1 (Deleted in Lymphocytic Leukemia 1) and SLERT (Small nucleolar RNA-ended lncRNA that enhances pre-rRNA transcription) also displayed promising diagnostic potential, but sensitivity figures were moderate for the former (0.77).

Dysregulated circulating RNAs in ovarian cancer

A total of 60 unique biomarkers were listed (Supplementary material – Figs. 1 and 2). Among all, miR-21 demonstrated the highest diagnostic potential with an AUC of 0.990 (95% CI: 0.970–1.000), sensitivity of 0.96, and specificity of 0.88, indicating excellent differentiating ability. Similarly, miR-193a-5p (AUC = 0.979, 95% CI: 0.949–1.000) and miR-1909-5p (AUC = 1.000) gave near-perfect AUC metrics. As observed in case of miR-27a and miR-205 in EC, miR-1909-5p was also investigated in small sample size, which may have inflated its true diagnostic accuracy. miR-1246 (AUC = 0.923), miR-200a (AUC = 0.914), and miR-145 (AUC = 0.910) also showed high diagnostic potential. Several lncRNAs including RP5-837J1 (AUC: 0.996; 95% CI: 0.983–1.000, sensitivity of 0.97, and specificity of 0.95), MALAT1 (0.884) and MIR4435-2HG (0.882) were highly promising as potential diagnostic markers.

Among circRNAs, circBNC2 (circular RNA derived from Basonuclin 2 gene) (AUC = 0.923, 95% CI: 0.882–0.965), circFOXP1 (circular RNA derived from Forkhead box P1 gene) (AUC = 0.915, 95% CI: 0.875–0.955), and circN4BP2L2 (AUC: 0.900, 95% CI: 0.870–0.940, sensitivity of 0.82, and specificity of 0.90) showed the highest diagnostic potential.

Subgroup analysis

Among the 13 biomarkers that were investigated in more than one disease group, subgroup analysis was performed for miR-21, miR-200c, and miR-145 as these were investigated in 3 or more studies (Fig. 3), while rest of the 10 markers were found to be assessed in 2 studies each only (Supplementary material – Figs. 4 and 5). miR-145 showed the highest diagnostic potential (pooled AUC = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.97) with high heterogeneity (I² = 87.3%), but a non-significant subgroup effect. The other two markers also showed high and moderate heterogeneity (miR-21: I² = 94.8%; miR-200c: I² = 59.9%), respectively, but sub-group effects were not significant here either. Risk of bias assessment

All the included case-control and cohort studies were evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, and the minimum score achieved by these studies was 8 out of 9. For the cross-sectional studies, the JBI checklist was employed, with all included studies achieving a minimum score of 7 out of 8. Notably, none of the included studies were found to be at high risk of bias. Given the high methodological quality of the included studies, a sensitivity analysis based on risk of bias was deemed unnecessary. The details of the risk of bias assessment, detailing all the sub-parameters for which the included studies were assessed, are provided as part of Supplementary material – Tables 1 and 2, and 3.

Discussion

Key circulating miRNAs and lncRNAs with potential diagnostic relevance in PCOS

Individually, miR-222-3p emerged as the most promising candidate in PCOS diagnosis, underscoring its potential as a non-invasive diagnostic tool. Interestingly, miR-222-3p has also been implicated in several other cancers, including breast, liver, prostate, and colorectal cancers, where it plays roles in promoting oncogenesis, angiogenesis, and resistance to therapy [70]. In breast cancer, for example, miR-222-3p is known to target cell cycle inhibitors such as p27 and p57, contributing to uncontrolled cell proliferation [71]. Functionally, it has been shown to interact negatively with Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α), thereby contributing to dysregulated glucose and lipid metabolism [72].

Furthermore, miR-4488 and miR-151a-5p also showed strong diagnostic potential with their levels positively correlated with key reproductive hormones, including anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH), Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and Luteinizing hormone (LH), highlighting their potential roles in the hormonal dysregulation of PCOS. Besides, their target genes have also been found to be involved in metabolic processes, cellular growth, and development, further implicating them in the complex metabolic and reproductive disturbances observed in PCOS [53]. Prior evidences have also linked miR-4488 to glioblastoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, where it promotes tumor cell migration by downregulating Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog Deleted on chromosome 10 (PTEN), suppressing E-cadherin (CDH1), and modulating the expression of Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), thereby facilitating Epithelial to Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) [73, 74]. miR-151a-5p has been implicated in gastric, colorectal, and hepatocellular cancers, promoting tumor progression via the PI3K/AKT, mTOR, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways [75]. It also targets PTEN and Wnt signaling inhibitors like Naked cuticle 1 (NKD1) and Glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK3β), enhancing β-catenin activity, and contributing to metastasis and chemoresistance [76]. These evidences suggest that these miRNAs may share regulatory roles in PCOS and certain cancers, and may have potential relevance as biomarkers. However, further studies are needed to clarify their specificity, functional roles, and broader diagnostic or prognostic utility.

Furthermore, combining miRNAs has shown greater promise. For example, miR‑151a‑5p, miR‑4488, and miR‑223‑3p together yielded high diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity: 0.92, specificity: 0.93) [53]. Also, miR-142-3p and miR-598-3p together demonstrated a stronger diagnostic performance for hyperandrogenic PCOS, and targeted genes were observed to be involved in metabolism, gene regulation, and stress response [77]. Several other miRNAs associated with insulin signaling, steroid biosynthesis, and folliculogenesis were also found to be dysregulated in PCOS [78]. Reduced expression levels of miR-24 also showed good discriminatory performance. Its target is CYP11A1 (Cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1) [79], which is involved in steroidogenesis and contributes to hyperandrogenism in PCOS [80]. Given PCOS’s hormonal and metabolic complexity, miRNAs regulating insulin signaling are of interest. miR-483 and miR-486, targeting Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 Receptor (IGF1R) and Phosphoinositide 3 kinase regulatory subunit 1 (PIK3R1), were significantly decreased in PCOS, correlating with PI3K/Akt pathway activation [81].

Recently, lncRNAs have emerged as promising candidates for biomarker development owing to their remarkable stability in blood and encapsulation within exosomes [82]. Among these, XIST, SRLR, ENST00000550337.1, GAS5 (Growth arrest specific 5), CTBP1-AS (Antisense long non-coding RNA derived from the C-terminal binding protein 1 gene) were observed to be of significant interest in our study. XIST, a well-characterized lncRNA downregulated in several cancers (including cervical, ovarian, and breast cancer), has emerged as a unique discriminator for PCOS compared to other gynecological disorders [83, 84]. ROC (Receiver-operating characteristic curve) analyses indicated that circulating XIST RNA can effectively distinguish PCOS patients from healthy controls [54]. It role in regulating tumor suppressor gene silencing via chromatin remodeling links it to multiple cancers [85]. Additionally, SRLR and ENST00000550337.1 demonstrated promising diagnostic performance, with AUC values exceeding 0.84, and are also implicated in oncogenic processes e.g., SRLR has been shown to promote sorafenib resistance in renal cell carcinoma via IL-6/STAT3 signaling, indicating its involvement in inflammatory and proliferative pathways relevant to both cancer and PCOS pathophysiology [86].

GAS5, typically downregulated in tumors, where it functions as a tumor suppressor [87], was observed to be significantly upregulated in PCOS patients [56], suggesting its potential as a biomarker [88]. NEAT1 (Nuclear paraspeckle assembly transcript 1) and MALAT1 regulate PCOS-associated genes by sponging miR-30d-5p and miR-30a-5p respectively, thereby influencing Androgen Receptor (AR), Follistatin (FST), and Insulin Receptor Substrate 2 (IRS-2) expression [57]. These interactions underscore their regulatory roles and diagnostic potential when assessed in combination with target miRNAs [59]. CTBP1-AS has been observed to be consistently overexpressed in PCOS cohorts from both China and India [89, 90], and may regulate AR through CTBP1 repression. Other transcripts like ENST00000550337.1, circ_RANBP9 and LINC00477 were also upregulated in PCOS, though their diagnostic performance remains to be validated.

Candidate circulating miRNAs with diagnostic significance in endometrial and ovarian cancer

In EC, miR-27a and miR-150-5p showed exceptional diagnostic potential (AUCs of 1.0 and 0.982, respectively) [69]. The AUC value reported for mR-27a was derived from a small sample size and needs to be validated in larger independent population to confirm its diagnostic potential. miR-27a has also been found to be upregulated in EC tissues, and is reported to promote tumor growth by inhibiting pro-apoptotic genes (FOXO, BAX) and increasing BCL2/BAX ratio [91]. While miR-150-5p has been found to be elevated in cervical squamous cell carcinoma as well, miR-27a appears downregulated in cervical cancer, suggesting cancer-specific roles [92].

Mechanistically, mir-27 has also been shown to influence the PI3K/Akt pathway and Wnt/β-catenin signaling which are central to cell growth and survival by targeting PTEN. In OC, miR-27a was shown to regulate the TGF-β signaling (via targeting SMAD2), which favors tumor metastasis by regulating EMT. It is also implicated in the regulation of androgen biosynthesis by targeting the CYP17A1 (Cytochrome P450 17A1) [93]. miR-150-5p targets several cancer-associated genes, including P2X7, MUC4, ZEB1, and SRCIN1, and modulates pathways such as AKT and NF-κB, suppressing MMP-9 and tumor migration.

miR-145, a key member of the miR-143/145 cluster, has emerged as a promising biomarker for EC diagnosis. This cluster is typically co-expressed across various cancer types [94] and contributes to disease pathophysiology by regulating targets such as MAPK-7, KRAS, JAM (Junctional adhesion molecule A), and Plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 [95]. Further, it exerts strong tumor suppressor effects by targeting oncogenic drivers such as EGFR, SOX2, and c-Myc, leading to inhibition of the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways, regulating MMPs, and inhibition of EMT [96].

For OC, miR-21, and miR-193a-5p emerged as potential diagnostic biomarkers with high discriminative performance. miR-21 exhibited the highest diagnostic accuracy, reinforcing its role as a well-established onco-miRNA across diverse cancer types and in PCOS as well. By targeting key tumor suppressors such as PTEN, PDCD4, and TPM1, it enhances PI3K/Akt signaling, MAPK pathway, and JNK-1/c-Jun pathway leading to increased cellular proliferation and invasion [97]. miR-1246 also showed strong diagnostic potential [98]. It targets tumor suppressors such as TP53INP1 and DYRK1A, facilitating EMT via Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β pathways, thereby promoting tumor growth [99], and is abundant in circulating exosomes [100] On the contrary, miR-193a-5p functions primarily as a tumor suppressor, targeting KRAS, ERBB4, and MCL1, thereby attenuating cell cycle progression and EMT [101].

Another significant group, the miR-200 family (comprising miR-200a, miR-200b, and miR-200c) showed significant diagnostic potential in both EC and OC. They act as tumor suppressors by directly targeting key ZEB1 and ZEB2 transcription factors (Zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox), which are essential for the repression of E-cadherin, a hallmark of EMT [102]. miR-200a particularly plays a dual role in regulating EMT by directly inhibiting ZEB1/2, thereby preserving E-cadherin expression, and modulating TGF-β and Notch signaling [103]. These miRNAs are highly stable and detectable in plasma, making them suitable for non-invasive diagnostics.

As seen for PCOS, combination of miRNAs was observed to deliver improved diagnostic performance. A panel of 6-miRNAs including, miR-204-5p, miR-195-5p, miR-143-3p, miR-20b-5p, miR-484, and miR-423-3p was shown to demonstrate high diagnostic value for EC [60]. A plasma miRNA panel: miR-151a-5p, miR-142-3p, and miR-146a-5p was shown to have a combined AUC of 0.789 for EC diagnosis [104].

Diagnostically relevant circulating lncRNAs and circRNAs in endometrial and ovarian cancer

Among lncRNAs, RP5-837J1, MALAT1, and MIR4435-2HG emerged as promising candidates for biomarker development in OC. A novel lncRNA, SLERT, was observed to be a promising non-invasive diagnostic biomarker (AUC = 0.84) in EC [67]. SLERT has been observed to promote EC metastasis by inducing EMT. It binds to METTL3 (Methyltransferase-like 3), facilitating its association with BDNF mRNA (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor), thereby enhancing BDNF expression and activating the oncogenic BDNF/TrkB pathway. Another lncRNA, DLEU1, was markedly upregulated in both tissues and serum of EC patients, with levels correlating with advanced disease stage and poor clinical outcomes. Mechanistically, it promotes EC cell proliferation and invasion by sponging miR-490 and regulating SP1 (Specificity Protein 1) [68].

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) represent a notable class of ncRNAs, gaining attention for their diagnostic potential [105]. Characterized by a covalently closed loop structure, they exhibit exceptional stability, resistance to degradation, and have been implicated in the regulation of key biological processes, including cell differentiation, glycolysis, survival, proliferation, and migration. Among these, circEPSTI1 has shown promising diagnostic potential in EC [66]. Although, its functional role in EC is still under investigation, it has been observed to target miRNAs, including miR-145, miR-874, and several genes, including EGFR, ELK1, FOXM1, etc., influencing major cancer-related signaling pathways (EGFR/MAPK/ERK, PI3K/Akt signaling, EMT cascade, JAK/STAT3 pathway, Wnt/β-catenin signaling), thereby altering the tumor progression, invasion, EMT, and chemoresistance [106–109].

Other circRNAs like circBNC2, circFOXP1, and circN4BP2L2 were also identified as potential early diagnostic markers. circBNC2 is particularly downregulated in OC and PCOS, implicating its loss in follicular dysregulation and tumor suppression [110]. circFOXP1 and circN4BP2L2, by acting as sponges for tumor-suppressive miRNAs (miR-145 and miR-129) upregulate oncogenes including FOXP1, BCL2, and activating PI3K/Akt pathway [111]. circFOXP1 has also been proposed to sponge tumor-suppressive miR-22 and miR-150-3p [18]. These circRNAs remain underexplored in gynecological cancers, and warrant further exploration for their biomarker potential.

Subgroup analysis showed that miR-21, miR-200c, and miR-145 have high diagnostic accuracy but with high heterogeneity and non-significant subgroup effect, which was probably due to a lack of sufficient studies and inter-study differences. Other biomarkers that showed promise but could not be included in the main meta-analysis due to lack of sufficient studies have been summarized in the Supplementary material – Figs. 4 and 5. Significant subgroup p-values suggest that their diagnostic ability might vary depending on the study sample size, type of sample utilized, and test protocol used for analysis. On the other hand, markers such as miR-93, miR-375, and miR-142-3p showed low heterogeneity, indicating their potential diagnostic significance across different clinical settings. Due to the limited number of studies (< 10) for significant markers in respective disease groups, we could not perform further subgroup analysis or meta-regression analysis to find out what actually caused these differences. The individual marker variations observed (as seen for miR-21) across different studies might have stemmed from differences in the study design, population ethnic variations, or platforms used for sequencing. Larger studies in independent, multi-centre cohorts, and diverse populations are essential to validate these findings before clinical application, or drawing any inferences regarding potentially shared pathogenesis mechanisms.

To summarize, this is perhaps the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis that evaluated the diagnostic potential of dysregulated circulating ncRNAs including miRNAs, lncRNAs, and circRNAs in PCOS, EC, and OC. In addition, the study identified shared circulating markers and explored their functional relevance based on existing evidences, highlighting candidates that merit further investigation. The results may guide further functional validation studies that may open up common pathogenic mechanisms and development of innovative diagnostic solutions. From a clinician’s point of view, these biomarkers, if validated in larger cohorts could support early diagnosis, personalized risk stratification, therapeutic response monitoring, and inform clinical decision-making. Besides, in conditions like Ovarian cancer, where tumor relapse is frequent, and repeated sampling is required, tissue biopsies are not feasible. In such cases, non-invasive sampling and assessment for these biomarkers offer a more convenient alternative for disease monitoring and management.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of circulating RNA markers for their diagnostic potential in PCOS, EC, and OC. It highlights candidate markers relevant to both disease-specific and potentially shared pathogenic mechanisms, offering insights that could aid in the development of precision diagnostics and novel therapeutic targets. A key strength of this systematic review and meta-analysis is the high methodological quality of the included studies. All studies met predefined quality thresholds, with appropriate control selection, adjustment for confounders, and robust exposure assessment. This reduces the risk of bias and enhances the reliability of our findings. In contrast, inclusion of low-quality studies could have led to inflated effect sizes and reduced generalizability.

However, this study also has limitations. There was high heterogeneity in sample size, sample type, and detection methods. Studies originated from varied geographic regions, limiting generalizability. Differences in RNA extraction and quality control protocols may also have influenced results. Additionally, we did not assess marker expression across disease stages, which was outside the scope of this meta-analysis. The dynamic nature of RNA expression may impact stage-specific relevance. Furthermore, only peer-reviewed English-language articles were included, possibly introducing language and publication bias. However, we did not encounter any relevant non-English peer-reviewed article during screening that met our inclusion criteria. Moreover, certain markers reported high diagnostic potential, but were assessed in small sample sizes, which may reflect overestimated diagnostic accuracy.

Conclusions

This comprehensive meta-analysis has compiled information on promising circulating RNAs that might have diagnostic potential in PCOS and EC/OC. Several candidates demonstrated high AUC values and consistent detectability in the peripheral circulation, underscoring their importance as non-invasive tools for early detection, monitoring disease progression, and therapeutic response. These findings need to be supported by larger studies in diverse populations with multiple markers before clinical translation.
