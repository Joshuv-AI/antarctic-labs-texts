In silico design and characterization of a novel multi-epitope mRNA vaccine candidate against Streptococcus pneumoniae
Authors: Jahangir Sabzevari, Omid Ali Adeli, Mona Shafaghi, Zohreh Bahadori, Mohammad Mehdi Ranjbar, Seyed Masoud Hosseini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStreptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) is a major cause of pneumonia, meningitis, bacteremia, and secondary infections following viral respiratory diseases such as influenza and COVID-19. Because current polysaccharide-based vaccines protect only against selected serotypes, there is a pressing need for serotype-independent strategies. In this study, a novel multi-epitope mRNA vaccine candidate against S. pneumoniae was designed using immunoinformatics approaches. The immunodominant regions of PsaA and PspA were fused with flexible linkers, and TLR agonist domains derived from Ply and PepO were incorporated as adjuvants to enhance immune activation. The resulting construct, named YAPO, was predicted to possess favorable physicochemical and immunological properties, including stability, solubility, antigenicity, non-allergenicity, and non-toxicity. Additional analyses—including IFN-γ epitope prediction, conformational B-cell epitope mapping, HLA docking, vaccine–TLR docking, molecular dynamics, and immune simulations— indicated potential to induce robust immune responses. The mRNA sequence was engineered with essential regulatory elements (5′ cap, UTRs, Kozak sequence, signal peptide, and poly(A) tail) to promote efficient expression, and codon optimization suggested compatibility with mammalian translation. In silico cloning into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector further supported potential construct feasibility. Overall, these findings highlight that YAPO-mRNA is a promising serotype-independent pneumococcal vaccine candidate that merits further experimental validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33595-2.Keywords: Pneumococcal surface adhesion a (PsaA), Pneumococcal surface protein a (PspA), Pneumolysin (Ply), Pneumococcal endopeptidase o (PepO), Immunoinformatics, Pneumococcal mRNA vaccineSubject terms: Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Streptococcus pneumoniae remains a major cause of fatal pneumonia, meningitis, and secondary infections following influenza or COVID-191. With an annual death toll surpassing one million—disproportionately affecting infants and the elderly—developing effective vaccines is crucial2. Current vaccines fall into two categories: unconjugated polysaccharide vaccines and protein-conjugated polysaccharides, each with distinct limitations3. The 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine offers broad serotype coverage but is less effective in young children due to its T cell-independent mechanism4. Conversely, conjugate vaccines elicit stronger immunity in high-risk groups but face challenges such as high cost, limited serotype coverage, and potential serotype replacement5. Highly conserved pneumococcal surface proteins, expressed throughout infection, represent promising serotype-independent vaccine targets due to their antigenic potential. Among these proteins, the most extensively studied are PsaA and PspA5.

PsaA, a highly conserved surface lipoprotein present in all S. pneumoniae strains, demonstrates promising efficacy in preclinical and clinical studies. As a key virulence factor, it facilitates manganese acquisition—critical for bacterial growth and replication—and enhances adherence to host epithelial cells, underscoring its central role in pathogenesis1,6. PspA, another well-studied antigen expressed in all clinical isolates, aids immune evasion by preventing complement deposition and neutralizing lactoferrin-derived peptides7,8. Structurally, PspA contains three domains (Supplementary Figure S1): an N-terminal α-helical domain (amino acids 1–288, subdivided into A, A′, and B regions), a central proline-rich domain (C region, 289–370), and a C-terminal choline-binding domain (371–571) responsible for bacterial surface anchoring9. The exposed α-helical and proline-rich regions trigger host immune responses, with the α-helical domain being particularly variable and immunogenic; protective epitopes are concentrated in its first and last ~ 100 residues (the A and B regions)10–12.

Adjuvants have long been used to enhance vaccine immunogenicity, with toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists recently emerging as leading candidates13,14. Certain bacterial proteins can activate TLR pathways and act as protein-based adjuvants. Unlike non-protein ligands, they can be engineered to improve immune stimulation while reducing toxicity, and when fused with antigens, they ensure coordinated delivery to immune cells, thereby strengthening both innate and adaptive responses14. Pneumococcal proteins such as Ply and PepO exemplify this potential1,15. Ply’s domain 4 activates TLR4 and induces hemolysis; notably, a triple mutation reducing its cytolytic activity enhances its suitability as a TLR agonist16,17. Similarly, the N-terminal domain of PepO can bind both TLR2 and TLR4, further supporting its role as a promising adjuvant18.

Multi-epitope vaccines offer targeted, balanced immune responses with minimal side effects, aided by immunoinformatics and computational design19,20. Peptide vaccines often show low immunogenicity, and DNA vaccines risk genomic integration, whereas mRNA vaccines are non-infectious, do not integrate into the host genome, rapidly producible, and elicit strong humoral and cellular immunity. A single mRNA can encode multiple epitopes, broadening protection21. Preclinical and clinical data confirm their safety and efficacy, establishing mRNA vaccines as versatile platforms for both preventive and therapeutic use, surpassing those of traditional subunit, peptide, and DNA approaches22.

Building on the success of COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, this platform can be rapidly adapted to pneumococcal antigens. To date, no mRNA-based pneumococcal vaccine has entered clinical trials, though feasibility is being explored23. A preclinical mRNA vaccine targeting Streptococcus pneumoniae has recently been described; however, these efforts have not utilized immunoinformatics-driven, multi-epitope rational design strategies24. In this study, one of the first multi-epitope mRNA vaccine candidates against S. pneumoniae is presented, comprising immunodominant epitopes from PsaA and PspA along with adjuvant domains from Ply and PepO. Designed via immunoinformatics and structural analyses, it aims to provide serotype-independent protection and serves as a basis for future in vitro and in vivo validation.

Sequence retrieval and primary analysis

Amino acid sequences of PsaA (ID: AAF70663.1), PspA (ID: AAF27710.1), Ply (ID: ABJ53672.1), and PepO (ID: ABJ55398.1) were retrieved from NCBI in FASTA format. PsaA and PspA were analyzed for B- and Th-cell epitopes, while Ply (TLR4 agonist) and PepO (TLR2/TLR4 agonist) were considered as adjuvants. Membrane topology of PsaA and PspA was predicted using TMHMM v2.025, and signal peptides were identified using SignalP 4.126.

Protein 3D structure retrieval and prediction

Conformational B-cell epitope prediction requires 3D protein structures. The experimentally resolved structure of PsaA was obtained from RCSB PDB (ID: 1PSZ)27. Since no structure exists for PspA, its 3D model was generated using the I-TASSER online server28 and refined with ModRefiner29 and GalaxyRefine30 tools. Model quality was assessed using PROCHECK31 and ERRAT32 (SAVES v6.1 server), and final structures were visualized in BIOVIA Discovery Studio.

Prediction of B-cell epitopes

Linear B-cell epitopes in PsaA and PspA were predicted using LBTope33 (SVM-based, ~ 81% accuracy) and ABCpred34 (RNN-based, ~ 66% accuracy), selected for their complementary machine-learning frameworks, high reliability, and rapid runtime (< 5 min)1. Surface accessibility was evaluated using the IEDB Emini tool35. Conformational epitopes were derived from 3D structures (PsaA: PDB 1PSZ; PspA: I-TASSER model) with Ellipro36, which integrates solvent accessibility, residue flexibility, and protrusion indices, and DiscoTope 2.037, which identifies discontinuous epitopes based on half-sphere exposure and propensity scores. All servers were run with default parameters.

Prediction of CD4 + T cell epitopes

CD4⁺ T-cell epitopes binding to MHC-II molecules were predicted using NetMHCIIpan 4.038 and IEDB MHC-II predictor39, both selected for their high accuracy and consensus-based reliability. NetMHCIIpan’s pan-allelic neural-network model enables identification of strong (rank < 1%) and weak (rank < 5%) binders, whereas the IEDB tool evaluates 15-mer peptides overlapping by 10 amino acids, with lower percentile ranks indicating stronger affinity. Eight common human HLA-DR alleles (01:01, 03:01, 04:01, 07:01, 08:01, 11:01, 13:01, 15:01) and three mouse alleles (H2-IEd, H2-IAb, H2-IAd) were analyzed. All tools were used with default parameters.

Design of the final mRNA vaccine construct

Peptides containing high-scoring B- and T-cell epitopes were selected for the multi-peptide construct, with linkers used to maintain immunogenicity: GGGS for epitopes from the same protein and GGSSGG between PsaA and PspA peptides. Adjuvant domains—Ply domain 4 and PepO amino acids 1–112—were fused to the N- and C-termini via EAAAK linkers, and a C-terminal 6×His tag was added. The multi-peptide construct was named “YAPO.”

For the mRNA vaccine (YAPO-mRNA), key regulatory elements were incorporated: a 5′ cap for stability and ribosomal binding; an untranslated region (UTR) derived from the cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate early 1 (IE1) gene at the 5′ end and a segment of the human growth hormone (hGH) 3′ UTR to enhance translation40,41; a Kozak sequence for initiation; and the tPA signal peptide (UniProt: P00750) to promote secretion and antigen presentation. A ~ 120-nucleotide poly(A) tail was added to improve stability and half-life.

Analysis of the properties of construct

Physicochemical properties of the YAPO construct (molecular weight, amino acid length, pI, instability index, aliphatic index, GRAVY) were analyzed using ExPASy ProtParam42. SOLpro predicted solubility upon E. coli expression43. Antigenicity was evaluated with VaxiJen v2.044 and ANTIGENpro45, allergenicity with AllerTOP v2.146, and toxicity using ToxinPred47, all with default parameters.

Secondary structure prediction of the construct

The secondary structure of the developed construct was predicted using the GOR IV48 and PSIPRED v4.049 servers, with all server settings kept at their default values.

3D modeling and validation of the construct

The 3D structure of the YAPO construct was modeled using Robetta server50, with refinement via GalaxyRefine. Structures were visualized in Discovery Studio Visualizer and validated using PROCHECK, ERRAT (SAVES v6.1 server), and ProSA server51.

Prediction of interferon-gamma inducing peptides

IFN-γ-inducing T-helper epitopes were predicted using IFNepitope52, which employs a curated dataset of MHC-II binding peptides with known IFN-γ response profiles.

Identification of conformational B-cell epitopes

Conformational B-cell epitopes within the refined 3D model of the multi-epitope vaccine were predicted using ElliPro (default settings) to identify potent, structurally dispersed epitopes for enhanced immunogenicity.

Molecular docking of predicted T-cell epitopes with HLA alleles

Molecular docking was performed to assess binding affinity between selected T-cell epitopes (from PsaA and PspA) and HLA alleles. Epitope 3D structures were generated using PEP-FOLD4 online tool53. Docking was conducted with HLA-DRB1*15:01 (PDB: 8TBP), HLA-DRB1*11:01 (PDB: 6CPL), and HLA-DPB1*04:01 (PDB: 5NI9) using HDOCK server54. HLA structures were prepared in UCSF Chimera, and results were analyzed with PDBsum tool55 and visualized in Discovery Studio.

Molecular docking of the construct with TLR receptors

Protein-protein docking was performed using ClusPro server56 to evaluate binding between the vaccine and TLR2 (PDB: 6NIG) and TLR4 (PDB: 3FXI). Structures were prepared in UCSF Chimera, with the vaccine as ligand and TLRs as receptors. Complexes were visualized in Discovery Studio, and interactions analyzed via PDBsum.

Molecular dynamics simulation using iMODS

The iMODS server57 was used to analyze molecular dynamics and assess the stability of protein structures and complexes by simulating their interactions in biological systems.

Molecular dynamics simulation using GROMACS

Molecular dynamics simulations were carried out using the GROMACS 2018 software suite58. System topologies were prepared according to the AMBER03 force field parameters. Each complex was positioned at the center of a dodecahedral periodic simulation box and subsequently solvated with TIP3P water molecules. System neutrality was ensured by substituting an appropriate number of water molecules with Na⁺ or Cl⁻ counterions. Prior to equilibration, energy minimization was performed using the steepest-descent algorithm to remove steric clashes and relax the initial configuration. The minimized systems were then equilibrated in two sequential steps: a 100 ps NVT ensemble at 298 K, followed by a 100 ps NPT ensemble maintained at 1 bar. Long-range electrostatic interactions were computed with the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, while covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Finally, a 10 ns production MD run was conducted under fully unrestrained conditions to monitor the structural and dynamic behavior of the complexes throughout the simulation.

Simulation of the immune system response

Immune response simulation for the vaccine construct was performed using C-ImmSim server59. Three doses were administered at 4-week intervals (simulated at 0, 84, and 168 h), with each time point representing 8 h. Default parameters were applied throughout.

Codon optimization and sequence analysis

The mRNA sequence was codon-optimized for human expression using the GenSmart Codon Optimization Tool (GenScript) and further analyzed with the Rare Codon Analysis Tool. Key parameters included GC content (optimal: 30–70%, reflecting mRNA stability and transcriptional efficiency), Codon Adaptation Index (CAI > 0.8, indicating high expression potential), and Codon Frequency Distribution (CFD, detecting rare or repetitive codons).

Prediction of mRNA secondary structure

The optimized coding sequence with its regulatory elements was converted into RNA using the DNA↔RNA→Protein tool. Structural stability was then assessed through RNAfold algorithm (ViennaRNA Package 2.0), which predicts minimum free energy (MFE) and centroid conformations, providing insight into the stability and translational potential of the mRNA construct.

In silico cloning of the mRNA vaccine

The pcDNA3.1(+) vector was selected as the backbone for in vitro transcription. The vaccine construct was inserted into the multiple cloning site (MCS) via NheI/NotI restriction sites using GenSmart Design tool for in silico cloning simulation.

Results

Extraction of protein sequences and preliminary analysis

Amino acid sequences of PsaA, PspA, Ply, and PepO were retrieved from GenBank in FASTA format (Supplementary Table S1). PsaA and three regions (A, B, C) of PspA (family 1, Clade 2) were used for B-cell and T-helper epitope prediction. The A, B, and C regions of PspA were selected due to their surface exposure, variability, and concentration of protective epitopes, which allow targeted immune responses while avoiding non-immunogenic segments of the full-length protein. Ply domain 4 (residues 360–471) was used as a TLR4 agonist, with mutations (Asp385Asn, Cys428Gly, Trp433Phe) introduced to abolish toxicity. A PepO fragment (residues 1–112), implicated in TLR2/4 interaction, was incorporated to further boost immunogenicity. SignalP analysis was used to identify and remove signal peptides in PsaA and PspA (Supplementary Figure S2). TMHMM analysis confirmed the absence of transmembrane helices in these proteins (Supplementary Figure S3).

Prediction, refinement, and validation of the 3D structure of PspA

The tertiary structure of PspA was generated with I-TASSER, and the top-ranked model was refined using ModRefiner and GalaxyRefine (Supplementary Figure S4-A). Validation with PROCHECK and ERRAT confirmed its reliability (Supplementary Figure S4B,C). The Ramachandran plot showed 82.1% residues in favorable regions, while ERRAT yielded an overall quality factor of 88.69%, indicating an acceptable model.

Identification of B-cell epitopes

Linear B-cell epitopes of PsaA and PspA were predicted using LBTope, ABCpred, Ellipro, and the Emini tool in IEDB, while discontinuous epitopes were identified via Ellipro and DiscoTope based on 3D structures. Predicted epitopes of PsaA are listed in Supplementary Tables S2–S3, and those in PspA regions A and B in Tables S4–S5. For region C of PspA, only experimentally validated epitopes from recent studies were included (Supplementary Table S6)10,60,61.

Identification of MHC-II epitopes

MHC-II epitopes of PsaA and PspA were predicted using IEDB and NetMHCIIpan for eight common human and three murine alleles. Epitopes with strong affinity (percentile rank < 10.0 in IEDB; %Rank < 1.0 in NetMHCIIpan) were retained. Predicted epitopes of PsaA and PspA (regions A and B) are listed in Supplementary Tables S7 and S8.

Development of the final mRNA vaccine construct

Epitopes consistently predicted with high scores across multiple B-cell and MHC-II prediction servers were considered, and their surface exposure and structural context in the 3D protein models were further assessed to finalize the immunodominant regions for construct design. The selected peptide sequences from PsaA and PspA (regions A–C) are listed in Table 2. Sequences from the same protein were joined with GGGS linkers, while GGSSGG linkers connected PsaA- and PspA-derived peptides, ensuring flexibility and correct folding. To enhance immunogenicity, pneumolysin domain 4 (112 aa, containing mutations D385N, C428G, W433F; Table 2) was fused at the N-terminus via an EAAAK linker, and the PepO fragment (1–112 aa; Table 2) was attached at the C-terminus with the same linker. A 6xHis tag plus glycine was also added for purification and detection. The resulting YAPO construct, comprising Ply, PsaA, PspA, and PepO fragments, is 482 aa in length (Fig. 2). For efficient expression, the mRNA was organized as: 5′ m7G cap–5′ UTR–Kozak sequence–signal peptide–YAPO construct–stop codon–3′ UTR–poly(A) tail, as shown in Fig. 2. For reproducibility, the full-length mRNA and protein sequences of the YAPO construct are provided in Supplementary Files S1 and S2 in FASTA format.

Evaluation of various properties of the YAPO construct

The YAPO construct’s physicochemical properties were analyzed via ProtParam (Table 3). Its molecular weight was 53.6 kDa, suitable for purification and vaccine development (< 110 kDa). The construct was acidic (pI 4.91), stable (instability index 34.53), thermally resistant (aliphatic index 68.09), and hydrophilic (negative GRAVY), indicating potentially high solubility. Predicted half-lives were 30 h in mammals, > 20 h in yeast, and > 10 h in E. coli. Furthermore, the construct’s solubility, antigenicity, allergenicity, and toxicity were assessed (Table 3). It was predicted to be highly soluble upon expression in E. coli. VaxiJen and ANTIGENpro scores (0.65 and 0.93) indicated strong antigenicity. AllerTOP analysis classified the protein as non-allergenic, and ToxinPred predicted it to be non-toxic for animals and humans.

3D structure modeling, refinement, and validation of YAPO

The 3D structure of YAPO was predicted using Robetta, with model 1 was selected and refined via GalaxyRefine. Five refined models were evaluated (Table 4), and model 3, with the highest Rama favored value, was chosen for further analysis (Fig. 3C). Model quality was assessed pre- and post-refinement using Ramachandran plots, ERRAT, and ProSA Z-scores (Fig. 4; Table 5). The refined model showed 92.1% residues in favored, 6.2% in allowed, and 1.7% in disallowed regions (initial: 89.8%, 8.6%, 1.7%). ProSA Z-score improved slightly from − 8.41 to -8.29, within the range of natural proteins. ERRAT scores remained high at 99.15, confirming overall model quality.

Prediction of IFN-γ-inducing peptides in YAPO

The IFNepitope server identified potential IFN-γ-inducing epitopes in YAPO. Discussion

Due to the high cost and limited serotype coverage of pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccines, research has focused on serotype-independent approaches63. Vaccines based on recombinant proteins, peptides, or DNA, targeting conserved pneumococcal antigens, enable cross-serotype protection15,64. Fusion proteins enhance immunogenicity compared with single-antigen formulations and streamline production and quality control4,65. Nevertheless, recombinant protein/peptide vaccines often show limited immunogenicity, while DNA vaccines raise genomic integration concerns. In contrast, mRNA vaccines offer strong immunogenicity with excellent safety, being non-infectious, degradable, and chemically modifiable to improve stability and translation21. Their cell-free production allows rapid, scalable, and cost-effective manufacture, as evidenced by robust immune responses during the COVID-19 pandemic41.

Unlike earlier protein-, peptide-, and DNA-based pneumococcal vaccines2,64, this study reports, to our knowledge, one of the first mRNA vaccines targeting Streptococcus pneumoniae. The construct was designed using a comprehensive immunoinformatics pipeline, representing a new direction for pneumococcal vaccine development. In silico approaches accelerate research, improve analytical accuracy, and allow evaluation of multiple scenarios before costly lab experiments66–68. Immunoinformatics predicts immune responses and guides selection of potent B- and T-cell epitopes to elicit robust antibody- and T cell–mediated immunity69. Here, immunodominant regions from two virulence factors, PsaA and PspA, were selected to construct a strong vaccine candidate. A 2015 animal study of a PspA-PsaA fusion protein showed significant antibody induction against both antigens5.

Although PsaA and PspA have been extensively studied, a fusion construct combining their immunodominant regions has not yet been explored either in silico or in vivo. This study is the first to design a multi-peptide fusion construct from these proteins, incorporating adjuvant sequences from Ply and PepO, as a novel pneumococcal vaccine candidate. Chiu et al. demonstrated that domain 4 of Ply activates TLR4, and a triple mutation (D385N, C428G, W433F) significantly reduces its cytolytic activity16,17. In a previous study by our research group, this mutated domain was successfully utilized as a molecular adjuvant15. Furthermore, Shu et al. identified PepO as a dual TLR2/TLR4 ligand, with the N-terminal region (residues 1–430) responsible for receptor binding18. Subsequent molecular docking analyses by our group refined this region to a shorter fragment (residues 1–112) as a potent TLR agonist1. Compared with related multi-epitope pneumococcal vaccines1,15, the inclusion of dual TLR agonists—mutated Ply domain 4 and the N-terminal fragment of PepO—represents a novel strategy expected to enhance antigen presentation and protective immunity. Given their distinct binding sites and non-overlapping structural domains, steric interference is unlikely, and overlapping TLR4 signaling may instead act synergistically to further potentiate these effects. Nevertheless, empirical evaluation is required to confirm the absence of adverse steric or signaling interactions.

Immunodominant regions from PsaA and PspA were identified using multiple predictive servers to ensure robust epitope selection (Table 2). For PsaA, two epitope-rich peptides (residues 60–114 and 158–209) were selected. For PspA, regions A, B, and C were targeted: region A included peptides 34–61 and 105–125; region B contained peptides 221–246 and 274–295; and region C incorporated the experimentally validated B-cell epitope APKPE, previously identified by Vadesilho et al.10,70. Peptides were linked with flexible GGGS/GGSSGG sequences to enhance solubility and domain mobility71. The TLR agonists were fused to the N- and C-termini via a rigid EAAAK linker for structural stability72. The resulting 482-amino-acid construct, designated “YAPO”, is shown in Fig. 2. Unlike previous studies using full-length PsaA and PspA, this design strategically combines multiple validated epitope-rich regions to broaden immune coverage. The engineered architecture is expected to enhance antigen presentation, promote balanced humoral and cellular responses, and improve overall protective potential. The inclusion of two distinct TLR agonists further introduces a novel multi-adjuvant element, highlighting YAPO as a rationally designed, multi-antigen pneumococcal vaccine candidate.

The immunogenicity of an mRNA vaccine hinges on its regulatory elements, which collectively enhance transcript stability, translation efficiency, and antigen presentation. Our construct includes a 5′ cap and a poly(A) tail to protect against degradation and promote ribosomal engagement, crucial for high protein yield73. Flanking UTRs from CMV IE1 (5′) and hGH (3′) were incorporated to boost translational efficiency in mammalian cells40,41. A Kozak sequence ensures accurate translation initiation, and the tPA signal peptide directs antigen secretion, enhancing immune exposure. Together, these features optimize both intracellular expression and extracellular antigen presentation, mirroring design principles of clinically approved mRNA vaccines and underscoring the translational viability of our candidate.

The favorable physicochemical and structural properties of YAPO align with previous reports on multi-epitope pneumococcal vaccine designs2,6. Its molecular weight (53.6 kDa) is suitable for recombinant expression and purification, and the acidic pI (4.91) may facilitate chromatographic isolation, as noted in other vaccine engineering studies1,15. The combination of a low instability index, relatively high aliphatic index, and strongly negative GRAVY value indicates a stable, thermostable, and hydrophilic construct—features previously linked to improved solubility and enhanced engagement with immune components, particularly B-cell receptors15. High predicted solubility upon overexpression further supports its manufacturability. Consistent with earlier computational vaccine designs1,15, YAPO also demonstrates strong predicted antigenicity alongside non-allergenic and non-toxic profiles. Its secondary-structure composition, including high coil content, is associated with favorable epitope presentation74. Tertiary-structure validation metrics (Ramachandran favored residues > 90%; acceptable ProSA and ERRAT scores) further confirm structural reliability. Overall, these computational data support the rational design of YAPO while emphasizing the need for experimental evaluation to verify its expression, stability, and immunogenicity.

In silico analyses assessed the vaccine’s immunogenic potential. IFNepitope predicted 441 peptides capable of inducing IFN-γ, a key cytokine against pneumococcal infections75 (Table 6). ElliPro identified five conformational B-cell epitopes in the 3D model (scores 0.675–0.7), indicating potential humoral responses (Table 7). Molecular docking of selected T-cell epitopes with HLA alleles showed strong interactions, supporting the construct’s efficacy76 (Table 8; Fig. 6).

Molecular docking results indicate potential interactions between the TLR-agonist domains of YAPO and TLR2/TLR4, suggesting that these domains may activate TLR-mediated signaling, consistent with previous studies showing that TLR agonist adjuvants can enhance innate immune responses77,78. The construct shows strong binding to TLR2 via PepO and to TLR4 via both Ply and PepO domains (Figs. 7 and 8). Molecular dynamics simulations further support the stability of these complexes, suggesting persistent TLR activation. In silico immune simulations indicate that the vaccine may stimulate primary, secondary, and tertiary responses, supporting its potential to elicit both humoral and cellular immunity.

The translational potential of the YAPO mRNA construct is supported by codon optimization, which yielded a CAI of 0.92 and a GC content of 57.69%, values within the range considered optimal for robust mammalian expression. Secondary structure predictions indicated thermodynamically stable folding with minimal inhibitory 5′ hairpins, facilitating ribosome access and efficient translation initiation. These findings align with previous computational mRNA vaccine design targeting major bacterial meningitis pathogens, where Fattahi et al. (2025)79 reported CAI of 0.92 and GC content of 58.16%, alongside stable mRNA secondary structures, supporting effective host translation. Such comparisons suggest that the YAPO construct possesses comparable translational efficiency, supporting its practical feasibility for expression in human cells. Nevertheless, as with all in silico predictions, empirical validation is required to confirm actual expression levels and translational stability.

Limitations

This study was conducted entirely in silico, and the vaccine’s immunogenicity and biological performance remain to be validated experimentally. Nevertheless, the computational findings suggest a promising basis for an mRNA vaccine candidate that merits further evaluation in vitro and in vivo. As with all bioinformatics-driven analyses, predictions depend on available algorithms and datasets and may not fully reflect biological complexity, potentially causing deviations from experimental outcomes. Moreover, variability in HLA allele distribution among individuals may influence immune responses, underscoring the need for validation in diverse models.

Conclusion

This study introduces YAPO-mRNA, a novel multi-epitope mRNA vaccine candidate against Streptococcus pneumoniae. Conserved epitopes from PsaA and PspA were combined with TLR-agonistic domains from Ply and PepO, supported by optimized regulatory elements to enhance stability and translation efficiency. Comprehensive immunoinformatics analyses predicted high antigenicity, structural stability, non-allergenicity, and potentially favorable interactions with HLA and TLR molecules. Codon optimization further suggested efficient expression in human cells. Collectively, these findings highlight the novelty and predicted feasibility of YAPO-mRNA as a serotype-independent pneumococcal vaccine candidate, while underscoring the necessity of in vitro and in vivo validation to establish its efficacy and safety.
