Inducing radiation resilience in frozen animal cells via mRNA coding for tardigrade damage-suppressor protein in support of space travel and Lunar storage
Authors: Mary Hagedorn, Pierre Comizzoli, Lynne R Parenti, Robert A Craddock, Paula Mabee, Bonnie Meinke, John C Bischof, Rebecca D Sandlin, Shannon N Tessier, Mehmet Toner, Robert Ambrose, Baptiste Journaux, Garret Fitzpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarth’s biodiversity is at risk. To help safeguard this biodiversity, a Lunar Biorepository at the Moon’s south pole has been proposed to hold cryopreserved samples of fibroblast cells from animal species that support fundamental ecosystems on Earth. These samples could be used for revitalization of ecosystems on Earth, genetic rescue, space travel, or possibly terraformation. Initially, samples will be primarily animal cells, but the biorepository would be designed to ultimately include all species on Earth. This biorepository will take decades to build and will potentially span millennia. To make this project a reality, certain prior steps must be taken, such as defining species selection and collection and sample management on Earth before launching in the biorepository. Many of the steps leading to the creation of this biorepository are known, with long-term radiation exposure and risk to the cells being one of the greatest challenges to overcome. It is essential to understand the radiation sensitivity of cells and design strategies to avoid genetic modification to further protect cells from radiation during space travel and long-term storage. After considering various options, this review specifically focuses on exposing fibroblast cells to nanoparticles delivering modified mRNA tardigrade damage-suppressor protein. This protein minimizes DNA damage due to radiation without obvious harmful effects to the tissue and cells. This also could serve as a testbed to study countermeasures against the radiation effects during long-duration, crewed space flights.Keywords: biodiversity, cosmic radiation, Lunar Biorepository, mRNA, tardigrades

Introduction

We are in a time of accelerated biological extinction. Loss of biodiversity and its critical functions are creating far-reaching consequences for Earth that will only intensify over time. To help safeguard this biodiversity, a Lunar Biorepository at the Moon’s south pole has been proposed (Hagedorn et al. 2024) to hold cryopreserved samples of fibroblast cells from animals that support fundamental ecosystems on Earth. These samples can be used for revitalization of ecosystems on Earth, genetic rescue, space travel, or possibly for terraformation. Initially, these samples will be primarily animal cells, but the Lunar Biorepository would be designed to ultimately include all species on Earth. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway protects Earth’s most critical crop seeds in a mainly passive biorepository held under the Arctic permafrost at −18°C (Fowler 2008). We envisioned a similar passive biorepository for animal life on Earth, but storing cryopreserved animal cells requires temperatures as low as −196°C, which is colder than any place on Earth. The Lunar South Pole’s Permanently Shadowed Regions (PSRs) maintain cryogenic temperatures year-round (Williams et al. 2019), making them ideal sites for long-term safe storage of animal cells. We chose to build this concept around fibroblast cells, which can be easily harvested from animal skin, cryopreserved for long-term storage (Fernandes Pereira et al. 2023), and then reprogrammed, as demonstrated in mice (Takahashi and Yamanaka 2006) to re-create reproductive cells and whole organisms with future potential applications once returned to Earth.

We outlined some of the conceptual steps and questions that must be addressed before the biorepository becomes a reality (Hagedorn et al. 2024). One of the greatest long-term risks faced by the cells within the biorepository is radiation damage (Chancellor et al. 2014). Within low Earth orbit (up to 2.000 km above Earth), the radiation risk is already 100 times that on Earth (Chancellor et al. 2014); radiation shielding is required to protect humans and tissues in these locations against solar particle events. Once the samples are placed in a biorepository beyond Earth on the moon, radiation shielding is not sufficient to block fast-moving cosmic radiation nuclei, creating a higher radiation risk that may increase damage to the cells.

Although the types of damage that can occur to cells during the steps leading to cryopreservation has been well established for many years (Mazur et al. 1972), less is known about the impact of radiation on cryopreserved cells stored on Earth. Even though cellular metabolism halts during cryopreservation, cosmic radiation can cause free-radical damage and double-stranded breaks to the DNA that become apparent on thawing. In a recent study, various radiation conditions producing a flux of neutrons caused double-stranded breaks to the DNA during storage at freezing temperatures (Rocheteau et al. 2022). Additionally, gamma rays increase reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to detrimental effects during post-thaw conditions (Rocheteau et al. 2022). When cells were cryopreserved and stored in radiation and terrestrial cosmic ray-free environments deep underground, they were relatively undamaged and could potentially be viable for up to 100 yr or longer (Rocheteau et al. 2022). The future of space exploration will center in part on understanding and testing novel approaches to help offset radiation risks for living materials as well as human beings in space.

After reviewing different strategies to protect future frozen biomaterials from radiation, we outline the steps needed to understand and mitigate radiation damage by loading fibroblast cells with nanoparticles delivering modified mRNA tardigrade damage-suppressor protein (Hashimoto et al. 2016).

Radiation issues associated with lunar storage of biomaterials—physical barriers to reduce damage

A Lunar Biorepository is envisioned to be passive with no staff or additional energy needed to maintain the materials and designed to last centuries at liquid nitrogen temperatures. Yet, even if the temperature remains stable for hundreds of years, long-term radiation damage poses a risk for the cells. Deep space holds greater radiation risks for biological samples than does a low Earth orbit or on the Earth where the magnetosphere and the thick atmosphere provide an excellent shield for radiation (see Table 1 for summary of radiation types). One of the most serious radiation threats in deep space is galactic cosmic radiation: high energy protons originating outside the solar system generated by supernova explosions (George et al. 2024). Much like in a particle accelerator, it is assumed that this type of radiation can strip the electrons off atoms causing ionization and thus damage to DNA. Solar energetic particles from solar flares are equally as damaging but are more intermittent in nature.

To offset this, we propose a multifaceted approach. This includes shielding samples from short-term exposure to radiation in transit from the Earth to the Moon. High intensity solar flares, such as X-class flares, create high cosmic radiation, are extremely infrequent, and can be predicted so that launches may be avoided during these times (Kusano et al. 2020). An additional method for shielding includes new multilayered material that incorporates aluminum, hydrogen-rich polyethylene, and boron among many other components. This is showing promising results to shield against galactic cosmic rays and secondary radiation (Gohel and Makwana 2022). We envision this biorepository to be constructed and stocked by uncrewed missions with rovers on the lunar surface. An ideal location for the biorepository would be in a deep crater within a PSR at the Lunar South Pole. The samples would be sent to the moon in a shielded, cryopreserved chamber and moved to a constructed chamber in the crater, shielded with multilayered shielding material paired (to help with secondary production of neutrons produced by galactic cosmic rays) with 8 to 10 m of lunar regolith to protect against long-term galactic cosmic rays, solar energetic particles, and physical damage from micrometeors. Two meters of regolith is considered a standard for short-term protection of biological life on the moon; as the depth is increased to ~8 m, the amount of radiation, largely secondary radiation, that penetrates is less than a flux of 10−4 (particles/cm2/s) (Adams et al. 2005). Regulatory limits suggest a dose of 1 mSv/yr yields a negligible health risk (US EPA 2014); although not exactly comparable, our target of 10−4 (particles/cm2/s) for the Lunar Biorepository is far below this. The biorepository would be maintained much like a modern warehouse to allow for rapid stocking and accessing of the material for future uses. There still could be risk to the samples from biorepository collapse over time allowing potential radiation impact on the samples. This could be mitigated by using a different biorepository design, whereby rovers drill into the lunar regolith and plant the samples in a grid-like pattern many meters below the surface. Rovers exist with this drilling technology, but these drills may have difficulty with soils at liquid nitrogen temperatures. Moreover, locating and accessing this material may become more difficult over time if location information is lost or if the labeling on the sample becomes illegible or unintelligible, especially one sample will be radiation-modified while the parallel partner will not.

Biological means of radiation mitigations

To make animal cells radiation-resistant for space, scientists have explored several options including genetic engineering, enhancing radioprotective capacity, or studying natural extremophiles to mimic nature’s toughest survivors (Clark-Hachtel et al. 2024). Difficulty, risk, and effectiveness of some approaches are summarized in Table 2. Identification of radiation-resistant genes in yeast and insertion into human cells has been attempted with success (Fritz et al. 2000). But it is challenging to regulate multigene edits in a genome. Other natural extremophiles such as the bacterium Deinococcus radiodurans can help us to understand their extreme DNA repair and survival mechanisms; we may then apply these principles to animal cells, as well (Lu et al. 2024). Similarly, bdelloid rotifers (desiccation and radiation tolerant) (Moris et al. 2024) or radiotrophic fungi (radiation-absorbing melanin) are excellent candidates for exploring new strategies to resist radiation damage. However, these mechanisms do not fully protect against direct DNA ionization and can be a metabolic burden on cells. Even if there are challenges including precision and off-target effects, enhancing DNA repair genes to mitigate radiation damage is another strategy, involving the activation of natural repair pathways like TP53 and Poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) via gene editing (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats; CRISPR) or gene therapy (Shakyawar et al. 2023). Finally, other strategies such as pharmacological conditioning via exposure to radioprotective agents targeting p53 or Rho-ROCK signaling could be an option.

Various approaches to protect cells from radiation.

Tardigrades are the only known animals that can survive in space-induced stress. They can withstand radiation doses up to 2,000 times greater than what human cells can withstand (Clark-Hachtel et al. 2024). One of the “secrets” of tardigrades, discovered by researchers in 2016 (Hashimoto et al. 2016), is a gene that produces a protein unique to tardigrades and with a sequence unlike any other known to exist in nature. Because it aids in preventing damage to DNA, the protein was named damage suppressor or Dsup. This unique protein works by physically binding to DNA, especially to nucleosomes (DNA wrapped around histones), forming a protective shield that prevents harmful molecules, such as hydroxyl radicals produced from radiation, from reaching and damaging the genetic material, while also modulating cellular repair pathways to enhance survival. Its highly charged, flexible structure allows it to stick tightly to DNA, reducing single- and double-stranded breaks and boosting cell viability under extreme stress, making Dsup a potential tool to protect human cells (Chavez et al. 2019). In culture, incorporating the Dsup protein from tardigrades into human cells makes them significantly more resistant to X-ray-induced radiation damage by shielding DNA and reducing radiation-induced DNA damage by 40% (Hashimoto et al. 2016). Among various approaches (Table 2), the most realistic near-term options for our Lunar Biorepository samples are introducing Dsup-like DNA shielding, enhancing DNA repair and antioxidant pathways, and using inducible, stress-responsive systems. Instead of genetically engineering the cells, we propose to incorporate modified mRNA to produce enough protein intracellularly. Studies have produced modified mRNAs with synthetic nucleotides, such as 5-methylcytidine, N6-methyladenosine, and 5-methyluridine, that are extremely stable and can be translated for a longer period of time (Hadas et al. 2017). Encapsulation of modified mRNA in nanoparticles (such as, lipid nanoparticles) is then a relatively easy incorporation mechanism and ensures the intracellular delivery via endocytosis in a matter of hours (Hou et al. 2021).

We regard tardigrade Dsup Protein Expression as the one of the best strategies to offset mixed field radiation because it is compatible with cryopreservation, helps protect against double-stranded DNA breaks, and reduces hydroxyl ion damage. It is relatively easy to implement and has low cytotoxicity and has proved radiation reduction in cultured mouse cells (Chavez et al. 2019; Kirtane et al. 2025). Its limitations are that it must target the nucleus, and its behavior is unknown in long-term cryogenic storage. However, two other approaches listed in Table 2 might be a reasonable adjuncts to assist in radiation protection. One is DOPA dioxygenase 1 (DODA1). This creates plant-like pigments called betalains that scavenge ROS before it can cause damage (Li et al. 2024). Certain antioxidants might also be useful, such as nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 and Superoxide Dismutase 2 might be the most useful nuclear and mitochondrial antioxidant gene expression targets (Okunieff et al. 2008; Ma 2013). These adjunct processes are highly effective at reducing damage from ROS, compatible with cryopreservation, and protect membranes and proteins especially during the post-thaw phase, but are dosage-dependent with overexpression disrupting redox signaling (Tavleeva et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024). Multiplexing these strategies may be possible, if the lipid nanoparticle delivery can be used to induce Dsup, DODA1, and antioxidants. The remaining approaches in Table 2 were deemed less desirable augmentation for radiation protection because of their limitations such as cytotoxicity, risk of mutagenesis, or stability under cryogenic conditions (Shakyawar et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2025).

Testing radiation protection on cryopreserved samples

Given a yet undetermined Lunar Biorepository design, we propose the most conservative measures to mitigate radiation over time to the cryopreserved cell samples by genetically modifying half of the cells from each sample collected for storage. Nanoparticles will deliver modified mRNA to the cells encoding for Dsup (Hashimoto et al. 2016; Hadas et al. 2017; Hou et al. 2021; Kirtane et al. 2025). The use of the tardigrade suppressor protein has already demonstrated transient suppression of radiation damage in cells in culture and in intact mice without intercalation into the genome (Kirtane et al. 2025) and holds promise for the long-term radiation protection of cells within the Lunar Biorepository.

Using a theoretically determined dosage of radiation that the samples might experience during transit to the moon and integrating possible delays in deposition of the samples into the biorepository (George et al. 2024), we will test radiation sensitivity on Earth prior to major collections. Specifically, the cells will be modified with suppressor protein, cryopreserved, and assessed post-thaw for damage. This will include DNA integrity, ROS levels, morphological changes, and whether the cells can grow, differentiate, and transform in culture among other tests. Simultaneously, as samples are collected to go to the moon, we would ideally construct a parallel bank on Earth in a low radiation environment, e.g., a deep underground location, such as the Soudan Underground Laboratory at the University of Minnesota. Archiving materials in parallel biorepository will allow us to compare radiation effects in the Lunar Biorepository over 5 to 10 yr to determine whether our radiation protections on the moon are sufficient.

Discussion

Our work on radiation protection may have uses beyond the transport and storage of cells in space, for instance, to prevent detrimental effects in patients undergoing cancer radio-treatments or to reduce the damaging impacts of radiation for humans and animals during long-term space travel. In addition to examining radiation protection, we may also examine factors such as DNA repair enzymes, and scaffolding, regulatory, and signaling proteins, among others (Figueroa-González and Pérez-Plasencia 2017).

The disciplinary backgrounds and expertise of our team are substantial; nevertheless, the decades-long efforts to bring a Lunar Biorepository into full operation will require an even fuller set of collaborators from broad sectors including academic, commercial, and nonprofit. Our current team includes highly qualified and collaborative wildlife and medical cryobiologists, marine and terrestrial reproductive biologists, Smithsonian museum experts (“Homepage | Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History”), ethicists, systematists, ecologists at the National Ecological Observatory Network (“Home | NSF NEON | Open Data to Understand our Ecosystems”), cryo-engineers at ATP-Bio (“About ATP-BIO”), lunar robotics specialists, lunar geologists, Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory engineers (“Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory (SAO)”), and space communication and engagement specialists. This large team can list the potential pitfalls and design corrective solutions as needed. Problem solving at this level will be extremely fast-paced and highly creative, producing numerous direct benefits. In addition, a project of this magnitude—both in concept and collaboration—is likely to generate beneficial translational science and commercialization opportunities. This project is large and multidisciplinary. We need the foresight to prioritize and take the necessary next steps to tackle the largest issues, such as protecting the samples against long-term radiation damage. In summary, the overall radiation mitigation plan to protect the samples long-term would include cryogenic storage, hydrogen-rich shielding (High-Density Polyethylene cryo-boxes holding the cryopreserved samples), sub-surface sample placement under meters of regolith with Dsup expression, and possibly antioxidant protection for the cells which can help protect against direct and indirect radiation with low implementation complexity. This plan would require minimum maintenance for the biorepository, protect the samples against mixed types of radiation, align well with current engineering capabilities, and minimize risk over many years.

At the heart of our collaboration are museums which have a long history of building and maintaining vouchered collections of zoological, geological, and ecological materials. Our deep knowledge of species and their reintroductions, taxonomy, genomics, ecological niches, and migrations, along with living and frozen biorepositories on Earth allows us to envision a biorepository in space. A Lunar Biorepository can help secure species and their ecological communities leading to self-sustaining ecosystems (Norris and Tweddle 2026), and provide a profound level of biosecurity for our Earth.

Conclusions

Maintenance of biodiversity is essential for life on Earth and for human society. Yet, biodiversity is at risk. Establishing a Lunar Biorepository would ensure the biosecurity of key species for our planet. This is a high-risk, high-reward interdisciplinary project that spans from ethics to space engineering. The first step is to understand and try to mitigate long-term radiation impacts on cryopreserved fibroblast cells by transient modification of the cells with a tardigrade protein, Dsup. These studies will advance our fundamental understanding of how cells survive in space and set the stage to preserve animal, plant, and microbial samples (prioritizing those at risk of extinction). It may also be essential to life on Earth and human exploration of the solar system, as the Lunar Biorepository could also store species that could provide food, water filtration, natural antibiotics, or be ecosystems engineers.
