Morel–Lavallee Lesion of the Hip in Children: About a Case and Literature Review
Authors: Salihou Aminou Sadjo, Florence Muller, Jean-Louis Lemelle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMorel–Lavallée lesion, also called closed degloving injury, is a rare clinical entity in routine pediatric surgical practice. We present a case of an 11-year-old girl presenting with a left hip lesion, treated with compression bandage, and the clinical outcome was favorable.

INTRODUCTION

Described in 1853 by the French surgeon Morel–Lavallée (ML), the condition that bears his name is defined as a posttraumatic detachment between the skin tissue and the underlying fascia of richly vascularized tissues, leading to a serolymphatic effusion. It is a rare clinical entity in children.[1] Its diagnosis is most often missed due to the free interval between the initial trauma and the onset of lesions. Since surgical treatment methods increase the risk of bacterial inoculation, nonoperative treatment has therefore been increasingly popular in children. The aim of our work was to report our experience of the management of ML lesions in children and to do a review of the literature.

CASE REPORT

It was an 11-year-old girl, with no relevant history, hospitalized 3 weeks earlier following a road accident. The history revealed that the patient, riding a scooter by the side of the road, was struck by a light vehicle traveling at 50 km/h. The initial assessment of the lesions indicated a closed mid-diaphyseal fracture of the right clavicle, a right renal contusion with subcapsular hematoma, a superficial wound of the left knee, and multiple skin abrasions. Her hemodynamic state as well as the biological assessment were satisfactory throughout her hospitalization. The initial management consisted of “elbow to body” immobilization of the right shoulder, strict bed rest for 1 week, analgesic treatment for the renal contusion, and local care for the knee wound. A favorable progress led to discharge on the 7th day of hospitalization. 3 weeks later, the patient reported the progressive onset of an arch in the left hip. The clinical examination found a mass on the left hip, painless, soft, fluctuating, without inflammatory signs, and measuring approximately 12 cm in length. The overlying skin was normal [Figure 1]. The rest of the examination was unremarkable. The ultrasonography of the mass noted a superficial, heterogeneous, predominantly anechoic compressible collection of the left gluteal region, which was 10 cm long and 1 cm deep and containing heterogeneous structures within [Figure 2]. A complementary X-ray of the pelvis and the left hip was carried out, and no underlying bone lesion was identified. Nonoperative treatment was initiated with compression bandages to be repeated once a week and ultrasound monitoring of the collection. With a follow-up of 4 months, the clinical progress was satisfactory, with an echographic regression of the collection.

DISCUSSION

ML lesion is a condition which predominantly affects the adults after a sport trauma or a road traffic accident.[1] To the best of our knowledge, very few pediatric cases have been reported in the literature. Preferentially located in the lower limb, ML lesions can be seen wherever there is a bony prominence and richly vascularized tissues. The localization in the trochanteric region in our patient is in line with the findings of Vanhegan et al. For the latter, the hip, thigh, and pelvis were the most involved sites with, respectively, 30.4%, 20.1%, and 18.6% of cases.[2] Exceptional observations of ML lesions on the scalp, the scapular region, and the arm have however been described in the literature. Because of the free interval between the initial trauma and the onset of this lesion, the diagnosis is most often missed in 44% of cases.[1] For most authors, the time between trauma and the onset of the lesion varies between a few days and several years.[3] The diagnosis is based on cardinal signs which are: the notion of trauma, a soft swelling, and an effusion on tissue imaging. Ultrasound is the preferred examination in children and allows both diagnosis and follow-up. Some authors recommend a computed tomography scan in case of unavailability of magnetic resonance imaging in emergency units. In our opinion, all these diagnostic modalities will be adapted to patients individually. In the therapeutic approach to this condition, there are nonoperative and operative methods. Our therapeutic attitude was a conservative treatment with a compression bandage of the lesion without aspiration. This treatment is similar to the one in the series by Shen et al., which was successful in 62.5% of cases.[4] For the latter, the failure was justified by an insufficient compression from the bandage. The compression bandage has demonstrated its effectiveness for lesions diagnosed early, localized on the hip or the lower limb, with a healing time between 4 and 6 weeks.[5] The other therapeutic methods, namely aspiration, sclerotherapy, drainage, and surgical debridement, have limited efficacy and are indicated in the cases of failure of conservative treatment, superinfection, or underlying fracture.[3] These methods increase the risk of bacterial inoculation and skin necrosis. With a follow-up of 4 months, the clinical evolution of our patient remains reassuring. Ultrasound monitoring shows a decrease in the collection. We envisage a monthly clinical and ultrasound follow-up until the complete disappearance of the collection.

CONCLUSION

ML lesion is a rare clinical entity and is sometimes unrecognized in the current pediatric orthopedic practice. Its diagnosis is both clinical and paraclinical through imaging, and conservative treatment in the first intention is the rule in children, with satisfactory results in cases of early diagnosis.
