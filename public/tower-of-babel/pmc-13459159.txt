Effectiveness of breastfeeding educational interventions during pregnancy on breastfeeding practices in rural Central Ethiopia: cluster randomized controlled trial
Authors: Belayneh Hamdela Jena, Tsedeke Lambore Gemecho, Ermias Abera Turuse, Biruk Assefa Kebede, Zehara Ahmed Adem, Lamesginew Mossie Melaku, Mulatu Abageda, Zeleke Dutamo Agde, Kassahun Alemu Gelaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite its well-established benefits, breastfeeding practices differ across communities, and many women exhibit suboptimal practices due to cultural influences and limited access to accurate information. Nulliparous pregnant women, in particular, lack prior breastfeeding experience and are often strongly influenced by family members such as mothers-in-law. However, there is limited evidence on whether involving these influential family members in breastfeeding education can improve breastfeeding practices. This study evaluated the effectiveness of breastfeeding educational interventions delivered to pregnant women together with their mothers-in-law, and pregnant women alone versus pregnant women in routine care in rural Central Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based, three-arm, cluster randomized controlled trial was conducted among 510 pregnant women enrolled before 16 weeks of gestation. The study arms were: (1) pregnant woman with her mother-in-law, (2) pregnant woman alone, and (3) control group receiving routine care. Fifty-one non-adjacent clusters were randomly assigned using simple randomization. The intervention consisted of eight home-based educational sessions: six during pregnancy and two postpartum. Outcomes included pre-lacteal feeding, colostrum feeding, early initiation of breastfeeding, and exclusive breastfeeding.ResultsIn the intervention groups (pregnant women and their mother-in-law paired, and pregnant women alone) there were significantly lower incidence of pre-lacteal feeding than pregnant women in the control group (9.1% and 11.4% vs. 19.8%, p = 0.011), and a higher incidence of colostrum feeding (92.7% and 90.4% vs. 81.4%, p = 0.004), early initiation of breastfeeding (80.4% and 72.5% vs. 53.3%, p < 0.001) and exclusive breastfeeding (80.9% and 74.3% vs. 59.9%, p < 0.001). Breastfeeding in both intervention groups have significant effect on breastfeeding practices; avoiding pre-lacteal feeding [RR = 2.17, 95%CI: (1.19, 3.85) and RR = 1.79(1.06, 2.86)], colostrum feeding [RR = 1.14(1.01, 1.29) and RR = 1.11(1.01, 1.23)], early initiation of breastfeeding [RR = 1.51(1.14, 1.99) and RR = 1.36(1.02, 1.82)], and exclusive breastfeeding [RR = 2.08(1.43, 3.03) and RR = 1.56(1.05, 2.33)] as compared to the control group.ConclusionInvolving mothers-in-law in breastfeeding educational interventions in addition to active interventions to pregnant women alone has broader practical implication for improving recommended breastfeeding practices.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov identifier (NCT number): NCT06236412, January 23, 2024.

Background

Globally, breastfeeding is widely acknowledged as one of the most effective ways to promote child health and survival [1]. This is particularly true in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia, where alternatives to breast milk may not be practical or accessible. Breast milk is naturally clean, safe, and rich in antibodies that protect infants from common childhood illnesses, including respiratory infections and diarrhea. It also provides essential macro- and micronutrients that meet an infant’s nutritional requirements during the first six months of life and continue to support growth when combined with complementary foods thereafter [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) advise that breastfeeding should begin within the first hour after birth and that infants should receive only breast milk—no additional liquids or foods, not even water—for the first six months [1].

In Ethiopia, breastfeeding for an extended period is a common practice, with 76% of mothers feeding their children for at least two years, and a median breastfeeding duration was 23.9 months [3]. Nonetheless, despite global recommendations, a significant share of infants are not exclusively breastfed (41%) [4], while 27–36% do not begin breastfeeding within the first hour after birth [5, 6]. Additionally, between 25% and 46% of newborns are given pre-lacteal feeds—such as butter, honey, or plain water—before breastfeeding is established [7, 8]. The first milk, or colostrum, is also frequently discarded or avoided (19–77%), often expressed and removed before feeding begins [6, 9]. The latest UNICEF report indicates that 39% of infants miss exclusive breastfeeding and 77% do not start breastfeeding within the recommended first hour of life [10].

These suboptimal breastfeeding practices are largely linked to inadequate access to accurate health information within communities about the risks associated with such behaviors [11, 12].

Research has shown that limited maternal knowledge and a lack of breastfeeding counseling are key contributors to inadequate breastfeeding practices [9, 13]. This highlights the importance of strengthening health education to enhance mothers’ understanding of recommended practices.

Such efforts are particularly crucial for first-time (nulliparous) women, who lack prior breastfeeding experience and may be more susceptible to the influence of family members, including mothers-in-law [14].

The World Health Assembly (WHA) established a goal of raising global exclusive breastfeeding rates to 50% by the year 2025 [15]. The WHO continues to advocate for breastfeeding as the optimal source of nutrition for infants and young children and supports efforts toward meeting this target. Ethiopia has demonstrated strong commitment in this area and surpassed the benchmark, achieving an exclusive breastfeeding rate of 59% in 2019 [4]. Sustaining this progress is essential to ensure that all children can grow and develop well, and to help the country meet the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 3, which focuses on reducing neonatal and child mortality.

Improving nutrition during pregnancy and early childhood remains a national priority for the Ethiopian government, and promoting appropriate breastfeeding practices during the neonatal period—followed by adequate complementary feeding—is one of the key strategies for enhancing child survival [16].

While pregnancy and childbirth are uniquely women’s experiences, the role of key family members—particularly husbands or partners and mothers-in-law—is vital for successfully implementing child health interventions such as breastfeeding [17, 18]. Husbands or partners often hold considerable influence in general household health decisions [19]. In Ethiopia, however, mothers-in-law tend to have an even greater impact, especially regarding breastfeeding practices like pre-lacteal feeding, colostrum avoidance, and exclusive breastfeeding. They are often the individuals who introduce pre-lacteal foods, encourage discarding colostrum, or pressure mothers to provide additional foods or liquids to newborns [20]. Younger mothers, in particular, tend to follow the advice of their mothers-in-law, who are viewed as knowledgeable and experienced in pregnancy, childbirth, infant feeding, and child-rearing [14]. Although Ethiopian women routinely access maternal and child health services, influential family members such as husbands and mothers-in-law are frequently excluded from educational activities [21]. Evidence from prior intervention studies shows that involving husbands in maternal and child health education can significantly improve service utilization [22, 23]. Similarly, an earlier intervention study carried out in Ethiopia that engaged influential community members—specifically Women Development Army (WDA) leaders—demonstrated improvements in both early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding practices [24]. Despite existing efforts, there is limited evidence [25] in Ethiopia on how breastfeeding education provided specifically to first-time (null-parous) pregnant women together with their mothers-in-law (paired), and null-parous pregnant women alone impacts the adoption of recommended breastfeeding practices compared to null-parous pregnant women in routine care. This gap is important given the country’s diverse socio-cultural settings and varying breastfeeding traditions. Generating such evidence is also valuable for broader decision-making, as improving breastfeeding behaviors can help reduce economic burdens associated with poor practices, including household spending on treatment and breast milk substitutes when children get ill, parents’ lost productivity while caring for sick infants, and increased pressure on the health care system [26]. Additionally, educating null-parous pregnant women has long-term benefits, as it can support sustained good breastfeeding practices in future births and promote child health and survival over time. Therefore, this trial aims to evaluate the effectiveness of breastfeeding educational interventions during pregnancy on key breastfeeding practices—pre-lacteal feeding, colostrum feeding, early initiation of breastfeeding, and exclusive breastfeeding—in rural Central Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting

The study was carried out in the rural areas of Hadiya Zone, located in Central Ethiopia. The zone’s capital, Hossana, lies 232 km from Addis Ababa, the nation’s capital. Hadiya Zone has a total population of 1,807,065, comprising 895,581 men (49.56%) and 911,484 women (50.44%). Women of reproductive age (15–49 years) account for 421,044 individuals (23.3%), and the estimated number of pregnancies is 61,092 (3.46%). Health services in the zone are provided through 1 comprehensive specialized teaching hospital, 3 primary hospitals, 62 health centers, and 311 health posts. Administratively, Hadiya Zone consists of 17 districts, including 12 rural districts and 5 city administrations (Hadiya Zone Health Department–Unpublished).

In Ethiopia, households are predominantly organized along patriarchal lines, where husbands—and at times extended relatives such as mothers-in-law—hold significant authority over family decisions [27]. Women’s independence is often restricted, especially regarding health, financial matters, and reproductive choices, which are usually determined by husbands or collectively within the family [28]. This dynamic is reinforced in rural and traditional communities where extended family structures are common. Mothers-in-law commonly reside in close proximity to their families, and in certain circumstances—particularly when they require substantial support in old age—they may live together in the same household. They play a decisive role in shaping women’s experiences, influencing areas such as reproductive health, maternal care, infant feeding practices, and domestic responsibilities [29]. Their influence is rooted in cultural traditions and age-based hierarchies that elevate elder family members within patriarchal households. As a result, newly married or pregnant women frequently have limited decision-making power, with mothers-in-law guiding choices related to pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and healthcare access [30].

This study focused on four rural districts—Mirab Soro, Gombora, Soro, and Duna—which together comprise 98 kebeles, the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia. Data collection for the study took place from March 24, 2024, to May 20, 2025.

Study design

A community-based, three-arm, parallel, cluster randomized controlled trial was conducted. The study used a post-test-only design, as the outcomes—specifically breastfeeding practices—were assessed only after follow-up, once the women had given birth. The clusters in the study were kebeles. The three intervention arms included: (1) pregnant women together with their mothers-in-law, (2) pregnant women alone, and (3) pregnant women receiving routine care (control group).

Baseline socio-demographic information was collected to ensure comparability among participants across the arms (to maintain randomization) and to describe their characteristics.

Randomization

Clusters were allocated to the study arms using a simple randomization method, employing a random number generator in Open Epi software version 3.01, by a biostatistician who was not involved in the study. The unit of randomization was the kebele (cluster). The clusters selected were non-adjacent to minimize the likelihood of participants from different clusters interacting during routine activities such as visits to health facilities, markets, or places of worship, thereby reducing information contamination.

The randomization process involved several steps. First, three opaque envelopes were prepared, one for each study arm (pregnant women with their mothers-in-law, pregnant women alone, and pregnant women receiving routine care) to conceal the cluster assignments. Second, the 51 non-adjacent clusters were arranged in alphabetical order and assigned unique codes from 1 to 51.

Third, an equal number of clusters per arm was planned. The first 17 clusters were randomly selected using the Open Epi random number generator, and the biostatistician drew one envelope to assign these clusters to an arm. Next, 17 clusters were randomly selected from the remaining 34 clusters, and one of the two remaining envelopes was drawn to determine the corresponding arm.

The final 17 clusters were assigned to the remaining envelope.

Following this allocation, the interventions were delivered to the two assigned arms, while the third arm received routine care. Due to the nature of a cluster trial, blinding participants and investigators was not feasible. However, single blinding was applied for outcome assessors (data collectors), who were unaware of the study objectives and only responsible for collecting data without influencing participants.

Population, Intervention, Control and Outcome (PICO)

Population

The study included nulliparous pregnant women who were between the end of their first trimester and the early second trimester (<16 weeks), had not given birth before, provided consent, and had lived in the study area for at least six months. This timeframe was chosen to allow at least five months for behavioral changes following the initial intervention. Women with a history of stillbirth, and pregnant women whose mothers-in-law had died or were unavailable were excluded.

In rural areas, many women in their first trimester are unaware of their pregnancy. To confirm pregnancy, a urine-based HCG test was conducted. Before testing, women were asked about their last menstrual period, contraceptive use, prior pregnancy testing, and any pregnancy-related symptoms. Based on these responses, women were registered for sampling, and pregnancy was then confirmed with the test. If a woman was found not to be pregnant, she was replaced by another eligible participant from the registration list. This targeted approach to pregnancy testing was used to reduce costs, as testing all potential participants would have been impractical. Baseline data collection and intervention activities commenced only after pregnancy was confirmed.

Intervention

The nutrition education intervention protocol was designed based on a review of WHO and USAID training-of-trainers manuals for counseling on maternal, infant, and young child nutrition [31].

The protocol focused on promoting optimal breastfeeding practices, including avoiding pre-lacteal feeding, encouraging colostrum feeding, supporting early initiation, and maintaining exclusive breastfeeding. The interventions were guided by social cognitive theory [32]. The health extension workers (HEWs) had given the intervention. During each education session, both intervention arms—the pregnant woman with her mother-in-law and the pregnant woman alone—received the same information on breastfeeding practices. The control group, by contrast, did not receive targeted educational interventions. Instead, they received routine care and general guidance on hygiene and maternal and child health services, including breastfeeding, with the same frequency and duration as the intervention arms, though the counseling was less detailed. Prior to discussing specific breastfeeding practices, participants were provided with general information about the nature and benefits of breast milk. The education sessions covered general information about breast milk, including its composition, ways to improve its quality, strategies to increase milk production, recommended breastfeeding frequency, how infants receive nutrients from breast milk, and the benefits for both mother and child. For the four specific breastfeeding practices, separate intervention modules were developed:

Intervention approach

Home visits

The interventions were delivered individually at the participants’ homes.

Frequency of interventions

A total of eight sessions were provided—six sessions during pregnancy (monthly from the 4th to the 9th month) and two sessions after delivery (within the first week and during the final week of the third month).

Mode of delivery

The education was provided verbally, accompanied by written leaflets prepared in the local language, Hadiyisa. For participants who were unable to read, voluntary readers were assigned from within their households when available. If no reader was present at home, a volunteer from the neighborhood was selected based on the woman’s preference.

Duration

Each session lasted approximately 45 to 60 minutes.

Control group

Women in the control arm received routine care, which encompassed existing community- and facility-based health services. At the community level, this includes services provided by health extension workers, covering hygiene (personal, food, and environmental) and family health services such as antenatal care, postnatal care, immunization, breastfeeding, and nutrition.

In this trial, the control group received counseling on antenatal care, facility-based delivery, postnatal care, dietary intake (including additional meals during and after pregnancy), hygiene, and basic breastfeeding guidance—such as on-demand feeding and continuing breastfeeding for at least 24 months. The content was less detailed than in the intervention arms but matched the frequency and duration of sessions to maintain balance.

Pregnant women in all study arms also received iron and folic acid supplementation for at least 90 days, in line with standard recommendations (90 + days) (Fig. 1).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software version 3.1.9.2, based on a 73% rate of early initiation of breastfeeding within one hour in Ethiopia [3], an anticipated 20% difference between the intervention and control arms, 90% power, a 5% significance level, a 20% allowance for loss to follow-up, and equal allocation to the three arms with a two-sided test. This resulted in 88 participants per arm.

To account for the cluster design and the need to analyze correlated data, an intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.1 and a cluster size of 10 were considered feasible. Using these parameters, a design effect of 1.9 was calculated, leading to 51 clusters in total (17 clusters per arm), based on standard formulas [33]. Consequently, with 51 clusters and 10 participants per cluster, the total sample size was 510, with 170 participants allocated to each study arm.

Sampling procedure

In this study, 51 clusters were purposefully selected from a total of 98 clusters to minimize information contamination from neighboring clusters. Within each cluster, eligible participants—pregnant women between the end of the first trimester and early second trimester—were selected using a simple random sampling method (random number generator in Open Epi software) after registration through house-to-house visits and the creation of a sampling frame. If an eligible participant declined to join the study, another participant from the list was randomly selected as a replacement using the same method. However, if a participant withdrew after the intervention had already started, they were recorded as withdrawn, and no replacements were made. This sampling procedure was consistently applied across all study arms.

Data collection procedures

A structured interview questionnaire, developed in English based on relevant literature, was used to collect baseline data. It was then translated into the local language, Hadiyisa, and back-translated by language experts to ensure consistency. Adjustments were made as needed following a pre-test conducted with 5% of participants in a similar setting in a district not included in the study.

Baseline data, including participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, were collected within two weeks of enrollment, which took place in June 2024.

The intervention materials, including short notes and leaflets, were initially prepared in English and then translated into the local language, Hadiyisa, with back-translation conducted by language experts fluent in both languages. Fifty-one Health Extension Workers (HEWs) registered pregnant women between the end of the first trimester and early second trimester (< 16 weeks) through house-to-house visits, using urine-based HCG tests to confirm pregnancy. Each registration included the village and house number as a code.

Twelve midwives and nurses with college diplomas, along with four supervisors holding Master’s degrees in public health, were involved in baseline and end-line data collection and supervision. Data collectors and supervisors received two days of training on data collection procedures. After participant identification and enrollment, the 51 HEWs serving as intervention implementers in their respective clusters, along with ten Master’s-level public health supervisors, underwent five days of training on the pre-designed intervention protocol.

End-line data collection began during the first week after delivery to capture three outcomes: pre-lacteal feeding, colostrum feeding, and early initiation of breastfeeding. Exclusive breastfeeding data were collected one week after the child reached six months of age. The same data collection schedule was applied to both intervention and control arms. The completeness of data and fidelity of intervention implementation were closely monitored (Fig. 2).

Quality control

Training was provided to data collectors, intervention implementers, and supervisors who were fluent in the local language and familiar with the culture of the study area. The HEWs responsible for delivering the interventions; they had previously completed training on 18 health packages, which include recommended breastfeeding practices, with health education being a cross-cutting component.

For the trial, HEWs received extensive training focused on the outcomes of interest—breastfeeding practices—using lectures and role-play. HEWs in the control arm were given less detailed training to provide counseling on hygiene and maternal and child health services, ensuring that the control and intervention arms were comparable in session frequency and duration. This approach also helped reduce potential information contamination in the control group.

Supervisors and the research team closely monitored the delivery of interventions and data collection to ensure proper implementation. Interventions were delivered using both verbal explanations and written notes to enhance participant understanding. A fidelity checklist was employed to verify that the intervention was conducted according to the designed protocol, and checked by supervisors. Data collectors were blinded to the study outcomes.

Analysis

The collected data were edited, coded, and entered into Epi Data version 3.5.1, then exported to R version 4.2.2 for analysis. Data cleaning and exploration were conducted, including checks for missing values and outliers. Continuous variables were summarized using means, medians, and standard deviations, while categorical variables were summarized with frequencies and percentages through cross-tabulations.

Baseline characteristics across the two intervention arms and the control arm were compared using chi-squared tests. To assess the intervention effects, the proportion of each outcome in the intervention arms was compared with the control arm. Outcomes were recoded as binary categorical variables. A generalized linear model (GLM) with a binomial family and log link function was used to estimate risk ratios (RRs).

Because this was a cluster-randomized trial, standard errors were adjusted for the 51 clusters (kebeles) using cluster-robust methods to account for clustering effects, minimizing errors and providing more accurate and precise parameter estimates. Intervention effects were reported as RRs, and attributable fractions (AF) were estimated for a more informative interpretation. The analysis followed an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach.

Results

Trial profile

For this trial, a total of 782 potential study participants were identified by collecting information on their parity status, current contraceptive use, any previous pregnancy tests, last menstrual period, and whether they believed they might be pregnant. A pregnancy test was conducted for all 782 potential participants who were nulliparous. After testing, 553 eligible participants were found to be pregnant. Of these, 13 refused to participate for various reasons, such as relocating for delivery, preferring care from a gynecologist at a private clinic, or for unspecified reasons. To preserve the intended sample size and study design balance, 30 eligible pregnant women were randomly excluded from participation. Consequently, a total of 510 nulliparous pregnant women who met the pre-specified eligibility criteria were enrolled in the study, with 170 participants allocated to each of the three arms (Fig. 3). Baseline characteristics of study participants

The mean age of the study participants was 24.8 ± 2.6 years, ranging from 18 to 29 years. The mean ages for the three arms were as follows: 25.3 ± 2.6 years for the pregnant women with their mothers-in-law arm, 24.6 ± 2.6 years for the pregnant women only arm, and 24.5 ± 2.6 years for the control arm (pregnant women receiving routine care). The age range in the mother-in-law arm was 19 to 29 years, in the women-only arm 20 to 29 years, and in the control arm 18 to 29 years.

Across all arms, the majority of participants were of Hadiya ethnicity, Protestant by religion, and engaged in farming as their main occupation. All participants were currently in a marital union. Chi-squared test results indicated that baseline characteristics were similar across the three trial arms, suggesting that socio-demographic and economic factors, which could potentially confound breastfeeding practices, were evenly distributed. This indicates that the randomization process was successful (Table 1).

Incidence of breastfeeding practices

The intervention and control arms were compared in terms of breastfeeding practices—including pre-lacteal feeding, colostrum feeding, early initiation of breastfeeding, and exclusive breastfeeding—using descriptive statistics (cross-tabulations and chi-squared tests). In both intervention arms, the incidence of pre-lacteal feeding was lower (9.1% in the pregnant women with their mother-in-law arm and 11.4% in the pregnant women alone arm) compared to the control arm (19.8%), p = 0.011.

Colostrum feeding was higher in the intervention arms (92.7% in the pregnant women with their mother-in-law arm and 90.4% in the pregnant women alone arm) than in the control arm (81.4%), p = 0.004. Similarly, early initiation of breastfeeding was more frequent in the intervention arms (80.4% in the mother-in-law arm and 72.5% in the women-only arm) compared to the control arm (53.3%), p < 0.001. Exclusive breastfeeding was also higher in the intervention arms (80.9% in the mother-in-law arm and 74.3% in the women-only arm) than in the control arm (59.9%), p < 0.001 (Table 5).

Effectiveness of breastfeeding educational interventions on breastfeeding practices

The effectiveness of the educational interventions on breastfeeding practices was assessed using a generalized linear model (GLM). Risk ratios (RR) and absolute risk (attributable fraction, AF) were calculated to estimate the effect and impact of the breastfeeding educational interventions, respectively. Crude relative risks were estimated using a generalized linear model (GLM).

Adjustment for confounding was not required, as baseline characteristics were homogeneous and no potential confounders were identified during follow-up. We also assessed residual confounding and interaction effects, but socio-demographic variables showed no association with breastfeeding practices and no significant effect modification. Therefore, reporting crude estimates of the intervention, with adjustment for clustering-related errors, was considered appropriate.

Mothers in both intervention groups were more successful in avoiding pre-lacteal feeding compared to the control group, with pregnant women paired with their mothers-in-law [RR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.19, 3.85] and pregnant women alone [RR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.06, 2.86]. Colostrum feeding was also higher in the intervention groups, with the mother-in-law paired group [RR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.29] and the women-only group [RR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.23] outperforming the control group.

Early initiation of breastfeeding was more frequent in the intervention arms, with the mother-in-law paired group [RR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.14, 1.99] and the women-only group [RR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.82] compared to the control group. Similarly, exclusive breastfeeding was better practiced in the intervention groups, with the mother-in-law paired group [RR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.43, 3.03] and the women-only group [RR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.05, 2.33] relative to the control group (Table 6).

Discussion

This interventional study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of breastfeeding educational interventions on key breastfeeding practices, including avoidance of pre-lacteal feeding, promotion of colostrum feeding, early initiation of breastfeeding, and exclusive breastfeeding, in rural areas of Hadiya Zone, Central Ethiopia. The findings indicated that educational interventions delivered to pregnant women—both with the involvement of their mothers-in-law and to pregnant women alone—had a positive effect on improving recommended breastfeeding practices compared to pregnant women receiving routine care.

In the pregnant women and mothers-in-law paired intervention group, mothers were twice more likely to avoid pre-lacteal feeding, 14% more likely to feed colostrum, 51% more likely to initiate breastfeeding early (within the first hour following childbirth) and twice more likely to breastfeed exclusively up to six months as compared mothers in the control group. The results suggest that nearly 54% of avoiding pre-lacteal feeding, 12% of colostrum feeding practice, nearly 34% of early initiation of breastfeeding and nearly 52% of exclusive breastfeeding were attributed to breastfeeding educational interventions given through the involvement of mothers-in-law. The results signify the importance of mothers-in-law in promoting good practices of breastfeeding, especially in rural settings that are underserved, hard-to-reach and numerous cultural practices related to breastfeeding exists. Although mothers-in-law are influential people in the community their impact on breastfeeding practices was unexplored and less recognized in global literature, especially in Ethiopia. However, previous studies, a qualitative study [25], and cross-sectional study [34] conducted in Indonesia highlighted an important role of mothers-in-law in promoting and supporting breastfeeding practice such as exclusive breastfeeding.

In the pregnant women alone intervention group, mothers were 79% more likely to avoid pre-lacteal feeding, 11% more likely to practice colostrum feeding, 36% more likely to initiated breastfeeding early (within the first hour following childbirth) and 56% more likely to breastfeed exclusively up to six months as compared to mothers in the control group. The results indicate that about 44% of avoiding pre-lacteal feeding, 10% of colostrum feeding practice, nearly 27% of early initiation of breastfeeding and nearly 36% of exclusive breastfeeding were attributed to breastfeeding educational intervention given for the pregnant women alone. The findings highlight that active breastfeeding educational interventions for pregnant women alone can be an alternative approach where involving mothers-in-law is not feasible. Previous interventional study in India revealed that active breastfeeding education to mothers significantly improved breastfeeding practice such as exclusive breastfeeding as compared to routine care [35]. A Cochrane review of 20 controlled trial reported that prenatal education of mothers about breastfeeding improved timely initiation of breastfeeding [36].

Breastfeeding educational interventions through the involvement of community’s most influential people such as mothers-in-law have a wider practical implication to enhancing recommended practices of breastfeeding in the rural settings, especially in the areas where multicultural practices and customs related to breast milk and its feeding practices exist. Mothers-in-law are highly influential people in most of Ethiopian society, as rearing children assumed the role of women and mothers-in-law are judged as the most experienced in caring and rearing for children. They can be used as ‘change agents’ for promoting good practices of breastfeeding.

Involvement of mothers-in-law can be integrated with the existing community health development programs such as the health extension program in which breastfeeding counseling is one of the activities in the package, community-based essential newborn care and the immunization programs. Where involvement of mothers-in-law is not feasible, active educational intervention of pregnant women alone, can be an alternative approach to enhance good practices of breastfeeding in the rural settings. There is a need for further interventional studies to evaluate the effectiveness of active educational intervention to mother-in-law, and mothers themselves to support the findings of this study.

The strength of this study is the use of community-based cluster randomized controlled trial using a three arms to evaluate the effectiveness of breastfeeding educational interventions via involvement of mothers-in-law, community’s influential people in the areas of maternal and child health care, in addition to mothers themselves. This study has addressed the less explored contribution of the mothers-in-law, especially in breastfeeding practices. Secondly, the intervention was given six times during pregnancy in monthly basis starting from the 4th up to 9th months and two times after childbirth (in the first week after childbirth and the last week of the 3rd month) via individual home visits and printed home-take reading materials that consists of key-messages of breastfeeding practices. Thirdly, the study focused on null-parous pregnant women who had no previous experience of breastfeeding. So the key-messages help them and their children in the future as they are expected to continue the good practices of breastfeeding.

Fourthly, to minimize recall bias, the outcome data were collected in two time points; firstly, in the first week of delivery to evaluate pre-lacteal feeding, colostrum feeding and time at initiation of breastfeeding, and in the first week after 6 months to evaluate exclusive breastfeeding. Despite these strengths, however, this study might have some limitations that deserve mention here. Firstly, recall bias still might have occurred although we attempted data collection in the two phases, especially time at initiation of breastfeeding, as it was based on the recall of the mother and someone who helped her during childbirth. Secondly, social desirability bias might have occurred, especially those who had received the interventions fear to report the poor practices since they were advised to demonstrate the good practices. Thirdly, this trial was limited to small geographic areas and might have lacked generalizability. Fourthly, the lack of data on the presence, characteristics, and influence of mothers-in-law in the non-intervention groups makes it difficult to determine whether the observed improvements are attributable solely to their involvement or to pre-existing household and social dynamics. Thus, further large scale studies that involve community’s most influential people like mothers-in-law are needed at regional or national levels to strengthen the evidence. Despite the limitations, the findings will have wider practical implication in the areas of child health care, especially in the rural settings for community-based new-born care and child survival programs.

Conclusion

Involving mothers-in-law in breastfeeding educational interventions in addition to active interventions to pregnant women alone has broader practical implication for improving recommended practices of breastfeeding in the rural community settings. Thus, concerned bodies and programs such as community-based health development programs like the health extension program, community-based essential newborn care programs, including the immunization programs, should consider community’s influential peoples like the mothers-in-law during breastfeeding promotion in addition to the mothers.
