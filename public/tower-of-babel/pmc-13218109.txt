Gambling trajectories among sexual and gender minority individuals in Canada: protocol for a 5-year mixed-methods longitudinal study
Authors: Magaly Brodeur, Andrée-Anne Légaré, Adèle Morvannou, Pasquale Roberge, Christine Loignon, Didier Jutras-Aswad, Anaïs Lacasse, Isabelle Dufour, Marie-Josée Fleury, Olivier Simon, Catherine Hudon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundGambling encompasses all activities that involve betting or wagering money. It is highly prevalent both in Canada and worldwide. While most individuals gamble without experiencing harm, some develop problem gambling, which is associated with serious psychological, relational and financial outcomes. Sexual and gender diversity (SGD) populations experience disproportionately high rates of mental health disorders, although little is known about their gambling trajectories. Knowledge in this regard is mainly based on cross-sectional studies, with no longitudinal evidence being available internationally. This gap in the literature restricts understanding of how problematic gambling emerges and evolves among SGD populations. It also limits the development of prevention and harm reduction strategies tailored to their realities.Methods and analysisThis five-year longitudinal study will use a mixed-methods explanatory and sequential design in two phases. The first phase is a prospective cohort study. A self-report questionnaire will be administered online via a web panel to Canadian residents who are 18 years of age or older, self-identify as SGD and have gambled at least once in the previous 12 months (n=2500). This survey will be repeated annually over the course of three years to describe respondents’ gambling habits, model their trajectories and identify factors associated with problematic gambling. The second phase is a descriptive qualitative study. Semi-structured interviews will be conducted with respondents from phase 1 who present problematic gambling (n=40) to explore their experiences and lived realities.Ethics and disseminationThis research project has been ethically and scientifically approved by the Research Ethics Committee and by the CIUSSS de l’Estrie—CHUS scientific evaluation committee on November 3, 2025 (reference number: 2026-6060 Trajectoires-JHA-LGBTQ). For all phases of the study, written or verbal consent will be obtained from each participant. A copy of the consent form and contact information will be sent to each participant.Keywords: Sexual and Gender Minorities, Longitudinal studies, Behavior, Risk Factors, Impulse control disorders

Background

Gambling encompasses all activities that involve betting or wagering money or something else of value, and it includes lotteries, scratch cards, slot machines, video lottery terminals and poker games.1 Gambling plays an important role in the consumption habits of the Canadian population.2 For instance, in 2018, approximately two thirds of Canadian adults (64.5%) reported having gambled in the past year.3 Whereas the majority of people who gamble have low-risk gambling behaviors and do not experience adverse consequences associated with their gambling, others have problematic gambling, that is, that they are at risk of developing a gambling disorder (GD) or have a probable GD.1 3 4 GD has significant repercussions for both those affected and their loved ones, including financial difficulties, job loss, relationship problems, psychological distress, depression, anxiety and even suicide.1 5

Internationally, the prevalence of problem gambling over the past year is estimated to be between 0.12% and 5.8%.6 In Canada, it is estimated to be 1.6% over the past year, which means that approximately 304 400 people in the country have a gambling problem.3 Certain sociodemographic characteristics have been identified as being associated with a higher risk of developing GD, for example, being male or a young adult.7 Concurrent mental health disorders, such as substance use disorders, mood disorders and anxiety disorders, are also very common among those with GD.1 Indeed, it is estimated that 96% of individuals with GD have at least one comorbid mental health disorder, and that 64% have three or more such disorders.1 8

Populations that face a greater risk of discrimination tend to have higher rates of mental health disorders, including addictions.9 Sexual and gender diversity (SGD) populations (i.e., two-spirit, lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, intersex individuals, and those who identify with various sexual or gender groups and use other terminologies, who are also referred to as 2SLGBTQI+10,15) are among the populations identified as being at particular risk of discrimination. In Canada, SGD individuals represent approximately 4.4% of the population aged 15 years and older, although this percentage does not account for the significant proportion of individuals who will, at some point in their life, question their sexual orientation or gender identity.16 17 The literature shows that SGD populations are at greater risk of developing mental health disorders such as depression, anxiety and substance use disorders.18 19 In fact, these disorders are two to four times more prevalent among SGD populations than among the general population.20 21 However, knowledge regarding problem gambling among SGD populations remains limited. To date, no longitudinal studies have been conducted on gambling among SGD populations, either in Canada or internationally.14 As a result, little is known about the transient or chronic nature of problem gambling among SGD populations or how it emerges and evolves over time.14 Unlike cross-sectional studies, which capture data at a specific point in time, longitudinal studies provide insight into gambling trajectories (i.e., changes in gambling habits over time), which is essential for developing prevention and harm reduction strategies that consider the specific characteristics of SGD individuals and how problematic gambling emerges and evolves over time.11 14

Objectives

This research project has four main objectives: (1) to describe the gambling habits of SGD individuals; (2) to model their gambling trajectories and describe individual profiles according to those trajectories; (3) to identify the factors associated with belonging to problematic gambling trajectories; and (4) to understand the experiences and lived realities of SGD individuals with problematic gambling trajectories.

Methods and analysis

Design

This longitudinal study is a mixed-methods explanatory and sequential design in two phases over 5 years (see figure 1).22 Phase 1 is a prospective cohort study and will begin in fall 2026.23 24 It will include data collection via a survey repeated annually over the course of 3 years, allowing for identification and description of the gambling trajectories of SGD individuals. The results of phase 1 will inform the development of phase 2,22 which is a descriptive qualitative study.24,26 This second phase will provide a better understanding of the experiences and lived realities of SGD individuals with problematic gambling trajectories.22

Mixed explanatory and sequential longitudinal design.

Phase 1: quantitative study

Phase 1 is a prospective cohort study.23 27 An online survey (n=2500), which will be administered as a self-report questionnaire via a web panel, will be repeated three times (once per year for 3 years) to describe the gambling habits of SGD individuals, model their gambling trajectories and identify factors associated with belonging to problematic gambling trajectories.

Eligibility criteria

Participants must self-identify as SGD, have engaged in at least one gambling activity in the past 12 months, be 18 years of age or older, speak English or French and reside in Canada. This information will be collected via self-report questions at the beginning of the questionnaire.

Sampling method and sample size

The target sample size is 2500. Recruitment for this study will be conducted through a firm specialising in web surveys and panels, which will ensure that the sample is representative of the Canadian population in terms of age, sex and gender, language and geographical region. As compensation, participants in the study will receive credits that can be exchanged for rewards (eg, gift cards) on the firm’s website. A draw offering various prizes will also be run by the firm to ensure the retention of participants at times T2 and T3.

The target cohort size (n=2500) was determined based on the minimum statistical threshold recommended for conducting latent class analyses (minimum 500; see the Data analysis section)28; the number of individuals per trajectory required for the development of regression models composed of 10 variables—that is, 10 individuals per variable (minimum 100)29; the prevalence of problematic gambling among SGD populations, which we estimate to be at least 4.8% (i.e., approximately three times the prevalence among the general population; see the Background section); the number of semi-structured interviews to be conducted in phase 2 (n=40; see the Data analysis section); the percentage of individuals who agreed to participate in phase 2 of our study on gambling practices among 2SLGBTQI+ populations (66%); and the attrition rate identified in previous longitudinal studies on gambling in Quebec and Canada (25%).30 31

Data collection

Data will be collected using a bilingual (French and English) online questionnaire. The questionnaire (administered once per year for three years), composed of validated tools and including approximately 85 items, will be divided into four sections: (1) sociodemographic profile; (2) gambling practices profile; (3) mental health profile; and (4) perceived discrimination. A pretest will be conducted by the specialised survey and web panel firm in collaboration with the research team, which will involve 30 participants and take place during the month preceding the questionnaire’s launch. Following completion of the final questionnaire at time T3, participants will be invited to indicate their interest in participating in phase 2 (i.e., the qualitative phase). The questionnaire will include the following measurement instruments:

Sociodemographic profile: Questions concerning sociodemographic variables will be drawn from the Canadian Community Health Survey.32 The variables of interest will include respondents’ age, language, marital status, education level, employment, income (personal and household), immigration status and racial or cultural group. Questions related to sex, gender and sexual orientation will be drawn from the LGBT-COVID survey previously conducted by the research team.33 All these variables will be assessed at only the first measurement time (T1), except for employment, income (personal and household), gender, sexual orientation and marital status, which will be assessed at each measurement time (T1, T2 and T3).

Questions related to sex, gender and sexual orientation will be drawn from the LGBT-COVID survey previously conducted by the research team.33 All these variables will be assessed at only the first measurement time (T1), except for employment, income (personal and household), gender, sexual orientation and marital status, which will be assessed at each measurement time (T1, T2 and T3).

Gambling practices profile: An inventory of gambling activities adapted from the University Student Gambling Habit Survey/Enquête sur les Habitudes de Jeu des Étudiants Universitaires survey30 will be used to assess participation in gambling activities (lottery, casinos, video lottery terminals, scratch cards, online gambling, etc.), the age at which gambling began (age of first gambling activity), the amount of money spent per week, the frequency of gambling per week and the number of hours spent gambling per week. The severity of problem gambling will be assessed using the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI).34 The PGSI consists of nine questions answered on a Likert scale. A score of between 0 and 3 is assigned to each response and the scores are added up to give a total score of between 0 and 27. Its cut-off scores determine gambling severity: non-problem (0), low-risk gambling (1–2), moderate-risk gambling (3–7) and high-risk gambling (>8). A score greater than or equal to three indicates problematic gambling (i.e., moderate-high risk).35 This validated tool is used in most research studies on gambling in Canada as well as by Statistics Canada.3 30 It was also used in our study on gambling among 2SLGBTQI+ populations during the COVID-19 pandemic.33 The harms associated with gambling will be assessed using the Short Gambling Harms Screen.36 Data on the gambling practices profile will be assessed at each measurement time (T1, T2 and T3).

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses and comparison tests will be performed to describe the gambling habits of SGD populations and compare groups (sex, gender, sexual orientation, etc.) at each measurement time. Repeated measures analyses of variance will be used to compare groups between different measurement times, considering their characteristics (PHQ-8 score, GAD-7, etc.). Latent class analyses will be used to model participants’ gambling trajectories based on the 0–27 score obtained on the PGSI for each participant in the T1–T3 questionnaires. This type of analysis allows individuals with similar gambling trajectory profiles to be grouped into different subgroups. More specifically, group-based trajectory models will be estimated to identify latent trajectory classes based on the scores obtained on the PGSI (i.e., risk level: problematic vs. non-problematic gambling).42 47 48 These analyses offer great flexibility due to considering the heterogeneity both within classes (each individual in a class follows an individual trajectory that varies randomly around the class mean) and between classes (different trajectories reflect the heterogeneous evolution of individuals over time).42 47 48

Different models will be tested to evaluate the number of trajectories obtained and the effect of time. Theoretically, for three measurement times and in the presence or absence of problematic gambling, according to the PGSI score, a maximum of eight trajectories is possible. The optimal number of trajectories will be established based on the Bayesian information criterion (where the smallest value will be prioritized), entropy (≥0.80) and the significance of the Vuong-Lo-Mendell-Rubin test.42 The most parsimonious and clinically interpretable model will be selected.49

According to Loughran and Nagin,50 500 participants are sufficient to model trajectories. This study will aim for a minimum of 5% of participants (i.e., 125 individuals) in each trajectory to ensure interpretability, and each participant will have a posterior probability of at least 70% of belonging to a given trajectory to ensure that participants are assigned to groups with a minimum level of confidence. The different trajectories will be described, as will the proportion and characteristics of participants belonging to each one. The descriptive analyses will enhance our description by profiling the individuals with each gambling trajectory (sex, gender, sexual orientation, age, GAD-7 score, PHQ-8 score, etc.).

To identify the variables associated with problematic gambling trajectories (i.e., trajectories involving problematic gambling—i.e., a score greater than or equal to three on the PGSI at least once), multivariate regression models (binary or polytomous logistic, depending on the number of trajectories identified) will be used. Participants’ belonging to different trajectories will be used as a categorical dependent variable, and certain variables such as anxiety or depressive symptoms, alcohol or substance use, type of gambling practiced, etc, assessed at the first measurement time (T1), will be used as independent variables. The interactions between sex, gender and sexual orientation will be tested for all the models.

All the multivariate models will be developed using recognized modeling techniques.51 52 The descriptive analyses and multivariate regression models will be performed using IBMS SPSS Statistics version 31 software, while the latent class analyses will be conducted using SAS version 9.4M9 software.

Phase 2: qualitative study

In phase 2, which will be a descriptive qualitative study, semi-structured interviews (n=40) will be conducted to understand the experiences and lived realities of SGD individuals with problematic gambling trajectories.24 25

Eligibility criteria

To participate in the semi-structured interviews, individuals must have participated in phase 1 of the study, have agreed to be contacted regarding phase 2 of the study at the end of the online questionnaire and present a problematic gambling trajectory.

Sampling method and sample size

The sampling process will be targeted and apply maximum variation53 based on the following criteria: age, sex, gender, sexual orientation and problematic gambling trajectories. This approach will facilitate a more in-depth understanding of the experiences and backgrounds of individuals with problematic gambling trajectories based on the different profiles identified in phase 1. Recruitment will be carried out by members of the research team via email based on the list of participants who agreed to be contacted for phase 2 and who meet the inclusion criteria. Individuals who do not respond to the initial invitation will be contacted again after seven days by telephone and/or email. A gift card (e.g., grocery store, pharmacy) will be given to each participant as compensation for their time.

The sample will consist of SGD individuals (n=40). In descriptive qualitative research, approximately 40 interviews are considered sufficient to provide rich and varied data,54 and this corresponds to the standards reported in the literature on gambling activities.55,58 If necessary, the sample size will be adjusted according to the observed data saturation.54

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews (lasting 60–90 min) will be conducted in French or English, virtually (or if not possible, by telephone), depending on the preferences of the participants. The interviews will be conducted by two research team members with expertise in qualitative methods and experience in semi-structured interviews.

Based on the results of phase 1, open-ended questions will be used to explore the participants’ problematic gambling trajectories (evolution over time, etc), how gambling fits into their lives, the impacts of gambling (on interpersonal relationships, work, study, mental health, etc), their experiences as individuals identifying as SGD (discrimination, etc) and how those experiences relate to their gambling practices, their experiences with healthcare and social services in relation to problematic gambling, and their proposals for strategies to prevent or reduce the harms associated with gambling among SGD populations. The interviews will be recorded in video format and then transcribed as anonymised verbatim by a professional transcriptionist.

Data analysis

A deductive and inductive thematic analysis53 will be performed. The verbatim transcripts of the interviews will first be independently coded and classified by themes by two members of the research team. The themes will then be grouped together to create a thematic tree structure. Iterative phases of analysis will be performed so that the research team members can assimilate the content of the analysis and the coding process, in addition to developing a common understanding of the context and the data studied.59 This iterative process will allow for triangulation of research team members’ expertise and knowledge (medical, psychological, social work, etc.) until data saturation is achieved.60 Matrices will also be created to present qualitative data based on the sex, gender and sexual orientation of SGD individuals. NVivo software will be used to analyze the qualitative data, including the production of matrices.

Integration phase

As is typical in a mixed-methods study, the quantitative and qualitative results will be integrated and compared. The results of phase 1 will be used to guide the semi-structured interviews in phase 2, while the results of phase 2 (i.e., the study of the experiences and lived realities of SGD individuals) will facilitate an in-depth understanding of problematic gambling trajectories and the factors associated with problematic gambling identified during phase 1. At the end of the integration process, matrices, diagrams, graphs and tables will be produced.

To formulate courses of action, a co-construction workshop will be run according to the Technique of Information Retrieval by Facilitating a Group of Experts method, which is an inductive and structured gathering method aimed at obtaining a consensus within a group.57 59 This workshop will be conducted with members of the advisory committee (see the Patient and public involvement section) and will provide an opportunity to formulate courses of action to prevent and reduce the harms associated with problem gambling among SGD populations.61 62

Ethics approval and dissemination

This research project has been ethically and scientifically approved by the Research Ethics Committee and by the CIUSSS de l’Estrie—CHUS Scientific Evaluation Committee on 3 November 2025 (reference number: 2026-6060 Trajectoires-JHA-LGBTQ). For all phases of the study, written or verbal consent will be obtained from each participant. A copy of the consent form and contact information will be sent to each participant.

Patient and public involvement

To ensure that the project is relevant to the needs of SGD populations and stakeholders in the field, and to ensure integrated knowledge mobilisation, we will form an advisory committee of six members who will meet three times a year. The six-member committee will be composed of four project partners and two individuals who have or previously had GD and who belong to the SGD community.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first international longitudinal study focusing on gambling activity among SGD populations. This study will facilitate better understanding of problematic gambling trajectories among SGD populations and of the specific characteristics of these populations. At the end of this study, courses of action will be proposed to contribute to prevention and harm reduction in this area. By fostering and strengthening alliances between the scientific community and relevant actors in the field (practice settings, community organisations, etc) and by involving various stakeholders, including through their participation in the advisory committee, the courses of action proposed in this study will be easier to implement. Indeed, it has previously been demonstrated that the close involvement of stakeholders in research studies plays a significant role in their willingness to use and adhere to the results of such research.63 Finally, this study will raise awareness of the risks of gambling activities among the general public, including SGD individuals.
