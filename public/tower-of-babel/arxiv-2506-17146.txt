A tutorial overview of model predictive control for continuous crystallization: current possibilities and future perspectives
Collin R. Johnson, Kerstin Wohlgemuth, Sergio Lucia

Abstract
This paper presents a systematic approach to the advanced control of continuous crystallization processes using model predictive control. We provide a tutorial introduction to controlling complex particle size distributions by integrating population balance equations with detailed models of various continuous crystallizers. Since these high-fidelity models are often too complex for online optimization, we propose the use of data-driven surrogate models that enable efficient optimization-based control. Through two case studies, one with a low-complexity system allowing direct comparison with traditional methods and another involving a spatially distributed crystallizer, we demonstrate how our approach enables real-time model predictive control while maintaining accuracy. The presented methodology facilitates the use of complex models in a model-based control framework, allowing precise control of key particle size distribution characteristics, such as the median particle size d 50 \displaystyle d_{50} and the width d 90 − d 10 \displaystyle d_{90}-d_{10} . This addresses a critical challenge in pharmaceutical and fine chemical manufacturing, where product quality depends on tight control of particle characteristics.

Introduction

Crystallization is a fundamental separation process widely applied in various industries, ranging from sugar production
[
1
]
to pharmaceuticals
[
2
]
, where achieving product purity and quality is critical. In the pharmaceutical sector, stringent demands on product quality require precise control over particle properties, such as particle size distribution, directly affecting drug effectiveness
[
3
]
. Traditionally, batch crystallization processes are favored when a specific particle size distribution is needed. Although continuous crystallizers have long been employed for petrochemicals, batch processes have predominated in the pharmaceutical industry
[
4
]
.

In recent years, continuous crystallization has been recognized as beneficial in many areas, including pharmaceutical processes
[
5
]
. Compared to batch methods, continuous processes offer advantages, such as reduced batch-to-batch variability and improved reproducibility. Additionally, they lower both operational and capital costs
[
2
]
.

Effective process control is essential to fully leverage the advantages of continuous crystallization. Model predictive control (MPC) is a widely used advanced control technique capable of handling nonlinear systems, constraints, and multiple control objectives
[
6
]
. MPC has been successfully applied to both batch and continuous crystallization processes, yet most implementations rely on moment-based methods to model the particle size distribution
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. While numerically simple, these methods struggle to accurately represent full particle size distributions. Furthermore, many applications found in the literature are limited to lumped parameter models that neglect spatial variations. However, many continuous crystallization systems, such as slug-flow crystallizers
[
11
]
and continuous oscillatory baffled crystallizers
[
12
]
, require detailed spatial modeling. The combination of spatially distributed crystallizer models with population balance equations often leads to complex partial differential equations (PDEs), which pose significant computational challenges for optimization-based methods like MPC.

Recent advances in machine learning have made these challenges manageable. Data-driven methods offer a promising solution by enabling optimization-based control even when detailed models for particle size distribution and crystallization dynamics are used. Surrogate models can approximate complex first-principle models while maintaining computational efficiency, making real-time optimization feasible. Applications of surrogate modeling in process optimization have been successfully demonstrated in
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. For crystallization,
Meyer et al. 2024
applied a data-driven approach, using process measurement data to train models for batch crystallization, and successfully integrated them into an MPC framework. However, most existing approaches only track auxiliary states, e.g. the concentration of the liquid phase, indirectly controlling the particle size distribution rather than explicitly regulating its key characteristics. For instance,
Lima et al. 2022
implemented a neural network-based nonlinear MPC scheme for batch crystallization to track solute concentration, while
Rohani et al. 1999
applied neural networks in autoregressive models with exogenous inputs (ARX) to regulate supersaturation in continuous crystallization.

Despite these advances, it has not yet been computationally feasible to develop MPC schemes that incorporate both a detailed particle size distribution model and a spatially distributed crystallizer model. As a result, there is currently no comprehensive overview of dynamic modeling techniques that address both aspects. This work aims to bridge this gap by providing a tutorial overview of the most suitable dynamic modeling strategies for particle size distribution and spatially distributed crystallizers. Additionally, we outline the steps required to develop machine learning-based surrogate models and demonstrate how they can be leveraged to construct computationally tractable MPC controllers enabling advanced process control for continuous crystallization processes. With this tutorial, we aim to foster stronger collaboration between the communities of advanced process control and continuous crystallization.

However, there is currently a gap in the literature regarding the use of detailed first-principle models and the potential of machine learning to develop advanced, computationally tractable controllers for continuous crystallization, which we aim to fill with this work. Population balance equation and solution methods

The population balance equation tracks the temporal evolution of some population and was first introduced by
Hulburt and Katz 1964
. Since its formulation is very general, it can be applied to a broad range of fields. Subsequently, we will use the terms common to the field of crystallization. Figure
2
shows the relation between a population of particles and the distribution function which the population balance equation aims to describe.

As described by
Ramkrishna 2000
, we split the particle state space into internal and external coordinates. The internal coordinates refer to the characteristics of the particles as such. We will use either some particle length
\displaystyle L
or volume
\displaystyle V
as an internal coordinate. In addition, it is possible to track the distribution of the shape of the particles, resulting in a multidimensional distribution. The external coordinates refer to the location of the particles. Following, we assume particles to be uniformly distributed in space and, therefore, omitting external coordinates within the population balance equation. For the one-dimensional case, we obtain the following population balance equation:

\frac{\partial n(L,t)}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial L}(G(L)n(L,t))=h(L,t).

(1)

The number density function is represented by
\displaystyle n
and is a function of time
\displaystyle t
, and the internal coordinate, where we use the particle length
\displaystyle L
. The number density function represents how many particles possess specific characteristics, in this case particle length, per unit volume at a given time. We define
\displaystyle G(L)
as the growth rate. For the source function
\displaystyle h(L,t)
, containing birth and death terms, we consider nucleation and agglomeration:

\displaystyle\displaystyle h(L,t)=N+B-D,

(2a)

with

\displaystyle\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\displaystyle=f(L,t),

(2b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{L}\beta(L^{\prime},L-L^{\prime})n(L^{\prime},t)n(L-L^{\prime},t)dL^{\prime},

(2c)

\displaystyle\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\displaystyle=n(L,t)\int_{0}^{\infty}\beta(L,L^{\prime})n(L^{\prime},t)dL^{\prime},

(2d)

where
\displaystyle N
refers to the nucleation rate,
\displaystyle B
is the birth term associated with agglomeration, and
\displaystyle D
is the corresponding death term. For the calculation of agglomeration, the kernel function
\displaystyle\beta
must be known. The population balance equation is therefore complex to solve, combining both integral and partial differential components.

Note that, to keep the notation uncluttered, we omitted the dependency on the properties of the continuous phase in (
1
) and (
2
). To obtain a meaningful model for our system we must couple the population balance equation with equations describing the continuous phase and functions, e.g., for nucleation rate
\displaystyle N
, growth rate
\displaystyle G
, and kernel
\displaystyle\beta
. These are usually dependent on other process parameters, such as concentration, temperature, and pressure. Coupling the population balance equation with the equations modeling the continuous phase, i.e., the mass and energy balances, is essential and will be covered in Section

2.1
Standard method of moments (SMOM)

A simple way to solve the population balance equation is to apply the moment transformation and track the temporal evolution of the moments. The method of moments was first introduced by
Hulburt and Katz 1964
and the moment transformation is given by:

\mu_{k}=\int L^{k}n(L)dL,

(3)

where
\displaystyle\mu_{k}
represents the
\displaystyle k
-th moment. Moments can be related to cumulative physical properties of the distribution. The
\displaystyle 0
-th moment, for example, corresponds to the total number of particles, whereas the
\displaystyle 1
-st moment corresponds to the cumulative length of the distribution when using a length as internal coordinate
\displaystyle L
. Applying the method of moments to the population balance equation leads to a set of
\displaystyle k
ordinary differential equations. However, for the general population balance equation from (
1
), which contains an arbitrary growth function as well as arbitrary agglomeration kernels, the set of equations is not necessarily closed, e.g. the set of equations describing the evolution of the first
\displaystyle k
moments might also contain the
\displaystyle k+1
-st moment. In practice, the standard method of moments is not applicable to size-dependent growth (
\displaystyle G=f(L)
) and arbitrary agglomeration kernels. Consequently, we apply the method of moments to the population balance equation considering only nucleation and size-independent growth. The resulting differential equation for the
\displaystyle k
-th moment is given by:

\frac{d\mu_{k}}{dt}=kG\mu_{k-1}.

(4)

By assuming nucleation to occur at
\displaystyle L=0
, we consider nucleation as a boundary condition for the
\displaystyle 0
-th moment:

\frac{d\mu_{0}}{dt}=N.

(5)

It is worth noting that it is possible to calculate characteristic properties of the number density function using the moments, such as mean diameters and their coefficients of variation. The mean length of the mass distribution
\displaystyle L_{43}
and its coefficient of variation
CV
can be calculated as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle L_{43}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{4}}{\mu_{3}},

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\mu_{4}-L_{43}^{2}}}{L_{43}}.

2.2
Quadrature method of moments (QMOM)

A method that also tracks the moments of the distribution but overcomes the challenge of a non-closed equation system is the quadrature method of moments
[
29
]
. Here, the moments of the distribution are approximated by a quadrature formula:

\mu_{k}=\int L^{k}n(L)dL=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{q}}L_{i}^{k}w_{i}.

(8)

The number density function
\displaystyle n(L)
is approximated at the abscissas
\displaystyle L_{i}
using the weights
\displaystyle w_{i}
:

n(L)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{N_{q}}w_{i}\delta(L-L_{i}),

(9)

where
\displaystyle\delta
represents the Dirac delta function. It is possible to calculate the first
\displaystyle 2N_{q}
moments using a quadrature formula of order
\displaystyle N_{q}
. To find the weights and abscissas given the moments, the nonlinear algebraic equation system shown in (
8
) must be solved. The combination of (
8
) with the differential equations of (
4
) leads to a differential algebraic equation system which can be solved directly using solvers for differential-algebraic systems (DAE)
[
30
]
. An alternative is the product-difference algorithm (PD)
[
31
]
as shown in
[
29
]
, which solves (
8
) efficiently given the moments. Using the approximation of the number distribution function shown in (
9
), the integrals needed for agglomeration in (
2
) can be transformed into sums. The calculation of agglomeration using the quadrature method of moments can be found in
Marchisio et al. 2003a
,
Marchisio et al. 2003b
.

The quadrature method of moments results in a closed equation system for the general population balance equation from (
1
). In contrast to the standard method of moments, however, it is only an approximation. In addition, to setup the differential equations as in (
4
), at each time step the PD-algorithm needs to be performed, leading to a slightly higher complexity compared to the standard method of moments.

2.3
Discretized population balance equation (DPBE)

For applications where knowledge of the full number density function is desired, we have to resort to other methods. The discretization of the distribution is a method that yields the evolution of the full distribution. We use the method of finite volumes to solve the population balance equation. The method discretizes the domain of
\displaystyle L
into
\displaystyle N_{D}
finite volumes or cells of width
\displaystyle\Delta L_{i}
where the average value of the number density in cell
\displaystyle i
is given by:

n_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{\Delta L_{i}}\int_{L_{i}^{-}}^{L_{i}^{+}}n(L,t)dL,

(10)

where
\displaystyle L_{i}^{+}
and
\displaystyle L_{i}^{-}
correspond to the faces of cell
\displaystyle i
, i.e. the particle length at the boundaries of cell
\displaystyle i
. Thus, the method leads to a system of
\displaystyle N_{D}
ordinary differential equations. Exemplary application to the population balance equation for cell
\displaystyle i
considering only growth leads to:

\frac{\partial n_{i}(t)}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{\Delta L_{i}}(G(L_{i}^{+})n(L_{i}^{+},t)-G(L_{i}^{-})n(L_{i}^{-},t))=0.

(11)

The accuracy of the method is highly dependent on the method used to determine the values of
\displaystyle n(L_{i}^{+},t)
and
\displaystyle n(L_{i}^{-},t)
on the cell faces. As shown by
Liu et al. 1994
, we use a weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO) scheme to calculate the fluxes between finite volumes. Nucleation is easily considered by adding the respective source term scaled by the width of the first cell to the differential equation of the first cell. Agglomeration is considered by computing the birth and death terms of (
2c
) and (
2d
) for each element. Performing this calculation for each cell is expensive.

An advantage of the discretization method is the possibility of using a geometric grid. Geometric grids are especially advantageous when particle sizes vary over multiple orders of magnitudes. In addition, they coincide with the nature of agglomeration, where two particles of the same mass form one particle of twice the mass. The calculation of agglomeration for a geometric grid in which the volume is doubled for each succeeding cell is shown in
Hounslow et al. 1988
. For some applications, it might be interesting to use a finer grid. Building on the work of Hounslow,
Litster et al. 1995
developed a scheme for a geometric grid where the volume of a succeeding cell is
\displaystyle 2^{1/q}
times the volume of the preceding cell, where
\displaystyle q
is an integer value.

2.4
Orthogonal collocation on finite elements (OCFE)

Another method that yields the full distribution function
\displaystyle n(L,t)
is orthogonal collocation on finite elements. The domain of the inner coordinate is discretized into finite elements, and the distribution function within each element is approximated by a polynomial.
Gelbard and Seinfeld 1978
first proposed this methodology for population balance equations using cubic polynomials. We use Lagrange polynomials for the approximation as described in
[
38
]
and
[
39
]
. An approximate solution of
\displaystyle n(L,t)
can be obtained by enforcing the original population balance equation at some collocation points within each finite element. Additionally, algebraic states can be introduced to enforce continuity and differentiability between finite elements. The introduction of algebraic states leads to the necessity of boundary equations. For nucleation-growth processes, the boundary equation for the first element can be easily found as
\displaystyle n(L=0,t)=\frac{N}{G}
. In general, the boundary equation must be chosen according to the process, where some suitable boundary equations can be found in the literature
[
38
]
.

Similarly to the discretization method, it is possible to define a geometric grid for the finite elements. Unfortunately, solutions for a geometric grid were found to be prone to instabilities. Additionally, by introducing algebraic equations, the OCFE method leads to a DAE system. The necessity of using a usually slower solver for DAE systems is a disadvantage of the method.

2.5
Monte Carlo (MC)

Monte Carlo simulations represent an entirely different approach compared to previous solution methods. Instead of tracking the distribution function
\displaystyle n(L,t)
, a large number of individual particles is generated and nucleation, growth, and agglomeration are simulated for the population of particles. An overview of different methods based on Monte Carlo simulations applied to the population balance equation can be found in
[
40
]
. We use a simple time-driven simulation technique proposed in
[
41
]
. Here, a time step
\displaystyle\Delta t
is chosen before the simulation and the manipulation of the particles is performed at each time step. Growth is considered using an Euler method formula:

L_{i,t+1}=L_{i,t}+G(L_{i,t})\Delta t,

(12)

where
\displaystyle L_{i}
represents the length of the
\displaystyle i
-th particle of the distribution. For the case of size-independent growth, this formulation is exact.

For nucleation and agglomeration, particles must be added or deleted from the simulated population of particles. Given the nucleation rate and the agglomeration rate, it is possible to determine the necessary number of nucleation events
\displaystyle N_{\text{nucl}}
and agglomeration events
\displaystyle N_{\text{agg}}
for a time step
\displaystyle\Delta t
. Then, for nucleation,
\displaystyle N_{\text{nucl}}
particles at
\displaystyle L=0
are added to the population. For agglomeration, we consider a constant kernel function. Hence,
\displaystyle N_{\text{agg}}
times, two random particles of the population are chosen, removed from the population, and a single new particle is added to the population. The internal coordinate
\displaystyle L
of the new particle must be calculated in accordance with the laws of mass conservation.

In the scope of this work, we use the Monte Carlo method to verify the other solution methods presented in cases where no analytical solution exists. In comparison to the simple MC method presented here, computationally more efficient and numerically superior methods exist. In practice, e.g. constant N methods (e.g.
[
42
]
) should be preferred where the number of simulated particles is kept constant.

Models for different crystallization concepts

Crystallization design targets specific particle characteristics, using the population balance equation to predict and control these properties. However, the parameters that characterize phenomena such as nucleation, growth, and agglomeration are functions of the state of the continuous phase. Hence, the states that are necessary to model the significant phenomena must be identified and modeled. For cooling crystallization, for example, the necessary states are temperature and concentration as well as potential additional parameters such as stirrer speed or flow velocity. Generally, the methods presented in Section
2
can be coupled with arbitrary models that describe the continuous phase. In this chapter, we present models for three different crystallizer concepts: a mixed suspension, mixed product removal (MSMPR) crystallizer, an MSMPR cascade, and a tubular crystallizer. For both MSMPR models we use the assumption that particles are uniformly distributed in space, which allows the usage of the population balance equation from (
1
). For the tubular crystallizer, we use a finite-volume discretization scheme to solve the partial differential equation, where particles are assumed to be uniformly distributed within each volume, again allowing the usage of (
1
). For all models, we assume the feed flow to contain seed crystals.

\displaystyle c_{p}
. The model can be adapted by using mixing rules to determine the density and specific heat capacity within each stage.

3.2
Tubular crystallizer

The tubular crystallizer we present consists of a tube-in-tube design as seen in
[
43
]
. A sketch can be seen in Figure
6c
. We assume the flow in the outer tube, containing the cooling medium, to flow co-currently to the flow within the inner tube. Nevertheless, the model of the cooling tube can easily be adapted to model counter-current flow, or even consisting of multiple segments running co-currently or counter-currently. The model of the tubular crystallizer is represented by the convection-diffusion-reaction equation, where crystallization can be interpreted as the reaction:

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-v\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}+D\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial z^{2}}-s(u),

(14)

where
\displaystyle u
can represent the concentration or temperature in the process medium as well as the temperature in the cooling medium. The respective sink and source terms
\displaystyle s(u)
contain the mass flow from the continuous phase to the discrete phase for the component balance and the heat flows for the heat balances. The flow velocity
\displaystyle v
in the convection term and the diffusion coefficient
\displaystyle D
in the diffusion term are considered to be constant along the spatial coordinate
\displaystyle z
. To solve (
14
), the spatial coordinate
\displaystyle z
is discretized into finite volumes. For the approximation of the convection term on the cell faces, we use the
\displaystyle 5
-th order WENO scheme
[
34
]
. The approximation of the diffusion term is performed by using a
\displaystyle 4
-th order finite difference scheme.

4
Transformation to a control-oriented model

In the previous sections, we showed methods to model the disperse phase, i.e. the crystals, as well as the continuous phase. When solved simultaneously, we obtain our full model. Both modeling components require decisions that balance model accuracy with computational cost. For the dispersed phase, this trade-off involves choosing solution methods and their parameters, such as the number of elements in orthogonal collocation on finite elements. The continuous phase offers fewer modeling choices, though the tubular model still requires selecting the number of finite volumes for discretization.

Detailed models can serve as a digital twin of existing systems, providing real-time insights for process monitoring and controller tuning. In addition, these digital representations facilitate the deployment of advanced control methods. In this work, the intent is to use the derived model for control, i.e. MPC, which can introduce some constraints on the model. Generally, the model must provide a certain level of accuracy for it to be useful. Secondly, the real-time usage of the model in our control scheme must be feasible. For MPC e.g. the model needs to be suitable for real-time optimization. For some cases, the required level of detail of the model leads to a model that is too complex to be used for real-time control. For these models, the model can be approximated using a data-based surrogate model to keep the required accuracy but achieve real-time feasibility. The price of using an approximation of the model is the limited validity. The data-based model should be used for interpolation only.

4.1
Surrogate model development

The general structure of approximating the high-fidelity first-principle model that we have obtained from the previous sections is simple. The first-principle model takes current states
\displaystyle x_{k}
and inputs
\displaystyle u_{k}
and computes the next state
\displaystyle x_{k+1}
:

x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k}).

(15)

For a single stage MSMPR using a moment-based solution method, the inputs at time step
\displaystyle k
, for example, could be
\displaystyle u_{k}=\{F_{\text{feed},k},T_{\text{feed},k},T_{J,\text{in},k}\}
and the states at time step
\displaystyle k
are
\displaystyle x_{k}=\{c_{k},T_{k},T_{J,k},\mu_{i,k}\}
. By gathering data in open-loop simulations, we can subsequently train a data-based model, usually a neural network, which approximates
\displaystyle f
in Equation (
15
).

For more complex models, i.e. models containing a larger number of states, it might not be desirable to use every state as input to the data-based model and to predict every state as output. To avoid an unnecessarily large data-based model, i.e. a large number of inputs and outputs of the neural network, we can choose to only use a subset of the states. This subset we will call measurements
\displaystyle y
. The measurements can be selected such that
\displaystyle y
corresponds to real measurements. This opens up the possibility of using the model in a state observer. In addition, states that are of interest to control the process can be added to
\displaystyle y
. This usually includes all states at the output of the crystallizer. As for the spatial dimension, where we only choose states at certain positions as measurements, it is useful not to predict the entire particle size distribution. Methods that provide the entire particle size distribution allow for the calculation of specific characteristic parameters, for example, the mode or median of the distribution, as well as any desired widths. To reduce the size of the data-based model,
\displaystyle y
should only contain the characteristic parameters of the particle size distribution that are important for the process. Further reduction of the complexity of the neural network can be achieved by applying principal component analysis to the input of the neural network.

To enable the prediction based on measurements, we use a nonlinear autoregressive model with exogenous inputs (NARX)
[
44
]
, which results in the following structure for our model:

y_{k+1}=f(y_{k},y_{k-1},\ldots,y_{k-l},u_{k},u_{k-1},\ldots,u_{k-l}).

(16)

For a NARX model, in addition to current measurement
\displaystyle y_{k}
and input
\displaystyle u_{k}
as inputs to the data-based model, past measurements and inputs up to time step
\displaystyle k-l
are used. The outputs still consist only of
\displaystyle y_{k+1}
. The significantly reduced number of outputs has a highly positive effect on the prediction accuracy.

5
Model predictive control for crystallization

The overall objective of this paper is to show the application of model predictive control to continuous crystallization. The system should be modeled with the objective function of the control scheme in mind. Applications that require control of the shape of the size distribution require solution methods that track the full distribution. Subsequently, it is possible to control some characteristic values of the distribution. In this chapter, we show the application of MPC to the two different models obtained in the previous section. The 2-stage MSMPR model was chosen as a low-complexity model, allowing comparison with an MPC controller using the first-principle model. By comparison to this benchmark solution, insights can be gained even though the presented methodology of approximating the model would not be necessary for this case. The second system, the tubular crystallizer using the DPBE method, is an example in which the proposed method is necessary. Due to its complexity, the resulting model is not suitable for optimization in MPC.

5.1
Model predictive control

The idea of MPC is to solve an optimization problem that produces an optimal sequence of inputs for a given prediction horizon of
\displaystyle N
steps and to apply only the first input of the sequence to the system. In the next time step, the optimization problem is solved again with the updated states, and again the first input is applied to the system. This procedure is repeated for the duration of the process. The algorithm therefore inverts the process of simulating a model. Instead of choosing input values and calculating the next states, we choose desired states (or at least a function of the states and inputs) and calculate a sequence of inputs that achieve the desired states in an optimal fashion for the prediction horizon.

The standard optimization problem for nonlinear MPC can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\min_{u_{k}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}l(s_{k},u_{k})+V_{f}(s_{N})

(19a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{k+1}=f(s_{k},u_{k})

(19b)

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{k}\in\mathcal{S}

(19c)

\displaystyle\displaystyle u_{k}\in\mathcal{U}

(19d)

\displaystyle\displaystyle s_{0}=s_{\text{initial}},

(19e)

where the states of the system at time step
\displaystyle k
are denoted as
\displaystyle s_{k}
and the inputs at time step
\displaystyle k
are
\displaystyle u_{k}
. We divide the cost function into stage cost
\displaystyle l
and terminal cost
\displaystyle V_{f}
. The system model is given by
\displaystyle f
, state constraints by
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
, and input constraints by
\displaystyle\mathcal{U}
. Furthermore, we add a constraint for the initial state
\displaystyle s_{0}
to be
\displaystyle s_{\text{initial}}
. For the solution of the MPC problem, do-mpc
[
45
]
and CASADi
[
46
]
was used with the IPOPT solver
[
49
]
.

6
Discussion and future perspectives

Figure 10
:
Decision tree showing the necessity of training a surrogate model depending on the modeling choices.

Figure

presents a decision tree illustrating when a data-based model may be necessary. In general, it is preferable to avoid the additional step of model approximation due to the limited validity of surrogate models outside the training data. However, the model used within the MPC controller must be as accurate as possible to ensure effective process control. Therefore, modeling should aim for the highest possible fidelity. In this context, using a surrogate of a detailed model can yield more accurate predictions than relying on an overly simplified model that does not require approximation. For continuous crystallization, we have shown that the direct use of a first-principles model is only feasible for MSMPR or multi-stage MSMPR systems, and only when the population balance equation is solved via a moment-based method. In all other cases, a surrogate model must be trained, an approach that, as demonstrated in the previous section, can also yield highly accurate results.

6.1
Novel continuous crystallizers

The key challenges in continuous cooling crystallization are controlling the residence time distribution, avoiding fouling, suspension of particles, and scale-up to large product quantities. In this work, standard models such as the MSMPR and a tubular crystallizer were used for the crystallizer models, which are idealized crystallizer concepts that have both advantages and disadvantages. Although particle suspension and scale-up are simple for MSMPR-based crystallizers, the residence time distribution of MSMPR-based crystallizers is broad which can lead to a broad particle size distribution
[
4
]
. Concepts like fines dissolution, i.e. the dissolution of small particles, have been developed to mitigate the issue of a broad particle distribution
[
50
]
.

Tubular crystallizers offer advantages in heat transfer and residence time distribution if backmixing can be avoided
[
51
]
. Therefore, the main objective for new tubular crystallizer concepts is to avoid backmixing and ensure a sufficient level of suspension of the particles. There are several concepts to optimize particle suspension and residence time distribution. The technically simplest is to ensure a turbulent flow. However, the necessary high flow velocities require a very long crystallizer to enable a sufficiently long residence time, which leads to high pressure losses. Therefore, other concepts for tubular crystallizers are preferred
[
51
]
.

An example of a tubular crystallizer which can achieve a narrow residence time distribution is a tubular crystallizer with static mixers
[
52
,
53
]
. By containing internal mixing elements, radial mixing is facilitated, which approximates plug flow behavior while simultaneously improving heat transfer. The open challenges are the possible settling of crystals in low-velocity regions and, accordingly, potential fouling. A different tubular crystallizer concept is the continuous oscillatory baffled crystallizer
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
12
]
in which baffles disturb the flow in the crystallizer and, in combination with an oscillating flow, lead to enhanced mixing. The open challenges for the continuous oscillatory baffled crystallizer are achieving a narrow residence time distribution as well as avoiding fouling
[
12
]
. Another concept of a tubular crystallizer is the slug flow crystallizer
[
57
,
11
,
58
,
59
,
60
,
43
]
. Here, the crystallization medium passes through the crystallizer alternately with an immiscible fluid, often air. If backmixing can be avoided, this leads to a very narrow residence time distribution and very good particle suspension because of the Taylor vortices created in the slugs. An open challenge for the slug flow crystallizer is also avoiding fouling
[
43
]
.

When creating models for novel crystallization systems, the division into mixed and spatially distributed systems, as well as the selection of the solution method, remains important. The description of new phenomena can further increase the complexity of the model and makes approximation by a data-based model even more important for the subsequent use in MPC. New directions in research also take recycle loops into account. These complicate the modeling and, above all, increase the computational cost significantly. An approximation of the model is therefore particularly interesting here.

6.2
Parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification

Parameter estimation is a major challenge in model development. In particular, for crystallization, the PBE parameters for nucleation, growth, agglomeration, and breakage must be fitted to data
[
61
]
. In practice, there are often only a few experiments with noisy measurements available. Consequently, challenges arise in the identifiability and sensitivity of the parameters
[
62
]
. The work in

[
61
]
provides a study on different techniques for identifying parameters within crystallization models using the PBE. They argue that a combination of global and local search methods works best for identifying PBE parameters.

More recently developed methods for determining parameters aim to additionally quantify the uncertainty of the parameters. A Bayesian approach to parameter estimation in which a prior belief in the parameters is used to calculate a posterior distribution from the data is well known
[
63
]
. Recent methods use, for example, machine learning to overcome computational limitations. The work in
[
64
]
presented a method in which Bayesian inference is approximated using neural networks. Although the method directly gives only the mean value of the posterior distribution, the efficient calculation using neural networks makes it easy to use a bootstrap algorithm to quantify the uncertainty.

Parameter estimation in continuous crystallization is particularly challenging due to the limited and often low-quality data available. For this reason, we expect that uncertainty quantification methods will play a pivotal role. Such approaches should make the most of the available information while quantifying uncertainty to assess the reliability of the predictions, enabling robust control approaches.

As described in this work, it is also possible to approximate the full first-principle model using a data-based model. When approximating the entire model, it is particularly important to also quantify the uncertainty of the model predictions. Valid predictions can only be obtained in the area of training data, that is, for interpolation. Data-based models that can quantify uncertainty have been part of research for a long time. Gaussian processes are standard data-based models that quantify the uncertainty of their predictions
[
65
]
. Unfortunately, they scale very poorly with larger data sets. Recent research focuses on neural network-based models that can determine the uncertainty of predictions that scale well with more data. Bayesian neural networks
[
66
]
and Bayesian last layer neural networks
[
67
,
68
]
yield Gaussian distributed predictions. However, Bayesian neural networks can only be evaluated with sampling methods in a tedious manner. Bayesian last layer by using only Gaussian weights distributed on the last layer leads to a tractable closed-form solution of the predictions. Other methods for obtaining data-based models with uncertainty quantification exist based on quantile regression
[
69
]
. Here, instead of a model that predicts the mean, models that predict quantiles are trained. The prediction is then given as a prediction interval bounded by the quantile predictions. These models are particularly advantageous for systems with heteroscedasticity where the variance of the predictions is not constant. In conformalized quantile regression
[
70
]
, the prediction is additionally conformalized, which means that the prediction interval is increased by a constant value to guarantee a certain prediction interval with a certain probability.

New research directions are focused on the further development of data-based models that can quantify the uncertainty of their predictions. The difference between epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty, i.e., uncertainty due to too little data or uncertainty due to variance within the data, is particularly important here. Furthermore, the application of data-based models that can quantify uncertainty in algorithms for control and observers is interesting. The challenge here is how the information about the uncertainty can be used in the most efficient manner.

6.3
Robust and stochastic model predictive control

After developing a model that produces uncertain predictions, whether through uncertain parameters or directly via a surrogate model, it becomes essential to effectively incorporate this uncertainty into the control algorithm. Robust MPC and stochastic MPC offer different approaches. The main trade-off is between not being too conservative and ensuring constraint satisfaction.

In robust MPC, a disturbance or an uncertain parameter is assumed to belong to a bounded set. Subsequently, constraint satisfaction should be guaranteed for all possible values of the disturbance
[
71
]
. A popular robust MPC method is tube-based MPC, where the constraints are tightened based on the disturbance
[
72
,
73
]
. Min-max MPC is a different robust MPC approach in which the cost function is optimized for the maximum disturbance, leading to the best possible trajectory for the worst-case scenario
[
74
]
. Another robust MPC approach is multistage MPC. Here, a scenario tree is constructed based on realizations of the uncertainty, and nominal MPC is performed including enforcing constraint satisfaction for all scenarios
[
75
,
76
]
.

In contrast to robust MPC, stochastic MPC assumes that the disturbance or uncertainty is stochastic. Constraints are satisfied with a certain probability. This consideration can therefore lead to a less conservative performance. Stochastic MPC methods exist for linear and nonlinear systems. Due to computational complexity, stochastic MPC methods are often based on sampling
[
77
]
.

The trade-off between control objectives and constraint satisfaction is inherent in these methods. The development of new methods is mainly concerned with improving computational efficiency and deriving theoretical guarantees, especially for nonlinear models. Still, more advanced methods have not been experimentally demonstrated on continuous crystallization applications. We believe that the tools presented in this tutorial can help in making advanced model-based robust control of continuous crystallization a reality.

7
Conclusion

This work presents a workflow that enables the application of MPC to complex continuous crystallization models. First, methods for solving the PBE are introduced and coupled with crystallizer models, demonstrating their respective advantages and limitations. The resulting models are transformed into optimization-friendly and control-oriented models using neural networks with a NARX structure. Data-based models are trained using training data obtained by the first-principle models and validation using unseen test data. Subsequently, the methodology is applied to two different case studies. First, a two-stage MSMPR with a rather simple model to allow comparison with an MPC using the first-principle model. Then we turn to a tubular crystallizer for which the model is complex. In both cases, the control performance is excellent, and the 2-stage MSMPR results are very close to those of the benchmark MPC. The case studies therefore demonstrate the successful use of complex models for continuous crystallizers in MPC. Whereas previously the particle size distribution was usually only controlled indirectly, it is now possible to directly control parameters of the particle size distribution in an economic cost function.
