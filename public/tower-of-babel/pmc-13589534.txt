Sleep Quality and Depressive Symptoms Among Pregnant Women in the Third Trimester: A Cross-Sectional Latent Profile and Network Analysis
Authors: Yeting Guan, Zhijuan Shen, Qian Du, Qin Tan, Xiaoru Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo identify heterogeneous sleep-quality profiles among pregnant women in the third trimester and characterize profile-specific sleep quality-depressive symptom network structures.Patients and MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 471 pregnant women in the third trimester attending antenatal care at a tertiary Grade A hospital in China. Sleep quality and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), respectively. Latent profile analysis was used to identify sleep-quality subgroups, followed by network analysis in subgroups with adequate sample sizes.ResultsFour sleep-quality profiles were identified: the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group (n=177, 37.6%), daytime dysfunction group (n=85, 18.1%), good sleep quality group (n=182, 38.6%), and low sleep efficiency group (n=27, 5.7%). Because of its small sample size, the low sleep efficiency group was excluded from formal network analysis. SDS11 (“emptiness in life”) had the highest strength in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment and good sleep quality groups, whereas SDS1 (“depressed mood”) had the highest strength in the daytime dysfunction group. PSQI6 (“daytime dysfunction”) had the highest bridge strength in all three analyzed networks. After Holm correction, the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group showed significantly higher global strength than the good sleep quality group (6.531 vs 4.839; adjusted P=0.012).ConclusionSleep quality showed substantial heterogeneity among pregnant women in the third trimester, and sleep quality-depressive symptom network characteristics differed across sleep-quality profiles. These findings provide exploratory evidence on subgroup-specific symptom associations and warrant further validation in prospective studies.Keywords: third trimester, sleep quality, depressive symptoms, latent profile analysis, network analysis

Introduction

During pregnancy, women undergo substantial physiological and psychological adaptations, and sleep quality may deteriorate as gestation advances.1,2 Sleep problems and depressive symptoms are common during pregnancy, although their reported prevalence varies considerably according to the population, geographic setting, assessment instrument, and threshold used. An international meta-analysis of 24 studies involving 11,002 pregnant women reported that 45.7% experienced poor sleep quality, defined as a Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score ≥5.3 A systematic review and meta-analysis of 589 studies involving 616,708 women from 51 low- and middle-income countries reported a pooled prevalence of antenatal depression of 26.3%.4 Importantly, prevalence estimates differed according to the assessment method, with higher estimates obtained using self-report screening instruments than structured diagnostic interviews; the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale was the most frequently used screening instrument.4 Therefore, epidemiological estimates based on screening instruments should not be interpreted as equivalent to clinically diagnosed depressive disorders. In the present study, “depressive symptoms” refers specifically to self-reported symptom severity assessed using the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), rather than a clinical diagnosis of a depressive disorder.

Poor sleep quality and depressive symptoms often co-occur and have been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as gestational diabetes mellitus, preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, and preterm birth, as well as potential long-term neurodevelopmental risks in offspring.5–7 Previous studies have suggested bidirectional associations between sleep quality and depressive symptoms during pregnancy.8,9 Mechanistically, sleep deprivation may heighten susceptibility to depression through both neurobiological and psychological processes, such as hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, increased proinflammatory cytokine activity, and negative cognitive rumination.10–13 Conversely, in depressive states, hyperarousal and negatively biased cognitive processing may further disrupt sleep continuity and sleep architecture.14,15 However, these proposed processes should not be interpreted as causal pathways in the present cross-sectional study. The third trimester was specifically selected because sleep and emotional problems may become particularly relevant as multiple pregnancy-related burdens accumulate. Hormonal fluctuations, pregnancy-related physical discomforts, including fetal movement, urinary frequency, and low back pain, together with psychosocial stressors, may collectively contribute to greater sleep impairment and depressive symptom burden in late pregnancy.16–19 As the uterus progressively enlarges, frequent nocturia, active fetal movement, accumulated lumbar and back discomfort, and psychological anticipation of childbirth may together make the third trimester a particularly vulnerable period for sleep and emotional problems.3,5 Thus, the growing physiological burden in late pregnancy may act not only as a major stressor in pregnant women’s daily lives but also as a factor contributing to sleep fragmentation and emotional fluctuation, with potential adverse effects on maternal health and quality of life.20–22

Although the association between sleep quality and depressive symptoms in late pregnancy has been widely documented, sleep quality is a latent construct that cannot be directly observed and is commonly measured using standardized scales that combine multiple observable dimensions. For example, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) assesses individual sleep status across several dimensions, including subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, hypnotic medication use, and daytime dysfunction, with higher scores reflecting poorer sleep quality.23 However, most previous studies have used the total PSQI score for broad group comparisons or regression analyses. Such an approach tends to treat pregnant women as a homogeneous population and may overlook variations across specific sleep dimensions, resulting in average-effect conclusions that are less informative regarding heterogeneous sleep patterns.24 As noted by Wang et al, sleep health during the perinatal period follows heterogeneous trajectories across individuals.21 Previous person-centered studies have also identified distinct sleep-quality subtypes during pregnancy. Zhang et al identified four sleep subtypes among pregnant women in the third trimester and reported differences in depression, memory impairment, and fatigue across these subtypes.25 Chen et al identified four distinct sleep-quality profiles among pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus.26 Longitudinal research has likewise demonstrated heterogeneous trajectories of sleep quality across pregnancy.27 Thus, sleep-quality heterogeneity during pregnancy has already been established. However, previous studies have primarily focused on identifying sleep profiles or trajectories and examining their associated characteristics or outcomes. The symptom-level association patterns between specific sleep dimensions and individual depressive symptoms within different sleep-quality profiles remain insufficiently characterized.

Accordingly, latent profile analysis (LPA) and network analysis were used to provide complementary information. LPA was used to identify subgroups of pregnant women characterized by distinct multidimensional patterns of sleep quality, whereas network analysis was used to characterize the conditional associations among specific sleep dimensions and depressive symptoms within these subgroups. For the present analysis, the hypnotic-medication component of the PSQI was excluded because hypnotic medication use was extremely rare in this sample and therefore provided insufficient variability for meaningful profile differentiation. In addition, a pregnancy-adapted 12-item version of the SDS was used because previous psychometric validation in Chinese pregnant women identified items with poor discrimination or low factor contribution and indicated that items overlapping with pregnancy-related physiological changes should be removed.28 On this basis, the present study aimed to identify latent sleep-quality profiles among pregnant women in the third trimester and to characterize and compare sleep quality-depressive symptom networks across profiles with adequate sample sizes.

Materials and Methods

Study Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited pregnant women in the third trimester who attended antenatal care at the obstetric outpatient department of a tertiary Grade A hospital in Xiangyang, Hubei Province, China, between September and December 2025. Participants were selected using convenience sampling. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) gestational age ≥28 weeks; 2) age ≥18 years; 3) normal reading comprehension and the ability to complete the questionnaire independently; and 4) provision of informed consent and voluntary participation in the study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) severe pregnancy complications, such as severe preeclampsia, placental abruption, or placenta previa; and 2) a confirmed diagnosis of organic sleep disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnea syndrome; and 3) a previous diagnosis or documented history of psychiatric disorders.

The sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: n = Z2α/2 × P(1 − P)/δ2.29 In this formula, P represents the prevalence. Based on a reported prevalence of poor sleep quality of 47.31% among pregnant women in the third trimester,30 and allowing for a 20% attrition rate, the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 332 participants. A total of 490 questionnaires were collected. After excluding 10 participants who did not meet the inclusion criterion for the third trimester and 9 invalid questionnaires, 471 participants were included in the final analysis.

Measures

Demographic Questionnaire

A self-designed demographic questionnaire was used to collect information on age, BMI, educational level, current employment status, monthly income, marital status, primiparity, history of miscarriage, prepregnancy alcohol use, secondhand smoke exposure, and pregnancy complications or comorbidities.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index

Sleep quality among pregnant women in the third trimester was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).23 The PSQI is a 19-item self-report instrument comprising seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of hypnotic medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored from 0 to 3, and the seven component scores are summed to yield a global score of 0–21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. Because hypnotic medication use was extremely rare and showed insufficient variability, this component was excluded; therefore, the remaining six component scores were entered into the analyses. The scale has demonstrated good reliability in Chinese populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.845 and a test-retest reliability coefficient of 0.994.31

Self-Rating Depression Scale

Depressive symptoms among pregnant women in the third trimester were assessed using a pregnancy-adapted 12-item version of the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), previously validated in Chinese pregnant women.28,32 The retained items assessed depressed mood, crying spells, decreased libido, cognitive confusion, psychomotor retardation, restlessness, sense of hope, irritability, indecisiveness, feelings of worthlessness, emptiness in life, and loss of interest. Each item was rated on a 4-point frequency scale from 1 to 4. Depressed mood, crying spells, restlessness, and irritability were scored in the symptom direction, whereas decreased libido, cognitive confusion, psychomotor retardation, sense of hope, indecisiveness, feelings of worthlessness, emptiness in life, and loss of interest were reverse-scored according to the original SDS scoring scheme, so that higher scores consistently indicated greater depressive-symptom severity. The pregnancy-adapted version was selected because the validation study identified items with poor discrimination or low factor contribution and removed items with greater overlap with pregnancy-related physiological changes. Nevertheless, residual conceptual overlap between some retained depressive-symptom content and normal late-pregnancy experiences cannot be completely excluded. The scale showed acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.811).28

Data Collection and Quality Control

During the study period, trained investigators screened pregnant women attending routine antenatal visits for eligibility using available medical records and on-site interviews. Eligible pregnant women were approached and invited to participate. The investigators used standardized instructions to explain the study purpose, procedures, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality of the collected information. Pregnant women who agreed to participate provided informed consent before completing the questionnaire through the Pregnancy School platform. Questionnaires were completed independently, and if participants had questions, the investigators provided objective explanations without leading their responses.

After completion, the questionnaires were submitted directly through the online system, and all personal information was kept confidential throughout the study. After data collection, two investigators independently reviewed questionnaire quality. Questionnaires were considered invalid if they met any of the following criteria: completion time <1 minute; missing items accounting for ≥20% of either main scale; or responses containing clear logical inconsistencies. Disagreements between the two investigators were resolved through discussion. The final datasets used for LPA and network analyses contained no missing values; therefore, no statistical imputation was performed.

Data Analysis

Descriptive and Demographic Analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to examine differences in the distribution of categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Latent Profile Analysis

Latent profile analysis was performed in Mplus 8.3 using robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR) based on the six PSQI dimensions. Models with one to five profiles were estimated sequentially using 2000 initial-stage random starts and 500 final-stage optimizations. Model selection was based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted BIC (aBIC), entropy, adjusted Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT), bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), profile size, and substantive interpretability. Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicated better relative fit, whereas higher entropy indicated clearer profile separation. Participants were assigned to their most likely latent profile based on the highest posterior class-membership probability, and classification quality was further evaluated using class-specific average posterior probabilities. Because downstream profile-specific network estimation required mutually exclusive subgroup membership, modal class assignment was used for the network analyses; posterior probabilities were reported to quantify classification uncertainty, but posterior-probability weights were not propagated into the network models.

Network Analysis

Network Estimation

Network analyses were conducted in R 4.5.2 using the qgraph, bootnet, networktools, and NetworkComparisonTest packages. Each network comprised 18 nodes: six PSQI dimensions and 12 SDS symptoms, predefined as the sleep-quality and depressive-symptom communities, respectively. Gaussian graphical models were estimated separately within each analyzed profile from Pearson correlation matrices using EBICglasso (γ = 0.15), with no additional thresholding and the same specification across profiles. Because the PSQI components and SDS items are bounded ordinal indicators, the Pearson-based GGM was treated as a working approximation on the observed score scale rather than as evidence that the item distributions were truly Gaussian; robustness to the correlation specification was evaluated using ordinal-aware and rank-based alternatives in sensitivity analyses. The same primary γ value was applied across profiles to avoid profile-specific tuning, and alternative γ values were examined because the EBIC tuning parameter directly influences network sparsity. Edges represent regularized partial correlations conditional on all other nodes. No missing values were present in the profile-specific datasets. Variables with zero within-profile variance were omitted from correlation estimation and retained as isolated nodes with zero incident edges.

Node and Bridge Centrality

Given the limited reliability of closeness and betweenness centrality estimates,33 strength centrality was used as the principal node-centrality measure. Strength was defined as the sum of the absolute edge weights connected to a node. Bridge strength was defined as the sum of the absolute edge weights connecting a node to nodes in the other predefined community.34 Higher values indicated greater statistical connectivity within the estimated network. Strength and bridge strength were interpreted as descriptive statistical features of the estimated cross-sectional networks, not as validated causal mechanisms or intervention targets.35

Network Accuracy and Stability

Edge-weight accuracy was evaluated using 1000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples to obtain 95% confidence intervals. Stability of strength and bridge strength was assessed using case-dropping bootstrap procedures with 1000 resamples and summarized using correlation-stability (CS) coefficients. CS coefficients ≥0.50 were interpreted as good, coefficients from 0.25 to <0.50 as moderate, and coefficients <0.25 as unstable. Centrality rankings with low CS coefficients were not treated as robust subgroup characteristics.33

Network Comparison Test

Pairwise network comparisons were performed using the Network Comparison Test with 1000 permutations for the three analyzed profiles.36 Overall network-structure invariance, global-strength invariance, individual edge weights, and node centrality were examined. The network-structure statistic (M), absolute global-strength difference (ΔGS), and relative global-strength difference were reported as measures of between-network difference magnitude. To control multiplicity, Holm adjustment was applied across the three pairwise global tests and within each pairwise comparison for edge-level and centrality-level tests. Both raw and Holm-adjusted P values were retained for reporting, and inference regarding individual edges or centrality differences was based on multiplicity-adjusted results.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine whether the network findings were sensitive to correlation specification, regularization strength, or unequal subgroup sizes. Given the bounded ordinal nature of the PSQI and SDS indicators, networks were re-estimated using ordinal-aware correlations obtained with qgraph::cor_auto and Spearman correlations, both with EBICglasso γ = 0.50. Regularization sensitivity was further examined under the primary Pearson correlation specification using alternative EBIC tuning parameters (γ = 0, 0.25, and 0.50). To assess the influence of subgroup-size imbalance, Profiles 1 and 3 were repeatedly subsampled to n = 85, corresponding to the size of Profile 2, across 200 repetitions. Global strength and the highest-strength and highest-bridge nodes were re-estimated in each subsample. These analyses were used to assess numerical robustness of the exploratory network findings and were not considered evidence of clinical or predictive validity.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 490 questionnaires were collected. After excluding 10 participants who did not meet the third-trimester eligibility criterion and 9 invalid questionnaires, 471 pregnant women were included in the final analysis. Most participants were aged <35 years, had a bachelor’s degree or above, and had a prepregnancy BMI of 18.5–25.0 kg/m2, accounting for 83.4%, 60.7%, and 66.9% of the sample, respectively. The proportions of participants with a history of miscarriage, pregnancy complications or comorbidities, prepregnancy alcohol use, and secondhand smoke exposure were 22.9%, 24.0%, 14.6%, and 43.1%, respectively. Sleep Quality-Depressive Symptom Network Analysis Across Sleep-Quality Subgroups

Because the low sleep efficiency group contained only 27 participants, formal network estimation and between-profile network comparisons were restricted to the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group (n = 177), daytime dysfunction group (n = 85), and good sleep quality group (n = 182) to avoid drawing inferences from an inadequately small subgroup. No network centrality or bridge conclusions were drawn for the low sleep efficiency group. PSQI3 (sleep duration) showed zero within-profile variance in each of the three analyzed groups and was therefore retained as an isolated node rather than interpreted as having no association with other symptoms. Consequently, 17 nodes varied within each analyzed profile and 136 pairwise edges were statistically estimable.

Figure 2 presents the three estimated networks. Among the 136 estimable edges among the 17 varying nodes, 61 nonzero edges were retained in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group (44.85%), 34 in the daytime dysfunction group (25.00%), and 46 in the good sleep quality group (33.82%). As shown in Figures 3 and 4, SDS11 (“emptiness in life”) had the highest strength in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group (strength = 1.175), SDS1 (“depressed mood”) had the highest strength in the daytime dysfunction group (strength = 0.941), and SDS11 had the highest strength in the good sleep quality group (strength = 1.127). PSQI6 (“daytime dysfunction”) had the highest bridge strength in each of the three primary networks, with bridge-strength values of 0.354, 0.112, and 0.146, respectively.

Pairwise Network Comparison Tests showed no significant differences in overall network structure after Holm correction: moderate multidimensional sleep impairment versus daytime dysfunction, M = 0.210 (raw P = 0.838; Holm-adjusted P = 1.000); moderate multidimensional sleep impairment versus good sleep quality, M = 0.210 (raw P = 0.541; Holm-adjusted P = 1.000); and daytime dysfunction versus good sleep quality, M = 0.293 (raw P = 0.225; Holm-adjusted P = 0.674). For global strength, the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment and good sleep quality groups differed significantly (6.531 vs 4.839; ΔGS = 1.692, relative difference = 29.8%; raw P = 0.004; Holm-adjusted P = 0.012). The moderate multidimensional sleep impairment versus daytime dysfunction comparison was not significant (6.531 vs 4.178; ΔGS = 2.353, 43.9%; raw P = 0.160; Holm-adjusted P = 0.320), nor was the daytime dysfunction versus good sleep quality comparison (4.178 vs 4.839; ΔGS = 0.661, 14.7%; raw P = 0.360; Holm-adjusted P = 0.360). No individual edge-weight or node-centrality differences remained statistically significant after Holm correction (Supplementary Table S2).

Sensitivity analyses showed high correspondence of edge weights with the primary networks under ordinal-aware (r = 0.881–0.993) and Spearman-based (r = 0.970–0.987) specifications. Highest-strength nodes were generally consistent across alternative specifications, whereas bridge-node rankings showed greater sensitivity, particularly in the daytime dysfunction group. Similar patterns were observed across alternative EBIC tuning parameters. In the equal-sample-size analysis, the original highest-strength nodes were retained in 57.5% and 66.5% of replicates for Profiles 1 and 3, respectively, compared with 51.5% and 41.0% for the highest-bridge nodes. Overall, strength-centrality findings were more consistent than bridge-centrality findings across sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

Using latent profile analysis, this study identified four heterogeneous sleep-quality subgroups among pregnant women in the third trimester: the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group (37.6%), daytime dysfunction group (18.1%), good sleep quality group (38.6%), and low sleep efficiency group (5.7%). These findings indicate that abnormal sleep quality in late pregnancy is not a single, homogeneous clinical presentation, but rather a stratified pattern formed by different combinations of sleep dimensions. Because of its small sample size, the low sleep efficiency group was not included in the network analysis. Further network analysis showed that the highest-strength nodes differed across the three analyzed sleep-quality profiles. “Emptiness in life” had the highest strength in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group and good sleep quality group, whereas “depressed mood” had the highest strength in the daytime dysfunction group. “Daytime dysfunction” had the highest bridge strength in all three analyzed networks. After Holm correction, the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group had significantly higher global strength than the good sleep quality group, whereas the other pairwise global-strength comparisons were not significant and no significant differences in overall network structure, individual edge weights, or node centrality remained. These findings suggest marked heterogeneity in the co-occurrence of sleep quality and depressive symptoms among pregnant women in the third trimester and provide exploratory evidence for integrated sleep and emotional health assessment in late pregnancy.

Previous studies have begun to use person-centered approaches to examine heterogeneity in sleep quality during pregnancy. For example, Zhang et al identified four sleep subtypes among women in the third trimester: “good sleep quality”, “poor sleep efficiency”, “daily disturbances”, and “poor sleep quality”, and found that these subtypes differed in levels of depression, memory impairment, and fatigue.25 Chen et al focused on pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus and identified four sleep profiles: “good sleep quality”, “poor sleep quality-long sleep duration”, “moderate sleep quality”, and “poor sleep quality-short sleep duration”; they further examined influencing factors such as Type D personality and history of gestational diabetes mellitus.26 From a longitudinal trajectory perspective, Wang et al classified changes in sleep quality during pregnancy into the constantly poor sleep quality group, the constantly decreasing sleep quality group, and the constantly good sleep quality group, suggesting that population differences also exist in the developmental course of sleep quality during pregnancy.27 The present study identified the “moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group”, “daytime dysfunction group”, “good sleep quality group”, and “low sleep efficiency group”, which not only supports the heterogeneity of sleep quality in late pregnancy but also further clarifies specific patterns of sleep impairment. In particular, the identification of a subtype characterized by synchronous moderate impairment across multiple sleep dimensions provides additional evidence for stratified assessment of sleep quality in late pregnancy and may inform future intervention research.

Based on the four latent sleep-quality profiles, this study further constructed sleep quality-depressive symptom networks in the three subgroups with adequate sample sizes to identify core symptoms and bridge symptoms within different sleep-quality subtypes, thereby providing evidence for understanding the connections between impaired sleep quality and depressive symptoms in late pregnancy. In the present study, core and bridge features refer to relative centrality within the estimated networks rather than validated causal mechanisms. Regarding core symptoms, “emptiness in life” occupied a relatively central position in both the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group and the good sleep quality group. This suggests that, despite differences in the degree of sleep impairment, reduced meaning in life, diminished interest or pleasure, and insufficient subjective fulfillment may occupy an important position within the sleep quality-depressive symptom network among pregnant women in the third trimester. Notably, its central position across both groups suggests that this finding is not specific to sleep impairment. The present study also cannot determine whether this feature is specific to pregnancy or reflects broader depressive psychopathology. Recent network studies of perinatal depressive symptoms have also shown that cognitive-affective symptoms, such as depressed mood, guilt, and anhedonia, often occupy central positions in antenatal depression networks.37 The prominent role of “emptiness in life” in the present study is consistent with the view that perinatal depression involves diminished experiences of self-worth, pleasure, and meaning in life. In the daytime dysfunction group, “depressed mood” emerged as the core symptom. This finding suggests that, among pregnant women in the third trimester whose sleep problems are mainly reflected in daytime sleepiness, reduced energy, or impaired daily functioning, negative mood may occupy a more central position within the symptom network. Women in late pregnancy are often exposed to overlapping stressors, such as increasing physical burden, disrupted sleep continuity, role transition stress, and concerns about childbirth. When sleep problems are expressed primarily as daytime functional impairment, greater helplessness and depressed mood may co-occur with impaired daytime functioning. Prior research has further indicated that poor sleep quality during pregnancy can predict later increases in depressive symptoms, supporting longitudinal associations between sleep problems and emotional symptoms.38 Therefore, assessment for pregnant women with prominent daytime dysfunction may extend beyond nighttime sleep duration and difficulty falling asleep to include daytime depressed mood, reduced activity, and impaired coping capacity.

Bridge symptoms provided further insight into how sleep quality and depressive symptoms were connected. However, the stability of bridge-strength estimates varied across subgroups. Bridge strength showed good stability in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group, moderate stability in the good sleep quality group, and limited stability in the daytime dysfunction group. Within this exploratory context, daytime dysfunction may represent a shared connecting feature between sleep quality and depressive symptoms across these subgroups. This pattern may reflect the close relationship between daytime functioning, perceived sleep quality, and emotional symptoms in late pregnancy. On the one hand, daytime dysfunction reflects reduced wakefulness, energy level, and daily activity capacity; on the other hand, these functional difficulties may coexist with helplessness, negative self-evaluation, and depressed mood. Notably, in the daytime dysfunction group, this symptom itself was the main feature of sleep impairment, and its bridging role indicates relatively strong cross-community connectivity in this subgroup. In the good sleep quality group, however, the presence of daytime dysfunction suggests that daytime functional decline may still be associated with emotional symptoms even when overall sleep status is relatively good. One possible explanation for the relatively high bridge centrality of daytime dysfunction is partial conceptual overlap between the daytime functional impairment assessed by the PSQI and depressive symptoms involving activity or functional status, which may partly contribute to its observed cross-community statistical connectivity. Previous studies of psychological symptom networks have suggested that bridge symptoms can connect different symptom clusters and may contribute to patterns of comorbidity.33,34 Overall, the observed bridging role of “daytime dysfunction” suggests that daytime functional impairment may represent a relevant connecting feature within the sleep quality-depressive symptom networks. These findings suggest that daytime functioning may warrant particular attention in symptom assessment and future intervention research among pregnant women with different sleep-quality subtypes.

In addition, comparison of global strength between the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group and the good sleep quality group yielded a noteworthy finding: the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group had significantly higher global strength than the good sleep quality group. Global strength reflects the overall degree of statistical connectivity among symptoms within an estimated network, with higher values indicating a more densely interconnected symptom structure.39 Global strength reflects the overall level of connections among symptoms within a network and can be used to compare the tightness of symptom systems across different populations.36 From this perspective, the good sleep quality group showed lower impairment across sleep dimensions, and the connection between sleep quality and depressive symptoms may have remained relatively weak. By contrast, the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group showed synchronous moderate impairment across multiple sleep dimensions, indicating that insufficient sleep restoration may have extended from a single sleep problem to a broader decline in sleep quality. Previous studies have shown that sleep loss can impair emotional functioning, reduce positive emotions, and increase the risk of negative emotions such as anxiety; sleep problems may also affect multiple processes involved in emotion generation and emotion regulation.40,41 Therefore, under conditions of moderate multidimensional sleep impairment, persistent and extensive insufficient sleep restoration may be associated with greater emotional sensitivity and tighter statistical connections between sleep-quality dimensions and depressive symptoms. Taken together, the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group may represent a more tightly connected network pattern between sleep quality and depressive symptoms in late pregnancy. Its higher global strength may suggest a greater risk of symptom linkage in this subtype. Timely multidimensional sleep management at this stage may have potential value in addressing the co-occurrence of impaired sleep quality and depressive symptoms.

This study provides several clinical implications. First, the identification of four sleep-quality profiles supports the potential value of stratified assessment. For the good sleep quality group, routine sleep health education and psychological follow-up may be appropriate. For the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group, daytime dysfunction group, and low sleep efficiency group, differentiated intervention strategies may be considered according to the prominent sleep dimensions of each subtype, although their profile-specific effectiveness requires further evaluation. Second, “emptiness in life” occupied a central position in both the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group and the good sleep quality group, suggesting that reduced meaning in life may represent a psychological feature warranting further attention among pregnant women in the third trimester. Positive psychological interventions and meaning-oriented support may be considered to enhance maternal role identity and a sense of life value.42,43 Third, the centrality of “depressed mood” in the daytime dysfunction group suggests that depressed mood may warrant closer attention when daytime functioning is impaired. In clinical assessment, greater attention to mood changes, reduced activity, and diminished interest may help identify pregnant women who require further psychological evaluation. Behavioral activation strategies and digital cognitive behavioral therapy have shown potential for addressing reduced activity and depressed mood and may be considered according to individual clinical needs.44,45 Fourth, the repeated prominence of daytime dysfunction across the primary networks suggests that daytime functioning may warrant attention in integrated sleep and emotional assessment. Nevertheless, because the stability of bridge strength differed across subgroups and was limited in the daytime dysfunction group, this finding should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than evidence of a validated intervention target. Finally, the higher global strength observed in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group suggests a more closely interconnected pattern of sleep-related and depressive symptoms in this subgroup. Pregnant women with concurrent impairment across several sleep dimensions may therefore benefit from more comprehensive assessment of both sleep and emotional status rather than focusing on a single sleep complaint. Taken together, these findings provide a preliminary basis for more differentiated assessment and supportive care in late pregnancy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the temporal relationships between sleep quality and depressive symptoms; therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify their dynamic associations. Second, participants were recruited from a single medical institution using convenience sampling, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Third, the low sleep efficiency group was not included in the network analysis or subsequent network comparison because of its small sample size; future studies with larger samples are needed to further examine the symptom network characteristics of this subgroup. Unequal subgroup sizes and the limited stability of some bridge-strength estimates, particularly in the daytime dysfunction group, may also reduce the reproducibility of bridge-node rankings. Fourth, the hypnotic medication use dimension of the PSQI was excluded from the analyses because of the extremely low rate of medication use and insufficient statistical variability, which may have affected the completeness of the sleep quality assessment. Finally, sleep quality and depressive symptoms were assessed using self-report questionnaires, which may have introduced recall or reporting bias. Although a pregnancy-adapted version of the SDS was used, some retained symptom content may still overlap with normal physiological experiences in late pregnancy, potentially affecting the interpretation of self-reported depressive symptoms. Because both instruments were self-report measures collected at the same time point, common-method variance and shared response tendencies may also have inflated some observed associations.

Conclusion

This study identified substantial heterogeneity in sleep quality among pregnant women in the third trimester and profile-specific characteristics in the sleep quality-depressive symptom networks. Latent profile analysis identified four sleep-quality profiles with distinct multidimensional patterns. Because of the small sample size of the low sleep efficiency group, network analyses were restricted to the other three profiles. Within these analyzed profiles, “emptiness in life” had the highest strength in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment and good sleep quality groups, whereas “depressed mood” had the highest strength in the daytime dysfunction group; “daytime dysfunction” had the highest bridge strength in all three networks. After Holm correction, global strength was significantly higher in the moderate multidimensional sleep impairment group than in the good sleep quality group, whereas no significant differences in overall network structure, individual edge weights, or node centrality remained. These findings provide exploratory evidence that sleep-quality profile characteristics and symptom-level network features may inform stratified assessment of sleep and emotional health in late pregnancy. The clinical value and predictive significance of these findings remain to be confirmed in larger prospective cohort studies and independent replications.
