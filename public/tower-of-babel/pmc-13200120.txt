The decent work level of nurses in China: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hang Li, Xuemei Xie, Xianhua Wang, Yunqiong Wang, Jun Tang, Jiangli Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecent work, as defined by the International Labour Organization, refers to employment that provides workers with fair income, safety, dignity, and an equal environment-core standards ensuring labor value is realized in a safe, fair, and dignified setting. At present, there is no meta-analysis on the level of decent work of nurses in China, making it impossible to quantify the level. Therefore, we aimed to assess the current level of decent work of nurses and provide some reference for further improving the development of nursing careers.MethodsFive Chinese and English databases (CNKI, PubMed, EMBASE, Web of science and CINAHL) were systematically searched for the research on the decent work level of nurses in China from the establishment of the database to March 2026. We used a random-effects model to analyse. Our primary outcome was the decent work level of nurses in China. The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality was used to evaluate the quality of the cross-sectional studies.ResultsA total of 38 cross-sectional studies involving 22,561 nurses in China were included. From the meta-analysis, we found that the level of decent work of nurses was 50.46 out of 80 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 47.77,53.15), the total score ranged from 16 to 80, the five sub-dimensions (Work reward, Work position, Career development, Professional recognition, and Work atmosphere) were 11.65 (95% CI: 10.66,12.65), 8.43 (95% CI: 7.65,9.20), 9.68 (95% CI: 8.93,10.43), 8.13 (95% CI: 7.48,8.78), and 10.33 (95% CI: 9.53,11.12), respectively. Subgroup analysis showed that male, high school diploma, registered nurses, nurses who were employed in non-supervisory clinical positions, those who had experienced workplace violence had a low level of decent work. We have indicated that the level of decent work for nurses in China is at a moderate level. Policymakers and managers should consider paying attention to nurses with lower levels of decent work and the career development plans of clinical nurses and whether a good working atmosphere has been formed, potentially laying a solid foundation for further improving the development of nurses.

Introduction

Nurses are a core component of the healthcare system and also the largest in numbers (Jennings and Astin, 2017). As a group that directly provides healthcare, nurses' work quality is closely related to patient safety (Lee et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). With the rising global aging population, recurring complex diseases, and frequent public health emergencies, nurses are facing increasing work pressure (2022, Musau et al., 2015). A high-pressure environment and work complexity may lead to a decrease in nurses' job satisfaction (Wang et al., 2022), which in turn affects their mental health, resulting in job burnout and high turnover rates (Jachmann et al., 2025; Li et al., 2019). This may also indirectly lead to a decrease in nursing quality, an increase in the likelihood of medical errors (Bell et al., 2023), and ultimately result in adverse outcomes, such as increased patient safety risks and prolonged hospital stays (Zabin et al., 2023; Zaitoun et al., 2023; Fajarini et al., 2025).

Decent work, as defined by the International Labour Organization, refers to employment that ensures workers receive fair income, safety, dignity, and equal treatment within a supportive environment, with these four core standards being essential for realizing the value of labor in a safe, fair, and dignified setting (Blustein et al., 2016). Decent work for nurses requires an environment of freedom, fairness, safety, and dignity. This concept is particularly significant in the nursing profession because it encompasses specific aspects that directly affect nurses' well-being and the quality of patient care (Sönmez et al., 2023; Asmoro et al., 2022). Its core connotation can be articulated through six interrelated dimensions: first, employment security, meaning nurses should have access to long-term, stable job positions with clear employment contracts, thereby reducing uncertainties, such as involuntary overtime and the use of temporary staff and ensuring predictable income and career prospects (Xue et al., 2024; Su and Chan, 2023); second, fair remuneration, meaning nurses' salaries should be commensurate with their workload, responsibility, and technical requirements and should increase reasonably with years of service and professional growth, reflecting their professional value and helping to avoid systemic discrimination based on gender, department, or other factors (Hult et al., 2024); third, occupational safety, meaning nurses must be provided with protective facilities and management systems that meet national standards to minimize occupational exposure to risks, such as needlestick injuries, infections, and workplace violence, complemented by psychological support and occupational health monitoring to safeguard their physical and mental well-being (Al-Qadi, 2021; Abdelmalik et al., 2023); fourth, social protection, meaning nurses are entitled to social insurance and paid leave in accordance with the law, ensuring their basic livelihood and professional security during illness, maternity, or other family emergencies (Diehl et al., 2021); fifth, workers’ rights, meaning nurses have the legal right to form and join trade unions, engage in collective consultation, and express their opinions and should also have the right to be informed and to participate in decision-making processes related to scheduling, performance evaluation, and task allocation, thereby preventing managerial arbitrariness and discrimination (Rasheed et al., 2020); sixth, respectful working conditions, meaning workplaces should foster a culture of mutual respect and collaborative teamwork, opposing verbal abuse and professional belittlement, recognizing the professional contributions of nurses, and providing reasonable task distribution and humanistic care to uphold their personal dignity and job satisfaction (Morvati et al., 2025). These six dimensions collectively form a comprehensive framework for understanding "decent work" in the nursing profession, not only echoing the International Labour Organization's core standards of fair income, safety, dignity, and equal opportunity but also precisely addressing the unique challenges and needs nurses face in their professional practice, and, by ensuring these conditions, decent work enables nurses to sustain their professional commitment, enhance patient safety, and contribute effectively to the healthcare system (Fang et al., 2025).

In recent years, decent work has increasingly been regarded not only as an ideal labor condition but also as a key factor for understanding the challenges nurses face, as they are confronted with growing numbers of precarious and indecent working conditions, particularly rising psychosocial risks (Hult et al., 2024; Isangula et al., 2022; Chaiban et al., 2023). Researchers have shown that when nurses experience higher levels of decent work, they tend to report better mental health, greater job satisfaction, and lower burnout (Xue et al., 2025). These factors are also directly related to nurse retention, when nurses perceive their work as fair, safe, and respected, they are more likely to remain in clinical positions, which can help alleviate, though not eliminate, the global shortage of nursing talent (Zoromba et al., 2025). At the same time, working in environments that meet basic standards of decent work appears to contribute to higher-quality patient care (Fang et al., 2025). Nurses who feel supported are generally more engaged, make fewer errors, and adhere more closely to safety protocols, which may help reduce adverse events and improve patient outcomes (Bell et al., 2023; Fajarini et al., 2025). Beyond its effects on individual nurses and patients, decent work can also strengthen the broader health system by helping to stabilize the nursing workforce, reducing turnover-related costs, and fostering a culture that values professional development and safe practice (Gül et al., 2023). However, despite its recognized importance, many nurses, particularly those in high-pressure settings, such as China, continue to face systemic barriers to achieving these conditions. Although China has called for efforts to continuously improve its social security system, eliminate obstacles that prevent workers from participating in and sharing the benefits of development, and strive to ensure that all workers can achieve decent work and all-round development, significant challenges remain in realizing these goals (Yang and Huang, 2023). Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the current levels of decent work among Chinese nurses to better understand the actual state of decent work in this profession.

With the rapid development of the economy and society, recent surveys on Chinese nurses' decent work levels have shown a consistent upward trend (Zheng et al., 2025). However, conclusions regarding these levels remain inconsistent, and significant variations may be attributable to factors, such as sex, educational attainment, and professional experience. In other words, the true status in China of nurses' decent work remains unclear, indicating a lack of robust empirical evidence in this field. A number of instruments have been developed and validated to measure decent work, including the 15-item Decent Work Scale (Duffy et al., 2017), a 15-item Decent Work Measurement Scale (Qin et al., 2015), and the 16-item Decent Work Perception Scale (Mao et al., 2014). Although several tools are available for assessing nurses' decent work, the Decent Work Perception Scale is the most frequently used instrument among researchers in China. This scale was explicitly designed based on the International Labour Organization's decent work framework, which aligns closely with the conceptual definition of decent work adopted in our study. Moreover, the Decent Work Perception Scale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity in the Chinese nursing population, with a high internal consistency for both the overall scale (Cronbach's α = 0.961) and its subscales (Cronbach'α = 0.837–0.946) (Zheng et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). It has been widely applied in this research area. To ensure measurement consistency and facilitate subsequent meta-analyses, we, therefore, focused on studies that have employed the Decent Work Perception Scale to measure nurses' decent work. We intended to clarify the current status of nurses' decent work and provide a possible reference for nursing managers in formulating management strategies, thereby potentially contributing to the further advancement of the nursing profession.

Methods

Design and registration

The systematic review and meta-analysis was registered on the PROSPERO website (registration number: CRD42023414565).

Search strategy

The citations related to the decent work level of nurses were retrieved in five Chinese and English databases (CNKI, PubMed, EMBASE, Web of science and CINAHL). Citations were retrieved using a combination of MeSH terms and free terms, spanning from the inception of the databases to March 2026. The main keywords included “nurse*” and “decent work.” To ensure comprehensive retrieval, the search terms were expanded to incorporate concepts and dimensions of decent work, such as “job security,” “fair remuneration,” “occupational safety,” “social protection,” “workers' rights,” and “decent employment,” Finally, the reference lists of the retrieved articles were manually searched to identify additional studies that might have been missed. We also sought assistance from seasoned librarians to refine the retrieval strategy for each database. For citations whose full text was not available, we reached out to the corresponding authors via email for assistance (Supplementary Material Appendix A).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) the subjects were nurses; (2) The decent work Perception Scale (Mao et al., 2014) was used to investigate the decent work level of nurses; (3) It can extract the mean±standard deviation (SD) of the score of nurses' decent work level; (4) Observational studies (cross-sectional, cohort studies, etc.).

Exclusion criteria: (1) The study object was a mixed population, and the mean ± SD of the decent work level of nurses scores could not be extracted separately; (2) Secondary research (meta-analysis, review, and similar); (3) Could not get the full text; (4) Low quality articles.

Quality assessment

The citations included in this study were all cross-sectional studies. Therefore, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality evaluation scale was used to evaluate their bias risk.

Data extraction

Two highly trained researchers (LH, XXM) independently screened citations and extracted data. If there were differences, they consulted the third researcher (HJL) for settlement. The main data extraction contents included: first author, publication year, region, research type, sample size, number of men, number of women, age, and score of nurses' decent work level.

Data synthesis

All the included studies were investigated using the Decent Work Perception Scale, so meta-analysis was used for quantitative data synthesis. Stata16.0 software was used to summarize the mean ± SD of the Decent Work Perception Scale score, and the average score was expressed as the weighted effect size and 95% confidence interval (CI). In the meta-analysis, considering that the subjects included in the study came from different regions, hospitals, and departments, we prioritized the random-effects model as the primary analytical approach to account for potential clinical and methodological heterogeneity, even when statistical heterogeneity (I²) was low. This model estimates the average effect size while incorporating between-study variance, offering a more robust interpretation of pooled results. In addition, subgroup analysis was conducted to explore the impact of sex, educational degrees, professional title, leadership roles, and experience of work-related violence on the level of decent work of nurses. Egger's test was used to determine publication bias (Egger et al., 1997).

Results

Search results

A total of 7473 relevant citations were obtained through systematic retrieval of five databases. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 38 high-quality cross-sectional studies were included, which were published from 2021 to 2025, involving 22,561 nurses. All the included citations used the Decent Work Perception Scale compiled by Mao et al. (2014). The scale was composed of 16 items and five sub-dimensions, and scored as 1–5 points from "completely disagree (1)" to "fully agree (5)". The higher the score, the higher the level of decent work of nurses. Included study characteristics

A total of 38 cross-sectional studies were included, published between 2021 and 2026. A total of 22,561 nurses were involved, including 13,533 female nurses and 6216 male nurses. The average age of nurses participating in the study was between 26 and 37 years old. All the studies were conducted in China, and all used the Decent Work Perception Scale. Some participants came from departments with busy clinical work (n = 4040, 17.91%), such as the emergency department, intensive care unit, and pediatrics, and some participants came from departments with special working environments and patients (n = 2009, 8.90%), such as oncology, psychiatry, and infectious diseases. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Quality appraisal of included studies

The Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality evaluation showed that among the 38 cross-sectional studies, 14 were rated as medium quality (score 6–7), and 24 studies were rated as high quality (score 8). The quality of the included studies was acceptable. However, the studies did not fully meet the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality evaluation criteria, as they lacked clarity on data sources, did not adequately describe the inclusion and exclusion criteria for exposed and non-exposed groups, and did not provide sufficient information on how to address missing data (Supplementary Material Appendix B).

Results of meta-analysis

Meta-analysis results indicated homogeneity in nurses' decent work level scores (Q test = 5.01, I2 = 0.0%, p = 1.000). Ultimately, the pooled mean score for nurses' decent work level across 38 studies amounted to 50.46 (95% CI: 47.77,53.15). Thirty-three studies reported the five sub-dimensions of the Decent Work Perception Scale. The meta-analysis results showed that there was no significant difference in the heterogeneity test of the five sub dimensions: Work reward (Q = 7.47, I2 = 0.0%, p = 1.000), Work position (Q = 17.59, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.971), Career development (Q = 3.38, I2 = 0.0%, p = 1.000), Professional recognition (Q = 24.03, I2 = 0.0%, p = 1.000), Work atmosphere (Q = 12.81, I2 = 0.0%, p = 0.997). The final pooled mean scores of the five sub dimensions were 11.65 (95% CI: 10.66,12.65), 8.43 (95% CI: 7.65,9.20), 9.68 (95% CI: 8.93,10.43), 8.13 (95% CI: 7.48,8.78), and 10.33 (95% CI: 9.53,11.12) respectively (Supplementary Material Figs. 1–5).

Subgroup analyses of the decent work level of nurses

A subgroup analysis was conducted to assess the pooled scores of the decent work level among nurses, as reported by various studies. However, the subgroup analysis did not detect statistically significant differences, which may be related to high heterogeneity within subgroups and the use of subjective scales to measure decent work. In other words, existing evidence does not support the presence of significant group heterogeneity in decent work. But the relevant results still have a certain practical significance; we found that female nurses scored higher (49.79) than their male counterparts (48.19). In terms of educational degrees, the nurses of master's or higher scored the highest (55.09), and the nurses of high school diploma scored the lowest (49.97); In terms of professional title, the nurses with cochief title or above scored the highest (51.95); meanwhile, registered nurses scored the lowest (49.50). The score of nursing managers (52.41) was higher than that of nurses who were employed in non-supervisory clinical positions (48.20). The score of nurses who did not suffer from work-related violence (52.94) was higher than that of nurses who suffered from work-related violence (47.60). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted after the scores of decent work level of nurses in the 38 studies were eliminated one by one. The combined effect value did not change significantly before elimination, and the results were relatively stable, suggesting that the meta-analysis results are reliable (Supplementary Material Fig. 11).

Risk of publication bias

The Egger's test was used to detect the publication bias of nurses' decent work level scores and draw the funnel diagram. Based on the results, the possibility of publication bias in this study was low (Supplementary Material Figs. 12, 13).

Discussion

Based on the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality criteria, most included cross-sectional studies were rated as moderate to high quality. However, common methodological limitations, such as the absence of clearly-stated exclusion criteria, insufficient explanation for patient exclusions, and inadequate description of missing data handling, may have introduced potential selection bias and reporting bias. Therefore, while the overall quality of evidence was acceptable, the potential for bias should be considered when interpreting the findings. At the same time, this also suggests that future cross-sectional researchers should pay attention to similar issues to improve quality.

From the meta-analysis, we estimated the level of decent work for nurses in China, providing evidence-based support for this field. To our knowledge, this is the first quantitative meta-analysis on the decent work level of Chinese nurses, summarizing the decent work levels of 22,561 nurses from 38 cross-sectional studies. Although this review is limited to studies conducted in mainland China, this national focus provided an opportunity to examine nurses’ decent work within a sociocultural, institutional, and policy context that differs markedly from that of many high-income countries. From a cultural perspective, China’s long-standing collectivist values, emphasis on social harmony and stability, and a healthcare system dominated by public hospitals have jointly shaped a distinctive model of doctor–nurse relationships and the organization of nursing work. At the institutional and policy levels, the public hospital–centered healthcare delivery system, structured around tiered diagnosis and treatment, together with a series of recent policies implemented under the Healthy China initiative and aimed at improving nursing services and promoting decent work for health workers, have formed a unique pathway for advancing nurses’ decent work in China (Li et al., 2025b; Yin and Zhang, 2025). However, restricting the analysis to China limits the generalizability of our findings and their comparability with international literature, and these limitations should be considered when interpreting the results.

The pooled mean score of nurses' decent work level was at a moderate level and higher than Egyptian nurses reported by El-Gazar (2025), similar to the results reported by Zoromba (2025). Regarding the sub-dimensions of the scale, the Work atmosphere scored highest, whereas Work position and Work rewards scored lowest. This pattern highlights an imbalance in the decent work experienced by Chinese nurses: although they perceive relatively favorable interpersonal relationships and emotional conditions at work, they report lower levels of professional fulfillment, economic compensation, and recognition. Within the context of China’s healthcare system, several structural, cultural, and policy factors may explain these findings. The relatively high score for work atmosphere can be attributed to the collective and hierarchical nature of hospitals, in which long-term co-location, shared professional values, and strong team cohesion foster solidarity and mutual support among colleagues (Cai et al., 2025). National policies and hospital initiatives that emphasize harmonious doctor–patient and colleague relationships have further promoted norms of mutual respect and teamwork, which are more readily perceived and recalled by frontline nurses (Yang et al., 2026).

In contrast, the lower scores for Work position and Work rewards reflect structural and institutional constraints. In China’s public hospital system, nurses generally occupy a lower position in the professional hierarchy, have limited influence on clinical and organizational decisions, and face few opportunities for career advancement. Their work is often task-oriented and closely supervised, which may reduce their sense of autonomy and professional identity. At the same time, salaries are constrained by budget limits and are often not closely linked to individual performance or workload. Heavy workloads, long working hours, and high occupational risks are not fully reflected in monetary rewards (Zhu et al., 2026; Chen and Liu, 2022). These factors may help to explain why nurses report lower scores satisfaction with their work position and rewards despite relatively positive interpersonal relationships at work. A good working atmosphere can effectively alleviate the professional pressure of nurses and is an important psychological support for nurses to adhere to high-intensity clinical work for a long time (Eva et al., 2024; Si et al., 2023). However, if they are in a contradictory situation of high effort and low return for a long time, it will ultimately lead to professional burnout and loss of nursing talents (Jiang et al., 2024). Therefore, to achieve decent work for nurses, we propose that managers optimize job management, enhance the reward system, and further consolidate the advantage of a positive working atmosphere, thereby elevating the level of decent work for nurses.

Subgroup analysis

Our subgroup analysis showed no statistically-significant differences according to sex, education level, professional title, leadership position, or exposure to workplace violence. This may be attributable to several factors, including substantial within-subgroup heterogeneity, imbalanced variable distributions, and the subjective nature of the decent work scale, which may be influenced by social desirability or emotional state. However, differences in the pooled scores were observed across these subgroups. In addition, based on clinical observation, we believe these factors still warrant further discussion, as they may provide a useful basis for future studies to refine the understanding of factors influencing decent work among nurses.

Sex

Subgroup analysis by sex did not reveal a statistically significant difference in decent work scores between male and female nurses, which may be related to the imbalanced sample size between male and female. In terms of the pooled scores, female nurses had higher overall decent work scores than male nurses; therefore, we still consider it necessary to further discuss the role of sex as a factor. Previous researchers have suggested that female nurses may report higher levels of decent work, potentially influenced by multiple factors, such as the characteristics of the nursing profession, sociocultural expectations, and sex role norms (Dhami et al., 2024), the current analysis does not confirm a significant gender disparity. In China's healthcare system, nursing has long been regarded as a female-dominated profession, giving rise to a strong occupational gender stereotype. Male nurses may therefore experience role conflict and identity pressure, feeling that their career choice is not fully recognized by their families, colleagues, or society. This, in turn, undermines their sense of professional fulfillment and occupational dignity (Tian et al., 2025). Compared with other professions, such as medicine, nursing generally offers lower career advancement opportunities, social status, and salary levels. Male nurses may be more sensitive to these disparities, which can easily lead to a sense of relative deprivation, reduce their job satisfaction and organizational commitment, and ultimately result in lower overall scores on decent work (Wu et al., 2025). In terms of job allocation, male nurses are often concentrated in high-intensity, high-risk departments, such as emergency and intensive care units (Wu et al., 2023). However, the corresponding salary incentives, training opportunities, and promotion pathways are not preferentially allocated to this group. This structural arrangement may amplify their occupational stress and sense of unfairness, thereby negatively affecting their overall evaluation of decent work. Male nurses are often reported to experience occupational gender role conflict, as societal expectations tend to associate men with more technical professions, whereas nursing is frequently perceived as auxiliary and service-oriented. This perceived mismatch may contribute to lower professional identity and self-doubt, which in turn could affect their sense of decent work (Sasa, 2019). Therefore, nursing managers should consider striving to create a supportive and inclusive working environment for male nurses. For example, they should address implicit gender biases and establish social support mechanisms, create visible career pathways for male nurses in specialized areas (e.g., critical care, emergency departments), where their technical skills can be fully leveraged and emphasize the value and professionalism of male nurses in diverse clinical roles (Younas et al., 2022). In addition, nursing managers should develop psychological support mechanisms to alleviate self-doubt and strengthen professional identity among male nurses. Such measures may help enhance the decent work experience of male nurses and support the continued professional growth and diversification of the nursing workforce. Finally, due to the current sample imbalance in the study, the results should be interpreted with caution. At the same time, we also hope that in the future, larger and more balanced samples of male nurses will be available for researchers to validate this preliminary finding.

Educational degrees

Higher education not only imparts theoretical knowledge but also emphasizes the cultivation of nurses' professional values (Beeber et al., 2019). Furthermore, nurses with a higher education level exhibit enhanced abilities in information acquisition, communication and expression, as well as a heightened awareness of rights protection (McCauley et al., 2020), which may enable them to access higher levels of decent work. However, broader literature suggests this relationship is more complex. Nurses with higher education often face elevated expectations from hospitals and colleagues, including assuming leadership roles, undertaking specialized clinical responsibilities, and engaging in evidence-based practice research (Montgomery and Patrician, 2022; Sarıköse and Türkmen, 2020). While these roles align with their advanced training, they also introduce additional responsibilities that may not be met with corresponding recognition, compensation, or workload adjustments (Zheng et al., 2025). Such mismatches can undermine perceptions of fairness and respect. Moreover, the impact of higher education on decent work largely depends on organizational support and the availability of aligned career development pathways (Xu et al., 2024). When institutions provide more opportunities and targeted professional development for highly educated nurses, these individuals are more likely to perceive their work as meaningful, secure, and rewarding. Conversely, in environments where career advancement is stagnant or their educational qualifications are underutilized, highly-educated nurses may feel undervalued, despite their potential to make greater contributions to patient care and team leadership (Zhao et al., 2023). In summary, higher education levels may not universally predict higher decent work scores. Their influence hinges on the alignment between individual capabilities, institutional expectations, workload distribution, and organizational support mechanisms. While higher education enhances professional competence and lays the foundation for specialized roles, it also presents unique challenges that must be addressed through proactive organizational policies (Yan et al., 2023). Future researchers should further explore these dynamics to develop strategies ensuring highly educated nurses can fully leverage their qualifications within supportive and equitable work environments. For nurses with lower educational levels, healthcare institutions should establish pathways to upgrade their academic qualifications. For instance, they can collaborate with academic partners to develop on-the-job training programs and provide financial or scheduling support to facilitate their career advancement. Consider establishing peer learning groups and academic leadership programs to create a supportive learning environment and reduce feelings of loneliness. This may not only improve their decent work conditions but also enhance the overall quality of the nursing workforce (Almarwani and Alzahrani, 2023).

Professional title

Professional titles are the recognition of nurses' professional abilities, work experience, and professional skills in nursing (Zhao et al., 2024). Nurses with higher professional titles often have richer practical experience and more systematic professional knowledge and have higher autonomy in their work (Jansson et al., 2021). At the same time, professional titles are usually directly linked to work rewards and career development opportunities (Madlabana et al., 2020). Therefore, to improve the overall decent work level of nurses, we should consider starting with optimizing the professional title system (Zhu et al., 2024), so that nurses at each professional title stage can clearly perceive their professional value and receive fair returns, ultimately improving the overall decent work level.

Leadership roles

Head nurses serve as role models for young nurses and contribute to shaping the social image of the nursing profession. Compared with ordinary nurses, they usually receive higher salaries, better benefits, and more comprehensive welfare protection, which may strengthen their sense of esteem and professional dignity (García-Sierra and Fernández-Castro, 2018; Clavo-Hall et al., 2018). Therefore, nursing managers should consider optimizing nursing team management. We advocate transforming the advantages of nursing leaders into shared value for the nursing team; that is, through empowerment, recognition, support, and similar actions, allowing ordinary nurses to perceive dignity and realize value in nursing work (Specchia et al., 2021; Manges et al., 2017), ultimately promoting the overall improvement of the decent work level of the entire nursing team.

Experience of work-related violence

Subgroup analysis showed that nurses who had experienced work-related violence had lower scores of decent work compared to nurses who had not. This result also reflects the negative impact of violence in the work environment on the professional dignity, rights protection, and professional experience of nurses (Sun et al., 2022). In China’s specific doctor–patient relationship, patients and their families often vent dissatisfaction toward nurses. This form of violence is not limited to physical or verbal attacks; it also constitutes symbolic violence arising from power imbalances, which damages nurses’ professional image and weakens their professional authority and occupational dignity (Ying et al., 2026). Repeated exposure to work-related violence is a typical chronic occupational stressor that can lead to emotional exhaustion and reduced personal accomplishment. When nurses remain in such an environment over the long term, they come to perceive their workplace as unsafe and disrespectful, severely eroding their professional dignity and motivation (Jang et al., 2022). To effectively combat workplace violence and systematically promote nurses' professional dignity, managers should consider optimizing the physical layout and staffing arrangements in high-risk departments, prioritizing the assurance of nurses' personal safety and professional dignity as core objectives. Furthermore, it is recommended that comprehensive support, including psychological counseling, legal assistance, organizational support, and other necessary measures be provided to nurses who have experienced workplace violence, aiming to help them repair psychological trauma and restore their professional dignity (Li et al., 2025a; Arnetz et al., 2025).

Researchers on decent work in nursing have highlighted its multiple positive effects on nurses. For instance, decent work can enhance nurses' psychological ownership, thereby promoting their work engagement and serving as a key mechanism for stimulating their enthusiasm and initiative (El-Gazar et al., 2024). It can also strengthen their sense of being insiders and their mental health, which in turn has a beneficial impact on their work ability and overall well-being, indicating that improving working conditions is not only crucial for nurses themselves but also a fundamental way to enhance their long-term work performance and physical and mental health (El-Gazar et al., 2025). Furthermore, decent work can reinforce nurses' sense of responsibility and organizational identification, significantly increasing their willingness to engage in internal whistleblowing and underscoring the importance of creating a supportive work environment that encourages nurses to speak up and safeguard patient safety when problems are identified (El-Gazar and Zoromba, 2025). In summary, a high level of decent work can generate positive effects for nurses themselves, patients, and healthcare organizations. However, we have found that there is still considerable room for improvement in the decent work levels of Chinese nurses. Therefore, we call on managers to attach importance to nurses' decent work by ensuring their rights are protected, providing fair income, guaranteeing safe working conditions, supporting their professional development, and respecting their social value, so as to jointly make efforts to enhance the decent work of nurses.

Implications

In this meta-analysis, we delved into the key theme of decent work for nurses. By integrating relevant literature, we have revealed the current situation of nurses' decent work level, which can provide some reference for different groups. For policymakers in China, it is recommended to improve nurse labor protection regulations by formulating and enforcing binding provisions on staffing levels, working hours, and overtime compensation to reduce excessive workloads. They should consider requiring regular reviews and public reporting of staffing data and implement a transparent, structured remuneration system that explicitly links salary progression to educational background, work experience, and professional responsibilities. For hospital managers, may be essential to systematically assess workloads, overtime, and leave balances, using these data to develop unit-specific staffing plans and adjust schedules to address chronic shortages. They should also establish transparent promotion criteria based on objective performance indicators, hold regular team debriefing meetings, and provide training for nurse managers in supportive supervision and conflict resolution. For clinical nursing units, likely to be important to establish peer-support and advocacy groups at the unit or organizational level to share experiences and address challenges collectively. Additionally, they should consider creating clear, low-barrier channels, such as anonymous surveys and regular forums, to enable nurses to provide feedback and participate in decisions regarding their working conditions. For nursing educators, it is advised to incorporate courses on decent work, such as labor rights, professional ethics, and workplace well-being-into nursing curricula, using case studies and simulation exercises for practical learning to enhance nurses' awareness of their rights and professional identity.

Limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis have several limitations that deserve special attention. Firstly, the study included only literature in either Chinese or English, which may result in the omission of literature in other languages. Secondly, the included studies were designed to be cross-sectional, so causal relationships cannot be inferred from observed associations, inevitably leading to design flaws. Thirdly, we included only studies conducted in mainland China, which limits the generalizability of the conclusions to other countries. Variations in health system structures, labor regulations, and cultural norms across countries mean that the specific policy levers identified here may not be directly applicable elsewhere. Consequently, caution is needed when comparing our results with international literature, and future researchers should include cross-country comparisons to examine how different health systems and policy environments influence nurses’ decent work.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we found that the level of decent work for nurses in China was at a moderate level, and there may be an urgent need to take corresponding measures to improve it, especially for those with lower levels of decent work, such as males, those with lower education levels, those with lower professional titles, nurses without supervisory positions, and those who have experienced work-related violence. Nursing managers should consider that low levels of decent work are not only a matter of individual well-being but may also be related to the stability of the nursing system. Low levels of decent work may lead to increased nurse turnover rates and decreased work engagement and ultimately affect nursing quality and patient safety. The value of this study lies in clarifying the priority intervention groups and providing precise targeting for possible policy formulation.
