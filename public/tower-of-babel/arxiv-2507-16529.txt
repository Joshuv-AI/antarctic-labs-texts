Abstract
We consider the problem of Bayesian causal discovery for the standard model of linear structural equations with equivariant Gaussian noise. A uniform prior is placed on the space of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) over a fixed set of variables and, given the graph, independent Gaussian priors are placed on the associated linear coefficients of pairwise interactions. We show that the rate at which the posterior on model space concentrates on the true underlying DAG depends critically on its nature: If it is maximal , in the sense that adding any one new edge would violate acyclicity, then its posterior probability converges to 1 exponentially fast (almost surely) in the sample size n n . Otherwise, it converges at a rate no faster than 1 / n 1/\sqrt{n} . This sharp dichotomy is an instance of the important general phenomenon that avoiding overfitting is significantly harder than identifying all of the structure that is present in the model. We also draw a new connection between the posterior distribution on model space and recent results on optimal hypothesis testing in the related problem of edge detection. Our theoretical findings are illustrated empirically through simulation experiments.



I
Introduction

Understanding the causes of phenomena influenced by multiple variables
can often be aided by models that represent causal relations via
directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)
[
24
]
.

Causal discovery
, or

structure learning
, is the identification of the true underlying
DAG or of other appropriate structures. Applications of causal discovery
span a broad range of different areas,
including, among many others, the problem of topology inference in wireless
networks
[
34
]
, the analysis of gene networks
[
20
]
,
the evaluation of medical
treatments
[
37
]
.
Applications have motivated extensive research resulting in a variety
of causal discovery tools, including constraint-based
methods
[
32
]
, score-based
approaches
[
25
,
22
,
39
]
, and Bayesian methods
[
1
,
8
,
11
,
2
,
9
,
3
]
.
In this work, we adopt a Bayesian approach,
in the context of the standard causal model based
on linear structural equations.
This is partly motivated by the fact that the
Bayesian setting
naturally provides tools for quantifying confidence
levels for discovered structures,
typically
in the form of posterior probabilities. Bayesian approaches have also been successful in quantifying epistemic uncertainty, facilitating downstream tasks such as causal effect estimation
[
35
]
.

The various methods which have been developed for quantifying uncertainty in causal discovery
include a bootstrap approach
[
10
]
;
confidence intervals
[
7
,
33
]
;
and the formulation of causal discovery as a series of composite Neyman-Pearson hypothesis tests
[
29
]
.
In addition, several effective inference techniques have been employed for the setting considered in this work, such as a Gibbs sampler
[
14
]
;
an efficient Metropolis-Hastings algorithm
[
36
]
;
and a variational inference method for sampling node permutation matrices
[
8
]
.

The main difficulty in the practical
application of Bayesian methods is the high computational
complexity of standard techniques,
especially in high-dimensions.
In order
to improve mixing in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
samplers, an ‘edge reversal’ move is introduced in
[
12
]
;
a sampler on the space of causal orderings rather than
causal networks is used in
[
11
]
;
and a combination of heuristics and pruning
is used in
[
11
]
.
Variational methods have also been extensively
used
[
2
,
1
,
8
,
19
]
.

This work focuses on the linear causal model
with homoscedastic additive Gaussian noise.
We place a uniform prior on the space
of all relevant DAGs and independent Gaussian priors
on the
edge weights
, that is,
on the linear coefficients of pairwise interactions.
Both
classical
[
33
,
6
]

and Bayesian
[
8
,
21
,
5
]

versions of this model have been considered,
and it is known to be identifiable
under mild conditions
[
25
]
.

Our main contributions are the following:

(i)

Let
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
denote the posterior
probability of a DAG
S
.
We show that, when
the observations
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

are generated under
S^{*}
,
the rate at which

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\to 1
heavily depends on

S^{*}
. If
S^{*}
is
maximal
, i.e., if adding any one
new edge to it would violate the acyclicity constraint,
then, as
n\to\infty
,
\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\to 1
exponentially fast
(almost surely).
Otherwise, the convergence is much slower,
of rate no faster than

1/\sqrt{n}
.

(ii)

In the special case where the nonzero edge weights
are
a priori
assumed to all be equal to 1,
we show that

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\to 1

exponentially fast
(almost surely), with exponent
equal to
1/2
.

(iii)

We demonstrate that Bayesian methods are optimal
for edge detection under the Neyman-Pearson causal discovery
framework of
[
29
]
.

(iv)
We illustrate and validate the above theoretical results on simulated
data.

The sharp dichotomy displayed by the posterior convergence
results in
(i)

can be viewed as an instance of
the following general phenomenon:
Avoiding overfitting is significantly harder than identifying all the
structure that is actually present in the model. Here,
this means that, when
S^{*}
is maximal, then
it is impossible to overfit, because it is impossible
to add any spurious edges to the true model,
so
the posterior converges quickly.
By contrast,
when
S^{*}
is not maximal, then much stronger evidence,
i.e., many more observations,
are required to rule out all extra
edges, leading to
much slower convergence. Notice that overfitting is not possible when all nonzero weights are fixed
a prior
(such as in
(ii)
), since it only occurs when
it is difficult to discern the absence of an edge
from its presence with an arbitrarily small but
still nonzero weight.
Earlier instances of this dichotomy have been noted,
e.g., in
[
28
,
15
,
4
,
31
]
.
In fact, as we will see in the proofs of our main
results in Sections
III

and
IV
, the analysis is
similar, at least in spirit,
to Schwarz’s derivation
[
28
]

of the Bayesian information criterion (BIC).

A similar phenomenon was recently observed in the
context of a different class of graphical models,
namely, tree models for time series.
Specifically, for the Bayesian context tree (BCT)
model class
[
17
,
16
]
,
it was shown
[
23
]

that the posterior on the model space converges
at a polynomial rate, unless the true underlying
model is the unique maximal
model, in which case the posterior convergence
rate is exponential.

II
The setting

II-A

The Bayesian linear causal model

We consider the linear structural equations model

X=AX+\epsilon.

(1)

Here,
X=(X(1),\ldots,X(d))^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

is a
d
-dimensional sample, and

\epsilon=(\epsilon(1),\ldots,\epsilon(d))^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

is Gaussian noise with distribution
N(0,\sigma^{2}I)
.
The noise variance

\sigma^{2}
is assumed to be fixed and known,
and
I
denotes the
d
-dimensional identity
matrix. The
d\times d
matrix
A
is
described in terms of its
structure

S

and
weights

\mathbf{w}
,
where
S
is the binary matrix
with entries
S_{ij}={\mathbb{I}}\{A_{ij}\neq 0\}

and
\mathbf{w}
is the concatenation
of all the nonzero entries of
A
.
We naturally view
S
as the
model

and the weights
\mathbf{w}
as the
associated
parameters
.

Throughout, we assume that
S

is the adjacency matrix of a
directed acyclic graph (DAG) on
\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
and we often identify
S
with the DAG it represents.
To ensure identifiability
[
25
]
,
in all our results we will assume

causal minimality

[
38
]
,
which is a mild
faithfulness
assumption
[
26
]
.
Causal minimality means that
the observations are generated
by a model
S^{*}
and parameters
\mathbf{w}^{*}
such that
the law of
X
in (
1
) under
(S^{*},\mathbf{w}^{*})

is not the same as its law under
(S,\mathbf{w})

for any subgraph
S
of
S^{*}
and any collection
of associated
parameters,
\mathbf{w}
.
Note that, often,
this model is equivalently written
as
X=A^{\intercal}X+\epsilon
,
instead of the form (
1
)
adopted here.

If we let
P_{j}(S,X)
,
with ‘
P
’ for ‘parents’,
denote the sub-vector of
X
consisting
of those of its components
X(i)
that influence the value of

X(j)
in (
1
), i.e., such that

S_{ji}\neq 0
, then
the
j
th equation
in (
1
) is

X{(j)}=\mathbf{w}{(j)}^{\intercal}P_{j}(S,X)+\epsilon{(j)},\quad j=1,\ldots,d,

(2)

where
\mathbf{w}(j)
is the column vector that consists of
the nonzero elements of the
j
th row of
A
.
In this notation,

\mathbf{w}
can be viewed as the concatenation of the vector
blocks
\mathbf{w}(j)
, as

\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{w}{(1)}^{\intercal},\ldots,\mathbf{w}{(d)}^{\intercal})^{\intercal}.

Clearly, any matrix
A
in (
1
)
can be described by its structure
S
and
weights
\mathbf{w}
. We write

A=m(S,\mathbf{w})

for this map, and we adopt the following
spike-and-slab prior on the space of matrices

A
. First, a uniform prior

\pi(S)\propto 1
, is used for
S
in the space
{\cal G}_{d}

of all adjacency matrices of DAGs on
\{1,\ldots,d\}

and then,
given
S
, we place independent
Gaussian

N(0,\sigma_{w}^{2})
priors on the
associated weights
\mathbf{w}
.

We will find it useful to think of
AX

as vector of linear combinations of the weights
\mathbf{w}
,

AX=M(S,X)\mathbf{w},

(3)

for an appropriate matrix
M(S,X)
.
Comparing (
3
) with (
1
)
it is not hard to see that
M(S,X)
is

\begin{bmatrix}\overbrace{P_{1}(S,X)^{\intercal}}^{s_{1}}&\!0&\!\cdots&0&0\\
\overbrace{0\cdots 0}^{s_{1}}&\!\overbrace{P_{2}(S,X)^{\intercal}}^{s_{2}}&\!\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\!\vdots&\!\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\overbrace{0\cdots 0}^{s_{1}}&\!\overbrace{0\cdots 0}^{s_{2}}&\!\cdots&\overbrace{0\cdots 0}^{s_{d-1}}&\overbrace{P_{d}(S,X)^{\intercal}}^{s_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\!.

Here,

s_{j}
denotes the dimension of each
P_{j}(S,X)
, where
the vectors
P_{j}(S,X)

are defined in (
2
).

II-B

The posterior

Given independent and identically
distributed (i.i.d.) observations
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

from the model (
1
),
and writing
\phi_{v}
for the
N(0,v)
density,
the associated likelihood is

f(\mathbf{X}^{n}|S,\mathbf{w})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\sigma^{2}}(X_{i}-m(S,\textbf{w})X_{i}).

First, we examine
the full conditional density of the parameters:

\pi(\mathbf{w}|S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
is proportional to

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{X}^{n}|S,\mathbf{w})\pi(\mathbf{w}|S)\pi(S)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-M(S,X_{i})\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{w}^{2}}\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}\Big(\Sigma_{M}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})+\frac{1}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}I\Big)\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\mathbf{w}^{\intercal}b(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})\Big\},

where,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{M}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M(S,X_{i})^{\intercal}M(S,X_{i}),

\displaystyle b(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}M(S,X_{i})^{\intercal}X_{i}.

Therefore,

\mathbf{w}|S,\mathbf{X}^{n}\sim N(\mu_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}}),\Sigma_{w}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})),

where,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{w}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})

\displaystyle:=\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}I+\Sigma_{M}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})\Big)^{-1},

\displaystyle\mu_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})

\displaystyle:=\Sigma_{w}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})b(S,\mathbf{X}^{n}).

Note that, because of the structure of
M(S,X)
,

\Sigma_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
is a block diagonal matrix, whose

j
th block,
\Sigma^{(j)}_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
,

1\leq j\leq d
, is given by

\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma_{w}^{2}}I+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{j}(S,X_{i})P_{j}(S,X_{i})^{\intercal}\Big)^{-1}.

(4)

Similarly, for
1\leq j\leq d
,
the vector
b(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
can be written in
terms of the vector blocks

b^{(j)}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n}):=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(j)P_{j}(S,X_{i}),

(5)

as
b(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})=(b^{(1)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})^{\intercal},\ldots,b^{(d)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
,
and
\mu_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
can be written in
terms of

\mu_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}}):=\Sigma_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})b^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}}),

(6)

as
\mu_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})=(\mu_{w}^{(1)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})^{\intercal},\ldots,\mu_{w}^{(d)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
.

Next, we examine the posterior on model space.
A simple computation shows that
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
is

\displaystyle\int f(\mathbf{X}^{n}|S,\mathbf{w})\pi(\mathbf{w}|S)\,d\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}b(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})b(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\times|\Sigma_{w}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})|^{1/2},

(7)

where
|B|
denotes the determinant of matrix
B
.
Substituting into (
7
)
the expression for
M(S,X)
,
using the notation in (
4
)–(
5
),
and simplifying, shows that
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
is proportional to

\displaystyle\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}b^{(j)}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})b^{(j)}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\prod_{j=1}^{d}|\Sigma^{(j)}_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})|^{1/2}.

(8)

This expression will be our starting point in Section
IV
.

III
Posterior concentration: Binary case

Before considering the posterior induced by the general
linear causal model in (
1
), in this section
we examine the simpler
binary
linear causal model,
where all the parameters in
\mathbf{w}
are equal to 1, i.e.,

A=S
:

X=SX+\epsilon.

(9)

In this case, every model
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
is causally minimal,
so we have identifiability
[
25
]
.
Also,
it is easy to obtain the
form of the posterior on models,
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
, as:

\pi(S|\mathbf{X}^{n})\propto f(\mathbf{X}^{n}|S)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\sigma^{2}}(X_{i}-SX_{i}).

(10)

Our main result here states that, when the observations
are generated according to some matrix
S^{*}
, its posterior
probability almost surely converges to 1 at an
exponential rate, with exponent equal to
1/2
.
Interestingly, the exponent is the same for all
S^{*}
.

Theorem 1
.

If
\{X_{n}\}
are i.i.d. observations generated
by the model (
9
) with
S=S^{*}
,
then, as
n\to\infty
:

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})=1-\exp\Big\{-\frac{n}{2}+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\Big\}\quad\mbox{a.s.}

For the proof, we will need the following two lemmas.
Lemma
1
,
proved in Appendix
A-A
,
computes the Kullback-Leibler
divergence
D(P_{S^{\prime}}\|P_{S})
between the laws
P_{S},P_{S^{\prime}}

of
X
in (
9
) corresponding
to
S,S^{\prime}
.

Lemma 1
.

For any
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
, let
P_{S}
denote the law of

X=(I-S)^{-1}\epsilon
in (
9
).
Then, for any pair
S,S^{\prime}\in{\cal G}_{d}
,

D(P_{S^{\prime}}\|P_{S})=\frac{1}{2}\big[\|(I-S)(I-S^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}-d\big],

where

\|B\|_{F}^{2}:=\sum_{ij}B^{2}_{ij}

is the Frobenius norm of
B
.

The exponent
1/2
in the theorem
will be seen to be based on the following
simple computation. The proof of
Lemma
2
is in Appendix
A-A
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
G\in{\cal G}_{d}
, we have,

\min_{S\in{\cal G}_{d},S\neq G}\|(I-S)(I-G)^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}=d+1,

or, equivalently,

\min_{S\in{\cal G}_{d},S\neq G}D(P_{G}\|P_{S})=\frac{1}{2}.

We are now ready to prove the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
S^{*}\in{\cal G}_{d}
be fixed.
From the expression for the posterior in (
10
) we have,

\displaystyle\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\mathbf{X}^{n}|S^{*})}{\sum_{S}f(\mathbf{X}^{n}|S)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\sigma^{2}}(X_{i}-S^{*}X_{i})}{\sum_{S}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\sigma^{2}}(X_{i}-SX_{i})},

where the sums are over all
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
.
Using the form of the
Gaussian density and the fact that each, we have

X_{i}=(I-S^{*})^{-1}\epsilon_{i}

from (
9
),
\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})
becomes,

\frac{\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\epsilon_{i}\|^{2}\big\}}{\sum_{S}\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|(I-S)(I-S^{*})^{-1}\epsilon_{i}\|^{2}\big\}},

and writing
D(S)=(I-S)(I-S^{*})^{-1}
, this further becomes

\frac{\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\epsilon_{i}\|^{2}\big\}}{\sum_{S}\exp\big\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\epsilon_{i}^{\intercal}D(S)^{\intercal}D(S)\epsilon_{i}\big\}}.

Letting
U_{n}(S)
denote the i.i.d. partial sums
U_{n}(S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(S)
,
with

Y_{i}(S)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\epsilon_{i}^{\intercal}[D(S)^{\intercal}D(S)-I]\epsilon_{i},

we can then express:

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})=\Big[1+\sum_{S\neq S^{*}}\exp\{-U_{n}(S)\}\Big]^{-1}.

For each
i
and any
S
,
it is easy to compute that,

\displaystyle\nu(S)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}(S)]=\frac{1}{2}\big[\|D(S)\|_{F}^{2}-d\big]

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big[\|(I-S)(I-S^{*})^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}-d\Big],

so that,

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})=\Big[1+\sum_{S\neq S^{*}}e^{-n\nu(S)}\exp\{-\bar{U}_{n}(S)\}\Big]^{-1},

where
\bar{U}_{n}(S)
are the centered partial sums

\bar{U}_{n}(S)=U_{n}(S)-n\nu(S)
.
In view of the law of the iterated logarithm (LIL), this already contains all the information
we need – the remainder of the proof is simple asymptotic estimates.

From (
11
) and Lemma
2
, we know that the
minimum of
\nu(S)
over all
S\neq S^{*}
is
1/2
.
Therefore,

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\geq\Big[1+e^{-\frac{n}{2}}\sum_{S\neq S^{*}}\exp\{-\bar{U}_{n}(S)\}\Big]^{-1}.

(12)

And by the LIL we know that, for each
S\neq S^{*}
,

|\bar{U}_{n}(S)|=O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\quad\mbox{a.s.},

(13)

as
n\to\infty
, since the variance,

{\rm Var}(Y_{i}(S))={\rm Var}\Big(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\epsilon_{i}^{\intercal}(D(S)^{\intercal}D(S)-I)\epsilon_{i}\Big),

is clearly finite. Combining (
12
) with (
13
) and
the elementary bound

(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
,
x\geq 0
,
gives

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\geq 1-\exp\big\{-\frac{n}{2}+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\big\}\quad\mbox{a.s.}

To obtain a matching upper bound, we note that

\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})\leq\big[1+e^{-n/2}\exp\{-\bar{U}_{n}(S_{1})\}\big]^{-1},

for any
S_{1}\in\mathcal{G}_{d}
,
S_{1}\neq S^{*}
,
and hence,

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\leq 1-\exp\big\{-\frac{n}{2}+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\big\}\quad\mbox{a.s.}

where we used
the elementary bound
(1+x)^{-1}\leq 1-x+x^{2}
,
x\geq 0
.
∎

IV
Posterior concentration: General case

In this section we consider the posterior
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})

on models, under the general linear causal model given
in (
1
) and (
2
).
We assume throughout that
\{X_{n}\}
are i.i.d. observations from the model
X=A^{*}X+\epsilon
,
with respect to the given true underlying matrix

A^{*}
with structure
S^{*}\in{\cal G}_{d}

and associated parameters
\mathbf{w}^{*}
.
Theorems
2

and
3
contain the main results of this paper.
They show that, if
S^{*}
is
maximal, then
\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
converges to 1 exponentially
fast, otherwise, it converges at
rate no faster than
1/\sqrt{n}
.

Recall the expression for the
posterior
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
given in (
8
).
Writing
T^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
,
namely,
1\leq j\leq d
,
for the
j
th summand in the
exponent
in (
8
),
namely,

b^{(j)}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w}^{(j)}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})b^{(j)}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n}),

(14)

we see that
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
is proportional to:

\exp\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}T^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})\Bigg)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\sqrt{|\Sigma_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})|}.

(15)

We first derive the first-order asymptotic behavior
of
\Sigma_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
and

T^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
.
Proposition
1
is proved in
Appendix
A-B
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
\{X_{n}\}
are i.i.d. observations
generated the model (
1
),
with respect to a matrix
A^{*}
with
model
S^{*}

and parameters
\mathbf{w}^{*}
,
such that
(S^{*},\mathbf{w}^{*})
is causally minimal.
Then, for any
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
and each
1\leq j\leq d
,
as
n\to\infty
, we have,
almost surely,

\displaystyle n\Sigma_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})

\displaystyle\to\Sigma_{\infty}^{(j)}(S),

\displaystyle T^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})

\displaystyle=nT_{\infty}^{(j)}(S)+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n}),

where,

\Sigma_{\infty}^{(j)}(S):=\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}[P_{j}(S,X)P_{j}(S,X)^{\intercal}]^{-1},

and
T_{\infty}^{(j)}(S)
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\big(\mathbb{E}[X{(j)}P_{j}(S,X)^{\intercal}]

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}[P_{j}(S,X)P_{j}(S,X)^{\intercal}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X{(j)}P_{j}(S,X)]\big),

or, equivalently,

\mathbb{E}[X{(j)^{2}}]-\min_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(X{(j)}-\eta^{\intercal}P_{j}(S,X)\big)^{2}\Big].

Next, recall from (
6
) the definition
of the
j
th component,
\mu_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
, of the mean
of
\mathbf{w}
under the the full conditional

\pi(\mathbf{w}|S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
.
Proposition
2
is
proved in Appendix

.

Proposition 2
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
1
,
for any
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
and each
1\leq j\leq d
, as
n\to\infty
, we have,
almost surely,

\mu_{w}^{(j)}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})\to\mu^{(j)}_{\infty}(S),

where,
\mu_{\infty}^{(j)}(S)
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[P_{j}(S,X)P_{j}(S,X)^{\intercal}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[X{(j)}P_{j}(S,X)]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg\,min}_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{s_{j}}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big(X{(j)}-\eta^{\intercal}P_{j}(S,X)\big)^{2}\Big].

(16)

In particular,
under the true model
S=S^{*}
,
we have
\mu^{(j)}_{\infty}(S^{*})=\mathbf{w}^{*}{(j)}
,

j=1,\ldots,d
.

As with
\mu(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
and
\Sigma_{w}(S,{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
,
we let

\mu_{\infty}(S):=(\mu^{(1)}_{\infty}(S)^{\intercal},\ldots,\mu^{(d)}_{\infty}(S)^{\intercal})^{\intercal}
,
and we write
\Sigma_{\infty}(S)
for the block
diagonal matrix with blocks

\Sigma_{\infty}^{(j)}(S)
,
1\leq j\leq d
.
Also, for any matrix
A
in (
1
),
let

denote the law of
X=(A-I)^{-1}\epsilon
.
The following is a straightforward generalization
of Lemma
1
. Its proof is identical to that
of Lemma
1
, and hence omitted.

Lemma 3
.

For any pair of matrices
A,A^{\prime}
with models

S,S^{\prime}\in{\cal G}_{d}
, respectively, we have:

D(P_{A^{\prime}}\|P_{A})=\frac{1}{2}\big[\|(I-A)(I-A^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}-d\big].

Recall that we call
S^{*}

maximal
if
adding any one more edge to it would necessarily violate
the acyclicity constraint.
In the following two lemmas, we estimate the posterior
log-odds between
S^{*}
and different models
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
.
These results,
proved in Appendix

, are the main technical
ingredients in the proofs of our two main results,
Theorems
2

and

below.

Lemma 4
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2
,
for any
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
such that
S^{*}
is not a subgraph of
S
,
as

we have, almost surely,

\displaystyle\log\frac{\pi(S|\mathbf{X}^{n})}{\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})}

\displaystyle=-nD(P_{A^{*}}\|P_{A(S)})

\displaystyle\quad+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})

where

and

.

Lemma 5
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2
,
and assuming

is non-maximal, there is an

such that

is a subgraph of

For that

\log\frac{\pi(S^{+}|\mathbf{X}^{n})}{\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})}=-\frac{\log n}{2}+\delta_{n}+o(1)\quad\mbox{a.s.},

,
where

We are now ready to state our main results.

Theorem 2

(Posterior concentration for maximal models)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2
,
if the true underlying model
S^{*}
is maximal,
then,

\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})=1-\exp\Big\{-nD(A^{*})+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\Big\},

almost surely as
n\to\infty
, where
D(A^{*})>0
is

D(A^{*}):=\min_{S\in\mathcal{G}_{d}:S\neq S^{*}}D(P_{A^{*}}\|P_{m(S,\mu_{\infty}(S))}).

(17)

Proof.

Since
S^{*}
is maximal, it is not a subgraph of any

S\in{\cal G}_{d}
,
S\neq S^{*}
.
Therefore, by Lemma
4
, for any

S\in{\cal G}_{d}
,
S\neq S^{*}
, we have, almosto surely as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\log\frac{\pi(S|\mathbf{X}^{n})}{\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})}

\displaystyle=-nD(P_{A^{*}}\|P_{A(S)})

(18)

\displaystyle\quad+O\big(\sqrt{n\log\log n}\big).

(19)

Let
S^{-}
achieve the minimum in (
17
).
Then,

\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{S\neq S^{*}}\frac{\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}}\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{\pi(S^{-}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}},

and the elementary bound
(1+x)^{-1}\leq 1-x+x^{2}
,

x\geq 0
, combined with (
19
) gives,
a.s. as
n\to\infty
,

\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})\leq 1-\exp\Big\{-nD(A^{*})+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\Big\}.

Similarly,
\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
is bounded below by

\displaystyle\Big[1+|{\cal G}_{d}|\max_{S\neq S^{*}}\frac{\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}\Big]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Big[1+|{\cal G}_{d}|\exp\Big\{-n\min_{S\neq S^{*}}D(P_{A^{*}}\|P_{A(S)})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\Big\}\Big]^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\exp\Big\{-nD(A^{*})+O(\sqrt{n\log\log n})\Big\}\quad\mbox{a.s.},

where we used (
19
) and the simple bound

(1+x)^{-1}\geq 1-x
,
x\geq 0
.
∎

Theorem 3

(Posterior concentration for non-maximal models)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
2
,
if the true underlying model
S^{*}
is non-maximal, then
its posterior probability,
\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
, converges
to 1 a.s. as
n\to\infty
, but this convergence
is no faster than at rate
1/\sqrt{n}
.
Specifically, as
n\to\infty
we have,

1-\pi(S^{*}|\mathbf{X}^{n})\geq(1+o(1))\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\quad\mbox{a.s.}

Proof.

Consistency follows from standard Bayesian model-selection
theory à la Schwartz
[
27
]
.
For more specific (and more general) results in the present
context see, e.g.,
[
7
,
3
]
.
For the bound on the rate,
with
S^{+}
as in Lemma
5
,
note that

\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{S\neq S^{*}}\frac{\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}}\\
\leq\frac{1}{1+\frac{\pi(S^{+}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})}}.

Then, using
Lemma
5
together with the
elementary bound
(1+x)^{-1}\leq 1-x+x^{2}
,
x\geq 0
,
yields,

\pi(S^{*}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})\leq 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(1+o(1))+O\Big(\frac{1}{n}\Big),

as required.
∎

V
Edge detection

In this section, we draw an interesting and potentially
practically useful connection between the Bayesian
causal discovery framework of Section
II

and optimal hypothesis testing for edge detection.
Given
n
i.i.d. observations
\mathbf{X}^{n}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
from
the general linear causal model (
1
),
the goal here is to estimate the support
\bm{\chi}(S)=S+S^{\intercal}

of the true underlying model
S
.

Suppose that, after
S
is drawn
according to the prior
\pi(S)
on
{\cal G}_{d}
,
the observations
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}
are (conditionally)
i.i.d. samples
from (
1
).
Given a
detector

\hat{\bm{\chi}}=\hat{\bm{\chi}}({\mathbf{X}^{n}})
,
following
[
29
]
we define the
false positive

and
false negative

detection rates, respectively, as,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i>j}\mathbbm{I}\{\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=1,\bm{\chi}_{ij}=0\}\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{i>j}\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}=0\}\big]},

(20)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i>j}\mathbbm{I}\{\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0,\bm{\chi}_{ij}=1\}\Big]}{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i>j}\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}=1\}\Big]}.

(21)

We aim to minimize
\varepsilon_{n}^{-}
, subject to
\varepsilon_{n}^{+}
being
below a given
pre-specified user tolerance

\alpha
:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\bm{\chi}}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-}\;\;\;\;\textrm{s.t.}\;\;\varepsilon_{n}^{+}\leq\alpha.

(22)

Let
{\cal C}_{ij}
denote the set of all
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
such
that
\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=[S+S^{\intercal}]_{ij}=0
,
and let
\rho(\cdot|S)
denote the marginal likelihood
of
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}
given model
S
.
Then
\rho(\cdot|S)
has density:

f({\mathbf{X}^{n}}|S)=\int f({\mathbf{X}^{n}}|S,\mathbf{w})\pi(\mathbf{w}|S)d\mathbf{w}.

We also define the probability measures,

\displaystyle\bar{\cal P}_{ij,n}(\cdot)=\sum_{S\in{\cal C}_{ij}}\frac{\rho(\cdot|S)}{\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}\pi(S),

\displaystyle\bar{\cal Q}_{ij,n}(\cdot)=\sum_{S\in{\cal C}_{ij}^{c}}\frac{\rho(\cdot|S)}{\pi({\cal C}_{ij}^{c})}\pi(S),

that describe the law of
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}
conditional on
S\in{\cal C}_{ij}

and on
S\not\in{\cal C}_{ij},
respectively.
Let,

u^{+}_{ij}=\frac{\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}{\sum_{i>j}\pi({\cal C}_{ij})},\qquad u^{-}_{ij}=\frac{\pi({\cal C}_{ij}^{c})}{\sum_{i>j}\pi({\cal C}_{ij}^{c})}.

Then for any detector

\hat{\bm{\chi}}=\hat{\bm{\chi}}({\mathbf{X}^{n}})

we have:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{+}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>j}u^{+}_{ij}\bar{\cal P}_{ij,n}(\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=1),

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{-}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i>j}u^{-}_{ij}\bar{\cal Q}_{ij,n}(\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0).

Letting
p_{ij,n}
and
q_{ij,n}
denote the densities
of
\bar{\cal P}_{ij,n}
and
\bar{\cal Q}_{ij,n}
, respectively,
the optimal detector, identified in
[
29
]
, is given by

\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0\iff\frac{p_{ij,n}({\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{q_{ij,n}({\mathbf{X}^{n}})}\geq\frac{u_{ij}^{-}}{u_{ij}^{+}}\gamma,

(23)

where
\gamma
is chosen so that the constraint on the
false positive rate in (
22
) is satisfied with equality.

As discussed in
[
29
]
, the optimal detector is typically computationally impractical, since the evaluation of
p_{ij,n}
and

q_{ij,n}
requires averaging over all models
S
in
{\cal C}
and in
{\cal C}^{c}
,
respectively, of which there are super-exponentially many
[
11
]
.
However, the following form for the optimal detector is more amenable
to numerical computations, for example using MCMC
model averaging. The utility of the
representation of the optimal detector in Proposition
3

is illustrated through simulation experiments in Section
VI-C
.

Proposition 3
.

The optimal detector in (
23
) can be equivalently expressed
in terms of the posterior as,

\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0\iff\pi(\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=0|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})\geq\frac{\gamma^{\prime}}{1+\gamma^{\prime}},

(24)

where
\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{u_{ij}^{-}\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}{u_{ij}^{+}\pi({\cal C}_{ij}^{c})}\gamma
.

Proof.

Since,

f({\mathbf{X}^{n}}|S)=\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})f({\mathbf{X}^{n}}),

where
f({\mathbf{X}^{n}})=\sum_{S}\int f({\mathbf{X}^{n}}|S,\mathbf{w})\pi(\mathbf{w}|S)\pi(S)\,d\mathbf{w}

is the marginal likelihood, we have

\displaystyle p_{ij,n}({\mathbf{X}^{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{f({\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}\sum_{S\in{\cal C}_{ij}}\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{f({\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}\sum_{S}\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=0\}\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})

\displaystyle=\frac{f({\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=0\}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}}].

Similarly,

q_{ij,n}({\mathbf{X}^{n}})=\frac{f({\mathbf{X}^{n}})}{\pi({\cal C}_{ij}^{c})}(1-\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=0\}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}}]).

Therefore, we have
\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0
if and only if,

\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=0\}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}}]}{1-\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{I}\{\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S)=0\}|{\mathbf{X}^{n}}]}\geq\frac{u_{ij}^{-}\pi({\cal C}_{ij})}{u_{ij}^{+}\pi({\cal C}_{ij}^{c})}\gamma.

The definition of
\gamma^{\prime}
and rearranging give (
24
).
∎

VI-C

MCMC edge detection

We compare the performance of the optimal detector
of
[
29
]
, with that of state-of-the-art methods
for causal discovery. We use the form of the
optimal detector in Proposition
3
,
computed via a simple MCMC sampler as follows.
Given observations
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}
, in order to decide whether

\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0
or 1, we run the sampler for

T
iterations, producing a collection of models

\{S^{(t)};1\leq t\leq T\}

approximately distributed according to
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
.
Then we average the values of

\bm{\chi}_{ij}(S^{(t)})
,
t=1,2,\ldots,T
and
declare
\hat{\bm{\chi}}_{ij}=0

if and only if that average is
\geq\tau
,
for an appropriate threshold
\tau
.
We call this the “optimal MCMC detector”.

Since, from (
8
),
we know
\pi(S|{\mathbf{X}^{n}})
explicitly up to normalization,
it is easy to come up with an appropriate Metropolis-Hastings
sampler. We use a simple
irreducible proposal kernel
on
{\cal G}_{d}
which, given
the current state
S
, proposes
a new
S^{\prime}
uniformly chosen among all neighbors of
S
,
namely, all
S^{\prime}\in{\cal G}_{d}
that
differ from
S
in exactly one edge. The remaining details are
straightforward and hence omitted. We only note that,
computationally, the most costly part
of the algorithm is the evaluation
of
\Sigma_{w}(S,\mathbf{X}^{n})
, which
requires

O(nd^{5})
operations per MCMC iteration,
and it is the main obstacle
in scaling the algorithm to
high dimensions.

The performance of the optimal MCMC detector
is compared against:
Neighborhood selection using
LASSO
[
22
]
; the continuous optimization formulation
NOTEARS
[
39
]
; the penalized maximum likelihood (ML)
estimator in
[
25
]
; and the two pseudo-Bayesian
methods (“resample” and “bootstrap”) of
[
11
]
.
We consider two classes
of problems, with dimensions
d=4
and

.
In each case, we first randomly generate

matrices

, and for each

{\mathbf{X}^{n}}

). To generate each

[

]
)

.

For each such data set
{\mathbf{X}^{n}}
, each of these six methods produces

\hat{\bm{\chi}}
of the support
\bm{\chi}
of the true

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Recall that,

\displaystyle D\big(N(0,\Sigma_{0})\|N(0,\Sigma_{1})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\rm tr}(\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\Sigma_{0})+\log\frac{|\Sigma_{1}|}{|\Sigma_{0}|}-d\right].

Here, we have
\Sigma_{0}=\sigma^{2}(I-S^{\prime})^{-1}(I-S^{\prime})^{-\intercal}
and

\Sigma_{1}=\sigma^{2}(I-S)^{-1}(I-S)^{-\intercal}
. Since

S^{\intercal}
and
S^{\prime\intercal}
are DAG adjacency matrices,
they are nilpotent, so the only eigenvalue of
(I-S^{\prime})

and
(I-S)
is 1, therefore
the determinants
|\Sigma_{0}|
and
|\Sigma_{1}|
are both equal
to
\sigma^{2d}
and,

D\big(N(0,\Sigma_{0})\|N(0,\Sigma_{1})\big)=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\rm tr}(\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\Sigma_{0})-d\right].

Also,

\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\Sigma_{0}=(I-S)^{\intercal}(I-S)(I-S^{\prime})^{-1}(I-S^{\prime})^{-\intercal},

and since the trace of the product of two matrices is the sum of the elements of their Hadamard product,
the trace of
\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\Sigma_{0})
is equal to the trace of
the product of
(I-S)(I-S^{\prime})^{-1}
and its transpose.
By one of the equivalent expressions for the Frobenius norm,
this is exactly
\|(I-S)(I-S^{\prime})^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}
,
and the claimed result follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2
.
In view of Lemma
1
,
the second statement follows trivially from the first.

For any
G\in{\cal G}_{d}
,

(I-G)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}G^{k}
,
since
G^{k}=0
for
k\geq d
because
G

is a DAG.
Therefore,
(I-S)(I-G)^{-1}
only has integer entries, and hence the square
of its Frobenius norm is a nonnegative integer. Moreover, by the nonnegativity
of relative entropy and identifiability,
it has to be at least
d
. And since
P_{S}\neq P_{G}
by
identifiability,
it must be strictly greater than
d
, i.e., at least
d+1
.
Therefore, in order to establish the lemma, it suffices to show that, for any
G\in{\cal G}_{d}
,
there is an
S\neq G
such that:

\|(I-S)(I-G)^{-1}\|^{2}_{F}=d+1.

(29)

If
G
is the all-zero matrix, then taking
S
to be any
matrix with only one 1
immediately yields (
29
).
Suppose
G
is not all-zero.
Then we can always find a pair of nodes
s\neq t
such
that has
s\to t
is an edge
in
G
(i.e.,
G_{ts}=1
) and
s
has
no parents in
G
. Let
S\in{\cal G}_{d}
be the same as
G

but with the edge
s\to t
removed
(i.e., with
S_{ts}=0
),
and let
\Delta\in{\cal G}_{d}
consist of only that one edge,
so that
G=S+\Delta
. Then,

\displaystyle(I-S)(I-G)^{-1}=(I-G+\Delta)(I-G)^{-1}

\displaystyle=I+\Delta(I-G)^{-1}=I+\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\Delta G^{k}.

Since the only nonzero entry of
\Delta
is
\Delta_{ts}=1
,
the product
\Delta G^{k}
is all zero except
for the
t
th row, which is

((G^{k})_{s1},\ldots,(G^{k})_{sd})
. But since
s
has
no parents in
G
, this entire
row must be zero for
k\geq 1
. Therefore, the only nonzero term in the sum
above is
k=0
, thus,

(I-S)(I-G)^{-1}=I+\Delta,

which implies (
29
)
and completes the proof.
∎

We begin by recalling the

Lemma 6
.

Let
X
be a
d
-dimensional random
vector with zero mean and positive definite
covariance
\mathbb{E}[XX^{\intercal}]
.
If
Y
is an arbitrary random variable
with finite variance, then,

\displaystyle\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\big(Y-\beta^{\intercal}X\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})-\mathbb{E}[YX^{\intercal}]\mathbb{E}[XX^{T}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[YX],

and the minimum is achieved by:

\beta^{*}=\mathbb{E}[XX^{T}]^{-1}\mathbb{E}[YX].

Let
\|B\|_{\rm op}
denote the
L^{2}
operator norm,
or spectral norm, of a matrix
B
.
The following is a simple bound on the operator
norm of matrix inverses.

Lemma 7
.

Let
Q
be an arbitrary square matrix with

\|Q\|_{\rm op}<1
. If
I-Q
is invertible, then

\displaystyle\|(I-Q)^{-1}\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\|Q\|_{\rm op}},

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad\|(I-Q)^{-1}-I\|_{\rm op}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|Q\|_{\rm op}}{1-\|Q\|_{\rm op}}.

(30)

Proof.
Since
\|Q\|_{\rm op}<1
, the series for
(I-Q)^{-1}

converges,

(I-Q)^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}Q^{i}.

For fixed
m\geq 1
,

\Bigg\|\sum_{i=0}^{m}Q^{i}\Bigg\|_{\rm op}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m}\|Q^{i}\|_{\rm op}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m}\|Q\|^{i}_{\rm op}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\|Q\|^{i}_{\rm op}

and letting
m\to\infty
gives the first bound.
For the second bound, we write,

(I-Q)^{-1}-I=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}Q^{i}=Q\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}Q^{i}=Q(I-Q)^{-1},

so that,

\|(I-Q)^{-1}-I\|_{\rm op}=\|Q(I-Q)^{-1}\|_{\rm op}\leq\frac{\|Q\|_{\rm op}}{1-\|Q\|_{\rm op}},

by the submultiplicativity of the operator norm.
∎
