Improving Recommendation Fairness without Sensitive Attributes Using Multi-Persona LLMs
Haoran Xin, Ying Sun, Chao Wang, Yanke Yu, Weijia Zhang, Hui Xiong, Technology (Guangzhou), School of Artificial Intelligence, Data ScienceUniversity of Science and Technology of China {hxin883, wzhang411}@connect.hkust-gz.edu.cn, yings@hkust-gz.edu.cn

Abstract
Despite the success of recommender systems in alleviating information overload, fairness issues have raised concerns in recent years, potentially leading to unequal treatment for certain user groups. While efforts have been made to improve recommendation fairness, they often assume that users’ sensitive attributes are available during model training. However, collecting sensitive information can be difficult, especially on platforms that involve no personal information disclosure. Therefore, we aim to improve recommendation fairness without any access to sensitive attributes. This is a non-trivial task because uncovering latent sensitive patterns from complicated user behaviors without explicit sensitive attributes can be difficult. Consequently, suboptimal estimates of sensitive distributions can hinder the fairness training process. To address these challenges, leveraging the remarkable reasoning abilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), we propose a novel LLM -enhanced framework for F air recommendation with O ut S ensitive A ttributes (LLMFOSA). A Multi-Persona Sensitive Information Inference module employs LLMs with distinct personas that mimic diverse human perceptions to infer and distill sensitive information. Furthermore, a Confusion-Aware Sensitive Representation Learning module incorporates inference results and rationales to develop robust sensitive representations, considering the mislabeling confusion and collective consensus among agents. The model is then optimized by a formulated mutual information objective. Extensive experiments on two public datasets validate the effectiveness of LLMFOSA in improving fairness.

Introduction

In the era of information explosion, recommender systems (RSs) play a crucial role in people’s daily lives, helping users discover content they might enjoy from vast amounts of information.
Despite their significant success, concerns about fairness in RSs have grown in recent years
[
37
,
23
,
46
,
40
]
.

The unfairness primarily stems from training on data that implicitly contains biased behaviors correlated with sensitive information (
e.g.,
gender, race),
even if such information is not explicitly available.

Unfairness can lead to unequal treatment for certain user groups, potentially harming user experience and the platform’s overall interests.
For instance, on job recruitment platforms, female users might receive fewer recommendations for high-paying jobs compared to their male counterparts
[
22
]
.

Extensive research efforts have been made to improve fairness in RSs.
Some works
[
42
,
1
]
directly employed fairness metrics as regularization terms to balance recommendations among different user groups.
Another line of research
[
4
,
45
,
40
,
45
,
11
]
applied adversarial learning to train filters to remove hidden sensitive information.

Recently, several studies
[
48
,
46
,
28
,
9
]
utilized mutual information (MI) to reduce the dependencies between sensitive and pure preference representations.

While most of these works assume that users’ sensitive attributes are readily available during model training, a subset of studied
[
11
,
45
]
has highlighted the limitations of this assumption
[
45
]
and explored achieving fairness with only limited sensitive information.

However, in practice, such sensitive information can be entirely unavailable.
For example, music streaming services like Spotify
[
19
]
usually only gather data that directly enhances user experience, such as musical preference, rather than personal attributes like gender, age or race.
Additionally, on completely anonymous platforms, such as Hush
1
1

1

https://hush.ac/
, no personal information that could identify users in real life is collected at all.
Unfortunatly, training fair recommendation models without any guidance of sensitive attributes poses significant challenges.
Firstly,

uncovering underlying sensitive patterns from complex user behaviors is difficult without labeled sensitive attributes.

In their absence, such patterns must be inferred indirectly through correlations between behaviors and the hidden sensitive structure in the data.
For example, recent studies
[
43
]
leverage training gradients as proxies for these correlations, assuming that larger gradients reflect more stereotypical and potentially unfair patterns.
However, due to the inherently diverse and multifaceted nature of user behaviors, these simple correlations tend to be supurious and unreliable.
Secondly,

estimated sensitive patterns often carry noise and inaccuracies, which can hinder the fairness training process.

As the true sensitive patterns are hard to uncover, the approximations may misrepresent the underlying structure, causing models to learn from suboptimal signals and ultimately degrading fairness performance.

Recently, large language models (LLMs) trained on extensive data have demonstrated impressive world knowledge and excelled in various tasks
[
26
,
33
,
27
]
.
Since their training corpus often contains user profiles and preferences, they could inherently enable an understanding of sensitive information beyond the implicit correlations within the data.
Therefore, we propose an
LLM
-enhanced framework for
F
air recommendation with
O
ut
S
ensitive
A
ttributes, named LLMFOSA.
Specifically, we first propose a
Multi-Persona Sensitive Information Inference
module to identify underlying sensitive patterns.
Given the complex nature of user behaviors, we employ multiple LLM-powered annotators with distinct personas to mirror diverse aspects of human understanding and infer sensitive attributes.
These multifaceted inferences enable a comprehensive analysis of complex user behaviors, improving inference precision while mitigating individual biases.
A meta summarizer further highlights the rationales behind the inference, enhancing sensitive information discovery.
To address potential inaccuracies in the inference, we devise a
Confusion-Aware Sensitive Representation Learning
module that refines robust sensitive representations from the inference.
This module captures each annotator’s mislabeling confusion and their collective consensus to correct sensitive distributions under noisy inferences.
Inference rationales are further incorporated to enhance the sensitive representation learning.
Finally, the model is optimized in a two-stage manner by the formulated MI optimization objective.
The contributions of this work are highlighted as follows:

•

We propose an LLM-enhanced framework to effectively improve recommendation fairness without sensitive attributes, tackling a practical scenario where no personal profile is disclosed.

•

We devise a Multi-Persona Sensitive Information Inference module to uncover latent sensitive patterns without explicit labels. This module employs multi-persona LLMs to mimic diverse human perceptions, enabling a comprehensive and unbiased inference of sensitive information.

•

We introduce a Confusion-Aware Sensitive Representation Learning module to develop robust sensitive representations under noisy inferences by considering annotators’ mislabeling confusion, their collective consensus, and incorporating fine-grained inference rationales.

•

We conduct extensive experiments on two public real-world datasets to validate LLMFOSA’s superiority in improving recommendation fairness without sensitive attributes.

Preliminaries

2.1
Problem Definition

We denote the user set and item set as
\mathcal{U}
(
|\mathcal{U}|=M
) and
\mathcal{V}
(
|\mathcal{V}|=N
), respectively.
Let
\mathcal{R}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{U}|\times|\mathcal{V}|}
represent the user-item interaction matrix, with entries denoted by
r_{uv}
.
In this study, we focus on the implicit feedback scenario, where
r_{uv}=1
indicates that the user
u\in\mathcal{U}
has an observed interaction with item
v\in\mathcal{V}
, and
r_{uv}=0
otherwise.
We denote the set of historical items that user
u
has interacted with as
h(u)
.
The core of modern RSs is to learn representative embeddings for users and items, defined as:

\bm{U},\bm{V}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{R})=[\bm{u}_{1},\bm{u}_{2},\ldots,\bm{u}_{M}],[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}_{N}]
,
where
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
is a collborative encoder.
Subsequently, the preference prediction
\hat{r}_{uv}
of a user
u
over an item
v
can be obtained based on the learned embeddings (
e.g.,

\hat{r}_{uv}=\bm{u}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{v}
in matrix factorization models).
Frequently used notations are summarized in
Table

3
.

When addressing fairness in recommendations, the goal is to ensure that
the items recommended based on the user embedding
\bm{u}
are independent of the user’s sensitive attributes.

Specifically, our study aims to improve recommendation fairness without relying on any sensitive attributes.
Formally, let
a_{u}
represent the sensitive attribute of user
u
, taking values from the sensitive attribute set
\mathcal{A}
,
i.e.,

a_{u}\in\mathcal{A}
. We state our problem as follows:

Problem 1
(Fair Recommendation)

Given the user set
\mathcal{U}
, item set
\mathcal{V}
and the interaction matrix
\mathcal{R}
, train a fair recommender
\mathcal{F}
without knowing the sensitive attribute
a_{u}
for any user
\forall u\in\mathcal{U}
. The recommender
\mathcal{F}
should deliver

fair recommendations which are independent of the user’s unobservable sensitive attribute
a_{u}
.

2.2
Mutual Information Formulation

Inspired by the success of recent MI-based fairness studies
[
46
,
48
,
36
,
9
]
, we propose to achieve recommendation fairness by formulating it as an MI optimization problem, which allows for precise control over the mutual influence of information.
Following
[
46
]
, for each user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, the original user embedding
\bm{u}
is first disentangled into the
sensitive-aware
embedding
\bm{s}_{u}
and the
sensitive-blind
embedding
\bm{p}_{u}
.
The sensitive-aware embedding
\bm{s}_{u}
contains sufficient sensitive information that could lead to unfair recommendations, while the sensitive-blind embedding
\bm{p}_{u}
is designed to be independent of sensitive information, making it suitable for fair recommendations.
For notational convenience, we denote
S
as the stochastic variable that takes values
\{\bm{s}_{u}|u\in\mathcal{U}\}
, and
P
as the stochastic variable that takes values
\{\bm{p}_{u}|u\in\mathcal{U}\}
.
Moreover, we let
A
and
R
be the stochastic variables representing sensitive attributes and user-item interactions, respectively.
Formally, the MI optimization problem proposed in this work is given by:

\min_{S,P}{-I(S;A)+I(S;P)-I(P;R|S)}.

(1)

Specifically, maximizing
I(S;A)
encourages the sensitive-aware embeddings to retain as much sensitive information as possible.
Minimizing
I(S;P)
ensures that the sensitive-aware embeddings contain information distinguishable from that in sensitive-blind embeddings.
Moreover, maximizing the conditional MI
I(P;R|S)
promotes the learning of collaborative knowledge in sensitive-blind embeddings while avoiding contamination by sensitive information.
In the following sections, We will elaborate on how to effectively estimate these MI terms, particularly
I(S;A)
, given the absence of sensitive attributes during training in our problem setting.

Methodology

Our proposed framework illustrated in
Figure

1
aims to uncover latent sensitive patterns and leverage them to facilitate fairness training when explicit sensitive attributes are unavailable.
Given the complexity of user behaviors, the
Multi-Persona Sensitive Information Inference
(
Section

3.1
) module employs LLM-powered agents to mimic diverse human perceptions, providing a comprehensive analysis of users’ sensitive information.
Since these potentially flawed inferences may lead to suboptimal sensitive distributions, the
Confusion-Aware Sensitive Representation Learning
module (
Section

3.2
) further incorporates inference rationales and models the agents’ confusion and consensus, similar to human reasoning, to develop robust sensitive representations for fairness training.
The fairness is ultimately achieved through the model trained with the formulated MI objectives (
Section

3.3
).
The dicussion on our framework’s applicability is provided in
Appendix

G
.

3.1
Multi-Persona Sensitive Information Inference

Given the absence of sensitive information during training, we leverage LLMs with their reasoning abilities to infer the missing sensitive information.
We first create a
persona editor
agent to characterize several
annotator
agents, each with distinct personas.
These annotators work concurrently to infer users’ sensitive attributes, providing a comprehensive analysis of user profiles.
To further enhance sensitive pattern discovery by deepening the understanding of these inferences, a
meta summarizer
agent is employed to distill, highlight and provide detailed rationales for the inferences.

Persona Editor.

Individual perceptions of subjective inferences vary significantly due to diverse backgrounds and experiences.
For instance, when identifying gender from ambiguous user profiles, an anthropologist might prioritize cultural cues, while a sociologist may focus on societal roles.
This diversity in perspectives yields varied interpretations and conclusions about the same subject.
A broad spectrum of viewpoints can fully unveil the subject and counteracts the bias that any single viewpoint might introduce.
To harness this diversity, we design the persona editor, an agent who generates a variety of personas, each reflecting unique background knowledge and interpretative biases.
These diverse personas enable more comprehensive and unbiased inferences of sensitive information.

Specifically, we employ a prompting method that enables LLMs to act as a persona editor, generating a diverse set of personas covering various background aspects:
\mathcal{O}=\left\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{N_{o}}\right\}=\texttt{LLM}\left(T^{pe}(N_{o})\right),

where
\mathcal{O}=\left\{o_{1},o_{2},\ldots,o_{N_{o}}\right\}
is the generated personas,
N_{o}
is the number of personas, and
T^{pe}(\cdot)
is the prompt template for persona editor which can be found in
Section

A.1
.

Annotators.

Using the generated personas, we develop a set of annotator agents tasked with inferring sensitive attributes by analyzing users’ historical behaviors.
Formally, given an annotator with persona
o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
and the historical behaviors
h(u)
of user
u\in\mathcal{U}
, the sensitivite attribute inference is given by
\tilde{c}^{i}_{u}=\texttt{LLM}(T^{inf}(o_{i},h(u))),

where
T^{inf}(\cdot)
is the prompt template for each annotator which can be found in
Section

A.2
.
Typically, the response
\tilde{c}_{u}^{i}
from an annotator includes their inferred attribute and a brief reason for their judgment.
To standardize these outputs, we employ a verbalizer to convert the textual response
\tilde{c}_{u}^{i}
into a categorial label
\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,|\mathcal{A}|-1\right\}
within the attribute space.

Meta Summarizer.

Beyond the sensitive attributes inferred by the annotators, the fine-grained rationales behind each annotator’s decision-making process are crucial for understanding the users’ sensitive information.
To thoroughly capture and distill these detailed rationales, we introduce a meta summarizer agent.
This agent analyzes the inference processes of each annotator, aggregating their insights to highlight key aspects of the decision-making process.
The meta summarizer provides a coherent summary that reveals the rationale behind the determination of a user’s sensitive attributes.

Moreover, these human-readable summaries also enhance the interpretability of our framework.

Specifically, given a user
u\in\mathcal{U}
with the historical interactions
h(u)
and the sensitive attribute inference
\tilde{c}_{u}^{i}
from each annotator
\forall o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
, the meta summarizer performs as
e_{u}=\texttt{LLM}\left(T^{sum}\left(h(u),\tilde{c}_{u}^{1},\tilde{c}_{u}^{2},\ldots,\tilde{c}_{u}^{N_{o}}\right)\right),

where
T^{sum}(\cdot)
is the prompt for the meta summarizer presented in
Section

A.3
.
Annotator and meta summarizer’s response examples are in
Appendix

H
.

3.2
Confusion-Aware Sensitive Representation Learning

Due to the hallucination issue
[
44
]
with LLMs, the inferred sensitive information may be potentially inaccurate and compromise the fairness training.
To counter this, we now introduce how to utilize these inferences to develop robust and effective sensitive-aware embeddings.
Recall that we have obtained user and item embeddings
\bm{u},\bm{v}
from the collaborative encoder
\mathcal{E}(\cdot)
.
For each user embedding
\bm{u}
, we employ a sensitive encoder
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
to transform it into the sensitive-aware embedding
\bm{s}_{u}
as:

\bm{s}_{u}=\mathcal{S}\left(\bm{u}\right),u\in\mathcal{U},

where
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is implemented as a multi-layer perceptron (MLP).
As shown in
Equation

1
, we will learn the sensitive-aware embeddings by maximizing the MI
I(S;A)
.

Confusion Modeling.

While diverse annotator personas offer rich insights into users’ sensitive attributes,
they can also cause variations in sensitive attribute inferences, potentially leading to inconsistencies in the sensitive representation learning process.
Therefore, we design a learnable confusion matrix for each annotator to model confusion probabilities and correct the MI estimation, ultimately learning robust representations.
In our case, the actual sensitive attribute
a_{u}
of the user is missing, making direct computation impractical.
We use the annotators’ inferred sensitive attributes to introduce
\widetilde{A}
, an approximation of
A
, which takes values in the inferred sensitive attribute labels.
Specifically, we maximize
I(S;A)
with the approximation
\widetilde{A}
by maximizing the lower bound:

\max_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{s}_{u},\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})\sim p(S,\widetilde{A})}\left[\log q(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u};\mathcal{C})\right],

(2)

where
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
is the classifier used to classify the sensitive label based on the sensitive-aware embedding, also implemented as an MLP. The derivation is provided in
Section

F.1
.

To introduce the confusion modeling, we first adopt the common assumption
[
12
,
34
]
that each inference is independent of the user’s profile given the ground-truth sensitive attribute.
Based on these assumptions, we formally define a confusion matrix
\bm{F}_{i}
for each annotator
i
(
i=1,2,\ldots,N_{o}
), which models the probabilities of the user’s true sensitive attribute being misclassified as the annotator’s inferred sensitive attribute:

(\bm{F}_{i})_{jk}\triangleq q(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}=k|a_{u}=j;\bm{F}_{i}),\forall u\in\mathcal{U}.

(3)

Using the confusion matrices in
Equation

3
, we can further decompose
Equation

2
into:

\max_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\bm{F}}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{s}_{u},\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})\sim p(S,\widetilde{A})}\left[\log q(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|a_{u};\bm{F}_{i})q(a_{u}|\bm{s}_{u};\mathcal{C})\right].

(4)

Finally, we derive the loss function that maximizes the objective in
Equation

4
to learn robust sensitive-aware embeddings:

\mathcal{L}_{cls}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{o}}\left[-\texttt{OneHot}(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})\cdot\log\left(\bm{F}_{i}\cdot\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}(\bm{u}))\right)\right],

(5)

Consensus Regularization.

Individuals with similar personalities often exhibit comparable cognitive patterns.
Based on this premise, we assume that annotators with similar personas are likely to experience similar levels of confusion.
To foster consensus among annotators, we introduce a concise similarity regularization term to align the confusion matrices of annotators with similar personas.
Specifically, we first define the annotator similarity for each annotator persona
o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
as:

\Gamma(o_{i})=\left\{o_{j}|d(o_{i},o_{j})\leq d(o_{i},\bar{o}_{i,K})\right\},\quad d(o_{i},o_{j})=\|\texttt{TxtEmb}(o_{i})-\texttt{TxtEmb}(o_{j})\|_{2},

(6)

where
\texttt{TxtEmb}(\cdot)
is a text embedding model,
\bar{o}_{i,K}
denotes the
K
-th nearest persona to
o_{i}
, with
K
being a hyperparameter.
The regularization term based on the annotator similarity is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{sim}=\sum_{o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}}\sum_{o_{j}\in\Gamma(o_{i})}\|\bm{F}_{i}-\bm{F}_{j}\|_{2}.

(7)

Fine-Grained Rationale Incorporation.

The decision-making rationales of annotator agents are crucial for sensitive pattern discovery, as they provide key insights into sensitive attribute inference.
However, the direct incorporation of text-based rationales poses challenges due to their heterogeneous nature.
To address this, we first utilize a text embedding model to convert the textual rationales into low-dimensional, semantic embeddings:

\bm{e}_{u}=\texttt{TxtEmb}(e_{u}),

where
e_{u}
is the rationale concluded by the meta summarizer, as described in
Section

3.1
.
These encoded representations serve as proxies for the fine-grained rationales underlying sensitive information inference.
We propose maximizing the MI between sensitive and rationale representations, thereby optimizing the integration of rationale knowledge into the sensitive-aware representation.
Formally, let
E
be the variable taking values
\{\bm{e}_{u}|u\in\mathcal{U}\}
.
For effective optimization
[
29
]
, we employ the lower bound of
I(S;E)
and apply contrastive learning methods to develop sensitive-aware embeddings. The lower bound is given by:

I(S;E)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{\texttt{DR}(\bm{s}_{u},\bm{e}_{u})}{\sum_{\bm{s}\sim p(S)}\texttt{DR}(\bm{s},\bm{e}_{u})}\right],

(8)

where
\texttt{DR}(\cdot)
is the density ratio implemented via inner product.
This approach allows us to maximizie
I(S;E)
by optimizing the sensitive-aware embedding
\bm{s}_{u}
using the following contrastive loss:

\mathcal{L}_{fine}=-\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\log\frac{\exp(\bm{s}_{u}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{e}_{u})}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{U}}\exp(\bm{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{e}_{u})}.

(9)

After defining the individual loss functions, we construct a composite loss function to simultaneously learn the sensitive-aware embeddings as:

\mathcal{L}_{sen}=\mathcal{L}_{cls}+\mathcal{L}_{bpr}+\lambda_{sim}\mathcal{L}_{sim}+\lambda_{fine}\mathcal{L}_{fine},

(10)

where
\lambda_{fine},\lambda_{sim}
are two hyperparameters, and
\mathcal{L}_{bpr}
is the Bayesian personalized ranking (BPR)
[
32
]
loss, which trains the collaborative encoder as:

\mathcal{L}_{bpr}=-\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{v\in h(u)}\log\left(\sigma\left(\bm{u}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{v}-\bm{u}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{v}_{-}\right)\right),

(11)

where
\sigma
is the activation function and
v_{-}
is a random negative item for each positive item
v\in h(u)
.

3.3
Model Optimization

As outlined in
Equation

1
, the model optimization objective comprises MI terms including
I(S;A)
,
I(S;P)
and
I(P;R|S)
.

Section

3.2
has introduced a novel approach for optimizing
I(S;A)
.
This section will describe how to estimate the remaining MI terms and present the overall training process.

For each user embedding
\bm{u}
, we apply a preference encoder
\mathcal{P}(\cdot)
implemented as an MLP to generate the sensitive-blind embedding

\bm{p}_{u}=\mathcal{P}(\bm{u}),u\in\mathcal{U}.

Then we optimize the following MI terms.

Minimizing
I(S;P)
.

Following
[
46
]
, we employ the contrastive log-ratio upper bound
[
8
]
of MI to derive the following loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{ub}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left[\log\mathcal{B}(\bm{p}_{u})-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}\log\mathcal{B}(\bm{p}_{u^{\prime}})\right],

(12)

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
is a neural network implemented as an MLP.
The derivation can be found in
Section

F.2
.

Maximizing
I(P;R|S)
.

We first use a pre-trained collaborative filtering (CF) model to obtain the embedding
\bm{r}_{u}
, representing variable
R
of interactions.
We apply conditional InfoNCE
[
14
]
to maximize the lower bound of conditional MI and derive the following loss function:

\mathcal{L}_{lb}=-\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left[\log\frac{\exp\left({\langle\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u}+\alpha\bm{s}_{u}\rangle}\right)}{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}\exp\left({\langle\bm{r}_{u^{\prime}},\bm{p}_{u}+\alpha\bm{s}_{u}\rangle}\right)}\right],

(13)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes cosine similarity and
\alpha
is a parameter. The derivations can be found in
Section

F.3
.

Finally, we employ a two-stage approach to train the LLMFOSA framework.
In the
first
stage, we use the loss function
\mathcal{L}_{sen}
in
Equation

10
to maximize
I(S;A)
.
Once the sensitive-aware embedding
\bm{s}_{u}
captures sufficient sensitive information, we proceed to the
second
stage, using the following loss function to jointly optimize
I(S;P)
and
I(P;R|S)
in an information bottleneck manner
[
35
]
:

\mathcal{L}_{b}=\mathcal{L}_{bpr}+\lambda_{ub}\mathcal{L}_{ub}+\lambda_{lb}\mathcal{L}_{lb},

(14)

where
\mathcal{L}_{bpr}
, defined in
Equation

11
, helps in learning representative user embeddings, and
\lambda_{ub}
and
\lambda_{lb}
are hyperparameters.
Upon optimal training of LLMFOSA, the learned sensitive-blind embedding
\bm{p}_{u}
is used to recommend items to users to achieve fairness.

4.2
Overall Performance

We conducted experiments on the ML-1M and LFM-360K datasets to evaluate the effectiveness of our LLMFOSA framework in improving recommendation fairness.
The overall performance of LLMFOSA, compared to baseline models, is presented in
Table

1
, where the mean results are derived from three random runs.
The NoFair refers to a naive recommendation backbone without any fairness considerations.
Particularly, we observed several key findings:

•

LLMFOSA outperforms all baselines in fairness, confirming its effectiveness even without sensitive information.

In contrast, baseline methods that infer sensitive attributes relying to the varing degrees on complex user behaviors without any semantic cues, experience a significant decline in fairness, especially when behavior poorly correlates with sensitive attributes.

•

FairGNN and FairLISA perform relatively well in fairness by partially relying on sensitive attributes, making them less prone to error from imprecise inferences. However, this reduced dependence compromises recommendation accuracy. Moreover, these methods still require some sensitive data, limiting their practical applicability.
In contrast, AFSR operates without sensitive attributes and achieves promising fairness outcomes. Nevertheless, it suffers from a high accuracy drop, as its approach of smoothing item embeddings can distort the original data semantics.

•

The naive NoFair method surprisingly shows competitive fairness compared to some baselines that explicitly address fairness.
This is possibly because fairness-aware models alter embeddings based on inferred sensitive labels. If these labels are inaccurate, such adjustments can misfire.

•

LLMFOSA shows a modest drop in accuracy compared to some fairness baselines.
This is likely because achieving fairness with relatively precise sensitive information in our framework can compromise more recommendation performance.
Nonetheless, LLMFOSA achieves a significant fairness boost (up to 13.3%) in scenarios where sensitive information is completely absent.

Related Works

Recommendation Fairness.

Prior studies
[
42
,
1
]
improved fairness by incorporating group fairness metrics as regularizers across user groups.
Another line of research
[
4
,
45
,
40
]
formulated the problem as a min-max game, using filters and discriminators to eliminate and identify sensitive information.
Recently, some works
[
48
,
46
,
28
,
9
]
also investigated fairness from an MI perspective.
As sensitive information is not always easily collectible in practice, a few works have addressed fairness with limited
[
11
,
45
]
or no
[
43
]
sensitive attributes.
However, these works either still rely on available sensitive information or unreliable correlations between behaviors and sensitive patterns.
In contrast, this work improves fairness without sensitive attributes by leveraging external knowledge from LLMs.

LLMs for Recommendation.

The rapid progress of LLMs has sparked interest in applying them to RSs.
Some works
[
39
,
31
]
treated LLMs as knowledge bases, retrieving useful information to support conventional recommendation models.
To better leverage training data, numerous works
[
3
,
25
,
38
,
24
,
49
]
have fine-tuned LLMs for recommendation tasks, though this increases computational overhead.

Instead of using LLMs as the core engine, we leverage LLM’s reasoning power to develop agents that infer users’ sensitive information, thereby enhancing recommendation fairness.

Fairness without Demographics.

Several works
[
17
,
2
,
47
,
41
,
6
]
have achieved fairness without demographics due to regulatory restrictions.
They generally follow Rawlsian Max-Min fairness
[
30
]
to minimize the risk for the group with the highest risk.
However, these methods depends on unreliable correlations between protected groups and observable features.
Additionally, they mainly focus on general machine learning tasks and may overlook the complexities of user behaviors in RSs.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we improved recommendation fairness without sensitive attributes during training.
We leveraged the capabilities of LLMs to uncover latent sensitive patterns, utilizing them to facilitate fairness training.
Inspired by human reasoning, we employed multi-persona LLMs to mimic diverse human perceptions for sensitive information inference.
We further considered the confusion and consensus among human-like agents to better leverage these inferences.
Comprehensive experiments confirmed the effectiveness of our proposed framework in improving recommendation fairness.

Appendix F
Bound Derivations

F.1
Maximizing
I(S;A)

Recall that we use the annotators’ inferred sensitive attributes to introduce
\widetilde{A}
, an approximation of
A
, which takes values in the inferred sensitive attribute labels. Then, based on the definition of MI, we have:

I(S;A)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{s}_{u},\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})\sim p(S,\widetilde{A})}\left[\log{\frac{p(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u})}{p(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})}}\right],

(15)

where
\tilde{a}^{i}_{u}
is the inferred sensitive attribute of user
u
by annotator agent
i
.
As
p(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u})
is intractable, we introduce the variational distribution
q(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u})
:

{I}(S;A)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{s}_{u},\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})\sim p(S,\widetilde{A})}\left[\log\frac{q_{(}\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u})}{p(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}\sim p(\widetilde{A})}\left[\mathrm{KL}\left(p(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u})\|q(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u})\right)\right].

(16)

Based on
Equation

16
, we maximize
I(S;A)
by maximizing the following lower bound:

\max_{\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C}}\mathbb{E}_{(\bm{s}_{u},\tilde{a}_{u}^{i})\sim p(S,\widetilde{A})}\left[\log q(\tilde{a}_{u}^{i}|\bm{s}_{u};\mathcal{C})\right],

(17)

where
\mathcal{C}(\cdot)
is the classifier used to classify the sensitive label based on the sensitive-aware embedding, also implemented as an MLP.

F.2
Minimizing
I(S;P)

We derive an upper bound of
I(S;P)
so as to effectively minimize the MI. We start by the proposition as follows:

Proposition 1

Given
\bm{s}\sim p(\bm{s}_{u})
, if the conditional distribution
p(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})
is known, then the MI
I(S;P)
can be upper bounded by:

I(S;P)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\log p(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}\log p(\bm{s}_{u^{\prime}}|\bm{p}_{u})\right],

(18)

The proof can be found in
[
8
]
.
Afterwards, we utilize a variational distribution
q(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})
to approximate the conditional distribution
p(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})
by minimizing the KL divergence:

\min_{q(\cdot)}\text{KL}\left(p(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})\|q(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})\right).

(19)

To simplify the optimization, we assume that the variational distribution
q(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})
is a Gaussian distribution and the loss function (
Equation

12
) for the MI minimization can be derived:

\mathcal{L}_{ub}=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left[\log\mathcal{B}(\bm{p}_{u})-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}\log\mathcal{B}(\bm{p}_{u^{\prime}})\right],

(20)

where
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
is a neural network to parameterize the distribution
q(\bm{s}_{u}|\bm{p}_{u})
.

F.3
Maximizing
I(P;R|S)

We derive another lower bound of
I(P;R|S)
to maximize the MI. We first introduce the following proposition:

Proposition 2

Given
\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u}\sim p(\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u}),\bm{r}^{\prime}\sim p(\bm{r}_{u}|\bm{s}_{u})
and a score function
f(\cdot)
, the MI
I(P;R|S)
can be lower bounded by:

I(P;R|S)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\log\frac{\exp f(\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u})}{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}\exp f(\bm{r}_{u^{\prime}},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u})}\right],

(21)

The proof can be found in
[
14
]
. Then, the loss function for the MI maximization can be derived:

\mathcal{L}_{lb}=-\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\left[\log\frac{\exp f(\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u})}{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}}\exp f(\bm{r}_{u^{\prime}},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u})}\right].

(22)

For the implementation, we take the score function
f(\cdot)
as the weighted cosine similarity:
f(\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u},\bm{s}_{u})=\langle\bm{r}_{u},\bm{p}_{u}+\alpha\bm{s}_{u}\rangle
, where
\alpha
is a hyperparameter.

Appendix G
Dicsussion on Applicability

In our proposed LLMFOSA framework, the multi-persona inference phase involves only lightweight inference operations, without the need for heavy fine-tuning, and is executed prior to the fairness training stage. Since this phase can be performed offline, it does not interfere with the primary fairness training process or the real-time recommendation generation, which rely solely on a lightweight recommendation model. This design ensures that LLMFOSA remains a practical and feasible solution for real-world applications, even with its initial computational requirements.

Appendix I
Limitations and Broader Impact

Although the proposed LLMFOSA framework achieves promising fairness improvements, it has some limitations.
First, the sensitive inference depends on the capabilities of the selected LLMs, and choosing an appropriate model may require some effort.
Second, the confusion modeling is based on the common assumption that the confusion is independent of the user’s behaviors given the ground-truth sensitive attribute. In the future, we plan to explore the instance-dependent transition matrix to enhance the confusion modeling.

Recommender systems significantly influence users’ daily decisions.
In this paper, we discuss the social responsibilities of recommender systems and consider a situation where no sensitive attributes are available.
We contribute to the development of recommender systems that are both ethically sound and practically deployable in privacy-conscious environments.
