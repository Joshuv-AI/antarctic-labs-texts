Nutritional Support Needs of Cervical Cancer Patients Undergoing Chemoradiotherapy: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Xuejiao Wang, Fenshuang Song, Yiwen Lin, Aiyuan Wu, Ya Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore the nutritional support needs of cervical cancer patients undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) from the perspectives of both patients and healthcare professionals (HCPs).Patients and MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 13 cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT and 7 multidisciplinary HCPs (4 physicians, 3 nurses) from an oncology department in eastern China. Data were analyzed using conventional content analysis.ResultsThree main themes were identified: (1) multidimensional nutritional support needs, (2) cognitive biases regarding nutritional support management, and (3) barriers to achieving effective nutritional support. Patients reported unmet needs in managing treatment-related adverse symptoms, acquiring dietary knowledge, and accessing professional guidance after discharge. Cognitive biases—including limited awareness, insufficient knowledge for autonomous decision-making, exposure to conflicting information, and skepticism toward nutritional management—were evident among both patients and HCPs. Barriers included inconsistent professional advice, cultural dietary restrictions, and economic constraints.ConclusionCervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT have complex nutritional needs that are inadequately addressed due to cognitive, cultural, and systemic barriers. A multidisciplinary, culturally adapted nutritional support system integrating in-hospital and post-discharge care is needed.Keywords: cervical cancer, nutritional support, qualitative research, radiotherapy, chemotherapy

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the seventh most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide and poses a significant threat to women’s health.1 China bears a particularly high disease burden, with approximately 500,000 new cases and 250,000 deaths reported annually. For patients with locally advanced cervical cancer who are ineligible for surgery, concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard treatment.2 While CCRT improves local tumor control and survival, its non-specific cytotoxicity damages normal tissues and increases treatment-related toxicity, including malnutrition.3

Malnutrition is highly prevalent among patients with gynecological cancers.4,5 It contributes to muscle wasting impaired immune function, reduced treatment toleranceand diminished quality of life.6 The prevalence of malnutrition in cervical cancer patients has been reported to increase from 17.7% before CCRT to 47.1% by the end of treatment.7,8 Early nutritional intervention may improve clinical outcomes in this population.

Despite awareness of the importance of nutritional support, clinicians frequently report deficiencies in nutritional knowledge and training.9,10 Many oncologists lack systematic education in clinical nutrition, limiting their ability to assess nutritional status, formulate individualized interventions, and manage complex nutrition-related complications.10 Some prioritize anti-tumor treatment over nutritional care, underestimating the impact of malnutrition on treatment tolerance and quality of life.11

Research on nutritional support for cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT remains limited. Prior studies have predominantly examined either patients’ experiences or providers’ perspectives in isolation, leaving a gap in understanding how the interplay between these perspectives shapes nutritional care.12,13 Furthermore, the influence of multidisciplinary team dynamics, family-centered caregiving cultures, folk dietary beliefs, and healthcare resource constraints on nutritional decision-making in Chinese clinical settings has not been sufficiently explored. The present study addresses this gap by simultaneously examining the perspectives of both cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT and multidisciplinary HCPs—including gynecologic oncologists, nurses, and dietitians—to identify alignments and discrepancies in their experiences of nutritional support. A descriptive qualitative design was chosen to capture the contextualized, firsthand experiences of participants and to generate insights for developing culturally tailored nutritional support strategies in China.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative design to explore the perspectives and experiences of cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT and HCPs regarding nutritional support. A descriptive qualitative approach was selected to provide a rich, straightforward description of participants’ experiences in real clinical contexts without the highly interpretive abstraction characteristic of other qualitative traditions.14 Drawing on the philosophical underpinnings of Husserlian phenomenology—which emphasizes bracketing researchers’ preconceptions to present participants’ subjective experiences authentically15 —the study maintained a focus on describing lived experiences as articulated by participants themselves. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Supplement 1).16

Setting

This study was conducted in the Department of Gynecological Oncology at Wuxi Maternity and Child Health Hospital, the largest maternal and child health hospital in Wuxi City, Jiangsu Province. The center provides standardized, precision-based, and individualized comprehensive treatment of gynecological malignancies, ranging from early screening and diagnosis to surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and rehabilitation, supported by an advanced tumor pathology diagnostic platform, precision radiotherapy equipment, and a multidisciplinary collaborative tumor support treatment team.

Participants and Recruitment

Purposive sampling with maximum variation was employed to recruit participants between October and December 2025.17 To enhance sample diversity, maximum variation was sought across age, education level, and duration of home rehabilitation for patients, and across professional roles and years of experience for HCPs. Cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT and HCPs from the same department were approached for participation during the study period.

Patient inclusion criteria were: (1) stage IIB–IVA cervical cancer confirmed by imaging and pathology; (2) scheduled for CCRT; (3) aged 20–75 years; (4) adequate communication ability; and (5) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe cardiac, cerebral, hepatic, or renal dysfunction; (2) history of substance dependence; (3) severe immunodeficiency; (4) other malignant tumors; and (5) prior pelvic radiotherapy.

HCP inclusion criteria were: (1) ≥5 years of work experience in gynecologic oncology; (2) awareness of the study and willingness to participate; (3) fluent verbal communication; and (4) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-regular staff (trainees, rotations, interns); and (2) absence due to sick or maternity leave.

Materials

Sociodemographic data for patients (age, marital status, education level, employment status, residence, number of children) were obtained from hospital records. For HCPs, data (gender, age, marital status, education level, occupation, years of work experience, department) were collected from departmental records.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive literature review (Supplement 2). For patients, the guide explored changes in nutritional behaviors before and after diagnosis, difficulties in nutritional intake during CCRT, sources of nutritional support, expectations regarding nutritional care, and preferences for receiving guidance. For HCPs, the guide focused on perceptions of the significance of nutritional management, commonly observed nutritional needs and problems, current practices, and recommendations for optimizing nutritional support. The guide was pilot-tested with two patients and two HCPs to ensure clarity and applicability.

Data Collection

Face-to-face interviews were conducted in Chinese between October and December 2025 by two trained female researchers: a postgraduate nursing student and a head nurse (bachelor’s degree, 20 years of clinical experience), both trained in qualitative research. Prior to each interview, the researchers introduced themselves, explained the study’s purpose, methods, and significance, and assured participants of confidentiality. After obtaining consent and establishing rapport, formal interviews commenced in private rooms within patient wards or outpatient clinics. Each interview lasted approximately 30 minutes.

The researchers employed active listening, probing, paraphrasing, and clarifying techniques to verify key information. Question order was flexibly adjusted based on conversation flow. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached. After each interview, a debriefing session was conducted. All invited participants agreed to participate; none withdrew.

Data Analysis

Conventional content analysis was used to analyze the interview data.14 Two researchers independently listened to the recordings multiple times and transcribed them verbatim within 24 hours. They independently read and re-read the transcripts to achieve immersion, highlighted significant statements, and generated initial codes. Through iterative comparison and discussion, codes were grouped into categories, which were further refined into themes and subthemes. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The final thematic structure was reviewed and confirmed by all members of the research team.

Rigor and Reflexivity

Several strategies were employed to enhance the trustworthiness of the findings. Both interviewers maintained reflective journals throughout the data collection and analysis process, documenting their personal assumptions, emotional responses, and potential influences on data interpretation. Regular debriefing sessions were held among the research team to critically examine how researchers’ backgrounds and preconceptions might shape the analysis. An audit trail was maintained, documenting key analytical decisions. Member checking was conducted by returning summarized findings to a subset of participants (three patients, two HCPs) to verify that interpretations accurately reflected their experiences. Prolonged engagement in the clinical setting and the inclusion of both patient and HCP perspectives served as forms of triangulation.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuxi Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. YLSL2025-0430-040). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. This consent included permission for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotes in academic journals. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing all transcripts; only the research team had access to the data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Nutritional support needs of patients undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy for cervical cancer.

Theme 1: Multidimensional Nutritional Support Needs

Needs for Managing Adverse Symptoms

Patients reported a range of treatment-related physical discomforts—including decreased appetite, nausea, vomiting, constipation, and diarrhea—and expressed feeling overwhelmed and unsure how to manage them effectively.

P6: Sometimes I feel a bloated sensation in my stomach; I just feel uncomfortable and do not know how to handle it.

P10: It’s been about two weeks since my discharge, and I still do not have much appetite. Just seeing the food makes me feel bad, and I have not had a proper bowel movement in many days.

P11: I have been having diarrhea for the past few days, going to the bathroom seven or eight times a day, and it’s mostly watery. I am afraid to eat anything.

H5 (Nurse): Many patients feel helpless when facing the adverse symptoms that arise after chemotherapy.

These accounts illustrate that symptom-related distress was compounded by a lack of accessible strategies for self-management, leaving patients in a state of uncertainty.

Nutritional Knowledge Needs

Patients demonstrated limited understanding of appropriate food selection, preparation methods, and dietary modifications suited to their treatment phase.

P2: It’s been several months since my surgery. I have gradually adapted to eating, but I am not sure if I can still eat the foods I used to enjoy.

P4: For example, seafood and beef or lamb are very nutritious. Can I eat those? Is it something I cannot eat now, or will I be unable to eat them in the future as well?

H6 (Physician): Last time, a patient asked me if he could eat chocolate because his family did not allow him to.

This knowledge gap was not limited to patients; HCPs acknowledged that the guidance they provided was often insufficiently specific.

Needs for External Consultation Pathways

Patients expressed a strong desire for accessible channels to consult healthcare professionals after discharge, reporting that the absence of such pathways left them without timely support.

P3: It would be best to have it explained through videos or face-to-face; otherwise, when issues arise, I really will not know what to do.

P8: I hope that after being discharged, I can communicate with my doctor in a timely manner to receive relevant guidance.

P13: I wish there was a place for dialogue between patients and doctors or nurses. Otherwise, after we are discharged, if we have questions, we cannot find anyone to consult. Who can trust the information found online? We still need to rely on the hospital.

Theme 2: Cognitive Bias Regarding Nutritional Support Management

Lack of Awareness

Both patients and HCPs demonstrated gaps in recognizing the importance of systematic nutritional support. Some patients overestimated the adequacy of their dietary intake, while others minimized the role of nutrition in their recovery.

P6: I really pay attention to nutrition management. I eat vegetables, fish, and meat every day.

P5: Nutrition management does not work for me. My liver function is poor, and eating is actually a burden; I can just manage with some thin porridge every day.

H1 (Nurse): When it comes to nutrition management, we are actually not doing enough. It’s not just a problem for the patients; we healthcare professionals also need to keep learning.

H3 (Physician): Regarding nutritional support for cancer patients, we sometimes do not take it seriously. We might just briefly mention it during discharge education and then fail to follow up.

Lack of Knowledge Limits Autonomous Decision-Making

Insufficient nutritional knowledge left patients vulnerable to misinformation and unable to make informed dietary choices.

P13: Others say I should not eat fibrous foods, but I do not know what foods contain fiber. I think vegetables and whole grains do, so I just avoid them.

P1: My family often makes fish soup that’s very rich, but I never eat the fish because I believe all the nutrition is in the soup; I only drink the broth.

P3: The traditional Chinese medicine doctor told me not to eat seafood, mushrooms, coarse grains, or even winter melon. He said they would be bad for my intestines. I do not know if he’s right, and I have no idea what else I can eat.

P10: The more experienced nurses tell us to strengthen our nutrition, but since they are not nutrition specialists, they do not know specifically what we should eat.

Conflicting Nutritional Support Information

Patients frequently received contradictory dietary advice from different healthcare providers, fellow patients, family members, and online sources, creating confusion and undermining confidence in nutritional guidance.

P4: I felt nauseous after drinking milk, so I asked the doctor. The doctor said milk is fine, but the person in the next bed said I should not drink milk at all and that it’s better to have camel or goat milk. I do not know whose advice to follow.

H3 (Physician): We usually advise patients to ensure adequate intake of high-quality protein. However, sometimes patients come to us with prescriptions from the Traditional Chinese Medicine department, asking if they should avoid certain ‘hair items” (fa wu). There is no unified nutritional guidance protocol between our department and the TCM department, which leaves patients confused.

H4 (Physician): The nutritional plans we provide during consultations are based on clinical evidence. But when we follow up, we find that many patients do not adhere to them. The attending nurses or fellow patients might tell them, “This is medicine, it’s not good to take too much”, or “That stuff is too artificial”. This non-professional intervention often prevents our plans from being implemented.

Questioning the Effectiveness of Nutritional Management

Skepticism about the tangible benefits of nutritional management was expressed by both HCPs and patients, with some viewing it as secondary to pharmacological treatment.

H3 (Physician): If the patient has a good appetite, there’s no need for nutritional support intervention; just a verbal education is enough. If they cannot eat, then we consider using parenteral nutrition.

H7 (Physician): Nutritional management does not significantly improve the patient’s condition. If their protein levels are not very low, then nutrition is not taken very seriously.

P9: Doctor, I have been eating according to what you said, but the chemotherapy side effects have not eased at all. Listening to you has not helped!

Theme 3: Barriers to Achieving Effective Nutritional Support

Desire for Professional Personalized Support

Patients described feeling isolated in managing their nutrition, citing insufficient emphasis from HCPs on dietary needs, vague and contradictory guidance, and a lack of individualized recommendations.

P12: I feel a bit lonely in the process of choosing food because the healthcare system is not designed to address these issues.

P8: I just hope the doctor can provide clear eating guidelines, like what I can eat and what I cannot. But the doctor did not give me a clear answer; they just told me to figure it out on my own.

P7: The advice from doctors is inconsistent. One tells me to eat more vegetables and fruits, while another says not to eat too many fiber-rich foods. I do not know who to listen to.

Cultural Dietary Constraints on Food Choices

Traditional cultural beliefs and folk dietary customs significantly shaped patients’ eating behaviors, often leading to unnecessarily restrictive diets.

P3: The clinic doctor said I should not eat rooster or carp, and my relatives said I cannot eat goose or pumpkin; they all say these are “triggering foods”, so I have not eaten any of them since.

H1 (Nurse): Many patients’ dietary taboos do not come from doctors, but from elderly family members or fellow villagers. For example, some patients have low white blood cell counts and need protein, but their families insist that “fish is a hair item (fa wu) and will cause cancer recurrence”. No matter how we explain, we cannot convince them. This kind of deeply ingrained belief is even harder to overcome than correcting the malnutrition itself.

H2 (Nurse): One of my patients developed radiation enteritis. I advised her to eat light, easily digestible high-quality protein. However, her daughter told me that their hometown tradition dictates that “when you’re sick, you can only drink porridge; eating meat traps the illness inside the body”. The patient herself firmly believed this, and as a result, she lost more than 5 kg in one month.

Economic Factors Restricting Adequate Nutritional Intake

Financial constraints posed a significant barrier to maintaining adequate nutrition, with patients reporting an inability to afford high-quality food, supplements, or professional nutritional consultation.

P7: This society is very realistic; everything costs money! I do not have money, so I can only eat steamed buns and pickled vegetables. How can I manage my diet and eat fish and shrimp? It’s just unrealistic to talk to me about dietary management.

P5: I know that a balanced diet is important, but that’s something for wealthy people. We cannot access nutritionists; we just eat whatever we have at home.

H5 (Nurse): I remember there was a patient in the ward whose family was not well-off; he could not bear to spend money on food. Another patient in the next bed even gave him 200 yuan to buy something to eat.

Discussion

This study concurrently explored the perspectives of cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT and multidisciplinary HCPs regarding nutritional support needs. Our findings reveal a substantial gap between the recognition of nutritional needs and their effective management in clinical practice, shaped by intersecting cognitive, cultural, and systemic factors.

Multidimensional Nutritional Needs and the Knowledge–Practice Gap

The finding that patients exhibited significant deficits in nutritional knowledge and symptom self-management skills is consistent with prior research demonstrating that cancer patients often lack adequate preparation for managing treatment-related nutritional challenges.18,19 However, our study extends these findings by revealing that knowledge deficits are not confined to patients alone—HCPs themselves acknowledged gaps in their nutritional expertise, consistent with international survey data showing that oncologists and nurses across multiple countries report inadequate training in clinical nutrition.9,10 A recent Irish qualitative study similarly found that cancer patients perceived healthcare providers’ dietary guidance as vague and inconsistent,12 echoing the experiences reported by our participants.20 These converging findings across cultural contexts suggest that the knowledge–practice gap in oncology nutrition is a widespread, systemic issue rather than one limited to specific healthcare settings. Addressing it requires embedding evidence-based nutritional education into routine oncology care pathways and strengthening the nutritional competencies of both patients and providers.

Cognitive Biases and Conflicting Information as Barriers

A key finding of this study is the pervasiveness of cognitive biases—including limited awareness of malnutrition risk, overreliance on personal beliefs, and skepticism toward nutritional interventions—among both patients and HCPs. These findings align with studies reporting that cancer clinicians often underestimate the prevalence and impact of malnutrition9 and that patients may hold misconceptions about the role of diet in cancer outcomes.21 Importantly, our data illuminate how these biases are amplified by the fragmented nature of nutritional guidance in the Chinese healthcare context, where patients simultaneously encounter advice from oncologists, nurses, TCM practitioners, fellow patients, and family members. The resulting information conflicts not only erode patients’ trust in professional recommendations but also create conditions in which non-evidence-based folk beliefs gain traction. This phenomenon has been documented in other cultural settings—for instance, studies among inflammatory bowel disease patients in Western countries have similarly described the distress caused by contradictory dietary advice from different healthcare providers20—but the influence of traditional Chinese dietary concepts (such as fa wu) and family-centered decision-making adds a culturally specific layer of complexity. These findings underscore the need for unified, evidence-based nutritional guidance protocols developed through multidisciplinary consensus.

Structural and Cultural Barriers to Nutritional Support

Our findings highlight three interconnected barriers: inconsistent professional guidance, culturally rooted dietary restrictions, and economic constraints. The absence of standardized nutritional care pathways and clear role delineation among oncology, nursing, and nutrition teams resulted in fragmented and sometimes contradictory advice—a challenge also reported in oncology settings internationally.22,23 Cultural dietary beliefs, particularly the concept of fa wu, exerted a powerful influence on patients’ food choices, often overriding medical advice. While culturally sensitive care is widely advocated, our data suggest that uncritical accommodation of folk dietary restrictions can exacerbate malnutrition risk. Economic barriers—including the cost of high-quality food, nutritional supplements, and professional dietary consultation—further restricted patients’ ability to implement nutritional recommendations, a concern echoed in global literature on cancer-related financial toxicity. Collectively, these findings indicate that effective nutritional support for this population requires a multi-level approach: at the clinical level, multidisciplinary teams with clear role definitions and evidence-based protocols; at the cultural level, culturally adapted nutrition education that respectfully addresses folk beliefs while promoting scientifically sound dietary practices; and at the policy level, mechanisms to reduce the financial burden of nutritional support for cancer patients.

Implications for Practice and Research

These findings have several implications. First, oncology departments should establish multidisciplinary nutritional support teams that include dietitians, nurses, and physicians with clearly defined roles and a unified communication protocol. Second, nutritional education programs should be developed that are not only evidence-based but also culturally adapted—explicitly addressing common folk dietary beliefs while providing practical, affordable dietary strategies. Third, post-discharge nutritional follow-up mechanisms, such as nurse-led telephone consultations or mobile health applications,24 could address the consultation gap identified by patients.25 Future research should evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of such multidisciplinary, culturally tailored interventions through rigorous implementation studies. Multi-center qualitative studies across diverse regions of China, as well as cross-cultural comparative research, would further elucidate how economic, cultural, and healthcare system factors interact to shape nutritional support experiences.

Limitations and Strengths

This study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital in eastern China with relatively well-developed resources; the experiences reported may not fully represent clinical settings with different resource levels. Future research would benefit from multi-center designs and more diverse sampling. Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. It is among the first to simultaneously examine the perspectives of both cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT and multidisciplinary HCPs, revealing convergences and divergences in their experiences of nutritional support. By systematically identifying context-specific barriers—including the influence of folk dietary customs, family-centered decision-making, and economic constraints on nutritional intake within the Chinese healthcare setting—the study provides locally grounded insights that can inform clinical practice and intervention design.

Conclusion

Cervical cancer patients undergoing CCRT have multifaceted nutritional support needs that remain inadequately addressed due to cognitive biases, culturally rooted dietary restrictions, fragmented professional guidance, and economic barriers. Establishing multidisciplinary nutritional support teams with standardized protocols, developing culturally adapted nutrition education, and implementing post-discharge follow-up mechanisms may improve nutritional outcomes in this population.
