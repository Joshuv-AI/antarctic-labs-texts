Interaction between sleep disorder and depression on early onset hypertension: a machine learning modelling approach
Authors: Fen Liu, Wen-Han Wang, Hong-Wei Pan, Jin He, Ya-Yu You, Zheng-Yu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe interrelationship between sleep disorders, depression, and hypertension remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the associations between these conditions.MethodsThe National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data categorized patients into hypertensive and non-hypertensive groups, with propensity score matching (PSM) applied for demographic adjustment. Weighted multivariate adjusted logistic regression identified independent risk factors for hypertension, specifically focusing on early-onset hypertension in the subgroup analysis. Restricted cubic spline analysis assessed the relationship between sleep duration, PHQ-9 score, and hypertension. An additive interaction model evaluated the combined effects of sleep disorders and depression on hypertension risk. Additionally, seven machine learning models were established to evaluate their predictive capacity for hypertension incidence. Finally, two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis determined the causal relationships between sleep disorders, depression, and hypertension.ResultsOf the 21,453 participants, 9,055 from each group remained after PSM. Weighted multivariate logistic regression showed that short and long sleep durations and sleep disorders independently increased hypertension risk by 17.2%, 35.8%, and 48.6%, respectively (p < 0.001). The association between sleep duration and hypertension was nonlinear, with an optimal duration of 6.92 h (p < 0.001). Depression was associated with a 44.1% increased hypertension risk (p < 0.001), showing no nonlinear relationship with the PHQ-9 score (p = 0.132). Compared with the overall hypertensive population, early-onset hypertension demonstrated stronger associations with short sleep duration, sleep disorders, and depression. The additive interaction model indicated that sleep disorders and depression combinations accounted for 21.4% of hypertension cases. The XGBoost, DT, LR, and RF models exhibited strong predictive capabilities for hypertension incidence. Two-sample MR analysis supported causal relationships between sleep disorders (OR = 1.497, 95% CI: 1.258–1.782), depression (OR = 1.266, 95% CI: 1.122–1.428), and hypertension.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the complex associations between sleep disorders, depression, and hypertension, particularly in early-onset cases. The findings indicate that sleep disturbance and depression independently cause hypertension, and their interaction contributes significantly to the overall hypertension burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01778-y.

Introduction

Hypertension is a pivotal preventable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and mortality, affecting approximately 30% of the global population [1]. The increasing prevalence of hypertension, driven by the ageing population and unhealthy lifestyle factors, has led to an increase in the incidence of hypertension among younger individuals [2]. Early-onset hypertension, defined as the onset of hypertension before the age of 35, is characterized by higher heritability and an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality, particularly from coronary artery disease [3–6]. Despite the availability of effective treatments, public awareness, and treatment rates, hypertension control remains suboptimal, with only 37.6% of individuals achieving adequate blood pressure control [7]. This highlights the need for a more comprehensive clinical approach to hypertension prevention, management, and treatment.

Sleep disturbances, including insufficient sleep, poor sleep quality, and sleep disorders, are associated with various health issues, including hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and metabolic disorders [8–10]. Sleep disorders are believed to influence blood pressure regulation through diverse mechanisms such as sympathetic nervous system activation [11], endothelial dysfunction [12], and activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [13]. However, clinical studies of the relationship between sleep disorders and hypertension have yielded conflicting results. An observational study indicates that insomnia increases the risk of hypertension by 27% (OR = 3.27; 95% CI: 1.20–8.96) [14], while another cohort study found no association between sleep duration and hypertension in Older adults [15].

Depression is a prevalent mental disorder with high incidence and relapse rates [16] and affects approximately 4% of the global population [17]. Studies suggest that depression can lead to autonomic nervous system dysfunction, inflammation, immune system activation, and lifestyle changes, potentially contributing to elevated blood pressure [18, 19]. Conversely, hypertension may increase susceptibility to depression, suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship [20]. Additionally, sleep disorders, both as a symptom and an independent risk factor for depression, are associated with its severity and other mental health issues. [21]. However, current research on the interrelationship of these three conditions is limited, and further studies are needed to identify new preventive and therapeutic strategies against hypertension.

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is an ongoing cross-sectional study conducted by the National Centre for Health Statistics, aiming at assessing the nutritional status and emerging public health issues within the U.S. population [22]. NHANES offers high-quality, large-scale, nationally representative data for evaluating associations between sleep profiles, depression, and hypertension risk. Mendelian randomisation (MR) represents an epidemiological method for causal inference using genome-wide association studies (GWAS) data. This approach uses random allele segregation in populations to simulate random assignment, employing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables to estimate exposure effects on outcomes [23]. Due to the irreversible and random allocation of genetic traits to clinical phenotypes, MR analyses based on large-scale population data are less susceptible to confounding factors and reverse causation. Consequently, MR is a valuable supplementary approach when clinical randomised controlled trials are not feasible.

Therefore, this study aimed to 1) utilise data from the NHANES database to assess the independent risk factors for hypertension and early-onset hypertension; 2) investigate the quantitative relationship between sleep duration, sleep trouble/disorders, depression 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) scores, and hypertension; 3) evaluate the synergistic interaction between sleep disorders and depression in relation to hypertension; and 4) employ data from GWAS databases to assess the causal relationships among sleep disorders, depression, and hypertension.

Methods

Study design

The study was conducted in two stages. First, the NHANES database described the observational associations between sleep profiles, depression, and hypertension. Subsequently, we performed MR analysis using summary statistics from GWAS to investigate the potential causal relationships between sleep disorders, depression, and hypertension.

Study population in NHANES

Data from the National Institutes of Health website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm) were downloaded for the years 2005–2014, resulting in 50,965 patient records after data aggregation, integration, and cleaning. Based on missing data, the following exclusions were made: 1) 24,973 patients were excluded due to missing PHQ-9 depression scores, sleep disorder data, or hypertension-related survey data; 2) 4,539 patients were excluded due to missing data on known risk factors for hypertension, such as hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, BMI, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Ultimately, 21,453 patients were included in the study, comprising 10,296 individuals in the hypertension group and 14,841 individuals in the non-hypertension group. Definition of sleep, depression, and hypertension in NHANES

Sleep duration was classified as short (less than 7 h per night), normal (7–9 h per night), or long (more than 9 h per night), with 7–9 h per night considered the reference group. Sleep duration data were obtained by responses to the question, “How much sleep do you usually get at night on weekdays or working days?” Sleep disorders and trouble sleeping were measured by answering the following questions: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have a sleep disorder?” and “Have you ever told a doctor or other health professional that you have trouble sleeping?” Depressive symptoms were measured using the PHQ-9 [24]. The PHQ-9 total score ranges from 0 to 27, with scores of 0–9 indicating no depression and 10 or higher indicating the presence of depression. There are typically four categories of depression symptoms: mild (5–9), moderate (10–14), moderate to severe (15–19), and severe (20–27).

Blood pressure measurements were conducted by trained medical professionals at a mobile examination centre using a mercury sphygmomanometer. Hypertension was defined by one of the following criteria: 1) an average blood pressure measurement from three readings with a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg; 2) self-reported physician diagnosis of hypertension; 3) the use of anti-hypertensive agents. Satisfying any one of these criteria qualifies a diagnosis of hypertension [25]. Early-onset hypertension was defined as meeting the diagnostic criteria for hypertension before the age of 35, excluding secondary causes of hypertension [26].

Covariates in NHANES

Participant characteristics included sex, age, race, marital status, education level, the ratio of family income to poverty (RIP), smoking status, alcohol status, BMI, diabetes, and hyperlipidaemia. Participants were categorised into five categories of race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races, including multi-racial) and five categories of education level (less than 9th grade, 9th-11th grade, high school graduate/GED, some college or AA degree, and college graduate or above). Marital status included married, widowed, divorced, separated, never-married, and living with a partner. The BMI was determined by dividing the weight (in kilograms) by the height squared (in meters). Smoking status was categorised into two groups: smokers (having smoked ≥ 100 cigarettes in their lifetime) and non-smokers (having smoked < 100 cigarettes in their lifetime). Alcohol consumption status was categorised into drinkers (having consumed at least 12 drinks of any type of alcoholic beverage in any year) and non-drinkers (having consumed fewer than 12 drinks of any type of alcoholic beverage in any year or none at all). Diabetes was defined by one of the following conditions: 1) HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; 2) using antidiabetic agents. 3) physician-diagnosed diabetes [27]. Hyperlipidaemia was defined by one of the following conditions: 1) total cholesterol level ≥ 200 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein level ≥ 130 mg/dL, triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL, or high-density lipoprotein levels ≤ 50 mg/dL for women and ≤ 40 mg/dL for males; 2) the use of lipid-lowering agents [28].

Machine learning

The extracted variables were utilised as features for the machine learning analysis. Seven algorithms, including extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), decision tree (DT), logistic regression (LR), multilayer perceptron (MLP), Naive Bayes classifier (NBC), K-nearest neighbour (KNN), and random forest (RF), were employed to develop classification models. The model performance, stability, and reproducibility were assessed through ten iterations of tenfold cross-validation. Receiver operating characteristic curves were plotted to evaluate the predictive performance of the models, while calibration curves were used to assess the agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the models in decision-making processes.

Data source of MR

Data were obtained from publicly available summary databases of GWAS (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/). Sleep disorder data were sourced from the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/en/access_results), which comprised 19,155 cases and 197,545 controls. The GWAS summary data for depression included 34 depression-related studies encompassing 170,756 cases and 329,443 controls. Hypertension data were obtained from the FinnGen database and included 42,857 cases and 175,935 controls. All the aforementioned data pertain to genetic polymorphisms in individuals of European ancestry.

Selection of instrumental variables in MR

The selection of instrumental variables (IVs) must satisfy three key assumptions: SNPs should be strongly associated with exposure, should not directly affect the outcome, and should influence the outcome solely through exposure while being independent of confounding factors (see Figs. 1 and 2). Two-sample Mendelian randomisation analysis

Eight different methods of two-sample Mendelian randomisation were used to estimate the causal association of sleep disorders and depression with hypertension based on the presence of heterogeneity and pleiotropy in IVs. A DT for the selection of the MR method is presented in Supplementary Figure S1. We used the IVW method for the primary analysis, considering it the most credible when IVs showed no heterogeneity or pleiotropic effects [29].

Statistical analysis

In the NHANES analysis, weights were accounted for, considering the complex probability clustering design of the statistical analysis. Continuous variables were described using mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group differences were assessed using Student's t-test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, and group comparisons were performed using the chi-square test. Multivariate adjusted logistic regression was used to assess the relationships between sleep profiles, depression, and hypertension. Three models with adjusted covariates were evaluated: 1) Model 1 was unadjusted; 2) Model 2 included age, sex, race, marital status, education level, and poverty-to-income ratio; 3) Model 3 was further adjusted for age, sex, race, RIP, education level, marital status, diabetes, BMI, hyperlipidaemia, smoking status, and alcohol status. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Subsequently, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed with matching variables, including race, marital status, education level, RIP, diabetes, BMI, hyperlipidaemia, smoking status, and alcohol status. Statistical description and logistic regression analysis were performed after PSM. Furthermore, linear regression was employed to investigate the linear relationships between variables, while Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) were used to explore non-linear relationships. An additive model was used to investigate the presence of interactions. The interaction between sleep troubles/disorders and depression related to hypertension was assessed by determining whether their combined effect exceeded the sum of their individual effects. Additive interactions were assessed using the Relative Excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion of interaction (AP), and the synergy index (S). An additive interaction was considered absent if the CI for RERI and AP were 0 and the CI for S was 1.

In the MR analysis, in addition to using IVW as the main method, weighted median, MR-Egger, maximum likelihood, and MR-pleiotropy residual sum outliers (MR-PRESSO) were also employed to evaluate the reliability and stability of the results. Compared to the IVW, weighted median methods produce consistent estimates of causal effects when effective IVs exceed 50% [30]. The maximum likelihood method was used to account for sample overlap by considering the probability of each nucleotide substitution in each group of sequence alignments and using the population stability index as a parameter [31]. The Cochran's Q test, MR-Egger intercept test, IVW, and MR-PRESSO methods were used to identify potential heterogeneity and directional pleiotropy. The intercept of the MR-Egger model was used to account for potential pleiotropy, where a deviation from 0 indicated directional pleiotropy [32]. The MR-PRESSO method was used to test and correct, if needed, the effects of outliers and horizontal pleiotropy and generate reliable causal estimates of heterogeneity [33]. The Cochran's Q test was used to assess the heterogeneity of individual genetic variation estimates [34]. If the p-value from Cochran's Q test was less than 0.05, indicating the presence of heterogeneity, a multiplicative random effects IVW model was employed; conversely, a fixed effects model was utilised. A p-value > 0.05 for MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger indicates no pleiotropy.

All analyses were conducted using the R software (Version 4.3.2). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.

Weighted logistic regression results of unmatched data

The results of the weighted logistic regression for the unmatched data are presented in Fig. 2. Based on these results, we progressively included the potential risk factors for hypertension as covariates. The final weighted multivariable logistic regression results indicate that, after adjusting for covariates, individuals who sleep less than 7 h are associated with 17.2% higher odds of hypertension compared to those who sleep 7–9 h (p < 0.001), while those who sleep more than 9 h are associated with 35.8% higher odds (p = 0.015). Patients experiencing sleep trouble and sleep disorders were associated with 48.6% higher odds of hypertension (p < 0.001), while those with depression were associated with 44.1% higher odds (p < 0.001). As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, the influence of the covariates was further minimised with the baseline characteristics and risk factors balanced between the two groups. After matching, the higher odds of hypertension in individuals sleeping less than 7 h decreased from 17.2% to 15.0% (p = 0.042). In contrast, the risk for those sleeping more than 9 h increased significantly from 35.8% to 69.3% (p = 0.010). The change in risk for patients with sleep difficulties and sleep disorders was minimal (48.6% vs. 46.6%). Meanwhile, the risk of hypertension in patients with depression decreased from 44.1% to 25.2% (p = 0.026).

RCS curve regression results

As shown in Fig. 3, the results of the RCS curve analysis indicate a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and the risk of hypertension, with an optimal curve turning point at 6.92 h (p < 0.001). This pattern was consistent for both men and women. However, for the same sleep duration, men exhibited a higher risk of hypertension than women. PHQ-9 depression scores showed no nonlinear relationship with the risk of hypertension (p = 0.132). Discussion

This study is the first to confirm that sleep disorders and depression are independent causal risk factors for hypertension, with their interactions accounting for 21.4% of all cases of hypertension. Furthermore, the XGBoost, DT, LR, and RF machine-learning models demonstrated strong predictive capabilities for hypertension incidence. We employed PSM to reduce confounding factors when assessing the impact of sleep disorders and depression on hypertension, achieving a more balanced comparison between the groups. However, PSM reduced sample size and may have introduced selection bias; therefore, the study reported combined results from both unmatched and matched data. Additionally, MR was used to assess causal relationships, adhering to stringent criteria for instrumental variable selection (p ≤ 5 × 10⁻⁸, r2 ≤ 0.001, and genetic distance ≥ 10,000 kb). Although these criteria are commonly utilised in genome-wide association studies, some scholars are concerned that they may exclude potentially valid SNPs, leading to weakened instrument bias [35]. Only three valid SNPs met the criteria to instrument sleep disorder. Although each instrument was reasonably strong (F-statistic > 10), the limited instrument set increases uncertainty (wider CIs) and susceptibility to weak-instrument bias in IVW models; therefore, the results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent and significant health threats to the population today and has become a major public health challenge, both in China and globally. Currently, approximately 31.1% of adults worldwide suffer from hypertension, amounting to 1.39 billion individuals, with incidence rates rising annually and showing a trend toward younger age groups [2]. Previous studies explored the association between sleep patterns and metabolic diseases. A cross-sectional study involving 10,308 participants found that individuals who slept 6 h per night had a 56% increased likelihood of developing hypertension compared to those who slept seven hours per night (95% CI: 1.07—2.27). Moreover, those who slept less than 5 h per night experienced a 94% increased probability of hypertension (95% CI: 1.08—3.50) [36]. A systematic review of 13 cross-sectional and longitudinal studies encompassing 225,858 participants identified a significant correlation between short sleep duration and hypertension (RR = 1.23, 95% CI:1.06–1.42) [37]. Conversely, no significant association was observed between long sleep duration and hypertension (RR = 1.02; 95% CI: 0.91–1.14) [37]. Notably, the study by Michael et al. indicated that excessively long sleep duration (≥ 10 h) is also considered a risk factor for hypertension (OR = 1.41, 95%CI: 1.33–1.49) [38]. The findings of this study indicate that after adjusting for confounding variables, the risk of developing hypertension was higher in individuals with insufficient sleep than in those with normal sleep duration (7–9 h), and this trend was also observed in patients with early-onset hypertension. These results are consistent with those of previous studies. Additionally, we identified an optimal sleep duration of approximately 7 h, at which the risk of hypertension was minimised; both shorter (< 7 h) and longer (> 9 h) sleep durations further increased the risk of hypertension. Furthermore, under the same sleep duration, men consistently appear to have a higher risk of hypertension than women, suggesting that sex differences should be particularly considered when formulating strategies for the prevention and management of hypertension.

Beyond sleep duration, other sleep-related profiles, such as sleep disorders and trouble, also contribute to an elevated risk of hypertension [39, 40]. Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between sleep and risk of hypertension. Sleep disorders may affect haemodynamics [41], circadian rhythms [42], ventilation function [43], and regulation of the central nervous system [44], leading to physiological and pathological changes in blood pressure. A cohort study of 2,667 patients with obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) showed that patients with OSA have a higher incidence of hypertension, and blood pressure appears to be dose-related to OSA [45]. Additionally, a study by Fernandez-Mendoza et al. [46] followed 1,741 participants for 7.5 years and found that individuals with insomnia problems had a higher risk of developing hypertension compared to those with normal sleep patterns (OR = 3.80, 95% CI = 1.6–9.0). Our findings were consistent with those of previous studies after adjusting for covariates, indicating that sleep problems and sleep disorders can increase the risk of hypertension and early-onset hypertension. Although the prevalence and incidence of hypertension are associated with sleep disorders, most studies have primarily employed a cross-sectional design. This study utilised a two-sample Mendelian randomisation approach to provide causal evidence.

Stress, depression, and anger are negative factors that affect sleep quality [47]. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant positive association between depression and elevated blood pressure [48]. Cohort studies have also observed that depression (OR = 1.29, 95% CI = 1.25–1.33) is a risk factor for hypertension [49]. Additionally, depression and sleep disorders mutually exacerbate each other, with sleep disorders worsening depressive symptoms [50], while insomnia and sleep difficulties can lead to anxiety and depression, creating a vicious cycle [51]. Furthermore, hypertension induced by anxiety, depression, and stress can significantly impact the sleep quality of patients with hypertension [52]. Consequently, depression and sleep disorders often coexist with other physical or mental health issues. Decreased sleep quality can lead to elevated blood pressure, weakened immunity, and psychological problems, and depression may also result in sleep difficulties and anxiety [53]. These findings align with the study results, which demonstrated that sleep disorders and depression independently increased hypertension and early-onset hypertension risk while confirming their synergistic effect. The study also provided evidence for causal relationships between sleep disorders, depression, and hypertension.

Several limitations exist in this study. The exclusion of individuals with missing sleep, depression, and blood pressure data from NHANES might have introduced selection bias. Although MR analysis detected no directional pleiotropy, residual pleiotropy might persist, a common limitation in MR studies. Despite using PSM and multivariate regression to control known confounding factors, other confounders, such as dietary and lifestyle factors, might have influenced the results. The study data predominantly represented non-Hispanic whites, limiting result generalization to other racial groups. Additional studies with larger, more diverse populations are needed to validate these findings. Data source limitations prevented determining secondary causes in hypertensive patients.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates independent associations between sleep profiles, depression, and hypertension, with synergistic effects. It confirms causal relationships between sleep disorders and depression in hypertensive patients. Future large-scale prospective studies should validate these findings and explore underlying mechanisms.
